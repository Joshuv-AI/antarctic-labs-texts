Sediment-modulated seafloor residence time controls efficient organic carbon burial in subduction zones
Authors: Mengfan Chu, Kejian Liu, Zhirong Cai, Troy Rasbury, Martin Kölling, Matthias Zabel, Cecilia McHugh, Michael Strasser, Ken Ikehara, Rui Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The subduction organic carbon (OC) flux is closely linked with seafloor sedimentation processes. In hadal trenches, sediment transport and resuspension caused by turbidity events provide rapid feedback of subducting carbon fate to seafloor sedimentation dynamics. Here we demonstrate that sediment sorting creates distinct OC burial patterns in the Japan Trench: Prolonged seafloor residence enables preferential removal of labile OC components and result in a refractory subsurface OC pool. Conversely, rapid sediment burial shortens seafloor residence time and promotes subsurface anaerobic OC degradation. We further suggest that these mechanisms result in at least 37.6% loss of the OC subduction flux in accretionary margins due to early diagenetic and thermal alteration processes, while erosive margins only loss 11% during subduction and preferentially transfer thermally stable OC into subduction zones. Our work bridges surficial depositional regimes and deep carbon transfer, highlighting the need to consider depositional settings when refining subduction zone carbon budgets.

Introduction

The organic carbon (OC) buried in marine sediments regulates Earth’s climate by sequestering atmospheric CO2 and supplementing the deep carbon reservior1–3. The journey of OC from the marine realm to the subduction zones involves complex lateral transport4,5, aging6,7, and early diagenetic processes8–10 that selectively filter the carbon fluxes into the deep Earth. These surficial processes determine the biogeochemical and thermal stability of OC prior to subduction, dictating its ultimate fate in Earth’s interior. Quantifying this surface-to-mantle OC connectivity remains a pivotal challenge for deep carbon cycle models3.

Hadal trenches ( > 6 km water depth) represent an extreme environment with dynamic OC cycling induced by tectonic forces. As the terminal OC depocenters on Earth’s surface, hadal trenches receive OC with complex biogeochemical reactivity shaped by variable sediment transport pathways across the continental margin11–13. Episodic turbidity currents drive repeated sediment resuspension14–16, which extends OC seafloor exposure time and intensifies oxidative degradation of labile components17,18. Complex trench morphology forms localized sediment depocenters with distinct sedimentation regimes, where rapid sediment burial enhances anaerobic diagenesis that establishes steep geochemical gradients across subsurface horizons10,19,20. Ultimately, these dynamic processes select the OC components that survive to the subduction zone and fuel the deep carbon cycle.

To elucidate the link between surface sedimentation processes and deep carbon dynamics, we focus on the Japan Trench, a high-energy erosive margin that experiences frequent seismically-triggered turbidity events21,22. We integrate stable and radiocarbon isotopes (δ13C, Δ14C) from three critical OC reservoirs in trench bottom waters, surface and subsurface sediments (down to 14 mbsf) to capture the OC transformation processes across the seafloor, subsurface sediment and subduction zone continuum. Our study addresses three objectives: First, to decipher the spatially-varying drivers of early diagenetic processes along the hadal trench axis; second, to compare OC preservation efficiencies under contrasting sedimentation dynamics; and third, to elucidate how seafloor processes constrain the carbon transfer to subduction zones. By bridging depositional regimes and deep carbon fluxes, our results identify hadal trenches as an active interface that modulates carbon burial and transfer within subduction zones.

Results and discussion

Prolonged OC residence at the Japan Trench seafloor induced by sediment resuspension

The carbon isotope signatures of suspended and sedimentary OC in the Japan Trench indicate a predominantly marine origin with minor terrestrial and petrogenic contributions (Fig. 1 & Text S1). The overall broad range of Δ14C values of sedimentary OC reflect the spatial heterogeneity of the OC sources entering the trench, and a broad spectrum of seafloor residence times after deposition. While the OC derives from vertical particle flux or tectonically-triggered turbidity currents23,24, the isotopic compositions are profoundly reshaped by pre- and post-depositional degradation. Early diagenetic alteration preferentially removes younger and more labile OC components, resulting in a residual OC pool enriched in older, more refractory material with lower Δ14C values (Text S1). For instance, surface trench sediments at several sites (M0087, 93 and 94) exhibit depleted Δ14C value (from –843‰ to –412‰) that exceeds values from sedimentary OC on the trench slope or suspended OC in the water column23,25 (Fig. 1), indicating prolonged seafloor residence and preferential degradation of labile OC following sediment remobilization at these sites.Fig. 1Isotopic signatures of suspended and sedimentary organic carbon (OC) in the Japan Trench.Carbon isotope characteristics (13C and 14C) of suspended (solid and open rectangles, from this study and ref. 23., respectively) and surface sedimentary OC at the Japan Trench seafloor (circles for high- and low-input sites, respectively, this study), as well as sedimentary OC at the trench slope (open circle, ref. 74.), plotted with reference endmembers. The carbon isotope difference between sedimentary and suspended carbon pools is particularly pronounced due to sediment resuspension and prolonged exposure at the seafloor, where suspended OC in trench bottom water shows systematically lower δ13C and Δ14C values than both vertically exported marine OC and slope-derived sediments (δ13C averaged –29.44‰ ± 0.05‰; Δ14C averaged –866‰ ± 80‰; Fig. 1), diverging markedly from its potential sources (Text S1). This isotopic disparity implies intense degradation within a thick, persistent benthic nepheloid layer, which is featured in tectonically-disturbed Japan Trench26. Events like the 2011 Tohoku-oki earthquake demonstrate how seismic shaking amplifies sediment resuspension and oxidative OC degradation for months16,27. Within these turbid zones, labile OC compounds are selectively remineralized, leaving 14C-depleted residues preserved in the trench sediments.

Repeated resuspension at the sediment-water interface creates different oxygen exposure times, driving divergent OC degradation patterns between suspended and sedimentary OC. Limited bottom water ventilation and the hydrodynamic isolation caused by trench morphology partially inhibits material exchange between the trench bottom water and the upper water column. Such isolation also promotes repeated degradation within the benthic nepheloid layer, where suspended OC can be reworked multiple times before final deposition. The non-unidirectional OC distribution patterns (Text S2 & Fig. S1) along the trench axis exclude the unidirectional bottom current as the primary control on OC preservation. Instead, turbidity currents orthogonally intersecting the trench may have dominated the OC transport dynamics28,29. These episodic, often seismically-triggered sediment flows repeatedly transport particles into the benthic nepheloid layer, intensifying OC degradation during transient suspension phases prior to re-deposition. Coupled with complex trench-fill topography, such progresses create localized depocenters where OC undergoes different extents of seafloor residence. Consequently, the combined effects of hydrodynamic isolation, repeated nepheloid-layer processing, and episodic turbidity-current forcing regulate OC residence times and degradation efficiency.

In essence, the sedimentation dynamic driven by localized turbidity currents and regional depositional settings regulate the OC residence and degradation at the Japan Trench seafloor. Carbon preservation in the Japan Trench is mediated by prolonged seafloor exposure induced by event-driven sediment resuspension, while downslope gravitational processes dictate spatial heterogeneity in degradation hotspots. The influence of sedimentary processes on OC residence highlights the impact of highly-variable sedimentation dynamics (resuspension, sorting, etc.) in the Japan Trench.

Hadal carbon preservation controlled by sediment sorting

To resolve how sedimentation regimes govern OC degradation, we classified Japan Trench sites by flow accumulation area (Fig. 2a), which offers a topographic metric quantifying the area of slope sediment funneled into the trench basins21. We divided the trench basin into high sediment input depocenters that bury slope-derived sediments in deep basins (water depth >7.6 km), and low-input sites that located in shallower zones with reduced sediment delivery (see the average flow accumulation areas of the two groups in Fig. 2). This classification does not represent a strict sediment delivery boundary, but rather serves as a practical framework for contrasting depositional settings in the trench. For instance, even within a high-input basin, not all areas necessarily receive uniformly high sediment flux. Nonetheless, compared to sedimentation rates, this approach transcends the spatiotemporally variable turbidity events and thereby emphasizes the role of morphology in OC transport and preservation30,31. Low-input sites exhibit OC features of sediment sorting during prolonged seafloor exposure including reduced TOC content and older 14C age (Fig. 2b). Particularly, the medium-grained sediment fraction (20–63 μm) exhibits a relatively low shear stress and is therefore more easily entrained by bottom currents. As a result, this “sortable silt” fraction is prone to repeated deposition-resuspension cycles32–35, leading to prolonged residence at the sediment-water interface and enhanced exposure to oxic conditions. Consistent with this behavior, this fraction exhibits the most depleted δ13C signature (Fig. 2b), indicating intensified oxidative degradation and aging of OC associated with repeated sediment-water exchange and lateral transport. These dynamics remove more modern OC in low-input systems than in high-input systems (Fig. 2c, see “Method” for interpretation), leading to preservation of a more refractory sedimentary OC pool.

Conversely, the conventional grain-size control on OC preservation is not always applicable in high-input regions. The coarse fraction ( > 63 μm) preserves more OC than the finer fraction ( < 20 μm, Fig. 2b) despite the fact that fine-fraction sediments generally retain abundant OC4,6. This reflects rapid turbidity-driven burial that sequesters coarse particles before oxidative OC degradation occurs, while the fine particles remain trapped in suspension clouds. Higher Δ14C disparities between suspended and sedimentary OC (Table S1) at the high-input sites also indicate that sedimentary OC in these regions is rapidly buried before significant intra-fraction Δ14C differences can develop, whereas suspended OC undergoes prolonged residence in the benthic nepheloid layer (Text S3).

Basin morphology also modulates OC fate through regulating sediment input. Thick turbidite muds, which characteristically contained aggregates of clay particles, can be deposited by ponding of highly sediment-concentrated bottom waters following major earthquakes36,37. Such ponding depends not only on the supply of sediment but also on basin geometry and slope morphology. For example, Site M0090 locates in a small and shallow basin, which limits the formation of thick ponded mud layers, resulting in relatively thinner turbidites compared to the deeper Site M0083 (Fig. S2) and lower sediment input (Fig. 2a). While the low-input sites exhibit conventional pattern where the <20 μm fraction particles account for most of the sediment specific surface area (SSA), the coarser fractions show more influence on the SSA in high-input sites (Text S4). This implies that basin size, connectivity, and morphology exert a critical control on the mineral protection and the subsequent burial of OC.

Together, these contrasts highlight sediment input regime and trench morphology as the primary controllers of carbon cycling in the trench. Low-input settings act as oxidative filters where resuspension cycles selectively mineralize labile OC, while high-input zones function as a rapid transfer pathway that preserve diverse OC components. It should be noted that both pathways are tectonically mediated: rapid sediment deposition can dominate the early stage of a turbidity flow, followed by a period of sediment stasis and extended OC residence in the nepheloid layers38,39 that allows for bioturbation in the top of event deposit40. The role of local morphology in shaping deposition and residence time further emphasizes the importance of integrating both input and geomorphological context when assessing OC fate in tectonically-active hadal trenches. This sedimentological filtering should be considered to accurately quantify carbon burial flux in the hadal trenches.

Impact of surface sedimentation dynamics on subsurface carbon cycling

We next investigate how the contrasting depositional settings influence OC degradation during subsurface burial in the trench. By exploiting the CE 869 Jogan Earthquake deposit as a chronostratigraphic marker38 (Fig. S2 & Text S5), we trace the evolution of the OC pool over 1,152 years of burial (CE 869 to CE 2021). The decrease of modern OC content (Fig. S2) in high-input regions indicates stronger subsurface degradation (11.5% modern OC loss vs. 7.5% in low-input systems, see Method for calculation), in contrast to their preservation advantage at the seafloor. On the other hand, prolonged seafloor exposure at low-input sites pre-ages OC, leaving refractory fractions that are less degradable by organoclastic sulfate reduction upon burial. Lower OC degradation rate in the low-input regions, despite comparable burial durations, is further confirmed by porewater sulfate and alkalinity profiles (Text S6). Specifically, high-input sites show a more rapid increase in alkalinity relative to sulfate consumption, consistent with enhanced organoclastic sulfate reduction, which generates more alkalinity per mole of sulfate reduced than anaerobic oxidation of methane in such depth. This steeper alkalinity-to-sulfate gradient reflects a stronger contribution of organoclastic sulfate reduction and thus more intensive OC mineralization in the subsurface at high-input sites. In contrast, the muted alkalinity production relative to sulfate drawdown at low-input sites indicates weaker organoclastic sulfate reduction, consistent with the predominance of refractory OC that is less susceptible to microbial degradation. In addition, reaction-transport modeling simulations yield substantially lower total OC degradation rates in low-input regions compared to high-input regions41. The consistently lower degradation rates at low-input sites support the interpretation that reduced OC reactivity limits subsurface OC degradation in these settings.

In tectonically active hadal trenches, such decoupling mechanism generates a series of carbon cycling processes including instant burial of labile OC component, extended oxidative degradation of suspended OC during gradual settling, and subsurface reactivation of buried OC. This creates a highly dynamic trench carbon cycle shaped by mass-wasting events42. Furthermore, the sedimentation-controlled dynamics partition carbon among distinct reservoirs in the trench. In low-input regions, the degradation products are released to the water column during extended seafloor residence cycles (Fig. 3a). Conversely, rapid sediment burial in high-input zones transfers reactive OC to subsurface realms, supplementing porewater dissolved organic and inorganic carbon reservoirs through accelerated anaerobic transformations. Large earthquakes can induce rapid shifts of the seafloor morphology, followed by transient depositional processes lasting for months39. Our findings thus highlight the need for incorporating hydrodynamic context and OC diagenetic history for accurate understanding the carbon cycling in such systems. Our 14C measurements also reveal that episodic seafloor perturbations and post-depositional processes can introduce significant inter-fraction age offsets. These biases, if unrecognized, may distort age model construction in carbonate-poor sediments43–45 and impede carbon flux estimation.

As one of the most seismically active hadal trenches worldwide, the Japan Trench recieves a sediment delivery that is overwhelmingly dominated by earthquake-triggered turbidity currents46–48. Episodic seismic events thus control sediment accumulation along the trench axis. OC input associated with background hemipelagic sedimentation is several orders of magnitude lower24, highlighting the dominant role of event-driven sediment transfer in shaping the trench sedimentary environment. This tectonically modulated sediment supply provides a unique natural setting to investigate how episodic sedimentation governs the fate of OC in hadal systems. The large volumes of OC episodically transferred into the trench by seismic events are subject to transformation within the trench’s unique depositional and hydrodynamic environment. For example, recent work implies that trench morphology and localized circulation can modulate carbon cycling processes in near-bottom waters49. Furthermore, carbon transformations occurring at the water-sediment interface regulate both the quantity and reactivity of OC transferred to deeper sediment layers, which in turn control microbially mediated carbon cycling in subsurface sediments19,50.

Together, these studies emphasized an active trench biogeochemical environment where OC undergoes significant alteration before long-term burial. However, without quantitatively linking depositional regimes to subsequent subsurface OC degradation and long-term burial efficiency, the extent to which surface sedimentation dynamics ultimately regulate carbon fluxes into subduction zones has remained unresolved. The present study advances these earlier efforts by providing a process-based framework that directly links surface depositional regimes to subsurface OC degradation and preservation. This conceptual bridge extends the perspective of trench carbon cycling from surface accumulation to deep burial and enables estimation of how contrasting sedimentation scenarios modulate the magnitude and composition of carbon ultimately entering subduction zones.

The tectonic filter on subduction zone carbon fluxes

Surface sedimentation dynamics may further regulate the thermal vulnerability of the subducting carbon pool by modulating the composition of OC delivered to depth, particularly the relative proportions of thermally labile versus refractory components, which directly determine how much OC is susceptible to early thermal transformation during subduction. Ramped pyrolysis-oxidation analysis of Japan Trench sediments reveals three distinct OC fractions with varying thermal stabilities (Fig. S6). The thermal fraction that decomposes at lowest temperature (C1 at <200 °C), which is often enriched in labile OC components like cellulose51–53, is more abundant in the high-input sites (8.2% of total OC versus 3.8% in low-input systems, Fig. S7). This temperature threshold corresponds to ~20 km subduction depth at the Japan Trench54, where compounds with low thermochemical stability undergo rapid transformation as temperature increases. Moreover, refractory OC can become progressively more reactive during burial, as increasing temperature and pressure alter molecular structures and weaken protective associations with minerals. Such burial-induced activation enhances the susceptibility of previously preserved OC to thermochemical and microbial degradation55. High-input environments thus have the potential to shuttle substantial thermally vulnerable carbon into subduction zone, amplifying the spatial heterogeneity in deep carbon fluxes3,56.

On global scale, the carbon budget of subduction zones is largely governed by the amount and nature of sediment delivered to the trench and the thermal structure of the forearc. Sediment supply also plays a first-order role in controlling the accretionary versus erosive characters of convergent margins. We present a conceptual framework designed to quantify how much of the deposited OC is transformed prior to subduction under different sedimentation regimes, emphasizing depositional and early diagenetic processes within the trench in disregard of OC sources prior to trench delivery. Large sediment input typically promotes accretionary wedge growth, whereas sediment-starved systems tend toward forearc erosion57,58. Accretion, and the tendency of accretionary margins to have steeper forearc geothermal gradients48, promote thermal alteration of OC3. The accretionary setting shows similarity with our high-input trench scenario with enhanced sediment input, rapid burial of labile carbon components and intensive carbon alteration in the subsurface (Fig. 3). Conversely, sediment-starved erosive margins receive smaller but more refractory OC fluxes. Although some erosive margins may experience high surface productivity (e.g., the Peru-Chile and Japan Trenches59–61), the overall sediment flux delivered to the trench is typically low. In such settings, even if substantial phytoplankton-derived OC is produced, these surface-derived OC undergoes prolonged exposure at the seafloor, promoting extensive oxidative degradation and thereby limiting the fraction of labile OC that can be buried into deeper sediment layers. Thus, differences in OC source (e.g., surface-derived versus laterally transported) are ultimately filtered by the sedimentation regime, which governs residence time and early diagenetic alteration prior to subduction. In summary, limited burial, as conceptually represented by our low-input trench scenario where prolonged seafloor exposure selectively preserves refractory fractions, restrain OC degradation to the seafloor, allowing stable organic compounds to transit deeper (Fig. 3).

Compared to the fate of inorganic carbon, the global subduction dynamics of OC remain less constrained62,63. Surface sediment OC content is often used to approximate the OC subduction flux56 assuming minor alteration prior to subduction. Our findings demand recalibration of global subducting carbon budgets, as conventional flux models underestimate OC transformation during sedimentary transit. Our quantification using the “high-input” and “low input” as a conceptual model for accretionary and erosive margins, respectively, shows that accretionary systems lose ~37.6% of trench-delivered carbon through early diagenesis and thermal alteration before deep subduction (Table 1). At accretionary margins, despite high sediment input leading to elevated OC delivery to the trench, a significant fraction of this OC may be lost to early degradation and pyrolazation during burial (11.5% and 5.6%, respectively, Fig. 4 & Table 1). In comparison, erosive margins exhibit inverse efficiency, with smaller initial carbon flux yielding proportionally greater deep transfer due to enrichment of refractory components (with only 11% losses to early degradation and pyrolazation, Fig. 4 & Table 1). After acounting for the OC loss prior to subduction, the difference of OC subduction fluxes is less than that of OC input fluxes between accretionary and erosive margins. These distinctions underscore the need of a subduction flux model that accounts for pre-subduction degradation modulated by seafloor residence time, OC thermal lability governed by depositional regimes, as well as tectonically-driven partitioning between shallow and deep carbon reservoirs. Instead of discriminate between OC sources (i.e., inputs associated with productivity or turbidity events), this model captures the net depositional outcome that regulates the extent of OC alteration prior to subduction. By focusing on depositional dynamics within the trench, we isolate sedimentation-controlled burial efficiency as a higher-order regulator that modulates source variability into measurable differences in degradation intensity prior to subduction.

Ultimately, hadal trenches emerge as reactive filters of Earth’s subducting carbon. Surface transient processes at the trench floor like turbidity currents creates pulsed burial events that cascade through subsurface and subduction systems, profoundly modulating OC fate and representing a geological feedbacks to plate subduction. The varying contribution of organic components to the total subduction carbon flux among accretionary and erosive settings may further result in spatial variation of manle composition. Future modeling should integrate these sedimentological filters with reaction kinetics to resolve how episodic deposition shapes the spatial and temporal varying deep carbon cycling. Such advances will position trenches as dynamic regulators in Earth’s carbon-climate system and sensitive recorders of geological disturbances.

Methods

Research area

The Japan Trench is a convergent margin where the Pacific plate subducts beneath the Okhotsk plate with a convergence rate of 80–86 mm/yr64. It is ~700 km long and ~200 km east of the Japan coastline65. Water depths at the trench floor are ~6.8 km in the northern part of the trench and increase southward to more than 8 km47,66. The Ogawara and Nakaminato canyons connect the Japan Trench with the continental shelf in the northern and southern trench, respectively. Although these canyon systems may facilitate limited downslope gravity-driven sediment transport during periods of reduced seismic activity, they have not been reported as major sediment-routing pathways to the trench floor, rendering seismically induced turbidity currents as the major sediment input pathway in the Japan Trench21,47,48. Accordingly, background gravitational transport is likely overwhelmed by episodic earthquake-triggered mass-wasting events. Active bending-related deformation of the subducting oceanic plate results in semi-isolated trench basins formed in horst-and-graben structures along the trench axis67, with typical vertical relief of a few hundreds of meters. These basins serve as an ideal location for sediment accumulation, effectively trapping sediments delivered by turbidites at the trench floor. The sedimentation rate in the Japan Trench is relatively high (100–400 cm/kyr66) due to frequent seismic events in the subduction zone42, while the average background sedimentation rates (i.e., approximated hemipelagic sedimentation) ranges between 80–300 cm/kyr in the central Japan trench66 with an average around 225 cm/kyr38.

Sample collection

Sediment samples were collected on R/V Kaimei during the International Ocean Discovery Program (IODP) Expedition 386 in 2021. Surface sediment samples were taken from the top 2 cm of the pilot cores (top 3 cm for Core M0094B), while subsurface sediment samples were taken from giant piston cores at 2 cm intervals (Table S2). Suspended OC was filtered through a pre-combusted GF/F filter from 1 L bottom water sample which was added with 1 mL 10% HCl. All samples are stored at –20°C before analysis. It should be noted that the giant piston core records (e.g., the depth framework in Fig. S2) may lack the uppermost sediment layers due to core recovery limitations as discussed in ref. 38,68. Therefore, aligning pilot core data (used for surface sedimentary OC) with piston core stratigraphy does not reflect the true absolute depth scale. However, since our analysis focuses on relative age differences between surface and subsurface sedimentary OC without considering depth-related sedimentation rate, these depth uncertainties do not affect the interpretation of OC aging patterns.

Mineral specific surface area analysis

The mineral specific surface area (SSA) of bulk sediment samples was determined using a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) technique on a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 surface area analyzer at Qingdao Institute of Bioenergy and Bioprocess Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Prior to analysis, samples were pre-combusted at 350 °C for 12 hours to remove organic matter. SSA was calculated from N2 adsorption data using a five-point BET method.

Total organic carbon content and isotopic analysis

Sediment samples were separated into different grain-size fractions ( < 20 µm, 20–63 µm, >63 µm) by wet sieving. Bulk sediments were passed through a series of stainless-steel sieves (63 μm and 20 μm mesh sizes, Retsch GmbH, Germany) under continuous rinsing with Milli-Q water69. The >63 μm and 20–63 μm fractions were washed off the sieves with Milli-Q water, while the <20 μm fraction was collected from the filtrate. All fractions were obtained as slurries, subsequently freeze-dried, and homogenized prior to geochemical analyses.

For sedimentary OC, acid fumigation was used as the pretreatment method for carbon content and isotopic analyses. Samples were HCl-fumigated at 60 °C for 72 h, followed by NaOH-fumigated at 60 °C for 72 h to neutralize the excess acid. TOC contents were measured by an elemental analyzer at Ocean University of China. The Elemental Technology Co. Ltd. reference material IVA33802180 was used as a routine working standard for carbon content determination of sediment samples. Samples were weighed into tin capsules before introduction into the EA. The single combustion/reduction reactor of the EA was maintained at 980°C, with the helium flow at 180 mL/min and the oxygen flow at 250 mL/min, and an O2 injection end time of 5 s. δ13C values were measured by an isotope ratio mass spectrometer at the National Ocean Sciences Accelerator Mass Spectrometry Facility (NOSAMS), Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, and are reported relative to Peedee Belemnite. Samples were weighed into tin capsules and combusted in the EA at 1025 °C. The generated CO2 was then trapped and separated from the helium carrier gas. Approximately 7% of the gas was taken for δ13C measurement via an isotope ratio mass spectrometer. The remaining gas was reduced with H2 to graphite and was taken for 14C measurement via an accelerator mass spectrometer by standard procedures at NOSAMS70. OX-I normalizing standards and secondary standards including IAEA C-2 and OX-II were routinely run at NOSAMS to estimate the measurement precision and accuracy of the instrument70.

Carbon degradation estimation in subsurface sediments

Assuming that the modern (radiocarbon-active) fraction of OC represents the more labile component that is preferentially degraded during burial, we applied a degradation correction factor derived from measurements in the Japan Trench to estimate OC loss via early degradation. Ramped pyrolysis-oxidation (RPO) analysis

Prior to RPO analysis, each grain-size fraction was pretreated with acid rinsing72. Specifically, 10 mL of 1 N HCl was added to the sample in a Teflon bottle, which was then agitated and incubated in a water bath at 60°C for 1 h. After centrifugation (1,080 g), the supernatant was removed by pipetting and decanting. To eliminate residual acid, the sample was rinsed three times with 10 mL Milli-Q water following the same agitation and centrifugation steps. The final residues were vacuum-filtered onto pre-combusted GF/F filters using a glass filtration setup, placed in glass Petri dishes, and dried under vacuum for 24 h.

RPO analysis were performed using an Organic and Elemental Carbon (OEC) analyzer (Model 5L-NDIR-O₂, Sunset Laboratory, INC, USA) following the procedure detailed in ref. 73. In brief, the dried sample was weighed and placed in a quartz reactor, where it was subjected to a controlled linear heating ramp from room temperature to 870 °C at 20°C/min. As the sample decomposed, carbon fractions were oxidized to CO2 in a manganese dioxide combustion furnace, and the resulting CO2 was continuously quantified.

The evolved CO2 signal from RPO analysis was deconvolved into three Gaussian-distributed components corresponding to distinct thermal stability windows (C1, C2, and C3). The fitting was performed using non-linear least squares regression in OriginPro employing the Peak Fit (Gaussian) module. Each thermogram was modeled using three Gaussian peaks superimposed on a constant baseline, with peak centers typically located around ~174°C (C1), ~343°C (C2), and ~534°C (C3). These thermal components represent OC pools with progressively increasing resistance to thermal decomposition. Peak fitting achieved generally high goodness-of-fit, with reduced chi-square values < 1 and R2 > 0.99. In addition to grain-size-specific profiles, a weight-normalized RPO reconstruction was further performed using the dry weight proportion of each grain-size fraction to produce integrated thermal profiles and component quantification for the bulk sediment (Fig. S7).
