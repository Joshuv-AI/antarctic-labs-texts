Physical activity in age-friendly cities and communities: a scoping review with recommendations to support policy and practice
Authors: Patricia C Jackman, Emily J Dargue-Fox, Rebecca M Hawkins, Niamh Murphy, Melanie Clarke, Christian Swann, Mo Ray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the global population continues to age, there is growing international interest in creating environments that support older persons’ health and wellbeing. Age-friendly cities and communities (AFCCs) aim to foster physical and social settings that enable healthy and active ageing through targeted policies, services, and infrastructure. Physical activity (PA) plays a vital role in promoting healthy ageing; however, limited research has explored how AFCCs integrate and promote PA. To address this gap, we conducted a scoping review to systematically map and synthesise the existing literature on PA in AFCCs. Following comprehensive searches of five electronic databases (APA PsycINFO, CINAHL Complete, MEDLINE, SOCIndex, SPORTDiscus) and the World Health Organisation’s age-friendly practices database up to May 2025, we included 59 research outputs and 77 practices across 35 countries. Our review demonstrates the wide range of opportunities that exist within and across the eight age-friendly domains to support PA opportunities for older persons. However, the findings also highlight the importance of considering a range of factors—spanning intrapersonal, interpersonal, environmental, cultural, and systemic domains—that influence the effectiveness and long-term sustainability of PA initiatives in AFCCs. Overall, this review provides valuable insights into the various factors that influence PA in age-friendly ecosystems. Drawing on the evidence, we propose six enablers needed to facilitate a whole-system approach to facilitating PA in AFCCs: (a) multi-level leadership and shared vision; (b) equity and empowerment of older persons; (c) effective governance and management; (d) integration of PA strategy into age-friendly ecosystems; (e) diverse and collaborative partnerships; and (f) capacity building across the system. Together, these enablers offer a strategic foundation for advancing PA policy and practice within AFCCs, supporting the development of inclusive, sustainable, and health-promoting PA environments for older persons.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25108-7.Keywords: Ageing, Exercise, Health

The global population is ageing. By 2030, one in six people in the world are expected to be 60 years and over, and by 2050, the number of people of this age will double, exceeding 2.1 billion [1]. While an ageing population presents challenges, it also offers opportunities (e.g., family, social, workplace, community and economic contributions), yet whether these opportunities can be realised, and for whom, depends strongly on the health of older persons. To address the challenges and realise the opportunities of population ageing, effective approaches to promote health and wellbeing as people age are needed, to enable older persons to live and do the things they value for longer. This ethos is at the heart of the United Nations (UN) Decade of Healthy Ageing 2021–2030 [2], a global initiative aimed at transforming the world into a better place for people to live and grow old in, and key to the UN’s [3] 2030 Sustainable Development Agenda and the World Health Organisation’s (WHO) Global Strategy and Action Plan on Ageing and Health [4].

Physical activity (PA) is evidenced as an effective way to support healthy ageing [5, 6]. The WHO recommends that on a weekly basis, older persons should engage in at least (a) 150–300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic PA, 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic PA, or an equivalent combination; (b) 2 + days of muscle-strengthening activities; and (c) 3 + days of multicomponent activities that emphasise balance and strength. However, despite the well-known benefits of PA for older persons [7–11], the prevalence of inactivity increases steeply in older age, with 43.5% of adults over 60 years old reported to be insufficiently active globally (95% UI = 41.0–46.2%; 12). Furthermore, PA is even lower among some sub-groups of older persons, including women [12]; those with disabilities [13], mild cognitive impairment [14], and dementia [15]; and care-home residents [16]. Therefore, from a public health perspective, it is not only vital to find effective ways to positively affect PA in older persons at a population level, but to achieve this in a manner that is inclusive of the heterogeneity and diversity of the older population. The importance of improving PA among older persons is emphasised by the Global Action Plan for Physical Activity 2018–2030 [17], which identifies older persons as a target group and outlines a specific action to “Enhance the provision of, and opportunities for, appropriately tailored programmes and services aimed at increasing physical activity and reducing sedentary behaviour in older adults…to support healthy ageing” (p. 83).

In response to the changing needs of an ageing population, the WHO [18] developed a framework for age-friendly cities. Since inception in 2007, interest in age-friendliness has grown, with over 1700 cities and communities across 60 countries now committed to a shared vision of making their community a great place to age in [19]. Age-friendliness has also attracted significant academic interest, with special issues in journals on age-friendly initiatives [20] and reviews on various elements of age-friendliness [21, 22]. Age-friendly cities and communities (AFCCs) implement policies, services and structures with the aim of creating physical and social environments that promote healthy and active ageing [18]. The WHO [18, 19] proposes that eight domains can support older persons to live healthily and age actively in place: (a) civic participation and employment; (b) communication and information; (c) community and health care; (d) housing; (e) outdoor spaces and buildings; (f) respect and social inclusion; (g) social participation; and (h) transportation.

With international policy advocating for whole-systems and whole-of-community approaches to promoting PA [17] and sharing accountability for developing actions that improve older persons’ lives [2], AFCCs could offer an ideal mechanism and policy framework to promote place-based PA. Despite the opportunities that might exist, there is limited understanding of how PA has been implemented in AFCCs and no clear guidance on how PA can be developed, enabled, embedded, and sustained across the age-friendly domains. Furthermore, although the evidence base on PA in AFCCs has grown—spanning diverse geographic and cultural contexts [23–26]—there has been no comprehensive review of existing evidence. Specifically, there is no synthesis detailing the characteristics of PA initiatives in AFCCs, how the age-friendly framework has been utilised to promote PA in AFCCs, what the outcomes of these PA initiatives are, and factors that influence implementation. Given the growing global policy emphasis on healthy ageing, the expansion of the AFCCs network, and the urgent need for scalable, sustainable approaches to support ageing populations, a review of PA in AFCCs is both timely and important to consolidate current understanding, inform future research and practice, and guide policy development.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to systematically map and summarise the available literature on PA in AFCCs. Accordingly, the review questions were: (a) What are the characteristics (e.g., methods, participants, activities) of PA initiatives in AFCCs?; (b) How, and to what extent, are the domains of the WHO age-friendly framework operationalised in PA initiatives in AFCCs?; (c) What are outcomes of PA in AFCCs?; and (d) What factors influence implementation of PA in AFCCs (i.e., enablers/facilitators, barriers/challenges)? By addressing these questions and mapping existing research and practice evidence on PA in AFCCs for the first time, we sought to provide insights that could help AFCCs seeking to develop coherent and robust strategies to support active ageing. These insights could subsequently inform policy development and contribute to the creation of sustainable, age-friendly ecosystems that integrate PA as a core component of healthy ageing and AFCCs.

Methods

Design and protocol

As we sought to map existing evidence on PA in AFCCs, we selected a scoping review methodology [27]. Our scoping review was conducted and reported in line with the 5-step framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [28], recommendations for enhancing this framework [29], and the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR; 30; see Appendix 1). Before commencing formal searches, we posted a protocol on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/37u9y/registrations).

Eligibility criteria

To increase the comprehensiveness of our review, we chose to include evidence from both traditional academic outputs and grey literature. Accordingly, we included: (a) traditional research outputs, consisting of peer-reviewed journal articles, books, or book chapters containing evidence on PA in AFCCs published from 2007 onwards (when the age-friendly framework was launched); and (b) age-friendly practices involving PA listed on the WHO’s Age-Friendly in Practice database (https://extranet.who.int/agefriendlyworld/age-friendly-practices/). As our review was exploratory, we included research outputs that included evaluated interventions, studies examining factors related to PA in AFCCs (e.g., correlations, facilitators, or barriers), and descriptive narratives (i.e., outputs describing PA initiatives in AFCCs but without evaluation evidence). We defined PA as “people moving, acting and performing within culturally specific spaces and contexts, and influenced by a unique array of interests, emotions, ideas, instructions and relationships” [30, p. 5]. Thus, sports participation, PA, exercise, dance, active travel, fitness activities, or any equivalent could be included. To meet our criterion for age-friendly, the PA must have been explicitly labelled as age-friendly and undertaken in line with the WHO’s age-friendly framework or have taken place within an age-friendly city or community, and where commitment to the AFCC is clear. In-keeping with the age-friendly concept, no specific age limit was applied. Studies that referred to age-friendliness without making reference to the use of WHO’s age-friendly framework and/or that used other age-friendly frameworks (e.g., 4Ms healthcare) were excluded.

Information sources

We employed three strategies to source relevant literature. First, to identify peer-reviewed outputs, we undertook systematic searches of five electronic databases (APA PsycINFO, CINAHL Complete, MEDLINE, SOCIndex, SPORTDiscus) on May 3, 2025. The first and last authors developed the search strategy through consultation after initial pilot searches. Adopting principles recommended for scoping reviews [31], the search string included blocks related to the Population (older persons), Context (physical activity), and Concept (age-friendly). We searched these three blocks at the full-text level and modified the search string for each database as necessary (see Appendix 2 for each electronic database search). No limits were placed on language, but the publication date was limited to 2007 onwards. Second, the first author undertook manual searches of Google Scholar, searching for the terms “physical activity” and “age-friendly” and screening titles of the first 500 records in May 2025. All records identified via electronic and manual searches were exported to an online software for literature reviews (Covidence). Duplicates were removed through the automatic de-duplication feature and manual screening. Finally, to identify age-friendly practices on the WHO’s Age-Friendly in Practice database, the first author used the “Advanced Search” function on the WHO website and searched for key terms (i.e., physical activity, exercise, sport, fitness, walking) in May 2025 to identify relevant practices. No filter was used to limit the search.

Screening

For the academic outputs, the first and second authors screened the titles and abstracts independently, prior to meeting to discuss their decisions and resolve any discrepancies. Full texts for the remaining records were then retrieved and screened independently again by these authors. Following this process, the first and second authors met to discuss their decisions, resolve discrepancies, and agree reasons for excluding articles. For the age-friendly practices, the first and fourth authors screened the information presented on the WHO’s Age-Friendly in Practice database for each return in the search independently. For practices that were not presented in the English language, Google Translate was used to enable assessment of eligibility. Both authors met to discuss their decisions and agreed on practices to be included.

Data charting

To aid familiarisation, the first author read all included studies and practices repeatedly, before extracting relevant data from each article. To facilitate the extraction of relevant information, we developed a data charting table. For the research outputs, the first author extracted information on each output’s: author(s); publication year; title; location; study design and methods; sample; PA information; and key findings related to our research questions. For the age-friendly practices, the first author extracted information presented on the WHO database, including: programme name; location; PA initiative; engagement with the wider community (e.g., collaborations, older person involvement); target groups; desired outcomes; sectors (i.e., more elaborative version of the age-friendly domains); age-friendly issues addressed; and challenges. To verify the accuracy of data extracted [32], the second and fifth authors checked the contents extracted for the academic outputs and age-friendly practices, respectively, with all extracted data consensually agreed.

Collating, synthesising and analysing

We followed recommendations for collecting, summarising and reporting findings in scoping reviews [29] and used a data-based convergent synthesis approach to combine information from different sources [33]. To enable the mixing of quantitative and qualitative data, we transformed quantitative data into qualitative codes [34]. We conducted a content analysis [35] to summarise the facilitators/enablers and barriers/challenges, deductively coding relevant data into the eight domains of the age-friendly framework. Due to the small number of studies that assessed outcomes of PA in AFCCs and limited cross-study evidence for specific outcomes, we summarised the quantitative and qualitative evidence and presented this as a narrative. Regarding challenges for the age-friendly practices, we inductively analysed information presented using content analysis [35]. Frequency counts were generated for all codes. In cases where data for the same PA programme or sample was reported across multiple outputs, we combined these into a single score to avoid double counting. After the first author conducted the initial analysis, she shared this with the co-authors, who were asked to appraise the interpretations and to offer feedback and alternative suggestions as ‘critical friends’ [36]. Following this engagement, we refined our analysis and crafted a narrative representing our results.

Results

Overall, 2004 academic outputs and 242 age-friendly practices were found through our searches. After screening these against our eligibility criteria, we included 59 academic outputs and 77 age-friendly practices (Fig. 1). An upward trend in academic outputs in recent years was identified, with 43 of the 59 outputs (72.3%) published since 2020. In total, 35 countries were represented across the academic outputs and age-friendly practices (Fig. 2). Our results are presented in two sections. Initially, we present findings stemming from the academic outputs (see Appendix 4 for references of all included academic outputs), followed by the age-friendly practices. Contextual information for all included outputs and practices is detailed in Appendix 5.

Academic outputs

In this section, we present the following information related to the academic outputs: contextual information; PA initiatives; facilitators/enablers of PA; barriers/challenges to PA; and outcomes of PA initiatives.

Contextual information

A summary of contextual information for included studies is presented in Table 1. We divided the academic outputs into three broad categories. Most outputs (k = 30) presented empirical evidence on PA and age-friendliness but did not focus on or evaluate a specific PA initiative. Seventeen outputs presented empirical findings on specific PA interventions in AFCCs; however, in some cases, multiple outputs pertained to the same programmes and samples (see Overview of Physical Activity Initiatives). Finally, 12 outputs were labelled as descriptive narratives, providing insights into PA in AFCCs but not presenting evidence on these initiatives. Of the 59 academic outputs, 47 recruited participants, with the remaining 12 consisting of reflective accounts or using methods that did not involve participant recruitment (e.g., document analysis). As some studies reported data across multiple outputs [37–41], the included outputs consisted of 45 independent samples and 16,352 participants, 95.4% of whom were older persons. Other participants included policymakers, local government staff, health professionals, carers, and PA practitioners. Most participants (N = 10540) were recruited in a single study [42] and only 13 samples comprised >100 participants. There were a higher proportion of women than men, although gender was not reported for 2743 participants. Of the 36 samples with older persons, 18 detailed the lower and upper age range of participants, seven presented the lower limit only, and nine outlined the mean or median only (range = 64.34–87.5 years). Twenty-nine outputs used qualitative methods, 17 employed mixed methods, and 13 adopted quantitative methods. Most studies collected information via interviews (k = 25) or surveys (k = 18). Thirteen studies included at least one measure of PA, with most using self-report measures (76.9%) and fewer employing observational tools (30.8%) or accelerometers (15.4%).

Overview of physical activity initiatives

A total of eight independent PA programmes across five countries were identified: the 12-week Seniors Exercise Park programme [23, 43, 44], including a tailored version for people living with dementia [45] and a peer-led version of the programme [46–48] in Australia; group PA classes in Canada [37–39, 49]; Elderly Fitness Corners in Hong Kong [1]; Safer Routes to Age in Place in the USA [1]; the Senior Change Makers initiative in the USA [50]; community-based social innovations that included PA in Japan [40, 41]; and community dance classes in Canada [51]. Prospective research designs were only employed for the Seniors Exercise Park and Senior Change Makers programmes, with the remainder using some form of observational study design.

Facilitators/enablers of physical activity

In total, 40 facilitators/enablers were identified across the included outputs, with each of the eight age-friendly domains represented (Fig. 3 and Appendix 6). At least four regions were represented in each of the eight domains, with these most commonly being Europe, Asia, North America and Oceania.

Civic participation and employment

Involving older persons as leaders and in decision making was fundamental to promoting PA. This involvement varied, ranging from consultation and inclusion of older persons’ views (e.g., [52]) to more empowering forms of citizen engagement, whereby older persons were central to leadership [51], decision-making [53], and advocacy (e.g., as local PA champions; [48]). Several PA programmes highlighted the importance of building capacity among older persons. For example, the Seniors Exercise Park involved the recruitment, onboarding, management, training, and ongoing support of volunteers to enable delivery of peer-led classes, a process facilitated and supported by dedicated staff from local councils [48]. Several studies also highlighted the value of volunteerism, such that older persons were either provided with volunteering opportunities [40, 41] or that volunteers working with older persons supported their health and wellbeing via PA classes [54].

Communication and information

Ensuring that information and communication, delivered in various formats, was accessible to older persons was identified as important. Different formats were reported for sharing information about PA, including printed brochures with simple messaging [40, 41] and word of mouth [38]. The only evaluated programme for older persons living with dementia [46] highlighted the importance of making adjustments to ensure classes were dementia-friendly (e.g., appropriate communication, exercise sheets/videos, verbal reinforcement).

Community and healthcare

A consistent finding across studies was the need for cross-community and local authority collaboration/support. Researchers highlighted the importance of framing health and wellbeing as a societal issue rather than an individual responsibility [55]. Several studies recognised the role of personnel from within local government departments, either in directly supporting PA initiatives or facilitating the development of relationships with local healthcare or leisure providers [23]. Working effectively with community organisations was also key, with examples including local aged-care centres [46], schools [1, 56], and religious groups [56]. Partnerships with local health services resulted in medical professionals encouraging PA in their engagement with older persons (e.g., medical appointments) and signposting older persons to physical activities in their locality [40, 41, 57, 58]. The delivery of PA classes by suitably trained professionals also supported older persons’ experiences [43, 50].

Housing

Residing near community services/amenities was a consistent factor facilitating PA. Living close to local services and amenities (e.g., < 20-minute walk) increased walking likelihood [57, 59, 60], both for utilitarian and leisure purposes [61]. The value of considering community walking within housing planning [54] and having exercise spaces close to aged-care centres [46, 54] was also highlighted.

Outdoor spaces and buildings

Access to green outdoor spaces and parks was consistently identified as a facilitator of PA. These public spaces could provide opportunities for walking [62], increased the attractiveness of PA [57], and enabled different forms of PA (e.g., ground golf – [40, 41]). Additionally, these spaces could further support PA via the installation of adapted PA equipment and complementary infrastructure (e.g., shelters overhead, instructional signage, resting areas – [23]) and creation of intergenerational spaces, which allowed older persons to exercise while their grandchildren played [63]. Benches and resting areas in parks and on public transport routes increased walkability [64] and opened avenues for active travel [65]. Well-maintained buildings [61, 65] and appealing surroundings [57, 63] also encouraged walking. The importance of safety [42] and accessible features within buildings hosting PA (e.g., wide doorways, heating, accessible bathroom, good lighting, barrier-free facilities − [51, 66]) were also highlighted.

Respect and social inclusion

Respectful and socially inclusive approaches to supporting PA incorporated various characteristics. Affordable/subsidised PA opportunities were explicitly reported as key across several studies, which ranged from free classes [23, 44] that sometimes focused on socio-economically disadvantaged older persons [60], to low or subsidised costs to use fitness facilities [54]. The role of the instructor was considered vital, with cross-study evidence indicating the importance of: understanding, valuing and recognising needs and aspirations of older persons; adopting a person-centred approach, where instructors actively sought to work with the capacities of older persons [37]; and creating autonomy-supportive environments [51]. Instructors could further support PA by facilitating social connections between group members ([e.g., encouraging conversation during breaks − [44, 45, 46]), being inclusive in their language and labelling of programmes [50], and fostering accepting, non-judgemental environments [51]. Additional strategies for fostering respect and social inclusion included challenging ageist stereotypes [51], delivery of culturally-sensitive classes [56], and prioritising engagement with older persons who typically have less opportunity to be active, thereby addressing health inequities [55].

Social participation

Engaging in community groups more consistently was positively related to PA [42] and access to community spaces (e.g., community centres, recreation centres, senior centres) that could facilitate PA classes was key to enabling participation. While access to classes specifically tailored to older persons [43, 53] and walking groups/tours [67] supported PA, several studies noted benefits of intergenerational events [1, 67]. Older persons also valued social support from instructors and their peers, with the reassurance and encouragement characterising these positive social experiences bolstering their interest and commitment to being active [38].

Transportation

There was consistent evidence that the provision of safe, accessible footpaths and walkable spaces could promote PA [1, 68]. Examples of such features included wide pavements, dropped kerbs, even surfaces, and presence of ramps and pedestrian crossings. Footpath use could be further facilitated by complementary infrastructure, such as shelters, shaded walkways, street lighting, benches, and heating [54, 63]. Safety was paramount in busy areas, with traffic-calming measures facilitating active travel [57]. Additional infrastructure that could promote active travel included bike sharing schemes [69], cycle paths [52], interactive maps showing active travel routes [69], and safe active travel initiatives [1, 57]. For some older persons, being provided with transport to activities was also important [40, 41].

Barriers/challenges to physical activity

Overall, 29 barriers/challenges were identified across the included studies (Fig. 3 and Appendix 6). These barriers/challenges were classified into six of the age-friendly domains, with none mapped onto civic participation and employment, or community and health care.

Communication and information

While there was limited cross-study evidence pertaining to barriers in communication and information, three issues were identified in two studies based in North America: complex registration process [49], issues with communication and information sharing [49], and challenges that arose from the reliance on technological devices and availability of internet access [70].

Housing

Only one study reported a barrier classified as being related to housing. Specifically, the poor integration of aged-care facilities in a Japanese community hindered walkability for older persons [65].

Outdoor spaces and buildings

A consistent barrier to PA was the absence, poor maintenance, or poor location of appropriate seating, which, in turn, made PA more difficult [63, 71, 72]. Other commonly identified issues included limited access to parks/green outdoor spaces [61], lack of appropriate equipment in parks for outdoor exercise [24], unappealing surroundings [65] and safety concerns [84].

Respect and social inclusion

One factor highlighted as a barrier to PA classes specifically was cost [49, 51]. In addition, the influence of individual biographies was also noted, such that limited exposure to dance [51] and negative prior experiences in PA (e.g., due to excessive intensity or complexity − [37]) left some reluctant to engage or re-engage in activities. Furthermore, older persons could feel excluded from group PA due to inaccessibility, a lack of culturally-tailored physical activities, language barriers, and failure to adjust the programme to individual capacities [49].

Social participation

A lack of opportunity for PA was noted in several studies, which included a lack of program availability [51], lack of exercise coaches [73], and eligibility criteria that might have prevented some older persons from partaking [49]. A lack of participant diversity was also reported by some as a barrier to participation [49].

Transportation

Issues with transport were identified across all regions. The most commonly identified barrier to PA, and active travel more specifically, was unsafe, inaccessible footpaths [63, 74, 75], which encompassed issues such as the absence of paved streets and footpaths, inadequate footpath width, poorly maintained surfaces, level changes, and lack of dropped kerbs and slip-resistant features. Further barriers to active travel were obstructions on footpaths [55], overgrown foliage [65], and poor street-crossing infrastructure (e.g., lights not staying on long enough, lack of audible traffic light signals − [74]). Active travel was also made more difficult by the high speed and density of traffic in urban and suburban areas [72] and poorly designed transport networks in urban locations [61]. Several studies also highlighted issues with inadequate infrastructure around footpaths/streets (e.g., lack of shelter, poor street lighting, and lack of railings on staircases) and narrow roads [65, 72], as well as traffic-related pollution [61, 72]. Other barriers included the need to navigate competing users of footpaths and streets [55], hilly topography [61], limited bike path access [74], lack of transportation [49] and lack of inadequate traffic-calming measures [65].

Outcomes of physical activity initiatives

Due to the small number of programmes evaluated and lack of consistency in the outcomes assessed or reported, it was difficult to meaningfully synthesise findings. The initial 12-week Seniors Exercise Park programme delivered in three parks significantly increased PA from baseline to post-intervention, with significant improvements in all physical function measures, self-rated quality of life, fear of falls, falls risk, depressive symptoms and loneliness at 12 weeks [23]. For the dementia-focused programme, no significant differences were found in physical, cognitive, or health outcomes across time points, but 100% of participants agreed/strongly agreed that they enjoyed the programme and 85.7% were satisfied with programme outcomes [45]. For the subsequent peer-led Seniors Exercise Park programme delivered in six parks, upgrading of the park increased park visitors number and PA volume among older persons [23]. An evaluation of the 8-week Senior Change Makers programme [50], which was designed to improve seniors’ advocacy skills and confidence related to community walkability, found no significant changes for any accelerometer-measured outcomes. Lastly, participation in group PA classes was significantly and positively related to active ageing in Japanese seniors [40]. Among qualitative studies, there was consistent evidence of social benefits among group PA programmes, although it should be noted that much of this evidence stemmed entirely from Canadian programmes. Specifically, benefits of participation in group PA classes for older persons were perceived to include social network expansion [49], enhanced social connections [38, 39], access to and receipt of social support [49], and opportunities for companionship [1]. Similarly, expert instructors perceived dance could act as a social hub for older persons [51]. Given the small number of studies presenting evidence on outcomes of PA in AFCCs, further research is needed to better understand the impacts of PA initiatives in AFCCs.

Age-friendly practices

In this section, we present the following information on the age-friendly practices: contextual information; application of age-friendly framework; and challenges for age-friendly practices.

Contextual information

A summary of contextual information for the age-friendly practices is presented in Table 2. The 77 practices were located in 67 independent age-friendly locations, with 36.4% of practices in Europe. The main target group was most commonly older persons in general and the domain of functional ability [76] listed most often was “be mobile”. Project leads for practices varied, but the most common was a local authority (58.4%). Most practices reported at least two collaborating organisations (54.5%). Of the 75 practices that described the involvement of older persons, 86.7% reported some involvement.

While some practices encompassed several components, we classified the 77 practices into 10 PA categories, with the highest proportion of these being PA classes/programmes. Some regional patterns in the PA practice categories were evident (Table 3). Physical activity classes/programmes represented at least one-third of practices in Europe, Asia, and North America, and over two-thirds in Oceania. Application of age-friendly framework

We found that AFCCs have sought to address a range of issues via PA initiatives, illustrating the potential cross-cutting impacts of PA. The majority referred to “healthy behaviours” (64.9%), with other prominent issues comprising “inclusion” (48.1%), “participation” (46.8%), “accessibility” (44.2%), “ageing in place” (27.3%), and ageism (23.4%). In total, 29 “sectors” (i.e., age-friendly domains) were represented within 70 practices; seven practices did not report this information. To align with the age-friendly framework, we mapped these 29 sectors onto the eight age-friendly domains (see Fig. 4 and Appendix 7). The practices typically featured two age-friendly domains (M = 2.11, SD = 1.35, range = 1–6), with 25 practices involving three or more domains.

Challenges for age-friendly practices

Challenges were reported in 54 practices, with the five categories representing these challenges illustrating the variety of factors and levels influencing implementation of PA practices in AFCCs (Table 4). Challenges surrounding the adoption and sustainability of practices were most common. The challenges associated with engaging older persons varied; some practices reported issues with participation and retention, whereas others identified difficulties with meeting demand. In addition, several practices highlighted concerns regarding reach, questioning whether the practices were engaging older persons who needed access to PA opportunities most. Numerous practices reported infrastructural challenges, which captured various systemic and organisational factors influencing PA practices. The complexity of working within and across multiple partners (e.g., local authority departments, volunteers) was salient, with several practices highlighting challenges with coordination, collaboration, logistics and organisation. Numerous practices emphasised the importance of addressing older persons’ priorities, ensuring that PA was responsive to the unique needs, capabilities and preferences of older persons. The remaining categories linked to the social and physical environment. Several practices identified environmental issues, with the ability to find and create accessible and inclusive spaces for PA among older persons most common. Others reported challenges with resourcing and supporting the workforce and volunteers, such as investing the necessary time and resources into delivering initiatives.

Discussion

In this review, we sought to map and summarise the available literature on PA in AFCCs, using the WHO’s age-friendly conceptual framework [19]. Our analysis of 59 academic outputs and 77 practices on the WHO’s age-friendly database offers the first comprehensive map of evidence on PA in age-friendly ecosystems. Aligned with ecological perspectives on ageing [77, 78] and key pillars of the UN’s Decade of Healthy Ageing [2], our findings highlight the need for systematic, multi-faceted, and multi-level approaches to addressing the wide range of environmental factors that can support (or hinder) opportunities for older persons to be active in AFCCs. While there has been a steady growth in the number of academic outputs presenting evidence or insight into PA in AFCCs, only a small number used prospective designs or assessed intervention outcomes. Thus, further research is needed to better understand the impacts of PA interventions in AFCCs in future.

As the first review of PA in AFCCs globally, this study reveals important trends and gaps in the evidence base, which can be used to inform future research and practice. While the inclusion of evidence spanning 35 countries across five regions offers an international perspective on PA in AFCCs, the majority of evidence—both in the academic literature and age-friendly practices—originates from high-income, urban areas in the Global North. This imbalance likely reflects the over representation of such locations within the global AFCC network and the urban-centric orientation of the WHO’s original age-friendly framework [18]. These characteristics limit the extent to which findings and initiatives can be generalised to non-urban contexts, including rural and coastal populations, middle- and low-income countries and territories in the Global South. The persistent underrepresentation of these locations is a critical limitation. Rural and coastal populations are ageing at a faster rate than urban regions and access to PA in these environments will be shaped by distinct place-based challenges and enablers, which will inevitability impact the implementation and long-term sustainability of age-friendly programmes [79, 80]. Equally, the higher levels of physical inactivity in middle- and low-income countries [12], coupled with projections that 80% of older persons globally will reside in these regions by 2050 [1], highlights the urgency of directing attention towards ageing and PA in these contexts. In particular, the absence of academic outputs and documented practices from the African continent highlights the need for targeted efforts to promote age-friendliness and PA in this region, as unique sociocultural, infrastructural, and health system challenges may require the adaptation of existing principles to ensure relevance, feasibility, and impact [81]. Ultimately, to promote PA and healthy ageing for all, irrespective of place, greater investment is needed in research and practice in underrepresented regions to ensure PA promotion is equitable, feasible, and contextually-appropriate.

Most evaluated interventions and age-friendly practices have tended to target older persons in general, with fewer focusing on distinct sub-groups. While there were some rare examples of practices that specifically addressed the needs of older persons living with dementia, women, and particular cultural groups, these remain exceptions rather than the norm. Failure to consider the diversity and heterogeneity of the older population risks overlooking and excluding sub-groups of older persons who are already less engaged and less visible in PA. Underpinned by a critical human ecology perspective [77], we call on AFCCs to give greater attention to aspects of diversity, such as gender, ethnicity, age, religion, socioeconomic position, living circumstances (e.g., community-based versus residential care settings), support needs, and geography, as well as the intersection of these. By designing and delivering PA in ways that are accessible, acceptable, and inclusive of the diverse needs, preferences, and capacities of older persons, AFCCs can help reduce disparities in PA participation. This, in turn, will enable more older persons to benefit from PA and contribute to realising the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing’s vision of leaving no one behind [2].

Finally, most PA practices in the academic literature and on the WHO’s age-friendly practices database were classified as PA classes or programmes. While some of these initiatives appeared to be offered on a continuous, long-term basis, many were described as short-term programmes, typically lasting only a few weeks or months. Short-term approaches to promoting PA often fall short because they fail to engage with the multi-level and interconnected factors that shape PA behaviour across the lifespan, in turn limiting the potential to create sustainable, lasting changes in PA. While PA classes and programmes delivered consistently over time should be an important and valued part of efforts to promote PA in AFCCs, there is a need to complement these programmes with long-term, systemic, multi-level strategies, including investing in active travel infrastructure, age-friendly neighbourhood planning, and accessible outdoor spaces.

This synthesis provides a foundation for future efforts to more deliberately integrate PA into age-friendly planning, ensuring that strategies and interventions can be inclusive, responsive, and aligned with the diverse realities of older persons across different settings. Nevertheless, we realise that these principles might not transfer to all contexts, given the relatively narrow geographic focus of evidence in this review. To address this, we suggest that AFCCs —and other settings aiming to promote PA—adopt a place-based approach that is sensitive to the unique opportunities, resources, and barriers present in different contexts.

Enabling a whole-system approach to physical activity in age-friendly cities and communities

International policy advocates for whole systems approaches to increase PA and reduce physical inactivity [17], yet few systems-informed approaches have been implemented and evaluated [82]. While our review has advanced understanding of facilitators, barriers and challenges across the age-friendly domains, realising opportunities to implement and sustain PA through the age-friendly framework relies on an enabling system [82]. Through an integrative analysis of evidence included in our review, we propose six system-level enablers and linked actions (Table 5) to facilitate the integration of PA into AFCCs. The proposed enabling principles reflect the WHO’s [17] commitment to systems-based approaches, recognising that single-policy approaches, short-term solutions, and isolated, behaviour change interventions will likely fail in achieving the core goals of improving age-friendly PA and uptake amongst older persons. The system-level enablers directly align with the guiding principles and areas of action for healthy ageing identified in the UN’s Decade of Healthy Ageing [2]. While we focus on AFCCs, we suggest that the principles are potentially transferable to other settings not (yet) subscribed to the age-friendly agenda. Nevertheless, we also recognise that various contextual factors (e.g., rural/urban setting, political and policy climate, economic situation, laws and regulations, demographics) will inevitably shape the capacity of AFCCs to adopt a whole-system approach. Therefore, efforts to enact these enablers should be flexible, locally-informed, and sensitive to the needs, capacities, and priorities of each community.

Strengths and limitations of the review

Our review has several strengths, including that we rigorously followed established guidance for scoping reviews and synthesised evidence from both research outputs and practices on the WHO’s database. Nevertheless, we acknowledge several limitations. While we included practices listed on the WHO database, it is possible that other practices have taken place and are ongoing in AFCCs but have not been posted on this database and were, therefore, not identified in our searches. Additionally, we may have missed further research studies if no reference was made to the location being committed to being an AFCC. As a result, the findings presented might not fully capture all PA practices delivered in AFCCs. In addition, we acknowledge that the findings and conclusions are susceptible to being biased towards high-income, Global North locations, which were overrepresented in the review and encourage further research in underrepresented regions to strengthen the evidence base. Finally, in keeping with guidance for scoping reviews, we did not undertake a quality assessment of the academic outputs, which limits our ability to comment on the robustness of the evidence base. Nevertheless, given that the evidence base is marked by a paucity of prospective and longitudinal research designs and long-term evaluations, these methodological shortcomings should be addressed in future research.

Conclusion

Our review illustrates that a growing corpus of literature has developed on PA in AFCCs, yet there remains further room for improvements to ensure the opportunities that exist to promote healthy ageing through PA in AFCCs are maximised. We suggest some of the challenges reported could be overcome by adopting a systems approach and harnessing opportunities that exist to promote PA for older persons across the eight age-friendly domains. Adopting a whole systems approach requires meaningful and sustained sharing of practice, and developing a shared language across groups, sectors and disciplines that have traditionally been siloed. This review offers an original contribution by framing PA promotion through a systems lens within the age-friendly movement and advancing six systems-level enablers for AFCCs—and other settings—seeking to adopt a whole-system approach to promoting PA in older persons. By adopting these enablers as part of a whole-system approach, we suggest that this can help AFCCs—and other settings—take the collective actions needed to create long-term, sustainable increase in PA for older persons.
