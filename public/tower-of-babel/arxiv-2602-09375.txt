Latent Poincaré Shaping for Agentic Reinforcement Learning


Abstract
We propose LaPha , a method for training AlphaZero-like LLM agents in a Poincaré latent space. Under LaPha , the search process can be visualized as a tree rooted at the prompt and growing outward from the origin toward the boundary of the Poincaré ball, where negative curvature provides exponentially increasing capacity with radius. Using hyperbolic geodesic distance to rule-verified correctness, we define a node potential and assign dense process rewards by potential differences. We further attach a lightweight value head on the same shared latent space, enabling self-guided test-time scaling with almost no additional overhead. On MATH-500, LaPha improves Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B from 66.0% to 88.2%. With value-head-guided search, LaPha -1.5B reaches 56.7% accuracy on AIME’24, and LaPha -7B further achieves 60.0% on AIME’24 and 53.3% on AIME’25.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) can solve challenging problems, but their default behavior is still single-pass generation: producing an entire solution in one trajectory.
For tasks that require multi-step reasoning, tool use, and self-correction, a common remedy is to scale test-time compute by explicitly searching over action–observation traces and selecting better outcomes from multiple candidates
(
Yao et al. 2022
;
Yao et al. 2023
)
.
In this view, a dialogue prefix (including tool feedback) defines a state, each next-step completion defines an action, and repeated branching induces a behavior tree.

Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) provides a principled way to explore such trees, and has historically paired well with a policy prior and a learned value function for strong planning
(
Silver et al. 2017
)
.
However, the “game of natural language” differs from board games in a key respect: the action space is not a small, fixed set.
An action is a variable-length token sequence, and the branching factor is dominated by semantic aliasing—many distinct strings correspond to near-identical meaning (paraphrases, formatting variants, and tool-call surface forms)
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Farquhar et al. 2024
)
.
Meanwhile, in RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR), rule-based checkers label only a tiny fraction of leaves as correct
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
.
Together, aliasing and sparse terminal signals make token-space exploration wasteful and credit assignment brittle.

This paper takes the position that token sequences are an inconvenient interface for search and learning, while backbone hidden states provide a more faithful representation of “state” and “policy”.
Ideally, we want a latent semantic space equipped with a distance such that progress along a reasoning trace can be measured geometrically.
If such a distance is available, it enables three capabilities: (i) converting sparse terminal verification into dense process rewards by shaping intermediate nodes via distance-to-goal; (ii) training a lightweight value estimator on the same latent states to guide MCTS without relying on heavyweight reward/value models at inference; and (iii) pruning redundant branches by clustering in latent space rather than by brittle string matching.

The obstacle is geometry.
Modern decoder backbones with normalization such as RMSNorm tend to produce representations with near-constant activation scale
(
Zhang & Sennrich 2019
;
Yang et al. 2025
)
, making Euclidean norms and Euclidean distances poor proxies for semantic progress.
More broadly, bounded or nonnegative-curvature geometries (including spherical representations) force deep trees to be packed into limited effective capacity, increasing crowding and eroding distance contrast across depths—exactly the failure mode that undermines distance-based progress signals.

A classical remedy is negative curvature.
Hyperbolic spaces expand exponentially with radius, matching the combinatorics of trees, and the Poincaré ball provides a practical model where points stay inside a unit ball while intrinsic (geodesic) distances remain unbounded
(
Poincaré 1882
;
Beardon 2012
)
.
Poincaré embeddings exploit this property to represent hierarchies with low distortion
(
Nickel & Kiela 2017
;
Ganea et al. 2018
)
.
We transfer the same geometric insight from word hierarchies to LLM behavior trees by mapping pooled backbone hidden states into a root-centered Poincaré latent space.

We propose
LaPha
(Poincaré
La
tent Al
Ph
aZero-like RL for
a
gentic reasoning), an AlphaZero-like agentic reinforcement learning framework in which a single shared, root-centered Poincaré latent space serves as the interface between search and learning (Fig.
1
c).
All components operate on this shared representation:

•

(i)
Geodesic potential shaping
converts sparse terminal verification into dense process rewards over the rollout tree;

•

(ii)
A lightweight value head
trained on the same pooled hidden state learns a correctness-aligned ranking signal, enabling self-guided Monte Carlo Tree Search for test-time scaling with minimal overhead;

•

(iii)
Latent-space pruning
clusters and removes paraphrastic near-duplicates to preserve diversity and reduce wasted expansions.

Across our math-reasoning experiments,
LaPha
yields consistent improvements; with self-guided search,
LaPha
on
Qwen2.5-Math-7B
achieves 60.0% on AIME’24 and 53.3% on AIME’25, outperforming
o1-mini
on these benchmarks.

Methodology

(b) A hyperbolic tiling in the Poincaré disk, visualizing the rapid expansion of effective capacity with radius in negative curvature.

mean-pools the backbone hidden states to obtain a node representation, maps it into a prompt-centered Poincaré latent state, and reuses this shared latent space for geodesic potential shaping, value estimation, and latent-space pruning in search.

2.1
Root-centered latent states in the Poincaré ball

In MCTS, the decision rule at a node can be written as a policy
\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)
. For an LLM-based agent, the state
s
at node
i
is the serialized dialogue prefix produced by
apply_chat_template
(including the system/user messages, previous assistant steps, and any tool feedback), while the action
a
is the next-step completion generated by the model (including an explicit end-of-turn token, in our case
<|im_end|>
). Concatenating the state and action yields a single context sequence of length
L
, and a forward pass through the decoder produces the final-layer hidden states
\mathbf{h}^{(i)}_{1:L}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times H}
.
As illustrated in Fig.
1
(a), compact geometries with bounded intrinsic scale can crowd deep states and reduce long-range contrast, motivating a hyperbolic latent construction for tree-structured reasoning.
We summarize this token-level representation into a single vector by mean pooling:

\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{L}m_{i,t}\,\mathbf{h}^{(i)}_{t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{L}m_{i,t}},\quad m_{i,t}=\mathbb{I}_{\left[t\neq\texttt{<|endoftext|>}\right]}

(1)

where
<|endoftext|>
is used as the padding token in our implementation (In practice, we input a batch of contexts with different lengths, so we pad sequences to a common length). Let
\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{0}
denote the pooled vector at the root. For any node
i
, we translate the hidden state by
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{0}
and then map it into the Poincaré ball:

\mathbf{y}_{i}=\exp_{0}\!\left(\frac{\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{0}}{\sqrt{H}}\right)\in\mathbb{D}^{H},\hskip 9.24994pt\mathbb{D}^{H}=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}:\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}<1\}.

(2)

The exponential map at the origin is defined as

\exp_{0}(\mathbf{v})=\tanh\!\left(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\right)\,\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}+\delta},

(3)

where
\delta
is a small constant for numerical stability (and we additionally apply a tiny projection margin in finite precision to ensure
\|\mathbf{y}_{i}\|_{2}<1
). Since
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\exp_{0}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}
, the agent’s reasoning tree in the Poincaré ball grows outward from the origin toward the boundary. Here the root node corresponds exactly to the original prompt
p_{0}
.

2.2
Poincaré potential shaping

We first define the hyperbolic geodesic distance on the Poincaré ball (curvature fixed to
1
in our implementation). For any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{D}^{H}
,

d_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{arcosh}\!\left(1+2\,\frac{\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{(1-\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2})(1-\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}^{2})}\right).

(4)

d_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
inherits the exponential expansion of effective capacity with radius, which is well matched to the branching structure of reasoning trees (Fig.
1
(b)).
After an MCTS rollout, each node
i
is associated with a latent point
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{D}^{H}
. In RLVR, terminal nodes can be verified by a rule-based checker (e.g., whether an extracted answer matches the ground truth); we denote the hidden states of verified-correct terminal leaves by
\mathcal{Y}^{+}
. Since we construct latents in a root-centered manner, the root node satisfies
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.

For any node
i
, we define its distance to the closest successful leaf and its distance to the root as

d^{\text{[goal]}}_{i}=\min_{\mathbf{y}_{\omega}\in\mathcal{Y}^{+}}d_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{\omega}),\quad d^{\text{[root]}}_{i}=d_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{0}).

(5)

We then construct a bounded geodesic potential

V(i)=\frac{d^{\text{[root]}}_{i}}{d^{\text{[root]}}_{i}+d^{\text{[goal]}}_{i}}\in[0,1].

(6)

By construction,
V(0)\approx 0
, and for any
\mathbf{y}_{\omega}\in\mathcal{Y}^{+}
we have
V(\omega)=1
. This is a standard potential-based shaping design: shaping with potential differences preserves the optimal policy of the underlying MDP
(
Ng et al. 1999
;
Wiewiora 2003
;
Devlin & Kudenko 2012
)
. Given an edge
(i\!\to\!j)
on a sampled reaction chain, we convert the potential into a step reward

r(i,j)=V(j)-V(i).

(7)

2.3
AlphaZero-like Monte Carlo Tree Search

We attach a lightweight value head to the same pooled backbone representation
\bar{\mathbf{h}}(s)
used to define the latent state (Fig.
1
(c)).
Concretely, the value head is a linear predictor with sigmoid,

f_{\phi}(\bar{\mathbf{h}}(s))=\sigma\!\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{h}}(s)+b\right)\in(0,1),

(8)

and we supervise it using the Poincaré potential target
V(s)
with an MSE loss,

\mathcal{L}_{\text{value}}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(f_{\phi}(\bar{\mathbf{h}}(s))-V(s)\big)^{2}\Big].

(9)

After training,
f_{\phi}
serves as a learned heuristic during search, while terminal nodes are still assigned verifiable outcomes by rule-based reward functions.

During rollout, we run MCTS where (i) candidate priors are induced by the model’s cumulative log-probabilities and (ii) unvisited edges are initialized by a convex combination of the value-head prediction and this likelihood prior; only verified terminal outcomes are backed up as returns.
The complete specification (search space, statistics, selection/expansion/evaluation/backup) is given in Appendix
A.1
.

Latent-space pruning.

Language actions admit heavy paraphrastic aliasing, which can waste search budget on near-duplicate branches.
We periodically prune the search frontier in the same root-centered Poincaré latent space:
we cluster visited non-terminal nodes by hyperbolic distance and disable a fraction of nodes within each cluster, then rebuild the frontier and continue search.
This pruning step improves action-space coverage under a fixed rollout budget and is summarized in Appendix
A.1
(Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
).

2.4
Policy Optimization

After batch rollout, we obtain search trees where terminal leaves are labeled as success/failure by rule-based verification.
To avoid degenerate learning signals (e.g., almost-always-correct or almost-always-wrong rollouts), we filter rollout trees by their empirical success rate and keep only those within
(0.0,\,0.8]
.
From the retained trees we sample
n
groups for each policy update.
Each group corresponds to one prompt
q
and a set of
G
sampled continuations
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
.

For each continuation
o_{i}
, we compute a scalar return
R(q,o_{i})
by aggregating the dense step rewards along its root-to-leaf path (and optionally adding lightweight format bonuses).
We then optimize the policy using Dr. GRPO
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
.
Define the token-wise likelihood ratio

r_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})},

(10)

and the centered group advantage (shared across tokens of the same continuation)

\hat{A}_{i}=R(q,o_{i})-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{k=1}^{G}R(q,o_{k}).

(11)

The clipped objective for one group is

\mathcal{L}_{\text{policy}}=-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\min\!\left(r_{i,t}\,\hat{A}_{i},\;\operatorname{clip}\!\left(r_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)\,\hat{A}_{i}\right),

(12)

and we average
\mathcal{L}_{\text{policy}}
across the sampled groups in the update batch.

In this work, we additionally keep an small KL regularization coefficient (
\beta=10^{-8}
) to damp abrupt distribution shifts; its effect is negligible in magnitude and we omit the explicit KL term from the main objective for clarity
(
Schulman et al. 2017
;
Shao et al. 2024
)
. Finally, we jointly optimize the policy and value objectives with a fixed weight:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{policy}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{value}},\quad\lambda=0.1.

General Performance of
LaPha

Table

3.3
Ablation: Geometry for Dense Process Rewards

We ablate the geometry used for process-reward shaping while keeping the base model, rollout budget, verification protocol, and policy optimization fixed.
All variants share the same root-centered latent points
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}
and the same return aggregation; the only difference is how the per-edge step reward is constructed.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{+}
be the set of verified-correct terminal leaves, and let
\textsc{Path}(i,j)
denote the set of nodes on the unique simple path from node
i
to node
j
in the rollout tree (including endpoints). We propagate leaf-level success to intermediate states by

\mathcal{P}^{+}=\Big(\bigcup_{\omega\in\mathcal{L}^{+}}\textsc{Path}(0,\omega)\Big)\setminus\{0\},

(13)

which contains exactly the non-root nodes that lie on at least one verified-correct trajectory.

We compare three shaping schemes:

(Sparse).

A binary
step reward
that marks whether the next node lies on any verified-correct trajectory,

r_{\text{0/1}}(i,j)=\mathbb{I}[j\in\mathcal{P}^{+}].

(14)

This baseline provides supervision only on edges belonging to successful trajectories, without introducing any distance-based notion of progress.

(Euclidean).

We keep the same potential form
V(i)
and the same conversion
r(i,j)=V(j)-V(i)
as in
LaPha
, but measure distances in the ambient Euclidean space,

d_{\mathrm{E}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{2}.

(15)

(Poincaré).

The default
LaPha
instantiation, where the same potential form and the same potential-to-reward conversion are used, but distances are computed by the Poincaré geodesic metric (defined in the previous subsection).

Discussion.

Table
2
shows that hyperbolic shaping consistently outperforms both the binary surrogate (0/1) and Euclidean shaping.
Notably, Euclidean shaping is even worse than the coarse 0/1 potential.
A plausible explanation is that naive Euclidean distances on bounded latent points tend to under-resolve deep states: as search depth increases, many nodes occupy a similar narrow region in the latent space, so
d_{\mathrm{E}}
yields weak separation and noisy nearest-goal structure.
This can inject misleading intermediate rewards, which is arguably why “semantic distance” has often been avoided in practice when the metric is not chosen carefully.
In contrast, even the 0/1 surrogate avoids such metric-induced false gradients by providing a coarse but stable signal.

Figure
2
(left-top) provides a geometric intuition.
In the Euclidean view (right), deep nodes exhibit stronger crowding and reduced value contrast.
In the Poincaré disk (left), points near the boundary may look close under the 2D Euclidean visualization, but the hyperbolic metric expands distances near the boundary, which preserves separation among deep branches; this is reflected by the clearer color stratification in
V_{\mathrm{P}}(i)
and leads to more informative dense rewards for long-horizon tree learning.

Journey of the Value Head

We inspect how the value head behaves under latent-space supervision, using

(1) Correctness-aligned ranking beyond likelihood.

(2) Value predictions are consistent with the geometry-derived potential.

3.5
Test-time scaling

Beyond improving exploration during training, the learned value head also enables test-time scaling without introducing any additional model.
We fix the trained
LaPha
checkpoint and vary the MCTS simulation budget at inference time.
For each prompt, MCTS is guided by the value head during selection, and we return the terminal leaf with the highest predicted state value.

to about
88\%
.
Horizontal reference lines are included for comparison.

Figure
3
shows a compute–performance trend: allocating more simulations yields better decisions.
Notably, the improvement is steep when increasing the budget from 1 to 4–16 simulations, and then gradually saturates, which is consistent with MCTS concentrating visits on a small set of promising subtrees once a reliable heuristic is available.

3.6
Pruning enables fuller action-space exploration

We keep the overall training and evaluation protocol fixed, and only ablate whether latent-space pruning is enabled.
When pruning is enabled, we run MCTS with a fixed simulation budget per prompt and periodically perform global clustering + pruning on visited non-terminal nodes.

Figure 4
:
Effect of latent-space pruning. Left: training loss. Right: AIME’24 validation score.

Figure
4
shows that pruning improves both optimization and generalization.
The pruning run begins with a slightly higher loss, consistent with pruning preventing the search from repeatedly expanding a small set of high-likelihood paraphrases and instead forcing exploration into less-visited regions.
After this transient, pruning converges faster and reaches a lower final loss, suggesting that rollout data become less redundant and provide higher-quality learning signals.

The right panel shows a persistent advantage on AIME’24 validation.
We attribute this to reduced semantic collapse of the frontier: without pruning, many expansions concentrate in dense paraphrase clusters, so a large fraction of simulations revisit near-duplicate states.
Pruning counteracts this by clustering nodes in the shared latent space and removing a fraction of near-duplicates, reallocating budget to under-explored semantic regions and increasing the chance of discovering rare-but-correct solution paths.

4
Related Work

Verifiable rewards for reasoning and outcome-supervised RL.
A growing body of work uses verifiable rewards—signals checked by unit tests, symbolic solvers, or strict formatting rules—to train reasoning behaviors in math and code
(
Wen et al. 2025
)
.
Outcome-supervised optimization methods built around GRPO and variants have shown strong empirical performance
(
Shao et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
, but they often inherit a core limitation of sparse supervision: many pipelines assign near-uniform reward across a successful trajectory, blurring credit across the chain and encouraging unnecessary detours or excessive self-verification
(
Xie et al. 2025
;
Peng et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
.
Our approach addresses this bottleneck by deriving dense process rewards from a geometry-defined potential over latent states.

Potential-based reward shaping.
Potential-based reward shaping (PBRS) converts a state potential into dense transition rewards while preserving optimal policies under standard conditions
(
Ng et al. 1999
;
Wiewiora 2003
;
Devlin & Kudenko 2012
)
.
Classical potentials often derive from task-specific distances in navigation or planning.
We adopt the same principle, but define the potential on a root-centered hyperbolic latent space induced by backbone hidden states, so that distance-to-goal can be computed on reasoning trees.

Search-based reasoning and MCTS for LLMs.
Tree-structured inference such as Tree-of-Thoughts and related planning formulations improve reasoning by exploring multiple partial solutions
(
Yao et al. 2023
;
Hao et al. 2023
)
.
Several works avoid purely heuristic search by learning value functions or reward models to guide expansion, including AlphaZero-like designs and MCTS-based self-training
(
Feng et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Guan et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2024
)
.

5
Conclusion and Discussion

Empirical results suggest that negative-curvature latent geometry is a practical interface for aligning search, reward shaping, and value estimation with the branching structure of agentic reasoning.

5.1
Discussion: unifying post-training via latent preference shaping

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on annotated reasoning traces is token-level maximum likelihood under teacher forcing.
Given a reference trajectory
\tau^{\star}=\{(s_{t}^{\star},a_{t}^{\star})\}_{t=1}^{T}
(e.g., an annotated chain-of-thought or tool-call trace),
SFT minimizes

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{\tau^{\star}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathtt{SFT}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}^{\star}\mid s_{t}^{\star}).

(16)

Here each
s_{t}^{\star}
is constructed from ground-truth prefixes, so training is performed on the expert state distribution rather than on the model’s own rollouts.
This distribution mismatch is a well-known source of compounding errors over long horizons (often referred to as exposure bias in sequence generation and covariate shift in imitation learning), where small local mistakes can quickly push the model into unseen states
(
Bengio et al. 2015
;
Ranzato et al. 2015
)
.

LaPha
suggests a complementary view: instead of treating correctness annotations as a policy to execute, we can treat them as anchors that induce a preference signal over the model’s own sampled actions.
Ignoring the clipping for exposition, the policy-gradient updates can be written as a weighted log-likelihood update on on-policy samples:

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\left[\hat{A}(\tau)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right],

(17)

where
\hat{A}(\tau)
is a centered group advantage shared across tokens of the same rollout.
Thus, the update increases the probability of the model’s own actions in proportion to a preference weight
\hat{A}(\tau)
, rather than forcing exact imitation of a teacher trajectory.
In
LaPha
, this preference weight is derived from latent-space progress: the Poincaré potential assigns dense intermediate rewards so trajectories that move closer to verified correctness receive larger advantages.

When correctness annotations are available, they can be incorporated without executing the annotated policy.
Concretely, we embed an annotated trace into the same root-centered Poincaré latent space and treat its latent states as an additional goal-anchor set
\mathcal{Y}^{\star}
.
Then the goal distance can be computed against both verified-correct leaves and the annotation anchors:

d^{\text{[goal]}}_{i}=\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{+}\cup\mathcal{Y}^{\star}}d_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}),

(18)

while the potential and step rewards remain unchanged.
This turns discrete correctness supervision into a shaped preference over the model’s on-policy rollouts, providing a path to inject annotated knowledge into RLVR even when the model rarely discovers “good cases” by itself.
In the limiting case where the preference weights collapse to a binary indicator on the annotated trajectory, the objective recovers standard SFT;
LaPha
corresponds to the softer, geometry-induced regime where supervision is expressed as a graded preference over the model’s current policy support.

A.1
Monte Carlo Tree Search

Figure 5
:
Rollout Trees

This appendix specifies the MCTS procedure used in
LaPha
, including the search space, maintained statistics, the PUCT selection rule, likelihood-induced priors, value-based initialization, terminal-only backup, and latent-space pruning.

Search space and statistics.

MCTS is performed over step-wise assistant actions.
A node corresponds to a dialogue state
s
(messages plus any tool feedback), and an edge corresponds to choosing one candidate completion
a
(ending with an end-of-turn token), leading to the next state
s^{\prime}=\texttt{apply\_chat\_template}(s,a)
.
For each state–action edge
(s,a)
, we maintain a visit count
N(s,a)
and an empirical action value
Q(s,a)
, defined as the running mean of terminal returns backed up through that edge.
We write
N(s)=\sum_{b}N(s,b)
for the total visits over outgoing edges.
For edges that have not received any terminal backup, we keep an initialization value
Q_{0}(s,a)
(defined below) which is used only for selection.

Selection (PUCT).

Starting from the root
s_{0}
, we repeatedly select actions by

a^{*}(s)=\arg\max_{a}\Big(\hat{Q}(s,a)+c\,P(s,a)\,\frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1+N(s,a)}\Big),

(19)

where
c
is the exploration constant and
P(s,a)
is a prior over candidate actions sampled at
s
.
The effective value is defined as

\hat{Q}(s,a)=\begin{cases}Q(s,a),&N(s,a)>0,\\
Q_{0}(s,a),&N(s,a)=0.\end{cases}

(20)

Expansion and likelihood prior.

When an expandable leaf state
s
is reached, we sample
B
candidate completions
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{B}
from the current policy.
For a sampled completion
a_{k}=(a_{k,1},\dots,a_{k,|a_{k}|})
, we compute its cumulative log-probability

p(s,a_{k})=\sum_{t=1}^{|a_{k}|}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{k,t}\mid s,a_{k,<t}),

(21)

and define the sibling prior by softmax normalization,

P(s,a_{k})=\frac{\exp(p(s,a_{k}))}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\exp(p(s,a_{j}))}.

(22)

Evaluation and initialization.

For each newly created child
s^{\prime}=\texttt{apply\_chat\_template}(s,a)
, we compute its pooled representation
\bar{\mathbf{h}}(s^{\prime})
(and cache its latent state
\mathbf{y}_{s^{\prime}}
), and obtain the value prediction
f_{\theta}(\bar{\mathbf{h}}(s^{\prime}))
.
Before any terminal backup is observed on
(s,a)
, we initialize its value for selection by mixing the value head with the likelihood prior,

Q_{0}(s,a)=\eta\,f_{\theta}(\bar{\mathbf{h}}(s^{\prime}))+(1-\eta)\,P(s,a),

(23)

where
\eta\in[0,1]
is a fixed mixing weight.

Backup (terminal-only).

A state is marked terminal if it triggers explicit answer extraction, reaches the depth limit, degenerates into echo/repetition, or hits a context-length limit.
Terminal nodes receive a verifiable return
R\in[0,1]
from rule-based reward functions.
Only these terminal outcomes are backed up: for every traversed edge
(s,a)
on the selected path, we update

\displaystyle N(s,a)\leftarrow N(s,a)+1,

(24)

\displaystyle Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\frac{R-Q(s,a)}{N(s,a)}.

Non-terminal value predictions are used only as a heuristic for selection and are not treated as ground-truth returns.

Latent-space pruning.

To mitigate paraphrastic aliasing, we periodically prune the search frontier using the same root-centered Poincaré latent space.
We cluster visited non-terminal nodes by hyperbolic distance and disable a fraction of nodes within each cluster, then rebuild the frontier and continue search.

A.2
Should Value Supervision Update the Backbone?

We isolate whether the value regression loss should be allowed to modify the shared backbone representation that also supports generation.
We compare two configurations under identical rollout, optimization, and evaluation settings: (a)
full joint training
, where gradients from the value regression loss are propagated into the backbone; and (b) a
stop-gradient
variant, where the value head is trained but its regression loss does not update the backbone (only the policy loss updates the shared layers).

Figure 6
:

Ablation on whether the value regression loss is allowed to update the backbone (full vs. freeze), reported by the value MSE and the resulting average correct rate.

Figure
6
shows a consistent long-horizon advantage for full joint training.
In the early phase of training, the stop-gradient variant can preserve the initial policy behavior more closely, but it plateaus earlier: the value MSE decreases more slowly and the average correct rate saturates at a lower level.
In contrast, when the backbone is allowed to receive value gradients, the model continues to improve in both value calibration (lower MSE) and outcome quality (higher average correct rate).

This result suggests that the geometry-driven targets are not merely useful as a post-hoc critic for search-time reranking.
Instead, they provide an additional learning signal that reshapes the internal representation so that progress becomes more readable from the shared pooled features
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{i}
.
Since the policy head and value head operate on the same latent, improving the representation for value prediction can also improve generation quality, rather than only enabling better selection at inference.
Concretely, on MATH500 the fully joint model reaches
76.4%
average accuracy, compared to
67.2%
for the stop-gradient variant under identical settings.
