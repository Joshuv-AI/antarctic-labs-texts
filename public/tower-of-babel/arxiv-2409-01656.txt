Graphons of Line Graphs
Sevvandi Kandanaarachchi, Cheng Soon Ong

Abstract
We consider the problem of estimating graph limits, known as graphons, from observations of sequences of sparse finite graphs. In this paper we show a simple method that can shed light on a subset of sparse graphs. The method involves mapping the original graphs to their line graphs . We show that graphs satisfying a particular property are sparse, but give rise to dense line graphs. This property, called the square-degree property , enables us to apply results on graph limits of dense graphs to derive convergence and characterize the space of converging graph sequences. In particular, star graphs satisfy the square-degree property resulting in dense line graphs and non-zero graphons of line graphs. Similarly, superlinear preferential attachment graphs and some mixture graphs give rise to dense line graphs almost surely. In contrast, dense graphs, including Erdős–Rényi graphs make the line graphs sparse, resulting in the zero graphon.

Notation and Preliminaries

A simple graph is a graph without loops or multiple edges between the same nodes. We only consider simple graphs and sequences of simple graphs in this paper.

2.1
Line graphs

Let
G
denote a graph. If
G
has at least one edge, then its line graph is the graph whose vertices are the edges of
G
, with two of these vertices being adjacent if the corresponding edges are adjacent in
G

(
beineke2021line
)
. Figure
3
shows an example of a graph and its line graph. The edges in the graph on the left are mapped to the vertices in the line graph (on the right) as can be seen from the numbers.

Figure 3:
A graph on the left and its line graph on the right.

We denote the line graph operation by
L

we denote its line graph by
H:=L(G)
. In terms of notation we make a distinction between graphs

and line graphs

H
, with and without subscripts, to denote line graphs.

Rather than a single graph

\{G_{n}\}_{n}
denote a graph sequence. The index

mostly denotes the number of nodes in
G_{n}
and let the number of edges be given by

. We denote the line graph of
G_{n}
by

as
H_{m}
has
m
nodes.

Definition 2.1
.

If
G
is a graph whose line graph is
H
, that is,

, then
G
is called the
root
of

.

From Whitney’s work
(
whitney1932congruent
)
it follows that the structure of a graph can be recovered from its line graph with one exception: if the line graph

is

, a triangle, then the root of

can be either
K_{1,3}
, a star or
K_{3}
a triangle. This follows from the following theorem as stated in

:

Theorem 2.2

(Whitney1932, Harary 1969)
.

Let
G
and

be connected graphs with isomorphic line graphs. Then
G
and

are isomorphic unless one is

and the other is

.

Based on this theorem many algorithms compute the root of a line graph when
H
is not
K_{3}

(
ROUSSOPOULOS1973108
;
Lehot74
;
liu2015iligra
)
.
We state some preliminary results on line graphs covered in Chapter 1 of
beineke2021line
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

be a non-null graph with

If

2.2
Graph sequences

The edge density of a graph
G
with
n
nodes and
m
edges is given by
\text{density}(G)=\frac{2m}{n(n-1)}
. Thus, from Lemma
2.3
(1) the edge density of
H=L(G)
is given by

\text{density}(H)=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sum(\text{deg }\,v^{2})-m}{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}\,,

(1)

where
\text{deg }v
denotes the degree distribution of graph
G
and
\text{deg }v^{2}
denotes the vector of squared degrees in
G
. We refer to the edge density simply as density.

Definition 2.4

(
Dense graph sequences).

A sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
is dense if the number of edges
m
grow quadratically with the number of nodes
n
, i.e.,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{m}{n^{2}}=c>0\,.

We denote the set of all dense graph sequences by
D
.

Definition 2.5

(
Sparse graph sequences).

A sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
is sparse if the number of edges
m
grow sub-quadratically with the number of nodes
n
, i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{m}{n^{2}}=0\,.

We denote the set of all sparse graph sequences by
S
.

For dense graph sequences, the density is bounded from below by a non-zero constant, whereas for sparse graph sequences it goes to zero.
The density or
m/n^{2}
of a sequence of dense graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
does not necessarily converge; the
\liminf
is strictly positive, i.e., any converging subsequence has strictly positive density as
n\to\infty
. In contrast, the density or
m/n^{2}
of sparse graphs converge to zero, i.e., the limit is equal to zero, not just the
\liminf
. The set of dense graph sequences
D
and the set of sparse graph sequences
S
is non-intersecting. Furthermore, the complement of the union of
D
and
S
,
\overline{D\cup S}
is non-empty. It contains graph sequences
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
such that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}m/n^{2}=0\neq\limsup_{n\to\infty}m/n^{2}
, i.e, it is a mixture of dense and sparse graph sequences with the density of different subsequences converging to different limits with some converging to zero.

2.3
Graphons

Next we turn our attention to graphons. A graphon is a symmetric, measurable function
W:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
often used to describe both the limiting properties of graph sequences as well as the graph generation process
(
borgs2011limits
)
. We define some terms often used in the graphon literature.

Definition 2.6
.

A
graph homomorphism
from
F
to
G
is a map
f:V(F)\to V(G)
such that if
uv\in E(F)
then
f(u)f(v)\in E(G)
. (Maps edges to edges.) Let
\text{Hom}(F,G)
be the set of all such homomorphisms and let
\hom(F,G)=|\text{Hom}(F,G)|
. Then
homomorphism density
is defined as

t(F,G)=\frac{\hom(F,G)}{|V(G)|^{|V(F)|}}\,.

The number of homomorphisms
\hom(F,G)
is given by

\hom(F,G)=\sum_{\phi:V(F)\to V(G)}\prod_{uv\in E(F)}\beta_{\phi(u)\phi(v)}(G)

where
\beta_{ij}(G)
is the weight of edge
ij
in graph
G
, which equals either 1 or 0 in unweighted graphs.
For a graphon
W
, the homomorphism density is defined as

t(F,W)=\int_{[0,1]^{|V(F)|}}\prod_{ij\in E(F)}W(x_{i},x_{j})\,dx\,.

A graph homomorphism is an edge preserving map from one graph to another. The homomorphism density is useful as it is bounded even when the number of homomorphisms
\hom(F,G)
go to infinity.

Definition 2.7
.

The
cut norm
of graphon
W

(
frieze1999quick
;
borgs2008convergent
)
is defined as

\lVert W\rVert_{\square}=\sup_{S,T}\left|\int_{S\times T}W(x,y)\,dxdy\right|\,,

where the supremum is taken over all measurable sets
S
and
T
of
[0,1]
.

Definition 2.8
.

Given two graphons
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
the
cut metric

(
borgs2008convergent
)
is defined as

\delta_{\square}(W_{1},W_{2})=\inf_{\varphi}\left\lVert W_{1}-W_{2}^{\varphi}\right\rVert_{\square}\,,

where the infimum is taken over all measure preserving bijections
\varphi:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
denote the space of graphons, i.e.,
\mathcal{W}=\{W\in\mathcal{W}\}
. Then, the cut metric is a pseudo-metric in
\mathcal{W}
because
\delta_{\square}(W_{1},W_{2})=0
does not imply
W_{1}=W_{2}
, i.e.,
\delta_{\square}(W_{1},W_{2})\geq 0
for
W_{1}\neq W_{2}
. However the cut metric
\delta_{\square}
is a metric on the quotient space
\tilde{\mathcal{W}}=\mathcal{W}/\sim
where
f\sim g
if
f(x,y)=g(\sigma x,\sigma y)
for some measure preserving
\sigma
.

Definition 2.9
.

Uniformly pick
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots x_{n}
from
[0,1]
. A
W-random graph

\mathbb{G}(n,W)
has the vertex set
1,2,\ldots n
and vertices
i
and
j
are connected with probability
W(x_{i},x_{j})
.

We can think of
W
-random graphs as graphs sampled from the graphon
W
.

The homomorphism density is used to define graph convergence.

Definition 2.10

(
(
borgs2008convergent
)
)
.

A graph sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
is said to be convergent if
t(F,G_{n})
converges as
n
goes to infinity for any simple graph
F
.

Every finite, simple graph
G
can be represented by a graphon
W_{G}
, which we call its empirical graphon.

Definition 2.11
.

Given a graph
G
with
n
vertices labeled
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we define its
empirical graphon

W_{G}:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow[0,1]
as follows: We split the interval
[0,1]
into
n
equal intervals
\{I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{n}\}
(first one closed, all others half open) and for
x\in I_{i},y\in I_{j}
define

W_{G}(x,y)=\begin{cases}1&\,\text{if}\quad ij\in E(G)\\
0&\,\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

where
E(G)
denotes the edges of
G
. The empirical graphon replaces the the adjacency matrix with a unit square and the
(i,j)
th entry of the adjacency matrix is replaced with a square of size
(1/n)\times(1/n)
.

The cut metric between graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
is defined as
\delta_{\square}\left(G,G^{\prime}\right)=\delta_{\square}\left(W_{G},W_{G^{\prime}}\right)
. The cut metric between a graph
G
and a graphon
U
is defined as
\delta_{\square}\left(G,U\right)=\delta_{\square}\left(W_{G},U\right)
.

The following theorem show that a graph sequence converges to a graphon when the corresponding homomorphism density converges. The subsequent theorem establishes the equivalence between homomorphism density and the cut metric convergence.

Theorem 2.12

(
borgs2008convergent
)
.

For every convergent sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
of simple graphs there is a graphon
W
with values in
[0,1]
such that
t(F,G_{n})\rightarrow t(F,W)
for every simple graph
F
. Moreover for every graphon
W
with values in
[0,1]
there is a convergent sequence of graphs satisfying this relation.

Theorem 2.13

(
borgs2011limits
)
.

A sequence of graphs
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
is convergent if and only if it is Cauchy in the
\delta_{\square}
distance. The sequence
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
converges to
W
if and only if
\delta_{\square}(W_{G_{n}},W)\to 0
. Furthermore, if this is the case, and
|V(G_{n})|\to\infty

such that
\lVert W_{G_{n}}-W\rVert_{\square}\to 0

Next we state a result in

Definition 2.14
.

(

Let a mass-partition to be a sequence

Given a mass-partition

A mass-partition can be defined for any graph using the sizes of its connected components.

Definition 2.16

For any graph

Theorem 2.17
.

(

If

(

A graph limit is a line graph limit if and only if it is a disjoint clique graph limit.

2.5
Related work

2.5.1
Graphons of sparse graphs

The fundamental problem of modeling sparse graphs using graphons is that the graphon converges to zero for sparse graphs. Collaborations led by Borgs and Chayes have resulted in considerable work on sparse graph limits
(
Borgs2017LD
;
borgs2019Lp
;
borgs2018sparse
)
. Their work encompasses a variety of analytic and measure theoretic solutions including new norms and convergence measures.
borgs2018sparse
overcome the zero graphon by stretching the domain of the graphon to the whole positive quadrant (stretched graphon) in one formulation and rescaling the graphon values to the positive reals (rescaled graphon) in the other.

It is worth noting that traditional graphons model graphs as discrete structures using the theoretical underpinnings of exchangeable random arrays
(
diaconis2008graph
)
and the Aldous-Hoover theorem.
caron2017sparse
demonstrate that treating graphs as continuous structures makes it possible to represent sparse graphs using a graphon based on Kallenberg exchangeability. They use point processes to model graphs and use exchangeability theorems for continuous domains in their regularity framework. This construction was later used by
veitch2015class
to introduce graphexes
\mathcal{W}=(W,S,I)
, a triple consisting of a graphon, a star function and isolated edges. Graphexes can model sparse graphs and was extended to multi-graphexes in
borgs2021limits
.

Edge-exchangeability is another avenue used to model sparse graphs. The theory of exchangeable random matrices is generally applied to the adjacency matrix and thus consider vertex exchangeable graphs, which are invariant under node permutations. If the node id is indicative of the time a node joins the graph, vertex exchangeability implies that the connections a node forms is independent of the time it joins. But some sparse graphs, such as preferential attachment graphs do not obey this rule and thus are not vertex exchangeable. For these kinds of graphs, the earlier a node joins, the more connections it makes. Instead of considering vertex exchangeability,
crane2018edge
;
Crane_Dempsey_2019
introduce edge-exchangeable network models and show that these models allow for sparse structure and power-law degree distributions.
cai2016edge
and
Janson2018
extend this model
show that graphs ranging from dense to very sparse graphs can be generated by using their construction.

Another way to model sparse graphs is to consider a real-valued sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n}
converging to zero and to consider a sequence of graphons
\{W_{n}\}_{n}
where
W_{n}=\rho_{n}W

do not exhibit hub structure, they are somewhat similar to Erdős–Rényi

Our work has similarities and differences to the work discussed above. The key difference in our work is that instead of working with the original graph we work with a transformed graph, viz., its line graph. We show that a subset of sparse graphs are dense under the line graph transformation. As vertex permutation in the line graph space is the same as edge permutation in the original graph space, we expect graphs corresponding to dense line graphs to be edge exchangeable. Thus, this work has some overlap with edge-exchangeability discussed above.

Graphon applications in machine learning

The theory of graphons is well-developed and is used in machine learning applications for modeling and explanation
(

Levie2023

3
Sparse graphs with dense line graphs

In this section, we show that there are sparse graphs whose line graphs are dense.
In particular we show in Theorem
3.6
that sparse graphs with square-degree property
(Definition
3.1
)
have corresponding line graphs that are dense, and vice versa.
This enables us to define a novel approach
to defining graph limits for sparse graphs by their associated line graphs.
Recall we denote graph sequences as
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
and the corresponding line graph sequence as
\{H_{m}\}_{m}
. If the sequences converge, then we consistently use
W
and
U
for graphons corresponding to
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
and
\{H_{m}\}_{m}
respectively.

Next we define a property of a graph sequence that we call the
square-degree property
.

Definition 3.1

(
Square-degree property
Sq
).

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
denote a sequence of graphs. We say that
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
exhibits the square-degree property if there exists some
c_{1}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N_{0}
we have

\sum\text{deg }v_{i,n}^{2}\geq c_{1}\left(\sum\text{deg }v_{i,n}\right)^{2}\,.

We denote the set of graph sequences satisfying the square-degree property by
S_{q}
, i.e. if
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
satisfies
Sq
then
\{G_{n}\}_{n}\in S_{q}
.

We note that Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives
c_{1}=1/n
, which is not satisfactory as we need a strictly positive lower bound
c_{1}>0
for all
n
.
The square-degree property says that the ratio between the sum of the degree squared
and square of the sum of degrees is bounded from below as
n
goes to infinity.
As the degree of a node is either zero or positive, this cannot be satisfied if the degree distribution is uniform, because then the sum of the mixed product terms
\text{deg }v_{i,n}\times\text{deg }v_{j,n}
would hold the bulk weight compared to the square terms
(\text{deg }v_{i,n})^{2}
, especially as there are
n\choose{2}
mixed product terms and only
n
square terms. Therefore, we expect a graph sequence satisfying this property to have some inequalities in the degree distribution. For example, it may contain a set of “big player” nodes with large degree values.

kandOng2025graphonMixtures
showed that the square-degree property is equivalent to a simpler condition called
max-degree
for graph sequences
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
that have converging line graphs.

Definition 3.2
.

(Max-degree condition)

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
be a sequence of graphs where
G_{n}
has
n
nodes and
m
edges. Let the maximum degree of
G_{n}
be denoted by
d_{\max,n}
. We say the
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
satisfies the max-degree condition if there exists
c>0
such that

\liminf_{n,m\to\infty}\frac{d_{\max,n}}{m}=c>0\,.

Let
S_{x}
denote the set of graph sequences satisfying the max-degree condition.

Lemma 3.3

(
kandOng2025graphonMixtures

.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
be a graph sequence with
H_{m}=L(G_{n})
. Suppose
\{H_{m}\}_{m}
converges to

. Then

\{G_{n}\}_{n}\in S_{q}\equiv\{G_{n}\}_{n}\in S_{x}\,.

This was proved recognizing that line graph limits can be represented by mass-partitions.
The focus of

Lemma 3.4

As

n(n-1)^{2}\geq\sum\text{deg }v_{i,n}^{2}\geq c_{1}\left(\sum\text{deg }v_{i,n}\right)^{2}=4c_{1}m^{2}\,,

(3)

we get

∎

3.2

Theorem 3.6
.

Proof.

1.

\sum\text{deg }v_{i,n}^{2}\geq c_{1}\left(\sum\text{deg }v_{i,n}\right)^{2}=4c_{1}m^{2}\,,

where

\displaystyle\text{density}(H_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(\text{deg }\,v_{i,n})^{2}-m}{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}\,,

(4)

\displaystyle=\frac{2c_{1}-\frac{1}{m}}{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2m}}\,.

Next we show

If

1.

\displaystyle\text{density}(H_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}nr^{2}-\frac{1}{2}nr}{\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}rn(\frac{1}{2}rn-1)}\,,

(42)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}rn(r-1)}{\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}rn\frac{1}{2}(rn-2)}\,,

(43)

\displaystyle=\frac{4(r-1)}{rn-2}\,.

(44)

Thus,

\displaystyle\text{density}(H_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}(1^{2}+(n-2)2^{2}+1^{2})-m}{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}(2+4(m-1))-m}{\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)}\,,

7.2
Superlinear preferential attachment graphs

Preferential attachment models
(
albert2002statistical
)
consider nodes connecting to more connected nodes with higher probability. Specifically the probability
\Pi(i)
that a new node connects to node
i
, which has degree
k_{i}
is given by

\Pi(i)=\frac{k_{i}^{\alpha}}{\sum_{i}k_{i}^{\alpha}}\,,

(47)

where
\alpha
is a parameter. The three regimes
\alpha<1
,
\alpha=1
and
\alpha>1
are called sublinear, linear and superlinear preferential attachment respectively. Suppose we start with
s_{0}
nodes and
s_{0}
edges and at each time step
t
a new node is added to the network with
s
edges. After
t
timesteps the network has

n=t+s_{0}\quad\text{nodes and }\quad m=s_{0}+ts\quad\text{edges.}

(48)

For growing networks with superlinear preferential attachment
Krapivsky2001
;
Krapivsky2011
state that the maximum degree
k_{\max}
satisfies

k_{\max}\sim n\,.

Sethuraman2019
prove a more rigorous version of the above statement. They show that,

P\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}k_{\max}=1\right]=1\,.

We will use this result to show that superlinear preferential attachment graphs satisfy the square-degree property almost surely.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n}
denote a sequence of graphs growing by superlinear preferential attachment satisfying equation (
47
) with
\alpha>1
. Then
\{G_{n}\}_{n}\in S_{q}
almost surely.

Proof.

Using the result from
Sethuraman2019
we know that for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

P\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}k_{\max}-1\right|<\epsilon\right]=1\,,

for all
n>N_{0}
. That is, almost surely

1-\epsilon<\frac{1}{n}k_{\max}<1+\epsilon\,,

for
n>N_{0}
.
Rearranging the equations for
n
and
m
(equation (
48
)) we get
ns=m+(s-1)s_{0}
giving us
ns>m
. Hence,

\sum(\text{deg }v_{i}^{2})>k_{\max}^{2}>(1-\epsilon)^{2}n^{2}>\frac{(1-\epsilon)^{2}m^{2}}{s^{2}}\quad\text{almost surely.}

Thus, for
n>N_{0}

P\left[\sum(\text{deg }v_{i}^{2})>\frac{(1-\epsilon)^{2}}{s^{2}}m^{2}\right]=1

showing that superlinear preferential attachment graphs satisfy the square-degree property (Definition
3.1
) almost surely for large values of
n
. From Theorem
3.6
they produce dense line graphs. If
\{t(F,H_{m})\}_{m}
converges for all graphs
F
, where
H_{m}=L(G_{n})
then Theorem
2.12

(
borgs2008convergent
)
ensures
\{H_{m}\}_{m}
converges to a graphon
U
. As
\{H_{m}\}_{m}
is dense
U\neq 0
.
∎

8
(U,W)
-mixture graphs

(U,W)
-mixture graphs were introduced in
kandOng2025graphonMixtures
, where the central idea is joining two graphs sampled from the graphons
U
and
W
where
U
is a disjoint clique graphon. Recall that a disjoint clique graphon can be represented by a mass-partition
\bm{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)
(Definition
2.14
) and a graph sampled from a disjoint clique graphon is a collection of disjoint cliques with the proportion of nodes in the
i^{\text{th}}
largest clique given by
p_{i}
. As the line graph of a star graph is complete, the root (Definition
2.1
) of a complete graph for
n>3
is a star graph. Thus, the root graph of a collection of disjoint cliques is a collection of disjoint stars.
(U,W)
-mixture graphs join graphs sampled from
W
with this collection of stars. The key is that the root graph of the graph sampled from
U
(or
\bm{p}
) is joined with the graph sampled from
W
making it possible to generate sparse graph sequences.

Definition 8.1
.

Given a graphon
W
, a disjoint clique graphon
U
, and positive, integer valued sequences
\{n_{d_{i}}\}_{i}
and
\{m_{s_{i}}\}_{i}
increasing with
i
, a
\bm{(U,W)}
-mixture graph sequence

G_{n_{i}}\sim\mathbb{G}\left(U,W,n_{d_{i}},m_{s_{i}}\right)
is constructed as follows:

1.

Let
G_{d_{i}}\sim\mathbb{G}(n_{d_{i}},W)
and
H_{s_{i}}\sim\mathbb{G}(m_{s_{i}},U)
, i.e.,
G_{d_{i}}
is a
W
-random graph (Definition
2.9
) and
H_{s_{i}}
is a
U
-random graph. Specifically,
H_{s_{i}}
is a collection of disjoint cliques with clique proportions satisfying mass-partition
\bm{p}
. Sequences
\{G_{d_{i}}\}_{i}
and
\{H_{s_{i}}\}_{i}
consist of dense graphs.

2.

As
H_{s_{i}}
is a collection of disjoint cliques, there exists a collection of stars
G_{s_{i}}
such that
H_{s_{i}}=L(G_{s_{i}})
.
Note that
\{G_{s_{i}}\}_{i}
is a sparse graph sequence.

3.

Join
\{G_{d_{i}}\}_{i}
and
\{G_{s_{i}}\}_{i}
satisfying graph joining rules in Definition
8.2
. Let
\{G_{n_{i}}\}_{i}
denote the resulting graph sequence.

We say
G_{n_{i}}\sim\mathbb{G}\left(U,W,n_{d_{i}},m_{s_{i}}\right)
is a
(U,W)
-mixture graph. We call
G_{d_{i}}
the
dense part
and
G_{s_{i}}
the
sparse part
of
G_{n_{i}}
.

The joining rules below preserve sparsity: if the disjoint union of
\{G_{d_{i}}\}_{i}
and
\{G_{s_{i}}\}_{i}
is sparse, then the joined graph sequence
\{G_{n_{i}}\}_{i}
remains sparse. In other words, the join does not change a sparse disjoint union to a dense graph sequence and vice versa.

Definition 8.2
.

(Graph Joining Rules)

Let
\{G_{s_{i}}\}_{i}
and
\{G_{d_{i}}\}_{i}
be two graph sequences. We are interested in joining
G_{s_{i}}
and
G_{d_{i}}
for every
i
. Let
G_{n_{i}}
denote the joined graph. We consider graph joins that satisfy the following conditions.

1.

No new nodes
: no new nodes are added as part of the joining process.

2.

No deletion
: No nodes or edges are deleted as part of the joining process.

3.

Random edges
: if edges are added, they are added randomly in the sense that nodes within a graph are equally likely to be selected to form edges.

4.

New edges
:
if edges are added, the number of new edges added
m_{new_{i}}
satisfies
m_{new_{i}}\leq c^{\prime}m_{d_{i}}
, for small

denotes the number of edges in

kandOng2025graphonMixtures
(Lemma 3.4) show that if

-mixture graphs in terms of graphons.

In Theorem

we consider the disjoint union of

and

In Theorem

-mixture graphs because the number of new edges in Definition

9
Further questions

A common thread between
(U,W)
-mixture graphs and graphexes
(
veitch2015class
;
borgs2021limits
)
is that both constructions add stars. Graphexes have a star function
S
that adds edges to each node depending on its ‘starness’ given by
S(\theta)
where
\theta
is the latent position of the node. In contrast,
(U,W)
-mixture graphs have a mass-partition
\bm{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)
that add stars given by the proportions of
\bm{p}
. Even though the mass-partition can have infinite elements,
\bm{p}
is discrete and is different from
S
, which is defined on a continuous domain. Exploring whether a connection exists between these two constructions offers a direction of inquiry.

We have investigated line graph limits and shown that a subset of sparse graphs have dense line graphs, endowing a structure on the space of converging graph sequences. In addition to the line graph, there can be other transformations that map subsets of sparse graphs to dense graphs. For example, are there transformations that map graphs in
S\backslash S_{x}
such as cycles and paths to dense graphs? This is a question of interest. More generally, what properties are needed in a transformation so that the transformed graph reveals novel structures that converge to a limit?
