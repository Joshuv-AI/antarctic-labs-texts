Calcification-Mediated Dual-Starvation and Immune Activation for Transarterial Chemoembolization
Authors: Yanqiao Ren, Bo Sun, Chao Chen, Wenlong Wu, GuoFeng Zhou, Bin Liang, Chuansheng Zheng, Yiming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTransarterial chemoembolization (TACE) remains a cornerstone therapy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but its efficacy is frequently limited by inadequate drug penetration and tumor dissemination.MethodsWe developed a thermosensitive PND nanogel co-encapsulating tirapazamine (TPZ) and folic acid (FA), TPZ/FA@PND. The system was evaluated for sustained drug release, physiological temperature-triggered sol-gel transition, in vitro antitumor effects under normoxia and hypoxia, calcification induction, biocompatibility, and in vivo therapeutic efficacy in a VX2 tumor-bearing rabbit TACE model.ResultsTPZ/FA@PND showed sustained release and physiological temperature-triggered gelation. In vitro, TPZ/FA@PND inhibited tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion under hypoxia while inducing extracellular calcification, with negligible hemolysis and minimal hepatotoxicity. In vivo, TPZ/FA@PND delivered via TACE achieved complete embolization, induced marginal calcification, reduced metastasis, inhibited VEGF and MMP-9 expression, enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and promoted systemic antitumor immunity.DiscussionThis dual-starvation nanogel strategy integrates embolization, calcification-mediated containment, and immune activation, offering a clinically translatable approach to improve the therapeutic efficacy and long-term outcomes of TACE for HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, largely due to its high recurrence and metastatic potential.1,2 Transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) has been established as a cornerstone locoregional therapy for patients with unresectable HCC, as it combines arterial embolization with high local concentrations of chemotherapeutic agents to induce tumor ischemia and cytotoxicity.3–5 However, the clinical benefits of TACE are frequently limited by two major biological barriers.6,7 First, the dense extracellular matrix and heterogeneous vascular architecture of HCC restrict deep drug penetration, resulting in a necrotic tumor core surrounded by viable peripheral tumor cells.8 Second, the highly invasive and metastatic nature of residual tumor cells, particularly under post-embolization hypoxic stress, promotes early intrahepatic dissemination and recurrence.9 These limitations collectively compromise the long-term therapeutic efficacy of TACE.

To address insufficient drug penetration, various nanoparticle-based delivery systems and embolic materials have been developed to enhance local drug retention and intratumoral distribution.10,11 For example, drug-eluting beads, liposomal formulations, and stimulus-responsive nanocarriers have been engineered to achieve sustained release and improved tumor accumulation.12–14 Meanwhile, hypoxia-activated prodrugs such as tirapazamine (TPZ) have been explored to exploit the hypoxic microenvironment created by embolization, selectively targeting tumor cells in the ischemic core.15–17 Other studies have attempted to remodel the tumor microenvironment or inhibit metastasis through anti-angiogenic agents, matrix-degrading strategies, or immunomodulatory nanoplatforms.18,19 Despite these advances, most approaches primarily focus on either enhancing intratumoral drug delivery or targeting hypoxic tumor cells, while failing to prevent the survival and dissemination of peripheral tumor cells.20,21 Moreover, many nanotherapeutic systems face challenges in spatial control, embolization compatibility, and clinical translatability.22,23 These limitations highlight the need for a strategy that can simultaneously induce metabolic starvation in the tumor core and physically restrain invasive tumor margins.

In this study, we proposed a dual-starvation strategy that integrates vascular embolization with peripheral calcification-mediated containment to simultaneously suppress tumor metabolism and prevent dissemination. To achieve this, we developed a thermosensitive PND nanogel (TPZ/FA@PND) co-encapsulating the hypoxia-activated prodrug tirapazamine (TPZ) and folic acid (FA). This nanogel exhibits sustained drug release and undergoes a sol-gel transition at physiological temperature, enabling efficient embolization following intra-arterial administration. Upon embolization, TPZ selectively targeted hypoxic tumor cells in the ischemic core, while the FA-mediated mineralization process induced marginal calcification, forming a physical barrier that restricts tumor cell invasion and spread. Through this combined metabolic interference and physical containment, the TPZ/FA@PND system established a dual-starvation therapeutic paradigm, offering a clinically translatable approach to enhance the efficacy of TACE for hepatocellular carcinoma. Methods

Materials and Reagents

Tilazamine (TPZ) and folic acid (FA) were purchased from Sigma (Missouri, America). Sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and potassium persulfate (KPS) were supplied by China National Pharmaceutical Group Corporation (Beijing, China). Dopamine Methacrylamide (DMA) was obtained from Aikon Biopharmaceuticals (Jiangsu, China). All reagents were of analytical grade and used as received. Solutions were prepared using ultrapure water (resistivity = 18.2 MΩ·cm).

Adult male and female New Zealand White rabbits (approximately 3.0 kg body weight) were sourced from the Laboratory Animal Research Center. Prior to experimentation, all animals were acclimatized for at least one week in environmentally controlled rooms. All experimental protocols complied with China National Guidelines for the Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Hubei Province, China). The IACUC Number is HUST-IACUC-2025-0010.

Preparation of PND

PND nanogels were synthesized by precipitation polymerization as reported before.14 In brief, N-isopropylacrylamide (0.935 g, 8.100 mmol) and SDS (0.020 g) were dissolved in deionized water and was purged with N2 under stirring for 30 min. After the temperature raised to 70°C, KPS (0.1 g) dissolved in deionized water (5 mL) was injected to initiate polymerization. Following the initial polymerization period, DMA (0.199 g, 0.9 mmol) dissolved in deionized water was introduced. The reaction proceeded under N2 at 70°C for 6 h, yielding a light-yellow PND nanogel dispersion. The product was purified by dialysis (MWCO: 14 kDa) against deionized water for 7 days with daily solvent replacement, followed by lyophilization to obtain light-yellow PND nanogel powder.

Preparation of TPZ/FA@PND

Various quantities of the lyophilized PND powder were accurately weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to obtain PND solutions with concentrations of 55, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100 mg/mL, respectively. Subsequently, FA (0.75 mg) and TPZ (1 mg) powders were introduced into the solutions, followed by continuous stirring at 20 °C for 2 h in a water bath to facilitate the formation of FA/TPZ-loaded PND complexes (TPZ/FA@PND).

Characterization of TPZ/FA@PND

The microstructures and morphologies of PND and TPZ/FA@PND nanogels were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Surface chemical composition, elemental states, and contents were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Molecular structures were determined using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (AV400, 400 MHz, Bruker) with CDCl3 as the deuterated solvent. Hydrodynamic diameters and zeta potentials were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments) using a 4 mW He-Ne laser (λ = 633 nm) at a 90° scattering angle.

Temperature-Sensitive Sol–Gel Phase Transition Behavior

The gelation temperature was defined as the point where samples formed solid gels. Dynamic viscoelastic properties were characterized using a stress-controlled rheometer (Kinexus ultra+, Malvern Instruments) with a 50 mm parallel plate (gap: 0.5 mm). Temperature sweeps (20–50°C) were performed at 0.1 Pa stress, 1.0 Hz frequency, and 2.0°C/min heating rate. PND and TPZ/FA@PND nanogel dispersions (40, 60, 80, and 100 mg/mL) were equilibrated at 4°C for complete swelling. Phase transition temperatures of injectable hydrogels were determined via the vial inversion method: 1 mL samples in 5 mL vials were subjected to temperature ramping (0.5°C/min) in a programmable water bath, with gelation confirmed by vial inversion at 1°C intervals.

In vitro Experiments

Cell Culture

The human hepatoma cell lines Huh-7, LM3 and a human normal liver cell line (LO2) were obtained from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Both tumor cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Beijing, China) while Lo2 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco). Cells were maintained at 37°C in humidified incubators with 5% CO2 under either normoxic (20% O2) or hypoxic (1% O2) conditions.

Cell Treatment

Logarithmic-phase Huh-7 or LM3 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. After 24 h incubation, cells were divided into 6 groups (24 h exposure): (1) PBS control; (2) TPZ alone (2 μg/mL); (3) TPZ/FA@PND (2 μg/mL); (4) Hypoxia alone (1% O2); (5) Hypoxia + TPZ (2 μg/mL); (6) Hypoxia + TPZ/FA@PND (2 μg/mL). Hypoxic conditions (1% O2) were maintained using an anaerobic incubator (Wuxi Puhe Biotechnology, China) throughout treatments.

CCK-8 Assay

Tumor cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were suspended in 200 μL complete DMEM (10% FBS) and seeded in 96-well plates. After 24h adhesion, 200 μL fresh medium was added to achieve 200 μL/well. Cells were treated per experimental design for 1–4 days. At the timepoint of 0h, 24h, 48h and 72h, 20 μL CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan) was added to 200 μL culture medium per manufacturer’s protocol. Following 2h incubation at 37°C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Transwell Assay

Both migration and invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers (8-μm pores; BD Biosciences, St. Louis, MO). For invasion assays only, chambers were pre-coated with 40 μL Matrigel (Corning, NY) diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium and polymerized for 4h at 37°C. Treated cells were trypsinized, resuspended in serum-free medium (5 × 104 cells/chamber), and seeded in the upper chamber. Complete medium containing 20% FBS (600 μL) was added to the lower chamber as chemoattractant. After 24h incubation (37°C, 5% CO2), cells that traversed the membrane were: (1) Fixed with 4% PFA (15 min); (2) Stained with 0.1% crystal violet (20 min); (3) Quantified by counting five random fields per membrane under brightfield microscopy (200× magnification).

Detection of Intracellular Free Ca2+ Concentration

The detection of intracellular free calcium ion concentration in Huh-7 cells was performed using the Fluo-4 calcium ion detection kit (Abbkine, China). Cells from different treatment groups were washed twice with PBS, and 1mL of Stain Solution was added to the cells. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 minutes. After incubation, the Stain Solution was discarded and the cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS. The fluorescence intensity was detected using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader under excitation wavelength of 494 nm and emission wavelength of 516 nm, which reflects the relative concentration of Ca2+ in the cells.

Cell Membrane Calcium Detection

Sterile glass slides were placed in a 24-well plate and pre-coated with 200 μL of high-glucose DMEM, incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Logarithmic-phase cells were trypsinized, resuspended at 5 × 104 cells/mL, and 500 μL aliquots were seeded per well. Following 24h incubation (37°C, 5% CO2) achieving 60–70% confluency, cells received designated treatments. After 24h-treatment, medium was aspirated and cells were: (1) Rinsed with ice-cold PBS; (2) Fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at RT; (3) Washed 3× with PBS (5 min/wash). Sequential staining was performed using: DiD (5 μM, 20 min); Calcein-AM (5 μM, 20 min); DAPI (1 μg/mL, 20 min). All stains were applied in darkness at RT with triple PBS washes (3 min/wash) between steps. Slides were mounted with 10 μL antifade medium and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. DiD and Calcein-AM were sourced from GLPBIO (Montclair, CA, USA).

Hemolysis Test

Whole blood (1 mL) was collected via the orbital vein from healthy mice into anticoagulant-treated tubes. Following collection, the blood was washed and centrifuged (600 × g, 4 °C for 10 min). The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Aliquots of the red blood cell (RBC) suspension were then incubated for 4 hours with either: 100 µL distilled water (positive control), 100 µL PBS (negative control), or 100 µL of TPZ/FA@PND solutions at varying concentrations. Post-incubation, samples were centrifuged (12,000 × g, 5 min). The absorbance of the supernatant was subsequently measured at 570 nm, and the hemolysis rate for each experimental group was quantified. To assess hepatic biocompatibility, LO2 hepatocytes were treated with material A (1, 5, and 10 μg/mL) for 24 h, followed by CCK-8 viability quantification.

In vivo Experiment

In vivo Antitumor Evaluation of TPZ/FA@PND on VX2 Tumor-Bearing Rabbits

New Zealand rabbits were used to establish the VX2 tumor model using a previously described method.15,16 Briefly, following a 12-hour fast, a VX2 tumor fragment (1.0 mm3) was surgically implanted into the left hepatic lobe of each rabbit. A gelatin sponge was placed to secure the tumor and prevent dislodgement. To minimize infection risk, rabbits received intravenous cefuroxime and local wound irrigation with gentamicin. Once CT scanning confirmed tumor volumes reached approximately 1.6 × 103 mm3, 24 tumor-bearing rabbits were randomly allocated to four experimental groups (n=6 per group): Group A (NS), Group B (TPZ), Group C (PND), and Group D (TPZ/FA@PND). All surgical procedures are performed under general anesthesia. Anesthesia induction is performed by intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 35–40mg/kg. The depth of anesthesia is confirmed by the disappearance of pedal reflex, stable respiratory rate, and lack of response to hind limb toe pinch stimulation. For intervention, an incision was made in the right inguinal region to expose the common femoral artery, and a 4-F catheter sheath (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted. Under digital subtraction angiography guidance (Siemens Bicor Top., Germany), a 2.7-F microcatheter (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced into the hepatic artery for angiography to identify tumor-feeding vessels. Subsequent superselective catheterization of the target vessel allowed slow infusion of the designated therapeutic agent. Complete embolization was defined by the cessation of blood flow within the target vessel (ie, absence of contrast perfusion in the tumor and its surrounding vasculature) while maintaining patency of the main hepatic artery. All interventional procedures were performed by experienced interventional physicians with over 10 years of practice.

CT scans will be performed on tumor rabbits on days 3, 7, and 14 after embolization to evaluate changes in tumor volume and calcification (Animal euthanasia method: intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 100–200 mg/kg). The tumor volume was assessed by CT imaging, and the scanning parameters were as follows: 80 kV, 100 mA, 1 mm slice thickness, 1.1 pitch, and 200×200 mm2 field of view. The calculation method for tumor volume in mm2 field of view is as follows: V=a × b × c × π/6, where a, b, and c represent the anterior posterior diameter, left-right diameter, and up-down diameter of the tumor, respectively.

Histology Study

VX2 tumor-bearing rabbits were euthanized on day 14 post-embolization. Tumor tissue and adjacent peri-tumoral normal tissue were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, and paraffin-embedded. Sections (5-μm thick) were prepared for immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Antibodies used in this study were: vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF; 1:150 dilution), matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9; 1:50 dilution), CD8+ T-lymphocyte marker (CD8; 1:100 dilution), and CD3+ T-lymphocyte pan-marker (CD3; 1:1000 dilution). All antibodies and kits were sourced from Wuhan Sevier Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Six images per sample were acquired at 400× magnification. The percentages of VEGF-positive, CD8-positive T lymphocytes, CD3-positive T lymphocytes, and MMP-9-positive cells were calculated for each group using Image Pro Plus image analysis software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD, USA), which automatically quantified the immunopositive areas. Tumor cell apoptosis was assessed using the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Germany). Proliferation status was evaluated via Ki67 immunofluorescence staining using an anti-Ki67 antibody (1:200; Wuhan Sevier Biotechnology, China). Following nuclear counterstaining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), fluorescence signals were detected at excitation/emission wavelengths of 488/520 nm (TUNEL) and 560/590 nm (Ki67). Six randomly selected fields per tissue section (400× magnification) underwent quantitative analysis and Image Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, USA) was used for image processing. Cells were considered positive when exhibiting specific marker fluorescence, with results expressed as the mean percentage of positive cells relative to DAPI-stained nuclei. Additionally, intra- and extrahepatic metastasis counts were recorded at euthanasia. Extrahepatic metastases primarily involved the lungs, peritoneum, and porta hepatis lymph nodes. For cytokine analysis, blood on post-procedural days 14 was centrifuged at 600 × g and 4°C to obtain supernatant. Serum interleukin-2 (IL-2), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) levels were subsequently measured using commercial ELISA kits.

Biocompatibility

Blood samples were collected pre-intervention and on post-procedural days 3, 7, and 14 to monitor hepatic/renal function. Plasma alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (CRE) levels were quantified using a DXC 8000 automated biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter Diagnostics, Brea, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and were analyzed using independent samples t-tests or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test, supplemented by Fisher’s exact test where applicable. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of TPZ/FA@PND

PND thermosensitive gel was constructed by the synergistic polymerization of thermosensitive polymer poly N-isopropylacrylamide (PNIPAM) and dopamine methylacrylamide (DMA). First, the chemical structure of the PND nanogels was confirmed by 1H-NMR spectroscopy. The characteristic resonances at 1.8–2.2 ppm and 7.0–7.6 ppm were attributed to the methyl and aromatic protons of the DMA units, respectively, while the signals at 3.7–3.9 ppm corresponded to the isopropyl protons of the PNIPAM segments, verifying the successful construction of the PND polymer (Figure 2A). The hydrodynamic diameters of PND and TPZ/FA@PND nanogels were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS). Both nanogels exhibited narrow size distributions with mean diameters of 201 ± 10.4 nm and 251 ± 9.5 nm (Figure 2B), respectively, indicating good monodispersity. Zeta potential analysis revealed surface charges of −6.5 ± 0.9 mV for PND and −21.3 ± 0.8 mV for TPZ/FA@PND (Figure 2C). The more negative surface potential of TPZ/FA@PND was attributed to the dissociation of free carboxyl groups in folic acid (FA), generating negatively charged -COO− groups upon FA incorporation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images further confirmed that both PND and TPZ/FA@PND nanogels possessed uniform spherical morphologies (Figure 2D). UV-vis spectroscopy of TPZ/FA@PND displayed three characteristic absorption peaks at 267 nm, 281 nm, and 461 nm, corresponding to FA, PND, and TPZ, respectively, demonstrating the successful encapsulation of both therapeutic agents (Figure 2E). The thermosensitive behavior of the PND nanogels was evaluated by UV-vis transmittance measurements. The nanogels exhibited high optical transparency (∼89.8%) at 550 nm under room temperature, which decreased progressively with increasing temperature, indicating a typical lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behavior. The LCST of PND in deionized water was determined to be 33.0 °C (Figure 2F). Moreover, the sol-gel phase transition temperature of both PND and TPZ/FA@PND slightly decreased as the nanogel concentration increased from 55 to 100 mg mL−1 (Figure 2G). Considering injectability and therapeutic applicability in transarterial embolization therapy, a concentration of 60 mg mL−1 was selected for subsequent studies. The temperature-triggered sol-gel transition of PND and TPZ/FA@PND was further characterized by rheological analysis. For PND, the loss modulus (G″) exceeded the storage modulus (G′) at temperatures below 33.1 °C, whereas G″ surpassed G′ at higher temperatures, indicating a clear sol-gel transition at this temperature (Figure 2H). A similar transition behavior was observed for TPZ/FA@PND, with a gelation temperature of 34.1 °C (Figure 2I), suggesting that the incorporation of FA and TPZ did not compromise the thermosensitive properties of the PND matrix. Drug release studies revealed that approximately 14.8% of FA and 10.4% of TPZ were released within the first 24 h (Figure 2J and K). This initial release was followed by a sustained and gradual release over the subsequent 10 days, demonstrating the prolonged drug-release capability of the TPZ/FA@PND nanogels. This sustained-release behavior is primarily attributed to the sol-gel phase transition, which effectively “locks” the drugs within the gel network after gelation, making the system particularly suitable for intratumoral drug retention following embolization. TPZ/FA@PND Inhibited Tumor Growth

Firstly, the proliferation of Huh-7 and LM3 cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions was evaluated following treatment with TPZ or TPZ/FA@PND, as shown in Figure 3A and B. The results demonstrated that TPZ/FA@PND exerted a more pronounced inhibitory effect on tumor cell growth compared with TPZ alone. Consistently, Transwell migration assays of Huh-7 and LM3 cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Figure 3C and D) revealed that TPZ/FA@PND achieved significantly greater suppression of cancer cell migration than TPZ. Furthermore, invasion assays (Figure 3E and F) showed a similar trend, with TPZ/FA@PND exhibiting the most potent inhibitory effect on tumor cell invasion under both oxygen conditions. Overall, these results indicate that TPZ/FA@PND provides superior antitumor activity compared with TPZ alone, particularly in hypoxic environments. Calcium Ion Generation and Extracellular Calcium Deposition

We postulated that TPZ-induced cytotoxicity under hypoxic conditions could trigger endoplasmic reticulum-mediated Ca2⁺ release. To test this hypothesis, intracellular free Ca2⁺ levels were quantified in Huh-7 cells treated with TPZ or TPZ/FA@PND under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Compared with the control group, both treatments induced a significant elevation of cytosolic Ca2⁺, with the most pronounced increase observed under hypoxia (Figure S1 and Supplementary Methods). Consistent with these findings, fluorescence imaging using DiD/Calcein-AM/DAPI staining revealed extensive calcium deposition, evidenced by intensified green Calcein signals, predominantly in the TPZ/FA@PND normoxia, TPZ hypoxia, and TPZ/FA@PND hypoxia groups (Figure 3G and H). Collectively, these results indicate that TPZ-based treatments, particularly under hypoxic conditions, effectively disrupt intracellular calcium homeostasis and promote subsequent mineralization.

TACE Efficacy on VX2-Tumor-Bearing Rabbit

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of TPZ/FA@PND, VX2 tumor-bearing rabbits were randomly assigned to four TACE treatment groups (n = 6 per group): physiological saline (Control), TPZ, PND, and TPZ/FA@PND (Figure 4A). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) confirmed that complete tumor embolization was achieved exclusively in the PND and TPZ/FA@PND groups, as evidenced by the disappearance of tumor blood flow signals after perfusion, confirming the excellent embolic performance of the PND gel (Figure 4B). After 12 days of treatment, TPZ/FA@PND induced a markedly greater reduction in tumor volume compared with PND embolization (Figure 4C), with representative images of tumor-bearing liver tissues shown in Figure 4D. These results indicate that the superior tumor growth inhibition achieved by TPZ/FA@PND likely arises from the synergistic effects of embolization-enhanced activation of the hypoxia-activated prodrug TPZ and FA-mediated calcium deposition-induced cytotoxicity. Subsequent CT imaging revealed progressive intratumoral calcification in TPZ/FA@PND-treated tumors, with initial calcific deposits appearing at the tumor margins as early as postoperative day 3 (Figure 4E). By day 12, the tumors were nearly completely replaced by dense calcified tissue. These observations were further corroborated by silver nitrate staining, which showed extensive calcific deposits (black staining) in TPZ/FA@PND-treated tumors, whereas no mineralization was detected in tumors from the Control group (Figure 4F). Quantitative histopathological assessment of H&E-stained sections showed that the TPZ/FA@PND group exhibited a TNR of 91.39% ± 1.60%, substantially surpassing those of the PND group (56.20% ± 3.41%), the TPZ group (12.36% ± 1.48%), and the NS control group (21.86% ± 4.33%) (Figure S2). Moreover, quantitative analysis of intrahepatic and extrahepatic metastases (Figure 4G and H) demonstrated that the TPZ/FA@PND group exhibited a significantly lower number of metastatic lesions compared with all other groups. Collectively, these findings indicate that TPZ/FA@PND not only effectively suppresses primary tumor growth but also markedly inhibits metastatic progression of hepatocellular carcinoma. The Evaluation of Post-Operative

Correspondingly, tumor cell apoptosis was significantly increased, as shown by terminal-deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining (Figure 5A and B), whereas Ki67 expression was reduced in tumor tissues after administration of the different treatments. To evaluate the impact of perfusion and embolization on the tumor microenvironment, we assessed key factors associated with tumor metastasis and angiogenesis. The results showed that the expression levels of MMP-9 and VEGF in the TPZ/FA@PND group were significantly lower than those in the other three groups (Figure 5C). The corresponding quantitative results are shown in Figures 5D and S3, indicating that TPZ/FA@PND embolization intervention therapy can indeed significantly alleviate the microenvironment of tumor metastasis and vascular regeneration. CD8⁺ and CD3⁺ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are critical biomarkers for evaluating the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in HCC. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the PND group exhibited significantly elevated CD8⁺ and CD3⁺ TIL infiltration compared to controls (Figure 5E, F, and Figure S4), suggesting enhanced anti-tumor immunity. Systemic immune activation was further evaluated by measuring serum TNF-α, IL-2 and IFN-γ levels. ELISA results showed that PND treatment markedly increased these cytokines compared with other groups (Figure 5G), indicating the induction of a robust systemic antitumor immune response. Although direct validation of the calcified layer’s influence on immune cell migration awaits specialized in vitro models, the in vivo enrichment of CD8⁺ T cells (Figure 5E–F) suggests that the calcified periphery may act as a chemokine-enriched niche conducive to T cell accumulation, which contrasts with the previously reported T-cell-exclusive role of fibrotic stroma24 but may be reconciled by concurrent inflammatory remodeling induced by TPZ and embolization.25 Furthermore, while canonical ICD markers, including surface CRT exposure, ATP release, and HMGB1 secretion, were not directly measured here, extensive literature supports TPZ as an effective ICD inducer. For instance, Wang et al demonstrated that TPZ-mediated chemotherapy elicits tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes via ICD,26 which aligns fully with our observations of increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, and systemic antitumor responses. Finally, although definitive proof of tumor-antigen specificity would require MHC multimer staining, multiple converging lines of indirect evidence suggest that the infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells are indeed antigen-driven: (i) TACE-induced tumor necrosis releases antigens that prime de novo adaptive immunity; (ii) TPZ promotes the generation of tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes through ICD; and (iii) the observed suppression of distant metastases argues for systemic, antigen-specific immunity rather than nonspecific bystander activation. Collectively, these results and mechanistic inferences establish that TPZ/FA@PND embolization therapy not only suppresses local tumor growth and remodels the TME but also effectively instigates a durable, antigen-specific adaptive immune response in hepatocellular carcinoma. Biocompatibility

Hemolysis assays revealed that TPZ/FA@PND exhibited negligible hemolytic activity across a concentration range of 0–20 μg/mL (Figure 6A). To evaluate potential hepatotoxicity, LO2 hepatocytes were exposed to TPZ/FA@PND at 1, 5, and 10 μg/mL for 24 h. CCK-8 assays demonstrated no significant reduction in cell viability at any tested concentration compared with the control group (Figure 6B and C). To further assess in vivo biocompatibility, hepatic and renal functions were systematically monitored prior to embolization and on postoperative days 3, 7, and 14. Serum ALT and AST levels exhibited a transient increase, peaking on day 3, followed by a gradual return to baseline by day 14 (Figure 6D and E). Although both PND and TPZ/FA@PND groups showed temporary elevations in renal function indicators (BUN and creatinine) on postoperative day 3, likely attributable to embolization-induced stress, these parameters fully recovered to pre-embolization levels by day 14 (Figure 6F and G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the favorable biocompatibility and biosafety of PND, supporting its potential as a promising embolic agent for clinical transarterial intervention in hepatocellular carcinoma. The favorable biocompatibility of TPZ/FA@PND, as demonstrated by its effects on hepatocytes and systemic biochemical parameters, indicates low toxicity to normal cells, including immune cell lineages. Conclusion

In summary, we developed TPZ/FA@PND, a multifunctional embolic agent capable of forming a post-embolization calcified barrier to effectively restrain tumor growth and metastasis. Beyond vascular occlusion, TPZ/FA@PND simultaneously promotes intratumoral immune cell infiltration and suppresses angiogenesis, thereby integrating embolization therapy, physical tumor containment, and immune microenvironment remodeling into a single platform. This combinatorial mechanism directly addresses major clinical limitations of TACE, including therapeutic resistance and tumor recurrence, with the potential to substantially improve patient prognosis. Importantly, this work advances locoregional therapy for HCC from passive vessel blockade toward an active, biology-driven strategy that reshapes the tumor microenvironment. By unifying hypoxia-activated chemotherapy, calcium-mediated structural confinement, and immune modulation within a clinically adaptable system, TPZ/FA@PND demonstrates strong translational promise. Its compatibility with existing TACE procedures further supports seamless integration into current clinical workflows, positioning TPZ/FA@PND as a compelling candidate for future translational and clinical studies in advanced HCC. However, how TPZ/FA@PND triggers calcification is not fully resolved. Our finding that TPZ raises intracellular Ca2⁺ (Figure S1) suggests that endoplasmic reticulum stress and consequent Ca2⁺ release may prime the local milieu for mineralization. FA, a calcium chelator, could facilitate the nucleation of calcium-phosphate precipitates. Two mechanisms appear possible: passive mineralization from elevated local ion concentrations, or active transdifferentiation of tumour-associated cells toward an osteogenic lineage. Transcriptomic data on markers such as Runx2, ALP and Bglap will be essential to distinguish between these possibilities.
