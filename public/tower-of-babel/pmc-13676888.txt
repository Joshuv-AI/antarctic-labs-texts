Arthroscopic Circumferential Labral Repair and Biceps Tenodesis in Elite Throwing Athletes: Technical Considerations
Authors: Michael Nocek, Brendon C Mitchell, Lorenz Fritsch, Alan D Villegas Meza, Caroline B Herrmann, Peter J Millett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSLAP lesions are a common cause of shoulder pain in overhead athletes and are often seen together with circumferential labral tearing, partial‐thickness rotator cuff tearing, and biceps pathology. Isolated repair of the superior labrum has produced inconsistent outcomes, with low return‐to‐play rates in professional throwers. In patients with these complex cases, instability and anterior shoulder pain often coexist, and isolated labral repair or tenodesis may not be adequate treatment. Conversely, tenodesis without superior labral repair may increase glenohumeral translation. The senior author believes long head of the biceps tendon release reduces traction on the SLAP repair and may support healing. We describe an arthroscopic technique for circumferential labral repair, with knotless all‐suture anchors performed in the beach‐chair position, combined with open subpectoral biceps tenodesis and adjunct subacromial decompression. This technique restores stability, removes the biceps as a pain generator, eliminates traction on the repaired labrum, and preserves motion.

Shoulder injuries in overhead athletes commonly result from repetitive, high‐load stress on the glenohumeral joint. 1 , 2 SLAP tears are frequent in this population and can compromise the biceps‐labral complex, presenting with pain, mechanical symptoms, or performance decline. 3 Concomitant intra‐articular pathology is also common, including posterior capsular laxity and posterior‐based partial‐thickness articular‐sided rotator cuff tears 1 , 2 , 4 (Video 1).

Further, baseball players are more likely to experience subluxations that cause labral damage compared with nonoverhead athletes. 5 This is attributed to repetitive microtrauma during the late cocking phase. 6 , 7

Return to play (RTP) after arthroscopic SLAP repair remains variable, particularly in pitchers, with lower rates of return to preinjury level than position players. 8 , 9 , 10 Nonoperative management is first line, with surgery reserved for persistent symptoms after comprehensive conservative treatment. 11

Here, we describe the senior author's (P.J.M.) technique for arthroscopic circumferential labral repair (including the superior labrum) with knotless all‐suture anchors using supraspinatus‐sparing portals, combined with cuff debridement, subacromial decompression, and open subpectoral biceps tenodesis. SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Anesthesia, Positioning, and Examination Under Anesthesia

Under general anesthesia with an additional interscalene block, the patient is positioned in the beach‐chair position. The shoulder is then prepped and draped in sterile fashion. Examination under anesthesia confirmed grade 2 anterior/posterior translation.

Portal Establishment and Diagnostic Arthroscopy

Standard arthroscopic portals were established with an additional Neviaser portal (Figure 1), with portal locations and roles summarized in Table 1. A 30° arthroscope is introduced through the posterior portal, and a systematic diagnostic arthroscopy is performed. Pathology includes an unstable type II SLAP lesion with detachment on probing, circumferential labral tearing with capsular laxity from 7 to 3 o'clock, posterosuperior articular‐sided cuff fraying consistent with internal impingement, and hypertrophic subacromial bursitis (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, 6). Debridement and Biceps Release

Debridement is performed with a shaver and electrocautery to prepare the glenoid, capsulolabral tissue, and articular‐sided cuff fraying. The long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) is released from the superior labrum in preparation for subpectoral tenodesis.

Posterior Labral Repair (7‐11 o’Clock)

With the arthroscope in the anterosuperior portal, the posterior glenoid rim is prepared to create a bleeding surface while preserving native labrum. Knotless all‐suture anchors (FiberTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed sequentially, in a posterior‐to‐superior direction with approximately 5 mm between the anchors, from 7 to 11 o'clock via an accessory posterolateral portal. A perpendicular trajectory is used to avoid cartilage violation (Figure 7). A total of 4 anchors is used. Sutures are passed through the capsulolabral complex with a curved lasso and secured with knotless fixation to restore the posterior bumper (Figure 8). Care is taken to avoid capsular overconstraint and preserve external rotation.

Arthroscopic view from the anterosuperior portal showing posterior labral repair. Knotless all‐suture anchors (FiberTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed along the posterior glenoid rim from 7 to 11 o'clock, with sutures passed through the capsulolabral complex to restore the posterior bumper effect while preserving motion. Each anchor is placed perpendicularly to the glenoid rim to maximize fixation strength and avoid damage to the articular cartilage.

Superior Labral Repair (12 o’Clock, SLAP Lesion)

An accessory anterolateral portal is established in the rotator interval under spinal‐needle guidance, and a 5 mm cannula (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is inserted with care to avoid violation of the supraspinatus. After light preparation of the superior rim, a knotless all‐suture anchor (FiberTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed at 12 o'clock. A percutaneous spinal needle is then introduced superiorly through the Neviaser portal region to pass a polydioxanone suture shuttle suture (size 1) beneath the superior labrum. Next, the repair suture is shuttled and captured within the knotless mechanism to re‐establish the biceps‐labral attachment (Figure 9). Careful tensioning is performed to maintain an anatomic reduction and avoid overtightening or medialization.

Arthroscopic view of the superior labrum repair. A knotless all‐suture anchor (FiberTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed at the 12 o'clock position from an anterolateral portal. A spinal needle is introduced from a superior approach through the region of a Neviaser portal. A switching stick is used as a retractor to elevate the superior capsule and rotator cuff. A PDS suture is shuttled through the spinal needle, and the repair suture is shuttled under the superior labrum with the PDS to reattach the superior labrum to the glenoid. (PDS, polydioxanone suture.)

Anterior Labral Repair (1‐3 o’Clock)

With the arthroscope in the posterior viewing portal, the anterior glenoid rim is prepared to create a bleeding surface, and 3 knotless all‐suture anchors (FiberTak 1.6 mm, Arthrex, Naples, FL) are placed sequentially from 1 to 3 o'clock through the anteroinferior working portal with a distance of 5 mm between the anchors (Figure 10). Sutures are passed with a left‐curved 25° lasso (Arthrex, Naples, FL), retrieved through the anterosuperior portal, and secured with knotless fixation. Fixation is performed with the arm in approximately 10° of external rotation to avoid anterior overconstraint and preserve throwing mechanics.

Arthroscopic view from the posterior portal showing anterior labral repair from 12 to 3 o'clock using knotless all‐suture anchors (FiberTak, Arthrex, Naples, FL). Repair sutures are passed through the anterior capsulolabral tissue and secured with the arm held in slight external rotation to restore anterior stability while preserving the throwing arc.

In total, 8 knotless anchors are placed circumferentially from 7 to 3 o'clock to restore a continuous anatomic bumper while maintaining physiologic capsular tension. After completion of the labral repair, the arthroscope is advanced to the subacromial space. An additional standard lateral working portal is established. Marked bursitis is addressed with thorough bursectomy using a shaver and hemostasis with electrocautery. The bursal surface of the rotator cuff is then inspected and confirmed intact, completing the arthroscopic portion of the case.

Subpectoral Biceps Tenodesis

Through a small axillary fold incision, an open subpectoral biceps tenodesis is performed. The previously released LHBT is delivered, and the bicipital groove is cleared of soft tissue. A unicortical all‐suture anchor (FiberTak 1.9 mm, Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed, and Krakow sutures are passed through the LHBT before securing it to the bone in an onlay configuration. This removes the pathologic biceps‐labral complex as a pain generator and relieves traction on the repaired superior labrum.

Closure and Postoperative Protocol

Portals are closed with absorbable sutures, and the axillary incision is closed in a subcuticular fashion with Monocryl (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ). Sterile dressings are applied, and the patient is placed in a sling.

Rehabilitation Protocol

During the first 3 weeks, use of a sling is mandatory. Only passive range of motion is permitted during this phase, with external rotation limited to 30°.

From weeks 3 to 4, the sling is discontinued. Active range of motion can begin and should be progressed as tolerated by the patient. During the first 6 weeks, resisted elbow flexion should be avoided. After 6 weeks, strengthening exercises may be initiated.

At approximately 4 months postsurgery, an interval throwing program may begin under the supervision of a physical therapist. RTP is based on the achievement of specific milestones.

DISCUSSION

Management of SLAP lesions in overhead athletes remains debated. These injuries frequently coexist with labral instability, biceps pathology, and partial‐thickness articular‐sided cuff tears, creating a multifactorial pain generator. 12 Partial‐thickness rotator cuff tears occur in 92% of throwing athletes. 13 Anterior or posterior labral lesions are present in 62% of asymptomatic baseball pitchers. 14

Although nonoperative treatment is first‐line, 11 high‐level throwers often require surgery when symptoms persist or performance declines. Outcomes after isolated SLAP repair remain variable, 8 , 9 particularly in pitchers, 8 , 10 supporting a comprehensive, pathology‐driven approach that addresses associated labral, biceps, and posterior‐superior cuff pathology.

Key Technical Elements

Circumferential Labral Repair

Attention should be paid especially to the extent of labral tears. In circumferential tears, repairing only the superior labrum may leave residual instability; 1 , 13 , 15 circumferential repair reconstitutes the labral bumper and helps constrain glenohumeral translation. We prefer knotless all‐suture anchors for their low‐profile footprint, which minimizes rotator cuff or chondral irritation, and their small drill diameter, which preserves glenoid bone stock. 16 , 17 We also avoid transrotator cuff portals whenever possible, particularly in elite throwers.

Biceps Tenodesis

Isolated SLAP repair in overhead athletes yields inconsistent outcomes, with pitchers particularly at risk of persistent pain and poor RTP. 8 , 18 , 19 The LHBT is now recognized as a frequent pain generator in this setting. 20 , 21 Subpectoral tenodesis removes the diseased intra‐articular portion of the tendon and eliminates peel‐back forces on the superior labrum, thereby addressing both intra‐ and extra‐articular sources of pain. 22 , 23 Where type II SLAP detachment coexists with biceps tendinopathy, labral repair plus subpectoral tenodesis restores stability, treats the biceps as a pain generator, and reduces traction on the repaired labrum.

Overall, both SLAP repair and biceps tenodesis show comparable return‐to‐sport rates, exceeding 90% among overhead athletes, with return rates of up to 80% reported in pitchers. Functional outcome scores are similarly favorable for both procedures, with American shoulder and elbow surgeons scores ranging from 85.5 to 94.5 after SLAP repair and from 91.7 to 96.8 after biceps tenodesis.

However, notable differences emerge in complication and failure profiles, particularly in long‐term follow‐up. Revision rates for SLAP repair have been reported as high as 40%, whereas rates after biceps tenodesis are markedly lower, approaching 0% in some series. 23 , 24 Additionally, biceps tenodesis is associated with minimal complication rates, whereas SLAP repair has shown complication rates of up to 21.8%. 24

Patient selection plays an important role in surgical decision‐making. Younger patients, particularly overhead athletes under the age of 30, may be considered better candidates for SLAP repair. 25 In contrast, biceps tenodesis is generally favored in patients older than 30 years. 26 It is important to note that, particularly among pitchers, both procedures carry a substantial risk—at least 20%—of failure to return to sport. 24

Motion Preservation in Pitchers

A central concern in surgical management of overhead athletes is balancing stability with motion. Even subtle losses of external rotation have been shown to negatively affect velocity, accuracy, and durability in pitchers. 27 , 28 Overtightening during anterior labral repair can shift the capsular balance and compromise pitching performance, 29 and postoperative motion loss is a key predictor of failed RTP after shoulder stabilization in throwers. 30 Accordingly, anterior fixation was performed with the arm in external rotation, and an inferior‐to‐superior capsular shift was avoided to preserve pitcher‐critical motion.

In conclusion management of SLAP tears in overhead athletes remains challenging, with variable outcomes after isolated repair. This technique of circumferential labral repair with subpectoral biceps tenodesis restores stability, addresses the biceps as a pain generator, and preserves motion critical for pitchers. It also avoids supraspinatus portal placement and reduces traction on the repaired superior labrum, offering a reproducible option for elite throwers who fail nonoperative care.
