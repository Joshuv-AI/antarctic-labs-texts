Wild Stacky Curves and Rings of Mod p p Modular Forms
Andrew Kobin, David Zureick-Brown

Abstract
We extend work of Voight and the second author to compute the log canonical ring of a wild stacky curve over a field of characteristic p > 0 p>0 , which allows us to compute rings of mod p p modular forms of level Γ 0 ​ ( N ) \Gamma_{0}(N) . Our approach also reveals that in characteristics 2 2 and 3 3 , there are infinitely many levels N N for which there are weight 2 2 modular forms of level Γ 0 ​ ( N ) \Gamma_{0}(N) that do not lift to characteristic 0 0 .



1
Introduction

The
canonical ring
of an algebraic curve
X
, defined as

R(X)=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}H^{0}(X,\Omega_{X}^{\otimes k})

where
\Omega_{X}
is the canonical line bundle on
X
, is a useful and well-studied algebraic invariant of
X
. Its graded pieces encode information about embeddings of
X
into projective space; for example, when
X
is hyperbolic (i.e. its genus
g=g(X)
is at least
2
) and not hyperelliptic,
\Omega_{X}
determines an embedding
X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{g-1}
whose image is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Proj}R(X)
. In general,
R(X)
captures essential geometric features of
X
and its projective models, deformations, etc.

In
[
VZB
]
, Voight and the second author extend the theory of
R(X)
to any tame log stacky curve
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
and provide a presentation of the corresponding
log canonical ring

R(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
in terms of explicit generators and relations. Our first main result extends their work to wild stacky curves.

For a (possibly wild) log stacky curve
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
, let
X
be the coarse space of
\mathcal{X}
, with coarse moduli map
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to X
, and set
g=g(X)
. Label the finitely many stacky points of
\mathcal{X}
by
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
and let
c_{i}
denote the coefficient of
\pi(P_{i})
in the
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
\pi_{*}K_{\mathcal{X}}
and set
c=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lfloor c_{i}\rfloor
. Put
\delta=\deg(\Delta)
. We call the tuple
(g;c_{1},\ldots,c_{r};\delta)
the
refined signature
of
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
5.11
)
.

For a (possibly wild) separably rooted log stacky curve
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
with refined signature
(g;c_{1},\ldots,c_{r};\delta)
, the log canonical ring
R(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
admits a presentation with generators in degrees
\leq 3e
and relations in degrees
\leq 6e
, where
e
is the largest denominator of
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}
when they are written in lowest terms. Moreover, when
g+c+\delta\geq 2
, there is a presentation with generators in degrees
\leq\max(3,e)
and relations in degrees
\leq 2\max(3,e)
.

This generalizes
[
VZB
, Thm. 1.4.1]
and extends
[
CFO
, Thm. 1.4]
to higher genus curves in the case of a log canonical divisor. A key principle in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is that “wild ramification forces generators into lower degrees”. This will be made precise in Section
5
and illustrated in the sequence of examples in §
5.2
.

1.1
Rustom’s conjecture in characteristic
p

One of the main applications of
[
VZB
, Thm. 1.4.1]
is to prove a conjecture of Rustom
[
Rus
]
concerning generators and relations in graded rings of modular forms, namely that for any
N\geq 1
, the graded ring

M_{\bullet}\left(N;\mathbb{Z}\left[\tfrac{1}{6N}\right]\right)=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{k}\left(N;\mathbb{Z}\left[\tfrac{1}{6N}\right]\right)

of modular forms of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{6N}\right]
is generated in weights
\leq 6
with relations in weights
\leq 12
. It is crucial that one inverts
6N
rather than just
N
, since the conjecture is actually false in the latter case (see Example
5.3
).

The connection between Rustom’s conjecture and log canonical rings of stacky curves is realized by the Kodaira–Spencer isomorphism
[
VZB
, Lem. 6.2.3]

M_{k}(N;\mathbb{C})\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}H^{0}(\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}},\Omega_{\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}}(\Delta)^{\otimes k/2})

(1)

between the space of weight
k
, level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
classical modular forms and an appropriate space of sections of the log canonical bundle on the stacky curve
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}
, the rigidification of the moduli stack of complex elliptic curves with level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-structure, equipped with
\Delta
, the divisor of cusps. Following Katz
[
Kat
]
, one can use the right side of (
1
) as a
definition
of modular forms over an arbitrary ring
R
, giving a geometric interpretation of the graded ring
M_{\bullet}\left(N;\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{6N}\right]\right)
in Rustom’s conjecture (see Section
2.2
).

From this geometric perspective, in
[
VZB
]
it was necessary to invert
6
because in characteristics
2
and
3
, the stacky curve
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)
is often
wildly ramified
and therefore the main theorem in [
loc. cit.
] does not apply. Indeed, Rustom’s conjecture as originally stated is false for
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{N}\right]
-coefficients, but only slightly. Using Theorem
1.1
, we prove the following refinement of the conjecture.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
5.4
)
.

For
N\geq 1
, the graded ring of modular forms
M_{\bullet}\left(N;\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{N}\right]\right)
has a presentation with generators and relations in weights
\leq 12
. Moreover, for
N>1
, the generators appear in weights
\leq 6
with relations in weights
\leq 12
, as in the case of Rustom’s original conjecture.

This answers a question of the first author
[
Kob1
, Question 1]
.
A similar statement should be true for any modular curve
\mathcal{X}_{H}
with
H\leq\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
a subgroup of finite index. See Section
7
for one example.

1.2
Serre’s modularity conjecture and ethereal forms

Our methods for proving Theorem
1.1
reveal another fascinating aspect of rings of mod
p
modular forms, by way of Serre’s modularity conjectures. Serre’s conjectures concern the modularity of certain types of Galois representations in the form of a correspondence between Galois representations and modular forms. More concretely, work of Deligne and Serre
[
DeSe
]
constructs, for every normalized cuspidal eigenform
f\in S_{k}(N;\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
, a continuous, odd, irreducible Galois representation
\rho_{f}\colon G_{\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
. The strong form of
Serre’s weight conjecture

[
Ser2
]
asserts the converse: for every such
\rho\colon G_{\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
, there are a well-defined weight
k
and level
N
and a normalized cuspidal eigenform
f\in S_{k}(N;\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
whose associated
\rho_{f}
is
\rho
. The conjecture was ultimately proven by Khare–Wintenberger
[
KW1
,
KW2
]
and Kisin
[
Kis
]
, while the generalization to number fields remains an open problem.

One of the difficulties on the modular form side of the story is that mod
p
modular forms may differ from their classical counterparts. As above, let
M_{k}(N;R)
denote the space of weight
k
, level
N
modular forms with coefficients in a ring
R
, in the sense of Katz
[
Kat
]
; see §
2.2
for the rigorous definition. For any prime
p
, there is a reduction mod
p
map

M_{k}\left(N;\mathbb{Z}\left[\tfrac{1}{N}\right]\right)\longrightarrow M_{k}(N;\mathbb{F}_{p})

which may fail to be surjective. That is,
not every mod
p
modular form lifts to a classical modular form
. Classical here means “after tensoring with
\mathbb{C}
”, in which case one obtains a modular form on the upper half plane.

By Serre’s modularity conjectures, the so-called
ethereal modular forms
, i.e. those lying outside the image of the reduction map for some
p
, give rise to Galois representations
\rho\colon G_{\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
which are “non-classical” in that they do not lift to a modular representation
G_{\mathbb{Q}}\rightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\overline{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
of the same weight and level as
\rho
. Additionally, as pointed out in
[
Buz
]
and
[
Sch
]
, the appearance of Galois representations coming from ethereal modular forms has an influence on the arithmetic statistics of number fields – in particular, their images in
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
are unusually large, as discussed in
[
Buz
]
.

In this article, we give a geometric explanation for the existence of some ethereal mod
p
modular forms by way of the stack structure of moduli problems of elliptic curves with level structure, i.e. stacky modular curves. This stacky approach also allows us to compute the ring of mod
p
modular forms with level structure, extending the methods in
[
VZB
]
to characteristic
p
.

Our key tool for discovering and computing ethereal modular forms is Theorem
1.2
. Indeed, one of the key predictors of ethereal modular forms mod
p
is the presence of wild ramification in the moduli stacks
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)
. Already for the moduli stack
\mathcal{X}(1)
of elliptic curves, wild ramification occurs in characteristics
2
and
3
, leading to a more exotic stacky structure in these characteristics (Proposition
2.9
) which propagates to higher levels through the tower of modular curves (§
4.2
).

The phenomenon of ethereal modular forms for the subgroups
\Gamma_{1}(N)
was first noticed by Mestre
[
Mes
]
and later studied extensively in
[
Buz
,
Sch
]
but, to our knowledge, there does not exist a thorough treatment of ethereal forms for
\Gamma_{0}(N)
in the literature. The present work can be regarded as a first step in the direction of a more comprehensive account of ethereal modular forms and their Galois representations. See Section
8
for further discussion and suggested directions of inquiry.

1.3
Computing ethereal modular forms

Here’s a brief outline of our approach to identifying and computing ethereal forms in
M_{\bullet}(\Gamma;\mathbb{F}_{p})
. In the present article,
\Gamma
is always
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, but can be an arbitrary Fuchsian group in principle (for one example, see Section
7
). The strategy can be divided into two parts:

(I)

Compute a presentation for the log canonical ring
R(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the modular curve satisfying
M_{k}(\Gamma;\mathbb{F}_{p})\cong H^{0}(\mathcal{X},\Omega_{\mathcal{X}}(\Delta)^{k})
; for
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(N)
, this is the rigidification
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}
(see
[
VZB
, Rmk. 5.6.8 and Lem. 6.2.3]
).

(II)

Use a known basis of (non-ethereal) modular forms and linear algebra to produce
q
-expansions of ethereal generators in low weights.

In the first step, one must determine the stack structure of
\mathcal{X}
, namely the number of stacky points and their automorphism groups. For the curves
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}
, this is done in Sections
2
and
3
and is summarized by the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
2.5
, Corollaries
3.3
and
3.7
)
.

Let
N\geq 1
and define

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}(N)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\prod_{\text{odd primes }\ell\mid N}\left(1+\left(\frac{-1}{\ell}\right)\right),&\text{if }4\nmid N\\
0,&\text{if }4\mid N\end{cases}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\varepsilon_{3}(N)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\prod_{\text{primes }3\not=\ell\mid N}\left(1+\left(\frac{-3}{\ell}\right)\right),&\text{if }9\nmid N\\
0,&\text{if }9\mid N.\end{cases}

Over any algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic not dividing
N
,
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}
is a stacky curve with coarse space
X_{0}(N)
, the usual modular curve of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, whose stacky locus is characterized as follows:

(1)

If
N=1
, then either

(a)

\operatorname{char}k\not=2,3
and
\mathcal{X}_{0}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}=\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
has a stacky
\mu_{2}
-point over
j=1728
and a stacky
\mu_{3}
-point over
j=0
; or

(b)

\operatorname{char}k=2
and
\mathcal{X}_{0}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
has a single wild stacky
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\rtimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
-point over
j=0
; or

(c)

\operatorname{char}k=3
and
\mathcal{X}_{0}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
has a single wild stacky
S_{3}
-point over
j=0
.

(2)

Otherwise,
N>1
and the stacky locus is characterized by:

(a)

If
\operatorname{char}k=0
or
\operatorname{char}k>3
, then
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}
has
\varepsilon_{2}(N)
stacky
\mu_{2}
-points over
j=1728
and
\varepsilon_{3}(N)
stacky
\mu_{3}
-points over
j=0
.

(b)

If
\operatorname{char}k=2
, then
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}
has
\varepsilon_{2}(N)/2
stacky
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
-points and
\varepsilon_{3}(N)
stacky
\mu_{3}
-points over
j=0
.

(c)

If
\operatorname{char}k=3
, then
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}
has
\varepsilon_{2}(N)
stacky
\mu_{2}
-points and
\varepsilon_{3}(N)/2
stacky
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
-points over
j=0
.

To compute the log canonical ring of
(\mathcal{X},\operatorname{cusps})
also requires knowing the ramification jump(s) at each wild stacky point. This can be computed indirectly using an appropriate étale cover
Y\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, where
Y
is a representable curve, usually
X_{1}(N)
for some
N\geq 5
. With the stacky structure determined, one may then use the wild stacky Riemann–Hurwitz formula
[
Kob1
, Prop. 7.1]
to compute a canonical divisor for
\mathcal{X}
. Finally, the main results in
[
ODorn
]
and
[
VZB
]
help in computing the log canonical ring of
\mathcal{X}
.

Completing task (I) already allows us to characterize the existence of ethereal modular forms for
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)^{\operatorname{rig}}
, which is summarized by our next main theorem.

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem
4.11
)
.

Fix a prime
p
and
N\geq 1
not divisible by
p
. Then the ring
M_{\bullet}(N;\mathbb{F}_{p})
of mod
p
modular forms of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
contains an ethereal form if and only if one of the following is true:

(1)

p=2
and
N
is a product of primes
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

(2)

p=3
and
N
is a product of primes
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.

In fact, the enumeration of stacky points in Theorem
1.3
allows us to count the number of ethereal modular forms in weight
2
(see Theorem
4.13
).

Task (II) is a purely computational exercise. Given a prime
p
, a subgroup
\Gamma\subseteq\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, a weight
k
and a precision
t>0
, we would like to produce a finite set
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{p}[[q]]/(q^{t+1})
consisting of truncated
q
-expansions of a basis for the space of modular forms
M_{k}(\Gamma;\mathbb{F}_{p})
, where
\dim M_{k}(\Gamma;\mathbb{F}_{p})=d
. For certain
\Gamma
and
p
, one must account for ethereal modular forms, as in
[
Sch
]
and Theorem
1.4
. We outline the strategy for
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(N)
, where
N\geq 1
is not divisible by
p
:

(i)

Compute a basis
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\}
for the space
M_{k}\left(N;\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1}{N}\right]\right)
of characteristic
0
modular forms, e.g. using Magma or Sage, and reduce the forms mod
p
, expressing them as truncated
q
-expansions
\bar{g}_{i}=\sum_{n=0}^{t}a_{n}(g_{i})q^{n}+O(q^{t+1})\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[[q]]
.

(ii)

Use the results of task (I), namely a presentation of
M_{\bullet}(N;\mathbb{F}_{p})
coming from Theorem
1.1
, to determine a basis for
M_{k}(N;\mathbb{F}_{p})
consisting of monomials of low-weight forms; by Theorem
1.2
, these can be found in weights
\leq 6
when
N>1
.

(iii)

Some of the low-weight forms will be ethereal (the number of ethereal basis elements can be determined by dimension formulas, as in Theorem
4.13
). Use linear algebra to deduce relations among the monomials and the
\bar{g}_{i}
, culminating in a maximal linearly independent set
\{\bar{g}_{1},\ldots,\bar{g}_{r},f_{r+1},\ldots,f_{d}\}
, where
f_{r+1},\ldots,f_{d}
are ethereal monomials.

In practice, only finitely many terms of the
q
-expansions
\bar{g}_{1},\ldots,\bar{g}_{r}
are needed to discover
q
-expansions for all ethereal generators in low weights; see Examples
6.3
–
6.9
for concrete implementations of this strategy.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect some background material on modular forms and stacky curves. In §
2.1
, we briefly review the theory of complex modular forms and then in §
2.2
, we generalize to geometric modular forms over a ring, in the style of Katz
[
Kat
]
. We then describe the structure of the modular curve
\mathcal{X}(1)
in §
2.3
in order to compute the ring of modular forms in all characteristics except
2
and
3
– these will be computed in Section
4
. Finally, in §
2.5

2.1
Classical modular forms

Let
\mathfrak{h}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon\operatorname{im}(z)>0\}
be the complex upper half-plane and define the completed upper half-plane to be
\mathfrak{h}^{*}=\mathfrak{h}\cup\{\infty\}\cup\mathbb{Q}
. The modular group
\Gamma(1)=SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
acts on
\mathfrak{h}
by fractional linear transformations, under which the quotient space
Y(1)^{\operatorname{an}}:=\mathfrak{h}/\Gamma(1)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}\smallsetminus\{\infty\}
, the once-punctured Riemann sphere. Its one-point compactification
X(1)^{\operatorname{an}}=\overline{Y(1)}^{\operatorname{an}}
is a proper Riemann surface isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{1}
.

A
(weakly) modular function
of weight
k
is a holomorphic function
f\colon\mathfrak{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that for all
g=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma(1)
,

f(z)=(cz+d)^{-k}f(gz).

Let
q=e^{2\pi iz}
. If the
q
-expansion
f(q)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n}
of a modular function
f
is holomorphic at
\infty
, i.e.
a_{n}=0
for all
n<0
, then
f
is a
modular form
of the same weight.
Cusp forms
are those modular forms whose
q
-expansions have no constant term, i.e.
a_{0}=0
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\mathcal{M}_{k}
(resp.
\mathcal{S}_{k}
) denote the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space of modular forms of weight
k
(resp. cusp forms of weight
k
). Since
-I\in SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
\mathcal{M}_{k}=\{0\}
whenever
k
is odd.

For a modular form
f\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
, with
k
even, one may define a (holomorphic) differential form on
\mathfrak{h}
by

\omega_{f}:=f(z)\,dz^{k/2}\in\Omega_{\mathfrak{h}/\mathbb{C}}^{k/2}(\mathfrak{h})=H^{0}(\mathfrak{h},\Omega_{\mathfrak{h}/\mathbb{C}}^{k/2}).

Notice that for every
g\in\Gamma(1)=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
g^{*}\omega_{f}=\omega_{f}
. Hence
\omega_{f}
descends to a differential form on
Y(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
, i.e.
\omega_{f}\in H^{0}(Y(1)^{\operatorname{an}},\Omega_{Y(1)^{\operatorname{an}}/\mathbb{C}}^{k})
. This computation is also compatible with the quotient structure of
X(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
, so we can identify
\mathcal{M}_{k}
with a certain subspace of
H^{0}(X(1)^{\operatorname{an}},\Omega_{X(1)^{\operatorname{an}}/\mathbb{C}}^{k})
.

It is well-known (cf.
[
DiSh
, 3.5]
) that this subspace is described by

\mathcal{M}_{k}=\left\{\omega\in H^{0}(X(1),\Omega_{X(1)/\mathbb{C}}^{k})\left\lvert\operatorname{ord}_{i}(\omega)\geq-\frac{k}{4},\operatorname{ord}_{\rho}(\omega)\geq-\frac{k}{3},\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(\omega)\geq-\frac{k}{2}\right.\right\}.

(2)

Consider the
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor
D=\frac{1}{2}i+\frac{2}{3}\rho+\infty
on
X(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
. Then we can interpret
\mathcal{M}_{k}
as the space of global sections
H^{0}(X(1)^{\operatorname{an}},\Omega_{X(1)^{\operatorname{an}}/\mathbb{C}}^{k}(\lfloor kD\rfloor))
. Further,
[
Beh
, Thm. 1.187]
suggests we may instead view
D
as an integral divisor on some stack
\mathcal{X}
whose coarse space is
X(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
. Indeed,
[
VZB
, 5.4.7]
provides this stacky interpretation of the above formula, which will be revisited in §
2.3
.

For each (even)
k\geq 4
, let
g_{k}
denote the weight
k
Eisenstein series and let
\delta\in\mathcal{S}_{12}
be the cusp form
\delta=(60g_{4})^{3}-27(140g_{6})^{2}
. Then multiplication by
\delta
gives an isomorphism
\mathcal{M}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{k+12}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Using Riemann–Roch to compute
H^{0}(X(1)^{\operatorname{an}},\Omega_{X(1)^{\operatorname{an}}/\mathbb{C}}^{k/2}(\left\lfloor kD\right\rfloor))
, we obtain the following well-known formula for
\dim\mathcal{M}_{k}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
k\geq 0
,

\dim\mathcal{M}_{k}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{k}{12}\right\rfloor,&k\equiv 1\pmod{12}\\[15.00002pt]
\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{k}{12}\right\rfloor+1,&k\not\equiv 1\pmod{12}.\end{cases}

2.2
Geometric modular forms

The geometric approach to modular forms over an arbitrary ring
R
is realized by identifying an appropriate (relative) curve
X/\operatorname{Spec}R
and line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
X
and forming the graded section ring

M_{\bullet}(X,\mathcal{L})=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}H^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k}).

For
X=X(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
over
R=\mathbb{C}
, we already saw that
\mathcal{L}=\Omega^{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}i+\frac{2}{3}\rho+\infty\right)
is a good candidate, but notice that
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes k}
isn’t quite the same as
\Omega^{k}(\lfloor kD\rfloor)
used in (
2
). We will explain how to get around this issue in Example
2.2
below.

2.2.1
The Tate curve

For each
\tau\in\mathfrak{h}
, set
q(\tau)=e^{2\pi i\tau}
. Then
E_{\tau}=\mathbb{C}/[\tau,1]
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{\times}/\langle q(\tau)\rangle
via the analytic map
z\mapsto q(z)
. The elliptic curve
\mathbb{C}^{\times}/\langle q(\tau)\rangle
is called the
Tate curve
, written
\operatorname{Tate}_{\mathbb{C}}(q)
. Explicitly,
\operatorname{Tate}_{\mathbb{C}}(q)
is the fiber at
\mathbb{C}((q))
of an elliptic curve
\operatorname{Tate}_{\mathbb{Z}}(q)
over
\mathbb{Z}((q))
given by the affine equation

\displaystyle y^{2}+xy=x^{3}+a(q)x+b(q)

where

\displaystyle a(q)=-5\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{3}(n)q^{n}=\frac{1-e_{4}(q)}{48}

and

\displaystyle b(q)=-\frac{1}{12}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(5\sigma_{3}(n)+7\sigma_{5}(n))q^{n}=\frac{1}{12}\left(\frac{1-e_{4}(q)}{48}+\frac{1-e_{6}(q)}{72}\right).

Here,
e_{k}
is the normalized Eisenstein series of weight
k
.

Over an arbitrary ring
R
, the
Tate curve over
R
is defined to be the base change

\operatorname{Tate}_{R}(q):=\operatorname{Tate}_{\mathbb{Z}}(q)\times_{\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}}\operatorname{Spec}R

which is an elliptic curve over
R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}((q))
. Write
\omega_{can}\coloneqq\pi_{*}\Omega_{\operatorname{Tate}_{R}(q)/R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}((q))}^{1}
, where
\pi
is the canonical projection
\operatorname{Tate}_{R}(q)\to\operatorname{Spec}(R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}((q)))
.

2.2.2
Modular forms over arbitrary rings

For a ring
R
, let
p\colon E\rightarrow\operatorname{Spec}R
be a (relative) elliptic curve and denote by
\omega_{E/R}:=p_{*}\Omega_{E/R}^{1}
the
Katz canonical sheaf
of
E/R
. A
geometric modular function of weight
k
over
R
is an assignment
F
of a section
F(E/A)\in H^{0}(A,\omega_{E/A}^{k/2})
for every
R
-algebra
A
and every elliptic curve
E\rightarrow\operatorname{Spec}A
which satisfies:

(1)

F(E/A)
is constant on the isomorphism class of
E/A
.

(2)

If
\varphi\colon A\rightarrow B
is a morphism of
R
-algebras and
E
is an elliptic curve over
A
with base change
E^{\prime}=E\times_{\operatorname{Spec}A}\operatorname{Spec}B
, then
F(E^{\prime}/B)=\varphi(F(E/A))
.

The data of a geometric modular function of weight
k
over
R
is equivalent to the assignment of an element
f(E/A,\omega)\in A
to every
R
-algebra
A
, elliptic curve
E\rightarrow\operatorname{Spec}A
and nonzero element
\omega\in H^{0}(E,\Omega_{E/A}^{1})
such that:

(1)

f(E/A,\omega)
is constant on the isomorphism class of
E/A
.

(2)

For all
\alpha\in A^{\times}
,
f(E/A,\alpha\omega)=\alpha^{-k}f(E/A,\omega)
.

(3)

If
\varphi\colon A\rightarrow B
is a morphism of
R
-algebras and
E/A
is an elliptic curve with base change
E^{\prime}=E\times_{\operatorname{Spec}A}\operatorname{Spec}B
and compatible sections
\omega\in H^{0}(E,\Omega_{E/A}^{1})
and
\omega^{\prime}\in H^{0}(E^{\prime},\Omega_{E^{\prime}/B}^{1})
, then
f(E^{\prime}/B,\omega^{\prime})=\varphi(f(E/A,\omega))
.

The Tate curve allows us to define
q
-expansions geometrically: the
q
-expansion of a geometric modular function
F
over
R
is defined to be the section
F(\operatorname{Tate}_{R}(q)/R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}((q)),\omega_{can})
in
H^{0}(R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}((q)),\omega_{\operatorname{Tate}_{R}(q)/R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}((q))})\cong R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}((q))
. A
modular form of weight
k
over
R
is a geometric modular function
F
of weight
k
whose
q
-expansion
F(\operatorname{Tate}_{R}(q)/R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}((q)),\omega_{can})
lies in
R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
. Further,
F
is a
cusp form
if its
q
-expansion lies in
R\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
.

Example 2.2
.

Any geometric modular form
F
over
\mathbb{C}
determines a classical modular form
f\colon\mathfrak{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of the same weight by setting

f(\tau)=F(E_{\tau}/\mathbb{C})\quad\text{(or $f(E_{\tau}/\mathbb{C},dz)$ in the alternate notation)}

and conversely. When
R=\mathbb{C}
, the affine curve
Y(1)=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{C}[j]
parametrizes complex elliptic curves up to isomorphism. Suppose there were a universal elliptic curve
\pi:{\bf E}\rightarrow Y(1)
. Set
\omega_{{\bf E}/Y(1)}=\pi_{*}\Omega_{{\bf E}/Y(1)}^{1}
. Then we could identify modular functions of weight
k
with global sections of
\omega_{{\bf E}/Y(1)}^{\otimes k/2}
on
Y(1)
via the Riemann existence theorem, which matches
Y(1)
and
Y(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
; moreover, if
\omega_{{\bf E}/Y(1)}
extended to a line bundle on
X(1)
, then a modular form of weight
k
could be identified with a global section of
\omega_{{\bf E}/X(1)}^{\otimes k/2}
on
X(1)
. The fact that no such
{\bf E}
exists over
Y(1)
should not deter us – in fact, formula (
2
) suggests interpreting
\mathcal{M}_{k}
as global sections over a stack.

Returning to the analytic theory for a moment, let us discuss the orbifold interpretation of classical modular forms. Let
\mathcal{Y}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}=[\mathfrak{h}/\Gamma(1)]
be the
modular orbifold curve
, which is a complex orbifold curve, i.e. a
1
-dimensional stack over the category of complex manifolds. For each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, there is a line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{k}
on
\mathcal{Y}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
whose total space is the quotient stack
\mathcal{L}_{k}=[\mathfrak{h}\times\mathbb{C}/\Gamma(1)]
, where
\Gamma(1)
acts on
\mathfrak{h}\times\mathbb{C}
by

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}(\tau,z)=\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},(c\tau+d)^{k}z\right).

For all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the vector space of classical (weakly) modular functions on
\mathfrak{h}
is isomorphic to
H^{0}(\mathcal{Y}(1)^{\operatorname{an}},\mathcal{L}_{k})
.

Further, there is an orbifold compactification
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}=[\mathfrak{h}^{*}/\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})]
of
\mathcal{Y}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
which can be constructed from
\mathcal{Y}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
by adding an orbifold point of order
2
at the cusp. Alternatively, one can construct
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
as an orbifold curve directly by gluing the affine orbifold curves
\mathcal{Y}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
and
[D^{2}/\mu_{2}]
along
[\mathfrak{h}/\langle-I,T\rangle]\cong[D^{2}\smallsetminus\{0\}/\mu_{2}]
, where
D^{2}
is the complex unit disk and
\langle-I,T\rangle
is the subgroup of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
generated by

-I=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\mathcal{L}_{k}
extends to
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}
, by abuse of notation also denoted
\mathcal{L}_{k}
, whose space of holomorphic sections
H^{0}(\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{an}},\mathcal{L}_{k})
are isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}_{k}
, the space of modular forms of weight
k

[
VZB
, §6.2]
. The following is well known; cf.
[
Beh
, Exs. 3.30 and 3.55]
or
[
VZB
, Ex. 5.6.12]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

There is an isomorphism of stacks
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}\cong\mathbb{P}(4,6)
, where
\mathbb{P}(4,6)
is the weighted projective line over
\mathbb{C}
, considered as a
1
-dimensional stack with generic
\mu_{2}
stabilizer. Under this isomorphism, each
\mathcal{L}_{k}
is identified with
\mathcal{O}(k)
.

Corollary 2.4
.

The graded section ring of the line bundles
\mathcal{L}_{k}
on
\mathcal{X}(1)
is

\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}H^{0}(\mathcal{X}(1),\mathcal{L}_{k})=\mathbb{C}[x_{4},x_{6}],

where
x_{i}
is a generator in degree
i
.

Proof.

This follows from the standard fact that for any weights
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
,

H^{0}(\mathbb{P}(a,b),\mathcal{O}(k))\cong\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\\
am+bn=k\end{subarray}}\mathbb{C}x^{m}y^{n}.

∎

This confirms the dimension formula in Proposition
2.1
, but implies much more: the graded ring of complex modular forms is given by

\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{M}_{k}\cong\mathbb{C}[e_{4},e_{6}]

(3)

where
e_{k}
is the normalized Eisenstein series of weight
k
. More importantly, a similar geometric approach allows one to compute the ring of modular forms over any base.

Over an arbitrary field
k
, let
\mathcal{Y}(1)
be the moduli stack of elliptic curves over
k
. As a moduli pseudofunctor,
\mathcal{Y}(1)
sends a
k
-scheme
T
to the groupoid
\mathcal{Y}(1)(T)
of elliptic curves
E\rightarrow T
, that is, smooth, proper, pointed
T
-curves whose geometric fibres are elliptic curves. It is well-known (cf.
[
Ols
, Ch. 13]
) that
\mathcal{Y}(1)
is an algebraic stack. Explicitly,
\mathcal{Y}(1)
is a stack in the étale topology admitting a smooth surjection

\mathbb{A}_{k}^{2}\smallsetminus Z(\Delta)\longrightarrow\mathcal{Y}(1),

where
Z(\Delta)
denotes the zero locus of
\Delta
in
k[x,y]
, presenting
\mathcal{Y}(1)
as an algebraic stack. When
\operatorname{char}k\not=2,3
,
\mathcal{Y}(1)\cong[(\mathbb{A}_{k}^{2}\smallsetminus Z(\Delta))/\mathbb{G}_{m}]
where
\mathbb{G}_{m}
acts on
\mathbb{A}_{k}^{2}
by
\alpha\cdot(x,y)=(\alpha^{-4}x,\alpha^{-6}y)
. Thus the coarse moduli space
Y(1)
of
\mathcal{Y}(1)
is the affine scheme
(\mathbb{A}_{k}^{2}\smallsetminus Z(\Delta))/\mathbb{G}_{m}\cong\mathbb{A}_{k}^{1}
. A similar argument works in
\operatorname{char}k=2,3
as well.

Over
k=\mathbb{C}
, the analytification of this
Y(1)
is the Riemann surface
Y(1)^{\operatorname{an}}=\mathfrak{h}/\Gamma
from §
2.1
. Using a stacky version of the Riemann existence theorem
[
VZB
, Prop. 6.1.6]
, one can show that the analytic orbifold corresponding to the algebraic stack
\mathcal{Y}(1)
is precisely the complex orbifold
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{an}}=[\mathfrak{h}/\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})]
.

On the algebraic side, the compactification
\mathcal{X}(1)
of
\mathcal{Y}(1)
constructed by Deligne and Mumford
[
DM
]
is a proper stack
\mathcal{X}(1)
which admits
\mathcal{Y}(1)
as an open substack and extends the moduli problem of
\mathcal{Y}(1)
. Its precise geometric structure over different ground fields will be our key to computing the ring of modular forms in the next subsection and Section
4.1
.

2.3
Mod
p
modular forms

In this section, we describe modular forms over a field of characteristic
p>0
. Let
\mathcal{X}(1)
be the Deligne–Mumford compactification of the modular curve
\mathcal{Y}(1)
. Then the algebraic version of Proposition
2.3
holds in characteristic
p>3
:

Theorem 2.5
.

Over any algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
\not=2,3
, there is an isomorphism of stacks
\mathcal{X}(1)\cong\mathbb{P}(4,6)
, where
\mathbb{P}(4,6)
is the weighted projective stack with weights
4
and
6
.

This can be deduced from the fact that in characteristic
\not=2,3
,
\mathcal{X}(1)
is a
\mu_{2}
-gerbe over a tame stacky curve, hence an iterated root stack over its coarse space
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, together with the following computation of its automorphism groups.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
Sil
, App. A]
)
.

Suppose
E
is an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
where
p^{2}\mid q
for a prime
p
, with
j
-invariant
j(E)
. Then the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(E)
is characterized by

\operatorname{Aut}(E)\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},&j(E)\not=0,1728\\
\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z},&j(E)=0\text{ and }p\not=2,3\\
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z},&j(E)=1728\text{ and }p\not=2,3\\
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z},&j(E)=0\text{ and }p=3\\
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\ltimes Q_{8},&j(E)=0\text{ and }p=2\end{cases}

where
Q_{8}
is the quaternion group on which
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
acts by permuting the generators
i,j,k
. Further, these descend to the following automorphism groups over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
:

R_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}\operatorname{Aut}(E)=\operatorname{Aut}(E)/\{\pm 1\}\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},&j(E)\not=0,1728\\
\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z},&j(E)=0\text{ and }p\equiv 1\pmod{3}\\
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z},&j(E)=0\text{ and }p\equiv 2\pmod{3},\,p\not=2\\
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z},&j(E)=1728\text{ and }p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\\
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},&j(E)=1728\text{ and }p\equiv 3\pmod{4}\\
S_{3},&j(E)=0\text{ and }p=3\\
A_{4},&j(E)=0\text{ and }p=2.\end{cases}

Remark 2.7
.

The automorphism groups displayed in Proposition
2.6
are only the abstract groups
\operatorname{Aut}(E)(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p})
, not the full group schemes
\operatorname{Aut}(E)\rightarrow\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{F}_{p}
. However,
\mathcal{X}(1)
is a Deligne–Mumford stack by
[
DM
]
and hence is characterized up to isomorphism by its coarse space, namely
X(1)\cong\mathbb{P}_{j}^{1}
, and its automorphism groups which are reduced, finite group schemes whose geometric points are precisely the finite groups in Proposition
2.6
. For our purposes this is enough: to compute the log canonical ring of
\mathcal{X}(1)
via the stacky Riemann–Hurwitz formula
[
Kob1
, Prop. 7.1]
, it is enough to know the geometric automorphism groups of
\mathcal{X}
and their ramification filtrations. The explicit calculations for
p=2,3
will be carried out in Section
4.1
.

The connection between modular forms and
\mathcal{X}(1)
is expressed by the isomorphism

R(\mathcal{X}(1),\Delta)\cong\mathcal{M}(\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))

(4)

between the log canonical ring of
\mathcal{X}(1)
, with
\Delta
its divisor of cusps, and the graded ring of modular forms for
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
; see
[
VZB
, Lem. 6.2.3]
or
[
Kob2
, Ex. 7.3]
.

Corollary 2.8
.

For any algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
p\not=2,3
, the graded ring of mod
p
modular forms is isomorphic to
k[x_{4},x_{6}]
, with
x_{i}
in degree
i
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.5
and
[
VZB
, Prop. 5.5.6]
or
[
Kob1
, Prop. 4.13]
, a canonical divisor of
\mathcal{X}(1)
may be taken to be

K_{\mathcal{X}(1)}=K_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}+5P+3Q=-2\infty+5P+3Q

where
P
(resp.
Q
) is the point corresponding to elliptic curves of
j
-invariant
0
(resp.
1728
). Therefore a log canonical divisor is
K_{\mathcal{X}(1)}+\Delta=-\infty+5P+3Q
and the log canonical ring of
\mathcal{X}(1)
is

R(\mathcal{X}(1),\Delta)=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}H^{0}(\mathcal{X}(1),\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{X}(1)}(-\infty+5P+3Q)^{\otimes k/2})\cong k[x_{4},x_{6}].

Finally, apply formula (
4
).
∎

This recovers formula (
3
) again, as well as
[
Del
, Prop. 6.1]
.

2.4
The mod
p=2,3
case

In characteristics
2
and
3
, the story is more complicated; cf.
[
Del
, Prop. 6.2]
and Theorems
4.2
and
4.4
. The stack
\mathcal{X}(1)
is still Deligne–Mumford with coarse space
X(1)\cong\mathbb{P}_{j}^{1}
. However, the points
j=0
and
j=1728
collide in these characteristics, producing a
wild
stacky point at
j=0
with automorphism group
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
in characteristic
3
and
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\ltimes Q_{8}
in characteristic
2
(see Proposition
2.6
). By
[
Kob2
, Sec. 8]
, in each case
\mathcal{X}(1)
can be constructed as a fiber product of tame and wild root stacks, at least once we remove the automorphism group at the generic point.

Let
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
be the rigidification of the stack
\mathcal{X}(1)
, which removes this generic
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and leaves us with a stacky curve which is birational to
\mathbb{P}^{1}

[
VZB
, Rmk. 5.6.8]
. The map
\mathcal{X}(1)\to\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
is étale and therefore induces an isomorphism on (log) canonical rings, albeit with a change in grading. To exploit the results on stacky curves in
[
VZB
,
Kob1
,
Kob2
]
, we work with
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
instead of
\mathcal{X}(1)
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{X}(1)=\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{1,1}
be the moduli stack of elliptic curves over an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
p
. Then étale-locally,

(a)

If
p=3
,
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
is an Artin–Schreier root stack over a tame square root stack at
j=0\in X(1)\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

(b)

If
p=2
,
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
is obtained by a sequence of two Artin–Schreier root stacks over a tame cube root stack at
j=0\in X(1)\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
is a stacky curve with coarse space
X(1)\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
containing a single stacky point at
j=0=1728
, étale-locally we can realize it as a quotient
[Y/G]
where
Y\rightarrow U
is a one-point cover of an étale neighborhood
U
of
j=0
and
G
is the automorphism group at this point
[
Ols
, 11.3.1]
. When
p=3
, the ramification filtration of
G
is
G=G_{0}=S_{3}
,
G_{1}=\cdots=G_{m}=\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
,
G_{m+1}=\{0\}
for some ramification jump
m
which will be computed in Section
4.1
. Thus we can apply
[
Kob2
, Thm. 5.8]
to obtain the result.

When
p=2
, the ramification filtration starts with
G=G_{0}=A_{4}
and
G_{1}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and the same result shows (2); see [
loc. cit.
, Ex. 5.9] as well for a discussion of
(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
-extensions vs.
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
-extensions.
∎

In fact,
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
is a
global
tame-by-wild root stack over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
in characteristics
2
and
3
. This is proven in Corollary
4.20
using modular forms.

In any case, now that we know the local stacky structure of
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
in characteristics

and

, the next step towards a description of modular forms mod
2
and
3
is to compute a canonical divisor and use the Riemann–Hurwitz formula to describe the log canonical ring. This is done in Section
4
.

2.5
Stacky curves and degenerations

Following
[
VZB
, Ch. 5]
, let
\mathcal{X}
a smooth, proper, geometrically connected Deligne–Mumford stack of dimension 1 over a field
k
. If
\mathcal{X}
contains a dense open subscheme then we say that
\mathcal{X}
is a
stacky curve
and otherwise we say that
\mathcal{X}
is a
gerby curve
. A smooth proper morphism
\mathcal{X}\to S
of stacks is a
relative stacky curve
if its geometric fibers are all stacky curves. We say that a stacky or gerby curve
\mathcal{X}/k
(resp. a relative stacky or gerby curve
\mathcal{X}/S
) is
tame
if its stabilizer groups all have order coprime to the characteristic of
k
(resp. the characteristic of each closed point of
S
); otherwise
\mathcal{X}
is said to be
wild
.

Remark 2.10
.

A gerby curve
\mathcal{X}
admits a rigidification morphism
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{\operatorname{rig}}
to a stacky curve; see
[
AOV
, App. A]
.

Remark 2.11
.

A stacky curve admits a coarse space morphism; see
[
VZB
, Section 5.3 and Proposition 5.3.3]
. A tame stacky curve is a root stack over its coarse space
[
VZB
, Lemma 5.3.10]
; in other words, it is determined by its coarse space plus the location and order of its stacky points, and moreover the stabilizer groups are all cyclic and isomorphic to
\mu_{n}
as group schemes.

In contrast, wild stacky curves are not determined by their coarse space and stabilizers
[
VZB
, Remark 5.3.11]
. For example, the quotient of an Artin–Schreier curve
C
(given locally by
y^{p}-y=f(x)
) by
\mathbb{F}_{p}
is a wild stacky curve with coarse space
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and a stabilizer group of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
at infinity; as
f
varies, the genus of
C
grows arbitrarily, and these quotient stacks are generally non-isomorphic, cf.
[
Kob1
, Ex. 6.12 and Rmk. 6.19]
.

Remark 2.12
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to S
be a relative stacky curve. In
[
VZB
, Example 11.2.2(b)]
, it is incorrectly claimed that the Euler characteristic of the fibers of a family of stacky curve is not constant, due to the possibility that stacky points collide. The example given was to consider a relative (non-stacky) curve
X\to S
with two sections
s_{1},s_{2}
whose images are generically disjoint, but which intersect transversely over a fiber, and to let
\mathcal{X}
be
X
rooted at the divisors
s_{1}(S)
and
s_{2}(S)
, say to orders
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
, respectively.

Tim Santens pointed out to the authors of
[
VZB
]
that
\mathcal{X}\to S
is not smooth at the stacky points over the intersection of
s_{1}(S)
and
s_{2}(S)
.
Indeed, such a fiber has an étale cover by the curve
x^{e_{1}}=y^{e_{2}}
, which is singular.

In fact, tame degenerations of stacky curves disallow colliding stacky points. This will follow from invariance of the Euler characteristic of a family of stacky curves.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to S
be a proper flat family of stacky curves over a connected quasi-compact base
S
and let
\mathcal{L}\in\operatorname{Pic}\mathcal{X}
be a line bundle. Then the function
s\mapsto\deg(\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{X}_{s}})
is constant.

Proof.

By
[
Alp
, Theorem 10.3]
some power of
L
descends to the coarse space of
\mathcal{X}
, so the statement follows from the corresponding fact for schemes

[
Ful
, Theorem 10.2]

∎

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to S
be a relative stacky curve over a connected base
S
. Then the Euler characteristic of fibers is constant, i.e., the function
S\to\mathbb{Q}
given by
s\mapsto\chi(\mathcal{X}_{s})=\deg(\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{X}_{s}})
is constant.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
2.13
by taking

{\mathcal{L}}=\Omega^{1}_{\pi}
.
∎

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to S
be a tame relative stacky curve over a connected base
S
. Suppose that
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are stacky points on the generic fiber of
\pi
, with multiplicities
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
. Then the closures
\overline{P_{1}}
and
\overline{P_{2}}
are disjoint.

Proof.

Since
\pi
is tame, each fiber is a root stack. By the usual Euler characteristic formula for a tame stacky curve
[
VZB
, Proposition 5.5.6]

\chi(\mathcal{X}_{s})=2g(\mathcal{X}_{s})-2+\sum_{P}\frac{e_{P}-1}{e_{P}}.

If two stacky points collided, since the Euler characteristic is constant, it would follow that

\frac{e_{1}e_{2}-1}{e_{1}e_{2}}=\frac{e_{1}-1}{e_{1}}+\frac{e_{2}-1}{e_{2}}=\frac{e_{1}e_{2}-1-(e_{1}-1)(e_{2}-1)}{e_{1}e_{2}}

which is a contradiction if
e_{1},e_{2}>1
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Here is an alternative perspective on the counting argument above. Suppose
N=\ell
is prime and write
\mathbb{F}_{\ell^{2}}=\mathbb{F}_{\ell}(b)

A=\mathbb{F}_{\ell^{2}}^{\times}/\mathbb{F}_{\ell}^{\times}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{Z}/(\ell+1)\mathbb{Z}
is induced by

. A primitive

and
(\ell+1)/3\pmod{\ell+1}

For

More generally, write

denotes the functor of length

, we have:

•

\mathbb{W}_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{a}

.

•

\mathbb{W}_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{\ell})\cong\mathbb{Z}/\ell^{a}\mathbb{Z}

•

\mathbb{W}_{a}(\mathbb{F}_{\ell^{2}})^{\times}\cong\mathbb{F}_{\ell^{2}}^{\times}\times\mathbb{W}_{a-1}(\mathbb{F}_{\ell^{2}})

Putting these together, we see that the order of

and one can similarly put the cosets in each factor in bijection with the

Remark 3.6

The main issue to resolve in the proof is that, while there are (separate) bases of

Proof of Theorem
3.5

The statement is multiplicative, so it suffices to prove it for
N=\ell^{n}
for some prime
\ell

N=\ell
different than the characteristic. Fix an elliptic point of
Y_{0}(N)
represented by some

with

G\coloneqq\operatorname{Aut}_{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{3}}(E)=\langle a,b\mid ab=b^{2}a,a^{4}=b^{3}=e\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}.

The action of
G
on the Tate module of

gives a representation

\rho\colon G\hookrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z_{\ell}}})\hookrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}).

From the known matrix representations of
i
and
\omega

A_{i}^{2}=-I
acts with trace
-2
. This is enough to identify
\rho

1

\chi

; so if
\rho
were a sum of two characters,

would have trace 2, not
-2

\rho

We can deduce from the character table that the stabilizers are cyclic. Indeed, one can check by hand (using the orthogonality relations) that for any non-cyclic subgroup
H

in
[
GitHub
]
).

Next, since the image of

,
\det a=1
; since

, we also have that

. Since
G
acts via
2

The three pairs

4.1
Tangent bundles in characteristic
2
and
3

Let
\ell
be a prime and let
K
be a field of characteristic different from
\ell
.
Let
X(\ell)
be the modular curve defined in
[
PSS
, 4.1]
; over a non-algebraically closed field this differs slightly from the “usual"
X(\ell)
, and paramaterizes pairs
(E,\iota)
, where
E
is an elliptic curve and
\iota
is a symplectic isomorphism

\iota\colon\mu_{\ell}\times\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}E[\ell],

where “symplectic" means that

is isomorphic to the “usual"

(or, depending on one’s definition of

\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{\ell})
(and over a general field

\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{\ell})

[

at

at

As in Section
2.3
, let
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
be the rigidification of the stack

; then

is a stacky
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with a single stacky point of order
6
at

Our goal is to compute the canonical divisor
K_{\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}}
of
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}

X(\ell)\xrightarrow{\pi}\mathcal{X}\xrightarrow{\phi}\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}.

The map
\phi
is given by rooting (tamely) at infinity to order
\ell
, while the map
\pi
is étale. In particular,

\pi^{*}K_{\mathcal{X}}=K_{X(\ell)}

(5)

and

\phi^{*}K_{\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}}+(\ell-1)\infty=K_{\mathcal{X}}.

Since
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
has only one stacky point, this is enough information to compute
\phi^{*}K_{\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}}

-2\infty
”) via linear equivalence,

K_{\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}}=a[0:1]

K_{\mathcal{X}}=a[0:1]+(\ell-1)\infty.

Taking degrees of (
5

\deg K_{X(\ell)}=\deg\pi^{*}K_{\mathcal{X}}=\#\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{\ell})\cdot\deg K_{\mathcal{X}}.

Putting this all together, we get

2g(X(\ell))-2=\#\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{\ell})\left(\frac{a}{6}+\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right).

(6)

Solving for

a=6\left(\frac{2g(X(\ell))-2}{\#\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(\mathbf{F}_{\ell})}-\frac{\ell-1}{\ell}\right).

Taking

4.2
Level structure

Consider the stacky modular curve
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)
for
N\geq 5
in characteristic
p=2
or
3
. We expect the stack structure to change in one of two ways (see Section
3
):

•

the stacky points can collide, as with
\mathcal{X}(1)^{\operatorname{rig}}
(corresponding to points over
j=0
and
j=1728
colliding); or

•

the automorphism groups at some points (stacky or non-stacky) can grow.

Before giving a general result (Theorem
4.11
), we first analyze some examples with prime level
\ell
.

Example 4.8
.

Let’s start with
\mathcal{X}_{0}(5)
in characteristic
2
. By Corollary
3.3
, in tame characteristic, there are two stacky points above
j=0
with automorphism group
\mu_{2}
, and above
j=1728
there are two stacky points with automorphism group
\mu_{4}
; in characteristic
2
, all four of these stacky points collide in a single point. After rigidification, we are left with a single stacky point with automorphism group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
(Corollary
3.7
). In particular, the point must have a ramification jump, so the log canonical divisor will have larger coefficients than in tame characteristics and there may be ethereal modular forms.

By
[
Maz1
, II.2]
, the map on coarse spaces

X_{1}(5)\rightarrow X_{0}(5)

is ramified exactly at the unique point
P_{0}
above
j=0=1728
with inertia group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and ramification jump
m=1
, which is confirmed by the following calculation. The map factors as

X_{1}(5)\xrightarrow{\pi}\mathcal{X}_{0}(5)^{\operatorname{rig}}\xrightarrow{\phi}X_{0}(5)

where
\pi
is étale of degree
\frac{1}{2}[\Gamma_{0}(5):\Gamma_{1}(5)]=2
and
\phi
is a wild square root stack at
P_{0}
with jump
m
. Let
P
denote the stacky point above
P_{0}
, which has degree
\frac{1}{2}
. Since
\pi
is étale,

. Using
[
Kob1
, Prop. 7.1]
, we get

K_{\mathcal{X}_{0}(5)^{\operatorname{rig}}}=\phi^{*}K_{X_{0}(5)}+(m+1)(2-1)\phi^{*}P_{0}=\phi^{*}K_{X_{0}(5)}+(m+1)P.

Then

-2=\deg K_{X_{1}(5)}=\deg\pi\cdot\deg K_{\mathcal{X}_{0}(5)^{\operatorname{rig}}}=2\deg(-2\infty+(m+1)P)=-4+(m+1)

which confirms

. So a log canonical divisor on
\mathcal{X}_{0}(5)^{\operatorname{rig}}
is

(using

) and the ring of mod
2

\Gamma_{0}(5)
has two generators in degree
2
(after reindexing/unrigidifying).

Compare this to the ring of classical modular forms of level
\Gamma_{0}(5)
, which has only one generator in degree
2
. This can be seen by computing a log canonical divisor
K_{\mathcal{X}_{0}(5)^{\operatorname{rig}}}+\Delta=P_{1}+P_{2}
, where
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
both have degree
\frac{1}{2}
. In particular, the mod
2
reduction map fails to be surjective on level
\Gamma_{0}(5)
modular forms, so one of the generators is ethereal.

In characteristic
3
, interesting things happen geometrically: points above
j=0
and
1728
collide (see Section

) so that
\mathcal{X}_{0}(5)^{\operatorname{rig}}
has two (tame) stacky
\mu_{2}
-points above

, say

and
P_{2}
. However,
P_{1}+P_{2}
is still a log canonical divisor, so the ring of modular forms is unchanged from the tame case.

(1b)
\iff

4.3
Spin divisors

Classically, there are no modular forms of odd weight and level
\Gamma
whenever
-I\in\Gamma
, since the transformation law
f(z)=(-1)^{-k}f(-Iz)
implies
f\equiv 0
for odd
k
. In particular, there are no odd weight modular forms for the full modular group
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. However, when
\Gamma
does not contain
-I
, there may be odd weight forms, even in weight
1
. These correspond to sections of powers of the line bundle associated to a
spin log canonical divisor
, also called a
half log canonical divisor
or
theta characteristic
; cf.
[
VZB
, Ch. 10]
.

Such a spin divisor arises when a log canonical divisor
D=K_{\mathcal{X}(\Gamma)}+\Delta
has even degree in
\operatorname{Pic}(\mathcal{X}(\Gamma))
, where
\mathcal{X}(\Gamma)
is the moduli stack of elliptic curves with
\Gamma
level structure. Equivalently, a theta characteristic is a line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{X}(\Gamma)
such that
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes 2}\cong\mathcal{O}(D)
.

However, not every square root of
\mathcal{O}(D)
has sections which are odd weight modular forms. In the classical case, where
\mathcal{X}(\Gamma)
can be replaced with a complex orbifold
\mathcal{X}(\Gamma)^{\operatorname{an}}
, there is a unique choice of
\mathcal{L}
(the Hodge bundle) such that the spin log canonical ring
R(\mathcal{X}(\Gamma),\Delta,\mathcal{L})
coincides with the graded ring of modular forms of all weights
[
VZB
, Lem. 10.2.2]
.

In our setting, where
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}(\Gamma)
is a moduli stack of elliptic curves in characteristic
p
, the algebraic Hodge bundle
\mathcal{L}
is again a theta characteristic and the identification
R(\mathcal{X}(\Gamma),\Delta,L)\cong\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
still holds by the theorem of Kodaira–Spencer; cf.
[
Kat
, A.1.3.17]
or
[
VZB
, Rem. 10.2.3]
.

Theorem 4.16
.

modular forms is

, where

is a generator in degree

has a spin divisor

; equivalently, the log canonical bundle

5.1
Log canonical rings for wild stacky curves

Definition
.

For a log stacky curve
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
over a field
k
, with coarse moduli map
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow X
and stacky points
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
, we define the
refined signature
of
\mathcal{X}
to be the tuple
(g;c_{1},\ldots,c_{r};\delta)
where
g=g(X)
is the genus of the coarse space
X
of
\mathcal{X}
,
c_{i}
is the rational coefficient of
\pi(P_{i})
in the pushforward
\pi_{*}K_{\mathcal{X}}
of the canonical divisor, and
\delta=\deg(\Delta)
.

Remark 5.9
.

The refined signature differs from the signature defined in
[
VZB
]
as follows. For a tame log stacky curve
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
of signature
(g;e_{1},\ldots,e_{r};\delta)
, the refined signature of
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
is
\left(g;\frac{e_{1}-1}{e_{1}},\ldots,\frac{e_{r}-1}{e_{r}};\delta\right)
. More generally,
[
Kob1
, Prop. 7.1]
shows that

c_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{|G_{P_{i},j}|-1}{|G_{P_{i}}|}

where
G_{P_{i}}
is the automorphism group at
P_{i}
and
G_{P_{i},j}
are its ramification subgroups in the lower numbering.

Recall the statement of the main theorem in
[
VZB
]
:

Theorem 5.10

(
[
VZB
, Thms. 8.4.1 and 9.3.1]
)
.

For a tame, separably rooted log stacky curve
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
with naive signature
(g;e_{1},\ldots,e_{r};\delta)
, set
e=\max\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\}
. Then the log canonical ring is generated in degrees
\leq 3e
with relations in degrees
\leq 6e
. If
g+\delta\geq 2
, the bounds are
\leq\max(3,e)
and
\leq 2\max(3,e)
, respectively.

Our main theorem in this section extends this to the wild setting.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
be a (possibly wild) separably rooted log stacky curve with refined signature
(g;c_{1},\ldots,c_{r};\delta)
, coarse space
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to X
and stacky points
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
. Let
e_{i}
be the denominator of
c_{i}
(in lowest terms) and set
e=\max\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\}
and
c=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lfloor c_{i}\rfloor
. Then the log canonical ring
R(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
is generated in degrees
\leq 3e
with relations in degrees
\leq 6e
. Moreover, if
g+c+\delta\geq 2
, the bounds are
\leq\max(3,e)
and
\leq 2\max(3,e)
, respectively.

Proof.

The theorem will follow once we verify the following statements:

(i)

If
g+c+\delta\geq 2
, then
R(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
is generated in degrees
\leq\max(3,e)
with relations in degrees
\leq 2\max(3,e)
.

(ii)

c>0
if and only if some
P_{i}
is wild.

(iii)

The remaining cases have
g=0
,
\delta=0
and
c=1
, i.e. no log divisor and exactly one wild stacky point, and have
R(\mathcal{X},\Delta)
generated in degrees
\leq 3e
with relations in degrees
\leq 6e
.

(i) The key is
[
VZB
, Thm. 8.3.1]
, which needs refinement in the stacky case. Let
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
be a tame-by-wild root stack (see
[
Kob2
]
, especially §5.1) over a stacky curve
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
with refined signature
(g;c_{1},\ldots,c_{r-1};\delta)

P
on
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. Factoring through an intermediate stacky curve if necessary, we may assume
P_{r}
is totally wildly ramified. By induction, we may further assume
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is a tame stacky curve. Write

. Then for all

. Thus we may replace

with a log stacky curve with refined signature
\left(g;c_{1},\ldots,c_{r-1},\frac{d_{r}}{e_{r}};\delta+n_{r}\right)
. Likewise, replace
(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\Delta)
with a tame log stacky curve with refined signature
(g;c_{1},\ldots,c_{r-1};\delta+n_{r})
.

Since

is tame,
[

, 8.3.2]
shows that for all

,

and as in [

are nonspecial. By

, there are elements

which generate

as an

-algebra. The rest of the proof of
[
VZB
, Thm. 8.3.1]
goes through as written, with the following small modification:

replace

with

.

Finally, combining this with the second statement in
[
VZB
, Thm. 8.4.1]
for

gives the result.

(ii) It follows from
[

, Prop. 7.1]
that

if and only if

is tame, in which case the main theorem of
[

(iii) We may assume the same setup as in (i), i.e. with

5.2
Base Cases

In
[
VZB
]

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a wild stacky curve with refined signature
(0;c_{1},\ldots,c_{r};0)

\mathbb{P}^{1}

by
[
Kob1
, Ex. 7.2]
, where
m
is the ramification jump. The only valid cases where

are those for which

p\leq(p-1)(m+1)<2p

which forces
3
3

3

In the
p=2

\gcd(m,p)=1
.

m=1

\deg\lfloor kK_{\mathcal{X}}\rfloor=-2k+\left\lfloor\frac{k(p-1)(m+1)}{p}\right\rfloor=-2k+\left\lfloor\frac{2k(p-1)}{p}\right\rfloor<0

for all

For a stacky point of order

\displaystyle\deg\lfloor kK_{\mathcal{X}}\rfloor

\displaystyle=-2k+\left\lfloor\frac{k((p^{2}-1)(m_{1}+1)+(p-1)(m_{2}-m_{1}))}{p^{2}}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=-2k+\left\lfloor\frac{2k(p^{2}-1)}{p^{2}}\right\rfloor<0.

Fix an integer

Remark 6.1
.

In
[
Sch
]
, Schaeffer considers the analogous situation for the modular curves

, which are honest algebraic curves for

or

, and deduces facts about ethereal modular forms with level

. The crucial difference with level

is that the curves

are stacky for infinitely many

is not surjective in general (see Theorems

and
4.13
). Other notable differences between the two cases include:

•

Ethereal forms for

always have weight
1

[

, Thm. 1.7.1]
, while for

•

•

and
\operatorname{div}Q=Q-C
; these are unique up to scaling. These represent elements of
H^{0}({\mathcal{X}},2(K_{{\mathcal{X}}}+\Delta)
). But they are also elements of
H^{0}(P-C)
(respectively
H^{0}(Q-C)
), which are 1 dimensional, spanned by
f
and
g
. Mod
p
,
P=Q
, so the reductions
\overline{f}
and
\overline{g}
mod
p
both lie in
H^{0}(P-C)=H^{0}(Q-C)
, which are still one dimensional. So
\overline{f}
and
\overline{g}
differ by a multiple, and we get a congruence.

It is trickier, however, to detect when modular forms that become congruent mod
p
are replaced by linearly independent ethereal forms, as seen in the proof of Theorem
4.11
and in the example above.

8
Future directions

Our results on the stacky curves
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)
and mod
p
modular forms of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
suggest several natural directions for generalization.

8.1
Nonstandard level structures

As discussed in Section
7
, our approach to the stacky curves
\mathcal{X}_{0}(N)
is well-suited to studying modular curves coming from other subgroups
H\subset\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. The beta version of the LMFDB offers many candidates in the
modular curves section
, including
\mathcal{X}_{sp}^{+}(N),\mathcal{X}_{ns}^{+}(N),\mathcal{X}_{ns}(N)
and
\mathcal{X}_{S_{4}}(\ell)
for
\ell
prime.

Problem 8.1
.

Give a systematic treatment of each of these families of stacky curves in characteristic
p
. Find any ethereal modular forms and describe the resulting ethereal Galois representations.

We saw in §
6.1
that in many examples, ethereal cusp forms of level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
are congruent to an Eisenstein series (mod constant term) and hence give rise to reducible mod
p
Galois representations. Frank Calegari suggested to the authors that this might always be the case for level
\Gamma_{0}(N)
forms. However, this raises the following questions for future investigation.

Question 8.2
.

Is there an ethereal cuspidal eigenform
f\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(H;\mathbb{F}_{2})
for some
H\subset\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\operatorname{im}(\rho_{f}^{\operatorname{ss}})=\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
?

Question 8.3
.

Is there an ethereal cuspidal eigenform
f\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(H;\mathbb{F}_{3})
for some
H\subset\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3})\subseteq\operatorname{im}(\rho_{f}^{\operatorname{ss}})\subseteq\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
?

8.2
Level divisible by the characteristic

To handle mod
p
modular forms of level
N
with
p\mid N
, more care is required. One approach would be to use Igusa curves
\operatorname{Ig}(p^{n})
, as in
[
KM
, 12.6.1]
, viewed as ramified covers of
X(1)
. One thing to note is that in the case
n=1
,
[
KM
, 12.8.2]
shows that
\operatorname{Ig}(p)
is isomorphic to the moduli problem of
(p-1)
st roots of the Hasse invariant. It is possible there is a connection between
\operatorname{Ig}(p)
and our root stack description of
\mathcal{X}(1)
in §
4.4
, though we did not attempt to realize this strategy in detail.

8.3
Higher dimensional moduli problems

In higher dimensions, one encounters many canonical rings of arithmetic interest. For example, for
g>1
, let
\mathcal{A}_{g}
be the moduli stack of principally polarized abelian varieties of dimension
g
, which is a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack of dimension
\frac{g(g+1)}{2}
. Then, for an appropriate compactification
\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{g}
of
\mathcal{A}_{g}
, there is an analogue of the Kodaira–Spencer map (
1
) identifying the canonical ring of
\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{g}
with the graded ring
\mathcal{M}_{\bullet}(\operatorname{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}))
of Siegel modular forms of dimension
g
. One similarly constructs rings of Siegel modular forms of level
N
from the moduli stacks
\mathcal{A}_{g}(N)
parametrizing principally polarized abelian varieties with level
N
structure.

Many results are known about the rings
\mathcal{M}_{\bullet}(\operatorname{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}))
, due to Igusa, Tsuyumine, and others
[
B+
]
. We close with a pair of questions that we hope will inspire further interest in rings of Siegel modular forms, especially in characteristic
p
. Note that a similar line of inquiry is also proposed in the PhD thesis of Cerchia
[
Cer
]
.

Question 8.4
.

For fixed
g>1
, is there a uniform bound on the weights of generators and relations in a minimal presentation of the graded ring of Siegel modular forms of dimension
g
and level
N
, where
p\nmid N
?

Question 8.5
.

Do there exist a dimension
g>1
, a prime
p
and a level
N
not divisible by
p
for which there are ethereal mod
p
Siegel modular forms of dimension
g
and level
N
in low weight?
