Identification of aberrant α-2,3 sialylation of cartilage associated with osteonecrosis of the femoral head based on integrated multi-omics analyses
Authors: Xueliang Lu, Xu Wang, Mingyue Xiong, Pengbo Wang, Yingkang Zhu, Xiangyu Wang, Xia Liu, Hanjie Yu, Ruiyu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ST3GAL2 upregulation elevates α2–3 sialylation in ONFH cartilage and MPS-induced chondrocytes.•Silencing ST3GAL2 reduces α2–3 sialylation, chondrocyte apoptosis and NF-κB activation.•18 glycogens show abnormal expression in ONFH vs control cartilage.•α2–3 sialylation is a key mediator in ONFH pathogenesis, offering a potential therapeutic target.

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a common and intractable orthopedic disease, affecting approximately 8.12 million individuals in China [1,2]. ONFH is characterized by the local necrosis of osteocytes and bone marrow in the femoral head [3]. Clinically, ONFH is categorized into two distinct categories according to etiology: traumatic and non-traumatic [4,5]. Although many pathophysiological mechanisms of ONFH have been proposed, considerable limitations remain in our understanding of ONFH pathogenesis [6,7]. Progressive avascular bone necrosis and joint degeneration of the hip are major causes of ONFH, and numerous studies have focused on this topic. However, there is increasing evidence that cartilage degeneration plays an important role in the progression of ONFH. Qiu et al. reported that the upregulation of Follistatin-like 1 facilitated the expression of inflammatory cytokines and cartilage degradation enzymes via activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [8,9]. Additionally, Yu et al. reported that hypoxia‐inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) enhances collagen degradation in cartilage by upregulating its downstream target genes and promotes cell apoptosis through the endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) pathway in a steroid‐induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head model [10]. These findings suggested that interventions against cartilage degeneration could be potential approaches to alleviate hip dysfunction and retard the progression of ONFH.

Glycosylation is a main type of posttranslational modification of protein. Alterations in glycosylation are not only characteristic of diseases but also contribute to pathological processes [11]. Song et al. reported that an increase of de-sialylated, de-fucosylated N-glycans and a decrease of fucosylated N-glycans in serum proteins are associated with the development of ONFH [12]. Our previous study revealed that the expression and glycosylation of cartilage glycoproteins, which were associated with the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, ECM‒receptor interactions, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and N-glycan biosynthesis were significantly altered in ONFH patients [13]. Studies by Yu et al. indicated that the disruption of α1–3 fucosylation silences the expression of MMP-13, impedes the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, and antagonizes the apoptosis induced by TNF-α in chondrocytes [14]. These findings suggest that glycosylation plays an important role in the development of osteoarthritis (OA). Although OA and ONFH share the similar pathological features, such as degradation of cartilage, they are different orthopedic diseases [15]. Therefore, revealing the role of glycosylation in ONFH can help elucidate the pathological mechanisms of this disease.

In this study, we focused our attention on the role of glycosylation in the pathogenesis of ONFH. Our study demonstrated that the expression of ST3GAL2 and the level of α2–3 sialylation were increased in the cartilage of ONFH patients and methylprednisolone (MPS)-induced chondrocytes. Silencing the expression of ST3GAL2 enhanced cell viability, reduced the expression of matrix metalloproteinases, and inhibited cell apoptosis by impeding the activation of NF-κB signaling pathways stimulated by MPS. Our findings may provide new insight into the pathological mechanism of ONFH.

Material and methods

Analysis of the expression data of glycogen in ONFH cartilage

We compared the gene expression profiles of hip cartilage between ONFH patients and fracture controls using the Human Gene Expression 4 × 44 K v2 Microarray (Agilent) from a previous study (GSE74089) [16]. In this study, we selected genes that participate in the synthesis or degradation of glycans (termed glycogens). The differentially expressed glycogens were analyzed by GEO2R online software (NCBI) with the following criteria: a fold change > 2 or < 0.5 and a p value < 0.05. Unsupervised hierarchical cluster analysis and volcano plot construction were performed by cloudtutu online software (http://cloudtutu.com.cn/). Principal component analysis (PCA) and protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network analysis were carried out by platform of oebiotech cloud (https://cloud.oebiotech.com/). Pathways associated with the differentially expressed glycogens were mapped by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG).

Ethics statement

The collection of human hip articular cartilage specimens was carried out in accordance with the approved guidelines and was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Board of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Written informed consent was obtained from all the subjects.

Collection of articular cartilage specimens

The hip articular cartilage specimens were collected as described previously [16]. Briefly, hip articular cartilage was obtained from operations performed in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. All study subjects were Chinese Han. All tissue samples had a final diagnosis provided by at least two orthopedic specialists. The inclusion criterion was as follows: an NFH articular cartilage grade of Grade III according to the Ficat system. Articular cartilage was collected from individuals who were undergoing total hip replacement within 24 h of traumatic femoral neck fracture. Patients with rheumatoid arthritis, infectious arthritis, traumatic arthritis or other immune system diseases were excluded. As a result, three NFH-control pairs, matched for age and sex, were enrolled for further analysis.

Histopathology

Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Safranine O and Fast Green staining were used to evaluate the degree of degradative changes in the cartilage. In brief, cartilage from ONFH patients and controls was embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 6 μm. After deparaffinization and hydration, the sections were stained with H&E (TEASEN Biotechnology, China) and a Safranin O and Fast Green Cartilage Staining Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell culture and treatment

The human chondrocyte cell line C28/I2 (C28) was cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10 % FCS (Gibco Thermo Fisher, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin (Solarbio), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Solarbio) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C in the presence of 5 % CO2. Before treatment, the chondrocytes were serum-starved for 12 h and then stimulated with MPS (Aladdin, China) at concentrations of 0, 1, 2, 4, and 8 μM for 48 h [17]. Unstimulated chondrocytes were used as controls. After that, the chondrocytes were collected and subjected to further analysis.

Total RNA isolation

Total RNA from chondrocytes and cartilage tissue was extracted by Total RNA Isolation Reagent (Epizyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The amount of RNA was measured using a Nano Photometer (IMPLEN; München, Germany).

Semi-quantitative PCR

In general, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed using Evo M-MLV RT Kit (Accrate Biotechnology, Hunan, China), and qPCR was performed using a Real-Time PCR system (Bioer Technology, USA). SYBR Green-based three-step RT–qPCR was performed using SYBR® Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accrate Biotechnology). The primer sequences were retrieved from the online PrimerBank database (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/index.html). The information of primers is summarized in Table S1. The expression levels of selected genes were normalized to the amount of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) in each sample, and quantitation of gene expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCT comparative method.

Extraction of cell/tissue proteins

The proteins from chondrocyte and cartilage tissues were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Briefly, the cells and cartilage were washed with sterile PBS, and then, 1 mL of precooled RIPA buffer was added and homogenized for 5 min in an ice bath. The lysate was transferred to a tube and incubated for 15 min on ice, and then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, and 1 % (v/v) protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE) was added. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nantong, China).

Lectin microarray analysis

The analysis of the lectin microarray was executed as described previously [18,19]. The proteins isolated from chondrocytes and cartilage tissue were labeled with Cy3 fluorescent dye (GE Healthcare, Biosciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA) and then were purified using a Sephadex-G25 column (GE Healthcare). After blocking with 3 % BSA in PBST (1 mM phosphate buffer containing 0.1 % Tween-20) for 1 h, the lectin microarrays were then incubated with 4 μg of labeled protein in the chamber at 37 °C for 3 h. After that, the microarrays were washed with PBST and PBS twice.

After washing and centrifugation, the slides were scanned using a confocal scanner (4000B, AXON Instruments, USA). The fluorescence intensities were extracted by GenePix 7.0 software (Axon). After filtration and normalization, the parallel datasets were compared with each other based on fold changes according to the following criteria: fold changes ≥ 1.50 or ≤ 0.67 and p < 0.05 indicated upregulation or downregulation, respectively. Significant differences in lectin between samples were evaluated using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test.

Transfection of small interfering RNA

A small interfering RNA (siRNA) specific to ST3GAL2 was designed with the coding sequences of humans by the online software DSIR (http://biodev.extra.cea.fr/DSIR/ DSIR.html). The siRNAs and scramble siRNA (negative control) were obtained from Sangon (Sangon, China). The information on the siRNAs was shown in Table S2. The transfection of siRNA was performed using Lipofectamine® 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, California, USA). The cells were harvested 48 h after transfection, and the knockdown effects were evaluated by semi-qPCR. For stimulation, MPS was added to the medium at a final concentration of 2 μM and cultured for another 48 h. After that, the cells were collected and subjected to follow-up analysis.

Immunoblot analysis

In brief, 8 μg of proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk or 3 % BSA in TBST (for phospho-specific antibodies) at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight with shaking. After being washed with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abways Technology, China) at room temperature for 2 h. The antigen–antibody complexes were detected by an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (YEASEN, China), and the protein bands were detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, China).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining

Firstly, the C28 cells were inoculated into a six-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well). The cells were transfected with scramble siRNA or siRNA against ST3GAL2 until the fusion of cells reached 50 %. After that, the cells were cultured in complete medium with or without MPS for 48 h. After washed with PBS, the cells were stained with Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), according to manufacturer's protocol.

Alcian blue staining

The ECM proteins (including proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans) of chondrocytes were stained with an alcian blue staining kit (Sbjbio Life Science, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the chondrocytes were transfected with siRNA-ST3GAL2 and treated with or without MPS for 48 h. The same treatment used for the siRNA-NC-transfected cells served as a control. After treatment, the chondrocytes were immobilized with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min. After washing, the chondrocytes were incubated with a solution of periodic acid for 10 min and then immersed in Schiff reagent for 30 min. Subsequently, the chondrocytes were rinsed with sulfite solution, and the nuclei were stained with hematoxylin. The chondrocytes were observed with a light microscope and photographed with a digital camera.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptotic cells were detected by Annexin V & Dead Cell Kit (Cytek Biosciences, Netherlands). Briefly, the chondrocytes were washed with cold PSB and then digested with 0.25 % trypsin (without EDTA and phenol red, Biosharp). After being neutralized with complete medium, the cells were centrifuged, and the cell pellet was washed with binding buffer (1 % (w/v) BSA in 1 mM PBS). The chondrocytes were incubated with the Annexin V & Dead Cell Kit for 20 min in the dark. Apoptotic cells were analyzed by MUSE Cell Analyzer (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany).

Results

The altered glycogens in ONFH cartilage

In this study, we compared the expression levels of glycogens between ONFH and control cartilage samples from the gene expression data of our previous study (GSE74089). The expression of 57 unique was detected in both ONFH and control cartilage. HCE analysis revealed that the ONFH and control samples were clustered into different classes, indicating that the expression patterns of these glycogens were obviously different between ONFH and control cartilage (Fig. 1A). The differences in gene expression were >2-fold, with a p value < 0.05. As a result, 14 and 4 glycogens were upregulated or downregulated, respectively, in ONFH cartilage compared with control cartilage (Fig. 1B). Detailed information about these altered glycogens was summarized in Table 1. As expected, the PCA results revealed a clear separation between the ONFH and control groups (Fig. 1C). These results indicated that the expression patterns of glycogens were altered in ONFH cartilage. The differentially expressed glycogens were subsequently subjected to protein‒protein interaction analysis, and the results indicated that glycogens such as MGAT5, MAN2A1 and ST2GAL2 were considered hub genes owing to their high degrees in the network (Fig. 1D). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the upregulated glycogens were enriched in the synthesis of the core structure of β1–4 branched N-glycans, mucin-type O-glycans and O-fucosylation (Fig. 1E). Methylprednisolone (MPS) affects the proliferation and ECM homeostasis of chondrocytes

Loss of ECM proteins (such as COL2A1 and proteoglycans) and overexpression and secretion of proteases (such as MMPs and Aggrecanases) are the main causes of ECM degradation in chondrocytes, which is the main pathological feature of necrotic cartilage of the femoral head. First, the chondrocytes were exposed to 0, 1, 2, 4 or 8 μM MPS for 48 h to evaluate the cytotoxicity and effects of MPS on chondrocytes. As shown in Fig. 2A, proteoglycans in the ECM were significantly lower in 2–8 μM MPS-treated chondrocytes than in control chondrocytes. In addition, the expression levels of MMP-9 and ADAMST-4 were significantly increased by treatment with 2–8 μM MPS for 48 h (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Therefore, we selected 2 μM MPS for the subsequent experiments. The results of the proliferation assay revealed a significant decrease in cell proliferation in chondrocytes treated with 2 μM MPS for 48–72 h compared with that in unstimulated chondrocytes (Fig. 2C). The immunoblotting results revealed that, after treatment with MPS, the expression of MMP-13 and SOX-9 was increased (Fig. 2D). The altered glycogens in ONFH cartilage

To investigate the abnormal glycosylation associated with ONFH, cartilage tissues were collected from ONFH patients and healthy controls. The clinical information about the ONFH patients and fracture control patients was summarized in Table 2. The radiograph results revealed that the morphology of the hip joint in patients with ONFH had undergone significant changes, such as localized hyperdense shadowing of the femoral head, collapse of the femoral head, flattening of the articular surface and the formation of subchondral osteosclerotic bands (Fig. 3A). In addition, disorganized chondrocyte arrangement and focal chondrocyte necrosis (nuclear consolidation and increased cytoplasmic eosinophilicity) were observed in ONFH cartilage (Fig. 3B). Compared with normal cartilage, ONFH cartilage presented significant loss of proteoglycans and disruption of the collagen network, uneven and light staining of Safranine O, a marked absence of staining in superficial cartilage, partial staining in deeper cartilage, broken and disorganized collagen fibers in Fast Green staining, and varying depths of staining (lighter or absent green in some areas) (Fig. 3C). The glycopatterns of the Cy3-labeled cartilage proteins from normal controls and ONFH patients were shown in Fig. 3D Compared with those of normal cartilage, the normalized fluorescence intensities (NFIs) of SNA (Sia2–6Gal/GalNAc binder), MAL-II (Siaα2–3Gal/GalNAc binder), RCA120 (β-Gal binder), PHA-E + L (bisecting GlcNAc, bi/tri- and tetra-antennary N-glycan binder), Jacalin (T and Tn antigen binder), MAL-I, ConA (high-mannose binder) and MPL (Galβ−1,4GlcNAc binder) were significantly increased, whereas the NFIs of WFA (GalNAcα/β1–3/6 Gal binder), LCA and PSA (Fucα−1,6GlcNAc binders) and LTL (Fucα1–3Galβ1–4GlcNAc binder) were significantly decreased in ONFH cartilage (Fig. 3E). Disruption of ST3GAL2 expression impeded ECM degradation and apoptosis induced by MPS

α2–3 sialylation of N-glycans is synthesized by ST3GAL1–6 (Fig. 5A). We found that ST3GAL2, 4, and 6 were expressed in both ONFH and normal cartilage. Among these genes, the expression of ST3GAL2 was significantly increased in ONFH cartilage compared with normal cartilage. Moreover, the results of qPCR revealed that the expression of ST3GAL2 was also upregulated in MPS-treated chondrocytes, which was consistent with the findings in ONFH cartilage (Fig. 5B). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the upregulation of ST3GAL2 promotes the synthesis of α2–3 sialylation and is involved in the development of ONFH. To this end, we silenced the expression of ST3GAL2 by transfection of siRNA (Fig. 5C). The cellular proteins of the transfected chondrocytes were subjected to lectin microarray analysis; as a result, the NFIs of MAL-II (α2–3-linked sialic acid binder) was also significantly reduced in the MPS-treated cells transfected with siRNA-ST3GAL2 compared with siRNA-NC-transfected cells (Fig. 5D). Our results suggested that silencing ST3GAL2 expression effectively antagonizes the increase in α2–3 sialylation induced by MPS. In addition, the results of SA ß-gal staining revealed that silencing expression of ST3GAL2 antagonized cell senescence induced by MPS treatment (Fig. 5E). In addition, the cell proliferation assay indicated that the proliferation of chondrocytes was impaired by MPS treatment, whereas repression of ST3GAL2 expression significantly increased the proliferation of MPS-treated chondrocytes (Fig. 5F). Compared with those of MPS-stimulated chondrocytes, the proportion of apoptotic cells, especially late apoptotic cells, were significantly reduced by downregulated ST3GAL2 expression (Fig. 5G). The immunoblotting results revealed that the expression of proinflammatory cytokine (IL-1β) and apoptosis-associated proteins (Caspase 3 and Caspase 8) were significantly increased in MPS-treated cells. Interestingly,silencing ST3GAL2 expression in MPS-treated chondrocytes significantly reduced the expression of these apoptosis- and inflammation-related proteins. We also found that silencing ST3GAL2 expression inhibited the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway by reducing the phosphorylation levels of IκBα and p65 (Fig. 5H). Discussion

Currently, osteonecrosis is known to be the primary pathological feature and etiology of ONFH, however, increasing evidence indicated that the degradation of cartilage is not only a hallmark of ONFH but also has a significant influence on the pathogenic process. In the context of ONFH, hip cartilage collapse could be attributed to structural degradation and regional alterations in the biomechanical properties of the necrotic femoral head [[20], [21], [22]]. Song et al. compared hip cartilage protein expression between ONFH patients and controls and found that 303 proteins involved in collagen turnover, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, metabolic pathways, and complement and coagulation cascades were differentially expressed in ONFH cartilage [23]. The glucocorticoid receptor (GR) plays a critical role in the development of steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SONFH), as glucocorticoids regulate gene transcription through cytoplasmic GR activation, nuclear translocation, and subsequent binding to glucocorticoid response elements. The activation of the GR and HIF pathways contributed to the occurrence of SONFH by regulating ECM homeostasis, apoptosis, and glycolysis in chondrocytes [7,24,25]. These findings suggested that preventing cartilage degradation is an effective approach for the treatment of ONFH.

Glycosylation is an important type of post-translational modification of proteins. It not only mediates the conformation and function of proteins but is also involved in biological processes, such as inflammation and apoptosis [26,27]. Glycosyltransferases are responsible for the synthesis of glycan. It was known that the abnormal expression of glycosyltransferases affects protein functions by altering the glycan structures of proteins and eventually accelerates the process of diseases, such as cancer [28,29]. Recent studies indicated that alterations in the glycosylation of cartilage were associated with cartilage lesions [30,31]. In this study, we compared the expression of glycogens in hip cartilage between ONFH patients and controls in our previous study. We found that the expression of 14 and 4 glycogens was up-regulated or down-regulated in cartilage from ONFH patients compared with controls. These altered glycogens were mainly involved in N-glycan precursor biosynthesis, O-linked fucosylation, glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, Type 1/2 chain of poly LacNAc, branched GlcNAc of N-glycan and sialylation. The upregulation of MGAT1 and MGAT5 promoted to the synthesis of complex-type N-glycans, which were reported to be associated with increased severity of cartilage degeneration [32]. ONFH and OA share certain similarities in pathological features, such as chondrocyte apoptosis and ECM degradation. This similarity makes it difficult to differentiate between advanced osteoarthritis and osteonecrosis [33,34]. However, several studies have indicated that the gene expression patterns and components of the ECM in cartilage were different between ONFH and OA, which is attributed to the distinct pathological mechanisms [35,36]. Our previous study revealed that the α1–3/6 fucosylation level was elevated in OA cartilage compared with that in normal cartilage [10,19]. In contrast, we found that the level of α1–3/6 fucosylation in ONFH cartilage was markedly lower than that in normal cartilage. Our findings revealed that the differences in the glycosylation of cartilage between OA and ONFH may reflect differences in the pathological processes of OA and ONFH.

Sialylation is a specific form of glycosylation that plays essential roles in multiple biological processes, such as inflammation and pathogen recognition [37]. Our findings indicated that the regulation of ST3GAL2 contributed to the accumulated α2–3 sialylation in both the cartilage of ONFH patients and MPS-treated chondrocytes. The stimulation of proinflammatory cytokines is an important factor that causes the apoptosis of arthritis chondrocytes [38,39]. Several studies reported that stimulation with IL-1β led to a shift from α2–6-linked sialic acids to α2–3-linked sialic acids on cartilage glycoproteins, which was correlated with the occurrence of common types of arthritis [40]. In addition, the study by Wang et al. revealed that loss of α2–6 sialylation and glycan remodeling promote the transformation of synovial fibroblasts into a proinflammatory phenotype in rheumatoid arthritis [41]. Considering the significant role of sialylation in arthritis, targeting aberrant sialylation, especially α2–3 sialylation in chondrocytes, could be a potential therapeutic strategy for ONFH. Our results indicated that silencing the expression of ST3GAL2 reduced the α2–3 sialylation level in chondrocytes and inhibited cell apoptosis and NF-κB activation. Studies by Estrella et al. reported that targeting sialylated glycoprotein (podoplanin) in chondrocytes could attenuate the inflammatory response mediated by the activated NF-κB pathway and protect against human cartilage breakdown in arthritis [42]. These results suggested that aberrant α2–3 sialylation facilitates chondrocyte apoptosis and expedites the progression of ONFH via the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. However, there are several limitations in this study. First, more cartilage samples from patients and glucocorticoid-induced animal models should be collected to investigate the relationship between the α2–3 sialylation level and the development of ONFH. Second, the exact molecular mechanism of the regulatory effect of α2–3 sialylation on the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and apoptosis in vitro have not been elucidated.

Conclusion

In summary, our study revealed that the elevated level of α2–3 sialylation caused by the upregulation of ST3GAL2 contributes to the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway as well as the apoptosis of ONFH chondrocytes and ultimately facilitates the ONFH process.
