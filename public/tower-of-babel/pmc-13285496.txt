Passage of a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube through the liver: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Jun Zhou, Dexian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPassage of a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube through the liver during PEG is a rare but potentially serious complication.Case presentationHere, we report the case of a 78-year-old male patient with dysphagia who underwent PEG in our hospital. Follow-up abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed that the PEG tube had passed through the left lobe of the liver. This situation poses a clinical dilemma as to whether to remove the tube or allow it to remain in place. Based on existing literature, transhepatic PEG tubes can be used for long periods of time. In our case, the sinus tract was immature and there were no immediate concerns with regards to serious complications. Therefore, we decided to retain the PEG tube and observe the patient, incorporating tube care, regular disinfection, and dressing changes. The patient has been using his PEG tube for over five years with no severe complications. To the best of our knowledge, this is the longest PEG tube has been allowed to remain in place.ConclusionsThe transhepatic insertion of a PEG tube is an extremely rare complication of PEG. Our experience indicates that a PEG tube can be retained and used for a long period of time in the absence of serious complications related to a transhepatic PEG tube. Caregivers should pay particular attention to the disinfection and daily management of PEG tubes.Keywords: Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy, Complications, Liver injury

Background

Patients with intact gastrointestinal functionality but an inability to consume sufficient calories to meet their metabolic needs may need to consider undergoing percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG), a commonly utilized method that provides support for enteral nutrition [1, 2]. PEG is typically employed for patients requiring enteral nutrition for longer than 30 days [3, 4]. However, PEG is associated with several complications, including bleeding, infection, liver damage, colon damage, and displacement or detachment of the PEG tube.

Notably, reports of cases involving a PEG tube inadvertently penetrating the liver are scarce [5–8]. This event can be associated with severe complications [9], involving hemorrhage [10], peritonitis and liver abscesses [11], collection of perihepatic fluid [12]. The PEG tube removed by laparotomy [10, 11] or upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (UGD) [12]. Cases describing the long-term use of transhepatic PEG tubes are even rarer [8, 13].

Here, we present a case of a PEG tube that inadvertently passed through the left lobe of the liver and explore the feasibility of long-term PEG tube placement by referring to the existing literature.

Case description

A 78-year-old male patient with a previous medical history of subtotal gastrectomy was admitted to the Neurology Department of our hospital for cerebral infarction. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head revealed a previous cerebral infarction and chronic ischemic changes in the white matter. The patient suffered from pneumonia after admission. Due to severe swallowing difficulties and an inability to eat orally, the patient was subsequently transferred to the Gastroenterology Ward. The estimated duration of enteral nutrition was over one month, and PEG was proposed. One week prior to PEG, an abdominal CT scan revealed a residual stomach, with a narrow pathway in front of the abdominal wall that was suitable for the insertion of a PEG tube according to the conventional path (Fig. 1).

Preoperative abdominal CT revealed a narrowed conventional PEG pathway(arrow). CT, computed tomography; PEG, Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy

The patient subsequently underwent PEG under intravenous anesthesia with propofol (Corden Pharma S.P.A) (Fig. 2). A 15 Fr gastrostomy tube (Fresenius Kabi AG) was placed endoscopically using the pull technique. The puncture site was in the left upper quadrant of the abdomen, approximately 2 cm below the costal margin in the midclavicular line. During the puncture, there was minimal resistance, and no significant bleeding was encountered. During surgery, the stomach was insufflated with gas to oppose the gastric and abdominal walls. On abdominal examination, the PEG tube was freely rotatable. The site was clean and dry, with no drainage. The patient received a three-day course of anti-infective treatment with Cefotaxime (1 g administered every 12 h), along with wound dressing changes, acid-suppressing medication, observation for any changes in the condition, and precautions to prevent bleeding.

Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG)

Enteral nutrition was started 24 h after the PEG tube had been inserted. However, the patient experienced abdominal discomfort but without abdominal pain or trouble advancing the PEG tube. Seven days post-PEG, follow-up abdominal CT showed that the PEG tube had inadvertently passed through the left lobe of the liver (Fig. 3). The patient did not experience ascites, abdominal infection, gastrohepatic fistula or subcapsular hemorrhage. The white blood cell count (WBC) was not elevated and the patient was devoid of serious complications. The patient’s condition was relatively stable and the sinus tract was immature. Therefore, we considered this as an unconventional pathway for the PEG tube but retained the tube for enteral nutrition. We reached our decision by referring to previous case reports [7, 8, 13]. We continued to disinfect the wound and perform dressing changes. Our patient was discharged after his condition stabilized nine days later.

Seven days post-PEG, abdominal CT scanning indicated that the PEG tube had penetrated the left lobe of the liver (arrow). CT, computed tomography; PEG, Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy

Following discharge, the patient suffered from dementia and cerebral infarction, rendering him unable to resume independent eating, necessitating long-term feeding via the PEG tube. For enteral nutrition, we utilized intact protein enteral nutrition powder (Milupa GmbH) which was sometimes mixed with a homemade homogenized meal; this was provided as a bolus injection of approximately 200 ml every four to six hours.

Caregivers provided care the PEG tube by regularly disinfecting the gastrostomy site and the external PEG tube and fixation plate to prevent infection. They also loosened the device one to two times a week, pushed the tube 2–4 cm into the stomach with simultaneous rotation. The tube was then pulled outwards in a gentle manner until resistance was felt. Then, the retention device and tubing was refixed and the fixed position of the PEG tube was adjusted to prevent buried bumper syndrome. The caregivers avoided food at extreme temperatures and flushed the tube with approximately 50 ml warm water using a syringe before and after tube feeding.

The patient had his PEG tube replaced with a 15 Fr gastrostomy tube (Fresenius Kabi AG) at our hospital one year later and then again almost three years later (Fig. 4). Both replacements were performed due to routine tube exchange without resistance. No immediate or delayed complications occurred during or after either replacement procedure. Abdominal CT scans, performed three years later, confirmed that the PEG tube had passed through the left lobe of the liver and that the retention plate was located in the stomach (Fig. 5), without the collection of perihepatic fluid, subcapsular hematoma, or hepatic abscess.

A CT scan performed almost three years post-PEG revealed that the PEG tube had passed through the left lobe of the liver (thin arrow), the retention plate is in the stomach (thick arrow). CT, computed tomography; PEG, Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy

Five years later, the patient went to the Outpatient Department of our hospital and subsequently passed away from pneumonia; his death was not associated with PEG-related infection or other tube-related complications. Therefore, the PEG tube had been in place in this patient for more than five years.

Discussion

PEG is less invasive than surgical gastrostomy. Direct gastrojejunostomy is relatively rare with a lower success rate than PEG. PEG placement is generally a safe and well-tolerated procedure. The inadvertent transhepatic placement of a PEG tube is an extremely rare complication of PEG. We performed a comprehensive review of the existing literature on the inadvertent transhepatic placement of PEG tubes in the PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Wanfang (https://c.wanfangdata.com.cn/periodical), and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) databases (https://www.cnki.net/). Stellato et al. [13] and other authors [5–8, 10–12, 14–19] reported seventeen cases involving PEG complications and a PEG tube passing through the liver. These cases are summarized in Table 1. Fifteen of these PEG tubes were inserted using the pull technique, two patients with history of subtotal gastrectomy [12, 13]. The time reported for transhepatic gastrostomy in the existing literature ranged from immediately after PEG to up to three years post-PEG. Abdominal pain was reported by eight cases (47.06%) while abdominal discomfort was reported by one patient (5.88%). One case (5.88%) presented with tube malfunction [5]. With regards to the management of PEG tubes passing through the liver, (1) ten patients had their PEG tubes removed [5–7, 10–12, 14–17]; eight by laparotomy and two by UGD [12, 16]. Following discovery of a transhepatic PEG tube, two patients had their PEG tubes retained for 8 days [12] and 3 months [7] before removal. Four patients had used their PEG tube 7 weeks [11], 2.5 months [5], 1 year [17], and 3 year [14] separately before the discovery of the transhepatic PEG tube. The reasons for intervention included perihepatic ascites, perihepatic fluid collection, hemorrhage, subcapsular hematoma, hepatic abscess and peritonitis, and difficulty advancing the PEG tube [5, 6, 10, 11, 14, 17]. (2) Seven patients were devoid of abdominal free fluid, abdominal infection, and retained their PEG tubes [8, 13, 18, 19]. Three patients used their PEG tubes for 6 weeks [8], 6 month [8] and 2 year [13]. One patient only used their PEG tube during period as an inpatient [19].

In our case, the PEG tube had inadvertently passed through the left lobe of the liver. This was associated with a small residual stomach and the absence of imaging guidance. This left us with a clinical dilemma: remove the tube or leave it in place. If treating a complication related to the PEG tube, it is necessary to remove the PEG tube by laparotomy, which is highly traumatic. Our patient’s condition was relatively stable, without severe PEG-related complication. While we considered this as an unconventional pathway for PEG, we decided to retain the tube and observe the patient. We planned to remove the PEG tube if any severe complications occurred. Previous case reports demonstrated the feasibility and safety of long-term maintenance when applied to transhepatic PEG tubes in some patients. Therefore, our decision to retain the PEG tube was based on a risk-benefit assessment.

Subtotal gastrectomy is a relative contraindication for PEG [4, 20]. Some scholars have intentionally performed transhepatic gastrostomy for patients with a residual stomach. Kanazawa et al. [21] reported four patients with a history of partial gastrectomy who underwent CT-guided percutaneous transhepatic PEG, with tube feeding for two to seven months without serious complications. Moriwaki et al. [22] reported one case who underwent percutaneous transhepatic PEG. These previous cases informed our clinical decision to retain the PEG tube in our current case.

When PEG tubes are used over long periods of time, caregivers should follow a practical approach for managing the PEG tube [3], including hand hygiene and regular, aseptic dressing changes of the stoma site. It is also important to loosen and refix the retention device to prevent buried bumper syndrome, and to prevent the PEG tube from twisting and folding. Caregivers should also be instructed to flush the tube with water using a syringe before and after tube feeding.

Our present case received feeding via a PEG tube that traversed the left hepatic lobe for over five years, thus representing a duration far exceeding those previously reported in the literature. Our case should serve as a reference guideline for the individualized treatment of similar cases in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, the transhepatic insertion of a PEG tube is an extremely rare complication of PEG. Without complications related to a transhepatic PEG tube, the PEG tube can be retained and used over a long period of time. Caregivers should pay particular attention to the disinfection and daily management of the PEG tube.
