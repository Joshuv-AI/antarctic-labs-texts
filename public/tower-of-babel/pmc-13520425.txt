Young adults’ perceptions of food classification criteria and barriers to healthy eating: a qualitative inquiry from eight Indian metropolises
Authors: Lalita Vatta, Shikha Panwar, Farheen Ahmadi, Anthony Worsley, Neha Rathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapidly transforming urban Indian food landscape has triggered unhealthy dietary habits among its younger cohort. Young adults’ classification of healthy and unhealthy foods is likely to have significant repercussions for their eating behaviour, and consequently, their long-term health and well-being. This qualitative research inquiry aimed to capture novel insights about the ways urban Indian young adults classify meals and snacks as healthy or unhealthy as well as their perceived barriers to healthy eating.MethodsGuided by the social constructivism paradigm, the present investigation employed an exploratory-descriptive qualitative research design. Purposive and snowball sampling strategies were used to recruit young adults (aged: 19 years – 29 years) from eight Indian metropolises. In-depth interviews were conducted either in-person or virtually until saturation. Sixty-eight young adults (51% female) participated in the interviews. The conversations were digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim for data analysis. Interview transcripts were imported to NVivo software and analysed using open thematic coding.ResultsFour main themes and associated sub-themes included: (i) Food classification criteria (Nutrient composition and food groups, Type of food preparation, Food preparation venue, Frequency of food consumption, Presence of sugar, Food processing and packaging, Price of food, Health issues); (ii) Barriers to healthy eating (Easy availability and accessibility of calorie-dense, nutrient-poor food, Healthy food is costly, Negative peer influence, Decline in home cooking, Independent living); (iii) Poor knowledge about government-led health campaigns; (iv) Digital health applications and social media.ConclusionsEmerging themes highlight various food classification criteria adopted by young adults to categorize food as healthy or unhealthy as well as several barriers to healthy eating. These findings highlight the need to equip young people with nutrition and food literacy and digital health literacy which could be attained by tailored behavioural nutrition interventions and integrating government-led health messaging into trusted social media platforms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28698-y.

Background

India is home to the largest young adult population in the world [1]. Sixty-five percent of India’s current population is under the age of 35 years [1]. Young adults serve as valuable resources for any nation by playing a critical role in bridging conventional values and contemporary influences, significantly contributing to societal transformation [2]. Young adulthood serves as a viable platform to establish a strong foundation for a healthy and productive middle and late adulthood as well as old age [2]. Despite their potential, young adults in India and worldwide often exhibit the least healthy eating habits of all other adult age groups, increasing their risk of developing chronic degenerative diseases [3–7].

Recent evidence from local studies indicates low intakes of fresh fruits and vegetables as well as increased consumption of ultra-processed fast foods and sugar-sweetened beverages in young Indian adults [4, 8, 9]. These unhealthy food choices have led to a significant increase in BMI levels particularly among young adults over the last couple of years [10, 11]. For example, a Mumbai-based study found that two-fifths of the study sample (N = 269) aged 18–24 years was obese [11]. Likewise, 11.2% of young adults (N = 1221) were diagnosed with hypertension and 33.3% had prehypertension in Kerala [12]. Additionally, a national screening campaign revealed that 13.3% of individuals (N = 27925; age below 30 years) had Type 2 diabetes [13], highlighting an escalating disease burden among young Indians. Therefore, it is critical to promote healthy dietary habits for combating the rising obesity and its associated disease burden during this pivotal phase of life.

One such approach for promoting healthy eating is to encourage youth to make informed food decisions [14, 15] by boosting their nutrition knowledge [16]. Nutrition knowledge significantly affects how individuals define and perceive what constitutes a nutritious and non-nutritious food or beverage item [16]. In an attempt to enhance young adults’ nutritional and health status, several public health campaigns and behavioural change interventions have focused on fostering better food choices by improving individuals’ nutrition knowledge [15, 17, 18]. The “Eat Right India” (ERI) program is one such initiative launched by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) in 2018 which aims to safeguard the health of its citizens and the planet by transforming India’s food landscape using a systems approach [19]. Its three core principles are “Eat Safe”, “Eat Healthy”, and “Eat Sustainable”. One of the primary objectives of the ERI campaign is to inculcate healthy food habits in Indian nationals through several information education and communication activities in the form of videos, flyers, brochure, and Eat Right Fair [19]. In the same vein, the Ministry of Education launched the “FIT India Movement” in 2019 with the aim of creating awareness among university and school students about various ways in which they can remain fit including adoption of healthy dietary practices [20]. However, whether these government-led campaigns have been effective in altering eating habits of Indian young adults is still not known. Nevertheless, both these healthy eating campaigns endorse nutrition knowledge as an important facilitator for adoption of healthy dietary habits.

Apart from influencing perceptions about healthy eating, nutrition knowledge has also been recognized as a potential determinant impacting individuals’ food classification criteria and consequently their dietary choices. Hence, to improve Indian young adults’ dietary health and minimize their risk for diet-related chronic illnesses, it is crucial to understand whether young adults are aware of healthy and unhealthy food and further explore the food classification criteria used by them during food selection. In addition, it is also important to investigate whether Indian youth is aware of the government-led public health campaigns designed for promoting healthy eating in citizens.

While empirical evidence about youths’ perceptions of healthy and unhealthy food is expanding, most of this evidence is limited to industrialized nations like Australia, Germany, the United Kingdom, Canada, and the United States of America. This emerging evidence may or may not be applicable to the Global South. The dearth of local evidence provides an opportunity to explore Indian young adults’ perceptions of their food classification criteria as well as challenges to encourage healthy eating in them. Therefore, this qualitative inquiry was designed to assess the ways Indian young adults classify food as healthy and unhealthy as well as their perceived barriers to healthy eating.

Methods

Study design

Guided by the social constructivism paradigm [21], the present investigation employed an exploratory-descriptive qualitative research approach [22] to assess Indian young adults’ food classification criteria. The social constructivist lens enabled the research team to gauge the respondents’ (i.e. young adults’) perspectives of the topic (i.e. criteria used to classify foods as healthy or unhealthy) under investigation through their lived experiences which were cultivated by their interactions with the society’s members. All aspects of the research methodology have been comprehensively reported as per the 32-item Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist [23] to warrant rigor and transparency in the current investigation (Supplementary File). Ethical approval for the research methodology was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Banaras Hindu University.

Research team

Five investigators were involved in implementing this qualitative inquiry. There were two female senior authors (LV, NR). LV is a professor of extension education and communication and acted as a third coder. NR is a seasoned qualitative researcher with expertise in community and behavioural nutrition. The third member is a female doctoral scholar (SP) of clinical nutrition and dietetics. NR supervised the interviews and was also involved in analysing the interview data. Interviews were conducted by one active female postgraduate student (FA was studying extension education and communication) as well as the doctoral scholar (SP). Prior to data collection, both the interviewers (FA, SP) received formal training in conducting in-depth qualitative interviews and coding from NR. Finally, the research team included an experienced male professor of food psychology (AW) with long experience in both qualitative and quantitative research methodologies, and he supervised the report writing. None of the authors shared any personal or professional relationships with the interviewees. The participation of both male and female researchers from diverse academic disciplines helped reduce any subjective or gender bias incurred during thematic coding of the transcripts.

Study setting and sample

Young adults aged 21–29 years residing in urban India formed the study sample. Young adulthood is marked by significant life events such as leaving home, pursuing tertiary education, seeking paid employment, and forming relationships including marriage [2, 24–26]. This transitional life stage also involves risk-taking and unhealthy behaviours, rendering young adults a vulnerable group [2, 27]. The exclusive focus on urban Indian young adults is justified by the fact that the prevalence of overweight and obesity as well as metabolic syndrome is higher among urban residents in comparison to rural residents in India [28, 29]. Considering this, the inquiry was administered at the PAN-India level, encompassing all of India’s major metropolitan cities. According to the Indian Census Commission, metropolitan cities are cities which have a population of four million or more [30]. As of now, India has eight major metropolitan cities, which includes Mumbai, Pune, Hyderabad, Kolkata, Ahmedabad, Chennai, Delhi, and Bengaluru [30]. These metropolises were selected for their vibrant cosmopolitan outlook and cultural diversity as they include individuals from all parts of the country; therefore, the sample reflected India’s urban young adult population.

Recruitment of sample

Various social media platforms (e.g., Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, LinkedIn) were used to advertise this research inquiry. A combination of purposive and snowball sampling techniques was employed to recruit young adults from the eight Indian metropolitan cities. Interested participants reached out to the research team via email or phone. Subsequently, a plain language statement outlining the research topic and data collection process, along with a consent form were shared with the potential participants either via email or in person. Upon receipt of written consent from the participants, the research team asked the participants to share their preferences with regards to date, time, mode (face-to-face or virtual) and location (for in-person only) for the interview. Several qualitative researchers have endorsed that virtual interviews (telephone and online) serve as viable substitutes to face-to-face interviews because the quality of interview data remains unchanged by the mode of data collection [31–34].

Data collection

Informed by the research aim and a review of previous qualitative studies [14, 15], a semi-structured interview guide comprising seven open-ended questions (Table 1) was developed in consultation with a senior dietician and a psychologist. Consequently, the guide was pilot tested with five young adults for refinement in December 2024. The interview guide was received well by the pilot study interviewees and therefore no amendments were made. Apart from the open-ended question, some socio-demographic information was also obtained from the interviewees which included age, city of residence, educational qualification, and employment status.

Semi-structured interview guide

Interviews were conducted by trained interviewers (FA and SP) who were fluent in English as well as Hindi in the presence of a note keeper. NR, an experienced qualitative researcher, was also present during the first ten interview sessions for smooth facilitation of the interviews. The interviews were conducted in the preferred language of the participants between January 2025 and November 2025. All interview sessions were audio recorded.

Data was collected until saturation was attained [35] at 68th interview. The duration of interviews extended from 11 to 50 min. Sixty-four interviews were carried out in virtual modality (Zoom − 9; Telephonic − 55) while four remaining interviews were carried out in-person (Three interviews were carried out in the workplace and one was conducted in interviewee’s home). For the in-person interviews, no one else was present in the interview room besides the interviewee, two interviewers and a note keeper.

Data analysis

Underpinned by Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework [36], inductive thematic analysis was carried out alongside the interviews to maintain a balance between data collection and analysis processes [37]. The two interviewers transcribed their respective interview recordings and translated them to English where required. To ensure accuracy, the transcripts were reviewed by the note keeper. Post transcription, all interviewees were requested to provide feedback on their transcripts, but only four interviewees shared their feedback. All four interviewees were satisfied with their transcripts. Inductive coding was performed by two coders; SP coded all the transcripts while NR coded 50% of the transcripts to improve reliability and minimize bias [38]. NVivo (Version 15, Lumivero 2024) software was used for the coding process. Any disagreement between the two coders (SP and NR) was fixed through consultation with the senior author (LV). In the first phase, the two coders engaged in repeated reading of the transcripts to familiarize themselves with the raw data and in the second phase initial codes were extracted. This was followed by assimilation of the initial codes into potential themes and in the fourth phase these themes were thoroughly reviewed and further refined. In the second last phase, each theme was clearly articulated and described in detail. Lastly, a comprehensive report (i.e. Results section) was formed by linking the themes with participants’ narratives to provide deeper insight into their perceptions. The coders’ (NR, SP) background in community nutrition and dietetics provided a nuanced, culturally informed lens to interpret the food classification criteria adopted by urban young adults as well as the barriers to healthy eating among them. Concomitantly, FA’s training in extension education and communication assisted in optimizing interview dynamics. Throughout the coding process, the coders maintained a reflexive stance, using consensus-building sessions to ensure the final themes accurately reflected the interviewees’ lived experiences rather than the coders’ professional expectations.

Half of the sample was employed whereas slightly more than one-tenth of the sample claimed to be married (Table 2).

Theme 1: Food classification criteria

Nutrient composition and food groups

One of the most frequently reported criteria for categorising healthy and unhealthy foods was based on nutrient composition and food groups. The participants acknowledged that meals and snacks comprising food groups like fruits, vegetables, cereals, and pulses were healthy while food groups like meat and meat products were considered unhealthy.

“I would classify fruits, vegetables ahhh… like leafy greens, rajma (red kidney beans), chapati (Indian flat bread), curd, and even a burrito bowl as healthy foods.” (YA12, M, 26 years).

“I would classify fruits, vegetables ahhh… like leafy greens, rajma (red kidney beans), chapati (Indian flat bread), curd, and even a burrito bowl as healthy foods.” (YA12, M, 26 years).

“I feel non-veg is okay only up to eggs and fish, but, uh, meat is not good for health. so, it is unhealthy” (YA66, F, 26 years).

“I feel non-veg is okay only up to eggs and fish, but, uh, meat is not good for health. so, it is unhealthy” (YA66, F, 26 years).

Furthermore, the participants noted that healthy food groups were powerhouse of vital nutrients like proteins, carbohydrates, vitamins and minerals.

“Ahh… for me, foods that has proteins, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals, and other vital nutrients are healthy.” (YA27, F, 19 years).

“Ahh… for me, foods that has proteins, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals, and other vital nutrients are healthy.” (YA27, F, 19 years).

Few of the interviewees perceived that Indian meals were healthier in comparison to Indian snacks as highlighted in the following excerpt:

“…Like our Indian meals are healthier than snacks as chapati gives carbs, dal gives protein, and vegetables give important micronutrients, while snacks like samosas or chips are mostly fatty…” (YA16, M, 24 years).

“…Like our Indian meals are healthier than snacks as chapati gives carbs, dal gives protein, and vegetables give important micronutrients, while snacks like samosas or chips are mostly fatty…” (YA16, M, 24 years).

Type of food preparation

The interviewees evaluated the healthiness of meals and snacks based on the type of food preparation method employed in preparing a meal or a snack. Food items that were deep fried or baked were considered unhealthy when compared to steamed, boiled, and sautéed foods. The following two quotes exemplifies this:

“…It depends on what it’s made of and how it’s made. For example, if the food is deep fried it’s obvious that it’s unhealthy…” (YA51, F, 27 years).

“…It depends on what it’s made of and how it’s made. For example, if the food is deep fried it’s obvious that it’s unhealthy…” (YA51, F, 27 years).

“I would consider a boiled egg healthier than an omelette because, uh…, it’s plain and doesn’t have oil added…” (YA65, M, 28 years).

“I would consider a boiled egg healthier than an omelette because, uh…, it’s plain and doesn’t have oil added…” (YA65, M, 28 years).

Food preparation venue

Young adults also classified food as healthy or unhealthy based on where it was prepared. They believed that meals and snacks prepared in homes are healthy because they are prepared using fresh ingredients in a clean hygienic environment. Meals and snacks prepared outside home such as roadside food carts and restaurants were regarded as unhealthy because of unhygienic conditions (e.g. flies sitting on food, use of dirty water for rinsing dishes) as well as use of poor-quality food ingredients (e.g. adulterated oil, stale vegetables) in food preparation.

“…and a clean and well-maintained home kitchen promotes healthy cooking and reduces the chances of unhealthy food preparation.” (YA37, F, 24 years).

“…and a clean and well-maintained home kitchen promotes healthy cooking and reduces the chances of unhealthy food preparation.” (YA37, F, 24 years).

“When I eat outside…I have no idea how the food is prepared…umm what kind of vegetables, oil, or ingredients are used, or how healthy the food actually is…” (YA2, M, 27 years).

“When I eat outside…I have no idea how the food is prepared…umm what kind of vegetables, oil, or ingredients are used, or how healthy the food actually is…” (YA2, M, 27 years).

Frequency of food consumption

Another parameter adopted by young adults for classifying healthy and unhealthy meals and snacks was the frequency of consumption. They reported that food, particularly energy-dense, nutrient-poor snacks, if consumed on a daily basis could be detrimental for health but if occasionally consumed may not have any negative health consequences.

“Yes… if you’re having junk or fast food regularly, like 2–3 times a week, then it’s unhealthy.” (YA26, M, 24 years).

“Yes… if you’re having junk or fast food regularly, like 2–3 times a week, then it’s unhealthy.” (YA26, M, 24 years).

“For me, the frequency of intake determines whether something is healthy or unhealthy — in moderation it’s healthy, but having it too frequently makes it unhealthy.” (YA7, F, 29 years).

“For me, the frequency of intake determines whether something is healthy or unhealthy — in moderation it’s healthy, but having it too frequently makes it unhealthy.” (YA7, F, 29 years).

Presence of sugar

A less frequently mentioned criterion to categorise foods was the presence of sugar. Brownies, biscuits and some Indian desserts were described by our young interviewees as unhealthy because they contained surplus sugar or artificial sweeteners. They further reported that consumption of artificial sweeteners was quite common in young adults. Few participants even claimed that consumption of sugar could result in ill health and endorsed the need to read sugar content on food labels before consumption.

“…biscuits and chocolate brownies have a lot of processed sugar. I consider them unhealthy and we should check the sugar content on label before consumption. …” (YA10, F, 21 years).

“…biscuits and chocolate brownies have a lot of processed sugar. I consider them unhealthy and we should check the sugar content on label before consumption. …” (YA10, F, 21 years).

“Since I know that sugar is not good for health… for me cold drinks seem unhealthy because they’re full of sugar that can lead to weight gain and other lifestyle diseases….” (YA62, M, 22 years).

“Since I know that sugar is not good for health… for me cold drinks seem unhealthy because they’re full of sugar that can lead to weight gain and other lifestyle diseases….” (YA62, M, 22 years).

Food processing and packaging

A frequently reported criterion for identifying healthy and unhealthy foods was food processing and packaging. Young adults perceived processed and packed food to be devoid of nutrients. They criticised the food processing techniques including freezing, addition of preservatives and other additives in the form of food dyes and flavouring agents (e.g. monosodium glutamate). They also expressed concern regarding the quality of packaging materials used for food packaging.

“…unhealthy snacks are the ones made with lots of food colours and additives just to enhance the flavour…” (YA54, M, 25 years).

“…unhealthy snacks are the ones made with lots of food colours and additives just to enhance the flavour…” (YA54, M, 25 years).

“Even though chips like Lays (Brand name) are made from potatoes, it contains mineral oils and additives as they’re processed… so I don’t think they’re healthy.” (YA45, F, 22 years).

“Even though chips like Lays (Brand name) are made from potatoes, it contains mineral oils and additives as they’re processed… so I don’t think they’re healthy.” (YA45, F, 22 years).

Price

Several young adults often used price to classify whether a food product was healthy or unhealthy. Fruits and organic produce were considered expensive and low-income families usually could not afford them. On the contrary, junk food was regarded as cheap, and the majority of young adults could easily afford junk food in their daily lives. These views are expressed through the quotations below:

“… I believe people think healthy foods, such as organic options, are too expensive, so they often choose unhealthy foods because of budget limitations.” (YA45, F, 22 years).

“… I believe people think healthy foods, such as organic options, are too expensive, so they often choose unhealthy foods because of budget limitations.” (YA45, F, 22 years).

“Umm… because wages are low, many people can’t afford high-protein or low-calorie foods and ahhh… that’s how they see what’s healthy or unhealthy.” (YA12, M, 26 years).

“Umm… because wages are low, many people can’t afford high-protein or low-calorie foods and ahhh… that’s how they see what’s healthy or unhealthy.” (YA12, M, 26 years).

“People with higher salaries… can buy what they want, but poor families can’t afford healthy foods and end up buying junk food instead.” (YA11, F, 21 years).

“People with higher salaries… can buy what they want, but poor families can’t afford healthy foods and end up buying junk food instead.” (YA11, F, 21 years).

Health issues

Some participants categorised food as healthy or unhealthy on the basis of the health impact caused by the consumption of that particular food item. They believed that food items which cause indigestion, inflammation and other health problems were unhealthy. Food items which were considered unhealthy included spicy and oily food as well as sugar substitutes like stevia.

“I think… substance like stevia and other sugar cause inflammation in our body and weaken our immune system. So… ahh… I see those kinds of foods as unhealthy since they can end up causing different diseases.” (YA61, F, 23 years).

“I think… substance like stevia and other sugar cause inflammation in our body and weaken our immune system. So… ahh… I see those kinds of foods as unhealthy since they can end up causing different diseases.” (YA61, F, 23 years).

“…eating too much oily food can lead to health issues like high cholesterol, so I see it as unhealthy.” (YA56, M, 28 years).

“…eating too much oily food can lead to health issues like high cholesterol, so I see it as unhealthy.” (YA56, M, 28 years).

“…because spicy and oily meals or snacks cause gastric issues which lead to indigestion, so those kinds of foods are unhealthy.” (YA20, M, 26 years).

“…because spicy and oily meals or snacks cause gastric issues which lead to indigestion, so those kinds of foods are unhealthy.” (YA20, M, 26 years).

Barriers to healthy eating

Easy availability and accessibility of unhealthy food

The widespread availability and accessibility of unhealthy food in urban settings emerged as a prominent barrier to healthy eating during the interviews. The participants mentioned that unhealthy food was tempting and readily available in many stores thus influencing them to consume such food on a daily basis. In contrast, they highlighted that healthy food was not so easily available in grocery stores and restaurants. Some participants further noted that healthy snack/meal options were rarely available on online food delivery applications making healthy eating quite challenging for them.

“Yeah… So, availability and accessibility are also very possible reasons behind all these things like… healthy food is less but you can find unhealthy things everywhere…” (YA46, M, 26 years).

“Yeah… So, availability and accessibility are also very possible reasons behind all these things like… healthy food is less but you can find unhealthy things everywhere…” (YA46, M, 26 years).

“Usually, you don’t find healthy food in small stores or local grocery shops. Junk food is what’s always easily available…” (YA13, F, 20 years).

“Usually, you don’t find healthy food in small stores or local grocery shops. Junk food is what’s always easily available…” (YA13, F, 20 years).

In line with the widespread ability of energy-dense, nutrient-poor, foods, the interviewees frequently mentioned the ubiquitous marketing of unhealthy food on social media. Many of them liked posting pictures of them consuming junk food in attractive settings (i.e. fancy café/restaurants) to enhance their status and fan following on social media platforms. Some participants noted that this pervasive digital promotion of unhealthy food influenced them to consume junk foods frequently as reflected in the following quotations:

“… these days, I mean, social media ads are, you know, everywhere… and, well, they just make people, like, more tempted to eat junk food.” (YA61, F, 23 years).

“… these days, I mean, social media ads are, you know, everywhere… and, well, they just make people, like, more tempted to eat junk food.” (YA61, F, 23 years).

“Gen Z and younger focus on eating out, café vibes, and sharing it on social media…which influences their food choices.” (YA10, M, 24 years).

“Gen Z and younger focus on eating out, café vibes, and sharing it on social media…which influences their food choices.” (YA10, M, 24 years).

Healthy food is expensive

One of the most frequently cited barriers against healthy eating was the high cost of healthy food compared to unhealthy food. In particular, young adults who did not have regular sources of income noted that they received limited amounts of money from their parents and therefore they could not afford fruits and organic produce on a regular basis. They further highlighted that their poor financial status often compelled them to purchase cheap ultra-processed packaged food. Fruits were only consumed when the young adults were ill to boost their immunity.

“Amm… healthy food is a bit more expensive, right… and junk food is cheaper, so that’s what people usually go for.” (YA65, M, 28 years).

“Amm… healthy food is a bit more expensive, right… and junk food is cheaper, so that’s what people usually go for.” (YA65, M, 28 years).

“The second thing is, uh… people feel organic food is very expensive, so, you know, they don’t really buy it.” (YA45, F, 22 years).

“The second thing is, uh… people feel organic food is very expensive, so, you know, they don’t really buy it.” (YA45, F, 22 years).

Negative peer influence

The negative influence of peers appeared to be a common barrier to healthy eating among young adults. The interviewees mentioned that they often dined more with friends than with their parents. They also noted that many of their peers indulged in unhealthy eating and so they consumed unhealthy food in the company of their friends. Some participants mentioned that if they did not consume unhealthy food with their friends then their friends would be offended. For these participants, peers took precedence over their personal likes as well as their health status.

“People eat more with friends than with family. So, I think all this plays a crucial role in developing unhealthy habits…” (YA8, M, 25 years).

“People eat more with friends than with family. So, I think all this plays a crucial role in developing unhealthy habits…” (YA8, M, 25 years).

“I didn’t really like it (unhealthy food), but because of my friends, I still eat it so they wouldn’t feel bad.” (YA49, F, 19 years).

“I didn’t really like it (unhealthy food), but because of my friends, I still eat it so they wouldn’t feel bad.” (YA49, F, 19 years).

Decline in home cooking

A commonly reported barrier to healthy eating was the decline in home cooking. Our interviewees viewed home cooking as a major source of healthy food. However, they claimed that they could not prepare meals at home because of their hectic work or academic schedules. Some of the participants also mentioned they lacked cooking skills which prevented them from cooking healthy meals at home. They perceived home cooking as a cumbersome, time-consuming activity and so preferred ordering food through online food delivery platforms or they purchased pre-cooked or ready-to-eat meals as it was easier and more convenient than cooking at home.

“You know… cooking takes time and hard work I don’t like it, spending an hour or two cooking. I feel like I’m wasting my time.” (YA46, M, 26 years).

“You know… cooking takes time and hard work I don’t like it, spending an hour or two cooking. I feel like I’m wasting my time.” (YA46, M, 26 years).

“Earlier, people used to learn how to cook, but now priorities are different, and they don’t have cooking skills to prepare healthy meals.” (YA12, M, 26 years).

“Earlier, people used to learn how to cook, but now priorities are different, and they don’t have cooking skills to prepare healthy meals.” (YA12, M, 26 years).

Independent living

Moving away from the parental home and staying independently either in an apartment or a hostel was cited as a deterrent to healthy eating many of the respondents. They acknowledged that food served in the parental home was healthy whereas food served in hostels was not nutritious and it was made with low quality food ingredients. They complained that after consuming hostel food of compromised quality, many of them had fallen ill. They commented that many hostels did not provide any facilities for preparing meals on their premises, preventing them from preparing and consuming healthy meals.

“I think… in Indian families, uh… when we stay with family, the diet is generally healthier.” (YA52, M, 24 years).

“I think… in Indian families, uh… when we stay with family, the diet is generally healthier.” (YA52, M, 24 years).

“Because I live in a hostel, I don’t have facilities to cook personalized meals.” (YA31, F, 23 years).

Most of the participants mentioned that they were unaware of the ongoing government-led public health campaigns including the “Eat Right India” and “Fit India Movement”. They noted that they had heard the names of these public health initiatives for the first time during the interviews and they tried to guess the aims of these initiatives based on their names. Only a handful of participants claimed to have some knowledge about the initiatives. They criticized the limited reach of these initiatives which resulted in poor utilization of them.

Discussion

This formative inquiry provides first-hand information on food classification criteria adopted by urban Indian young adults which could potentially inform the development and implementation of effective behavioural nutrition interventions. The young interviewees used a variety of criteria to judge the healthiness of meals and snacks which included level of food processing, nutrient content, presence of sugar, place and type of food preparation. The inquiry also uncovered several perceived barriers to healthy eating among young people such as the perceived high costs of healthy food, independent living, and decline in household cooking. The rich descriptive data further highlighted that young interviewees possessed inadequate knowledge about the government-led public health campaigns, and they primarily used various social media platforms for navigating health information. Overall, these perspectives complement published evidence on young adults’ food classification criteria [15, 39–41] and barriers to healthy eating [42–45] as well as offer novel insights from the global south.

One of the most frequently and extensively used dimensions to gauge the healthiness of a meal or snack was nutrient composition and food groups. In general, young interviewees perceived fruits and vegetables as healthy food groups because they were rich in important nutrients like vitamins and minerals while red meat was acknowledged as an unhealthy food group because high consumption of red meat was associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease and type two diabetes. These views echo conclusions from both quantitative [40] and qualitative [15] studies that the nutrient content of food impacts young adults’ perceptions of healthiness of food products.

Consistent with past research [15, 39], the study participants also characterized food as healthy or unhealthy based on the type and place of meal preparation. Home cooked meals and snacks were considered more healthier in comparison to meals and snacks prepared or available outside home because the interviewees were not sure about the quality of ingredients used or whether hygienic culinary practices were adopted in meal or snack preparation outside the home environment. This is in agreement with the beliefs of Indian household dietary gatekeepers that food prepared at home was far more healthy, hygienic, and unadulterated compared to meals or snacks available outside the home environment [46]. Several local studies have confirmed poor hygiene and food handling practices in eating establishments (e.g. restaurants, hostels) and among food handlers [47–50], calling for stringent enforcement of food safety laws in retail food establishments in the country.

Meals or snacks prepared using deep-fat frying methods were considered unhealthy because they contained excess oil which resulted in gastrointestinal complications. In line with this, interviewees also reported that Indian snacks were unhealthier when compared to traditional Indian meals. The majority of the Indian snacks are prepared using deep-fat frying methods [51, 52]. Unhealthy snacking is quite common among Indian adolescents [53, 54] as well as adults [55–58] with adverse consequences for human health. This is reflected in a review by Green and colleagues which suggests that a dietary pattern high in sweets and snacks was associated with increased risk of diabetes in Indian consumers compared with a traditional diet rich in rice and pulses [56]. Besides diabetes, increased consumption of both savoury and sweet snacks has been associated with higher risk of obesity among Indian adults [58]. Therefore, there is a need to improve physical and economic access to healthier snacks in educational and workplace settings to curb unhealthy snacking and associated metabolic risks [57, 58].

The study respondents judged the healthiness of meals and snacks based on the level of food processing involved and naturalness of the food products. It is noteworthy that all our young consumers perceived food processing in a negative way, a belief shared by Brazilian [59], German [59], Mexican [39] and Swiss [41] adults. Aligning with previous findings [15, 39, 59], the study participants believed that ultra-processed foods or foods containing additives were unhealthy while natural or minimally processed foods such as fruits and vegetables were healthy. These beliefs mirror the NOVA food classification system (i.e. NOVA Group 1 – Unprocessed or minimally processed foods; NOVA Group 2 – Processed culinary ingredients; NOVA Group 3 – Processed foods; NOVA Group 4 – Ultra-processed foods) [60, 61].These findings enhance the credibility of the NOVA system [41] and support its use in nutrition communication with young consumers [15].

Several overseas studies have documented that both young and older adults use cognitive cues like nutrition labels and health-related claims to infer food healthiness [15, 40, 62]. Paradoxically, none of the study participants evaluated food healthiness based on food labels and health claims. This disparity in views could be attributed to lack of food label literacy since most Indian consumers fail to decipher nutrition information displayed on food labels [63–66]. Food label literacy is an important component of the on-going “Eat Right India” campaign [19]. However, most of the interviewees were ignorant about “Eat Right India” and the “Fit India Movement”, which may be partly responsible for the interviewees’ limited food label literacy. This calls for integrating food label literacy into Indian school curricula and effective communication of government-led health education campaigns to support consumers in making healthier and sustainable food choices [65, 66]. Preferably, Indian schools and tertiary educational institutions should integrate all four domains of the robust food literacy framework (i.e., planning and management; selection; preparation; eating) [67] into their curricula to assist young people in healthy food selection and preparation which could potentially lead to improvements in their diet quality and eventually better health outcomes [68, 69].

An important criterion raised by the interviewees was the amount of sugar present in the meal or snack. Food and beverage products with high sugar content were perceived as unhealthy. Similar to past research [15, 70, 71], the interviewees expressed strong concerns about the ill-effects of added sugar and artificial sweeteners in bakery products and desserts. Despite this awareness, high consumption of sugary food and drinks has been reported among urban Indian adults, predominantly in the younger cohort [8, 72–74]. This could be attributed to a multitude of factors. For example, India is one of the largest sugarcane cultivators globally and therefore sugar forms an integral part of a traditional Indian meal and festive food [74, 75]. Sugarcane farming is highly politicized as current trade policies incentivize Indian farmers to cultivate more sugarcane in comparison to fruits and vegetables [8]. Also, the aggressive marketing of sugary food and beverages by multinationals probably triggers over consumption of these products among Indian youth [75–77]. These factors call for a major policy reset in the country whereby taxing sugary food and drinks could be a viable option [78, 79]. Additionally, public health policies should aim to curtail the ubiquitous marketing of sugary food and drinks. Another feasible policy intervention could be a shift in cropping patterns whereby environmentally sustainable crops like coarse grains and fruits and vegetables are grown more than rice and sugarcane which have large environmental footprints [8].

The interviewees often used price to classify their meals and snacks as healthy or unhealthy. Healthy foods like organic produce and fruits were regarded as expensive whereas ultra-processed and street foods were viewed as cheap foods. Indeed, two cross-sectional surveys conducted in New Delhi and its adjoining areas indicate that Indian consumers perceived organic food to be highly priced when compared to conventionally grown food [80, 81]. In line with this, fruits were also found to be the most expensive food groups in South-East Asia including India when compared to other parts of the world [82, 83]. The high cost of nutritious foods was seen as a major deterrent to healthy eating during the interviews.

Mirroring the views of Turkish [42] and Mexican [43] young adults, our study participants noted that it was challenging for them to purchase fruits because of their high cost and therefore they would only purchase them during times of illness. To overcome this hurdle, the Indian Government should focus on improving the food environment by implementing comprehensive policies to make healthy sustainable foods the convenient, affordable and preferred choice [84, 85]. These could include commodity-specific food vouchers (e.g. fruits, vegetables, legumes, millets), taxes on energy-dense, nutrient-poor food and sugar sweetened beverages, reduced production cost and subsidies on healthy food, marketing and advertising restrictions for energy-dense, nutrient-poor food and sugar sweetened beverages, and easy to comprehend front-of-pack food labels [84, 85].

Another significant deterrent to healthy eating was the decline in home cooking. A major reason for this decline was attributed to lack of basic cooking skills among our participants, a concern also raised by American [44] and Brazilian [86] young adults. The interviewees saw cooking as a cumbersome activity and their busy academic or work schedules did not permit them to do much cooking. Similar challenges were reiterated by Spanish [87] and Chinese [88] young adults. In addition, interviewees who were residing in hostels complained about the lack of facilities to cook their own meals and therefore they were compelled to consume poor quality meals served in the student accommodations, a barrier widely published in the literature [45]. Perhaps, hostels should provide kitchen facilities so that young adults can practice basic cooking skills and prepare healthy meals for themselves [89]. Another viable alternative to inculcate this essential life skill could be through development and implementation of community-based cooking interventions aimed at young adults who want to equip themselves with culinary skills [90]. In fact, three systematic reviews have highlighted that cooking interventions have been successful in improving dietary intake (i.e., increased fruit and vegetable intake), cooking confidence, culinary skills, and socialization [91–93] in adults. These interventions could be conducted in a university setting, student residence or workplace [94, 95] over the weekends or after office hours. Additionally, public health practitioners could also design and implement either a video-based nutrition education program (e.g. cooking videos) [95] or a food skills course with a teaching kitchen [96] for enhancing university students’ culinary skills and dietary intake.

Besides the barriers, some interviewees also reported their experiences of using mobile apps (e.g., Healthify) to monitor their diet and physical activity. Hitherto, mixed reviews have been reported regarding the effectiveness of app-based interventions in improving dietary and physical activity behaviours. For example, the use of calorie counting mobile apps by Indian young adults did not demonstrate any significant change in anthropometry or food consumption [97]. In the same vein, the use of weight-related, self-monitoring applications was found to be associated with increased likelihood of disordered eating among emerging adults in the United States [98]. On the other hand, a Norwegian study showed that young adults using mobile apps were better at maintaining diet and physical activity behaviours than nonusers [99]. Correspondingly, a systematic review confirmed that mHealth interventions had some positive effects on dietary intake, but negligible impact on anthropometric indices in South Asian adolescents and adults [100]. Probably, the use of app-based intervention combined with other behaviour interventions could prove more effective in yielding positive changes in health behaviours [97, 101].

A large proportion of the sample sought health-related information from social media influencers, a finding widely published in the literature [102–105]. A recent systematic review indicates that young adults view digital influencers as both trusted health communicators and potential sources of misinformation [106]. Intriguingly, young adults from the Ajmer district in Rajasthan, India showed more trust on health advice circulated on various social media platforms rather than health messages communicated by the government [104]. This emphasizes the significance of enhancing digital health literacy among young adults [106] as well as integrating government-led health communications into trusted social media platforms [104]. In addition, policymakers need to implement regulatory safeguards to facilitate young people’s correct judgement of health information available on digital platforms [106].

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this research inquiry lies in the novelty of exploring how young adults classify meals and snacks as healthy or unhealthy using in-depth qualitative interviews. It is one of the first research inquiries conducted in India to offer rich insights into the food classification criteria adopted by young adults. The use of a multi-centre approach whereby the sample was recruited from eight Indian metropolitan cities added additional strength to this inquiry by improving the representation and transferability of our findings.

Nonetheless, the emerging findings should be treated within the context of several limitations. Our sample was dominated by educated young adults as 63% of the sample had attained an undergraduate degree and 29% had secured a postgraduate degree which could probably limit the transferability of our results to less educated young adults. Therefore, future studies should replicate this investigation in a less educated population to elicit additional criteria used for classifying meals and snacks. A further limitation is that the present findings may not be generalized to the Indian young adult population at large since this inquiry was focused primarily on an urban sample. This warrants the need to extend this inquiry to rural as well as tribal settings in India. Also, there is a possibility that the data could be biased by our sample recruitment using social media platforms as the young adults who are very active on social media would have been more likely to volunteer for the conversations. Nonetheless, social media-based recruitment is likely to have been more effective in boosting the reach of the study as well as increasing the representativeness of the sample than traditional recruitment methods (e.g., telephone, flyers) [107, 108].

Conclusions

The insights from this inquiry contribute to the limited evidence on food classification criteria by exploring how Indian young adults classify meals and snacks as healthy and unhealthy. On the whole, this study revealed a number of criteria adopted by our sample to classify foods as healthy or unhealthy which included level of food processing, nutrient content, presence of sugar, place and type of food preparation. In addition, the young adults also identified multiple challenges to healthy eating such as the perceived high costs of healthy food, independent living, and the decline in domestic cooking. Young adults had limited awareness of on-going public health campaigns, and they predominantly used social media for seeking diet and health-related information. These unique insights underscore the need to empower young people with nutrition and food literacy as well as digital health literacy which could be achieved by community-based culinary interventions and also integrating government-led health communications into trusted social media platforms.
