Clinical characteristics and causes of non-cardiac pulmonary hypertension in two major tertiary teaching hospitals in Ethiopia: a cross-sectional descriptive study
Authors: Dejuma Yadeta, Amsalu Bekele, Tola Bayisa, Abdusemed Adem, Dufera Mekonnen, Senbeta Guteta, Gashaw Solela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe development of pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a critical event in the prognosis of many cardiopulmonary diseases. Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is elevated pulmonary artery pressure and indicates poor prognosis in many cardiopulmonary diseases [1, 2]. In 2024, the European Respiratory Society redefined PH as a mean pulmonary artery pressure >20 mmHg at rest, measured by right heart catheterization [3]. However, right heart catheterization is often impractical in low-resource settings. Echocardiographic estimates of pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) provide a reliable alternative, with sensitivity of 0.79–1.0 and specificity of 0.60–0.98 for detecting PH [4].

Left-sided acquired and congenital heart diseases (Group 2 PH) are the most common causes of PH. Non-cardiac causes of PH include pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH; Group 1), chronic obstructive and restrictive lung diseases (Group 3), pulmonary thromboembolism (Group 4), and other miscellaneous causes like human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), connective tissue diseases, and schistosomiasis (Group 5) [3].

The key pathophysiological mechanisms of non-cardiac PH include pulmonary vasoconstriction, vascular remodeling, inflammation, thrombosis, and loss of vascular bed. In Group 1 (PAH), genetic mutations, endothelial dysfunction, and smooth muscle proliferation cause progressive narrowing of small pulmonary arteries. In Group 3, chronic lung diseases and hypoxia trigger hypoxic vasoconstriction and fibrotic remodeling. Group 4 involves chronic thromboembolic occlusion of pulmonary arteries, increasing resistance and redistributing blood flow. Group 5 includes complex or unclear mechanisms, such as hematologic or systemic diseases that impair vascular function. These changes lead to increased pulmonary vascular resistance and progressive right ventricular overload and failure [5, 6].

Cor pulmonale, defined as right ventricular (RV) enlargement or dysfunction due to lung disease, is a leading cause of heart failure (HF) in Africa [7]. In Ethiopia, PH constituted 4.1% of cardiovascular diagnoses across six referral hospitals [8], and cor pulmonale accounted for 9% of cardiovascular diseases at Ayder Referral Hospital [9]. Previous reports in Ethiopia found chronic bronchitis, pulmonary tuberculosis (TB), and chronic asthma as major causes of chronic cor pulmonale [10, 11]. Common risk factors for non-cardiac PH, such as pulmonary TB, HIV, asthma, and biomass fuel exposure, are prevalent in Ethiopia [12, 13], with many patients presenting with advanced right-sided HF.

There is a paucity of data with respect to non-cardiac PH in Ethiopia. Hence, this study aimed to describe the clinical characteristics of non-cardiac PH and identify common underlying pathologies by using echocardiography as a means of a noninvasive predictive test for PH.

Methods

Study design, area, and period

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted on patients diagnosed with non-cardiac causes of PH at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital and St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College from January 1, 2017, to June 30, 2022. These two hospitals, the largest referral centres in the country, provide specialized cardiovascular and pulmonary care for patients older than 13 years of age at adult cardiac and chest clinics. All new cardiovascular and pulmonary patients referred to the clinics are evaluated by cardiologists and pulmonologists for diagnosis, initiation, and optimization of treatment. The clinics have standard diagnostic tests like echocardiography, chest X-ray, computed tomography (CT) scan, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), and other diagnostic modalities.

Sample size and sampling

As this was a descriptive study aimed at identifying the clinical characteristics and underlying causes of PH, no formal sample size calculation was performed. Instead, all eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period were consecutively included.

Eligibility criteria

Adult patients aged > 18 years with non-cardiac PH, defined by echocardiographic evidence of high probability of PH and absence of acquired or congenital heart defects explaining the condition, were included in the study.

Operational definitions

High probability of PH was defined according to the 2015 ESC/ERS echocardiographic screening criteria as a peak tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity (TRV) >3.4 m/s regardless of other echocardiographic findings, or TRV >2.8 m/s combined with either a dilated inferior vena cava with reduced collapsibility or dilatation of the right ventricle (RV) or main pulmonary artery (MPA) [14].

COPD was defined by persistent airway obstruction, characterized clinically by chronic cough and confirmed by irreversible airflow limitation on lung function testing (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio < 0.7), consistent with GOLD criteria [15].

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) was diagnosed in patients presenting with progressive dyspnea, cough, hypoxia, and/or impaired lung function, together with diffuse bilateral infiltrates or fibrosis on chest X-ray or CT [16].

Post-TB bronchiectasis/fibrosis was suggested when there was a history or clinical evidence of prior tuberculosis alongside radiologic findings of bronchiectasis or fibrotic changes localized to lung zones previously affected by TB [17].

Asthma was diagnosed in patients demonstrating reversible airway obstruction on spirometry [18].

Pulmonary embolism was confirmed by CT pulmonary angiography [19].

Data collection procedure

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed by qualified adult cardiologists using GE echocardiography machines with full standard function, including tissue Doppler studies to identify participants with PH. Those patients who fulfilled echocardiographic criteria of high probable PH and gave consent for the study were referred to chest clinics for detailed evaluation of socio-demographic and clinical characteristics. Pulmonary function tests were performed by trained nurses and interpreted by pulmonologists. Laboratory tests and imaging data were collected by nurses. The final classification and diagnosis of underlying lung diseases were made by pulmonologists.

Data on known risk factors for PH, like cigarette smoking, cooking methods, history of pulmonary TB, HIV infection, or deep vein thrombosis, were collected. Physical examination was done with a focus on body mass index (BMI), and cardiovascular, respiratory, and musculoskeletal systems.

Nurses trained in the study protocol participated in the interview of patients for consent and collection of socio-demographic data. All data were collected by using a standardized pre-tested questionnaire. The investigators were also involved in the training of data collectors and supervision of data collection.

Data quality control

Quality assurance of the data was made in a two-tier fashion. First, the supervisor checked for the completeness of the data. Incomplete and poor-quality questionnaires were filled again after feedback was given to the data collectors to fill missing data and correct incorrect ones. The second tier was a rejection of the incomplete data for which corrections could not be made.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into SPSS version 23 for descriptive analysis. Prevalence of risk factors and type of underlying lung diseases were calculated as the percentage of patients with the given risk factors and diseases among the total number of study participants with echocardiographic evidence of PH. Continuous variables such as age, hemoglobin values, and MPA diameter were displayed as means for normally distributed data and medians for non-normally distributed data.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 134 patients aged > 18 years with non-cardiac pulmonary hypertension were included in this study. The majority (67.9%) were female. The median age of the participants was 50 years (interquartile range: 38–65). The majority of participants (65.3%) were from Addis Ababa and the urban areas of the surrounding Oromia Region (Table 1). The mean follow-up duration at the two hospitals was 2.5 years. Among the comorbidities, systemic hypertension was observed in 22 patients (16.4%), and diabetes mellitus in 6 patients (4.5%).

Cough, fatigue, and/or peripheral edema were the main presentations (67%). The majority (60.6%) had World Health Organization (WHO) functional class II PH (dyspnea or fatigue at ordinary physical activity), and 44.8% had a history of cough. Nearly one-third (31.2%) had symptoms and signs of right-sided HF at presentation, with bilateral leg edema being the most common finding. Cyanosis was observed in 34% of the patients. Laboratory and imaging findings

The median hemoglobin value was 16 g/dL (interquartile range: 13.1–17.9 g/dL). Approximately one-quarter of participants (22.5%) had polycythemia, defined as a hemoglobin concentration exceeding 16.5 g/dL in females and 18.5 g/dL in males. Pulmonary function testing was done for 69 patients (51.5%) and revealed that 33.6% and 29.1% of the study participants had severe restrictive lung disease and severe obstructive lung disease, respectively. Forty patients (29.9%) underwent electrocardiography; of these 16 (40%) had electrocardiographic signs of right ventricular hypertrophy. On echocardiography, the mean MPA diameter was 32 mm, and severe tricuspid regurgitation was observed in 25 patients (18.7%). Chest CT scans were performed in 78 patients (58%), with lung fibrosis and bronchiectasis as the predominant findings. The utilization of imaging modalities was limited by clinical priorities and resource constraints.

Risk factors for non-cardiac pulmonary hypertension

Of the 134 participants, 99 (73.9%) used biomass fuels (wood and/or coal) for cooking, and 60 (44.8%) were primarily responsible for household cooking. Thirty-five (26.1%) had a history of pulmonary TB, and 17 (12.7%) reported a history of deep vein thrombosis. Cigarette smoking was uncommon, with 10 participants (7.5%) identified as smokers. Six participants (4.5%) were HIV-positive, all of whom were receiving antiretroviral therapy. The risk factors for non-cardiac pulmonary hypertension are summarized in Table 2.

Underlying pulmonary pathologies

Post-TB pulmonary sequelae were the most common diagnoses, accounting for 22.3% of the study participants. The two main pulmonary complications of TB were post-TB lung fibrosis in 17 patients (12.7%) and post-TB bronchiectasis in 12 patients (9%). Of those participants who recalled the methods of diagnosis of TB, 52% reported that the diagnosis of pulmonary TB was made with positive sputum examination. The second most frequent pulmonary pathology identified was interstitial lung disease with or without pulmonary fibrosis (ILD/PF), found in 23 participants (17.2%). ILD/PF was more commonly diagnosed in women than men and was also more frequent in those who used biomass fuels for cooking compared to those who did not. The third most common diagnosis was chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diagnosed in 19 patients (14.2%). Chronic persistent asthma was found in 15 patients (11.2%), with 6 of these 15 patients (40%) having admissions for severe exacerbations. Among patients who treated as having bronchial asthma, 43% had non-reversible obstructive lung disease. Furosemide was prescribed to approximately one-third of patients with decompensated right-sided HF, while spironolactone was given to nearly one-fifth of them. Discussion

This study, the first of its kind in Ethiopia, revealed that post-TB pulmonary sequelae remained the most common non-cardiac cause of PH. It also showed a higher prevalence of PH in women than men, as well as an increasing proportion of ILD and pulmonary thromboembolism compared to a previous study conducted two decades ago at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital [11]. These trends could be due to better diagnostic tools such as CT scans and Doppler ultrasound, which have been more readily available in routine clinical practice than before. The higher prevalence of thromboembolism could also be explained by the fact that most patients were from the main referral hospitals, where more accurate diagnoses were made, and the patients had a higher prevalence of risk factors for thromboembolism.

The majority of patients in this study presented with advanced stages of PH with decompensated right-sided HF. This may be due to less health-seeking behavior of the patients or late diagnosis of PH due to the nonspecific early symptoms and lack of diagnostic tests at lower healthcare levels. This has also been demonstrated in another study where the diagnosis of PH was made at a far advanced stage of the disease [20]. Advanced lung disease was also evident, based on spirometry, where close to three-quarters of the patients had moderately and severely reduced vital capacity. These findings highlight that patients typically seek medical care at a late stage, by which time significant parenchymal lung damage has already developed.

Our study also looked at possible risk factors for PH. The most common risk factors identified included use of biomass fuel, previous history of pulmonary TB, and a history of bronchial asthma. Compared to developed countries, where cigarette smoking is the most common risk factor, the prevalence of smoking in our study was close to the prevalence in the general population (5%) [21].

About two-thirds of the women who participated in the study used biomass fuel for cooking and were primarily responsible for this activity, with an average cooking duration of 8.4 years. Indoor air pollution from biomass fuel use has been associated with various health problems, notably cardiovascular abnormalities [22]. Previous studies have also linked indoor air pollution to a higher prevalence of TB, ILD, bronchial asthma, and lung cancer [23–27]. In our study, biomass fuel use and indoor air pollution are reported as an observational co-occurrence with non-cardiac pulmonary hypertension rather than a proven contributing factor.

Post-TB lung sequelae have been shown to be significant causes of PH. The risk of developing PH with pulmonary TB increases with the extent of lung damage. Current thought also includes damage to the pulmonary vasculature with subsequent pulmonary thrombosis and fibrosis resulting in PH [28–30].

Among patients treated as having bronchial asthma, 43% had non-reversible obstructive lung disease, suggesting COPD. Even though non-reversibility with bronchodilators does not rule out bronchial asthma, given the older mean age of the participants, COPD is a likely diagnosis in these patients. Given the important therapeutic implications, every attempt should be made to differentiate between the two using validated COPD scoring systems and spirometry [31].

Our study has some limitations. First, the lack of right heart catheterization, which is the gold standard for diagnosing pulmonary hypertension, limited the certainty of our diagnosis. Second, because this was a descriptive study, we were unable to prove statistical associations between various risk factors and non-cardiac PH. Third, since data were collected from two major tertiary referral hospitals, the findings may overrepresent severe cases of pulmonary hypertension and may not accurately reflect the broader population-level distribution. Finally, incomplete imaging may also introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

A history of pulmonary TB and use of biomass fuels for cooking were the two most common risk factors reported in patients with non-cardiac PH at two major tertiary teaching hospitals in Ethiopia. These findings highlight the need for further research to explore causal relationships and inform targeted public health interventions. The absence of right heart catheterization, the gold standard for confirming PH, was a key limitation of this study.
