Zubarev response approach to polarization phenomena in local equilibrium
Youyu Li, Shuai Y. F. Liu

Abstract
Using the expansion of Zubarev’s density operator, we develop a linear response approach to study various spin physics in a locally equilibrated medium, particularly focusing on various polarization phenomena in heavy-ion collisions. Specifically, we connect familiar correlation functions and diagrammatic methods to the Zubarev formalism, enabling the use of established techniques like the Matsubara/imaginary time formalism to facilitate calculations. For a spin-1/2 particle, we re-derive its vector polarization using this Zubarev response approach, which exactly reproduces with our previous results based on Luttinger’s method. For a spin-1 particle, we calculate the vector polarization and find the expected contributions from vorticity, temperature gradients, and shear, which are identical to those for spin-1/2 particles except for a factor of 4/3 as expected. For the tensor polarization and spin alignment of a spin-1 boson, we explicitly prove that the non-dissipative contribution is zero at leading order in gradients, and briefly reiterate our previous findings for the dissipative contribution with further discussions on several concerns. Additionally, we discuss several relevant subtleties and questions, including an alternative derivation for Zubarev response approach, the covariance issues of different spin density matrix definitions, a further explanation of slow and fast modes, the mode selection scheme, etc. We also discuss skeleton expansions, higher-order contributions, and non-perturbative methods, particularly their potential connection to lattice field theory. In summary, this work discusses the foundations and subtleties of Zubarev response approach, with specific examples from spin physics in heavy-ion collisions.

II
Zubarev response approach

For completeness, we briefly review Zubarev’s formalism following two references
[
57
,
64
]
for non-equilibrium physics. This formalism is primarily used to construct non-equilibrium transport equations. However, in this work, instead of deriving transport equations, we focus on developing the linear response approach based on Zubarev’s density operator for a locally equilibrated hydrodynamic system, where we assume small flow velocities (in the rest frame of the medium) and small flow gradients.

The core of Zubarev’s formalism is to construct a stationary density matrix in the Heisenberg picture that satisfies
d\rho/dt=0
, but in terms of time-dependent dynamical parameters. In the hydrodynamic scenario discussed here, we choose these parameters as
\beta^{\mu}(x)=\beta(x)u^{\mu}(x)
, where
\beta(x)
represents the local inverse temperature at a given spacetime point
x=(t,\bm{x})
, and
u^{\mu}(x)
is the flow velocity of the medium. Based on the maximum information entropy
-\text{Tr}[\rho\ln\rho]
for a density operator
\rho
with constraint
\langle T^{0\nu}\rangle=\text{Tr}[\rho T^{0\nu}]

[
57
,
11
]
, we obtain a local equilibrium density operator of the form
\propto\exp[-\int d^{3}x\,\beta_{\mu}T^{0\mu}]
, which is an intuitive extension of the global equilibrium density operator
\propto\exp[-\beta H]
. However, it is straightforward to see that
d\rho/dt\neq 0
, meaning that it does not represent a physical density operator in the Heisenberg picture. To make it stationary, we define a time-averaged operator of the exponent
\beta_{\mu}T^{0\mu}

[
57
,
64
]
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(t,\bm{x})=\epsilon\int^{t}_{-\infty}dt^{\prime}e^{-\epsilon(t-t^{\prime})}\beta^{\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})T_{0\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})

(1)

where the derivatives
d\mathcal{B}(t,\bm{x})/dt=0
in small
\epsilon
limit. Meanwhile, after performing a partial integration over time, a more useful expression is obtained as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(t,\bm{x})=\beta^{\gamma}(t,\bm{x})T_{0\gamma}(t,\bm{x})-\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{-\epsilon(t-t^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\times\left[\partial^{0}\beta^{\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})T_{0\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})+\beta^{\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})\partial^{0}T_{0\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})\right]

(2)

The first term represents the expected local equilibrium density operator, which is at the same time
t
with our operators at a given
t
. Therefore, there is no time evolution or dynamics when using the first term to trace the operator at a given
t
, resulting in no dissipation in this term. On the other hand, all dynamics are contained in the second term, where dissipative contributions can be found. The
e^{-\epsilon(t-t^{\prime})}
term becomes negligible if all quantities converge to zero properly in the infinite past. To simplify the notation, we make the
e^{-\epsilon(t-t^{\prime})}
implicit; however, whenever convergence issues arise, we will reintroduce it. Considering the equation of motion for energy momentum tensor
\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0
is
\partial^{0}T_{0\nu}=-\partial^{i}T_{i\nu}
, we can further simplify the expression as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(t,\bm{x})=

\displaystyle\beta^{\gamma}(t,\bm{x})T_{0\gamma}(t,\bm{x})-\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\big[\partial^{\lambda}\beta^{\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})T_{\lambda\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})

\displaystyle-\partial^{i}\big(\beta^{\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})T_{i\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x})\big)\big]

(3)

For the density operator
\rho=Z^{-1}\exp[-\int d^{3}\bm{x}\mathcal{B}(t,\bm{x})]
, with
Z=\text{Tr}\{\exp[...]\}
, the last term is just a surface integral that is typically assumed to be zero. Additionally, to make the “covariance” structure of the theory more apparent, we specify the hypersurface
d\Sigma^{\mu}=(dxdydz,\bm{0})
and use
u^{\mu}=(1,\bm{0})
as the frame vector for the medium rest frame. With all this, the density operator can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\rho=

\displaystyle Z^{-1}\exp\Big[-[\int d\Sigma^{\lambda}\beta^{\gamma}(x)T_{\lambda\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle-\int^{t}_{-\infty}d^{4}x^{\prime}\partial^{\lambda}\beta^{\gamma}(x^{\prime})T_{\lambda\gamma}(x^{\prime})]\Big]

(4)

The first term in the exponent represents the local equilibrium part, while the second term characterizes the non-equilibrium dynamical properties. Different choices for the frame vector and hypersurface
[
17
,
19
]
lead to different results. In this work, we do not explore these alternatives but instead select the “almost” medium rest frame for the subsequent calculations, in which the flow vector is chosen as
u(x)=(1,\bm{u}(x))
, where
\bm{u}
represents the infinitesimally small three components of the flow vector. We use
\bm{u}
to generate the gradients and then set it to zero. For simplicity, we also use the same shorthand
u^{\mu}
for
u^{\mu}(x)=(1,\bm{u}(x))
and
u^{\mu}=(1,\bm{0})
, where the distinction can be inferred from the context.

With this set up, we will expand every thing into linear order and re-express Eq. (
4
) in a more tractable form as

\displaystyle\rho=Z^{-1}\exp[-\beta(H+\tilde{H})]\;\;,\tilde{H}\equiv\tilde{H}_{\text{nd}}+\tilde{H}_{\text{d}}

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{nd}}=\frac{1}{\beta}\int d^{3}\bm{x}\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(x)T^{0\gamma}(x)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{d}}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\int^{t}_{-\infty}dt^{\prime}\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\gamma}(x^{\prime})T^{\lambda\gamma}(x^{\prime})

(5)

with
\tilde{\beta}^{\mu}(x)=(\tilde{\beta}(x),\beta\textbf{u}(x))
,
\beta(x)=\beta+\tilde{\beta}(x)
.
Z=\text{Tr}\{\exp[-\beta(H+\tilde{H}_{\rm nd}+\tilde{H}_{\rm d})]\}
.
The
\beta
is a constant inverse temperature
1/T
and
\tilde{\beta}(x)
is a small term deviated from
\beta
, and thus we have
\partial^{\mu}\tilde{\beta}^{\nu}=\partial^{\mu}\beta^{\nu}
at leading order of gradients. We will directly apply the identity derived by Luttinger
[
56
]
(also see
[
64
]
(a sign error in the paper)) to expand the exponential operators for small
\tilde{H}_{\text{nd}}
and
\tilde{H}_{\text{d}}
into linear order terms

\displaystyle e^{-\beta(H+\tilde{H})}=(1-\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau e^{-\tau H}(\tilde{H}_{\rm nd}+\tilde{H}_{\rm d})e^{H\tau})e^{-\beta H}+...

\displaystyle\rho=(1-\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau e^{-\tau H}\tilde{H}e^{H\tau}+\beta\langle\tilde{H}\rangle)\rho_{G}+o(\tilde{H})

(6)

in which the
\rho_{G}=e^{-\beta H}/Z_{0}
with
Z_{0}=\text{Tr}[e^{-\beta H}]
and

\langle..\rangle\equiv\text{Tr}\{...\rho_{G}\}=\text{Tr}\{...e^{-\beta H}\}/Z_{0}
. The last term in the bracket is the term comes from the denominator(trace), which will create cancellation between disconnected diagrams.
The same formula can be obtained by using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula to expand the exponential in Eq. (
5
), when one carefully recombines the linear-order terms.
In this work, we focus on the linear order in gradients, with the study of higher-order effects deferred to future studies.
Meanwhile, we will first calculate everything in the medium rest frame. Then, we will extrapolate the results to an arbitrary frame by assuming the covariance of the final expression, which is the standard procedure employed in the literature
[
65
,
66
,
17
]
.

Non-dissipative part

We will first study the non-dissipative part originating from
\tilde{H}_{\rm nd}
in Eq. (
6
) for a generic operator
O^{a}(t)
with generic index
a
, where the deviation of its average value from global equilibrium is denoted as
\delta O^{a}(t)\equiv\text{Tr}[O^{a}(\rho-\rho_{G})]

There is a key identity for later manipulation

\displaystyle T^{0\gamma}(t,\bm{x})=\int^{t}_{-\infty}dt_{o}\partial_{t_{o}}T^{0\gamma}(t_{o},\bm{x})+T^{0\gamma}(-\infty,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{-\infty}dt_{o}\,i[H,T^{0\gamma}(t_{o},\bm{x})]+T^{0\gamma}(-\infty,\bm{x})\,.

(7)

If we regulate

\delta O^{a}
as

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm nd}(t)=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\bm{x}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(x)}{\beta}\langle-O^{a}(t)\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau e^{-H\tau}T^{0\gamma}(x)e^{H\tau}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\bm{x}\int^{t}_{-\infty}dt_{o}\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(x)}{\beta}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\times\langle O^{a}(t)e^{-H\tau}i[T^{0\gamma}(t_{o},\bm{x}),H]e^{H\tau}\rangle

(8)

With the same procedure in Eq. (
8
), it is not hard to show
\langle\tilde{H}\rangle
vanish with regulation
T^{0\gamma}(-\infty,\bm{x})=0
. Therefore, there is no trace correction and correction of the disconnected diagrams using this regulation. For those do not satisfy this regulation, in Sec.
II.4
, we provide an alternative derivation for Zubarev response approach with more considerations on this boundary term. In short, this term will not affect our calculations later.

Then, in Eq. (
8
), we can integrate over
\tau
first, rather than
t
, where the identity
\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau e^{-H\tau}[X,H]e^{H\tau}=\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau\partial_{\tau}(e^{-H\tau}X(t_{o})e^{H\tau})
for a generic operator
X
solves the integral. Meanwhile, we can obtain the relation
\langle O^{a}(t)e^{-\beta H}\tilde{H}_{\rm nd}(t)e^{\beta H}\rangle\equiv\langle\tilde{H}_{\rm nd}(t)O^{a}(t)\rangle
by noticing the
e^{-\beta H}
inside
\langle..\rangle
and moving the
O^{a}
around using the trace property. With all these steps, we can finally obtain a compact expression for
\delta O^{a}(t)
.

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm nd}(t)=\int d^{3}\bm{x}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(x)}{\beta}(-i)\langle O^{a}(t)\int^{t}_{-\infty}dt_{o}\big(T^{0\gamma}(t_{o},\bm{x})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-e^{-\beta H}T^{0\gamma}(t_{o},\bm{x})e^{\beta H}\big)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}\bm{x}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(x)}{\beta}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dt_{o}(-i)\theta(t-t_{o})\langle[O^{a}(t),T^{0\gamma}(t_{o},\bm{x})]\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\int d^{3}\bm{x}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(x)}{\beta}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dt_{o}u_{\lambda}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(t-t_{o},\bm{x})

(9)

Following this convention,
the retarded Green function for generic operators
X^{a}
and
Y^{b}
typically denoted as
G^{ab}_{R}\equiv-i\theta(t-t_{o})\langle[X^{a},Y^{b}]\rangle
.
In Eq.(
9
), we should note that the time variable in
\tilde{\beta}(x)
is
x=(t,\bm{x})
, which differs from the time variable in the operator
T^{0\gamma}(t_{o},\bm{x})
. Additionally, as should be mentioned, Eq.(
9
) can also be derived from the typical linear response with adiabatic switching of the interactions
[
67
]
. However, to obtain Eq. (
9
), we need to treat the

in
\tilde{\beta}(t,\bm{x})
as an external parameter, rather than as a dynamical time during the switching process.

This retarded Green function can then be expressed using the spectral functions as follows:

\displaystyle G_{R}^{a\lambda\gamma}(\omega,\bm{x})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega^{\prime}\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{-\text{Im}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega^{\prime},\bm{x})}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}+i\epsilon}

\displaystyle G_{R}^{a\lambda\gamma}(t-t_{o},\bm{x})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega(t-t_{o})}G_{R}^{a\lambda\gamma}(\omega,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=i\theta(t-t_{o})\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}(t-t_{o})}2\text{Im}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega^{\prime},\bm{x})

(10)

The last line uses residue theory to carry out the integral over
\omega
. Using the second line of Eq.(
10
) and Eq.(
9
) and integrating over
t_{o}
to create a
\delta(\omega)
, the non-dissipative contribution
\delta O_{\rm nd}(t)
becomes:

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm nd}(t)=\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\int d^{3}\bm{x}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(t,\bm{x})}{\beta}u_{\lambda}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega,\bm{x})

(11)

If both

and
\tilde{H}_{\rm nd}
are Hermitian operators, the limit of

is just equal to the real part of it, i.e.,
\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\text{Re}G^{\lambda\nu}_{R}(\omega,\bm{x})
, and the imaginary part is zero. In time-reversal processes, although the retarded Green function

will become the advanced Green function

, their real parts are equal,
\text{Re}G_{R}=\text{Re}G_{A}
. Therefore, it is time-reversal even in this sense. As we should expect, the local equilibrium part of the density operator with

has the time variable

, which is at the same time

in the operator

. Therefore, there is no time evolution between two operators, and it is equivalent to evaluating a static operator average in an ensemble, so that no dissipation linked to dynamics exists. The results in terms of the Green function are consistent with this intuitive expectation. In this sense, the time evolution in terms of

in the Green functions is auxiliary and just a trick for doing operator averages. This is also the reason we call it operator time

to distinguish it from the dynamical time

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm d}(t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dt^{\prime}\theta(t-t^{\prime})\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\gamma}(x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\lim_{\omega^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}2\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{\prime}}\text{Im}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm d}(t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\gamma}(t,\bm{x}^{\prime})\lim_{\omega^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{\prime}}[\text{Im}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime})]

II.3
Analytical continuation, Euclidean time and lattice QFT

The discussion in the previous section connects the Zubarev formalism to the retarded Green functions. As the correlation function only depends on two time variables, with translation symmetry in the time direction, its analytical structure is quite simple and can be related to, or more exactly, analytically continued to Euclidean/imaginary time space. Then, we can either use the diagrammatic technique to perform perturbation theory or employ non-perturbative methods such as the Dyson-Schwinger equation
[
60
]
, functional renormalization group
[
61
]
, or lattice QCD to evaluate the correlation function in Euclidean time at first. With the results in Euclidean space, we perform the analytical continuation back to real time for the final results.

Using the Kallen-Lehmann representation and splitting the diagonal and off-diagonal parts, we have the matrix elements of operators as
\langle m|X|n\rangle=X_{mn}+X_{m}\delta_{mn}
,
\langle m|Y|n\rangle=Y_{mn}+Y_{m}\delta_{mn}
for two bosonic operators.
For the time-dependent operator
X(t)=e^{iHt}Xe^{-iHt}
, we can express the correlation functions as

\displaystyle\langle X(t)Y(0)\rangle=Z^{-1}_{0}e^{-\beta E_{n}}X_{nm}Y_{mn}e^{-i(E_{m}-E_{n})t}+\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\langle Y(0)X(t)\rangle=Z^{-1}_{0}e^{-\beta E_{m}}Y_{mn}X_{nm}e^{-i(E_{m}-E_{n})t}+\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\sum_{m}[\rho_{G}]_{m}X_{m}Y_{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}=\langle X\rangle\langle Y\rangle\;\text{some cases}

\displaystyle\langle[X(t),Y(0)]\rangle=\int d\omega A(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}

(16)

where we will briefly explain “some cases” in Sec.
II.4
. The spectral function in Eq. (
16
) is defined to be

\displaystyle A(\omega)=

\displaystyle Z_{0}^{-1}\sum_{m,n}(e^{-\beta E_{n}}-e^{-\beta E_{m}})X_{nm}Y_{mn}

\displaystyle\times\delta(\omega-(E_{m}-E_{n}))

(17)

For retarded Green function, it will be

\displaystyle G_{R}(t)=-i\theta(t)\langle[X(t),Y(0)]\rangle\equiv-i\theta(t)\int d\omega A(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}

\displaystyle G_{R}(\omega)\equiv\int dte^{i\omega t}G(t)=\int d\omega^{\prime}\frac{A(\omega^{\prime})}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}+i\epsilon}

(18)

With Eq. (
18
), according to our convention the imaginary part of
G_{R}
and the spectral functions are related as

\displaystyle A(\omega)=\frac{-\text{Im }G_{R}(\omega)}{\pi}

(19)

For imaginary time operators and correlation functions, we have
X(\tau)=e^{H\tau}Xe^{-H\tau}
and
G(\tau)=-\langle T_{\tau}[X(\tau)Y(0)]\rangle=-\langle X(\tau)Y(0)\rangle
within
\tau\in[0,\beta]
, which can be represented in spectral representation as

\displaystyle G(\tau)

\displaystyle=-Z_{0}^{-1}e^{-\beta E_{n}}X_{nm}Y_{mn}e^{-(E_{m}-E_{n})\tau}-\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle G(i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau e^{i\omega_{n}\tau}G(\tau)

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}_{0}\frac{(e^{-\beta E_{l}}-e^{-\beta E_{m}})(X_{lm}Y_{ml})}{i\omega_{n}-(E_{m}-E_{l})}-\beta\mathcal{C}\delta_{n0}

\displaystyle\equiv\int d\omega^{\prime}\frac{A(\omega^{\prime})}{i\omega_{n}-\omega^{\prime}}-\beta\mathcal{C}\delta_{n0}

(20)

where the
i\omega_{n}=2n\pi/\beta
(bosonic) is the Matsubara frequency.
Comparing the last line of Eq. (
20
) and Eq. (
18
), we can identify that analytical part of
G_{R}(\omega)
and
G(i\omega_{n})
will have the relation

\displaystyle G_{R}(\omega)=G(i\omega_{n})|_{i\omega_{n}\rightarrow\omega+i\epsilon}

(21)

with non-analytical part
\mathcal{C}
neglected.

This formula in Eq. (
21
) is mostly used for the case that the analytical formula is known.
For numerical continuation, the results are discussed later. Making it more specific for the
T^{\mu\nu}
and
O^{a}
operators, we define the Euclidean Green function
G^{\mu\nu}
as

\displaystyle G^{a\mu\nu}(\tau,\bm{x})=(-)\langle T_{\tau}[O^{a}(\tau)T^{\mu\nu}(0,\bm{x})]\rangle\,.

(22)

With this Euclidean-time Green function, the non-dissipative/local equilibrium part of the contribution, i.e., Eq. (
9
), becomes

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm nd}(t)=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}\bm{x}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(x)}{\beta}\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau\,u_{\lambda}\big[G^{a\lambda\gamma}(\tau,\bm{x})

\displaystyle+\langle O^{a}\rangle\langle T^{\lambda\gamma}(\bm{x})\rangle\big]

(23)

The last term is originated from the trace correction at Eq. (
6
).
As shown, for the non-dissipative contribution, everything is well-defined in Euclidean space. As already demonstrated in many non-perturbative approaches, such as the DSE , FRG , and lattice QFT, calculations in Euclidean space are much simpler and more stable compared to real-time calculations. This gives us a good chance to study the related response using non-perturbative methods. Especially, with lattice QCD, a wide range of observables can be calculated from first principles.

For the dissipative contribution, a direct real-time calculation or analytical continuation is unavoidable since it involves the true dynamics in non-equilibrium states. It is well known that the Euclidean-time Green function can be connected to the real-time/frequency spectral function as (see Ref.
[
62
]
and references therein):

\displaystyle G(\tau)=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n}e^{-i\omega_{n}\tau}G^{\mu\nu}(i\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\omega\frac{e^{\beta\omega}e^{-\omega\tau}}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}\frac{-1}{\pi}\text{Im}G_{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle\equiv-\int^{\infty}_{0}d\omega\frac{\cosh[(\tau-\beta/2)\omega]}{\sinh[\beta\omega/2]}(\frac{-1}{\pi})\text{Im}G_{R}(\omega)

(24)

Mathematically, it is an integral equation that can be solved by taking the inverse of the kernel
K(\omega,\tau)=\cosh[(\tau-\beta/2)\omega]/\sinh[(\beta/2)\omega]
, reconstructing
\text{Im}G^{\mu\nu}_{R}(\omega)=\pi\int d\tau K^{-1}(\omega,\tau)G(\tau)
. However, in practice, the properties of
K^{-1}
are highly problematic. In the diagonal basis, the kernel contains eigenvalues close to zero
[
68
]
, which means its inverse will be close to infinite. This simply implies that small errors in some specific modes will be infinitely enlarged when using
K^{-1}
to map
G(\tau)
to reconstruct
\text{Im}G_{R}(\omega)
. This basically states that the inverse/analytical continuation back to real time is fundamentally difficult. Nevertheless, with some ansatz, we can still extract useful information from this reconstruction. For this approach, we refer to a most recent attempt for such a procedure for the heavy-quark diffusion coefficient
[
69
]
, where one can gain insight into how these methods work by reading the paper and the references cited therein.

II.4
Alternative derivation to Zubarev response approach

As shown in Eq. (
6

G(\tau-\tau^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})=-\langle T_{\tau}[X(\tau,t)Y(\tau^{\prime},t^{\prime})]\rangle

\displaystyle G(\tau-\tau^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega_{n}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}G(i\omega_{n},\omega)

\displaystyle G(i\omega_{n},\omega)=2\pi[\frac{A(\omega)}{i\omega_{n}-\omega}-\beta\mathcal{C}\delta(\omega)\delta_{n0}]

The spectral function
A(\omega)
above is defined in Eq. (
17
) and Eq. (

With the relations in Eq. (

\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau
of the

\displaystyle-\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau\langle X(0,t)Y(-\tau,t^{\prime})\rangle=\int^{\beta}_{0}d\tau G(\tau,t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int d\omega\frac{A(\omega)}{-\omega}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}-\beta\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{2\text{Im}G_{R}(\omega)}{\omega}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}-\beta\mathcal{C}

(26)

Inserting
\tilde{H}_{\text{nd}}
from Eq. (
5
) and
O^{a}
into Eq. (

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm nd}(t)

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}\bm{x}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(t,\bm{x})}{\beta}u_{\lambda}\int d\omega\frac{1}{-\omega}\frac{-\text{Im}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega,\bm{x})}{\pi}

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\int d^{3}\bm{x}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(t,\bm{x})}{\beta}u_{\lambda}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega,\bm{x})

(27)

The second line is obtained with the help of Eq. (
18
) and Eq. (
19
). The static correlation term
-\mathcal{C}+\langle O^{a}\rangle\langle\tilde{H}_{\rm nd}\rangle
in the second line is neglected temporarily for the last line, and we will discuss how it contributes in the end.
Using Eq. (
26
), Eq. (
5
) and Eq. (
6
), and following a similar procedure, the dissipative part can be expressed as

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm d}(t)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\int^{t}_{-\infty}dt^{\prime}\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\gamma}(x^{\prime})

(28)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}e^{-i\omega^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})}\frac{2\text{Im}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\gamma}(t,\bm{x}^{\prime})\lim_{\omega^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega^{\prime}}[\text{Im}G^{a\lambda\gamma}_{R}(\omega^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime})]

The only remaining term to be evaluated is the static correlation term that is typically neglected or vanish. To proceed, we will use an alternative expression for Eq. (
1
) rather than Eq. (
3
), which is

\displaystyle\tilde{H}=\frac{1}{t-t_{0}}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}dt^{\prime}\frac{1}{\beta}\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(x^{\prime})T^{0\gamma}(x^{\prime})

(29)

where we switch from Abel regularization
\epsilon\int^{t}_{-\infty}dt^{\prime}e^{-\epsilon(t-t^{\prime})}

to a more intuitive time-average regularization (note

Then, we denote the static correlation of the

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{C}}^{a\lambda\gamma}(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\beta\langle O^{a}\rangle\langle T^{\lambda\gamma}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\rangle-\beta\mathcal{C}^{a\lambda\gamma}(\bm{x}^{\prime})

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{a\lambda\gamma}(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{m}[\rho_{G}]_{m}[O^{a}]_{m}[T^{\lambda\gamma}(\bm{x}^{\prime})]_{m}\,.

(31)

With this static correlation, we will have an additional contribution to the operator average
\delta O^{a}_{\rm cs}(t)

\displaystyle\delta O^{a}_{\rm cs}(t)=\frac{1}{t-t_{0}}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}dt^{\prime}d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(t^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\beta}u_{\lambda}G_{\mathcal{C}}^{a\lambda\gamma}(\bm{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\frac{\overline{[\tilde{\beta}]}_{\gamma}(t,\bm{x}^{\prime})}{\beta}u_{\lambda}G_{\mathcal{C}}^{a\lambda\gamma}(\bm{x}^{\prime})

Starting from this section, we will apply the Zubarev response approach discussed in the previous section to polarization phenomena in local equilibrium. The first example, as discussed in this section, is the vector polarization for spin-1/2 particles.

The polarization for spin-1/2 particles is related to the famous
\Lambda

[

]
. One major progress in understanding this puzzle is the discovery of shear-induced polarization

18

20

19

[

[

III.1
Main formalism

We begin the derivation by presenting the energy-momentum tensor
[
17
]
of the spin-1/2 free fermion as follows:

\displaystyle T^{\lambda\gamma}=\frac{i}{4}\bar{\psi}(\gamma^{\lambda}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial^{\gamma}}+\gamma^{\gamma}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial^{\lambda}})\psi

f_{2}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\partial_{\mu}}f_{1}=f_{2}(\partial_{\mu}f_{1})-(\partial_{\mu}f_{2})f_{1}

[
11
]
in this work.
The operator we will calculate is

Its Wigner form

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{5}^{\mu}(t,\bm{x},\bm{p})=\varepsilon_{\bm{p}}\int d^{3}\bm{y}e^{-i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{y}}\bar{\psi}\left(t,\bm{x}_{-}\right)\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\psi\left(t,\bm{x}_{+}\right)

For Eq. (

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt\times e^{i\bm{p}\cdot\bm{y}}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})}G_{R}^{\mu\lambda\gamma}(\omega,\bm{p},\bm{q})\,

\displaystyle\equiv-i\theta(t-t_{o})\big\langle[\bar{\psi}\left(t,\bm{x}_{-}\right)\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\psi\left(t,\bm{x}_{+}\right),T^{\lambda\gamma}(t_{o},\bm{x}^{\prime})]\big\rangle

With these replacements, we can re-express the Eq. (

\displaystyle=\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\frac{\varepsilon_{\bm{p}}}{\beta}\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\frac{d^{3}\bm{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}G_{R}^{\mu 0\gamma}(\omega,\bm{p},\bm{q})e^{i\bm{q}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})}\tilde{\beta}_{\gamma}(t,\bm{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left[\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}(k_{\rho}\gamma^{\rho}+m)\gamma^{\xi}(k^{\prime}_{\sigma}\gamma^{\sigma}+m)\right]=k_{\rho}k_{\sigma}^{\prime}(-4i\varepsilon^{\rho\xi\sigma\mu})\,.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{\perp}_{\xi}}(q^{\perp}_{\sigma}+(q\cdot u)u_{\sigma})=\bar{\Delta}^{\xi}_{\sigma}-\frac{p_{\perp}^{\xi}}{\varepsilon_{u}}u_{\sigma}

III.2
Mode selection: slow and fast modes

When we evaluate the integral over
k_{0}
and
k^{\prime}_{0}
in Eq. (
44
) for the full
J^{\mu}_{5}
, we will find that it contains four modes, corresponding to the four “
\pm
” combinations of
(s_{k_{0}},s_{k^{\prime}_{0}})
in particle-antiparticle. We will call them particle density mode (pp) (positive, positive), antiparticle density mode for the
-p
direction (nn) (negative, negative), particle-antiparticle axial vector meson mode (incoming) (pn), and antiparticle-particle anti-axial vector meson (outgoing) mode (np).

The previous two modes have poles around
\tilde{\omega}\sim 0^{+}
or
0^{-}
, so they are slow modes or zero modes. Meanwhile, the axial meson modes have poles around
\tilde{\omega}\approx 2\varepsilon_{\bm{p}}
, which are fast modes and can be regarded as virtual axial vector mesons close to the thresholds.

Both slow and fast modes will survive in the slow limits, where we take
\tilde{\omega}\rightarrow 0
before taking
\bm{q}\rightarrow 0
, although there is a factor of
T/\varepsilon_{\bm{p}}
suppression since it is away from the pole. In the fast limit,
\bm{q}\rightarrow 0
is taken first before
\tilde{\omega}\rightarrow 0
, which makes the slow modes vanish but allows the fast modes to survive as expected. Therefore, if we use the slow limits minus the fast limits, i.e., the “slow minus fast” scheme as discussed in Ref.
[
17
]
, we effectively select the slow modes corresponding to the modes of particle/antiparticle polarization density, which are the modes related to the final observables.

As discussed, the correct modes for the particle’s polarization can be extracted if we only integrate the region covering the poles corresponding to
(s_{k_{0}}=1,s_{k^{\prime}_{0}}=1)
(pp) mode. This procedure will select the mode close to
\tilde{\omega}\sim 0^{+}
. To elaborate further, we can check the time structure of
\langle J_{5}^{\mu}\rangle
for operator defined in Eq. (
36
)
Schematically, for a free field at global equilibrium, its time structure can be represented as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{5}^{\mu}(t)\propto C_{\rm pp}^{\mu}\langle a^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}a_{\bm{k}}\rangle e^{-i(\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})t}+C_{\rm nn}^{\mu}\langle b^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}b_{\bm{k}}\rangle e^{-i(\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}})t}

\displaystyle+C_{pn}^{\mu}\langle a_{\bm{k}}b_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\rangle e^{-i(\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})t}+C_{np}^{\mu}\langle a^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}b^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\rangle e^{i(\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}})t}

(52)

which is corresponding to the pole structure in Fourier space as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{5}^{\mu}(\tilde{\omega})\propto G^{\mu}_{R}(\tilde{\omega})\propto

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\rm pp}^{\mu}\langle a^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}a_{\bm{k}}\rangle}{\tilde{\omega}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}}+\frac{C_{\rm nn}^{\mu}\langle b^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}b_{\bm{k}}\rangle}{\tilde{\omega}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{pn}^{\mu}\langle a_{\bm{k}}b_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\rangle}{\tilde{\omega}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}}+\frac{C_{np}^{\mu}\langle a^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}}b^{\dagger}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\rangle}{\tilde{\omega}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}}

(53)

In quantum field theory, when we would like extract the modes from a correlation functions, typically we use the LSZ reduction techniques
[
73
]
. The most straightforward realization of the LSZ procedure is to multiplying the whole correlation function in the momentum space with the pole, taking it to zero to select the residues out as shown in the below:

\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{5,+}(\tilde{\omega})=\frac{\lim_{\tilde{\omega}\rightarrow\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}}(\tilde{\omega}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}})\mathcal{J}_{5}^{\mu}(\tilde{\omega})]}{\tilde{\omega}+\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}}

(54)

Note that the
\tilde{\omega}
structure of
J_{5}^{\mu}
is inherited from the retarded Green function
G_{R}
. Therefore, the standard LSZ reduction to select out
J^{\mu}_{5,+}
is equivalent to only selecting the density mode (pp), as done in the previous section. For the off-shell case, it will be more complicated, and the pole selection procedure will be more obscure. However, with the understanding of the physical meanings of the four modes, the selection procedure practiced in this work can still be regarded as a generalized LSZ scheme for off-shell correlation functions.

Vector polarization for spin-1

Following the discussion of vector polarization for spin-1/2 particles, we now extend the calculation to vector polarization for spin-1 particles using the same Zubarev response approach. In this section, we will elaborate on the details of the calculation of the vector polarization results that have already briefly reported in Ref.
[
21
]
. Meanwhile, many aspects of the calculation of vector polarization are intrinsically connected to the tensor polarization/spin alignment calculation. Therefore, obtaining expected results for vector polarization can serve as a benchmark for the correctness of the tensor polarization/spin alignment calculations.

IV.2
Main formalism

Similar to the procedures done in the literature

IV.3
For the covariance of density matrices

There is a discussion in the literature regarding different definitions of the spin density matrices
[
38
]
, which differ from those used in Ref.
[
75
]
. Meanwhile, the different definitions will lead to different results. We realized this subtlety when calculating the vector polarization in our work
[
21
]
. In this section, we will try to clarify it use the vector boson as an example.

The first definition is the one used in Refs.
[
46
,
75
,
21
]
, in which the (density of) density matrix is

\displaystyle\varrho^{(w)}_{s^{\prime}s}(t,\bm{x},\bm{p})=\epsilon_{\mu,s^{\prime}}(\bm{p})W^{\mu\nu}(t,\bm{x},\bm{p})\epsilon^{*}_{\nu,s}(\bm{p})

(81)

On the other hand, the second one is explained in Ref.
[
38
]
, which is

\displaystyle\varrho^{(a)}_{s^{\prime}s}(t,\bm{x},\bm{p})=\int\frac{d^{3}\bm{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\times\epsilon_{\mu,s^{\prime}}(\bm{p}-\bm{q}/2)W^{\mu\nu}(t,\bm{q},\bm{p})\epsilon^{*}_{\nu,s}(\bm{p}+\bm{q}/2)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\bm{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{x}}\langle a^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}-\bm{q}/2,s^{\prime}}a_{\bm{p}+\bm{q}/2,s}\rangle+o(\partial)

(82)

To obtain third line from the second line, we need
\varepsilon_{\bm{p}}/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\bm{k}}\varepsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}}\sim 1
in the small
\bm{q}
limit.

Superficially, both the Wigner-based definition
\varrho^{w}
in Eq. (
81
) and the “
a^{\dagger}a
”-based definition
\varrho^{(a)}
in Eq. (
82
) are justified. In the following, we will discuss their transformation properties under Lorentz boosts, where the differences and subtleties appear.

First, we use the notation
\Lambda
to define a boost that takes
p=(\varepsilon_{\bm{p}},\bm{p})
to
p^{\prime}=(\varepsilon_{\bm{p}^{\prime}},\bm{p}^{\prime})
, and
x=(t,\bm{x})
to
x^{\prime}
. On the other hand,
L(p)
is the standard boost, taking
(m,\bm{0})
to
p
(see Ref.
[
76
]
). Then, the little group
W
denotes the “rotations” contained in
\Lambda
and is represented by
W(\Lambda,p)=L^{-1}(p^{\prime})\Lambda L(p)
, which is an element of the SO(3) group.
D^{(1)}_{ss^{\prime}}(W(\Lambda,p))\equiv D^{(1)}_{ss^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\bm{p})
is the representation that transforms the spin indices of a spin-1 particle at momentum
p
under the boost
\Lambda
. The transformation of these indices will have the effect that
D^{(1)}_{\sigma^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\bm{p})\epsilon_{\mu^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{p}^{\prime})=\Lambda_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\;\;\gamma}\epsilon_{\gamma,s^{\prime}}(\bm{p})
(repeated indices imply summation).

With all the preparation above,
\varrho^{(w)}_{s^{\prime}s}
defined in Eq. (
81
) will transform as

\displaystyle\varrho^{(w)}_{s^{\prime}s}(x,\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\;\;\gamma}\epsilon_{\gamma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{p})\Lambda_{\nu^{\prime}}^{\;\;\lambda}\epsilon^{*}_{\lambda,\sigma}(\bm{p})\Lambda^{\mu^{\prime}}_{\;\;\mu}\Lambda^{\nu^{\prime}}_{\;\;\nu}W^{\mu\nu}(x,\bm{p})

\displaystyle=D^{(1)}_{\sigma^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\bm{p})\epsilon_{\mu^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{p}^{\prime})W^{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}(\Lambda^{-1}x^{\prime},\bm{p}^{\prime},u^{\prime}...l^{\prime})\epsilon^{*}_{\mu^{\prime},\sigma}(\bm{p}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\times D^{(1)*}_{\sigma s}(\Lambda,\bm{p})

\displaystyle=D^{(1)}_{\sigma^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\bm{p})\varrho^{(w)}_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}(\Lambda^{-1}x^{\prime},\bm{p}^{\prime},u^{\prime}...l^{\prime})D^{(1)*}_{\sigma s}(\Lambda,\bm{p})

(83)

Here,
u^{\prime}=\Lambda u
and
l^{\prime}=\Lambda l
represent the boosted generic vectors inside the expressions of the Wigner function or density matrices, in addition to
x
and
p
. This is what we expect for a tensor with spin indices
s,s^{\prime}
transformed under the little group of a Lorentz transformation.

For the second definition, i.e.,
\varrho^{(a)}_{s^{\prime}s}
in Eq. (
82
), the transformation is approximately shown as below:

\displaystyle\varrho^{(a)}_{s^{\prime}s}(x,\bm{p})

\displaystyle\approx\int\frac{d^{3}\bm{q}^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{q}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{x}^{\prime}}D^{(1)}_{\sigma^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\bm{p}-\frac{\bm{q}}{2})

\displaystyle\times\langle a^{\dagger}_{\bm{p}^{\prime}-\bm{q}^{\prime}/2,\sigma^{\prime}}a_{\bm{p}^{\prime}+\bm{q}^{\prime}/2,\sigma}\rangle D^{(1)*}_{\sigma s}(\Lambda,\bm{p}+\frac{\bm{q}}{2})

(84)

The approximation also simply means neglecting some higher-order contributions in
\bm{q}
. As shown, for scalar particles, there is no problem with this definition. However, for particles with nonzero spins, the two definitions will differ. The transformation in Eq. (
84
) does not seem to have a simple factorization, as shown in Eq. (
83
). Therefore, to maintain the covariance of the final operator average, some effort is required, which will be discussed in the following.

We will first discuss the commonly used spin operator
S^{\mu}_{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{p})
with spin indices, which is expressed as

\displaystyle S_{ss^{\prime}}^{\mu}(\bm{p})=\frac{1}{m}\varepsilon^{\nu\mu\alpha\beta}(-ip_{\nu})\epsilon_{\beta,s}(\bm{p})\epsilon^{*}_{\alpha,s^{\prime}}(\bm{p})

(85)

In the particle rest frame,
\epsilon^{\alpha}_{\pm}=\mp(0,1,\pm i,0)/\sqrt{2}
,
\epsilon^{\alpha}_{0}=(0,0,0,1)
, and
\epsilon^{\alpha}_{s}(\bm{p})
and
\epsilon^{\alpha}_{s}(\bm{0})
are connected by
L(p)
. For
S_{ss^{\prime}}^{\mu}(\bm{p})
in the particle rest frame,
S^{0}
is zero, and
S^{i}
are the familiar representations of the rotational generator for a spin-one particle.

\displaystyle\bm{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\begin{pmatrix}0&\;1&\;0\\
1&\;0&\;1\\
0&\;1&\;0\end{pmatrix}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i&\;0\\
i&\;0&-i\\
0&\;i&\;0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&\;0&\;0\\
0&\;0&\;0\\
0&\;0&-1\end{pmatrix}\end{pmatrix}

(86)

The
S^{\mu}(\bm{p})
and
S^{\mu}(0)
are also connected by
L(p)
. However, if we further transform
S^{\mu}_{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{p})
by the boost
\Lambda
, the transformation law is

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mu}_{\;\;\nu}S^{\nu}_{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{p})=D^{(1)}_{\sigma s}(\Lambda,\bm{p})S^{\mu}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{p}^{\prime})D^{(1)*}_{\sigma^{\prime}s^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\bm{p})

(87)

As shown, the transformation for a general
S^{\mu}
also includes a little group rotation on the spin indices. However, if we trace this
S^{\mu}
with the density matrices
\varrho^{w}
, the rotation will cancel each other, as shown below:

\displaystyle\text{Boost }\text{Tr}[S^{\mu}\varrho^{(w)}]

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{\mu}_{\;\;\nu}\text{Tr}[D^{*-1}S^{\nu}D^{*}D^{*-1}\varrho^{(w)}D^{*}]

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{\mu}_{\;\;\nu}\text{Tr}[S^{\nu}\varrho^{(w)}]\;,

(88)

which finally becomes the expected transformation for a boost of a general vector.

For the second definition of
\varrho^{(a)}
in Eq. (
82
), if we want to achieve covariance for the operator average, we need to define a different
S^{\mu}
operator
S^{\mu}(\bm{p},\bm{q})
at
\bm{p},\bm{q}
space. Schematically, we should have

\displaystyle S_{ss^{\prime}}^{{(a)\mu}}(\bm{p},\bm{q})=\frac{1}{m}\varepsilon^{\nu\mu\alpha\beta}(-ip_{\nu})\epsilon_{\beta,s}(\bm{p}+\bm{q}/2)\epsilon^{*}_{\alpha,s^{\prime}}(\bm{p}-\bm{q}/2)

\displaystyle S_{ss^{\prime}}^{(a)\mu}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{p})=\int\frac{d^{3}\bm{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\bm{q}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})}S^{(a)\mu}_{ss^{\prime}}(\bm{p},\bm{q})

\displaystyle S^{(a)\mu}(\bm{x},\bm{p})=\int d^{3}\bm{x}^{\prime}\text{Tr}\{S^{(a)\mu}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{p})\varrho^{(a)}(t,\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{p})\}

(89)

Basically, we need a different and non-local definition

for the spin matrix in the spinor space. Note that, in this case, for small
\bm{q}
limits, the definition of
S^{\mu}
includes a Berry connection contribution as shown below

\displaystyle S_{ss^{\prime}}^{(a)\mu}(\bm{p},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=S_{ss^{\prime}}^{\mu}(p)+\frac{1}{2m}\varepsilon^{\nu\mu\alpha\beta}(-ip_{\nu})\epsilon^{*}_{\alpha,s^{\prime}}(\bm{p})\bm{q}^{i}\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\frac{\partial}{\partial{\bm{p}^{i}}}}\epsilon_{\beta,s}(\bm{p})\,.

(90)

Therefore, if we use different definitions of density matrices, we are also required to use different definitions of the operator to match the density matrix.

We will begin with the definition of the tensor polarization and spin alignment based on the discussions in the previous section. Unlike the vector polarization, which is related to the anti-symmetric part of the Wigner function, the tensor polarization will be related to the symmetric and traceless part of the Wigner function with proper projection. This projection can be implemented by the shell projector defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}\equiv\delta W^{\langle\mu\nu\rangle}\equiv\tilde{\Delta}^{\langle\mu}_{\;\lambda}\tilde{\Delta}^{\nu\rangle}_{\;\gamma}\delta W^{(\lambda\gamma)}

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}^{\langle\mu}_{\;\lambda}\tilde{\Delta}^{\nu\rangle}_{\;\gamma}=\tilde{\Delta}^{(\mu}_{\;\lambda}\tilde{\Delta}^{\nu)}_{\;\gamma}-\frac{1}{3}\tilde{\Delta}^{\mu\nu}\tilde{\Delta}_{\lambda\gamma}

(93)

With the tensor polarization defined above, the spin alignment

With the notations discussed above, we can express the non-dissipative part of the tensor polarization following similar procedures in Eq. (

\displaystyle-\eta^{\lambda\gamma}[(k\cdot k^{\prime}-m^{2})\eta_{\alpha\beta}-k_{\beta}k^{\prime}_{\alpha}]A^{\alpha(\nu}(k)A^{\mu)\beta}(k^{\prime})

V.3

High Orders, Non-Perturbative Methods–

The concrete calculations presented so far in this work represent the leading non-zero effects. However, within the expansion scheme discussed in this section, many improvements can be made in the future.

For example, even if we still keep the discussion within the leading skeleton order, we can improve the self-energies through microscopic calculations or through external phenomenological inputs. To go beyond, we can perform two or more loop calculations to include vertex corrections and other more sophisticated considerations
[
77
,
80
]
. On the other hand, we have many non-perturbative theoretical tools for the evaluation of the correlation functions, especially in Euclidean time. For example, the vertex structure and the self-energies can be evaluated using the DSE
[
60
]
or FRG
[
61
]
methods mentioned previously. Meanwhile, four-point functions can be resolved by the Bethe-Salpeter equation or its reduced 3D
T
-matrix equations
[
63
,
62
]
. We might even employ lattice QFT to directly calculate these correlation functions from first principles, as discussed in Sec.
II.3
.

VI
Summary and perspective

In this work, we have discussed the theoretical foundation and essential techniques of our Zubarev response approach for calculating the vector and tensor polarizations of particles in a locally equilibrated hydrodynamic medium. In particular, we have systematically explained how to incorporate familiar correlation functions and diagrammatic techniques within the Zubarev response approach.

For the concrete application of the Zubarev response approach, we list several outcomes and relevant subtleties that warrant some attention:

(1) We have reproduced the recently discovered local vector polarization formula for spin-1/2 particles
[
17
]
using the Zubarev response approach, including the shear-induced polarization contributions.

(2) The local vector polarization for spin-1 particles has been derived, and it follows the same form as the spin-1/2 case, with an additional factor of 4/3.

(3) We explicitly prove that non-dissipative part of the first-order gradient contribution to spin alignment vanishes.

(4) We present an alternative derivation for Zubarev response approach.

(5) We discuss the subtleties and relations between slow and fast modes, the slow-fast limits.

(6) We illustrate the subtleties and relations between different definitions of density matrices and how to make the final operator average covariant for various definitions.

(7) We explain the skeleton expansion implicitly used in previous calculations and show how to include higher-order terms, and how to relate to non-perturbative methods, including lattice QFT.

In the future, a meaningful extension of this work is to go beyond the linear order of gradients to include non-linear response terms, which have been explored in other methods
[
40
,
58
,
59
]
. Also, as a diagrammatic approach, we can explore higher-order (interaction) effects using typical perturbation theory. More ambitiously, since polarization quantities can have non-perturbative definitions, we can calculate various polarizations using non-perturbative methods such as DSE, FRG, and lattice QFT, which may ultimately lead to a much deeper understanding of the polarization phenomena in heavy-ion collisions and help address the ongoing puzzles in the field.
