Integrating ultrasound biomechanics and clinical assessments to examine breastfeeding function: novel insights into tongue mobility, milk transfer, and reflux symptoms
Authors: Diklah Barak, Orit Bart, Liat Ashkenazi Hoffnung, Ron Berant, Sigal Portnoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreastfeeding is a dynamic co-occupation between mother and infant, influenced by both anatomical and functional factors. Tongue mobility plays a critical role and may impact infants’ feeding efficiency and quality. Breastfeeding biomechanics, evaluated using tools such as ultrasound, and clinical aspects, assessed through questionnaires and examinations, have largely been studied independently. The aim of this study was to evaluate the association between breastfeeding biomechanics and clinical measurements of breastfeeding quality. Specifically, to assess the correlations between clinical aspects including Gastro-Esophageal Reflux (GER) symptoms, maternal nipple pain and mean milk transfer rate (MTR) with biomechanical measurements including distances and angles between the tongue, nipple, and hard-soft-palate-junction (HSPJ).MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Israel including 21 mother-infant dyads (infants aged 3–6 weeks). Data collection included demographic questionnaires, clinical assessments of tongue mobility and breastfeeding self-efficacy, as well as real-time ultrasound imaging of the tongue and nipple during feeding. Mean milk transfer rate was measured by pre- and post-feed infant weight. Statistical analyses included Mann-Whitney tests, Spearman’s correlations, and linear regression to explore associations between clinical and biomechanical parameters.ResultsMean milk transfer rate was significantly associated with ultrasound measurements, including tongue-nipple distances and angles, and maternal nipple pain scores (r and p 0.489, 0.029 and 0.593, 0.012, respectively). A transfer rate threshold of 4.5 g/min demarcated qualitative changes in the ultrasound measurements. Higher pain scores and restricted tongue mobility correlated with lower mean milk transfer rates (r and p 0.509, 0.036 and 0.558, 0.020 respectively). Gastroesophageal reflux symptoms were negatively correlated with nipple diameter changes during feeding (r=-0.598, p = 0.009).ConclusionsThis study highlights the interplay between breastfeeding biomechanics and clinical symptoms. Mean milk transfer rate, influenced by both maternal and infant factors, emerged as an indicator of breastfeeding quality. Our findings suggest a potential mechanistic link between feeding quality and the presence of gastroesophageal reflux symptoms. Incorporating time-based milk transfer assessments and ultrasound measurements into clinical practice may enhance diagnostic precision and therapeutic strategies. Clinicians are encouraged to assess breastfeeding quality in cases of reflux symptoms.

Introduction

Breastfeeding is the biological norm for human infant nutrition. Approximately 92% of Israeli infants were ever breastfed, and 63% were exclusively breastfed any period of time. However, this rate declines with only 44.1% exclusively breastfeeding at four months of age and only 15.3% meeting the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations of exclusive breastfeeding at six months [1]. In addition, 27% of mothers report pain and/or breastfeeding difficulties as the reason for earlier than desired weaning. Although Israel’s population is approximately 10 million, these breastfeeding patterns are comparable to those reported in the United States [2] and thus may represent global trends.

Breastfeeding is a co-occupation, shared by the mother and infant, and as such influenced by both occupational performances and impacts both parties’ wellbeing [3]. The mother’s cultural beliefs and values, her adjustment and performance in her new maternal role are only some of the parameters that influence the breastfeeding. Furthermore, the mother’s physiology and breast and nipple anatomy may affect both her baby’s ability to latch on the breast and her innate milk supply [4, 5]. In turn, the infant’s anatomy, neuro-behavioral state, and motor skills are among the factors that affect the baby’s breastfeeding efficiency which correlates with the mother’s milk production [4, 5].

During breastfeeding, the infant latches to the breast using the lips and tongue. The anterior portion of the tongue primarily stabilizes the breast against the palate and moves en bloc with the mandible in a limited superior–inferior direction [6, 7], contributing to the establishment of a baseline intraoral vacuum [8, 9]. The mid-posterior portion of the tongue then moves on a superior–inferior axis. In the superior (‘tongue up’) position, the nipple tip is compressed against the hard palate, closing the nipple pores. As the tongue moves inferiorly (‘tongue down’), intraoral vacuum increases, drawing the nipple towards the hard–soft palate junction and enabling milk transfer into the oral cavity [6, 8, 10].

The tongue participates in both oral and pharyngeal phases of feeding [11]. Due to its central role in breastfeeding, anomalies that restrict tongue mobility might interfere with breastfeeding. Tongue tie, also referred to as short frenulum or ankyloglossia, is the most common congenital anomaly. It is correlated with poor infant weight gain, feeding fatigue, nipple pain and trauma, nipple vasospasm, gastroesophageal reflux-like symptoms, aerophagia, hiccups and choking after breastfeeding and early weaning [7, 12–16]. Poor tongue mobility and poor lip seal were identified as possible causes of aerophagia, a condition in which a large volume of air is swallowed by the infant [16–20]. Aerophagia shares symptoms with Gastro-Esophageal Reflux (GER), a common parental complaint. The diagnosis and the form of treatment of Ankyloglossia are controversial [21–28]. Over the last two decades, there has been a significant effort to better define and categorize it into levels of severity [21, 22, 26, 29]. Different infants may show substantially different breastfeeding outcomes with apparently similar tongue-tie severity [30]. This is likely because the tongue is part of a complex and dynamic system, and other components may contribute to breastfeeding difficulties or success. These mechanisms have yet to be thoroughly investigated.

Effective breastfeeding depends on coordinated infant oral biomechanics, distances and angles between the infant’s tongue and the nipple provide quantitative indicators of mouth geometry and tongue–nipple interaction, which might influence latch stability, milk transfer efficiency, and maternal comfort during feeding. To quantify breastfeeding biomechanics, previous studies have used methods and tools such as ultrasound (US) and vacuum measurements [8]. These studies demonstrated that the anterior part of the tongue moves en bloc with the mandible in a limited superior–inferior direction, while the mid-posterior part undulates independently in a peristaltic-like motion. The coordinated inferior–superior motion of the mid-posterior tongue is associated with intraoral pressure changes and the efficiency of milk removal from the breast [6, 10, 31, 32]. These biomechanical interactions directly affect the deformation of the nipple during feeding, providing a link between tongue motion and maternal experience. Changes in nipple diameter correlated with maternal nipple pain [33]. Maternal nipple pain is a common complaint and may be a result of a poor latch or suboptimal breastfeeding biomechanics [34]. Gastroesophageal reflux (GER) in infancy is a high prevalence (10–50%) parental complaint [35–37], commonly explained by esophageal sphincter maturation and other esophageal dysfunctions [38]. GER is also correlated to ankyloglossia (tongue-tie) [16], which is an anatomical variant of the infant’s mouth, distinct from the esophageal system, motivating our study. Tongue-tie is correlated to increased nipple pain, reduced maternal self-efficacy and an increased risk of earlier than desired weaning [12, 39].

To better understand the mixed relationships between tongue mobility and clinical symptoms, the aim of our study was to evaluate the association between breastfeeding biomechanics and clinical measurements of breastfeeding quality. Specifically, to assess the correlations between clinical aspects including GER symptoms and mean milk transfer rate (MTR) with biomechanical measurements including distances and angles between the tongue, nipple, and hard-soft-palate-junction (HSPJ).

Methods

Population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at an ambulatory clinic at Schneider Children’s Medical Center in Israel. The subjects were not patients of the clinic. Twenty-one mothers with 3–6 weeks old infants were recruited between March 2023 and January 2024. Enrollment of mother and infant dyads was via social media and the snowball method, i.e. participating mothers were asked to share study information with other eligible mothers in their social networks. Interested mothers then contacted the research team directly and were screened for eligibility prior to enrollment. Inclusion criteria included, (a) Full-term healthy infants (b) aged 3 to 6 weeks (21–48 days). Exclusion criteria were (a) Maternal or infant chronic illness; (b) Poor milk supply (less than 75% breastfeeding); or (c) Former frenotomy.

Tools

The study tools included three questionnaires, four clinical evaluations, and an US examination. Breastfeeding at birth, and exclusive breastfeeding information was derived from the demographic questionnaire.

Mean milk transfer rate (MTR) was evaluated by weighing the infant before and after the feed, while measuring the duration of the feed. The absolute weight difference in grams is typically used clinically, while the rate (grams per minute) is used in research settings [10].

Average daily weight gain was calculated by subtracting birth weight from weight at the time of testing, and dividing by the infant’s age in days. This served as a general estimate of growth rate.

Maternal nipple pain was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), with mothers asked to rate their pain on a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (extreme pain) [40]. Mothers were asked to rate pain at two specific moments (attachment, and after a few minutes of feeding). The NRS has demonstrated high test–retest reliability (r = 0.95) and good construct validity, as evidenced by strong correlations with the Visual Analog Scale (VAS; correlation coefficients ranging from 0.85 to 0.95) [41].

Breastfeeding efficacy was assessed using the Breastfeeding Self Efficacy-Short Form (BFSE-SF). This is a 14 item questionnaire, filled by the mother. Each item is scored from 0 (not at all confident) to 5 (always confident), leading to a total score between 0 and 70. Higher scores indicate better breastfeeding function and higher maternal confidence [42]. The short form is highly correlated with the longer form version (r = 0.99), and is predictive of future-continued breastfeeding (p < 0.001) [42].

To assess tongue mobility, we used the Lingual Frenulum Protocol for Infants (LFPI) [27]. The protocol consists of two parts: a brief breastfeeding history section and a clinical evaluation, which includes an anatomo-functional assessment and an evaluation of sucking. The history section is scored from 0 to 8, the anatomo-functional component from 0 to 12, and the sucking evaluation from 0 to 5, yielding a total possible score of 0 to 25. A score above 9 in the clinical evaluation or a total score exceeding 13 indicates possible lingual frenulum restriction, for which a frenotomy may be recommended [27]. The protocol’s demonstrated significant criterion validity (p < 0.001) and construct validity (p < 0.005) [27].

GERD and GER symptoms were evaluated using the Infant Gastroesophageal Reflux Questionnaire- revised (I-GERQ-R) [43]. This 12-item questionnaire is filled by the primary caregiver. The score range is 0 to 42, where a higher score indicates a greater symptom load. The questionnaire test-retest reliability had an Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.85 and a high construct validity (p < 0.05) [43].

Nipple and attachment pain were evaluated using a numeric rating scale [40]. The mother was asked to rate her pain on a 0 (no pain at all) to 10 (highest imaginable pain) scale at attachment and after the infant is actively breastfeeding.

US was used for evaluation of intraoral tongue movement, nipple displacement and diameter measurements. US examinations were conducted using L14-5sp or C10-3 transducers (Shenzhen Mindray Biomedical Electronics, China and Germany). A midsagittal submental approach was used, as it is preferable for the visualization of the palate and tongue [44]. All assessments were done by DB, a licensed occupational therapist and international board certified lactation consultant (IBCLC), with training in infant feeding assessment. To ensure consistency across evaluations, the same examiner assessed all participants using standardized, previously validated assessment tools, as described in the original validation studies cited above.

Procedure

Helsinki approval (#0257-21RMC) was obtained from the Rabin Medical Center Helsinki committee and an ethics approval was granted by the Tel Aviv University ethics committee. Both parents signed an informed consent form.

The mother was asked about demographic information, and to fill out the BFSE-SF and I-GERQ-R questionnaires. The infant’s mouth anatomy was assessed using the LFPI. The mother breastfed and answered the NRS, while the US examination was recorded by a trained physician or a trained researcher. Pre- and post-feeding weight of the baby was measured for milk intake assessment, and the feed was timed. No changes in the infant’s clothing or diaper were performed during the feeding session [45]. End of feed was determined when the infant spontaneously detached from the breast. All feeds were conducted from a single breast only, to ensure consistency across dyads.

US imaging was performed using a midsagittal submental approach, with the transducer gently placed under the infant’s chin to visualize the midline oral cavity structures. To minimize interference, mothers settled into a comfortable cradle breastfeeding position, and only then the transduct was introduced. Clips were reviewed offline, and only segments showing nutritive sucking and proper anatomical visualization without disruption of latch or posture were included in the analysis.

Post analyses: Data were acquired in video form that was analyzed offline to extract the desired features [6, 31, 44]. Several US clips were recorded for each infant during breastfeeding. Out of these, a single clip was selected for analysis. Selection criteria were nutritive sucks and recording quality – allowing tongue up (TU) and tongue down (TD) frames from the same clip with the ability to mark all relevant anatomical structures. Using RADIANT software (Medixant, 2023.1, Poland), we extracted the following parameters from TU and TD frames: nipple diameter, distance, and angles between HSPJ the nipple (Fig. 1). The superior-inferior movement of the tongue was assessed using the intra-oral space, measured as the distance between the hard-soft palate junction (HSPJ) to the closest peak of mid-tongue and the distance between HSPJ and the nipple (Fig. 1). Nipple diameter was measured 2 mm from the tip of the nipple, as described by [46]. The angles between the HSP and the nipple were measured using the same point selecting method.

Data analyses

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 (IBM). Normality of the measurements was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and most variables were not normally distributed. We used Spearman’s rho for correlations and the Mann-Whitney test for comparison between groups. To correct for multiple comparisons, we used the Bonferroni correction with a threshold of α = 0.005 (10 US measurements), α = 0.0071 (7 clinical measurements), or α = 0.0125 (4 comparisons with nipple diameter change). Effect size of Mann-Whiteny was computed as Cohen’s r [47]. Linear regression was used to assess the association between standardized clinical assessment (LFPI), and real-time functional US measurements.

To verify the sensitivity of the results to our heuristic selection of a threshold of 4.5 g/min, we scanned a range of MTR threshold values and saw that indeed a value of 4.5–5.0 g/min corresponds to the qualitative change (Fig. 3). Discussion

In this study we found significant correlations between clinical and sonography parameters that improve our understanding of the biomechanics of breastfeeding. We found that the reflux symptoms were associated with smaller change in nipple diameter during feeding. MTR emerged as a significant parameter in breastfeeding assessment, demonstrating correlations with both clinical and ultrasound-based measurements.

MTR was found to be a significant parameter in the breastfeeding assessment. It was predicted by maternal pain at attachment and the angle between the HSPJ and nipple in TU position. MTR also correlated with other ultrasound measurements: nipple diameter in the TU position and angle to nipple in the TU position. The observed associations between MTR, maternal nipple pain at attachment, and US-derived biomechanical measures suggest that both mechanical and physiological mechanisms may underlie feeding efficiency. In our results, maternal pain at attachment was independently associated with MTR, alongside tongue–nipple angle in the TU position, supporting a functional link between maternal experience and milk transfer performance. These findings may be explained by two different mechanisms – a biomechanical one and a biological one. Biomechanically, inadequate attachment or poor tongue mobility may lead to inefficient milk transfer and a variety of compensatory responses by the infant, such as increased vacuum, biting and nipple compression. Insufficient intraoral vacuum changes can impair milk transfer. Increased compensatory vacuum and mechanical pressure can both lead to maternal nipple pain and nipple injury [8, 10, 32, 33, 48, 49]. A second, biological mechanism may involve hormonal regulation. Oxytocin plays a central role in the milk ejection reflex, and stress or acute pain can disrupt its release [50, 51]. In addition, increased adrenergic activity acts as a suppressor of milk ejection. Together, these processes provide a plausible pathway through which maternal pain may negatively influence MTR.

We found that smaller nipple diameter change was significantly correlated with higher reflux symptom scores on the I-GERQ-R. This is the first attempt to link reflux symptoms with breastfeeding biomechanics that emphasize the feed quality. Biomechanically, a small change in nipple diameter could indicate restricted tongue elevation, which plays a key role in bolus propulsion and milk duct compression. Such restriction may impair the infant’s milk flow control and swallow efficiency, which has been proposed to contribute to aerophagia and reflux-like symptoms in previous studies [16–20]. We therefore suggest that clinicians consider assessing breastfeeding quality upon observation of reflux symptoms.

The LFPI score was designed guide clinicians in frenotomy decisions [52, 53]. Our finding that high LFPI scores correlated with higher maternal pain reports, despite the absence of pain items in the LFPI tool, are consistent with many reports of correlation between pain and a short frenulum [19, 54]. The correlation we found between high LFPI and low BSES-SF scores in the same dyads is consistent with ankyloglossia treatment improvements measured separately [19, 25]. Exclusive breastfeeding was associated with established indicators of normative breastfeeding (low LFPI scores and reduced nipple pain) and with smaller nipple diameter in the TU position, which correlated with higher MTR. Together, these likely supported sustained breastfeeding. The LFPI was designed to support clinicians in making frenotomy decisions. In this research, only one infant had LFPI score indicating such a decision. However, the associations with other clinical and ultrasound measurements suggest it can be used in a broader scope in assessing breastfeeding function.

Breastfeeding is a complex multifactorial process, and while this study measured many variables, it obviously omitted others. For instance, infant palate anatomy, maternal breast and nipple size and elasticity are all factors that were not included in our study and should be examined in the future. Including more variables will require a larger cohort size to obtain good statistics. The many variables measured in the current study render the cohort relatively small. However, due to the sensitivity of the measurements and when compared to similar studies, the sample size is seemly, as evidenced by the moderate to large effect sizes in all comparisons. To minimize intervening factors, we restricted the infants’ minimum age to avoid challenges associated with the breastfeeding initiation period and set a maximum age to ensure better infant cooperation. Additionally, to control for variables such as weight gain, MTR, and behavioral response to low milk flow, we only included mothers with sufficient milk supply. These inclusion criteria resulted in a sample of “normal breastfeeding” dyads, thus limiting the range of breastfeeding difficulties represented. Therefore, the results of the study cannot be generalized to a larger population. The findings related to MTR are intriguing, but there are also possible intervening variables to consider. MTR, as measured in this study, reflects a snapshot of feeding performance. It is influenced by many factors, such as milk ejection reflex patterns, maternal milk supply, feed duration and more. Given natural variations in these factors, a lower MTR does not necessarily indicate dysfunction. Nevertheless, MTR was correlated with multiple clinical and biomechanical measurements, and hence can be a complementary tool to assist dyads with breastfeeding difficulties. Regarding the ultrasound measurements, only one pair of frames (TU and TD) was selected per dyad, based on image clarity and anatomical visibility. While this approach aligns with prior methods in the field [46], it does not account for intra-feed variability in suck dynamics. This may limit the generalizability of frame-based measurements, and future work using full-cycle or multi-suck analysis is warranted.

Conclusions

We conclude that integrating biomechanical US measurements, maternal pain reports, and MTR may enhance breastfeeding assessment by offering a more comprehensive picture of the dyad’s feeding dynamics. While MTR alone is subject to biological variability and should not be used as a stand-alone clinical marker, it may serve as a supportive measure when interpreted in conjunction with other clinical findings. For that, we suggest adding time measurement as part of breastfeeding assessment, using the 4.5 g/min threshold as a rule of thumb. This will allow a more accurate assessment of the breastfeeding quality and better therapeutic guidelines such as oral sensory-motor exercises. Future research should further investigate the reliability and applicability of MTR across diverse populations and feeding contexts. Different features of US measurements could be measured in a Point of Care UltraSound (POCUS) approach and thus provide clinicians therapeutic directions.

Future research should include varied populations to expand our understanding of the breastfeeding challenges.
