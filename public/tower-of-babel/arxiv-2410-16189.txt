Quantum Algorithms for Non-smooth Non-convex Optimization
Chengchang Liu, Chaowen Guan, Jianhao He, John C.S. Lui

Abstract
This paper considers the problem for finding the ( δ , ϵ ) (\delta,\epsilon) -Goldstein stationary point of Lipschitz continuous objective, which is a rich function class to cover a great number of important applications. We construct a novel zeroth-order quantum estimator for the gradient of the smoothed surrogate. Based on such estimator, we propose a novel quantum algorithm that achieves a query complexity of 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d 3 / 2 ​ δ − 1 ​ ϵ − 3 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3/2}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-3}) on the stochastic function value oracle, where d d is the dimension of the problem. We also enhance the query complexity to 𝒪 ~ ( d 3 / 2 δ − 1 ϵ − 7 / 3 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3/2}\delta^{-1}\epsilon^{-7/3}) by introducing a variance reduction variant. Our findings demonstrate the clear advantages of utilizing quantum techniques for non-convex non-smooth optimization, as they outperform the optimal classical methods on the dependency of ϵ \epsilon by a factor of ϵ − 2 / 3 \epsilon^{-2/3} .

Preliminaries

We introduce preliminaries for quantum computing model and non-smooth non-convex optimization in this section.

2.1
Preliminaries for Quantum Computing Model

Here we formally review the basics and some concepts from quantum computing that we work with. For more details, please refer to
Nielsen and Chuang [40]
.

Quantum Basics.

A quantum state can be seen as a vector
{{\bf x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m})^{\top}
in Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{m}
such that
\sum_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}=1
. We follow the Dirac bra/ket notation on quantum states, i.e., we denote the quantum state for
{{\bf x}}
by
|x\rangle
and denote
{{\bf x}}^{\dagger}
by
\langle x|
, where
\dagger
means the Hermitian conjugation.

Given a state
|\psi\rangle=\sum^{m}_{i=1}c_{i}|i\rangle
, we call
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
the amplitude of the state
|i\rangle
.
Given two quantum states
|{{\bf x}}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}^{m}
and
|{{\bf y}}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}^{m}
, we denote their inner product by
\langle{{\bf x}}|{{\bf y}}\rangle\triangleq\sum_{i}x^{\dagger}_{i}y_{i}
.
Given
|{{\bf x}}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}^{m}
and
|y\rangle\in\mathcal{H}^{n}
, we denote their tensor product by
|{{\bf x}}\rangle\otimes|{{\bf y}}\rangle\triangleq(x_{1}y_{1},\cdots,x_{m}y_{n})^{\top}\in\mathcal{H}^{m\times n}
.
If we measure state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}|i\rangle
in computational basis, we will obtain
i
with probability
|c_{i}|^{2}
and the state will collapse into
\ket{i}
after measurement, for all
i
. A quantum algorithm works by applying a sequence of unitary operators to a initial quantum state.

Quantum Query Complexity.
Corresponding to the classical query model, quantum query complexity considers the number of querying a black box of a particular function which needs to be invoked in order to solve a problem.
In many cases, the black box corresponds to the process that has the highest overhead, and therefore reducing the number of queries to it will effectively reduce the computational complexity of the entire algorithm.
For example, if a classical oracle
{\bf C}_{f}
for a function
f
is a black box that, when queried with a point
{\bf x}
, outputs the function value
{\bf C}_{f({\bf x})}=f({\bf x})
,
then the corresponding quantum oracle
{\bf U}_{f}
can be a unitary transformation that maps a quantum state
\ket{\x}\ket{q}
to the state
\ket{\x}\ket{q + f(\x)}
.
Moreover, given the superposition input
\sum_{{\bf x},q}\alpha_{{\bf x},q}\ket{\x}\ket{q}
, applying the quantum oracle once will, by linearity, output the quantum state
\sum_{{\bf x},q}\alpha_{{\bf x},q}\ket{\x}\ket{q + f(\x)}
.

2.2
Preliminaries for Non-convex Non-smooth Optimization

We introduce the necessary background for non-convex non-smooth optimization, with the following mild assumption that the object function is Lipschitz continuous.

Assumption 1
.

We assume the stochastic component
F(\cdot;\xi)
of the objective
f(\cdot)
satisfies that
|F({\bf x};\xi)-F({\bf y};\xi)|\leq L\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|

for every
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Besides, we assume
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is lower bounded and denote
f^{*}\triangleq\inf_{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f({\bf x})
.

The Rademencher’s theorem indicates that
f(\cdot)
is differentiable almost everywhere under Assumption
1
, which allows us to define its Clarke sub-differential as follows
[
9
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Clarke sub-differential)
.

The Clarke sub-differential of a Lipschitz function at point
{\bf x}
is defined by
\partial f({\bf x})\triangleq{\text{\rm conv}}\left\{{\bf g}:{\bf g}=\lim_{{\bf x}_{k}\to{\bf x}}\nabla f({\bf x}_{k})\right\}
.

We then introduce the Goldstein sub-differential
[
21
]
and the
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationary point
[
61
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Goldstein sub-differential)
.

The Goldstein sub-differential of a Lipschitz function at point
{\bf x}
is defined by
\partial_{\delta}f({\bf x})\triangleq{\text{\rm conv}}\left\{\cup_{{\bf y}\in{\bf B}_{\delta}({\bf x})}\partial f({\bf y})\right\}
.

Definition 2.3

(
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationary point)
.

We call
{\bf x}
the
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationary point of a given Lipschitz function if it satisfies that

{\text{\rm dist}}(0,\partial_{\delta}f({\bf x}))\leq\epsilon,

where
\partial_{\delta}f({\bf x})
is the Goldstein sub-differential.

Next we define the smoothed surrogate of
f(\cdot)
as follows.

Definition 2.4

(
\delta
-smoothed surrogate)
.

The
\delta
-smoothed surrogate of
f
is defined by

\displaystyle f_{\delta}({\bf x})\triangleq{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf w}\sim{\mathcal{P}}}\left[f({\bf x}+\delta{\bf w})\right],

(2)

where
{\mathcal{P}}
is the uniform distribution on a unit ball.

Although
f(\cdot)
is non-smooth, its smoothed surrogate
f_{\delta}(\cdot)
enjoys some good properties as presented in the following proposition
[
57
,
14
,
33
,
5
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
f(\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
1
, its smoothed surrogate
f_{\delta}(\cdot)
satisfies that:

•

|f_{\delta}(\cdot)-f(\cdot)|\leq\delta L

and

|f_{\delta}({\bf x})-f_{\delta}({\bf y})|\leq L\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|
.

•

\nabla f_{\delta}(\cdot)
is
c\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}
-Lipschitz for some constant
c>0
, i.e.
\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf y})\|\leq c\sqrt{d}L\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|
.

•

\nabla f_{\delta}(\cdot)\in\partial_{\delta}f(\cdot)
, where
\partial_{\delta}f(\cdot)
is the Goldstein sub-differential.

Remark 2.2
.

Proposition
2.1
implies that the task of finding the
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationary point of
f(\cdot)
is equivalent to finding the
\epsilon
-stationary point of a smoothed function
f_{\delta}(\cdot)
, i.e. finding some point
{\bf x}
such that
\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})\|\leq\epsilon
.

3
Zeroth-order Based Stochastic Quantum Estimator

In this section, we present a novel quantum estimator for the gradient of the smoothed surrogate
f_{\delta}(\cdot)
by using quantum stochastic function value oracle, which is essential for designing our quantum algorithms for non-convex non-smooth optimization.

3.1
Quantum Estimators via Quantum Stochastic Function Value Oracle

In this section, we construct quantum estimators for the gradient of the smoothed surrogate by
O(1)
-queries of quantum stochastic function values.

We start with the definition of the stochastic function value oracle.
Classically, a stochastic function value evaluation is defined as
F({{\bf x}},\xi)
for a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
with
\xi
being such that
{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[F({\bf x},\xi)]=f({\bf x}).

In this work, we assume the access of a
quantum
stochastic function value oracle
{\bf U}_{F}
for
f(\cdot)
, which is defined as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Quantum stochastic function value oracle)
.

For
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the quantum stochastic function value oracle, denoted by
{\bf U}_{F}
, works as:
{\bf U}_{F}:|{{\bf x}}\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\longmapsto|{{\bf x}}\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle\otimes|b+F({{\bf x}},\xi)\rangle,

where
F({\bf x},\xi)
is sampled from a distribution
p_{\xi}(\cdot)
such that
{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[F({\bf x};\xi)]=F({\bf x}).

One can construct the following stochastic gradient estimator for
\nabla f_{\delta}(\cdot)

[
39
,
33
,
5
,
30
,
34
]
:

\displaystyle{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)\triangleq\frac{d}{2\delta}\left(F({\bf x}+\delta{\bf w};\xi)-F({\bf x}-\delta{\bf w};\xi)\right)\cdot{\bf w},

(3)

where
{\bf w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is uniformly distributed on a unit sphere.
The following proposition shows that
{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)
is a good estimator of
\nabla f_{\delta}(\cdot)
.

Proposition 3.1

(
[
5
, Proposition 3 and 4]
)
.

Under Assumption
1
, i.e. the random variable
\xi
satisfies that

\displaystyle|F({\bf x};\xi)-F({\bf y};\xi)|\leq L\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|~~~\text{and}~~~{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[F({\bf x};\xi)]=f({\bf x}),

(4)

hold for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
then
{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)
defined in
eq.

3
satisfies that
{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf w},\xi}[{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)]=\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})
,

{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf w},\xi}[\|{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})\|^{2}]\leq c\pi dL^{2}
, and
{\mathbb{E}}_{{\bf w},\xi}[\|{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)-{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf y};{\bf w},\xi)\|^{2}\ \leq\frac{d^{2}L^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{2}
, where
c=16\sqrt{2}\pi
.

Next, to exploit the power of quantum algorithms, we generalize
eq.

3
to its quantum counterpart. Building on
eq.

3
and Proposition
3.1
,
{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)
can be interpreted as a random variable. In the quantum setting, accessing a random variable typically involves querying a
quantum sampling oracle
, which returns a quantum superposition over the associated distribution.

Definition 3.2

(Quantum sampling oracle)
.

For a random variable
X
with sample space
\Omega
, its quantum sampling oracle
{\bf O}_{X}
is defined as
{\bf O}_{X}:|0\rangle\longmapsto\sum_{{{\bf x}}}\sqrt{\Pr[X={{\bf x}}]}|{{\bf x}}\rangle\otimes|\psi_{{\bf x}}\rangle,

where
|\psi_{{\bf x}}\rangle
is an arbitrary quantum state for every
{{\bf x}}
.

The content in second quantum register can also be viewed as possible quantum garbage appeared during the implementation of the oracle. Observe that if we directly measure the output of
{\bf O}_{X}
, it will collapse to a classical sampling access to
X
that returns a random sample
{{\bf x}}
with respect to probability
\Pr[X={{\bf x}}]
. Note that the output of
{\bf O}_{X}
can be represented as integral over continuous random variables as well, as used in
[
7
,
46
]
.

Hence, based on our observation that
{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)
can be viewed as a random variable, our target oracle
{\bf O}_{{\bf{g}}_{\delta}}
–quantum stochastic gradient oracle–is essentially a quantum sampling oracle. Given upon this, we formally define the quantum
\delta
-estimated stochastic gradient oracle as follows.

Definition 3.3

(Quantum
\delta
-estimated stochastic gradient oracle)
.

For
f_{\delta}(\cdot):{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, its quantum
\delta
-estimated stochastic gradient oracle is defined as

{\bf O}_{{\bf{g}}_{\delta}}:|{{\bf x}}\rangle\otimes|{\bf{0}}\rangle\otimes|{\bf{0}}\rangle\longmapsto|{{\bf x}}\rangle\otimes\sum_{\xi,{{\bf w}}}\sqrt{\Pr[{{\bf w}},\xi]}|{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)\rangle\otimes|\psi_{{{\bf w}},\xi}\rangle,

where the random variable
{{\bf w}}
is uniformly distributed on a unit sphere and
\xi
satisfies
eq.

4
.

Proposition
3.1
implies
{\bf{g}}_{\delta}(\cdot)
can serve as an estimator of
\nabla f_{\delta}
, and it can be calculated with access to a quantum
\delta
-estimated stochastic gradient oracle as defined above. The following theorem shows that such oracle can be built with only
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
access to the quantum stochastic function value oracle.

Lemma 3.2
.

Given access to a quantum sampling oracle
{\bf O}_{\xi,{{\bf w}}}
to the joint distribution on
(\xi,{{\bf w}})
, one can construct a quantum
\delta
-estimated stochastic gradient oracle (as defined in
Definition

3.3
) with two queries to the quantum stochastic function value oracle
{\bf U}_{F}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that in Lemma
3.2
, we assume a black box access to quantum sampling oracle
{\bf O}_{\xi,{\bf w}}
following
Sidford and Zhang [46]
.
We present the explicit construction of such oracle in Appendix
A
.

Similarly, we can also constructed the estimator of
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf y})
by the following oracle:

{\bf O}_{\Delta{\bf{g}}_{\delta}}:|{{\bf x}}\rangle\otimes|{{\bf y}}\rangle\otimes|{\bf{0}}\rangle\otimes|{\bf{0}}\rangle\longmapsto|{{\bf x}}\rangle\otimes|{{\bf y}}\rangle\otimes\sum_{\xi,{{\bf w}}}\sqrt{\Pr[{{\bf w}},\xi]}|{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf x};{\bf w},\xi)-{\bf g}_{\delta}({\bf y};{\bf w},\xi)\rangle\otimes|\psi_{{{\bf w}},\xi}\rangle,

with only
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
-queries of stochastic quantum function value oracle.

Corollary 3.4
.

Under the same condition as in Lemma
3.2
, one can construct
{\bf O}_{\Delta{\bf{g}}_{\delta}}
with four queries to the quantum stochastic function value oracle
{\bf U}_{F}
.

Remark 3.5
.

We assume a black-box access to quantum sampling oracle
{\bf O}_{{{\bf w}},\xi}
like the previous work
[
46
]
,
In Appendix

3.2
Mini-batch Quantum Estimators via Quantum Mean Estimation

We constructed the quantum oracles
{\bf O}_{{\bf g}_{\delta}}
and
{\bf O}_{\Delta{\bf g}_{\delta}}
with
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
-queries of quantum function value oracles in Section
3.1
.
These oracles produce outputs in the form of random variables. Specifically,
{\bf O}_{{\bf g}_{\delta}}
provides an output with expectation
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})
with the input
{\bf x}
, and
{\bf O}_{\Delta{\bf g}_{\delta}}
provides an output with the expectation
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf y})
for
{\bf O}_{\Delta{\bf g}_{\delta}}
with the inputs
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
.

The variance of the outputs can be reduced by constructing the mini-batch estimator.
Inspired by the recent advance on quantum mean estimation
[
11
,
10
,
46
]
which improve the classical mini-batch estimator for multi-dimensional random variables,
we construct improved estimators for
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})
and
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf y})
.
We formally present the results in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.6
.

Under Assumption
1
, and given access to a quantum sampling oracle
{\bf O}_{\xi,{{\bf w}}}
to the joint distribution on
(\xi,{{\bf w}})
, it holds that:

1.

there exits an algorithm that can construct an unbiased quantum estimator
\hat{{\bf g}}
of
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})
such that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\hat{{\bf g}}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})\|^{2}\right]\leq\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{1}
within
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(dL\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{-1})
queries of
{\bf U}_{F}
in expectation.

2.

there exits an algorithm that can construct an unbiased quantum estimator
{\Delta}{\bf g}
of
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf y})
such that
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\Delta}{\bf g}-(\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf y}))\|^{2}\right]\leq\hat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
within
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(d^{3/2}L\|{\bf y}-{\bf x}\|\hat{\sigma}_{2}^{-1}\delta^{-1})
queries of
{\bf U}_{F}
in expectation.

Remark 3.7
.

Compared to the classical mini-batch estimator for
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})
, which requires
{\mathcal{O}}(dL^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{-2})
queries of
{\bf C}_{F}
to achieve
\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
variance level (
[
5
, Corollary 2.1]
), our mini-batch quantum estimator for
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x})
in Theorem
3.6
reduces a factor of
L\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{-1}
without increasing the dimension dependency.

4
Quantum Algorithms for Finding the Goldstein Stationary Point

In this section, we develop novel quantum algorithms for finding the
(\delta,\epsilon)
-Goldstein stationary point of a non-smooth non-convex objective
f(\cdot)
.
Instead of finding the stationary point directly, we consider finding the
\epsilon
-stationary point of its smoothed surrogate
f_{\delta}(\cdot)
, which is equivalent to the original problem according to Remark
2.2
.
The classical zeroth-order methods based on such equivalence require to access the gradient estimator to

Appendix A
Explicit Construction of Quantum Sampling Oracles

In this section, we propose a novel quantum process to realize quantum sampling oracle
{\bf O}_{{{\bf w}},\xi}:|{\bf{0}}\rangle\longmapsto\sum_{{{\bf w}},\xi}\sqrt{\Pr[{{\bf w}},\xi]}|{{\bf w}},\xi\rangle|\psi_{{{\bf w}},\xi}\rangle
with uniform distribution, where
\xi
is uniformly distributed on
\{0,\cdots,N-1\}
and
{\bf w}
is sampled uniformly on a discrete unit sphere.

The uniform distribution of
\xi
in quantum state can be constructed by using Hadamard gates.
The construction of the uniform distribution on a discrete unit sphere is more tricky.
Classically, such a distribution can be constructed by sampling each coordinate from a standard Gaussian distribution, and then normalize the vector to have unit length by dividing by its norm.
However, preparing a superposition state with Gaussian amplitude is not trivial because Gaussian distribution is defined in a infinite interval.
Constructing such state with Grover’s method
[
23
]
will lead to some issues in dealing with the domain and normalization of the measurement probability.
Instead, here, starting with the simple uniform superposition state, we use central limit theorem to construct the standard Gaussian distribution.

The overall quantum algorithm proceeds as follows:

Step
1
.

Prepare initial quantum state
\ket{0}^{\otimes m_{1}}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(dm_{2})}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(d\log m_{2})}
. Set
k=0
. Apply
H^{\otimes m_{1}}\otimes H^{\otimes dm_{2}}\otimes I
, that is, apply Hadamard gates to the first and the second registers. Here,
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N_{+}}
.

Step
2
.

Define
h:\{0,1\}^{m_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
h({\bf{j}})=2\sqrt{m_{2}}\left(\frac{j_{1}+j_{2}+\dots+j_{m_{2}}}{\sqrt{m_{2}}}-0.5\right)
. Apply
I\otimes U_{h}^{\otimes d}
, where
U_{h}
, the unitary transform corresponding to
h
, maps quantum state
\ket{\vj}\ket{0}
to quantum state
\ket{\vj}\ket{0 \plus h(\vj)}
. The
k
-th
U_{h}
takes the
k
-th
m_{2}
qubits in the second register as input, and the output is stored in the
k
-th
\log m_{2}
qubits in the third register, for all
k\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}
.

Step
3
.

Consider the third register as a
d
-dimension vector
{{\bf w}}^{\prime}
, with
\log m_{2}
qubits to store each coordinate
{{\bf w}}^{\prime}_{k}
. Apply
U_{\text{norm}}:\ket{\vw}\ket{0+\norm{\vw}}
, the result is stored in an additional ancillary register. Then normalize
{{\bf w}}^{\prime}
to have unit length by dividing by
\norm{\vw}
in each component.

Analysis and Correctness.

In Step
1
, it starts with the quantum state
\ket{0}^{\otimes m_{1}}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(dm_{2})}\otimes\ket{0}^{\otimes(d\log m_{2})}
, all of the registers initial to
0
. The first register is prepared for creating the superposition of
\xi
, and the second and third registers are prepared for creating the superposition
{\bf w}
.
We apply Hadamard gates to the first and the second registers, to obtain a uniform superposition of computation basis, which gives

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{m_{1}dm_{2}}}}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m_{1}}-1}\ket{i}\otimes\sum_{j^{(0)}_{1},\dots,j^{(0)}_{m_{2}}=0}^{1}\ket{j^{(0)}_1\dots j^{(0)}_{m_2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\sum_{j^{(d-1)}_{1},\dots,j^{(d-1)}_{m_{2}}=0}^{1}\ket{j^{(d-1)}_1\dots j^{(d-1)}_{m_2}}\otimes\ket{\0}.

Let
m_{1}=\lceil\log N\rceil
, and
we relabel the first register to obtain

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{m_{1}dm_{2}}}}\sum_{\xi}\ket{\xi}\otimes\sum_{j^{(0)}_{1},\dots,j^{(0)}_{m_{2}}=0}^{1}\ket{j^{(0)}_1\dots j^{(0)}_{m_2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\sum_{j^{(d-1)}_{1},\dots,j^{(d-1)}_{m_{2}}=0}^{1}\ket{j^{(d-1)}_1\dots j^{(d-1)}_{m_2}}\otimes\ket{\0}.

After Step 2, as each
U_{h}
operates in the same manner, we take one as an example,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{m_{1}dm_{2}}}}\sum_{\xi}\ket{\xi}\otimes\dots\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{2}}=0}^{1}\ket{j_1j_2\dots j_{m_2}}\dots\ket{2\sqrt{m_2}\left(\frac{j_1+j_2+\dots+j_{m_2}}{\sqrt{m_2}}-0.5\right)}\dots.

Once measure,
j_{1},\dots,j_{m_{2}}
are independent and identically distributed random variables with mean of
0.5
and variance of
0.25
.
By the central limit theorem, the average of
\{j_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m_{2}}
approximates the Gaussian distribution when
m_{2}
is large.
We obtain the standard Gaussian distribution after shifting and scaling the average of them.
We denote
{{\bf w}}_{k}^{\prime}\triangleq 2\sqrt{m_{2}}\left(\frac{j^{(k)}_{1}+j^{(k)}_{2}+\dots+j^{(k)}_{m_{2}}}{\sqrt{m_{2}}}-0.5\right)
,
then the measurement results of
\sum_{{\bf{j}}^{(k)}}\ket{\vw_k^{\prime}}
follow the distribution of
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
.

After Step 3, the vector in the third register is mapped into the unit sphere, which follows the uniform distribution on a discrete unit sphere. Rearrange the order of the registers, denote all the garbage qubits as
\ket{\psi_{\vw,\xi}}
, we obtain

\sum_{{{\bf w}},\xi}\sqrt{\Pr[{{\bf w}},\xi]}\ket{\vw,\xi}\ket{\psi_{\vw,\xi}},

where
{{\bf w}}={{\bf w}}^{\prime}/\norm{\vw^{\prime}}
is sampled uniformly on a discrete unit sphere and
\xi
is uniformly distributed on
\{0,\cdots,N-1\}
.
This realizes the discrete version of the quantum sample oracle
{\bf O}_{{{\bf w}},\xi}
with uniform distribution.

Remark A.1
.

In the ideal scenario where we do not need to limit the number of qubits, allowing
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
to be sufficiently large, we can achieve
\int_{{{\bf w}}\in S^{d-1}}\sqrt{\mu({{\bf w}})d{{\bf w}}}|{{\bf w}}\rangle
as needed in Proposition
3.1
.
Specifically, our process requires
m_{1}+m_{2}\times d
Hadamard gates,
{\mathcal{O}}(dm_{2})
fundamentally arithmetic operations and
1
calls to norm circuits.
Here,
m_{1}=\lceil\log N\rceil
and
m_{2}
is the number of random variable we used to approximate the Gauss distribution.
Note that many gates here can be performed in parallel, for example, all the
H
gates can be performed simultaneously, and the sum of
m_{2}
variables can be implemented in a depth
{\mathcal{O}}(\log m_{2})
circuit.
The total depth complexity is
{\mathcal{O}}(\log m_{2}+\log d)
, which indicates that the circuit depth will remain small when
m_{2}
increases,.
This ensures that our construction is feasible even under the context of NISQ quantum computer
[
42
]
, which only supports low depth circuits.
Notably, this procedure does not require querying
{\bf U}_{F}
, thereby not increasing the query complexity of
{\bf U}_{F}
.
This kind of ideal scenario is reasonable as considered in previous work

[
46
,
7
]
since we focus only on the query complexity of
{\bf U}_{F}
.

Remark A.2
.

If
\xi
is sampled from distribution other than uniform distribution,
there still exist quantum techniques which can construct such quantum sample oracle.
When the detailed classical sampling circuits are known,
we can make it reversible by replacing gates in the classical circuits with reversible quantum gates such as Toffoli gate
[
40
]
,
so as to obtain a quantum circuit
[
55
]
.
When there is only a black box access to the classical circuit, we discuss the construction by cases.
For the continues case where the distribution is described by a probability density function, we can use the Grover’s method
[
23
]
, which requires an efficient integrating circuit.
For the discrete case, we can extend the Grover’s method by the QRAM data structure.
The complexity of constructing QRAM data structure depends linearly on the size of sample space.
Once it is constructed, the complexity of generating the quantum sample oracle depends only logarithmly on the size of sample space
[
28
]
.

Appendix E
The Proof of Theorem
4.1

Proof.

According to the variance level we set,
{\bf g}_{t}
satisfies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\bf g}_{t}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}.

According to Proposition
2.1
,
f_{\delta}(\cdot)
is a nonconvex function, with
(\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1})
-Lipschitz gradient, which implies that

\displaystyle f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})+\langle\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}),{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\rangle+\frac{\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}}{2}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-\eta\langle\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}),{\bf g}_{t}\rangle+\frac{\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}}{2}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}

Taking expectation on both sides of the above inequality, we have

\displaystyle f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-\eta\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\bf g}_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-\eta\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}+\eta^{2}\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}\left(\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\bf g}_{t}-\nabla_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-\left(\eta-\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}\eta^{2}\right)\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}+\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}\eta^{2}\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2},

We let
\eta=\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt{d}L}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq 2\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}\left(f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})\right)+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}.

Summing up the above inequality, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{T}\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}}{T}\right]\leq\frac{2\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}(f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{0})-f_{\delta}^{*})}{T}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}(f(x_{0})-f^{*}+2\delta L)}{T}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}.

By setting

\displaystyle T=\left\lceil 2\epsilon^{-2}(4\sqrt{d}L^{2}+2\sqrt{d}L\delta^{-1}\Delta)\right\rceil,

and choosing
{\bf x}_{\text{out}}
randomly from
\{{\bf x}_{0},\cdots,{\bf x}_{T-1}\}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{\text{out}})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{T}\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}.

Using Theorem
3.6
, we require

\displaystyle b=\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(dL\epsilon^{-1}),

to achieve the desired variance level.
Thus the total quantum query of
{\bf U}_{F}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle b\cdot T=\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(d^{3/2}\left(\frac{L\Delta}{\epsilon^{3}\delta}+\frac{L^{2}}{\epsilon^{3}}\right)\right).

∎

The Proof of Theorem

Proof.

We denote
L_{\delta}\triangleq\frac{\sqrt{d}L}{\delta}
.
We also denote
\hat{{\bf g}}_{t+1}
as the unbiased estimator of
\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})
we have constructed in line 6 and
\Delta{\bf g}_{t+1}

we have constructed in line 8.
We can see that
{\bf g}_{t+1}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle{\bf g}_{t+1}=\begin{cases}&\hat{{\bf g}}_{t+1}~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\text{with probability $p_{t}$}\\
&{\bf g}_{t}+\Delta{\bf g}_{t+1}~~~~~\text{with probability $1-p_{t}$}\end{cases}.

According to the variance level we set in Theorem
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\hat{{\bf g}}_{t+1}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})\|^{2}\right]\leq\hat{\sigma}_{1,t+1}^{2}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2},

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\Delta{\bf g}_{t+1}-(\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t}))\|^{2}\right]\leq\hat{\sigma}_{2,t+1}^{2}=\epsilon^{2/3}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}\frac{L^{4/3}d}{\delta^{2}}.

According to Proposition
2.1
,
\nabla f_{\delta}(\cdot)
is
L_{\delta}
-Lipschitz continuous, which means

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})&\leq f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})+\langle\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t}),{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\rangle+\frac{L_{\delta}}{2}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}\\
&=f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})+\langle\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-{{\bf g}}_{t},{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\rangle+\langle{{\bf g}}_{t},{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\rangle+\frac{L_{\delta}}{2}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}\\
&\leq f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-\frac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\|{{\bf g}}_{t}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2\eta}-\frac{L_{\delta}}{2}\right)\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(9)

On the other hand, we track the variance of
{{\bf g}}_{t+1}
by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{{\bf g}}_{t+1}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})\|^{2}\right]\\
&=p_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\hat{{\bf g}}_{t+1}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})\|^{2}\right]\\
&~~~~~~~~~~~~+(1-p_{t}){\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\bf g}_{t}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})+(\Delta{\bf g}_{t+1}-(\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})))\|^{2}\right]\\
&=p_{t}\epsilon^{2}+(1-p_{t})\|{\bf g}_{t}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}+(1-p_{t})\cdot\frac{L^{4/3}\epsilon^{2/3}d}{\delta^{2}}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}.\end{split}

(10)

We have
p_{t}\equiv p
and can denote
\Phi_{t}\triangleq f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-f^{*}+\frac{\eta}{2p}\|{\bf g}_{t}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}
. Combining
eq.

9
and
eq.

10
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi_{t+1}\right]&={\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t+1})+\frac{\eta}{2p}\|{\bf g}_{t+1}-\nabla f({\bf x}_{t+1})\|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})-\frac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\|{\bf g}_{t}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}-\left(\frac{1}{2\eta}-\frac{L_{\delta}}{2}\right)\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}\right]\\
&~~~~~~~~~+\frac{\eta}{2p}{\mathbb{E}}\left[p\epsilon^{2}+(1-p)\|{\bf g}_{t}-\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}+(1-p)\frac{L^{4/3}d\epsilon^{2/3}}{\delta^{2}}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi_{t}\right]-\frac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}-\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{2\eta}-\frac{L_{\delta}}{2}-\frac{\eta(1-p)}{p}\cdot\left(\frac{L^{4/3}d\epsilon^{2/3}}{\delta^{2}}\right)\right)}_{A}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta\epsilon^{2}}{2}.\end{split}

(11)

We have chosen

\displaystyle\eta=\frac{1}{2L_{\delta}}~~\text{and}~~p=\frac{\epsilon^{2/3}}{L^{2/3}},

(12)

such that

\displaystyle A\geq\frac{\sqrt{d}L}{2\delta}-\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt{d}L}\cdot\frac{L^{2/3}}{\epsilon^{2/3}}\cdot\frac{L^{4/3}d\epsilon^{2/3}}{\delta^{2}}=0.

Then,

11

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{t})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\eta}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi_{t}-\Phi_{t+1}\right]+\epsilon^{2}.

(13)

Since it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\eta}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi_{0}-\Phi_{T}\right]

summing up

13
from

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\nabla f_{\delta}({\bf x}_{i})\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2}{\eta T}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Phi_{0}-\Phi_{T}\right]+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}.

Appendix G
Improved Results for Quantum Stochastic Smooth Non-convex Optimization

Algorithm 3

Fast Quantum Gradient Method (QGM+)

1:

Construct
{\bf g}_{0}
as an unbiased estimator of
\nabla f({\bf x}_{0})
with variance at most
\hat{\sigma}_{1,0}^{2}
.

2:

for

t=0,1\dots T

3:

{\bf x}_{t+1}={\bf x}_{t}-\eta{{\bf g}}_{t}

4:

Flip a coin
\theta_{t}\in\{0,1\}
where
P(\theta_{t}=1)=p_{t}

5:

If

\theta_{t}=1

then

6:

Construct
{{\bf g}}_{t+1}
as an unbiased quantum estimator of
\nabla f({\bf x}_{t+1})
with variance at most
\hat{\sigma}_{1,t+1}^{2}
.

7:

else

8:

Construct
\Delta{{\bf g}}_{t+1}
as an unbiased quantum estimator of
\nabla f({\bf x}_{t+1})-\nabla f({\bf x}_{t})
with variance at most
\hat{\sigma}_{2,t+1}^{2}
.

9:

{\bf g}_{t+1}={\bf g}_{t}+\Delta{{\bf g}}_{t+1}
.

10:

end for

Sidford and Zhang [46]
introduced Q-SPIDER for
smooth non-convex
optimization, with the query complexity of
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(d^{1/2}\epsilon^{-5/2})
on the quantum stochastic gradient oracle.
Using the same framework of QGFM+ , we propose the fast quantum gradient method (QGM+), which further improves the query complexity of Q-SPIDER.

We present the QGM+ in
Algorithm

3
.
The main difference between QGFM+ and QGM+ is that QGM constructs the estimators for
\nabla f({\bf x})
and
\nabla f({\bf x})-\nabla f({\bf y})
instead of their smoothed surrogates in line 6 and line 8 by using the quantum stochastic gradient oracle directly
[
46
, Definition 4]
.
We present the setting for Q-SPIDER as follows for being self-contained.

Assumption 2

(
[
46
, Setting of Theorem 7]
)
.

We assume that we are able to access the quantum stochastic oracle that outputs
\nabla F(\cdot;\xi)
which is a stochastic gradient of
f(\cdot)
that satisfies

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}[\nabla F({\bf x};\xi)]=\nabla f({\bf x}),~~~{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\left[\|\nabla F({\bf x};\xi)-\nabla f({\bf x})\|\right]\leq\sigma^{2},

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\left[\|\nabla F({\bf x};\xi)-\nabla F({\bf y};\xi)\|^{2}\right]\leq l^{2}\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{2}.

We also present the definition of
\epsilon
-stationary point of a smooth function.

Definition G.1
.

We say
{\bf x}
is an
\epsilon
-stationary point of a smooth function

, if it satisfies that

.

According to the variance level we set in Theorem

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\hat{{\bf g}}_{t+1}-\nabla f({\bf x}_{t+1})\|^{2}\right]\leq\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{1,t+1}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\Delta{\bf g}_{t+1}-(\nabla f({\bf x}_{t+1})-\nabla f({\bf x}_{t}))\|^{2}\right]\leq\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{2,t+1}=\frac{l^{2}\epsilon^{2/3}}{\sigma^{2/3}}\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\begin{split}f({\bf x}_{t+1})&\leq f({\bf x}_{t})+\langle\nabla f({\bf x}_{t}),{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\rangle+\frac{l}{2}\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|^{2}\\

(15)
