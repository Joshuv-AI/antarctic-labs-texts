Sub-community-level network analysis reveals distinct microbial dynamics in a lake littoral sediment
Authors: Vincent Tardy, Emilie Lyautey, Victor Frossard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLake littoral sediments are dynamic environments supporting diverse ecological functions, yet the temporal dynamics of their microbial communities remain understudied. This study investigated the temporal changes in microbial communities within the surficial sediments of Lake Bourget, with a high temporal resolution (2.3 ± 2 days over five months). We hypothesized that microbial temporal dynamics are shaped by different ecological processes (deterministic vs. stochastic), depending on the organizational scales considered. To test this, we examined patterns at both the whole-community and sub-community (module) scales, combining β-diversity metrics with co-occurrence network analysis.ResultsAt the community scale, temporal changes in microbial composition were relatively constrained. Null model analysis indicated that stochastic processes, such as ecological drift and homogenizing dispersal, predominated, while deterministic processes from environmental variables played a minimal role. This was supported by variance partitioning, which showed that environmental parameters explained only 17% of the temporal variation in community structure. By reducing the community into smaller co-varying groups of taxa (modules) using network-based clustering, we identified six microbial modules, each with distinct temporal patterns. The two largest modules showed contrasting responses to environmental variables, highlighting distinct ecological niches succeeding over time. At the module scale, environmental variables explained a larger fraction of temporal variation (up to 51% for Module M3), indicating that deterministic processes, particularly environmental filtering, played a more dominant role than at the whole-community scale.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that considering multiple organizational scales within microbial communities provides deeper insights into the ecological processes driving microbial temporal dynamics. Background

Characterizing the temporal dynamics of microbiomes is a major challenge in microbial ecology [1]. Microbial communities harbor vast species and metabolic diversity and have short generation times, making it essential to investigate their temporal patterns in order to predict ecosystems trajectories under global change. Advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have greatly facilitated time series studies across diverse environments, including aquatic [2–4] and terrestrial [5, 6] ecosystems as well as human the gut [7]. These studies consistently show that microbial community composition shift over time, driven by both biotic and abiotic factors specific to each ecosystem.

From conceptual and experimental framework, temporal dynamics of microbial communities result of the combination between deterministic and stochastic processes. Deterministic processes involve non-random mechanisms—such as environmental filtering or biotic interactions (e.g., competition, predation)—that consistently favor certain taxa under specific conditions. In contrast, stochastic processes include random events—such as ecological drift, random colonization, or extinction—that influence community composition regardless of environmental conditions [8]. The intensity and prevalence of these processes over time can vary based on habitat specificity [1, 9, 10], microbial taxa [11, 12], the initial size of communities [13] and the time scale considered [14, 15]. For example, the recent study of Stadler and colleagues [9] demonstrated that the strength of microbial selection processes within a large boreal aquatic network varied depending on the hydrological conditions of the habitat. In another work, Ayarza and Erijman [16] found that in lab-scale bioreactors with activated sludge flocs, stochastic processes became more frequent as species richness increased within the microbial community.

Despite these insights, a clear consensus on the balance between these processes remains elusive. The relative contribution of stochastic and deterministic drivers appears to vary depending on habitat type and community richness. These apparently contrasting results also point to a deeper conceptual issue: stochasticity may either reflect true random processes (e.g., drift, dispersal limitation) or emerge from the masking of deterministic signals in highly diverse communities, where taxa respond idiosyncratically to environmental changes. As result, the microbial dynamics in natural systems may not be governed by a single dominant process, but rather by different processes operating at different levels of organization.

In time series studies, the temporal dynamics of microbial communities are often characterized using β-diversity indices to decipher temporal shifts at the whole community scale [17]. Weighted network analysis offers a complementary and powerful approach to analyze DNA-based time series by identifying groups of species that are strongly interconnected over time. This method enables a hierarchical decomposition of the community into smaller, ecologically meaningful clusters of co-occurring taxa, referred to as “module”, getting closer to ecological niche concept [16]. This “downscaling” involves detecting modules through network-based clustering, thereby revealing fine-scale temporal variations that might be obscured when analyzing the entire community. For instance, Aylward et al. [18] applied module analysis from weighted networks to monitor daily microbial transcripts from planktonic communities across two different oceanic provinces, revealing fine-scale temporal shifts. Similarly, Lemonnier et al. [19] employed this method to evaluate seasonal influences on free-living bacterioplankton communities, identifying distinct patterns associated with ecological strategies of bacterial species, such as oligotrophs and copiotrophs.

In this study, we hypothesized that microbial dynamics are structured by different ecological processes (deterministic vs. stochastic) depending on the organizational scales considered— from the whole-community level, where stochasticity may dominate due to high diversity and spatial heterogeneity, to sub-community modules, where environmental filtering might emerge more clearly. To test this, we investigated the temporal dynamics of bacterial and archaeal communities (hereafter referred to as the microbial community) in littoral surficial sediments of a large peri-alpine lake over five consecutive months. In lake ecosystems, littoral sediments are subjected to seasonal environmental fluctuation and variable inputs from the watershed (e.g. nutrients, organic and mineral particles [20]). These areas can also be impacted by wind-induced hydrodynamic events (i.e. waves) which act as disturbances by resuspending surface layers and homogenizing the microbial habitat [21, 22]. At each time point, sediment microbial communities were characterized using high-throughput sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene, and environmental parameters – including a wave energy index derived from fetch-based modeling –were measured to quantify hydrodynamic disturbance. Temporal patterns were analyzed at both the whole-community and sub-community levels using two complementary statistical approaches.

Following the framework of Stegen et al. [23], we interpreted temporal changes in terms of three main ecological processes: selection, homogenizing dispersal and ecological drift. We expected that stochastic meteorological events would lead to temporal shifts among these processes. For instance, homogenizing dispersal was expected to prevail during wave energy periods, causing brief peaks in community turnover followed by a phase of reduced temporal variability due to community homogenization. Conversely, under more stable environmental conditions (i.e. low wave energy), environmental selection was anticipated to structure communities through changes aligned with local environmental gradients. Ecological drift was inferred when observed communities were statistically indistinguishable from random expectations – that is, when community variation over time did not significantly differ from a null model approach simulating stochastic fluctuations.

To capture microbial dynamics across different organizational levels, we applied two complementary approaches. Temporal changes in whole-community were assessed using phylogenetic beta-diversity metric, while sub-community patterns were investigated using a weighted co-occurrence network analysis. The latter allowed us to identify modules of co-varying OTUs and to relate their temporal profile to environmental drivers through module eigengene analysis. This dual approach provided a comprehensive understanding of the temporal dynamics shaping sediment microbial communities.

Methods

Site and sample collection

Lake Bourget (France) is a deep peri-alpine lake (45° 43′ 46.842" N, 5° 52′ 10.484" E, maximum depth of 147 m, length = 18 km, Additional file 1, Fig. S1a-b). Its littoral areas are mainly located in the Northern and Southern parts of the lake. The dominant wind regime is oriented from North to South (Additional file 1, Fig. S1b-c). The study site was selected in the Southern littoral area because of its high wind exposure, which is likely to induce wind-driven hydrodynamics (Fig. 1b). The sampling site (45° 39′ 8.821" N, 5° 52′ 24.888" E) consisted of a 10 × 10 square area (100m2) at 2.5 m depth. Due to this hydrodynamic exposure, the substrate was composed of sandy sediment (d50 = 210 ± 5 µm) with a low organic matter content (loss on ignition, LOI = 1.8%) [24]. From July 17th to December 10th 2015, 44 sampling events were conducted at an average interval of 2 ± 2 days. At each date, three replicate samples of surficial sediment (top 2 cm) were randomly collected within the sampling area. Samples were collected by scuba diving using 50-ml tubes dragged horizontally over ~ 50 cm. They were kept at 4 °C during transport to the laboratory and stored at −20 °C within 6 h prior to subsequent analyses.

Environmental characteristics monitoring

Water temperature and oxygen saturation were monitored hourly using a miniDOT logger installed 10 cm above the sediment surface. Water oxygen saturation remained stable around 100% over the experimental period (Additional file 1, Fig. S2) and was not selected in further analyses.

Sediment porewater was collected by centrifuging 50 mL of sediment at 6000 × g for 15 min immediately after sampling. Total phosphorus and nitrate were quantified using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES, NF EN ISO 11885) and Ionic Chromatography (NF EN ISO 10304–1) respectively.

Bulk sediment organic matter content (OM, mg g−1) was estimated by loss on ignition following [25] and total organic carbon content (TOC, mg g−1) was inferred using the following Eq. (1) from [26] and [27].

Total nitrogen was quantified using a Flash 2000 Elemental Analyzer. TOC/TN ratios (hereafter C/N ratio) were then computed to qualify the organic matter available in the sediment.

The wave energy flux (Kw m−1) at sampling site was computed using the R package waver according to Eq. (2) following [28] and [29]. Hourly measurements of wind speed at 10 m (U10, m s−1) and associated directions over the studied time period were retrieved from the meteorological station nearby the sampling site (Météo-France, Aix-les-Bains, see Appendix 1D for the wind rose). Bathymetric data from [30] were used to get water depth and support the computation of fetch distances (i.e. distances on the lake over which wind blows, m).

Every hour, the fetch distance (F) at any local spatial point was determined by identifying the minimal difference between the actual wind direction and 16 bearings (directions) at regular 22.5° increments. Once the dominant bearing was identified, the average fetch distance for 9 sub-bearings spread at 2.5° angles (from −10° to 10°) around the dominant bearing were computed to provide a robust estimate of the fetch distance. This procedure was repeated for any spatial points present in a 100 m-radius buffer around the sampling site and averaged to get robust estimates for wave energy flux at the study site.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Total sediment DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of wet sediment using a NucleoSpin Soil Kit (Macherey–Nagel EURL) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and using SL1 lysis buffer and additive Enhancer SX buffer. The extracted DNA was quantified fluorometrically after staining with QuantiFluor dsDNA Dye (QuantiFluor dsDNA System, Promega) using a Plate Chameleon™ fluorometer (Hidex; excitation: 485 nm, emission: 590 nm).

PCR amplification for high throughput 16S rRNA sequencing was carried out with the universal primer pair 515 F (5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTA-3′) and 909R (5′-CCCCGYCAATTCMTTTRAGT-3′) targeting the V4-V5 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene [31]. Indexes were integrated to both primers following the dual-indexing procedure described by [32]. Triplicate PCR amplifications for each sample were realized with a total amount of ~ 5 ng of DNA per reaction. Amplicon products were quantified using Picogreen assay (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA) and pooled equimolarly. The final pool was purified with CleanPCR beads (CleanNA). Sequencing was realized by Fasteris (Geneva, Switzerland) on an Illumina HiSeq with 2 × 300 bp. The analysis yielded 12 Gb of sequences with average error rate of 1.945%, and average Q30 of 79%.

Bioinformatic analysis

Adapters were removed using trimmomatic [33] and barcode sorted using a Fasteris internal script (Perl script). Sequences were then processed using the FROGS (Find Rapidly OTUs with Galaxy Solution) Galaxy-supported pipeline [34]. Paired-ends reads were joined using FLASH [35] and quality check was performed using FastQC. Sequences with primers having no mismatch were kept. They were then filtered by size (between 350 and 420 bp) and those containing N bases were discarded. The 16S rRNA gene sequences were then denoised and clustered using the Swarm method [36] with a 3 bases maximum difference, deletion of clusters having less than 0.005% abundance and cluster occurrence in minimum 2 samples of the total library. Chimeras were removed using uchime of vsearch package [37]. Affiliation was done using the Silva SSU database 138 [38] through BLAST [39] with allowed multiple affiliation and manual curation. Chloroplast and mitochondrial sequences were searched and removed from the dataset. In total, illumina sequencing yielded 5,591,163 high-quality 16S rRNA sequences and described 1083 OTUs. Sequencing data was then rarefied to the smallest sample in order to remove bias comparison of communities’ similarity indices between sampling dates [40], accounting 20,491 quality sequences per sample. All analyses were done on the Galaxy instance of the INRA MIGALE bioinformatics platform (http://migale.jouy.inra.fr).

Statistical analysis

Environmental parameters

Temperature and inferred wave energy were averaged over two days before the 44-sampling date to account for prior environmental conditions that could constraint microbial communities. For nutrients, porewater nitrate and phosphorus concentrations as well as bulk sediment C/N ratios at sampling times were used.

Characterizing the dynamic of microbial community structure

The temporal dynamic of the microbial community structure was characterized using weighted Unifrac distance [41] and visualized through a Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) ordination. Weighted UniFrac was chosen because it accounts for both phylogenetic relatedness and relative abundances of OTUs, thereby capturing community turnover while minimizing the disproportionate effect of sequencing noise associated with very rare taxa. An analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) was performed to test whether microbial community structure significantly differed across sampling dates.

To quantify the magnitude of temporal changes in community composition, we calculated weighted UniFrac distances between replicates (n = 3) across successive sampling dates, which reflects the degree of compositional turnover overtime. For each sampling date, mean intra-replicate distances were also quantified and compared with inter-timepoint distances using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. In addition, the relationship between inter-timepoint UniFrac distances and temporal lag (days between sampling dates) was examined using linear regression.

Following the framework proposed by Stegen et al. [22], we used a null model approach to infer the dominant ecological processes (deterministic vs. stochastic) driving these changes. The null model was built by calculating 1000 Inter Sample Distance (ISD) values between random pairs of samples drawn from the entire series, providing an expected distribution of ISD under the assumption of random community variation. Observed ISD (ISDobs) values were then compared to the null distibution (ISDnull) to identify the dominant process at each time step. If ISDobs > 90th percentile of ISDnull, community turnover was greater than expected by chance, suggesting that environmental filtering (deterministic selection) was the dominant process. If ISDobs < 10th percentile of ISDnull, community turnover was lower than expected by chance, indicating homogenizing dispersal, where physical mixing (e.g., sediment resuspension) may have led to similar communities across time. Finally, if ISDobs fell between the 10th and 90th percentiles of ISDnull, community change was not significantly different from random expectations, and ecological drift (random demographic fluctuations) was inferred to be the prevailing process.

To explore the influence of environmental factors on these temporal dynamics, we conducted a distance-based redundancy analysis (dbRDA) [42], using the best-fitting model selected by forward selection. The significance of the overall model and individual axes was tested using permutation-based ANOVA.

Weighted co-occurrence network analysis

Microbial co-occurrence network was built from pruned matrix containing OTUs with relative abundance more than 0.01% of the total number sequences. The resulting matrix contained 962 OTUs occurring at least in 25 times in 20% of sediment samples analyzed and accounted for 98.9% of the rarefied sequences dataset. Network modules (i.e., groups of strongly co-varying OTUs) were identified using the “WGCNA” R package [43] based a Pearson correlation adjacency matrix, with a soft thresholding power of 14. To enhance network robustness, the adjacency matrix was converted into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM), and OTUs were hierarchically clustered into modules using a dynamic tree cut algorithm. Different minimum module sizes (10, 20, and 30 OTUs) were tested, and a threshold of 30 OTUs was retained as it allowed modules to be clearly separated at a 70% similarity distance, while avoiding the merging of clusters sharing high similarity (> 70%). For each module, we computed a single representative abundance profile — hereafter referred to as the “module profile” – by calculating the first principal component (eigengene) of the standardized abundance table of all OTUs belonging to that module, as implemented in the moduleEigengenes() function of WGCNA [44]. This module profile summarizes the main temporal trend of variation shared by the OTUs within the module. Hub OTUs (i.e., the most central taxa within each module) were identified using the chooseTopHubInEachModule() function from the WGCNA package, which selects the node with the highest intramodular connectivity.

To assess how microbial network modules responded to environmental variation, Pearson correlations were computed between modules profiles and environmental variables. In addition, Pearson correlations were also calculated between individual OTU abundances and environmental parameters. To estimate the proportion of variance explained by environmental factors, variance partitioning analysis was performed. Significant environmental drivers were selected through a stepwise regression procedure that maximized the adjusted r2 and minimized the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) [45]. Statistical significance was assessed by 1000 permutations of the reduced model.

Significant Pearson correlations between all pairs of 962 OTUs (p < 0.05, post Benjamini–Hochberg correction [46]) with R > 0.6 or R < −0.6 were retained for network visualization and calculation of topological parameters using Gephi software (version 0.10.0).

All statistical analyses were performed using with the free R software (version 3.5.3). Data and R codes are available at the Zenodo repository 10.5281/zenodo.14615243.

Results

Environmental context

Water temperature decreased over the sampling period from 27 °C in July to 10 °C in December (Fig. 1a) with an average daily variation of 1.5 °C. The inferred wave energy revealed several brief and well-defined events occurred in September, October and late November, each lasting only a few days and standing out from the background variability. (Fig. 1b). Nitrate and phosphate measured in sediment porewater fluctuated through the sampling period (Fig. 1c) and ranged from 0.003 to 0.88 mg l−1 and from 0.03 to 0.2 mg l−1, respectively. Higher nitrate concentrations were observed in November and December period. The organic matter content of the sediment ranged from 1.05% to 2.54%, with higher values observed in November (Additional file 1, Fig. S2). The C/N ratio of the sediment organic matter decreased over sampling time period ranging on average from 20 in July and August to 14 in November and December.

Temporal dynamic and drivers at the community-scale

The NMDS ordination from weighted Unifrac distances revealed temporal shifts in bacterial community structure (confirmed by ANOSIM: R = 0.5, p = 0.001; Fig. 2a) although substantial variability among replicates from the same sampling date was also observed. Quantification of intra- versus inter-timepoint dissimilarity confirmed that inter-timepoint UniFrac distances were significantly higher than intra-replicate distances (Wilcoxon test, p = 0.003; Fig. S3a), demonstrating that temporal turnover exceeded replicate-level variability. Moreover, inter-timepoint UniFrac distances increased significantly with temporal lag (R2 = 0.18, p < 0.001; Fig. S3b), indicating progressive divergence of microbial communities over time.

dbRDA analysis on weighted Unifrac distances with our environmental constraints explained 20% of microbial community variation over time (Fisher test, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2b). According to variance partitioning, temperature was the primary driver of the microbial variability accounting for 12.4% of the total variance. It was followed by the inferred wave energy (3%) and C/N ratio (2%).

To further investigate temporal dynamics of microbial community, we used the mean weighted Unifrac distance values among triplicates between successive sampling times compared to those generated from our null model, (Fig. 2c) to identify possible ecological processes involved in the microbial community dynamics. The ISD values fluctuated between 0.04 to 0.10, with none exceeding the 90th percentile of the null model associated to selection process. ISD values falling below the 10th percentile of the null model, indicative of homogenizing processes, were primarily observed in July and October. The remaining ISD values fell within the range encompassing 80% of the null model’s values and were attributed to ecological drift.

Microbial co-occurrence network and module identification

The microbial co-occurrence network built using all replicates over the 44 sampling sessions comprised 962 nodes (OTUs) linked by 4711 edges (Fig. 3a and Additional file 1, Fig. S4 for taxonomic information). The modularity, which reflects the tendency of the network to subdivide into subcommunities (modules) that are more highly connected among themselves than with the rest of the network, was 0.61.

The weighted network analysis based on TOM dissimilarity matrix identified 6 modules (Fig. 3a) defining cluster of OTUs with similar co-variation patterns across the entire time series. Modules were characterized by different sizes and microbial composition (Fig. 3b, 3c and Additional file 1, fig. S5). Examination of the correlation structure within modules showed that all intramodular associations were positive, indicating that modules reflect groups of OTUs with synchronous temporal dynamics. The two major modules (M2 and M3) involved 255 and 222 OTUs accounting for 25% and 36% of the total amplified microbial sequences, respectively. M2 was dominated by Bacteroidetes and Betaproteobacteria affiliated OTUs (23% and 20% of module sequences) while M3 was dominated by Gammaproteobacteria affiliated OTUs (42% of module sequences for 34 OTUs). The modules M4, M5 and M6 had similar richness and sizes as they comprised from 133 to 148 OTUs and accounted respectively for 13%, 11% and 12% of the total number of reads but differed in their OTU compositions. Specifically, M4 had the most even distribution of microbial classes with co-dominance of Gammaproteobacteria and Deltaproteobacteria affiliated OTUs (25% and 20% of module sequences, respectively) followed by Chlorobi, Betaproteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes. M5 was mainly composed of Deltaproteobacteria representing 43% of the module sequences while M6 had a co-dominance of Cyanobacteria and Betaproteobacteria (23% and 19% of module sequences, respectively). Finally, the minor module (M1) accounted for only 1.7% of the total amplified microbial sequences and was dominated by Bacteroidetes and Verrucomicrobia (24% and 16% of module sequences, respectively).

Temporal dynamics and environmental drivers at the microbial module scale

Each of the 6 identified modules (M1 to M6) displayed distinct temporal dynamics, as illustrated by their module profile (Fig. 4a). M1 showed no clear tendency of temporal variation and was characterized by a pronounced peak in early September (mean eigengene score = 0.20 ± 0.27). M2 increased steadily from mid-July to a maximum in mid-August (0.25 ± 0.004), before gradually declining toward December (−0.06 ± 0.04). M3 showed low activity from July to September (e.g., eigengene score < 0), followed by a marked increase in October (0.15 ± 0.04), a plateau phase through mid-November, and a subsequent decline in late November. M4 and M5 exhibited similar bimodal patterns: both peaked in mid-July (M4 = 0.17 ± 0.06; M5 = 0.28 ± 0.14), declined afterward, and showed a secondary increase in November–December. M6 followed a distinct dynamic, remaining stable and low until mid-September, then peaking sharply (maximum = 0.19 ± 0.008 in September), before returning to lower values in October. A second rise was observed from early November to early December.

Multiple regression model with environmental parameters allowed to explain the dynamics of microbial modules (Fig. 4b and 4c). Temperature was the best driver, significantly explaining the variation in five module profiles. Temperature showed a positive correlation with the profiles of M2 and M5, while it was negatively correlated with M3, M4, and M6 (Fig. 4b). The quality of sediment organic matter, as indicated by the C/N ratio, accounted for 3%, 4%, and 6% of the variation in the profiles of M1, M2, and M3, respectively. Wave energy was identified as the third key driver, explaining 3%, and 13% of the temporal dynamics of modules M3 and M6. It was positively associated with M3 and negatively with M6. Nitrate concentration explained variations in the M3 module profile, whereas total phosphorus concentration did not emerge as a significant driver for any of module profiles.

Interestingly, contrasting patterns in response to environmental parameters were observed between the two dominant modules, M2 and M3 (Fig. 4b). Notably, when significant correlations were detected, module profiles generally exhibited stronger associations (positive or negative) than the overall OTU response (Fig. 4b).

Discussion

This study aimed at tracking the temporal dynamics of the microbial (bacterial and archaeal) community in the littoral surficial sediment of a large peri-Alpine Lake (Lake Bourget). We specifically focused on fine time scale of investigations to align with time scale (i.e., days) over which microbial community can respond to environmental changes [20, 47]. Temporal pattern of sediment microbial community was scrutinized at two different organizational scales: the community and sub-community scales (modules). We evaluated how microbial dynamics unfold over time at varying levels of organization and determined the ecological processes (deterministic versus stochastic) underpinning these dynamics.

Deciphering temporal pattern at community scales and underlying ecological processes

Sediment microbial community showed temporal changes; however, this pattern was not strongly pronounced, as microbial compositions from different dates tended to overlap. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that microbial communities in sediments exhibit greater stability compared to those in the water column, likely due to the more stable conditions in the sediment environment [48, 49]. Additionally, sediments act as repository environment, storing DNA from both active and inactive microbial communities, including dead or dormant species, as well as microbial DNA transferred from the water column by sedimentation [50]. In times-series studies based on DNA analysis this accumulation can mitigate the detection of true temporal dynamics within sediment microbial communities.

Interestingly, high intra-replicate variability was observed over time within the sediment sampling area. Similar findings have been reported in various aquatic environments [51, 52], suggesting strong sediment heterogeneity at small spatial scales. In littoral lake zones, this fine-scale spatial variability may be influenced by localized differences in nutrient and organic matter composition, particle size distribution, and the presence of other organisms [53]. Sediment heterogeneity in the sampling area may also contribute to dampening the temporal patterns observed at the community scale. Although intra-replicate variability was substantial, it remained lower than temporal turnover across the sampling period. This pattern is consistent with ecological observations in other systems, where the majority of variance is often expressed at the smallest examined scales [54], but in our case temporal dynamics still emerged as the dominant structuring factor.

Temperature acted as the dominant environmental driver of the microbial temporal pattern while wave-induce hydrodynamic as well as C/N ratio had a significant yet secondary influences. Ranking temperature as major microbial driver is due to the strong thermal gradient covered by our study during the autumn–winter period (27 °C to 10 °C), hence acting as press perturbation for sediment microbial communities over time. This result is in line with the clear seasonal successions commonly observed for microbial communities in open water [55–57]. C/N ratio is also a common driver constraining microbial communities inhabiting lake sediment [58, 59]. In our case, C:N ratio tended to decreased over time and was associated to temperature drop suggesting a link between temperature and resource quality. One possible explanation is the sedimentation of decaying littoral macrophytes and/or phytoplanktonic communities along water column during autumnal-winter period, enriching surficial sediment with low C:N organic material [60].

The non-negligible implications of wave-induced hydrodynamic as independent from temperature and C:N ratio clearly signing its rather independent effect from seasonal variation due to the stochastic occurrences of meteorological events. Wave-induced hydrodynamic is expected to enhance homogenization processes, thereby disrupting organization of microbial niches. In addition, wave-induced hydrodynamics contribute at supplying nutrient and organic matter to sediments supporting possible temporal variations at local scale [22, 61, 62]. Overall, the three environmental parameter outputs from our model explained a restricted fraction of microbial temporal pattern (only 17.6% of variance explained) suggesting that other abiotic and/or biotic variables not measured in this study should have been taken into account.

In line with the sparse variance explained by environmental drivers, selection did not appear at any time as an ecological process shaping microbial dynamic during sampling time period (ISD never exceeded 90% quantile of null model). Although dbRDA showed that temperature explained a modest but significant fraction (~ 12%) of the total variance variance across the whole time series, this apparent discrepancy highlights a scale dependence: while stochastic processes dominate short-term transitions between sampling dates, deterministic selection associated with the broader seasonal temperature gradient becomes detectable at longer temporal scales. In addition, microbial temporal dynamic did not reveal clear peaks of community composition change (ISD values) following meteorological events, as was initially expected. Thus, our hypothesis that wave-induced hydrodynamic would provoke extensive microbial rearrangements, followed by stable meteorological conditions associated with periods of low microbial turnover, was not supported. However, 45% of ISD values were lower than the 10% quantiles of our null model, indicating episodes of homogenizing dispersal. These occurred at several occasions outside meteorological events, such as in late July and more occasionally from mid-September to mid-November. This suggests that periods of homogenizing dispersal may also be driven by other factors, such as biological perturbations or hydrodynamic processes at larger scales (e.g. internal seiches [63]). The studied area shelters benthivorous fish (e.g. carps, breams) as well as diving birds (e.g. coots), that can actively resuspend surficial sediment over several centimeters depth and participate in the homogenizing dispersal process of microbial community. Complementarily, the remaining 55% of ISD values fell within the 10%−90% quantiles of the null model, suggesting that community drift was a dominant ecological process shaping sediment microbial community. Taken together, these results indicate that short-term sediment microbial dynamics were primarily driven by stochastic processes (drift and homogenizing dispersal), whereas deterministic selection associated with seasonal gradients (e.g., temperature decrease from 27 °C to 10 °C) became apparent over longer temporal scales.

Sub-communities analysis revealed distinct temporal pattern

Module detection applied in co-occurrence network enable to explore the temporal dynamics of sediment microbial community at a finer organizational scale. The resulting microbial network exhibited high modularity index (0.61) indicating a strong tendency for the network to partition into distinct modules [64]. In network theory, modules represent groups of species that occupy similar ecological niches [16] often reflecting habitat heterogeneity and niche diversification within the ecosystem [65]. In this study, six distinct modules were identified, each differing in size (relative sequence abundance), diversity (number of OTUs per module), and taxonomic composition, suggesting the coexistence of distinct microbial niches over the examined period. This finding aligns with the observed high intra-replicate variability at the community level, underlining the considerable heterogeneity of sediment within the sampling site.

As expected, analyzing microbial sub-communities through network-derived modules revealed temporal patterns that differed from those observed at the broader community scale. We identified six distinct temporal patterns, indicating a succession of module variation over time. Temporal dynamic of microbial modules appeared to be linked to changes in environmental conditions during the sampling period, which spanned from late summer to early winter. Variance partitioning analysis on module eigengenes revealed similar drivers than community scale, but the overall explained variation was more significant. For instance, temperature was identified as the most significant driver of module temporal patterns, accounting for up to 45.8% of the variation in the two largest modules (M2 and M3), as compared to the 12% obtained at the community level. This suggests that deterministic processes, such as selection by environmental filters, play a more dominant role in the microbial dynamics at the module scale than at the community scale, where stochastic events might have a greater influence. Although statistical effects of grouping cannot be entirely excluded, the modular approach highlights subsets of taxa that respond coherently to environmental drivers, thereby revealing ecological signals that remain hidden at the broader community scale. While we recognize the potential influence of compositionality and temporal autocorrelation, our objective was not to infer direct ecological interactions but rather to detect groups of OTUs with similar temporal trajectories. In this respect, our use of WGCNA with Pearson correlations follows previous studies that successfully applied this framework to microbial temporal dynamics [18, 19, 66].

Among these, the two largest microbial modules (M2 and M3) illustrate the strength of this approach. They exhibited contrasting associations with key environmental variables (temperature and C:N ratio), highlighting their distinct and potentially complementary ecological niches, succeeding over time. This pattern underlines that even within the same sediment community, different groups of taxa can follow divergent ecological trajectories in response to environmental change. The OTUs within these two modules both belong to the β-Proteobacteria class, but are affiliated with distinct genera, with different ecological characteristics. The most connected OTU (i.e., the top hub based on intramodular connectivity) in M2 was associated with the genus Comamonas. Members of this genus are aerobic and known for their diverse metabolic abilities, including the degradation of aromatic compounds, nitrate reduction, and denitrification [67]. This high metabolic versatility makes Comamonas a key player in oligotrophic sediments, where it can adapt to various nutrient conditions [68]. The most connected OTU in M3 was affiliated with Dechloromonas, a genus commonly found in lake sediments. Dechloromonas species are anaerobic and versatile, recognized for their ability to participate in anaerobic processes such as denitrification and the degradation of xenobiotic and recalcitrant organic compounds [69]. This genus can utilize nitrate and other electron acceptors under anoxic conditions, making it well-suited to lake sediments where environmental conditions often fluctuate between oxic and anoxic states.

The succession of these two central OTUs, along with their co-varying OTUs, may be influenced by the enrichment of surficial sediments with low C:N organic material during the autumn–winter period. Although oxygen was not measured in the surficial sediment, this organic enrichment can create a low-oxygen environment, favoring the establishment of Dechloromonas and other associated species over bacterial communities that interact with Comamonas. A similar seasonal pattern was observed by Yu et al., [70], who reported high abundances of Dechloromonas during autumn and winter in the sediments of the Zhangjiayan reservoir, linked to a decrease in dissolved oxygen. Consistent with these findings, Xue et al., [71] demonstrated that the addition of plant residues to sediment microcosms significantly increased the abundance of Dechloromonas within microbial communities. Interestingly, the succession observed between modules M2 and M3 might also reflect shifts in bacterial life-history strategies, with temperature changes differentially favoring slow- versus fast-growing taxa [72, 73]. Although our study did not directly infer growth strategies from 16S rRNA operon copy numbers, further investigations are needed to directly link taxonomic turnover with functional traits such as growth strategies.

As observed at the community scale, the press perturbation characterized by the seasonal decrease in temperature had a stronger influence on the littoral microbial community than pulse perturbations expressed through short-term fluctuations of wave energy. At the module scale, wave-induced hydrodynamic activity accounted for a significant 13% of the variation in the M6 module profile, where it negatively impacted the abundance of its constituent OTUs. The central OTU in this module was affiliated with the Desulfobacteraceae family, which belongs to the group of Sulfate-Reducing Bacteria (SRB). Members of this family are strictly anaerobic and are involved in the breakdown of complex organic materials, contributing to the degradation of detrital substances in lake sediments [74]. The observed decline in the M6 module profile in response to wave-induced hydrodynamics could be attributed to the resuspension of sediment particles, leading to reoxygenation of the microbial habitat and a temporary decrease in the population of strict anaerobes.

Conclusion

Applying ecological concepts to microbial communities remains challenging, and there is still no consensus on the relative contributions of stochastic versus deterministic processes in shaping microbial dynamics within ecosystems. Microbiomes are characterized by immense diversity, making it difficult to accurately assess species dynamics and their driving mechanisms at the community-scale. Our high-frequency survey in the littoral zone of a large peri-alpine lake suggests that random processes, such as drift and homogenizing dispersal, predominantly govern microbial dynamics in surficial sediments. However, when we downscaled from the community level to the module level, deterministic drivers, particularly environmental selection, appeared to play a more significant role. The presence of microbial modules that exhibit specific responses to environmental factors indicates the coexistence and succession of distinct microbial niches in littoral sediments over the study period-patterns that were not evident at the community scale. These findings suggest that considering multiple organizational scales within microbiomes can provide deeper insights into the balance between deterministic and stochastic processes driving microbial dynamics, revealing ecological patterns that may be overlooked when only the community scale is examined.
