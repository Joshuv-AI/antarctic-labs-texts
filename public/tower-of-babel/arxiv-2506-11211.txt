Anyon dispersion from non-uniform magnetic field on the sphere
Mina-Lou Schleith, Tomohiro Soejima (副島智大), Eslam Khalaf

Abstract
The discovery of fractional quantum anomalous Hall states in moiré systems has raised the interesting possibility of realizing phases of itenerant anyons. Anyon dispersion is only possible in the absence of continuous magnetic translation symmetry (CMTS). Motivated by this, we consider anyons on the sphere in the presence of a non-uniform magnetic field which breaks the SU ⁡ ( 2 ) {\rm SU}(2) rotation symmetry, the analog of CMTS on the sphere, down to a U ⁡ ( 1 ) {\rm U}(1) . This allows us to study the energy dispersion of the anyons as a function of L z L_{z} angular-momentum, while maintaining the perfect flatness of single-particle dispersion. Parametrizing the non-uniform field by a real parameter R R which concentrates the field at the north (south) pole for R > 1 R>1 ( R < 1 R<1 ), we use exact diagonalization to verify nonzero energy-angular momentum dispersion for any R ≠ 1 R\neq 1 . For our choice of non-uniform field, we show that any p p -body correlation function evaluated in the space of Laughlin quasiholes for any value of R R can be mapped exactly to a corresponding p p -body correlation function in uniform field. In the thermodynamic limit, this enables us to analytically compute the interaction-generated spatially varying potential felt by the anyons and the resulting anyon dispersion exactly, up to an overall scaling constant, for the cases of short-range and Coulomb interaction. The anyon dispersion in our model describes azimuthal motion around the sphere at a constant height, similar to spin precession. Our work therefore serves as a concrete demonstration that interaction alone can generate nonzero anyon dispersion in the presence of inhomogeneous magnetic field.

Non-uniform quantum geometry or magnetic field can induce anyon dispersion in the following way. Consider the wavefunction of anyon localized at location
\xi
. Since we are setting the kinetic energy to zero, the energy of this anyon is determined by interactions alone. Non-uniformity of quantum geometry implies the effective interaction felt by the anyon is spatially dependent, resulting in an effective potential
V[\xi]
for anyons. The latter leads to anyon dispersion, even in the absence of any single-particle kinetic energy.

In this work, we provide an explicit demonstration of this mechanism by studying anyon dispersion
on the sphere
in the presence of a non-uniform magnetic field. The spherical geometry
Haldane 1983
allows us to construct anyon wavefunctions without the subtleties arising from topological ground state degeneracy, making the analysis more transparent than that on a torus.

We numerically show that the anyon energies depend on angular momentum, corresponding to anyons that move around the
z
axis. Remarkably, we find a choice for non-uniform magnetic field which enables us to compute the anyon dispersion from the knowledge of two-body functions
in the uniform field
. This allows us to develop an analytic understanding of the anyon energetics, leading to an analytic formula for the anyon dispersion in the thermodynamic limit. Our work therefore serves as a concrete demonstration that interaction alone can generate nonzero anyon dispersion in the presence of non-uniform magnetic field or quantum geometry. We anticipate that, while the physics of our setup is likely distinct from that of periodically modulated magnetic field, relevant to ideal flat bands, the energetic origin for the dispersion in the two setups is the same.

Mapping to uniform field
—
We now show that the dispersion of of the quasiholes with non-uniform field can be understood from correlation functions of quasi-holes at
R=1
. We proceed by assuming the interaction is short-ranged such that we can write
\hat{V}=\hat{V}_{1}+\Delta\hat{V}
with sufficiently small
|\Delta\hat{V}|/|\hat{V}_{1}|
.
The dispersion in this limit can be obtained by the first order perturbation theory:
E_{m}=\bra{\Psi_{m}}\Delta V\ket{\Psi_{m}}/\braket{\Psi_{m}|\Psi_{m}}
, where
\ket{\Psi_{m}}
is the ground state for
V_{1}
with angular momentum
m
.

The zero modes of
V_{1}
can be constructed exactly along the usual line of argument for the pseudopotential interaction
Trugman and Kivelson 1985
.
The first pseudopotential
\hat{V}_{1}=\nabla_{g}^{2}\delta-G(z_{1},z_{2})
penalizes any pair of particles with relative angular momentum 1. This leads to
N+1
distinct zero modes with angular momenta
M=-\frac{N}{2},\dots,\frac{N}{2}
whose wavefunctions are given by:

\Psi^{R}_{M}(\{z_{i}\})={\rm P}_{M+N/2}(\{z_{i}\})\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{3}\prod_{i}e^{\frac{1}{2}{\rm Q}^{R}(|z_{i}|)}.

(7)

Here,
{\rm P}_{k}(\{z_{i}\})
is an
k^{\rm th}
order symmetric polynomial obtained by expanding
\prod_{i=1}^{N}(z_{i}-w)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}P_{k}(\{z_{i}\})(-w)^{N-k}
.
Clearly, we can relate the wave functions at arbitrary
R
to those at
R=1
via
\Psi_{M}^{R}(\{z_{i}\})=\Psi_{M}(\{z_{i}\})\prod_{i}\Gamma_{R}(z_{i})
where
\Gamma_{R}(z_{i})=e^{\frac{1}{2}({\rm Q}_{R}(|z_{i}|)-{\rm Q}(|z_{i}|))}
and dropping the
R
index indicates the uniform case

.

The second quantized form of these wavefunctions are given by

\ket{\Psi^{R}_{M}}=\int\prod_{i}d\mu(z_{i})\Psi^{R}_{M}(\{z_{i}\})\prod_{i}c_{R;z_{i}}^{\dagger}\ket{0}

(8)

where
c_{R;z}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m}\phi^{R}_{m}(z)c^{\dagger}_{m}
.
We show in the SM that the second quantized wavefunctions are related to each other by a simple relation
|\Psi_{M}^{R}\rangle=\hat{T}_{R}|\Psi_{M}\rangle
where
\hat{T}_{R}
is the invertible but non-unitary transformation
\hat{T}_{R}=\hat{T}_{R}^{\dagger}=e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\beta^{R}_{m}\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{m}}
. These relations do not depend on the form of the non-uniform field.

Transforming the interaction
— We now derive explicit formulae for
\epsilon_{M}^{R}
. We first note that, by explicit computation (see SM), we have

\displaystyle\hat{V}^{R}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(d(z_{1},z_{2}))c^{\dagger}_{R;z_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{R;z_{2}}c_{R;z_{2}}c_{R;z_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(d(Rz_{1},Rz_{2}))c^{\dagger}_{z_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{z_{2}}c_{z_{2}}c_{z_{1}}

(11)

with associated change in the distance function
d(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})=R\sqrt{h_{R}(z_{1})h_{R}(z_{2})}d(z_{1},z_{2})

where
h_{R}(z)=(1+|z|^{2})/(1+R^{2}|z|^{2})
. Thus, the role of the non-uniform field can be
exactly
absorbed into non-uniform interaction.
Since this is not a unitary transformation, the spectrum of the new Hamiltonian will be generally different from the old Hamiltonian. In particular, the
n

V^{n}(z_{1},z_{2})\to(R\sqrt{h_{R}(z_{1})h_{R}(z_{2})})^{-(2n+2)}V^{n}(z_{1},z_{2}).

(12)

Therefore, the transformed pseudopotential shares the same set of zero modes as the original pseudopotential.
We thus simply need to evaluate the energy of the zero modes with respect to this modified Hamiltonian, leading to

\epsilon_{M}^{R}=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})g_{M}(z_{1},z_{2}),

(13)

where
g_{M}(z_{1},z_{2})
is the pair correlation function at
R=1
.

We can understand the role of non-uniform field from Eq. (
11

1/R
for large

R
for small

\theta\approx\pi

The above mapping already allows us to relate

\Psi_{\xi}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=C_{\xi}\prod_{i}(z_{i}-\xi)\prod_{i<j}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{3}\prod_{i}e^{\frac{1}{2}Q^{R}(|z_{i}|)},

where

is the position of the quasihole, and

is the normalization constant. The wavefunctions

can be understood as spin coherent state defined as

Using the relation between

and

, we can extract the dispersion

\epsilon_{M}^{R}=\frac{(N/2+M)!}{(N)!(N/2-M)!}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi d\bar{\xi}}\right)^{N/2-M}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{N}v_{\xi}^{R}\Big|_{\xi=0}.

(16)

In the thermodynamic limit,

\epsilon_{\kappa}^{R}=v^{R}_{\xi=\sqrt{\frac{1-\kappa}{1+\kappa}}}

\xi=0

From Eq. (

. Physically, this corresponds to a quasihole at the south pole, so we denote this as

We can now compute the quasihole energy as

which shows

.

The quasihole pair correlation function

can be obtained numerically for large system sizes by performing quantum Monte Carlo

;

Thermodynamic limit
— Rather remarkably, we can derive the asymptotic form of the dispersion in the thermodynamic limit by utilizing asymptotic behavior of
g_{\rm SP}(z_{1},z_{2})
at long distances.
The pair correlation function depends on
z_{1},z_{2}
via three distance functions
d(z_{1},z_{2})
,
d(z_{1},\xi)
and
d(z_{2},\xi)
, so we can write
g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=F(d(z_{1},z_{2})/\ell_{B},d(z_{1},\xi)\ell_{B},d(z_{2},\xi)/\ell_{B})
, where we measure the distance relative to the magnetic length
\ell_{B}
. Following the plasma analogy, the dependence on these functions should be such that it saturates at distances large compared to magnetic length
\ell_{b}
due to screening. This allows us to define
g_{\rm far}(z_{1},z_{2})=F(d(z_{1},z_{2})/\ell_{B},\infty,\infty)
.
Defining
\delta g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})-g_{\rm far}(z_{1},z_{2})
, the non-uniform part of the potential energy
\delta v_{\xi}^{R}
comes from replacing
g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})

Discussion
—
Our exact mapping reveals that inhomogeneous magnetic field effectively serves as a spatially varying potential for the quasihole. Thus, our setup resembles the physics of an FQHE droplet in a confining potential
Xie et al. 1993
and our states are the anyonic analog of Fock-Darwin states. However, we emphasize that our dispersion is generated purely from interactions, while the single-particle dispersion remains flat. It is worth noting that the dynamics of anyons in our model are distinct from those in a periodic potential. For instance, a quasihole localized at a point on the sphere can only move in the azimuthal direction similar to spin precession and unlike true two-dimensional dispersion. This suggests that the physical consequences of dispersion are likely different and the model may not harbor phases such as anyon superconductor.

On the other hand, we expect the energetic mechanism for the generation of an effective potential that leads to anyon dispersion to be generic and applicable to the case of non-uniform magnetic field on the torus, which describes ideal flat bands with non-uniform quantum geometry. For an interaction of the form
\hat{V}=\alpha\hat{V}_{1}+\Delta V
with
\alpha
large, the effective anyon potential on the torus in the presence of periodic magnetic field modulation
\delta B
will be given by
v^{\delta B}_{\xi}=\int d^{2}{\bm{r}}_{1}d^{2}{\bm{r}}_{2}\Delta V({\bm{r}}_{1},{\bm{r}}_{2})g^{\delta B}_{\xi}({\bm{r}}_{1},{\bm{r}}_{2})
. In the limit of small
\delta B
, we can expand
g^{\delta B}_{\xi}({\bm{r}}_{1},{\bm{r}}_{2})
in power of
\delta B
with the coefficients expressed in terms of correlation functions evaluated for uniform field (which will contain terms beyond two-body). This describes an effective interaction that breaks CMTS and leads to a periodic potential for the anyons which goes as
\delta B
for weak fields. In a Chern 1 band, the effective magnetic field variations are proportional to Berry curvature variations
\delta\Omega
leading to quasihole dispersion which goes as
\sim\delta\Omega|\Delta V|
. This suggests the effect is leading order in the non-uniformity of the quantum geometry.

Our model allows us to extract the dependence of any correlation function of quasiholes on the non-uniform magnetic field by computing the correlation functions in the uniform field. This can enable a systematic study of how interaction between anyons is modified in the presence of non-uniform magnetic field and allows us to tackle questions such as the formation of multi-anyon bound state
Xu et al. 2025
;
Gattu and Jain 2025
for non-uniform field.

Appendix A
Lowest Landau level on the sphere

A.1
Stereographic projection

We perform a stereographic mapping from spherical coordinates to complex coordinates
z=x+iy
,
\bar{z}=x-iy
. The complex coordinate
z
is given in terms of the azimuthal and polar angles
(\phi,\theta)
by

z=re^{i\phi}=\cot\bigg(\frac{\theta}{2}\bigg)e^{i\phi}.

(22)

We also define the Haldane spinor to be

u=\cos\frac{\theta}{2}e^{i\phi,}\quad v=\sin\frac{\theta}{2},

(23)

such that
z=u/v
. These will be useful for writing down many-body wavefunctions.

We can compute how the metric is transformed from the sphere to the complex plane. The line element
ds
on the sphere is given in terms of
(\phi,\theta)
by

d^{2}s=d^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta d^{2}\phi.

(24)

Using
d\theta=2dr/(1+r^{2})
, we can easily convert this to

d^{2}s=\frac{4}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}(d^{2}r+r^{2}d^{2}\phi)=\frac{4}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}(d^{2}x+d^{2}y),

(25)

which coincides with the usual line element
d^{2}s=d^{2}r+r^{2}d^{2}\phi
of the plane up to the position-dependent factor. In the usual cartesian coordinates, the metric is therefore given by

g=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{4}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{4}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

(26)

The associated volume form is
\sqrt{g}\equiv\sqrt{|\det g|}=\frac{4}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}
. Unless otherwise specified,
g
will denote the metric in the cartesian coordinates in the following.

We next map the Laplacian on the sphere to the plane. The Laplacian on the sphere is given by

\Delta=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\partial^{2}_{\phi}.

(27)

Using
\partial_{\phi}\to\partial_{\phi}
and
\partial_{\theta}\to\frac{(1+r^{2})}{2}\partial_{r}
, we get

\Delta_{g}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\left(r\partial_{r}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{\phi}^{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\Delta_{\mathrm{plane}},

(28)

where
\Delta_{\mathrm{plane}}
is the usual Laplacian on the plane:

\Delta_{\mathrm{plane}}=\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\left(r\partial_{r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\partial_{\phi}^{2}=\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}

(29)

\Delta_{g}
is in fact the Laplace-Beltrami operator with the metric
g_{ab}
given by Eq. (
26
):

\Delta_{g}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{a}(\sqrt{g}g^{ab}\partial_{b})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}).

(30)

Let us define complex derivatives as

\displaystyle\partial_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y}),\quad\partial_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y})

(31)

such that we can write the Laplacian in complex coordinates as

\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}=4\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}.

(32)

On the stereographically projected plane, we instead get

\Delta_{g}=\frac{4}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}=(1+|z|^{2})^{2}\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}.

(33)

The integration measure can be written as

\int\sqrt{g}dxdy=\int\frac{4dxdy}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}=\int\frac{dzd\bar{z}}{2i(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}\equiv\int d\mu(z).

(34)

A.2
Uniform and non-uniform magnetic fields on the sphere

In this section, we mainly follow
Murugan et al. 2019
. The sphere is a Kähler manifold, since it is equipped with a complex structure, a Riemannian structure, and a symplectic structure. The corresponding Kähler potential
K(|z|)
is a smooth, real-valued function characterized by

\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}K(|z|)=\sqrt{g}.

(35)

For the planar and spherical geometries, the Kähler potential is given by

K(|z|)=\begin{cases}|z|^{2}\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\text{plane},\\
4\log(1+|z|^{2})\qquad\text{sphere}.\end{cases}

(36)

The magnetic field
\bm{B}=B\hat{\bm{\Omega}}=\frac{2s\Phi_{0}}{4\pi}\hat{\bm{\Omega}}
in which the particles move is given in terms of the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic components
A_{z}=A_{x}+iA_{y}
and
A_{\bar{z}}=A_{x}-iA_{y}
of the magnetic vector potential as

B=\frac{2i}{\sqrt{g}}(\partial_{\bar{z}}A_{z}-\partial_{z}A_{\bar{z}})=\frac{4i}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{\bar{z}}A_{z},

(37)

where for the last equality we used the Coulomb gauge
\partial_{\bar{z}}A_{z}+\partial_{z}A_{\bar{z}}=0
. If we define the
magnetic potential

Q(|z|)
as

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{z}Q(|z|)=-2eA_{z},\quad-i\hbar\partial_{\bar{z}}Q(|z|)=2eA_{\bar{z}}

(38)

and combine this with equation
37
, we obtain a second order differential equation for the magnetic potential

\Delta_{g}Q(|z|)=\frac{4}{\sqrt{g}}\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}Q(|z|)=-\frac{2e}{\hbar}B(|z|).

(39)

where
\Delta_{g}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator. For simplicity, in the rest of the paper we will set
e=\hbar=1
.

In the case of homogeneous magnetic flux through the spherical surface, the magnetic potential
Q(|z|)
can be chosen to be proportional to the Kähler potential as

Q(|z|)=-\frac{s}{2}K(|z|)=-2s\log(1+|z|^{2})

(40)

where
s
is the magnetic monopole strength.

To make the magnetic field inhomogeneous, we deform the magnetic potential:

Q^{R}(|z|)=2\log(1+|z|^{2})-2(s+1)\log(1+|z|^{2}/R^{2}).

(41)

For
R=1
, this reduces to the homogeneous magnetic field in equation
40
. The corresponding magnetic field is then given by

B^{R}(|z|)=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{4\pi}\biggl(2(s+1)\frac{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}{(1+|z|^{2}/R^{2})^{2}}-2\biggr).

(42)

To see that this has the same magnetic field as a function of
R
, we note that the difference of the magnetic potential

\Delta Q^{R}(z)=Q^{R}(z)-Q(z)=2(s+1)\log\frac{1+|z|^{2}}{1+|z|^{2}/R^{2}},

(43)

has no zero or singularity. In fact, this allows for a wide choice of non-uniform magnetic fields:

Q^{R}_{a}(|z|)=2(a+1)\log(1+|z|^{2})-2(s+a+1)\log(1+|z|^{2}/R^{2}).

(44)

We focus on the
a=0
case in our paper, as it gives us analytical handle over anyon dispersion.

A.3
LLL wave functions on the sphere

The Pauli Hamiltonian for spin polarized electrons on a Riemann surface is given in terms of the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic components of the kinetic momentum
\pi_{z}=-i\hbar\partial_{z}-eA_{z},\,\pi_{\bar{z}}=-i\hbar\partial_{\bar{z}}-eA_{\bar{z}}
by
Iengo and Li 1994

H=\frac{1}{2m}\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\pi_{z}\pi_{\bar{z}}+\frac{2-g_{s}}{8}e\hbar B\biggr),

(45)

where
g_{s}
is the Lande g-factor which is approximately
2
for spin-
1/2
particles. Using the magnetic potential, we can rewrite the Hamiltonian

H=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{g}}\biggl(\partial_{z}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{z}Q^{R}(|z|)\biggr)\biggl(\partial_{\bar{z}}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\bar{z}}Q^{R}(|z|)\biggr)+\frac{2-g_{s}}{4}B.

(46)

It is now natural to introduce the transformation
\tilde{H}=e^{\frac{1}{2}Q^{R}}He^{-\frac{s}{2}Q^{R}}
such that the transformed Hamiltonian reads

\tilde{H}=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{g}}\biggl(\partial_{z}-\partial_{z}Q^{R}(|z|)\biggr)\partial_{\bar{z}}+\frac{2-g_{s}}{4}B.

(47)

Clearly, any holomorphic wave function is a zero mode of the first term of the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
. For generic
g_{s}
, the second term, which is inhomogeneous for
R\neq 1
, spoils the solvability of the Hamiltonian. If we set
g_{s}=2
, however, these modes are degenerate and constitute the LLL of the free fermion. For
R=1
, the simplest choice of basis for the LLL is then given by
Haldane 1983

\phi_{m}(z,\bar{z})=\alpha_{s,m}z^{s+m}e^{Q(|z|)/2},

(48)

where
z^{s+m}
is the holomorphic part satisfying
\partial_{\bar{z}}z^{s+m}=0
. The range of angular momentum is limited to
m=-s,\ldots,s
as the wavefunction becomes singular or unnormalizable otherwise.

In a non-uniform field, the LLL eigenstates are given by

\phi^{R}_{s,m}(z,\bar{z})=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{m}}\alpha_{s,m}z^{s+m}e^{\frac{1}{2}Q^{R}(|z|)}

(49)

with
Q^{R}(|z|)
parameterized as in equation
41
. The degeneracy remains
N_{\phi}+1
for the LLL basis, with
m=-s,-s+1\dots,s
. The wave functions are normalizable with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\int\frac{4dxdy}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}\overline{\phi_{s,m}^{R}(z,\bar{z})}\phi_{s,m}^{R}(z,\bar{z}).

(50)

For our choice of magnetic potential
Q_{R}(|z|)
in equation
41
, this reduces to a simple form which can be calculated as follows

\displaystyle e^{\beta_{m}}

\displaystyle=|\alpha_{s,m}|^{2}\int\frac{4dxdy}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}\frac{|z|^{2(s+m)}(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}{(1+|z|^{2}/R^{2})^{2(s+1)}}

(51)

\displaystyle=|\alpha_{s,m}|^{2}\int 4dxdy\frac{|z|^{2(s+m)}}{(1+|z|^{2}/R^{2})^{(2s+1)}}

(52)

\displaystyle=R^{2(s+m+1)}|\alpha_{s,m}|^{2}\int 4dxdy\frac{|z|^{2(s+m)}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2(s+1)}}

(53)

\displaystyle=R^{2(s+m+1)},

(54)

where we used
|\alpha_{s,m}|^{-2}=\int 4dxdy\frac{|z|^{2(s+m)}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2(s+1)}}
.

Appendix B
Interacting Hamiltonian

B.1
Interactions on the sphere

We consider spherically symmetric interactions, such that the sole source of rotational symmetry breaking arises from the single-particle part of the Hamiltonian. To achieve this, we demand that the interaction only depends on the chord distance:

d(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})=2\sin\left(\frac{\Omega_{12}}{2}\right),

(55)

where
\Omega_{12}
is the angle between
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
. The Coulomb interaction is given by

V_{\mathrm{Coulomb}}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})=\frac{1}{d(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})}.

(56)

The
n
-th pseudopotential, which can be used to expand short-range interaction
Trugman and Kivelson 1985
, is defined by

V^{n}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}(\Delta)^{n}\delta(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}),

(57)

where
\Delta
is the standard Laplacian on the sphere. For the spin-polarized fermionic problem of interest, only odd
n
contribute.

To map this to the plane, we first note that the distance function can be written as

d(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{2|z_{1}-z_{2}|}{\sqrt{1+|z_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|z_{2}|^{2}}}.

(58)

The form of the Coulomb interaction immediately follows.
The pseudopotential can be written as

\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\Delta_{g}^{n}\left(\frac{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}{4}\delta(x_{1}-x_{2})\delta(y_{1}-y_{2})\right),

(59)

where we used the mapping

\delta_{g}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}{4}\delta(x_{1}-x_{2})\delta(y_{1}-y_{2}).

(60)

The correctness of this map can be confirmed by noting

\int d\mu(z)\delta_{g}(z,z_{0})f(z)=f(z_{0}),

(61)

as expected for a delta function.

B.2
Second quantized form of the interaction

For the numerical ED, we evaluate
2
-body scattering elements between fermions in the LLL. Let us start by writing the most general form of a
2
-body interaction in the angular momentum basis in second quantization

\hat{V}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{4s-1}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=-s}^{s}V_{\ell;mm^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{m}c^{\dagger}_{\ell-m}c_{m^{\prime}}c_{\ell-m^{\prime}},

(62)

where
\ell
is the total
L_{z}
angular momentum of the particle pair and
m
and
m^{\prime}
are their relative angular momenta. The interaction matrix element
V_{\ell;mm^{\prime}}
can be calculated from the following integral

\displaystyle V_{\ell;mm^{\prime}}=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})\phi_{s,m}^{R,*}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1})\phi_{s,\ell-m}^{R,*}(z_{2},\bar{z}_{2})V(z_{1},z_{2})\phi^{R}_{s,\ell-m^{\prime}}(z_{2},\bar{z}_{2})\phi^{R}_{s,m^{\prime}}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1}).

(63)

For spherically symmetric interactions, we can expand
V(z_{1}-z_{2})
in terms of the complete and orthonormal set of spherical harmonics
{\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}
as

\displaystyle V(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}V_{\ell}\bar{\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1}){\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}(z_{2},\bar{z}_{2}).

(64)

The spherical harmonics
{\rm Y}_{\ell,m}
are joint eigenfunctions of the angular momentum operators
\bm{L}^{2}
and
L_{z}
with eigenvalues
-\ell(\ell+1)
and
m
respectively.

The interaction matrix element given in equation
63
therefore separates into two independent integrals and can be written as

V_{\ell;mm^{\prime}}=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{2s}\sum_{m^{\prime\prime}=-\ell}^{\ell}V_{\ell^{\prime}}\bar{\lambda}_{\ell,m;R}^{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime}}\lambda_{\ell,m^{\prime};R}^{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime}},

(65)

where the form factors
\lambda_{\ell,m^{\prime};R}^{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime}}
are given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell,m^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime};R}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z)\phi^{R}_{s,m^{\prime}}(z,\bar{z})\phi_{s,\ell-m^{\prime}}^{R}(z,\bar{z})Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime\prime}}(z,\bar{z})

(66)

In the spherically symmetric case, these form factors have been evaluated
Wu and Yang 1976
;
Wu and Yang 1977

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell,m^{\prime}}^{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z)\phi_{s,m^{\prime}}(z,\bar{z})\phi_{s,\ell-m^{\prime}}(z,\bar{z})Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime\prime}}(z,\bar{z})

(67)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{2s+\ell}(2s+1)\bigg(\frac{(2\ell+1)}{4\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}s&s&\ell^{\prime}\\
m^{\prime}&\ell-m^{\prime}&-m^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}s&s&\ell^{\prime}\\
s&s&0\end{pmatrix},

(68)

where the matrices are
3
-j symbols that are proportional to the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients. In order for the
3
-j symbols to be non-zero, the triangle relation
0<\ell^{\prime}\leq 2s
has to be fulfilled. Furthermore, momentum conservation guarantees that
\ell-m^{\prime}=0
. In the case of non-uniform magnetic field, we calculate the form factors numerically.

Let us finally compute
V_{\ell}
for Coulomb and pseudopotential interactions. It is convenient to compute
V_{\ell}
in the spherical basis, and then map things to the plane.
The long-ranged Coulomb interaction can be expanded as

\frac{1}{d(\bm{\Omega}_{1},\bm{\Omega}_{2})}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-2\cos\theta_{12}}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{\rm P}_{\ell}(\cos\theta_{12}),

(69)

where
\theta_{12}
is the angle between the particles on the sphere. Using the spherical harmonic addition theorem

{\rm P}_{\ell}(\cos\theta_{12})=\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\bar{\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}(z_{1},\bar{z}_{1}){\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}(z_{2},\bar{z}_{2}),

(70)

we get

V_{\ell,\mathrm{Coulomb}}=\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}.

(71)

The pseudopotential, on the other hand, can be obtained by expanding the delta function and acting with the Laplacian on it:

\displaystyle V^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell,m}\Delta^{n}\bar{\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}(\Omega_{1}){\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}(\Omega_{2})

(72)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell,m}(-\ell(\ell+1))^{n}\bar{\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}(\Omega_{1}){\rm Y}_{\ell}^{m}(\Omega_{2}),

(73)

from which we can read off

V^{n}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}(-\ell(\ell+1))^{n}.

(74)

B.3
Transforming the Hamiltonian

The treatment above was quite general, and it applies to any choice of inhomogeneous magnetic field, not just the one we used in the main text. Our choice for
Q^{R}
, however, allows significant simplifications.

To see this, let us evaluate the second quantized Hamiltonian slightly differently. To do so, let us first rewrite the density-density interaction:

\displaystyle:\hat{\rho}_{z_{1}}^{R}\hat{\rho}_{z_{2}}^{R}:

\displaystyle=c^{\dagger}_{R;z_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{R;_{2}}c_{R;z_{2}}c_{R;z_{1}}

(75)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3},m_{4}}\overline{\phi^{R}_{m_{1}}(z_{1})}\overline{\phi^{R}_{m_{2}}(z_{2})}\phi^{R}_{m_{3}}(z_{2})\phi^{R}_{m_{4}}(z_{1})c^{\dagger}_{m_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{m_{2}}c_{m_{3}}c_{m_{4}},

(76)

where we used the projected position operator
c_{R;z}=\sum_{m}\phi^{R}_{m}(z)c_{m}
. Note that
c_{m}
does not carry
R
labels, since their commutation relations do not depend on
R
.

Integrating this against the interaction, we get

\displaystyle\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(z_{1},z_{2}):\hat{\rho}_{z_{1}}^{R}\hat{\rho}_{z_{2}}^{R}:

(77)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(z_{1},z_{2})\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\overline{\phi^{R}_{m_{1}}(z_{1})}\overline{\phi^{R}_{m_{2}}(z_{2})}\phi^{R}_{m_{3}}(z_{2})\phi^{R}_{m_{1}+m_{2}-m_{3}}(z_{1})c^{\dagger}_{m_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{m_{2}}c_{m_{3}}c_{m_{4}},

(78)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(z_{1},z_{2})R^{-4}\frac{(1+|z_{1}|^{2})^{2}}{(1+\frac{|z_{1}|^{2}}{R^{2}})^{2}}\frac{(1+|z_{2}|^{2})^{2}}{(1+\frac{|z_{2}|^{2}}{R^{2}})^{2}}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\overline{\phi_{m_{1}}(\frac{z_{1}}{R})}\overline{\phi_{m_{2}}(\frac{z_{2}}{R})}\phi_{m_{3}}(\frac{z_{2}}{R})\phi_{m_{1}+m_{2}-m_{3}}(\frac{z_{1}}{R})c^{\dagger}_{m_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{m_{2}}c_{m_{3}}c_{m_{4}},

(79)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})\sum_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}\overline{\phi_{m_{1}}(z_{1})}\overline{\phi_{m_{2}}(z_{2})}\phi_{m_{3}}(z_{2})\phi_{m_{1}+m_{2}-m_{3}}(z_{1})c^{\dagger}_{m_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{m_{2}}c_{m_{3}}c_{m_{4}}

(80)

where we used

\phi^{R}_{m}(z_{1})=e^{-\frac{\beta_{m}}{2}}\frac{\displaystyle\left(1+\lvert z_{1}\rvert^{2}\right)^{(s+1)}}{\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\lvert z_{1}\rvert^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)^{(s+1)}}\;\phi_{m}(z_{1})=\alpha_{s,m}\,R^{-(s+m+1)}\frac{\displaystyle z_{1}^{\,s+m}}{\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\lvert z_{1}\rvert^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)^{s}}\;\frac{\displaystyle\left(1+\lvert z_{1}\rvert^{2}\right)}{\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\lvert z_{1}\rvert^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)}=R^{-1}\,\frac{\displaystyle\left(1+\lvert z_{1}\rvert^{2}\right)}{\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{\lvert z_{1}\rvert^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)}\;\phi_{m}\!\Bigl(\frac{z_{1}}{R}\Bigr)\,.

(81)

Therefore, the spectrum of the interacting Hamiltonian with
V(z_{1},z_{2})
and non-uniform field
Q^{R}
can be reproduced by the interaction
V(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})
with uniform field
Q
.

Appendix C
Mapping to uniform field

The wavefunctions in the non-uniform magnetic field are given in Eq.
49
. We introduce an invertible, non-unitary transformation in second quantization:

\hat{T}_{R}=\hat{T}_{R}^{\dagger}=e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}\beta_{m}^{R}\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{m}},\qquad\hat{T}_{R}\hat{c}_{m}\hat{T}_{R}^{-1}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{m}^{R}}\hat{c}_{m}.

(82)

The quasihole states
|\Psi_{M}\rangle
with total momentum
M
then transform as

|\Psi^{\rm R}_{M}\rangle=\hat{T}_{R}|\Psi_{M}\rangle.

(83)

Now consider a (first-quantized) interaction
V(z_{1},z_{2})
and write the anyon dispersion as

\epsilon_{m}^{R}=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(z_{1},z_{2})g_{M}^{R}(z_{1},z_{2})

(84)

where
d\mu(z)=\sqrt{g(z)}d^{2}z=\frac{4}{1+|z|^{2}}d^{2}z
is the integration measure and
g_{m}^{R}(z_{1},z_{2})
is the pair correlation function defined as

g_{M}^{R}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\langle\Psi_{M}^{R}|c_{R;z_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{R;z_{2}}^{\dagger}c_{R;z_{2}}c_{R;z_{1}}|\Psi_{M}^{R}\rangle}{\langle\Psi_{M}^{R}|\Psi_{M}^{R}\rangle}

(85)

where we made the
R
dependence for the electron creation/annihilation operators explicit. These operators can be expanded in terms of angular momentum operators
c_{R;m}
as

c_{R;z}=\sum_{m=-s}^{s}\phi_{s,m}^{R}(z,\bar{z})c_{R;m}=e^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta Q^{R}(|z|)}\hat{T}_{R}c_{z}\hat{T}_{R}^{-1}

(86)

Substituting in (
85
) yields

g_{M}^{R}(z_{1},z_{2})=e^{\Delta Q^{R}(|z_{1}|)}e^{\Delta Q^{R}(|z_{2}|)}\frac{\langle\Psi_{M}|c_{z_{1}}^{\dagger}c_{z_{2}}^{\dagger}T_{R}^{2}c_{z_{2}}c_{z_{1}}|\Psi_{M}\rangle}{\langle\Psi_{M}|T_{R}^{2}|\Psi_{M}\rangle}.

(87)

This formula works for any choice of
Q^{R}
, as long as we can evaluate the action of
T_{R}
.

Appendix D
Extracting anyon dispersion

D.1
Quasihole wavefunctions in angular momentum and coherent state representations

The quasiholes
|\Psi_{m}\rangle
form a representation of the angular momentum operators. Since there are
N+1
quasiholes states with
m=-\frac{N}{2},\dots,\frac{N}{2}
, this corresponds to the
l=\frac{N}{2}
representation. This means that we can express the normalized quasihole wavefunctions in terms of the lowest angular momentum state via

|\Psi_{m}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{(l-m)!}{2l!(l+m)!}}L_{+}^{m+l}|\Psi_{-l}\rangle

(88)

The explicit wavefunction for the quasihole with
m=-l
can be written as

\Psi_{-l}(\{u,v\})=C\prod_{i<j}(u_{i}v_{j}-u_{j}v_{i})^{3}\prod_{i}v_{i}

(89)

where
C
is a normalization constant that only depends on
N
. The raising operator takes the form
L_{+}=\sum_{i}u_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{i}}
. We can verify that it commutes with
\prod_{i<j}(u_{i}v_{j}-u_{j}v_{i})^{3}
. Its action on the last part
\prod_{i}v_{i}
takes the form

L^{k}_{+}\prod_{i}v_{i}=k!P_{k}(\{z_{i}\})\prod_{i}v_{i}

(90)

where
z=u/v
and
P_{k}
are the symmetric polynomials

P_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=\sum_{r_{1}<r_{2}<\dots<r_{k}}z_{r_{1}}\dots z_{r_{k}}

(91)

For concreteness, consider
N=3
. Then

\displaystyle\prod_{i}v_{i}=v_{1}v_{2}v_{3},\qquad L_{+}\prod_{i}v_{i}=v_{1}v_{2}u_{3}+v_{1}u_{2}v_{3}+u_{1}v_{2}v_{3}=(z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3})v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}

(92)

\displaystyle L_{+}^{2}\prod_{i}v_{i}=2(u_{1}u_{2}v_{3}+u_{1}v_{2}u_{3}+v_{1}u_{2}u_{3})=2(z_{1}z_{2}+z_{1}z_{3}+z_{2}z_{3})v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}

(93)

\displaystyle L_{+}^{3}\prod_{i}v_{i}=6u_{1}u_{2}u_{3}=6z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}

(94)

We can then write the normalized quasihole wavefunctions at arbitrary angular momentum
m
as

\Psi_{m}(\{z_{i}\})=\sqrt{\frac{(l-m)!}{2l!(l+m)!}}L_{+}^{m+l}\Psi_{-l}=\sqrt{\frac{(l-m)!(m+l)!}{2l!}}P_{m+l}(\{z_{i}\})\Psi_{-l}(\{z_{i}\})

(95)

Now instead of consider angular momentum basis, we can also consider the wavefunction for a quasihole localized at point
\xi
given by

\Psi_{\xi}(\{z_{i}\})=C_{\xi}\Psi_{-l}(\{z_{i}\})\prod_{i}(z_{i}-\xi)=C_{\xi}\Psi_{-l}(\{z_{i}\})\sum_{m=-l}^{l}P_{l+m}(\{z_{i}\})(-\xi)^{l-m}

(96)

where
C_{\xi}
is a normalization constant that generally depends on
\xi
. Using Eq. (
95
), we can relate the normalized second quantized wavefunctions
|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle
and
|\psi_{m}\rangle

|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle=C_{\xi}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\binom{2l}{l-m}^{1/2}(-\xi)^{l-m}|\Psi_{m}\rangle

(97)

which allows us to determine the normalization constant
C_{\xi}
via

1=\langle\Psi_{\xi}|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle=|C_{\xi}|^{2}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\binom{2l}{l-m}|\xi|^{2(l-m)}=|C_{\xi}|^{2}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{2l},\quad\implies\quad C_{\xi}=\frac{1}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{l}}

(98)

Another way to write this expression is to note that

\xi^{2l}e^{-\xi^{-1}L_{+}}|\Psi_{-l}\rangle=\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\frac{(-\xi)^{l-m}}{(m+l)!}L_{+}^{m+l}|\Psi_{-l}\rangle=\sum_{m=-l}^{l}(-\xi)^{l-m}\binom{2l}{l+m}^{1/2}|\Psi_{m}\rangle=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{l}|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle

(99)

leading to

|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle=\frac{\xi^{2l}}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{l}}e^{-\xi^{-1}L_{+}}|\Psi_{-l}\rangle

(100)

This is the familiar expression for spin coherent states.

We can also express
|\Psi_{m}\rangle
in terms of
|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle
through

|\Psi_{m}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{(l+m)!}{(2l)!(l-m)!}}\left(-\frac{d}{d\xi}\right)^{l-m}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{l}|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle\Big|_{\xi=0}

(101)

This allows us to relate the expectation value of any operator
\hat{O}
in the states
|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle
and
|\Psi_{m}\rangle
using

\langle\Psi_{m}|\hat{O}|\Psi_{m}\rangle=\frac{(l+m)!}{(2l)!(l-m)!}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi d\bar{\xi}}\right)^{l-m}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{2l}\langle\Psi_{\xi}|\hat{O}|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle\Big|_{\xi=0}

(102)

If the operator
\hat{O}
commutes with
\hat{L}_{z}
, we can also write

\langle\Psi_{\xi}|\hat{O}|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle=\frac{1}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{2l}}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\binom{2l}{l-m}|\xi|^{2(l-m)}\langle\Psi_{m}|\hat{O}|\Psi_{m}\rangle

(103)

In particular, by choosing
\hat{O}
to be the second quantized form of the interaction
V(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})
, we can get

\epsilon_{m}^{R}=\frac{(l+m)!}{(2l)!(l-m)!}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi d\bar{\xi}}\right)^{l-m}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{2l}v_{\xi}^{R}\Big|_{\xi=0},\qquad v_{\xi}^{R}=\frac{1}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{2l}}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\binom{2l}{l-m}|\xi|^{2(l-m)}\epsilon_{m}^{R}

(104)

We will find it more helpful to derive an alternative inversion formula. For this, it is useful to define the variable

y=\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2}}=\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}

(105)

which goes from 0 at the north pole to 1 at the south pole. We can then use Eq.
104
to write (we will drop the
R
superscript for simplicity)

v_{y}=\sum_{m=-l}^{l}\binom{2l}{l-m}y^{(l+m)}(1-y)^{(l-m)}\epsilon_{m}=\sum_{m=-l}^{l}B^{2l}_{l+m}\epsilon_{m}=\sum_{m=-l}^{l}B^{2l}_{l+m}(y)\epsilon_{m}

(106)

where
B_{m}^{q}(y):=\binom{q}{m}y^{m}(1-y)^{q-m}
is the Bernstein basis. For a function expressed in the Bernstein basis, we have the inversion formula

\epsilon_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{m+l}\frac{v_{0}^{(k)}}{k!}\binom{m+l}{k}\binom{2l}{k}^{-1},

(107)

where
v_{0}^{(k)}
is the
k
-th term in the taylor series expansion of
v_{y}
. This expression particularly simplifies in the thermodynamic limit where
l
is taken to be large. It is then useful to also introduce the variable
\kappa=\frac{m}{l}
with
-1\leq\kappa\leq 1
. Then we can simplify

\binom{m+l}{k}\binom{2l}{k}^{-1}=\left(\frac{1+\kappa}{2}\right)^{k}+O(1/l)

(108)

which leads to the expression for the dispersion

\epsilon^{R}_{\kappa}=v^{R}_{y=\frac{1+\kappa}{2}}=v^{R}_{\xi=\sqrt{\frac{1-\kappa}{1+\kappa}}}

(109)

where we have restored the
R
-dependence.

D.2
Laughlin quasihole pair correlation functions

It follows from Eqs.
104
,
107
,
109
that it suffices to compute the anyon potential energy
v_{\xi}^{R}
to determine the anyon dispersion. The latter is expression in terms of the pair correlation function for a Laughlin quasihole at
\xi
for uniform field

v^{R}_{\xi}=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2}),\qquad g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=\langle\psi_{\xi}|f_{z_{1}}^{\dagger}f_{z_{2}}^{\dagger}f_{z_{2}}f_{z_{1}}|\Psi_{\xi}\rangle,

(110)

We will find it easier to write the expression for
g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})
using the first-quantized wavefunctions. First, we define

\gamma(z_{1},z_{2}):=\frac{2(z_{1}-z_{2})}{\sqrt{1+|z_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|z_{2}|^{2}}}=\frac{2(u_{1}/v_{1}-u_{2}/v_{2})}{\sqrt{1+|u_{1}/v_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|u_{2}/v_{2}|^{2}}}=2(u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1})

(111)

\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})
is a complex function whose absolute value is the arc length defined earlier in Eq.
58
,
|\gamma(z_{1},z_{2})|=d(z_{1},z_{2})
. The Laughlin state is given by

\Psi(\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\})=\prod_{i<j}\gamma(z_{i},z_{j})^{3}.

(112)

Likewise the Laughlin QHs are

\Psi_{\xi}(\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\})=\prod_{i}\gamma(z_{i},\xi)\prod_{i<j}\gamma(z_{i},z_{j})^{3}

(113)

which implies that we can write

g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=d(z_{1},z_{2})^{6}f_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})

(114)

where

f_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=d(z_{1},\xi)^{2}d(z_{2},\xi)^{2}\int\prod_{i=3}^{N}d\mu(z_{i})d(z_{1},z_{i})^{6}d(z_{2},z_{i})^{6}d(z_{i},\xi)^{2}\prod_{2<i<j}d(z_{i},z_{j})^{6}

(115)

While
g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})
is a complicated functions of
\xi
,
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
, it is easy to see it is invariant under simultaneously applying any isometry for
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
, and
\xi
since both the distance function
d
and the measure
\mu
are invariant under isometries. This means that
g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2},\xi)
only depends on the distances between the three points
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
, and
\xi
:

g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=g(d(z_{1},z_{2}),d(z_{1},\xi),d(z_{2},\xi))

(116)

This allows us to express the pair correlation function for a hole at an arbitrary point
\xi
in terms of the pair correlation function for a hole at the noth or south pole. For simplicity, we choose the south pole
\xi=0
and define the möbius transformation
w_{\xi}(z)=\frac{z-\xi}{1+\bar{\xi}z}
which preserves the metric and distance and satisfies
w_{\xi}(\xi)=0
leading to

g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=g_{\rm SP}(w_{\xi}(z_{1}),w_{\xi}(z_{2}))

(117)

where
g_{\rm NP}(w_{1},w_{2})
is the pair correlation function for a hole at the south pole. Note that this does not only depend on the distance between
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
but also their distance to the south pole.

D.3
Dispersion in non-uniform field

Eq.
117
and
110
allow us to compute the dispersion for any given interaction from the knowledge of the pair correlation function for a single quasihole localized at the south pole. We do this by moving the dependence of
\xi
in (
110
) completely to the interaction

v^{R}_{\xi}=\int d\mu(w_{1})d\mu(w_{2})V(Rz_{\xi}(w_{1}),Rz_{\xi}(w_{2}))g_{\rm SP}(w_{1},w_{2})

(118)

where
z_{\xi}(w):=\frac{w+\xi}{1-\bar{\xi}w}
satisfies
z_{\xi}(w_{\xi}(z))=z
. As a consistency check, we can verify that for
R=1
and for
V
invariant under isometries, we get
V(z_{\xi}(w_{1}),z_{\xi}(w_{2}))=V(w_{1},w_{2})
and the integral is independent on
\xi
as expected. We can further simplify this expression by assuming the interaction is only a function of the distance
d(z_{1},z_{2})
and using the relations

\displaystyle d(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})=R\frac{2|z_{1}-z_{2}|}{\sqrt{1+R^{2}|z_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{1+R^{2}|z_{2}|^{2}}}=R\sqrt{h_{R}(z_{1})h_{R}(z_{2})}d(z_{1},z_{2}),\qquad h_{R}(z)=\frac{1+|z|^{2}}{1+R^{2}|z|^{2}}

(119)

\displaystyle h_{R}(z_{\xi}(w))=\frac{(1+|\xi|^{2})(1+|w|^{2})}{(1+|\xi|^{2})(1+|w|^{2})+(R^{2}-1)|w+\xi|^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{R^{2}-1}{4}d^{2}(w,-\xi)}

(120)

which together, yields

d(Rz_{\xi}(w_{1}),Rz_{\xi}(w_{2}))=R\frac{d(w_{1},w_{2})}{\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}-1}{4}d^{2}(w_{1},-\xi)}\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}-1}{4}d^{2}(w_{2},-\xi)}}.

(122)

Substituting in (
110
) or (
118
) yields

\displaystyle v_{\xi}^{R}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{1})V\left(R\sqrt{h_{R}(z_{1})h_{R}(z_{2})}d(z_{1},z_{2})\right)g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(w_{1})d\mu(w_{1})V\left(R\frac{d(w_{1},w_{2})}{\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}-1}{4}d(w_{1},-\xi)^{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}-1}{4}d(w_{2},-\xi)^{2}}}\right)g_{\rm SP}(w_{1},w_{2})

(123)

This expression allows us to compute the effective potential felt by quasiholes and the resulting dispersion, using Eq.
107
, for any interaction for finite or infinite system once we know
g_{\rm SP}(w_{1},w_{2})
. In the following, we will discuss further simplifications for specific interactions or in the thermodynamic limit.

D.3.1
Coulomb interaction

The above expression immediately implies the following quasihole energy for Coulomb interaction:

\displaystyle v_{\xi}^{R}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{1})\frac{1}{Rd(z_{1},z_{2})\sqrt{h_{R}(z_{1})h_{R}(z_{2})}}g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(w_{1})d\mu(w_{2})\frac{\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}-1}{4}d^{2}(w_{1},-\xi)}\sqrt{1+\frac{R^{2}-1}{4}d^{2}(w_{2},-\xi)}}{R}g_{\rm SP}(w_{1},w_{2})

(124)

D.3.2
Pseudopotentials

Psuedopotentials are expansions of short-range interactions in terms of derivatives of the delta function. For convenience, let us collect the results on the curved space that we will use. First, the integration measure acquires a metric dependence.

d\mu(z)=\frac{4}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}dxdy

(125)

In order for the delta function to satisfy the expected property

\int d\mu(z)\delta_{g}(z,z_{0})f(z)=f(z_{0})

(126)

its metric dependence should cancel the part in the integration measure

\delta_{g}(z,z_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\delta(z-z_{0})=\frac{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}{4}\delta(z-z_{0}),

(127)

where
\delta(z-z_{0})=\delta(x-x_{0})\delta(y-y_{0})
.
This can be written in terms of
\gamma(z,z_{0})
defined in Eq. (
58
) by noting that

\delta(\gamma(z,z_{0}))=\frac{(1+|z|^{2})(1+|z_{0}|^{2})}{4}\delta(z-z_{0})=\frac{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}{4}\delta(z-z_{0})=\delta_{g}(z,z_{0})

(128)

The Laplace operator (Eq. (
33
)) takes the form

\Delta_{g}=(1+|z|^{2})^{2}\partial_{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}.

(129)

It satisfies

\int d\mu(z)g(z)\Delta f(z)=\int d\mu(z)f(z)\Delta g(z)

(130)

In particular, this implies the identity

\int d\mu(z)f(z)\Delta^{n}\delta_{g}(z,z_{0})=\Delta^{n}f(z)\Big|_{z=z_{0}}

(131)

which implies

\int d\mu(z)d(z,z_{0})^{2m}\Delta^{n}\delta_{g}(z,z_{0})=\begin{cases}(n!)^{2}&:m=n\\
0&:m>n\end{cases}

(132)

Under a dilation
z\mapsto Rz
, the metric changes by
\sqrt{g(z)}\mapsto\sqrt{g(Rz)}=R^{2}h_{R}(z)^{2}\sqrt{g(z)}
. which means that

\delta_{g}(z,z_{0})\mapsto\frac{1}{R^{2}h_{R}(z)^{2}}\delta_{g}(z,z_{0}),\qquad d\mu(z)\mapsto R^{2}h_{R}(z)^{2}d\mu(z),\qquad\Delta\mapsto\frac{1}{R^{2}h_{R}(z)^{2}}\Delta

(133)

Thus, the energy for the pseudopotential
V_{3}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{3!^{2}}\Delta^{3}\delta_{g}(z_{1},z_{2})
is given by

\displaystyle v_{\xi}^{R}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3!^{2}}\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})\frac{1}{R^{8}h_{R}(z)^{8}}(\Delta^{3}\delta_{g}(z_{1},z_{2}))d(z_{1},z_{2})^{6}f_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(z)\frac{1}{R^{8}h_{R}(z)^{8}}f_{\xi}(z,z)=\frac{1}{R^{8}}\int d\mu(w)\left(1+\frac{R^{2}-1}{4}d^{2}(w,-\xi)\right)^{8}f_{\rm SP}(w,w)

(134)

Appendix E
Anyon dispersion in the thermodynamic limit

A main simplification for evaluating the anyon dispersion in the thermodynamic limit is the following. As the system size
s
increases (for a fixed radius of the sphere), the magnetic length
l_{B}\sim 1/\sqrt{s}
decreases. The pair correlation function
g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})
is a function of the distances
d(z_{1},z_{2})
,
d(z_{1},\xi)
and
d(z_{2},\xi)
. For each pair of variables, we expect the function to depend on this distance only up to few magnetic lengths and then to saturate to its value when such pair of variables are very far away. This follows from the plasma analogy where the charges are screened over a distance
\sim l_{B}

(
)

. Thus, we can write

g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=F\left(\frac{d(z_{1},\xi)}{l_{B}},\frac{d(z_{2},\xi)}{l_{B}},\frac{d(z_{1},z_{2})}{l_{B}}\right)

(135)

Now clearly, if both
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are far away from
\xi
, then the dependence on
\xi
drops from this function and we get a constant contribution to
v_{\xi}^{R}
which does not contribute to the dispersion. Thus, we only get contributions to the dispersion if either or both
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are close to
\xi
. This contributions are expected to be negative compared to the asymptotic value of the function since they correspond to a dip where
z_{1,2}
approaches the position of the quasihole. We distinguish two cases.

1.

Short-range interaction
V(d(z_{1},z_{2}))=W_{\eta}(d(z_{1},z_{2})/\eta)
with
\eta\lesssim l_{B}
:

In this case, both
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
will be close to
\xi
. For non-uniform field, we have
d(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})=R\sqrt{h_{R}(z_{1})h_{R}(z_{2})}d(z_{1},z_{2})
but the function
h_{R}(z)
changes slowly (on the scale of the radius of the sphere), so we can replace
z
inside
h_{R}(z)
by
\xi
up to corrections which are small in
l_{B}
. Effectively, this means the simplification
V(d(Rz_{1},Rz_{2}))=V(Rh_{R}(\xi)d(z_{1},z_{2}))
which amounts to the replacement

\eta\mapsto\eta_{\xi}^{R}=\frac{\eta}{Rh_{R}(\xi)}=\frac{1+R^{2}|\xi|^{2}}{R(1+|\xi|^{2})}\eta

(136)

We can then expand the interaction in pseudopotentials

V(d(Rz_{1},Rz_{2}))=\sum_{n=0}(\eta_{\xi}^{R})^{2(2n+2)}c_{n}\hat{V}_{2n+1}(z_{1},z_{2}),\qquad\hat{V}_{m}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{1}{m!}\Delta^{m}\delta(d(z_{1},z_{2}))

(137)

Note here that, usually, short-range interactions are defined with a normalization that also depends on
\eta
. For instance,
V_{\eta}(d(z_{1},z_{2}))=\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}e^{-\frac{d(z_{1},z_{2})^{2}}{2\eta^{2}}}
which ensures
V_{\eta\rightarrow 0}(d(z_{1},z_{2}))\propto\delta(d(z_{1},z_{2}))
. However, our transformation only affects the
\eta
that enters in the functional form combined with
d(z_{1},z_{2})
but not the normalization. This means that the dependence on
\eta_{\xi}^{R}
in the power series has an extra power of 2 compared to the standard expression. For instance, for
V_{3}
, we get
(\eta_{\xi}^{R})^{8}
not
(\eta_{\xi}^{R})^{6}
which is also consistent with Eq.
134

Each of the terms
\hat{V}_{2n+1}
is rotationally symmetry and corresponds to the pseudopotential in the uniform field case. Since the function
g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})
is also rotationally symmetric, the integral
\int d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})V_{2n+1}(z_{1},z_{2})g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})
is independent of
\xi
. Thus, all the energy dependence is encoded in the factors
\eta_{\xi}^{R}
, leading to the expression

v_{\xi}^{R}=a_{0}(R)-\sum_{n=1}(\eta_{\xi}^{R})^{2(2n+2)}a_{n}c_{n}

(138)

where
a_{0}(R)
comes from the asymptotic value of
g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})
where they are far away from
\xi
. The minus sign encodes the fact that the deviation of
g
from its asymptotic value is expected to have a negative integral. If our residual interaction is
\hat{V}_{3}
, such that
c_{1}=1
and
c_{n>1}=0
, this further simplifies to

v_{\xi}^{R}=a_{0}(R)-a_{3}\left(\frac{1+R^{2}|\xi|^{2}}{R(1+|\xi|^{2})}\right)^{8},

(139)

Thus, the spatial variations in the potential felt by the quasihole is determined fully analytically up to an overall multiplicative constant and a shift. Using Eq.
109
leads to the dispersion

\epsilon^{R}_{\kappa}=a_{0}(R)-a_{3}\left(\frac{1+\kappa+R^{2}(1-\kappa)}{2R}\right)^{8}

(140)

2.

Coulomb interaction:

For Coulomb interaction, the dominant contribution to the integral (
124
) comes from having one of
z_{1,2}
close to
\xi
and one far away (having both close to
\xi
have a smaller phase space). Without loss of generality, we can choose
z_{1}
to be far away from
\xi
and
z_{2}
to be close to
\xi
. Then we can make the replacements

g_{\xi}(z_{1},z_{2})=F(\infty,d(z_{2},\xi)/l_{B},\infty),\qquad d(Rz_{1},Rz_{2})=R\sqrt{h_{R}(z_{1})h_{R}(\xi)}d(z_{1},\xi)

(141)

The integral over
z_{2}
can then be done giving a constant that does not depend on
\xi
(since the integrand only depends on
d(z_{2},\xi)
). Then we are left with the expression

v_{\xi}^{R}\propto\frac{1}{R\sqrt{h_{R}(\xi)}}\int d\mu(z)\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_{R}(z)}d(z,-\xi)}\propto\frac{\sqrt{1+R^{2}|\xi|^{2}}}{R}\int d^{2}z\frac{(1+R^{2}|z|^{2})^{1/2}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}\frac{1}{|z+\xi|}

(142)

Noting that the integral depends only on
|\xi|
, we can assume
\xi
is real and write

\displaystyle I(R,\xi)

\displaystyle:=\int d^{2}z\frac{(1+R^{2}|z|^{2})^{1/2}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}\frac{1}{|z+\xi|}=\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\frac{(1+R^{2}r^{2})^{1/2}}{(1+r^{2})^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\xi^{2}+2r\xi\cos\phi}}

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\frac{(1+R^{2}r^{2})^{1/2}}{(1+r^{2})^{2}(r+\xi)}K\left(\frac{4r\xi}{(r+\xi)^{2}}\right)

(143)

where
K(r)
is the elliptic integral of the first kind. This yields the expression for the potential

v_{\xi}^{R}=\alpha(R)-\beta\frac{\sqrt{1+R^{2}|\xi|^{2}}}{R}\int_{0}^{\infty}drr\frac{(1+R^{2}r^{2})^{1/2}}{(1+r^{2})^{2}(r+\xi)}K\left(\frac{4r\xi}{(r+\xi)^{2}}\right)

(144)

The dispersion is obtained by simply substituting
\xi=\sqrt{\frac{1-\kappa}{1+\kappa}}
in this equation.
