On the emergence of quantum memory in non-Markovian dynamics Preprint: APS/123-QED
Alexander Yosifov, Aditya Iyer, Vlatko Vedral, Jinzhao Sun

Abstract
Quantum systems are often hindered by decoherence due to impact from the environment. While memoryless Markovian collision models are commonly used to approximate such evolution, non-Markovian dynamics (with memory) is typical in practice, with memory effects being harnessed as a resource for many tasks like quantum error correction and information processing. Yet, the type of memory—classical or quantum—necessary to realize the dynamics of many collision models is not known. In this work, we extend the quantum homogenizer to the non-Markovian regime by introducing intra-ancilla interactions mediated by Fredkin gates, and study the nature of its memory. Using entanglement measures and relying only on the local dynamics as a witness, we prove the model can be realized with either classical or quantum memory, depending on the initialization of the reservoir and the propagation of non-classical correlations within the reservoir. We further explore how quantum memory emerges across a wide range of practical scenarios. The results shed light on the origin of memory in open quantum systems and can advent the design of near-term quantum technologies for a variety of applications.

How quantum memory emerges?
—Our goal is to identify how different physically-relevant conditions—such as the initial state of the reservoir and its entanglement structure—dictate the nature of the emerging memory effects from the system’s reduced dynamics.

In contrast, perturbed GHZ states contain weaker, more delocalized correlations, requiring stronger coupling to activate memory effects—highlighting the Bell state as a natural benchmark for the minimal interaction strength needed to reveal quantum memory.

This observation has important consequences for exchange-based models. While the Heisenberg exchange is optimal in gate count

In these cases, even high-fidelity local operations may be subject to non-Markovian errors. By identifying when classical or quantum memory is needed, our framework provides a classification of quantum channels implementability via repeated interactions. While memoryless CPTP maps (e.g., depolarizing and amplitude damping) can be simulated with classical memory, structured environments with asymmetric or coherent entanglement require quantum memory.

Meanwhile, for surface codes in exchange-based systems, it is known that temporally correlated errors on syndrome qubits

While we leave a complete theoretical framework characterizing the emergence of quantum memory for future work, our results provide strong evidence that these features play a decisive role in shaping memory behavior. Specifically, we observe that even globally entangled states may not necessitate quantum memory, while certain coherent superpositions with separable marginals do. These findings suggest that the emergence of quantum memory is governed not just by entanglement structure of the reservoir qubits, but also by the propagation of non-classical correlations through the reservoir via controlled-

Conclusion.
—In this work, we extended the standard quantum homogenizer to the non-Markovian regime by incorporating ancilla-ancilla interactions with Fredkin gates. The motivation for this was twofold. First, CMs have attracted significant attention lately for their role in providing a tractable framework for studying the dynamics of open quantum systems. Second, non-Markovianity has been successfully used as a resource in many schemes, where its memory effects have proven critical in improving quantum control and error correction tasks on solid state, NMR and superconducting qubits. However, to fully utilize such non-Markovian CMs for other tasks and streamline the performance of quantum devices, we must first answer the question: What type of memory is needed to realize the dynamics, and how is that related to the entanglement structure of the environment?

In the main part of this work, we utilized a criterion that only looks at the local dynamics as a witness to the quantumness of the memory. Considering the entanglement of assistance and the concurrence as a measure, we examined how the proposed system evolves, and presented conditions for determining the required memory type. To study the nature of the memory, we consider the minimal model for the reservoir to describe the non-Markovian dynamics.
Crucially, we showed that the dynamics of the non-Markovian homogenizer can be realized either way, depending on the initialization of the reservoir and the entanglement structure. That is, while an initial product state allows for classical memory, an initial
asymmetric
entangled state (prevalent in many realistic noise models) or perturbed state necessitate truly quantum memory, Figs.
3
c and
3
d. Moreover, we numerically demonstrated the state convergence properties (i.e., the effectiveness) of this machine (see Fig.
2
) and compared it against the traditional Markovian homogenizer. Interestingly, our simulation corroborated earlier results
vlatko2
that analytically predicted the trajectories of the evolving states.

Our results suggest that for many practical models, even local interactions between the system and the reservoir reveal genuinely quantum correlations that cannot be reproduced by classical memory alone. In addition, small imperfections in structured environments can activate memory effects even earlier, underscoring the need for error correction schemes that account for correlated, history-dependent noise in realistic quantum devices. Our findings not only reveal when and how quantum memory becomes necessary to describe non-Markovian evolution, but also suggest a route toward engineering and characterizing memory in physical systems. The ability to tune memory activation via initial entanglement structure or reservoir interactions may prove useful for designing noise models that better reflect real hardware behavior, or even for constructing memory-aware error correction schemes that exploit environmental coherence.
