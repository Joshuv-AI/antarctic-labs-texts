CD86 costimulation enhances the antitumor activity of NKG2D CAR-Macrophages and synergizes with Anti-PD-L1 therapy to suppress prostate cancer progression
Authors: Abdulrahman Ibrahim, Zihao Liang, Wan Liu, Xiaofei Di, Lawan Rabiu, Rong Li, Pengchao Zhang, Muhammad Auwal Saliu, Maoxuan Liu, Guizhong Zhang, Xiaochun Wan, Dehong Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer remains one of the most prevalent solid tumors in men worldwide and poses a major therapeutic challenge due to its immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME), which is characterized by low T cell infiltration, limited antigen presentation, and high levels of immunosuppressive factors. While chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment of hematologic malignancies, it has yielded limited success in solid tumors such as prostate cancer. Macrophage-based CAR (CAR-M) therapies have emerged as promising alternatives, owing to macrophages’ natural tumor-infiltrating capacity, phagocytic activity, and ability to modulate the TME. However, conventional CAR-Ms exhibit limited capacity to prime adaptive immunity due to insufficient co-stimulatory signaling. To address this limitation, we engineered two novel NKG2D-targeted CAR constructs for macrophages: one containing the intracellular Fc receptor common γ chain (FcRγ) alone (CAR-M), and another incorporating both FcRγ and the intracellular domain of the co-stimulatory molecule CD86 (CD86-CAR-M). These constructs were expressed in Raw264.7 macrophages or and evaluated for antigen-specific phagocytosis, tumor cell cytotoxicity, cytokine secretion, and T cell activation in vitro. In vivo efficacy and safety were assessed using both xenograft and syngeneic mouse models of prostate cancer, including combination therapy with anti-PD-L1 checkpoint blockade. Both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M exhibited robust, antigen-specific phagocytosis and cytotoxicity against NKG2D ligand-expressing prostate cancer cells in vitro. CD86-CAR-Ms demonstrated enhanced M1 polarization, higher expression of CD86 and superior activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, accompanied by increased secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α. This interaction triggers the phosphorylation of ERK, a central mediator of M1 macrophage polarization and inflammatory responses. In vivo, CD86-CAR-Ms achieved greater tumor suppression and survival benefit than CAR-Ms in immunocompetent models and significantly enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 antibody therapy without systemic toxicity. The improved anti-tumor effect was associated with increased T cell, NK cell infiltration and macrophages MHC-II expression, indicating TME reprogramming from cold to hot one. This study presents the first demonstration of CD86-enhanced CAR-macrophages targeting NKG2D ligands in prostate cancer. By integrating innate and adaptive immune activation, CD86-CAR-Ms represent a novel and potent strategy for overcoming immunosuppression and improving the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in solid tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02548-7.Keywords: CAR-Macrophages (CAR-M), Immunotherapy, NKG2D ligands, Prostate cancer, Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 10 million deaths annually. Among solid malignancies, prostate cancer (PCa) exhibits the highest incidence in men globally [1]. Despite advances in cancer immunotherapy, its clinical efficacy in PCa has been limited. Prostate tumors are considered immunologically “cold,” characterized by low tumor mutational burden, impaired antigen presentation, and minimal infiltration of effector T cells [2]. Although chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy has revolutionized treatment for hematologic malignancies, its effectiveness in solid tumors including PCa has been hindered by poor T cell trafficking, antigen heterogeneity, and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [3, 4]. The TME in PCa is enriched with regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), all of which limit CAR-T persistence and effector function [5]. Additionally, immune checkpoint blockade, particularly PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, has shown minimal clinical benefit in PCa, likely due to poor T cell priming and extensive immune exclusion [6]. These challenges highlight the need for innovative approaches that can penetrate, remodel the TME and enhance antitumor immunity.

NKG2D (natural killer group 2 D) is a type II transmembrane activating receptor expressed on NK cells and subsets of CD8⁺, CD4⁺, and γδ T cells. It recognizes a range of NKG2D ligands (NKG2DLs) including MHC class I-related polypeptides (MICA, MICB) and UL16-binding proteins (ULBPs) in humans or MHC class I-like molecules (Rae-1, MULT1, and H60 family members) in mice, which are frequently upregulated on malignant cells but largely absent from healthy tissues [7]. Unlike prostate-associated antigens such as PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen) or PSCA (prostate stem cell antigen), NKG2DLs are inducibly expressed across diverse tumor types and are less susceptible to antigen escape due to their upregulation in response to oncogenic stress, DNA damage, and immune surveillance [2, 3]. Although NKG2D-targeted CAR-T cells have demonstrated promising antitumor activity in preclinical studies, their clinical translation remains limited due to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, restricted T cell infiltration, and poor persistence [8, 9].

Macrophages, as innate immune effectors and professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), are uniquely positioned to address these limitations. Compared to T cells, macrophages are less prone to exhaustion and cytokine-release syndromes when used as adaptive cellular therapy [6, 10], however, mechanistic studies have shown that host monocytes/macrophages drive CAR-T induced CRS which can be mitigated by pathway blockade [11, 12]. Apart from a few case reports of exceptional clonal expansions usually associated with inopportune lentiviral integrations into oncogenes, CAR-Ms do not undergo “massive clonal expansion” [13, 14]. Preclinical studies demonstrate antigen-specific activity with controlled inflammation, suggesting distinct cytokine profiles compared to CAR-T [15], making them suitable for targeting immunologically resistant tumors such as PCa. CAR-Ms have emerged as a promising alternative to CAR-T therapy due to their intrinsic ability to infiltrate solid tumors, mediate phagocytosis, and reshape the TME through cytokine secretion and antigen presentation [6, 16]. Moreover, incorporating macrophage-specific signaling domains may further enhance their functionality. Second-generation CAR-Ms engineered with Fc receptor (FcR)-based intracellular domains have demonstrated superior antitumor activity through enhanced phagocytosis and proinflammatory activation [17, 18]. However, their capacity to costimulate and prime T cells remains suboptimal.

CD86 is a key co-stimulatory molecule expressed on pro-inflammatory (M1) macrophages that plays a critical role in promoting macrophage activation, as well as T cell priming, proliferation, and survival [19]. This interaction triggers downstream signaling events, including the upregulation of ERK, a central mediator of M1 macrophage polarization and inflammatory responses [20]. Therefore, by incorporating CD86 into CAR macrophages, we expect to synergize the tumor-targeting properties of CARs with enhanced T cell engagement, thereby promoting a more robust and sustained anti-tumor function. This approach aligns with the concept of harnessing immune cell activation to potentiate the therapeutic potential of CAR-macrophages in cancer immunotherapy [21, 22]. In this study, we sought to augment the immunostimulatory capacity of NKG2D CAR-macrophages by integrating the intracellular domain of CD86 into the CAR construct containing FcRγ signaling (designated NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms). We employed both in vitro and in vivo mouse models to compare the anti-tumor efficacy of NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms with that of conventional NKG2D CAR-Ms in the treatment of prostate cancer. Notably, NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms demonstrated a superior ability to enhance immune checkpoint therapy in preclinical prostate cancer models.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Mouse macrophage cell line Raw264.7, human monocytic cell line THP-1, human prostate cancer cell line PC3, murine prostate cancer cell line RM-1, murine melanoma cell line B16-F10 and human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T were provided by the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) Cell Bank. They were cultured in RPMI-1640 (VivaCell, China) or DMEM (VivaCell, China) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (VivaCell, China) and 1% antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin-0.1 mg/mL streptomycin) (Beyotime, China) at 37 ℃ and under 5% CO2. The medium were changed every 2–3 days, and cell passage was performed once the cells grew to about 70–80% confluence. Raw264.7 cells were maintained without enzymatic dissociation to preserve their phenotype and adherence properties. Instead, adherent cells were gently detached by repeatedly pipetting the culture medium to dislodge them from the culture surface. The resulting cell suspension was collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in fresh complete medium and subcultured at a routine ratio of 1:5. This procedure was consistently applied to ensure optimal cell viability and stable growth characteristics across passages. The PC-3, B16-F10 and RM-1 cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding luciferase or mCherry to construct target cells. Flow cytometry were performed to confirm the efficiency of transduction.

Experimental animals

C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Zhuhai Bestest Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Zhuhai, China). NCG (NOD/ShiltJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt) mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). These animals were housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in the Animal Facilities of the Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology (SIAT), CAS, with a 12-hour light-dark cycle. All in vivo animal experiments were performed in accordance with the institutional guidelines for the ethical treatment of animals and received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of SIAT, CAS under the protocols SIAT-IACUC-200,617-YYS-YDH-A1293 (from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2023), SIAT-IACUC-231,124-YYS-YDH-A2393 (from 1 December 2023 to 31 October 2025) and SIAT-IACUC-231,208-YYS-YDH-A2407 (from 1 January 2024 to 30 November 2025).

Plasmic design and lentiviral production

The synthetic NKG2D CAR were constructed as previously described [23]. In our design, we replaced the CD3ζ signaling domain with the FcRγ signaling domain (amino acids 45–86, UniProt accession P30273). The sequence for the CD86 costimulatory domain was derived from the internal signaling region of CD86, spanning amino acids 269–329 (UniProt accession P42081). The construct was synthesized by Genewiz (China) and cloned into pWPXLd vector (Addgene, USA), yielding two constructs: CAR-M (without CD86 costimulatory domain) and CD86-CAR-M (with CD86 costimulatory domain) while GFP only encoding plasmid were used as control macrophages (CTRL). These CARs expressed under the control of the EF1α promoter. Lentivirus were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the lentiviral backbone vector containing the constructs of interest alongside the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G (Addgene, USA), using polyethylenimine linear (PEI) 4000 (Yeasen, China) as the transfection reagent. The plasmids were introduced at a ratio of 5:3:2, respectively. The medium was replaced with fresh medium 6 h post-transfection. Lentiviral particles were collected from the cell culture supernatant 48 h post-transfection.

CAR macrophage cell production

Lentiviral supernatant harvested at 48 h post-transfection were filtered through a 0.45 μm PES filter (Merck Millipore, USA). Subsequently, Raw264.7 cells at a confluency of approximately 30–50% were transduced with the lentivirus encoding CAR constructs in the presence of 5 µg/ml polybrene (Beyotime, China). Similarly, the human monocytic cell line THP-1 was also transduced with the CAR-encoding lentivirus. To enhance viral transduction efficiency, the cells were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 60 min. The culture medium was replaced 24 h post-transduction, and CAR expression was evaluated 72 h later.

Flow cytometry

Cells were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, VivaCell, China) and incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C. A detailed list of antibodies used for flow cytometry can be found in Additional file: Table S1. For CAR surface staining, the anti-human/mouse NKG2D monoclonal antibody labeled with PE was sourced from Biolegend. To evaluate the expression of human NKG2DL, PE-anti-human MICA/B were used and for murine NKG2DL, we used a two-step staining protocol: primary stain with Armenian hamster anti-mouse MULT1 antibody, followed by secondary stain with mouse anti-Armenian hamster IgG-PE.

For tumor infiltrating immune cells, tumor leukocytes were isolated as previously described [24]. Briefly, tumors excised from RM-1 tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice were aseptically dissociated using a cocktail of collagenase I, IV, dnase and hyaluronidase (Sigma, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h. Tumor-infiltrating leukocytes were collected, washed, and resuspended in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS before antibody staining. Viability was determined using the Zombie NIR Fixable Viability Kit. Flow cytometry was performed on a Cytek® Aurora system and data were analyzed using SpectroFlo software (https://cytekbio.com/pages/spectro-flo, accessed on 14 November 2024).

Western blotting

Western blotting was conducted as previously described [25], following collection, cells were washed twice with PBS. Total proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland) to prevent degradation. After whole-cell lysis, samples were centrifuged at 4 °C to pellet cellular debris. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was quantified using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by electrophoresis on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels (Genefist, China) and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, USA). Membranes were probed with specific antibodies, as listed in Additional file: Table S1. Detection of membrane-anchored proteins was performed with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Yeasen, China) with an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare, UK).

RT-qPCR

Following coculture of CAR-Ms with tumor cells, GFP-positive CAR-Ms were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) MA900 cell sorter (Sony, Japan). Total RNA was isolated from CAR-Ms using AG RNAex Pro RNA reagent (Agbio, China) followed by concentration determination with a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Reverse transcription was carried out with Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit (Agbio, China) to generate cDNA templates. Quantitative PCR amplification was conducted on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Mix Kit (Agbio, China). The 2 − ΔΔCt method was employed for relative quantification with β-actin serving as the endogenous control. All target-specific primers (listed in Additional file: Table S2) were commercially synthesized by Genewiz (China).

Phagocytosis assay

FACS based phagocytosis assay was performed as previously described [26] with some modification. Briefly, CTRL, CAR-Ms or CD86-CAR-Ms were cocultured with mCherry-expressing target cells for 4 h at 37 °C. After coculture, total cells were harvested, washed with PBS and then analyzed by flow cytometry. The percentage of mCherry-expressing cells within the GFP-positive macrophage population represented the target cells phagocytosed by macrophages.

Cytotoxicity assay

Luciferase-base cytotoxicity assay was performed as previously described [14]. Briefly, luciferase-expressing PC-3, RM-1 and B16-F10 cells were used as targets cells and were placed at different E: T ratios with macrophages in a black opague 96-well plate. Prior to measuring bioluminescence, D-luciferin at a concentration of 150 µg/mL was added and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min before readings were taken using a SPECTROstar Omega microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Baden-Württemberg, Germany). The percentage of specific lysis was determined based on the relative luciferase signal (total flux) in comparison to the signal from tumor cells alone. The calculation was performed using the formula: % specific lysis = [(Signal from the sample - Signal from tumor alone)/(Background signal - Signal from tumor alone)] × 100.

T cell isolation and co-culture with CAR-M

CAR-Ms, RM-1 cells, and T cells were co-cultured as previously described [27]. In brief, CTRL, CAR-Ms or CD86-CAR-Ms were incubated with RM-1 cells for 4 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. CD3+ T cells were isolated from C57BL/6 mouse spleens using the MojoSort™ Mouse CD3 T Cell Isolation Kit (BioLegend, Cat# 480023) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The freshly purified T cells were then introduced into the pre-established CAR-M/RM-1 co-culture system and maintained for 48 h under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). After incubation, culture supernatants were collected, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent cytokine analysis.

Cytokine release assays

The concentrations of mouse IFN-γ (Dakewe, Cat# 1210002), TNF-α (Dakewe, Cat# 1217202), TGF-β (Dakewe, Cat# 1217102), IL-13 (Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd, Cat# EK0425), and IL-1β (Dakewe, Cat# 1210122) in cell culture supernatants, blood samples, and tumor lysate were measured using ELISA kits following the manufacturers’ protocols.

Xenograft tumor models

To establish a prostate cancer xenograft model, immunodeficient NCG mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were subcutaneously inoculated with 5 × 105 PC-3 cells on the right flank, with this day designated as day 0. Tumor growth was monitored through regular observation and measurement of tumor diameters using calipers. Once tumors reached a palpable size of approximately 100 mm3, mice were stratified into three groups (n = 4 per group). Each group was then administered 200 µL of either CTRL, CAR-M, or CD86-CAR-M cells (2 × 106 cells per mouse) via intravenous (I.V.) injection. Body weight and tumor dimensions of the mice were recorded every 2 days up to day 18, with the longest longitudinal measurement considered as the length and the widest transverse measurement as the width. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: V = length ×width2 × 0.5. On day 18, animals were humanely euthanized and vital organs and tumor tissues were harvested weighed, and prepared for further analysis.

Syngeneic tumor models

To establish a prostate cancer syngeneic model, C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were subcutaneously inoculated with 5 × 105 RM-1 cells on the right flank, with this day designated as day 0. Tumor growth was monitored through regular observation and measurement of tumor diameters using caliper. Once tumors reached an approximate size of approximately 100 mm3, mice were stratified into three groups (n = 4 per group). Each group was then administered 200 µL of either CTRL, CAR-M, or CD86-CAR-M cells (2 × 106 cells per mouse) via intravenous (I.V.) injection. For combination therapy with anti-PD-L1 antibody, mice bearing tumors of approximately 100 mm³ were stratified into eight groups (n = 4 per group): UTD, CTRL, CAR-M, CD86-CAR-M, αPD-L1, CTRL + αPD-L1, CAR-M + αPD-L1, and CD86-CAR-M + αPD-L1. CAR macrophages (2 × 106 cells/mouse) were administered intravenously on days 6 and 12, while anti-PD-L1 antibody (200 µg/mouse) was administered intraperitoneally every three days starting from day 6 (days 6, 9, and 12). Body weight and tumor dimensions were recorded every two days until day 18, with the longest measurement defined as tumor length and the widest as tumor width. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: V = (length × width²) × 0.5. On day 18, mice were humanely euthanized, and tumor tissues and vital organs were harvested, weighed, and processed for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA) was used to conduct statistical analyses. Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed by Student’s t test when only two value sets were compared. One-way ANOVA was used when the data involved three groups. P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001 or P < 0.0001were considered statistically significant and indicated by *, **, *** or **** respectively.

Results

Human NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms have the similar anti-prostate tumor activity as human NKG2D CAR-Ms in vitro

In this study, two distinct chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) were engineered and designated as CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M, with a control group referred to as CTRL. Both CAR constructs incorporated the extracellular domain of human NKG2D, which has been previously shown to selectively recognize NKG2D ligands commonly expressed on tumor cells. The signal peptide, hinge, and transmembrane regions were derived from human CD8α to ensure efficient membrane localization and structural integrity. For intracellular signaling, CAR-M utilized the Fc receptor common γ chain (FcRγ), a canonical macrophage immunoreceptor that contains immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs), which mediate phagocytosis and proinflammatory activation. In the CD86-CAR-M construct, an additional intracellular signaling domain from CD86 was incorporated to provide costimulatory signaling, aiming to enhance T cell priming and support a more robust anti-tumor immune response. Our CAR constructs also contain green fluorescent protein (GFP) to serve as a marker for transduction. Raw264.7 cells transduced with a GFP-empty vector, named control (CTRL) were used as controls in this research (Fig. 1A). Using flow cytometry, we assessed the surface expression of classical NKG2D ligands (NKG2DLs), including MICA/B, on the human prostate cancer cell line PC-3. As anticipated, PC-3 cells exhibited high levels of human NKG2D ligands (MICA/B) confirming their suitability as a target model for NKG2D-mediated recognition. To validate the specificity of our assay, we included the well-characterized NKG2DL-negative cell line B16-F10 [8], which, as expected, showed negligible expression of NKG2D ligands (0%) (Fig. 1B). For CAR-Ms production, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that over 70% of macrophages were GFP-positive at 72 h after lentiviral infection in the CTRL, CAR-M or CD86-CAR-M group. To further confirm CAR expression on transduced macrophages, PE labelled anti-human NKG2D antibodies were incubated with the transduced macrophages and then detected by flow cytometry. Similarly, the flow cytometry results revealed that CAR-positive macrophages comprised over 70% of the cells in the CAR-M or CD86-CAR-M group, closely matching the proportion of GFP-positive macrophages detected, and this was further supported by a robust increase in the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CAR expression in the CAR-M or CD86-CAR-M group (Fig. 1C).

To assess the functional activity of engineered macrophages, we first evaluated their tumoricidal activity using a luciferase-based cytotoxicity assay. Both CAR-M variants exhibited potent, ratio-dependent killing of PC-3 cells, achieving ~ 70% elimination at an 8:1 effector: target (E: T) ratio, with no significant difference between NKG2D CAR-Ms and NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms. Importantly, neither construct induced cytotoxicity against B16-F10 cells (Fig. 1D), further validating the requirement for NKG2D ligand expression. We next examined their phagocytic capacity using flow cytometry. When co-cultured with NKG2D ligand-expressing PC-3 cells, both NKG2D CAR-Ms and NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms demonstrated significantly enhanced phagocytosis compared to control macrophages, engulfing approximately 27% of target cells, while showing minimal uptake of NKG2D ligand-negative B16-F10 cells (Fig. 1E and S1A). To assess the translational relevance of these findings, we next generated human CAR-macrophages using the THP-1 monocytic cell line. Following lentiviral transduction, over 90% of THP-1 cells were GFP-positive in the CTRL, CAR-M or CD86-CAR-M group, and CAR expression of THP-1 cells in the CAR-M or CD86-CAR-M group exceeded 90% (Fig. S1B). Differentiation of CAR-expressing THP-1 cells into macrophages was induced by treatment with 100 ng/mL PMA for 48 h, as confirmed by significant upregulation of the macrophage marker CD11b expression of THP-1 cells in the CTRL, CAR-M or CD86-CAR-M group compared with untreated counterparts (Fig. S1C). We then evaluated the tumoricidal activity of human CAR-THP-1-derived macrophages against PC-3 cells using a luciferase-based killing assay. Both NKG2D CAR-Ms and NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms demonstrated robust cytotoxic activity compared with the CTRL group (Fig. S1D). Although the cytotoxic potencies of NKG2D CAR-Ms and NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms were comparable, these findings indicate that the CAR construct retains full functional capacity in a human macrophage context. Collectively, these results confirm that human CD86-CAR-Ms mediate antigen-dependent tumoricidal activity comparable to CAR-Ms in vitro.

Within the tumor microenvironment, macrophages are frequently reprogrammed by tumor- and stroma-derived signals into an M2-like tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) phenotype that promotes tumor progression. To ensure sustained anti-tumoral efficacy, it is critical that CAR-engineered macrophages resist this polarization and maintain an M1-like pro-inflammatory state following interaction with tumor cells. To investigate the phenotypic stability of CAR-Ms, we analyzed the expression of M1 (CD80, CD86) and M2 (CD206) surface markers by flow cytometry after co-culture with NKG2DL-positive PC-3 prostate cancer cells. Both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M groups exhibited significantly increased expression of CD80 and CD86 compared to the control (CTRL) macrophages. Notably, CD86-CAR-Ms demonstrated a markedly higher expression of CD86 than CAR-Ms alone. In contrast, CD206 expression was either reduced or unchanged in both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M groups relative to CTRL macrophages, indicating resistance to M2 polarization (Fig. 1F and S1E). We further characterized the macrophage phenotype by assessing cytokine secretion profiles in the co-culture supernatants. Human CAR-Ms and CD86-CAR-Ms secreted elevated levels of Th1-type pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β (> 100 pg/mL), TNF-α (> 300 pg/mL), and IFN-γ (> 200 pg/mL), consistent with an M1-like phenotype. Conversely, CTRL macrophages produced high levels of immunosuppressive cytokines IL-13 (> 200 pg/mL) and TGF-β (> 100 pg/mL), indicative of an M2-like state (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that human NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms have the similar anti-prostate tumor activity as human NKG2D CAR-Ms in vitro.

Human NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms have the similar capability to suppress prostate tumor progression in immunodeficient mice as human NKG2D CAR-Ms

Because NCG mice lack functional T, B and NK cells due to defective lymphocyte development, we utilized this human prostate cancer PC-3 xenograft model to primarily assess tumor suppression and safety of human CAR-M therapy (Fig. 2A). Both CAR-M variants significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to the control group, with no significant difference observed between CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M treatments (Fig. 2B-D). Body weight monitoring indicated no signs of cachexia or treatment-related emaciation (Fig. 2E). Moreover, ELISA analysis of tumor homogenates revealed elevated levels of proinflammatory IL-1β in both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M-treated tumors, while control tumors exhibited higher levels of the immunosuppressive cytokines TGF-β and IL-13 (Fig. 2F). These results suggest that CAR-M treatment induces a proinflammatory TME, which may contribute to anti-tumor immunity.

Toxicity remains a major concern in cell-based therapies, particularly due to the risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS). Serum cytokine profiling revealed only moderate levels of IL-1β, IL-13, and TGF-β (< 100 pg/mL), with no significant differences among groups (Fig. 2G), and no clinical signs of CRS were noted. As CAR-Ms preferentially accumulate in the liver following intravenous infusion [28], we assessed hepatic toxicity by measuring serum ALT levels. No increase in ALT activity was observed in CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M treated mice compared to controls (Fig. 2H), and no significant differences in the weights of vital organs (liver, lung, and spleen) following treatment were observed between control and CAR-M-treated groups (Fig. 2I), further confirming treatment safety. Collectively, these results demonstrate that human NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms have the similar capability to suppress the progression of syngeneic prostate tumor-bearing NCG mice as human NKG2D CAR-Ms without inducing systemic toxicity.

Murine NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms have the similar anti-prostate tumor activity as murine NKG2D CAR-Ms in vitro

To evaluate the functional performance of our engineered NKG2D-based CAR macrophages (CAR-Ms) and CD86 costimulatory-enhanced CAR macrophages (CD86-CAR-Ms) in an immunocompetent prostate cancer setting, we developed murine versions of our CAR constructs. These constructs were generated by replacing the human NKG2D, human FcRγ and human CD86 sequences with their murine counterparts while retaining the overall CAR architecture (Fig. 3A). We confirmed robust CAR expression by flow cytometry at 72 h post-transduction, with GFP positivity and surface CAR expression exceeding 90% across CTRL, CAR-M, and CD86-CAR-M groups (Fig. 3B). Further, we assessed the surface expression of MULT1, a canonical NKG2D ligand, on the murine prostate cancer cell line RM-1. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that approximately 59.5% of RM-1 cells expressed MULT1, while B16-F10 showed almost no expression of MULT1 (no more than 1.1%), which meant B16-F10 could be used as the mouse NKG2DL-negative cell line (Fig. 3C). Functionally, murine NKG2DL targeting CAR-Ms and CD86-CAR-Ms demonstrated comparable cytotoxic effect and phagocytic activity against RM-1 cells, while neither CAR-Ms and CD86-CAR-Ms induced cytotoxicity and phagocytosis against B16-F10 cells (Fig. 3D-E and S2). These results indicated that the antigen-dependent specificity of both mouse CAR-Ms-mediated tumoricidal function, and murine NKG2D CD86-CAR-Ms have the similar anti-prostate tumor activity as murine NKG2D CAR-Ms in vitro.

Murine NKG2D-CD86-CAR-macrophages suppress syngeneic prostate-tumor growth in NCG mice to the same extent as murine NKG2D-CAR-macrophages.

To validate our in vitro observations, we established a syngeneic prostate tumor model in NCG mice, which are deficient in functional T cells. CTRL, CAR-M, or CD86-CAR-M cells were administered intravenously on days 6 and 12 following tumor inoculation, and mice were euthanized on day 18 for endpoint analysis (Fig. 4A). Both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M treatment groups demonstrated comparable tumor control relative to the CTRL group, as evidenced by tumor image, volume, and weight measurements (Fig. 4B-D). Moreover, no significant changes in body weight or major organ weights were observed during or after treatment (Fig. 4E, F), indicating that both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M therapies were well tolerated in vivo. These results suggest that in the absence of adaptive immune cells, both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M exert comparable tumor control, highlighting the potential contribution of T cell-mediated immunity to the enhanced antitumor efficacy.

Mouse CD86-CAR-Ms promote stronger T cell activation via enhanced costimulation and MAPK-dependent M1 polarization than mouse CAR-Ms

To investigate macrophage polarization in response to tumor engagement, we analyzed the expression of classical M1 and M2 markers following co-culture with RM-1 cells. Both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M groups exhibited significant upregulation of M1 markers CD80 and CD86 and a concomitant reduction in the M2 marker CD206. Notably, CD86-CAR-Ms displayed a more pronounced increase in CD80 and CD86 surface expression than CAR-Ms (Fig. 5A and S3A). These phenotypic observations were corroborated by cytokine profiling, where both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M secreted elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β (> 300 pg/mL), TNF-α (> 400 pg/mL), and IFN-γ (> 200 pg/mL), alongside suppressed IL-13 (< 100 pg/mL) and TGF-β (< 200 pg/mL) levels (Fig. 5B). Importantly, CD86-CAR-Ms secreted significantly less immunosuppressive TGF-β (< 100 pg/mL) compared to CAR-Ms (> 200 pg/mL), indicating enhanced resistance to tumor-mediated immunosuppression. To determine whether these phenotypic differences were reflected at the transcriptional level, GFP⁺CAR-macrophages were FACS-sorted after co-culture with RM-1 tumor cells and subjected to qPCR analysis. CD86-CAR-Ms showed significantly higher mRNA expression of CD80 and CD86 compared to CAR-Ms, although no significant differences were observed in the expression of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β between the two groups (Fig. 5C). These results suggested that mouse CD86-CAR-Ms had a superior M1 polarization profile compared to mouse CAR-Ms in response to tumor antigen stimulation.

Given the known role of M1-polarized macrophages in stimulating adaptive immune responses through enhanced antigen presentation and costimulatory signaling, we hypothesized that CD86-engineered CAR macrophages (CD86-CAR-Ms) might be superior to CAR-Ms in priming T cells. To evaluate this, CD3⁺T cells were isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6 mice and co-cultured with CAR-Ms or CD86-CAR-Ms that had been previously exposed to RM-1 cells. ELISA revealed that T cells co-cultured with CD86-CAR-Ms secreted significantly higher levels of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ; >1500 pg/mL) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α; >1000 pg/mL), compared to those stimulated by CAR-Ms (IFN-γ < 1000 pg/mL, TNF-α < 500 pg/mL) (Fig. 5D). These findings indicate that CD86-CAR-Ms promoted stronger T cell effector responses.

To elucidate the molecular underpinnings of this enhanced M1 phenotype, we assessed activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, a known downstream effector of innate immune receptor signaling, including TLRs and Fc receptors. Western blot analysis of CAR-Ms following co-culture with tumor cells demonstrated markedly elevated phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) in CD86-CAR-Ms relative to CAR-Ms (Fig. 5E and S3B-C). This suggests that CD86 costimulation potentiates MAPK signaling cascades, thereby promoting transcriptional activation of M1 polarization genes. Taken together, these data reveal that mouse CD86-CAR-Ms promote stronger T cell activation via enhanced costimulation and MAPK-dependent M1 polarization than mouse CAR-Ms.

CD86-CAR-Ms can more significantly inhibit the prostate cancer progression in immunocompetent mice compared to CAR-Ms through turning a cold tumor to a hot one

To evaluate the immunological contribution of endogenous T cells to the antitumor activity of CD86-CAR-Ms, we employed an immunocompetent syngeneic mouse model bearing RM-1 prostate cancer cells. This model was chosen to better mimic the intact immune landscape of patients and overcome the limitations of immunodeficient xenograft models. CAR-M or CD86-CAR-M cells were administered intravenously on days 6 and 12 post-tumor inoculation, and mice were sacrificed on day 18 for analysis (Fig. 6A). Tumor growth was significantly reduced in mice treated with CD86-CAR-Ms compared to control or CAR-M groups, as evidenced by tumor volume measurements, photographic documentation, and tumor weight at endpoint (Fig. 6B-D). Body weight remained stable in all groups throughout the study, with no signs of cachexia or systemic toxicity (Fig. 6E). Moreover, CD86-CAR-M treatment prolonged survival relative to control or CAR-M groups (Fig. 6F). Importantly, no significant differences were observed in the weights of vital organs including liver, lung, and spleen across all treatment groups, suggesting no off-target toxicity (Fig. 6G).

To explore the immunological basis for the superior antitumor effect of CD86-CAR-Ms, we analyzed tumor-infiltrating immune cells by flow cytometry (Fig. S4A-B). A significantly higher frequency of CD45⁺leukocytes, CD3⁺T cells, and NK cells was detected in the CD86-CAR-M-treated tumors compared to other groups, however, no significant differences were observed in total macrophages, dendritic cells and B cells across the treatment groups (Fig. 6H and S5A-E). Importantly, macrophages in the CD86-CAR-M group exhibited significantly higher MHC class II expression compared to other groups (Fig. 6H and S5B), indicating more capable of presenting antigen to T cells via the MHC II–TCR axis. To further characterize the TME, we assessed cytokine profiles in tumor homogenates. Notably, CD86-CAR-M-treated tumors exhibited elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β (> 1000 pg/mL), IFN-γ (> 200 pg/mL), and TNF (> 300 pg/mL), while levels of immunosuppressive cytokines IL-13 (< 150 pg/mL) and TGF-β (< 100 pg/mL) were more reduced when compared to other-treated tumors (Fig. 6I). Furthermore, despite enhanced T cell infiltration, CD86-CAR-M treated groups showed low serum cytokine levels as CAR-M treated groups (Fig. S5F), indicating that the therapeutic effects were tumor-localized and did not induce systemic CRS. These data suggest that CD86-CAR-Ms can more significantly inhibit the prostate cancer progression in immunocompetent mice compared to CAR-Ms through turning a cold tumor to a hot one.

CD86-CAR-Ms can more effectively enhance anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy for prostate cancer compared to CAR-Ms

Prostate cancer exhibits poor responsiveness to immunotherapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), largely due to low T cell infiltration and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [29–31]. Our prior results showed that CD86-CAR-M promoted T cell infiltration and proinflammatory cytokine production in the TME. We investigated whether combining CD86-CAR-M with anti-PD-L1 antibody therapy could enhance T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Using a syngeneic RM-1 prostate cancer model, C57BL/6 mice were treated with control macrophages (CTRL), CAR-M or CD86-CAR-M cells, either alone or combined with an anti-PD-L1 antibody, and the untreated group (UTD) was as the control (Fig. 7A). Our results revealed no significant difference in tumor growth between the UTD and CTRL group (Fig. 7B-D), suggesting that the adoptive transfer of control macrophages does not confer measurable anti-tumor activity beyond the natural course of tumor growth. Consistent with prior research [29, 30], immune checkpoint monotherapy did not inhibit prostate tumor growth (Fig. 7B-D). Moreover, the combination therapy with CTRL group did not delay tumor progression compared to the UTD group (Fig. 7B-D). While both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M monotherapies significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to UTD or CTRL group, combination therapy with anti-PD-L1 antibody further delayed tumor progression (Fig. 7B-D). Notably, the CD86-CAR-M plus anti-PD-L1 antibody group showed the most pronounced tumor suppression (Fig. 7B-D), underscoring the essential role of T cells in mediating therapeutic efficacy. Importantly, combination treatment did not affect body weight or organ weights (Fig. 7E and F), suggesting a favorable safety profile. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that combining CD86-CAR-M with PD-L1 blockade offers a synergistic anti-tumor effect in prostate cancer by enhancing T cell-dependent immunity without inducing systemic toxicity, thus representing a promising strategy for overcoming immune resistance in solid tumors.

Discussion

Among CAR-based therapies, CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) hold significant promise for treating solid tumors due to their intrinsic phagocytic capacity and ability to promote antigen presentation, thereby enhancing adaptive immune responses. In this study, we engineered two novel CAR constructs: one incorporating the Fc receptor common γ chain (FcRγ) alone (CAR-M), and another combining FcRγ with the intracellular costimulatory domain of CD86 (CD86-CAR-M). In designing our constructs, we incorporated the intracellular domain of CD86 to emulate the macrophages activation state. CD86 upregulation marks M1 polarization and correlates with enhanced antigen presentation and cytokine secretion. Although the cytoplasmic signaling functions of CD86 remain less defined, previous evidence suggests that CD86-derived motifs can reinforce macrophage activation and promote ERK-mediated pro-inflammatory signaling [32]. Integrating this intracellular domain into the CAR design therefore aim to provides a macrophage-relevant costimulatory cue that sustains the antigen-presenting, tumoricidal phenotype and enhances resistance to M2-like repolarization within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Our findings demonstrate that while both constructs mediated antigen-specific tumor cell killing, CD86-CAR-Ms exhibited markedly enhanced M1-like polarization and superior activation and costimulation of T cells, primarily via ERK signaling. More importantly, CD86-CAR-Ms achieved greater tumor suppression than UTD, CTRL and CAR-Ms in immunocompetent models and significantly enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 antibody therapy without systemic toxicity. The improved anti-tumor effect was associated with increased T cell and NK cell infiltration and reprogramming of the TME from cold to hot one.

Our comparative analyses in immunodeficient NCG mice versus immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice provide important mechanistic insights into the role of the adaptive immune system in mediating CD86-CAR-M efficacy. In NCG mice, which lack functional T, B, and NK cells, CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M achieved similar tumor control, indicating that the intrinsic cytotoxic and phagocytic functions of the engineered macrophages were preserved but that the CD86 domain did not confer additional benefit in the absence of adaptive immunity. By contrast, in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice, CD86-CAR-Ms exhibited superior tumor suppression compared to CAR-Ms, which correlated with enhanced CD3⁺ T cell infiltration and reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment. These findings demonstrate that the therapeutic advantage of CD86 costimulation is fundamentally T cell-dependent, underscoring the importance of evaluating novel CAR-M platforms in immunocompetent models that more closely recapitulate the complex cellular interactions of the human tumor immune microenvironment. This distinction highlights the translational relevance of CD86-CAR-Ms as a strategy to bridge innate and adaptive immunity for durable antitumor responses.

Macrophages are uniquely positioned within the immune system to infiltrate solid tumors and modulate the tumor microenvironment. While CAR-T cells have shown remarkable success in hematologic malignancies, their application in solid tumors, including prostate cancer, is limited by poor tumor infiltration, antigen heterogeneity, and immunosuppressive TMEs [14, 33]. In contrast, macrophages naturally localize within tumors and possess antigen-presenting capabilities that enable crosstalk with the adaptive immune system [34]. These features have inspired a surge in interest in CAR-M-based strategies, as evidenced by recent studies demonstrating that M1-polarized CAR-Ms can reprogram the TME, phagocytose tumor cells, and stimulate T cell responses [26, 35].

Consistent with prior reports, our NKG2D-targeted CAR-Ms effectively recognized and phagocytosed prostate tumor cells expressing NKG2D ligands (NKG2DLs) such as MICA/B and MULT1, while sparing ligand-negative cells [8, 23, 36]. Importantly, both CAR-M and CD86-CAR-M maintained M1-like phenotypes following tumor engagement, as evidenced by upregulated CD80/CD86 expression, increased secretion of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, and resistance to M2-polarizing cues such as TGF-β and IL-13. These findings reinforce earlier work by Klichinsky et al. and Lei et al., who showed that FcRγ- or TLR4-based CAR-Ms polarize toward proinflammatory states, enabling robust anti-tumor effects without systemic cytokine release [14, 18]. However, we further show that CD86 inclusion enhances the expression of MHC-II and promotes T cell activation via ERK/MAPK signaling critical steps in bridging innate and adaptive immunity.

One of the most significant findings from this study is the enhanced ability of CD86-CAR-Ms to stimulate T cell activation. While FcRγ signaling alone induced cytokine release and phagocytosis, CD86-enhanced CAR-Ms significantly upregulated costimulatory molecule expression and elicited strong IFN-γ and TNF-α responses in co-cultured CD3⁺T cells. This agrees with previous studies reporting that CD86 primarily engages CD28 on naïve T cells to promote Th1 polarization and cytotoxic T lymphocyte expansion [37, 38]. Consistent with the established role of ERK/MAPK signaling in macrophage activation [39], our western blotting analysis revealed elevated ERK phosphorylation in CD86-CAR-Ms compared with CAR-M and CTRL groups, supporting the role of CD86 in promoting ERK/MAPK pathway activation and further validating its inclusion as a functional enhancer for macrophage-based immunotherapies. The ERK pathway is a well-characterized driver of M1 macrophage polarization [40], as ERK1/2 phosphorylation accompanies the induction of pro-inflammatory mediators, whereas pharmacological inhibition of ERK attenuates these responses [41]. Given this strong literature precedent, our study focused on ERK as a representative and tractable pathway to establish a mechanistic link between CD86 costimulation and enhanced M1 polarization. However, it is important to note that CD86 signaling likely engages additional pathways, including PI3K-AKT and NF-κB, which are critically involved in regulating macrophage polarization and co-stimulatory signaling. Exploration of these pathways represents an important direction for future investigation. Specifically, future studies may employ pharmacological inhibition and genetic perturbation approaches to systematically dissect the contributions of PI3K-AKT and NF-κB cascades in CD86-CAR-M mediated polarization and tumor microenvironment reprogramming. While the present findings establish ERK activation as one mechanistic foundation, expanding this analysis will provide a more comprehensive understanding of how CD86 signaling orchestrates macrophage activation and enhances antitumor immunity.

In vivo, both CAR-M variants suppressed tumor growth in xenograft models without inducing toxicity, underscoring the safety of macrophage-based therapies. Importantly, only CD86-CAR-Ms exhibited enhanced therapeutic efficacy in immunocompetent syngeneic models prolonging survival, increasing T cell infiltration, and elevating proinflammatory cytokines within tumors. These findings suggest that while FcRγ signaling is sufficient for macrophage-mediated cytotoxicity, CD86 is essential for engaging endogenous T cells and reprogramming “cold” tumors into “hot” immunologically active sites. This aligns with the growing consensus that effective immunotherapies must integrate both innate and adaptive arms of immunity for durable tumor control [3, 42].

Furthermore, our combination therapy experiments demonstrated that CD86-CAR-Ms synergize with PD-L1 blockade, significantly improving tumor control compared to monotherapy. Notably, this synergy occurred without systemic toxicity or cytokine release syndrome, which are major concerns in CAR-T cell therapy [43]. These results mirror findings from Pierini et al., who showed that HER2-CAR-Ms sensitize tumors to anti-PD1 treatment, and provide further support for the rational design of CAR-Ms to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade in solid tumors [6]. Given the unsatisfactory clinical activity of immune checkpoints blockade monotherapy in prostate cancer, our data suggest that CAR-Ms may provide a needed immunological “spark” to improve outcomes.

Despite promising preclinical results, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the short lifespan and non-proliferative nature of macrophages may limit therapeutic duration. And clinical-scale manufacturing particularly cryopreservation and handling remains challenging [44, 45]. Long-term safety data, including off-target effects and sustained immune activation, are also lacking. Addressing these issues will be critical for clinical translation. Importantly, heterogeneity and potential shedding of NKG2D ligands, such as soluble MICA and MICB, which may down-modulate receptor signaling and blunt CAR-M activity in vivo [46]. Previous studies have highlighted ligand shedding as a mechanism of immune evasion in prostate and other cancers, and strategies such as metalloprotease inhibition or engineering shedding-resistant NKG2D receptors could enhance therapeutic durability in future designs [47]. In addition, while the syngeneic RM-1 model in C57BL/6 mice allowed evaluation of CAR-M in an immunocompetent setting, this model does not fully recapitulate the suppressive features of the human prostate cancer microenvironment, particularly the abundance of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, regulatory T cells, and other inhibitory components [29]. To more accurately model clinical disease, subsequent studies will require validation in humanized mouse systems and patient-derived xenograft models that better reflect the genomic and immunological complexity of prostate tumors. Moreover, this study did not include extended serum chemistry analyses (e.g., AST, ALT, bilirubin, and creatinine levels), comprehensive histopathological evaluation of major organs such as the liver, lungs, and kidneys at the study endpoint [27]. Incorporating these assessments in future investigations will be critical to establish a more complete safety profile of CD86-CAR-M therapy and to strengthen its translational relevance. Notably, given the potential involvement of myeloid cells in cytokine release syndrome, systematic monitoring of serum cytokines and the development of mitigation strategies will be essential during CAR-M clinical translation. To overcome these challenges, recent approaches employing induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) or hematopoietic progenitors as renewable sources for generating CAR-Ms have shown considerable promise in improving scalability, uniformity, and safety of therapeutic macrophage products [48]. Second, intravenous administration of macrophages results in preferential liver accumulation [49, 50]. Approaches such as intratumoral injection, chemokine receptor engineering, or nanoparticle-guided delivery may enhance tumor localization and efficacy [50, 51]. Third, tumor heterogeneity and antigen loss remain barriers to long-term control [52, 53]. Future CAR designs may incorporate bispecific or multi-antigen targeting strategies, or be engineered to secrete immunostimulatory molecules such as IL-12, IL-2, IFN-γ, or bispecific T cell engagers (BiTEs), in order to broaden and amplify the anti-tumor immune response [6, 54, 55].

Moreover, to mitigate potential off-target effects, future strategies could incorporate safety switches such as inducible caspase-9 systems or ligand-controlled signaling domains that allow for rapid termination of CAR-M activity in case of adverse events [56]. Engineering approaches that enhance ligand discrimination, such as affinity-tuned CARs or dual-antigen recognition circuits, may also reduce the risk of targeting stressed but healthy cells [57]. Future investigations should consider slower-growing tumor models or orthotopic/prostate-specific models for extended in vivo monitoring to assess durability response, potential autoimmune manifestations and delayed toxicities, including macrophage-associated inflammatory syndromes, through comprehensive immunopathological analyses [29, 58]. Collectively, these approaches will enable a more thorough evaluation of the safety profile and translational potential of CD86-CAR-M therapy.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that CD86 stimulation significantly enhances the therapeutic activity of NKG2D CAR-Ms by improving antigen presentation, T cell activation, and inflammatory reprogramming of the TME. This approach not only augments macrophage cytotoxicity but also potentiates the effects of immune checkpoint inhibitors (Anti-PD-L1) offering a powerful and safe strategy for tackling immunologically refractory solid tumors such as prostate cancer. These insights lay a strong foundation for future clinical development of multifunctional CAR-M therapies, either as monotherapy or in combination with existing immunotherapeutic modalities.
