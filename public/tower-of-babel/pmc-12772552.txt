A 3-year experience of an Australian short-stay Psychiatric, Alcohol and Non-prescription Drug Assessment Unit: unit activity and the patient experience
Authors: Jane Ellen Carland, Yvonne Nguyen, Hannah Paul, Nadine Ezard, Matthew Bode, Bash Jagarlamudi, Mayura Sandrasegaran, Bridin Murnion, Richard Osborne Day, Paul Thomas Preisz, Jacqueline Huber, Jonathan Brett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Psychiatric, Alcohol and Non-Prescription Drug Assessment (PANDA) Unit was opened at St Vincent’s Hospital Sydney in 2020 to provide short-stay medical monitoring and care for people presenting to the emergency department with alcohol or other drug intoxication or behavioural disturbance with co-existing general medical, drug and alcohol and/or mental health issues. The aim of this study was to describe the activity of the PANDA Unit across the first 3 years of operation and explore patient experience.MethodsA mixed methods design was used. A retrospective record review (2020–2023) captured patient demographics, admission characteristics and discharge disposition. Brief, structured interviews were conducted with PANDA patients to explore their admission experience, including the care provided. Interviews were analysed using an inductive approach to develop themes.Results2473 patients had 4108 admissions to PANDA, median 333 (range, 296–396) admissions per quarter. Median patient age was 40 (range, 16–93) years, most (64.5%) were male and 11.2% had ‘no-fixed abode’ documented. Most admissions were associated with alcohol and stimulant intoxication and/or suicidality. Median PANDA length of stay was 21.2 hours (range, 0.5–883.1); 20% of admissions were >48 hours. Most patients (83.8%) were discharged from PANDA to their usual place of residence, 4.9% self-discharged and 15% were readmitted to PANDA within 28 days of discharge, 56 people were readmitted three or more times. The experiences of interview participants (n=14) regarding the PANDA Unit was described by three key themes: (1) patients receive non-judgemental, whole-person care from a large multidisciplinary team, (2) PANDA provides a transition point to further care and (3) PANDA is a comfortable and busy space.ConclusionsPANDA provided tailored care in a short-stay context for people presenting to emergency department with complex comorbidities. Its unique model of care addressed barriers to healthcare access, such as stigma and siloed care.Keywords: emergency medicine, substance-related disorders, observational study, qualitative research, mental health

Introduction

People with substance use disorders (SUDs) and/or mental health disorders have relatively high utilisation of emergency departments (EDs) and acute services, putatively related to difficulties in accessing community services.1,3 Around 10% of presentations to EDs in Australia, Ireland and the UK—countries with comparable health systems—are alcohol-related,4,6 1% involve any drug use disorder7 and around 4% of admissions are mental health-related, with 27% of these involving self-harm and 9% involving attempted suicide.8 The 1-year mortality of those presenting with alcohol-related causes or non-fatal opioid overdose is 5.4%9 and 5.5%,10 respectively, and around 30–40% of people who die from suicide had an ED admission in the year prior to death.11

SUDs and mental health disorders are chronic, frequently comorbid disorders that coexist with homelessness and medical comorbidities necessitating regular access to care.12 Frequent ED presentations provide an opportunity to engage this population and coordinate holistic care. However, EDs historically lack the resources and staff to implement this and are typically high-stimulus environments that can be stressful or traumatising for these patients.13 Ultimately, this can lead to underdiagnosis, undertreatment, self-discharge, negative patient and staff experiences and ongoing high rates of morbidity and mortality.14,17 Different service models have been trialled to better support this patient cohort in EDs, including liaison drug and alcohol and mental health services,18 19 screening/brief interventions20 and peer support.21 These services can improve patient outcomes, but face structural and logistical barriers, notably compartmentalised services.22 There is a need for new models of care for people presenting to ED with complex comorbid mental health symptoms, SUDs and other medical and social concerns.

The Psychiatric, Alcohol and Non-Prescription Drug Assessment (PANDA) Unit was opened at St Vincent’s Hospital Sydney in 2020. Shortly after, a similar unit integrated within ED—the Hub—was opened at St Vincent’s Hospital Melbourne.23 PANDA is a short-stay unit co-located with ED with a model of care for people with alcohol or other drug intoxication or behavioural disturbance with co-existing general medical, drug and alcohol and/or mental health issues. In this study, we describe the activity of the PANDA Unit since its opening and explore the patient experience through qualitative interviews.

Methods

Study design

We performed a mixed methods study involving two components: a retrospective record review for the first 3 years of PANDA activity (2020–2023) followed by interviews with patients admitted to PANDA (April and May 2023) to capture the patient experience of admission.

Context

Geographically, St Vincent’s Hospital Sydney serves a large population of people experiencing complex mental health and alcohol and drug concerns as well as homelessness. The PANDA Unit was conceived to improve care of this patient cohort by providing a low-stimulus environment, separate to ED, with a unique model of care allowing access to specialised medical, psychiatric, nursing and allied health staff. The unit is funded by the jurisdictional government as a standalone service, separate to ED.

PANDA is an inpatient unit under the governance of clinical toxicology to ensure bed pressures in ED and psychiatric units do not result in admission of non-PANDA eligible patients. Admission criteria include alcohol or other drug intoxication or behavioural disturbance along with co-existing general medical, drug and alcohol, and/or mental health issues or an acute overdose. Eligibility criteria were written to be as inclusive as possible to allow a ‘no wrong door’ approach. Patients must be 15 years and 9 months, with an expected length of stay (LOS) <48 hours (online supplemental material 1). Patients can be admitted to PANDA from three sources. Patients are primarily admitted through the ED. They are initially seen and assessed by an ED doctor; those potentially appropriate for PANDA admission are identified and discussed with the PANDA doctors before admission. As PANDA is also an acute toxicology unit, if needed, patients with acute drug overdose can be stepped down from the intensive care unit (ICU) once meeting admission criteria. Patients can be admitted to PANDA from other medical wards if they meet eligibility criteria.

PANDA is a six-bed medical unit with natural light, a small communal area and kitchenette and connected via a short corridor to ED. The reduced patient number and foot traffic through the unit contributes to the low-stimulus environment. To facilitate shared patient care and knowledge, PANDA shares an adjoining clinical work station with the psychiatric emergency care centre (PECC). PECCs focus on psychiatric crisis management. In contrast, PANDA provides care for people with alcohol or other drug intoxication with co-existing general medical, drug and alcohol, toxicological and/or mental health concerns. Patients can be transferred from PANDA to PECC if their medical issues have resolved but they require ongoing short-stay psychiatric care. PANDA is staffed by both clinical toxicology and emergency medicine consultants on a 24-hour, 7-days-per-week on-call roster, alongside medical and emergency specialty trainee doctors (including interns, registrars and residents), a distinct pool of nurses, a drug and alcohol clinical nurse consultant, a pharmacist, a social worker and a peer worker. Patients are admitted under a nominated PANDA consultant, with consultation from other specialist teams as needed. The cross-silo care, with peer worker involvement, reflects the acceptance that specialties must work together to implement a ‘no wrong door’ approach and manage the needs of complex patients. All PANDA beds have the capacity for continuous cardiac and pulse oximetry monitoring, allowing acute medical care, including administration of intravenous fluids and medicines. Staff work with patients to form care plans, which involve integration with ongoing community care postdischarge through written and verbal communication with care providers. The PANDA Unit has formed links with both public and third-sector treatment providers to ensure smooth transitions of care. Community care providers are invited to monthly multidisciplinary case review meetings to discuss patients identified as having high care needs such as those frequently re-presenting. Patients may be discharged from PANDA or transferred to other hospital wards, including Mental Health wards.

Reflexivity statement

Researchers consisted of an emergency psychiatrist, ED director, PANDA team members (medical leads, peer worker, clinical toxicologists, addiction specialists, nurses), psychiatry registrar, senior hospital scientist and a medical student. This constitutes a range of lived, research and clinical experience, which will have impacted study design and analysis.

PANDA Unit activity

Data collection

Patients admitted to PANDA between 1 November 2020 and 31 August 2023 were identified using the hospital electronic patient management system via the code assigned to the unit of admission. Coded data available in electronic medical records were collected. Data collected included demographics (age, sex, country of birth), Aboriginal identity, primary homelessness (address ‘no-fixed abode’), admission source (ED, ICU), mode of arrival (ambulance, other), triage time, triage category (using the Australasian Triage Scale Category),24 ED LOS and PANDA LOS. We identified the frequency of primary International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) diagnoses, diagnostic-related groups (DRG), admissions with psychiatric symptoms (based on ICD-10 codes:F20-F51, F53-F63, F65-F69, F80-99, R45 or X60-X84), primary substance of concern as identified in collaboration between the patient and the medical team and injecting drug use. Patients’ discharge destination, discharges against medical advice and PANDA readmissions within 28 days of discharge were captured. ‘Code blacks’, representing episodes of acute severe behavioural disturbance requiring assistance from a team composed of security and clinical staff, were extracted from the security database from 1 September 2021 to 31 August 2023 for PANDA, PECC and ED.

Data analysis

Categorical data were summarised as numbers and percentages of total number of PANDA admissions, continuous data as median (range). We quantified the quarterly number of admissions to PANDA for full quarters included in the study period. Analyses were performed in RStudio.

Patient interviews

Recruitment

Researchers not involved in patient care employed a convenience sampling methodology. Patients who were available for an interview prior to discharge were approached and invited to participate in the study. Patients were invited to participate in an interview regardless of LOS or discharge disposition.

Data collection

The interview guide was designed with input from four authors (JEC, YN, JH, JB) and then pilot tested with two patients to assess suitability and acceptability (online supplemental file 1 interview guide). Senior members of the research team listened to the pilot interviews and considered the interviewer’s reflective notes. In light of the descriptive clinical data obtained in the retrospective record review, the guide was then streamlined to focus on the patient’s experience of the PANDA Unit, including the physical space, the care provided, the staff and any discharge planning. The refined interview guide was more concise and structured, minimising any discharge delay. Once consent was obtained, interviews were conducted in person. Interviews were a median of 4 (range, 2–14) min in length, audio-recorded and transcribed.

Interview data were complemented by a manual review of participant electronic and paper medical records and discharge summaries of interview participants. Data collected included demographics (age, gender) and admission characteristics (presenting complaint(s), discharge diagnoses, ICD-10 and DRG codes, prior PANDA admissions, Mental Health Act, role and specialty of staff providing care, discharge plans). Data collection was undertaken by two researchers (HP, YN).

Data analysis

Interview analysis was thematic and inductive, guided by Braun and Clarke,25 aiming to capture a range of participant experiences. De-identified transcripts were analysed independently by three authors (JEC, YN, HP) to enhance rigour and encourage reflexivity. Transcripts were first read to become familiar with the data, authors then worked to develop codes and broader themes to describe patterns in the data. The authors met periodically to discuss codes and refine themes. Interviews were continued until no new codes or themes were generated. Participant demographic and admission data were reported using descriptive statistics.

Results

Across the study period, there were 4108 admissions of 2473 patients to PANDA, median 333 (range, 296–396) admissions per quarter. Median patient age was 40 (range, 16–93) years and 64.5% were male (table 1). Of these, 17.7% were First Nations (Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander peoples) and 11.2% had ‘no-fixed abode’. Most patients (97.3%) were admitted to PANDA via ED, the majority arrived by ambulance (68.7%) as triage category 3 (59.5%). The median PANDA LOS was 21.2 (range, 0.5–883.1) hours; 20% of admissions had LOS >48 hours. Six patients had admissions >40 days. Longer LOS were associated with medical and/or social complications, such as drug-induced psychosis and homelessness, as well as systemic bed flow pressures, unrelated to care delivered in PANDA, which meant that patients could not be moved to other wards.

The top five ICD-10 and DRG codes were related to alcohol or stimulant intoxication and suicidality. The top two primary substances of concern were ethanol (36.6%) and methamphetamine (14.4%) and 19.1% of presentations were related to psychiatric symptoms. Most patients (83.8%) were discharged from PANDA to their usual place of residence, 4.9% discharged against medical advice and 15% readmitted to PANDA within 28 days of discharge; 56 people were readmitted three or more times. Patient interviews

Of the 24 patients invited to participate, 19 consented and 14 completed an interview (response rate: 58%). Reasons provided for non-participation included not wanting to delay discharge and being too tired to participate. Five patients consented to an interview postdischarge but were uncontactable.

Most participants were male (n=8) and half presented with alcohol intoxication, most arriving by ambulance. Half had a prior admission to PANDA (online supplemental table S1). All participants received care from doctors, nurses and a pharmacist and most (64%) from the PANDA social worker. All participants received input from other specialties. On discharge, PANDA provided referrals to community-based services (86%) and/or co-ordinated follow-up appointments (79%). There are no data on how many patients followed through with discharge care plans.

Three themes describe the participant’s experience of PANDA: (1) patients receive non-judgemental, whole-person care from a large multidisciplinary team, (2) PANDA provides a transition point to further care and (3) PANDA is a comfortable and busy space.

Patients receive non-judgemental, whole-person care from a large multidisciplinary team

Participants described an active, attentive team that provided a different care experience to previous hospital admissions; “…there is a lot more support for us than the other units or any other unit” (P19). Several participants described a patient-centred in-reach model; “I just lay in the bed and whoever comes to me to chat, comes to me to chat” (P22), care provided by a range of medical and allied health specialities. One participant also described support provided by the PANDA peer worker. “The peer worker…I guess innately, even though I don’t know his story, I feel comfortable talking to him about stuff” (P20).

Participants felt staff were highly trained and had experience in providing care for people with complex comorbidities; “…it’s more specific for drug and alcohol and mental health. I found that I got a lot more support here” (P15). Participants described friendly, empathetic and non-judgemental staff who collaborated with them to develop holistic care plans for immediate and postdischarge needs. “They took the time to actually listen to me, clearly no judgement or criticism, once again very supportive…they’re all very knowledgeable, very empathetic, generally here to help people and to support you…” (P10). A few participants reported concerns about missed/delayed medications, although one noted staff had explained “…you’ve still got alcohol in you so we can’t give you anything” (P13). Overall, participants felt that all their needs were taken care of. “They understood more about my mental health, even though I had medical side of things as well” (P16).

PANDA provides a transition point to further care

Participants described how staff listened and understood their needs, provided access to specialist teams during their admission and facilitated continued care, including referrals to outpatient drug and alcohol treatment programmes and support groups. PANDA facilitated access to social support services, including accommodation support, non-government organisations and government funding, for some. The focus on social factors was a point of difference. “I never had the opportunity before to help myself to set up homecare, NDIS [government funding] all that kind of stuff…insulin dependent [endocrinology consultations], all that kind of stuff. It’s pretty different. It was the first time I’ve had access to it like that” (P21). The admission of one participant was extended while accommodation was organised; “[they] listened to me and let me stay tonight…” (P10).

For many, the comprehensive discharge planning provided was necessary to achieve their long-term health goal “…to get better” (P7) as “I can’t do it on my own” (P9). For some, PANDA facilitated access to drug and alcohol residential rehabilitation or inpatient withdrawal management units. One participant described how these units provided people with time away from alcohol and/or drugs, but also “…give you the tools…” (P22).

PANDA is a comfortable and busy space

Participants’ experience of the physical PANDA space was largely positive, privacy afforded despite the busyness. They described a clean and comfortable environment, “…obviously it is a clinical ward, but I think it looks fresh, it looks clean, it’s easy to navigate” (P14). Participants remarked positively about the amenities, “…in terms of having a TV to watch, amazing…the food, the access to the fridge, the coffee, those facilities are really good” (P10), which developed a sense of independence. Despite the lack of walls between beds, PANDA provided participants with a sense of privacy and security. “You get your own little thingy, your own little cocoon here” (P22).

When asked how PANDA could be improved, more food options and ways to manage noise—including TV noise and frequent movement through the unit—were sought; “…there should be a curfew on televisions, it’s going all night…I didn’t sleep AT ALL last night. Not one blink” (P11) and “The bloody doors never stop bangin’ [laughs] like NONSTOP like bang, bang, bang! It drives you a bit crazy but, oh well, it is what it is” (P22). However, PANDA noise and activity were often accepted by participants and offset by the staff who were “…incredibly friendly and tolerant of a really tough, it is a tough working environment” (P20).

Discussion

This study explored the activity and patient experiences of PANDA, a multidisciplinary and interdisciplinary, short-stay unit that provides care for people with complex psychiatric, substance-related and medical comorbidities. Historically, public intoxication has been criminalised; individuals left to sober up in police cells or watch houses.26 The contemporary response to intoxication involves transport to an ED, particularly when there are co-existing mental health concerns. While providing a safer space, this approach may also be suboptimal. ED resource limitations mean that complex comorbidity may be underappreciated and undertreated. The high-stimulus environment and nascency of trauma-informed care may also precipitate trauma responses and behavioural disturbance among this population with a high prevalence of past traumatic experiences.27 Across its first 3 years, most admissions to PANDA were related to ethanol, methamphetamines and/or suicidality. Participants perceived their stay in PANDA as different to previous healthcare experiences, despite being ‘clinical’ (P7).

PANDA has proven feasible to implement, de-siloing care and addressing some known barriers to care access for people with complex comorbidities. Participants described a busy clinical space where they received non-judgemental care from specialised staff. Healthcare staff attitudes play a key role in outcomes for patients with SUDs and psychiatric disorders; non-stigmatising care can enhance treatment engagement and compliance.13 28 Other service models have responded to this patient cohort in ED, including liaison drug and alcohol and mental health services.18 19 However, these services face structural and logistical barriers, notably siloed care.22 Short-stay units, such as Behavioural Assessment Units,29 share similarities with PANDA. However, their focus is management of behavioural disturbances in ED, with limited focus on comorbidity. PECCs and Emergency Psychiatry Assessment, Treatment and Healing Units30 focus on psychiatric crisis management,31 thus are not suited to manage patients who require acute or complex non-psychiatric care. In contrast, PANDA provides comprehensive, across-silo care, tailored by staff who understand the complexities of these chronic conditions. The focus on substance use and physical health differentiates PANDA from short-stay psychiatric and behavioural assessment units. PANDA is able to provide acute medical care and monitoring, as well as shared psychiatric care. Interviews with staff are needed to understand provider acceptability of this approach and the importance of interdisciplinary relationships to staff well-being and satisfaction.

While 15.8% of all patients had been re-admitted to PANDA within 28 days of a prior admission, re-presentation is common in this population,3 18 and may not always be a negative outcome. Recovery from these relapsing and remitting chronic conditions generally occurs over months to years. Representation to PANDA may be a response to the ‘no wrong door’ policy and patient perceptions of non-stigmatising, safe and effective care. The continuity of therapeutic relationships formed over the course of successive admissions may also facilitate a shift in patients’ motivations for positive behaviour changes.

Despite trying to provide a low stimulus environment, interview participants reported noise disturbances. Given PANDA admissions occur throughout the day, environmental adaptations, including single rooms, sound-absorbing ceilings or quietly closing doors, or providing patients with resources, such as earplugs, may minimise the impact of noise and further improve outcomes. Furthermore, while benefits of community follow-up have been shown for intoxication, SUDs and suicidal ideation,32 33 postdischarge follow-up is one element currently missing from the PANDA model of care. Feasibility, including funding, needs to be determined to support this extension.

While providing evidence of feasibility of implementation and patient acceptability, along with efficacy signals, this study was unable to demonstrate the effectiveness of the PANDA model of care in improving morbidity and mortality outcomes or cost-effectiveness. Larger, adequately resourced studies, such as a stepped-wedge randomised controlled study or a study linking routinely collected health data to track outcomes before and after PANDA admission, may provide more definitive evidence. In resource-limited settings, questions also remain around the key components of such a complex intervention and whether a similar model of care can be implemented without additional resources. We hypothesise that the key components of the PANDA model of care include the de-siloed, non-stigmatising multidisciplinary care and the low stimulus environment; however, this remains to be proven.

Limitations

Descriptions of PANDA activity were based on admissions rather than patients; some patients had multiple presentations. PANDA readmissions likely underestimate readmissions to hospital, as patients may have been admitted to another unit or hospital. Furthermore, homelessness is likely under-reported. Data on security attendances were not available across the study period, precluding precomparison/post-comparison. Similarly, as the cohort of patients admitted to PANDA was poorly defined in routinely collected ED data prior to 2020, we were not able to determine the impact of PANDA on clinical outcomes. Interview recruitment was initially slow. The brief structured interview guide helped overcome this barrier, ensuring uniform exploration of participant experiences, while reducing interview time, thus minimising any delays to discharge that were noted as a patient concern. We acknowledge the potential for sampling bias to affect recruitment, whereby those with a strong opinion about their PANDA experience may be more likely to participate. As interviews were conducted prior to discharge, the patient experience of the entire discharge process or uptake of referrals or community services was not captured. Future interview studies could consider a longitudinal approach.

Conclusion

PANDA provides a non-stigmatising multidisciplinary team model that provides individualised patient care across a brief admission, addressing some of the barriers to care access for people with co-occurring SUDs and mental health disorders. Extending the model of care to include community follow-up could be facilitated with extra funding and help patients achieve their long-term health goals—‘to get better’.
