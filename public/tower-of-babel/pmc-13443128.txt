Melatonin and Lactation in Ruminants Dairy Animals: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of Preclinical Studies
Authors: Reza Sattarpour, Maryam Noori, Parvaneh Sadeghimoghadam, Nasim Sattarpour, Parvin Bastani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsMelatonin is present in breast milk with a diurnal pattern, potentially supporting early circadian rhythm development. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of exogenous melatonin supplementation, compared with no treatment or placebo, on milk yield, milk composition, somatic cell count, colostrum composition, and neonatal growth outcomes in lactating ruminant livestock.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar was conducted up to March 15, 2026. All continuous outcomes were pooled as standardized mean differences (SMD) to account for variability in measurement scales across studies.ResultsWe identified 25 eligible studies in lactating animals. Melatonin supplementation did not significantly alter milk yield across species (SMD = 0.49). Significant improvements were observed in milk fat (SMD = 0.77) and protein content (SMD = 0.39), particularly in cows. Melatonin also significantly reduced somatic cell count (SMD = −1.98). Other components such as lactose, total solids, and solid not fats showed no significant change. Neonatal outcomes, including birth weight, weight at weaning, and daily gain, showed no significant improvement. Colostrum analysis showed a small increase in lactose content but no consistent effects on fat, protein, or IgG. However, most pooled analyses demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I2 frequently > 90%).ConclusionExogenous melatonin shows potential for improving milk quality and mammary health in ruminant dairy animals, but its impact on milk yield and neonatal development remains unclear. These preclinical findings are hypothesis‑generating, and carefully designed clinical studies would be required before considering melatonin use in lactating women.

Introduction

Melatonin is generated mainly by the pineal gland in the brain, which regulates the body's circadian rhythm and sleep‐wake cycle. The hormone is commonly referred to as the “sleep hormone” since it influences sleeping patterns [1]. Light exposure modulates melatonin production, with levels rising in the evening as darkness falls and decreasing in the morning. Melatonin has several additional roles in the body in addition to regulating sleep [2]. Melatonin is a powerful antioxidant that protects cells from oxidative stress and free radical damage, regulates immunological function and may help the body fight infections [3, 4, 5]. Melatonin also impacts reproductive cycles in some animals, influencing seasonal breeding in ruminants like sheep and goats, which is crucial for the dairy industry [6]. While these findings in animal models are informative and biologically plausible, they do not necessarily translate directly to humans.

Breast milk contains a range of bioactive compounds and is more than just a source of nutrition [7]. Interestingly, Arslanoglu et al. [8] demonstrated that breast milk includes melatonin, which may assist in regulating baby sleep patterns and promoting the establishment of circadian rhythms. There is a diurnal pattern to the melatonin levels in breast milk, which are higher at night and lower throughout the day [9]. Infants are born with immature circadian systems [10]. For the first few months of life, they rely on external cues to establish their internal biological clocks. Melatonin from breast milk acts as one of these critical cues, helping to entrain the infant's circadian rhythm [8, 9, 11]. This is especially important because the neonatal pineal gland does not produce melatonin rhythmically for several months after birth; this deficiency can be even more prolonged in preterm infants [12]. Although preliminary data suggest that taking exogenous melatonin while breastfeeding may be safe, the literature lacks robust, well‐controlled studies.

Despite the lack of human clinical trials on melatonin supplementation during breastfeeding, it has been used for years in other mammals especially in the dairy industry. Therefore, this systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of exogenous melatonin supplementation, compared with no treatment or placebo, on milk yield, milk composition, somatic cell count, colostrum composition, and neonatal growth outcomes in lactating ruminant livestock. This will help on better understanding potential mechanisms and generate hypotheses for future human research.

Methods

Search Strategy

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and the completed PRISMA checklist is provided as supporting material [13]. An internal protocol outlining the research question, eligibility criteria, and statistical analysis plan was developed a priori and agreed upon by all authors.

From the beginning up to March 15, 2026, we thoroughly searched electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Additionally, the first 100 results of the Google Scholar search engine were screened for eligible articles. Melatonin and breastfeeding‐related keywords along with Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms were combined in the search strategy. The search strings for each database were provided in Table S1. We also manually searched the reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles to identify additional eligible studies by citation.

Eligibility Criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study consisted of 1) experimental original research articles; 2) studies conducted on lactating subjects (human or animal); 3) studies designed to have at least two research arms one of which was assigned as a control group; 4) studies with at least 10 subjects included per study group; and 5) studies published in peer‐reviewed journals. These studies were required to investigate the relationship between melatonin and breastfeeding outcomes and provide quantitative data on exogenous melatonin effects in breastfeeding. Studies in which melatonin was administered directly to infants or pregnant women without lactation‐related outcomes in breastfeeding mothers were excluded. While human and non‐ruminant animal studies were not excluded a priori, no such studies met the prespecified inclusion criteria for quantitative synthesis. Consequently, the final analysis was restricted to preclinical studies in ruminant livestock.

Specifically, our research question was: “In lactating subjects, does melatonin supplementation, compared with no treatment or placebo, affect milk yield, milk composition, and neonatal growth outcomes?” Additionally, only full‐text articles available in English were considered. If the full‐texts of the articles were not accessible online, we contacted the corresponding authors via email to request the relevant materials. Conversely, studies were excluded if they were case reports, reviews, editorials, or conference abstracts.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Literature screening and management were conducted using EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics) for initial duplicate removal. Two independent reviewers (RS and MN) screened the titles and abstracts of all identified studies. Full‐text articles of potentially eligible studies were then assessed for inclusion. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (RS and MN) using pre‐piloted Excel templates. The following information was extracted from each included study: first author, year of publication, study design, country of origin, animal types and their breeds, sample size for each group, time of melatonin administration, time of first milk record, routine of milking, schedule and dose of melatonin administration, and main outcomes. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (PS).

Outcomes

The outcomes analyzed were related to lactation performance and neonatal development following melatonin supplementation in lactating animals. These included milk yield, milk composition (fat, protein, lactose, total solids, and solid not fats), and somatic cell count as an indicator of udder health. Neonatal outcomes included birth weight, live weight at weaning, and average daily gain. Colostrum composition, including fat, protein, lactose, and IgG was also examined to assess passive immunity transfer.

Quality Assessment

Risk of bias in the included animal studies was evaluated independently by two reviewers using the SYstematic Review Centre for Laboratory animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) risk of bias tool, which is specifically adapted for preclinical intervention studies. Risk‐of‐bias judgments were used to inform the interpretation of the quantitative synthesis. All eligible studies were included in the primary meta‐analyses regardless of risk‐of‐bias rating to avoid potential selection bias and loss of information. This tool assesses ten domains across six bias types, including selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other sources of bias. For each domain, reviewers applied the accompanying signaling questions to judge risk as “low,” “high,” or “unclear” based on study reports. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer adjudicated.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Effect sizes were calculated using the meta esize command in Stata. All continuous outcomes were pooled as standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Raw mean differences are most appropriate when studies report the same outcome using identical units and comparable measurement procedures. In contrast, the present evidence base included multiple ruminant species with distinct biological baselines, differing lactation physiology, and variable reporting formats. Under these circumstances, SMD provides a scale‐free estimate that reduces dependence on species‐specific units and permits synthesis across studies with heterogeneous outcome metrics.

Meta‐analyses employed a random‐effects model (DerSimonian‐Laird method) [14] to accommodate expected heterogeneity arising from differences in species, melatonin dosing regimens, and study design. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, which describes the percentage of total variation across studies attributable to between‐study heterogeneity rather than chance, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% denoting low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [15]. The primary analyses, including pooled estimates for milk yield, milk composition, somatic cell count, and neonatal outcomes, were prespecified based on the study objectives. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on animal species and were also defined a priori. We conducted sensitivity analyses by omitting individual studies one at a time (leave‐one‐out analysis) to evaluate their influence on the overall effect size. For outcomes that included at least 10 study arms, small‐study effects and potential publication bias were assessed using Egger's regression test. A p‐value < 0.05 in Egger's test was considered indicative of small‐study effects. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all statistics.

Results

The systematic search resulted in 604 records of which 43 were duplicates and excluded. Of the remaining 561 studies, 525 studies were excluded after title/abstract screening. Furthermore, the full‐text of three articles was not available. Following the full‐text screening of 33 records, two studies did not use exogenous melatonin [16, 17], two studies included less than 10 subjects per study group [18, 19], two studies did not report the outcomes of interest [20, 21], one study reported repetitive results [22], and one study reported insufficient data [23]. Finally, 25 animal studies met the eligibility criteria and were included (Figure 1). Although studies on both human and animal lactating subjects were eligible, no human and non‐ruminant animal studies met the criteria for quantitative synthesis; therefore, the systematic review was restricted to preclinical studies in ruminant livestock.

Baseline Characteristics of Included Studies

Table 1 summarizes 25 preclinical studies conducted between 2005 and 2025 that examined the effects of melatonin supplementation on milk production and related outcomes in lactating animals. These studies were conducted in diverse geographical regions, including China, Spain, Italy, Poland, the USA, Canada, Greece, Mexico, Australia, and New Zealand, reflecting a broad interest in melatonin's role in animal lactation across different climates and production systems. The interventions varied considerably, with melatonin administered during pregnancy, lactation, or both, and through multiple routes such as subcutaneous implants, injections, or oral supplementation. Sample sizes differed widely, from small controlled trials involving fewer than 20 animals per group to large‐scale studies with over 2000 subjects, particularly in Ewe research. Milk yield, milk composition, and offspring development were among the key outcomes measured at various postpartum intervals.

Effect of Melatonin on Milk Yield

The meta‐analysis included 21 study arms evaluating the effect of melatonin on milk yield in lactating animals across different species. A single study in does reported a significant increase in milk yield (SMD: 1.53, 95% CI: 0.65 to 2.42), while another study in goats noted a significant decrease following melatonin administration (SMD: −0.95, 95% CI: −1.77 to −0.13). Among buffalos, cows, ewes, and sheep, melatonin had no effect on milk yield. The overall pooled effect across all species demonstrated no significant change in milk yield with melatonin administration (SMD = 0.49, 95% CI: −0.35 to 1.33). Nevertheless, substantial heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 98.75%) (Figure 2). The very high heterogeneity suggests substantial variability in effect sizes across studies, likely reflecting differences in animal species, melatonin dosing regimens, and study conditions. Also, this high heterogeneity indicates that the pooled SMD should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as an exploratory summary of highly variable study effects, rather than as a precise estimate of a common treatment effect.

Effect of Melatonin on Milk Content

Fat

The meta‐analysis included 26 study arms evaluating the effect of melatonin on milk fat content across buffalos, cows, does, ewes, goats, and sheep. In cows, multiple studies showed a generally positive effect on fat content (SMD = 1.71, 95% CI: 0.66 to 2.76), with SMDs ranging from −0.57 to 3.20, though with significant between‐study variability (I2 = 86.62%). Likewise, melatonin supplementation increased the fat content of milk among the subgroup of ewes (SMD = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.10 to 1.46; I2 = 96.26%). Among other species, a negligible effect of melatonin on fat content of milk was reported. Overall, melatonin supplementation was associated with a statistically significant increase in milk fat content, with a pooled SMD of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.31 to 1.22), although heterogeneity was considerable (I2 = 95.08%) (Figure 3A).

Protein

Figure 3B presents a meta‐analysis of the effects of melatonin supplementation on milk protein content across various ruminant species. In cows, there was a positive effect of melatonin on protein content of milk with a SMD of 1.59 (95% CI: 0.21 to 2.97; I2 = 92.94%). Despite the variability across species and individual studies, the overall pooled effect revealed a significant positive impact of melatonin on milk protein content, with an SMD of 0.39 (95% CI: 0.04 to 0.74). However, the analysis indicated high heterogeneity (I2 = 91.85%), suggesting substantial variability in study outcomes that may be attributed to differences in species, dosage, treatment duration, or study design.

Lactose

The impact of melatonin supplementation on milk lactose content was evaluated across 25 study arms, as shown in Figure 3C. Among ewes, melatonin supplementation resulted in increased lactose content with a SMD of 2.09 (95% CI: 0.20 to 3.98; I2 = 99.48%). Also, a single study carried out in goats found a large positive effect of melatonin and milk lactose content (SMD = 1.60, 95% CI: 0.70 to 2.49). The lactose content did not differ between intervention and placebo groups after melatonin regimen in studies conducted in buffalos, cows, does, and sheep. Overall, melatonin did not influence lactose concentration in milk (SMD = 0.88, 95% CI: −0.32 to 2.09), although the heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 99.28%).

Total Solids

Regarding milk total solids contents, seven study arms, conducting on buffalos, cows, and ewes were included (Figure 3D). The only study on cows reported a negative association between melatonin supplementation and milk total solid content (SMD = −0.71, 95% CI: −1.40 to −0.01). Notably, the overall effect size demonstrated no effect of melatonin on total solids (SMD = −0.03, 95% CI: −0.33 to 0.28) with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 34.37%).

Solid Not Fats

According to Figure 3E, nine study arms were included to the meta‐analysis. Following melatonin supplementation, the milk solid not fat content had no significant difference between intervention and control groups, neither among subgroups of species, nor in overall effect size (SMD = 0.76, 95% CI: −1.08 to 2.59; I2 = 98.17%).

Somatic Cell Count

Based on Figure 3F, 15 study arms were included in the meta‐analysis of melatonin supplementation and milk somatic cell count across multiple livestock species. The meta‐analysis reveals species‐specific variations in response: buffalos showed mixed and non‐significant results (SMD = 0.50, 95% CI: −0.28 to 1.28), while cows consistently demonstrated significant reductions (SMD = −2.49, 95% CI: −3.79 to −1.20) across six studies. Sheep exhibited the largest treatment effect (SMD = −3.87, 95% CI: −4.53 to −3.21), followed by goats (SMD = −1.67, 95% CI: −2.58 to −0.77), while ewes showed a substantial but non‐significant reduction (SMD = −2.18, 95% CI: −4.97 to 0.61). Finally, a significant overall reduction in milk somatic cell count, with a pooled SMD of −1.98 (95% CI: −3.00 to −0.96) and considerable between study heterogeneity of (I2 = 97.60%).

Effect of Melatonin on Birth Outcomes

Birth Weight of Offspring

The forest plot analysis of melatonin's effect on offspring birth weight across multiple livestock species revealed consistently minimal effects across all animal groups (Figure 4A). Among cows, does, ewes, and goats, melatonin had no effect on birth weight, while in sheep, there was a significant positive trend (SMD = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.71 to 2.45). The overall meta‐analysis across all species indicated no significant effect of melatonin supplementation on birth weight (SMD = −0.06, 95% CI: −0.46 to 0.35), with high evidence of heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 92.07%).

Live Weight at Weaning of Offspring

The meta‐analysis evaluating the effect of melatonin supplementation on live weight at weaning of offspring showed a negative association among cows (SMD = −2.23, 95% CI: −2.87 to −1.59) and a positive association among sheep (SMD = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.74 to 2.50) (Figure 4B). The overall effect size indicated no significant impact of melatonin on live weight at weaning (SMD = −0.32, 95% CI: −1.16 to 0.52) and there was high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 98.81%).

Average Daily Gain of Offspring

Figure 4C illustrates the effect of melatonin supplementation on average daily gain of offspring across does, ewes, and goats. In does, a single study demonstrated that melatonin significantly increased average daily gain (SMD = 1.61, 95% CI: 0.91 to 2.31). Among ewes and goats, melatonin had a negligible and non‐significant effect. The overall effect size indicated a non‐significant increase in average daily gain with melatonin supplementation (SMD = 0.42, 95% CI: −0.04 to 0.87) and there was substantial heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 85.96%).

Effect of Melatonin on Colostrum Content

Fat

The meta‐analysis of melatonin supplementation on colostrum fat content across eight study arms showed a small, non‐significant increase with a SMD of 0.19 (95% CI: −0.01 to 0.38), indicating a slight upward trend toward fat content that did not reach statistical significance, with moderate heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 38.40%) (Figure 5A).

Protein

The forest plot analysis of melatonin's effect on colostrum protein content showed considerable variation among studies with no significant overall effects (SMD = 0.24, 95% CI: −0.17 to 0.64) with high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 86.56%), indicating substantial inconsistency in treatment responses across different experimental conditions (Figure 5B).

Lactose

The meta‐analysis of melatonin supplementation on colostrum lactose content demonstrated a small but statistically significant positive effect. The overall pooled effect size was SMD = 0.14 (95% CI: 0.01 to 0.27), indicating a significant increase in colostrum lactose content with minimal heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 2.54%) (Figure 5C).

IgG

The pooled analysis of melatonin's effect on colostrum IgG content revealed highly variable and inconsistent results across studies. The overall effect was non‐significant (SMD = −0.59, 95% CI: −2.72 to 1.54) with extremely high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.38%), indicating substantial inconsistency in treatment effects across studies (Figure 5D).

Leave‐One‐Out Sensitivity Analysis

Overall, the results were stable, as excluding any single study did not materially change the direction or statistical significance of most outcomes. Milk yield remained non‐significant, while milk fat content and somatic cell count remained consistently significant across all iterations, indicating robust findings. Milk protein content showed minor variations in borderline cases but remained generally stable. Other outcomes, including milk lactose, solid not fats, total solids, and neonatal parameters, remained consistently non‐significant. Colostrum‐related outcomes showed slight fluctuations in some iterations; however, these did not meaningfully affect the overall interpretation. Overall, the findings suggest that the results were robust and not driven by any single study (Table S3).

Publication Bias

For outcomes with at least 10 study arms, Egger's regression test suggested evidence of small‐study effects for milk yield (p = 0.001), milk fat (p < 0.001), milk protein (p < 0.001), and milk lactose (p = 0.03). In contrast, Egger's test was not statistically significant for somatic cell count, birth weight, live weight at weaning, or average daily gain (all p > 0.05), indicating no clear evidence of small‐study effects for these outcomes. These findings suggest that publication bias and/or small‐study effects may partly contribute to the large effect sizes observed for some milk composition outcomes, and the corresponding pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Quality Appraisal of Primary Studies

The methodological quality of the 25 included animal studies was evaluated using SYRCLE's Risk of Bias tool. Sequence generation was rated as low risk in 18 studies (72%), and baseline characteristics were adequately reported in 23 studies (92%). However, allocation concealment and random housing were poorly reported, with most studies rated as unclear risk. The most frequent concern was blinding of caregivers/investigators, rated as high risk in 22 studies (88%). Similarly, blinding of outcome assessors and random outcome assessment were often rated as unclear or high risk, indicating limited implementation of blinding procedures. In contrast, incomplete outcome data and selective outcome reporting were well managed, with all studies rated as low risk in these domains. Other sources of bias were judged as low risk in 18 studies (72%). Overall, while reporting and attrition bias were generally low, performance and detection biases were common due to insufficient blinding procedures (Table S2).

Discussion

Our comprehensive meta‐analysis investigated the effects of melatonin supplementation on lactation performance and offspring outcomes in various ruminant livestock species. Although melatonin is recognized for its regulatory role in circadian and reproductive physiology, its impact on lactation‐related parameters appears highly variable and species‐specific. Overall, melatonin did not significantly influence milk yield across species, despite individual studies in does and goats reporting contradictory outcomes. However, beneficial effects were observed for milk composition, with significant improvements in fat and protein content, particularly in cows. A robust and consistent reduction in somatic cell count, especially in cows and sheep, suggests melatonin's potential for improving udder health. Meanwhile, birth and growth outcomes in offspring did not show significant improvements. These apparent results occurred in the context of high between‑study heterogeneity for most outcomes, which substantially limits the certainty and generalizability of the pooled effect sizes.

Effect of Exogenous Melatonin on Milk Volume

The relationship between melatonin and sleep is well established. Literature indicates the sleep deprivation directly affects prolactin concentration in the blood, which may reduce breast milk production [48, 49]. A case study documented a strong correlation between stage N3 (slow wave sleep) duration and milk production. This research revealed that milk production is positively linked to subjective sleepiness in the morning while negatively correlated with the number of awakenings [50]. Direct measurements of milk volume have confirmed the relationship between sleep and lactation outcomes [51]. A study of 29 first‐time mothers found that poor sleep quality was the only significant predictor for lower milk volume (β = −0.70) [52]. Additional research with hospitalized mothers found that stress, postnatal depression, and fatigue are negatively associated with milk volume, while sleep‐related psychological states may negatively influence milk volume [53, 54]. Theoretically, melatonin's ability to regulate circadian rhythms and improve antioxidant capacity should optimize mammary gland function during the energy‐demanding lactation period. While this background is relevant to understanding why human breastfeeding researchers may find these questions important, the present analysis is restricted to ruminant models and these human observations cannot be directly linked to our findings. Results from our meta‐analysis and previous animal studies showed that melatonin administration generally does not have a consistent or significant effect on milk yield across various lactating animal species.

It is suggested that melatonin acts as a marker of photoperiod manipulation, indicating its potential role in regulating seasonal physiological processes [43, 55]. The presence of melatonin receptors (MT1 and MT2) in the mammary gland indicates a direct involvement of melatonin in regulating mammary physiology during lactation [56, 57]. Also, the exact hormonal interaction of melatonin is not clear but most studies suggest that prolactin is affected [17, 19, 42, 58]. Studies reported contrasting results on the interaction of melatonin and prolactin, which is required for development of the mammary glands and the initiation and maintenance of lactation. Some studies have suggested that exposure to short photoperiods or melatonin supplementation to mimic short‐day conditions can reduce circulating prolactin levels and downregulate prolactin receptor gene expression in mammary epithelial cells in several species, including cattle [19, 36, 42, 46, 57, 59, 60]. Others indicated that melatonin's mechanism of action involves the repression of estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) and thereby affecting mammary gland development and proliferation [57].

Melatonin's interaction with growth hormone and insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1) pathways could be another explanation for understanding its effects [61, 62]. Studies indicate that long days increase IGF‐1 in heifers and lactating cows, which result in increasing milk yield. While melatonin feeding suppressed the increase of IGF‐1 in heifers induced by long days but did not affect milk yield [58, 60]. Despite controversial results of individual studies, our overall pooled effect across all species indicated no significant change in milk yield after melatonin supplementation. A study by Chemineau et al. demonstrated that melatonin reduced milk production when applied immediately after kidding in sheep and goats [63]. This was confirmed by another study in which melatonin directly modulated milk yield by decreasing the expression of β‐casein in cultured mammary epithelial cells of dairy goats [17]. On the other hand, it was shown that improving melatonin production through tryptophan enhanced regimens, stimulated the secretion of prolactin and milk volume [64, 65]. As a result, the direct effect of melatonin supplementation on milk yield is questioned. Further preclinical and translational research is needed to determine whether these mechanisms operate similarly in human lactation, and to identify the biological and methodological factors contributing to the observed heterogeneity.

Effect of Exogenous Melatonin on Milk Composition

Based on our meta‐analysis melatonin supplementation demonstrated a pronounced positive effect on milk fat and protein content, particularly in bovine species. Research indicates that melatonin can protect against mycotoxin‐induced damage to mammary cells by enhancing the AKT‐mTOR‐PPARγ‐ACSL4 pathway, which is important for milk fat synthesis [30, 66, 67]. Additionally, melatonin's ability to mediate signaling pathways in various tissues, including adipose tissue, may play a role in these changes [51]. Nevertheless, inconsistency across studies was evident regarding melatonin's effect on milk fat content. One possible reason is that melatonin may affect the particular fat profile of milk rather than its total concentration [31, 39]. For example, research in sheep found that melatonin induces an increase in saturated fatty acids and a decrease in unsaturated and volatile fatty acids in milk, which might result in varying total fat content depending on species [39].

Similarly, milk protein content showed significant improvement. These findings suggest that melatonin may enhance the nutritional quality of milk by increasing its primary macronutrient components. These improvements align with melatonin's documented antioxidant properties and its role in protecting mammary epithelial cells from oxidative stress [56, 68]. Melatonin enhances the metabolic activity of mammary epithelial cells, affecting milk composition positively [27]. According to El‐Mokadem et al. and Lin et al., melatonin may lead to a rise in serum glucose concentration [69, 70]. Increasing glucose concentration enhances amino acid absorption by mammary tissues, leading to more efficient milk protein synthesis [71]. IGF‐1, also promotes protein synthesis in bovine mammary epithelial cells and may impact the production of milk‐specific proteins [72].

In contrast, melatonin showed no significant effects on other milk composition parameters. Lactose content remained largely unaffected across most species. Total solids and solid not fat content demonstrated no significant overall changes. This is in contrast with the results of a recent study that indicates activation of melatonin receptor 1 (MT1) downregulates prolactin receptor gene expression in mammary epithelial cells and subsequently reduces lactose synthesis in the Golgi apparatus [19]. Additionally, other studies found that melatonin feeding at doses of 2.5 mg/kg/d and 5 mg/kg/d significantly lowered milk lactose content in a dose‐dependent manner, indicating that melatonin's effects on milk composition are not only mediated through gene expression changes but also through its dosage and administration timing [38, 73].

Implications for Somatic Cell Count and Milk Quality

A rise in somatic cell count reflects the influx of neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes recruited during mastitis or sub‐clinical inflammation. Elevated somatic cell count diminishes lactose synthesis and, oxidizes milk lipids. Our study showed a clinically significant reduction in somatic cell count after melatonin supplementation across multiple species, with an overall pooled SMD of −1.98, suggesting the presence of its anti‐inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. Generally, lower somatic cell counts are directly associated with improved milk quality and reduced mastitis incidence. Studies demonstrated that melatonin can reduce inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α while enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities [56, 74, 75, 76]. Also, melatonin may cause a transient increase in immunoglobulin levels (IgG and IgM) in subclinical mastitis. This immune enhancement could lead to improved milk composition by reducing the inflammatory response [77]. This dual action of reducing inflammation while boosting cellular defense mechanisms creates an optimal environment for mammary gland health.

Effects of Melatonin on Infants

Infants, particularly preterm neonates, are unable to produce sufficient endogenous melatonin mainly because of the immature pineal gland. Plasma melatonin concentrations of infants born before 34 gestational weeks measured at birth and on day 3 were below detectable levels (≤ 7 pg/mL) in 78% and 81%, respectively. Very preterm infants (gestational age ≤ 29 weeks + 6 days) are unable to produce detectable plasma levels of melatonin during their first days of life [78]. Melatonin can remove free radicals and strengthen the body's antioxidant defense. It also can modulate inflammation, which helps the baby's immune system [79, 80, 81]. Outside the scope of our inclusion criteria, a pilot study of 36 preterm newborns found that early melatonin administration to infants (0.5 mg/kg once daily) significantly reduced lipid peroxidation markers (i.e., F2‐isoprostane) at 48 h, but this trial did not involve lactating mothers or breastfeeding outcomes and was therefore not included in our meta‐analysis [79].

Multiple animal studies have shown that melatonin improves neurological function, restores glymphatic function, maintains blood‐brain barrier integrity, alleviates brain edema, and reduces brain atrophy [82, 83]. A body of literature has explored melatonin's potential neuroprotective, cardioprotective, anti‐inflammatory, and antioxidant properties in human preterm neonates, including studies addressing infants' skin allergic responses, sepsis, necrotizing enterocolitis, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia [1, 7, 84, 85, 86, 87]. Although these outcomes were not evaluated in the present meta‐analysis, the anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms documented in our ruminant synthesis may represent shared biological pathways.

Despite these potentially beneficial effects reported in various infant and neonatal clinical contexts, our meta‐analysis of ruminant offspring showed that melatonin supplementation had no statistically significant effect on neonatal growth parameters, including birth weight, weaning weight, and average daily gain. These findings indicate that, based on current preclinical evidence, improvements in certain aspects of milk composition do not clearly translate into measurable benefits in offspring growth. Therefore, these findings do not provide strong evidence for beneficial effects on neonatal growth or development. The discrepancy between improvements in milk composition and the lack of observable neonatal benefits may be related to differences in study design, duration of exposure, or species‐specific responses. Accordingly, extrapolation of animal data to human infants should be made with caution, and the available human neonatal studies should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating rather than confirmatory.

Limitations

Our findings provide preclinical mechanistic insights that may inform the design of future translational research; however, they cannot be directly extrapolated to breastfeeding women without further evidence from human studies. The high heterogeneity observed across all parameters (I2 ranging from 86% to 99%) represents a limitation that demands careful interpretation. This substantial variability suggests that the effects of melatonin supplementation are highly dependent on multiple factors not fully captured in the current meta‐analysis. Differences in experimental design, including treatment timing, delivery methods, and measurement protocols as well as differences in species physiology and lactation stage are plausible contributors to this variability. Also, the variation in study quality and methodological approaches across different research groups likely contributes to the observed heterogeneity. Seasonal variations, photoperiod exposure, stress levels, and nutritional status can all influence melatonin's effectiveness. These contextual factors are often not standardized across studies, leading to increased variability in outcomes. Furthermore, although we developed an internal protocol before conducting the review, it was not prospectively registered, which may introduce a risk of selective reporting and should be considered when interpreting our findings. In addition, the great number of studies with a high or unclear risk of performance and detection bias, primarily due to insufficient blinding of caregivers and outcome assessors, may have influenced the pooled estimates, and this should be taken into account in our interpretation of the results. In addition, non‐English studies were not retrieved in our study. This language restriction may have introduced publication bias and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, all included studies were conducted in ruminant dairy species, and the biology of commercial milk production differs in important ways from human lactation physiology, neonatal feeding behavior, and maternal endocrine regulation. As such, the translational relevance of these findings to human breastfeeding remains speculative and requires direct clinical evaluation.

Research Gaps and Future Directions

Despite the high heterogeneity, the consistent direction of effects for key parameters (fat, protein, and somatic cell count) suggests genuine biological activity worthy of further investigation. While exogenous melatonin use during breastfeeding appears to be relatively safe, more research is needed to fully understand its effects and establish clear guidelines. Future human studies should first establish the safety, pharmacokinetics, and biological effects of maternal melatonin supplementation during lactation before evaluating potential effects on milk composition and mammary gland health.

Conclusion

Our meta‐analysis provides evidence supporting melatonin's potential as a modulator of lactation‐related outcomes in ruminant dairy species. The significant improvements in milk quality indicators such as fat and protein content, coupled with substantial reductions in somatic cell count, suggest biologically plausible mechanisms that may be relevant to mammary gland health more broadly. However, melatonin supplementation did not affect milk yield and neonatal growth parameters. Given that the current evidence is derived exclusively from animal studies and high level of heterogeneity, caution is required when interpreting the findings. Although the observed effects may indicate potential biological relevance, their translation to human lactation remains unclear. Future research in humans should be considered exploratory and aimed at confirming these preliminary observations.
