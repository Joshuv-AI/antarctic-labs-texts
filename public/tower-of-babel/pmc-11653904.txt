Serotype distribution and clinical characteristics of Group B Streptococcus Bacteremia in nonpregnant adults: a 15-Year Multicenter Study in Korea
Authors: Minji Kim, Su-Mi Choi, Sehyeon Ji, David Cho, Doyoung Han, Ahrang Lee, Hae Seong Jung, Sarah Kim, Sung Un Shin, Uh Jin Kim, Seung-Ji Kang, Kyung-Hwa Park, Sook In Jung, Seong Eun Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGroup B Streptococcus (GBS) bacteremia in nonpregnant adults is of increasing concern, particularly among the elderly in underlying conditions. This study analyzed the serotype distribution, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and clinical characteristics of GBS bacteremia in nonpregnant adults over a 15-year period in two tertiary hospitals in Korea.MethodsFrom 2007 to 2021, patients aged ≥ 19 years with GBS bacteremia were identified via retrospective electronic medical record review. GBS isolates were collected through a hospital-wide surveillance system and confirmed via MALDI-TOF-MS. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was conducted for serotype VIII and undetermined serotype isolates. Clinical and laboratory data were analyzed to assess trends and mortality risk factors.ResultsA total of 264 episodes of GBS bacteremia were identified, with 147 isolates successfully re-cultured and 125 isolates and patients included in the clinical characteristic analysis. Serotype VIII emerged as the most common serotype (42.1% in 2019–2021) with a significant increase in prevalence over time (P = 0.02). MLST of serotype VIII revealed ST2 as the dominant sequence type (87.8%). Antimicrobial resistance rates for erythromycin, clindamycin, and levofloxacin were 27.2%, 30.4%, and 23.2%, respectively, with notable variability among serotypes. The 30-day mortality rate was 12.8%. Male sex (aOR: 2.18; 95% CI: 1.15–4.13, P = 0.02) and SOFA score (aOR per unit increase: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.12–1.38, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with mortality.ConclusionsThis study highlights the emergence of serotype VIII as a predominant cause of GBS bacteremia and its association with ST2 in South Korea. Male sex and higher SOFA scores were independent risk factors for mortality. The findings emphasize the need for ongoing surveillance and consideration of serotype-specific strategies in clinical management and vaccine development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10337-9.Keywords: Group B Streptococcus, Bacteremia, Serotype distribution, Nonpregnant adults, Korea

Introduction

Streptococcus agalactiae, commonly known as Group B Streptococcus (GBS), is a Gram-positive bacterium that predominantly colonizes the gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts. This bacterium is stratified into ten serotypes— Ia, Ib, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, and IX—each characterized by distinct cell wall polysaccharides [1]. GBS is well-known for posing significant health risks to pregnant women and newborns [2, 3]. Beyond these populations, GBS also impacts nonpregnant adults, marking a notable global increase in invasive GBS disease, particularly among the elderly and those with underlying diseases [4, 5].

The distribution of GBS serotype in invasive diseases varies across different populations. In pregnant adults and neonates, serotypes Ia, III, and V predominate globally [3], whereas serotype VI to IX are more commonly found in Asia [6, 7]. In nonpregnant adults, the serotype distribution is similar in Europe and the US [4, 5]. Previous studies in Europe and the US have shown that the rising incidence of invasive GBS disease is primarily due to an increase in serotype V [8–10]. However, there is a scarcity of studies on the epidemiology of invasive GBS disease in nonpregnant adults in Asia, particularly in South Korea.

In this study, we aim to evaluate GBS bacteremia in nonpregnant adults over a 15-year period in South Korea, focusing on the clinical characteristics, outcomes, and serotype.

Materials and methods

Participating hospitals and subjects

This study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals located in the southwestern region of South Korea: Chonnam National University Hospital (CNUH), a 1,000-bed tertiary teaching hospital, and Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital (CNUHH), a 700-bed branch tertiary teaching hospital. These two hospitals serve as tertiary care centers for approximately 3.3 million people in the region, accounting for 66.7% of all tertiary hospital beds in the serving area (total of 2,550 beds). This study was conducted from January 2007 to December 2021.

Patients and isolates were initially selected as potential study subjects based on the following criteria obtained from electronic medical records review: (a) aged ≥ 19 years, (b) not being pregnant at the time of infection, and (c) the presence of S. agalactiae identified using conventional methods with ID-GP Vitek 2 cards (bioMérieux, Vitek Inc., Hazelwood, MO, USA). Initial isolates were collected through a hospital-wide surveillance system at CNUH and CNUHH and stored at -70℃. Species identification was confirmed via MALDI-TOF-MS (Vitek MS, BioMérieux, SA) performed on successfully re-cultured isolates. Patients and isolates were finally included in the study if isolates were successfully re-cultured and confirmed as S. agalactiae through MALDI-TOF-MS.

Incidence rate

The incidence rate was calculated using the cases meeting above three initial criteria as the numerator. For the denominator, the Institution-Based Incidence Rate (IBIR) was calculated using the total patient-years of individuals aged ≥ 19 years treated at CNUH and CNUHH. To calculate a Population-Based Incidence Rate (PBIR), the denominator was the population aged ≥ 19 years in the institutional coverage area, which includes Gwangju Metropolitan City and Jeollanam-do Province. An estimated population-based tertiary care GBS bacteremia incidence rate (ePBIR) was also calculated by multiplying the population-based incidence rate by the bed ratio (2,550 total tertiary hospital beds of covered area/1,700 beds in study-enrolled hospitals) for this area.

Data collection

We collected clinical and laboratory information, including age, sex, underlying diseases, body mass index (BMI), Charlson comorbidity index score, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, and all-cause 30-day mortality. Antimicrobial susceptibility tests were conducted using automated methods with ID-GP Vitec2 cards (bioMérieux, Vitek Inc., Hazelwood, MO, USA).

Serotype determination

The agglutination test of the SSI Diagnostica ImmuLex™ Strep-B kit (Hillerød, Denmark) was used, which contains 10 serotypes (Ia, Ib, II to VIII and IX), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For serotyping, a single colony was isolated, subcultured on blood agar, and used for serotype and gene detection tests.

Multilocus sequence type analysis (MLST).

MLST was conducted exclusively on strains identified as serotype VIII or undetermined serotype strains to investigate the genetic diversity and confirm the genetic relatedness within these groups. Genomic DNA was extracted from each isolate using the Exgene Cell SV kit (GeneAll) following the Gram-positive bacterial protocol. The internal fragments of the seven housekeeping genes (adhP, pheS, atr, glnA, sdhA, glcK, and tkt) were PCR-amplified using oligonucleotide primers. The PCR reaction conditions were denaturation at 94℃ for 1 min, annealing at 55℃ for 45 s, and extension at 72℃ 1 min for 30 cycles [11]. Subsequently, the amplification products were sequenced, and the identified alleles were submitted to the GBS MLST database (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/streptococcus-agalactiae) for designation. Sequence types (ST) that shared six identical alleles of the seven loci were clustered into a clonal complex (CC) using goeBURST (https://www.phyloviz.net/goeburst/) [12].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U-test or Student’s t-test, depending on data distribution, which was determined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For comparisons across three or more groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test or one-way ANOVA was used, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test depending on suitability. The chi-square test for trend was used to assess the significance of changes in the proportion of serotype VIII cases over the study periods and Poisson Regression analysis was used to analyze changes in incidence rates. Variables with a P-value less than 0.1 in the univariate analysis for all-cause 30-day mortality were included in the multivariate regression model to identify independent risk factors, with the adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. The log-rank test was used to compare time-to-death between serotypes of patients. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Results

In an electronic medical record review, a total of 264 episodes of S. agalactiae bacteremia were identified using conventional methods during the study period. Among them, 147 were successfully re-cultured and 130 were confirmed as S. agalactiae using MALDI-TOF-MS. Serotyping was successfully performed on 125 cases.

Of the total 125 patients, 88 were diagnosed with GBS bacteremia at CNUH and 37 at CNUHH. The median age was 69 years (interquartile range 57–75) and among them 72 (57.6%) patients were older than 65 years. There were 59 male subjects (47.2%). Most patients (109, 87.2%) had at least one underlying medical disease. Hypertension (72, 57.6%) was the most common medical condition, followed by diabetes mellitus (51, 40.8%) and solid organ malignancy (49, 39.2%). The most common primary infection sites for S. agalactiae bacteremia were primary bacteremia (44, 35.2%), followed by bone and joint infection (22, 17.6%) and skin and soft tissue infection (15, 12%).

Serotype analysis revealed significant shifts in the distribution of GBS serotypes over the study period. During the initial study period from 2007 to 2009, serotype Ib and V were most common serotype, accounting for 22.2% of cases, followed by serotype Ia, II, III, VI and VIII each contributing 11.1%. However, in the most recent years, serotype VIII emerged as the predominant serotype, responsible for 42.1% of cases. Concurrently, the prevalence of previously dominant serotypes, such as serotype V, decreased significantly dropping to 10.5% in the same period (Table 1; Fig. 1A). A chi-square test for trend indicated a significant increase in the proportion of serotype VIII compared with all other serotypes combined over the study period (P = 0.02). Serotype analysis also showed that serotype VI was significantly associated with primary bacteremia (Chi-square test, P = 0.01), while serotype Ia was more frequently linked to pneumonia (Chi-square test, P = 0.01).

The incidence rates of S. agalactiae bacteremia were calculated, with IBIR ranging from 2.39 to 4.93 cases per 100,000 person-years, and the ePBIR ranging from 4.78 to 10.9 cases per 1,000,000 population. Poisson regression analysis demonstrated a significant increase in both IBIR and ePBIR (P < 0.001), with an incidence rate ratio of 52.85 (95% CI: 46.9–59.5, both), indicating a statistically significant upward trend. These rates and trends over time are presented in Fig. 1B and detailed in Supplementary Table.

All isolates tested were susceptible to penicillin. Clindamycin resistance was detected in 38 isolates (30.4%), with the highest rates observed in serotype V (11 out of 15, 73.3%) and serotype III (12 out of 18, 66.7%). Erythromycin resistance was present in 34 isolates (27.2%), particularly among serotype V isolates (11 out of 15, 73.3%). Levofloxacin resistance was found in 29 isolates (23.2%), with the highest rate seen in serotype Ib (22 out of 27, 81.5%) (Fig. 2).

The all cause 30-day mortality rate was 12.8% across all serotypes. When analyzing mortality rates by serotype, no significant differences were observed (Log rank test, P = 0.39). Male sex, polymicrobial infection, myocardial infarction, chronic heart failure, and SOFA score were associated with higher mortality in univariate analysis (Table 3). However only male sex (adjusted odds ratio (aOR): 2.18, 95% CI: 1.15–4.13, P = 0.02) and SOFA score (aOR: 1.25 per unit increase, 95% CI: 1.12–1.38, P < 0.001) remained significant risk factors in multivariate analysis (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the serotypes and clinical characteristics of GBS bacteremia in nonpregnant adults from two tertiary hospitals in South Korea over a 15-year period (2007–2021). These two institutions, which serve approximately 3.3 million people in the southwestern region of South Korea and account for 66.7% of tertiary beds in the area, are representative of tertiary care in this region. Our study revealed a notable upward trend in both incidence rates (IBIR, ePBIR) over the study period, accompanied by significant changes in serotype distribution, including an increase in serotype VIII cases, reflecting a growing disease burden in the region.

The prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and solid organ malignancy among patients with GBS bacteremia in this study is consistent with previous studies on invasive GBS disease [5, 13, 14]. The overall 30-day mortality rate was 12.8%, which is comparable to previous studies of invasive GBS disease in nonpregnant adults [14–16]. In our multivariate analysis, male sex and SOFA score were significantly associated with higher mortality rates. Although previous studies have reported a higher incidence of invasive GBS disease in males aged 60 years and older [17, 18], there have been no studies to date demonstrating increased mortality rates among males with invasive GBS disease. To further explore this relationship, we compared the mortality rates between males and females within each serotype in GBS bacteremia. While no individual serotype showed a statistically significant difference in mortality between sexes, serotype VIII demonstrated the closest approach to statistical significance when separately analyzed by serotype (Log rank test, P = 0.07), suggesting a trend toward higher mortality in males. The higher mortality observed in males may be attributed to biological factors, such as sex-specific immune responses with serotype VIII. Additionally, social factors, including delays in seeking medical care or differences in comorbidities, may also play a role. Further investigations, including basic research and studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to validate these hypotheses.

Serotype VIII emerged as the most common serotype (42.1%, 2019–2021) causing GBS bacteremia in this study, contrasting with a meta-analysis of 29,731 isolates from nonpregnant adults where serotype VIII accounted for only 0.2%, while serotype V, Ia, and III were more prevalent [4]. Regionally, GBS serotype VIII was reported more frequently in Korea and Japan than in other regions. Previous studies in these regions have reported colonization of GBS serotype VIII in 20–35% of pregnant women [6, 7]. However, serotype VIII has not been previously reported as the predominant serotype causing invasive GBS disease in nonpregnant adults in the existing literature [16, 19]. This likely reflects regional and temporal trends in GBS epidemiology. A prior Korean study analyzing isolates from 2006 to 2015 found no significant contribution of serotype VIII to invasive GBS infections [16]. In contrast, our study, incorporating data after 2016, highlights the emergence of serotype VIII as a predominant serotype. Such findings underscore the importance of continuous surveillance to capture dynamic shifts in serotype prevalence and their potential clinical implications.

The decision to perform MLST on serotype VIII isolates was motivated by its unusually high prevalence observed in our study compared with previous reports [16, 19]. We aimed to investigate the genetic diversity within serotype VIII to understand its increased prevalence. In our study, ST2 accounted for 87.8% of serotype VIII cases. The high prevalence of ST2 may help explain regional variations, as certain ST can drive the predominance of serotype VIII in specific areas. For instance, a previous study from Canada identified serotype VIII in invasive GBS diseases, with ST42 being the predominant ST [20], indicating that regional differences may indeed be shaped by particular STs.

In our study, resistance to erythromycin, clindamycin, and levofloxacin was observed in 29.6%, 33.3%, and 31.5% of isolates, respectively, aligning with prior findings from South Korea [16]. However, notable variations in antimicrobial resistance were observed across different serotypes, highlighting the heterogeneity within GBS. The highest resistance rates to clindamycin (73.3%) and erythromycin (73.3%) were found in serotype V, followed by serotype III (66.7% and 55.6%, respectively), consistent with previous studies identifying serotype V as a major contributor to antimicrobial resistance in GBS infections [16]. In contrast, serotype VIII exhibited remarkably low resistance rates to clindamycin (12.2%), erythromycin (9.8%), and levofloxacin (2.4%), a finding of particular significance given its increasing prevalence in our study population. Interestingly, serotype Ib demonstrated an exceptionally high levofloxacin resistance rate of 81.5%, underscoring the diversity of resistance patterns among serotypes. These variations suggest that changes in serotype prevalence, such as the rise of serotype VIII, may alter the antimicrobial resistance landscape of GBS.

This study has several limitations. First, it does not provide an explanation for the higher mortality in men compared with women. While a trend was observed for higher mortality in males with serotype VIII, the association did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the small sample size. Second, this study was conducted in two hospitals covering one region in South Korea and may not represent nationwide epidemiological data. Third, we could not include all cases of GBS bacteremia identified by the medical record review because many isolates could not be collected or were not viable. This limitation was more pronounced in older specimens, leading to a higher proportion of identification, serotyping, and MLST being performed on recently acquired samples. However, the older specimen data in our study were consistent with previous serotype studies conducted in Korea, and the recent increase in serotype VIII prevalence – the central focus of this study – was confirmed using the majority of collected samples. Thus, while the exclusion of older specimens may have introduced some selection bias, this does not undermine the importance of our findings regarding recent serotype VIII trends.

In conclusion, this study showed that serotype VIII has become the major serotype causing GBS bacteremia in Korea, and male patients with GBS bacteremia experienced worse outcomes. With the trend of increasing life expectancy and underlying diseases in nonpregnant adults, it is necessary to monitor the epidemiology of strains causing invasive GBS infections in adults. Understanding the serotype distribution of GBS strains and identifying risk factors will be of great benefit for epidemiological insights in this population. Currently, S. agalactiae vaccine development primarily targets pregnant women to prevent neonatal infections and reduce mortality rates [21]. However, with the increasing incidence of invasive GBS disease in nonpregnant adults, ongoing monitoring of disease trends could guide discussions on future broader GBS vaccine applications, supported by evidence from long-term epidemiological studies.
