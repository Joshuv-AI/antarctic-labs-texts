Assessment of child-parent dyads’ health-related quality of life in learning disorder care: a cross-sectional study in eastern China
Authors: Lidan Sun, Lejing Guan, Meiying Gao, Xiaorui Fan, Fangfang Li, Ying Shen, Xia Wang, Weijun Chen, Guannan Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLearning difficulties, often stemming from neurodevelopmental disorders such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), learning disabilities (LD), and intellectual disabilities (ID), profoundly impact children’s academic performance and family well-being, particularly in highly competitive societies like China. Therefore, we aimed to compare multidimensional health-related quality of life (HRQoL; physical, emotional, social, school functioning) of children across four learning difficulty diagnostic subgroups (ADHD-only, LD-only, ID-only, ADHD + LD); as well as the HRQoL of their caregivers.MethodsFour hundred and ninety-two child-parent dyads were included in this questionnaire-based study. HRQoL of children was measured by proxy-reported Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) Generic Core Module (GCM), and parents’ HRQoL was measured by PedsQL Family Impact Module (FIM). Independent t-tests compared the mean PedsQL GCM and FIM scores (total and scales) between each clinical group and healthy controls. The clinical relevance of group differences was evaluated using Cohen’s d effect size. Furthermore, we applied general linear models (GLMs) to examine differences in scale and total scores between each clinical group (ADHD, LD, ID, and ADHD + LD) and healthy controls, adjusting for covariates.ResultsThe median age of children was 8.8 years, and the majority were boys (71.5%). Children in the clinical groups—ADHD, LD, ID, and ADHD + LD—exhibit significantly lower HRQoL scale scores and total scores in both children and their caregivers compared to healthy controls. The pattern of HRQoL impairments differs across these clinical groups.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the profiles of HRQoL in children with ADHD, LD, ID, ADHD + LD, as well as HRQoL patterns of their parents in a clinical sample in China. Our findings advocate for attention on children with learning difficulties in both public health agendas and clinical practice to address both child development and caregiver well-being.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), learning disabilities (LD), intellectual disabilities (ID), and comorbid ADHD + LD exhibit significantly lower health-related quality of life (HRQoL) across multiple domains compared to healthy controls, with distinct impairment patterns across subgroups. Parents in all clinical groups reported impaired HRQoL, particularly in emotional functioning and worry, with largest effects in parents of children with ID.

What is known and what is new?

• Neurodevelopmental disorders impair children’s HRQoL, but previous research focused on single conditions, primarily in high-income countries.

• First systematic comparison of HRQoL profiles across four clinical subgroups (ADHD-only, LD-only, ID-only, ADHD + LD) in a Chinese cohort, simultaneously assessing both child and caregiver HRQoL, revealing condition-specific impairment patterns and compounded family burden in China’s sociocultural context where academic achievement ties to familial honor.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should adopt condition-specific approaches recognizing that HRQoL impairments vary across etiologies. Healthcare systems should integrate routine HRQoL assessments for both children and caregivers into practice, enabling early identification of at-risk families. Public health initiatives should address stigma and raise awareness about neurodevelopmental disorders as medical conditions, particularly in competitive educational environments like China. Multidisciplinary intervention programs should simultaneously support child development and caregiver well-being, including access to respite care, mental health services for parents, and tailored educational accommodations.

Introduction

Children’s academic performance has a significant effect on their lives. In the context of escalating competition within contemporary society, children’s academic struggles have become a major family concern, significantly diminishing the quality of life for both children and their caregivers (1,2). In China, specialized neurodevelopmental clinics (often called ‘Learning Disorder Clinics’ or ‘Learning Difficulty Clinics’) have emerged in recent years. Given the current absence of standardized diagnostic criteria and evidence-based intervention guidelines in China, it is essential to emphasize that learning difficulties encompass a heterogeneous syndrome rather than constituting a discrete disease diagnosis (3). Through extensive case studies, these clinics have demonstrated that learning difficulties in school-aged children stem from neurodevelopmental etiologies, including neurodevelopmental disorders, emotional disturbances, and family-related factors (3). This challenges the traditional view of the public that attributes such difficulties primarily to a lack of study motivation.

Before diagnosing learning disabilities (LD), it is necessary to pay attention to differentiating learning difficulties caused by other conditions, among which attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is the most prevalent, followed by LD, intellectual disabilities (ID), and the comorbidity of ADHD with LD. ADHD frequently manifests with core symptoms of deficits in sustained attention and executive dysfunction that directly impair academic performance. The prevalence of ADHD in children and adolescents was estimated from 2% to 16% (4-6). LD includes selective deficits in academic domains such as reading (dyslexia), writing, or arithmetic (dyscalculia), typically exhibiting lifelong persistence. A 16-years follow-up study in Finland revealed that LD had negative influences on school performances and academic achievements (7). A large-scale survey in China demonstrated that the prevalence of dyslexia and dyscalculia was 6.8% and 7.1%, respectively, while in adolescents it was 5.9% and 6.4%, respectively; 40–50% of children with ADHD meet criteria for LD (8). These dual-diagnosis cases present with heightened neuropsychological deficits, particularly in executive functions such as working memory and behavioral inhibition. In contrast, ID is less common with a prevalence ranging from 0.05–1.55% globally (9). It significantly impairs academic achievement and school participation, with affected children demonstrating marked difficulties in studying, social integration, and adaptive functioning within educational settings.

Despite growing attention on learning difficulties, most existing studies focus mainly on clinical symptom management and medical treatments (10). A literature search revealed that there is a paucity of studies on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) specifically focused on isolated etiological conditions associated with learning difficulties, such as ADHD, LD, ID, or comorbid cases (e.g., ADHD with LD) (10-13). Furthermore, most existing evidence comes from high-income countries, while data from low-income and middle-income regions remain strikingly scarce. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that ADHD was associated with substantially suboptimal HRQoL of affected children (11). Matteucci et al. reported significantly lower level of psychosocial health and HRQoL in children with specific learning disorder compared with the general population (14). Children with ID often face difficulties in learning, communication, and social interactions, which leads to exclusion, reduced self-esteem, and fewer life experiences, negatively impacting their HRQoL (13,15). ADHD with comorbid disorders such as LD means more impairments in functioning, and those children may have worse HRQoL (12). In addition, the distinct HRQoL impairment patterns across different disorders that cause learning difficulties remain uncharacterized. Finally, there is a notable lack of research addressing the impact of China’s specific sociocultural environment, which places a significant emphasis on academic performance (16), on the HRQoL of children with learning difficulties as well as their caregivers.

Therefore, our study compared multidimensional HRQoL (physical, emotional, social, school functioning) of children across four learning difficulty diagnostic subgroups (ADHD-only, LD-only, ID-only, ADHD + LD) using standardized tools [i.e., Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) Generic Core Module (GCM)]; as well as the HRQoL of their caregivers. Addressing these issues necessitates the integration of multidisciplinary perspectives to develop a comprehensive intervention system and provide better health care. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-1-0182/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

The present study was a single-center, cross-sectional study and was conducted in a tertiary children’s hospital in China. Parental caregivers whose children visited the Learning Disorder Clinic between December 2024 and May 2025 were invited to participate in the survey. The inclusion criteria for the study participants were caregivers of children (I) who have attended school, mainly school-age children or preschool children, being exposed to school environment or having learning experience in kindergarten; (II) who visited our Learning Disorder Clinic; (III) who have been examined thoroughly and got a clear medical diagnosis from the specialists for ADHD, LD, ID, or ADHD + LD; and (IV) whose caregivers voluntarily participated in the survey and had sufficient cognitive ability to complete the questionnaire. In addition, we also recruited children who visited the Health Management Center in the same hospital for regular health check as healthy controls. These children had no diagnosis of the above-mentioned conditions and no manifestation of learning difficulty or other physical/mental disorders. Exclusion criteria comprised children who had severe physical or mental disorders. Ultimately, 576 parent-child dyads were included in the survey.

Ethical approval statements

The study protocol was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2024-IRB-0359-P-01). All parents gave their consents and voluntarily participated in the study.

Data collection

Initially, an expert panel was convened, comprising two senior pediatricians, a specialist in HRQoL, and a linguist to develop the questionnaire. Following two rounds of discussion, the questionnaire was formulated and converted into an electronic format using Wenjuanxing website (https://www.wjx.cn/), a widely utilized online platform for creating survey questionnaires. The questionnaire included four sessions, i.e., general characteristics of the child and family, clinical characteristics, children’s HRQoL and caregivers’ HRQoL measures. Then, a pre-test pilot study was conducted involving 40 caregivers of children attending the Learning Disorder Clinic. During this phase, it became apparent that caregivers encountered difficulties completing the electronic questionnaire due to frequent interruptions from using other applications on their smart phone or incoming phone calls. Consequently, we opted to distribute a paper-based version of the questionnaire that was integrated with other paper-based assessment instruments used for diagnosis purpose, such as the Wechsler Intelligence Scale, thereby allowing caregivers sufficient time and focus to complete the questionnaire.

Data obtained through the paper-pencil-based questionnaire administered in the clinic were directly transcribed into an electronic database utilizing EpiData software, a tool designed for epidemiological research by the EpiData Association (www.epidata.dk). The manual data entry into the electronic system was conducted by two full-time pediatricians, who also served as research assistants for this study. To ensure the integrity of the data entry process, the principal investigator performed random audits of the questionnaires. To minimize errors, the original questionnaires were meticulously reviewed, and caregivers were contacted for clarification when necessary. Open text fields were transcribed into the electronic database verbatim from the questionnaires. All measurements were thoroughly validated by analyzing the data for ranges, distributions, means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges (IQRs), outliers, and logical inconsistencies. Data outliers and missing values were verified against the original documentation.

Measurements

General and clinical characteristics

Information on social demographic characteristics including child’s age, gender, maternal/paternal educational level, residence (urban/rural areas) and IQ scores. Education was categorized into three subsequent levels based on the Chinese Standard Classification of Education: high level is a bachelor’s degree or above; middle level includes high school, technical school, vocational secondary school, and vocational high school; and low level refers to no education, primary school or middle school. IQ scores were assessed using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children-Fourth Edition (WISC-IV) Chinese version (17), which measures four core indices: verbal comprehension, perceptual reasoning, working memory, and processing speed. Based on WISC norms, IQ scores were classified into seven categories: ≥130 (very superior), 120–129 (superior), 110–119 (high average), 90–109 (average), 80–89 (low average), 70–79 (borderline), and ≤69 (extremely low).

PedsQL 4.0 GCM

To assess children’s HRQoL, we applied the proxy-reported PedsQL™ 4.0 GCM. Prior to data collection, authorization to use this instrument was obtained through a request on the Mapi Research Trust website (https://eprovide.mapi-trust.org/instruments/pediatric-quality-of-life-inventory). The proxy-reported PedsQL™ 4.0 GCM is administrated to measure HRQoL in children and adolescents ages 2–18 years in the past month (18). It contains 23 items and four scales, i.e., physical (eight items), emotional (five items), social (five items), and school functioning (five items). It is validated in China with good reliability and validity (19,20).

PedsQL™ 3.0 Family Impact Module (FIM)

To evaluate the impact of learning difficulties on caregivers’ HRQoL and family function, we applied the PedsQL™ 3.0 FIM (PedsQL™ 3.0 FIM) (21,22). It contains 28 items and six scales, i.e., physical functioning (six items), emotional functioning (five items), social functioning (four items), cognition functioning (four items), communication (three items), worry (five items), daily activity (three items) and family relationships (five items).

Statistical analysis

In total, 576 parent-child dyads were invited to participate in this study. We excluded 72 questionnaires due to unverifiable outpatient identifiers (IDs), as the absence of unique IDs precluded linkage to children’s medical records. Additionally, two children were excluded due to duplicate medical record numbers. We excluded children with more than 50% of the PedsQL GCM and FIM scale items missing (n=10). This threshold was applied in accordance with the standard PedsQL™ scoring algorithm, which stipulates that at least 50% of items must be completed to ensure the validity and reliability of the scale scores (18). This resulted in a final analytical sample of 492 parent-child dyads.

First, we evaluated normality using Shapiro-Wilk tests, which indicated a non-normal distribution. Thus, we reported medians and IQRs. For categorical variables, numbers and percentages were presented. Second, differences in categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-squared tests, while non-parametric tests were employed for continuous variables across the four clinical groups (ADHD, LD, ID, and ADHD + LD) and the healthy control group. Third, independent t-tests compared the mean PedsQL GCM and FIM scores (total and scales) between each clinical group and healthy controls. The clinical relevance of group differences was evaluated using Cohen’s d effect size, calculated as the absolute difference in mean scores divided by the largest standard deviation. Effect sizes were interpreted as: small (0.2≤d<0.5), moderate (0.5 ≤d<0.8), and large (d≥0.8) (23). Furthermore, general linear models (GLMs) adjusting for relevant covariates were employed to examine differences in mean scale and total scores between each clinical group (ADHD, LD, ID, and ADHD + LD) and healthy controls. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

The majority of the children were boys (71.5%), with the highest proportion observed in the learning difficulty and ADHD group (82.4%). For paternal education, the highest proportion of high education was found in the healthy control group (84.2%), while the lowest was in the ID group (41.8%). Maternal education followed a similar pattern, with the highest proportion of high education in the healthy control group (89.5%) and the lowest in the ID group (43.6%). Regarding registered residence, the majority of children lived in urban areas (67.9%), with the highest proportion in the healthy control group (82.5%) and the lowest in the ID group (52.7%). The distribution of IQ scores was significantly lower in the clinical groups when compared with the healthy controls (P values <0.05). The overall median IQ score was 96.0 (IQR, 81.0 to 109.0). The healthy control group had the highest median IQ score (113.0), while the ID group had the lowest median score (56.0).

As presented in Table 2, significant differences were observed in PedsQL GCM mean scores across the four clinical groups compared to healthy controls. The total score, as well as social and school functioning domain scores, were also significantly lower across all clinical groups compared to healthy controls (P values <0.05). Notably, the largest effect size was observed in social functioning for children with ID (d=1.84), indicating a substantial difference relative to healthy controls. Table 3 presents comparative analyses of PedsQL FIM scale scores and total scores across four clinical groups relative to healthy controls. Significant between-group differences were observed across multiple domains. In the physical functioning domain, children with ADHD demonstrated significantly lower scores than healthy controls (78.05 vs. 84.96, P<0.05, d=0.36). For social functioning, the ID group exhibited particularly pronounced impairments (74.32 vs. 84.76, P<0.05, d=0.47). Cognitive functioning scores were significantly reduced in both the ID group (71.73 vs. 81.40, P<0.05, d=0.38) and the comorbid LD + ADHD group (71.69 vs. 81.40, P<0.05, d=0.41) compared to healthy controls. The communication domain showed significant impairments across three clinical groups: LD (86.82 vs. 93.28, P<0.05, d=0.41), ID (77.88 vs. 93.28, P<0.01, d=0.82), and comorbid LD + ADHD (86.40 vs. 93.28, P<0.05, d=0.43). Notably, the comorbid LD + ADHD group displayed significantly impaired daily activity scores (70.10 vs. 82.53, P<0.05, d=0.54). All clinical groups showed significantly lower scores than controls in emotional functioning, worry, and total scores (P<0.05 for all comparisons). The largest effect size emerged in the worry domain for the ID group (d=1.59), indicating large difference relative to controls. Discussion

The present study demonstrates that children with learning difficulties due to specific etiologies—ADHD, LD, ID, and ADHD comorbid with LD (ADHD + LD)—exhibit significantly impaired HRQoL profiles of both children and their caregivers compared to healthy controls. Importantly, the pattern of HRQoL impairments varied across these conditions, reflecting distinct clinical profiles. These findings align with previous research emphasizing condition-specific HRQoL patterns in neurodevelopmental disorders, while highlighting the necessity of tailored interventions addressing unique functional challenges posed by each etiology.

ADHD

We found that children with only ADHD had significantly lower emotional, social, and school functioning compared to the healthy controls, which is related to the core symptoms of ADHD (inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity). Emotional and social difficulties—such as trouble with social cues, impulse control, and emotional regulation—were also notable (24). Peer rejection due to disruptive behaviors and emphasis on group harmony in Chinese culture may worsen social challenges (25). In addition, ongoing academic and social struggles, along with high family expectations, likely contribute to increased anxiety and low self-esteem in children with ADHD (26). ADHD symptoms directly affect learning (27). In China, intense academic pressure and competitive educational environments may further disadvantage these children. Limited awareness of ADHD among educators and peers can lead to stigma and less support (28).

Raising a child with ADHD has multifaceted impacts on families. Our study shows that parents experience impaired physical and emotional functioning, higher levels of worry, and an overall lower quality of life. The ongoing demands of managing challenging behaviors, advocating for the child’s needs, facing potential social judgment, and feeling responsible for the child’s difficulties contribute to sustained caregiver stress and emotional exhaustion (29-31). Access to adequate mental health support for caregivers remains limited, and stigma may inhibit help-seeking (32). Over time, the cumulative stress of caregiving can negatively affect physical and emotional functioning. These findings align with previous research underscoring the chronic strain on families of children with ADHD and highlight the need for greater support and resources for caregivers (29,31). Elevated parental anxiety in the context of ADHD often stems from concerns over the child’s current struggles and long-term future within a competitive society, compounded by stressors such as navigating educational/healthcare systems, securing interventions, and potential social stigma (29-31,33). The cultural emphasis on not “falling behind” further intensifies this anxiety.

Learning disorder

Children with LD exhibited impairments in school and social functioning compared to their healthy peers. This can be attributed to the core features of LD, such as difficulties in processing information, language comprehension, and executive functioning, which directly impact academic performance and classroom participation (34). These academic challenges may subsequently hinder the development of self-esteem and peer relationships, as repeated failure and frustration can lead to social withdrawal or problem behaviors. Moreover, limited social skills, difficulties in interpreting social cues, and an increased risk of peer rejection have been documented among children with LD (35).

Our research suggested that parents of children with LD experienced diminished HRQoL across several domains, including emotional functioning, communication, and worry. The persistent stress associated with supporting a child with LD can lead to increased caregiver anxiety, burnout, and depression (36,37). Communication within families and with external networks may be negatively impacted by several factors. A limited societal awareness and understanding of LD, coupled with prevailing stigma and misconceptions—such as the erroneous belief that LD constitutes a psychological disorder—often compel parents to conceal their child’s diagnosis (38). This concealment restricts the sharing of experiences and discourages the pursuit of assistance, thereby exacerbating social isolation. Additionally, concerns regarding their child’s future educational and occupational opportunities, particularly within a highly competitive social environment, contribute to persistent worry about the child’s long-term well-being. Crucially, stigma and the fear of negative judgment continue to pose significant obstacles to open dialogue and the seeking of support, further diminishing the quality of life for these families.

Intellectual disability

Our findings demonstrated that children with ID exhibited significantly lower HRQoL across multiple dimensions—including physical, emotional, social, and school functioning, and total HRQoL scores—compared to healthy controls, with effect sizes ranging from moderate to large (Cohen’s d: 0.50–1.84). Multivariate regression analyses confirmed these disparities after adjusting for covariates, except for the emotional functioning scale. Children with ID frequently demonstrate notably diminished scores in physical functioning, which is deeply intertwined with overlapping medical conditions. These include neurological complications such as epilepsy, movement coordination deficits (e.g., gait abnormalities), and metabolic dysregulation such as obesity (39). Furthermore, sensory impairments in vision, speech or hearing, combined with congenital disorders linked to genetic anomalies like Down syndrome, amplify these functional limitations (40-42). These health issues collectively impair children’s physical functioning and daily activities. Notably, the significantly reduced social functioning observed in children with ID (d=1.83 compared to HC, ADHD, LD, and ADHD + LD) may arise from intrinsic deficits in cognitive capacity and adaptive social skills, both of which are fundamental diagnostic criteria for ID (43,44). In contrast to ADHD or LD, which primarily involve impairments related to attention/executive function or specific academic skills, the deficits associated with ID broadly undermine essential aspects of social perception, reciprocal interaction, and daily adaptive functioning, which results in compounded challenges across interpersonal and societal contexts (45). We observed a relatively low score of school functioning in children with ID compared with healthy children (d=1.61), predominantly attributable to core impairments in cognitive processing, such as abstract reasoning, working memory, information processing speed, as well as in adaptive skills essential for classroom participation (46,47). These results are consistent with clinical expectations, given that the pervasive deficits associated with ID in both intellectual capacity (IQ <70) and daily adaptive functioning significantly constrain the acquisition of academic skills within traditional educational settings. Based on these findings, it stands to reason that children with ID in our study experienced the lowest HRQoL with the largest effect size (d=1.45), primarily due to their cognitive limitations, daily adaptive challenges, and insufficient tailored educational support.

Our study found that parents of children with ID had the lowest HRQoL across most domains and total scores (except daily activities). Adjusted regression analysis revealed significantly poorer parental, emotional functioning, communication, worry, and total HRQoL compared to healthy controls, with small-to-large effect sizes (0.42–1.59), especially for parental worry which was markedly higher than other groups. Parents of children with ID face diminished quality of life stemming from the sustained demands of caregiving—such as navigating daily adaptive challenges and behavioral needs—and socioeconomic barriers like limited access to specialized education and healthcare (33,48). Persistent societal stigma and insufficient policy measures addressing respite care or financial aid further deepen psychological strain and systemic inequities (49,50).

ADHD & learning disorder

Our study revealed that children with comorbid ADHD and LD exhibited significantly lower scores in social functioning, school functioning, and overall quality of life compared to healthy controls. These differences remained statistically significant even after controlling for covariates. Notably, the comorbid ADHD-LD group demonstrated poorer outcomes across these domains when compared to the ADHD-only and LD-only groups. This suggests that the interaction between attentional deficits and academic skill deficiencies results in compounded functional impairments. Our finding can be ascribed to the interaction of dual cognitive load mechanisms: deficits in sustained attention and inhibitory control associated with ADHD intersect with LD-specific impairments in working memory and information processing (51-53). This intersection overwhelms the prefrontal-parietal networks responsible for executive functions and adaptive learning. Neuroimaging studies support these findings, indicating reduced activation and connectivity in the prefrontal cortex, which is involved in planning and behavioral regulation, and the parietal lobe, which is involved in attention modulation, in individuals with comorbid conditions (54). These neural inefficiencies contribute to a detrimental cycle where academic challenges, such as suboptimal academic performances and increased grade retention rates, exacerbate difficulties in social adaptation, further diminishing self-esteem and psychological resilience.

We also observed the impairments in parents’ HRQoL who raised up children with comorbid ADHD and LD. Specifically, they had worse outcomes in emotional functioning, worry, daily activities domains and overall HRQoL. This likely arises from the cumulative stressors associated with caregiving for overlapping neurodevelopmental challenges. The behavioral dysregulation associated with ADHD, such as impulsivity and inattention, combined with the academic difficulties related to LD, such as persistent homework conflicts and the demands of specialized tutoring, together exacerbate parental emotional exhaustion. Parents frequently encounter dual pressures and dual responsibility, such as managing ADHD-induced emotional outbursts, including oppositional behaviors, while concurrently addressing LD-specific educational interventions, such as reading and writing remediation, which disrupts daily routines. Additionally, societal stigma surrounding neurodevelopmental disorders disproportionately isolates these families, limiting their access to peer support networks. Concurrently, systemic deficiencies in educational accommodations and respite care services further exacerbate their unmet needs.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several novel contributions to the field of learning difficulties research. First, it provides one of the few systematic assessments of HRQoL patterns across four major neurodevelopmental conditions (ADHD, LD, ID, and ADHD-LD comorbidity) in a real-world clinical cohort, addressing the scarcity of data in this clinical population. Second, our dual focus on both child and parental HRQoL—rarely explored in prior studies—reveals the compounded socioeconomic and psychological burdens faced by families. Finally, the dataset enriches the global evidence base with robust China-specific insights, aligning with national priorities to optimize human capital development through targeted educational and healthcare policies.

This study has several methodological limitations worth noting. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, recruitment from a single specialty clinic may introduce selection bias, as participants likely represent more severe cases compared to those managed in community settings. Third, reliance on parental self-reports for both child and caregiver outcomes risks social desirability bias and underrepresentation of teacher/child perspectives.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the profiles of HRQoL in children with ADHD, LD, ID, ADHD + LD, as well as HRQoL patterns of their parents in a clinical sample in China. Our findings advocate for attention on children with learning difficulties in both public health agendas and clinical practice to address both child development and caregiver well-being.
