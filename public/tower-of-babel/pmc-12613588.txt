Learning curve in evaluation of graded progressive image-model teaching in echocardiography
Authors: Xianfeng Guo, Yilin Li, Xinyu Zhou, Cuihong Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEchocardiography is an essential diagnostic tool in clinical practice, and its effective teaching is crucial for medical students, especially for those in their internship stage. Traditional teaching methods often struggle to fully equip students with the practical skills proficiently. Therefore, there is a growing need for more innovative, structured approaches to improve the quality and efficiency of echocardiography training.ObjectiveThe application value of the graded progressive image-model teaching was evaluated in echocardiography teaching for undergraduate students using learning curves.MethodsA total of 20 undergraduate students who interned at our hospital from September 2021 to December 2024 were selected as study participants. They were randomly assigned into an experimental group (n = 10) and a control group (n = 10) using a random number table. The experimental group received graded progressive image-model teaching method, while the control group received traditional teaching method. After three months of teaching, the students’ echocardiography practical skills and theoretical exam scores were assessed. Additionally, a questionnaire survey was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the teaching approach. Learning curves for echocardiography examination time were constructed for both groups using the cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis.ResultsThe experimental group demonstrated significantly shorter times in 2D imaging and measurements, M-mode imaging and measurements, Doppler imaging and measurements, and total echocardiography operation time compared to the control group, and their theoretical exam scores were significantly higher (p < 0.05). The learning curve analysis showed that the experimental group entered the proficient mastery stage earlier and had a shorter learning growth stage than the control group. Questionnaire results indicated that the experimental group scored significantly higher in six aspects: active learning ability, clearly identifying personal learning shortcomings, enhanced spatial understanding of cardiac structures, appropriate learning difficulty, more targeted teacher feedback, and high interest in practical operation learning (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe graded progressive image-model teaching in echocardiography education helps students quickly reach proficiency in practical skills, improving both theoretical knowledge and practical abilities in echocardiography.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08191-7.Keywords: Graded progressive, Learning curve, Echocardiography, Teaching

Introduction

Echocardiography, as a non-invasive, real-time, and dynamic imaging modality, has been widely applied in the diagnosis, monitoring, and efficacy evaluation of cardiovascular diseases, making it an indispensable tool in clinical practice [1, 2]. With the rapid development of ultrasound medicine, there is an increasing demand for highly qualified professionals in cardiac ultrasound. However, the current medical education system still exhibits significant challenges in training such specialized personnel [3, 4]. Particularly during the undergraduate internship stage, students often struggle to meet the practical requirements due to insufficient theoretical knowledge, operational skills, and clinical reasoning ability. Echocardiography is a highly specialized and practical examination technique that involves complex cardiac anatomy, hemodynamic principles, and diverse pathophysiological changes. This demands that operators possess a solid foundation of theoretical knowledge, proficient technical skills, and sharp clinical judgment. However, the traditional teaching mode often separates theory and practice, and lacks systematization and hierarchy. This leads to difficulties in knowledge integration and clinical practice, and makes it difficult for students to effectively master the core skills of cardiac ultrasound within the limited internship time [5, 6]. To help undergraduate interns quickly acquire cardiac ultrasound skills during their training and lay a solid foundation for future clinical practice, there is an urgent need for innovative and targeted teaching strategies.

The graded progressive image-model teaching is a systematic teaching model designed to gradually increase the difficulty of learning content. This method introduces content in a structured, step-by-step manner, using tools such as three-dimensional (3D) anatomical models and mind mapping to assist students in understanding cardiac structure and function, thereby gradually developing their ultrasound operation skills and clinical application capabilities. Studies have shown that this model significantly enhances students’ learning interest, self-directed learning ability, and clinical reasoning skills, contributing to a comprehensive improvement in overall competence [7, 8]. Given the high level of difficulty associated with cardiac ultrasound in ultrasound education and the strong clinical and research demands in this field, this study designed a graded progressive image-model teaching approach tailored to the learning characteristics and needs of undergraduate interns. This study evaluates the effectiveness of the teaching intervention through learning curve analysis and questionnaire surveys, aiming to provide theoretical support and practical guidance for the reform of cardiac ultrasound education and to explore its value in clinical teaching.

Methods

Study design and participants

A prospective controlled experimental design of research was used in this study. The participants of the study were 20 undergraduate students who interned in the department of ultrasound medicine at our hospital from September 2021 to December 2024. Among them, there were 6 males and 14 females, aged between 21 and 23 years. Participants were randomly assigned into either a control group or an experimental group using a random number table, with 10 students in each group. The experimental group received the graded progressive image-model teaching method, while the control group received traditional teaching method. Both groups of students received theoretical instruction as well as hands-on training in cardiac ultrasound. At the end of the internship, learning curves were plotted and questionnaire surveys were conducted to evaluate the outcomes. Two instructors were assigned to each group. All four instructors had over eight years of clinical experience in cardiac ultrasonography. They regularly receive training in different teaching methods and ensure the consistency of teaching quality and the persistence in standardized teaching practices during their teaching period. All subjects signed an informed consent form. By comparing the general data of the undergraduate students in both groups, the results suggested no statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Teaching methods

Teaching method for the control group

The control group received traditional instruction, which primarily involved the instructors’ oral instruction, demonstration operations, and students’ practical exercises for teaching. This teacher-centered approach focused on knowledge transmission, with instructors leading the class and students passively receiving information [9]. Cardiac ultrasound procedures were demonstrated by instructors on the ultrasound machine, while students learned and comprehended the techniques through practical exercises. Teaching content included cardiac anatomy structure, ultrasound imaging principles, standard section acquisition methods, and ultrasound diagnosis of common cardiac diseases. The teaching period lasted for three months, with a total of 360 instructional hours.

Teaching method for the experimental group

The experimental group adopts a graded progressive image-model teaching method. The teaching duration was also three months, with a total of 360 instructional hours (as depicted in Fig. 1). This method comprised three core modules:

(2)3D heart model and 4D cardiac imaging teaching:

3D heart model and 4D cardiac imaging teaching:

The 3D heart model is a realistic anatomical replica consistent with the structure of a normal human heart. By disassembling the physical model, students can intuitively observe the spatial relationships between the cardiac chambers and major vessels, and clearly compare the morphological features of different structures. This hands-on approach not only reinforces students’ memory of cardiac anatomy but also deepens their understanding of the heart’s three-dimensional structure.

4D cardiac ultrasound imaging involves the use of a 3D ultrasound probe to acquire detailed anatomical and hemodynamic data, which is reconstructed into dynamic 4D cardiac ultrasound images. From the perspective of 3D ultrasound visualization, this technique helps students comprehend the heart’s ventricular and atrial contraction, valvular motion, and intracardiac blood flow direction in a dynamic state.

(3)Mind mapping teaching:

Mind mapping teaching:

Students were guided to create their own mind mapping based on echocardiography images. The mind mapping construction involves comparing different ultrasound cardiac planes, distinguishing between normal and abnormal echocardiography images, correlating abnormal ultrasound planes with clinical diseases, and differentiating between various clinical cardiac diseases through ultrasound. The teacher first introduces the mind mapping theme, allowing students to create their own mind mapping. During the drawing process, students identify problems, seek answers, and finally, the teacher corrects and supplements the content of the mind mapping [10, 11].

Echocardiography practical operation content

The practical operation teaching content of echocardiography includes three aspects: (1) Acquiring basic 2D echocardiographic views and accurate measurements: including obtaining standard views of the aortic short axis, left ventricular long axis, four-chamber view, and five-chamber view, as well as correctly measuring relevant conventional parameters of echocardiography [12]. (2) Acquiring M-mode echocardiogram of the left ventricular long axis and accurate functional analysis: including clearly obtaining the long-axis M-type echocardiography of the left ventricle and conducting accurate functional measurement and analysis of the left ventricle [12]. (3) Doppler echocardiographic examination and measurement: including clearly acquiring spectral doppler and color doppler blood flow images of the aortic, pulmonary valve, and the mitral and tricuspid valve orifices, and accurately annotating and measuring the spectral doppler images [13].

The practical operations were carried out on real human subjects. Standard ultrasound views were acquired and measurements performed while ensuring image quality. It is important to emphasize that a supervising instructor will be present during the students’ operation to observe and record the examination time. The instructor will not intervene in the students’ performance and will only provide feedback and guidance after the completion of the ultrasound examination. The total examination time for each student to complete all three echocardiography practical operations is recorded after each session.

Experimental indicators

Establishment of echocardiography learning curve

Cumulative sum analysis (CUSUM) is a method that calculates the cumulative sum of the differences between observed values and target values during the process of skill acquisition. As an objective evaluation criterion, CUSUM can quantify the learning process and be used to assess the minimum number of operation cases required to acquire a certain operational skill. In this study, CUSUM analysis was applied to plot the learning curves of average echocardiographic examination times for each student group. Based on the peak of the learning curve, the students’ learning process was divided into two stages: the learning growth stage and the proficiency mastery stage. Additionally, scatter plots were generated to illustrate the relationship between the average examination time and the number of operations performed by each group. The final practical operation examination time for each group of students after three months of training was recorded as the final practical operation exam score.

Echocardiography theoretical assessment

After three months of training, both groups of students underwent an echocardiography theoretical exam. The theoretical exam was a written test, with a total score of 100 points. The exam content covered basic and clinical theoretical knowledge related to echocardiography.

Teaching effectiveness assessment

We developed a questionnaire aimed at assessing the teaching effectiveness for two groups of students. The questionnaire is provided in supplementary materials. The questionnaire covers six aspects: active learning ability, clarity identifying personal learning deficiencies, enhanced spatial understanding of cardiac structures, appropriate learning difficulty, more targeted teacher feedback, and high interest in practical learning. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale, with higher scores indicating better teaching effectiveness. Students were invited to complete the anonymous survey after the course. The questionnaire’s cover page outlined the study’s purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality measures, and implied consent upon submission.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 27.0). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (), and independent t-tests were performed to compare groups. Categorical data were presented as frequency and percentage (n [%]), with Fisher exact test applied where appropriate. The learning curve fitting equation was estimated using curve fitting analysis. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Necessity of applying graded progressive image-model teaching in medical education

Echocardiography, as a non-invasive, radiation-free, and real-time imaging modality, plays a vital role in the diagnosis, monitoring, and therapeutic evaluation of cardiovascular diseases [14]. However, due to its technical complexity and the involvement of multidisciplinary knowledge such as cardiac anatomy, hemodynamics, and pathophysiology, it presents a steep learning curve and poses significant challenges for beginners. The current traditional teaching model often separates theory from practice, resulting in limited hands-on opportunities, delayed feedback, and fragmented teaching content, making it difficult to effectively enhance students’ clinical operation skills [15].

Therefore, an efficient teaching method is urgently needed to reduce the learning time while ensuring that students can master echocardiography examination skills effectively to meet the clinical demand for such professionals. In this study, we designed and implemented a teaching method based on a " graded progressive image-model teaching.” This method incorporates tiered teaching strategies, 3D cardiac models, 4D ultrasound imaging, and mind mapping, providing a structured and layered learning pathway. This comprehensive teaching strategy facilitates students’ understanding of cardiac structure and function while gradually mastering ultrasound operation skills and clinical application capabilities.

Our results showed that the experimental group achieved significantly shorter times in 2D imaging, M-mode imaging, Doppler imaging, and total echocardiography examination time, along with higher theoretical exam scores compared to the control group (Table 1). These findings suggest that the graded progressive image-model teaching method helps students more quickly acquire echocardiography operational skills and deepen their understanding of theoretical knowledge. Moreover, the questionnaire survey results revealed that students in the experimental group scored higher in areas such as active learning ability, clearly identifying personal learning shortcomings, enhanced spatial understanding of cardiac structures, appropriate learning difficulty, more targeted teacher feedback, and high interest in practical operation learning (Table 2). This reflects that the graded progressive image-model teaching method not only improved students’ technical competence but also boosted their learning motivation and satisfaction. Therefore, applying this teaching model in clinical cardiac ultrasound education holds significant value.

Value of graded progressive image-model teaching in echocardiography examination education

Graded progressive teaching follows the principles of moving from the simple to the complex and advancing step by step. It systematizes and stratifies cardiac ultrasound knowledge based on its difficulty, enabling students to build a progressively more complex conceptual framework of skills while mastering fundamental knowledge. This approach reduces the frustration caused by knowledge gaps, enhances learning interest and self-confidence, and facilitates knowledge internalization and skill transfer [16]. Students can focus on relevant content at different stages and flexibly adjust their learning pace according to their individual level of mastery, fostering self-directed learning and self-assessment abilities. Graded progressive teaching is flexible and systematic, significantly enhancing students’ learning experience and practical abilities, making it an effective teaching strategy in modern education. Li [17] et al. proved that graded teaching content in ultrasound education significantly helps beginners memorize and familiarize themselves with operational procedures. Similarly, Wang [18] and others found that graded progressive teaching helped students grasp the key steps and details in the operation process, assisting beginners in quickly achieving proficiency. This finding aligns with the results of the current study. In this study, after three months of training, the experimental group significantly outperformed the control group in both echocardiography practical operations and theoretical scores (p < 0.05).

The image-model teaching method utilizes mind mapping to clarify knowledge structure, while 3D heart model and 4D dynamic cardiac imaging help students develop spatial awareness and functional understanding of cardiac anatomy. This enables a deep integration of theory with practice. The results of this study showed that students in the experimental group outperformed those in the control group in both practical echocardiography skills and theoretical assessments. Questionnaire data also indicated a higher level of satisfaction with this teaching approach.

Furthermore, the graded progressive image-model teaching method contributes to the development of students’ clinical reasoning abilities. By guiding students to construct mind maps based on cardiac ultrasound images, it helps them understand the logical relationships between cardiac anatomy and pathological manifestations, thereby promoting the integration of ultrasound imaging with clinical disease interpretation. The use of 3D heart model and 4D cardiac imaging enhances intuitive understanding of the spatial structure and dynamic processes of the heart, effectively overcoming the abstract and complex nature of cardiac ultrasound learning. This approach addresses key challenges and difficulties in teaching cardiac ultrasound.

Exploration of evaluating echocardiography practical skills based on learning curves

In this study, we employed learning curves to assess the effectiveness of the graded progressive image-model teaching approach in echocardiography education. The core of echocardiography education lies in improving practical operation skills. However, traditional evaluation methods primarily rely on theoretical assessments and single practical operation assessments, which fail to comprehensively reflect students’ learning progress and skill acquisition. Therefore, we innovatively introduced a learning curve evaluation system, using echocardiography examination time as a quantitative indicator to dynamically track the improvement in students’ proficiency.

Firstly, learning curves provide a foundation for stepwise learning, making them highly suitable for graded and personalized teaching, aligning with the principles of the graded progressive teaching method used in this study. Combining these approaches enables both teachers and students to monitor individual learning progress more effectively and tailor personalized learning plans accordingly. Secondly, learning curves visually demonstrate the changing trends in students’ operation times, allowing educators to identify turning points, bottlenecks, and breakthrough moments in the learning process and investigate their causes [19]. Lastly, instructors can adjust teaching content and strategies according to student performance at different stages, enhancing overall instructional effectiveness and establishing an efficient feedback mechanism. Students, in turn, can modify their learning strategies based on their progress to improve learning efficiency.

In this study, we established a CUSUM learning curve based on echocardiography examination time, dividing the learning process into a growth stage and a proficiency stage. With an increasing number of operation cases, the average examination time of students gradually decreased and stabilized, indicating a progressive improvement in operational proficiency (Fig. 2). The turning point in the learning curve for the experimental group occurred at the 40th case, while it occurred at the 46th case in the control group (Fig. 3). This indicates that students in the experimental group reached the proficiency stage more quickly, suggesting that this teaching model has certain advantages in improving learning efficiency and fostering practical abilities.

Therefore, the learning curve, as a supplementary tool for evaluating teaching effectiveness, possesses strong scientific validity and practical value. It not only assists in the development of personalized teaching plans but also facilitates the establishment of a dynamic feedback mechanism during the instructional process, thereby enhancing the precision and efficiency of teaching.

Strengths and limitations

The learning process of echocardiography is complex. This study innovatively applies a graded progressive image-model teaching method, which is highly suitable for echocardiography education. It meets the needs of both cardiac operation training and clinical practice. First, this approach allows students to absorb knowledge progressively, alleviating their fear of new technologies and stimulating their learning motivation and confidence. Secondly, the innovative use of 3D anatomical models and 4D ultrasound imaging in teaching significantly enhances students’ understanding of cardiac spatial structures and hemodynamics. The integration of mind mapping teaching further strengthens the connection between echocardiography education and clinical practice. Finally, this study employs learning curves to evaluate teaching effectiveness, providing an intuitive understanding of students’ progress at different stages of learning, which facilitates a more accurate assessment of teaching outcomes. However, this study also has certain limitations, including a small sample size and being a single-center study. Future research will require a larger sample size and a multicenter approach for further validation.

Conclusion

This study implemented a teaching plan based on a graded progressive image-model teaching. By systematically structuring key teaching content into different stages and incorporating mind mapping along with 3D heart models and 4D cardiac imaging, students were able to reach proficiency in practical skills more quickly. This approach effectively enhanced their theoretical understanding and hands-on echocardiography skills. Additionally, the establishment of learning curves provided a scientific foundation for future echocardiography education.
