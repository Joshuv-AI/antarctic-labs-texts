CeO2‐Based Electrocatalysts for the Oxygen Evolution Reaction: Design, Dynamic Mechanisms, and Electrolyte Adaptability
Authors: Zhen Cui, Ailing Song, Zirui Cui, Manman Duanmu, Zhipeng Ma, Hao Liu, Guangjie Shao, Xinyi Tan, Guoxiu Wang, Hao Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCeO2‐based electrocatalysts are promising for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) owing to their distinctive Ce3+/Ce4+ redox flexibility, large oxygen‐storage capacity, and lattice adaptability. More importantly, CeO2 can dynamically regulate catalyst surface reconstruction and electronic structure while significantly enhancing catalytic activity and long‐term stability. In this review, we first focus on CeO2‐based electrocatalysts for OER from structural characteristics, design principles and strategies, structure–property relationships, interfacial structure, as well as critical evaluation of design strategies and selection principles. The mechanistic roles of CeO2 in OER are highlighted, including redox chemistry and oxygen‐vacancy (Ov) dynamics, electronic‐structure modulation and charge transfer, interfacial coupling and surface reconstruction, as well as operando evidence and the controversy over lattice‐oxygen participation. The performance of CeO 2 ‐based OER electrocatalysts in different electrolytes, including alkaline, acidic, and seawater media, is also summarized to demonstrate their applicability under diverse operating conditions. Finally, from the perspectives of electronic‐structure evolution, redox‐state transitions, lattice evolution, and catalytic pathways, this review summarizes the underlying mechanisms by which CeO2 regulates the dynamic catalytic behavior of OER and provides an outlook on future design strategies for developing highly efficient and durable CeO2‐based electrocatalysts.Keywords: CeO2‐based electrocatalysts, interface engineering, lattice evolution, oxygen evolution reaction, surface reconstruction

Introduction

In renewable energy systems such as water electrolyzers, fuel cells, and metal–air batteries, the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) stands as a half‐reaction of pivotal significance [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Alkaline water electrolysis, specifically, has drawn intense interest as a clean and enduring pathway for large‐scale hydrogen production driven by renewable power. This development is widely regarded as a linchpin of the emerging hydrogen economy [6, 7, 8]. Yet translating this promise into practice is fundamentally constrained because OER suffers from sluggish kinetics and high overpotential, thereby imposing a stubborn efficiency ceiling. As shown in Figure 1a, at its root, the OER, as a four‐electron transfer process (4OH− → O2 + 2H2O + 4e−), derives its slow kinetics from multi‐scale complexity [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. More specifically, the reaction typically proceeds through a series of intermediates such as *OH, *O, and *OOH. It encompasses a multitude of factors intricately intertwined, including the O‐H bond activation, the electron‐transfer kinetics of each elementary step, the adsorption affinities of the key intermediates, and the extent of proton‐electron transfer. Together, these tightly coupled contributions give rise to kinetic barriers that stretch across both temporal and spatial scales [12, 13, 14]. Within this context, it proves exceptionally difficult for an individual catalytic system to, all at once, refine the adsorption energetics of assorted intermediates and maintain structural robustness under highly oxidizing potentials. This fundamental hurdle imposes stringent limits on both catalytic activity and durability during practical use. Cocatalysts, however, can effectively coordinate these multi‐scale processes: they not only optimize the local electronic structure, but also modulate the synergistic efficiency of energy transfer pathways at different hierarchical levels, ultimately breaking through the intrinsic bottlenecks that limit the OER rate [15, 16, 17]. Therefore, it is imperative to introduce cocatalysts through modification strategies to construct multi‐component synergistic interactions, thereby enhancing OER performance [18, 19, 20, 21].

Introducing cocatalysts into catalyst systems has emerged as a highly effective modification strategy [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. As shown in Figure 1b, based on their mechanistic roles, cocatalysts can be categorized into structural cocatalysts (e.g., hexagonal boron nitride stabilizing active component dispersion through physical encapsulation [29], Ir‐Ta‐based metallic glass in situ constructing bilayer nanostructures, locking active‐site coordination environment [30]) and functional cocatalysts (e.g., Sr/Cr optimizing the electronic structure of active sites through bidirectional electronic regulation [31]). The core value of cocatalysts in the OER lies in coordinating the multi‐scale kinetic processes spanning from electron transfer to macroscopic transport [15, 16, 17]. On the one hand, interactions with the primary catalyst induce band bending and charge redistribution, significantly reducing interfacial charge transfer impedance (especially under high current density conditions) and ensuring efficient electron transport capability [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. On the other hand, multiphase synergy allows one to fine‑tune the adsorption energy barriers of key oxygen‑containing intermediates (*OH, *O, *OOH). It might also reveal alternative reaction pathways, such as cation‑coupled electron transfer, which can bypass the limitations set by traditional scaling relationships [39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44]. Furthermore, cocatalysts can serve as protective buffer layers, guarding against structural disintegration and metal leaching over extended electrolysis [45, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51]. Nevertheless, such conventional interfacial regulation is largely established on a static structural and functional electronic framework, which may be insufficient to capture the complex evolution of catalytic interfaces under OER conditions. Cerium dioxide (CeO2), among the candidate materials, has become a representative rare‐earth promoter that serves structural roles owing to the high crustal abundance of Ce and its distinctive 4f‐orbital electronic configuration. Particularly, its unusual capacity under operando catalysis distinguishes it from traditional cocatalysts [52, 53, 54, 55, 56]. Its principal mechanistic contributions can be broken down into three areas: (1) Redox‐mediated dynamic cycling of Ov. The Ov ⇆ O2− generation–healing cycle, driven by the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple of CeO2 (Figure 1c), not only enables rapid interfacial electron storage and release. By dynamically tuning lattice oxygen activity, this cycle also directly drives down the formation potential of the OER rate‐determining step [57, 58, 59]. (2) Synergistic regulation of electronics and adsorption at heterointerfaces: The oxophilic interface provided by CeO2 promotes the directional migration and enrichment of OH− species, while the strong electronic coupling effect established with the primary catalyst optimizes the d‐band center position of active sites, thereby balancing intermediate adsorption energies and accelerating reaction kinetics [60, 61, 62, 63]. (3) Self‐healing stability based on the fluorite structure: Its stable calcium fluoride crystal structure imparts excellent corrosion resistance, and the self‐healing mechanism of Ov effectively compensates for surface oxygen loss and anchors metal single atoms/nanoclusters, thereby suppressing metal leaching and irreversible surface reconstruction under harsh alkaline conditions and preserving structural integrity over long‐term service [54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60]. It is precisely this coupled regulation of electronic structure, redox and lattice oxygen chemistry, and self‐healing properties that distinguishes CeO2 from conventional static cocatalysts and makes it a promising platform for highly efficient and cost‐effective CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts [64, 65, 66].

To gain an in‐depth understanding of the promotional mechanism of CeO2 in the OER, this review systematically surveys the design principles and strategies, interfacial structures, structure–activity relationships, as well as critical evaluation of design strategies and selection principles of CeO2‐based OER catalytic systems organized by their structural features. Existing reviews have mainly provided systematic summaries of Ce‐based catalysts in terms of material classification, synthesis methods, morphological control, defect and oxygen vacancy engineering, heterostructure construction, as well as the effects of Ce doping, supports, and supported structures on OER activity and stability [67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74]. Diverging from these reviews, which primarily focus on “how materials are constructed and how structural regulation enhances performance,” this review further highlights the dynamic evolution of CeO2‐based OER catalytic systems from their initial structures to their real working states and links this dynamic evolution to functional transitions under different electrolyte environments. Specifically, this review integrates CeO2 in different forms including as a support, a heterogeneous component, a doped phase, or a main component into a unified analysis. Centered on the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle and the dynamic evolution of Ov, and combined with the lattice oxygen chemistry of the host catalyst, it constructs a dynamic analytical framework of the “redox–lattice oxygen interaction,” further correlating electronic structure modulation, interfacial charge transfer, oxygen vacancy generation and migration, and adaptive surface reconstruction, and revealing how CeO2 progressively transforms from a functional component in the initial structure into a dynamic regulatory unit that governs active centers and interfacial structural evolution under OER working conditions. Subsequently, this review systematically discusses Ov generation, electronic structure modulation, surface reconstruction, as well as operando evidence and the controversy over lattice‐oxygen participation, and further compares the performance of CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts in alkaline, acidic, and seawater electrolytes and their environment‐dependent roles. Finally, it identifies the core challenges that remain and outlines future research directions. By integrating structural design, dynamic evolution under working conditions, and environmental adaptability, this review aims to provide systematic mechanistic understanding and design principles for understanding the structure–activity relationships of CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts under practical reaction conditions and for developing catalytic systems that combine high activity, long‐term stability, and adaptability to complex environments.

Structural Characteristics and Design of CeO2‐Based Electrocatalysts

As shown in Figure 2, the performance of CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts is ultimately determined by their structural composition and the underlying design principles that modulate catalytic activity across multiple scales, from the atomic to the macroscopic. Rational structural design aims to optimize active‐site accessibility, electronic structure, and mass‐transport pathways. This is achieved through deliberate modulation of morphology, surface defects, and interfacial architecture. CeO2‐based electrocatalysts can be structured as nanosheets, porous frameworks, and other morphologies that provide high surface areas and well‐defined, stable interfaces. These features are crucial for maximizing the exposure of catalytic sites and facilitating charge transfer at interfaces. The structural advantages are vital for enhancing the accessibility of active sites, promoting electronic transport, and accelerating reaction kinetics. However, designing highly efficient catalysts under demanding electrochemical conditions requires balancing intrinsic catalytic activity with structural stability, representing a complex multiscale optimization challenge. At the macro‐scale, hierarchical porosity optimizes mass transport of reactants and products; at the meso‐scale, nanostructures such as nanowires and nanosheets expose high‐activity facets to the greatest extent; at the atomic level, doping heteroatoms or anchoring single atoms allows precise tuning of electronic structures and local coordination environments. Beyond these intrinsic factors, the engineering of composite structures and interfaces is equally critical to overall catalytic performance. When CeO2 is integrated with other functional materials via heterostructures, core‐shell architectures, or solid solutions, its abundant Ovs and reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox properties confer electron‐buffering capacities that can modulate interfacial charge transfer and reaction energetics. Collectively, these insights establish a coherent framework linking structural features to catalytic function, laying a foundation for subsequent discussions on the structural characteristics, design principles, and interfacial phenomena of CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts.

Structural Features

As shown in Figure 3a, CeO2 adopts the fluorite structure (space group Fm‐3m), in which every Ce4+ is coordinated by eight O2− anions, forming an open‐framework architecture. This configuration not only endows the material with significant thermal and chemical stability but also facilitates reversible oxygen absorption and desorption via Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycling, complemented by a high density of intrinsic Ov, and local coordination adjustment, enabling CeO2 to achieve lattice adaptability while maintaining the stability of the fluorite structure [67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76]. Looking at the electronic‐structure origins, the Ce 4f orbitals are strongly localized, and their energy level sits right between the O 2p valence‐band maximum and the transition‐metal 3d orbitals. This energetic positioning triggers a cascade: it first creates a with a low calculated barrier Ce3+/Ce4+ couple and, via O 2p bridging, establishes a gradient “d‐p‐f” electronic coupling conduit to the transition metal (TM) 3d states (Figure 3b). Taking the TM/CeO2 heterojunction as an example, the TM 3d and Ce 4f orbitals each couple with O 2p via σ‐ and π‐type bonding, which establishes a 3d–4f gradient orbital linkage right across the interface (Figure 3c). The favorable energetic alignment enables the Ce 4f states to act as an electron buffer, thereby facilitating d–p–f orbital coupling and providing an effective route for precise modulation of the interfacial electronic structure [77, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82]. Under OER polarization, Ce4+ (4f0) can capture electrons rapidly and be reduced to Ce3+ (4f1), and the reverse transformation proceeds with comparable efficiency. The redox pair acts as an electronic buffer valve: it soaks up excess charge accumulating on the TM sites of the host catalyst, thereby curbing peroxidation, while simultaneously releasing electrons in the reverse direction to sustain high‑valent active intermediates [83, 84]. Exploiting the compositional tunability of the fluorite lattice, the incorporation of lower‐valent or size‐mismatched transition‐metal cations into the CeO2 matrix induces marked lattice distortion and local strain [65]. Furthermore, lattice mismatch between the fluorite host and secondary phases often produces atomically abrupt heterointerfaces; these interfacial regions serve as privileged sites for oxygen‑vacancy enrichment and, through interfacial reconstruction, can generate unusual coordination environments or amorphous/crystalline dual‑phase motifs [85, 86, 87, 88, 89]. Owing to their facet‑selective growth habit, CeO2 fluorite crystals can self‐assemble into hierarchical architectures via hydrothermal or solvothermal routes, thereby dramatically boosting the specific surface area and the exposure of active sites. By optimizing synthetic strategies, CeO2‐based electrocatalysts with tailored nanostructures can be rationally fabricated [65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 88, 89, 90]. It should be stressed that this fluorite structure displays pronounced corrosion resistance in alkaline seawater environments. When deployed as a protective shell, CeO2 effectively shields the catalytically active core from poisoning by Cl− and other impurities, thereby ensuring high durability during seawater electrolysis [86].

Design Principles and Strategies

Optimizing the OER performance of CeO2‐based electrocatalysts hinges upon meticulous structural engineering, guided by well‐chosen synthesis strategies. At the core of the design is a drive to establish clear, causal links between synthesis conditions and microstructural traits, so that high activity, long‐term stability, and resilience across aggressive environments can be achieved all at once, particularly strongly acidic or alkaline electrolytes [62, 91, 92]. Structural manipulation mainly targets three aspects: the interfacial contact between CeO2 and active components, the geometric architecture, and the density of Ov; together, these determine both the inherent activity of catalytic centers and their durability under operation [72, 93, 94]. Picking a synthetic method thus boils down to striking a balance among active‐site accessibility, interfacial coupling strength, and the structural robustness demanded by prolonged service. A few proven synthetic pathways have emerged that permit fine control over composition‐structure‐property relationships. Hydrothermal and solvothermal approaches are a mainstay for constructing CeO2‐based heterostructures, which are favored for their experimental simplicity. Here, the underlying logic is to tune thermodynamic and kinetic handles, such as precursor concentration, reaction temperature and dwell time, in order to steer the formation of well‑defined morphologies (e.g., nanosheets, nanospheres) and sharp heterointerfaces [85, 95, 96]. As shown in Figure 4a, electrodeposition works by a different mechanism: it relies on in situ nucleation and crystallization directly on a conductive substrate, which markedly tightens interfacial electron‐transport channels. This in situ nature makes the technique particularly attractive for the integrated design of self‑supporting electrodes [97]. The molten salt method, for its part, draws on the high ionic conductivity and fluidity of a molten salt bath to accelerate elemental mass transport and crystal growth. Under high‑temperature chemical conditions, such an environment permits deep‐seated adjustments of crystal structure and micromorphology that, in turn, boost catalytic performance [98]. When targeting real‐world electrolysis, structural design must also contend with electrolyte corrosion. In strongly alkaline environments, the emphasis of structural engineering pivots to utilizing defect engineering to modulate the local electronic structure of CeO2. Such modulation effectively suppresses structural collapse caused by the oxidation of Ce3+, thereby sustaining long‐term operational stability at elevated current densities [59]. Conversely, in strongly acidic media, severe corrosion and dissolution pose critical challenges, while structural tailoring relies on lattice matching strategies and strain engineering. By respectively adjusting the lattice parameters and interfacial stress between CeO2 and metal oxide counterparts, these approaches synergistically enhance both the tolerance and activity of the catalyst under acidic oxygen evolution potentials [99, 100]. Notably, to overcome the intrinsic limitations of conventional wet‐chemical methods in crystallinity control and interfacial coupling strength, several innovative auxiliary synthetic techniques have begun to intervene in structural regulation at increasingly microscopic scales. For instance, the transient high‐temperature and high‐pressure microenvironments generated by ultrasonic cavitation effects can induce the exposure of high‐energy, highly reactive crystal facets on the CeO2 support surface while simultaneously inducing surface roughening. When combined with low‐temperature electron tomography for near‐native‐state monitoring of the penetration depth of the primary catalyst within the CeO2 lattice, this approach enables atomically precise anchoring of active components onto the support surface [101]. Additionally, laser direct writing technology utilizes the localized thermal effect of millisecond pulsed laser irradiation to in situ induce the formation of hierarchical nanosheet arrays on CeO2 substrates, while simultaneously allowing for the precise regulation of Ov concentrations (Figure 4b) [102]. Although the aforementioned cutting‐edge strategies have yet to achieve full industrial maturity, these emerging methods establish a new paradigm for atomic‐scale structural manipulation, providing the requisite theoretical foundations and design concepts to circumvent the limitations of conventional synthesis.

Structure‐Property Relationships

Optimizing catalyst structures across spatial dimensions is the central approach to boosting the OER activity of CeO2‐based materials, and it calls for a coordinated design that bridges macroscopic morphology down to atomic arrangements. At the macroscopic end, constructing hierarchical pore networks dramatically raises the fraction of accessible active sites while accelerating mass transport (Figure 5a–c) [103, 104]. This advantage depends on matching pore dimensions with connectivity: smaller pores provide accessible surfaces, connected larger pores facilitate electrolyte supply and oxygen removal, and the continuous solid framework supports electron transport. Bubble behavior should therefore be considered rather than pursuing high porosity alone [105]. Such architectures can be realized through dealloying and templating. In dealloying, selective etching together with high‐temperature phase transformations creates a three‑dimensionally interconnected pore system, markedly enhancing mass transfer [106]. Templating routes, for instance those derived from MOFs, use high‐temperature calcination to yield porous oxide composites that inherit the high specific surface area and porosity of the precursor [107]. Pore dimensions, pore throats, and wall thickness can be adjusted through template size and packing, the extent of etching, and calcination conditions. A load‐bearing framework and firm interfaces should be retained to reduce the risk of fracture and detachment caused by excessive pore formation. Studies of hierarchical porous Ni‐based OER electrodes demonstrate that surface pore formation can be compatible with mechanical integrity [108]; prolonged electrolysis using porous rare‐earth high‐entropy perovskites provides a complementary example [109]. Descending to the nanoscale through mesoscopic structural engineering brings precise morphological control. Configurations such as one‐dimensional nanowires, two‐dimensional nanosheets, and three‐dimensional nanoarrays or nanospheres enable directional tuning of active‑site distribution, exploit spatial confinement to optimize charge‑transport pathways, speed up reactant diffusion, and preferentially expose highly reactive crystal facets [53]. Hydrothermal and solvothermal synthesis provides fine command over morphology and heterostructures (e.g., nanosheets, nanospheres) simply by adjusting precursor concentration, reaction temperature, and time [85, 95, 96]. A representative case is CeO2‐coated CoSe2 nanoneedles: a solvothermal process builds core‐shellCeO2@CoSe2 nanoneedles directly on carbon cloth, with the needles self‐aligning vertically into three‐dimensional flower‐like arrays, thereby substantially enlarging the electrochemically active surface area [85]. A study on the organic electrooxidation of CeO2@CuFe‐LDH provides a reference for the synergistic design of mesopores and interfacial charge transport: CeO2@CuFe‐LDH possesses mesopores of approximately 4.6 nm, which favor active‐site accessibility, while its crystalline‐amorphous heterointerface exhibits lower charge‐transfer resistance. Although this study focuses mainly on organic electrooxidation, its strategy of synergistically improving site utilization and charge transport through mesopore construction and interfacial coupling can offer inspiration for the structural design of CeO2‐based OER catalysts [110]. At the atomic scale, the emphasis shifts to atomistic precision. As shown in Figure 5d–f, a hollow, peanut‐shaped nitrogen‐doped carbon support is first prepared by a templating method, after which CeO2 nanoparticles are grown on its surface; subsequent high‐temperature annealing then confines and immobilizes atomically dispersed Ni species. This architecture merges the high exposure intrinsic to atomically dispersed Ni, the excellent electronic conductivity and mass‐transport channels offered by the hollow carbon framework, and a robust CeO2‐Ni interfacial interaction that stabilizes the metal centers [111]. Moreover, coupling ultrasonic cavitation with cryogenic electron tomography permits near‐native‐state observation of the subsurface anchoring depth of active components inside CeO2, which makes it possible to achieve sub‐nanoscale integration between the support and the active phase (Figure 5g–k) [101]. Defect engineering provides a particularly powerful atomic‐scale lever for CeO2‐based electrocatalysts because it can magnify intrinsic material properties. Through doping (hydrothermal/solvothermal routes), reductive treatments (H2 annealing), or morphological tailoring (acid/alkali etching), one deliberately engineers synergistic oxygen vacancy Ov‐Ce3+ entities within the CeO2 lattice, thereby elevating the catalytic performance [112]. A review of lanthanum‐based OER materials likewise emphasizes coordinated defect, interface, and stability engineering [113]. An appropriate pore architecture should be determined at the intended current density by jointly assessing transport impedance, bubble detachment, and framework integrity after operation. In summary, designing high‐performance CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts rests on a multi‐scale cooperative strategy, weaving together macroscopic morphological regulation, nanoscale pore engineering, and atomic‐level dispersion to deliver fully optimized OER performance from the macroscopic scale down to the atomic level.

Interfacial Structure

CeO2 alone is not an efficient OER active phase. Combining it with synergistic components and constructing heterogeneous interfaces can fabricate highly catalytically active and stable OER electrocatalysts. This constitutes the fundamental approach to designing OER electrocatalysts, predicated on the redox characteristics of Ce, the kinetics of Ov, and the adaptability of the lattice structure [37, 114]. As further extensions of the aforementioned properties, doping, solid‐solution formation, and heterojunction engineering are strategies for constructing such atomic and lattice‐matched/mismatched heterointerfaces. By creating atomic and lattice heterointerfaces, it is possible to enhance the CeO2‐like functionalities inherent to CeO2 itself or those inherited by replaced Ce within the lattices of other metal oxides. In practice, the large ionic radius of Ce within CeO2 together with the strict requirement for homogeneous and stable dopant incorporation renders Ce salt precursors the conventional doping route [115, 116, 117, 118]. As shown in Figure 6a, Ce‐doped transition metal oxides prepared through a hydrothermal process followed by calcination exhibit Ce substitution that preferentially targets the octahedral metal sites of spinel oxides (e.g., NiCo2O4 and Co3O4). This substitution induces lattice expansion and, importantly, introduces surface Ov, driving charge redistribution. Such a structural arrangement permits precise manipulation of both the oxygen vacancy concentration and the metal valence states. Notably, it can even reroute the OER pathway from the conventional adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM) to the kinetically more favorable lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM), bypassing the inherent scaling relationship limitation of AEM, ultimately bestowing exceptional OER activity and stability on the catalyst [119, 120]. Given the fluorite structure of CeO2, the construction of substitutional solid solution oxides likewise represents another common strategy for composite material design (Figure 6b). Using CeO2 as the host matrix, Ir atoms uniformly occupy Ce lattice sites to form a single‐phase structure, which, by virtue of the intrinsic structural stability of the solid solution and the characteristic properties of CeO2, simultaneously delivers high acidic OER activity and long‐term operational stability at low Ir loading, making it well‐suited for practical application in proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers [121]. Similar to these methods, heterointerface engineering plays a crucial role in the design of CeO2‐based electrocatalysts (Figure 6c–f). From a structural perspective, lattice matching/mismatching, interfacial strain, defect distribution, and geometric contact configurations in CeO2‐based heterointerfaces collectively govern Ov evolution, oxygen migration, active‐phase reconstruction, and interfacial stability [60, 99, 114, 122]. For example, Ru‐O‐Ce linkages and local lattice strain formed at the RuO2‐CeO2 interface can enhance interfacial structural stability, thereby favoring long‐term operation under acidic OER conditions [99]. Heterojunctions are routinely fabricated via hydrothermal, solvothermal, and electrodeposition techniques. By regulating the components, strains, defects, and geometric contact forms on both sides of the interface, the interfacial structure and oxygen kinetics behavior at the interface can be customized [111]. Therefore, the construction of CeO2‐based heterointerfaces can further regulate Ov evolution and active‐site reconstruction. CeO2 can be integrated with other components in several distinct configurations, for instance, as a supporting substrate or as a surface modifier [123, 124, 125, 126]. To illustrate the substrate role, one may consider a heterojunction formed between CeO2 and a transition metal oxide, such as CoO. Meanwhile, CeO2 acting as a surface modifier traps hydroxyl radicals through interfacial Ov, accelerating O‐O bond formation [128]. Overall, rational construction of CeO2‐based interfacial structures can improve OER activity and structural stability by regulating lattice coupling, defect distribution, oxygen migration, and active‐phase reconstruction [60, 99, 111, 114, 122]. The charge transfer, built‐in electric fields, orbital coupling, and changes in adsorption energy that arise after interface formation are discussed collectively in Section 3.2 [114, 129]. Throughout this review, “lattice‐oxygen participation” refers to the lattice oxygen of the host catalyst unless the involvement of the own lattice oxygen of ceria is explicitly stated and supported by direct evidence.

Critical Evaluation of Design Strategies and Selection Principles

The aforementioned Ce doping, Ov engineering, heterointerfaces, solid solutions, and morphology control can all improve the performance of CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts, but their functional emphases and applicable conditions are not the same. Overall, Ce doping, moderate Ov, and strongly coupled CeO2 heterointerfaces can directly regulate the local coordination of active centers, metal valence states, and interfacial electronic structure, and are therefore more favorable for enhancing intrinsic activity [78, 112, 119, 120, 127, 130, 131, 132]; hierarchical porous structures, self‐supported arrays, and morphology engineering mainly improve geometric activity at high current densities by increasing accessible sites, improving mass transport, and promoting bubble detachment [103, 106, 133, 134, 135]. Accordingly, different strategies should adopt matched evaluation metrics: intrinsic effects need to be assessed in combination with electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) or site‐normalized activity; engineering effects should focus on high‐current polarization and mass transport; and stability should be judged by combining structural evolution and metal dissolution [136]. For Ni‐based alkaline systems, interference from trace Fe in KOH and from Ni foam substrates should also be ruled out [137]. From the perspective of intrinsic activity, Ce doping and regulation of CeO2 heterointerfaces are the most direct. After Ce enters the transition metal oxide lattice, it can alter the electronic states of neighboring metal centers through Ce‐O‐M bonding, 4f electron buffering, and local Ov [78, 119, 120, 138]; however, excessively high Ce content may cause precipitation of a separate CeO2 phase, so extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) or atomic‐resolution imaging is required to confirm actual substitution. Heterointerfaces can improve OER performance through interfacial charge transfer, OH− enrichment, and active‐phase reconstruction [50, 85, 97, 127, 139, 140], but excess CeO2 may cover active sites and increase resistance, indicating that the effective interface proportion is more critical than simply increasing the CeO2 content [141, 142]. Ov engineering also has an optimal window: moderate Ov is beneficial for regulating Ce3+/Ce4+ and adsorption behavior, whereas excessive defects may weaken lattice stability, and some Ov may be refilled under strongly anodic potentials [112, 130, 131, 132]. The operating environment further determines strategy priority. In alkaline media, Ce doping, controlled Ov, and heterointerfaces that promote reconstruction are more suitable for addressing intrinsic kinetic limitations; when entering high‐current‐density operation, hierarchical porous structures, self‐supported arrays, or in situ electrodeposition should be combined to improve mass transport, charge transport, and bubble release [97, 103, 106, 133, 134, 135]. In acidic OER, stability takes precedence over further increasing defects; therefore, CeO2 is more suitable as a stable support for Ir/Ru or as a solid‐solution component, where metal‐support interactions, Ir‐O‐Ce bonding, and electron buffering suppress overoxidation and dissolution [99, 100, 111, 121, 143, 144, 145]. For seawater OER, OER selectivity and resistance to Cl− corrosion should be prioritized; CeO2 heterointerfaces and selective CeOx overlayers are more targeted [86, 146], but an overlayer that is too thick will increase mass transport resistance. Therefore, there is no single optimal strategy for CeO2‐based OER catalysts that applies to all conditions. On this basis, we provide a critical comparison of the major design strategies in Table 1 and summarize their applicable conditions. A more rational design should be selected according to the actual rate‐limiting step: when intrinsic kinetics are insufficient, doping, controlled Ov, and strongly coupled interfaces should be prioritized; for high‐current operation, porous structures and self‐supported electrodes should be emphasized; in acidic environments, stable supports or solid solutions should be prioritized; and in seawater environments, Cl−‐resistant interfaces and protective layers should be emphasized. At the same time, Ce content, interface proportion, and Ov concentration generally each have an optimal range, beyond which site dilution, increased resistance, or decreased structural stability may occur [130, 141, 142].

Mechanistic Roles of CeO2 in OER

From the perspective of synergistic atomic and electronic structure modulation, the multifunctional catalytic characteristics of CeO2‐based electrocatalysts for the OER are fundamentally rooted in the dynamic evolution of Ce3+/Ce4+ redox coupled with Ov, the reversible redox switching governed by the unique 4f electron configuration, and the consequent driving forces for interfacial reconstruction (Figure 7). At the atomic scale, abundant Ovs within the CeO2 lattice not only serve as active sites that directly participate in the reaction pathway by finely tuning the distribution of adsorption free energies of oxygenated intermediates to lower the reaction barrier, but also induce an asymmetric redistribution of local charge density, thereby potentially triggering lattice oxygen oxidation mechanisms (LOM) that are kinetically more favorable. Concomitantly, the unique 4f electron configuration of cerium allows it to shuttle easily between Ce3+ and Ce4+, giving rise to what is known as a “d‐p‐f gradient orbital coupling” effect. Consequently, CeO2 operates as an electronic buffer: it continually adjusts the d‐band center position and the electron filling state of neighboring active metals. This behavior stabilizes centers in a high oxidation state and, at the same time, promotes rapid interfacial charge movement. Separately, Ov capture OH− preferentially, which provides both thermodynamic and kinetic impetus for dynamic surface reconstruction. This impetus drives the targeted and accelerated transformation of hydroxide precursors into the catalytically superior oxyhydroxide phase. As the conversion proceeds, well‐defined heterointerfaces form between the two phases; these interfaces cooperatively refine the migration pathways and the adsorption/desorption characteristics of oxygen species. It should be noted that the above intrinsic properties provide a common mechanistic basis for different Ce configurations, but their dominant role still depends on the structural identity and spatial location of Ce species in the catalytic system [147]. When CeO2 acts as a cocatalyst, it mainly functions through charge redistribution at heterointerfaces, adsorption modulation, and promotion of the reconstruction of neighboring active phases, while CeO2 itself is not necessarily the main OER active center [93, 147]; when it acts as a support, in addition to interfacial regulation, it can also improve structural stability by anchoring and stabilizing active species and inhibiting overoxidation and dissolution [121]. In contrast, when Ce is incorporated into the host lattice in a doped form, its role arises more from charge rearrangement, lattice distortion, and defect modulation caused by local Ce‐O‐M coordination, thereby altering the local electronic structure of the host material [119]; whereas when CeO2 serves as the main component or continuous matrix, its Ce3+/Ce4+ redox and Ov‐related defect chemistry participate more directly in the regulation of the entire catalytic system [121]. Therefore, although different configurations share a common intrinsic physicochemical origin of Ce, their dominant action pathways exhibit pronounced configuration dependence [93, 119, 121, 147]. In essence, the structural and chemical features of CeO2‐based systems are brought into alignment across atomic, electronic, and mesoscopic length scales, a multiscale coherence that serves as the theoretical basis for a concerted improvement of intrinsic activity and long‐term durability in the oxygen evolution reaction.

Ov Generation

Ovs in CeO2‐based OER catalysts have long drawn attention as a central theme in electrocatalysis because their dynamic behavior is so tightly coupled to catalytic performance. As shown in Figure 8a–e, the fluorite structure of CeO2 confers a remarkable oxygen‐storage capacity, while the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple facilitates the formation and stabilization of Ovs through coupled charge compensation. In addition to the generation of Ov coupled with Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle, it has been observed that surface Ov can dynamically evolve through thermodynamically driven surface reconstruction. In a reducing atmosphere (e.g., CO), surface oxygen atoms desorb preferentially, leaving behind vacancies, while nearby Ce atoms migrate to relieve lattice strain. Under oxidizing conditions (e.g., CO2), the vacancies are sealed again by reverse oxygen refilling, a process intimately linked to the Ce3+→Ce4+ valence transition (Figure 8f) [148]. The spatial distribution of these vacancies, however, is far from uniform. Previous data show that the (111) facet is markedly more stable due to its higher oxygen coordination number, which translates into a higher vacancy formation energy, whereas the (100) and (110) planes tend to arrange vacancies into periodic superstructures (Figure 8g) [149]. Such crystal‐plane selectivity offers a rationale for vacancy engineering: by deliberately tuning the synthesis parameters, one can generate Ovs‐rich surfaces that deliver substantially boosted catalytic activity. Within the OER, Ov sites can serve directly as active centers, adjusting the adsorption free energies of oxygenated intermediates and thereby lowering the reaction barriers [150, 151, 152]. A case in point is CeO2‐decorated Ni3S2, where Ov‐induced reconstruction of a NiOOH interface, which markedly enhances OH− adsorption and cuts the OER overpotential (Figure 8h–i) [50]. In contrast to its role as a cocatalyst, complementary DFT calculations reveal that the CeO2 surface preferentially forms Ov, thereby inducing local charge redistribution and a mixed Ce3+/Ce4+ valence state. This electronic perturbation, working in concert with interfacial electron transfer across the heterojunction, fine‐tunes the binding of critical LOM intermediates and ultimately brings about a pronounced reduction in the O2 desorption energy barrier [59]. Notably, Ov concentration can also be experimentally adjusted; tuning it to an appropriate level further enhances OER activity (Figure 8j–l) [130]. The Ov in CeO2‐based OER catalysts serves both as providers of active sites and regulators of structural evolution. Precise modulation of Ov enables synergistic optimization of the catalyst's electronic structure and reaction kinetics, providing new insights for designing efficient and stable non‐noble metal OER catalysts.

Electronic Structure Modulation

CeO2, owing to its unique 4f electronic configuration and tunable redox properties, can be modulated through strategies such as doping, heterostructure construction, and strain engineering to tailor its electronic structure, thereby significantly optimizing OER activity and stability [147, 153]. This section systematically summarizes the key mechanisms and recent advances in the modulation of the electronic structure of CeO2. Unlike Section 2.4, which focuses on interfacial configurations and structural roles, this section emphasizes the electronic effects that emerge after these structures form, including charge transfer, orbital coupling, and changes in adsorption energy [114, 129]. In terms of doping engineering, CeO2 possesses a fluorite‐type cubic crystal structure, and the ionic radii of Ce3+ and Ce4+ are 1.14 and 0.97 Å, respectively. If CeO2 is introduced as a dopant into other materials, the larger ionic radius induces lattice distortion, thereby reducing the structural stability. Moreover, the Ov occupied core functionality of CeO2 may be isolated. Therefore, more homogeneous doping is achieved by incorporating Ce in its ionic form, where the electronegativity difference between Ce and the host catalyst metal and the unique 4f orbital characteristics enable precise electronic regulation: because Ce possesses significantly lower electronegativity than transition metals, it tends to donate electrons to adjacent metal sites and coordinated oxygen, thereby avoiding excessive oxidation of the active metal into easily dissolved high‐valence species (Figure 9a–f) [119, 138]. Simultaneously, through the construction of Ce(4f)‐O(2p)‐Co(3d) gradient orbital coupling units by introducing single‐atom Ce, the unoccupied Ce 4f orbitals serve as an electronic buffer that provides electronic compensation to the active center during OER. This mechanism suppresses the over‐oxidation of Co‐O bonds, preserves Co‐O covalency, and optimizes the occupancy of Co‐3d‐eg orbitals, thereby balancing the adsorption strengths of oxygen intermediates and significantly enhancing catalytic activity [78]. The optimization of the electronic structure concurrently modulates the d‐band center of the host catalyst, further reducing the Gibbs free energy of the OER rate‐determining step. For instance, in CeO2‐CoOOH, the free energy of the rate‐determining step in the AEM pathway decreases substantially [154]. It has also been demonstrated that employing CeO2 as a support retains its unique Ov and Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple while allowing the introduction of other dopants for synergistic catalysis, further optimizing OER performance [155]. For example, doping Ni into CeO2 ultrathin nanosheet assemblies (NSAs) significantly increases the Ce3+ fraction; the enrichment of Ce3+ enhances the adsorption of oxygenated intermediates such as OH−. Concurrently, heteroatom doping induces more Ovs, which lower the deprotonation energy barrier, improve electrical conductivity, and increase the number of active sites, thereby synergistically optimizing the oxygen evolution performance [65]. As a cocatalyst, the most extensively studied strategy for CeO2 is the construction of heterojunctions. CeO2‐based heterojunctions can encompass virtually all electronic modulation mechanisms. Because differences in work function and Fermi level commonly exist between distinct components, formation of a heterointerface can induce directional charge transfer and establish a built‐in electric field, thereby altering the local electronic states of adjacent active sites [114, 129]. For example, Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) analysis of a NiTe/CeO2 heterostructure shows that the work functions of CeO2 and NiTe are 4.26 and 4.49 eV, respectively, yielding a work‐function difference of 0.23 eV that drives electron transfer from CeO2 to NiTe until Fermi‐level alignment and forms a built‐in electric field at the interface. Consequently, the electron density increases on the NiTe side and decreases on the CeO2 side, effectively modulating the interfacial electronic structure; the resulting positive charge accumulation on the CeO2 side enhances OH− adsorption [114]. As shown in Figure 9g–i, similar to the single‐atom Ce doping mechanism, CeO2 can modulate the electronic structure of the host catalyst through the heterointerface, altering the d‐band center and charge distribution of active sites and accelerating charge transfer [52, 85, 156, 157, 158]. At the interfacial region where CoO and CeO2 are in close contact, strong electronic coupling reconstructs the electronic structure and generates highly active sites [127]; CeO2 acting as a surface modifier can also facilitate interfacial electron transfer [129]. In addition, in the CeO2/Ce‐Co3O4 system, the interfacial built‐in electric field and interatomic charge transfer through the Co‐O‐Ce configuration synergistically regulate electron localization and the d‐band center at Co sites: the Bader charge further decreases from −1.29 eV for Co3O4 and −1.27 eV for Ce‐Co3O4 to −0.85 eV, the d‐band center upshifts from −1.993 to −1.645 eV, and the RDS energy barrier decreases from 1.55 eV to 1.44 eV, thereby enhancing OER activity [129]. Meanwhile, it effectively tunes the electronic structure of the active sites, optimizing the adsorption and desorption energies of oxygenated intermediates (*OH, *O, *OOH), thus accelerating the O‐O bond formation step (*O → *OOH) and enhancing the overall OER electrocatalytic efficiency (Figure 9j) [106, 159, 160]. During the OER process, the unique Ce3+/Ce4+ reversible redox couple in CeO2 acts as an efficient electronic mediator, lowering the energy barrier for valence‐state transitions of metal ions in the host catalyst and triggering the conversion of Co or Ni species into highly active valence states at lower anodic potentials [52, 85, 157]. Beyond these widely investigated regulation strategies, there are also fewer common approaches. For instance, in situ grown CeO2 nanoparticles induce interplanar spacing compression while modulating the electronic structure through interfacial charge transfer, thereby altering the oxygen evolution reaction pathway [100]. When designing CeO2 to enhance OER activity through electronic structure modulation, it is highly desirable to comprehensively exploit the above‐mentioned characteristics to simultaneously improve both activity and stability.

Surface Reconstruction

For OER electrocatalysts, elucidating the origin of their high activity and investigating surface reconstruction and dynamic evolution processes are crucial. As mentioned in the previous subsection, CeO2‐based heterointerfaces can drive the in situ reconstruction of primary catalysts [161, 162]. The dynamic surface reconstruction process exhibits multi‐level regularity across temporal and spatial scales. This section systematically elucidates the role of CeO2 within composite structures and interfaces in regulating OER kinetics from a spatiotemporal perspective, governing dynamic evolution with emphasis on interfacial coupling and surface reconstruction (Figure 10a–d). It should be noted that CeO2‐induced reconstruction is still driven by the applied potential; compared with unmodified catalysts, interfacial coupling modifies the pathways of metal oxidation, deprotonation, and local coordination evolution, rather than constituting a mechanism independent of the applied potential [93, 157]. This interfacial regulation is illustrated by representative Ni‐based systems, in which interfacial charge redistribution modifies the initial Ni oxidation response at lower polarization potentials [163]. For example, in CeO2/NiO, electron transfer from NiO to CeO2 increases the Ni3+ content; the study links this higher Ni oxidation state to enhanced Ni‐O covalency [150]. As the potential rises and polarization proceeds, this modified electronic environment facilitates Ni oxidation; together with OH− adsorption and surface deprotonation, it favors conversion of the hydroxylated surface into NiOOH, helping explain its appearance at lower potentials [50, 163]. Once the active surface develops, interfacial coupling can continue to influence Ni‐O bonding and stabilize the Ni oxidation state during OER [150]. CeO2 therefore affects both active‐phase formation and the electronic environment of the reconstructed interface, without necessarily producing a different phase: Ce‐containing Ni‐Fe‐Co oxides still form metal oxyhydroxides under operating conditions [163], while post‐OER signals in CeO2‐modified Ni3N have been assigned to β‐NiOOH [164]. A lower formation potential alone does not establish a faster reconstruction rate. The pathway also remains system‐dependent: in acidic Co3O4/CeO2, CeO2‐induced shortening of Co‐O bonds modifies the Co‐O bonding environment, which facilitates the formation of OER‐active Co4+ species and thereby bypasses the pronounced reconstruction process associated with charge accumulation [157]. The Ov in CeO2 acts as a catalytically active defect site. The Ov forms upon the generation of interface and the exposure of surrounding Ce3+ ions (originally Ce4+) [97, 103]. The low‐coordination state of Ce3+ enhances its Lewis acidity, enabling preferential adsorption and enrichment of OH− ions in the solution through electrostatic interactions, thereby providing a driving force for the surface reconstruction of active surfaces, such as transition metal oxides [50]. The strong d‐p‐f gradient orbital coupling driven by interfacial interactions between CeO2 and transition metals is another critical factor that triggers surface reconstruction. The role of CeO2 varies across different systems and environments. When CeO2 is in an oxidized state (Ce4+‐dominant), it tends to accept electrons from low‐oxidation‐state metals (e.g., Co2+). In Ov‐rich CeO2‐x, where the Ce3+ ratio increases, CeO2 tends to donate electrons to high‐oxidation‐state metals, thereby accelerating the reconstruction process [97, 127]. As shown in Figure 10e–i, during the initial OER stage, the Ov and electron transfer effects of CeO2 initiate localized reconstruction. CeO2‐modified transition metal compounds exhibit peaks of active phases at lower potentials [85]. As the potential increases to the medium‐potential region, rapid reconstruction occurs, forming highly active structures at the interface between transition metals and CeO2. The reconstructed active interface stabilizes over time, with denser interfacial structures [97]. The CeO2 modification layer preferentially induces oxidation of transition metals on the primary catalyst surface through interfacial interactions. The internal structure gradually transforms into amorphous CoOOH after OER, while the bulk retains part of its initial structure (Figure 10j–l) [85]. After reconstruction, CeO2 does not merely play an initiation role: in situ X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) of CoOx/CeO2 shows that the Co valence changes with potential while the Ce L3‐edge remains essentially unchanged; combined with changes in surface Co/O structure, this supports that CeO2 continuously regulates the electronic structure of the active interface rather than proving that Ce directly participates in the oxygen evolution cycle [93]. In addition, a selectively permeable CeOx layer on NiFeOx can suppress Fe leaching while allowing OH− and O2 to pass through without reducing its OER activity, providing another mechanism for continuously stabilizing the active components; however, this protective‐layer effect cannot be directly equated with the role of all CeO2 heterointerfaces [165]. Therefore, participation in interfacial regulation after reconstruction should be clearly distinguished from acting as a direct reaction center, and the reversible valence change of Ce and the defect cycle still require in situ evidence from the corresponding systems. CeO2 not only accelerates reconstruction but also maintains interfacial stability through dynamic equilibrium. During OER, filling of Ov by OH− or O species oxidizes Ce3+ to Ce4+, whereas regeneration of Ov reduces Ce4+ back to Ce3+ [112]. This cyclic process stabilizes Ov concentration during prolonged testing, ensuring sustained reconstruction [97]. Differences exist in the reconstruction pathways of transition metals across systems. For example, Ni phosphides and sulfides modified with CeO2 are more prone to fracture, releasing S/P anions to form Ov‐rich NiOOH interfaces. This indicates more thorough reconstruction in sulfide/phosphide systems [46]. Nevertheless, CeO2‐mediated promotion of transition metal OER catalyst reconstruction follows universal mechanisms. As a co‐catalyst, CeO2 significantly accelerates surface reconstruction of transition metal catalysts through Ov‐induced OH− adsorption, interfacial electron transfer, and dynamic Ov replenishment, while optimizing the electronic structure and reaction pathways of the reconstructed interfaces.

Operando Evidence and the Controversy Over Lattice‐Oxygen Participation in CeO2

The preceding discussion demonstrates that CeO2 can continuously alter the local structure and reaction pathways of OER‐active phases through oxygen‐vacancy evolution, interfacial electronic regulation, and surface reconstruction. However, a central question remains unresolved: whether the lattice oxygen of CeO2 itself directly enters the OER catalytic cycle, or whether CeO2 mainly regulates the lattice‐oxygen activity of neighboring transition‐metal active phases through interfacial interactions. Because increased Ov concentration, changes in the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio, pH dependence, and DFT predictions are largely indirect evidence, a rigorous distinction between these possibilities still requires isotope tracing combined with operando structural characterization. At present, 18O isotope labeling coupled with differential electrochemical mass spectrometry and online electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS/OLEMS) remains one of the most direct approaches for determining whether lattice oxygen enters evolved O2. Huang et al. detected 16O18O and 18O2 in an Ov‐rich Fe2O3@CeO2 heterostructure, demonstrating lattice‐oxygen participation and showing that Ovs preferentially formed on the CeO2 side [59]. In Ce‐doped CoTeO3, 18O‐DEMS together with in situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and Raman also supported defect evolution, surface reconstruction, and LOM characteristics [166]. Nevertheless, these results cannot be generalized as direct proof that fluorite CeO2 lattice oxygen universally enters O2: the former cannot spatially distinguish oxygen from the CeO2 and Fe2O3 sublattices, whereas the latter contains Ce as a dopant rather than an independent CeO2 phase. Moreover, residual H2 18O, structural water, isotope exchange, and detection sensitivity can complicate DEMS‐based LOM assignment [167]. Existing operando spectroscopy further reveals how CeO2 regulates adjacent active phases. Operando HXAS of Fe‐CoO/CeO2 showed that CeO2 modifies the potential‐dependent oxidation behavior of Co and Fe [168], while in situ Raman of CeO2@NiSx/C3N4 revealed Ce‐promoted NiOOH formation and modulation of the interfacial water network [169]. In addition, operando X‐ray diffraction (XRD) of NiCeOxHy showed that Ce incorporation facilitates the transformation toward γ‐NiOOH with exposed (003) facets, directly linking Ce‐induced structural evolution to the formation of the OER‐active phase [170]. Thus, XAS, Raman, and XRD provide complementary information on oxidation states, local coordination, and phase reconstruction, but none alone can determine the oxygen source in evolved O2. The information provided by representative operando/in situ techniques and their main limitations are summarized in Table 2. Overall, lattice‐oxygen participation in CeO2‐based OER systems is strongly configuration‐dependent. Current evidence confirms that CeO2 or Ce species can regulate defects, electronic structure, and reconstruction and can promote LOM in selected systems, but it remains insufficient to demonstrate universal, direct participation of CeO2 lattice oxygen. Future studies should combine selective 18O labeling and DEMS with operando X‐ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (XAFS), Raman, and XRD, while EPR and low‐dose liquid‐phase transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provide complementary information on defect cycling and interfacial evolution. Only when oxygen origin, local structure, and electronic state are consistently correlated within the same catalyst can direct CeO2 lattice‐oxygen participation be distinguished from predominant regulation of neighboring active phases.

Performance of CeO2‐Based OER Catalysts in Different Electrolytes

The catalytic behavior of CeO2‐based electrocatalysts for the oxygen evolution reaction is likewise profoundly influenced by the electrolytic environment, with their structural adaptability and interfacial response mechanisms manifesting distinct functional pathways under varying pH and ionic strength conditions (Figure 11). Under alkaline conditions, what primarily unlocks the catalytic performance of CeO2 is atomic‐scale electronic‐state engineering combined with defect‑chemical design. Doping with heteroatoms, such as sulfur, nitrogen, or phosphorus, or constructing composite architectures with Co‐ or Ni‐based transition metals raises the Ov population and redistributes the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio. This, in turn, tunes the adsorption energetics and desorption barriers of oxygenated intermediates at surface active sites, speeding up OER kinetics. Central to these strategies is a synergistic promotion of active‐facet exposure and charge‐transport properties, achieved through structural tuning that ranges from the mesoscopic down to the atomic level. In strongly acidic electrolytes, CeO2 rarely serves as the primary active phase. Rather, it exploits its electron‐buffering capacity and the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple to dynamically modulate the valence distribution of active centers and to suppress the dissolution and leaching of metallic constituents at elevated potentials. This is typically accomplished by forming heterointerfaces or solid‐solution‐type coupled structures with noble‐metal components (e.g., Ir, Ru). Such stabilization markedly extends the catalyst's operational lifetime while preserving high intrinsic activity. The underlying stabilization mechanism is, in essence, a synergistic consequence of interfacial electronic reconfiguration and chemical‑environmental buffering. When the electrolyte environment shifts to high‐salinity, highly corrosive seawater systems, CeO2‐based electrocatalysts exhibit a distinctive adaptive interfacial micro‐environmental response. Consistent with hard‐soft acid‐base theory, OH− species preferentially accumulate on the CeO2 surface, spontaneously creating a local alkaline reaction micro‐zone. At the same time, arising from the surface‑charge characteristics of CeO2, electrostatic repulsion effectively shields against nucleophilic attack by chloride ions. This repulsion suppresses the competing chlorine‑evolution side reaction while ensuring high Faradaic efficiency and long‐term operational stability of the OER process. Fundamentally, this effect represents a cross‑scale protective strategy governed by interfacial charge distribution and ion‑selective transport. To summarize, the functional behavior of CeO2 across diverse electrolytic scenarios stems from its structural plasticity and the tunability of its interfacial chemistry: it stabilizes active components through heterostructures, reshapes electronic states and reaction pathways via doping and defect engineering, and excludes corrosive species through surface‑chemical micro‐environmental exclusion. These cross‐scale mechanistic features collectively constitute the design foundation for CeO2‐based oxygen evolution electrocatalysts to deliver efficient, highly stable operation in multi‑scenario water electrolysis systems.

The role of CeO2 in the OER under different environments.

Alkaline Electrolytes

During water electrolysis, alkaline media pose a markedly lower corrosion risk to electrode and electrolyzer materials than acidic environments do. Running the process under acidic conditions, by contrast, forces the use of noble‐metal catalysts, such as platinum, iridium, and ruthenium, simply to withstand the aggressive corrosion, which drives up costs considerably. For this reason, alkaline water electrolysis remains the prevailing industrial route today. Against this backdrop, CeO2‐based electrocatalysts have drawn substantial research interest as highly active electrocatalysts for OER in alkaline electrolytes. Their Ov defects, reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couples, and excellent oxygen storage/release capacity make them promising low‐cost components of composite OER catalysts that can reduce the reliance on noble‐metal catalysts (e.g., IrO2, RuO2) [174, 175, 176]. However, pure‐phase CeO2 often exhibits unsatisfactory OER performance, such as high overpotential and slow reaction kinetics, due to its intrinsic poor conductivity and limited exposed active sites [131]. To significantly enhance its catalytic performance, researchers have developed various effective modification strategies, mainly including heteroatom doping, constructing heterostructures with other functional materials, morphology and defect engineering, and designing self‐supporting electrodes. As shown in Figure 12a–c, in terms of heteroatom doping, the introduction of non‐metallic elements (e.g., S, N, P, B) and transition metal elements (e.g., Fe, Ni) is a key means to modulate the electronic structure of CeO2 and increase Ov concentration, thereby enhancing activity [94, 131, 132, 177]. Representative non‐metal doping is S‐doped CeO2 (S‐CeO2), which requires an overpotential of only 190 mV to reach 10 mA cm−2 in 1 M KOH, with a Tafel slope of 83 mV dec−1, and demonstrates excellent stability exceeding 20 h; theoretical calculations indicate that S doping introduces impurity energy levels near the O2/H2O energy levels, promoting electron transfer and optimizing the adsorption energy of oxygen‐containing intermediates [131]. Transition metal doping, particularly Fe doping and Ni/Fe co‐doping, can induce highly disordered structures and numerous Ovs in CeO2 [94, 132]. In transition metal‐doped and co‐doped CeO2 nanostructures, the Ov concentration increases markedly; concurrently, modulation of the electronic orbital structure facilitates the LOM pathway (Figure 12d–f) [94, 139]. In constructing heterostructures, compounding CeO2 with other metal oxides (e.g., CoO, NiO), phosphides (e.g., CoP), noble metal oxides (e.g., RuO2), or carbon materials (e.g., graphene, reduced graphene oxide rGO) can synergistically enhance conductivity, increase ECSA, and optimize the adsorption/desorption energy of reaction intermediates through interfacial electronic effects (Figure 12g–i) [112, 133, 134, 140, 178, 179, 180, 181, 182, 183, 184]. However, the performance gain cannot be directly equated with newly created Ce active sites. In CeO2/Ni(OH)2/carbon foam, the Ni oxidation peak shifts from 1.46 to 1.41 V, and the integrated peak area increases by approximately 1.7‐fold, supporting the role of CeO2 in promoting the formation of high‐valent Ni active species [185]. At the CeO2/NiFeCr hydroxide interface, DFT indicates that d‐f coupling promotes charge transfer and improves the reaction thermodynamics of host sites [160]. These results support regulation of the host catalyst by CeO2, but they cannot quantify the respective contributions of newly created Ce sites versus host regulation, nor can they rule out Ce‐related interfacial activity in other systems. Distinguishing these two contributions should match loading and testing conditions, compare the host catalyst, CeO2, physical mixtures, and heterostructures, and combine reliable site normalization with operando spectroscopy; an increase in oxidation peak area or ECSA is not equivalent to a Ce‐site contribution. For example, in carbon material composite strategies, the CeO2‐hydroxylated graphene hybrid catalyst (C‐hG) exhibits an OER onset potential superior to IrO2. DFT calculations reveal that the covalent interface between surface oxygen atoms of CeO2 and low‐hydroxylation‐coverage graphene is key to activating catalytic sites [181]. Single‐atom catalyst (SAC) design further improves atom utilization, such as single atoms of W, Cu, and Co oxides anchored on CeO2‐rGO (rGO‐ED‐CeO2‐WCuCo‐30). High‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) and XPS confirm the presence of isolated metal atoms, abundant Ovs, and multivalent metals [186]. Morphology control and defect engineering are likewise decisive. CoCe/C HFs with three‐dimensional nanoflower‐like structures or nanoneedle arrays are a representative case that renders a substantially larger population of active sites accessible [133]. In ZIF‐67‐derived Co3O4/CeO2 heterostructures, porous channels emerge via the self‐assembly of two‐dimensional nanosheets, an arrangement that facilitates electrolyte diffusion [134]. Additionally, self‐supporting electrode architectures (e.g., directly grown on nickel foam) do away with binders altogether, which, in turn, strengthens mechanical stability and durability under high current densities [135]. A self‐supporting Co/CeO2 heterojunction electrode achieves an OER overpotential of 400.3 mV at a high current density of 1 A cm−2, and overall water splitting requires a cell voltage of only 1.92 V to reach 1 A cm−2 [135]. Acidic Electrolytes

The OER in an acidic environment is a core process in proton exchange membrane (PEM) water electrolysis technology, an emerging and more efficient method for water splitting. However, the highly corrosive nature of acidic media poses significant challenges for catalyst development [187, 188, 189, 190]. In recent years, CeO2‐based composite materials have exhibited a significant enhancement in stability for the acidic OER through catalyst structure engineering strategies, such as interface engineering and valence state regulation. In terms of interface engineering, CeO2 compounded with a main catalyst in the form of a heterojunction (e.g., CeO2/Co3O4) enables OER at low overpotentials in acidic environments and further suppresses acid corrosion and enhances stability through carbon coating that promotes OH coverage (Figure 13a) [191]. Similarly, composites of CeO2 with iridium oxides (IrOx), such as IrOx/CeO2 nanowires, utilize the electron buffering effect of CeO2 to modulate Ir valence states, achieving an overpotential of 220 mV and 300 h of stability in acidic media, with a mass activity as high as 167 A g−1 [143]. The introduction of CeO2 enhances OER performance primarily through the following mechanisms: First, the Ov and redox properties of CeO2 promote electron transfer and intermediate adsorption. As shown in Figure 13b, in CeO2/Co‐Ni‐P‐Ox, CeO2 forms Ce‐O‐Co bonds with cobalt oxide, increases the valence state of Co, and in situ generates Co3+‐O active sites, lowering the deprotonation energy barrier of water molecules. DFT‐based analysis identifies the reducible Co3+‐O sites as the origin of the optimized OH and OOH adsorption [192]. Equally critical is the interfacial interaction between CeO2 and noble metals (e.g., Ir, Ru): strong metal‐support interactions (MSI) suppress metal dissolution, thereby markedly enhancing stability (Figure 13c,e–g) [144]. The effect is plainly seen in the Ir dissolution rate of IrOx/CeO2, which is a mere 0.81%, a sharp contrast to the 12.35% recorded for carbon‐supported analogues [145]. Furthermore, morphology and the adopted composite strategy further dictate performance. Zero‐dimensional/two‐dimensional (0D/2D) heterostructures, such as CeO2 quantum dots decorating SrIrO3 nanosheets (Figure 13d), expand the population of interfacial active sites; carbon supports (CeO2@C), on the other hand, confer improved conductivity and dispersibility. Table 4 summarizes representative acidic OER performance data. It should be noted, however, that an excessive CeO2 content can dilute the proportion of active sites and, in turn, compromise catalytic activity [141, 142]. In summary, CeO2‐based electrocatalysts provide efficient and stable solutions for acidic OER through valence state regulation, interface engineering, and electron buffering effects. Future work needs to further explore low‐cost, large‐scale preparation and practical PEMWE applications.

Seawater Electrolytes

CeO2‐based electrocatalysts exhibit outstanding OER activity and stability in seawater electrolysis. Their mechanisms of action primarily include Cl− blocking, electronic structure modulation, surface reconstruction optimization, and other aspects [194, 195]. Based on the Hard and Soft Acids and Bases principle (HSAB), CeO2, as a hard Lewis acid, preferentially adsorbs OH− over Cl−. This creates an OH−‐enriched local microenvironment on the electrode surface, effectively inhibiting Cl− adsorption and the consequent competitive chlorine evolution reaction (ClER) and electrode corrosion processes (Figure 14a–c) [196, 197]. For instance, introducing CeO2 and Cl− intercalation into CoFe LDH (CoFeOOH‐Cl−/CeO2) results in a catalyst that, in alkaline seawater, shows an overpotential of only 329 mV at a current density of 1000 mA cm−2 and can operate stably for 1000 h. As shown in Figure 14d, the mechanism originates from the Lewis acidity of CeO2, which preferentially binds OH−, while intercalated Cl− lowers the adsorption energy of chloride on the metal centers. This dual effect that substantially enhances reaction selectivity and structural robustness [198]. Beyond modulating adsorption selectivity, CeO2 also establishes a physical electrostatic barrier against Cl− erosion: its point of zero charge (PZC) is roughly 7, so under alkaline conditions the surface acquires a negative charge and repels the like‐charged Cl− ions, thereby suppressing electrode corrosion and side reactions [146]. Through interface engineering, ceria further safeguards the active phase by combining physical isolation with electronic modulation. The resulting in situ architecture defends the catalyst against corrosion while preserving high OER selectivity [146, 199]. In situ characterization, for instance, tracks the dynamic evolution of CeO2 during OER and confirms its conversion into a nanoparticle film (ca. 1 nm thick) that coats the active phase surface, thereby boosting stability (Figure 14e) [146]. It is worth noting that this CeO2 overlayer typically possesses a disordered structure that allows OH− ions to diffuse unhindered to the active sites beneath, ensuring sustained OER activity. An electrode prepared by a two‐step electrodeposition method, Ce‐Co(OH)2@FeOOH, utilizes this mechanism, and operates stably for 2500 h at a high current density of 2 A cm−2 in simulated seawater without hypochlorite formation [146]. On the other hand, the introduction of CeO2 optimizes the electronic structure of the catalyst. Its unique adjustable Ov and Ce3+/Ce4+ redox pairs can modulate the valence state and electron configuration of adjacent metal active centers (e.g., Co, Ni, Fe) through strong electronic interactions, thereby lowering the OER energy barrier and promoting the in situ formation of highly active species (e.g., CoOOH) [200, 201]. In the CeO2/CoNiP heterojunction, the negative zeta potential of CeO2 under alkaline conditions helps electrostatically repel Cl−, reducing the leaching of the active component cobalt (Figure 14f). Simultaneously, CeO2 promotes the adsorption of the *OH intermediate through its Ov, significantly reducing the energy barrier of the rate‐determining step [197]. In the NiFe‐LDH system, the synergistic effect of CeO2 and (W2O7) [2−] intercalation, by expanding the interlayer spacing and enhancing Lewis acidity, causes Ni and Fe sites to exhibit higher valence states, preferentially adsorbing OH−. DFT calculations confirm that the Cl− adsorption energy weakens from −0.52 to −0.25 eV, while the OH− adsorption energy strengthens from −0.24 to −0.69 eV (Figure 14 g) [200]. The above adsorption differences not only involve chemical bonding interactions but may also be related to changes in the local coordination environment of Ni/Fe sites. In terms of chemical bonding, surface hydroxyl groups on CeO2 can coordinate with Ce sites through the oxygen end to form Ce‐OH, providing a basis for the specific adsorption of oxygen‐containing species [202]. In the aforementioned example where CeO2 and (W2O7) [2−] intercalation jointly regulate the electronic structure of Ni/Fe sites, from the perspective of ligand field theory, changes in ligand arrangement, local symmetry, and bond length may further tune the d‐orbital energy levels of Ni/Fe sites and metal 3d‐O‐2p orbital hybridization, thereby affecting the adsorption behavior of oxygen‐containing intermediates [203, 204]. For example, in the La‐doped NiFe alloy, the introduction of La upshifts the d‐band center of NiFe from −1.15 to −0.99 eV (NiFeMo: −1.03 eV), thereby enhancing OOH adsorption and lowering the RDS (O → *OOH) energy barrier [203]. Moreover, previous studies have shown that the introduction of Ce can modulate the electronic structure and metal‐oxygen covalency of neighboring metal centers through Ce 4f‐O 2p‐transition metal 3d orbital coupling. For example, in Ce single‐atom‐modified CoO, the introduction of Ce negatively shifts the Co 2p3/2 binding energy by about 0.50 eV and shortens the Co‐O bond length from about 1.90 to 1.77 Å, thereby enhancing Co‐O covalency and optimizing the adsorption of oxygen‐containing intermediates [78], offering a possible orbital‐level explanation for enhanced OH− adsorption. The preferentially adsorbed OH− may also limit Cl− adsorption through competitive site occupation. The microscopic morphology and structural design of the catalyst are also crucial. A bionically designed CeO2/Ni/carbon cloth catalyst mimicking the sea anemone tentacle structure provides a high specific surface area and a superhydrophilic surface, achieving an overpotential of 220 mV at 10 mA cm−2 in 1 M KOH + 0.6 M NaCl. CeO2 induces a blue shift in the d‐band center of Ni, optimizing the adsorption energy of oxygen intermediates while enhancing Cl− repellency [201]. Conclusions and Perspectives

As shown in Figure 15, this review systematically summarizes recent research progress on CeO2‐based electrocatalysts for OER. With the rapid advancement of operando characterization techniques and the growing understanding of dynamic catalytic processes, increasing evidence demonstrates that the functionality of CeO2 extends far beyond static interfacial regulation. Originating from the highly localized yet electronically accessible Ce 4f orbitals, the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle of CeO2 enables dynamic electron storage and transfer coupled with Ovs evolution. These intrinsic properties enable CeO2 to mediate catalytic evolution across multiple length scales, ranging from atomic‐level electronic structure modulation to macroscopic structural stabilization. Through multiscale structural engineering, from hierarchical porous architectures to atomically dispersed configurations, and advanced synthetic strategies including hydrothermal growth, electrodeposition, and dealloying, CeO2 has been integrated with a broad range of non‐precious‐metal compounds (e.g., Co‐, Ni‐, and Fe‐based materials) and noble‐metal oxides (e.g., IrO2 and RuO2), constructing intimate interfaces with tailored gradient d‐p‐f electronic coupling pathways. At these interfaces, CeO2 regulates OER activity and durability through a synergistic “redox–lattice oxygen interplay,” involving dynamic charge redistribution, oxygen vacancy‐mediated lattice oxygen activation, and adaptive surface reconstruction:

First, the plentiful Ov serve dual functions: they act as active centers for the preferential adsorption and activation of water molecules or hydroxyl groups, and they induce interfacial charge redistribution, which optimizes the adsorption free energies of the OER intermediates (*OH, *O, *OOH). This may even alter the reaction pathway from the conventional AEM to the more efficient LOM.

This adsorption supplies the driving force for in situ hydroxylation/oxidation of transition metal compounds (sulfides, phosphides, etc.), thereby accelerating the generation of highly active amorphous (oxy)hydroxide phases. The ongoing Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle, together with Ov, sustains the long‑term stability of the reconstructed interface.

Ce redox and multiscale engineering in CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts.

It is worth noting that recent studies have further indicated that such multi‐level synergy endows CeO2‐based electrocatalysts with striking adaptability across distinct electrolytic environments. In aggressively acidic media, CeO2 operates as an “electron buffer layer” that pins the valence state of precious metals and suppresses their leaching, leading to a pronounced enhancement in durability. Under alkaline conditions, combining CeO2 with varied non‐noble metal components allows the catalyst to fully exploit its Ov and Ce3+/Ce4+ redox couple, thereby attaining high activity alongside stability. When deployed in highly corrosive seawater electrolytes, HSAB theory predicts that the CeO2 surface, behaving as a hard Lewis acid, preferentially binds OH− while electrostatically repelling Cl−. This creates a localized alkaline microenvironment at the electrode surface, fundamentally inhibiting the ClER and electrode corrosion, fully demonstrating its selective protection capability.

Leveraging its unique oxygen‐vacancy dynamics, interfacial electronic modulation, and capacity to trigger dynamic reconstruction, CeO2 effectively combines the roles of a structural promoter for boosting stability and a functional promoter for optimizing the electronic structure and reaction pathway. This lays a robust material groundwork and provides theoretical direction for designing more efficient, durable, and cost‑competitive next‑generation OER electrocatalytic systems than traditional precious‐metal systems, especially for large‑scale hydrogen production via water electrolysis.

Despite significant achievements in the research of CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts, their industrial application still faces numerous challenges. Future research should delve deeper into the following directions (Figure 16):

First, the static understanding of the action mechanisms of CeO2 must be broken, moving toward in‐depth dynamic mechanism analysis under operating conditions. The current understanding of the cocatalytic function of CeO2, such as how it promotes lattice oxygen participation to modulate reaction pathways, facilitates interfacial charge transfer, and stabilizes low‐valence active centers, still relies heavily on idealized models constructed by ex situ characterization and density functional theory calculations. However, under real oxygen evolution potentials and in highly alkaline or acidic corrosive media, CeO2 itself and the interface between CeO2 and the primary catalyst are far from static. Future work needs to integrate multiple in situ characterization techniques. Although there have been in situ studies based on Ce oxidation state changes and oxygen vacancy regulation, the issues related to “dynamic evolution of the working state” are still in the exploration stage. In particular, whether the lattice oxygen of CeO2 itself directly enters the OER catalytic cycle or CeO2 mainly regulates the lattice‐oxygen activity of neighboring active phases remains unresolved; selective 18O isotope labeling coupled with DEMS/OLEMS is therefore required to distinguish these possibilities. Further monitoring and analysis through the following in situ characterization methods are required. For instance, in situ XAFS can quantitatively analyze the continuous fluctuation of the Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio under applied potential, as well as the dynamic evolution of oxygen vacancy concentration and coordination unsaturation. Operando Raman/XRD and EPR can provide complementary information on active‐phase reconstruction and defect evolution. In situ environmental transmission electron microscopy (ETEM) and electrochemical liquid‐phase TEM (ELP‐TEM) can capture, at the nanometer or even atomic scale, the dynamic reconstruction of the interface between CeO2 nanoislands and active oxyhydroxide, the formation of an amorphous layer, particle migration and agglomeration, and the dissolution–redeposition cycle of components. Through these and other in situ characterizations, future work may precisely reveal the evolution laws of the true active centers and the critical conditions for reaction pathway switching.

Second, attention should be paid to designing interface structures with “self‐healing” capabilities. Under high current density and strong polarization, the surface oxidative reconstruction, cation dissolution, and lattice oxygen consumption lead to irreversible deactivation of the catalyst. Extreme environments such as acidic media and seawater make the durability bottleneck even more critical. Coupling the unique reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox, oxygen storage/release capacity, and adaptive lattice reconstruction of CeO2 provides a chemical driving force for a self‐healing interface. Existing studies have shown that CeO2 can promote oxygen species exchange between the catalyst and the electrolyte and exhibits a certain “self‐healing” behavior. However, to date, operando evidence for Ce valence cycling has not been widely observed. On this basis, it can be further speculated that its self‐healing process may originate from the dynamic cycle between Ce3+/Ce4+ and oxygen vacancies: when local oxygen deficiency or coordination unsaturation occurs at the interface during OER, CeO2 can buffer the local oxidation state by forming oxygen vacancies and Ce3+/Ce4+ transformation, while promoting the migration and exchange of oxygen‐containing species such as OH− to defect sites, allowing the damaged metal‐oxygen coordination environment to be recompensated; as the oxygen sites recover, Ce3+ is further reoxidized to Ce4+, thereby forming a reversible “defect generation—oxygen species compensation—local structure recovery” process. This dynamic process is expected to regulate the interfacial charge distribution and local oxygen chemical potential, reduce structural degradation caused by over‐oxidation, and indirectly inhibit the irreversible dissolution of active metals, thereby maintaining the long‐term stability of the OER active structure. It should be pointed out that this self‐healing is more likely to manifest as dynamic recovery of oxygen defects and local coordination structures, rather than complete regeneration of metal components that have already been extensively dissolved. To translate this proposed self‐healing behavior into durable electrodes, interface design should ensure stable contact between CeO2 and the OER active phase while preserving accessible redox sites, strong substrate adhesion, and uniform composition across large areas. Simple, low‐waste, and reproducible synthesis routes should be developed alongside catalyst layers compatible with electrode fabrication and, where applicable, membrane electrode assembly. The amount and distribution of CeO2 must also be optimized: its limited electronic conductivity may increase resistance in thick or highly loaded catalyst layers and reduce the fraction of material that participates in OER. For acidic operation, possible Ce dissolution should be quantified; where a proton‐exchange membrane is used, any migration of dissolved Ce species into the membrane or ionomer and its potential effect on ionic transport should be examined. These issues make the transition from laboratory evaluation to industrial operation particularly important. Most CeO2‐based OER catalysts are assessed on small electrodes in three‐electrode cells, whereas large‐area electrodes and complete electrolyzers operate at higher current densities for much longer periods. On scale‐up, bubble accumulation, mass‐transport limitations, ohmic losses, and local electrolyte changes may obscure the activity and stability benefits measured in the laboratory. Accordingly, the proposed self‐healing function should be verified under practically relevant current densities, catalyst loadings, temperatures, and electrolyte compositions, including start–stop or fluctuating‐load operation where relevant. Beyond overpotential and Tafel slope, full‐cell voltage, energy efficiency, Faradaic efficiency, gas production, long‐term voltage degradation, Ce and active‐metal dissolution, and post‐operation interfacial changes should be reported. Relating these results to catalyst loading, Ce utilization, and preliminary material and energy costs would clarify whether the mechanistic advantages of CeO2 can be retained in scalable electrodes and translated into durable electrolyzer operation.

Third, machine learning should be actively fused with high‐throughput automated robotic experimentation platforms to accelerate the rational design and iterative optimization of CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts. Multidimensional operando characterization will generate massive, multimodal dynamic structure–activity data, from which traditional analytical methods can scarcely extract the complex nonlinear relationships buried within. Through machine learning, one can directly learn from the data the deep mapping linking key features, such as Ce3+/Ce4+ dynamic evolution, oxygen‐vacancy migration, and interfacial restructuring to catalytic activity, selectivity and self‐healing capacity, thereby constructing stability and deactivation prediction models that operate across diverse working conditions. These models, in turn, inversely guide the design of catalyst compositions and interfacial architectures endowed with superior self‐repair ability and corrosion resistance. Concurrently, coupling with automated robotic experimentation platforms enables the efficient synthesis and screening of large candidate libraries following the predictions of the machine‐learning models, forging a closed “prediction‐synthesis‐characterization‐feedback” loop that rapidly validates and refines the models. This integration not only promises to substantially compress the development cycle from mechanistic understanding to industrially relevant process windows, but also reveals multi‐component synergy and dynamic adaptation mechanisms that tend to elude empirical intuition. It thereby furnishes an entirely new research paradigm for breaking through durability bottlenecks in complex industrial scenarios, including acidic electrolytes, high current densities, seawater, and the like.

Future perspectives of CeO2‐based OER electrocatalysts.

In summary, future breakthroughs require a deeper mechanistic understanding, innovative design concepts, substantial advances in the performance of material systems under industrial scenarios, including acidic environments, high current densities, and tolerance to complex media, as well as the integration of machine learning with automated experimentation to accelerate data‐driven catalyst discovery. This will provide crucial OER material support for the clean energy transition.
