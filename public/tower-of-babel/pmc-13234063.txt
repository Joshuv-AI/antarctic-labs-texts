MXene quantum dot polymer nanocomposites as smart platforms for targeted drug delivery and multifunctional biomedical applications: an updated review
Authors: Mohammad Hossein Karami, Omid Moini Jazani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMXenes (Ti3C2TX) and their composites exhibit exceptional electrical conductivity, flexibility, and biocompatibility, making them ideal for creating smart microrobots for targeted drug delivery, especially in cancer treatment. Their customizable surfaces enable precise navigation and targeted therapeutic release, improving efficacy and reducing side effects. They can also co-deliver multiple drugs for synergistic therapies. However, challenges in scalable production, stability, and regulation remain. Simultaneously, combining MXenes with quantum dots in polymer nanocomposites is revolutionizing multifunctional materials by merging electrical, optical, and catalytic properties. These composites enhance applications in drug delivery, tissue engineering, flexible electronics, and energy storage. Stimuli-responsive polymers allow controlled activation of embedded agents, though issues with scalability and clinical translation persist. This review underscores the potential of MXene- and quantum dot-based nanocomposites to drive innovation in biomedicine and sustainable technologies.Keywords: 2D MXenes, MXene QDs, Surface modification, Polymer composites, Drug delivery systems, Wound healing, Toxicity

Introduction

Advances in medical science, particularly in drug delivery systems, have significantly improved healthcare by enabling the effective treatment of a wide range of diseases. Despite the progress that has been achieved, there are still several challenges that hinder patients from fully benefiting from these innovations. These challenges may include issues related to the efficacy, accessibility, and safety of new drug delivery methods, which need to be addressed to enhance patient outcomes [1–3]. Drug delivery systems, particularly those utilizing nanoparticles, have shown significant effectiveness in treating a range of ailments, with cancer being a notable example. These systems enhance the physicochemical properties of pharmaceuticals, which contributes to their potential in biomedical applications. This innovative approach allows for more targeted and efficient delivery of therapeutic agents, improving treatment outcomes [4–6]. Nanoscale systems, owing to their unique physicochemical properties, improve the delivery of therapeutic agents by enhancing drug absorption and distribution. Advances in nanomedicine allow precise dosage control, minimizing side effects while maximizing therapeutic outcomes, particularly in cancer treatment. Smart two-dimensional nanosheets have enabled targeted delivery of therapeutic compounds to tumors, increasing the accuracy and effectiveness of cancer therapies [7–9]. The advanced nanosheets referenced are designed to release therapeutic agents in response to specific triggers such as temperature shifts, light exposure, or pH fluctuations. Despite their potential, a major obstacle persists: inefficient or inaccurate loading of bioactive molecules onto these nanocarriers. This limitation can undermine their targeting precision, increasing the risk of off-target interactions with healthy tissues instead of focusing on diseased cells. Overcoming this challenge is essential to improve both the therapeutic effectiveness and the safety profile of these delivery platforms [10, 11]. MXene is indeed a notable example of a two-dimensional transition metal carbide that serves as a smart nanocarrier in the field of nanomedicine. Its unique properties, such as hydrophilicity due to surface functional groups, enhanced biocompatibility, and efficient photothermal conversion, contribute to its effectiveness in drug delivery. These characteristics enable MXene to effectively transport poorly hydrophilic drugs, utilizing various polymer coatings to specifically target cancer cells. This capability positions it as a promising tool in the advancement of targeted cancer therapies [12].

MXene demonstrates significant versatility beyond drug delivery, particularly in the fields of photoacoustic imaging and photothermal therapy. This comprehensive review emphasizes its potential as an effective carrier for poorly hydrophilic drugs, showcasing its multifunctional applications in cancer treatment. Additionally, it provides critical insights into the design and therapeutic potential of MXene, reinforcing its promise in advancing cancer therapies [13–15].

In this review, we focus specifically on MXene quantum dot (QD) polymer nanocomposites as smart platforms for targeted drug delivery and multifunctional biomedical applications. While numerous studies have summarized the broad properties, synthesis methods, and diverse applications of MXenes (Ti3C2TX), this review provides a distinct perspective by systematically highlighting the unique advantages of combining QDs with polymer matrices. Such integration enhances drug loading capacity, improves cellular uptake, enables stimuli-responsive release, and augments photothermal and photoluminescent performance compared to conventional MXene or MXene QD systems alone. Additionally, we offer a quantitative and comparative evaluation framework, examining key performance metrics, including cytotoxicity, biocompatibility, therapeutic efficacy, and multifunctional capabilities, while also addressing potential limitations such as stability and synthesis complexity.

By emphasizing these novel aspects, this review delineates the rationale for focusing on MXene QD polymer nanocomposites and provides a coherent framework for their assessment in advanced biomedical applications. This study provides a focused overview of MXenes, covering their synthesis techniques and key properties, including cytotoxicity, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and stability under in vitro and in vivo conditions. Surface modification strategies aimed at enhancing MXene functionality are also discussed. Applications in biomedical fields, such as drug delivery, wound healing, and tissue engineering, are briefly highlighted. Finally, current challenges in MXene synthesis and potential future research directions are outlined to accelerate the development of this versatile two-dimensional material (Fig. 1).

Methods for synthesizing MXene particles

Top-down methods

Hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching

In the context of HF etching, the removal of A from the MAX phase occurs through a chemical reaction rather than mechanical shearing. This is due to the metallic characteristics of the M-A molecules, which make mechanical methods impractical for this process. The chemical reaction effectively facilitates the etching by selectively targeting and removing the A component from the MAX phase material [16, 17]. Chemical etching, which employs substances like HF (hydrofluoric acid) and HCl (hydrochloric acid), is viable because of the chemically active characteristics of M-A, in contrast to the more resilient M-X. The effectiveness of HF in this process is depicted in Fig. 3 [18].

Schematic representation of acid etching synthesis, involving various stages including etching and intercalation, to produce delaminated Mxene

In-situ HF etching

The new in-situ HF etching method utilizing fluoride salts such as LiF, NaF, and KF in conjunction with HCl presents a promising alternative to traditional HF etching techniques, particularly in terms of reducing environmental impacts. This method is characterized by a brief intercalation duration and the ability to perform concurrent etching, which can enhance efficiency. The established HCl/LiF etching technique, as highlighted in the study by Cockreham et al. on T3C2TxMXene using CoF2 and HCl, further supports the viability of this approach in industrial applications [19].

Titanium nanoparticles have demonstrated effectiveness in photocatalytic processes, including benzene removal, and contribute to the formation of heterojunctions that enhance reaction efficiency. These findings highlight promising directions for future research in environmental remediation and materials science [20].

Molten salt etching method

The molten salt method is an effective technique for separating MXene layers, demonstrating significant stability in Tin + 1Nn structures. This method also successfully removes nitride-based layers from the MAX phase. Developed by Gogotsi, this technique was utilized to create the initial MXene nitride, specifically Ti4N3Tx, through an etching process conducted at 550 °C for 30 min using a salt blend of KF, LiF, and NaF [21]. The method described involves redox reactions to selectively etch the A-site within MAX phases, leading to the formation of MXenes. Subsequent research has focused on improving this technique, particularly highlighting the impressive solar energy absorption capabilities of MXene nanofluids, which can achieve photothermal efficiencies of up to 91.9%. Additionally, it is noted that modifying surface terminations can enhance the conductivity and electrochemical properties of these materials [22].

Electrochemical etching

The MAX phase, when utilized as an electrode, undergoes an electrochemical process that selectively extracts layers of MXene molecules, including compounds like HCl, NaCl, and NH4Cl. This process results in the incomplete removal of titanium, while carbon from the carbides accumulates on the surface of the MXene. Consequently, this leads to the formation of functional groups such as chlorine (Cl), hydroxyl (–OH), and oxygen (–O) instead of fluorine (–F) terminations in Ti2CTx [23].

This method has proven effective in the preparation of MXenes, resulting in fluoride-free MXene with enhanced performance in electrochemical capacitors. An electrochemical etching technique has been employed to produce Cl-containing MXene in an alkaline environment, achieving an impressive efficiency of 92.2% [24]. Furthermore, a more reliable approach has been identified that utilizes diluted hydrochloric acid for the synthesis of Ti2CTx MXene on porous Ti2AlC electrodes; however, issues related to over-etching have been noted [25].

The production of lithium-ion batteries utilizing electrochemical etching has demonstrated enhanced cycling stability, which is linked to the fluorination of Ti3C2Fx. Additionally, nanocarbon structures that are produced from the etching of Ti3AlC2 in hydrofluoric acid contribute to this process. This indicates that fluorinating certain materials, along with employing hydrofluoric acid during the etching process, can substantially enhance the performance of lithium-ion batteries [26].

Bottom-up methods

MXene synthesis can be carried out using several techniques such as Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD), templating strategies, and Pulsed Laser Deposition (PLD). Generally, MXenes are fabricated via two main approaches: top-down and bottom-up. The top-down approach entails breaking down bulk precursors into individual nanosheets, often leading to smaller crystallite dimensions and the introduction of structural imperfections. Conversely, the bottom-up strategy builds MXenes atom-by-atom or molecule-by-molecule, typically resulting in superior crystalline quality and reduced defect density, making it more suitable for specific uses [27]. Variations are present in the precursors, synthesis conditions, and surface properties, with Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD) and Pulsed Laser Deposition (PLD) utilizing controlled temperatures for the fabrication of MXenes. CVD is a technique that employs high temperatures and specific chemicals (such as Cu, Mo, and CH4) within a controlled environment to produce MXenes, whereas PLD is utilized to create a Mo2C layer. CVD typically results in higher crystallinity and conductivity, while PLD enables the production of MXene crystals at lower temperatures [28]. The synthesis is progressing steadily. Template synthesis is a bottom-up technique employed to synthesize Mxene [29]. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the precursor requires the addition of salt crystals, followed by a coating process. The combination of the precursor and salt undergoes an ammoniating process (NH3-based), ultimately resulting in the production of MoN sheets after a subsequent washing process [30].

Template Mxene synthesis methods, using crystals of salts for the production of MoN sheets

In chemical vapor deposition (CVD), methane is introduced onto a Cu/Mo substrate to synthesize ultrathin Mo2C at temperatures above 1085 °C. This method yields high-quality Mo2C; however, defect-free growth strongly depends on precise control of temperature and growth time. The resulting films, typically 10–100 μm in size, are unsuitable for medical applications, where surface engineering and nanoscale dimensions are critical for effective cellular interaction and penetration [31–33]. Alternative synthesis techniques for MXenes have been explored, including template methods and plasma-enhanced pulsed laser deposition (PELPD), which successfully produced ultrathin Mo2C films using methane plasma. Scalable salt-template approaches have enabled the synthesis of 2D nitrides such as MoN; however, reports on bottom-up fabrication of biocompatible MXenes remain limited, highlighting the need for further study [34]. Both top-down and bottom-up methods require reliable and safe strategies for large-scale production. Toxicity and biocompatibility of MXenes

Toxicity and biocompatibility are critical parameters in evaluating the biosafety of MXenes for biomedical applications. Although these terms are frequently used interchangeably, they describe distinct biological concepts. Toxicity refers to the ability of a material to induce adverse effects at the cellular, tissue, or organismal level, whereas biocompatibility denotes the absence of such harmful responses under specific application conditions [36]. Recent reviews and experimental studies published between 2024 and 2025 emphasize that this distinction is essential when interpreting MXene biosafety data, as materials exhibiting low short-term toxicity may still pose long-term biocompatibility risks. Williams defined biocompatibility as the ability of a material to elicit an appropriate host response in a particular application, highlighting the dynamic interplay between material properties and the biological environment [37]. In line with this definition, recent 2024–2025 studies demonstrate that the biological response to MXenes is strongly dependent on physicochemical parameters such as lateral size, thickness, surface terminations, oxidation state, and synthesis methodology rather than the MXene family alone. Accumulating experimental evidence indicates that the sharp edges of MXene nanosheets can mechanically disrupt cellular membranes, leading to cytoplasmic leakage and loss of membrane integrity [38]. Furthermore, recent in vitro investigations (2024–2025) report that smaller MXene flakes are more readily internalized via endocytosis, where they may interfere with intracellular organelles and metabolic pathways, thereby amplifying cytotoxic effects [39]. Beyond physical damage, MXenes have been increasingly shown to induce oxidative stress. Multiple 2024–2025 studies identify reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation as a dominant mechanism of MXene-induced cytotoxicity, particularly under near-infrared irradiation, which enhances their photothermal activity. This effect is further intensified by the presence of variable-valence transition metals that facilitate Fenton-like reactions, leading to elevated ROS levels and subsequent cell death [40]. The influence of synthesis and processing routes on toxicity has also been strongly supported by recent literature. Pandey et al. reported that Nb2C and Nb4C3 MXenes significantly reduce intracellular glutathione levels in bacteria, directly linking their antimicrobial toxicity to oxidative stress pathways [41]. Importantly, follow-up studies published in 2024–2025 confirm that the magnitude of this effect is highly dependent on the exfoliation and purification procedures. Conventional hydrofluoric acid–based etching remains a major safety concern, as even trace residues of unreacted HF can induce severe cytotoxicity. Consequently, recent reports stress the necessity of rigorous washing and post-synthesis treatment to ensure biosafety [42].

In response to these challenges, fluorine-free etching strategies and bottom-up synthesis approaches, such as chemical vapor deposition, have gained increasing attention in 2024–2025 studies due to their improved control over surface chemistry and reduced toxicological risks. Moreover, comparative analyses indicate that MXene composition plays a decisive role in biological responses; for example, Ti3C2 has been consistently reported to exhibit higher cytotoxicity than Ti2C, a trend attributed to differences in surface reactivity and oxidation behavior [43]. Recent systematic studies reinforce that such compositional effects must be considered when designing MXenes for biomedical use [44].

Overall, evidence from recent 2024–2025 literature confirms that MXene toxicity is not an intrinsic material property but a multifactorial outcome governed by composition, synthesis route, surface functionalization, and exposure conditions. Therefore, future research should prioritize standardized toxicity assessment protocols, long-term in vivo evaluations, and cross-comparative studies among different MXene families. Such evidence-based approaches are essential to accurately define the biocompatibility limits of MXenes and to support their safe and responsible translation into biomedical and clinical applications [45].

Biodegradability of MXenes

Biodegradation refers to the gradual breakdown of substances through specific biological processes. Despite advancements that enhance the stability of MXenes in biological environments, they still undergo degradation [44]. MXene-PEG, which possesses a defect structure, exhibits deterioration over time when exposed to various solutions such as H2O, PBS, and RPMI medium at a temperature of 37 °C. Additionally, it is noted that MXenes, including MXene-PEG, are typically administered either orally or intravenously, allowing them to circulate through the bloodstream and reach different organs [45]. MXene cellular uptake occurs primarily via endocytosis, including caveolae/lipid-mediated pathways and macropinocytosis, as demonstrated using inhibitors such as amiloride and nystatin. The localization of MXenes within endocytic vesicles has been confirmed using RB-labeled V2C. Biodistribution studies show that MXenes derived from early transition metals mainly accumulate in the liver, with lower levels detected in the spleen, kidneys, lungs, and heart. These concentrations decrease over time, accompanied by increased excretion through feces and urine [46].

The half-life of altered MXenes in plasma varies significantly between different types. For example, Nb2C-PVP has a half-life of 3.8 h, whereas GdW10@ MXene has a much shorter half-life of 49.5 min. The biodegradation of MXenes primarily occurs in the gastrointestinal tract, with the liver playing a critical role in their metabolic elimination. Kupffer cells, which are macrophages located in the liver, are instrumental in the phagocytosis and subsequent lysosomal breakdown of nanomaterials larger than 200 nm. Additionally, Nb2C-PVP MXene can be degraded by human myeloperoxidase in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), an enzyme predominantly found in neutrophils [47–49]. Hydrogen peroxide–driven myeloperoxidase activity in Kupffer cells may enable lysosomal biodegradation of MXenes in vivo. MXenes also accumulate in clearance organs such as the spleen, where particles larger than ~ 100–200 nm are excluded by splenic sinus endothelial gaps, influencing biodistribution and clearance [51]. MXene stability

By 2021, the theme of “stability” became central in MXenes research, highlighting a growing focus among scholars on this aspect. The chemical stability of MXenes is influenced by various factors, and degradation occurs gradually at room temperature when MXenes are exposed to light, water, and oxygen. This exposure can lead to a decline in their structural integrity and unique properties, making their vulnerability notable when compared to more stable compounds [52]. For example, maintaining MXene in water-based colloidal solutions at room temperature in open vials results in complete structural degradation within 20 days. After one week of exposure, oxidation begins at the edges of the nanosheet, leading to the formation of “branches” of anatase (TiO2) and transforming MXene into aggregates composed of anatase and disordered carbon, which ultimately results in total structural failure [53]. It is believed that exposure to light accelerates the degradation process by promoting chemical reactions; however, the stability of MXenes can be improved through optimized storage conditions, thereby extending their lifespan. The key factors that affect their longevity include ambient temperature, light exposure, availability of oxygen and water, pH level, and solution concentration [54]. Zhang C. and colleagues showed that maintaining MXene at 5 °C in airtight vials filled with argon, which removes oxygen, greatly improves its stability and prolongs its shelf life to two years. In comparison, MXene stored in open vials experienced degradation rates of 42%, 85%, and 100% after 5, 10, and 15 days, respectively. These results suggest that lower storage temperatures significantly reduce degradation, consistent with the principles described in the Arrhenius equation [55]. MXenes can undergo various modifications with stabilizing agents, which enhance their stability in aqueous and biological environments while also improving their biocompatibility. Well-known antioxidants, including ascorbic acid, sodium ascorbate, citric acid, and tartaric acid, form chelates with the surfaces of titanium atoms, creating a redox barrier that protects against oxidation. However, some compounds, such as malic acid and 2,4-hydroxyphenyl ethanol, have been shown to be ineffective, while others, like 4-hydroxyphenyl, demonstrate positive effects [56]. Invitro toxic effects of MXenes

Concerns regarding the toxicity of MXenes extend beyond their synthesis routes to include their prospective medical applications. Consistent with current nanotoxicology practices, preliminary safety evaluations of engineered nanomaterials are predominantly conducted using in vitro cell-based assays. Among the MXene family, MXene nanosheets have been the most extensively investigated, providing a foundational understanding of MXene–cell interactions [57]. Recent studies published in 2024–2025 increasingly emphasize that such in vitro assessments, while informative, must be interpreted in a dose-, time-, and cell-type–dependent manner. Zhang et al. reported that MXene nanosheets at concentrations ranging from 100 to 500 μg/mL did not induce acute cytotoxicity in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). However, metabolomic analyses revealed significant alterations in cellular metabolism at higher doses, including enhanced glycolysis and lipid accumulation, despite unchanged cell viability [58]. These findings, corroborated by more recent 2024–2025 studies, suggest that conventional viability assays alone may underestimate sublethal metabolic stress induced by MXenes. In contrast, Gu et al. demonstrated that Ti3C2 quantum dots (QDs) exhibited markedly higher cytotoxicity than Nb2C QDs in HUVECs. Although Ti3C2 QDs maintained over 70% cell viability at 25 μg/mL, a dose-dependent decline in viability was observed at higher concentrations, whereas Nb2C QDs showed minimal cytotoxic effects across the tested range [59]. This comparative evidence highlights the critical role of MXene structure, composition, and dimensionality in governing biological responses—a conclusion that aligns with recent 2024–2025 literature emphasizing structure–toxicity relationships in 2D nanomaterials. Cell-type–specific toxicity has also been reported. MXene nanosheets exhibited pronounced cytotoxic effects on human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) at concentrations exceeding 50 μg/mL, with severe inhibition of cell proliferation observed at 100 μg/mL. Similarly, primary neural stem cells (NSCs) displayed dose-dependent sensitivity, with lower concentrations being relatively well tolerated, while significant cytotoxicity emerged at 25 μg/mL [60]. These observations reinforce recent findings (2024–2025) that stem cells are particularly sensitive to nanomaterial-induced stress due to their high metabolic activity and limited antioxidative capacity.

Importantly, MXenes demonstrate selective cytotoxicity, exhibiting stronger inhibitory effects on cancer cells compared to normal cells [61]. This phenomenon has attracted increasing attention in recent oncological nanomedicine studies, as it suggests potential therapeutic windows for anticancer applications. Scheibe et al. confirmed that various MXene morphologies induce elevated cytotoxicity in HeLa cells. While oxidative stress is commonly implicated in the toxicity of two-dimensional materials, their work revealed that MXenes generate higher levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) than their MAX phase precursors. Notably, this enhanced toxicity was attributed not only to ROS generation but also to direct physicochemical interactions with cellular membranes, underscoring the multifaceted nature of MXene-induced cytotoxicity [62]. Overall, evidence from both earlier and recent 2024–2025 studies indicates that MXene toxicity is highly context-dependent, governed by material structure, dose, exposure duration, and cell phenotype. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating metabolic, oxidative, and membrane-interaction analyses into future toxicity assessments to support the safe design and biomedical translation of MXene-based materials. Toxic effects of MXenes in vivo

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) have emerged as a widely accepted in vivo model for nanotoxicity assessment due to their high genetic homology with humans, small size, rapid development, and optical transparency during early life stages [64]. These characteristics enable sensitive evaluation of developmental, neurological, and systemic toxicity. In a study by Nasrallah et al., MXene nanosheets were evaluated in zebrafish embryos, revealing no mortality at 50 μg/mL, whereas exposure to 100 μg/mL resulted in a 21% mortality rate. The calculated LC50 after 96 h was 257.46 μg/mL [65]. At lower concentrations, embryos exhibited normal locomotor behavior and preserved neuronal and muscular function, leading to the classification of MXene as “essentially nontoxic” under these conditions. Recent 2024–2025 in vivo nanotoxicology studies emphasize that such dose-dependent responses highlight the importance of defining safe exposure thresholds rather than relying on binary toxic/nontoxic classifications[66].

Contrasting results have been reported in other embryonic models. Alhussain et al. investigated MXene exposure in chicken embryos and observed a markedly higher mortality rate of 46% at a dose of 30 μg per embryo, accompanied by significant inhibition of angiogenesis. This effect was attributed to the downregulation of genes critical for vascular development [65]. The discrepancy between zebrafish and avian models underscores findings from recent 2024–2025 literature, which stress that species-specific developmental pathways and exposure routes can substantially influence nanomaterial toxicity outcomes. Rodent studies provide additional insight into systemic toxicity. In vivo evaluation of Nb2C Tx nanosheets in Kunming mice revealed no significant alterations in hematological or biochemical parameters, indicating minimal inflammatory responses. Histopathological analysis further confirmed the absence of notable tissue damage across major organs [66]. These results suggest that certain MXene compositions may exhibit favorable biosafety profiles in mammalian systems. However, other investigations have reported organ-specific accumulation. For instance, MXene MXene films demonstrated promising performance in targeted bone regeneration with low inflammatory responses, yet biodistribution studies in ICR mice revealed preferential accumulation in the lungs and liver [67]. This observation aligns with recent 2024–2025 reports identifying the reticuloendothelial system as a primary site for nanomaterial sequestration, raising concerns regarding long-term pulmonary and hepatic effects. Beyond animal models, environmental and ecological toxicity has also been documented. Studies on Ti3AlC2 nanosheets demonstrated that concentrations of 100 and 1000 μg/mL significantly inhibited plant seedling growth through ROS-mediated oxidative stress. At 100 μg/mL, stomatal opening increased to 78.6% and trichome density doubled, effects attributed to the release of titanium ions. At higher concentrations, severe abiotic stress was observed, including membrane damage, reduced biomass, and decreased chlorophyll content, ultimately impairing plant growth [68]. These findings, increasingly discussed in 2024–2025 environmental nanotoxicology studies, highlight the broader ecological implications of MXene release [69]. Despite these insights, current in vivo toxicity assessments of MXenes remain limited. Only a small number of studies systematically investigate long-term exposure, environmentally relevant doses, or realistic administration routes. As summarized in Table 3, the existing literature provides fragmented and model-dependent evidence, restricting comprehensive risk evaluation [69]. Recent studies suggest that Ti3AlC2 nanosheets may pose potential in vivo toxicity risks, raising concerns regarding both their biomedical applicability and environmental safety in agricultural contexts [70].

Overall, evidence from recent 2024–2025 studies indicates that MXene in vivo toxicity is highly dependent on material composition, dosage, exposure duration, and biological model. These findings underscore the urgent need for standardized long-term in vivo studies, integrated biodistribution analyses, and environmentally relevant exposure assessments to ensure the safe and responsible development of MXene-based technologies. Properties

Structural properties

Once MXene was obtained from the MAX phase, the resulting substance exhibited surface groups including F, O, and OH. MXenes terminated with O and OH species are regarded as more stable, since F is typically replaced by OH when MXene comes into contact with water. M. Pogoreilov et al. presented a diagram of M3X2 MXene, derived from its M3AX2 precursor, highlighting the removal of a layer from titanium carbide (fcc lattice) along the (111) plane. The etching process removes the A-layer, leaving dynamic M atoms on the surface and X atoms positioned internally [70].

The produced MXene possesses a hexagonally close-packed (HCP) configuration, with stacking arrangements determined by the atomic sequence of metals, represented by the n values. Experimental fabrication has generated 100 stoichiometric structures, encompassing 20 distinct configurations, such as Ti2N, Ti3C2, Nb4C3, V4C3, and Nb2C. Metal adsorption and thermal processing can modify surface groups, making their configurations more complex depending on the MXene's fundamental material and type [71].

Electrical properties

MXenes exhibit hydrophilic properties and high electrical conductivity, which can enhance the performance of polymer-based nanocomposites for targeted drug delivery. These properties can be optimized through control of defects, surface terminations, and flake size [72].

As the temperature rises from 77 to 300 K, the resistance decreases, exhibiting a behavior also seen in Ti3CNTx MXene. Fluctuations in resistance are associated with inherent characteristics, surface terminations, and defects introduced during processing. MXenes, including those based on graphene oxide, exhibit high electrical conductivity, which can be tailored to enhance the performance of polymer nanocomposites for targeted drug delivery [73].

Mechanical properties

MXene exhibits exceptional mechanical properties, making it ideal for various applications. Its remarkable physicochemical characteristics—including stability, scalability, and durability—along with a multilayered structure similar to that of 2D graphene, make it suitable for use in supercapacitors and lithium-ion batteries [74].

The tensile strength of MXene ranges from 81.71 to 561.4 N/m, exceeding that of many other two-dimensional materials. Recent research indicates that the variety of MXene compositions is limited; however, many options remain uninvestigated [75].

The mechanical properties of MXenes depend on specific combinations of transition metals and X elements, but research on this topic is limited [76]. Understanding three-dimensional binary transition metal nitrides and carbides could further deepen our knowledge of MXenes. Importantly, nitrogen and carbon exist in the interstitial spaces, forming an M–X rock salt structure, where the bonding exhibits covalent, ionic, and metallic characteristics among M, nitrogen, and carbon [77]. Recent research has demonstrated advanced MXene composites, including one developed by Y. Xue et al., which incorporates phenyl phosphonic diaminohexane within the MXene layers, leading to enhanced stiffness and remarkable thermal stability. A separate study conducted by M. Wu and colleagues showed that adding MXene to polyacrylamide organohydrogels significantly improved their mechanical properties across different temperatures [78].

Electrochemical properties

Ti3C2 MXene shows great potential for energy storage in electrochemical systems and batteries, but its performance is limited by restacking, which hinders ion transport and reduces electrolyte access to active sites. Incorporating metal ions (Li⁺, K⁺, Na⁺, Mg2⁺, NH4⁺) and polar carbon-based compounds into the MXene layers can help overcome this issue.

MXene electrochemical performance is strongly influenced by surface functional groups and flake size. Using sonication and density gradient techniques, MXene flakes ranging from 0.1 to 5 μm were produced. Flakes around 1 μm exhibited an optimal capacitance of 290 F g⁻1 at 2 mV s⁻1, highlighting their potential for electrochemical actuator applications [79].

Bioactive properties

The beneficial aspect ratio of MXenes enhances their antimicrobial properties and biocompatibility. MXene (Ti3C2) doped with polyvinyl fluoride exhibits stronger antimicrobial effects against E. coli than Ti2C. Oxidation generally reduces the effectiveness of 2D nanomaterials against bacteria; however, MXenes demonstrate robustness and retain their antimicrobial properties. J. Yoon and colleagues documented a biocompatible, oxidation-resistant MXene featuring a halogen-free surface termination, created through traditional hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching. Their research highlighted the harmful effects associated with traditional methods [80].

HF- MXene flakes produced through NaOH etching exhibit a significant red shift, high levels of –O and –OH terminations, improved biocompatibility, and minimal cytotoxicity, making them promising for biomedical applications [81].

Thermophysical properties of MXene compounds

The thermal stability of MXenes significantly influences their prolonged lifespan. Thermal stability is affected by factors such as chemical composition, terminal groups, and environmental conditions. Evaluating thermal stability involves examining degradation behavior, chemical reactivity in different states (gaseous, liquid, solid), and self-crosslinking mechanisms [82]. The decomposition characteristics, particularly during heat treatment under vacuum, provide insights into the thermal stability of different MXene type s[83].

Initial degradation occurs in two stages: the detachment of surface terminal groups, followed by a phase transition triggered by the desorption of water, both of which influence charge transfer [84].

The stability of Ti3C2TX flakes under electron-beam irradiation depends on surface stability influenced by terminal groups and reveals the presence of Ti vacancies. Heating to 500 °C results in the formation of monolayered Ti3C2 flakes, while higher temperatures, such as 1000 °C, significantly enhance the development of h-TiC, producing larger regions of Ti5C4 and Ti4C3 [85].

Mo2C exhibits a thermal conductivity of 48.4 W/m·K at room temperature, which can be enhanced by increasing the temperature or through doping. Ti3C2 MXenes remain stable up to 800 °C under inert conditions but oxidize in oxygen-rich environments [86]. Additionally, studies on MXene-CNT membranes reveal the possibility of forming innovative chemical bonds and self-crosslinking mechanisms, resulting in efficient ion rejection and minimized swelling. These studies highlight the diverse characteristics of MXenes shaped by thermal factors and chemical interactions, which are essential for their durability in various applications [87].

Magnetic properties

MXenes, a family of two-dimensional multilayer materials, exhibit diverse magnetic properties that depend on their composition, structural configuration, and surface functionalization. While most MXenes are non-magnetic due to strong M–X and M–T bonds, certain types demonstrate magnetic order, including ferromagnetism and antiferromagnetism [88]. Nitrogen-containing MXenes often show significant magnetic moments from partially filled d-orbitals, whereas other MXenes display varied magnetic behaviors depending on functionalization and oxygen content. These materials also show tunable magnetic properties under mechanical strain, with some MXenes increasing their magnetic moments while others experience reductions [89].

Surface terminations play a crucial role in modulating spin polarization and controlling transitions between magnetic phases, influencing both the ground state and overall magnetic response. Studies on a range of MXenes, including Cr-, Mn-, Ti-, and V-based compounds, reveal that functionalization can induce half-metallic ferromagnetism, metal-to-insulator transitions, and variations in magnetic anisotropy. The Curie temperature of MXenes varies widely and is strongly dependent on the chemical composition and surface modifications, demonstrating their potential for high-temperature magnetic applications [90].

The interplay between structure, composition, and surface chemistry allows MXenes to exhibit complex and tunable magnetic behaviors, which are important for designing advanced nanocomposites. This tunability, combined with their hydrophilic nature and high electrical conductivity, makes MXenes attractive candidates for functional polymer nanocomposites in biomedical applications, including targeted drug delivery [91]. Overall, understanding and controlling the magnetic properties of MXenes is essential for exploiting their full potential in multifunctional nanomaterials, where magnetic behavior can complement electronic, optical, and biocompatibility features in practical applications [92].

Optical properties of MXenes

MXenes exhibit remarkable optical properties, including tunable absorption, reflectivity, and infrared response, which depend on their composition, surface terminations, and structural features [93–95]. Modifications such as strain, doping, or Janus-type engineering further enhance these properties. Such tunability makes MXenes highly adaptable for diverse applications, including polymer-based nanocomposites for biomedical purposes, where their optical characteristics can support imaging, sensing, or targeted drug delivery, highlighting their multifunctional potential in advanced nanomaterial designs [96–101].

Methods of surface functionalization

Controlling surface terminations

Modifying the surface of MXenes requires altering their termination (T) groups, which in turn adjusts properties such as concentration, electrochemically active sites, and electronic configurations [102–106]. Surface terminations like fluorine and oxygen can be introduced using fluoride-containing salts [107]. By controlling etching concentration and duration, the types of surface terminations can be tailored. One study investigated how different surface terminations impact physical properties, revealing that the presence of Ti–O bonds promotes favorable electronic scattering [108]. Heating the material to 380 °C under vacuum increased the work function from 3.9 to 4.8 eV, attributed to the removal of –OH groups and impurities. However, further heating between 500 °C and 750 °C reduced the work function due to fluorine release [109]. Density Functional Theory (DFT) analyses showed that surface terminations influence the density of states at the Fermi level, with a decrease in –F concentration at higher annealing temperatures. Additionally, Ti–C vacancies affect magnetization, underscoring the impact of structural changes on magnetic properties [110].

Small molecules

Modifying MXene surfaces with small molecules such as C12E6 improves their mechanical stability, electrical performance, and solution stability. Xia et al. demonstrated enhanced molecular interactions via hydrogen bonding, confirmed by the appearance of a fan-shaped texture in microscopy images [111]. Lei et al. synthesized sulfonic acid-functionalized MXene, Ti3C2–SO3H, through a one-step reaction with diazonium salt; SEM images revealed granular formations, contrasting sharply with the layered lamellar structure of unmodified Ti3C2 [112]. Jin et al. employed a basic solvothermal/hydrothermal method to functionalize Nb2C MXenes, enhancing their electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorption by increasing functional group density and interlayer spacing, which promotes multiple reflections and improved absorption [113]. Other small molecules, including bismuth ferrite and sodium aluminum hydride, have also been used for MXene functionalization. Wang et al. introduced amino groups to MXenes to enable biomolecule immobilization and addressed large-scale delamination by applying diazonium chemistry to disrupt M–X bonds, thereby facilitating cellulose cross-linking [114].

Surface-initiated polymerization

MXenes, featuring multiple active surface groups, facilitate both ex situ mixing and in situ polymerization, thereby enhancing MXene/polymer composites through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. Sun et al. fabricated conductive MXene@polystyrene nanocomposites via electrostatic assembly followed by compression molding [115]. The surface functional groups on MXenes enable their assembly on polystyrene (PS) through van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions. Ling et al. developed Ti3C2/PVA and Ti3C2/PDDA composites utilizing these interactions [116, 117]. Covalent bonding between MXenes and polymers is achieved through in situ polymerization of monomers such as ε-caprolactone and pyrrole, as demonstrated by Boota et al. In PPy/Ti3C2 composites, hydrogen bonds form between Ti3C2’s –O groups and pyrrole’s hydrogen and nitrogen atoms, while soybean phospholipid improves Ti3C2’s biocompatibility and functionality [118]. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) helps maintain solution stability, whereas polydopamine (PDA) enhances Ti3C2’s capacitive performance when combined with carbon nanotubes (CNTs) through various covalent and non-covalent interactions. Surface-initiated polymerization methods have been explored using V2C and PDMAEMA, employing –OH initiators and peroxide approaches to study polymerization within MXenes [119]. Functionalization of MXenes with macromolecules significantly enhances their properties. Silane compounds bearing functional groups form strong covalent bonds between organic and inorganic materials through silanol groups [120].

For instance, Ti3C2 has been functionalized with AEAPTMS, which bonds to –OH groups, enabling reactions with epoxy resin curing agents and supporting surface-initiated polymerization [30]. These improved MXene composites have broad applications in energy storage, biosensors, fuel cells, catalysis, and drug delivery systems [121].

Single heteroatom method

MXenes encounter electrochemical challenges in Na-ion and Li-ion batteries; however, increasing their interlayer spacing through single heteroatom doping significantly enhances their performance. For example, N-doped Nb2C MXene was synthesized via a hydrothermal method, maintaining its layered structure and uniform distribution of C, O, and Nb. Similarly, heat treatment with triphenylphosphine produced p-doped V2C with an expanded interlayer spacing of 1.03 nm [122]. Additionally, varying synthesis temperatures enabled the production of N-doped V2C with different nitrogen concentrations, achieving uniform elemental distribution. Further advancements include cation doping of Ti3C2 and doping of boron, germanium, and silicon in various MXenes, all contributing to improved electrochemical properties [123].

Effect of surface functinalize on properties

MXenes display diverse electronic behaviors that can shift from metallic to semiconducting depending on their surface functional groups. For example, MXenes terminated with fluorine or oxygen typically exhibit semiconducting properties, whereas their unmodified counterparts maintain metallic behavior. Similarly, magnetic properties are influenced by surface modifications; some alterations can convert ferromagnetic MXenes into antiferromagnetic semiconductors [124]. While certain modifications reduce magnetic responses, oxygen-containing groups often enhance ferromagnetism, resulting in half-metallic behavior.

Surface terminations also significantly affect the mechanical properties of MXenes. Variants terminated with oxygen demonstrate higher stiffness compared to those with hydroxyl or fluorine groups [125]. Moreover, incorporating carbon nanotubes and polymers markedly improves tensile strength, toughness, and flexibility [125].

Optical characteristics are similarly impacted by functionalization. Pristine Ti3C2 shows high transmittance across the visible spectrum, but Ti2C functionalized with oxygen, hydroxyl, and fluorine groups exhibits a notable reduction in transmittance, affecting absorption and reflectivity in both ultraviolet and visible ranges [126].

Finally, the hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity of MXenes depends on their surface groups. Hydrophilic terminations may lead to degradation in the presence of water and oxygen, but surface treatments can convert MXenes into hydrophobic forms, thereby enhancing their stability and resistance to degradation. MXene in drug delivery systems

This section critically reviews the recent advances in MXene-based drug delivery systems, emphasizing both opportunities and challenges. Recent studies demonstrate engineered MXene nanosheets with enhanced cellular internalization and stimuli-responsive drug release, such as spikey biodegradable MXene platforms that improve drug loading and targeted release in cancer therapy [133]. These innovations underscore the potential of MXene structures for precision drug delivery, particularly when combined with responsive coatings to ensure site-specific release. Smart MXene-based microrobots integrating targeted delivery with synergistic therapies exemplify a cutting-edge application that allows external control and real-time monitoring of therapeutic outcomes [134]. However, variation in MXene composition, surface functionalization, stability in physiological environments, and comprehensive in vivo toxicity evaluations remain limitations that need systematic investigation before clinical translation. Overall, MXene materials continue to show promise for sophisticated targeted drug delivery, but further mechanistic studies and long-term safety data are necessary [135].

Xu et al. developed TPDMGP, an innovative cancer therapy that combines photothermal treatment using MXene nanosheets with drug delivery via mesoporous silica nanosheets. This system enhances photothermal therapy by converting hydrogen peroxide into oxygen and releasing phloretin through intracellular decomposition. The low-temperature approach aims to improve treatment efficacy while minimizing damage to healthy tissues and inflammation, offering new strategies for cancer therapy [136].

Cheng et al. designed an intelligent fabric for wound care that features thermoresponsive drug release and temperature monitoring. The fabric integrates a drug-loaded nanofiber film, a heating element, and a flexible printed circuit board (FPCB) control chip to precisely regulate temperature and accelerate healing [125].

Wubin Bai’s research focuses on MXene-coated microneedles for controlled drug delivery, improving therapeutic efficacy and reducing side effects. Leveraging the conductive properties of MXenes, these microneedles enable electrically controlled drug release and multidrug administration, advancing bioelectronic applications and wound healing technologies [125].

Zhou et al. developed a novel MXene/Gellan gum hydrogel for tumor therapy activated by near-infrared light. This hydrogel improves photothermal efficiency, stability, drug release control, and biocompatibility, providing targeted cancer treatment [126].

Chen et al. developed a near-infrared (NIR) responsive hydrogel containing dexamethasone (Dex) that releases the drug at 42 °C upon NIR irradiation. This biocompatible system enhances bone regeneration by promoting anti-apoptosis and osteogenic differentiation, offering a targeted and noninvasive strategy for osteogenesis [126].

In another study, researchers designed a locally injectable therapeutic system by integrating 2D MXene nanomaterials (PEG-MXene) with immunoadjuvants R837 and cisplatin (DDP) within a hydrogel matrix. The resulting PEG-MXene@DDP@R837 hydrogel exhibited excellent photothermal performance, stability, and biocompatibility, showing promising antitumor efficacy in a lung cancer model [126].

Chemodynamic therapy (CDT) uses transition metal ions to generate hydroxyl radicals for targeting cancer cells, but achieving optimal efficacy remains challenging. A novel drug delivery system called DMTP combines doxorubicin with hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide nanoparticles and MXene quantum dots to enhance both treatment and MRI imaging. DMTP reduces glutathione (GSH) levels, releases ions for CDT and MRI, and boosts the effectiveness of photothermal therapy (PTT), resulting in inhibited tumor growth and improved tumor visualization [127].

Soo et al. developed titanium carbide-based MXene quantum dots (FHMQDs) for breast cancer treatment, showcasing both theranostic and phototherapeutic properties. These 3 nm quantum dots efficiently encapsulate and rapidly release doxorubicin in acidic environments, selectively targeting cancer cells. They induce cytotoxic effects and generate reactive oxygen species, offering promising applications in theranostics and bioimaging [128].

Researchers have developed an advanced drug delivery system that combines MXene nanosheets with agarose to efficiently carry the anticancer drug doxorubicin (DOX) and control its release using 808-nm near-infrared (NIR) light (Fig. 7). The process begins by etching raw MXene flakes to remove the aluminum layer, producing MXene nanosheets. These nanosheets are then mixed with a heated, low-melting-point agarose solution and DOX, forming a drug-loaded MXene/agarose hydrogel (referred to as DOX-loaded MXene@Hydrogel) upon cooling [129].

MXene/agarose hydrogel (designated as DOX-loaded MXene@Hydrogel)

Incorporating MXene into the agarose hydrogel greatly enhances its photothermal conversion efficiency, allowing the MXene@Hydrogel to reach temperatures of 60 °C within 5 min under NIR irradiation. Notably, the MXene nanosheets exhibit excellent photothermal stability when embedded in the hydrogel, enabling precise, on-demand drug release through controlled on/off NIR light exposure. With excellent biocompatibility, the MXene@Hydrogel holds strong promise for targeted and smart drug delivery applications in cancer therapy [130].

Zhong et al. explored the combination of traditional Chinese medicine and nanotechnology by utilizing Sanguinarine (SAN) to treat wound infections. They developed the SAN@AHEP@Ta4C3 antibacterial compound, which enables pH- and near-infrared (NIR) light-controlled drug release, thereby promoting enhanced wound healing [131].

Sun et al. highlighted the need for improved bone repair materials following tumor resections and introduced multilayered MXene/nano-hydroxyapatite composites. These composites provide superior drug delivery, photothermal performance, and biocompatibility, supporting effective bone regeneration [132].

Breast cancer remains a global health challenge requiring personalized treatments to prevent recurrence. Photothermal therapy, which uses spectral radiation to induce apoptosis, shows promise in this regard. Our research demonstrated that combining MXene, a two-dimensional material, with UV treatment enhanced cytotoxicity and apoptosis in MCF-7 breast cancer cells, indicating a potential therapeutic strategy [133].

Bone defects resulting from tumor excision demand materials capable of chemotherapy delivery, photothermal therapy, and tissue regeneration. One study introduced multilayered MXene/nano-hydroxyapatite composites synthesized via a straightforward hydrothermal process. These composites exhibit high drug-loading capacity, sustained drug release, strong affinity for doxorubicin, efficient photothermal performance, stability, and biocompatibility, making them excellent candidates for bone defect repair and regeneration [134, 135].

Wang et al. highlight the challenge of integrating biocompatibility, degradability, therapeutic delivery, and imaging capabilities into microrobots for clinical use. They developed Ti3C2 MXene-based microrobots (MXBOTs) featuring electrostatically assembled layers of Ti3C2 and Fe3O4 on biodegradable helical microstructures. These MXBOTs can navigate magnetic fields, perform photoacoustic imaging, transport fluorescent molecules, and deliver chemotherapy drugs in response to changes in temperature and pH [136].

Conventional tumor treatments, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, face several limitations such as significant side effects, poor tumor targeting, and slow therapeutic response. In contrast, photothermal therapy (PTT) offers distinct advantages, including deep tissue penetration and precise tumor targeting while sparing surrounding healthy cells. Thermal imaging technology can monitor temperature changes induced by photothermal agents (PTAs) during PTT, enabling real-time treatment tracking (Fig. 8) [137].

Photothermal therapy offers targeted, effective, antibiotic-free treatment

MXene has emerged as an exceptionally effective PTA due to its strong near-infrared (NIR) light absorption and efficient conversion of this energy into heat. This capability allows MXene to rapidly increase the local temperature under NIR irradiation, making it a critical component in PTT applications. Beyond cancer treatment, PTT is also valuable for combating bacterial infections. With the rise of antibiotic resistance diminishing the effectiveness of traditional antibiotics, PTT offers a promising, antibiotic-free alternative. It combines biocompatibility, deep tissue penetration, and effective antimicrobial action without contributing to resistance development [138–141].

WoundHealing, antibacterial action and bioimaging

MXene-based materials, including hydrogels and composites, show promise in wound healing due to their antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-regenerative properties. Recent studies (2025) highlight multifunctional systems such as MXene –silver hydrogels and MXene-integrated microneedles that enhance healing in infected or diabetic wounds. While results are promising, optimization of composition and thorough in vivo safety evaluations are still needed for clinical translation.

Nanoparticles are increasingly explored for biomedical applications, including wound healing. A ZnO–MXene–Ag nanocomposite demonstrated strong antimicrobial activity and, when incorporated into a gel at 1 mg/mL, promoted superior tissue regeneration and reduced inflammation compared to higher concentrations. This highlights the importance of optimizing nanoparticle dosage to maximize therapeutic effects while minimizing cytotoxicity. Overall, the nanocomposite-loaded gel shows promise for advanced wound healing and antimicrobial treatments [147, 149, 150, 168].

Both in vitro and animal studies demonstrated enhanced cell migration, increased collagen deposition, and improved angiogenesis. Wang et al. developed the MN-PGA-MXene-GOx microneedle patch for diabetic wounds, encapsulating glucose oxidase-loaded MXene in γ-PGA. Medical imaging plays a crucial role in cancer diagnosis and treatment, particularly with advanced therapies such as photothermal therapy (PTT), by providing precise tissue analysis through methods like MRI and CT. MRI employs non-ionizing electromagnetic waves and excels at visualizing soft tissues, making it ideal for detecting tumors in organs such as the brain and liver [201]. In contrast, CT scans deliver highly detailed, high-resolution images of anatomical structures, enabling accurate tumor localization and aiding surgical planning [202]. Photoacoustic (PA) imaging is an innovative technique that uses near-infrared (NIR) radiation to achieve deep tissue penetration and real-time monitoring of organ vibrations during therapy [203]. This is especially valuable for tracking the thermal effects of PTT, ensuring treatment is applied accurately and efficiently. Combining imaging-guided PTT with chemotherapy presents a powerful approach to cancer treatment. These imaging modalities enable immediate observation and adjustment during therapy, allowing clinicians to optimize treatment efficacy while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues [204].

Medical imaging, particularly MRI, provides detailed information on tumor size, location, and treatment response, supporting the optimization of combined photothermal therapy and chemotherapy. MRI excels at soft-tissue imaging without ionizing radiation, aiding diagnosis of tumors, neurological disorders, and joint injuries. As combined therapies advance, imaging will play an increasingly critical role in enabling precise, personalized cancer treatment, improving outcomes and patient survival [205, 206].

Chen et al. developed a novel Ta4C3 composite incorporating Fe2O3 nanoparticles, exhibiting strong superparamagnetic properties and high potential as a magnetic resonance (MR) imaging contrast agent. With a transverse relaxivity of 205.46 mM⁻1 s⁻1, it significantly enhances T2-weighted MR imaging, improving tumor visualization [207].

The composite’s advanced design incorporates MnOx elements that improve its performance in T1-weighted MR imaging. Under the acidic conditions typical of cancerous tissues, Mn–O bonds in MnOx break down, releasing Mn2⁺ ions. These ions interact with surrounding water molecules, enhancing contrast in T1-weighted MR imaging [208]. This pH-responsive mechanism enables highly precise imaging capable of distinguishing healthy tissue from cancerous tissue based on their microenvironment, thereby improving tumor identification accuracy. The effectiveness of this pH-sensitive contrast approach has been demonstrated in MnOx/Ti3C2-SP and MnOx/Ta4C3-SP MXene composites, which show strong potential for improving MR imaging in cancer diagnosis and treatment monitoring [209].

HMDN reduces glutathione, generating Mn2⁺ ions that act as T1 MRI contrast agents and catalyze chemodynamic therapy (CDT) via Fenton-like reactions in acidic tumor environments. MXene enhances photothermal therapy (PTT) by converting NIR light into heat, sensitizing cancer cells and boosting CDT. The acidic microenvironment also triggers DOX release, enabling targeted chemotherapy, making this MXene-based platform a multifunctional strategy for imaging-guided, synergistic cancer treatment [211, 212].

The development of MXene-based composites and platforms has enabled precise, real-time tumor imaging with enhanced contrast and targeted therapeutic efficacy through approaches such as photothermal therapy (PTT) and chemodynamic therapy (CDT). These features not only boost imaging sensitivity and specificity but also support the advancement of multifunctional treatment modalities. Overall, MXenes hold tremendous promise in transforming bioimaging and theranostic applications, paving the way for more effective and personalized cancer therapies [236].

Polymer nanocomposites in tissue engineering

2D Ti3C2 MXene in 3D-printed scaffolds demonstrates potent tumor eradication and bone regeneration capabilities.

Upon near-infrared (NIR) exposure, Ti3C2 MXene induces photothermal hyperthermia, effectively eliminating bone tumors in vivo while promoting tissue regeneration when integrated into glass-based bioactive scaffolds [237].

3D scaffolds composed of ultrathin MXene nanosheets combined with niobium carbide target osteosarcoma and support bone healing.

The controlled release of niobium enhances angiogenesis, oxygen delivery, and immune cell recruitment, while scaffold degradation facilitates bone remodeling and mineralization [238].

Niobium carbide MXene scaffolds further advance tumor ablation, vascularization, and bone repair.

Nanocomposite membranes made from ultralong hydroxyapatite nanowires and titanium carbide MXene improve osteogenic cell proliferation, mechanical strength, and hydrophilicity, boosting bone regeneration [239].

Composite scaffolds combining Ti3C2 MXene, hydroxyapatite, and sodium alginate were 3D printed with consistent structure and mechanical strength to support bone regeneration.

In vitro studies with bone mesenchymal stem cells demonstrated the scaffold’s biocompatibility, enhanced cell proliferation, osteogenic gene expression, and mineralization. In vivo experiments using a rat calvarial defect model showed significant osteogenic activity and bone regeneration, indicating the scaffold’s promising potential as an innovative treatment for bone defects [240].

A novel composite integrating titanium carbide MXene with polyethylene glycol hydrogels was fabricated using 3D printing techniques. This composite exhibited no cytotoxicity toward cardiomyocytes while enhancing the expression of key cardiac genes. Additionally, it improved cardiomyocyte beating and conduction speed, demonstrating promising potential for applications in managing myocardial conditions [241–244].

A recent study developed multifunctional composite hydrogels using a “one-pot” synthesis approach by integrating 2D MXene nanoflakes into alginate and oxidized gelatin matrices. The resulting hydrogels demonstrated superior self-healing capabilities, enhanced fibroblast adhesion, and promoted cell migration. These improvements are attributed to the exceptional electrical conductivity and hydrophilicity provided by the embedded MXenes, making the hydrogels promising candidates for advanced tissue engineering and wound healing applications.

Electroactive MXene composite hydrogels, composed of bacterial cellulose and titanium carbide, significantly enhance wound healing by promoting electrically induced cellular activities. These hydrogels exhibit superior electrical conductivity, excellent biocompatibility, and high water absorption capacity, outperforming commercial Tegaderm film in therapeutic efficacy [245–249].

The MXene hydrogel composite promotes fibroblast proliferation and accelerates wound healing when combined with electrical stimulation, positioning it as a promising candidate for advanced skin dressings. Despite their significant biomedical potential, research on the cytotoxicity and biocompatibility of MXenes remains limited. Therefore, further comprehensive studies are essential to evaluate their safety profiles, cellular interactions, and pharmacokinetics for biomedical applications [250–255]. Challenges and clinical translation of MXene quantum dot polymer nanocomposites

MXene quantum dot (QD) polymer nanocomposites have emerged as promising platforms for targeted drug delivery and multifunctional biomedical applications due to their tunable surface chemistry, high surface-to-volume ratio, and unique optical and electrical properties. Recent studies from 2024–2025 demonstrate significant progress in engineering MXene QD-based systems for enhanced drug loading, stimuli-responsive release, and multimodal imaging capabilities. Despite these advancements, several challenges remain that must be addressed before clinical translation [292–294]. A major limitation is the controlled synthesis and scalability of MXene QD polymer nanocomposites. Variability in size, surface functionalization, and polymer integration can significantly influence biological responses, drug release profiles, and therapeutic efficacy. While fluorine-free etching and bottom-up synthesis techniques have improved reproducibility and reduced toxic by-products, standardized methods compatible with large-scale production are still lacking [295].

Long-term biosafety and biocompatibility represent another critical hurdle. Although preliminary in vitro studies indicate that polymer-wrapped MXene QDs exhibit reduced acute cytotoxicity, recent in vivo studies show organ-specific accumulation, potential oxidative stress, and sublethal metabolic effects that are not captured by conventional assays. Comprehensive chronic toxicity studies and mechanistic investigations into clearance pathways are essential to ensure safety [296].

The complexity of nano-bio interactions further challenges clinical translation. Polymer matrices used to enhance stability and targeting specificity must be carefully tuned for hydrophilicity, biodegradability, and immune compatibility. Recent 2024–2025 reports highlight that PEGylation, peptide conjugation, and surface modification strategies can improve circulation time and cellular uptake, yet the heterogeneity of biological environments necessitates further investigation to predict in vivo behavior accurately [297].

From a regulatory perspective, the lack of standardized evaluation frameworks for multifunctional nanomedicines impedes clinical adoption. Regulatory agencies require clear evidence of safety, efficacy, and reproducible manufacturing, but current guidelines do not fully account for the multifunctional properties of MXene-based nanocomposites. Harmonized protocols for physicochemical characterization, batch consistency, and functional performance benchmarks are needed to facilitate clinical translation [298].

Finally, interdisciplinary collaboration among materials scientists, chemists, pharmacologists, and regulatory experts is essential to overcome these challenges. Addressing synthesis scalability, comprehensive safety profiling, nano-bio interactions, and regulatory preparedness will be critical to advance MXene QD polymer nanocomposites from proof-of-concept studies to safe and effective clinical therapeutics [299].

Critical analysis

Recent developments from 2025 to 2026 in composites of MXene quantum dots and polymers have shown great promise as multifunctional carriers for targeted drug delivery and theranostic applications [300]. Compared with conventional nanocarriers, these composites provide higher drug-loading capacity, controlled release triggered by stimuli such as pH changes or near-infrared light, and improved cellular uptake through surface modifications [301]. The combination of the large surface area of MXene quantum dots and the responsive polymer network allows selective drug release at tumor sites while supporting imaging and photothermal therapy functions. Despite these advantages, several challenges remain. Long-term biocompatibility, stability in living systems, and potential immune responses are not yet fully understood because most studies are still in preclinical stages [302]. Variations in synthesis methods result in differences in particle uniformity, drug-loading efficiency, and release kinetics, highlighting the need for standardized procedures. Furthermore, mechanistic understanding of cellular uptake, intracellular transport, and interactions with biological systems is still limited. Approaches using clinically relevant animal models, predictive toxicity simulations, and machine-learning-assisted synthesis are being explored to overcome these limitations and accelerate clinical translation [303]. Overall, composites of MXene quantum dots and polymers represent highly versatile smart therapeutic platforms, but further mechanistic studies, standardized characterization, and comprehensive in vivo evaluations are essential to realize their full clinical potential [304].

Challenges and future perspectives

MXenes distinguish themselves from other two-dimensional materials through a variety of synthesis methods, allowing for relatively straightforward large-scale production via the selective etching of ceramic precursors [305]. For example, hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching enables the rapid and extensive production of MXenes, which feature abundant hydrophilic groups that improve their compatibility in biological environments. Over the next decade, researchers aim to address several key challenges to advance the development of MXenes [306]. Key challenges include developing eco-friendly and efficient synthesis methods, as well as improving the chemical and thermal stability of MXenes. Additionally, optimizing their electronic, optical, and thermal properties while precisely controlling surface chemistry is essential. Ongoing efforts also focus on refining self-assembly techniques to produce well-aligned MXene films and expanding the range of studied MXenes beyond the commonly used MXene, including exploration of alternative surface terminations such as sulfur and nitrogen [307]. Understanding the influence of confined ions and molecules on MXenes in various applications, particularly in electrochemical energy storage, is a key research priority [308]. Researchers are also working to transfer the exceptional mechanical properties of MXenes into ceramic, metal, and polymer composites. In addition, evaluating the toxicity and environmental safety of different transition metals and surface terminations is essential, along with investigating the use of MXenes as precursors for the synthesis of new materials. Major challenges remain, such as achieving delamination of MXenes without organic intercalants, synthesizing them from non-aluminum MAX phases, and understanding the mechanisms of charge transport between individual flakes [309]. Expanding the chemical diversity of the X element in MXenes, improving theoretical prediction methods, and developing new two-dimensional transition metal carbides, nitrides, and borides are all essential goals. Addressing these challenges will help researchers standardize characterization techniques and fully harness the transformative potential of MXenes as an exceptional class of materials with outstanding properties [310, 311].

Conclusion

In conclusion, MXenes and MXene quantum dots (QDs), particularly Ti3C2Tx, represent a rapidly advancing class of two-dimensional materials with significant potential for biomedical applications. Recent studies have demonstrated that their hydrophilic, functionalized surfaces and high surface-to-volume ratios enable efficient drug loading, enhanced cellular interactions, and improved therapeutic efficacy, especially in cancer-related applications such as bioimaging, targeted drug delivery, and photothermal therapy. Advances reported in 2024–2025 further highlight the role of surface engineering and polymer integration in enhancing stability, targeting ability, and biocompatibility. Despite these notable advancements, several limitations remain. The inherent chemical instability of MXenes under physiological and environmental conditions continues to hinder their scalable production, long-term storage, and clinical translation. While short-term in vitro and in vivo studies generally report low toxicity, comprehensive evaluations of long-term biocompatibility, cellular uptake pathways, biodegradation behavior, and environmental impact remain limited. Future efforts should therefore focus on developing environmentally sustainable and scalable synthesis strategies, coupled with systematic studies on the biological fate, clearance mechanisms, and long-term safety of both Unmodified and functionalized MXenes. Establishing standardized testing protocols and robust regulatory frameworks will be crucial for their safe and responsible application in biomedicine. Overall, ongoing progress in material design, surface engineering, and polymer-assisted functionalization is expected to broaden the scope of MXene-based systems, creating new opportunities for advanced drug delivery, wound healing, and next-generation biomedical technologies.
