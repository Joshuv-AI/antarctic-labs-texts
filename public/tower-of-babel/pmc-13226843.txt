Emergency open repair of war-related traumatic carotid artery injury caused by shrapnel in a resource-limited setting
Authors: Paing Oo Htut, Naw ZuZae Nar Blue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Penetrating carotid artery injuries are rare but life-threatening, particularly in resource-limited and conflict settings. We report a case of a 23-year-old male presenting with hypovolemic shock, respiratory arrest, and active neck hemorrhage secondary to a shrapnel injury. Emergency open exploration and primary repair of the carotid artery were performed in a field hospital without access to anesthesiology support, advanced imaging, or intensive care facilities. The patient survived without neurological deficit and was discharged on postoperative day 14. This case highlights the feasibility of life-saving vascular repair in austere environments using fundamental surgical principles.

Introduction

Penetrating carotid artery injuries are associated with high mortality due to hemorrhage and cerebral ischemia. In unstable patients presenting with active bleeding, immediate surgical exploration is recommended, particularly in settings where imaging and endovascular interventions are not available [1], [2]. Case presentation

The patient was a 23-year-old male who presented approximately 3 h after sustaining a penetrating neck injury caused by shrapnel. He was managed in the emergency operating area of a resource-limited field hospital in a conflict zone (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Primary repair of the carotid artery after vascular control was achieved.Fig. 2

On arrival, the patient was hemodynamically unstable with a blood pressure of 70/40 mmHg, heart rate of 124 beats per minute, and oxygen saturation of 87%. He experienced respiratory arrest and repeated generalized seizures prior to surgical intervention. Active pulsatile bleeding was observed from the neck wound.

Initial resuscitation was initiated, and 2 units of fresh whole blood were transfused. Due to the resource-limited setting, there was no anesthesiology service, no intensive care unit, no anesthesia machine, and limited laboratory capability. Surgical and anesthetic care were therefore delivered by the treating physician with assistance from a trained physician assistant and community health workers.

Given the patient's critical condition and ongoing hemorrhage, immediate surgical exploration was prioritized. Airway and ventilation were supported using ketofol sedation with assisted airway maintenance and basic monitoring. Definitive airway instrumentation was deferred in order to prioritize rapid hemorrhage control.

Emergency exploration of the neck was performed. Proximal and distal control of the carotid artery was achieved. A focal arterial laceration with viable vessel edges was identified, consistent with a Zone II neck injury.

Intra-arterial heparin was administered locally after vascular control, followed by flushing prior to final closure. Primary repair of the carotid artery was performed using 6-0 Prolene sutures. Clamp time was kept as short as feasible, and restoration of arterial flow was confirmed.

A small shrapnel fragment (∼3 mm) located near the cervical spine was not removed due to the risk of neurological injury. A drain was placed, and the wound was closed in layers.

Postoperatively, the patient remained hemodynamically stable and neurologically intact, with no focal deficits. Due to the absence of ICU facilities, postoperative care was provided within the field hospital. The patient was started on aspirin 75 mg daily and was discharged in stable condition on postoperative day 14.

Discussion

Penetrating carotid artery injuries are rare but life-threatening, with high risk of exsanguination and cerebral ischemia [3]. In unstable patients presenting with active hemorrhage, immediate surgical exploration remains the recommended management, particularly when advanced imaging or endovascular options are not available [1], [2].

In well-resourced settings, management may include endovascular techniques, temporary shunting, or interposition grafting. However, such options are frequently unavailable in conflict zones and resource-limited environments. In these contexts, rapid open surgical control and simple, definitive repair are often the only feasible life-saving interventions.

In the present case, the patient arrived in hypovolemic shock with neurological compromise and active arterial bleeding. The decision to proceed with immediate open exploration was guided by hemodynamic instability and lack of access to imaging or endovascular techniques. Primary repair was selected due to the presence of a short segment injury with viable vessel edges, allowing restoration of flow without delay. Carotid ligation was avoided due to the risk of cerebral ischemia.

No carotid shunt was used due to lack of availability and the need to minimize operative time. Limited intra-arterial heparin was administered after vascular control to reduce the risk of thrombosis while minimizing systemic bleeding risk.

The retained shrapnel fragment was left in situ due to its proximity to the cervical spine and the risk of neurological injury associated with removal.

This case demonstrates that adherence to fundamental surgical principles—rapid hemorrhage control, preservation of cerebral perfusion, and pragmatic decision-making—may be more critical than the availability of advanced technology in achieving survival in austere environments. The novelty of this report lies in the successful primary repair of a traumatic carotid artery injury in a field hospital without access to anesthesiology support, advanced monitoring, or critical care facilities.

This report may provide practical guidance for surgeons working in similarly resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

Emergency open repair of traumatic carotid artery injury can be successfully performed in resource-limited conflict settings. Adherence to fundamental surgical principles allows life-saving intervention even in the absence of advanced infrastructure.
