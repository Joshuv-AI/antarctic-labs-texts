Food insecurity and at-home vegetable and sugar-sweetened beverage availability: TX Sprouts randomized controlled trial
Authors: Keally Haushalter, Shreela V. Sharma, Marissa Burgermaster, Natalie S Poulos, Erin A. Hudson, Jennifer M. Daspin, Mariela Jimenez Rivero, Jaimie N. Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Food insecurity and the at-home availability of vegetables and sugar-sweetened beverages both influence dietary intake. However, most research on food insecurity and the at-home availability of vegetables and sugar-sweetened beverages is cross-sectional, which is problematic as both change frequently. Therefore, this study assessed how changes in food insecurity were associated with changes in the at-home availability of vegetables and sugar-sweetened beverages among those in the intervention and control groups of TX Sprouts. Parents who participated in TX Sprouts, a gardening, cooking, and nutrition cluster-randomized trial conducted with primarily Hispanic, low-income 3rd-5th-grade students, completed surveys at baseline and at the nine-month follow-up regarding household food insecurity and the at-home availability of vegetables and sugar-sweetened beverages (n = 839). Changes in food insecurity were measured as a continuous variable and categorized according to guidelines at baseline and follow-up. Given that the TX Sprouts intervention improved the at-home availability of vegetables, and there was a significant interaction between changes in food insecurity and the intervention on the at-home availability of vegetables, the intervention and control groups were analyzed separately. Multivariable linear regression estimated how changes in food insecurity, as a continuous measure, were associated with changes in vegetable and sugar-sweetened beverage availability among those in the intervention and control groups. Analysis of covariance was used to analyze how changes in food insecurity, as a categorical measure, were associated with changes in vegetable and sugar-sweetened beverage availability among those in the intervention and control groups. In the control group, a one-point increase in food insecurity, indicative of becoming more food insecure, was associated with a decrease in the availability of vegetables (ß=-0.27, P<.001). In the intervention group, a one-point increase in food insecurity was associated with an increase in the availability of sugar-sweetened beverages (ß =0.05, P=.03). Further, in the control group, those with periodic food insecurity had a larger decrease in the availability of sugar-sweetened beverages compared to those with persistent food security (P=.02). In addition, in the intervention group, households with persistent food security experienced an increase in at-home vegetable availability, while households with periodic (P=.02) and persistent food insecurity (P<.001) experienced a significant decrease in at-home vegetable availability. Future interventions should improve the availability of healthy items for food-insecure households.Clinical Trial Registration Number: NCT02668744. Introduction

In 2023, 13.5% of United States (U.S.) households (~ 47 million people) experienced food insecurity, a condition defined by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) as a “household-level economic and social condition of limited or uncertain access to adequate food”1,2. Compared to the U.S. average (13.5%), Hispanic households (21.9%), households with children (17.9%), and households with low-income (33.5–38.7%) faced significantly higher rates of food insecurity1. Further, children experiencing food insecurity have a higher risk of developing poor cognitive and physical health and an unhealthy dietary intake3–6.

At-home food and beverage availability influences dietary behaviors. Studies have found that a higher at-home availability of unhealthy foods is associated with lower fruit and vegetable intake and higher snack and high-calorie drink intake7–9. Further, as children age, the availability of less nutritious foods increases10. In addition, food-insecure homes, compared to food-secure homes, have a higher availability of unhealthy foods, such as candy, high-calorie beverages, and snacks, and a lower availability of fruits and vegetables11,12. A diet that is rich in added sugars and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) and lacks fruits and vegetables can increase one’s risk for developing chronic diseases, such as diabetes, overweight, and obesity13–15, which children with food insecurity already have an increased risk for3,4,16.

Most studies analyzing food insecurity and the at-home availability of vegetables and SSBs are cross-sectional17–22. These studies may not capture the true relationship between these measures, as food insecurity status is dynamic and changes over time. For example, in 2022, one-fourth of food-insecure households experienced chronic food insecurity, while three-fourths of food-insecure households experienced food security at various points throughout the year23. Further, households can experience fluctuations in their food insecurity status within the month24, indicating the food insecurity status can vary even in a short time span, based on when it is assessed. However, cross-sectional studies only assess food insecurity status at one time, treating food insecurity as a chronic state rather than a dynamic state23. Therefore, a longitudinal study assessing changes in food insecurity status may more appropriately analyze the relationship between food insecurity and at-home vegetable and SSB availability.

Gardening interventions are effective in improving food insecurity and improving the nutrient quality of foods and beverages in the home25,26. TX Sprouts was a nine-month cluster-randomized school-based gardening, cooking, and nutrition study in 3rd -5th -grade primarily Hispanic students with low income. Previous research found that while TX Sprouts did not improve food insecurity, it did improve the at-home availability of vegetables25,27. However, it is unknown if there was a significant interaction between the TX Sprouts intervention and changes in food insecurity on the changes in vegetable and SSB at-home availability. Further, it is unclear how changes in food insecurity were associated with changes in at-home vegetable and SSBs availability in the intervention and control groups.

There are three objectives for this study. The first objective was to determine if there is a significant interaction between the TX Sprouts intervention and changes in food insecurity on changes in the at-home availability of vegetables and SSBs. The second objective was to analyze how continuous changes in household food insecurity over nine months are associated with changes in at-home vegetable and SSB availability among those in TX Sprout’s intervention and control groups. The third objective was to compare how changes in at-home vegetable and SSB availability differ based on USDA categorical food insecurity groups among those in the intervention versus control groups.

Methods

Description of study

This study is a secondary analysis of TX Sprouts, a cluster-randomized school-based gardening, cooking, and nutrition trial. The methods and main outcomes of TX Sprouts have been published elsewhere25,27–32. Briefly, TX Sprouts was conducted in 3rd-5th-grade students from 16 elementary schools that served primarily Hispanic youth with low income in the Austin, Texas area from 2016 to 2019. The TX Sprouts intervention included the construction of a school garden that was used to teach 18 student and nine parent garden and nutrition lessons, each of which featured a cooking or tasting component. The study was approved by both The University of Texas at Austin (IRB: 2014-11-0045) and the participating school districts’ Institutional Review Boards, and all research was performed in accordance with the regulations and guidelines. Parents and students signed a written informed consent and assent, respectively. As described elsewhere, the intervention significantly improved dietary intake, academic performance, glucose control, and the availability of vegetables at home25,28–32. The study was funded by the National Institutes of Health [1R01HL123865, 2015–2020], Whole Kids Foundation, Home Depot, and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov on 01/29/2016 (registration number: NCT02668744).

Measures

At baseline and follow-up (approximately nine months later), parents in both the control and intervention schools completed a survey that included questions regarding at-home food and SSB availability, food insecurity, and demographics. Surveys were available in both English and Spanish.

At-home vegetable and sugar-sweetened beverage availability

Previous research has shown that vegetable and SSB intake increases with increased at-home availability33–35. Given that vegetables are under-consumed and added sugar is overconsumed in this age group36, a scale from the Texas, Grow! Eat! Go! Trial were used to assess the availability of vegetables and SSBs in the home37–39. In other pediatric populations, the scale has been proven to be a reliable measure of at-home food and beverage availability (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.7)37,38,40. The scale assesses the at-home availability of six items: vegetable juice, fresh vegetables, canned, frozen, or dried vegetables, salad, cut-up fresh vegetables in a place that is easy for children to reach, and SSBs. For each item, parents reported how often it was available in their home within the last week using a four-point Likert scale (“never”, “some of the time”, “most of the time”, or “all of the time”). The responses for each item were converted to numeric values, in which a higher score indicates higher availability (“never”=0, “some of the time”=1, “most of the time”=2, “all of the time”=3). To assess overall vegetable availability, the vegetable scores across the five sub-scores were summed (vegetable juice, fresh vegetables, canned, frozen, or dried vegetables, salad, cut-up fresh vegetables in a place that is easy for children to reach). The composite vegetable availability score and SSB availability score have been used in other pediatric populations to assess the relationship between food availability and consumption38,39.

To assess how at-home vegetable and SSBs availability changed throughout the year, a change score was calculated by subtracting the availability at baseline from the availability at follow-up (post-pre, possible range of individual item/SSBs: -3 to + 3, vegetable range: -15 to + 15). A positive change score indicated that the item became more available, while a negative change score indicated that the item became less available.

Food insecurity

Household food insecurity was measured using the 18-item USDA Household Food Security Survey Module, which assesses if and how often various experiences of food insecurity occurred within the last 12 months41. In line with the guidelines, responses were converted to numeric values in which affirmative responses (“a lot” and “sometimes”) receive a score of one and negative responses (“never”) receive a score of zero. Scores are summed (range: 0 to 18), in which a score of zero indicates no food insecurity and a higher score indicates more experiences of food insecurity. Surveys were scored at both baseline and follow-up. As with at-home vegetable and SSB availability, a change score was calculated to assess changes in food insecurity status by subtracting the baseline score from the follow-up score (post-pre, range: -18 to + 18). A positive change score indicates one became more food insecure, and a negative change score indicates that one became less food insecure (more food secure).

At both baseline and nine-month follow-up, food security status was categorized as food secure (score 0–2) or food insecure (score 3–18) using the USDA guidelines41. Participants were then grouped into one of three groups: those who remained food secure (persistent food security), those who remained food insecure (persistent food insecurity), and those whose food insecurity status changed (secure to insecure or insecure to secure, labeled as periodic food insecurity).

Demographics

Parents completed questionnaires reporting their child’s demographics, including age (years), race and ethnicity (White, Hispanic, Black/Asian/Multiracial), sex (male, female), and free and reduced-priced lunch program enrollment (receives, does not receive)28–30.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to display the baseline demographics of the analytic sample as a whole and by intervention groups (control vs. intervention). Previous analysis found that while the TX Sprouts intervention did not improve food insecurity, it increased the at-home availability of vegetable juice and fresh vegetables25,27. Therefore, the multivariable linear regression were used to assess the interaction between changes in food insecurity and the intervention on the at-home availability of vegetables and SSBs, controlling for the child’s baseline age (years), race and ethnicity (White, Hispanic, Black/Asian/Multiracial), sex (male, female), free and reduced-price lunch enrollment (receives, does not receive), school, baseline at-home vegetable and SSBs availability, and baseline food insecurity. As the interaction was significant, all further analyses were stratified by intervention group. Multivariable linear regression estimated how changes in food insecurity, as a continuous variable, were associated with changes in at-home vegetable and SSB availability in the control and intervention groups, adjusting for the same covariates. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) assessed how adjusted mean changes in at-home vegetable and SSB availability differed by the three categorical changes in food insecurity groups (persistent food security, periodic food insecurity, and persistent food insecurity) in the intervention and control groups, controlling for the same covariates. Post-hoc Tukey was conducted to assess significant differences between changes in food insecurity groups when the ANCOVA tests were significant. Covariates were chosen due to past research showing their influence on food insecurity1,42–44. Results were considered significant when P < .05. All analysis was conducted in R Studio 2024.12.1 + 563.

Results

Participants

In total, 4239 students were enrolled across the 16 elementary schools included in TX Sprouts. Approximately 78% (n = 3302) of parents consented for their child to participate, and 71% (n = 2357) completed the baseline survey with all variables of interest (age, sex, race, and ethnicity, free and reduced-price lunch enrollment, at-home vegetable and SSB availability, and food insecurity status). Of those who had complete data at baseline, 839 (36%) also had complete data at follow-up and were included in this analysis. Compared to those enrolled in the clinical trial but not included in the analytic sample (n = 2296), the analytic sample had a significantly lower proportion of male children (49% vs. 42%, P<.001). There was no other significant difference between sociodemographic variables between those in the clinical trial who were not included in the analytic sample and those who were in the analytic sample. The sociodemographic characteristics of those in the control versus the intervention are displayed in Table 1. Just over half of the analytic sample (53%, N = 445) was in the control, and 47% (N = 394) were in the intervention. Compared to those in the intervention, those in the control had a significantly higher at-home availability of vegetable juice at baseline (0.98 vs. 0.82, P=.02). There were no other significant sociodemographic or at-home availability of vegetables at SSBs differences between study groups (P>.05).

Interaction between the intervention and changes in food insecurity on changes in at-home vegetable and SSBs availability

The interaction between changes in food insecurity and the intervention on changes in at-home vegetable (ß=0.27, 95% CI [0.14, 0.40], P<.001) and SSB availability (ß=0.06, 95% CI [0.00, 0.12], P=.04) was significant (data not shown). Therefore, the remaining analyses were stratified by intervention groups (intervention, control).

Changes in food insecurity and changes in at-home vegetable and SSBs availability

Table 2 displays associations between changes in food insecurity and changes in at-home vegetable and SSB availability. In the control group, a one-point increase in food insecurity, indicative of becoming more food insecure, was associated with a decrease in the at-home availability of vegetables (ß=-0.27, 95% CI [-0.41, -0.13], P<.001). In the intervention group, a one-point increase in food insecurity, indicative of becoming more food insecure, was associated with an increase in the at-home availability of SSBs (ß=0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.10], P=.03). There was no significant relationship between changes in food insecurity and changes in SSBs availability in the control group or between changes in food insecurity and changes in vegetables availability in the intervention group.

Changes in at-home vegetable and SSB availability by categorical changes in food insecurity

Table 3 presents the change in at-home vegetable and SSB availability by categorical change in food insecurity groups (persistent food security, periodic food insecurity, and persistent food insecurity). In the control group, compared to those with persistent food security, those with periodic food insecurity had a larger decrease in the at-home availability of SSBs (adjusted means −0.16 vs. -0.30, P=.02).

In the intervention group, those with persistent food insecurity had decreased at-home vegetable availability, whereas those with persistent food security had an increase in at-home vegetable availability (-0.95 vs. 0.60, P<.001). Similarly, those with persistent food insecurity had a larger decrease in the changes of vegetable availability at home than those with periodic food insecurity (-0.95 vs. -0.14, P=.005). Finally, those with persistent food security had an increase in the at-home availability of vegetables while those with periodic food insecurity had a decrease (0.60 vs. -0.14, P=.02). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze changes in food insecurity and at-home vegetable and SSB availability among predominantly Hispanic children with low income. Results indicate that becoming more food insecure was associated with a decrease in the at-home availability of vegetables in the control group and a significant increase in the availability of SSBs in the intervention group. In addition, among those in the control group, those with periodic food insecurity had a significantly larger decrease in the availability of SSBs than those with persistent food security. Among those in the intervention group, households with persistent food security experienced an increase in at-home vegetable availability, while households with periodic and persistent food insecurity experienced a significant decrease in at-home vegetable availability.

These findings align with the current literature that suggests food-insecure households have unhealthier foods available at home than food-secure households7–9. However, most studies are cross-sectional17–22 and therefore are unable to assess changes in food insecurity23. This is problematic as food insecurity status changes. The dynamic nature of food insecurity was recently demonstrated in a study that followed a nationally representative cohort of 7,326 children from kindergarten to eighth grade and measured food insecurity status four times45. Across the four-time points, 19.5% of children experienced periods of both food security and food insecurity. Given the fluctuations in food insecurity status that many households experience, assessing changes in food insecurity may better capture a household’s experience with food insecurity. Therefore, this study builds upon current literature by analyzing the changes in food insecurity with changes in at-home vegetable and SSB availability.

It is important to acknowledge that the at-home vegetable and SSB availability decreased throughout the year in both the intervention and control groups, except for households with persistent food security in the intervention group, who had an increase in at-home vegetable availability. The authors believe this decrease in availability could be due to multiple reasons. First, the TX Sprouts intervention intended to teach children about the importance of gardening and nutrition at school, but did not provide fresh fruits or vegetables at home29. Previous studies have shown that the TX Sprouts intervention did improve the at-home availability of fresh vegetables, which was believed to be through the impact of the program’s culturally tailored recipes and the influence children can have on parents’ purchasing behavior25. Second, most of the items assessed in the at-home availability survey were healthy items that take time to prepare (i.e., salad, fresh vegetables). Time to prepare healthy items has been reported as a barrier to healthy eating in Hispanic families with low income46. At the beginning of the school year, when the baseline survey was conducted, parents may be more motivated to ensure these healthy items are available in their homes, but as the school year progresses and time becomes scarce, they may opt to have more convenient food options in their homes. Therefore, the overall decrease in at-home availability of these healthy items may reflect the follow-up survey being distributed at the end of the school year.

In addition to the time of year, the time of the month the survey was conducted may have also influenced the at-home vegetable and SSB availability. Most of the baseline surveys were distributed in the first half of the month, and most of the follow-up surveys were distributed in the second half of the month. At the beginning of the month, families often have more money and therefore may be more willing to purchase fresh fruits and vegetables, which are generally more expensive47. However, as the month continues and money dwindles, fresh, healthy items may become out of budget48. This cycle is especially true of low-income families who rely on food assistance programs, such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), that provide the supplement at the beginning of the month24,47–50. Studies have found that SNAP benefits are only sufficient for approximately the first two weeks of the month24,48,49. To cope with the limited financial resources, families reduce their food spending and consumption24,47–50. As a result, their diet quality at the end of the month is significantly worse than their diet quality at the beginning of the month50. Therefore, the overall decrease in vegetable availability may have reflected this monthly cycle caused by financial restraints.

Current strategies to improve food insecurity center on providing families with food or resources to purchase food. SNAP is the largest government food and nutrition program in the United States and provides low-income households with a monthly allowance to purchase authorized foods, including fruits, vegetables, meat, dairy, and snacks51,52. Food pantries, another strategy to improve food insecurity, are local distribution centers run by various organizations that provide families with food directly, most of it being shelf-stable foods53. However, while these programs give families access to food, the nutritional value of the food is often poor. As a result, SNAP participants, compared to income-eligible non-participants, have significantly lower diet quality scores and consume fewer fruits and vegetables and more added sugars54–56. In addition, the nutritional quality of food available at food pantries has consistently been found to be inadequate to meet the individual’s dietary needs57,58. Low-income and minority families are also more likely to live in a low-income, low-access areas that have an overabundance of unhealthy food options and a lack of healthy food options59. Therefore, access to healthy food, not just sufficient calories, is crucial.

Various programs have begun to recognize this discrepancy and are aiming to improve the nutritional value of the food provided60–64. By doing so, programs are no longer focused on improving just food insecurity, which measures the access an individual has to food, but nutrition security, which measures the access one has to healthy, safe, and affordable foods that are essential to an individual’s optimal health and well-being65. Measuring nutrition security, a relatively new metric, ensures that food-insecure families are not only provided with enough food but with healthy food that improves their diet and overall health. Further, a study among SNAP participants found that nutrition security and food insecurity were only moderately correlated66, indicating that they are two distinct concepts that both need to be monitored and addressed.

This study, to the authors’ knowledge, is the first to examine changes in food insecurity with changes in at-home vegetable and SSB availability. Further, this study focused on Hispanic youth, a population that experiences significantly high rates of food insecurity. However, this study is not without its limitations. First, the study relied on self-reports to assess at-home vegetable and SSB availability. While self-report at-home food and beverage availability is highly correlated and validated with in-home inventories,67 the specific survey used in this study to assess at-home vegetable and SSB availability has not been validated against in-home inventories and, therefore, is subject to self-report bias. Further, a study found that compared to an in-home inventory, Hispanic households with children over-reported the availability of vegetables and under-reported the availability of SSBs68. While the differences in availability were small, the actual availability of these items may be slightly different than the actual availability. Second, this study only analyzed changes across nine months. Since food insecurity status changes throughout one’s life, a study conducted over a longer time may capture more changes in food insecurity. There was also a significantly higher proportion of male children in the analytic sample compared to the clinical trial sample, and only 36% of those who completed the baseline survey also completed the follow-up survey. Given that families of low socioeconomic status are less likely to complete follow-up testing69, potential selection bias may have occurred, resulting in a lower proportion of food-insecure families in the analytic sample compared to the clinical trial sample.

Conclusions

Overall, this study found that becoming more food insecure was associated with a decrease in the at-home availability of vegetables in the control group and a significant increase in the at-home availability of SSBs in the intervention. In the control group, households with periodic food insecurity experienced a greater decrease in SSB availability than households with persistent food security. In the intervention group, households with both persistent and periodic food insecurity had larger decreases in the availability of vegetables than households with persistent food security. This study supports the shift towards providing households with food insecurity with sufficient nutrients, not just sufficient food. As access to healthy, nutrient-rich foods improves, families with food insecurity will have the opportunity to consume a diet that protects them from diet-related diseases, rather than a diet that provokes diet-related diseases.
