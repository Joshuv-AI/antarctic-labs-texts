Interindividual variability in platelet aggregation among aspirin-treated patients: a large retrospective analysis
Authors: Eleonora Schiera, Simone De Corci, Sergio Terracina, Gioacchino Galardo, Gianluca Di Pietro, Massimo Mancone, Fabio M. Pulcinelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Assessment based on AA alone is insufficient to study aspirin efficacy.•A multiagonist approach improves detection of residual reactivity and identifies thromboxane A2-dependent and -independent HARPR.

Introduction

Antiplatelet therapy plays a key role in preventing systemic thrombotic events in high-risk patients and in those undergoing percutaneous cardiac interventions.1 Nevertheless, clinical efficacy of antithrombotic therapy is influenced by multiple factors (eg, genetic) that vary widely among patients.2 This variability may result in either insufficient platelet inhibition, increasing thrombotic risk, or excessive inhibition, increasing bleeding risk. High on-aspirin platelet reactivity (HAPR) represents a heterogeneous condition associated with a reduction of antiplatelet drug efficacy an increased risk of thrombotic events.3,4 Platelet function assays, especially light transmission aggregometry (LTA), have been proposed as a strategy to guide antiplatelet therapy and simultaneously optimize safety and efficacy outcomes.5, 6, 7

Patients with reduced sensitivity to aspirin, as assessed by platelet function assays, have been shown to be at higher risk of cardiovascular events and morbidity than with aspirin responders.8,9

In a previous analysis from our group, based on our large database of 11 913 patients, we demonstrated a marked interindividual variability in platelet function across both untreated and antiplatelet-treated populations.9 A subsequent subanalysis based on sex differences demonstrated also platelet variability both untreated and antiplatelet-treated populations and on-aspirin female patients exhibit higher platelet reactivity. The major evidence of this higher platelet reactivity is in response to collagen, a platelet agonist linked to aspirin action, as it correlates with clinical data and strongly suggests that PA may be useful to identify patients less sensitive to aspirin function.9

Arachidonic acid (AA)–induced platelet aggregation (PA) is considered to be the most specific method for evaluating aspirin efficacy, because aspirin exerts its antiplatelet effect through irreversible inhibition of cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1).10 However, platelet activation can also occur through COX-1–independent pathways. AA-induced aggregation has been demonstrated to linearly correlate with thromboxane A2 (TxA2) production after aspirin treatment; they also show that AA-induced PA of <20% COX-1 is inhibited in more than 99% patients.11 Several studies have suggested that using AA in LTA alone may not be sufficient to identify patients who are at risk, as multiple mechanisms contribute to poor clinical outcomes in aspirin-treated patients (including COX-1 inhibition), despite aspirin therapy.12,13 Therefore, additional agonists such as adenosine diphosphate (ADP), epinephrine (EPI), and collagen may provide complementary information on platelet reactivity in aspirin-treated patients.13,14 Although AA-induced PA provides information on COX-1 inhibition, residual platelet reactivity is a multifactorial phenomenon involving several platelet activation pathways. Therefore, characterization of platelet responsivity based on a single agonist may underestimate biologically relevant residual platelet activation. A more comprehensive assessment integrating multiple agonists, may better identify patients with persistent platelet reactivity despite antiplatelet therapy, and support personalized strategies.

In this study, we analyzed PA in a large cohort of 3065 aspirin-treated patients using a standardized LTA methodology. We aimed to identify whether aspirin treated-patients with low AA response presents residual platelet reactivity using a multiagonist approach and how AA-induced aggregation alone reflects platelet responsiveness so as to determine high platelet reactivity (HPR), low platelet reactivity (LPR), or optimal platelet reactivity (OPR), as previously suggested.15

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective analysis conducted on 3065 patients who sequentially underwent platelet function assays at Policlinico Umberto I University Hospital, Unit of Advanced Diagnosis Platelet Disorders (Rome, Italy) between 1 January 2004 and 31 December 2022. The study population from this large database is represented by patients treated exclusively with low-dose aspirin (ASA; 75-150 mg/d). The complete database, as well as the detailed description of blood sampling and laboratory assessment procedures have been previously reported.9

Compliance to antiplatelet therapy was evaluated by face-to-face interview carried out by health care staff and patients who omitted at least 1 dose of aspirin once a week were designated as noncompliant. In addition, in case of unexpected AA-induced PA response, a subsequent dedicated interview was performed to confirm treatment adherence. Patients in whom adherence could not be reliably confirmed, were therefore excluded from this population. Participants were excluded if any of the following were present: comorbidities associated with abnormal platelet function, including concomitant use of drugs interfering with platelet function; and doses of aspirin different from those required to be included in the study. Exclusion criteria have been reported in detail in our previous study.9 In summary, all patients with platelet disorders, abnormal bleeding, diseases or conditions affecting platelet function, or taking therapies influencing platelet function and those taking different dosages of antiplatelet therapies noncompliant were excluded.

We used a standardized methodology previously reported.9 To provide a comprehensive evaluation of PA we assessed aggregation percentage at 4 minutes specifically using ADP 2μM, collagen 2 μg/mL, epinephrine (EPI) at 10μM, and AA 0.75mM.9

Study end points

In this study, we identified a value of 16% AA-induced PA, corresponding to the third quartile of ASA group previously reported.9 This threshold was used as an exploratory stratification cutoff. Patients were, therefore, divided into 2 groups: ≤16% PA (n = 2650) and >16% PA (n = 415).

The primary end point was the comparison of PA responses between the 2 groups. PA was assessed using LTA after stimulation with ADP (2μM), EPI (10μM), and collagen (2 μg/mL).

Secondary end point included sex-related differences in PA within each group and the characterization of platelet reactivity across different agonists and concentrations.

In addition, we aimed at identifying the proportion of patients with OPR, LPR, and HPR using median and interquartile range (IQR).9

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of the PA%. Categorical variables of patients are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using 2 tailed Fisher exact test or the Pearson χ² test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD. Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables. Data were also reported as median and 25th (Q1) and 75th (Q3) percentiles (IQR) values of the PA% at 4 minutes and presented using tables and scatter dot plots. Differences were calculated using Kruskal-Wallis test. To mitigate selection bias across groups the inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on covariate balance was assessed and the absolute standardized mean differences (ASMD) were reported to assess balancing across groups; variables with absolute standardized mean differences <0.10 were considered as balanced; weighted means (SD) and percentages were reported after IPTW. In this approach, each patient is assigned a weight based on their propensity score, to create a pseudopopulation with balanced baseline characteristics. Following weighting, covariates were well balanced between treatment groups. Following weighting, covariates were well balanced between the groups. Importantly, in this weighted pseudopopulation generated by IPTW, summary statistics no longer reflect raw patient counts but instead represent the sum of the weights assigned to individuals. Each subject contributes proportionally to their weight, and tables showing descriptive statistics are, therefore, interpreted in terms of weighted totals.

A P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, version 9.1.0. and R-4.6.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

EPI

In response to EPI 10μM, patients with AA-induced PA ≤16 and >16 the mean ± SD of PA% was 36.8 ± 30.7 with median (IQR) of 42 (0-60); whereas those with AA >16% showed a higher PA reported as mean ± SD of 57.4 ± 26.5, with median (IQR) of 60 (47-75) (P < .0001) (Figure 1; Table 3).

Collagen

In response to collagen 2 μg/mL, with AA-induced PA ≤16 and >16 the mean ± SD of PA% was 27.2 ± 27.4 with median (IQR) of 22 (0-44); whereas those with AA >16% showed a higher PA reported as mean ± SD of 38.3 ± 32.3, with median (IQR) of 38 (0-61) (P < .0001) (Figure 1; Table 3).

HPR, LPR, and OPR among patients with AA ≤ or >16% PA

Based on our previous study, we defined HPR for PA >75%, OPR for PA ≥25% and PA ≤75%, and LPR for PA <25%.9

ADP

In response to ADP 2μM we found the following subpopulation distribution. In the ≤16% group, 10% of patients showed HPR, 32% LPR, and 58% OPR. In the >16% group, HPR increased to 27%, whereas LPR decreased to 9% and OPR was 64%.

EPI

In response to EPI 10μM, we found the following subpopulation distribution. In the ≤16% group, HPR was observed in 11%, LPR in 36%, and OPR in 53% of the patients. In the >16% group, HPR rose to 32%, LPR decreased to 12%, and OPR was 56%.

Collagen

In response to collagen 2 μg/mL, we found the following subpopulation distribution. In the ≤16% group, 8% of patients had HPR, 52% had LPR, and 40% had OPR. In the >16% group, HPR increased to 16%, LPR decreased to 37%, and OPR was 47%.

Overall, patients with AA-induced PA ≤16%, were predominantly characterized by LPR across several agonists. In contrast, HPR was uncommon in this subgroup. Conversely, patients with AA-induced PA>16% showed a more heterogeneous phenotype distribution, with a substantial proportion of patients falling into OPR range and a higher prevalence of HPR across all agonists.

These findings indicate that the AA PA threshold strongly influences platelet reactivity profiles, with ≤16% population characterized by markedly suppressed PA, consistent with effective COX-1 inhibition. Values of >16% are associated with less inhibition and increased residual platelet reactivity.

Discussion

In this monocentric study we investigated and confirmed a marked interindividual variability in platelet reactivity among patients receiving aspirin therapy.9

A key finding was that AA-induced aggregation alone is insufficient to fully characterize platelet responsiveness. Although patients with AA ≤16% are generally considered responders to aspirin, a substantial proportion of these patients with apparently adequate AA inhibition still exhibited HPR when stimulated with other platelet agonists, such as ADP, EPI, or collagen.

Using AA-induced PA, patients were stratified according to a 16% threshold, corresponding to the third quartile previously identified in a large aspirin-treated population.13 Values above this cutoff were considered indicative of reduced sensitivity to aspirin, likely reflecting incomplete COX-1 inhibition and residual TxA2 production.4 A discrepancy between these pathways has already been described, although in a limited sample size, by the lack of concordance between HAPR defined by serum TxB2 and AA-induced PA.13 Previous studies11,13 used an AA-induced PA value ≥20% as the cutoff to identify HAPR. However, these thresholds were derived from smaller patient cohorts than those included in the present study and were not proposed to investigate residual platelet reactivity through a multiple-agonist approach.

Our findings extend these observations in a large population and demonstrate that inhibition of the COX-1 pathway alone is not sufficient to fully suppress platelet activation. Instead, COX-1–independent pathways appear to play a substantial role in residual platelet reactivity.

Among the tested agonists, collagen-induced aggregation emerged as highly informative. As expected, patients with AA>16% showed globally higher platelet reactivity across all agonists, suggesting persistent platelet activation through alternative pathways.

However, an important observation is that a subset of patients with AA ≤16%, generally considered responders to aspirin, still displayed HPR when platelets were stimulated with ADP, EPI, or collagen. This finding suggests that inhibition of the COX-1 pathway alone does not fully suppress platelet activation, and that COX-1–independent pathways may significantly contribute to residual platelet reactivity.4 Although HPR was less frequent in the AA ≤16% group, a proportion of these patients still exhibited high collagen-induced aggregation (∼8%), which remains clinically relevant when compared with larger populations previously reported.9 These findings indicate that reliance on AA-induced aggregation alone may underestimate residual platelet reactivity.

Emerging evidence supports the clinical relevance of this pathway. In particular, increased glycoprotein VI (GPVI) expression leading enhanced GPVI-dependent platelet activity have been reported in patients with acute and advanced coronary artery disease. GPVI, through its association with the Fc receptor γ-chain, triggers downstream signaling via ITAM-dependent pathways involving key mediators such as SLP-76 and phospolipase C gamma 2, leading to platelet activation independently of TxA2 production.17 In addition, collagen signaling involves synergistic interactions between GPVI and integrin α2β1, which stabilizes platelet adhesion and promotes spreading as previously described.18 High GPVI expression has been associated with increased residual platelet reactivity and higher risk thrombotic complications despite treatment. Furthermore, phospolipase C gamma 2-dependent signaling requires cooperation between GPVI and integrin α2β1, ultimately leading to αIIβ3 activation.19

Our findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting the role of collagen-induced aggregation in identifying patients with reduced aspirin responsiveness, although these studies were conducted in smaller cohorts.20, 21, 22 Taken together, these data support the use of a multiagonist approach to better characterize platelet function in aspirin-treated patients.

The clinical relevance of residual platelet reactivity has been previously demonstrated, as both COX-1–dependent and COX-1–independent pathways have been associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events.12,23 In this context, our results reinforce the concept that AA testing alone may not fully capture thrombotic risk.

Furthermore, aspirin resistance has been associated with impaired inhibition of PA and increased expression of P-selectin, a marker of α-granule secretion linked to atherosclerosis progression.24 This highlights the importance of identifying patients with reduced responsiveness to aspirin, who may remain at increased cardiovascular risk despite therapy.

We also observed sex-related differences in platelet reactivity. Female patients exhibited higher PA compared with male patients, particularly in response to ADP and collagen in the AA ≤16% subgroup. These findings suggest that women may present a more reactive platelet phenotype despite apparent adequate inhibition of the AA pathway, potentially reflecting differences in COX-1–independent platelet activation mechanisms. This observation is consistent with previous studies suggesting reduced aspirin responsiveness in female patients, which has been recently clinically confirmed.16,25

From a clinical perspective, our data support the potential value of a multiagonist approach to improve identification of residual platelet reactivity during aspirin therapy. However, these findings should be considered in future prospective multicenter studies including clinical outcome data, which are needed to better characterize platelet residual reactivity in aspirin treated patients and the activation pathways in this context. Reliance on AA-induced aggregation alone may underestimate residual platelet reactivity and thrombotic risk.

Importantly, increasing aspirin dose does not appear to overcome residual platelet reactivity in this setting. In our cohort, aspirin dosage (75 vs 150 mg/d) did not significantly affect AA-induced aggregation in patients with AA ≤16% (data not shown), suggesting that dose escalation may not be an effective strategy. Moreover, previous studies have suggested that aspirin efficacy may decline over time.26

Despite widespread use of aspirin in secondary prevention, the absolute risk of recurrent cardiovascular events remains substantial, ranging from 8% to 18% at 2 years,1,27 highlighting the clinical relevance of residual platelet reactivity and the need for improved therapeutic strategies. This observation further underlines the complexity of platelet residual reactivity and the need to better characterize activation pathways in this context.

Our findings confirm the marked interindividual variability in aspirin response using LTA, considered gold standard for platelet function assessment.14 Compared with prior studies conducted in smaller cohorts, our analysis provides a more robust characterization of platelet reactivity patterns in aspirin-treated patients.9 The strengths of our study include the large sample size, >3000 patients, and the use of a standardized methodology for platelet function testing. However, this study has some limitations, including its retrospective design and the lack of clinical outcome data. Taken together, these results highlight the complex and multifactorial nature of platelet activation in aspirin-treated patients and support the use of a multiagonist approach, particularly including collagen, emerged as particularly informative in platelet function assessment. In the context of precision medicine, evaluating responses to multiple agonists after initial AA assessment may improve patient stratification. In patients with AA-induced aggregation >16%, further evaluation with additional agonists, including collagen, may help refine risk assessment and guide therapeutic decisions. In conclusion, a substantial proportion of aspirin-treated patients exhibit residual platelet reactivity driven by 2 distinct mechanisms: incomplete COX-1 inhibition and COX-1–independent platelet activation. A comprehensive assessment using multiple-agonists, particularly collagen, may improve patient stratification and support personalized antiplatelet strategies.

Overall, our findings also support current approaches favoring guided antiplatelet therapy, as increasing aspirin dose alone may not be sufficient to reduce thrombotic risk in high-risk patients.5,6

The present study is limited by its retrospective single-center design that limits causal inference and by the lack of clinical outcome data, which precludes assessment of associations between residual platelet reactivity patterns and cardiovascular or bleeding event.

In conclusion, a substantial proportion of aspirin-treated patients exhibit residual platelet reactivity driven by 2 distinct mechanisms: incomplete COX-1 inhibition and COX-1–independent platelet activation, representing a TxA2-independent hyperactive platelet phenotype. Both mechanisms can be effectively identified using PA testing.

These results support the use of a multiagonist platelet function testing approach, including collagen stimulation. This approach may improve identification of aspirin-treated patients with residual platelet reactivity and potentially higher cardiovascular risk. However, the present findings do not allow definitive causal inference within a multifactorial thrombotic context. Larger prospective studies are needed to better define the multiagonist approach in antiplatelet therapy evaluation efficacy.

Advancing our understanding and clinical integration of all determinants of antiplatelet drugs to fully characterize the benefit-risk profile constitutes an important step forward in the progression to personalized antiplatelet therapy. In this context, incorporating the stratification based on AA and multiagonist response into clinical decision-making could be considered as a valuable tool in optimizing antiplatelet therapy, paving the way toward more individualized treatment strategies to assess the residual cardiovascular risk optimizing clinical decisions.28
