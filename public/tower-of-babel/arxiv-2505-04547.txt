Birkhoff normal form via decorated trees
Jacob Armstrong-Goodall 1, Yvain Bruned 2

Abstract
We derive an explicit tree based ansatz for the Birkhoff normal form up to any order in the context of Hamiltonian PDEs. To do so we make use of a tree based representation of iterated Poisson brackets to encode the nested Taylor expansions along flows of a sequence of symplectic transformations. As an example we consider the cubic Schrödinger equation.

Introduction

The Birkhoff normal form is a classical technique in the study of Hamiltonian systems. Its purpose is to reduce the Hamiltonian to a simpler form via a series of symplectic transformations, leaving only resonant terms and a non-resonant remainder. Perhaps the most prevalent application of the Birkhoff normal form is in the development of KAM theory, which seeks to establish the long-term existence of quasi-periodic solutions under small perturbations in the energy. This problem was posed by Kolmogorov in 1954 and proved independently by Moser and Arnol’d in
[
24
]
and
[
1
]
, respectively. The extension to infinite dimensions took place throughout the ’80s and ’90s in various works by Kuksin, Pöschel, Craig, Wayne, Bourgain, and many others
[
8
,
12
,
20
,
21
,
30
]
. A generalization of the Birkhoff normal form theorem to Hamiltonian PDEs was performed by Bambusi in
[
2
]
, where it is proved that there exists a canonical transformation which reduces the Hamiltonian to its normal form up to order (N). This was followed up in work with Grébert
[
6
]
, which demonstrates the applicability of this technique to a wide range of PDEs with a tame modulus condition. In
[
5
]
, the stability result of
[
6
]
was extended to a general class of fully resonant Schrödinger equations by introducing the rational normal form reduction. For a survey on the topic of normal forms in the context of PDE, see
[
16
]
. Since then, applications in the study of PDEs include demonstrations of global well-posedness
[
4
,

[

, long-term behavior of small solutions

[

, Krikorian proves that the Birkhoff normal form is generally divergent, determining which of the two alternatives of Pérez-Marco’s theorem in
[

]
is true.

Another well-known technique in the study of Hamiltonian systems is the Poincaré-Dulac normal form. This technique is more flexible than the Birkhoff variety studied here because it can be applied to Hamiltonian systems where the energy is not conserved. This flexibility comes at the expense of tracking information about the geometry of the space, meaning Poincaré-Dulac offers only a local picture of the dynamics. The Poincaré-Dulac approach is widely used to establish local well-posedness, for example in

In the present work, we introduce an explicit formula for the Birkhoff normal form using decorated trees. The formulation of these trees is distinct from those in
[
10
,
11
]
and is uniquely suited to describe Taylor expansions along Hamiltonian flows. The approach involves defining the symplectic transformations recursively and encoding the structure of the Poisson brackets into trees, resulting in a relatively intuitive formulation of the Birkhoff normal form to any order. One of the benefits of this approach is its generality; although the Schrödinger equation is used as an example, the rules for generating the correct trees are independent of the equation. This work may also pave the way for a deeper understanding of the combinatorial structure of resonances in Hamiltonian PDEs. It is somewhat surprising that no explicit form for such a widely used technique has appeared prior, but to our knowledge, this is the first presentation of such in the literature.

Let us briefly introduce this formalism.
One starts with an Hamiltonian of the form:

H=H_{0}+H_{1}

where in the case of NLS,
H_{0}
is a monomial of degree
2
and
H_{1}
is a monomial of degree
4
. The first step is to decompose
H_{1}
into

H_{1}=H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{res}}}+H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}

where
H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{res}}}
is the resonant part and
H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}
is the non-resonant part. One gets

H=H_{0}+H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{res}}}+H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}.

Then, one wants to compose
H
with a symplectic transform
F
for eliminating
H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}
and get higher resonant terms. It means that

H\circ F=H+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{{\{H,F\}}^{n}}{n!}+R_{N+1}

with some remainder
R_{N+1}
and we ask
F
to satisfy

{\{H_{0},F\}}=-H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}.

This allows us to make disappear the term
H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}
. This procedure can be iterated with successive
F_{i}
and it produces the Birkhoff normal form. The main result of this paper, Theorem
4.3
, provides an explicit formula of this decomposition with decorated trees:
One has for
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with
m<\ell
.

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{m}^{\ell}=(H\circ F_{1}\circ\cdots\circ F_{m})^{\ell}

\displaystyle=H_{0}+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!<m+2}_{r}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)}+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!m+2}_{\circ}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}_{\circ}^{m+2,\ell}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)}

Moreover, one has

F_{i}=\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!i+1}_{n}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)},\quad{\{H_{0},F_{i}\}}=-\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!i+1}_{\circ}}\frac{(\Pi T)^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}}{S(T)},

where

•

\mathscr{H}^{\ell}_{m}
is the
m
-th order normal form truncated at order
2\ell
.

•

\mathcal{T}^{\!m}_{\circ}
(resp.
\mathcal{T}^{\!m,\ell}_{\circ}
) are decorated trees encoding iterated brackets where the outer bracket is
{\{\cdot,\cdot\}}
and with the size
p
of the monomials to be equal to
2m
(resp.
2m<p\leq 2\ell
).

•

\mathcal{T}^{\!<m}_{r}
encodes monomials of size strictly smaller than
2m
with outer bracket
{\{\cdot,\cdot\}}^{\text{\tiny{res}}}
. This means that one keeps only the resonant part of the iterated bracket.

•

\mathcal{T}^{\!m}_{n}

encodes monomials of size
2m
with outer bracket
{\{\cdot,\cdot\}}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}_{\Phi}
. This means that one keeps only the non-resonant part of the iterated bracket divided by a well-chosen phase.

•

F_{i}
is an order
2i+2
symplectic Hamiltonians,

•

The operator
\Pi
maps the tree back to a term consisting of iterated Poisson brackets while
S(T)
corresponds to the coefficient of the Taylor expansion.

This theorem presents an explicit expression for the normal form up to any order. It is proved via induction. Below, we provide some examples of decorated trees:

2
Hamiltonian Equations

Consider the infinite-dimensional phase space
M
with coordinates
\mu_{k}
and momenta
\nu_{k}
, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then the Hamiltonian is a function
H:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and the solution to the Hamiltonian system is a curve
{{\left(\mu_{k}(t),\nu_{k}(t)\right)}}
in
M
which obeys the Hamiltonain equations of motion

\dot{\mu}_{k}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\nu_{k}},\quad\dot{\nu}_{k}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mu_{k}}.

(1)

When the Hamiltonian is time independent,
\partial H/\partial t=0
, the value of the function
H(\mu,\nu)
is conserved over the evolution of the solutions of the equation. This conservation law can also be expressed via the symplectic form,

\omega(\mu,\nu)=\int d\mu(t)\wedge d\nu(t)dx=\int d\mu_{0}\wedge d\nu_{0}dx,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(2)

The pair
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold. This identity corresponds to the conservation of energy in physical systems.

Remark 2.1

The evenness of the dimensionality of the phase space is a corrolary of the well know theorem of Darboux which states that any point of
M
has local coordinates
(\mu,\nu)={{\left({\{\mu_{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}},{\{\nu_{k}\}}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\right)}}
, in which the symplectic structure has the form
\omega=\sum_{k}d\mu_{k}\wedge\partial\nu_{k}
. These are called the symplectic or canonical coordinates.

For a smooth function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)
the symplectic form associates a smooth vector field
X_{f}
on
M
implicitly defined by
\omega(X_{f},\cdot)=df
where
df
is the exterior derivative of
f
.
This definition allows us to introduce the Poisson bracket as an operation on functions, that is, as the symplectic form
\omega(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{k}\mu_{k}\wedge\nu_{k}
between two Hamiltonian vector fields
X_{F}
and
X_{G}

{\{F,G\}}=\omega(X_{F},X_{G}).

Definition 2.2

The Poisson bracket, defined as a differential operator on functions
f,g:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is given in canonical coordinates by:

{\{f,g\}}=i\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{\left(\partial_{\mu_{k}}f\partial_{\nu_{k}}g-\partial_{\mu_{k}}g\partial_{\nu_{k}}f\right)}

Furthermore, a Hamiltonian vector field
X_{F}
can be applied as a directional derivative to a sufficiently smooth function
g

X_{F}(g)={\{g,F\}}.

Specifically the vector field
X_{H}
associated to a Hamiltonian
F
is defined as

X_{F}={{\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mu_{k}},-\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nu_{k}}\right)}}=JdF

where

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{k}\\
-I_{k}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad dF=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu_{k}}\\
\frac{\partial F}{\partial\nu_{k}}.\end{pmatrix}

In this formulation for example if
F=\mu_{k}
then
X_{F}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu_{k}}

This allows us to write the following definition:

Definition 2.3

(Hamiltonain Taylor Expansion)

We define the Taylor expansion of
g
along the flow generated by the Hamiltonian vector field by

g\circ F=g+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{{\{g,F\}}^{n}}{n!}+R_{N+1}

where the remainder term is,

R_{n}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\{f,H\}}^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}(1-s)^{n+1}ds.

(3)

Notation 2.4

Here we use the notation

{\{X,Y\}}^{n}={\{{\{\dots{\{X,Y\}},\dots,Y\}},Y\}}

where the right hand side has
n
nested brackets.

Next we will introduce the cubic Schrödinger equation as an example of a Hamiltonain PDE exhibiting a conserved symplectic structure

2.1
The Cubic Schrödinger Equation

As a running example, we consider the defocusing cubic Schrödinger equation on the
d-
dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
given by:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta u=|u|^{2}u\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\end{cases}\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}.

(4)

This equation has two conserved quantities. The first is the mass described by the
L^{2}
norm

{{\left|{\left|u\right|}\right|}}_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|u_{k}|^{2}.

The second conserved quantity is the Hamiltonian which in physical space is:

\mathscr{H}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}{{\left|{\left|\nabla u\right|}\right|}}^{2}_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}||u||^{4}_{L^{2}},

which can likewise be written under the Fourier transform as

\mathscr{H}_{0}(u,\bar{u})=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{{\left|k\right|}}^{2}{{\left|u_{k}\right|}}^{2}+\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}\end{subarray}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}.

(5)

For the Schrödinger equation with integer nonlinearity the Fourier coefficients introduced by the nonlinear term in the Hamiltonian are zero unless
k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}-k_{4}=0
. This is the result of the Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u_{k}e^{ikx},\quad u_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}ue^{-ikx}dx.

From this the nonlinear or interaction part of the Hamiltonian becomes

H_{1}=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{G}_{1234}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}

where

\mathcal{G}_{1234}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}{e^{i(k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}-k_{4})x}}dx=\begin{cases}2\pi&k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}-k_{4}=0\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

From now on for clarity of presentation, we will assume this condition to hold in the case of our Hamiltonain, and later the symplectic transforms introduced to eliminate non-resonant terms. The Hamiltonian
5
allows us to write the nonlinear Schödinger equation in its Hamiltonain form as

\partial_{t}u_{k}=\partial_{\bar{u}_{k}}\mathscr{H}_{0}(u,\bar{u}),\quad\partial_{t}\bar{u}_{k}=-\partial_{u_{k}}\mathscr{H}_{0}(u,\bar{u}).

(6)

We denote the phase function on
\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2n}
for a given term in the Hamiltonian by

\Phi_{n}(\bm{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(-1)^{i-1}k_{i}^{2}.

For instance the phase function corresponding to the quartic term in the Hamiltonian
5
is

\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})=k_{1}^{2}-k_{2}^{2}+k_{3}^{2}-k_{4}^{2}.

We define the set of frequencies for which this is ‘nearly resonant’ by

\mathcal{R}_{n;N}={\{\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2n}:{{\left|\Phi_{n}(\bm{k})\right|}}\leq N\}}.

A special case of this is when the phase is identically zero. In that case we have

\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})=0,\quad k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}-k_{4}=0,

meaning that the terms must be paired together. We do not restrict ourselves to this setting because almost-resonance as defined above is very useful, for instance when bounding Sobolev norms in
[
13
]
. By convention we will just refer to resonant terms when we mean almost resonant, with ‘purely resonant’ as a special case. The indicator function over the resonant frequencies
\mathcal{R}_{n}
is written

\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{R}_{n}}(\bm{k};N)=\begin{cases}1,\quad{{\left|\Phi_{n}(\bm{k})\right|}}\leq N\\
0,\quad{{\left|\Phi_{n}(\bm{k})\right|}}>N.\end{cases}

Definition 2.5

With the phase and indicator function defined as above we will introduce functions of the following form to filter out resonant terms in the Birkhoff normal form,

\mathcal{F}_{n,N}(\bm{k})=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{R}_{n}^{c}}(\bm{k})}{2\Phi_{n}(\bm{k})}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\Phi_{n}(\bm{k})},\quad&{{\left|\Phi_{n}(\bm{k})\right|}}>N\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(7)

These definitions provide us with the ingredients required to explain the Birkhoff normal form in the case of (
4
). This process easily generalises to other Hamiltonian PDEs.

3
The Birkhoff Normal Form Reduction

We have the Hamiltonian

\mathscr{H}_{0}=H_{0}+H_{1}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{{\left|k\right|}}^{2}{{\left|u_{k}\right|}}^{2}+\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}.

The algorithm works by splitting
H_{1}
into resonant part
H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{res}}}
and non-resonant part
H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}
before composing with a symplectic transform that removes the non-resonant terms. For the first iteration we write

H_{1}=H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{res}}}+H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}},

where

\displaystyle H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{res}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}\\
\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})\leq N\end{subarray}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}},

\displaystyle H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}\\
\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})>N\end{subarray}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}},

and

\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})=k_{1}^{2}-k_{2}^{2}+k_{3}^{2}-k_{4}^{2}.

The complete Hamiltonian can now be written as

\mathscr{H}_{0}=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{{\left|k\right|}}^{2}{{\left|u_{k}\right|}}^{2}+\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}\\
\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})>N\end{subarray}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}+\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}\\
\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})\leq N\end{subarray}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}.

(8)

We then introduce a symplectic transform
\Gamma_{F_{1}}
such that

{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}}=-H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}.

Such a transform can be written as

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}}\mathcal{F}_{2,N}(\bm{k})u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}

\displaystyle:=-H_{1,\Phi}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}.

(9)

The second line is a convenient notation for the the first which follows from definition
2.5
with,

\mathcal{F}_{2,N}(\bm{k})=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{R}_{2,N}^{c}}(\bm{k})}{2\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})}.

It’s easy to check that with
F_{1}
defined above the identity
{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}}=-H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}
holds. One has

\displaystyle{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}}\mathcal{F}_{2}(\bm{k}){\left(\partial_{u_{k}}(|k|^{2}|u_{k}|^{2})\partial_{\bar{u}_{k}}(u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}})\right.}

\displaystyle{\left.-\partial_{\bar{u}_{k}}(|k|^{2}|u_{k}|^{2})\partial_{u_{k}}(u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}})\right)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}}(k_{1}^{2}-k_{2}^{2}+k_{3}^{2}-k_{4}^{2})\mathcal{F}_{2}(\bm{k})u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{R}_{2,N}^{c}}(\bm{k})u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k}\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{4}\\
\Phi_{2}(\bm{k})>N\end{subarray}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}=-H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}.

(10)

When we write
F_{1}=H_{1,\Phi}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}
the subscript of
\Phi
refers to the phase function introduced by the laplacians in the kinetic part of the Hamiltonain in the Poisson bracket
{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}}
, which we will see used frequently when defining
F_{i}
.

The Birkhoff normal form is constructed as a sequence of Taylor expansions along the flows of each
F_{i}
, which by construction leaves only resonant terms and a non-resonant remainder. We will now introduce a norm for quantifying the order of terms in the Hamiltonain Taylor expansion which is introduced in
[
21
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Sobolev Norm on Sequences)

Define the space
\ell^{2,p}_{b}
to be the set of bi-infinite sequences such that

{{\left|{\left|u\right|}\right|}}_{a,p}^{2}={{\left|q_{0}\right|}}^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{{\left|u_{k}\right|}}^{2}{{\left|k\right|}}^{2p}e^{2{{\left|k\right|}}a}<\infty.

We also make use of
[
21
, Lemma 2]
which is the following bilinear estimate.

Lemma 3.2

For
a\geq 0
and
p\geq\frac{1}{2}
, the space
\ell^{a,p}_{b}
is a Hilbert algebra with respect to the convolution of sequences, and

{{\left|{\left|u\star u^{\prime}\right|}\right|}}_{a,p}\leq c{{\left|{\left|u\right|}\right|}}_{a,p}{{\left|{\left|u^{\prime}\right|}\right|}}_{a,p},

where
c
depends on
p
only.

In this norm the interaction Hamiltonian of the cubic Schrödinger equation is
H_{1}=\mathcal{O}({{\left|{\left|u\right|}\right|}}_{a,p}^{4})
. There are other norms on iterated Poisson brackets such as the one used by Bourgain in
[
9
, Section 3]
, used also in
[
13
, Equation 2.12]
.
By composing
H
with this transform we obtain the first iteration of the Birkhoff normal form,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{1}

\displaystyle=H\circ F_{1}

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{1}+{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}}+{\{H_{1},F_{1}\}}+\frac{1}{2}{\{{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}},F_{1}\}}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{1}(1-s){\{{\{H_{1},F_{1}\}},F_{1}\}}ds

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{res}}}+{\{H_{1},F_{1}\}}+\frac{1}{2}{\{{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}},F_{1}\}}+\mathcal{O}({{\left|{\left|u\right|}\right|}}^{8}_{a,p})

where we have constructed the desired cancellation

{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}}=-H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}.

Now we look at the lowest order terms which still contains non-resonant frequencies which are

{\{H_{1},F_{1}\}},\quad\frac{1}{2}{\{{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}},F_{1}\}}.

We introduce a second symplectic transform
\Gamma_{F_{2}}
characterised by the property that

\displaystyle-{\{H_{0},F_{2}\}}

\displaystyle={\{H_{1},F_{1}\}}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}+\frac{1}{2}{\{{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}},F_{1}\}}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}

Theorem 4.3

Let
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with
m<\ell

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{m}^{\ell}=(H\circ F_{1}\circ\cdots\circ F_{m})^{\ell}

\displaystyle=H_{0}+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!<m+2}_{r}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)}+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!m+2}_{\circ}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)}

\displaystyle+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}_{\circ}^{m+2,\ell}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)}

Moreover, one has

F_{i}=\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!i+1}_{n}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)},\quad{\{H_{0},F_{i}\}}=-\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!i+1}_{\circ}}\frac{(\Pi T)^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}}}{S(T)}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
m
. We suppose the property proved for
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

m=1
. In that case, one has

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{1}^{\ell}=(H\circ F_{1})^{\ell}

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{1}+{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}}+{\{H_{1},F_{1}\}}+\frac{1}{2}{\{{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}},F_{1}\}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}{\{{\{H_{1},F_{1}\}},F_{1}\}}+\sum_{n=3}^{\ell}\frac{{\{H,F_{1}\}}^{n}}{n!}.

By definition, one has

{\{H_{0},F_{1}\}}=-H_{1}^{\text{\tiny{non-res}}},

which gives

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{1}^{\ell}=

\displaystyle H_{0}+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!<3}_{r}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)}+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}^{\!3}_{\circ}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)}+\sum_{T\in\,\mathcal{T}_{\circ}^{3,\ell}}\frac{(\Pi T)}{S(T)},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\{{\{H_{1},F_{1}\}},F_{1}\}}+\sum_{n=3}^{\ell}\frac{{\{H,F_{1}\}}^{n}}{n!}.
