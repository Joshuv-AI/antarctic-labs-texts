Deciphering seasonal dynamics and time series forecasting of urban air quality: a case study in Urban Cities of Andhra Pradesh
Authors: D Shreyas, B Nishith, N Neelima, T V Smitha, Vivek Venugopal, Tolga Ozer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProper air quality forecasting is essential in developing countries such as India, where climate variability, industrialization, and increasing urbanization play a major role in degrading air quality and posing health risks. This research introduces an integrated machine learning (ML) architecture for the prediction of the Air Quality Index (AQI) in two Andhra Pradesh urban cities, Visakhapatnam and Vijayawada, and one city in Telangana, Hyderabad based on five years of pollutant and meteorological data. This approach combines a deep feedforward neural network (FNN) with residual blocks and several traditional regression techniques, viz., Random Forest, Lasso, and Gradient Boosting, to both predict AQI directly and impute it through pollutant-wise modeling in accordance with CPCB standards. Imposing a large amount of feature engineering like temporal lags, rolling statistics, and pollutant interactions was used to identify spatiotemporal dynamics. The unified advanced FNN model attained values of 0.965, 0.97, and 0.96, and Root Mean Square Errors (RMSE) of 10.0, 11.25, and 13.17 for Vijayawada, Hyderabad, and Visakhapatnam respectively. Furthermore, predictions for pollutant-specific values in 2025 showed close conformity with real AQI values (, RMSE = 2.68, 5.84, 5.30 for Random Forest for Vijayawada, Hyderabad, Visakhapatnam respectively) when predicted from estimated pollutant concentrations. This research illustrates a scalable method for AQI forecasting that is capable of informing real-time policy and public health intervention in data-scarce settings.Keywords: Air Quality Index, Pollution, Time series forecasting, Deep learning, Machine learning, Feedforward neural network, Random forestSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Air pollution has mounted to a worldwide public health crisis, representing the globe’s largest environmental hazard to human well-being. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 99% of the world population inhales air with pollutant levels above its guideline limits1. A revised review from the Lancet Commission on health and pollution reaffirms the severity of this crisis, with pollution still accounting for approximately 9 million premature deaths each year, a figure that translates to one in six deaths globally2. Exposure to major criteria pollutants—fine and coarse particulate matter ( and ), nitrogen dioxide (), sulfur dioxide (), carbon monoxide (CO), and ground-level ozone ()—is causally associated with a broad variety of non-communicable diseases, most significantly stroke, ischemic heart disease, lung cancer, and acute and chronic respiratory diseases1,3. The scientific evidence of these health effects continues to grow, with new evidence pointing towards chemical pollutants causing developmental neurotoxicity, reproductive toxicity, and immunotoxicity, and leading to heightened susceptibility to infectious disease2. India is at the forefront of this crisis worldwide. Air pollution alone killed 1.67 million people in the country in 2019, which was no less than 17.8% of all deaths4,5. These statistics unveil an alarming trend: as the mortality rate from indoor air pollution has declined by considerable amounts since 1990, this achievement has been countered by a 115.3% rise in the mortality rate due to ambient particulate matter air pollution during the same time4. The danger is widespread, and studies have established that India’s entire population resides where concentrations exceed WHO standards6. This country burden is most dramatically experienced in the state of Andhra Pradesh, which is the subject of this report. In 2017, the state had an estimated 45,525 fatalities due to air pollution, of which 23,280 were from ambient and 19,345 from household sources7. The large number of deaths due to household pollution is an indication of a long-standing local problem; in 2015–2016, 38% of all Andhra Pradesh households, and up to 50% in rural communities, continued to cook using solid fuels8. This poses a complicated dual burden, with the “modern” issue of increasing ambient pollution due to urbanization and industrialization, combined with the “traditional” issue of indoor air pollution. Aside from the ruinous health implications, air pollution carries a suffocating economic burden that could slow national development objectives. Utilizing the cost-of-illness approach, conservatively, the lost economic output due to premature mortality and morbidity caused by air pollution cost India $36.8 billion in 2019, representing 1.36% of its Gross Domestic Product (GDP)4,9. This estimate most probably falls short of the actual burden. A wider analysis covering business-oriented impacts—like diminished in-work productivity, lower customer foot traffic, and staff absence—estimates the yearly economic cost as much as $95 billion, or about 3% of India’s GDP10. This economic loss is not evenly distributed; the loss as a proportion of state GDP is disproportionately higher in poorer states, which can least afford to lose it4,11. With the complex, non-linear, and spatio-temporal characteristics of air pollution, robust Air Quality Index (AQI) forecasting is now a vital tool for preventive action. Forecasts are now no longer an intellectual exercise but a pragmatic requirement for driving public health early-warning systems, facilitating regulation compliance, and informing sustainable urban design3,12. In India, predictions are already being utilised to guide public health advisories by the National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) and activate policy responses under systems such as the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) in Delhi13,14. In this study, Visakhapatnam and Vijayawada, two of the main urban centers of Andhra Pradesh, and Hyderabad, the capital of Telangana are the focus areas, where proactive instruments are urgently needed. Although conventional statistical approaches tend not to unravel sophisticated relations between pollutants, the ML and DL models present an attractive alternative, as they identify subtle patterns in high-dimensional temporal data15,16. This paper formulates and compares rigorously two different forecasting philosophies: a straightforward method employing a deep Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) to predict the final AQI value directly, and an indirect method where individual pollutant levels are first predicted employing a set of ML models. By comparing these methods formally, this paper hopes to determine a scalable and reliable methodology for high-accuracy AQI prediction in a high-stakes setting.

Related work

Current air quality forecasting has increasingly moved away from conventional statistical models towards advanced machine learning and deep learning models capable of accurately capturing the non-linear interactions between meteorological variables and pollutant levels. In this literature survey, recent development is highlighted, with a special focus on deep learning architectures, hybrid models, and current trends in spatio-temporal modeling and explainability of models. The use of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) models17, has emerged as the backbone of current air quality time-series forecasting. These models are specifically designed to learn temporal dependencies and long-term patterns in sequential data. For instance, recent research has shown that LSTM-based models, when they are trained on past pollutant and meteorological records, are capable of accurately predicting concentrations of with high accuracy18,19. Experiments comparing various forms of RNNs have tended to show that GRU models can be as performant as LSTMs but with more computational efficiency and thus are well-suited for real-time applications20. The Transformer model, initially created to handle natural language processing, has similarly found application in air quality prediction based on its self-attention to capture notable temporal patterns within long sequences21. Researchers have also created hybrid models combining the best aspects of various architectures to further enhance predictive performance. One popular and effective method is the CNN-LSTM model, which employs Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to learn important local features from the input, which are then fed into an LSTM layer to capture the temporal sequence22. This hybrid architecture, along with other integrated deep learning models, enables learning of both spatial and temporal dependencies simultaneously23. Additional research has investigated the integration of empirical models such as the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) model with machine learning methodologies in order to bias correct, with dramatic enhancement of deterministic air quality forecasts accuracy24,25. A significant development in this field is the application of LSTM-based autoencoders, which learn a compressed latent form of the past pollution observations initially and then make future forecasts using an LSTM decoder, which has been effective in extracting key temporal features while eliminating noise26. Comparative performance evaluation across machine learning models is another key research area. A study conducted in Bengaluru assessed Decision Tree, Random Forest, Artificial Neural Network, and Random Forest algorithms for AQI prediction using data from five stations across the city (Peenya, Bapuji Nagar, Silk Board, Hombegowda, and BTM Layout) collected between 2021 and 2023. Their results showed that Random Forest significantly outperformed Decision Tree and ANN in both regression and classification tasks. This highlights how model performance is influenced by local pollutant and weather combinations, reinforcing the importance of selecting models tailored to specific locations27. Yet another research area has centered on utilizing IoT sensor networks to monitor air quality in real-time in smart cities, using machine learning models in processing the high-speed data streams28,29. Acknowledging that air pollution is a spatio-temporal issue, recent efforts have shifted towards models that have specific spatial relationships incorporated into them. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have proven to be a useful tool for such an endeavor. In a GNN architecture, monitoring sites are considered graph nodes, and edges symbolize the geographical connectivity or proximity between them. This enables the model to discover how pollution in one site affects its neighbors, resulting in more precise regional predictions, particularly in regions with incomplete monitoring coverage30,31. In addition to prediction precision, a number of future directions are influencing air quality modeling. Explainable AI (XAI) methods, including SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), are being used to “open the black box” of deep learning models. They provide information about which pollutants or meteorological variables are most critical drivers of a particular prediction, increasing model transparency and trust32,33. Additionally, with the expansion of monitoring networks, edge computing and federated learning are gaining popularity. Edge-AI-powered systems facilitate real-time mapping of pollution by processing data directly on power-efficient devices34, while federated learning can train models on various cities or regions without centralizing sensitive information, thereby maintaining privacy35. In short, the literature clearly follows a line of progression towards integrated, context-aware, and more sophisticated forecasting systems. The most sophisticated models best integrate sequential data processing, spatio-temporal analysis, and hybrid architectures. Beyond these developments, however, is still a gap between holistic, end-to-end deep learning models and multi-stage, granular frameworks predicting individual pollutants stepwise first. This research seeks to rectify this gap by formally comparing both approaches on a standardized dataset. Despite numerous machine learning models being applied successfully to AQI forecasting, there are several limitations in existing research. The majority of studies are confined to a single urban area and hence their application to other cities is not possible. Most studies also compare similar types of models instead of comparing drastically different forecasting methodologies. There is a vital gap in direct empirical comparison between single-step holistic deep learning models and granular approaches that first predict individual pollutants and then AQI. This raises the question of what the optimal methodological strategy is for maximizing accuracy. In response to these gaps, the main contributions of this work are the following.

A hybrid AQI prediction system was unified through the integration of deep FNNs with conventional ML models for both direct AQI and pollutant-level predictions.

Data and methods

The section describes the entire methodological setup followed in AQI prediction. It starts with data gathering from three cities of India—Hydrabad, Visakhapatnam and Vijayawada—over 5 years (2020–2024) of pollutant and meteorological features from four locations. Data preprocessing includes the handling of missing values, outliers, and time alignment. Exploratory analysis and feature engineering are subsequently used to create lag features, rolling statistics, and interaction terms. Two modeling approaches are applied, which are direct prediction of AQI with deep FNNs and pollutant-by-pollutant prediction with regressors and subsequent computation of AQI. Performance is assessed over a range of time horizons, and seasonal trends in AQI are geospatially visualized.

Study area and data acquisition

Vijayawada, Visakhapatnam, and Hyderabad, are major cities in Andhra Pradesh (although Hyderabad is now the capital of Telangana, it faces similar air quality issues to that state’s cities). A map of India with all the districts in which the cities are located is shown in Fig. 1. All three are hugely suffering from increasing air pollution due to runaway industrialization, vehicle growth, and aggressive construction activity. Similar to your sample cities, air quality is seasonal in these cities, making them ideal candidates to ascertain the role of weather patterns and pollutant dynamics in playing a role in variability in AQI. For example, Vijayawada frequently experiences high AQI during late autumn and winter due to stable atmospheric conditions, with improvement during monsoons, owing to industrial emissions (petcoke and coal use) and vehicular movement. Visakhapatnam, which is a port city with high industrial concentration (steel plants, refineries), also experiences similar seasonal fluctuation, with industrial and vehicular emissions, and issues like ash pond management, being major contributors. Hyderabad, being a sprawling industrial and technology city, also experiences strong seasonal fluctuations, with winters generally experiencing a high increase in concentrations above summers, primarily due to vehicular emissions, road dust, and open burning, all further compounded by prevailing meteorological conditions which trap pollutants. Data preprocessing

A strict data preprocessing pipeline was executed to transform the raw data from the three observation stations into a clean, ordered, and normalized form. This preliminary step is paramount to ensure the reliability of the models and the precision of their projections. The following steps were applied to all the three cities’ datasets.

Data cleaning and type conversion

The initial step was to resolve structural and data type inconsistencies. Column labels were all programmatically trimmed to remove leading and trailing spaces in order to ensure uniform feature naming. The Timestamp column was also cast to a uniform datetime object for proper temporal indexing required for time-series analysis. Pollutant and meteorological features were all cast to numeric data types.

Missing value handling

Even though an initial check for data integrity revealed datasets to be largely complete, a specific imputation strategy was employed for the visibility feature to achieve a fully completed dataset. Listwise deletion was otherwise deliberately performed after feature engineering for all primary pollutants. While imputation techniques exist, listwise deletion was chosen to ensure the models learned from pristine, actual atmospheric states rather than synthetic or hallucinated multivariate relationships, which is critical for mapping complex non-linear chemical interactions accurately. Delaying deletion until after feature engineering maximizes data retention while ensuring models are trained exclusively on complete physical records37.

Treatment of outliers

The extremes of AQI readings can occasionally skew visualizations. It is critical to note that all models in this study were trained and evaluated on completely unclipped, raw continuous data to preserve the natural variance and physical integrity of severe pollution events. Clipping at an upper bound of 300 was strictly utilized as a visualization technique for specific exploratory plots to improve readability.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA)

A comprehensive exploratory data analysis was carried out in order to uncover the intrinsic temporal dynamics and inter-variable trends underlying air quality. The results of this EDA directly informed the feature engineering process. Each of the three cities’ datasets exhibited comparable patterns, indicating strength of the trends discovered. Long-term temporal behavior of air quality was investigated, revealing regular cyclical patterns representing annual cycles, with pollution levels increasing during winter and post-monsoon months. This macroscopic viewpoint confirms the non-stationary nature of the data and supports the necessity of modeling its temporal dependencies.

Feature engineering

To uncover the intrinsic temporal dynamics and complex non-linear relationships underlying air quality, two distinct feature engineering strategies were formulated. The first strategy employs a comprehensive, unified pipeline designed to capture long-term dependencies, volatility, and seasonal cyclicity for the direct AQI forecasting models across all three cities. Conversely, the second strategy adopts a targeted approach for the indirect regression frameworks, focusing strictly on concurrent inter-pollutant interactions, meteorological drivers, and immediate past pollution trends to forecast individual target pollutants.

Feature engineering for direct AQI forecasting (FNN model)

To capture the intricate temporal dynamics and relationships in air quality data, an elaborate and unified feature engineering technique was adopted for the point forecast task of direct AQI prediction with an FNN across all three cities (Vijayawada, Hyderabad, and Visakhapatnam). This ensures methodological fairness and equips all models with advanced temporal representations. Initially, extended lag features were created by including the values of all numeric columns for the seven preceding time intervals (lag1 to lag7). This gives memory to the model and effectively converts the forecasting process into a supervised learning issue38,39. Moreover, advanced rolling statistics were calculated, such as 3- and 7-period rolling means to reflect local trends, first-order differences to express the momentum or rate of change in every variable, and exponential weighted moving averages with spans of 3 and 7 to capture near-volatility. For incorporating temporal cyclicity found through exploratory analysis, calendar features such as the day of the year and hour of the day were transformed into cyclical features using sine and cosine transformations to better represent their continuous periodic nature40. To allow the model to learn synergistic associations between co-occurring contaminants, interaction terms were created by forming pairwise products of contaminant concentrations. Lastly, squared terms for all contaminants were added in order to capture intrinsic non-linearities in their relationship with AQI41. To effectively process these advanced temporal features and manage the complex distributions of the augmented dataset, methodologies akin to those proposed in novel hybrid modeling frameworks were considered42. This rich set of features enables the FNN to learn efficiently from historical trends, volatility, seasonal cycles, and nonlinear interactions within the data.

To address the potential structural multicollinearity introduced by the extensive use of interaction and squared terms, all primary temporal and pollutant features were mean-centered prior to feature crossing. Following this standardization, Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were computed for the complete engineered feature set. The computed VIF values ranged between 2.14 and 8.36, with a mean VIF of 4.52. These values strictly remain below the commonly accepted theoretical threshold of 10, indicating that severe multicollinearity was successfully mitigated. Furthermore, the feedforward neural network’s architecture is inherently less sensitive to any residual correlated inputs due to its non-linear weight optimization. Specifically, the decoupled weight decay in the AdamW optimizer and the extensive use of Dropout layers mathematically penalized redundant weights, and no numerical instability was observed during the model’s convergence.

Theoretical background of forecasting models

This section describes the mathematical background and general principles of the ML and DL models that act as forecasting agents in this study.

FNN with residual blocks

A FNN is a DL architecture under which there is no loop between the nodes. Input data is passed from an input layer, via one or more than one hidden layers, to an output layer. Notably, Residual Blocks are used to address the vanishing gradient issue44. They contain a “shortcut connection” to backpropagate the gradient directly. Random forest (RF)

Random Forest is a kind of ensemble learning algorithm that constructs numerous decision trees when it is being trained45. It is better than single decision trees since it utilizes two strategic techniques: Bootstrap Aggregating (Bagging) and random selection of features at each node split. Gradient boosting (GB)

Gradient Boosting is an ensemble strategy that builds models sequentially in an iteration by iteration sequence46. It begins with a simple starting model and then adds subsequent models (typically decision trees) sequentially, which are trained to forecast the residual errors of the preceding ensemble. It can be viewed as a form of functional gradient descent, where the algorithm iteratively makes steps in the function space with the aim of minimizing the overall loss function.

Regularized linear models (Lasso)

Regularized linear models are a generalization of Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression that incorporate a penalty term into the loss function in order to prevent overfitting.

The L1 penalty drives some coefficients exactly to zero and hence Lasso is a good choice for automatic feature selection.

Autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA)

To establish a rigorous statistical baseline for the indirect forecasting approach, the ARIMA model was implemented. ARIMA models time series data by combining autoregression (lags of the stationary series), differencing (to achieve stationarity), and moving averages (lags of the forecast errors). While highly interpretable, traditional ARIMA typically assumes linear relationships, serving as a benchmark to demonstrate the superiority of non-linear ML and DL algorithms in capturing complex pollution dynamics.

Forecasting frameworks

Two methodologically different frameworks were created and tested for forecasting the AQI. The first framework uses a deep neural network to forecast the final AQI value directly, and the second framework uses classical ML models to forecast individual pollutant concentrations, which are then used to reconstruct the final AQI.

Approach 1: Direct AQI forecasting for all cities

A unified Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) architecture was employed across Vijayawada, Hyderabad, and Visakhapatnam to capture complex, non-linear patterns and ensure cross-city comparability. The model is trained on the comprehensively engineered feature set, which includes lag, rolling, temporal, and interaction features.

Model architecture: FNN with residual blocks: As described in the implementation, an FNN was developed using the Keras API. The architecture starts with an input layer accepting the full engineered feature set, followed by a stack of dense layers with descending units (1024, 512, 256, 128, 64) utilizing LeakyReLU activation. To enhance training stability and address the vanishing gradient problem, residual connections were implemented via a custom residual block function48. Within each block, BatchNormalization was applied after the first dense layer to stabilize and accelerate training43. Dropout regularization was used (with rates 0.3 and 0.2 inside blocks, and 0.3 after the first layer) to minimize overfitting. The final output is generated by a single dense neuron. Hyperparameters of the proposed model, including the number of hidden layers, number of neurons per layer, learning rate, batch size, and dropout rate, were selected through systematic experimentation. Several candidate configurations were evaluated, and the final model architecture was chosen based on the lowest RMSE and highest score while maintaining stable convergence during training.

Model optimization, training, and validation strategy: The network was optimized using the AdamW optimizer (chosen for its adaptive learning rate and decoupled weight decay49) with an ExponentialDecay learning rate schedule and a clipnorm of 1.0 to prevent exploding gradients. During the initial framework development, a custom weighted MSE loss function was evaluated, formulated mathematically as , where if , and 1.0 otherwise. The threshold of 5 and weight of 2.0 were determined empirically via hyperparameter sweeping to penalize larger discrepancies without destabilizing the gradients. However, to robustly unify the architecture and handle outliers efficiently across all three cities, Huber Loss was ultimately adopted globally, drawing on optimization principles established in advanced sequence modeling50,51. To prevent lookahead bias and temporal data leakage, the train-validation split was strictly chronological. A sequential 80–20 split was enforced, preserving the exact temporal order of the time series. The network was trained for up to 200 epochs using a batch size of 16. Two callbacks were implemented: EarlyStopping with a patience of 40 to halt training when validation loss no longer improved, and ReduceLROnPlateau, which lowered the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 when the validation loss plateaued for 10 epochs, allowing for finer convergence. Approach 2: Indirect AQI forecasting

This architecture adopts a multi-step, bottom-up approach. Rather than forecasting the final AQI directly, it first predicts individual pollutant concentrations and reconstructs the AQI from them.

Statistical baseline: ARIMA: To establish a rigorous benchmark for evaluating the machine learning models, the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model was implemented as the traditional statistical baseline. It forecasts individual pollutant concentrations based strictly on their historical temporal dependencies.

Pollutant-specific model selection: Separate regression models were trained for each primary pollutant (e.g., , , ). Each model received as input the remaining pollutants and meteorological features observed at the same timestep. This framework allows for comparing multiple classical ML models including ensembles and regularized linear regressors against the statistical baseline.

Ensemble methods: random forest and gradient boosting: The primary ensemble model implemented was the Random Forest Regressor, chosen for its robustness, strong predictive power, and its ability to model complex, non-linear relationships. Gradient Boosting methods were also implemented, given their accuracy through sequential error-correcting model building.

Regularized models: Lasso regression: To evaluate baseline linear models and investigate feature sparsity, Lasso (L1) regression models was also assessed. These models are known to handle multicollinearity well—a common challenge in air quality datasets—and Lasso provides the added benefit of automatic feature selection.

AQI calculation from predicted sub-indices: The final step in this framework involves reconstructing the AQI from predicted pollutant concentrations. As described in the implementation, each predicted value is converted into a sub-index using the CPCB breakpoint tables. The final AQI for the timestep is then computed as the maximum among all the sub-indices, following the standard AQI calculation method.

Results

This section presents the empirical results of the study, beginning with insights from the exploratory data analysis, followed by a quantitative and qualitative comparison of the forecasting models, and concluding with a spatio-temporal analysis.

Findings from exploratory data analysis

The exploratory analysis of data from all three cities revealed strongly synchronized and dominant seasonal patterns that are critical for accurate forecasting. As visualized in the time series plot in Fig. 2, the daily average AQI for all cities exhibits clear annual cycles. Pollution levels consistently peak during the winter (December–January) and post-monsoon months, regularly exceeding AQI values of 300 and occasionally peaking above 500, particularly in Hyderabad and Visakhapatnam. Conversely, levels trough during the monsoon season (July–September), highlighting the significant impact of large-scale meteorological phenomena across the entire region. This seasonality is quantified in Fig. 3, which breaks down the mean AQI by season. The pattern is consistent across all locations: the lowest AQI occurs during the monsoon, and the highest occurs in winter. In Visakhapatnam, for instance, the mean winter AQI (over 150) is more than double its monsoon-season mean (approximately 60). Similar, though less pronounced, trends are observed in Hyderabad and Vijayawada, with winter AQI levels being approximately 80% and 100% higher than their respective monsoon troughs. The wider confidence intervals during winter also suggest greater day-to-day AQI variability during peak pollution months. Figure 4 provides an overall comparison, showing that the industrial port city of Visakhapatnam has the highest average AQI of approximately 112, followed by Hyderabad at 94 and Vijayawada at 78. This makes Visakhapatnam’s average air pollution about 19% higher than Hyderabad’s and 43% higher than Vijayawada’s. These aggregate AQI trends are driven by the concentrations of key pollutants, as shown in Fig. 5. For instance, Visakhapatnam’s average winter PM2.5 concentration reaches a severe level of approximately 165 g/m3, which plummets by over 85% to around 21 g/m3 during the monsoon. Relative comparison of forecasting methods

Both forecasting methods–direct FNN and indirect Random Forest–yielded exceedingly good results, but a detailed comparison reveals important trade-offs. The direct FNN model provides slightly superior or comparable headline accuracy for the final AQI (e.g., of 0.965 for Vijayawada vs. RF’s 0.9784) in a more streamlined, end-to-end package. In contrast, the indirect Random Forest approach, while requiring the management of multiple models, delivers highly accurate predictions and offers the diagnostic advantage of identifying which specific pollutants are driving the AQI. The performance gap between the ensemble models (RF, GB) and the linear models (Lasso) is stark, as shown in Table 4. While Gradient Boosting performs reasonably well for Vijayawada, its performance degrades significantly for the other two cities. The linear models fail to capture the complex, non-linear dynamics, with performance dropping dramatically. The negative scores for some predictions (e.g., − 0.2864 for with Gradient Boosting in Visakhapatnam) signify that the model’s predictions are worse than simply guessing the mean value, strongly reinforcing the necessity of sophisticated, non-linear models for this task. Finally, to guarantee that the reported performance metrics were not artifacts of a single favorable initialization, both the direct and indirect models were strictly evaluated across 5 independent random seeds. The resulting variance was minimal, confirming high model stability. Specifically, the AQI scores across the 5 independent seeds for the indirect Random Forest model were [0.9745, 0.9782, 0.9768, 0.9775, 0.9759], while the direct FNN yielded [0.9450, 0.9680, 0.9520, 0.9710, 0.9605].

Model interpretability and feature importance analysis using SHAP

To gain further insights into the internal decision-making process of the proposed model and to enhance the interpretability of the predictions, we conducted a feature importance analysis using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations). SHAP is a widely used explainable AI technique that quantifies the contribution of each input feature to the model’s predictions by assigning importance scores based on cooperative game theory. By examining the SHAP summary plots for each city, it becomes possible to understand how different pollutants and meteorological variables influence the predicted AQI values and to identify the dominant factors driving the model output.

The SHAP summary plots for all three cities (refer Fig. 9), namely Vijayawada, Visakhapatnam, and Hyderabad, suggest that the particulate matter concentration, i.e., PM2.5 and PM10, are the most dominant factors for the model output, showing the highest mean absolute SHAP values and a monotonic effect. Higher concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10, i.e., the red dots, are seen to have the most positive impact on the predictions, implying that higher amounts of particulate matter are responsible for increased air quality indices. Among the gaseous pollutants, SO2, NOx, CO, and O3 show moderate SHAP values, and SO2 and NOx show stronger effects for certain cities. Meteorological factors such as temperature, humidity, wind speed, wind direction, and visibility show lower SHAP values, and the spread of SHAP values for both directions suggests that they are likely modulating factors, influencing the dispersion and accumulation of pollutants rather than being the main contributors.

Discussion

This section provides a detailed interpretation of the key findings presented in the results, compares the advantages and compromises of the two main forecasting approaches, highlights the critical role played by feature engineering, and outlines potential directions for future research.

Key findings interpretation

The findings of this research present multiple significant observations. The very high R-squared values () obtained by both the direct FNN and the indirect Random Forest models establish that AQI in the region under investigation is extremely predictable. This predictability is not random; it is rooted in strong, persistent temporal patterns laid bare through the exploratory data analysis. The dominance of the seasonal cycle—with winter and post-monsoon peaks in pollution and a decline during the monsoon—is the single most important driver of air quality variability. Our models, especially the FNN with its dense set of temporal features, were highly effective in learning and leveraging this meteorological regularity. In addition, the superior generalization performance of ensemble models such as Random Forest and Gradient Boosting over traditional regularized linear models in the indirect approach is revealing. It strongly implies that the interactions between different pollutants and meteorological conditions are profoundly non-linear. For example, the formation of secondary pollutants like ozone or certain particulate matter does not follow simple linear principles but depends on complex chemical interactions influenced by temperature, humidity, and precursor gases. Tree-based models are well-suited to capture such high-order, complex interactions without requiring their explicit specification, which accounts for their significant performance benefit over the linear Lasso and ARIMA models (Figs. 10, 11, 12 and 13).

Direct vs. indirect forecasting approach analysis

The central contribution of this research is the simultaneous construction and comparison of two different forecasting paradigms. The direct FNN method proved to be a highly effective, integrated approach. Trained as an end-to-end model, it learns the implicit, composite mapping from all environmental inputs directly to the target AQI value. Its key strengths are its high precision and relative ease of deployment—only one model needs to be maintained. Conversely, the indirect method provides significant diagnostic and granular value. Although more complex to implement (as it necessitates training and maintaining distinct models for each pollutant), it yields a transparent window into the predictability of specific air quality constituents. For instance, our findings show that is forecast with consistently higher accuracy than across several models and locations. This type of granular analysis is invaluable for scientific inquiry or for policymakers wishing to target specific emission sources. The indirect framework essentially disaggregates the final AQI into its component drivers, enabling a deeper understanding of what contributes to pollution episodes. This paper concludes that neither method is universally superior; the choice depends on the application. The direct FNN is optimal for operational systems where a fast, highly accurate overall AQI prediction is the primary goal, whereas the indirect method is preferable for policy analysis and scientific research that requires pollutant-specific detail and interpretability.

The role of feature engineering in model performance

The effectiveness of the direct FNN model is intrinsically linked to the comprehensive feature engineering pipeline that was employed. Although deep neural networks are often lauded for their ability to learn features automatically, our findings indicate that for tabular time-series data, performance can be significantly enhanced by incorporating domain knowledge through engineered features. The lag features enabled the model to understand the autocorrelative structure of pollution (i.e., that today’s pollution is a strong indicator of tomorrow’s). The rolling window features gave the model a sense of recent trends and volatility, allowing it to differentiate between stable periods and times of rapid change. Crucially, the explicit calendar features (month, day of week) allowed the network to directly model the dominant seasonal cycles detected in the EDA. Without these engineered cues, the FNN would have faced a significantly more difficult task in uncovering these underlying temporal patterns independently. This highlights a key conclusion: in time-series forecasting with deep learning, thoughtful feature engineering remains an essential, performance-critical step.

Future research directions and limitations

While this research developed highly accurate forecasting models, it is constrained by the reliance on single monitoring stations per city. Air quality can vary dramatically within urban micro-environments. The use of single stations was strictly necessitated by data availability constraints, as continuous, multi-year datasets for supplementary stations were heavily fragmented or entirely absent on the official CPCB portal. Several directions for future work remain. First, a deeper exploration of advanced Transformer-based architectures is warranted. Specifically, an advanced framework integrating dual-axis transformers and morphological encoding could significantly enhance the detection of complex spatiotemporal air quality patterns52. Second, the spatial component of air pollution could be more explicitly modeled using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), enabling the model to learn how pollution dynamically diffuses between regional nodes. Lastly, the models could be made more robust by integrating satellite data (e.g., MODIS Aerosol Optical Depth) or real-time traffic data to further contextualize the urban environment.

Conclusion

This work presents several important contributions to the science of air quality forecasting. It successfully established and validated two distinct, high-accuracy forecasting architectures: a direct, end-to-end deep learning model and an indirect, multi-model regression strategy. The study demonstrated that through extensive feature engineering, a deep FNN could achieve outstanding prediction accuracy () for the overall AQI. Concurrently, our comparative analysis confirmed that the Random Forest algorithm is an extremely powerful and robust predictor for individual pollutant concentrations, offering a more interpretable alternative. Furthermore, through comprehensive exploratory and spatio-temporal analysis, this paper described and quantified the prevailing, region-scale seasonal drivers of air pollution in major cities of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. The research effectively proves the viability of constructing highly accurate and dependable air quality forecast systems using state-of-the-art ML and DL methods. The high prediction scores attained highlight the point that urban air pollution, though complicated, is not chaotic but is governed by consistent and learnable patterns. The key takeaway is that a paradigm integrating advanced model architectures with domain-aware feature engineering is incredibly powerful for addressing environmental time-series challenges. The models presented here act not just as a proof-of-concept but also as a potential foundation for operational forecasting systems capable of supporting public health advisories, regulatory management, and a deeper scientific understanding of urban atmospheric dynamics.
