Mapping fibroblast plasticity across non-cancer lung diseases at single-cell resolution
Authors: Junhui Yang, Xin Zhang, Minghui Tang, Yuanpei Jiang, Zhiying Chen, Cuinan Lai, Jingyi Bao, Jin Su, Dan Cui, Jia Li

Background

Fibroblasts are essential stromal cells that maintain lung structural integrity, orchestrate tissue repair, and contribute to extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling in response to injury [1]. Under physiological conditions, fibroblasts support alveolar homeostasis by producing ECM components and interacting with epithelial and immune cells [2]. However, in response to chronic or severe injury, fibroblasts undergo phenotypic and functional transitions, acquiring myofibroblast-like features that drive excessive matrix deposition and irreversible tissue remodeling [3]. This remarkable capacity to adopt diverse activation states—termed fibroblast plasticity—is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of disease outcomes across a wide spectrum of pulmonary disorders [4].

In malignancy, fibroblast plasticity has been extensively characterized through studies of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), revealing distinct transcriptional states and regulatory programs that promote tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance [5–7]. In contrast, the transcriptional and regulatory determinants of fibroblast state transitions in non-malignant lung diseases remain less systematically characterized. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and COVID-19–associated acute lung injury are among the most impactful causes of respiratory morbidity and mortality worldwide [8, 9]. In all three conditions, fibroblasts play pivotal yet context-dependent roles in shaping inflammatory or fibrotic microenvironments [10]. Despite their clinical significance, whether fibroblasts in these diseases share conserved programs or exhibit disease-specific activation states remains largely unknown. Understanding how fibroblast plasticity contributes to chronic versus acute lung injury is essential for elucidating disease mechanisms and developing targeted therapies.

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have been applied to illuminate the heterogeneity of lung fibroblasts in individual diseases [11, 12]. Studies of IPF and COVID-19 have identified pathogenic fibroblast subsets but with inconsistent nomenclature [11, 13, 14]. In contrast, fibroblasts in single-cell studies of COPD have not yet been systematically analyzed or functionally characterized [15–19]; current evidence from experimental and bulk transcriptomic studies suggests that COPD fibroblasts adopt inflammatory and remodeling-associated phenotypes [10]. Moreover, most existing datasets have been analyzed in isolation, with limited cross-disease integration. Furthermore, these datasets are often constrained by small patient cohorts, sparse fibroblast representation, and inconsistent subtype annotations [13, 15, 20], which hinder cross-study integration and systematic exploration. Together, these limitations have hindered the establishment of a unified, high-resolution understanding of fibroblast diversity across chronic and acute non-malignant lung pathologies. It remains unclear whether fibroblast activation represents a continuum of shared states or distinct, disease-specific programs.

Here, we hypothesized that fibroblast plasticity manifests as both shared and disease-specific transcriptional programs, reflecting the distinct pathophysiological contexts of chronic and acute fibrotic injury. To test this hypothesis, we constructed a comprehensive single-cell fibroblast atlas integrating 1.47 million cells from 16 public datasets spanning healthy, COPD, pulmonary fibrosis (PF), and COVID-19 lungs. Through integrative transcriptomic and regulatory network analyses, we systematically delineated fibroblast subtypes and their disease-associated transcriptional remodeling. Comparative integration with a pan-cancer fibroblast reference allowed us to distinguish non-malignant from cancer-associated fibroblast programs.

We resolved five fibroblast subtypes commonly reported across studies [11, 12, 21]—adventitial, alveolar, myofibroblast, fibromyocyte-like, and lipo(fibroblast)-like—with conserved marker patterns. We highlight NR2F2, HIF1A, and TWIST1 as representative regulons with subtype- and disease-dependent activity, consistent with hypoxia-driven fibrogenesis (HIF1A) and EMT programs (TWIST1) reported previously, while NR2F2 shows context-specific roles in lung fibrosis biology. We further defined chronic and acute fibrosis gene signatures that correlated strongly with lung function decline and were conserved across fibrotic organs, including heart and kidney. Finally, we identified ANTXR1 as a representative chronic fibrosis gene, functionally validated its role in fibroblast activation in vitro, and confirmed its upregulation in vivo in fibrotic lung tissues and experimental models.

Together, this study provides a comprehensive cross-disease, single-cell atlas of fibroblast plasticity in non-malignant lung pathology, bridging chronic and acute fibrotic conditions. By integrating transcriptomic, regulatory, and functional evidence, our work uncovers shared and disease-specific fibroblast programs that define the molecular basis of fibrotic remodeling, offering new insights into fibroblast heterogeneity and potential therapeutic targets in respiratory diseases.

Methods

Cohort and dataset collection

This study included publicly available single-cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets for atlas construction and comparative validation, as well as human lung tissue samples and primary human lung fibroblasts for histological and in vitro validation. No new human participants were recruited for the single-cell atlas construction. All public human datasets were collected and enrolled by the investigators of the original studies under their respective institutional review board approvals, informed consent procedures, and study-specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. The present study used only publicly available raw count matrices and the accompanying sample- and cell-level metadata. Where necessary, disease labels, sample identifiers, and cell annotations were manually curated and harmonized across studies before integration.

Human lung single-cell RNA-seq datasets were identified from public repositories, including the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA), and were included if they contained lung tissue samples from healthy donors or patients with non-cancer lung diseases, including COPD, PF, or COVID-19—associated acute lung injury. Datasets derived exclusively from cell lines, organoids, non-lung tissues, or bulk transcriptomic profiles were excluded from the primary human lung atlas.

The primary human lung atlas included 16 publicly available studies comprising 287 samples and 370 sequencing libraries, including 113 healthy donor samples, 125 PF samples, 43 COPD samples, and 8 COVID-19 samples (total n = 1,470,730 cells). PF included IPF and other fibrotic interstitial lung disease samples, as annotated in the original studies and harmonized in this study. The included datasets were GSE122960 [20, 22], GSE128033 [23, 24], GSE128169 [25, 26], GSE132771 [11, 27], GSE135893 [13, 28], GSE136831 [15, 29], GSE150068 [30, 31], GSE227136 [32, 33], GSE171541 [18, 34], GSE168191 [16, 35], GSE173896 [17, 36], GSE196341 [19, 37], GSE158127 [38, 39], GSE171668 [40, 41], GSE150247 [30, 42], and ERP114453 [43, 44]. For each dataset, the study title, publication date, journal, PMID, accession number, phenotype, sample number, and library number are provided in Additional file 2: Table S1.

These public human lung scRNA-seq datasets were reprocessed with a unified pipeline and used for quality control, data integration, fibroblast extraction, fibroblast subtype annotation, celltype abundance analysis, differential gene expression analysis, gene set scoring, pseudotime trajectory analysis, transcriptional regulatory network analysis, and cross-disease comparison among healthy donor, PF, COPD, COVID-19 and cancer samples.

Additional datasets were used for comparative and validation analyses but were not included in the primary human lung atlas.

A pan-cancer cancer-associated fibroblast dataset, GSE246215 [5, 45], was used to compare fibroblast states identified in non-cancer lung diseases with previously reported cancer-associated fibroblast programs. Lung cancer and precancerous lesion scRNA-seq datasets, including GSE131907 [46, 47], GSE189357 [48, 49], HRA000154 [50, 51], and HRA001130 [52, 53], were used to evaluate the activity of acute and chronic fibrosis signatures in malignant and premalignant lung contexts.

Heart fibrosis dataset GSE217494 [54, 55] and kidney fibrosis datasets GSE211785 [56, 57] and GSE171668 [40, 41] were used for cross-tissue validation of acute and chronic fibrosis signatures. Mouse pulmonary fibrosis datasets GSE129605 [58, 59], GSE131800 [60, 61], and GSE132771 [11, 27] and the mouse COPD dataset GSE168299 [62, 63] were used to evaluate whether the fibroblast states identified in human non-cancer lung diseases were conserved in experimental mouse models. Human lung spatial transcriptomic dataset S-BSST1410 [64, 65] and mouse lung spatial transcriptomic dataset STT0000032 [66, 67] were used to assess the spatial patterns of candidate genes.

The public GSE47460 bulk lung microarray transcriptome dataset (n = 213; 91 controls and 122 IPF/UIP patients) [68, 69], was used as an independent cohort to evaluate the clinical relevance of acute and chronic fibrosis signatures. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA, v1.42.0) [70] was applied to calculate sample-level signature scores, which were then correlated with pulmonary function parameters, including predicted DLCO and predicted pre-bronchodilator FVC.

Human lung tissue samples from IPF patients and non-diseased controls were obtained with informed consent and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (2021G-29). These tissue samples were used for immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence validation of candidate fibroblast markers, including ANTXR1.

Primary human lung fibroblasts (hPLFs) were isolated from human lung tissues and used for in vitro functional validation experiments.

Study design

We constructed a cross-disease single-cell fibroblast atlas to define fibroblast subtypes and disease-associated transcriptional and regulatory programs across non-malignant lung diseases. Comparative analyses with CAFs and healthy donors were performed to identify non-cancer–specific signatures. By comparing acute (COVID-19) and chronic (PF) fibrosis, we identified disease-specific fibroblast states and delineated acute and chronic fibrosis signatures. ScRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and in vitro functional experiments were used to validate key chronic fibrosis signature genes.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data preprocessing and integration

Publicly available human lung single-cell RNA-seq datasets from healthy donors and patients (PF, COPD, and COVID-19) were downloaded and processed individually. Raw count matrices were uniformly processed using the Seurat (v4.1.1) [71] R package. Cells were filtered based on gene detection (400–7000 genes per cell) and mitochondrial RNA content (< 15%). Doublets were detected and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) [72], with pK estimated following the "no ground-truth" strategy and expected doublet rates estimated via a regression formula derived from 10 × Genomics recommendations (For each sample, expected doublet rate is defined as (cell number * 0.0008 + 0.0527)*0.01). Gene symbols from different Cell Ranger versions were harmonized to Cell Ranger v2 equivalents via Ensembl gene ID mapping.

After per-dataset quality control, individual datasets were converted to AnnData objects by SeuratDisk (v0.0.0.9019) [73] R package and concatenated using the Scanpy (v1.9.1) [74], retaining 30,566 shared genes across datasets. Normalization, log-transformation, identification of top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs), scaling, and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed using Scanpy following the Scanpy PBMC 3 k tutorial pipeline. Harmony (v0.1) [75] integration was applied to correct batch effects across samples. Neighborhood graph computing, Leiden clustering and UMAP embedding were conducted based on Harmony-corrected PCA coordinates. Cell-type annotations were performed using canonical marker genes.

Fibroblast subtype identification and annotation

Fibroblast populations were subset from integrated datasets and reprocessed using Seurat. Data normalization, identification of HVGs, scaling (with regression of mitochondrial content), PCA, and Harmony integration were applied following Seurat’s pbmc3k_tutorial. Neighborhood graph computing, Leiden clustering at multiple resolutions and UMAP embedding were performed based on Harmony-corrected PCA coordinates. Fibroblast subtype annotations were transferred from a reference dataset [12] using Seurat’s label-transfer method (FindTransferAnchors() and TransferData() functions). Marker genes distinguishing fibroblast subtypes were identified using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers() function with parameters: only.pos = TRUE, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25. SCP (v0.4.8) [76] was used to visualize marker gene expression heatmaps. Single-cell Gaussian kernel densities were estimated and visualized using Scanpy.

Comparative analysis of fibroblasts from non-cancer lung diseases and CAFs

Fibroblast populations from this study were integrated with a previously published CAF dataset (Yang Gao et al., Progenitor-ike fibroblasts, ADH1B + alveolar Fib and MyoFibroblast were used) [5], obtained from the NCBI GEO database with accession number GSE246215 [45]. Data processing and integration were performed as described above, except without regression of mitochondrial content. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between diseased and healthy conditions in adventitial fibroblast and myofibroblast were identified using Seurat’s FindMarkers() with parameter min.pct = 0.25. Significant DEGs were defined with thresholds: p_adj < = 0.01, and either |avg_log2FC|> = 0.4 or |pct.1-pct.2|> = 0.2. UpSetR (v1.4.0) [77] was used to visualize gene overlap. As for cell proportion analysis, we restricted the analysis to samples containing at least 30 cells per subtype in each sample [5]. Differential cell-type abundance across disease conditions was then assessed using scCODA [78], a Bayesian framework designed for compositional single-cell data that accounts for the dependency structure of cell-type proportions.

Intra-subtype heterogeneity analysis of myofibroblasts, adventitial fibroblasts and alveolar fibroblasts

Myofibroblast heterogeneity was further analyzed using scVI-tools (v1.1.6) [79] for dimensionality reduction due to nFeature_RNA-driven clustering. Neighborhood graph, Leiden clustering, and UMAP visualization were performed on the scVI latent space. Heterogeneity analysis for adventitial fibroblast and alveolar fibroblast followed the above Seurat pipeline, without mitochondrial regression. DEGs defining subclusters were identified using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers() function. Pathway scores were calculated using Seurat’s AddModuleScore() function and visualized using SCP.

Pathway changes in fibrosis across fibroblast subtypes

Disease-associated transcriptional changes were assessed by comparing COVID-19, PF, and COPD to healthy controls across all fibroblasts and within each subtype. Differentially expressed genes were identified using Seurat’s FindMarkers() function (parameters: min.pct = 0.1, logfc.threshold = 0). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed based on ranked gene lists using the clusterProfiler (v4.2.0) [80] and msigdbr (v7.5.1) [81] R packages. Pathway activity was quantified using AddModuleScore() and visualized using SCP.

Regulatory network inference using SCENIC

Gene regulatory networks were reconstructed using pySCENIC (v0.11.2) [82], following the standard SCENIC protocol. Regulon specificity scores (RSS) were calculated for each combination of disease and fibroblast subtype. Top-ranked transcription factor regulons were selected and visualized using heatmaps (ComplexHeatmap v2.10.0 [83]). Enrichment analysis of regulon target genes was conducted with clusterProfiler.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Pseudotime trajectory inference was performed for each fibroblast subtype using Monocle2 (v2.22.0) [84] to identify disease-dominant fibroblast states. Disease-specific genes were defined by pseudotime-based differential expression analysis.

Instead of HVGs, we used the union of chronic and acute fibrosis-specific genes as input features, following the above Seurat pipeline, without mitochondrial regression.

Correlation between fibrosis signature scores and lung function

To assess the clinical relevance of fibrosis-specific gene signatures, we applied GSVA to the GSE47460 bulk lung microarray transcriptome dataset (n = 213; 91 controls and 122 IPF/UIP patients) [68, 69]. GSVA scores for the chronic, acute, and fibroblast signature genes were computed for each sample, representing the relative expression activity of each signature. These scores were then correlated with pulmonary function parameters, including predicted DLCO and predicted pre-bronchodilator FVC, using Pearson correlation analysis.

Cross-tissue validation of fibrosis signatures

Heart fibrosis: Count matrix from scRNA-seq dataset GSE217494 [54, 55] was downloaded and processed following the above quality control and Seurat pipeline, without mitochondrial regression. Cell-type annotations were performed using canonical marker genes. Chronic and acute fibrosis gene signature scores were computed using AddModuleScore() and compared across disease groups, including healthy donors, non-ischemic cardiomyopathy (NICM), ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM), and acute myocardial infarction (AMI).

Kidney fibrosis: Counts and metadata from GSE211785 (comprising controls [Control], diabetic kidney disease [DKD], and hypertensive kidney disease [HKD]) [56, 57] were downloaded. Only snRNA-seq samples were retained. Count matrix from GSE171668 (COVID-19–associated nephropathy) [40, 41] were downloaded and filtered by the above quality control. Then these two datasets were merged and analyzed following the above Seurat pipeline, without mitochondrial regression. Cell-type annotations were performed using curated kidney marker genes from the literatures [56, 85, 86]. Stromal cells were subset and re-clustered using Seurat. Chronic and acute fibrosis gene signature scores were computed using AddModuleScore() and compared across disease conditions.

Processing of mouse single-cell RNA-seq datasets

To assess Antxr1 expression dynamics in murine models of pulmonary fibrosis and COPD, we collected single-cell RNA-seq datasets from three bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis models (GSE129605 [58, 59], GSE131800 [60, 61], GSE132771 [11, 27]) and one cigarette smoke–exposed COPD model (GSE168299 [62, 63]). Each dataset was downloaded and processed individually. Raw count matrices were uniformly processed using Seurat. Cells were filtered based on gene detection (400–7000 genes per cell) and mitochondrial RNA content (< 15%). Data normalization, identification of HVGs, scaling (without regression of mitochondrial content), PCA, and Harmony integration were performed following Seurat’s pbmc3k_tutorial. Neighborhood graph construction, Leiden clustering at multiple resolutions, and UMAP embedding were conducted using Harmony-corrected PCA coordinates. Cell type annotations were assigned based on canonical marker genes.

Spatial transcriptomics analysis

Publicly available human and murine spatial transcriptomics datasets were downloaded and processed.

Human lung fibrosis: Space Ranger output matrices and Cell2Location-annotated files were obtained from the S-BSST1410 dataset [64, 65]. The dataset comprised fresh-frozen lung tissues from four healthy donors and four IPF patients, each contributing three spatial sections representing mild (B1), moderate (B2), and severe (B3) fibrosis. Data processing, including quality control and SCT normalization, was performed using Seurat (v4.3.0). Inferred myofibroblast abundance was obtained from Cell2Location annotation.

Mouse PF model: Stereo-seq data from the dataset STT0000032 were downloaded [66, 67]. The dataset included lung tissues from C57BL/6 mice subjected to bleomycin (BLM, 1.8 U/kg in 50 µl saline) or saline control, collected at days 7 (D7) and 21 (D21) post-treatment. Raw data were processed using stereopy (v1.2.0) [87] to generate h5ad objects, which were subsequently converted into Seurat objects for quality control, SCT normalization and visualization.

Isolation and culture of human primary lung fibroblasts (hPLFs) and TGF-β treatment

Human primary lung fibroblasts (hPLFs) were isolated and cultured using the following protocol: Briefly, lung tissues were washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; 14040133, Gibco, Invitrogen) to remove surface bloodstains, and then minced into small fragments and placed in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; C11965500BT, Gibco, Invitrogen) for digestion. After digestion, the cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer to remove tissue debris, and cells were collected from the supernatant. The isolated hPLFs were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; FSD500, ExCell), 2 mM L-glutamine (25030081, Gibco, Invitrogen), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (15070063, Gibco, Invitrogen), and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Primary fibroblasts of high purity were obtained after more than three passages. For experimental treatments, cells were seeded in six-well plates and stimulated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β (75362, Cell Signaling Technology) for 48 h.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

Paraffin-embedded lung tissue Sects. (6 μm) were dewaxed and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by microwaving the sections in 1 × Tris–EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH 9.0; G1218, Servicebio) for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using Endogenous Peroxidase Blocking Buffer (RM70001, Abclonal) for 15 min at room temperature. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h, sections were incubated with primary antibodies anti-ANTXR1 (ab241067, Abcam; 1:200) and anti-ɑ-SMA (67735–1-Ig, Proteintech; 1:100) at 4 °C overnight. This was followed by incubation with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG or goat anti-mouse IgG (RK50015, Abclonal; 1:200) for 30 min at room temperature. Chromogenic development was carried out using peroxidase-conjugated avidin/biotin and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (RK50015, Abclonal), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (G1101, Servicebio) for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (T8787, Sigma-Aldrich), and blocked with 10% BSA (A1933, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with an anti-ANTXR1 (ab241067, abcam, 1:200) and anti-ɑ-SMA (67735–1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:200) antibody at 4 °C for 12 h. Afterward, the cells were incubated for 1 h with secondary antibodies: Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor™ 488 and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor™ 568 (A11001, Thermo Fisher Scientific; 1:500). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 62248, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a dilution of 1:5000 for 15 min at room temperature. All immunofluorescence images were acquired using a Carl Zeiss inverted microscope.

Western blotting analysis

Protein expression was analyzed by western blot according to standard procedures. Briefly, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Millipore, USA) to extract total proteins. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-ANTXR1 (23494, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), anti- collagen I (72026, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), and anti-ɑ-SMA (67735–1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:1000), anti- fibronectin (66042–1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:1000) and anti-β-Actin (66009–1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:7000). Protein signals were detected using a SuperSignal West Dura Trial kit (37071, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.1.3). Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, unless otherwise specified. Enrichment analyses were corrected for multiple testing (Benjamini–Hochberg). Correlations between signature scores and clinical variables were assessed using Pearson correlation tests.

Results

Identification and characterization of fibroblast subtypes across non-malignant lung diseases

To systematically characterize fibroblast heterogeneity in non-cancerous human lungs under healthy and diseased conditions, we curated and integrated large-scale single-cell RNA-seq datasets comprising a total of 1,470,730 cells from 16 datasets, including samples from healthy donors (n = 113), and patients with COPD (n = 43), PF (n = 125), and COVID-19 (n = 8) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A, Additional file 2: Table S1). After batch correction (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B), we performed unsupervised clustering of all single cell gene expression profiles. We identified major lung cell types, including epithelial, endothelial, lymphoid, myeloid, and stromal populations, using canonical markers (Additional file 1: Fig. S1C-D). Fibroblasts were characterized by unique expression of classical markers, such as COL1A1 and COL1A2 (Additional file 1: Fig. S1E). There are total 47,066 fibroblasts cells spanning 51 samples (Fig. 1A; Additional file 3: Table S2). Fibroblasts were further re-clustered with batch effects removed, revealing distinct transcriptional subpopulations (Fig. 1B; Additional file 1: Fig. S2A-B). We next examined how fibroblast subtype proportions and transcriptional states varied across non-cancer lung disease conditions. We found that myofibroblasts were significantly increased in COPD (median 20.10%), with further elevations observed in PF (median 29.37%) and COVID-19 (median 24.94%) compared to healthy donors (median 7.30%) (Fig. 1D-E). Density distribution analyses further revealed disease-specific transcriptional shifts within each fibroblast subtype, indicating that disease influences not only fibroblast abundance but also the transcriptional states of individual subtypes (Fig. 1F). In summary, we identified five fibroblast subtypes in non-cancer lung disease tissues and delineated their dynamic alterations across healthy control, PF, COPD and COVID19 lungs. These results lay the foundation for comparative analyses with CAFs and for uncovering shared and divergent regulatory programs across diverse lung pathologies.

Fibroblasts in non-cancer lung diseases exhibit distinct transcriptional programs and subtype structures from CAFs

Fibroblasts are key stromal components that orchestrate tissue remodeling in both malignant and non-malignant settings. To determine whether fibroblasts in non-malignant fibrotic lung diseases share transcriptional features with CAFs, we integrated our annotated fibroblast populations with a reference CAFs atlas from a recent pan-cancer study [5], which identified 20 transcriptionally distinct fibroblast subtypes across diverse tumor types.

We focused on the three dominant fibroblast subtypes in the lung—adventitial fibroblasts, alveolar fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts—and performed joint clustering of fibroblasts from healthy donors, COPD, PF, COVID-19, and lung cancer samples (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A). Density plots revealed distinct, condition-dependent transcriptomic segregation for alveolar fibroblasts, adventitial fibroblasts and myofibroblasts across cancerous and non-cancerous states (Additional file 1: Fig. S3B). Differential expression analysis showed that CAFs underwent the most extensive transcriptional remodeling, with a large set of uniquely upregulated genes compared to alveolar fibroblasts, myofibroblasts and adventitial fibroblasts from COPD, PF, and COVID-19 (Additional file 1: Fig. S3C-E), reflecting a predominantly cancer-specific program.

To investigate myofibroblast heterogeneity across malignant and non-malignant conditions, we identified five transcriptionally distinct subclusters after correcting for technical and batch effects (Fig. 2A–B; Additional file 1: Fig. S4A). Each subcluster was characterized by unique marker gene expression profiles (Additional file 1: Fig. S4B). Subclusters C0 and C1 were predominantly enriched in PF, COVID-19, and cancer tissues, whereas C2 were largely depleted in COVID-19 and cancer samples (Additional file 1: Fig. S4C). Differential expression analysis identified 92 upregulated and 60 downregulated genes shared between PF myofibroblasts and CAFs, as well as 88 upregulated and 40 downregulated genes shared between COPD myofibroblasts and CAFs, relative to healthy donor myofibroblasts (Fig. 2C). We further identified 35 PF-specific and 33 COPD-specific upregulated genes compared to CAFs (Fig. 2C). Notably, NR1D1, STEAP1, and FBLN1 were selectively upregulated in PF myofibroblasts, whereas CAMK2N1, HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB5, and HLA-DRB1 were specifically elevated in COPD myofibroblasts (Fig. 2D). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and gluconeogenesis were most strongly activated in cancer-associated myofibroblasts compared to other chronic disease states (Fig. 2E). In contrast, regulation of integrin-mediated signaling was most enriched in PF, while surfactant metabolism was most prominently upregulated in COPD (Fig. 2E).

Adventitial fibroblasts also exhibited marked heterogeneity across malignant and non-malignant lung conditions (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A-E). Unsupervised clustering identified five distinct subclusters with unique marker profiles and disease-skewed distributions. Compared with CAFs, adventitial fibroblasts from COPD, PF, and COVID-19 showed even greater transcriptional divergence than myofibroblasts, suggesting higher context-dependent plasticity (Additional file 1: Fig. S5F). PF adventitial fibroblasts prominently upregulated profibrotic regulators such as COMP, HMGA1, NFATC1, and NFATC2, consistent with activation of matrix remodeling and interleukin signaling pathways. In contrast, COPD adventitial fibroblasts, similar to their myofibroblast counterparts, specifically upregulated HLA-related genes, reflecting enhanced immune activation (Additional file 1: Fig. S5G-H).

Within alveolar fibroblasts, we resolved five transcriptionally distinct subclusters, each exhibiting specific marker gene signatures (Additional file 1: Fig. S6A-D). Density plots further illustrate the transcriptional heterogeneity of alveolar fibroblasts across non-malignant and malignant contexts. Subclusters C0 were depleted in donor samples but expanded significantly in PF and cancer, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S6E). Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis revealed that approximately 50% of DEGs are shared between PF and cancers, suggesting a partially similar activation program of alveolar fibroblasts in these two conditions. In contrast, only about 30% of DEGs overlap between COPD and cancers, indicating more distinct regulatory programs governing alveolar fibroblasts in cancer versus COPD-associated fibrosis and chronic inflammation (Additional file 1: Fig. S6F). Notably, FGF2, JUND, and LTBP4 were specifically upregulated in COPD, whereas PLAUR, NNMT, and CCL2 were selectively upregulated in PF (Additional file 1: Fig. S6G). Pathway enrichment analysis further showed that fibrin clot dissolution–related pathways were enriched in PF, while antigen binding–related processes were enriched in COPD (Additional file 1: Fig. S6H).

In addition, systematic comparison with previously defined fibroblast states in pulmonary fibrosis revealed that several of our subclusters partially recapitulated known fibroblast features. For example, adventitial subcluster C3 exhibited PLIN2 + and HAS1 + fibroblast features, whereas adventitial subcluster C2 displayed myofibroblast-like features, consistent with previous single-cell RNA-seq analyses of pulmonary fibrosis [13, 15]. However, the unique marker genes defining our myofibroblast, adventitial fibroblast, and alveolar fibroblast subclusters showed only limited overlap with previously reported inflammatory, profibrotic, and transitional fibroblast states (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). These findings suggest that our atlas captures previously underappreciated fibroblast heterogeneity and context-dependent transcriptional states, thereby extending current models of fibroblast states diversity.

Collectively, these findings indicate that alveolar fibroblasts, adventitial fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts undergo context-dependent reprogramming, adopting distinct profibrotic and inflammatory transcriptional programs across fibrotic and malignant conditions.

We next focus on the transcriptomic differences of fibroblasts among non-cancerous diseases. The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 2F) using ranked genes revealed significant activation of the Hallmark EMT program and collagen formation/degradation pathways in fibroblasts from PF and COVID-19–infected lungs. In contrast, TNFα signaling via the NF-κB pathway was specifically upregulated in PF fibroblasts but downregulated in both COVID-19 and COPD. The platelet activation pathway was likewise selectively elevated in PF fibroblasts, while COVID-19 fibroblasts exhibited selective downregulation of DNA damage checkpoint and related signaling pathways. Notably, COPD fibroblasts exhibited marked enrichment of immune-related pathways, including the KEGG antigen processing and presentation pathway and interferon-γ signaling.

Quantitative module score analysis (Fig. 2G–I, Additional file 1: Fig. S8A–C) confirmed significant upregulation of these pathways in their respective disease contexts. The expression patterns of representative genes mirrored the pathway-level activation (Additional file 1: Fig. S8D). Patient-level analyses further demonstrated that these transcriptional activations were consistent across individuals rather than driven by outlier samples (Additional file 1: Fig. S8E–G).

In summary, these results demonstrate that fibroblast subtypes undergo disease-specific transcriptional remodeling in non-malignant lung diseases, with acute (COVID-19) and chronic fibrosis (PF) engaging distinct signaling programs. These findings provide a molecular framework for understanding fibroblast state transitions and functional specialization across diverse pathological contexts.

Rewiring of fibroblast subtype-specific regulatory networks in chronic and acute fibrotic lung disease

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms underlying disease-associated transcriptional programs, we reconstructed upstream transcriptional networks across fibroblast subtypes and disease states. Using pySCENIC, we inferred transcription factor (TF)–centered gene regulatory networks and computed regulon specificity scores (RSS) to quantify regulatory activity. Pairwise Jaccard similarity analysis among the top 25 regulons from each disease and fibroblast subtype revealed moderate subtype-specific, rather than disease-specific, regulatory similarity (Additional file 1: Fig. S9A). Heatmap analysis further demonstrated that the high regulatory activity of TF in control was broadly diminished in disease conditions in a subtype-specific manner (Additional file 1: Fig. S9B). These findings indicate that although disease perturbs regulatory landscapes, fibroblast subtypes remain transcriptionally distinct.

To further delineate the regulatory basis of fibroblast heterogeneity, we next identified key transcriptional regulons driving subtype-specific gene expression programs across disease conditions (Fig. 3A, Additional file 1: Fig. S9C). Notably, very few TFs were shared among the three fibroblast subtypes within the same disease condition (Additional file 1: Fig. S9D), indicating that each fibroblast subtype engages largely distinct regulatory programs. These results suggest that, even under the same pathological context, fibroblast subtypes exhibit remarkable heterogeneity in transcriptional regulation, which may underpin their specialized functional roles in disease progression.

To further delineate disease- and subtype-specific regulon along fibroblasts trajectory, we identified NR2F2, HIF1A and TWIST1 as three representative specific regulons, corresponding to alveolar fibroblasts in COPD, adventitial fibroblasts in PF and myofibroblasts in COVID19 lungs, respectively (Fig. 3B, Additional file 1: Fig. S9E). At the patient level, these regulons were concordant with known disease-associated regulatory modules, including NFATC1 [88], KLF2 [89], and STAT2 [90] in PF, COPD, and COVID-19, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). We next examined the downstream target genes underlying these regulons and identified distinct, disease-specific transcriptional signatures reflecting their divergent regulatory activities. For example, NR2F2 target genes were preferentially downregulated in PF, consistent with previous reports [91], but activated in COPD-associated alveolar fibroblasts, reflecting subtle alterations in fibroblast activation. HIF1A target genes were enriched in PF-associated adventitial fibroblasts, in line with hypoxia-driven fibrogenesis. In contrast, TWIST1-regulated targets were specifically upregulated in COVID-19–associated myofibroblasts, underscoring their role in robust epithelial–mesenchymal transition and acute tissue repair responses (Fig. 3C–D). Collectively, these results indicate that fibroblast subtype- and disease-specific regulons not only delineate pathological states but also orchestrate distinct downstream transcriptional programs that may drive disease progression.

Chronic and acute fibrosis gene signatures correlate with lung function decline and exhibit cross-tissue relevance

To identify molecular signatures that distinguish chronic and acute fibrotic fibroblast states, we systematically defined gene sets specific to PF and COVID-19–associated acute lung injury, respectively. Chronic and acute fibrosis signature genes were selected based on the following criteria: (a) significant upregulation in PF or COVID-19–infected lungs in at least two fibroblast subtypes compared to donor samples; (b) fibroblast-specific expression; and (c) significant differential expression between PF and COVID-19–infected lungs. Using this strategy, we totally identified 41 and 35 gene signatures for chronic and acute fibrotic fibroblasts states, respectively. These 76 gene signatures exhibited robust differential patterns between chronic and acute states (Fig. 4A), thereby providing a transcriptional framework to distinguish persistent remodeling processes in PF from transient injury-driven responses in COVID-19–associated fibrosis. At the single-cell level, these curated gene signatures robustly separated fibroblasts derived from PF, COVID-19, and donor lungs (Fig. 4B, Additional file 1: Fig. S11). Dot plots confirmed that each gene set showed cell type-specific and condition-specific expression patterns: chronic fibrosis genes were highly expressed in PF fibroblasts, acute fibrosis genes in COVID-19 fibroblasts, and fibroblast signature genes in donor fibroblasts (Additional file 1: Fig. S12).

To further quantify these patterns, we calculated chronic and acute signature scores for each fibroblast across disease conditions. PF fibroblasts consistently displayed the highest chronic scores, whereas COVID-19 fibroblasts exhibited the highest acute scores, validating the discriminative power of the identified gene sets (Fig. 4C). All two signatures displayed strong fibroblast specificity, with minimal expression in non-fibroblast cell types (Additional file 1: Fig. S13A).

We next explored the relevance of these signatures beyond fibrosis by applying them to fibroblasts from donor lungs, precancerous lesions, and lung cancers. Strikingly, chronic scores progressively increased from donors to adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), peaked at AIS, and then declined in minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) and stage I–III tumors. By contrast, acute scores rose steadily from donors through progressive cancer stages, reaching their maximum in stage III lung cancer. These results suggest that chronic signatures may be linked to early fibrotic remodeling that facilitates malignant transformation, whereas acute signatures track ongoing, injury-like fibroblast activation that intensifies during tumor progression (Fig. 4D). These signatures remained fibroblast-specific in the tumor microenvironment (Additional file 1: Fig. S13B). Together, these analyses highlight the utility of chronic and acute fibroblast gene signatures as molecular tools to dissect fibroblast heterogeneity across fibrotic lung disease, precancerous lesions, and cancer.

To link fibroblast gene signatures with clinical outcomes, we performed correlation analyses between chronic and acute gene signatures and lung function parameters, including predicted diffusion capacity (predicted DLCO) and forced vital capacity (predicted FVC, pre-bronchodilator), using bulk microarray gene expression data from IPF and control lung tissues (n = 213; 91 controls and 122 IPF patients). Both chronic and acute signatures showed significant inverse correlations with predicted DLCO (chronic: r = –0.47, p = 7.7 × 10⁻12; acute: r = –0.49, p = 5.5 × 10⁻13) and with predicted FVC (chronic: r = –0.37, p = 4 × 10⁻⁸; acute: r = –0.40, p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁹), suggesting that higher expression of these fibrotic programs is associated with impaired pulmonary function (Fig. 4E). Importantly, the applicability of these signatures was not limited to the lung: both chronic and acute signatures were also enriched in fibroblast populations from cardiac and kidney disease datasets, underscoring their cross-tissue relevance and generalizability (Fig. 4F-G, Additional file 1: Fig. S14-15).

Together, these findings demonstrate that chronic and acute fibroblast gene signatures not only capture conserved, cross-organ regulatory states but also provide quantitative predictors of disease severity, as reflected by their strong association with lung function decline in IPF.

Functional validation of chronic fibrosis signature gene—ANTXR1

We next selected ANTXR1, one of the representative chronic fibrosis signature genes, to validate its functional role. Across multiple datasets, ANTXR1 was consistently markedly upregulated in PF fibroblasts compared with donor, COPD, and COVID-19 samples, highlighting its disease specificity and potential role in chronic fibrotic remodeling (Fig. 5A, Additional file 1: Fig. S16). Spatial transcriptomic analysis further revealed that ANTXR1 expression was enriched in fibrotic regions and spatially co-localized with COL1A1 and FN1 in IPF lung sections (Fig. 5B, Additional file 1: Fig. S17). Quantitatively, the proportion of spots with high ANTXR1 expression was significantly increased in IPF tissues compared with healthy controls (Fig. 5C). Consistently, Antxr1 expression was also upregulated at the single-cell level and in whole-lung slides of BLM-induced mice lung compared with control mice (Fig. 5D,E, Additional file 1: Fig. S18). Together, these findings establish ANTXR1 as a robust and conserved marker of fibrotic remodeling across pulmonary fibrosis patients and experimental models of pulmonary fibrosis.

Based on the observed upregulation of ANTXR1 in pulmonary fibrosis, we designed two pairs of siRNAs to effectively knock down its expression in primary human lung fibroblasts (hPLFs). To assess the functional consequences, siRNA-transfected hPLFs were stimulated with TGF-β. Compared to siControl, ANTXR1 knockdown markedly downregulated the expression of COL1A1, FN1, and ACTA2, both at the transcriptional and protein levels, under TGF-β stimulation (Fig. 6E–F). Discussion

By integrating over one million single-cell transcriptomes from healthy lungs and from patients with COPD, PF, and COVID-19–associated acute lung injury, we constructed a comprehensive fibroblast atlas that captures the full continuum of fibroblast phenotypes across non-malignant lung diseases. This large-scale integrative analysis not only resolves disease-specific transcriptional programs with unprecedented granularity but also provides a unified framework to contextualize fibroblast states relative to both healthy donors and CAFs. Through systematic cross-disease comparison, we defined conserved chronic and acute fibrosis gene signatures that distinguish fibroblast activation trajectories between progressive, long-standing fibrosis and rapidly evolving virus-induced injury. Notably, these signatures exhibit strong inverse correlations with lung function metrics, highlighting their potential as biomarkers of disease severity and progression. Complementing these computational insights, our functional assays revealed that suppression of ANTXR1 markedly attenuates the induction of canonical fibrotic markers—including COL1A1, FN1, and ACTA2—upon TGF-β stimulation, implicating ANTXR1 as a previously unrecognized upstream node controlling fibroblast activation. Together, these findings establish a foundational reference for fibroblast biology in non-cancer lung diseases and uncover mechanistic principles that may inform therapeutic strategies targeting fibroblast plasticity.

Our atlas fills a notable gap between existing CAF-focused fibroblast atlases and disease-specific studies of non-malignant fibrosis. While recent pan-fibroblast atlases have mapped fibroblast heterogeneity across tissues and cancers [5–7], they were predominantly derived from tumors or mixed datasets in which non-cancer fibroblasts were underrepresented. Consequently, transcriptional diversity within non-cancer fibroblasts—which often drive tissue repair rather than tumor progression—has remained underexplored. By focusing exclusively on non-malignant lung diseases, our study provides the first integrative cross-disease atlas that resolves shared and disease-specific fibroblast states under chronic and acute lung injury conditions. This resource establishes a baseline reference for fibroblast heterogeneity in non-cancer contexts, against which pathological remodeling in tumors or other fibrotic organs can be benchmarked.

A notable observation from our dataset is that fibroblast transcriptional alterations in non-cancer lung diseases are relatively modest compared to the extensive reprogramming observed in CAFs. This difference likely reflects their distinct microenvironmental and functional contexts. CAFs reside in a highly complex tumor microenvironment characterized by persistent and multifactorial stimulation—including metabolic stress, hypoxia, and immune evasion signals [92–94]—as well as dense intercellular crosstalk with malignant and immune cells [95, 96]. These integrated cues collectively drive the sustained activation of key signaling pathways in CAFs, such as TGF-β/SMAD, IL-1/NF-κB, and IL-6/JAK-STAT3, and impose broad transcriptional plasticity on CAFs to support diverse pro-tumorigenic functions, ultimately giving rise to aggressive and phenotypically heterogeneous fibroblast populations [95, 97]. In contrast, fibroblast activation in non-cancer diseases is often stage-dependent [98], and reversible in early stages [99], with fewer transcriptionally distinct fibroblast subpopulations identified compared to CAFs [11, 13, 14, 21, 100]. These differences suggest that the dramatic transcriptional reprogramming observed in CAFs reflects their pathologically sustained activation, while fibroblasts in non-cancer lung diseases retain a more controlled and adaptive response pattern. This conceptual distinction emphasizes the need to delineate the transcriptional and functional boundaries between CAFs and inflammation-associated fibroblasts when interpreting fibroblast plasticity across disease contexts.

To further characterize our defined fibroblast subtypes in the context of previously identified fibroblast states, we first compared them with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) described in [5]. In that dataset, most CAF clusters exhibit relatively discrete fibroblast states with limited overlap in transcriptional programs. Using the same reference framework, we found that our defined fibroblast subtypes display features of multiple CAF-associated states. Specifically, Adv0–Adv4 fibroblasts broadly co-express Progenitor-Fib, Inflammatory-Fib, and ap-Fib signatures, while Alv0–Alv4 populations simultaneously exhibit both Tissue-specific-Fib and Inflammatory-Fib features.

We next extended this comparison to published pulmonary fibrosis single-cell atlases [13, 15, 101]. Overall, previously reported marker genes alone were insufficient to clearly resolve our newly defined subtypes. Nevertheless, we observed partial correspondence between our subtypes and published fibroblast programs. For example, Adv3 expresses both PLIN2⁺ and HAS1⁺ fibroblast features described in [13]; Adv0, Adv1, and Adv3 each show partial overlap with fibroblast signatures reported in [15] and [13]; and Adv2 displays mixed features corresponding to multiple fibroblast programs defined in both [13] and [101]. In the alveolar compartment, Alv0 broadly expresses multiple published fibroblast signatures, Alv2 shows features of both PLIN2⁺ and HAS1⁺ fibroblasts, while Alv3 and Alv4 exhibit more sparse overlap with previously defined programs. In contrast, Myo0–Myo4 populations show relatively heterogeneous and non-exclusive expression of published marker genes.

Collectively, these results suggest that, compared with the more discrete CAF states and previously reported pulmonary fibrosis fibroblast states, our identified fibroblast subtypes are transcriptionally more complex and display mixed features across multiple CAF-associated and lung fibrosis-associated programs, highlighting both shared and distinct aspects of fibroblast state organization across fibrotic and tumor contexts. Together, these findings indicate that our atlas and previously reported CAF and pulmonary fibrosis frameworks are complementary, jointly extending the continuum and diversity of fibroblast states across disease settings, especially for non-cancer diseases.

Through cross-disease comparison, we identified two transcriptional signatures: the chronic fibrosis signature was predominantly enriched in PF, whereas the acute signature characterized fibroblasts from COVID-19–associated lung injury. These distinct patterns likely reflect fundamental differences in the temporal and immunological landscapes of chronic versus acute tissue remodeling: persistent injury and unresolved inflammation in PF drive sustained fibroblast activation, while transient viral injury in COVID-19 elicits transient but intense fibroblast responses that subside upon resolution [102]. Notably, both signatures correlated inversely with lung function indices (FVC and DLCO), suggesting clinical relevance. Moreover, these transcriptional signatures could facilitate patient stratification by dominant fibroblast activation state and serve as molecular endpoints for evaluating antifibrotic interventions—for example, targeting chronic-dominant profiles while avoiding excessive suppression of transient reparative fibroblasts during acute inflammation. Future studies incorporating temporal sampling and pathway-level dissection will be essential to define the mechanistic underpinnings of these signatures and their roles in fibrotic progression or resolution.

Among the genes highlighted in our atlas, ANTXR1 emerged as a key regulator of fibroblast activation and ECM remodeling. In this study, ANTXR1 was consistently upregulated in PF fibroblasts across single-cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets and bleomycin-induced mouse models. Silencing ANTXR1 in primary human lung fibroblasts markedly attenuated TGF-β–induced expression of fibrotic markers COL1A1, FN1, and ACTA2, revealing its critical role in promoting ECM deposition and myofibroblast differentiation. Mechanistically, recent studies have shown that ANTXR1 modulates ECM homeostasis through interactions with TGFβ receptors and downstream SMAD/YAP signaling pathways in cardiac fibroblasts, consistent with our observations that it contributes to fibrotic remodeling in chronic lung disease [103, 104]. Moreover, TEM8/ANTXR1 is highly expressed in tumor-associated fibroblasts, where it facilitates collagen internalization and metabolic support of neighboring cells [105], and has been successfully targeted using antibody–drug conjugates to suppress tumor growth [106]. These findings collectively suggest that ANTXR1, as a key effector integrating mechanical and immune cues to sustain fibroblast activation, represents a promising therapeutic target for chronic fibrotic diseases. Future studies are warranted to dissect the detailed signaling pathways downstream of ANTXR1 in pulmonary fibroblasts, as well as to evaluate the translational potential of ANTXR1-targeted interventions in fibrotic lung diseases.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the available single-cell datasets are cross-sectional and observational, limiting causal inference and temporal resolution. Second, while we validated signature gene expression in human tissues and fibroblast cultures (Fig. 6), in vivo functional studies are needed to fully elucidate the roles of specific regulators. Third, the COVID-19 cohort represents acute, virus-induced injury and may not fully capture other forms of acute fibrosis. Fourth, we did not directly assess epigenetic mechanisms; DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling likely contribute to longer-term stabilization of chronic fibroblast states and merit focused investigation. Expanding this framework to include additional time points and fibrotic stimuli will refine our understanding of fibroblast plasticity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this work establishes the first cross-disease single-cell atlas of non-cancer lung fibroblasts, revealing how disease-specific microenvironments sculpt distinct transcriptional programs and activation trajectories. By defining clinically relevant chronic and acute fibrosis gene signatures and identifying ANTXR1 as a functional regulator, we provide both a conceptual framework and molecular entry points for dissecting fibroblast plasticity and developing targeted antifibrotic therapies in chronic and acute lung diseases.
