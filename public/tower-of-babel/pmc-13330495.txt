Female genital mutilation: prevalence and factors associated with its type among women of reproductive age group in Somaliland: further analysis of 2020 Demographic and Health Survey Data
Authors: Teshome Gensa Geta, Saad Ahmed Abdiwali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFemale genital mutilation is a major public health and human rights concern, with the highest burden reported in countries of the Horn of Africa. Despite long-standing legal bans and prevention efforts, the practice remains nearly universal in Somaliland, driven by deeply rooted social and cultural norms. Evidence on the prevalence, types, and population-level determinants of female genital mutilation in Somaliland remains limited. This study assessed the prevalence, types, and associated factors of female genital mutilation among women of reproductive age in Somaliland.MethodsThis population-based study analyzed data from 5,143 women aged 15–49 years who participated in the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey. A two-stage cluster sampling design was used, involving the selection of enumeration areas followed by households. Descriptive analyses were conducted to estimate prevalence and types of female genital mutilation. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with the pharaonic type of female genital mutilation in comparison with other types.ResultsThe overall prevalence of female genital mutilation was 99.49% (95% confidence interval: 99.30–99.70). The most common form was type three (pharaonic), affecting 66.97% of women, followed by type one (sunni) at 22.01% and type two (intermediate) at 9.50%. Women from nomadic communities, those with no formal education, those in lower wealth households, women who had never used the internet, and those circumcised by traditional practitioners had significantly higher odds of experiencing the pharaonic form of female genital mutilation.ConclusionsFemale genital mutilation remains nearly universal among women of reproductive age in Somaliland, with severe forms predominating. Social disadvantage, limited access to education and information, and reliance on traditional circumcisers are key population-level drivers of harmful practices. Interventions that expand educational and digital access and engage traditional practitioners may be critical to reducing the persistence and severity of female genital mutilation in Somaliland.

Introduction

Female genital mutilation (FGM) refers to procedures involving partial or complete removal of the external female genitalia or any other form of harm to the female genital organs performed for non-medical purposes [1]. Different types of FGM have been reported in different countries. The World Health Organization (WHO) divides the practice into four categories: type I (clitoridectomy) involves total or partial removal of the clitoris. This is called sunna in some settings. Type II (intermediate) is the total or partial removal of the clitoris and labia minor, with or without excision of the labia major. Type III (pharaonic) involves the creation of a covering seal by cutting and positioning the labia minora or labia majora with or without excision of the clitoral prepuce and glans. This phenomenon is known as infibulation. Type IV is defined as any act of harm to the genital area for nonmedical purposes. These types of female genital mutilation are mostly performed by traditional circumcisers. Currently, healthcare professionals are involved in this practice. Globally, the medicalization of FGM has been increasing, and nearly 18% of FGMs are performed by medical professionals [2].

Female genital mutilation is a global health concern. The United Nations (UN) estimates that more than 200 million women and girls in 30 countries have undergone this procedure. The highest FGM burden has been reported in Africa. The prevalence of FGM in Africa ranges from 0.3% to 99.2%. Over the past three decades, the prevalence of FGM has decreased; however, some countries have experienced insignificant changes and it is still widely practiced [3]. It is a universal practice in some African countries, including Somalia [4]. According to a systematic review and meta-analysis study, the prevalence of FGM has declined for both women and girls in 26 African nations, whereas it has increased for women in three of those countries: Somalia, Guinea-Bissau, and Burkina Faso. Among these, Somalia has the highest global prevalence of FGM [5]. In Somaliland, 98% of women aged between 15 and 49 years have undergone circumcision. Of the women who were circumcised, 61% had the pharaonic type, 7% the intermediate type, and 29% had the Sunni type [6].

Female genital mutilation is associated with adverse health outcomes. This includes both immediate and long-term negative health effects. Immediate negative health consequences include severe pain, excessive bleeding (hemorrhage), genital tissue swelling, infections, wound healing problems, shock, and even death. The long-term consequences include keloid scars, painful urination and menstruation, painful sexual intercourse, decreased sexual satisfaction, childbirth complications, and psychological problems [7]. Common obstetric complications, including increased requirement for emergency cesarean section, prolonged second stage of labor, perineal tears, and increased demand for neonatal intensive care units, are attributed to the practice of FGM [8].

The research showed different factors associated with FGM. Sociodemographic factors, including lower levels of education, residence, and wealth index, were positively associated with FGM [9, 10]. Women of a low socioeconomic status tend to have a positive attitude towards it [11]. However, awareness level, health education, and behavioral change communication regarding harmful traditional practices were negatively associated with FGM [12]. It is clear that tradition, social pressure, and religion are the main motives for performing FGM in most African countries [13–15]. This study guided by the Socio-Ecological Model (SEM); this study conceptualizes female genital mutilation (FGM) as the outcome of interacting influences operating at multiple levels. At the individual level, women’s and girls’ characteristics such as age, education, knowledge and attitudes toward FGM, and personal exposure to the practice shape their risk of being cut or continuing the practice for their daughters [16].

In Somalia, FGM has numerous social supports, including virginity certification, men’s preference for circumcised women in marriage, and perceived religious expectations [16]. Considering FGM as a Muslim religious requirement [17] and stigmatizing those who are not circumcised in society [18, 19] are among the main reasons for its performance. By adopting a socio-ecological framework and leveraging high-quality, standardized DHS-program data, the study generates granular, context-specific evidence that goes beyond generalized African or Somali trends. The findings can inform targeted, locally grounded interventions for example, identifying subpopulations at highest risk for Type III FGM, tailoring messaging to address specific religious misconceptions, and prioritizing geographic and socio-demographic clusters where harmful practices remain most entrenched. Consequently, the study provides a critical evidence base for policymakers, religious and community leaders, and health and human rights advocates seeking to design and evaluate more effective, culturally sensitive strategies to accelerate the abandonment of FGM in Somaliland.

Due to these social norms, negative health consequences are overlooked among Somalia people. Despite its devastating health consequences and deep-rooted societal norms, only a few studies have assessed existing practices and contributing factors in Somalia. While literature exists, there is a gap in recent, nationally representative secondary analyses focusing specifically on the severity (Pharaonic type). Therefore, the current study aimed to assess the prevalence and types of FGM in Somaliland and associated factors using the first nationally representative survey, the 2020 Somaliland Health and Demographic Survey (SLHDS).

Methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Somaliland, located in the Horn of Africa. It is bordered by Ethiopia to the south, Somalia to the east, and Djibouti to the west. The bulk of Somaliland’s 850 km coastline, which comprises its 176,119.2 km2 territory, is located along the Gulf of Aden. The nation is divided into six regions: Awdal, Marodijeh, Sahil, Togdheer, Sanaag and Sool [6]. The Somaliland people are ethnic Somalis and Muslims. The total population of Somaliland was estimated to be 3.6 million in 2014. It was expected to increase to 4.2 million by 2020 at a growth rate of 2.93%.

Study design and study period

This study used data from the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey. The survey was the first nation-level cross-sectional study. It started in August 2018 and ended in December 2019, and was reported as the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS) [6].

Sample size and sampling

This study is a secondary analysis of data from the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS). We analyzed a weighted sample of 5,143 women aged 15–49 years.

The original SLDHS employed a stratified, two-stage cluster sampling design. First, enumeration areas were defined using Geographic Information System (GIS) mapping of residential structures and field verification. From 2,806 primary sampling units across urban and rural areas, 35 enumeration areas per region were selected using probability proportional to size. In the second stage, households within each selected enumeration area were listed, and a sample of households was drawn to produce nationally representative estimates. The information was collected from head of house hold either husband or wife based on their availability. Sampling weights provided in the SLDHS were applied in all analyses to account for the survey design and ensure population-level inference [6].

Variables

Dependent variable

Female genital mutilation (FGM) was dichotomized based on its type (pharaonic or other).

Independent variables

Sociodemographic characteristics included respondents’ age, age at circumcision, residence, region, educational level, and wealth index level. Exposure to social media included watching television, ever using the internet, frequency of hearing the radio, and use of mobile phones. Other factors were women’s perceptions of FGM, religious perceptions of the practice, and who performed it.

Sampling and data collection methods

During SLDHS, data collection followed the standardized DHS Program protocols, including the use of validated questionnaires and electronic data collection (CAPI) to ensure data quality and privacy.

Urban and rural data collections [6]

The study sampled 30 households from each of 10 selected enumeration areas per regional stratum, using complete household listings. Data were collected by trained teams using an Android-based CSPro platform, with support from listing teams skilled in map reading and identification of enumeration area boundaries and selected households.

Nomadic data collections [6]

Nomadic households were listed one day prior to enumeration in temporary nomadic settlements to ensure updated household lists. Thirty households were then selected per enumeration area, and trained enumerator teams collected data based on lists provided by supervisors.

Data quality assurance

A total of 92 supervisors and interviewers were trained, with pre-tests conducted to ensure adequate skills in both manual and CAPI data collection. Fieldwork was closely supervised, and GPS tracking was used to support geo-referenced and well-monitored data collection [6]. UNFPA trained four Somaliland master trainers in Kigali in October 2017 over one month, and these experts also contributed to the development and review of the data collection tools.

Data management and analysis

A weighted sample was considered in the analysis to maintain representativeness of the survey data. The data were exported to STATA software, version 14. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were computed. To assess the factors associated with FGM, multilevel analysis was considered; however, due to insignificant variability among different clusters (ICC = 0.039), bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were applied. Moreover, due to the very few observations of the outcome variable ‘non-circumcision events’, 26 out of the 5,143 samples, the assumption of regression was not fulfilled. Hence, associated factors were assessed for the pharaonic type of FGM by dichotomizing the outcome variables as pharaonic type and other types. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were considered as candidates for multivariable binary logistic regression analysis. Finally, in the multivariable analysis, adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals and p-values of less than 0.05 were declared to have a statistically significant association.

Ethical considerations

This study was based on SLHDS data, which did not contain any identifying information. The formal letter was written to the responsible body to allow access to and ethical clearance for the SLHDS data. The confidentiality of data was strictly maintained. This study is an independent secondary analysis conducted after obtaining permission from the Ministry of Health to use the dataset. The ethical approval letter (Ref: MOHD/DG:2/1038/2023) was written by the ethics committee of the Ministry of Health in the Republic of Somaliland. Detailed information on the method and ethical issues is available from the 2020 Somaliland Health Demographic Survey Report [20]. Consent for participation was obtained by the SLHDS team at the time of data collection in accordance with national ethical guidelines.

Factors associated with type of female genital mutilation

The results indicated a high prevalence of female genital mutilation among participants. The majority were of the type III(pharaonic) type. The factors associated with the pharaonic type compared to other types of FGM were analyzed using bivariable and multivariable logistic regressions. In multivariable binary logistic regression, region, residence, educational level, wealth quintiles, person who performed circumcision, and internet use were significantly associated with the pharaonic type of genital mutilation. Women with no formal education had a nearly two times higher odds of experiencing pharaonic circumcision (AOR = 2.22; 95 CI: 1.46, 3.36; P < 0.001) compared to women who had attained a higher educational level. Among total circumcised women, those circumcised by traditional circumcisers had 8.33 times higher odds of experiencing the pharaonic type than those circumcised by health professionals. Women who had never used the internet had a 52% higher likelihood of experiencing pharaonic circumcision than their counterparts (Table 4).

Discussion

The prevalence of female genital mutilation in Somaliland is unexpectedly high. The prevalence reported in the current study was comparable to that reported in a study conducted in the Somali region of Ethiopia [21]. A study on the global prevalence of FGM also reported the highest prevalence in Somalia [5]. Again, a demographic and health survey report from Ethiopia showed that the Somali regional state had a higher prevalence of FGM (97.3%) [22] which supports the findings of the current report. Although the practice of FGM has decreased in most African countries [23], no change in prevalence was observed in three countries: Guinea-Bissau, Burkina Faso, and Somalia [5]. The reported prevalence in the current study is similar to that in the 2011 Somaliland FGM report. This indicates that, despite governmental and non-governmental organizations working towards banning this practice, it remains the same. This could be because of strong social support, traditional practices, and religious expectations [24]. Deep-rooted perceptions among the people of Somalia that uncircumcised women are hypersexual, unclean, and will not be chosen for marriage promote the practice [25]. This could also be due to the lack of national legislation that enforces the Somalian constitution, which states that “circumcision is prohibited” [26, 27].

The majority of people in Somaliland do not distinguish between the possible factors influencing the practice of female genital mutilation. The participants in this study did not identify a clear link between religious and cultural beliefs in the practice of FGM. Although there is no script in the Holy Quran indicating that FGM is a religious obligation [28], nearly half of respondents said it was a requirement of the Muslim religion. Moreover, nearly two-thirds of participants suggested that the practice should be continued. This is in line with a study conducted in the eastern part of Ethiopia, which found religious perception to be one of the main factors influencing the performance of FGM in Muslim communities [29]. Other widespread traditional beliefs promote FGM, including men’s preference for marriage, means of maintaining virginity, and sexual desensitization to prevent women from looking for other men to satisfy sexual desire when they are married [14].

More than half (57.87%) of circumcisions were performed at 5–9 years of age. The second most common age group was 10–14 years; 40.74% of girls had undergone circumcision. This finding is in line with those of other studies conducted in Ethiopia [25], Egypt [30] and Sudan [31]. Another study conducted in the Somali region of Ethiopia indicated that the practice is usually performed during infancy on the eighth day after birth or between 7 and 10 years of age [32]. The most common age range, 5–10 years, might be due to a cultural preference for genital cutting before menarche [25]. This timing for genital cutting greatly affects the emotion of these young girls and results in different psychological problems [33].

Regarding who was involved in performing female genital mutilation, the majority (82.03%) of FGM cases were performed by traditional circumcisers, followed by 15.42% by traditional birth attendants. This is in line with the UNICEF report, which showed that most procedures are performed by traditional circumcisers [24]. Moreover, the current study showed that a small proportion of FGMs were performed by health professionals. This contradicts the WHO and UNICEF calls to end the medicalization of FGM [28, 34]. Similar practices for medicalizing FGM have been reported in Egypt [35] and Iraq [36]. It is assumed to reduce immediate consequences, including severe pain, bleeding, and infections, but cannot avoid long-term medical consequences. Moreover, medicalizing FGM contradicts the medical code of ethics because ‘do less harm’ still contradicts the Hippocratic Oath to ‘do no harm’ [37].

The women of Somaliland experienced different types of female genital cutting. The most common type was pharaonic (type III). This is a dangerous type of FGM, by which practitioner remove tissue from the clitoris, labia minora, and parings of the inner walls of the labia majora. The two edges of the labia major were tightly stitched, leaving small holes for urination and menstruation. In Somaliland, approximately two-thirds of the women in the reproductive age group had experienced the pharaonic type of FGM. Studies from different African countries have shown that, even though there is an observed reduction in the practice of severe types of FGM, it is still practiced in some countries, such as Mali and Sierra Leone [23]. A study conducted in Ethiopia also showed that girls in Somalia and Afar regions were victims of this severe form of FGM [22]. However, the proportion of the pharaonic type in the current study was higher than that reported in Ethiopia [38]. It is also much greater than pooled prevalence of pharaonic (type III) FGM in Africa [4]. This could be due to deep-rooted social norms regarding FGM practices. The other most common type reported in this study was sunni (type I). This kind of FGM involves cutting the clitoris, a type mainly perceived as a fulfillment of a belief in religious requirements. This is the most common type of FGM practiced in African countries [4, 5].

The current study identified the factors associated with the pharaonic type of FGM. Variables including no formal education, lower wealth index level, circumcision by traditional practitioners, no exposure to the internet, and nomadic residence were positively associated with the pharaonic type of FGM. The pharaonic type of FGM is more prevalent among women with no formal education than among their counterparts. This finding is in line with the results of other studies conducted in Nigeria [39] and Ghana [40]. This might be due to poor awareness of the severe health consequences of this type of FGM. Research has shown that educated women are more likely to weigh the benefits and risks of different types of FGM, and make more autonomous decisions about their health than their counterparts [41].

Women who use the internet are less likely to have undergone a pharaonic type of female genital mutilation than their counterparts. This is in line with another study showing that exposure to mass media decreases the practice of FGM [42]. This could be due to an increased awareness of the health consequences of FGM as a result of accessing information on the internet. Awareness level is the main factor in changing individuals’ attitudes to FGM. This is in line with another study that showed that awareness level and attitude influence FGM practices [43]. This is also in line with a report from another study that showed that internet use and the sharing of information could help decrease female genital mutilation [44]. Regarding the person performing FGM, traditional circumcisers are more likely to perform the pharaonic type than health professionals. This could be because traditional circumcisers have poor knowledge of the immediate and long-term negative health effects of the pharaonic type. In addition, they are more likely to advocate traditional practices than are trained health professionals. Studies have shown that traditional circumcisers, parents, and grandparents make the decisions regarding FGM and type of practice [45].

Residence and region are among the factors associated with FGM. Women from nomadic residences were more likely to have undergone the pharaonic type of FGM than those from urban residences. This finding is in line with the results of a previous study that revealed the impact of geographical location on FGM [18]. This could be because people living in nomadic communities are less educated and have inadequate health information literacy. Information literacy is the best way to combat harmful traditional practices that are accepted as social norms rather than creating hard and fast rules and regulations [46].

Limitations and strengths of the study

This study analyzed a large data sample from the 2020 Somaliland Demographic and Health Survey, the first such survey to be conducted in the country. However, some limitations should be taken into consideration. As a secondary analysis, we are restricted to the variables available in the 2020 SLDHS. Because there were so few observations of no practice of FGM, binary logistic regression was conducted on the types of FGM (pharaonic type or other types). Hence, regression analysis was used to assess the predictors of pharaonic types. In addition, important variables, including parents’ educational status and knowledge of the negative health consequences of FGM, were not assessed by the DHS. The cross-sectional design of the survey prevents the establishment of temporal or causal relationships between the identified factors and the occurrence of FGM. Finally, the study relies on self-reported data, which is susceptible to recall bias (regarding the type or timing of the procedure) and social desirability bias, where participants might under-report certain aspects of the practice due to ongoing national anti-FGM campaigns.

Conclusion and recommendations

Somaliland continues to report a high prevalence nearly 100% practice of female genital mutilation, where it remains a deep-rooted traditional practice and Type III (pharaonic) genital mutilation more used in this population compared to other countries. Residing in nomadic communities, lack of formal education, lower wealth index, limited internet access, and the use of traditional circumcisers were identified as significant predictors associated with the pharaonic type. Being deeply rooted in traditional practices and influenced by religion makes it challenging to find quick solutions. However, it is recommended that governmental and non-governmental organizations strengthen stepwise strategic frameworks aimed at addressing this practice. Targeted interventions focusing on nomadic populations, poverty reduction, and the enhancement of educational status may contribute to a long-term reduction in prevalence. Furthermore, leveraging diverse media platforms to disseminate FGM-related health information is essential. Future research should utilize qualitative methodologies to explore the complex socio-cultural barriers to FGM prevention strategies within the Somaliland context.
