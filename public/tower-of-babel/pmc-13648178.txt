Pre-loss grief among family caregivers: An evolutionary concept analysis
Authors: Ying Qian, Meng Xu, Lili Yang, Ye Song, LinYan Yao, JiaYing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to clarify the evolving characteristics of pre-loss grief among family caregivers by identifying its attributes, antecedents, consequences, and related concepts.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across English databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, CINAHL, APA PsycInfo, and APA PsycArticles) and Chinese databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure, SinoMed, VIP, and Wanfang). These databases were searched from inception to April 2026. Rodgers’s evolutionary concept analysis method was used to analyze the included literature.ResultsA total of 36 articles were included. Four defining attributes of pre-loss grief among family caregivers were identified: multidimensionality of perceived loss; an emotionally and cognitively complex grief response; temporal and dynamic unfolding; and contextual embeddedness within ongoing caregiving roles. Antecedents were identified at four interrelated levels: illness-related, caregiver-related, relational, sociocultural and contextual levels. Consequences included psychological distress, caregiving strain, relational difficulties, and bereavement-related vulnerability; whereas meaning-making and strengthened relational connections were identified in specific caregiving contexts. The analysis clarified conceptual distinctions between pre-loss grief and related constructs such as anticipatory grief. Pre-loss grief among family caregivers can be understood as a grief-specific, multidimensional, and dynamic response to perceived losses before the care recipient’s death. This response is embedded within ongoing caregiving relationships and responsibilities.ConclusionsThis concept analysis provides a clearer conceptual foundation for grief-specific assessment for caregivers, development of measurement tools, and delivery of context-sensitive nursing support across the illness trajectory.

Introduction

Family caregivers play a central role in supporting individuals with serious, progressive, or life-limiting illnesses. Across dementia care, cancer care, palliative care, long-term care, and hospice care settings, they provide practical, emotional, and decision-making support [1]. These responsibilities often extend over months or even years and are shaped by uncertainty, changing family roles, and increasing support needs [2]. Many caregivers assume these responsibilities with limited preparation or prior experience while simultaneously adapting to progressive changes in the care recipient’s condition and functioning [3].

During serious illness, family caregivers may experience grief before the care recipient’s physical death. Perceived losses may include functional decline, cognitive or communicative changes, increased dependence, altered reciprocity, disrupted relationship patterns, and anticipated separation [4,5]. In dementia caregiving, such losses may emerge long before biological death as caregivers face progressive cognitive and relational decline [4,6]. In cancer and palliative care, caregivers may also grieve while witnessing deterioration, continuing to provide care, and facing approaching death [2,7]. Higher levels of grief before death have been associated with poorer psychological health, greater caregiving strain, and increased vulnerability during bereavement [8,9]. However, these experiences are not uniformly detrimental across caregivers or caregiving contexts [10]. Rather, the nature and intensity of grief before death may fluctuate across the illness and caregiving trajectory in response to disease progression, changing care demands, relational changes, and awareness of approaching death. The expression of grief before death is shaped by illness progression, care settings, family relationships, communication patterns, and sociocultural expectations regarding responsibility, emotional expression, and death [11].

Despite growing recognition of grief before death, the terminology used to describe this experience remains inconsistent, including anticipatory grief [2,12], anticipatory mourning [13], pre-death grief [4,6], caregiver grief [14,15], dementia grief [16], and pre-loss grief [7,11]. These terms are often used interchangeably, sometimes without clear conceptual distinctions [4,5,17,18]. Singer identified more than 18 terms and 30 definitions in the literature describing grief experienced by family members before a patient’s death [5]. Some studies define pre-loss grief as a grief-specific response to ongoing illness-related losses and the anticipated death or bereavement of a loved one [4,5]. Other studies employ broader or overlapping operationalizations of pre-loss grief, examining its associations with psychological distress, depressive symptoms, caregiver burden, preparedness, and adjustment throughout the end-of-life caregiving trajectory [7,19,20].

For nursing practice, such conceptual ambiguity has important clinical implications. When pre-loss grief is subsumed under caregiver burden, depression, anxiety, preparedness, or general psychological distress, its grief-specific features may be overlooked. Failure to recognize these grief-specific features may delay grief-specific assessment, referral, and support, and may also make it difficult to select appropriate measures or compare findings across studies [12,17,19].

To address these gaps, this study aimed to clarify the concept of pre-loss grief among family caregivers by examining its evolution in the literature. Specifically, we sought to identify its defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, surrogate terms, related concepts, and implications for nursing research and practice. Rodgers’s evolutionary concept analysis was employed because it is well suited for concepts that are dynamic, context-dependent, and shaped by disciplinary use over time [21]. This method is particularly appropriate for pre-loss grief among family caregivers because the concept has been discussed across nursing, palliative care, dementia care, psycho-oncology, psychology, and gerontology, yet its terminology and conceptual boundaries remain unsettled [19]. To navigate this complexity, we used “pre-loss grief” in this analysis as an operational focal term that deliberately highlights loss before death [9].

Methods

We followed Rodgers’s evolutionary concept analysis method [21,22]. The method involved: 1) identifying the concept of interest; 2) selecting the literature search realm and setting; 3) collecting relevant literature; 4) extracting and synthesizing data related to concept use; 5) identifying an exemplar, where appropriate; and 6) generating implications for further concept development.

Search methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, CINAHL, APA PsycInfo, APA PsycArticles, Web of Science, and Scopus, as well as four Chinese databases: China National Knowledge Infrastructure, SinoMed, VIP, and Wanfang. Searches covered records from the databases’ inception to April 5, 2026.

The search strategies combined terms related to pre-loss grief and other expressions of grief before death with terms related to family or informal caregiving. Search terms and field tags were adapted for each database. The full database-specific search strategies are presented in Appendix A.

Study selection

Articles were eligible if they addressed pre-loss grief or related expressions of grief before death in family or informal caregiving; provided conceptual, empirical, measurement-related, or conceptual boundary material relevant to grief-specific responses before death; addressed at least one component of Rodgers’s concept analysis; and were published in English or Chinese in peer-reviewed medical, nursing, psychological, or allied health journals. Eligible source types included qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods, psychometric, review, theoretical, conceptual, and clinical discussion papers. Articles were excluded if they were protocols or proposals; were duplicate publications; were not available in full text; were published in languages other than English or Chinese; did not focus on family or informal caregivers; or focused only on general psychological distress, depression, anxiety, caregiver burden, or preparedness without sufficient conceptual relevance to the focal concept.

After duplicate records were removed, two researchers independently screened titles and abstracts according to the eligibility criteria. Because the purpose of this study was concept clarification rather than effect synthesis, the included literature was evaluated primarily for conceptual relevance rather than appraised using conventional risk-of-bias criteria [23]. The full texts of potentially eligible articles were then reviewed independently. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third researcher resolved discrepancies. Reference lists were also checked to identify additional conceptually relevant articles.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were extracted using a standardized Excel-based coding matrix. Extracted information included citation number; author and year; country/region or literature scope; study design or source type; illness or caregiving context; study aims or themes; terms or constructs used; the role of the evidence in the concept analysis; and the main conceptual contribution. Information relevant to Rodgers’s analytic components was also coded for cross-study synthesis.

Two researchers independently extracted the data. The extracted information was cross-checked, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. When consensus could not be reached, a third researcher reviewed the data and made the final decision.

Data were analyzed using Rodgers’s evolutionary approach [21]. Structured content coding was used to organize and compare textual data as part of Rodgers’s evolutionary concept analysis; it did not replace the concept analysis [24]. Text segments relevant to concept use, definitions, attributes, antecedents, consequences, related concepts, surrogate terms, empirical referents, and contextual features were coded iteratively. Similar codes were compared across illness contexts, caregiver relationships, study designs, cultural settings, and disciplinary contexts and were then clustered into themes. Through repeated comparison and refinement, the defining attributes, antecedents, consequences, related concepts, and synthesized definition were developed.

Terms were classified as surrogate terms when they were used interchangeably to denote substantially similar experiences of grief before death, whereas related concepts were defined as distinct constructs that overlapped with but were not equivalent to pre-loss grief.

The search retrieved 2,001 records. The included literature covered different illness and caregiving contexts, particularly dementia care, cancer care, palliative and end-of-life care, and broader family caregiving settings. The geographic scope of the included articles encompassed the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Portugal, Denmark, Germany, the Netherlands, Japan, Singapore, and China, as well as international or multiregional contexts. Evolution of the concept

The concept of grief before death has developed through a gradual shift from general theories of mourning to anticipatory grief and, more recently, to pre-death and pre-loss grief terminology in family caregiving research. Freud’s work was frequently cited in this historical context, but primarily in relation to mourning after death; it did not directly address the grief experienced by family caregivers while the patient was still alive [13,17,26,30].

The reviewed literature described Lindemann’s work on acute grief as advancing this discussion by identifying grief reactions that could arise in anticipation of possible death or separation, thereby showing that grief may begin before bereavement rather than only after death [13,17,26]. The idea was later developed through the concepts of anticipatory grief and anticipatory mourning, which were used to describe grief, separation distress, and other emotional responses occurring before an expected death [17,20,25].

From the late twentieth century onward, anticipatory grief terminology was increasingly applied in clinical settings, palliative care, dementia care, psycho-oncology, and family caregiving. In these contexts, grief before death was used to describe emotional, cognitive, relational, and behavioral responses among patients, relatives, and caregivers experiencing progressive illness, deterioration, and impending death [17,19,20]. However, this literature also demonstrated considerable conceptual variation. Although anticipatory grief was sometimes framed as a potentially preparatory or adaptive process, it was also used to describe present-oriented illness-related losses, separation distress, psychological distress, caregiver burden, preparedness, coping, and even bereavement-related outcomes [13,17,26].

As these boundary problems became more evident, timing-based and loss-focused terms such as pre-death grief, predeath grief, pre-bereavement grief, pre-loss grief, and preloss grief became increasingly relevant in the dementia care, cancer care, palliative care, and broader family caregiving literature. These terms helped position grief before physical death while drawing attention to ongoing losses during caregiving, including functional decline, cognitive and communicative changes, altered reciprocity, relational disruption, role changes, and anticipated separation [4,5,27].

Taken together, the conceptual evolution of grief before death reflects a shift from a primarily anticipatory and sometimes adaptive framing toward a more grief-specific, temporally dynamic, and embedded in caregiving understanding of loss before death. Because no single term was used consistently across the literature, this analysis used pre-loss grief as the operational focal term while preserving the original terminology used by each source.

Attributes

Multidimensionality of perceived loss

Pre-loss grief encompasses current interwoven illness-related losses, relational disruption, and loss of self or family identity [5,27]. Illness-related losses include the patient’s physical and functional deterioration, cognitive decline, altered personality or communication, increasing dependence, and anticipated future separation or death [4,28].

Relational disruption is central to pre-loss grief. Caregivers may grieve changes in attachment, the care recipient’s familiar identity, reciprocity, shared roles, and the sense of “who we are together,” even while the person remains physically present [2]. In progressive conditions such as dementia, caregivers may repeatedly experience the familiar person as disappearing or the previous relationship as becoming increasingly difficult to sustain [4,16]. In rapidly deteriorating or terminal illness, visible decline, dependence, disrupted plans, and limited remaining time may make anticipated separation more immediate [2,28,30].

Caregivers may also experience perceived losses of prior roles, identity, autonomy, daily routines, social participation, and shared expectations for the future, particularly in spousal cancer caregiving and dementia caregiving contexts [[31], [32], [33]]. These losses may accompany transitions from being a spouse, partner, or adult child to becoming a primary caregiver; however, the defining feature is the caregiver’s grief-related perception of loss rather than the role transition itself [10,11].

Emotionally and cognitively complex grief response

Pre-loss grief is more than general distress because its emotional and cognitive content is organized around ongoing and anticipated losses before death [5,9,19]. It is directed not only toward the care recipient’s anticipated death, but also toward losses that have already occurred or continue to unfold while the person remains alive [4].

Core emotional experiences include yearning for the person as they once were, sadness, emotional pain, anger, guilt, helplessness, separation anxiety, and difficulty accepting disease progression or approaching death [4,7,30,34]. These emotions often coexist and may conflict with one another. For example, caregivers may simultaneously hope for stability or recovery while wishing for the patient’s suffering to end [2,28]. Caregivers may continue providing care while experiencing exhaustion, fear, guilt, or emotional vulnerability [10,30].

Temporal and dynamic unfolding

Pre-loss grief occurs during the care recipient’s lifetime, while caregiving responsibilities, decision-making, and relationship maintenance may continue [4,5]. This temporal positioning distinguishes pre-loss grief from post-death bereavement and bereavement adaptation.

Pre-loss grief is not a static emotional state occurring at a single point before death. Rather, it fluctuates across the illness trajectory in response to disease progression, functional decline, treatment failure, escalating care demands, reduced communication, transition to palliative care, and heightened awareness of impending death [2,29,35]. It may also recur when caregivers encounter new cues of loss, such as changes in the patient’s familiar self, difficulty sustaining the previous relationship, or the collapse of shared plans [16,36]. Qualitative evidence also describes this temporal process as a tension between holding on and letting go during terminal illness caregiving [37].

Contextual embeddedness within ongoing caregiving roles

The fourth attribute is contextual embeddedness within ongoing caregiving roles and responsibilities [2,38]. Unlike post-death bereavement, pre-loss grief occurs while caregivers continue to provide daily care, communicate with healthcare professionals, coordinate care within the family, and make end-of-life decisions [2,10,30].

This embeddedness shapes the expression of pre-loss grief. Pre-loss grief may not be expressed through overt emotional disclosure but rather through sustained presence, vigilant observation, protective caregiving, maintenance of family routines, emotional restraint, and efforts to preserve connection [10,28].

Antecedents and contextual conditions

Illness-related antecedents

Illness-related antecedents were the most immediate conditions associated with pre-loss grief. These included a diagnosis of a life-threatening, progressive, or incurable illness; worsening disease; increasing symptoms; cognitive, functional, or communication decline; uncertainty about the disease course; and awareness of approaching death [2,4,28]. These factors expose caregivers to repeated signals that the patient, relationship, and future are changing irreversibly.

The influence of illness-related antecedents lies not only in disease severity but also in how illness makes loss visible, cumulative, ambiguous, or difficult to integrate. Progressive decline may expose caregivers to repeated losses before death, whereas terminal deterioration may make future separation more definite and urgent [4,16,27]. The illness trajectory should therefore be understood as a source of loss-related stressors rather than as the grief experience itself.

Caregiver-related antecedents

Caregiver-related antecedents included increasing care intensity, expanding practical responsibilities, decision-making pressure, perceived caregiver burden, insufficient preparedness, limited prior caregiving experience, limited coping resources, and preexisting psychological vulnerability [19,39]. These conditions may be linked to greater exposure to loss, reduced perceived control, and intensified grief responses [10,19].

Some studies suggest that insufficient preparedness, limited coping resources, or uncertainty about the dying process may be associated with more intense grief [19,40]. Demographic characteristics such as spousal status, gender, education, or socioeconomic status have been reported as potential vulnerability factors in some studies, but findings were inconsistent and should not be considered defining conditions [7].

Relational antecedents

Relational antecedents included close kinship, spousal or partner roles, strong emotional attachment, attachment insecurity, role transitions, unresolved relational tension, unfinished communication, and difficulty discussing death or future separation [28,41].

Family communication and support also shape the intensity and expression of grief. Avoidance, denial, emotional distance, family conflict, and difficulty communicating about disease progression or death may heighten grief-related distress, whereas acknowledgment, open communication, family cohesion, and practical support may help caregivers tolerate uncertainty and maintain connection [28,42].

Sociocultural and contextual antecedents

Sociocultural and contextual antecedents included social support, communication with healthcare professionals, access to palliative or dementia care services, recognition of caregiver grief, care settings, help-seeking norms, and cultural expectations regarding family responsibility, emotional expression, and communication about death [29,43].

Studies in Chinese contexts have indicated that filial responsibility, family obligation, emotional concealment, and avoidance of direct death-related communication may influence caregivers’ experience and expression of grief before death [10,11]. Western and multicultural studies similarly emphasize social recognition, support networks, healthcare communication, care environments, and hidden or disenfranchised grief [42,43].

Consequences

Consequences of pre-loss grief are outcomes that may follow, accompany, or be intensified by grief before death. At the psychological and caregiving domains, high levels of pre-loss grief have been associated with poorer psychological health, lower caregiver well-being, and greater caregiver burden [7,9,19,44]. Qualitative evidence also highlighted increased support needs among families experiencing grief before death [2]. These outcomes should be distinguished from the emotional content of pre-loss grief itself, such as yearning, sadness, emotional pain, guilt, and anger. At the caregiving and functional levels, pre-loss grief may contribute to increased caregiving strain, greater support needs, difficulty sustaining care, insufficient self-care, and impaired social or occupational functioning [2,7]. Relational consequences may include emotional withdrawal, avoidance of death-related conversations, conflict, altered intimacy, and difficulty maintaining connection with the care recipient or other family members [10,30]. Longitudinal and cohort evidence further suggests that elevated pre-loss or pre-death grief may be associated with post-bereavement vulnerability, including post-loss grief, depressive symptoms, and persistent emotional difficulties [8,41].

Some qualitative and culturally specific studies have reported meaning-oriented or relational responses in the context of pre-loss grief, including valuing remaining time, renewed appreciation of everyday life, relational closeness, emotional regulation, resilience, personal growth, and efforts to balance holding on with letting go [11,16,45]. These responses should not be interpreted as universal positive consequences. Rather, they appear more likely when caregivers have sufficient communication, preparedness, social support, emotional regulation, cultural or spiritual meaning-making, and opportunities for meaningful interaction [29,42].

Surrogate terms and related concepts

Clarifying surrogate terms and related concepts was necessary because the included literature used multiple overlapping expressions to describe grief before death. In this analysis, no unequivocal surrogate term was identified that could be used interchangeably with pre-loss grief across all illness contexts and caregiver populations [5,17,20]. Timing-based expressions, including pre-death grief and pre-bereavement grief, locate grief before death but vary in conceptual scope across dementia, palliative care, and broader life-limiting illness contexts [4,5,9]. Anticipatory grief was the most frequently used concept; it overlaps with pre-loss grief in temporal location but may also imply future-oriented anticipation, preparation, adaptation, coping, or broader psychological distress [5,9,20].

Preparedness for death, caregiver burden, psychological distress, depression, anxiety, ambiguous loss, and bereavement grief were treated as related or contrasting concepts rather than equivalent terms. Preparedness refers to perceived readiness rather than grief-specific suffering [19,40]. Caregiver burden, depression, anxiety, and psychological distress are non-grief-specific constructs [14,17,19]. Ambiguous loss helps explain physical presence with psychological or relational alteration, especially in dementia caregiving [4,36], whereas bereavement grief occurs after death [5,19].

Exemplar

The following exemplar was drawn from Mio’s publicly available autobiographical graphic memoir: I Am a Caregiver: Becoming a Caregiver [46]. This exemplar was not used as a source of analytic evidence; rather, consistent with Rodgers's method, it provided a concrete illustration of the four attributes. Mio, a young Malaysian Chinese woman, became a family caregiver after her father was diagnosed with cancer. During caregiving, she assisted with daily care, provided emotional support, and gradually assumed responsibilities beyond practical care. As his condition deteriorated, ordinary caregiving moments became reminders of progressive loss. For example, when his lung function worsened and he could no longer drink through a normal straw, she repeatedly cut the straw shorter to help him drink. The shortened straw became a concrete reminder of his fading strength and of her painful awareness that the familiar father she knew was gradually disappearing. Mio [46] described the caregiver as being “locked in a transparent box of sadness,” unable to find the key even after leaving the caregiving scene.

This exemplar illustrates the four defining attributes of pre-loss grief. Mio’s grief was tied to multidimensional losses, including physical decline, altered reciprocity, role changes, daily disruption, and anticipated separation. Her emotional pain unfolded dynamically as new signs of decline emerged, and her grief remained embedded in ongoing care responsibilities, emotional restraint, and efforts to maintain family stability.

Synthesized definition of pre-loss grief among family caregivers

Based on this synthesis, pre-loss grief among family caregivers can be defined as a grief-specific, multidimensional, and dynamic response that occurs before the physical death of the care recipient. It arises from caregivers’ perceived losses, including illness-related decline, relational disruption, role change, loss of reciprocity, altered identity, disrupted routines, and anticipated future separation. It is manifested through emotionally and cognitively complex grief responses, such as yearning, sadness, emotional pain, guilt, anger, helplessness, separation anxiety, and difficulty accepting deterioration or approaching death. This grief unfolds over time, fluctuates across the illness trajectory, and is embedded within ongoing caregiving relationships, responsibilities, decision-making, and sociocultural contexts. Discussion

A core theoretical contribution of this analysis is the differentiation of pre-loss grief from related concepts of grief before death, particularly anticipatory grief. Traditionally, anticipatory grief has been widely used to describe emotional reactions that occur before an expected death and may facilitate psychological adjustment to anticipated loss [17]. In contrast, the present analysis suggests that pre-loss grief may be better conceptualized as grief-specific, loss-oriented suffering that emerges during chronic or terminal illness and caregiving rather than as a generalized adaptive coping process. Based on the reviewed literature, this analysis identifies the defining attributes, commonly described antecedents, and potential psychosocial consequences of pre-loss grief. However, the stability, temporal ordering, and causal relationships among these elements require further empirical testing through longitudinal, psychometric, and intervention research. This conceptual distinction may help address a methodological limitation in prior studies, namely the interpretation of negative emotions before death solely through the lens of anticipatory grief [9].

Transition Theory provides a useful interpretive lens for understanding why pre-loss grief unfolds across illness and caregiving trajectories, although it was not the analytic framework for this concept analysis [47]. The attribute of temporal and dynamic unfolding is consistent with ongoing health-illness and role transitions; the multidimensionality of perceived loss reflects changes in identity, relationships, daily life, and the anticipated future; and contextual embeddedness within ongoing caregiving roles highlights that these transitions occur while caregivers continue to provide care. The emotionally and cognitively complex grief response reflects caregivers’ psychological appraisal of these overlapping transitions and includes loss awareness, yearning, guilt, uncertainty, and difficulty accepting progressive deterioration.

Cultural context also shapes how pre-loss grief is expressed and recognized. In Chinese caregiving contexts represented in the included studies, family obligation, filial responsibility, emotional restraint, indirect communication about death, and the moral meaning of “doing enough” may affect both the visibility and interpretation of grief [10,11]. These patterns caution against assuming that open emotional disclosure is always the preferred or only valid expression of grief. Silence, emotional control, continued treatment efforts, and devotion to caregiving represent culturally shaped ways of maintaining responsibility, protecting the patient, and sustaining relational connection [10,11].

Existing tools for assessing grief before death vary in their specificity, target populations, illness contexts, and cultural validation [12,19]. Future measurement efforts should prioritize grief-specific indicators, such as separation distress, yearning, and emotional pain, while distinguishing them from caregiver burden, psychological distress, preparedness, and post-death bereavement symptoms [5,12,19]. The timing of assessment should account for critical junctures, including declines in communication abilities, functional deterioration, transition to palliative care, and the period approaching death [12,19].

Beyond measurement and conceptual refinement, future research should also prioritize nursing interventions for caregivers experiencing pre-loss grief. Intervention studies may target modifiable processes closely related to pre-loss grief, including preparedness, family communication, coping, meaning-making, caregiving confidence, perceived support, and relational connection. Evidence from dignity-oriented, family-centered, meaning-focused, and reminiscence-based approaches points to potential intervention directions; however, their effects on pre-loss grief and caregiver psychosocial adjustment require further testing [48,49]. Future research should clarify the active components, optimal timing, cultural adaptation, delivery formats, and clinically meaningful outcomes of these interventions across different illness trajectories and care settings.

Implications for nursing practice

This concept analysis suggests that nurses should assess pre-loss grief as a grief-specific experience rather than solely as caregiver burden or general distress. In routine caregiver assessments, nurses may consider asking about yearning, relational deprivation, guilt, emotional pain, difficulty accepting deterioration, and feared future separation. They may also ask about both actual and anticipated losses, for example, “What aspects of your relationship feel most altered by the illness?” or “What feels already lost, and what are you afraid of losing next?” These questions may help identify caregivers whose grief is hidden beneath competent caregiving, emotional restraint, or continued devotion to care.

In Chinese clinical contexts, some caregivers may express pre-loss grief indirectly, particularly when factors such as emotional restraint, filial responsibility, family harmony, or the wish to protect the patient are salient within the family. Nurses should therefore avoid relying solely on explicit verbal disclosure of sadness. Sleep disturbance, somatic complaints, irritability, withdrawal, excessive vigilance in care, persistent guilt about care decisions, or difficulty discussing deterioration may serve as cues for further grief-specific assessment.

Assessment should be repeated across the illness trajectory, especially at critical transition points, such as treatment failure, rapid functional decline, loss of communication, transition to palliative or hospice care, end-of-life decision-making, and visible signs of approaching death. Nursing support can then be tailored to caregivers’ changing needs by validating grief responses, facilitating communication about illness progression and care goals, supporting role transitions and unfinished conversations, and preparing families for separation while maintaining meaningful presence. Severe or persistent pre-loss grief should be documented to inform psychosocial referral and bereavement follow-up after death.

Limitations

This analysis has several limitations. First, terminology in the literature remains heterogeneous, and the classification of surrogate terms and related concepts may vary across disciplinary traditions, illness contexts, and cultural settings. Second, evidence remains uneven across illnesses, cultures, and caregiver relationships, with much of the literature concentrated in dementia, cancer, palliative care, and end-of-life contexts. Findings may therefore be less transferable to other serious illnesses, earlier stages of illness, younger caregivers, or health systems with different family caregiving arrangements. Third, many included studies were cross-sectional, limiting causal interpretation and understanding of temporal change, transition mechanisms, and the relationship between pre-loss grief and bereavement outcomes. Fourth, although the search covered English-language and Chinese-language literature, relevant studies published in other languages may have been missed. As with all concept analyses, these findings are interpretive and influenced by the available literature and researchers’ analytic judgments.

Conclusions

This concept analysis clarified that pre-loss grief among family caregivers can be understood as a grief-specific, multidimensional, and dynamic response to perceived losses before the care recipient’s death. This response is embedded within ongoing caregiving relationships and responsibilities. By distinguishing pre-loss grief from adjacent but non-equivalent constructs, this analysis provides a clearer conceptual foundation for nursing research, measurement development, and clinical practice.
