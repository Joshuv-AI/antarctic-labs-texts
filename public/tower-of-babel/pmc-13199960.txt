Reflections and lessons learned from implementing the Ontario Health Equity Impact Assessment Tool in community practice
Authors: Francis Routledge, Sabrina Campanella, Yona Lunsky, Johanna Lake, Kenneth Po-Lun Fung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIn this commentary, we describe our experience using the Ontario Ministry of Health's Health Equity Impact Assessment (HEIA) Tool as part of a project evaluating the implementation of an Acceptance and Commitment Training (ACT) workshop for family caregivers of neurodivergent people and people with disabilities from multiple communities across Canada.Study design/methodsCaregiver and clinician ACT facilitators completed the HEIA Tool to promote awareness of potential health (in)equities related to the workshop.ResultsFacilitators reported challenges understanding and implementing the Tool, which prompted our team to customize it for this context.ConclusionsWe outline recommendations based on the implementation strategies we used, which may have relevance to other community-based programs or initiatives seeking to conduct a health equity assessment.Keywords: Health equity, Public health, Caregivers, Community collaboration, Implementation science

Introduction

In this commentary, we describe our experience using the Ontario Health Equity Impact Assessment (HEIA) Tool in the context of a research project [1] studying the implementation of a collaborative group-based Acceptance and Commitment Training (ACT) workshop [2] to support the well-being of family caregivers of neurodivergent people and people with disabilities, co-delivered by caregiver and clinician facilitators in the community.

ACT is a third-wave, evidence-based therapy that promotes psychological flexibility through six core processes: acceptance, defusion, contact with the present moment, self-as-context, values and committed action [3]. As part of this project, we engaged 11 community-based ACT facilitation teams from across Canada to use the HEIA Tool to identify caregiver populations who may not have equitable access and/or encounter barriers when accessing and participating in the workshop. Each team included at least one family caregiver and a clinician facilitator who were trained to co-deliver the workshop.

The Health Equity Impact Assessment Tool

The Ontario HEIA Tool was published in 2012 by the Ontario Ministry of Health and Long-Term Care to be a practical, adaptable decision support tool for health-related organizations and staff to conduct health equity assessments as part of a policy, program or initiative [4]. The Tool aims to promote awareness of health (in)equities, prompt reflections, and ultimately, facilitate the creation of plans to promote health equity.

The HEIA Tool consists of five steps:

1.Scoping: Identify potential populations that may be affected by a project or initiative (e.g., racialized communities, immigrants and refugees, Indigenous populations, people with disabilities, 2SLGBTQ+ communities, etc).

2.Potential Impacts: Assess potential unintended negative and positive impacts of a project or initiative on the identified populations. Examples of unintended negative impacts may include affected communities being excluded from a program or made to feel uncomfortable in how the services are delivered leading to dropouts or other adverse consequences. Examples of unintended positive impacts may include community building and social support.

Health equity assessments in public health practice

The HEIA Tool in its original form does not reflect advancements made in the Canadian health and social services systems over the past decade, including the recently updated Canadian public health core competencies with a focused effort on health equity and social justice [5]. This is reflected in challenges other organizations encountered using the Tool, including the time and resources to complete it [6]. This is consistent with barriers to implementing health equity tools more broadly and highlights important mitigating strategies, for example, providing interprofessional education on health equity concepts, ensuring sufficient time to complete health equity assessments and collaborating with community partners [[7], [6], [8]].

Methods

Implementing the HEIA Tool

As part of a larger study, caregiver and clinician facilitators were asked to complete the HEIA before implementing their ACT workshop to help identify caregiver populations who may not have equitable access and/or encounter barriers when accessing or participating in the workshop. Many of our community collaborators from health and social services fields, had little, if any, knowledge or experience of health equity impact assessments or public health terms used to describe health (in)equities more broadly. Although information about the Ontario HEIA Tool and how to conduct a health equity assessment was widely available online at the time of our study (e.g., Public Health Ontario [5]), our facilitation teams, consisting of clinicians and family caregivers working together, found the materials (e.g., workbook, videos, supplements) cumbersome and difficult to understand. When we first introduced the Tool, the teams reported confusion around whom it was intended for and how to complete it during our virtual meetings. The time needed to familiarize themselves with the Tool (e.g., workbook, courses, webinars), and the health equity-related concepts, also presented as barriers to using it with busy clinical and caregiver schedules.

Based on this feedback, we sought to customize the HEIA Tool to make it more accessible and user-friendly. This resulted in three implementation strategies: 1) simplifying language and providing clear examples; 2) providing education on public health equity literacy education along with guided support to promote equitable and meaningful community engagement; and 3) converting complex tables and frameworks into a step-by-step process through a guided survey tool.

Results

Strategy 1: Simplifying language and providing clear examples

Recommendation: Using plain language and providing clear examples helped people understand the purpose of the Tool and how it could support their work.

Prioritizing accessible language and providing concrete, relatable examples was the first strategy we employed when adapting the HEIA Tool for implementation with our facilitation teams. Guided by the Plain Language Association International [9], we modified some of the language in the Tool so that teams could easily understand its purpose and the steps to complete it. This was an iterative process whereby research team members sought feedback from facilitation team partners including clinician and caregiver end users, as well as project team members with expertise in plain language writing and public health, then made adaptations based on this information.

Strategy 2: Providing health equity literacy education and support

Recommendation: Providing education on health equity concepts and public health terminology, along with guided support, promoted equitable and meaningful engagement.

Research team members supported facilitation teams by hosting education sessions on health (in)equities. In some cases, research team members also supported facilitation teams in completing the Tool. Given that an aim of the HEIA Tool is to promote health equity by embedding equity into projects and highlighting avoidable health disparities, it is important that the Tool is accessible and useable by those working and learning outside of the public health sector, regardless of their education and experience.

Strategy 3: Enhancing online useability

Recommendation: Improving usability of the Tool by converting complex tables and frameworks into an online step-by-step process through a guided survey tool.

Given the geographic distribution of the different teams, completing the HEIA Tool in-person was not possible. The Tool, which is available online as a non-fillable Portable Document Format (PDF), was difficult for people to complete together virtually. Accordingly, we customized the Tool for easier use by converting it into an interactive online survey. This involved utilizing a step-by-step process and text prompts to create dynamic paths based on previous responses. Instead of completing the assessment as an obligatory checklist through a non-fillable PDF, guided prompts helped stimulate critical thinking and discussion amongst team members around who had challenges accessing the ACT workshops and why, as well as barriers they may encounter during participation.

Discussion

The HEIA Tool was designed to make programs and initiatives more equitable; however, our experience using the Tool identified that it was not accessible for everyone. In line with human and user-centered design principles, we must design tools with the input and involvement of the people who will use them [10]. Further, our experiences highlighted the importance of ongoing refinement so that public health tools remain responsive to evolving social and structural contexts, while also aligning with updated public health core competencies and principles of culturally responsive care [11].

In the context of the HEIA Tool and other health equity assessments, our findings suggest a need for user-friendly design, accessible education in health equity and public health concepts, and practical support to help users complete these assessments [12]. Based on our lessons learned, we recommend the following strategies for using the HEIA Tool in community practice: 1) simplifying language and providing examples; 2) providing health (in)equity literacy education and support; and 3) enhancing online usability.

The role of a health equity impact assessment in developing responsive and equitable health and social initiatives is crucial, and this must begin with accessible tools that can be understood and implemented in a meaningful way by a diverse range of users.
