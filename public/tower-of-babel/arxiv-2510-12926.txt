Structure of solutions to continuous constraint satisfaction problems through the statistics of wedged and inscribed spheres
Jaron Kent-Dobias

Abstract
The study of random landscapes has long relied on counting stationary points: metastable states and the barriers between them. However, this method is useless for describing flat regions, common in constraint satisfaction problems. We introduce a characterization of flat regions by counting the number of spheres that can be uniquely inserted into them, either by wedging spheres of fixed radius or by inscribing spheres of variable radius. The ratio of these counts constrains the topology of the solution space. We apply this characterization to the spherical perceptron and show the existence of at least two topological regimes.

Machine learning and constraint satisfaction

. These problems can exhibit

quite different from those of glasses and spin glasses, often operating in a
regime where metastable states are uncommon and ground states can be easily
reached by dynamics

]
. They also exhibit structure quite different from that of
glasses and spin glasses: the ground state
is often a continuous set of points, all of which have zero energy. When the ground
state is a continuous set, one cannot talk of stationary ‘points,’ and the
methods developed to analyze stationary point properties become useless for
characterizing its structure. New approaches must be developed.

What structure of the set of solutions might we be interested in? Consider the following questions: is
the set connected? Are its connected components simply connected?
Are its connected components convex? What is the distribution of sizes
of its connected components? How does the quality of a solution depend on the
properties of the component it is found in? This manuscript cannot clam to answer most of these questions, but their spirit guides what we develop here.

Existing methods for characterizing continuous sets of solutions fall into
several camps. First, a zero-temperature equilibrium calculation provides
insight into the existence and clustering properties of the solution set

[
21
,
22
]
. However, this approach does
not clearly answer any of the preceding questions, since it is not possible to
understand whether the clustering it predicts arises from, e.g., a connected
nonconvex set or a union of disjoint convex sets (as in sublevel sets of the energy
landscape of spin glasses in the vicinity of the ground state). Multi-point
correlations can give some insight into the distribution of component sizes,
but it is difficult to disentangle the effects of nonconvexity and connectivity

[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

Information about connectivity of the solution set can be gained through
drawing a line or curved path between two points drawn from the solution set
and sampling along it
[

,

,
27
,
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
. If all sampled points are also
solutions, then the process that drew the two starting points must typically
sample a single connected component. This also gives insight into the geometric properties of the sampled component, but not others. However, if a
zero-energy path is not found, little can be concluded: the points may belong
to different components, or they may belong to a geometrically complex
component and connected by a likewise complex path. Connectivity in such
situations may be studied by the dynamics of random walks

[
32
]
.

We recently introduced an approach to understanding the topology of solution
sets when the set is a manifold
[
33
]
. The approach, which
calculates the typical value of the Euler characteristic of the set by counting
stationary points of a Morse function defined on it, gives direct insight into the
connectivity and simple connectedness of the solution set. However, it is applicable to few
relevant problems: those characterized by equality constraints. In most neural
networks, rectified activation implies that the set of solutions is described
by inequalities, and therefore is not a manifold but a manifold with a
boundary, and the boundary is not smooth. There is little hope to extend a
Morse-function based Euler characteristic analysis to such sets.

In this paper, we introduce cost-independent geometric characterizations of the
solutions to constraint satisfaction problems with inequality constraints.
They involve counting the number of spheres that can be inserted into the
space of solutions under various conditions. First, we count the number of
spheres of fixed radius inserted in the solution space having enough contacts
with the edge of the solution space to uniquely specify them; we refer to these
fixed-radius spheres as
wedged
spheres. Second, we count the number of
spheres of variable radius that can be
inscribed
in the solution space.

There are two significant things that can be learned from these
characterizations. First, the existence, number, and clustering properties of
wedged and inscribed spheres provide complimentary geometric information to the
traditional zero-temperature equilibrium and entropy-weighted analyses. For
instance, the phase diagram for the clustering properties of inserted spheres
differs from the equilibrium one in both the location of its phase boundaries
and its topology. We argue that the inserted-sphere method disentangles
geometry of different layers in the solution space that the equilibrium
treatment smears together. In some settings inscribed spheres correspond with
inherent states, and understanding their properties is a project of active
research
[
34
]
.

Second, the ratio of the counts of wedged and inscribed spheres puts
constraints on the topology of the set of solutions. We argue that inscribed and
wedged spheres correspond to the internal vertices and leaves of a graph
defined on the solution space, and their counts constrain the topology of this
graph. When inscribed spheres are dramatically more numerous, internal vertices
dramatically outnumber leaves and the graph must be very loopy. When their
counts are comparable in magnitude, the graph must be tree-like, with few
loops. These regimes have direct implications for the likely topology of the
corresponding set of solutions.

Sphere jamming

The jamming of

3.1
Wedging in spheres of fixed radius

With its radius fixed, a sphere is uniquely specified by its center. Therefore
in
D
dimensions one must fix the boundary of the sphere at
D
points to
uniquely specify it, as shown in Fig.
2
. Inspired by their visual appearance, we call these
wedged spheres
.
We want to count the number of spheres of radius
r
that lie inside the
solution space and touch the constraint boundaries at
D
points. To do this,
split the set of constraints into two parts:
D
constraints that the sphere
should touch, and
M-D
constraints that the sphere should satisfy with margin.
We integrate over potential locations
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
for the
center of the sphere. The first subset of constraints is fixed with Dirac

\delta
functions, including the determinant of their associated Jacobian,
while the second subset of constraints is fixed with Heaviside
\theta

functions. Finally, we sum over every possible way to split the constraints in
two parts. Altogether, this results in the expression

\displaystyle\#_{r}(\kappa)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}d\boldsymbol{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\subset[M]\\
|\sigma|=D\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{\mu\in[M]\backslash\sigma}\theta\big(h^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})-\kappa-r\big)\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{\mu\in\sigma}\delta\big(h^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})-\kappa-r\big)\bigg)

(7)

\displaystyle\times\left|\det\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}h^{\sigma_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\cdots&\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}h^{\sigma_{D}}(\boldsymbol{x})\end{bmatrix}\right|

where
[M]=\{1,\ldots,M\}
is the set of positive integers up to
M
.

Because this expression depends on
\kappa
and
r
only through the
combination
\kappa+r
, the count of inserted spheres of fixed radius can be
directly related to the count of inserted spheres of
zero
radius in a
problem with different margin by
\#_{r}(\kappa)=\#_{0}(\kappa+r)
. Inserting
spheres of zero radius is the same as counting the number of points lying at
the intersection of
D
constraint boundaries, as shown in
Fig.
2
. Therefore, only the count of such points as a function of
margin is necessary to understand the count of wedged spheres of any radius.

Our count of wedged spheres assumes that each decision boundary

h^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})=\kappa
makes at most one contact with each sphere. If
the decision boundaries are themselves not convex, one may have situations
where a wedged sphere contacts a given one more than once. In the specific
constraint satisfaction problems given as examples in the previous section, the
decision boundaries are convex and this situation cannot arise. In situations where such multi-contacts may be frequent, the formula above needs to be suitably modified.

3.3
Relation between sphere counts and solution topology

The system of inscribed and wedged spheres defines a graph on the solution
space, as shown in Fig.
4
. This graph is formed by starting from
wedged points, which are the leaves of the graph, and tracing the centers of
wedged spheres as their radius is increased from zero. The centers of different growing
wedged spheres meet precisely at the centers of inscribed spheres, which are
the internal vertices of the graph, where they either merge or annihilate. If
they annihilate and no wedged sphere remains at larger radius, then the
inscribed sphere corresponds to an inherent structure

[
34
]
. Otherwise, they merge and a single wedged sphere
remains at larger radius. All internal vertices corresponding to inscribed
spheres have degree
D+1
, and all other vertices are leaves corresponding to
wedged points.

The relationship between this graph and the topology of the solution set can be understood by growing the graph and the set of solutions together by continuously lowering the margin from a value at which no solutions exist. As the margin is lowered, a simple void will appear. If the problem is nonconvex and therefore the jamming is isostatic,
the simplest void involves
D+1
boundaries,
each of which intersect all others. One sphere can be inscribed in such a void,
and
\binom{D+1}{D}=D+1
points can be wedged in it. Therefore, the solution
space starts as a single simply connected component, and the graph starts as
the star graph
S_{D+1}
. The graph can be further defined by tracing the
location of wedged points in space as the margin continues to lower. Besides
the appearance of new voids and their corresponding star graphs, there are two
ways for the topology of the graph to change: a receding boundary can be split
by the appearance of a formally hidden decision surface, which results in a new
vertex of degree
D+1
, or two wedged points can annihilate, leaving a new edge
connecting two such vertices. Both these topology-changing processes are shown
in Fig.
4
.

Figure 4:

Wedged and inscribed spheres constrain solution topology.

Left:
The sets of wedged and inscribed spheres define a graph on the space of
solutions whose internal vertices are centered on inscribed spheres and
have degree
D+1
and whose leaves are wedged points. The relative numbers
of inscribed spheres and wedged points inform the topology of this graph
and therefore of the solution space.
Right:
There are three ways for
the topology of the graph to be modified as the margin is continuously varied. An existing leaf can split into

D
new leaves at a point where a previously obscured decision boundary is
uncovered. Alternatively, two leaves can meet and annihilate, forming an
edge between the internal vertices they were previously connected to.
Finally, a new isostatic void can appear where one was not previously, adding a
vertex with
D+1
leaves (not pictured).

For a single simply-connected component of the solution space, the resulting
graph is a connected tree. A connected tree with
n
internal vertices of degree
D+1

(corresponding to
n
inscribed spheres) contains

n(D-1)+2
leaves (corresponding to wedged points). The ratio of the numbers of wedged points to inscribed spheres for this tree is

\frac{\#_{0}}{\#_{\text{insc}}}=\frac{n(D-1)+2}{n}=D+O(D^{0})

(9)

If the solution space has
m
simply-connected components, its graph is a
forest of
m
connected trees. If the
i
th tree has
n_{i}
internal vertices,
then this ratio becomes

\frac{\#_{0}}{\#_{\text{insc}}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}(n_{i}(D-1)+2)}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}}=D-1+\frac{2m}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}}=D+O(D^{0})

(10)

since
\sum_{i=1}^{m}n_{i}\geq m
for positive integers
n_{i}
. Therefore, when the
solution space is composed of disjoint simply connected components the ratio of
these counts will be of order
D
independent of the number of components.

If the solution space is not simply connected, then the graph we have defined
is not necessarily a tree.
2
2

2

Note that understanding the solution space as simply connected or not must be made considering the number of intersections constraints have with each other. Consider the following situation: in higher than two dimensions, a simply connected region gains a loop because two wedged points annihilate at the intersection of
D
constraints and a void opens between them, leaving one of the constraint boundaries without a
D
-fold intersection with the others. As the margin continues to lower, this constraint boundary may loose contacts that change the topology of the solution space but do not change the graph construction because the contacts in question are hypostatic and therefore do not involve any wedged points. We must understand sections of the disallowed space that are only hypostatically connected to each other as disconnected for the sake of this construction.

Adding loops to a connected graph with
n
internal
vertices decreases its number of leaves when compared to the tree, so that the
number of corresponding wedged points is lower when compared with the number of
inscribed spheres.

In our analysis of the counts of wedged points and inscribed spheres, we
calculate the expected value of their logarithm, which we expect to coincide
with the logarithm of their typical value. Therefore, when comparing the
magnitudes of these counts by comparing the their expected logarithm, we are in
effect estimating the logarithm of the ratio of their typical value, or

\overline{\log\#_{0}}-\overline{\log\#_{\text{insc}}}=\log\#_{0}^{\text{typ}}-\log\#_{\text{insc}}^{\text{typ}}=\log\frac{\#_{0}^{\text{typ}}}{\#_{\text{insc}}^{\text{typ}}}

(11)

Given the considerations above, measurements of the average logarithm of these counts can imply two regimes of solution topology.

•

\overline{\log\#_{0}}>\overline{\log\#_{\text{insc}}}
:
The solution space is not
simply connected and its corresponding graph is very loopy.

•

\overline{\log\#_{0}}\simeq\overline{\log\#_{\text{insc}}}
:
The solution space is
composed of one or many simply connected components.

The difference
\#_{0}^{\text{typ}}-\#_{\text{insc}}^{\text{typ}}
between the counts of wedged points and inscribed spheres would
provide information about the number of simply connected components in the
solution space. However, to compute this difference it is necessary to
calculate the expectation value to subleading order in
N
, which we do not explore here.

3.4
Modification for problems with non-Euclidean configuration space

In many interesting problems the configuration space is not Euclidean (in the
next section we will study the
spherical
perceptron so-called because
its configuration space is a sphere). In non-Euclidean conditions the formulae
developed in the previous sections must be amended. We restrict attention to problems whose
D
-dimensional configuration space can be embedded in
N
-dimensional Euclidean space. Suppose that the configuration space can be implicitly defined by the
N-D
smooth constraint functions

g_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=0\qquad i=1,\ldots,N-D

Specifying spheres of fixed radius still requires fixing their boundary at
D
points. Introducing

\displaystyle\#_{r}(\kappa)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}d\boldsymbol{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\subset[M]\\

(13)

where the Jacobian now contains additional columns corresponding to the

\displaystyle\#_{\text{insc}}(\kappa)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}d\boldsymbol{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\subset[M]\\

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt\times\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{N-D}\delta\big(g_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)\bigg)\left|\det\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}h^{\sigma_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\cdots&\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}h^{\sigma_{D+1}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})&\cdots&\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g_{N-D}(\boldsymbol{x})\\

This is because the convex satisfiability problem is hypostatic, meaning that
the number of contacts between decision boundaries at jamming is less than

therefore that no wedged points exist. The region between the satisfiability transition and the typical vanishing of wedged points has

When
\kappa

phases are possible. First, the line along which wedged points become atypical

for small

two disconnected

transition corresponds precisely with the vanishing of wedged points, which is
expected in nonconvex problems where jamming is isostatic. At any finite

5
Conclusions

We have introduced a geometric characterization of continuous constraint
satisfaction problems whose constraints are sets of inequalities. Our method is
based on an enumerative geometry problem: how many spheres can be inserted into
the solution space such that they are uniquely defined by their contacts with
the decision boundaries? Two related procedures result in two counts: wedged
spheres have a fixed radius, while inscribed spheres have a locally maximal
radius. The relation between the counts given by these two procedures constrain
the topology of the solution space.

These counts can be calculated in specific problems using a Kac–Rice style
formula, and we have shown the result in the specific case of the spherical
perceptron. When the problem is convex and the
sat
–
unsat

transition is hypostatic, the typical number of inserted spheres vanishes
before the transition, whereas in nonconvex problems the vanishing of inserted
spheres coincides with the transition because it is isostatic. Two topological
regimes of the perceptron are revealed: one where the typical number of
inscribed spheres is much larger than that of wedged points and the solution
set is expected to be connected but very loopy, and another where the typical
numbers of inscribed and wedged spheres are similar in magnitude and the
solution set is expected to be made of simply-connected components.

The presence of a one-step replica symmetry broken (
1rsb
) phase in the configurations of
a constraint satisfaction problem can act as a barrier to optimization
[
48
]
.
However, we show that the onset of
rsb
phases is at larger values of

\alpha
in the statistics of wedged points than it is in the total volume of
solutions. This suggests that algorithms that attempt to find and follow
intersections between pieces of the cost function consisting of many
constraints may succeed at loads
\alpha
where intersection-blind algorithms
fail.

Understanding the structure of zero-cost solutions is complementary to
understanding the structure of pieces of the landscape at higher cost,
including in the
unsat
regime. Recent work explored ways to use the
average Euler characteristic to characterize sublevel sets in this latter
regime
[
49
]
. For certain cost funtions it may be
possible to study the structure of sublevel sets using wedged and inscribed spheres.

In the random Lorentz gas, the center of a subset of inscribed spheres are inherent states
[
34
]
. These
correspond to inscribed spheres whose origin, decreasing
\kappa
from a large
value, is in the formation of an isostatic void rather than the revealing of a
previously obscured decision boundary (as in Fig.
4
). In the
random Lorentz gas, the two cases can be distinguished by the geometry of the
vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mu}-\boldsymbol{x}
: if the vectors from the contacted
obstacles to the sphere center satisfy the equation
\sum_{\mu=1}^{D+1}c^{\mu}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mu}-\boldsymbol{x})=0
for
nonnegative
coefficients

c^{\mu}
, then the inscribed sphere arose from an appearing void and corresponds
to an inherent state. Understanding how to condition on such a quality in
mean-field calculations would allow one to compute the number of inherent states in
such constraint problems. The number of inherent states is expected to be more closely related to the number of connected components of the solution space.
