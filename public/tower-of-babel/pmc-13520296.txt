From recruitment to regret: a crime script analysis of illegal kidney donation in Pakistan
Authors: Javeria Saleem, Raja Sajjad Asghar, Uzma Sarwar, Fatima Ali, Shahzad Ahmad Daula, Usman Javaid, Zainab Naeem, Gholamreza Abdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organ transplantation is regarded as a lifesaving procedure, and a worldwide organ shortage has catalysed the market of illegal organ trade, especially for kidneys, due to high demand and need in low and middle-income countries like Pakistan. This process is often facilitated by sophisticated, organised crime networks acting within or parallel with licit healthcare systems, exploiting the vulnerable communities. Therefore, this study aims to explore the lived experiences of individuals who illegally donate their kidneys in Pakistan, with special focus on the whole process to map the findings on the crime script framework. The present study employs a qualitative, exploratory design using a semi-structured interview guide. Detailed in-depth interviews were conducted with five kidney donors using the snowball sampling technique. A thematic analysis approach was employed to interpret data, aided by crime script analysis to analyse the systemic organisation of the illegal kidney trade. Findings were divided into three major themes: Before Donation, During Donation, and After Donation. Participants explained that they were deceived by brokers, blindfolded, taken to hidden sites, and subjected to coercive surgery. Most suffered from health decline, psychological trauma, and economic instability after donation, with no long-term benefits. The crime script framework identified the sequential pattern of exploitation by brokers and collusive medical staff. Pakistan’s illegal kidney trade is operated through an intricate yet predictable cycle based on poverty and poor regulation. Targeted policy change, institutional responsibility, and donor-focused protective measures are desperately needed to end exploitation.

Introduction

Organ transplantation is a critical and lifesaving treatment for end-stage organ failure. Yet, a severe global shortage of organs has fueled their illegal trade [1] that commodifies human organs for financial or material gain [2]. Although organ trading is prohibited worldwide, with the sole exception of Iran [3], these illegal markets thrive because formal procurement systems cannot meet global demand [4]. According to the Global Observatory on Donation and Transplantation, more than 150,000 organs are sold each year, a figure that has risen sharply, though precise data remain scarce, reflecting the trade’s hidden but expanding reach [5], making it one of the world’s most profitable criminal enterprises, generating an estimated $840 million to $1.7 billion annually [1, 6]. “The Guardian” reports that the World Health Organisation estimates around 10,000 black-market organ transplants occur annually [7].

Despite the global prohibition of organ sales under international laws and regulations [8, 9], kidneys are the most frequently trafficked organ worldwide [10, 11], comprising an estimated 75% of the illicit global organ trade [7]. This black market of human organs, especially kidney trading, has increasingly become more sophisticated and transnational criminal activity, frequently involving brokers, recruiters, transporters, and even licensed healthcare professionals [3, 12]. While earlier studies depicted the organ trade as a predominantly ‘mafia-style’ enterprise, recent evidence underscores a more insidious dynamic in which illicit networks strategically infiltrate and exploit legitimate institutions, such as blood banks, hospitals, and dialysis centres, thereby deliberately eroding the distinction between lawful and unlawful transplantation practices [1, 6].

Kidney transplantation was introduced in Pakistan in 1979, growing from around 50 annual procedures to nearly 1,000 by the year 2000. While initially serving local patients, the country later evolved into a hub for transplant tourism, with private hospitals reportedly performing approximately 1,500 transplants for foreign recipients in 2007 [13–15]. During this period, Pakistan also became both a source and destination for the illegal organ trade, especially in Karachi and Punjab, where brokers often exploited impoverished individuals to meet the rising demand for kidneys [12, 16]. In direct response to this commercialisation and lack of oversight, the government initiated formal regulation with the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Ordinance in 2007, which was later strengthened by a federal act in 2010. This legislation banned commercial trade and established a national regulatory framework, overseen by the Human Organ Transplantation Authority (HOTA). Following the devolution of health sector governance under the 18th Constitutional Amendment in 2011, Punjab adopted this federal law as the Punjab Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues (Amendment) Act, 2012, which led to the creation of the Punjab Human Organ Transplantation Authority (PHOTA) in 2017 to regulate transplantation within the province [17]. However, other developing countries have also documented similar organ-trafficking patterns, as evidenced by Bangladesh, where agents influence the migrant population through payments that are promised but never fulfilled [3]. Similarly, in India, brokers mediate for low-resource populations (agricultural workers), making organ trading one of the solutions to cover their debts [18]. These patterns explain how illegal organ trade markets persist across South Asia, where economic disparities, limited healthcare access, and weak regulation are the main determinants [19].

Despite regulatory reforms, Pakistan remains a hub for organ trafficking, driven by entrenched poverty, weak enforcement, and corrupt networks [13, 14, 20]. Understanding organ trafficking requires examining the prevailing socio-economic disparities and a persistently underfunded healthcare system [21–23]. Pakistan’s health care budget remains explicitly low, causing limited access to healthcare, poor regulatory mechanisms, and inadequate protection for high-risk populations, leading to exploitive organ trafficking [23–27]. For many impoverished individuals, selling a kidney is an act of desperation, a means to settle debts or gain short-term relief [28–30].

The illegal kidney-selling process, therefore, is a manifestation of an exploitive criminal act incorporated by structural inequalities and health injustice. Yet evidence indicates that most experience worsened health and only transient economic benefit, often followed by lasting physical, psychological, and financial hardship [30, 31]. Similar patterns of post-donation decline have been documented in Egypt, India, and Israel, underscoring the profound and persistent human cost of this trade [12, 18, 30, 32].

By utilising the Critical Theory Approach and the Political Economy of Health, the selling process shows how vulnerable populations become trapped in criminal activity due to poverty, weak regulatory authorities, and weak health systems. Existing worldwide evidence documented illegal kidney selling experiences, as paths of coercion, psychological and physical harm, and misinformation [3, 12]. Much of this evidence is descriptive; none of the research assesses organisational dynamics or the entire process of operating the illegal sale. Criminal scholarship should emphasise the importance of evaluating the facilitation network, operational processes, and opportunity paths that enable the normalisation of illicit acts [33, 34]. Crime script analysis, therefore, offers a stronger analytical framework, allowing for structural mapping of the whole illicit kidney-selling process from recruitment to post-donation [1]. This qualitative study addresses that gap by examining the cases of five kidney donors in Pakistan who were recruited, operated on, and subsequently abandoned. Their accounts reveal the operational mechanisms, psychological impacts, and concealment methods of these networks. By framing these narratives within the broader landscape of global organ-trade networks, this study moves beyond descriptive accounts to critically examine how illegal donation processes shape kidney donors’ lived experiences. Theoretical framework

The study employs a ‘Critical Theory’ and the ‘Political Economy of Health’ framework. This dual framework approach allows for a critical analysis of illegal kidney donor perspectives beyond mere biomedical aspects. Critical theory helps us understand how social structures (health systems, state organisations, and economic systems) produce inequalities and cause exploitation. This framework approach places the illegal donation process as an outcome of syndemic forces (healthcare system failures, regulatory environment disruptions, and socio-economic disparities) rather than a single individual decision.

‘Political Economy of Health’ framework depicts how the wider level issues (economic, health care system) shape individual-level behaviours. In Pakistan, the kidney-selling process has become a rational way to obtain economic benefits rather than a devastating act, due to these disruptions to the system.

Additionally, the Crime Scripts analysis framework [1, 35], developed to analyse organ trafficking type scripts, was used to frame the functional stages of surgery from pre-donation to post-donation.

Methodology

Study design

The study employs a phenomenological study design to explore the lived experiences of individuals involved in hidden, criminalised, and illegal practices like kidney selling. Phenomenology is particularly well-suited for this inquiry, as it enables an in-depth exploration of participants’ perceptions, emotions, and anticipations. In criminalised settings where direct observation is impossible and official data are often absent or unreliable, phenomenological inquiry provides a powerful lens for capturing pain, suffering, agency, and victimisation through participants’ own narratives, thereby illuminating the social meanings underpinning these experiences. Given the clandestine and illegal nature of the kidney trade, the study also incorporates crime script analysis, an analytical framework used by other scholars [1, 6] to systematically map the procedural stages of illicit activities. Integrating crime script analysis enables the study to structurally organise the kidney donation process into three phases: pre-donation, donation, and post-donation.

Participant recruitment

The study included five participants who had sold their kidneys illegally in Pakistan between 2016 and 2023. Recruiting individuals involved in illegal organ transactions posed substantial challenges, as organ selling is both highly stigmatised and criminalised. Potential participants were often reluctant to disclose their experiences due to fears related to legal consequences, social repercussions, and personal safety. These contextual factors created significant barriers to access and rendered conventional sampling strategies impractical.

To reach this hidden population, snowball sampling was employed as the most feasible and ethically appropriate approach. Recruitment began with an initial participant who had recently experienced labour exploitation and belonged to a low-income community. While snowball sampling enabled access to participants who would otherwise be unreachable, it also introduced the possibility of network bias. Because referrals often occur within closely connected social and economic networks, the resulting sample was relatively homogenous and predominantly composed of individuals from low-income backgrounds. This limitation is acknowledged. While the homogeneity of the sample (all males, low-income, rural-adjacent) provides analytical depth and allows focused exploration of how structural vulnerability operates within a specific subgroup, it necessarily constrains transferability. We do not claim that these five accounts represent the full spectrum of illegal kidney donation experiences in Pakistan. In particular, the absence of female donors, urban middle-class sellers, or individuals from other provinces limits the generalizability of our findings to those populations. The concept of information power supports our sample size for the specific aim of mapping crime scripts among low-income rural males, but saturation claims are confined to this subgroup. Further research with diverse demographic groups is essential before drawing broader conclusions about the national phenomenon.

Despite repeated visits to the community to establish trust, many potential participants declined to participate due to fear of legal repercussions, social stigma, or possible retaliation from brokers or agents. To address these concerns, strict measures were put in place to ensure anonymity and confidentiality. Participants were assured that their identities would remain protected, participation was entirely voluntary, and no personal information would be disclosed. All interviews were conducted in safe, neutral settings chosen by the participants themselves to maximise their comfort and sense of security.

The sample size of 5 participants, although small, provides qualitative evidence, with a focus on in-depth insights into the phenomenon rather than on quantitative numbers, similar to the concept of “information power” [36]. This concept holds that a small sample size is sufficient when the aims are highly specific and clear, and when the data collection methods are stringent. In the present evidence, all 5 participants have direct experience of the whole kidney selling process, including broker exploitation, coercive procedural paths, and no follow-up care. Additionally, no new information emerged from the interviews, suggesting that the data have reached saturation within the study’s scope.

Data collection

The authors gathered data through semi-structured interviews. Each interview lasted approximately 45–60 min and was audio-recorded with informed consent. An interview guide facilitated open-ended discussion, enabling participants to describe their experiences in their own words while ensuring systematic exploration of key themes, including motivations for organ selling, recruitment pathways, the donation process, and post-donation consequences. This interview guide was developed specifically for this study and is provided as a supplementary file.

Interviews were conducted in Urdu or Punjabi, according to participant preference. In addition to recordings, detailed field notes were maintained to capture contextual details and non-verbal cues. The study followed ethical guidelines for research involving criminalised practices and vulnerable populations. To prevent any form of coercion, no financial incentives were offered. Methodological rigour and transparency were further supported by adherence to the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) checklist.

Ethical considerations

This study explored the sensitive issue of illegal organ trade, which posed potential risks to participants, including psychological distress, stigma, and possible personal or legal repercussions. To minimise risks of emotional distress and potential re-traumatisation, interviewers received sensitivity training before data collection. Strict measures of confidentiality, anonymity, and secure data handling were maintained, and no identifiable personal information was collected.

No direct benefits or incentives were provided to participants for their involvement in this study. This approach was adopted to avoid coercion or undue influence, given the participants’ vulnerable circumstances. Although participants did not receive immediate or personal gains, the study was designed to generate knowledge that could inform future policies and protective measures for vulnerable populations.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Green International University. Lahore, Pakistan vide registration number GIU/REC/25 − 02. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without consequence,

Artificial intelligence tools (such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and QuillBot) were used solely to check grammar, refine phrasing, and improve clarity of ideas in the manuscript. The authors take full responsibility for the content and interpretation of the findings.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated from Urdu and Punjabi into English by two members of the research team who were fluent in the local languages and experienced in qualitative research. To ensure accuracy and preserve contextual meaning, translations were cross-checked by the wider research team, with culturally specific idioms either translated directly or adapted to an equivalent expression to maintain interpretive integrity.

The analytic process began with multiple readings of each transcript to achieve immersion and to develop a comprehensive understanding of participants’ accounts. Interpretive Phenomenological Analysis [37, 38] was used to explore how participants made sense of their experiences, decision-making processes, and interpersonal relationships, dimensions central to understanding illegal kidney-selling practices.

Building on this interpretive foundation, a thematic analysis was undertaken following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework [39, 40] Both inductive and deductive approaches were applied. NVivo software supported the organisation, storage, and retrieval of coded data. Initial codes were generated independently by members of the research team and subsequently refined through iterative team discussions. These meetings involved comparing interpretations, merging overlapping codes, and grouping related codes into preliminary subthemes. Themes were then reviewed, named, and defined to ensure conceptual coherence and analytic clarity.

To enhance understanding of the organisational structure of the organ trade, the study also employed crime script analysis, originally developed by Cornish [35] and more recently adapted to the context of organ trafficking by Ambagtsheer and Bugter (2023) [1]. Crime script analysis enables illicit activities to be mapped into sequential stages, highlighting the actors, resources, locations, and opportunity structures that facilitate the crime. Integrating this approach with the thematic findings provided a structured framework for organising the identified patterns, thereby offering a comprehensive account of how illegal kidney selling unfolds in practice.

Reflexivity

A reflexive approach was integrated throughout the study to ensure transparency and analytical rigour. During data collection, interviewers actively worked to minimise power imbalances by adopting a non-judgemental and empathetic stance, fostering an environment in which participants felt comfortable sharing their experiences despite the stigma and criminalisation associated with illegal organ selling.

The research team comprised individuals from diverse professional backgrounds, including public health, transplant regulation, medical research, and qualitative inquiry. This multidisciplinary composition offered multiple interpretive perspectives but also introduced the need for careful reflection on how researchers’ backgrounds, professional roles, and underlying assumptions might influence data interpretation. In particular, the involvement of a regulatory authority official (RSA) had the potential to shape both participants’ openness and the analytical lens applied to their narratives. These positional influences were recognised and accounted for in the analytic process.

To maintain reflexive awareness, team members documented reflexive memos after each interview, noting assumptions, emotional reactions, and possible sources of bias. Regular analytic meetings allowed researchers to discuss emerging interpretations, challenge individual assumptions, and ensure that coding and theme development remained closely grounded in participants’ accounts. Interpretations were cross-checked against participants’ own language wherever possible to preserve contextual meaning and avoid over-interpretation. Continuous engagement with transcripts and reflective field notes further supported an ongoing reflexive stance throughout analysis.

These strategies strengthened the study’s interpretive transparency and enhanced confidence in the trustworthiness of the findings.

Triangulation and rigour

Several strategies were employed to enhance the study’s credibility and trustworthiness. First, triangulation was achieved by cross-checking interview data with field notes to strengthen the accuracy of interpretations. Second, member checking was conducted with volunteer participants, who reviewed and validated preliminary interpretations. Third, an audit trail was maintained to ensure transparency in the coding process and the development of themes. Finally, peer debriefing sessions with qualitative research experts were conducted to minimise researcher bias and enhance analytical rigour.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

The study sample consisted of five participants, aged between 19 and 43 years, who reported having sold their kidneys between the ages of 18 and 35. Educational attainment was generally low: three participants had no formal education, while the remaining two had completed matriculation. Before donating, all participants were engaged in low-paying, informal employment, such as daily wage labour or selling corn husks. Following the sale of their kidneys, employment trajectories showed slight improvement. Most participants continued to work as labourers, with only one transitioning to driving a rickshaw.

The illegal transplant procedures were carried out in major urban centres, specifically Islamabad and Lahore. Despite the surgeries taking place in metropolitan hospitals, all participants resided in socioeconomically deprived areas, including Okara, Sahiwal, or nearby villages. The sample found was almost homogenous, comprising all males from low socio-economic rural backgrounds, indicating major structural realities of illegal organ practices in Pakistan. This concentration of illegal process within similar background participants further reveals that this criminalised act was not randomly distributed but structurally infused among vulnerable populations.

But this homogeneity in the sample limits the research from gathering diverse experience data, including perspectives of women, urban, and higher-income groups. However, these demographic and contextual characteristics highlight the participants’ structural vulnerability, which rendered them susceptible to exploitation within the illegal organ trade market (Table 1).

Theme-I: recruitment and preparation

Theme I captures the pre-donation phase, which outlines the processes and facilitating factors that lead to the recruitment of potential donors. This stage typically begins with identifying vulnerable individuals and communities, particularly those burdened by poverty, debt, or limited education. Brokers often establish contact through friends, relatives, or local intermediaries, at times leveraging networks of individuals who have previously donated an organ. At this point, persuasion and consent are secured, frequently through misinformation, undue influence, or coercion. Once agreement is obtained, logistical arrangements are made, including transportation, preliminary medical tests, and basic coordination in preparation for surgery.

Two sub-themes emerged from this theme: persistent poverty and debt as contributing factors, and the significant influence of brokers and peers in motivating individuals to sell a kidney.

Sub-theme-I-A: persistent poverty, continuous debt, and low income

Severe financial strain was the most significant driver of participants’ decisions to sell a kidney. Across the interviews, narratives reflected ongoing struggles with poverty, escalating debt, job insecurity, and inadequate income. Participants described how their circumstances left them with little choice but to view the sale of a kidney as a means of survival or family support.

One participant with responsibility described his economically desperate situation as: “I belong to a family of 10 members. We live in a cemetery in a single room. Our family is financially strained, so last year, when I was 21, I decided to sell a kidney.” (P1).

P1’s narrative collapses spatial, economic, and familial precarity into a single, urgent decision. Living in a cemetery, a physical symbol of death and marginality, underscores how structural abandonment normalises extreme risk-taking. Critically, his phrasing “I tried to do something for them” reveals an agentic framing of what is objectively a self-harming act, illustrating how vulnerability is internalised as responsibility. From a crime script perspective, this internalisation is precisely what brokers exploit: the donor arrives already convinced that sacrifice is his only remaining role.

Others shared similar pressures: “I am 25 years old and work as a labourer. I never went to school. I have nine siblings. My income was very low before selling a kidney. I used to sell corn husks, but life was tough. Debt payment kept increasing. Therefore, I decided to sell one of my kidneys to pay off my debts and expenses. When I did this process, I was 22 years old.” (P2).

This quote indicates how social pressures, including unpaid medical bills and health emergencies involving a close family member, pressured people to commit illegal acts. Across participants’ accounts, it was also observed that financial difficulties were never experienced in isolation, but were connected to family responsibilities and societal expectations, especially for males. These interconnected paths lead to the decision to commit exploitative acts.

Another highlighted urgent medical needs as the deciding factor: “It has been 8 years since the kidney sale. I am 43 now. At that time, I had some unpaid bills and loans that I could not manage. Moreover, my son’s hands were broken, and no relative was willing to help in his treatment. Therefore, I sold the kidney.” (P3).

Younger participants also reported being forced into the decision at an early age: “I sold a kidney at the age of 18, and now I am 19. My father and I both worked as labourers, but it was hard to cover daily expenses. We also had a huge debt. Therefore, I took this step to sell a kidney to make our lives easier.” (P5).

These accounts illustrate how structural poverty and debt rendered participants highly vulnerable, leaving organ sale as a perceived last resort.

The phenomenological-interpretive approach suggests that selling kidneys was the only option left to them to meet their needs. More specifically, this concept was explained by critical theory, which situates participants’ accounts within a broader social context in which inequality has deprived marginalised communities of better alternatives. Similarly, the political economy of health frameworks describes how macro-level determinants, including unemployment, limited access to health care, and economic barriers, shape individual behaviours. Overall, the findings suggest that structural vulnerability, including poverty, does not merely operate as a background factor but exists strategically, making it easy for the vulnerable community to access.

Sub-theme-1-B: influence of brokers and peers

Participants’ decisions were rarely made in isolation. Instead, they were heavily shaped by brokers, relatives, and peers who either directly facilitated the process or normalised kidney sale as a viable financial solution. This social influence created an ecosystem where desperation was exploited through personal connections and commission-driven arrangements.

As one participant explained: “My uncle told me about someone who could pay off my debt, but only if I donated him a kidney. He also wanted 15 thousand PKR as commission. Later, he took 400 thousand PKR from me.” (P3).

Others emphasised how community-level experiences reinforced the idea: “In my area, some people had already donated their kidney and received money. Therefore, they convinced me to do this process to earn some money.” (P2).

Similarly, chance encounters served as entry points into the trade: “During a wedding, I met one of my friends and told him about my situation. He gave me the idea of selling one of my kidneys to cover my expenses. Then he gave me the number of the broker to start the process.” (P4).

These testimonies demonstrate how brokers and social networks functioned both as gatekeepers and exploiters, presenting kidney sale as a seemingly accessible solution to financial desperation while masking its exploitative nature.

Theme-II: orchestration and execution of surgical procedure

This theme corresponds to the donation phase, characterised by secrecy, coercion, and inadequate medical safeguards, though some participants also reported experiences of supportive treatment from brokers. Donors frequently described a lack of informed consent and medical clarity, as they were compelled to sign documents without explanation and received no counselling about the risks of living with one kidney. Many also recalled coercive measures, such as blindfolding, sedation, and phone confiscation, used to enforce secrecy and disorientation, effectively stripping them of autonomy. In contrast, a minority of participants reported receiving some care and assistance from brokers, who provided food, medicine, or reassurance during the process. Together, these sub-themes illustrate the contrasting dynamics of control and selective support that defined the orchestration and execution of illegal kidney donation.

Sub-theme-II-A: coercive control and forced disorientation

A recurring account across participants was the deliberate use of secrecy, disorientation, and coercive control during the donation process. Donors were often blindfolded, sedated, or transported under strict surveillance, with their phones confiscated to cut off communication. These measures were deliberately employed to prevent them from identifying locations or seeking justice afterwards.

One participant recalled: “My friend and agent took me to Islamabad for the process with a group of other women and men. They told me not to talk to anybody. When we reached the location in Islamabad, my eyes were covered with a black cloth. (P2)

Another explained: My procedure took place in Islamabad. The agent took me there in a coaster and also took my phone so that I couldn’t contact anyone. When we arrived, they put me in a car with tinted windows so I couldn’t see outside. (P3)

A third participant shared a more distressing account: “When it was time for the procedure, the broker called me in Islamabad. As soon as I got there, they tied a black cloth over my face. Then they gave me a drug, and I lost consciousness. The next thing I remember was waking up in a hospital in Lahore. My surgery had already been done, and I was in a state of shock and confusion.” (P4).

These narratives illustrate how manipulation, forced disorientation, and isolation were systematically deployed to dominate donors and ensure their compliance. These cautious acts cannot be categorised as an incidental event, but reveal a clear, planned, strategic procedure of organ trafficking. Brokers effectively neutralised the process while convincing the patient to withdraw consent or decline post-operative care. This indicates the concept of Ambagtasheer [1], which shows how trafficking networks destroy legal process, indicating that not only the donor’s health is destroyed, but their understanding of being aware and resisting is also exploited.

Applying a critical theory lens, these accounts reveal more than tactical deception; they expose the operationalisation of structural power asymmetries. Brokers and their medical collaborators exercise almost total control over donors’ bodies, mobility, and sensory perception,a power derived not from individual astuteness alone but rooted in donors’ pre-existing structural vulnerability (poverty, lack of education, absence of institutional protection). The blindfold and confiscated phone are thus material expressions of how abstract structural inequality translates into concrete, embodied domination. Furthermore, donors’ constrained agency is evident: while no participant explicitly consented to disorientation, their ability to refuse was already foreclosed by the financial desperation that brought them into the network. The critical perspective thus reframes the situation not as a series of coerced individuals but as a system of exploitation that systematically produces compliant subjects.

Sub-theme-II-B: lack of informed consent and medical clarity

Participants described the medical aspects of the donation process as unclear and opaque. They reported that informed consent was neither sought nor explained, nor were they given adequate time to consult with others or ask questions. Many were compelled to sign a document without knowing whether it constituted a legal agreement. Crucially, no information was provided regarding the medical risks or long-term consequences of living with a single kidney. This lack of transparency and counselling underscores the procedure’s exploitative and clandestine nature.

As participants mentioned, no one informed me about the long-term issues after kidney donation. And I never sign any document. Before surgery, they do some blood and urine tests. (P4)

Another explained: For surgery, they did some blood tests in Lahore, but the major urine test and ultrasound were done in Islamabad. I signed the agreement under pressure as they did not allow me to leave. (P3)

Similarly, another quoted: “I did not know about any agreement or document.” They did not ask me to sign anything. (P5)

Sub-theme II-C: negotiated trust and perceived support

In contrast to the majority who described coercion and secrecy, a few participants reported more positive experiences, highlighting instances where brokers and agents provided support and care during the donation process. These donors recalled being offered food, medicine, and reassurance, and, in some cases, expressed satisfaction with the care they received.

One participant shared, “The broker took good care of me throughout the entire process. The doctors provided me with food and medicine, and I am satisfied with how everything went” (P1).

Similarly, another noted, “The broker and my friend were helpful during the whole process” (P5).

Such accounts suggest that, alongside coercive practices, brokers occasionally sought to establish trust and maintain donor compliance by offering selective forms of care. This sub-theme reflects the complex, sometimes contradictory strategies used to manage donor experiences.

Theme-III: consequences and reflections

The theme captures the post-donation phase that is characterised by a mix of betrayal, health complications, and emotional turmoil, underscoring the enduring consequences of illegal kidney donation. Participants’ accounts reveal how broken promises, declining health, and profound regret became recurring outcomes, with only a few reporting limited satisfaction. Three sub-themes capture these realities as follows.

Sub-theme -III-A: false promises and betrayal by agents

A central theme emerging from the interviews was the sense of betrayal that participants experienced at the hands of agents and intermediaries. Despite being promised large sums of money, ranging from PKR 400,000 to 2,000,000, most received only a fraction of the agreed amount, if anything at all. The agents often disappeared after the surgery, leaving donors not only financially devastated but also emotionally scarred.

One participant recalled: “The agent promised 20 lakhs rupees, but I received only two lakhs rupees. I felt cheated as there was no evidence. My friends who brought me to the agent also went away.” (P2).

Another reported continued deception even after surgery: “It has been six days since my surgery. When I demanded my prize money, the broker said he would give it to me. But till now, I have not been rewarded with anything. Instead, my broker threatens me that there is no agreement between us regarding money.” (P4).

Only a single participant described receiving the promised payment in full: “I was promised 5 lakhs. The agent said he would buy me a loader rickshaw and give me the remaining amount. As promised, he bought me a rickshaw and gave me 2 lac PKR through my friend.” (P1).

These accounts highlight how false promises and betrayal reinforced donors’ vulnerability and deepened their sense of exploitation.

Sub-theme -III-B: adverse health impacts

Beyond financial losses, participants described enduring physical and psychological harm as a result of kidney donation. While initial recovery was manageable for some, long-term health consequences included persistent weakness, breathing difficulties, and limited capacity for physical labour. Several participants also reported heightened sensitivity to illness and significant emotional distress.

One participant explained: “My health has been badly impacted. Now I cannot do hard tasks or climb stairs due to continuous weakness and breathing problems. Every summer, the situation worsens.” (P3).

Another reflected on both the physical and emotional toll: “My body has become sensitive. I easily get ill and cannot do difficult tasks. I am living with one kidney, very little money, and the pain of betrayal.” (P2).

For some, the consequences extended to mental health: “I am mentally disturbed; my peace of mind has gone.“(P3).

Yet, one participant offered a more positive outlook: “I know that might sound strange to some people, but I genuinely feel that life is about making the best choices available to you, even if those choices are not ideal. For me, this was a path toward stability. I have been living with one kidney without any major health problems.” (P1).

These reflections reveal how adverse health outcomes, both physical and mental, became lifelong burdens for most donors, with rare exceptions of resilience. The deteriorated health patterns narrated by participants reveal striking consequences that not only did they face the biological exploitation of surgery, but also did not receive post-operative care and support, as in a normal, regulated process. This further reveals how donors were disregarded after the organ extraction, reflecting a common feature of organ trade, causing systemic abandonment.

Sub-theme III-C: reflections and regret over decisions

Participants’ reflections highlighted deep regret over their decisions, with most describing the donation as a failed attempt to resolve financial difficulties. For many, the trade-off resulted in lasting physical harm, emotional distress, and unfulfilled promises. Only one participant expressed limited empowerment, though even he questioned the value of his sacrifice.

As one participant expressed: Health was gone, peace of mind was gone. Nothing left. Life remains as difficult. I did not even get any money. I really request everyone, please do not do this kind of act for the sake of some money.” (P4).

Another echoed similar regret: “I regret this kidney-selling decision. I risked my whole life for temporary relief.” (P5).

In contrast, one donor felt a measure of empowerment, though still critical of the financial compensation: “I am completely fine, and therefore, I don’t have any regrets. I am satisfied that a loader rickshaw would help me to start earning money. My only regret is that one should not sell a kidney for so little. One must demand at least 50 lakh PKR for this. I will also sell my second kidney if I could live after this.” (P1).

These divergent reflections emphasise how regret, betrayal, and disillusionment overwhelmingly defined participants’ post-donation experiences, with rare instances of partial satisfaction.

The narratives from participants across the three phases of their donation trajectory (pre-donation, during donation and post-donation) form an internally consistent pattern of structural vulnerability giving way to lived exploitation. In the pre-donation phase, participants reported enduring challenges of poverty, debt and family responsibilities as main providers, limiting choices with organ sale perceived to be more of a necessity than voluntary. These vulnerabilities were compounded by the very control mechanisms that perpetuated them during the donation phase: deception, diminished agency, and the limited skill-builder’s ability to provide informed consent, implemented through power asymmetries between donors and intermediaries. Many participants indicated that they did not know specific procedural details or felt unable to ensure their withdrawal, signifying compromised agency. In the post-donation period, their expectations of financial relief from not having to pay for medical expenses were unmet, and participants continue to experience economic hardship, with many indicating worsening physical health problems and psychological distress. With some variation in awareness and coercion, the same pattern emerged: initial economic desperation gave way to chronic marginalisation. Collectively, these findings reveal that the illegal kidney trade functions not as a single phenomenon but rather more like an ordered sequence in which socio-economic disadvantage is harnessed and replicated at each step of the transplantation pathway.

Discussion

This study reveals the destructive nature of the illegal kidney trade in Punjab, Pakistan, demonstrating that it is not a series of isolated individual acts but a system-level crime embedded within broader social and economic inequalities. Informed by a critical theory perspective, we interpret participants’ trajectories, from recruitment to post-donation abandonment, as a dialectical process. Structural conditions (such as poverty and regulatory failure) yield exploitable subjects, and criminal networks then exploit and deepen those same structural inequalities. Power, in this framework, is not merely held by brokers but is continuously exercised to restrict donors’ agency at each scripted stage. Economic power limits their pre-donation choices; physical power enables coercive control during surgery; and informational power allows post-donation betrayal without consequence. Kidney selling is a structurally driven process shaped by intersecting factors such as poverty, regulatory failures, and deliberate exploitation. The trade operates through well-organised networks that enable and sustain the illegal organ trade. Consequently, the phenomenon reflects how structural inequalities are reproduced and deepened through exploitative practices, further entrenching the vulnerabilities of those most affected. Participants’ structural vulnerabilities—including poverty, limited education, and unstable employment—rendered them highly susceptible to exploitation. Although they underwent high-risk surgery in major urban centres, this did not translate into meaningful or sustained socio-economic improvement. Instead, their outcomes highlight how the illegal kidney trade deepens existing inequalities by extracting value from those already marginalised. By employing a crime script analysis framework, this study situates organ selling within a broader organisational process, mapping the roles of actors, opportunity structures, and enabling environments that perpetuate this illicit practice. These insights align with and extend prior scholarship on organ trafficking, which has emphasised the systemic drivers of exploitation and the limitations of regulatory enforcement in addressing hidden and criminalised markets [1]. Participants’ accounts reveal that legal opportunity structures, including hospitals, transplant systems, and administrative management—collude to conceal illegal activities, rendering the transplantation process a hybrid of white-collar and criminal behaviour. These findings align with global evidence [1, 41] showing that recruitment, surgery, and evasion are meticulously orchestrated within formal medical infrastructures. Similarly, another report from Pakistan describes how organised trafficking rings operate through private clinics and cooperating offices, sometimes employing untrained staff, underscoring deep-seated structural failures [42].

The decision to sell a kidney was rooted in generalised poverty, indebtedness, and pervasive hardship, rendering it a last resort for individuals confronting severe financial distress. These findings are also consistent with another thematic synthesis, which found that poverty, misinformation, and broker coercion were the main drivers of kidney selling in developing nations such as Pakistan, India, and Nepal [28]. These processes are usually facilitated by brokers, as a participant mentioned, who entice vulnerable groups with promises of money. Such an act described the signature features of deceptive processes, also mapped out in another study that used crime script analysis aimed at breaking the victim’s means of communication, to prevent accountability and possible escape [6]. In addition, a professional silence was observed in which brokers and doctors cover up illegal activities, termed the ‘wall of secrecy,’ maintaining a complex, difficult-to-detect system [43].

The majority of participants, as described above, also faced poor health outcomes such as chronic pain, fatigue, and breathing problems after kidney selling. These are consistent with previous Pakistan’s study findings, where kidney vendors (illegal sellers) face worse outcomes as compared to controls (legal donors) with increased risk of chronic kidney diseases [44]. These observations highlight a systematic lack of follow-up care, along with no preventive procedure, leading to long-term morbidity. Despite the promise of instant economic benefit, most participants, as in previous research, did not achieve sustained economic improvement. 85% of respondents used receivable payments primarily to clear debt, with no durable improvement in socio-economic status, aligning with another study [31]. This illustrates the difficulties faced by participants in poverty, reinforcing social and economic inequalities rather than providing a genuine way out.

Deep-rooted psychological distress among kidney sellers, along with emotional turmoil including loneliness, emptiness, and regret, was noticed. This points towards the unaddressed psychological aftermath of this sale [31]. These perspectives align with a systematic review indicating that some donors regret their decision and report lower life satisfaction after kidney donation [45]. Furthermore, a recent Guardian investigation documented cases in Pakistan and the region where donors were trafficked under false promises of economic relief, and then underwent emotional disintegration and disappointment as brokers often did not provide compensation, which left donors mentally disfigured and helpless [46]. This highlights that psychological distress is as disturbing as physical, yet it remains unacknowledged by formal health systems.

We also recognise that our interpretations of coercion and betrayal may have been subtly shaped by the professional backgrounds of team members, particularly the involvement of a regulatory authority official (RSA, co-author Raja Sajjad Asghar) who works for PHOTA. While this insider knowledge was invaluable for understanding the regulatory landscape and validating procedural details, it may also have predisposed the team to emphasise systemic failure and enforcement gaps rather than, for example, individual donor agency or resilience. To mitigate this, we systematically cross-checked interpretations against participants’ own emotional valences (e.g., P1’s reported satisfaction) and actively discussed alternative explanations during analytic meetings. Our public health orientation may also privilege structural over psychological explanations; we have therefore given explicit attention to psychological trauma as a distinct theme, even where it was not the primary lens of the theoretical framework.

Although the study findings are organised across the chronological phases of pre-donation, donation, and post-donation, they align coherently with the stages of crime script analysis. This alignment uniquely illustrates that the illegal kidney trade operates as a structured process rather than a series of discrete events, as has been traditionally assumed [1, 47, 48]. Participants’ narratives of enduring poverty and debt in the pre-donation phase illustrate how structural vulnerability serves as an essential site of access, which brokers facilitate through planned recruitment practices. This resonates with broader criminological knowledge that organised crime networks strategically target fractionalised communities who are economically deprived and have little institutional protection [49]. The donation phase features control aspects including deception, captivity, and nonconsensuality presenting how asymmetries of power are operationalised within illicit configurations as per sociological interpretations of exploitation (power held by some and denied to many) or restricted agency in the context of inequality providing both a theoretical basis for conceptualising exploitative relationships and enabling claims that transactional contexts manifestoes constrain freedom regardless of jockeying status at points in a care continuum [3]. At the post-donation phase, respondents narrated their worsening health, poverty and mental distress; corroborating previous evidence that organ selling does little to bring lasting economic benefit and contributes to structural marginalisation [50]. Significantly, although a shared structural schema is evident, diverging participant experiences (e.g., differences in coercion factors, knowledge of surgeries and post-operative outcomes) mirror the plurality by which exploitation occurs and is experienced. This highlights the need to go beyond blanket narratives and acknowledge that vulnerability is nuanced and context-specific. This study builds on and extends the existing literature by integrating phenomenological insights into crime script analysis to provide a process-oriented and theory-driven understanding of the illegal kidney trade as a system of exploitation embedded in wider structures of inequality and health injustice.

This study also has some limitations. The small sample size (n = 5), despite rich contextual data, limits the generalizability of findings. The stigmatised and sensitive nature of the study context led to limited access towards potential participants, and many declined to participate due to fear of being prosecuted or retaliation by agents. Moreover, all were male and from low-income backgrounds, which may not reflect the experiences of women and urban residents. Consequently, while our crime script findings are analytically generalisable to structurally similar populations, they should not be statistically generalised to all illegal kidney sellers in Pakistan. Transferability is best assessed by readers considering the fit between our sample characteristics and their own contexts of interest. Further research incorporating diverse populations is needed to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the subject. The study did not systematically collect detailed socio-familial characteristics, such as participants’ status as primary earners, family support systems, or reasons for non-disclosure to relatives. This reflected the sensitivity of the topic and participants’ reluctance to discuss personal or family matters, which may also have limited their understanding of the deeper context. Despite these, the study offers analytical perspectives that guide both policy and research frameworks on organ selling and exploitation in developing countries.

Policy recommendations

System-level reforms are required to curb the illegal kidney trade in Pakistan. First, the government must promote and operationalise deceased organ donation and transplantation to improve legitimate options, thereby reducing demand-driven pressure. Second, the government must prioritise systemic social protection, including targeted debt-relief programs, health financing models, and financial support for daily wage workers in high-poverty districts, reinforced by community awareness campaigns (via mosques, Lady Health Workers, and local media) about recruitment scams, legal consequences, and available alternatives. Third, health regulatory authorities and law enforcement must pivot from a reactive enforcement model to a proactive prevention framework against organised criminal networks: the Punjab Healthcare Commission and PHOTA should intensify unannounced hospital surveillance in metropolitan areas. At the same time, police focus resources on low-income recruitment zones to disrupt broker pathways and prosecute brokers (not donors). Finally, identified illegal donors must not be abandoned and must receive post-donation rehabilitation, including physical follow-up and mental health counselling.

Conclusion

The illegal kidney trade is not just an illicit act and a public health issue: it is an expression of structural inequality, system exploitation, and criminal opportunism within the healthcare infrastructure. Addressing this issue requires coordinated policy interventions, strengthened ethical oversight, and the establishment of robust social protection mechanisms to safeguard vulnerable populations. By amplifying the lived experiences of those exploited in this trade, the present study underscores the urgent need for rights-based reforms to dismantle the networks that thrive on poverty, misinformation, and secrecy.
