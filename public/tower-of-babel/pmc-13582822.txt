RET signaling as a mediator of estrogen receptor positive breast cancer brain metastasis
Authors: Simeng Liu, Geoffrey Pecar, Ye Cao, Fangyuan Chen, Abdalla Wedn, Osama S Shah, Jennifer M Atkinson, Jagmohan Hooda, Steffi Oesterreich, Adrian V Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain metastasis (BrM) occurs in 10–15% of patients with estrogen receptor–positive breast cancer (ER + BC) and remains a major clinical challenge with limited therapeutic options. Median survival after BrM diagnosis is approximately 18 months, underscoring the need for new druggable targets. We previously identified recurrent overexpression of the receptor tyrosine kinase RET in BrM relative to matched primary tumors. RET’s principal ligand, Glial Cell-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (GDNF), is highly expressed in the brain. Here, we show that RET expression and GDNF-responsive gene signatures are elevated in BrM from ER + BC patients. RET signaling promotes metastatic phenotypes in vitro, all of which are suppressed by Pralsetinib, a potent RET-selective inhibitor. Using RET-overexpressing and RET-deficient ER + BC models, we demonstrate that RET activation enhances downstream signaling and drives metastatic behaviors in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo. In ex vivo mouse brain slice co-culture, RET overexpression increases colonization and invasion, which are abrogated by Pralsetinib or RET knockdown. In vivo, RET overexpression enhances brain colonization and reduces survival. Proteomic profiling reveals novel GDNF-mediated signaling interactions, including PLCγ, P70S6K, STAT3, and CREB. These findings identify RET as a targetable driver of BrM from ER + BC and support therapeutic targeting of RET in affected patients. Subject terms: Breast cancer, Metastasis

Introduction

Brain metastases (BrM) affect approximately 10–15% of patients with estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer (ER + BC)1. Current therapeutic strategies consist primarily of surgical resection, cytotoxic chemotherapy, and radiation, yielding a median overall survival of approximately 18 months2. While multiple systemic therapies are under active investigation for the management of BrM from ER + BC, strategies of interest are dominated by ER-directed therapy and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibition3, underscoring a need for novel therapeutic targets. Using genome-wide exome capture RNA sequencing, we have previously identified recurrent overexpression of the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) RET in breast cancer BrM relative to patient-matched primary tumors4. The principal ligand for RET, Glial Cell-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (GDNF), is highly expressed in the brain and peripheral nervous tissue, where it functions in the maintenance of both neurons and glial cells5. Recently, RET has been shown to drive adhesion and ex vivo brain colonization in organoid models, providing evidence for a role in the establishment of secondary CNS lesions6. Here, we expand on the current understanding of a role for RET in BrM from ER + BC by describing novel signaling interactions and demonstrating the in vivo effects of RET overexpression.

RET is a single-pass transmembrane RTK involved in the development, maturation and maintenance of the kidney and the enteric nervous system, and has recently been shown to regulate phenotypic changes associated with lactation and mammary duct involution7,8. Activation of RET signaling requires binding of GDNF family ligands (GFLs) to GDNF family receptor-α (GFRα) family co-receptors. The subsequent formation of ternary GFL-GFR-RET complexes triggers RET dimerization and kinase domain phosphorylation, which induces phosphorylation of numerous downstream targets including ERK, AKT, and PLCγ-17. RET contributes to tumorigenesis through two major mechanisms, gain-of-function mutations (which include point mutations and gene fusions lading to constitutive kinase activity) and increased expression of wild-type receptors7. RET alterations are associated with medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC), and have been reported in breast cancer9–11. RET and GFRα1 are expressed in approximately 30–70% of breast cancers12, especially ER+ tumors, which comprise approximately 70% of all breast tumors13–15. In ER + BC, RET expression is elevated in recurrent tumors compared to normal tissues or corresponding primary lesions, and is correlated with larger tumor size, more advanced stage, and worse survival outcomes13,14,16,17. Previous studies have demonstrated that activation of RET signaling promotes breast cancer cell proliferation, survival, and migration in vitro14,16,18. While the function of RET in primary tumorigenesis and endocrine therapy resistance has been extensively studied, its role in breast cancer BrM is less clearly understood.

RET is a target of multiple multi-kinase inhibitors (MKIs), including Cabozantinib and Vandetanib, which inhibit RET less potently than their principal targets such as VEGFR1/2/3, MET, or EGFR19,20. Increased recognition of the function and prevalence of RET alterations in multiple cancers has led to the development of the first generation of RET-selective kinase inhibitors (Selpercatinib and Pralsetinib), which are both FDA approved for the treatment of RET-mutant cancers21–23. The potency of RET blockade by Pralsetinib has been demonstrated in NSCLC and MTC cell lines with constitutively active RET mutations, with IC50 values 10-100-fold lower than MKIs21, as well as clinical activity in lung and thyroid carcinoma patients (NCT03037385). Notably, Pralsetinib and Selpercatinib also show therapeutic benefit in intracranially implanted NSCLC cell lines and in BrM patients harboring RET fusions21,24,25. This suggests that the current RET-selective inhibitors may accumulate beyond the blood-brain barrier at therapeutic concentrations, making them a promising strategy for BrM treatment.

In this study, we provide evidence that increased RET activation is significantly enriched in clinical samples of brain metastasis compared to corresponding primary tumors and compared to metastases in other organs. We demonstrate that RET overexpression amplifies activation of multiple downstream targets including ERK and AKT and promotes multiple pro-metastatic properties. We demonstrate that the RET-selective inhibitor Pralsetinib potently abrogates GDNF-mediated RET signaling, thereby suppressing cell growth and pro-metastatic phenotypes. Ex vivo brain slice co-culture models reveal that RET overexpression in breast cancer cell lines potentiates colonization and invasion of brain tissue. Further, RET overexpression facilitates brain colonization of breast cancer cells in vivo and is associated with reduced overall survival of the xenografts. In summary, this study identifies and characterizes RET as a targetable driver of breast cancer brain metastasis, and further describes the action of Pralsetinib in ER-positive breast cancer cell lines, providing insights to improve current targeted therapies for patients with breast cancer brain metastasis.

Results

RET expression and activation is increased in breast cancer brain metastases

Activation of RET by GDNF-GFRα complexes initiates multiple downstream pathways involved in promotion of cell proliferation and survival, facilitating tumor initiation and progression7. Given elevated RET expression is enhanced in breast cancer BrM samples, we further analyzed the relationship between RET-mediated downstream signaling activation and brain metastasis. To this end, we utilized a proliferation-independent GDNF-responsive gene set (GDNF-RGS) developed by Morandi et al.26 to examine RET expression and activation of GDNF-RET signaling axis in RNAseq datasets of both primary and metastatic ER+ breast tumors.

Considering the extensive interactions between RET and ER15,16, we analyzed our previously reported cohort of 67 ER-positive (ER + ) clinical samples containing 35 tumors from four metastatic sites (brain, bone, gastrointestinal tract, and ovary) and matched primary tumors. Median RET expression was highest in brain metastases among all four metastatic sites (Fig. 1A). While no statistical significance in Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) scores of GDNF-RGS was observed between metastatic sites, BrM lesions exhibited the highest median GDNF-RGS score (Fig. 1B). Among 9 ER+ primary-BrM pairs, RET expression was increased in 7 cases, with a concurrent increase in GDNF-RGS expression in 5 cases (Fig. 1C), demonstrating a recurrent gain in both expression and activation of RET in BrM lesions. Analyses of multiplatform genomic profiles of matched brain-metastatic and primary breast tumors from AURORA27 revealed the highest median RET expression and GSVA score in BrM among all metastatic sites (Fig. S1A, B). Higher median RET expression was observed in primary tumors corresponding to BrM than those corresponding to other metastatic sites (Fig. S1C, D).

We next examined the relationship between RET expression and activation and clinical outcomes in breast cancer patients. We analyzed the METABRIC dataset with a record of histopathology and RNAseq data of primary tumors grouped by their future metastatic sites and disease-free survival data acquired through follow-up. Consistent with our cohort, BrM lesions showed the highest median GDNF-RGS scores, and the second-highest median RET expression among metastatic sites with 20 or more samples (Fig. 1D, E). Increased GDNF-RGS was significantly associated with poor survival (Fig. 1F). Interestingly, RET expression was significantly associated with increased disease-free survival (p = 0.033) (Fig. 1G). Neither GDNF-RGS nor RET expression were predictive of BrM-free survival within the ER+ cases that subsequently developed brain metastases (Fig. S1G, H), possibly due to reduced cohort size.

Taken together, we found a general elevation of RET expression in BrM relative to the matched primary breast tumor and other metastatic sites.

Pralsetinib potently inhibits RET activation in RET-overexpressing and in non-overexpressing breast cancer cell line models

Pralsetinib, a RET-selective kinase inhibitor, has demonstrated potent RET blockade in NSCLC and MTC cell lines21. In contrast, its efficacy in breast cancer remains largely unexamined. To explore the GDNF-RET signaling axis in breast cancer, a cohort of 60 breast cancer cell lines was screened for mRNA levels of RET, GFRα1 and GDNF, from which 4 models were selected for further study. These comprise 2 models of invasive breast carcinoma (no special type), and 2 models of invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) (Fig. 2A), representing RET-positive models of the two most common breast cancer histologic subtypes. T47D cells, with undetectable levels of RET (Fig. 2B, F) and GFRα114,16 served as a negative control. GDNF expression was minimal across all 4 cell lines, suggesting no endogenous or autocrine RET activation. We next assessed the impact of exogenous GDNF on each cell line model. After overnight serum starvation in low (0.5%) serum medium followed by 30 min treatment with 100 ng/mL GDNF, RET phosphorylation at Y905 was significantly increased in MCF-7 and SUM44 cells, but was minimally upregulated in the MM134 cells (Fig. 2B). T47D cells displayed no significant change in RET, ERK, or AKT phosphorylation in response to GDNF, presumably due to absence of GFRα1 and low RET expression. shRNA-mediated RET knockdown in MCF-7 and SUM44 cell lines significantly reduced RET expression and phosphorylation in response to GDNF, along with a concurrent reduction in downstream ERK and AKT phosphorylation, confirming specificity of GDNF-induced RET signaling (Fig. 2C, D). However, RET-GDNF-mediated phosphorylation of ERK and AKT persisted in RET-deficient SUM44 model (Fig. 2D), suggesting potential involvement of alternate receptors for GDNF such as NCAM128 or syndecan 329. Pralsetinib pretreatment exhibited stronger abrogation of GDNF-RET signaling than RET knockdown in both cell lines (Fig. 2C, D). RET overexpression was required for studies of RET signaling in T-47D and MM134 cell lines. In both models, RET and downstream phosphorylation in response to GDNF was significantly increased after RET overexpression, and was potently inhibited by Pralsetinib (Fig. 2E, F). RET (Y905) and ERK exhibited ligand-independent phosphorylation in MM134 RET overexpressing (RET-OE) cells which persisted in the presence of pralsetinib, consistent with reports of ligand-independent RET and MEK activation in NIH-3T3 cells following exogenous RET expression due to receptor autophosphorylation11,12. Treatment with recombinant GFRα1 facilitated GDNF-induced RET signaling in T-47D cells (Fig. 2F), consistent with the limited co-receptor expression in this specific cell line. Interestingly, T-47D RET-OE cells were more sensitive to Pralsetinib-mediated abrogation of ERK1/2 and AKT phosphorylation than empty vector (EV) controls. MCF-7 cell line models were selected for subsequent in vitro studies based on the predictable effects of RET overexpression and knockdown on the response to GDNF in this cell line.

The relationship between duration of GDNF treatment and RET signaling activity was examined in MCF-7 cells with basal (EV) or elevated (RET-OE) RET expression (Fig. 2G). GDNF rapidly induced RET phosphorylation in MCF-7 RET-OE cells, with phosphorylation of RET and downstream ERK and AKT significantly elevated after 15 min of GDNF treatment. RET activation peaked after 12 h of treatment, and persisted through 24 h in the MCF-7 RET-OE model. In SUM44 cells, peak RET activity was observed at 30 min, followed by a sharp decline after 1 h (Fig. S2A). These results indicate that GDNF elicits a rapid and durable effect on MCF-7 cells, which serve as the representative RET+ breast cancer model in subsequent studies.

Dose-response assays were used to examine the efficacy and potency of Pralsetinib in inhibiting GDNF-RET signaling. Pralsetinib has demonstrated ≥10-fold greater potency over approved MKIs against oncogenic RET variants, with >50% inhibition of RET and AKT activation at concentrations as low as 5 nM21. Here, we assessed Pralsetinib activity against RET signaling in breast cancer models with elevated RET expression. Pralsetinib reduced GDNF-mediated RET phosphorylation at both Y1062 and Y905 with an IC50 of 0.5 nM after 16 h treatment, reaching 90% inhibition at 5 nM (Fig. 2H–J). ERK1/2 and AKT phosphorylation were also reduced at 5 nM, though phosphorylation of ERK1/2 was less sensitive in RET-OE cells compared to EV control (Fig. 2H). Phosphorylation of ERK and AKT was completely blocked by Pralsetinib at 500 nM (Fig. 2J). In the GDNF-responsive ILC model SUM44, Pralsetinib reduced RET phosphorylation at 0.5 nM (Fig. S2B) and fully suppressed ERK1/2 activation at 500 nM (Fig. S2C), in agreement with findings in MCF-7 model. Interestingly, AKT phosphorylation in SUM44 cells persisted at an intermediate level despite increasing Pralsetinib concentration, suggesting alternative AKT activating pathways. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Pralsetinib effectively abrogates GDNF-mediated RET and downstream signaling pathways in RET-overexpressing breast cancer cell line models.

RET overexpression and activation confer pro-metastatic properties in vitro

GDNF-RET signaling has previously been shown to promote metastatic phenotypes by facilitating cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion. GDNF treatment significantly enhanced proliferation of MCF-7 EV and RET-OE cells in adherent (2D) conditions (Fig. 3A). RET overexpression did not further increase GDNF-dependent proliferation, suggesting that basal RET expression in MCF-7 cells is sufficient to drive GDNF-dependent cell growth30,31. Pralsetinib treatment (500 nM) fully abrogated the stimulatory effect of GDNF on 2D proliferation in both EV and RET-OE cell lines (Fig. 3A). shRNA-mediated RET-knockdown cells showed no response to GDNF in 2D culture, however, non-targeting control shRNA (shNTC) also eliminated the response to GDNF (Fig. S3A), possibly due to off-target effects of transfection and selection procedures on overall cellular fitness in adherent culture. To verify the effects of RET knockdown on MCF-7 cell growth, we utilized Ultra-Low Attachment (ULA) culture, which minimizes the effects of cell attachment and contact by facilitating suspended growth over a hydrogel surface32. GDNF increased cell proliferation in both EV and RET-OE cell lines, with no additional effect from RET overexpression (Fig. 3B). In contrast to the results observed in adherent culture, GDNF-stimulated cell proliferation was decreased by shRNA-mediated RET knockdown in ULA conditions, confirming the RET-dependence of GDNF-induced cell growth (Fig. S3B). Pralsetinib effectively abrogated GDNF-promoted growth in both EV and RET-OE cell lines.

The potency of Pralsetinib in inhibiting ULA cell proliferation was further assessed to characterize the inhibitor’s properties in breast cancer cell lines. In the absence of GDNF, no significant difference in response to Pralsetinib treatment was observed between MCF-7 EV and RET-OE cells, with IC50 values of approximately 5 μM (Fig. 3C). In the presence of GDNF, potency of pralsetinib was markedly enhanced in both cell lines, with RET-OE cells requiring a 10-fold higher dose for growth suppression (43.4 nM versus 3.12 nM, Fig. 3C). Given GDNF’s abundance in the brain microenvironment, these data suggest that Pralsetinib may efficiently inhibit the proliferation of RET-expressing breast cancer cells at low doses.

Formation of colonies improves tumor cell survival away from the primary lesion, thereby facilitating distant metastasis. We evaluated colony formation using sphere formation assays, which encourages intercellular contacts via adherens junctions in a ULA environment33,34. GDNF increased sphere size in both MCF-7 EV and RET-OE cells (Fig. 3D). While RET overexpression did not further promote sphere formation, RET-deficient cells lost GDNF responsiveness, indicating the specificity of GDNF-RET signaling in promoting breast cancer cell proliferation and colony formation (Fig. S3C). The stimulatory effect of GDNF was blocked by both RET knockdown and Pralsetinib treatment. We employed soft agar gel assays to further examine anchorage-independent colony formation35,36. RET overexpression increased both colony number and size in this assay (Fig. 3E, Fig. S3D). GDNF-promoted colony formation was effectively abrogated by Pralsetinib in both cell lines. RET-OE cells formed more colonies in soft agar than EV controls in the absence of GDNF, suggesting that RET may promote anchorage-independent survival independently of GDNF.

GDNF has been reported to increase cell motility in various cancers, including breast cancer and glioma14,37. To investigate the role of RET overexpression in cell migration, we utilized wound healing assays, in which an artificial gap is introduced in a cell monolayer and subsequent wound closure reflects migratory capacity. GDNF significantly promoted cell migration in RET-overexpressing cells compared to EV controls, with migration inhibited by Pralsetinib in both cell lines (Fig. 3F). We next applied Boyden chamber assays, with GDNF as a chemoattractant (Fig. 3G). While MCF-7 cells with basal RET expression displayed limited chemotaxis toward GDNF, RET overexpression increased chemotaxis, suggesting high RET expression may promote colonization of a GDNF-rich environment. Pralsetinib abrogates cell migration in this system, with complete inhibition at 500 nM (Fig. 3F), reinforcing its therapeutic potential.

Parental MCF-7 cells with basal RET expression typically exhibit minimal invasiveness, but GDNF administration has been shown to enhance cell invasion14,38. To assess the effect of RET overexpression on cell invasion, we employed Collagen I-coated Boyden chamber assay as previously described14. In contrast to chemotaxis studies, GDNF induced limited invasion in MCF-EV cells (Fig. 3H). However, GDNF significantly promoted cell invasion in MCF-7 RET-OE cells compared to EV control. Pralsetinib abrogated GDNF-promoted cell invasion in both RET-OE cells and EV control.

These observations demonstrate the stimulatory effect of GDNF-RET signaling on pro-metastatic phenotypes, including cell proliferation, colonization, migration and invasion. Notably, RET overexpression itself did not confer metastatic properties in MCF-7 cells, highlighting the necessity of GDNF for sufficient RET activation and phenotypic changes.

RET overexpression drives colonization and invasion of ex vivo brain slice cultures

To assess the effect of RET expression on colonization and invasion of brain tissue, we adapted an organotypic coculture system utilizing mouse brain slices39,40. RFP-labeled RET-OE or EV control cells were cultured on mouse brain slices maintained in enhanced brain culture media (Fig. 4A). As GDNF expression in mouse liver is undetectable41, liver slices served as negative control (Fig. 4B). RET overexpression increased MCF-7 colonization threefold in brain slice co-cultures compared to EV control, and sixfold relative to liver slices (Fig. 4B). Daily treatment of cocultures with Pralsetinib (500 nM) reduced brain colony formation of MCF-7 RET-OE cells to levels comparable to EV controls (Fig. 4B). These data demonstrate that RET overexpression enhances breast cancer cell tissue colonization, particularly in brain tissue. Further, RET inhibition effectively reduces brain colonization, suggesting RET as a druggable target for BrM.

To examine the effects of RET overexpression on brain tissue invasion, we utilized an organotypic co-culture system of Matrigel-embedded breast cancer cells and mouse brain slices as described by Chuang et al.39 in order to better mimic physiological conditions. Cells were resuspended in a Matrigel spheroid (90% Matrigel, 10% culture medium) and placed adjacent to the brain slice for five days (Fig. 4C). RET overexpression significantly increased colonization and invasion of co-cultured brain slices, which were quantified by colony number and invaded distance within brain slice imaging fields respectively (Fig. 4D). Invasion of brain tissue by shNTC-expressing cells was minimal, and while RET knockdown reduced invasion, the difference was not statistically significant, likely due to the minimal invasion achieved by cells with basal RET expression. In sum, these data indicate RET expression is key in facilitating invasion and colonization of brain tissue, concurrent with the GDNF-induced metastatic phenotypes documented above. Taken with in vitro experiments demonstrating a role for GDNF in invasion and chemotaxis, our ex vivo data suggest that brain-derived GDNF may act as a chemoattractant for RET-overexpressing breast cancer cells, thereby enhancing colonization.

Taken together, these findings indicate that components of the brain microenvironment specifically activate RET signaling, thus enhancing both invasion and colony formation of breast cancer cells in ex vivo brain tissue. Combined with the relatively high GDNF levels in brain relative to liver, these results support the hypothesis that GDNF in the brain microenvironment facilitates colonization, invasion, and migration of RET-overexpressing breast cancer cells, thereby promoting dissemination of these cells in the brain.

RET overexpression enhances brain colonization of MCF-7 cells in vivo

To investigate whether RET overexpression facilitates BrM formation in vivo, we established xenografts in female NSG mice via intracardiac injection of RFP-luciferase labeled MCF-7 RET-OE cells. Intracardiac inoculation was employed to determine whether RET overexpression enhances the ability of MCF-7 cells to colonize the brain following introduction to systemic circulation. Mice inoculated with MCF-7-EV cells were used as a control. Successful injection was confirmed by immediate systemic luminescence (Fig. S4A). At 7 weeks post-injection, RET-OE xenografts exhibited significantly greater growth in brain compared to EV controls, reflected by a significant difference in area under the curve (AUC) of brain bioluminescence relative to week 0 (Fig. 5A, B). Further, RET-OE xenografts harbored a significantly higher fold change in brain luminescence at death or euthanasia (Fig. 5C). Bioluminescence and x-ray overlays acquired 7 weeks post-injection confirmed brain metastases in 100% of animals injected with RET-OE cells, compared to only 50% of animals inoculated with EV control cells. Sites of metastasis were confirmed by fluorescent microscopy (Fig. S5C). Overall survival was recorded according to euthanasia performed at pre-defined veterinary endpoints as established by IACUC guidelines. RET-OE xenografts exhibited reduced overall survival relative to the control group (p = 0.03) (Fig. 5E). Similar numbers of macro-metastases were found in non-brain organs in both RET-OE and EV groups (Fig. 5F), with RFP detected in adrenal glands, ovaries, kidney, liver, and gallbladder (Fig. S4C). Diffuse RFP signals were detected in lungs, but no lung macro-metastases were observed. These observations indicate that high levels of RET expression facilitate brain-tropic colonization of breast cancer cells, which is associated with reduced survival in NSG mice.

To investigate the role of RET signaling in lobular breast cancer BrM progression, we used shRNA to knock down RET in SUM44 cell line. RFP-luciferase labeled SUM44PE shNTC and shRET cells were injected into the mammary fat pad of NSG mice. Primary tumors were surgically excised upon reaching ~200mm3, after which brain metastasis formation and progression were tracked longitudinally by bioluminescence imaging (BLI). Survival analysis indicated a trend of longer median survival in shRET group compared to the shNTC group (Fig. S5A). Brain metastases formed in 6/7 shNTC mice and 5/6 shRET mice (Fig. S5B, Table S1). The first detectable brain metastasis signal was set as the baseline. Brain metastasis progression was assessed at early (week 5) and late (week 10) time points using IVIS imaging (Fig. S5C, D). One mouse per group was excluded due to absence of BrM. At both early and late time points, in spite of statistical significance, the SUM44-shNTC group consistently demonstrated a trend of greater fold increase in brain metastasis signal intensity compared to SUM44-shRET group, suggesting more rapid metastatic growth in the presence of RET. Though 11 mice were initially used in each group, complications during primary tumor resection and subsequent deaths reduced sample size limiting the statistical significance. Nonetheless, the observed trend suggests a potential role for RET in facilitating the growth of lobular breast cancer BrM.

CREB or P70S6K knockdown attenuates GDNF-induced RET signaling and cell migration

To further examine signaling mechanisms involved in RET-mediated BrM development, we employed a Human Phospho-Kinase Array (R&D) to assess phosphorylation levels of 43 kinase phosphorylation sites and expression of 2 proteins involved in the RET signaling network. Increased phosphorylation of numerous targets including P70-S6 Kinase (P70S6K), ERK1/2, AKT, and STAT3 were observed in GDNF-stimulated EV control, with stronger effects in RET-OE cells (Fig. 6A). Interestingly, the phosphorylation of Src kinase family members (e.g., Lyn, Src, Fyn) was downregulated by GDNF. To gain a comprehensive view of GDNF-mediated RET signaling in breast cancer, we performed reverse phase proteomic analysis (RPPA) in GDNF-treated samples of MCF-7 (EV and RET-OE) and SUM44 (shNTC and shRET). Overall, 133 significant (p < 0.05) changes in phosphorylation were detected in MCF-7 samples (Fig. S6B), with 40 targets exhibiting the highest fold change in RET-OE samples (Fig. 6B). RPPA results were consistent with the phospho-kinase array results, confirming enhanced GDNF-responsiveness with RET overexpression. Specifically, ERK (MAPK), AKT, P70S6K, and CREB phosphorylation were well-conserved and ranked among the top 5 RET-activated targets by logFC in both characterization strategies, as well as in confirmatory western blots (Fig. 6C). PLCγ-1 activation also increased in response of GDNF stimulation after RET overexpression. Consistent with our observations in RET-overexpressing MCF-7 cells, activation of ERK, CREB, and PLCγ-1 was reduced by RET knockdown in SUM44 cells (Fig. 6B), as confirmed by immunoblot.

Increased activation of these proteins was confirmed by immunoblot in RET-OE cells. Altogether, identification of these downstream targets suggests possible molecular mechanisms of GDNF-RET pathway-mediated tumor progression.

Discussion

The diagnosis of brain metastasis poses a remarkable clinical challenge for breast cancer patients42. The reported incidence of BrM is rising due to early identification of BrM lesions and improved control of extracranial metastases, which extend survival but raise lifetime risk of developing BrM43. The current understanding of BrM largely derives from studies of TNBC and HER2-positive subtypes, for which multiple systemic therapies are available, including HER2-targeting TKIs and trastuzumab conjugates44–47. In contrast, specific drivers of BrM from ER + BC remain poorly characterized, leaving patients with BrM from this most common subtype with few therapeutic options. We previously reported RET, a receptor tyrosine kinase corresponding to a brain-specific growth factor GDNF, as one of the most frequently enriched clinically actionable genes in patient-matched brain metastases relative to primary tumors, with expression gains in 38% of BrM samples and a pronounced enrichment in ER+ cases4. Given that GDNF is abundantly and specifically expressed in the brain microenvironment, we hypothesized that the availability of GDNF in the brain provides a permissive microenvironment for RET-overexpressing breast cancer cells to form brain metastases, and that RET inhibition is a potential therapeutic approach for RET-driven breast cancer BrM.

This study extends previous findings6 showing that RET overexpression promotes cancer cell adhesion, invasion and outgrowth in the brain, where EGFR was required for a pro-BrM phenotype. While the earlier study focused on BDNF, our work investigates GDNF, another neurotrophic factor, in RET-mediated brain colonization. Mice inoculated with MCF-7 RET-OE cells exhibited significantly higher brain tumor burden over the course of study compared to MCF-7 EV-bearing animals, providing in vivo evidence that RET overexpression significantly enhances brain metastatic potential in a physiologically relevant context. Furthermore, we clarify downstream signaling pathways activated by RET. This detailed mechanistic analysis offers new insights into how RET contributes to brain metastasis through its signaling network, highlighting potential therapeutic strategies targeting RET in breast cancer BrM. Thus, our study reinforces and extends the current understanding of RET’s role in brain metastatic progression.

In this study, we characterized RET as a driver of breast cancer BrM originating from ER + BC. Both RET expression and activation were upregulated in BrM relative to the matched primary tumors and lesions from other metastatic sites. Elevated RET expression and GDNF-RGS score were associated with poor prognosis for ER + BC patients, supporting the prognostic significance of RET, which is consistent with prior findings across multiple ER-positive cohorts (NKI295, Pawitan, TransBig)26, underscoring the predictive power of gene signatures to assess prognosis of ER + BC.

Using cell line models of the two most common histologic subtypes of breast cancer, we found that activation of RET, along with downstream ERK and AKT was upregulated in RET overexpressing cells in response to GDNF, which was potently inhibited by Pralsetinib. The specificity of GDNF-RET signaling was confirmed through shRNA-mediated RET knockdown. We observed a time-dependent increase in the level of RET phosphorylation, which peaked at different time points in MCF-7 and SUM44 cells after GDNF treatment. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of RET activation. Further, we confirmed a role for RET overexpression and GDNF-induced activation in the mediation of pro-metastatic properties in vitro, including migration, invasion and anchorage-dependent growth. However, RET overexpression alone does not facilitate cell growth, highlighting the critical role of ligand availability in RET-driven breast cancer phenotypes. GDNF-induced migratory ability is abrogated by Pralsetinib, indicating its therapeutic potential. Furthermore, brain organotypic co-culture experiments revealed that RET overexpression specifically promoted brain tissue colonization and invasion compared to liver, which was reversed by Pralsetinib.

In vivo studies provided additional support for RET’s role in BrM. RET overexpression was associated with earlier BrM formation, increased brain luminescence, and shorter survival in NSG mice. However, numbers of macrometastases were similar between RET-OE and EV control groups, suggesting that RET enhances colonization and metastatic growth rather than seeding. These findings reinforce the critical role of RET signaling in BrM progression and suggest therapeutic targeting of RET as a strategy to delay or prevent brain metastases. Although the RET-specific inhibitor, Pralsetinib, was not employed in vivo, we investigated the brain metastatic capability through the RET-deficient orthotopic model. In this model, while the overall metastatic burden was not significantly reduced, we noted a trend that RET knockdown may slow brain metastasis growth upon initial detection. This leads us to hypothesize that RET signaling may be more critically involved in the post-colonization outgrowth of brain metastases rather than in the initial “seeding” process. Future studies employing Pralsetinib in RET-dependent BrM animal models will be crucial to definitively validate its potential as a therapeutic target for established brain metastases.

Human Phospho-Kinase Array and RPPA identified numerous signaling molecules responsive to GDNF-RET activation, including ERK1/2, AKT, PLCγ, P70S6K, STAT3 and CREB, which are associated with various malignant processes. Knockdown of CREB or P70S6K attenuated GDNF-stimulated RET pathway activation and resulting enhanced cell migration, consistent with previous publications documenting the role of PI3K/AKT and ERK/MAPK in the regulation of P70S6K and CREB. CREB activation via PI3K/AKT and ERK/MAPK pathways mediates cell proliferation and motility in prostate cancers48, while treatment with specific AKT and ERK inhibitors reduces CREB activation49. Moreover, inhibition of either PI3K or ERK induces prompt and prolonged suppression of p70S6K activation50. However, reverse regulation from P70S6K/CREB to ERK/AKT has not been reported, suggesting a potential positive feedback loop between P70S6K, CREB, ERK, and AKT. PLCγ-1 is activated in conditions of cellular stress or oxidative damage51. Overexpression of PLCγ-1 has been reported in various cancers including colorectal cancer and head and neck cancer, though the oncogenic mechanism remains unclear52,53. However, while GDNF-induced RET and ERK phosphorylation were reduced by PLCγ-1 knockdown, AKT remained active, and cell migration was unaffected. Inhibition of AKT has been shown to reduce cell migration in breast and gallbladder cancer cells54,55. Collectively, these findings suggest that GDNF promotes cell migration in RET overexpressing breast cancer cells via crosstalk through the AKT/ P70S6K/CREB signaling pathways.

Overall, our findings support the hypothesis that RET overexpression increases metastatic potential of ER + BC cells with a specific increase in colonization of brain tissue, the principal site of RET ligand expression. We show that Pralsetinib is a potent inhibitor of RET’s function in breast cancer cells in vitro and ex vivo. Critical future directions of research include validation of the activity RET-selective inhibitors in in vivo models of breast cancer BrM. Additionally, our proteomic studies outline GDNF-RET signaling networks for further studies. Other recent studies6 have identified novel signaling partners for RET in the setting of BC BrM, including EGFR and NTRK2. Given the known relationship between RET and TRK overexpression in CNS cancers56 and the availability of potent dual RET-TRK small molecule inhibitors57, clarification of the relationship between RET, TRK receptors, and the downstream pathways identified in this study warrant further investigation. Additionally, RET expression has recently been shown to be upregulated in the setting of ESR1 fusions58, and we have previously shown that ESR1 mutations are enriched in ER + BC BrM samples59. Thus, interactions between RET and altered ER in brain-metastatic disease merit future study.

In summary, our study provides insight into the role of RET in breast cancer BrM, and provides initial characterization of RET-selective inhibitors. Given recent FDA approval of Pralsetinib and Selpercatinib for MTC, our findings show strong potential for clinical translation.

Methods

Analysis of gene expression from published & public datasets

Tissue sample collection and processing procedures for generation of publicly available data analyzed in this study are described in refs. 4,62. RNAseq data from paired primary and metastatic tumors were obtained from refs. 27,60,61. Quality control was performed by FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and summarized with multiQC (https://multiqc.info/). RNA expression was quantified with Salmon63 at quasi-mapping mode, and normalized to log2 trimmed mean counts per million (log2TMMcpm). For gene set scoring, Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)64 was performed on normalized data with selected gene sets as specified in individual sections.

In addition to public RNAseq data specified above, this study also used TCGA and METABRIC in analysis. METABRIC RNA microarray data (cbioportal.org/study?id=brca_metabric) was downloaded from Synapse (RRID:SCR_006307) and log normalized65. GSVA of selected gene set was performed with GSVA R package, in ssgsea or gsva mode with default parameters64,66. We used open-source packages in python (3.10.16) and R (4.4.0) for analysis. Codes for analysis was deposited in GitHub: https://github.com/ChelseaCHENX/RET-Brain-Metastasis-Analysis. Python packages used include pandas (v2.2.3), numpy (v2.1.3), seaborn (v0.13.2), matplotlib (v3.10.0), and scipy (1.15.1). R packages used include GSVA (v1.46), survival (v3.7.0), survminer (v0.5.0), dplyr (v1.1.4), gtools (v3.9.5), and ggplot2 (v3.5.2).

To analyze breast cancer cell line RNA sequencing data, we obtained RNAseq datasets from the Invasive Lobular Carcinoma Cell Line Encyclopedia (ICLE) study67 (https://github.com/leeoesterreich/ILC_CellLine_Encyclopedia). The dataset also incorporated non-special-type (NST) breast cancer cell line data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE)68 which was reprocessed to generate a harmonized and integrated breast cancer cell line gene count matrix.

Data analysis was performed in R v4.4.3. Gene expression levels were log-transformed and normalized as z-score across cell lines. To evaluate RET signaling activity, we focused on the expression of RET, GFRA1 and GDNF. Normalized expression values were visualized using the ComplexHeatmap package69, annotated by estrogen receptor status (ER+ or ER-), histological subtypes (ILC, ILC-like and NST) and Study (CCLE or ICLE). Associated code and data is available at https://github.com/leeoesterreich/RET_Project_Analysis.

For survival analysis, only estrogen receptor positive (ER +) and LumA patients were selected to avoid influence caused alone by PAM50 subtype. DormSig ssGSEA scores were stratified into low and high levels with optimized threshold using in surv_cutpoint and surv_categorize implemented in R survminer package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/survminer/index.html). Kaplan-Meier curve was plotted for each group using disease free survival, with p value from log-rank test, and hazard ratio (HR) calculated using univariate Cox regression (coxph in R survival package, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/survival/index.html).

Tissue culture and cell model generation

MCF-7 (RRID: CVCL_0031), T-47D (RRID: CVCL_0553), MDA-MB-134-VI (MM134, RRID: CVCL_0617), and HEK293T (293T, RRID: CVCL_0063) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). SUM44PE cells (SUM44, CVCL_3424) were purchased from Asterand. Cell identity was authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat DNA profiling (University of Arizona), and cells were routinely tested to be mycoplasma free.

To establish RET overexpressing (RET-OE) cell line models in MCF-7, MM134, and T-47D, a lentiviral vector carrying RET or paired empty vector control (VectorBuilder) was first packaged in HEK293T cells to generate viral particles by PEI transfection with pMDL, pRev and pVSVG. Supernatant was collected at 48 h, 60 h, and 72 h post-transfection to infect the target breast cancer cells followed by blasticidin selection. Cell lines utilized in this study were derived from pools of selected cells.

For stable RET deficient (shRET) breast cancer cell line model development, 3 shRNAs with distinct sequences to target RET were designed (Dharmacon) with one non-targeting control shRNA provided (Dharmacon). Lentiviral shRNA was packaged with psPAX2 and pMD2.G vectors in HEK293T cells, and virus-containing supernatant was collected at 48 h, 60 h, and 72 h post-transfection to infect the target breast cancer cells (MCF-7 and SUM44PE) followed by puromycin selection.

To label the established cell line models with tRFP and luciferase, a lentiviral vector was designed that carries both turbo-RFP and luciferase. The same lentivirus packaging procedures was followed as described above in shRNA transfection. The lentivirus-infected target cells were selected for 2 weeks with optimized concentration of G418 (400 µg/ml for MCF-7 RET overexpression and RET knockdown models as well as SUM44 RET knockdown models). Cells with top 15% RFP expression level were gated and collected from Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS, BD Biosciences) and recovered in G418 selection medium added with 1X Penicillin-Streptomycin for an extra week before back into regular culture medium.

Human phospho-kinase array analysis

The commercial Proteome Profiler Human Phospho-Kinase Array Kit (R&D Systems) was employed for Phospho-Kinase Array. Cells were plated in 1 10 cm dish per condition and serum-starved in 0.5% FBS medium (0.5% CSS medium for SUM44PE) for 18 h, followed by 30 min treatment of 100 ng/ml GDNF or vehicle (distilled water) in 0.5% FBS medium (0.5% CSS medium for SUM44PE). Whole cell lysates were prepared following the instruction provided by the manufacturer. Protein concentrations were determined by Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Pierce™) and 600 µg protein was calculated accordingly to incubate with each membrane. The blotting and development procedures were also completed following the manufacturer’s handbook. Finally, the developed blots were exposed to films for 1, 2, 5, 8, and 10 min followed by processing using Konica SRX-101A Film Processor, and 8-min exposure was optimized for quantification. Films were scanned under colorimetric-gray mode in ChemiDoc XRS+ Imaging System (Bio-Rad) and the .scn files were analyzed using Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad). Background of the blots was subtracted and integrated density (pixel) of each dot on the blots was quantified through Image Lab - Volume Tools.

For data processing, relative protein levels were determined by normalization to the integrated density of PBS spots provided by the manufacturer on the blot as negative control (calculated as “0”) and to the conserved reference spots as positive control (calculated as “1”). Comparison between different treatment groups and the control group (vehicle-treated empty vector (EV, GDNF-) group) was accomplished by using limma package in R, with results sorted by adjusted p value. All 39 significant (adjusted p value < 0.05) targets were plotted into heatmap ranked by LogFC in RET-OE + GDNF group. Heatmaps of all significant targets in hierarchical clustering analysis could be found in Fig. S669.

Reverse phase protein array (RPPA)

Before treatment, cells were incubated in 0.5% FBS medium (0.5% CSS medium for SUM44PE) for 18 h and then switch to 100 ng/ml GDNF or vehicle (distilled water) in 0.5% FBS medium (0.5% CSS medium for SUM44PE) for 30 min. Cell lysate was prepared following a protocol modified from that provided by MD Anderson Center. Cells were harvested in RPPA lysis buffer with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce™). Then, lysates were mixed with RPPA lysis buffer and 4X SDS/B-Me sample buffer (40% Glycerol, 8% SDS, 0.25 M Tris-HCL, pH 6.8 and Beta-mercaptoethanol (B-Me) added before use at 1/10) to have final concentration of 1.22 μg/μl for further process by MD Anderson Center. Cell lysate samples were serially diluted two-fold for 5 dilutions (undiluted, 1:2, 1:4, 1:8; 1:16) and arrayed on nitrocellulose-coated slides in an 11 × 11 format to produce sample spots. Sample spots were then probed with antibodies by a tyramide-based signal amplification approach and visualized by DAB colorimetric reaction to produce stained slides. Stained slides were scanned on a Huron TissueScope scanner to produce 16-bit tiff images. Sample spots in tiff images were identified and their densities quantified by Array-Pro Analyzer.

For data processing, comparison between different treatment groups and the corresponding control group (vehicle-treated empty vector (EV, GDNF-) group in MCF-7 or vehicle-treated non-targeting control (shNTC, GDNF-) group in SUM44 models, respectively) was accomplished by limma package in R, with results sorted by adjusted p value (Supplementary Data 4). Top 40 significant (adjusted p value < 0.05) targets were selected and plotted into heatmap ranked by LogFC in RET-OE + GDNF group (shNTC + GDNF group in SUM44). Heatmaps of all significant targets in hierarchical clustering analysis could be found in Fig. S6.

Western blotting (WB)

Cells were all pre-incubated in low serum medium (0.5% FBS medium for MCF-7, T-47D, and MM134 and 0.5% CSS medium for SUM44) for 16 h (serum starvation) followed by 30 min of GDNF treatment. For samples treated with Pralsetinib (Blueprint Medicine, GAVRETO™), pralsetinib (or DMSO vehicle control) was provided during serum starvation and during GDNF (or distilled water) treatment. T-47D cells were additionally treated with, 10 ng/ml recombinant GFRα1 protein during GDNF treatment. Cell lysates were harvested in RIPA buffer with HALT Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Single-Use Cocktail added. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit. 50 µg protein were loaded to each well of a 15-well 8% denaturing gel. Both Li-Cor Odyssey Western Blotting protocol and Bio-Rad General Western Blotting protocol were followed as adaptive to Odyssey® Imaging System (Li-Cor) and ChemiDoc XRS+ Imaging System (Bio-Rad), respectively. For quantification, the intensity (pixel) of the bands was measured by Image J. Relative abundance was calculated by normalization to protein level of β-actin, and intensity of the target in GDNF-treated group without application of the inhibitor was set as 100%.

Cell growth assay

Cells were serum-starved in 0.5% FBS medium overnight before seeding in 96-well flat bottom tissue culture plates for 2D study or Corning 96 Well Clear Flat Bottom Ultra Low Attachment Microplates for ULA growth assay, both at 3000 cells per well in 60 μl 0.5% FBS medium. 24 h after cell seeding, cells were treated with drugs in extra 40 μl 0.5% FBS DMEM to reach a final concentration of 100 ng/ml GDNF (distilled water as vehicle) and 500 nM Pralsetinib (DMSO as vehicle, ATCC). For GDNF dose-response assay, 9 different concentrations of the drug were prepared by 1:2 serial dilution beginning at 100 ng/ml, with distilled water as vehicle. ATP levels in the culture medium were determined using a CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay according to manufacturer protocols to reflect cell proliferation. Luminescence was acquired using GloMax Microplate Reader or DTX 880 Multimode Detector. For data processing, luminescence of each well was corrected with the reads of the blank (0.5% FBS medium without any cells). All reads were normalized to Day 0 plates and fold change relative to Day 0 was used to reflect cell growth. Statistical significance was determined.

Pralsetinib dose-response assay

MCF-7 cells were seeded as described above. For drug preparation, 9 different concentrations of Pralsetinib were prepared by 1:5 serial dilution of a 4 mM solution. ATP levels in the culture medium were measured using CellTiter-Glo assay as discussed in the previous section. Nonlinear regression analysis and IC50 determination were performed in GraphPad Prism.

Sphere formation assay

MCF-7 cells were plated in Corning® 96 Well Clear Round Bottom Ultra Low Attachment Microplate at 5000 cells in 50 μl 0.5% FBS DMEM per well. After overnight incubation, cells were treated with additional 50 μl medium to reach a final concentration of 100 ng/ml for GDNF and 100 nM for Pralsetinib, with distilled water and DMSO as the vehicle, respectively. Spheres formed by MCF-7 cells were imaged by Olympus IX83 Microscope (4X) after 7-day culture. Sphere diameter and area were calculated using ImageJ.

Soft agar assay

Soft agar assay protocol was modified based on procedures described by Tasdemir et al.38. To generate the agar layers, 0.6% agar was prepared by 1:1 mixture of 1.2% Agar stock (1.2 g Agar in 100 ml distilled water) and enhanced medium (DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS, 1X NEAA (Thermo Scientific) and 1X Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Thermo Scientific). After solidification of the agar layer, cells resuspended in 0.4% agar in enhanced media were seeded at 10,000 cells per well in 6-well plates. After overnight incubation, GDNF (100 ng/ml) and/or Pralsetinib (100 nM) were administered. GDNF- or Pralsetinib-containing medium was renewed every 7 days, and cells were incubated for 7 weeks, after which the cells were fixed and stained in Crystal Violet solution. 4X magnification brightfield images were taken through Olympus IX83 Microscope, from which colony number and colony size were quantified in Image J.

Wound scratch assay

Cells were pre-incubated in DMEM supplemented with 0.5% FBS overnight before seeding in Incucyte® ImageLock 96-well plate, which was freshly coated with 50 μl 100 µg/ml Growth Factor Reduced Matrigel (Corning) diluted in regular culture medium. 1.5 × 105 cells were seeded per well in 8 replicates. MCF-7 cells formed 100% confluent cell monolayers 24 h after cell seeding. Medium was replaced with 100 μl DPBS before wound scratching. Incucyte® WoundMaker™ was employed to create homogeneous wounds in the middle of cell monolayer at a width of 700–800 μm. Cells were then treated with 100 ng/ml GDNF and 500 nM Pralsetinib or vehicle (DMSO) in 0.5% FBS DMEM. Mitomycin C (Sigma-Aldrich) was applied to prohibit cell proliferation at 5 μg/ml70. Cell migration was monitored by IncuCyte ZOOM® Live-Cell Analysis System with 10X images taken every 4 h. Relative wound density was quantified in built-in Incucyte wound scratch module, reflecting portion of region healed by migrated cells to initial wound.

Boyden chamber transwell migration assay

Cells were pre-incubated in 0.5% FBS DMEM overnight before seeded in Trans-parent PET membrane 8 μm pore size insert (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 500,000 cells per insert were seeded in 100 μl of DMEM supplemented with 0.5% FBS in 24-well plates. For transwell invasion assay, inserts were coated on both sides with 25 μg/ml Collagen I, Rat Tail (Corning) cross-linked in 0.02 N acetic acid. For both chemotaxis and invasion assays, 100 ng/ml GDNF or vehicle (distilled water) in 700 μl 0.5% FBS DMEM was added to the bottom chamber, while the inhibitor Pralsetinib (500 nM) was applied to the upper chamber (insert) where the cells were seeded. Cells were incubated for 48 h, after which the excess cells in top chambers were removed and cells attached to the bottom side of the inserts were fixed and stained in Crystal Violet solution (10% Methanol, 1% Paraformaldehyde in PBS, and 0.01% Crystal Violet). 1X images were taken through Olympus SZX16 dissecting microscope, from which migration percentage was quantified in Image J using the Area Fraction function to reflect the area occupied by purple-stained migrated cells divided by the area of whole insert.

Brain slice colony formation assay

Brains were obtained from 6 to 8-week-old NSG mice. Brain slices were sectioned at 300 microns using a vibratome generously provided by Dr. Gerard Apodaca’s lab. Sections were placed on a Millicell Cell Culture Insert (30 mm, hydrophilic PTFE, 0.4 µm, Millipore Sigma) and cultured overnight for recovery before cell addition. After 24 h, 50,000 RFP-labeled MCF-7 cells were seeded on the slices restricted by a sterile plastic cylinder and cultured for 5 days. Tissues were then gently washed in PBS (with calcium and magnesium) for 5 min, followed by fluorescent imaging (Olympus IX83 Microscope, 4X). Quantification of cell number in each group was completed in Image J. Each dataset represents n = 36 samples scored between 3 independent experiments. Unpaired t-test was performed for statistical analysis.

Brain slice invasion assay

Brain slices were generated as described above and placed on the Millicell Cell Culture Insert. After overnight recovery, 100,000 RFP-labeled MCF-7 cells (empty vector, RET-overexpressing, non-targeting control shRNA, or shRET) were resuspended in Growth Factor Reduced Matrigel (Corning) (90% matrigel, 10% full-serum culture medium) and the Matrigel spheroid was positioned next to the brain slice as illustrated in Fig. 4C. After 5-day co-culture, brain slices were fixed in 10% formalin followed by staining with Hoechst dye (ThermoFisher, 1 to 3000 dilution) for confocal microscope imaging (Nikon A1R Confocal Microscope, 10X magnification, 90 micron Z-stack with 5 micron steps). Z-stacks were projected 90 microns down from a point just below the surface of the brain slice (as observed by Hoechst staining) to ensure imaged invading cells were not on the surface of the coculture. Z-stack images were then exported to form composite images in Image J. Composite images as presented in this manuscript were used for quantification. A maximum of two visual fields were captured per tissue slice. Quantification was completed in a double-blind manner. Cell colonies invaded into brain slices were quantified using multipoint tool in Image J. For invaded distance, the distance (pixel) from the farthest invaded cell to tangent line of the edge of brain slice was measured in Image J. Unpaired t-test was used for statistical analysis.

In vivo xenograft study

All animal experiments were approved by the IACUC of the University of Pittsburgh (protocol #21028901). Four-week-old female NSG immunocompromised mice (NOD/NSG, Jackson Laboratory, #005557) were used for all in vivo studies. Mice were housed in groups of five per cage with environmental enrichment. The maximal tumor size permitted by the IACUC was 1600 mm³, and this limit was not exceeded in any experiment. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Animal studies were not pre-registered. Estradiol supplementation and analgesia: One day prior to cell injection, all mice were implanted subcutaneously with a sterilized 0.5 mg slow-release Estradiol (E2) wax pellet. To maintain estrogen levels following decline of pellet-released E2, 8 μg/ml Estradiol was provided in drinking water starting from the 7th week post-injection. Ketoprofen (Covetrus North America, Cat# 005487) was administered intraperitoneally at 5 mg/kg once daily on the day of surgery and for the following two days. Ethiqa XR (Covetrus North America, Cat# 072117) was additionally administered at 3.25 mg/kg on the day of surgery and the second day after surgery. Intracardiac injection model (MCF-7): To assess brain colonization following introduction to systemic circulation, RFP-luciferase labeled MCF-7 EV and RET-OE cells were resuspended in PBS at 1,000,000 cells/ml, and 100 μl (100,000 cells) was injected into the left ventricle of NSG mice (n = 9 for EV, n = 10 for RET-OE). Intracardiac injections were performed by Mrs. Christy Smolak. Successful injection was confirmed immediately by intraperitoneal injection of D-Luciferin (GoldBio, Cat# LUCK; 150 mg/kg in 100 μl) and whole-body bioluminescence imaging (BLI) using the IVIS Lumina X5 Imaging System, with a 5-minute diffusion period prior to imaging. IVIS imaging was subsequently performed weekly. Numerical source data from bioluminescent images are available in Supplementary Data 1. Orthotopic mammary fat pad injection model (SUM44PE): To assess brain metastasis progression in a lobular breast cancer model, RFP-luciferase labeled SUM44PE shNTC and shRET cells were resuspended in phenol red-free DMEM/F-12 at 100,000,000 cells/ml and mixed 1:1 with Growth Factor Reduced Basement Membrane Matrix (Corning, Cat# 356255) immediately prior to injection. A 100 μl aliquot (5,000,000 cells) was injected into the 4th left mammary fat pad of NSG mice (n = 11 per group). Tumor dimensions and animal weight were monitored biweekly using calipers and a scale. Primary tumors were surgically excised upon reaching approximately 200 mm³. To track metastatic progression to brain following resection, BLI was performed twice weekly using the IVIS Lumina X5 Imaging System following intraperitoneal injection of D-Luciferin (GoldBio, Cat# LUCK; 150 mg/kg in 100 μl), with a 5-minute diffusion period prior to imaging. Animal survival data were recorded. Source data for animal health and tumor size are available in Supplementary Data 2. Tissue collection and endpoint criteria: For both models, mice were euthanized upon observation of the following pre-defined IACUC endpoints: greater than 20% body weight loss, difficulty ambulating, anorexia, piloerection, hunched posture, rough or ungroomed appearance, excessive scratching or licking, mutilation of a painful area, pallor due to severe anemia, tumor size exceeding 1600 mm³, respiratory distress, or unresponsiveness to external stimuli. Overall survival was recorded at the time of euthanasia. Brain macrometastases and other potential organs of metastatic spread (lung, liver, and urogenital tracts) were harvested and imaged under an Olympus SZX16 dissecting microscope to detect RFP expressed by implanted tumor cells. Blinding and randomization: All injections and imaging procedures were performed within the same time window, and mice were housed in standard cages without randomization of cage placement. No specific strategies were used to minimize potential confounders such as treatment order, measurement order, or animal/cage location. Blinding was not employed during animal allocation, experimental procedures, IVIS imaging, or tissue collection due to logistical constraints and the need for continuous health monitoring by the same investigator. However, brain metastasis quantification by IVIS imaging was performed with the analyst blinded to group assignments. Outlier exclusion: All survived animals were included in data analyses with the following exception: one mouse in the MCF-7 RET-OE group whose brain luminescence value exceeded two standard deviations above the group mean was excluded from the analysis shown in Fig. 5B to prevent overestimation of the between-group difference. No a priori criteria were used to establish exclusions.

Tissue culture and cell model generation

MCF-7 (RRID: CVCL_0031), T-47D (RRID: CVCL_0553), MDA-MB-134-VI (MM134, RRID: CVCL_0617), and HEK293T (293T, RRID: CVCL_0063) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). SUM44PE cells (SUM44, CVCL_3424) were purchased from Asterand. Cell identity was authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat DNA profiling (University of Arizona), and cells were routinely tested to be mycoplasma free. Cells were maintained in continuous culture for less than 3 months with medium as follows: MCF-7: DMEM, 10% fetal bovine serum; T-47D: RPMI-1640, 10% fetal bovine serum; MDA-MB-134-VI: DMEM:L15 = 1:1, 10% fetal bovine serum; SUM44PE: DMEM/F12, 2% charcoal stripped serum, 5 μg/ml insulin, 1 μg/ml hydrocortisone, 10 mM HEPES, 5 mM ethanolamine, 5 μg/ml transferrin, 10 nM triiodothyronine, 50 nM sodium selenite; HEK293T: DMEM, 10% fetal bovine serum.

To establish RET overexpressing (RET-OE) cell line models in MCF-7, MM134, and T-47D, a lentiviral vector carrying RET (pLV[Exp]-EGFP-T2A-Bsd-EF1A>hRET, VectorBuilder, Cat #VB190923-1153aty) or paired empty vector control (VectorBuilder, Cat #VB191024-2380bjs) was first packaged in HEK293T cells to generate viral particles by PEI transfection with pMDL, pRev, and pVSVG. Supernatant was collected at 48 h, 60 h, and 72 h post-transfection to infect the target breast cancer cells followed by blasticidin (Thermo Fisher Science, Cat #A1113903) selection.

For stable RET deficient (shRET) breast cancer cell line model development, 3 shRNAs with distinct sequences to target RET were designed (Dharmacon) with one non-targeting control shRNA provided (Dharmacon). Lentiviral shRNA was packaged with psPAX2 and pMD2.G vectors in HEK293T cells, and virus-containing supernatant was collected at 48 h, 60 h, and 72 h post-transfection to infect the target breast cancer cells (MCF-7 and SUM44PE) followed by puromycin (Thermo Fisher Science Life Technology, Cat #A11138-03) selection.

To label the established cell line models with tRFP and luciferase, a lentiviral vector pLEX_TRC211-Luciferase2N-TurboRFP-c was designed that carries both turbo-RFP and luciferase. The same lentivirus packaging procedures was followed as described above in shRNA transfection. The lentivirus-infected target cells were selected for 2 weeks with optimized concentration of G418 (400 µg/ml for MCF-7 RET overexpression and RET knockdown models as well as SUM44 RET knockdown models). Cells with top 15% RFP expression level were gated and collected from Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS, BD FACSAria™ Fusion Cell Sorter, BD Biosciences) and recovered in G418 selection medium added with 1X Penicillin-Streptomycin (Hyclone, Cat #SV30010) for an extra week before back into regular culture medium.

Reverse phase protein array (RPPA)

Cells were plated in triplicates in 6-well plates, 2 wells per treatment. Before treatment, cells were incubated in 0.5% FBS medium (0.5% CSS medium for SUM44PE) for 18 h, and then switch to 100 ng/ml GDNF or vehicle (distilled water) in 0.5% FBS medium (0.5% CSS medium for SUM44PE) for 30 min. Cell lysate was prepared following a protocol modified from that provided by MD Anderson Center. Cells were harvested on ice by washing in ice-cold PBS twice, and lysing immediately in 6-well plates in RPPA lysis buffer (1% TritonX-100, 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 100 mM NaF, 10 mM Na4P2O7, 1 mM Na3VO4, 10% Glycerol) with freshly added protease inhibitors (cOmplete™, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, Roche, Cat #05056489001 and PhosSTOP™, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat #049068-37001). The plates were incubated on ice for 30 min and shaken every 5 min, followed by rocking at 4 °C for 10 min. Lysates were collected on ice into 1.5 ml eppendorf tube, incubated for 20 min and vortexed at 5 min interval. Cell lysates were sonicated for 5 min and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce™, Cat #23225). Then, lysates were mixed with RPPA lysis buffer and 4X SDS/B-Me sample buffer (40% Glycerol, 8% SDS, 0.25 M Tris-HCL, pH 6.8 and Beta-mercaptoethanol (B-Me) added before use at 1/10) to have final concentration of 1.22 μg/μl for further process by MD Anderson Center.

Cell lysate samples were serially diluted two-fold for 5 dilutions (undiluted, 1:2, 1:4, 1:8; 1:16) and arrayed on nitrocellulose-coated slides in an 11 × 11 format to produce sample spots. Sample spots were then probed with antibodies by a tyramide-based signal amplification approach and visualized by DAB colorimetric reaction to produce stained slides. Stained slides were scanned on a Huron TissueScope scanner to produce 16-bit tiff images. Sample spots in tiff images were identified and their densities quantified by Array-Pro Analyzer.

For data processing, relative protein levels for each sample were determined by interpolating each dilution curve produced from the densities of the 5-dilution sample spots using a “standard curve” (SuperCurve) for each slide (antibody). SuperCurve is constructed by a script in R, written by Bioinformatics. Relative protein levels are designated as log2 values (RawLog2). All relative protein level data points were normalized for protein loading and transformed to linear values, which are designated “Normalized Linear” (NormLinear). “Normalized Linear” values were transformed to log2 values (NormLog2) and then median-centered for hierarchical clustering analysis (NormLog2_MedianCentered). Comparison between different treatment groups and the corresponding control group (vehicle-treated empty vector (EV, GDNF-) group in MCF-7 or vehicle-treated non-targeting control (shNTC, GDNF-) group in SUM44 models, respectively) was accomplished by limma package in R, with results sorted by adjusted p value (Supplementary Data 4). Top 40 significant (adjusted p value < 0.05) targets were selected and plotted into heatmap ranked by LogFC in RET-OE + GDNF group (shNTC + GDNF group in SUM44). Heatmaps of all significant targets in hierarchical clustering analysis could be found in Fig. S6.

Western blotting (WB)

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and were subjected to desired treatment at 70–80% confluency. Cells were all pre-incubated in low serum medium (0.5% FBS medium for MCF-7, T-47D and MM134 and 0.5% CSS medium for SUM44) for 16 h (serum starvation) followed by 30 min of GDNF treatment. Pralsetinib (Blueprint Medicine, GAVRETO™) was provided not only in the low serum medium during serum starvation, but also applied during GDNF (or distilled water) treatment, DMSO as the vehicle control. Specially, 10 ng/ml GFRα1 (R&D Systems, Cat #714-GR-100) was applied together with GDNF for T-47D.

After rinsing the cells with cold PBS, cell lysates were harvested in 1.5X RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Nonidet P-40 (IGEPAL), 0.5% Sodium Deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) with 1X Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Single-Use Cocktail (100X, Thermo, Cat # 78442) added. The lysates were sonicated for 5 min and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce™, Cat #23225). 50 µg protein was loaded to each well of a 15-well 8% denaturing gel, and was transferred in Tris-Glycerol buffer with 20% Methanol at 90 V for 90 min. PageRuler Plus Prestained protein ladder was used for protein weight indication, expect in Fig. 2c where Spectra Multicolor High Range Protein Ladder was used.

Both Li-Cor Odyssey Western Blotting protocol and Bio-Rad General Western Blotting protocol were followed as adaptive to Odyssey® Imaging System (Li-Cor) and ChemiDoc XRS+ Imaging System (Bio-Rad), respectively. Briefly, as described in Li-Cor Odyssey Western Blotting protocol, PVDF membrane was blocked in PBS blocking buffer (Fisher Scientific, Li-Cor, Cat # NC1660556) for 1 h, and incubated in 1:1000 diluted primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Membrane was washed in 0.1% PBS-T and incubated with conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000, IRDye® 800CW Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), Li-Cor, Cat #926-32211 or IRDye® 680LT Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L), Li-Cor, Cat #925-68020) for 55 min at RT. After washing, the membrane was imaged by Li-Cor Odyssey® Imaging System.

As for a Bio-Rad General Western Blotting protocol, PVDF membrane was blocked in 5% BSA diluted in the washing buffer, 0.1% TBS-T, instead. In this method, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used (see Supplementary Data 3). Membrane was finally developed using Clarity™ Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, Cat #1705061) and imaged by ChemiDoc XRS+ Imaging System (Bio-Rad) or exposed to films which were developed through Konica SRX-101A Film Processor. Of note, phospho-RET (pRET) bands were not able to be visualized following the Odyssey WB protocol so all the pRET blots were generated from the second method.

To strip and re-probe the blots, 1X NewBlot Stripping buffer (Li-Cor, Cat #928-40032) was used standardly following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical parameters including the exact sample sizes (n), definition of replicates, and statistical significance are reported in the figures and corresponding figure legends. For in vitro experiments, data represent at least two independent biological replicates. Each technical replicate is represented by one dot in each figure. Western blot analyses were performed with at least two independent biological replicates, with similar results obtained. All p values were obtained through unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA as indicated in the corresponding figure legends with a 5% false discovery rate. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was employed to explore overall survival in in-vivo study. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Full, uncropped images of all western blots are provided in supplementary information (Fig. S8).
