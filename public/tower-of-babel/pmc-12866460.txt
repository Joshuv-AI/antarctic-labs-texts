Associations of sarcopenia with depression and suicidal ideation: the sex-specific mediating role of loneliness
Authors: Youjuan Zhang, Shuai Zhou, Yang Chao, Xinxin Cai, Min Yang, Boda Zhou, Xue Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global aging population amplifies sarcopenia’s burden, a syndrome linked to physical decline and comorbid mental health risks. Despite growing understanding of the associations between sarcopenia, depression, and suicidal ideation, the mediating role of loneliness and sex-specific patterns remains underexplored. This study investigates whether loneliness mediates the sarcopenia–negative mood relationship and examines sex differences in these pathways among Hong Kong older adults.MethodsWe analyzed data from 2126 adults aged ≥ 60 (69 ± 6.5 years, 57% women) in the 2024–2025 Panel study of Active Ageing and Society (PAAS). Sarcopenia was assessed using the SARC-F questionnaire (score > 4). Negative mood included depressive symptoms (CES-D-8 scale) and past-week suicidal ideation (binary item). Loneliness was measured via a 3-item UCLA-based scale (score 3–9). Linear regression (depression) and probit models (suicidal ideation) tested associations, adjusted for sociodemographic and health covariates. Entropy balancing and propensity score matching were used to address selection bias. KHB decomposition analysis quantified the total, direct and indirect effects of the loneliness mediation, stratified by sex.ResultsSarcopenia prevalence was 9.6%, disproportionately affecting women, socioeconomically disadvantaged adults, and patients with comorbidities. Adjusted models revealed sarcopenia was positively associated with depressive symptoms (B = 3.41, SE = 0.28) and a 17.6% greater probability of suicidal ideation (B = 0.83, SE = 0.11). Effects were stronger in women (depression: B = 3.48 vs. 3.11 in men). Loneliness mediated 23.73% of the sarcopenia–suicidal ideation association and 20.54% of the sarcopenia–depression association. Sex-dimorphic pathways emerged: loneliness mediated more of sarcopenia’s effect on suicidal ideation in men (27.98%) than women (22.48%), despite women’s stronger direct effects.ConclusionLoneliness significantly explains the sarcopenia–mental health relationship, with men exhibiting stronger indirect pathways via loneliness. This highlights the need for integrated interventions targeting sarcopenia (e.g., resistance training) and loneliness (e.g., group-based social activities), tailored to sex-specific mechanisms. Scaling such approaches is urgent for the aging society, with highly prevalent sarcopenia and mental health problems among older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06551-y.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Depression, Suicidal ideation, Loneliness, Mediation, Panel study of active ageing and society, Sex-specific role, Older adults

Introduction

With the world’s highest life expectancy, Hong Kong exemplifies the population ageing trend, where one-third of residents will be 65 years or older by 2040 [1]. This demographic shift amplifies the health burden of sarcopenia, a progressive geriatric syndrome characterized by widespread decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength [2]. Globally, sarcopenia affects 5–16% of community-dwelling adults aged 60 to 70 years, and the prevalence rises sharply to 11–55% among those aged 80 years or older [3–5]. Among adults aged 65 and above in Hong Kong, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 9.0% [6], representing an alarming trend that deserves a deeper understanding of psychosocial needs among older adults living with sarcopenia.

Sarcopenia places considerable strain on healthcare systems through heightened risks of physical disability, frailty, and mortality [7, 8]. Critically, comorbid mental health conditions exert an additive impact on adverse clinical outcomes such as increased risk of cardiovascular diseases and mortality [9, 10]. Depression represents the most prevalent mental health comorbidity of sarcopenia, with meta-analyses suggesting 57% higher odds of developing depression compared to those without sarcopenia [11]. Furthermore, physical frailty can lead to impairments in activities of daily living (ADLs) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), increasing feelings of fear and negativity toward ageing, which can adversely impact mental health and result in depressive symptoms [12–14]. More specifically, frailty can lead to difficulties in performing daily activities, such as deficits in everyday cognitive functioning in handling financial affairs, which may make older adults more susceptible to financial exploitation [15]. Research indicates that frail older women living in Greek communities perceive more financial exploitation than non-frail older women [16]. Additionally, older individuals who experience financial exploitation often report poorer physical health and worsening depressive symptoms, including suicidal thoughts, highlighting the clear association between daily living limitations and deteriorating mental health among frail older people [17]. Prospective data reveal substantially higher depression incidence in women with sarcopenia (14.55%) than in men (10.05%) [18]. Beyond depression, sarcopenia may directly exacerbate suicide risk [19], a critical concern given that the suicide rates among older adults are the highest of any age group [20, 21]. This risk critically manifests through suicidal ideation (ranging from transient suicidal thoughts to detailed planning), which serves as a significant predictor for suicidal attempts and deaths [22]. There emerged a pronounced sex-specific pattern: while suicidal ideation prevalence is typically 2 times higher in older women [20], older men with sarcopenia demonstrate 8.28 times higher adjusted odds ratios of suicidal ideation independent of depressive symptoms [23]. This sarcopenia-associated reversal of typical sex patterns aligns with broader suicide epidemiology, where older men aged 75 years and above experience five times higher suicide mortality than women [24]. These findings highlight sarcopenia as a novel sex-specific risk factor that may modify conventional understanding of late-life depression and suicidality, warranting further investigation despite the current limited evidence base.

Beyond established biological pathways (e.g., chronic inflammation and hormonal imbalances), behavioral (e.g., nutritional deficiencies and physical inactivity) and psychosocial (e.g., social disconnection) mediators may significantly influence the association between sarcopenia and mental health issues [25, 26]. These mechanisms exhibit sex-dimorphic expression: women demonstrate stronger inflammation-depression links [27], while men show greater sensitivity to functional decline-related mood disturbances [28]. This complexity necessitates further mechanistic investigation to inform policy and interventions more effectively.

Loneliness, defined as a distressing emotional state arising from a discrepancy between desired and actual social relationships, represents a significant yet under-recognized social determinant of health [29, 30]. Loneliness is prevalent across the whole lifespan around the world, and recent evidence from a meta-analysis showed a pooled prevalence of 26% among older adults, ranging from 13% for Oceania to 38% for North America [31]. Studies indicate that factors such as deficient social relations, lack of meaningful social roles, inability to participate actively in community life, low socioeconomic status, physical health problems, and poor marital status exacerbate loneliness [32]. In Hong Kong, even higher proportion, i.e. more than half of adults aged 60 years and older report loneliness [33, 34], with identified risk factors such as material deprivation, limited instrumental activities of daily living, and poor self-rated health [34, 35]. Theoretical frameworks posit that loneliness triggers adverse social, cognitive, and biological processes [36], elevating vulnerability to depression and suicidal ideation. In older adults, sarcopenia-induced physical limitations, such as reduced mobility and fatigue, may restrict social participation and activities, fostering loneliness [37]. This dynamic can amply sarcopenia’s detrimental effects on mental health, particularly given the potential role of loneliness in increasing risks of depression and suicidal ideation.

This study examines the association between sarcopenia and negative mood (i.e., depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation) in older adults as well as the mediating roles of loneliness in such a relationship, with particular attention to sex-specific pathways. Grounded in the Stress Process Model [38], which posits that health stressors trigger psychosocial sequelae influencing mental wellbeing, we propose:

H1: Sarcopenia is positively associated with greater depressive symptoms and a higher risk of suicidal ideation;

H2: Loneliness mediates the association between sarcopenia and both depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation.

Given evidence of sex-dimorphic pathways - where men exhibit stronger loneliness-mediated effects on suicidality and women show more direct biological links to depression - we further investigate whether: (1) the strength of H1 associations differs by sex; and (2) the mediating role of loneliness (H2) varies between older men and women (Figure 1).

Data and methods

Sample and participants

The study retrieved data from the second wave of the Panel Study of Active Ageing and Society (PAAS), a large-scale, city-wide, representative survey of older adults aged 50 and above in Hong Kong [39, 40]. Given the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, PAAS adopted the telephone survey mode facilitated by a computer-assisted interview system. A list of 26,000 landline and telephone numbers was randomly generated, following the numbering plan of the Office of the Communications Authority of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region Government, and then reached by trained interviewers. For each older household, the survey team selected one eligible older Chinese-speaking respondent aged 50 or above who was willing to participate in the survey using the ‘next birthday’ rule. To maximize sample representativeness, participants were recruited and stratified by age (50–64 and 65 or above), sex, and district, based on the 2021 Population Census of Hong Kong. In the baseline survey conducted during June 2022 and November 2022, 8,303 eligible respondents were approached and further screened (excluding those who refused or could not be contacted after five attempted calls), resulting in a total of 5007 successful interviews with data collected through structured questionnaires. The second wave was conducted starting in December 2024, gathering additional information on the risk of sarcopenia among older adults. This study used data from 3000 completed interviews in this latest wave.

In our study, we selected respondents aged 60 or older for two reasons. First, traditional Chinese culture tends to regard those aged 60 or above as older people. More importantly, the risk of sarcopenia increases with ageing-related transitions such as health decline. Although many studies define “older adults” as those aged 65 and above, we chose to include individuals aged 60 and above because physical and mental health, including cognitive function, begins to decline gradually during this period [41–43]. In particular, older adults begin to experience a decline in muscle mass around the age of 50, with the rate of loss accelerating after the age of 60 [44]. Additionally, approximately one-quarter of suicide incidents worldwide occur among individuals aged 60 and older [42]. Therefore, we adopt a culturally sensitive definition and include individuals aged 60 and older in our research. A total of 2126 respondents were included in the analysis. Missing information in key variables was handled by three approaches during and after the survey: (1) interviewer onsite checks; (2) supervision team re-checking after the interview and calling back; and (3) linking time-invariant information to baseline responses. The survey was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the Institutional Review Board at the first author’s affiliated university approved the survey procedures. More information about the PAAS study can be found elsewhere [39, 40, 45].

Measurements

Negative mood. This study assessed negative mood using depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation. The eight-item Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES-D-8) scale [46] was used to measure the negative mood due to its brevity and efficiency, making it highly suitable for extensive longitudinal surveys involving older adults, where respondent burden is a significant consideration. Even in its short form, it was a validated assessment tool for screening depressive symptoms in older populations, including older Chinese adults [47]. The eight items asked respondents about the frequency of experiencing six negative behavioral symptoms or feelings and two positive emotions (reverse coded) over the past week, rated on a Likert-type scale from 0 (rarely or none) to 3 (most or all the time). The sum of the eight items ranged from 0 to 24, with a larger score indicating more severe depression (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85). The Chinese version of CES-D-8 shows a reliable consistency with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.80 to 0.87 [47]. Using 9 as the cut-off value, the CES-D-8 can identify clinically significant depressive symptoms, with a Cohen’s κ of 0.79 [48]. Moreover, suicidal thoughts typically involve a set of negative attitudes towards life worthiness, consideration about death, and suicidal attempts [49]. Due to the influence of traditional cultural taboos, Chinese people often avoid talking about death directly, so using a gentler way to ask questions works better for the Chinese older adults as a measurement tool [50]. Following existing literature [51, 52], the survey focused on passive suicidal ideation by integrating thoughts about meaninglessness in life and desire for death into one question: “In the last week, have you thought that my life is meaningless and not worth living?”. The question was rated on a four-point scale: 1 = very rarely, 2 = sometimes, 3 = frequently, and 4 = most of the time. We recoded the item into two categories, with one indicating that such a thought (answers 2 to 4) had occurred over the past week and zero otherwise.

Loneliness. Three items adapted from the Chinese version of the UCLA loneliness scale [53, 54] were used to assess the frequency of feeling (1) lacking companionship, (2) isolated from others, and (3) left out. The response was recorded on a three-point scale, with the following options: 1 = hardly ever, 2 = sometimes, and 3 = often. This study calculated the total score of three loneliness items, ranging from 3 to 9. The UCLA-3 Chinese version is a concise and culturally adapted tool whose effectiveness has also been validated (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87) [54]. In our sample, the loneliness scale had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91.

Sarcopenia. This study employed the Chinese version of the SARC-F questionnaire [55–57] to identify older adults with sarcopenia. The SARC-F contains five components, including lifting, walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls. The extent of difficulty in performing the first four components was rated on a three-point Likert-type scale from 0 = none to 1 = some and 2 = very difficult/unable to do. Regarding falls, the number of falls was categorized into three groups: 0 = none, 1 = three falls or fewer, and 2 = four or more falls. The total score of the five components ranged from 0 to 10, with a score larger than 4 indicating the presence of sarcopenia. The SARC-F is simple to use and representative of muscle-related adverse outcomes, making it suitable for large-scale longitudinal studies [56, 58, 59]. The Chinese version of the SARC-F demonstrates excellent specificity [57] and acceptable reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.73 among the Chinese community-dwelling older adults [56]. In our study, it has shown an acceptable Cronbach’s alpha (0.78).

Covariates. This study included demographic, economic, and physical health conditions. Basic demographic characteristics included age, sex, place of birth, educational level, marital status (in marriage or not), and living arrangement (living alone versus living with others). The economic situation was indexed by housing type (public, subsidized, or private housing), employment status (working or not working), and personal monthly income (natural log transformed). We also included comorbidity status, which indicated whether the respondents had two or more chronic illnesses, including musculoskeletal disorders, diabetes, gastrointestinal disease, urologic disease, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hyperglycemia, cardiovascular diseases, stroke, mental health problems, and others (yes or no). Self-rated health was measured using a single question asking the respondents to rate their physical health status: “Overall, how do you think about your physical health status?” The response ranged from 1 (very poor) to 5 (very good).

Analytical strategy

Following descriptive statistics of the whole sample, group differences were tested by comparing older adults with and without sarcopenia. To examine the associations between sarcopenia and negative mood, this study employed probit models for suicidal ideation and linear models for depressive symptoms. Unadjusted and fully adjusted coefficients, together with standard errors (SE), were reported. Given concerns about selection bias due to sarcopenia-related risk exposure, we further identified the treatment effects of sarcopenia on negative mood using different matching approaches, including propensity score matching [60] and entropy balancing [61], which is a more flexible matching strategy that can effectively adjust for unbalanced covariate moments in observational studies.

To explore the mediating mechanisms, we examined whether sarcopenia might be associated with loneliness using a multiple linear model. Then, we introduced sarcopenia and loneliness simultaneously into the outcome models to examine whether the direct effects of sarcopenia on suicidal ideation and depressive symptoms were meaningful or not. Using the KHB mediation decomposition method [62], the mediating roles of loneliness in the total sample and its subgroups were investigated. The KHB analysis decomposes the total effect of sarcopenia on each negative mood indicator into direct and indirect effects in linear or nonlinear models. The proportion of indirect effect represents the extent to which the effect of sarcopenia on negative mood indicators can be explained by loneliness, ideally ranging from 0 to 100.

Results

Descriptive profiles

The descriptive characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the respondents was 69 years (SD = 6.5). The sample had 57% of older women and 73% of participants born in Hong Kong or Macau (considered as native residents). In the sample, 205 respondents were diagnosed with sarcopenia (9.6%). The table reveals that older, female, non-native, less educated, and non-partnered older adults had a higher proportion of participants with sarcopenia compared to their counterparts. Older adults with sarcopenia tended to live alone, reside in public housing, withdraw from the labor force, and have a lower personal income, indicating more vulnerable living and economic situations. Compared to sarcopenia-free participants (32.5%), approximately 63% of older people with sarcopenia had at least two chronic conditions. Older adults with sarcopenia rated their health status poorer than their counterparts.

Results of mediational decomposition analysis

To further investigate the mediating roles of loneliness, we performed a set of regression analyses to examine the relationships between sarcopenia, loneliness, and mental well-being (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7). First, sarcopenia was found to be positively associated with loneliness (B = 0.91, SE = 0.12). These results indicate that older adults with sarcopenia may develop a stronger feeling of loneliness, offering partial evidence of its mediating role. When introducing sarcopenia and loneliness into the outcome models simultaneously, it could be observed that sarcopenia was positively associated with suicidal ideation (B = 0.68, SE = 0.11, equal to a 13.4% increase in the probability of suicidal ideation) and depressive symptoms (B = 2.40, SE = 0.24) among older adults in the total sample. Meanwhile, loneliness was positively associated with suicidal ideation and depressive symptoms, as expected. The positive associations between sarcopenia, loneliness, and negative mood were observed in both sexes, although the absolute effect sizes of sarcopenia in the outcome models were noticeably reduced.

Discussion

This study examined the complex interplay between sarcopenia, loneliness, and mental health outcomes - specifically depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation - in a representative sample of community-dwelling older adults in Hong Kong. In our study, sarcopenia demonstrated a substantial and robust association with poorer mental health outcomes, especially among older women. Moreover, loneliness significantly mediates the association between sarcopenia and depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation; such a mediational role was slightly stronger among older men than older women.

Our study identified a sarcopenia prevalence rate of 9.6% among Hong Kong adults aged 60 years and above, which is slightly higher than earlier local community-based estimates (9.0%). Approximately 16% of our participants exhibited suicidal ideation, which was much higher than the 5% reported two decades ago in pre-COVID eras [63], yet consistent with the drastic post-pandemic elevations in psychological distress [64]. The mean values of depressive symptoms (CES-D-8: 5.5) and loneliness were substantially higher in older adults with sarcopenia, corroborating existing evidence linking sarcopenia to depression [11] and loneliness [37].

The sociodemographic profile of sarcopenic older adults in our sample reveals a concentration of vulnerability that aligns with the health inequity patterns. The overrepresentation of older age, women, non-native residents, less educated individuals, and those without partners reflects intersecting social determinants documented worldwide and locally in Hong Kong [65, 66]. Economic disadvantage was evident through higher rates of public housing residency, labor force withdrawal, and lower personal income, mirroring the “triple jeopardy” phenomenon (material deprivation, social isolation and loneliness, and health decline) among poorer aging adults in Hong Kong [35, 67]. The comorbidity disparity (62.9% with ≥ 2 chronic conditions vs. 32.5% in non-sarcopenic) suggested that sarcopenia signals advanced health deterioration in community-dwelling older adults [68]. These substantial differences identified in our study regarding physio-psycho-social wellbeing underscore the profound health burden associated with sarcopenia.

The significant mediating role of loneliness in the relationship between sarcopenia and negative mood - particularly depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation - is theoretically supported by the stress process model. Our decomposition analyses quantitatively confirm this pathway, demonstrating that loneliness mediates 20.54% of the association between sarcopenia and depressive symptoms and 23.73% of its association with suicidal ideation. These findings align with mechanistic evidence from biomarker studies, where sarcopenia and loneliness jointly elevate pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., C-reactive protein, CRP) [69, 70] and dysregulate hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity [71, 72], creating neurobiological conditions that exacerbate mood disorders [73]. Behavioral pathways may further reinforce this stress-response process. Sarcopenia-induced functional decline may limit engagement in instrumental activities (e.g., community events, family gatherings), thereby reducing opportunities for meaningful social interaction [74]. Longitudinal data indicate that each unit increase in functional disability corresponds to a 5% rise in loneliness among women, which was substantially higher in men (16%) [75]. Empirical evidence from Asian aging studies corroborates this finding; for example, a study using the Longitudinal Aging Study in India (2017–2018) found that social disengagement and loneliness mediated 22.0% and 3.08%, respectively, of the effect of functional health on depression [76].

More importantly, our study reveals sex-dimorphic mediation patterns. Although women exhibit stronger direct effects of sarcopenia on depression and suicidal ideation, loneliness mediates a larger proportion of the total effect in men - particularly for suicidal ideation (27.98% in men vs. 22.48% in women). This divergence may reflect a gendered pattern of socialization: older men often anchor their identity and self-worth in physical autonomy and productivity [77]. This can be linked to traditional masculine norms that emphasize strength, independence, and the ability to contribute materially to society [78]. Sarcopenia-induced dependency threatens masculine identity more acutely, amplifying shame-driven social withdrawal, potentially leading to negative mood [79]. Conversely, women’s stronger direct pathways may stem from biological factors (e.g., heightened inflammatory sensitivity and hormonal changes) [80] or socioeconomic vulnerabilities (e.g., widowhood-related resource depletion) [81, 82]. This pattern elucidates the “sarcopenia paradox” in suicidality: while women generally report higher suicidal ideation, men with sarcopenia face disproportionately elevated suicide risk due to isolation-driven despair [83].

Our findings suggest that policy initiatives should integrate sarcopenia prevention and management - such as promoting accessible resistance training and nutritional support - into broader aging and mental health strategies. Moreover, we underscore loneliness as a modifiable target with significant implications for interventions targeting mental health in sarcopenia patients. Combining resistance training (the most evidence-based approach to mitigate sarcopenia-related muscle decline) [84, 85] with structured social interaction (e.g., group-based exercise or community clubs), could disrupt the sarcopenia-loneliness-psychopathology pathway, particularly among older men. Empirical evidence supports that such integrated approaches can significantly reduce loneliness than physical health-based interventions [86, 87] and alleviate depressive symptoms [88]. Considering the rapidly aging population in Hong Kong - where more than half of older adults report loneliness - scaling up these interventions is a public health imperative. Future research should validate these mechanisms longitudinally and explore cultural moderators (e.g., filial piety expectations) that may shape mediation effects in collectivist societies. Exploring other potential mediators (e.g., self-efficacy, fear of falling, specific inflammatory biomarkers) and moderators (e.g., type of social support, cultural attitudes towards ageing) would provide a more comprehensive understanding. Notably, intervention studies are needed to test the efficacy of integrated programs combining physical rehabilitation to combat sarcopenia with psychosocial strategies to reduce loneliness, explicitly evaluating sex-specific approaches.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits definitive causal inferences regarding the directionality of the observed relationships. Second, while validated instruments were used, reliance on self-reported measures (SARC-F, CES-D-8) may introduce potential measurement error. This susceptibility could arise from reporting bias and cognitive impairment (e.g., dementia), which may compromise participants’ evaluation [89]. Future research would be strengthened by incorporating objective measures of muscle mass/strength, clinician-administered interviews, and collateral information from family members or caregivers. Third, despite adjusting for key confounders, unmeasured factors (e.g., specific nutritional deficiencies, genetic predispositions, detailed social network characteristics) may also influence the pathways between sarcopenia and mental wellbeing. Fourth, our focus on sarcopenia and loneliness limits the scope of factors considered, while depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation are multifactorial outcomes that are also strongly influenced by factors such as particular health conditions like cancer [89], frailty [90], and ADL and IADL limitations [91]; and social factors such as family relationship [92], and social support [93]. Finally, the context-specific nature of the Hong Kong sample may affect generalizability to other cultural or healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that sarcopenia is significantly associated with an increased risk of depression and suicidal ideation among older adults, with loneliness mediating a substantial portion (approximately 20–24%) of this relationship. The identification of distinct pathways - characterized by stronger direct associations in women versus a stronger mediating role of loneliness in men - provides critical nuance for understanding the psychological impact of muscle loss. Addressing both the physical decline of sarcopenia and the accompanying psychosocial burden, particularly loneliness, through integrated and potentially sex-tailored approaches, is essential for mitigating the mental health consequences of ageing populations worldwide.
