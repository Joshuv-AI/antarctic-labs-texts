Adaptive Resolution for Finite-Rank Gaussian Processes
Jaehoan Kim, Anirban Bhattacharya, Debdeep Pati

Abstract
Finite-rank approximations are widely used to scale Gaussian process (GP) regression, but their posterior behavior can differ from that of the corresponding parent GP prior. We study a class of finite-rank GP priors built from locally supported basis expansions with dependent Gaussian coefficients. Our framework covers finite-element approximations based on the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) representation of Matérn GPs and regular-grid GP interpolation schemes. We show that, with a suitable prior on the resolution parameter N N , these finite-rank expansions inherit the same posterior contraction rate as the corresponding parent GP prior under the same bandwidth specification used for that parent prior. Consequently, the interpolation construction under a squared-exponential parent GP attains the minimax-optimal rate up to logarithmic factors under a hierarchical prior on the bandwidth parameter and on N N , while the SPDE construction attains the same rate under a bandwidth scaling depending on the sample size and the smoothness of the true function, together with a prior on N N . We also develop a posterior sampler for the hierarchical interpolation model that jointly updates the resolution and bandwidth parameters, and we provide numerical studies that support the theory.



Keywords:
adaptive, finite element, Gaussian process; posterior contraction, stochastic partial differential equation, Toeplitz

1
Introduction

Gaussian process (GP) methods are a standard tool for Bayesian learning of function-valued parameters, but exact implementation requires
O(n^{2})
memory and
O(n^{3})
floating point operations. This quickly becomes infeasible even when
n
is only moderately large. As a result, scalable GP approximations have received substantial attention over the last few decades. Broadly speaking, these methods fall into three main categories: sparse approximations, low-rank approximations, and distributed computing methods. For methods that combine features of the first two categories, see
Sun et al. (2012)
;
Heaton et al. (2019)
for reviews.

Despite this broad methodological development, there is still limited theoretical understanding of when such approximations retain the statistical guarantees of the corresponding parent GP prior. Many popular sparse and low-rank methods can be viewed through the lens of Vecchia’s approximation
(
Vecchia, 1988
)
, which replaces the full Gaussian likelihood by a product of lower-dimensional conditional distributions. Other examples of sparse approximations include pseudo-likelihood methods
(
Varin et al., 2011
)
, spectral methods
(
Fuentes, 2007
)
, and covariance tapering
(
Furrer et al., 2006
)
. Representative low-rank constructions include process convolutions
(
Higdon, 2002
)
, fixed rank kriging
(
Cressie and Johannesson, 2008
)
, predictive processes
(
Banerjee et al., 2008
)
, lattice kriging
(
Nychka et al., 2015
)
, and the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) approach
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
)
. Distributed approaches aggregate local experts trained on subsets of the data; see, for example, product-of-experts
(
Deisenroth and Ng, 2015
;
Cao and Fleet, 2014
)
and hierarchical mixture-of-experts
(
Ng and Deisenroth, 2014
)
.

Recent theory has started to address this question for several scalable GP procedures.
Stein et al. (2004)
showed that the Vecchia likelihood is the best Kullback–Leibler approximation of the target Gaussian distribution under a sparsity constraint on the Cholesky factor of the precision matrix.
Kang et al. (2024)
established that Vecchia prediction can approximate exact GP prediction when the conditioning set size grows suitably. Posterior contraction theory has also been developed for Vecchia Gaussian processes and related directed-graph constructions
(
Szabó and Zhu, 2024
;
Zhu et al., 2024
)
. For distributed Bayesian procedures, posterior convergence and adaptation have been studied in
(
Szabó and van Zanten, 2020
;
Szabó and Van Zanten, 2019
;
Guhaniyogi et al., 2023
)
. For sparse variational approximations,
Nieman et al. (2022)
showed that variational posteriors can attain minimax-optimal contraction rates when the number of inducing variables is chosen appropriately; see also
Nieman and Szabó (2025)
for recent results on adaptive hyperparameter selection through variational Bayes.

A common feature of these results is that statistical optimality depends critically on hyperparameter choice. Examples include truncation levels, smoothness or range parameters in the covariance kernel, and approximation-specific tuning parameters such as the number of inducing variables or the size of local conditioning sets. For finite-rank approximations, a central approximation-specific parameter is the resolution
N
, which determines the dimension of the expansion and hence the support of the induced prior. This motivates studying finite-rank constructions in a way that keeps the role of
N
explicit.

In this article, we study a broad class of finite-rank GP approximations of the form
f_{N}(x)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}w_{j}\psi_{j}(x)
, where
\mathcal{J}_{N}
is an index set and
\{\psi_{j}:j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}
is a collection of basis functions on
[0,1]^{d}
. We endow the coefficient vector
w^{N}
with a dependent Gaussian prior of the form
w^{N}\mid N,\theta\sim N(0,\Sigma_{N,\theta})
, where
\Sigma_{N,\theta}
is a non-diagonal positive definite covariance matrix and
\theta
denotes the kernel hyperparameters. The covariance
\Sigma_{N,\theta}
is induced from the covariance kernel
K_{\theta}
of an underlying full Gaussian process
f\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,K_{\theta})
, which we call the
parent GP
. The way
\Sigma_{N,\theta}
is obtained from
K_{\theta}
depends on the construction. To complete the prior specification, we later place a prior on
N
,
while
\theta
is either taken to be a sample-size-dependent sequence inherited from the corresponding parent-GP theory or endowed with an independent hyperprior. This
defines the general class of finite-rank GP priors considered throughout the paper;
the formal specification is given in Section
2
.

A prominent example of this framework is process convolution
(
Higdon, 2002
)
, where
\psi_{j}
are kernel basis functions and the coefficients
w_{j}
are independent Gaussian. With locally supported bases and independent coefficients, however, the global smoothness of
f_{N}
is limited by the fixed regularity of the basis functions. As a result, sufficiently smooth targets may not be well approximated
(
de Jonge and van Zanten, 2012
)
. A natural alternative is to endow the coefficient vector
w^{N}
with a dependent Gaussian prior. Then
f_{N}
can inherit the global regularity of its parent GP while retaining the computational advantages induced by local support.

We study two representative constructions within the form of (
2
)–(
3
). The first is the finite-element SPDE approximation
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
)
, where the coefficient vector is Gaussian with sparse precision structure induced by the SPDE representation. Related finite-element representations of Gaussian processes have also been studied from the perspective of balancing numerical and statistical accuracy
(
Sanz-Alonso and Yang, 2022
)
. The second is a lattice-based GP interpolation approach used in computer emulation
(
Maatouk and Bay, 2017
;
Zhou et al., 2019
;
Zhou et al., 2024
;
Maatouk et al., 2025
)
, where the coefficients on a regular grid are assigned a Gaussian prior induced from a parent GP, typically together with a bandwidth parameter.

A key point is that the role of
N
is not merely computational. Conditional on fixed
(N,\theta)
, the prior is supported on
\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{j}:j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}
. Hence for small
\epsilon>0
, the event
\{\|f_{N}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon\}
can have negligible, or even zero, conditional prior probability unless
N
is sufficiently large. In general, the required size of
N
depends on additional hyperparameters through
\theta
. Thus, even when the parent GP prior is statistically well understood, a finite-rank approximation need not inherit that behavior unless the prior on
N
places enough mass on sufficiently large resolutions.

Our main result is that a suitable prior on the resolution
N
allows the finite-rank approximation to inherit the posterior contraction behavior of the corresponding parent GP prior. We call this an
adaptive-resolution result
: the approximation resolution is learned through the prior on
N
, while the bandwidth or range parameter is treated according to the parent-GP theory used for the corresponding construction. For the interpolation construction with a squared-exponential parent GP, we place hierarchical priors on both the bandwidth parameter and
N
, yielding the minimax-optimal posterior contraction rate up to logarithmic factors. For the SPDE construction, we place a prior on
N
and use the bandwidth scaling inherited from the parent Matérn contraction theory. Thus the common contribution across the two constructions is adaptation over the finite-rank resolution, showing that the finite-dimensional approximation can preserve the parent-GP contraction rate when sufficiently large resolutions receive enough prior mass.

The SPDE result also connects two related strands of literature. On the one hand,
Sanz-Alonso and Yang (2022)
study finite-element GP representations from the perspective of numerical and statistical approximation error at fixed resolution. On the other hand,
Fang and Bhadra (2025)
establish posterior contraction results for rescaled and hierarchical Matérn GP priors. Our SPDE theorem links these viewpoints by showing that, under the bandwidth scaling used in the parent Matérn theory, a suitable prior on the finite-element resolution preserves the parent contraction rate for the finite-rank approximation.

While the adaptive-resolution hierarchy is theoretically attractive, it, however, introduces a computational challenge because the coefficient vector
w^{N}
changes dimension with
N
. We address this by integrating out
w^{N}
when updating
N
and the kernel hyperparameters, and then sampling
w^{N}
conditionally from its Gaussian posterior. This avoids dimension-changing reversible-jump updates
(
Green, 1995
)
. The resulting computations exploit the local support of the basis functions through sparse design matrices. Thus, the data-dependent part of each iteration scales linearly in sample size
n
, and the remaining cost is governed by the coefficient dimension
|\mathcal{J}_{N}|
. This involves
O(|\mathcal{J}_{N}|^{3})
calculation for the GP interpolation method, and is further reduced for the SPDE method due to sparsity in the posterior precision matrix.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the two approximation frameworks based on the SPDE formulation and lattice-based interpolation. Section
3
outlines the reproducing kernel Hilbert space structure associated with (
2
). Posterior contraction theory for the SPDE-based approximation and the interpolation-based approximation is developed in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
, respectively. Section
4
discusses computational strategies, followed by numerical experiments in Section
5
. We conclude with a discussion in Section
6
. Proofs of theoretical results are provided in a supplemental document.

2
Finite rank approximation of Gaussian processes

We focus on the nonparametric regression model with Gaussian errors

\displaystyle y_{i}=f(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\quad\epsilon_{i}\overset{ind.}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^{2}),

(1)

with the observed data
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
. Throughout the article, for simplicity of exposition, we assume
\sigma
to be known and the design points
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to be fixed. The infinite-dimensional parameter of interest is
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Throughout, parent Gaussian process priors are written as
f\sim\mbox{GP}(0,K)
, where
K
denotes the covariance kernel. We represent the regression function
f
in (
1
) by a finite basis expansion form

f_{N}(x)=\sum_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}w_{\bf j}\psi_{\bf j}(x),

(2)

where
\{\psi_{\bf j}:{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}
is a collection of basis functions on
[0,1]^{d}
with local support and
\mathcal{J}_{N}:=\{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}):j_{k}\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}\}
.
We endow the coefficient vector
w^{N}=(w_{\bf j})_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
with a dependent Gaussian prior of the form

w^{N}\mid N,\theta\sim N(0,\Sigma_{N,\theta}),

(3)

where
\Sigma_{N,\theta}
is a non-diagonal positive definite covariance matrix induced from a parent GP, and
\theta
denotes the hyperparameters involved in the covariance kernel of the parent GP. In both constructions studied below,
\theta
is an inverse bandwidth parameter
\kappa
. The treatment of
\kappa
differs across the two methods: in the SPDE approach,
\kappa=\kappa_{n}
with
\kappa_{n}
depending on the sample size and smoothness of the true function, inherited from the parent Matérn GP theory, while in the GP interpolation approach,
\kappa
is assigned a hyperprior. In both cases, a prior on
N
is placed for the adaptive resolution. We refer to a prior of the form (
2
)–(
3
) with locally supported
\{\psi_{\bf j}\}
and non-diagonal
\Sigma_{N,\theta}
as a
finite rank GP with dependent coefficients and locally supported bases
(
d-frGPloc
).

2.1
Finite element approximation to GP as a solution to SPDE

As our first candidate for d-frGPloc, we consider the SPDE-based approach of
Lindgren et al. (2011)
. Recall that the stationary Matérn covariance kernel with smoothness parameter
\nu
, marginal variance
\tau^{2}
, and inverse length-scale parameter
\kappa
is

\displaystyle K(x_{1},x_{2})=\tau^{2}\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\,(\kappa\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2})^{\nu}\,K_{\nu}(\kappa\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}),

(4)

where
K_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind. A well-known result from
Whittle (1963)
is that on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a Matérn GP can be characterized as the stationary solution to an SPDE of the form

\displaystyle(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\beta/2}f=\kappa^{\beta-d/2}W,

(5)

where
\beta=\nu+d/2
,
W(\cdot)
is Gaussian white noise, and
\Delta
denotes the Laplacian operator
\Delta(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}
. The marginal variance parameter
\tau^{2}
in (
4
) acts only as a scaling factor and will be suppressed in the SPDE representation below.

Starting from this result, the SPDE-based finite-rank prior is constructed as follows. We first use the SPDE representation to define the parent GP, and then approximate its weak solution in a finite-dimensional space spanned by locally supported finite-element basis functions. The resulting approximation has the form of the general expansion in equation (
2
), with a sparse precision matrix for the coefficient vector. See
Lindgren et al. (2011)
;
Sanz-Alonso and Yang (2022)
for details on the finite-element SPDE construction.

In this article, we take
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the bounded domain
D=[0,1]^{d}
. For an SPDE constructed on a bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the boundary condition is required for its solution to be well-defined. In this article, we impose homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, as in Section 2.3 of
Lindgren et al. (2011)
and Section 3 of
Sanz-Alonso and Yang (2022)
.
Other boundary conditions may also be considered, but they lead to slightly different finite-element solutions and are not pursued here. Under this choice, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}=\kappa^{2}\,\mbox{id}-\Delta
is self-adjoint and positive on
L^{2}(D)
, and
(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\beta/2}
is understood via spectral powers of
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}
. When
\beta
is even, this coincides with
\beta/2
iterated applications of
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}
.

For the given SPDE, a random function
g
is called a generalized solution if it satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\phi,(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\beta/2}g\rangle\overset{d}{=}\langle\phi,\kappa^{\beta-d/2}W\rangle

(6)

for every smooth test function
\phi
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denoting inner product on
D
. Following the Galerkin construction in section 2.3 of
Lindgren et al. (2011)
, we approximate the solution by considering a finite collection of test functions
\{\phi_{\bf j}\}_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
:

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{\bf j},(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\beta/2}g\rangle\overset{d}{=}\langle\phi_{\bf j},\kappa^{\beta-d/2}W\rangle,\quad{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}.

(7)

If one sets
\phi_{\bf j}=\psi_{\bf j}
for
\beta\geq 2
and
\phi_{\bf j}=(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{1/2}\psi_{\bf j}
for
\beta=1
, then the resulting Galerkin solution takes the form (
2
).
We then define the consistent finite-element mass and stiffness matrices by

C^{(c)}_{{\bf i},{\bf j}}=\langle\psi_{{\bf i}},\psi_{{\bf j}}\rangle,\qquad G^{(c)}_{{\bf i},{\bf j}}=\langle\nabla\psi_{{\bf i}},\nabla\psi_{{\bf j}}\rangle,\qquad{\bf i},{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}.

(8)

Let
C=\mathrm{diag}(C^{(c)}\mathrm{1})
be the lumped mass matrix, where
\mathrm{1}
is a vector of ones of appropriate dimension. We write
G
for the corresponding mass-lumped stiffness matrix. We define

L_{N}=\kappa^{2}I+C^{-1}G.

Here,
L_{N}
is a mass-lumped finite-element coefficient-coordinate discretization of
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}=\kappa^{2}\mathrm{id}-\Delta
. The precise transition from the consistent matrices to the mass-lumped tensor-product matrices is given in Appendix
B.1
.

From the local support of the finite-element basis, the resulting
L_{N}
becomes a sparse matrix. Note that the sparsity is obtained by replacing
C^{(c)}
with the lumped matrix
C
, since although
C^{(c)}
is a sparse matrix,
\big(C^{(c)}\big)^{-1}
may be a dense matrix.

For
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
, define the scale-corrected precision matrix

Q=\kappa^{-(2\beta-d)}C\,L_{N}^{\beta}.

(9)

Then the approximate finite-rank solution of the SPDE in equation (
5
) is written in the form of equation (
2
), with the prior in equation (
3
) where
\theta=\kappa
and

\Sigma_{N,\theta}=Q_{N,\theta}^{-1},

with
Q_{N,\theta}=Q
.
We henceforth refer to inference based on this prior as the
SPDE method
. The local support of the finite-element basis makes
L_{N}
and
Q
sparse for integer
\beta
. The sparsity in
Q
is the main computational advantage of the SPDE construction.

As an illustration, consider
D=[0,1]
and the piecewise linear hat basis
\{\psi_{j}\}
,

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(x)=\Big(1-N\Big|x-\frac{j}{N}\Big|\Big)^{+},\quad j=0,\ldots,N,

(10)

where
x^{+}:=\max(x,0)
. The lumped mass matrix
C
and stiffness matrix
G
then assume the form

\displaystyle C=\frac{1}{2N}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&2&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&2&0\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad G=N\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
-1&2&-1&\cdots&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&-1&2&-1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&-1&1\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

The bandwidth of
Q
is determined by
\beta
.

2.2
Interpolating GP on a regular grid

Our second construction starts from a parent GP prior and approximates it by interpolation on a regular grid
Maatouk and Bay (2017)
;
Zhou et al. (2019)
. In contrast to the SPDE route, which is driven by a weak-form discretization of a differential operator, the interpolation route is driven directly by the finite-dimensional distributions of the parent GP. This leads to structured covariance matrices on the grid and is particularly convenient for hierarchical modeling of the bandwidth parameter and the resolution
N
.

We first describe the construction in one dimension. Here we specifically focus on the nodal hat basis
\{\psi_{j}\}
in (
10
), which satisfies
\psi_{j}(k/N)=\mathrm{1}_{\{j=k\}}
. With this basis, the representation

f_{N}^{\star}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}f^{\star}(j/N)\,\psi_{j}(x)

linearly interpolates a function
f^{\star}
between the grid values
\{0,1/N,\ldots,1\}
.
A natural finite-rank GP prior on (
2
) is then obtained by taking a parent GP prior
f\sim\mbox{GP}(0,K)
and setting

w^{N}=\big(f(0),f(1/N),\ldots,f(1)\big)^{\top},

so that
w^{N}\sim N(0,G_{N,\theta})
with
(G_{N,\theta})_{ij}=K(i/N,j/N)
. This gives another instance of the prior in equation (
3
), with
\theta
denoting the hyperparameters of the parent GP covariance kernel and
\Sigma_{N,\theta}=G_{N,\theta}
. We refer to inference based on this prior as the
GPI (GP interpolation) method
.

If
K
is stationary with
K(x,y)=k(x-y)
, then
G_{N,\theta}
has a Toeplitz structure:

\displaystyle G_{N,\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}k(0)&k\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)&\cdots&k\left(\frac{N}{N}\right)\\
k\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)&k(0)&\cdots&k\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
k\left(\frac{N}{N}\right)&k\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)&\cdots&k(0)\end{bmatrix}.

(12)

For
d\geq 2
, one can extend the above construction using a tensor-product hat basis on a regular grid. Define the grid points

\Big\{\frac{{\bf j}}{N}:=\Big(\frac{j_{1}}{N},\ldots,\frac{j_{d}}{N}\Big):{\bf j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d})\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\Big\}\subset[0,1]^{d}.

For
f^{\star}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, define

f_{N}^{\star}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\sum_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}w_{{\bf j}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{d}\psi_{j_{k}}(x_{k}),

(13)

with
w_{{\bf j}}=f^{\star}({\bf j}/N)
, so that
f_{N}^{\star}
interpolates
f^{\star}
at the grid points. As before, one can induce a prior on (
2
) by taking
w_{{\bf j}}=f({\bf j}/N)
for a parent GP
f\sim\mbox{GP}(0,K)
, yielding a multivariate normal prior on
w^{N}=(w_{\bf j})_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
. If
K
is stationary, then
G_{N,\theta}
again inherits additional structure; for example, for
d=2
,
G_{N,\theta}
is a block Toeplitz matrix with Toeplitz blocks (BTTB); see, for example,
Akaike (1973)
.

3
Theoretical properties of d-frGPloc

As discussed in Section
2
, for fixed
N
, d-frGPloc defines a finite-rank GP prior on
f_{N}
.
This section studies two aspects of that prior. First, we describe its reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), which determines support and the approximation term appearing in posterior contraction arguments.
Second, we study posterior contraction after placing a prior on the resolution parameter
N
.
While most of the literature deals with fixed resolution
N
, the common new hierarchy introduced in this paper is the prior on the resolution parameter
N
, which yields adaptive-resolution results for both finite-rank Gaussian process approximations. The treatment of the other hyperparameters follows the corresponding parent-GP theory.

3.1
RKHS of a finite-rank GP

We begin with the RKHS of finite-rank GP prior since it identifies the support and the RKHS norm is directly used in the concentration function defined later. Since the models in Section
2
yields Gaussian distribution on the coefficients conditioned on
(N,\theta)
, the RKHS can be written explicitly in terms of the coefficient covariance.

The reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) determines the support and the geometry of a GP
(
van der Vaart and van Zanten, 2008b
)
. For a mean-zero GP
f\sim\mathrm{GP}(0,K)
, denote by
\mathbb{H}_{0}
the set of functions

\displaystyle t\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}K(s_{i},t),\qquad s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\in[0,1]^{d},\ \alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R},\ k\in\mathbb{N}.

On
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, define an inner product by

\displaystyle\Big\langle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}K(s_{i},\cdot),\sum_{j=1}^{l}\beta_{j}K(r_{j},\cdot)\Big\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}K(s_{i},r_{j}),

and let
\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\langle h,h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
for
h\in\mathbb{H}_{0}
. The RKHS of
f
, denoted by
\mathbb{H}
, is defined as the closure of
\mathbb{H}_{0}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}}
. The closure of
\mathbb{H}
in the ambient Banach space
\mathbb{B}
coincides with the support of the GP
(
van der Vaart and van Zanten, 2008b
)
. Additional examples of RKHSs associated with different choices of the covariance kernel
K
can be found in
Van Der Vaart and Van Zanten (2011)
.

Recall that d-frGPloc is defined through the basis expansion (
2
), where coefficient vector
w^{N}=(w_{\bf j})_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
follows a Gaussian distribution
w^{N}\sim N(0,\Sigma_{N,\theta})
conditioned on
(N,\theta)
. The following lemma describes the RKHS of
f_{N}
for fixed
(N,\theta)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f_{N}=\sum_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}w_{\bf j}\psi_{\bf j}
be the Gaussian process defined on
[0,1]^{d}
with linearly independent basis functions
\{\psi_{{\bf j}}:{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}
and
w^{N}\sim N(0,\Sigma_{N,\theta})
, where
\Sigma_{N,\theta}
is a positive definite matrix indexed by
\mathcal{J}_{N}
. Then the RKHS of
f_{N}
is
\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{\bf j}:{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}
. Moreover, for

g=\sum_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}v_{\bf j}\psi_{\bf j}\in\mathbb{H}
with
v^{N}=(v_{\bf j})_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
,

\|g\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=(v^{N})^{\top}\Sigma_{N,\theta}^{-1}v^{N}.

Lemma
3.1
shows that for fixed
N
, the support of
f_{N}
is the linear span of
\{\psi_{j}\}
, and the RKHS norm is determined by the coefficient precision matrix
\Sigma_{N,\theta}^{-1}
. In particular, approximation of a globally smooth function depends on both the size of
N
and the dependence structure in
w^{N}
.

3.2
Posterior contraction rates

We now introduce the posterior contraction framework common to the two constructions. The key new ingredient relative to the parent GP theory is the prior on
N
, and the main focus is how to connect the prior on the coefficient vector
w^{N}
with the behavior of the corresponding infinite-dimensional parent GP.

Let
P_{2}
denote the prior on
N
and
\Pi(\cdot\mid N,\theta)
denote the conditional finite-rank GP prior on
f_{N}
given
N
and
\theta
. When the hyperparameter vector
\theta
is also assigned a prior (independent of
N
), let
P_{1}
denote its probability measure and
p_{1}
denote its density. With a prior on
\theta
, the marginal prior on
f_{N}
is

\Pi(\cdot)=\int\Pi(\cdot\mid N,\theta)dP_{1}(\theta)dP_{2}(N),

and let
\Pi(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
denote its posterior distribution under (
1
). When
\theta=\theta_{n}
is treated as a deterministic sequence depending on
n
and the smoothness of the true function,
P_{1}\equiv\delta_{\theta_{n}}
is a Dirac measure at
\theta_{n}
.

We define
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
to be a posterior contraction rate of
\Pi(\cdot\mid\mathcal{D}_{n})
with respect to a metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)
if there exists
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f^{*}}\Pi\!\left(f:d(f,f^{*})>M\epsilon_{n}\mid\mathcal{D}_{n}\right)\to 0.

(14)

A commonly used metric is the empirical
L_{2}
norm
\|h\|_{n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x_{i})^{2}

(
van der Vaart and van Zanten, 2008a
)
. For regular fixed designs, the minimax rate over
C^{\alpha}[0,1]^{d}
in this metric is
n^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+d)}

(
Tsybakov, 2008
)
.

The concentration function is a useful tool to study posterior concentration in standard statistical models with Gaussian process priors
(
van der Vaart and van Zanten, 2008b
)
. Since our model is Gaussian conditional on
(N,\theta)
, we consider the conditional concentration function

\displaystyle\phi_{f^{*}}^{(N,\theta)}(\epsilon)=\inf_{h\in\mathbb{H}_{N,\theta}:\ \|h-f^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon}\frac{1}{2}\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}_{N,\theta}}^{2}\;-\;\log\Pi\!\left(\|f_{N}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon\mid N,\theta\right),

(15)

where
\mathbb{H}_{N,\theta}
is the RKHS of
f_{N}
under
\Pi(\cdot\mid N,\theta)
. This quantity controls the conditional prior mass around
f^{*}
up to constants
(
van der Vaart and van Zanten, 2008b
)
. Under the marginal prior,

\Pi\big(\|f-f^{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon\big)=\int\Pi\big(\|f_{N}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon\mid N,\theta\big)\,p_{1}(\theta)\,d\theta\,dP_{2}(N).

The required resolution
N
to make the prior mass
\Pi\big(\|f_{N}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon\mid N,\theta\big)
positive depends on the Hölder smoothness of
f^{*}
. For
\alpha>0
, let

\underline{\alpha}
denote the largest integer strictly smaller than
\alpha
, and
for a multi-index
{\bf k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
write
|{\bf k}|=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
for
its order and
D^{\bf k}=\partial^{|{\bf k}|}/(\partial x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{k_{d}})
for the
corresponding mixed partial derivative. The Hölder space
C^{\alpha}[0,1]^{d}
is the
set of functions
f:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
whose derivatives
D^{\bf k}f
exist for all

|{\bf k}|\leq\underline{\alpha}
and for which

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}}:=\max_{{\bf k}:\,|{\bf k}|\leq\underline{\alpha}}\,\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}|D^{\bf k}f(x)|+\max_{{\bf k}:\,|{\bf k}|=\underline{\alpha}}\,\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|D^{\bf k}f(x)-D^{\bf k}f(y)|}{\|x-y\|_{2}^{\,\alpha-\underline{\alpha}}}<\infty.

(16)

The following lemma gives a resolution level, as a function of
\alpha
, that
suffices to approximate
f^{*}
within supremum-norm error
\epsilon
using the
tensor-product hat basis defined in Section
2.2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
is the tensor-product hat basis on the regular grid
\{j/N:j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}\subset[0,1]^{d}
. For all
f^{*}\in C^{\alpha}[0,1]^{d}
, there exists
N\asymp\epsilon^{-1/(\alpha\land 2)}
such that

\Big\{f_{N}\in\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{j}:j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}:\ \|f_{N}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon\Big\}\neq\emptyset.

Lemma
3.2
implies that for the approximation at accuracy
\epsilon
, the resolution
N
to be sufficiently large, with the order depending on the smoothness of
f^{*}
. This motivates assigning a prior on
N
to achieve adaptivity.

3.3
Posterior contraction rate of the SPDE approach

We first consider the posterior contraction rate result of the SPDE method. The main theorem in this subsection implies that with a prior placed on
N
, the finite-rank SPDE prior inherits the same contraction rate as the parent Matérn GP, under the same sample-size- and smoothness-dependent bandwidth scaling.

The main technical difficulty is to connect the parent GP under a Neumann boundary condition with the finite rank SPDE prior. The property of the parent GP is analyzed based on spectral decomposition and is compared with the Gaussian prior on the coefficient vectors as suggested in
Sanz-Alonso and Yang (2022)
, while the parent-GP contraction result is taken from
Fang and Bhadra (2025)
.

As mentioned in Section
2
,
f_{N}
derived from the SPDE method is defined on the bounded domain
D=[0,1]^{d}
with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. The operator
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}=\kappa^{2}\mbox{id}-\Delta
admits an orthonormal eigenbasis indexed by
{\bf k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,

e_{{\bf k}}(x)=\prod_{m=1}^{d}e_{k_{m}}(x_{m}),\qquad e_{0}(t)=1,\quad e_{\ell}(t)=\sqrt{2}\cos(\ell\pi t)\ (\ell\geq 1),

and

\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}e_{{\bf k}}=\{\kappa^{2}+\pi^{2}\|{\bf k}\|_{2}^{2}\}e_{{\bf k}}.

We use this representation to define a Besov-type smoothness class adapted to Neumann boundary conditions, following Section 3 of
Sanz-Alonso and Yang (2022)
. This class measures smoothness through how quickly the low-frequency components approximate the function in supremum norm. Let
S
be an even function on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying

0\leq S\leq 1,\quad S(x)=1\ \text{for }x\in[-0.5,0.5],\quad S(x)=0\ \text{for }|x|>1,

and define
S_{j}(x)=S(2^{-j}x)
. For
f=\sum_{{\bf k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}f_{{\bf k}}e_{{\bf k}}
, define

\tilde{S}_{j}f=\sum_{{\bf k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}S_{j}(\pi\|{\bf k}\|_{2})\,f_{{\bf k}}e_{{\bf k}}

and

B^{\alpha}_{\infty,\infty}=\Big\{f=\sum_{{\bf k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}f_{{\bf k}}e_{{\bf k}}:\ \|f\|_{B^{\alpha}_{\infty,\infty}}:=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}2^{\alpha j}\|\tilde{S}_{j}f-f\|_{\infty}<\infty\Big\}.

This is a Neumann-adapted Besov-type function class with smoothness
\alpha
.

Theorem 3.4 of
Fang and Bhadra (2025)
shows that for the parent Matérn GP with covariance kernel (
4
), if the true function has Hölder and Sobolev smoothness
\alpha
and

\kappa_{n}=n^{\frac{\beta-d/2-\alpha}{(2\alpha+d)(\beta-d/2)}},

(17)

then under
\alpha<\beta-d/2
the posterior contracts at the minimax rate
n^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+d)}
. The theorem below shows that, under the same bandwidth scaling, adding a prior on the resolution
N
preserves this rate for the finite-rank SPDE prior. Thus the SPDE result is adaptive in
N
, with the bandwidth parameter inherited from the parent Matérn theory.

Theorem 3.3
.

(Posterior contraction for the SPDE method)

Let
0<\alpha<\beta-d/2
, let
f^{*}\in B^{\alpha}_{\infty,\infty}
, and let
f_{N}
be a d-frGPloc defined in §
2.1
. Suppose
\kappa_{n}
satisfies (
17
) and the prior on
N
satisfies

P_{2}(N\geq x)\geq K_{1}\exp\!\big(-K_{2}(\log x)^{K_{3}}\big),\quad\forall x>x_{0},

(18)

for some constants
x_{0},K_{1},K_{2},K_{3}>0
. Then (
14
) holds with
\epsilon_{n}=n^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+d)}(\log n)^{C}
for some
C>0
.

The tail condition on
P_{2}
is mild; for example, any polynomial prior
P_{2}(N=m)\propto m^{-a}
with
a>1
, or log-normal prior on
N
satisfies (
18
).

The proof is based on a spectral comparison between the continuous Neumann operator
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}
and its mass-lumped finite-element discretization. Evaluating the Neumann eigenfunctions
e_{{\bf k}}
at the grid points yields vectors that are generalized eigenvectors of the discrete precision matrix
Q
with respect to the lumped mass matrix
C
; the resulting discrete eigenvalues are comparable to the continuous ones up to a factor depending only on
\beta
(Lemma
B.5
in the Supplementary Material). This equivalence implies that the RKHS norm and centered small-ball probability of the finite-rank prior match those of the parent Matérn GP up to constants for large enough
N
. From this, the three conditions of the posterior contraction theorem of
van der Vaart and van Zanten (2008a)
(prior concentration, sieve probability, and metric entropy) are verified at the same rate (up to logarithmic factors) as for the parent GP. The lower tail condition (
18
) ensures that such resolutions receive enough prior mass. Additionally, a single sieve can be utilized to cover all sufficiently large
N
, leading to the same order of metric entropy up to logarithmic factor. The finite-element setup follows the bounded-domain Neumann framework of
Sanz-Alonso and Yang (2022)
, while the parent Matérn benchmark is adapted from
Fang and Bhadra (2025)
.

3.4
Posterior contraction rate of the GP interpolation approach

We next turn to the interpolation construction.
Unlike the SPDE result above, the GPI result assigns priors to both the bandwidth parameter
\kappa
and the resolution parameter
N
. Thus the interpolation construction retains the adaptive bandwidth behavior of the parent squared-exponential GP while also learning the finite-rank resolution.
The argument combines the parent squared-exponential GP concentration result with an interpolation error bound showing that, once
N
is large enough relative to
\kappa
, the finite-rank approximation is accurate at the relevant scale.

Motivated by
van der Vaart and van Zanten (2009)
, we consider a squared-exponential covariance kernel with inverse-bandwidth parameter
\kappa
,

\displaystyle K(x_{1},x_{2})=\exp\big(-\kappa^{2}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}^{2}\big).

(19)

van der Vaart and van Zanten (2009)
showed that when the original GP is used as a prior for
f^{*}
, the minimax optimal posterior contraction rate can be achieved by placing a gamma-type prior on
\kappa
.

We show that adding a prior on
N
to the interpolation construction preserves this parent-GP adaptive bandwidth behavior, while allowing the adaptive finite-rank resolutions.

Theorem 3.4
.

(Adaptive posterior contraction of GPI)
Let
f^{*}\in C^{\alpha}[0,1]^{d}
with
\alpha>0
and
f_{N}
be a d-frGPloc defined in §
2.2
with parent GP covariance (
19
), and assume independent priors on
\kappa
and
N
satisfying

\displaystyle C_{1}\kappa^{p}\exp\!\big(-D_{1}\kappa^{d}(\log\kappa)^{q}\big)

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}(\kappa)\leq C_{2}\kappa^{p}\exp\!\big(-D_{2}\kappa^{d}(\log\kappa)^{q}\big),

(20)

\displaystyle P_{2}(N\geq x)

\displaystyle\geq K_{1}\exp\!\big(-K_{2}(\log x)^{K_{3}}\big),\quad\forall x>x_{0}

(21)

for some
C_{1},C_{2},D_{1},D_{2},p,q,K_{1},K_{2},K_{3},x_{0}>0
.
Then
\epsilon_{n}=n^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+d)}(\log n)^{C}
for some
C>0
satisfies (
14
).

The condition on
p_{1}
is the gamma-type inverse-bandwidth prior used in
van der Vaart and van Zanten (2009)
; the constants control the polynomial prefactor and logarithmic correction and affect only the logarithmic factor in the contraction rate.

The proof of Theorem
3.4
makes use of the optimal prior concentration of a GP with the squared-exponential kernel
(
van der Vaart and van Zanten, 2009
)
. We select
N
large enough, depending on
\kappa
, so that the interpolation error
f_{N}-f
is negligible at the scale relevant for prior concentration, where
f
denotes the parent GP. Combining this with (
21
) yields the desired prior concentration, and a single sieve can be utilized to cover all sufficiently large
N
, leading to the same order of metric entropy up to logarithmic factor. See Appendix
C
of the Supplementary Material.

4
Posterior computation for the adaptive resolution

In practice, the adaptive-resolution formulation is computationally inconvenient because the dimension of the coefficient vector
w^{N}
changes with
N
.
We introduce an efficient computational scheme below, where we avoid reversible-jump updates by first sampling
N
and the kernel hyperparameters from the
marginal posterior
obtained after integrating out
w^{N}
, and then sampling
w^{N}
from its Gaussian conditional posterior.
This method gives an additional benefit for the SPDE construction, because the marginal posterior of
N
and
\theta
can be evaluated efficiently using the sparse precision matrix of
w^{N}
conditional on
N
and
\theta
.

Recall from Section
2.2
the finite-rank representation

f_{N}(x)=\sum_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}w_{\bf j}\psi_{\bf j}(x),\qquad\mathcal{J}_{N}=\{{\bf j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}):j_{k}\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}\},

(22)

and let
m_{N}=|\mathcal{J}_{N}|=(N+1)^{d}
.
For both GPI and SPDE methods, a prior distribution on the coefficient vector
w^{N}=(w_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
given the resolution
N
and the hyperparameter
\theta
can be written as

w^{N}\mid N,\theta\sim N(0,\Sigma_{N,\theta}),

For GPI,
\theta=\kappa
and
\Sigma_{N,\theta}=G_{N,\theta}
, where
G_{N,\kappa}
is the parent-GP covariance matrix on the grid. For SPDE,
\theta=\kappa
and
\Sigma_{N,\theta}=Q_{N,\theta}^{-1}
, where
Q_{N,\theta}
is the mass-lumped finite-element precision matrix.
Since
\theta=\kappa
for both constructions, we write
\kappa
in place of
\theta
for the remainder of this section.

We place independent priors
N\sim p(N)
and
\kappa\sim p_{1}(\kappa)
. For the theoretical SPDE result in Theorem
3.3
,
\kappa
is fixed at the sample-size-dependent value
\kappa_{n}
. In that case, the update below is applied only to
N
. When a hierarchical implementation is used, as in the simulations, the marginal posterior of
(N,\kappa)
is calculated.

Let
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
and define the design matrix
\Phi_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m_{N}}
by

(\Phi_{N})_{i,j}=\psi_{j}(x_{i}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,\ j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}.

(23)

Then
(f_{N}(x_{1}),\ldots,f_{N}(x_{n}))^{\top}=\Phi_{N}w^{N}
holds and the likelihood under (
1
) is written as

p(y\mid w^{N},N,\kappa)\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(y-\Phi_{N}w^{N})^{\top}(y-\Phi_{N}w^{N})\Big\}.

(24)

Since
\{\psi_{j}\}
are locally supported, each row of
\Phi_{N}
has at most
2^{d}
nonzero entries, so
\Phi_{N}^{\top}y
and
\Phi_{N}^{\top}\Phi_{N}
can be formed in
O(n)
operations. By conjugacy, the conditional posterior of
w^{N}
given
(N,\kappa)
is Gaussian:

w^{N}\mid N,\kappa,{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\sim N(\mu_{N,\kappa},\Sigma^{*}_{N,\kappa}),

(25)

where

(\Sigma^{*}_{N,\kappa})^{-1}=\Sigma_{N,\kappa}^{-1}+\sigma^{-2}\Phi_{N}^{\top}\Phi_{N},\qquad\mu_{N,\kappa}=\sigma^{-2}\Sigma^{*}_{N,\kappa}\Phi_{N}^{\top}y.

(26)

Therefore, it remains to sample
(N,\kappa)
from the marginal posterior. From

p(N,\kappa\mid{\mathcal{D}_{n}})\propto p(N)p_{1}(\kappa)p({\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mid N,\kappa)=p(N)p_{1}(\kappa)\int p(w^{N},{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mid N,\kappa)dw^{N},

(27)

we obtain

\log p({\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mid N,\kappa)=C_{y}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma_{N,\kappa}|+\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma^{*}_{N,\kappa}|+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{4}}y^{\top}\Phi_{N}\Sigma^{*}_{N,\kappa}\Phi_{N}^{T}y,

(28)

where
C_{y}
does not depend on
(N,\kappa)
.
From this, we update
N
using a local proposal on its discrete support and update
\kappa
using a Metropolis–Hastings proposal density
q_{\kappa}(\cdot\mid\kappa)
on
(0,\infty)
.
Given a current state
(N,\kappa)
, propose
(N^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
and accept with probability

\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{p(N^{\prime})p_{1}(\kappa^{\prime})p({\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mid N^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})}{p(N)p_{1}(\kappa)p({\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mid N,\kappa)}\cdot\frac{q_{N}(N\mid N^{\prime})q_{\kappa}(\kappa\mid\kappa^{\prime})}{q_{N}(N^{\prime}\mid N)q_{\kappa}(\kappa^{\prime}\mid\kappa)}\Big\},

(29)

where
p({\mathcal{D}_{n}}\mid N,\kappa)
is evaluated via (
28
).
After updating
(N,\kappa)
, we draw
w^{N}
from (
25
).

The dominant computation per iteration is governed by the coefficient dimension
m_{N}
rather than the sample size
n
:
forming
\Phi_{N}^{T}y
and
\Phi_{N}^{\top}\Phi_{N}
costs
O(n)
, while dense linear algebra for evaluating (
28
) and sampling (
25
) costs
O(m_{N}^{3})
.
Thus the procedure is computationally attractive when
m_{N}\ll n
, even though dense factorizations remain the bottleneck as
d
and
N
grow.
When the parent kernel is stationary and the grid is regular,
\Sigma_{N,\kappa}
has Toeplitz structure for
d=1
and BTTB structure for
d=2
, which can be exploited to accelerate linear algebra operations involving
\Sigma_{N,\kappa}
, although not for
\Sigma_{N,\kappa}^{*}

(
Ray et al., 2020
)
.
For the SPDE method, however, both
\Sigma^{-1}_{N,\kappa}
and
(\Sigma^{*}_{N,\kappa})^{-1}
remain sparse. This is because of the local support of the basis functions, leading to the sparsity of
\Sigma^{-1}_{N,\kappa}
and
\Phi_{N}^{\top}\Phi_{N}
.

Therefore, utilizing sparse Cholesky factorization, the SPDE method avoids dense
m_{N}\times m_{N}
factorizations. In one dimension, where the precision matrix is banded, the coefficient-space linear algebra is linear in
m_{N}
up to constants depending on the bandwidth. In higher dimensions, the exact cost depends on the sparsity in Cholesky factorization, which requires at most
O(m_{N}^{3})
. Thus a direct implementation of GPI has cost
O(n+m_{N}^{3})
per iteration. For the SPDE method, the Cholesky factorization of
\big(\Sigma_{N,\kappa}^{*}\big)^{-1}
can be obtained more efficiently thanks to its sparsity. For instance, in one-dimensional case, the Cholesky decomposition of
\big(\Sigma_{N,\kappa}^{*}\big)^{-1}
requires
O(m_{N})
as it has bandwidth
\beta
, leading to per iteration computational cost
O(n+m_{N})
.
Therefore, both methods remain scalable for larger
n
, by avoiding dense
n\times n
parent-GP covariance matrix operations.

Simulation studies

We evaluate the empirical performance of the two finite-rank approximations introduced in Section
2
under the fixed-design regression model (
1
).
The simulations are designed to examine two questions.
First, how closely do the adaptive finite-rank procedures track the corresponding parent GP procedures?
Second, how much is gained by learning the resolution parameter
N
rather than fixing it in advance?

For each experiment we generate design points
x_{i}
uniformly on
[0,1]^{d}
and sample

y_{i}=f(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\qquad\epsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2}),

(30)

with
\sigma^{2}=0.01
treated as known.
As a point estimator we use the posterior mean

\hat{f}(x)=\mathbb{E}\{f(x)\mid{\mathcal{D}_{n}}\},

(31)

approximated by Monte Carlo averages of the retained posterior draws.
Performance is measured by the average mean squared error (AMSE) on a regular evaluation grid:

\mathrm{AMSE}(\hat{f})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{K}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{G}_{K}}\{\hat{f}(t)-f(t)\}^{2},

(32)

where
\mathcal{G}_{K}
denotes the
K^{d}
regular grid on
[0,1]^{d}
.
In one dimension we take
K=1000
, and in two dimensions we take a
25\times 25
grid.

For the SPDE method, Theorem
3.3
covers adaptive resolution under the Matérn bandwidth scaling depending on the true function smoothness. In the simulations, we report an empirical hierarchical version that updates
\kappa
jointly with
N
. This implementation is used to examine practical sensitivity to bandwidth learning, while Theorem
3.3
deals with the fixed
\kappa
scaling depending on the true function smoothness.

with

.

(34)

For both GPI and SPDE we use piecewise linear basis functions in

N

).

6
Discussion

This paper establishes that finite-rank Gaussian process approximations with locally supported bases and dependent Gaussian coefficients can inherit the posterior contraction rates of their corresponding parent GP priors when a suitable prior is placed on the resolution parameter
N
. We have shown this for two representative constructions: the finite-element SPDE approximation of Matérn GPs and the lattice-based interpolation of squared-exponential GPs. The common methodological contribution is
adaptive resolution
: the finite-rank dimension
|{\mathcal{J}}_{N}|=(N+1)^{d}
is learned from data rather than fixed in advance, preserving the parent-GP contraction rate while maintaining computational advantages from the locally supported basis structure.

For the SPDE approach (Theorem 3.3), adaptation is over the resolution
N
under the bandwidth scaling
\kappa_{n}
inherited from the parent Matérn theory
(
Fang and Bhadra, 2025
)
. The proof relies on spectral comparison between the continuous Neumann eigenbasis and the mass-lumped finite-element discretization, showing that the finite-rank RKHS norm and small-ball probability match those of the parent GP up to constants for sufficiently large
N
. This connects the numerical-approximation perspective of
Sanz-Alonso and Yang (2022)
with the statistical contraction theory of Matérn GPs.

For the GPI approach (Theorem 3.4), adaptation is over both the bandwidth
\kappa
and the resolution
N
. The hierarchical specification with horseshoe or gamma-type priors on
\kappa
recovers the minimax-optimal rate up to logarithmic factors, matching the fully adaptive parent squared-exponential GP result of
van der Vaart and van Zanten (2009)
. The proof uses an interpolation error bound to show that
\|f_{N}-f\|_{\infty}
is negligible when
N
is large enough relative to
\kappa
, allowing the finite-rank prior concentration to inherit the parent-GP behavior. This strategy has a conceptual parallel with the oracle-GP comparison approach of
Castillo and Randrianarisoa (2025)
, where an oracle GP serves as an intermediate reference for establishing contraction rates.

Extensions and open problems.
Several natural directions remain open:

Higher dimensions.
The
O((N+1)^{3d})
cost makes direct application challenging for
d\geq 5
. Structured approaches such as additive models
f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}f_{k}(x_{k})
or block-additive models offer a natural route to mitigate the curse of dimensionality: each component uses a one-dimensional grid, so the total cost scales as
O(dN^{3})
rather than
O(N^{3d})
. Extending Theorem 3.4 to this setting would require studying when the finite-rank additive structure preserves the parent-GP contraction rate.

Adaptive smoothness for SPDE.
Theorem 3.3 uses the oracle bandwidth
\kappa_{n}
depending on the unknown smoothness
\alpha
. Whether full adaptation over both
N
and
\kappa
is achievable for the SPDE construction remains open. The challenge is that the sparse precision structure of the SPDE prior depends critically on the smoothness parameter
\beta
, so hierarchical modeling of
\beta
would require understanding how the sparsity pattern changes with
\beta
. A systematic comparison with sparse variational GP methods
(
Nieman and Szabó, 2025
)
in terms of both computation and statistical accuracy also merits further study.

A.1
Properties of finite basis expansion

We begin with the proof of Lemma 3.1, which describes the RKHS norm associated with
f_{N}
.

Proof of Lemma 3.1.

We use Theorem 4.1 of
van der Vaart and van Zanten (2008b)
, which characterizes the RKHS associated with a (finite) Gaussian series representation.

Define the feature vector
\Psi(x)=(\psi_{j}(x))_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)^{d}}
, so that

f_{N}(x)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}w_{j}\psi_{j}(x)=\Psi(x)^{\top}w^{N}
.
Then the covariance kernel of
f_{N}
is

K(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathbb{E}\{f_{N}(x_{1})f_{N}(x_{2})\}=\Psi(x_{1})^{\top}\Sigma_{N,\theta}\Psi(x_{2}).

(39)

Let
\Sigma_{N,\theta}=V\Lambda V^{\top}
be an eigendecomposition, with
V
orthogonal and

\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
where
m=(N+1)^{d}
and
\lambda_{\ell}>0
for
\ell\in[m]
.
Set
\widetilde{\Psi}(x)=V^{\top}\Psi(x)
and write

\widetilde{\Psi}(x)=(\widetilde{\psi}_{1}(x),\ldots,\widetilde{\psi}_{m}(x))^{\top}
.
Then

K(x_{1},x_{2})=\widetilde{\Psi}(x_{1})^{\top}\Lambda\widetilde{\Psi}(x_{2})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}\,\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}(x_{1})\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}(x_{2}).

(40)

By Theorem 4.1 of
van der Vaart and van Zanten (2008b)
, the RKHS
\mathbb{H}
consists of all functions

h(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{\ell}\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}(x)
equipped with norm

\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}a_{\ell}^{2}/\lambda_{\ell}.

(41)

Since
\{\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}\}
span the same space as
\{\psi_{j}\}
, we have

\mathbb{H}=\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{j}:j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}
.

Now let
h=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}v_{j}\psi_{j}
and write
v^{N}=Va
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then

\|h\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=a^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}a=(v^{N})^{\top}V\Lambda^{-1}V^{\top}v^{N}=(v^{N})^{\top}\Sigma_{N,\theta}^{-1}v^{N}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We next prove Lemma 3.2, which gives a sufficient condition on
N
for approximating
f^{*}
by a tensor-product hat basis.

Proof of Lemma 3.2.

With
d
-dimensional hat basis functions defined in (
38
), define the multilinear interpolation of
f^{*}
on the regular grid as

f_{N}(x)=\sum_{{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}f^{*}({\bf j}/N)\,\Psi_{{\bf j}}(x),\qquad x\in[0,1]^{d}.

(42)

By construction,
f_{N}\in\mathrm{span}\{\Psi_{\bf j}:{\bf j}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}
and
f_{N}({\bf j}/N)=f^{*}({\bf j}/N)
for all grid points.

Write
h=1/N
and fix a cell
Q=\prod_{r=1}^{d}[i_{r}h,(i_{r}+1)h]
.
For any
x\in Q
,
f_{N}(x)
is a convex combination of the vertex values
\{f^{*}(u):u\in\mathcal{V}(Q)\}
, i.e.

f_{N}(x)=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}(Q)}\theta_{u}(x)f^{*}(u)
with
\theta_{u}(x)\geq 0
and
\sum_{u}\theta_{u}(x)=1
,
and every vertex
u\in\mathcal{V}(Q)
satisfies
\|x-u\|_{\infty}\leq h
.

We consider three regimes.

Case 1:
0<\alpha\leq 1
.

Since
f^{*}\in C^{\alpha}([0,1]^{d})
, there exists
C_{f^{*}}>0
such that

|f^{*}(x)-f^{*}(u)|\leq C_{f^{*}}\|x-u\|_{\infty}^{\alpha}\leq C_{f^{*}}h^{\alpha}
for all
u\in\mathcal{V}(Q)
.
Therefore,

|f^{*}(x)-f_{N}(x)|=\Big|\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}(Q)}\theta_{u}(x)\{f^{*}(x)-f^{*}(u)\}\Big|\leq\sum_{u}\theta_{u}(x)\,C_{f^{*}}h^{\alpha}=C_{f^{*}}h^{\alpha}.

(43)

Case 2:
1<\alpha\leq 2
.

Then
\nabla f^{*}
exists and is
(\alpha-1)
-Hölder. For each vertex
u\in\mathcal{V}(Q)
,
a first-order Taylor expansion around
x
yields

f^{*}(u)=f^{*}(x)+\nabla f^{*}(x)^{\top}(u-x)+R_{u},\qquad|R_{u}|\leq C_{f^{*}}\|u-x\|_{\infty}^{\alpha}\leq C_{f^{*}}h^{\alpha}.

(44)

Multilinear interpolation reproduces affine functions exactly, hence

\sum_{u}\theta_{u}(x)\{f^{*}(x)+\nabla f^{*}(x)^{\top}(u-x)\}=f^{*}(x)
.
Thus

|f^{*}(x)-f_{N}(x)|=\Big|\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}(Q)}\theta_{u}(x)R_{u}\Big|\leq\sum_{u}\theta_{u}(x)\,C_{f^{*}}h^{\alpha}=C_{f^{*}}h^{\alpha}.

(45)

Case 3:
\alpha>2
.

Then all second-order partial derivatives (including mixed partial derivatives) exist and are bounded.
A second-order Taylor expansion gives

f^{*}(u)=f^{*}(x)+\nabla f^{*}(x)^{\top}(u-x)+\tfrac{1}{2}(u-x)^{\top}Hf^{*}(\xi_{u})(u-x),

(46)

for some
\xi_{u}
on the line segment between
x
and
u
.
Again the affine part is reproduced exactly by multilinear interpolation, hence

|f^{*}(x)-f_{N}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{z\in[0,1]^{d}}\|Hf^{*}(z)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}(Q)}\theta_{u}(x)\|u-x\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim h^{2}.

(47)

Taking the supremum over
x\in[0,1]^{d}
yields

\|f_{N}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq C\,h^{\alpha\land 2}=C\,N^{-(\alpha\land 2)}

(48)

for a constant
C>0
depending only on
f^{*}
(through its Hölder norm) and
d
.
Therefore, choosing
N\geq(C/\epsilon)^{1/(\alpha\land 2)}
ensures
\|f_{N}-f^{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon
.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem 3.3

The proof of Theorem 3.3 compares the finite-element SPDE prior with the corresponding continuous Matérn prior under the same choice of
\kappa_{n}
. The comparison is mainly carried out between the eigenvalues of the differential operators in the SPDE defining the parent GP and the generalized eigenvalues of the finite-rank GP. Lemma
B.3
to Lemma
B.5
establish the comparison between the discrete precision eigenvalues and the continuous precision eigenvalues. Lemma
B.7
to Lemma
B.10
use this comparison to control the RKHS approximation term, centered small-ball probability, and entropy of the finite-rank prior. Section B.1 collects these technical lemmas, and Section B.2 verifies the three posterior-contraction conditions.

B.1
Technical lemmas

This section gives the finite-element matrix details used in the compact SPDE construction in Section 2.1 of the main manuscript.

For the SPDE method, we use several representations of the same function. For
f\in B^{\alpha}_{\infty,\infty}
, the spectral representation is written as

f=\sum_{k}z_{k}e_{k},

where
\{e_{k}\}
denotes the eigenbasis functions of
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}=\kappa^{2}\mathrm{id}-\Delta
under Neumann boundary condition.

The
restriction operator

\mathcal{R}_{N}
maps a continuous function to its vector of nodal values,

(R_{N}f)_{i}=f\biggl({\frac{i_{1}}{N}},\ldots,\frac{i_{d}}{N}\biggr),\qquad i\in\mathcal{J}_{N}.

(49)

Next, the
extension operator

\mathcal{E}_{N}
maps the nodal values into a piecewise linear function:

f_{N}=\mathcal{E}_{N}w=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}w_{i}\Psi_{i},

(50)

where
\{\Psi_{\bf i}:{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}
is the tensor-product hat basis defined in (
38
).
Finally, the
multilinear interpolation operator
on the regular grid is defined as

\mathcal{I}_{N}=\mathcal{E}_{N}\circ\mathcal{R}_{N}.

(51)

The interpolation operator
\mathcal{I}_{N}
maps a continuous function into a piecewise linear function sharing the same nodal values on the grid.

Let

V_{N}=\operatorname{span}\{\Psi_{{\bf i}}:{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}\}.

Recall that the one-dimensional consistent mass matrix and stiffness matrix are defined as

C^{(c)}_{ij}=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{i}(t)\psi_{j}(t)dt,\qquad G_{ij}=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{i}^{\prime}(t)\psi_{j}^{\prime}(t)dt,\qquad 0\leq i,j\leq N.

(52)

The corresponding
d
-dimensional consistent finite-element matrices are

(C_{d}^{(c)})_{{\bf i},{\bf j}}=\int_{\Omega}\Psi_{{\bf i}}({\bf x})\Psi_{{\bf j}}({\bf x})d{\bf x},\qquad(G_{d}^{(c)})_{{\bf i},{\bf j}}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Psi_{{\bf i}}({\bf x})^{\top}\nabla\Psi_{{\bf j}}({\bf x})d{\bf x}.

(53)

For coefficient vectors
u=(u_{{\bf i}})_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
and
v=(v_{{\bf i}})_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}
, write

u_{N}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}u_{{\bf i}}\Psi_{{\bf i}},\qquad v_{N}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}v_{{\bf i}}\Psi_{{\bf i}}.

Then

v^{\top}C_{d}^{(c)}u=\langle v_{N},u_{N}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad v^{\top}G_{d}^{(c)}u=\int_{\Omega}\nabla v_{N}({\bf x})^{\top}\nabla u_{N}({\bf x})d{\bf x}.

More precisely, for
u_{N}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}u_{{\bf i}}\Psi_{{\bf i}}
, define

z_{N}=\sum_{{\bf i}\in\mathcal{J}_{N}}z_{{\bf i}}\Psi_{{\bf i}}\in V_{N}
by

\langle v_{N},z_{N}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla v_{N}({\bf x})^{\top}\nabla u_{N}({\bf x})d{\bf x}\qquad\mbox{for all }v_{N}\in V_{N}.

In coefficient coordinates this identity is

v^{\top}C_{d}^{(c)}z=v^{\top}G_{d}^{(c)}u\qquad\mbox{for all }v\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)^{d}}.

Hence

C_{d}^{(c)}z=G_{d}^{(c)}u,\qquad z=\big(C_{d}^{(c)}\big)^{-1}G_{d}^{(c)}u,

because
C_{d}^{(c)}
is positive definite under linearly independent
\{\Psi_{\bf i}\}
. Therefore

\big(C_{d}^{(c)}\big)^{-1}G_{d}^{(c)}
is the consistent Galerkin matrix for

-\Delta
in coefficient coordinates, and

L_{N,d}^{(c)}=\big(C_{d}^{(c)}\big)^{-1}\big(\kappa^{2}C_{d}^{(c)}+G_{d}^{(c)}\big)=\kappa^{2}I+\big(C_{d}^{(c)}\big)^{-1}G_{d}^{(c)}

is the matrix leading to coefficient-coordinate version of
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}=\kappa^{2}\mathrm{id}-\Delta
.

By the tensor-product form of
\Psi_{{\bf i}}
,

C_{d}^{(c)}=(C^{(c)})^{\otimes d},

(54)

and

G_{d}^{(c)}=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\Big((C^{(c)})^{\otimes(r-1)}\otimes G\otimes(C^{(c)})^{\otimes(d-r)}\Big).

(55)

In practice, the SPDE prior uses mass lumping for
C^{(c)}
to induce sparsity in the final precision matrix. In one dimension, only the mass matrix is replaced by

C:=\mathrm{diag}(C^{(c)}\mathbf{1}_{N+1})=\frac{1}{2N}\mathrm{diag}(1,2,\ldots,2,1),

while the one-dimensional stiffness matrix
G
is unchanged. For the tensor-product basis, the mass-lumped matrix is

C_{d}:=\mathrm{diag}(C_{d}^{(c)}\mathbf{1}_{(N+1)^{d}})=C^{\otimes d},

and the stiffness matrix uses
C^{(c)}
replaced with
C
:

G_{d}:=\sum_{r=1}^{d}\Big(C^{\otimes(r-1)}\otimes G\otimes C^{\otimes(d-r)}\Big).

(56)

Thus
G_{d}=G_{d}^{(c)}
when
d=1
, while
G_{d}
generally differs from
G_{d}^{(c)}
when
d\geq 2
.
L_{N,d}
is obtained from
L_{N,d}^{(c)}
by replacing
C_{d}^{(c)}
and
G_{d}^{(c)}
to
C_{d}
and
G_{d}
, respectively.
From this point onward,
C_{d}
and
G_{d}
denote the mass-lumped matrices used in the SPDE prior. The main manuscript suppresses the subscript
d
and writes these matrices as
C
and
G
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
. The scale-corrected precision matrix is

Q_{d}=\kappa^{-(2\beta-d)}C_{d}L_{N,d}^{\beta}

(57)

and the conditional prior is

w^{N}\mid\kappa,N\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,Q_{d}^{-1}),\qquad f_{N}=\mathcal{E}_{N}w^{N}.

(58)

Next, we record the continuous spectral coefficients corresponding to the parent Matérn
SPDE. Since

\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}e_{k}=\left(\kappa^{2}+\pi^{2}\|k\|_{2}^{2}\right)e_{k},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}=\kappa^{2}\mathrm{id}-\Delta,

the SPDE

\kappa^{-(\beta-d/2)}\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}^{\beta/2}f=W

implies that, for
f=\sum_{k}z_{k}e_{k}

z_{k}\sim N(0,\Lambda_{k}^{-1}),

Equivalently,

\kappa^{-(2\beta-d)}\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}^{\beta}e_{k}=\Lambda_{k}e_{k}.

We then introduce the results related to eigenvectors
e_{{\bf k}}
. We start with

k\geq 1

e_{k}(s)-e_{k}(t)=-2\sqrt{2}\sin(k\pi(s+t)/2)\sin(k\pi(s-t)/2),

combined with
|\sin x|\leq\min(|x|,1)
, we obtain
|e_{k}(s)-e_{k}(t)|\leq\min(2\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}k\pi|s-t|)
for
0\leq k\leq N

Lemma B.1
.

For
{\bf s},{\bf t}\in[0,1]^{d}
and

|e_{{\bf k}}({\bf s})-e_{{\bf k}}({\bf t})|\leq d\cdot 2^{d/2+1-\rho}\|\pi{\bf k}\|^{\rho}_{2}\|{\bf s}-{\bf t}\|_{\infty}^{\rho}.

Proof.

First, observe

\displaystyle|e_{{\bf k}}({\bf s})-e_{{\bf k}}({\bf t})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{d}|e_{{\bf k}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r},t_{r+1},\ldots,t_{d})-e_{{\bf k}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r-1},t_{r},\ldots,t_{d})|

\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{2})^{d-1}\sum_{r=1}^{d}|e_{k_{r}}(s_{r})-e_{k_{r}}(t_{r})|.

Since
\min(a,b)\leq a^{1-\rho}b^{\rho}
holds for all
0<\rho<1
and

, we observe

|e_{k}(s)-e_{k}(t)|\leq\min(2\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}k\pi|s-t|)\leq(2\sqrt{2})^{1-\rho}(\sqrt{2}k\pi|s-t|)^{\rho}.

Combining the above two display, we obtain

|e_{{\bf k}}({\bf s})-e_{{\bf k}}({\bf t})|\leq d\cdot 2^{d/2+1-\rho}\|\pi{\bf k}\|_{2}^{\rho}\|{\bf s}-{\bf t}\|_{\infty}^{\rho}.

Lemma B.4

(

Proof of Theorem 3.4

The proof of Theorem 3.4 builds on the parent Gaussian process with squared-exponential covariance kernel (

van der Vaart and van Zanten (2009)
. As introduced in Section
2.2
, GPI method can be represented as

where

is a parent GP with mean zero and squared-exponential covariance kernel, and

is an interpolation operator defined in (

with high probability under the law of parent GP. Section

C.1

Let

\mathbb{E}\{g(s)g(t)\}=\exp\big\{-\kappa^{2}\|s-t\|_{2}^{2}\big\},

(116)

where
\kappa>0
is the inverse bandwidth parameter.
Define the canonical pseudometric

d_{g}(s,t)=\Big[\mathbb{E}\{g(s)-g(t)\}^{2}\Big]^{1/2}.

(117)

For
\epsilon>0
, define

\displaystyle B^{\infty}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\Big\{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]^{d}:\|s-t\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon\Big\},

\displaystyle B^{g}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\Big\{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]^{d}:d_{g}(s,t)\leq\epsilon\Big\}.

(118)

Let

be the regular grid and
\{\psi_{j_{r}}\}
be the hat basis in each coordinate.
Define the

Lemma C.1
.

For all

(120)

In particular,
B^{\infty}_{\epsilon}\subset B^{g}_{\kappa\sqrt{2d}\,\epsilon}

Proof.

The result follows from

for

and

for

.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

There exists a universal constant
K>0
such that for all
\kappa>0
and all integers

,

\mathbb{E}\sup_{(s,t)\in B^{\infty}_{1/N}}\{g(s)-g(t)\}\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{osc}}\,\kappa d}{N}\Big(\log(C_{\mathrm{vol}}N)\Big)^{1/2},

(122)

, there exists a universal constant

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{(s,t)\in B^{g}_{\kappa\sqrt{2d}/N}}\{g(s)-g(t)\}

.

,

P\big(g\notin\mathcal{E}(\epsilon)\,\big|\,\kappa\big)\leq 4\exp\Big\{-2\epsilon^{-d/\alpha}\log^{C}(1/\epsilon)\Big\}.

(152)

Let
\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)
be the sieve defined in Section 5 of
van der Vaart and van Zanten (2009)
with parameters
(M,r,\delta)
chosen so that

P\big(g\notin\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)\big)\leq\exp\Big\{-2\epsilon^{-d/\alpha}\log^{C}(1/\epsilon)\Big\}.

(153)

Define the stabilized sieve

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)\cap\mathcal{E}(\epsilon).

(154)

Finally, define the universal-
N
sieve for
\{f_{N}\}_{N\geq 1}
by

\mathcal{F}^{*}(\epsilon)=\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)+\epsilon\mathbb{B}_{1}\big)\cup\bigcup_{1\leq N\leq N_{0}(\epsilon)}\mathcal{I}_{N}\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)\big),

(155)

where
\mathbb{B}_{1}
denotes the unit ball of
C[0,1]^{d}
under
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}

Proof of the sieve condition (
132
).

Let
f_{N}=\mathcal{I}_{N}g
.
If
g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)
and
N\leq N_{0}(\epsilon)
, then
f_{N}\in\mathcal{I}_{N}(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon))\subset\mathcal{F}^{*}(\epsilon)
by (
155
).
If
g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)
and
N\geq N_{0}(\epsilon)
, then
g\in\mathcal{E}(\epsilon)
and by (
145
),

\|f_{N}-g\|_{\infty}\leq\sup_{(s,t)\in B^{\infty}_{1/N_{0}(\epsilon)}}|g(s)-g(t)|\leq\epsilon/2,

(156)

so
f_{N}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)+\epsilon\mathbb{B}_{1}\subset\mathcal{F}^{*}(\epsilon)
.
Therefore, for all
N\geq 1
,

\{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)\}\subset\{f_{N}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}(\epsilon)\}.

(157)

Hence

P\big(f_{N}\notin\mathcal{F}^{*}(\epsilon)\big)\leq P\big(g\notin\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)\big).

(158)

Using (
154
) and a union bound,

P\big(g\notin\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)\big)\leq P\big(g\notin\mathcal{G}(\epsilon)\big)+P\big(g\notin\mathcal{E}(\epsilon)\big).

(159)

The first term is controlled by (
153
).
For the second term,

P\big(g\notin\mathcal{E}(\epsilon)\big)\leq P_{1}(\kappa>\kappa_{\epsilon})+\sup_{\kappa\leq\kappa_{\epsilon}}P\big(g\notin\mathcal{E}(\epsilon)\,\big|\,\kappa\big),

(160)

which is bounded by (
148
) and (
152
).
Combining these bounds yields (
132
) after adjusting constants.
∎

Proof of the metric entropy condition (
133
).

Since
\|h\|_{n}\leq\|h\|_{\infty}
, it suffices to work under
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
We first use the shift bound

{\mathcal{N}}\big(2\epsilon,\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)+\epsilon\mathbb{B}_{1},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\leq{\mathcal{N}}\big(\epsilon,\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\leq{\mathcal{N}}\big(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big).

(161)

Next, by (
144
),

{\mathcal{N}}\big(\epsilon,\mathcal{I}_{N}(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\leq{\mathcal{N}}\big(\epsilon,\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\leq{\mathcal{N}}\big(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big).

(162)

Using (
155
) and a union bound for covering numbers,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}\big(2\epsilon,\mathcal{F}^{*}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{N}}\big(2\epsilon,\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)+\epsilon\mathbb{B}_{1},\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)+\sum_{N=1}^{N_{0}(\epsilon)}{\mathcal{N}}\big(2\epsilon,\mathcal{I}_{N}(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}(\epsilon)),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)

(163)

\displaystyle\leq\big(1+N_{0}(\epsilon)\big){\mathcal{N}}\big(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big).

(164)

Taking logarithms yields

\log{\mathcal{N}}\big(2\epsilon,\mathcal{F}^{*}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big)\leq\log\big(1+N_{0}(\epsilon)\big)+\log{\mathcal{N}}\big(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\big).

(165)

From Section 5 of
van der Vaart and van Zanten (2009)
,
\log{\mathcal{N}}(\epsilon,\mathcal{G}(\epsilon),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})\lesssim\epsilon^{-d/\alpha}\log^{C}(1/\epsilon)
.
Moreover, (

)–(

) imply that
N_{0}(\epsilon)
grows at most polynomially in
\kappa_{\epsilon}
and
\epsilon^{-1}
up to a logarithmic factor, hence
\log(1+N_{0}(\epsilon))\lesssim\log(1/\epsilon)
.
This proves (
133
).
∎
