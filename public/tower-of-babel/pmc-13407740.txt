A Three-dimensional Finite-element Analysis of von Mises Stress and Strain Distribution Generated by Nickel–Titanium Rotary Files in Primary Root Dentin
Authors: Vaishnavi Mathawala, Madhura Pawar, Shraddha Shirsat, Sneha Panamgipalli, Sejal Patil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundThe aim of the present study was to evaluate and compare the mechanical behavior of two commercially available nickel–titanium (NiTi) pediatric rotary file systems with different cross-sectional geometries and tapers using three-dimensional (3D) finite element analysis (FEA).MethodologyTwo pediatric NiTi rotary file systems, varying in cross-sectional design and taper, were selected for this study. Detailed 3D models of both systems were generated using advanced computer-aided design (CAD) software. These models were then subjected to finite element simulations replicating clinical conditions to evaluate mechanical parameters. The primary variables assessed included von Mises stress distribution, maximum displacement, and strain patterns when subjected to simulated intracanal forces during root canal instrumentation.ResultsThe FEA demonstrated significant differences in the mechanical behavior of the two file systems. The file with a triangular cross-sectional design and greater taper exhibited higher stress concentrations, particularly in the apical third, increasing susceptibility to deformation or fracture. On the other hand, the file with an S-shaped cross-section and reduced taper showed a more uniform stress distribution pattern, lower maximum displacement, and reduced strain, indicating superior mechanical performance under simulated clinical loads.ConclusionThe design characteristics of pediatric NiTi rotary files, including cross-sectional geometry and taper, play a crucial role in determining their mechanical behavior. Files with favorable cross-sectional designs and lower taper demonstrate better stress distribution and deformation resistance, thereby enhancing their clinical safety and efficiency.Clinical significanceUnderstanding the design variables on the mechanical performance of pediatric NiTi rotary files influences optimal instrument selection, promoting more efficient endodontic treatment in primary teeth.

Introduction

Dental caries is one of the most prevalent diseases, according to the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD), and a common challenge faced by pediatric dentists in daily practice. If untreated, it can lead to pulpal necrosis, periapical infection, and eventual tooth loss. Preserving primary teeth is crucial, as they act as natural space maintainers for permanent teeth.1 Thus, the primary goal of pulp therapy is to eliminate infection and maintain the health and function of the tooth and its supporting tissues. To achieve this objective, knowledge of the root canal morphology of primary teeth is of prime importance. Root canal morphology of primary teeth renders endodontic treatment difficult, as root canals of primary teeth are tortuous, ribbon-shaped, and sometimes curved. Therefore, the proper usage of endodontic instruments and irrigation protocol becomes essential to perform a successful pulpectomy.2

The thickness of the remaining root dentin after biomechanical preparation is directly related to the fracture resistance of a tooth. Reamers, hand files, sonic instruments, mechanical apparatus, and nickel–titanium (NiTi) rotary file systems are used for root canal preparation. The use of hand files is time-consuming and sometimes causes iatrogenic errors, such as ledge formation, canal transportation, zipping, and apical blockage.3 Barr et al. in 2000 introduced NiTi endodontic instruments for pulpectomy, which enhanced the ability of clinicians to maintain the original anatomic shape of the primary root canals, highlighting their advantages of faster canal preparation, cost-effectiveness, and uniform shaping.4

Over time, NiTi instruments have undergone several modifications in terms of cross-sectional design, size, taper, and manufacturing processes to improve their flexibility, resistance to fracture, and clinical efficiency.5 Controlled memory (CM) wire technology has further improved the mechanical properties of NiTi instruments by reducing nickel content, providing excellent flexibility, resistance to cyclic fatigue, and the ability to precurve the files within the canal.6 Additionally, heat treatments producing blue or gold titanium oxide layers on these instruments have enhanced their mechanical performance, providing greater flexibility, ductility, and improved fracture resistance.7 Despite these advancements, instrument fracture remains a significant concern in endodontics. File separation often occurs due to complex canal anatomy, operator technique, or metallurgical limitations of the instruments. According to Laxmanan et al., fracture incidence is reported to be 1.3–10% for rotary instruments.8,9

Finite element analysis (FEA) is a noninvasive three-dimensional (3D) computational technique that simulates clinical conditions to evaluate stress distribution, deformation, and mechanical behavior of materials. Widely used in engineering and increasingly adopted in dentistry, FEA provides valuable insights into how endodontic instruments perform under various mechanical conditions.10,11 Recently published literature investigated the mechanical behavior of NiTi rotary files in dentin of two different moduli of elasticity on simulated models.12

Thus, within this frame of reference, this prospective study aims to evaluate the mechanical behavior of two commercially available NiTi rotary files used in pediatric endodontics, Reciproc Blue and Endogal Rotary Kids files, with different cross sections and conicity, using a 3D FEA.

Methodology

Ethics approval was given by Dr D Y Patil Vidyapeeth's Institutional Ethics Committee, Pune (Ref. No. DYPDCH/DPU/EC/582/136/2023). Two endodontic files composed of heat-treated alloy were considered: Reciproc Blue (VWD dent, Munich, Germany) and Endogal Rotary Kids (Endogal Galician Endodontics Company, Lugo, Spain). First, reverse engineering was carried out on an original file using a Vernier caliper and a datasheet of the file (Fig. 1). Then, the modeling and assembly of the endodontic files were done using 3D computer-aided design (CAD) software (Solidworks 2023, Dassault Systèmes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France) (Figs 2 and 3).

For the canal model, the mesiobuccal canal of the primary maxillary second molar was considered. The cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan of the tooth was imported into dedicated software (Geomagic, 3D Systems, North Carolina, United States) to generate the point cloud of the canal. The point cloud was used to generate the stereolithography (STL) model of the canal. Then the STL model was fed into the 3D CAD modeling software (Solidworks 2023, Dassault Systèmes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France) to create the 3D model of the canal. A simulated canal with 45° curvature, 11 mm length, and 0.5 mm diameter was created within a 32 × 20 × 6 mm rectangular solid. One specific canal was created for each instrument.

These generated 3D models were then imported into computer-assisted engineering software (ANSYS 19.2, ANSYS Company, Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, USA). The number of nodes and elements for each set was established by creating a mesh for each of the three 3D models (Fig. 4). Meshing is the process of subdividing, that is, discretizing the complex geometry into a suitable set of smaller “elements” of finite dimensions. The points connecting two or more discrete elements are called nodes or nodal points. The corner nodes are called primary external nodes. A grid independence check was carried out to assess the response of the solution for different mesh sizes. Technical features of the 3D models are summarized in Table 1.

Each structure involved in this study has a specific property. The finite element models were considered isotropic, homogeneous, and linearly elastic, with specific Young's modulus and Poisson's ratios. Mechanical properties: modulus of elasticity of primary root dentin—19.89 GPa.13 For heat-treated instruments, a modulus of elasticity of 24.6 GPa was considered.12 The Poisson ratio of dentin is 0.3, and for files is 0.33.

Based on the file's movement into the canal, it was assumed that the instrument and canal had a frictional contact of 0.2. Nodal displacements at the artificial canal's apex were subject to boundary conditions in all directions. Boundary conditions specify how forces are applied to the model and how it is fixed in the finite element (FE) space.

Simultaneous testing under two distinct conditions was carried out for each instrument. The bending moment that the instrument undergoes upon insertion into the canal is replicated in the first condition. Factors such as the instrument's cross-section, elastic modulus, and degree of deflection influence the magnitude of the reaction force during bending. Static-structural analysis was used to measure the reaction force of each instrument.

In the subsequent simulation phase, the previously calculated reaction force (N) was applied to simulate the rotational moment of the instrument inside the root canal. The torque during the instrument's rotation was reproduced by the second loading condition. For this condition, the maximum torque specified by each manufacturer was applied. During the loading phase, the instrument underwent a unidirectional rotation moment, exerting pressure on the dentin walls as it navigated the canal's morphology. Quasi-static analyses were conducted at this phase, assuming linear static behavior for all components and neglecting the possibility of failure. von Mises stress for the instruments and strain and von Mises stress for the canals were used to record the results.12

Discussion

The present investigation analyzed the impact of two commercially available heat-treated NiTi alloy pediatric rotary endodontic files on the root. When evaluating the strain and stress, two conditions were taken into account. Using scans of the natural tooth, the root canal model utilized in this investigation was described. Instruments with 8 and 6% conicity were examined in a corresponding canal model with matching conicity in the current investigation. This matching of conicity was implemented to replicate realistic conditions and ensure a consistent frictional surface for comparison. This approach followed the methodology of Prati et al.12

With the exterior surface of the instrument under tension and the concave part under compression, endodontic rotary files typically experience multidirectional forces and a mix of compressive and tensile stresses in the canals.14 Both files showed stress in the apical region. From a mechanical standpoint, recent in vitro research validated the impact of heat treatments on the torsional and cyclic fatigue resistance of rotary instruments.12 Reciproc Blue displayed a low-stress distribution along the canal and a reduced stress peak value in the dentin in the current investigation. Reciproc Blue is produced using CM-Blue Wire NiTi alloy and exhibits a stable martensite phase.15,16 It has increased cyclic fatigue resistance and greater flexibility, which enables it to adapt to the shape of the root canal.17 Endogal Rotary Kids showed more stress concentration in the cross-section as well as along the dentin canal. It is manufactured using heat-treated NiTi alloy with a triangular cross-section. In an in vitro study, when compared to Reciproc Blue, Endogal Rotary Kids led to more extensive canal preparation in the coronal thirds.15

The phase structure of NiTi endodontic instruments at body temperature is a critical factor in determining the mechanical properties of the files.18 By increasing the proportion of martensite and R phases in NiTi instruments through heat treatment, their elastic modulus decreases. Blue heat-treated NiTi instruments have a controlled memory effect, enhanced cyclic fatigue resistance, and superior flexibility. When tested in simulated clinical settings, this enhanced flexibility leads to better von Mises stress and strain patterns.19 Endodontic instruments made of NiTi shape memory alloys in their martensitic state have significantly improved overall performance compared to their austenitic counterparts (regular superelastic NiTi instruments), especially in flexibility and resistance against cyclic fatigue.20

Preventing unexpected instrument separation or fracture within the root canal is crucial from a clinical standpoint. Usually, NiTi instruments rotate for around 30 seconds inside the root canal.21 However, in the case of intricate canal anatomy, extra time may be required. Studies indicate that 40–120 seconds might be sufficient for fractures.22 To reduce the chance of instrument fracture, the clinician should be able to determine which part of the canal is under more stress.23

A cross-section of an instrument is the most crucial factor in von Mises stress distribution as well as the instrument's mechanical performance. Endogal Rotary Kids has a triangular cross-section and shows more stress concentration than Reciproc Blue, which has a double S-shaped cross-section. A study by Zhang et al. indicated that NiTi endodontic instruments are more prone to failure due to torsional excess than bending excess.24 They also established the correlation between the shape of a file's cross-section and its susceptibility to fracture. Previous studies showed that files with triangular cross-sections are less resistant to torsional forces. Versluis et al. discovered that stress accumulation at the top and bottom margins of the cross-section during bending tests resulted in less flexibility for endodontic rotary files with larger cross-sectional surface areas than for those with smaller cross-sectional surface areas.25

Schafer et al. proposed that the configuration of the cross-section is the primary factor in determining the bending properties of instruments.26 It is plausible to hypothesize that an instrument with a greater diameter, cross-section, or taper will possess higher bending stiffness. However, a study by Miyai et al. stated that other factors, such as the transformation characteristics of the alloy, would influence the bending property as well as the torsional property of an instrument.27 Literature has shown that Reciproc Blue exhibits greater flexibility and cyclic fatigue resistance due to the addition of a titanium oxide layer to its surface.17 These findings can explain the lower stress concentration in the cross-section of Reciproc Blue.

All instruments' contact with the root canal models is a simplification that does not occur in clinical settings. To standardize the processing phase and facilitate result comparability, this contact pattern was considered in the current study. Calculating the ideal coefficient for the instrument and dentin tissue will require further in vitro research.

The FEA showed that the stress propagated throughout a wide region of deep dentin due to the rotational forces of an instrument inside the root canal. This implies that the rotational movement of the instrument applies stress to the entire thickness of the apical root dentin. A “stress tunnel” effect was apparent, as stress propagation was observed throughout the root curvature's radius, extending from the coronal to the apical region.

While this study employed a transient simulation, it did not account for dynamic effects related to the instrument's rotational speed. Phase transformations can be induced by heat generated from the rotation of the files during loading and unloading, as well as by frictional heat from the file sliding against the canal. This study did not examine the impact of austenite-martensite phase transformations on fatigue response. However, prior research indicates that increased strain does not always correlate with reduced fatigue life, as higher martensite fractions can enhance performance under cyclic loading.28 Currently, a standardized method for incorporating this effect into FE simulations is not established.

Limitations

While simulated root canal anatomy provides valuable foundational insights, it often fails to capture the extensive anatomical variations and complex curvatures characteristic of primary teeth in a clinical setting. Furthermore, because this analysis was conducted under static, simplified loading conditions, it overlooks dynamic clinical variables such as cyclic fatigue, operator variability, and torsional stress during continuous rotation. To address these limitations and enhance clinical relevance, future finite element analysis (FEA) studies should integrate dynamic instrumentation movements, cyclic loading, torsional forces, and temperature variations to more accurately replicate real-world conditions. Ultimately, combining FEA with robust in vitro mechanical testing and longitudinal clinical evaluations is essential to validate these simulation models and advance pediatric endodontic outcomes.

Conclusion

The study highlights the importance of instrument design and material properties in endodontic procedures. Understanding stress distribution in dentin is essential for preventing iatrogenic (treatment-induced) damage. In both file systems, the von Mises stress induced by the instruments propagated throughout the dentin, from cervical to apical regions, with the highest stress concentration observed at the instrument's apex, as it is the most engaged part of the instrument. The lower stress distribution may be attributed to the instrument's cross-section and elasticity, which significantly impact mechanical performance. Overall stress induced by the instruments was lower in Reciproc Blue.

Clinical Significance

The success of endodontic treatment in primary teeth largely depends on the choice of rotary files that are safe, flexible, and efficient. This study highlights how the design and taper of pediatric rotary files can directly influence their mechanical performance during clinical use. A better understanding of these properties can help clinicians select suitable instruments, minimize the risk of file fracture, and ensure safer and more predictable outcomes in pediatric endodontics.
