Saffron sporopollenin modified with graphene oxide for efficient extraction of acetaminophen from serum using deep eutectic solvent prior to HPLC
Authors: Mohammad Khodadadi, Fahimeh Amirkhani, Javad Feizy, Haleh Omoori Sarabi, Gholamali Dorooshi, Morteza Pourfarzam, John P Giesy, Hasan Badibostan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA facile and environmentally benign or “green” analytical method was developed for the extraction of acetaminophen (APAP) from human blood serum. Fe3O4-saffron stamen sporopollenin/graphene oxide (Fe3O4-SSSP/GO) and deep eutectic solvent (DES) were employed as natural adsorbent and desorption components, respectively. The structure of magnetic nanoparticles and DES was investigated through Fourier-transformed infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The main factors influencing the analytical response were optimized by central composite design (CCD). Under the optimum conditions, the linearity of this method ranged from 15.6 to 1500 µg L− 1 and 3500–50,000 µg L− 1, with coefficient of determinations (R2) of 0.991 and 0.993, respectively. The relative standard deviations (RSDs) were less than 6.3%. The limits of detection (LOD) and the limit of quantification (LOQ) were 5.1 to 15.6 µg L− 1. The developed method has been successfully applied to the quantification of APAP in spiked samples of blood serum, with recoveries between 81% and 98.2%. The developed method shows significant potential for the bioanalysis of APAP in medical laboratories, given its green characteristics and satisfactory performance metrics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29533-x.Keywords: Paracetamol, Bioanalysis, Green chemistry, Central composite design, Solid-Phase extraction, Human healthSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

APAP is a widely used analgesic and antipyretic drug1. In therapeutic doses, APAP is safe and primarily converts into inactive metabolites2. However, in toxic doses, high levels of the toxic by-product N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) are produced. The depletion of intracellular GSH resources along with excessive production of NAPQI, create reactive oxygen species (ROS) which can cause hepatocyte death, extensive hepatic necrosis, and even fulminant liver failure3. The liver is a crucial organ in the human body, performing important functions such as detoxification, biotransformation, and plasma protein synthesis4. In fact, APAP hepatotoxicity is the leading cause of acute liver failure (ALF) in many countries and has received significant attention in clinical settings5. APAP overdoses are the leading cause of ALF in the United States, accounting for 50% of all cases of ALF and carry a 30% mortality rate6. Quantification of APAP in human blood serum is a vital step in the management of patients with APAP poisoning7. Some patients do not know they have had toxic effects of APAP or do not admit that they have been abusing this readily available pain reliever. Thus, measurement of APAP and its metabolites in blood samples is a useful diagnostic tool.

Preparation of samples is a vital step to concentrate, purify, and remove interfering matrices prior to identification and quantification by instrumental analysis. In recent decades, liquid–liquid extraction (LLE) and solid-phase extraction (SPE) have been the most frequently used methods for sample preparation8. LLE has two major limitations: the consumption of large amounts of toxic organic solvents and the fact that it is time-consuming and labor-intensive. In comparison with LLE, SPE requires lower volume of solvent. However, SPE is relatively expensive and labor-intensive. Magnetic solid-phase extraction (MSPE) is a new type of SPE that employs magnetic adsorbents. In this procedure, analytes are adsorbed into the adsorbent and separated using an external magnetic field8.

In recent years, natural polymers have been extensively used for the extraction and pre-concentration of pharmaceutical and chemical compounds9,10. Sporopollenin (SPP) is a naturally occurring biopolymer that can be obtained from plant spores and pollen grains. SPP exhibits unique chemical properties, including inertness, thermal stability, a large inner cavity and uniformity in size and shape. It is a cheap, extremely porous, eco-friendly biomaterial with stable microencapsulation capacity11. Based on these characteristics, SPP has been used for the encapsulation of enzymes, solid-phase synthesis of peptides, synthesis of magnetic nanoparticles, and chromatography12. Fe3O4-modified SPP has been used for the adsorption of polychlorinated biphenyls and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from water13. The magnetized SPP was modified with cyanopropyltriethoxysilane to obtain an eco-friendly biosorbent. This biosorbent was then employed for the extraction of selected non-steroid anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) from aqueous samples14. In another study, the magnetic SPP was modified with N-methylglucamine-functionalized calix arene. This adsorbent was employed for removal of boron (B) from the aqueous environment15. A novel adsorbent, based on magnetite-SPN/graphene oxide (GO) (Fe3O4-SPP/GO), was developed for the removal of polar organophosphorus pesticides from vegetables16. This adsorbent combines the merits of superior adsorption capacity of the modified SPP with GO and Fe3O4, allowing for easy separation16.

Recently, an emerging class of green solvents known as DESs have been proposed as an alternative to traditional organic solvents in extraction techniques17. They are liquids composed of hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) and hydrogen bond acceptors (HBAs). DESs offer numerous benefits, including simple preparation, minimal toxicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability18,19. DESs have been used in combination with methods of extraction for the separation and pre-concentration of various analytes20,21. Due to the properties of DESs, they have been used in various methods for desorption of analytes22,23. These green solvents are able to increase the extraction efficiency via hydrogen bonding and dipole-dipole interactions24. Based on this property, an eco-friendly and cost-effective method based on MSPE was reported for extraction of parabens from water and urine25. Similarly, DESs were used as an elution solvent for extraction of hydroxylated metabolites of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from urine samples26.

Despite the advances in SPE technology, synthesis of an extremely efficient, cost-effective, and eco-friendly sorbent for selective extraction of APAP from complex biological matrices remains an analytical challenge. Therefore, the main aim of this study was to synthesize a novel, sorbent by natural resources for selective extraction of APAP. In this research, we introduce a novel and environmentally friendly analytical method for APAP extraction by synergistically combining Fe3O4- SSSP/GO, with DES as the eluent. The Fe3O4- SSSP/GO derived from natural sporopollenin, offers effective adsorption capacity with multi-modal mechanisms and allows for fast separation with its magnetic core. The use of a DES as a green eluent was also investigated as a way to enhance the sustainability of the method. The adsorption and desorption parameters of the developed method were optimized to achieve the greatest efficiency of extraction.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemicals and reagents were of reagent grade or higher. APAP and Trichloroacetic acid (TCA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). HPLC grade of water and acetonitrile (ACN, 99.9% purity) was purchased from Duksan (Seoul, South Korea). A standard stock solution of APAP was made by dissolving APAP in a mixture of water: ACN (50:50 v/v) at a concentration of 1780 mg L− 1. This solution was wrapped in aluminum foil and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C. The stock solution was diluted accordingly to obtain working standard solutions with different concentrations. Saffron samples were collected from Khorasan Razavi Province of Iran. Samples of blood serum were collected from volunteers with permission and informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. (Ethical code: IR.ARI.MUI.REC.1402.271). The absence of APAP in the initial blank serum matrix was verified by performing an HPLC analysis on the pooled sample.

Synthesis of saffron stamen Sporopollenin

Twenty-five grams of saffron stamen sporopollenin (SSSP) was refluxed in a round-bottom flask containing acetone and ultrapure water. SSSP were filtered through nylon mesh with pores of 250 μm in diameter and filter paper with pores of 6 μm. Two gram of obtained sample was added to 25 mL of diethyl ether and stirred at 400 rpm and 25 °C for 120 min. Finally, SSSP were collected and dried in a fume hood for 12 h27.

Synthesis of magnetic SSSP

Fe3O4- SSSP was prepared as described previously16. Briefly, 4.3 mmol of FeCl2·4H2O and 8.6 mmol of FeCl3·6H2O (1:2) were added to 200 mL deionized water in a three-necked flask. A 0.5 g aliquot of SSSP was transferred to this solution. This solution was sonicated and degassed for 30 min and stirred for 1 h at 50 °C in the presence of N2 gas stream. A volume of 10 mL of 32% ammonia solution was added dropwise with continuous stirring until a black precipitate solution was observed at pH 9. The mixture was stirred for 50 min, and the heat was then turned off, after which the mixture was stirred continuously for about 5 h. Magnetic-SSSP were separated using an external magnet, then washed sequentially with pure water followed by ethanol. The obtained materials were dried overnight at 100 °C.

Preparing and screening of DESs

To acquire an appropriate DES, various DESs were prepared with a variety of compounds, including thymol, ethylene glycol (EtGl), urea, propylene glycol and L-carnitine. To prepare DESs, these compounds at significant molar ratios (Table 1) were mixed in a screw cap-glass test tubes and placed in an oil bath. Then the mixtures were continuously stirred using a magnetic stirring apparatus (70–90 °C for 1–2 h) until clear and homogenous liquids formed. The obtained liquid DESs were maintained at room temperature.

APAP extraction

A 100 µL serum sample was placed in a microtube. Efficient removal of protein was achieved by adding 100 µL of TCA to the human blood serum. The solution was then mixed by vortexing and centrifuged at 8,000 rpm to separate the solid precipitate. Five mg of sorbent were added to the supernatant, and the mixture was vortexed for 5 min. After that, a strong magnet was placed at the bottom of the tube until the solution became clear, and the sorbents were collected. The separated sorbents were eluted with an appropriate DES solvent. Three obtained DESs were tested as elution solvents. Under the same desorption conditions, 100 µL of DES was added to the sorbents and vortexed for 2 min. Subsequently, the sorbents were collected with a magnet, and the eluent was injected into an HPLC.

Chromatographic analysis

The analysis of APAP was performed using an Elite LaChrom HPLC System with L-2400 UV multi-wavelength detector (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). A C18 column with 5 μm particle size (Macherey Nagel, Germany), 250 mm length, and 4.6 mm internal diameter was used for separation at room temperature. The mobile phase was a mixture of water: ACN (70:30, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min− 1 and run time of 14 min. The injection volume and the detection wavelength was 20 µL, and 272 nm, respectively.

Results and discussion

Characterization

FT-IR analysis

Functional groups present in the nano-materials synthesized from Fe3O4, GO, SSSP, Fe3O4-SSSP, and Fe3O4-SSSP/GO were investigated and characterized using FT-IR spectra. The broad peak observed at 3418 cm− 1 for all synthesized materials is attributed to the stretching vibration of –OH groups. Peaks at 1670 cm− 1 and 1695 cm− 1 are associated with the C = O stretching vibration of the COOH group found in Fe3O4-SSSP/GO and SSSP, respectively. The peaks at 2898 cm− 1 and 2872 cm− 1 correspond to CH2 and C-H groups present in SSSP and Fe3O4-SSSP. The peak at 580 cm− 1 confirms the successful conversion of Fe3O4 to Fe3O4-SSSP, as indicated by the Fe-O bond. Additionally, C = C stretching vibrations are represented by peaks at 1603 cm− 1 and 1630 cm− 1 in Fe3O4-SSSP/GO and Fe3O4-SSSP, respectively. The peak near 1220 cm− 1 in Fe3O4-SSSP/GO was attributed to the stretching vibration of C-O (carboxyl) groups. The stretching vibration of C-O (epoxy) observed at approximately 1450 cm− 1 is characteristic of Fe3O4-SSSP and SSSP. In the case of Fe3O4-SSSP/GO, the C-O (Alkoxy) stretching peak is noted at around 845 cm− 1. This finding suggests the presence of various functional groups, including -OH, C = O, and C-O on the surface of the SSSP. Additionally, the absorption bands located at approximately 579 cm− 1 and 550 cm− 1 are associated with the Fe-O stretching mode in both Fe3O4- SSSP and Fe3O4- SSSP/GO, confirming the successful modification of Fe3O4- SSSP and Fe3O4- SSSP/GO. The gradual reduction of CH2 and CH3 in SSSP and Fe3O4- SSSP (Figure S1 a, b), further supports the successful modification of SSSP and the conversion of GO into Fe3O4-SSSP/GO (Figure S1 c). Upon modification of Fe3O4 with SSSP, peaks corresponding to epoxy, C = C, and CH2 are observed at 1450, 1630, and 2898 cm− 1, respectively, indicating the effective modification of Fe3O4 to Fe3O4-SSSP.

The formation of hydrogen bonding between the chloride anion of ChCl and EtGl is the driving force for the formation of DES. FT-IR spectra of ChCl, EtGl, and ChCl: EtGl: water DES were investigated (Fig. 1). The broad band at 3292 cm− 1 corresponding to the stretching vibration of the O–H group in EtGl shifted to 3332 cm− 1 in ChCl: EtGl: water DES. This shift towards higher wavenumber with the incorporation of ChCl, water and EtGl was due to the decrease in the extent of intermolecular hydrogen bonding between EtGl molecules. The spectrum of DES was dominated by EtGl, however, an additional characteristic band at 953 cm− 1 originating from ChCl was observed. This band was attributed to the C–N+ stretching. In addition, DES exhibited vibrational bands at 2934 cm− 1 and 2875 cm− 1 referring to C − H stretching, 1480 cm− 1 to the CH2 bending of an alkyl group, and 1084 cm− 1, 1036 cm− 1, and 882 cm− 1 to functional groups, namely C − O stretching, C − C−O asymmetric stretching, and C − C−O symmetric stretching. These results indicated the successful synthesis of ChCl: EtGl: water DES.

SEM analysis

The morphology of Fe3O4-SSSP/GO was investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The Fe3O4 nanoparticles exhibit a spherical shape and show partial aggregation due to magnetic attraction (Fig. 2a). The graphene oxide (GO) occurs as a sheet (Fig. 2b), while the SSSP has pores (Fig. 2c). Following the reaction with Fe3O4, there was a change in the distribution of Fe3O4 within the cavities of SSSP (Fig. 2d), which indicated successful modification of the surface. A notable increase in the surface area for the absorption of Fe3O4- SSSP/GO is evident (Fig. 2e), particularly after modification with GO (Fig. 2d). The presence of GO sheets on the SSSP-modified magnetite surface suggests that analytes can more easily interact with the functional groups of SSSP, GO, and Fe3O4​. This leads to improved adsorption, interaction, and retention of the analytes.

XRD analysis

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the different nanoparticles were acquired using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker, D8ADVANCE, Germany), with results shown in Fig. 3. The XRD pattern for the iron oxide nanoparticles exhibited characteristic peaks at 2θ values of 31, 35.5, 43.5, 62.8, and 74.5°, which correspond to the crystal planes (220), (311), (400), (442), and (511), respectively. These results confirmed the magnetite phase by aligning with the standard bulk magnetite pattern (JCPDS File no.19–0629)28. Following coating with SSSP, a broad, diffuse peak emerged in the 2θ range of 5 to 17°, which is attributed to the amorphous structure of the surrounding SSSP.

The selection of elution solvent

The selection of the appropriate eluting solvent is an important step in the SPE method and directly impacts both the recovery yield and the overall greenness of the method.The desorption efficiency of the DESs was dependent on their physicochemical properties, particularly their hydrogen bonding strength and polarity. DES3 was found to exhibit the highest APAP recovery. This superiority may be attributed to its balanced composition. DES3 appears to benefit from the strong HBA capacity of choline chloride, which could competes with the sorbent surface to disrupt the strong hydrogen bonds with APAP. In addition, the combination of two different HBDs (ethylene glycol and propylene glycol) can provides an ideal solvating environment. The slightly higher hydrophobicity introduced by propylene glycol compared to water (used in DES2) improves the DES’s ability to disrupt the π-π interaction and hydrophobic interactions between the APAP aromatic ring and the Fe3O4- SSSP/GO surface. This synergistic solvation power makes DES3 the most effective eluent for releasing the APAP from the sorbent surface. Therefore, DES3 was used as the elution solvent (Fig. 4).

Experimental design and statistical analyses

The effective factors influencing the analytical response were studied by using face-centered central composite design (FCCD). Based on a survey of the pertinent literature, concentrations of TCA (A), vortex time (B), sorbent amount (C), desorption solvent volume (D) and sample volume (E) were selected as effective factors to be optimized in this study. According to the experimental design matrix, 48 different experimental sets including 42 non-center runs and 6 center points, respectively, were performed in the predetermined range of values: A (3.8–26.2% (w/v)), B (1.15–7.8 min), C (5.7–39.3 mg), D (314.6–985.4 µL), and E (0.7–2 mL) (Table S1). Each factor can influence extraction recovery either positively or negatively. A positive estimated effect suggests that an increase in the variable leads to an enhancement in the response. Conversely, a negative estimated effect implies that a more favorable response is achieved at lesser values of the variable. The p-value was used to assess the significance of each coefficient, representing the strength of interaction between each independent variable. It was determined that the data were adequately represented by the normal probability plot before employing analysis of variances (ANOVA). The normal probability plot of the residuals provides valuable insights into the normality of the error distribution and the independence of the residuals (Fig. 5). The points plotted show a scattering around the regression line, suggesting that the data set is approximately normally distributed.

Residual plots: (a) normal plot of residuals (b) residuals vs. run number.

The regression model underwent a verification process using diagnostic graphs, which are presented as Fig. 5. One crucial graph analyzed was the normal probability plot, specifically for the residuals related to the “recoveries” (Fig. 5a). This particular plot offers valuable insights into whether the model’s errors are normally distributed and whether they are independent of one another. Observation of Fig. 5a revealed that the plotted data points largely clustered around the central regression line. This pattern strongly suggests that the underlying data set closely approximates a normal distribution. Consequently, based on this visual assessment, it was concluded that the model’s residuals exhibit a normal distribution, satisfying a key assumption. To provide additional evidence for the model’s reliability, a “residuals versus run” plot (Fig. 5b) was also examined. The vertical spread of the points appears relatively consistent across the plot, suggesting that the assumption of homogeneity of variance is likely met for the data, and there are no obvious outliers.

The quadratic regression model was statistically significant (p < 0.05. F-test, ANOVA). The F-value for lack of fit was 0.88, which was not significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that the model accurately represents the experimental data.

Three-dimensional response surface plots

The recovery was increased proportionally with the concentration of TCA in the range of 10–16% (Fig. 6a). However, greater concentrations of TCA had little effect on the response. This behavior can be attributed to the fact that at concentrations more than 16% the TCA can decompose APAP, which results in lesser recoveries. While vortex time did not have a significant effect on the recovery of analyte, the recovery was proportional to amount of sorbent in the range of 15–24 mg (Fig. 6b). However, larger amounts of adsorbent resulted in a lesser magnitude of analytical signal. This may be due to the fact that when the amount of sorbent amount is greater than 24 mg, the aggregation of adsorbent particles occurred and therefore, the specific surface area decreased, and the active sites for adsorption of APAP molecules are diminished29. The combined effects of sample volume and amount of sorbent (Fig. 6c) were related to the efficiency of removal of analyte. Sample volume was directly correlated with the analytical signal. Conversely, recovery is significantly and inversely proportional to the amount of sorbent because greater amounts of adsorbent increase the probability of coagulation. Sample volume was directly proportional to the recovery of analyte removed (Fig. 6d). Also, the greater volume of desorption solvent resulted in greater recoveries. This behavior can be explained by the fact that with greater volumes of sample and desorption solvent more space was available onto which nanoparticles were dispersed and the probability of coagulation was minimal, so that the rates of adsorption and desorption were greater when volumes of sample and desorption solvent were greater.

Confirmation test and analytical figures of merit

To assess the reliability of the RSM design, a verification experiment was conducted under the optimal conditions proposed for the method by the model. The analysis of the 3D surface plots and regression of the independent variables yielded the following optimal parameters for the extraction of APAP from a sample of blood serum sample: 20% TCA concentration, 6 min vortex time, 20 mg sorbent amount, 800 µL of desorption solvent volume, and 1.2 mL sample volume. The predicted values were 99.9%, and the observed efficiency of APAP closely corresponded with the predicted efficiencies of extraction (Table 3).

In order to validate and evaluate the proposed method, the analytical features including LOD, LOQ, repeatability, and accuracy were determined under optimal conditions. LOD and LOQ were calculated based on signal to noise ratio30. These parameters were determined in a series of experiments with blood serum spiked with known amounts of APAP (Figure S2). Accuracy and precision were evaluated by spiking three replicates human blood serum with five concentrations, 0.05, 0.5, 5, 25 and 50 mg L− 1. The LOD and LOQ were 5.1 and 15.3 µg L− 1, respectively. Percent relative standard deviations (RSD%) were between 2.1 and 11.4. The proposed method has excellent linearity in the range of 15 to 1500 µg L− 1 and 3000 to 50,000 µg L− 1with R2 of 0.991 and 0.993, respectively.

Comparative study

Magnetic-molecular imprinting polymer (m-MIP) resulted in a small LOD (0.17 µg L− 1)31 (Table 4). However, m-MIP required a larger volume of blood serum. When the stir-membrane liquid–liquid microextraction (SM-LLME) was employed, the LOD was 0.5 µg L− 1 APAP32. However, SM-LLME had a greater time of extraction (30 min) and also used a larger volume of sample. While the Fe3O4-SSSP/GO offers advantages in terms of green chemistry, its analytical performance, particularly in terms of LOD, is slightly less sensitive compared to m-MIPs (0.17 µg L− 1). The majority of sorbents utilized previously were synthetic, whereas Fe3O4-SSSP/GO sorbent is derived from natural sources. The benefits of employing natural sorbents for extraction include their accessibility from renewable resources, minimal toxicity, and biodegradability. Also, this method is more compatible with workers and the environment because it does not use toxic reagents or solvents. The extraction efficiency of the sorbent towards APAP is likely attributed to the synergistic effect of multiple intermolecular adsorption mechanisms. Firstly, the π-π interaction plays a dominant role, arising from the strong affinity between the aromatic ring of APAP and GO sheets. Secondly, hydrogen bonding occurs between the polar functional groups of APAP (specifically the hydroxyl (-OH) and amide (-NHC = O) moieties(and the rich oxygen-containing groups -OH and –COOH present on both the GO surface and the SSSP. This dual-site hydrogen bonding provides highly specific points for the analyte. Furthermore, hydrophobic interactions between the non-polar sections of APAP and the hydrophobic components of the SSSP can be a driving force for the analyte transfer from the aqueous biological matrix to the sorbent phase.

The π-π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions between the analytes and the compound imply that Fe3O4-SSSP/GO has shown significant potential for extracting pharmaceuticals, pesticides, metals, and organic pollutants from the environment.

While the methodology developed is green, easy, and effective for the extraction of APAP, there are some limitations compared to other methodologies. The method sensitivity, with an LOD of 5.1 µg L− 1, is not as low as other more synthetically complex methods such as the m-MIP method, with an LOD of 0.17 µg L⁻¹. This means that for applications requiring extremely high sensitivity, further optimization may be required.

An online heart-cutting two-dimensional liquid chromatography technique was developed for the quantification of APAP and its main metabolites including N-Acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) and protein-derived cysteine (APAP-CYS) conjugates. This method provided LOD of 0.03, 0.13 and 0.01 mg L− 1 for APAP, NAPQI and APAP-CYS, respectively. This technique could successfully separated metabolites with different polarity, but it relied on toxic conventional solvents33.

A chromatographic method was introduced for simultaneously detection of APAP, NAPQI and N-acetyl-l-cysteine. In the proposed method, 1 mL of plasma samples were mixed with 3 mL of acetonitrile and centrifuged for 30 min. The supernatant was evaporated under nitrogen. The analytes were re-dissolved in mobile phase consisting of water, methanol, and formic acid in a proportion of. 20 µL of this solution was introduced to the HPLC. In comparison with our method, this technique is time-consuming and has high LOD of 300 µg L− 134.

The developed method, demonstrated satisfactory performance compared to previous APAP extraction methods in biological samples (Table 4). With good recovery and a very wide linear range, this method is suitable for monitoring therapeutic and toxic concentrations of APAP in clinical settings. However, it must be acknowledged that the Limit of Detection (LOD) of this method (5.1 µg L− 1) is higher than that of some more specialized and advanced extraction techniques, such as the m- MIP method (0.17 µg L− 1)31. This difference in sensitivity represents a reasonable trade-off for the outstanding advantages of this method in terms of green chemistry, which include the simplicity of the natural adsorbent synthesis, high extraction speed, and the elimination of hazardous organic solvents by replacing them with DES.

For simplicity and practicality, the proposed method maintains the sample preparation step simple and fast (with an extraction time of only 6 min) without needing time-consuming centrifugation, filtration, or long extraction times (such as 30 min in SM-LLME). its sensitivity is significantly better than the reported LOD of a single LC-MS/MS method and it is less expensive because it uses HPLC-UV instead of costly LC-MS/MS instrumentation.

Solid-phase microextraction: SPME; GC-ECD: Gas chromatography-Electron capture detector, LC-MS/MS: Liquid Chromatography Tandem Mass Spectrometry; SM-LLME-LC-UV: Stir-membrane liquid–liquid microextraction technique- Liquid Chromatography- ultraviolet; m-MIP: Magnetic molecularly imprinted polymer; 2-D LC system: Two-dimensional liquid chromatography.

Limitations and future opportunities

Despite its potential for use in medical laboratories, the method has some limitations. The DES synthesis and the Fe₃O₄-SSSP/GO adsorbent preparation are multi-step processes that although yielding a greener method, are time-consuming processes. Future work should prioritize developing simpler, single-step, and cost-effective synthesis routes. Also, greenness evaluation such as AGREE, AGREEprep, and GAPI is required. For its successful clinical applications, the method must be validated using a large cohort of real clinical samples from patients to thoroughly assess matrix effects and interference from co-administered drugs and metabolites.

Conclusions

A new method for the extraction of APAP from blood serum based on Fe3O4- SSSP/GO and DES was developed, optimized, and tested. It uses a natural adsorbent, Fe₃O₄-SSSP/GO, and a DES for desorption. Under the optimum conditions, the proposed method shows good linearity in the range of 15 to 1500 µg L− 1 and 3000 to 50,000 µg L− 1with R2 of 0.991 and 0.993, respectively. The LOD and LOQ were 5.1 and 15.6 µg L − 1, respectively. Furthermore, the method was successfully applied to spiked blood serum samples, achieving high recoveries between 81% and 98.2%. The method is greener and more efficient by using DES, which is safer, sustainable and reduces reagent use.

Though this method effectively demonstrated successful recoveries on spiked human serum samples, the effectiveness of this method upon real clinical samples, which could also harbor a substantial variety of interfering substances, was not specifically assessed. This method provides a high potential for APAP bioanalysis in clinical laboratories because of its greener aspects and acceptable result performances.
