Reply to R. Naeije:

We thank our esteemed colleague R. Naeije for his interest in our meta-analysis [1], and agree that the discussion around the correct upper limit of normal (ULN) for the pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) is not new. We also agree that even a PAWP difference of a single mmHg matters in clinical practice, as it can have direct and profound implications for clinical decision-making, emphasising the importance of this discussion.

For our meta-analysis, we utilised three independent thoroughly searched databases that enabled us to include the sizeable number of 960 healthy subjects, the largest available number of subjects with invasive pulmonary haemodynamic measurement to date. Of note, in our opinion it is unlikely that a larger dataset of invasive pulmonary haemodynamic measurements in healthy subjects will be available in the future, as nowadays this diagnostic approach is not deemed ethical in healthy subjects. Using the preferred statistical method (the random effects model with a 97.5 percentile) the ULN of PAWP based on all considered studies was 13 mmHg. When only analysing subsets of this whole cohort, the ULN in some cases was lower, at 12 mmHg, due to lower estimated standard deviations. However, from a statistical point of view, a larger sample size is to be preferred over a smaller sample size, following the “law of large numbers”, a principle that was first described by Gerolamo Cardano in the 16th century and, more famously, formalised for binary data by Jacob Bernoulli. Therefore, when including all subjects from all studies, we may get a more accurate estimate of the “true” standard deviation and the ULN of PAWP based on all identified subjects was 13 mmHg. Here, we would also like to highlight the limitations of any calculated ULN in medicine: even using the preferred method of the 97.5 percentile, this still leaves 2.5% of the subjects above the ULN, although they are healthy.

When it comes to the “right” thresholds for resting PAWP in clinical practice, the discussion, as also highlighted in our meta-analysis, is more complex and many more factors need to be included in the equation beyond the threshold of normality [2]. Our manuscript, indeed, aimed to assess the normal value of resting PAWP only. Decisions regarding the most appropriate threshold for clinical diagnosis and therapy or other purposes need to be done elsewhere, first and foremost by guideline committees and task forces of scientific societies [3–5].

In conclusion, we appreciate the discussion of this important topic and hope that our meta-analysis at least provided clarification for one aspect of a complex question.
