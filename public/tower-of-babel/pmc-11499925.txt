Reversible hydrogen storage in Y2C MXene under the influence of functional groups (F, Cl, OH)
Authors: Thanasee Thanasarnsurapong, Suchanuch Sringamprom, Weekit Sirisaksoontorn, Sirichok Jungthawan, Thanayut Kaewmaraya, Adisak Boonchun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe hydrogen storage potential of the bare MXenes Y2C and terminated Y2CT2, where T is O, OH, or F, were studied using density functional theory (DFT). Hydrogen adsorption and desorption behaviors are simulated by ab initio molecular dynamic simulations. The interaction of the H2-molecules on the Y2C-family was investigated within the projected density of states. Only bare Y2C and Y2C(OH)2 provided Kubas-type interactions. The calculated maximum capacity of H2 in bare Y2C can reached up to 5.041 wt%, whereas the reversible capacity of H2 was up to 2.036 wt%. In Y2C(OH)2, the maximum capacity of H2 storage is 3.477 wt% and the reversible capacity was 1.769 wt%. This indicated that Y2C and Y2C(OH)2 are practical candidates for hydrogen storage applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75662-0.Keywords: Hydrogen storage, MXenes, First-principlesSubject terms: Fuel cells, Density functional theory

Introduction

Hydrogen energy is a clean alternative to fossil fuels, but challenges remain. Its flammable and explosive nature makes storage and transportation difficult1–6. For a potential solution, researchers have begun to explore two-dimensional (2D) materials, and incredibly thin nanostructures with a large surface area. This large surface area allows these materials to trap hydrogen molecules effectively, providing opportunities for a more practical hydrogen fuel future7–17.

MXenes (, n = 1, 2 or 3; M=transition metal, X= C or N), a recently discovered class of 2D materials, showing promise as candidates for hydrogen storage applications18–20. MXenes are synthesized by exfoliating their MAX-phase precursors using hydrofluoric acid, unlocking their exceptional energy storage potential21,22. Their unique properties, such as a highly stable structure and exceptionally large surface area, have garnered significant interest from researchers.

The hydrogen storage properties of the MXenes , , and have been investigated using density functional theory (DFT)23–25. The hydrogen storage capacities of the MXenes and are 8.6 wt% and 9.0 wt%, respectively23,24. MXene surfaces naturally bond with oxygen (O), hydroxyl (OH), and fluorine (F) groups19. However, research on , , and has focused on bare carbine MXenes. Yadav et al. have used theoretical calculations to predict that MXene could store up to 7.6 wt% hydrogen without specifying the terminating functional group25.

However, recent DFT research suggests that surface-bound functional groups (such as O, OH, and F) on MXenes can enhance their interactions with hydrogen. Li et al. have studied the hydrogen storage properties of and its surface terminated group via DFT26. The calculations predicted a maximum hydrogen storage capacity of 8.56 wt% for MXene. However, the reversible capacity, where hydrogen can be released for use, was estimated as 3.42 wt%. Meanwhile, the reversible hydrogen capacity of MXene is 2.66 wt%.

The successful experimental synthesis of functionalized MXene suggested that surface-bound functional groups form readily during the etching process of 27–30. The asymmetric surface terminated groups CClH and CFH have been theoretically investigated in terms of electronic and thermoelectric properties31.

Herein, we investigate the MXene and its surface-bound functional group where T is O, OH, or F using DFT. The interaction of -molecules absorbed on the and surface were investigated within the projected density of states (PDOS). Additionally, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were used to understand how temperature affects the release of molecules. Finally, our work investigated the hydrogen storage capacity of the MXenes and . and are promising candidates for hydrogen storage applications.

To understand how the hydrogen molecules () interacted with MXene sheets, the PDOS of the entire system was examined. Fig 3b illustrates significant hybridization between the Y-d and H-s orbitals from the first hydrogen layer. This hybridization occurred within the energy range of − 2.00 eV to 0.00 eV, close to the Fermi level. This proximity signifies chemisorption, a strong chemical bonding, between the hydrogen atoms and the MXene monolayer. For the second hydrogen layer, the orbital (bonding orbital) hybridized with Y-d and C-p orbitals in the range of − 1.00 to − 0.50 eV.

Additionally, Fig 3b shows the hybridization between the Y-d and C-p orbitals with the antibonding orbital around 2.00 eV to 4.50 eV. These observations suggested a Kubas-type interaction, involving bonding and antibonding orbitals33–35. Finally, the PDOS of the third hydrogen layer reveals hybridization of the orbital with Y-d and C-p orbitals between 3.00 eV and 4.50 eV. However, this energy range is far from the Fermi level, indicating a weak interaction. The difficulty locating the orbital states further supports this notion. These findings suggested that the interaction between the third hydrogen layer and the MXene is weak, characterized by physisorption.

The charge density around the H atoms suggests a strong chemical interaction between the Y atoms and the first layer of H atoms. The presence of Kubas-type interactions between and the molecules in the second layer was confirmed through the observed charge transfer between the second layer of molecules and the sheet. The charge density between the molecules and the sheet indicates that the Y atoms transfer electron density into the orbital of the molecules. Conversely, the charge density around the molecules suggests that the hydrogen molecules transfer electron density from the orbital to an unoccupied d-orbital on the Y atoms. Lastly, the charge density of the third layer of molecules were hardly seen indicated that the third layer adsorbed on +18H through physisorption. Moreover, we also calculated the electron localization function (ELF) and confirmed the molecules had not chemical interaction with the sheet, as shown in Fig. S9(a).

From the previous analysis, it was suspected that H atoms could not desorb from the monolayer due to their chemical bonding interactions. In contrast, molecules in the second layer, which interact via Kubas-type interactions, were expected to adsorb and desorb from under normal conditions. To confirm this, we performed AIMD simulations at temperatures ranging from 100 to 400 K. As shown in Fig. 5a for the final configurations at 300 K and in Fig. S4 for other temperatures, as well as the fluctuations of potential energy as functions of time shown in Fig. 5b for various temperatures, the results indicated that hydrogen atoms in the first layer remained bonded with . In contrast, molecules in the third layer desorbed due to their weak interaction with via physisorption. The adsorption of molecules in the second layer was maintained by Kubas-type interactions up to around 300 K, after which all molecules desorbed. These results suggest that the hydrogen desorption temperature of is approximately 300 K.

Conclusion

In this work, we have systematically investigated the hydrogen storage properties of MXenes and (T = F, Cl or OH) by means of first-principles calculations. In hydrogen adsorption , H-atoms prefer to behave as chemisorption in the first layer, while -molecules prefer to behave as Kubas adsorption in the second layer and act as physisorption in the third layer. The maximum hydrogen capacity of MXenes is 5.041 wt%, including the reversible hydrogen storage capacity of 2.036 wt%, which is from the contribution from the Kubas-type interaction. Moreover, the effect of Cl, F, and OH functional group termination is further studied to clarify the hydrogen storage performance. The OH-group-decorated has the greatest advantages among functional terminated according to the highest reversible hydrogen storage capacity up to 1.769 wt%. These results can provide useful theoretical guidance for future experiment exploration of hydrogen storage to MXenes and extend the knowledge of the hydrogen storage on MXenes.

Methods

DFT calculations were performed with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional37,38, built into the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP)39,40. The projector augmented wave (PAW) method with a plane-wave basis set was used41. For plane-wave expansion, a kinetic energy cutoff of 520 eV was used. To model layered materials accurately, the DFT-D3 developed by Grimme is used to avoid the long-range van der Waals (vdW) interactions42. In geometry relaxation, the process was continued until the cumulative Hellmann-Feynman forces reached a criterion of 0.01 eV/Å, while maintaining energy convergence at a threshold of eV with a supercell. A k-point grid of was integrated over the first Brillouin zone within the -centered Monkhorst-Pack scheme43. A vacuum size of 30.0 Å was added perpendicular to the crystal layer to avoid interactions with neighboring images. We performed ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations with the NVT ensemble using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat, with a total simulation time of 5.0 ps and a time step of 1.0 fs44,45. The VESTA software package was performed to visualize the atomic structures and isosurfaces46. The VASPKIT software was utilized for post-processing the VASP data, which included the density of states analysis and charge density difference analysis47.
