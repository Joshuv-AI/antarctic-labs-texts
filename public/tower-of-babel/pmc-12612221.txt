A bi-objective robust fuzzy programming model to configure a microgrid-based cement supply chain
Authors: Erfan Babaee Tirkolaee, Hêriş Golpîra, Salah Bahramara, Ahvan Javanmardan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesigning sustainable cement supply chains presents a complex challenge due to their high energy consumption, emission intensity, and sensitivity to uncertainty. This study proposes a novel bi-objective robust fuzzy Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) model to configure a microgrid-based cement supply chain that simultaneously considers environmental and economic objectives. The proposed model incorporates local renewable energy resources through microgrids to meet the significant electrical demand of cement production, while also embedding carbon emission caps and dust emission control across the network. To handle uncertainty in demand, energy cost, and emission parameters, a robust fuzzy programming approach is adopted. The model further applies the Weighted Sum Method (WSM) to obtain Pareto-optimal solutions and support multi-criteria decision-making. A realistic illustrative case demonstrates the applicability of the model, and sensitivity analyses reveal how key parameters such as income ratio and satisfaction level serve as powerful managerial levers. The model’s ability to find optimal solutions in under two seconds underscores its computational efficiency. Overall, this research fills a critical gap by offering a comprehensive, uncertainty-aware, and sustainability-driven optimization framework for cement supply chains. Introduction

The increasing global emphasis on sustainability, particularly under the United Nations’ 2030 Agenda1, has prompted industries to incorporate environmental, social, and economic considerations into their Supply Chains (SCs)2. Among these, energy usage has emerged as a central focus due to its direct link to environmental degradation and greenhouse gas emissions3–6. Improving energy efficiency and reducing dependence on non-renewable resources are critical for industries seeking to meet sustainability goals while satisfying growing energy demands7–9.

The cement industry is one of the most energy-intensive sectors worldwide10,11, heavily reliant on fossil-fuel-based electricity generation. Simultaneously, it is a major contributor to environmental pollution, especially in terms of Carbon Dioxide (CO₂) emissions and mineral dust12–15. Recent efforts toward sustainability have shown interest in integrating renewable energy sources such as solar and wind16–18, but actual implementation remains limited, with only around 7% of mining-related industries currently deploying renewable energy19.

To address the dual challenge of energy sustainability and emission reduction, microgrids have emerged as promising energy infrastructures. These localized energy systems can supply electricity to specific regions or facilities through Distributed Generation (DG), thus reducing reliance on large, inefficient central grids20–25. Despite their potential, the application of microgrids in designing sustainable SCs, especially in high-emission industries like cement, remains largely unexplored.

At the same time, uncertainty pervades the operational environment of the cement supply chain (CSC)26,27. Fluctuations in demand, energy prices, emission levels, and resource availability introduce significant complexity into planning and decision-making28–30. Hence, incorporating uncertainty modeling into SC design is crucial to developing realistic and robust solutions31,32. While traditional deterministic models fail to capture this variability, fuzzy and robust optimization (RO) techniques can offer more adaptive and resilient strategies33,34. In fact, there are three main categories which have been typically employed in the literature: (i) stochastic programming, which models uncertainty through known probability distributions, (ii) robust optimization, which seeks solutions resilient to worst-case scenarios, and (iii) fuzzy programming, which addresses vagueness and imprecision in parameters without requiring complete probabilistic information. While stochastic and classical RO approaches are effective when precise statistical data or a set of real scenarios are available, they are less suitable for situations dominated by cognitive uncertainty; that is, uncertainty stemming from human judgment, expert opinion, or imprecise measurement, as is the case in this study for parameters such as demand forecasts, emission rates, and energy costs. In particular, since energy price behavior cannot usually be estimated solely based on prior information in volatile markets, energy cost-related parameters are subject to cognitive uncertainty due to a lack of information. Uncertainty about the amount and rate of emission, which is typically expressed linguistically as “high emission” and “low emission”, is also cognitive; therefore, fuzzy theory is suitable for modeling it. In addition, the research background is rich in studies that have modeled demand forecasting using fuzzy theory, due to the inherent effect of user cognitive bias. For this reason, we adopt a robust fuzzy programming framework, which combines the resilience of RO with the flexibility of fuzzy modeling, thereby ensuring both solution feasibility under worst-case conditions and adaptability to imprecise parameter estimates.

Therefore, this study tries to address the following key research problem: How can a CSC be designed to meet both economic and environmental goals under uncertainty, while also utilizing local renewable energy resources in a microgrid structure?

Background

The background section is divided into two main parts. The first part reviews related studies, and the second part examines research gaps.

Review of related studies

Several recent studies have aimed to improve sustainability in the cement and mining industries by focusing on energy efficiency and emission reduction. For instance, Sahoo, Kumar35 reviewed energy-saving technologies in cement production, while Khan and Sinha36 proposed a location allocation model for sustainable distribution centers without considering renewable energy integration.

Other works, such as Gawusu, Zhang12 and Min, Yan37, addressed sustainability aspects through carbon tax and CO₂ emission reduction, but again without focusing on energy sourcing or SC structure. Similarly, Dinga and Wen27 investigated energy conservation in cement manufacturing without modeling the full SC.

Luo, Liu38 introduced a multi-objective model for using supplementary materials in cement, while Xu, Jiang39 focused on hazardous waste reverse logistics in cement kilns. However, none of these works integrate renewable energy sourcing into a holistic SC model.

On the other hand, studies by d’Hubert, Borguet40, Golpîra et al.41,42, and Molavi, Shi43 explore microgrid applications in industrial systems like mining, ports, or residential energy management. These studies highlight the potential of microgrids to improve energy sustainability but do not focus on the CSC configuration or integrate uncertainty modeling in a detailed, operational planning model.

Moreover, despite the increasing interest in uncertainty modeling in operations research, only a limited number of studies have implemented fuzzy or RO techniques in the context of energy-intensive SCs, particularly those related to construction and cement. For instance, while Rahbari, Khamseh44 and Gharye Mirzaei, Gholami45 developed RO models under pandemic and agricultural settings, respectively, and Luo and Deng46 applied RO in perishable food SCs, such approaches are rarely found in the CSC literature. Even when robust or fuzzy programming has been considered, such as in the works of Chen et al.47,48 or Abdolazimi, Entezari49, the focus remains mostly on general construction networks or specific material flows, without holistically integrating the entire multi-tier SC under energy and emission constraints. This gap is particularly important given the cement industry’s heavy energy consumption and emission footprint.

Furthermore, bi-objective optimization models that jointly address economic performance and environmental sustainability remain scarce in the literature. Although Violi, De Maio50, Valizadeh, Boloukifar51, and Huang, Bi52 proposed models with dual objectives, these works were often limited to healthcare, vaccine distribution, or technological planning in general SCs, and rarely tackled simultaneous profit maximization and pollution reduction within a unified framework. Even those models that considered multiple criteria (e.g., Farazmand, Pishvaee53, Rasmi and Türkay54, and Arbabi, Sadeghi55 either did not capture the uncertainty or failed to integrate local energy resource utilization, microgrids, or dynamic multi-product flows.

Identification of research gaps

Despite a growing body of literature addressing various aspects of sustainability and energy efficiency in the cement and mining industries, a thorough examination of existing studies reveals several significant research gaps. Previous works have individually explored topics such as emission reduction through carbon emission policy, cost minimization in energy planning, or the integration of supplementary materials in cement production. However, these studies often adopt a limited perspective by focusing either on production optimization without considering the full structure of the SC or on environmental impact without incorporating renewable energy or uncertainty.

Moreover, while some researchers have proposed models for energy-efficient systems in specific industrial settings (e.g., ports, buildings, or construction), the application of microgrid-based energy systems within an integrated CSC network has not been adequately addressed. Notably, the literature rarely considers the joint optimization of economic and environmental objectives using RO techniques under uncertain parameters such as fluctuating demand, cost variability, and emission limits. Furthermore, very few studies investigate the role of local energy resources in powering different SC entities such as suppliers, production units, and distribution centers.

Accordingly, the literature shows several critical gaps:

Problem definition

The cement industry is among the most emission-intensive and energy-dependent sectors, posing significant challenges for sustainable operations. Designing a CSC that is both profitable and environmentally responsible has become a pressing necessity, particularly in the face of uncertainty stemming from fluctuating demand, energy availability, and environmental regulations. This study addresses this issue by proposing an optimization-based framework that simultaneously considers economic and environmental performance while integrating local renewable energy resources into the network via microgrids.

As shown in Fig. 1, the SC network considered in this study consists of three main echelons: suppliers of essential raw materials (including marl, limestone, silica, gypsum, and slag), production plants encompassing three distinct stages (clinker production, bulk cement production, and packaging for bagged cement), and distribution centers that deliver final products namely bulk cement, bagged cement, and clinker to market centers. Each product type has its own transportation requirements, which are addressed through a set of predefined transportation modes aligned, with its physical and logistical characteristics.

Graphical representation of the CSC.

The decision-making process within this SC involves the selection of suppliers and energy sources, the allocation of material and energy flows among different echelons over multiple time periods. In addition, energy provisioning is managed through the operation of DG units, Battery Energy Storage (BES) systems, and, where needed, energy trading with the main power grid. These energy-related decisions are integrated directly into the SC planning framework to ensure both operational feasibility and environmental responsibility.

The problem is formulated as a bi-objective MILP model. The first objective is to maximize the total profit of the SC by considering revenues from product sales and deducting all relevant costs, including procurement of raw materials, production activities, energy provisioning, handling, transportation, and interactions with the power grid. The use of profit maximization as the economic objective, as opposed to cost minimization, allows a more realistic reflection of the trade-offs faced by decision-makers in practice. Profit incorporates both revenue generation and cost control, which is critical for firms aiming to remain competitive while being environmentally compliant. The main stakeholder in this model is assumed to be a centralized CSC manager or a planner aiming to configure an optimized and sustainable network. The second objective is to minimize total dust emissions generated throughout the SC, including emissions from raw material extraction and handling, production processes, and transportation activities, which is especially critical given the environmental footprint of cement operations. To reflect the complexities of real-world operations, the model incorporates various practical constraints. These include limitations on the power generation capacity of DGs, charging and discharging bounds of BESs, and power flow balance across all units. Additionally, product flow constraints ensure that supply meets demand at each stage while accounting for the storage capacity of facilities. Carbon emission constraints are introduced to comply with sustainability policies and environmental regulations, ensuring that total emissions from suppliers, plants, and logistics activities remain within the allowable carbon cap.

To address the inherent uncertainty in key parameters such as demand levels, energy prices, and emission coefficients, the model incorporates a robust fuzzy programming approach. This allows the decision-maker to simultaneously consider fuzziness (to account for imprecise information) and robustness (to ensure feasible solutions under worst-case scenarios). Furthermore, the bi-objective nature of the model is handled using the Weighted Sum Method (WSM), which enables the transformation of the dual objectives into a scalar optimization problem and facilitates the generation of Pareto-optimal solutions.

Through this integrated approach, the proposed model aims to configure a CSC that not only aligns with financial goals but also complies with environmental and energy sustainability standards under realistic and uncertain conditions. Based on the above explanations, the assumptions presented are as follows: (1) The SC includes three types of products: bulk cement, bagged cement, and clinker, (2) The network is multi-period and is considered seasonally, (3) Suppliers and production plants are capacity constrained, (4) Inventory and shortages are not considered over the planning horizon, (5) The electrical energy demand at all nodes is fully or partially satisfied through local renewable energy via a microgrid infrastructure, (6) Dust emissions are calculated based on the mass flow of materials and the type of activity (e.g., production, transport), (7) Carbon cap and dust emission constraints are imposed to ensure compliance with environmental policies, and (8) Parameters such as production cost, transportation cost, energy cost, demand levels, and emission coefficients are treated as fuzzy variables.

Uncertainty modelling

Fuzzy chance-constraint programming

The fuzzy chance-constrained programming approach is selected to handle uncertainty in parameters such as demand, emission rates, and energy costs due to its compatibility with situations involving vague, linguistic, imprecise, or expert-based estimations66,67. Compared to probabilistic or stochastic models that require precise distribution data68,69, fuzzy programming allows modeling of ambiguity using trapezoidal fuzzy numbers and necessity measures, providing flexible and robust solutions under real-world uncertainty where statistical data may be limited or unavailable70–72. This approach is one of the most compatible and applicable models to treat the uncertainty of parameters used in the objective function and constraints73–76. It is a well-structured approach that depends on deep-rooted mathematical theorems, such as the expected value of a fuzzy number in objective functions, as well as the possibility and necessity measures for constraints77. According to Inuiguchi and Ramık78, triangular and trapezoidal fuzzy numbers are the two well-known forms to cope with the fuzzification of uncertain models. Here, the trapezoidal fuzzy distribution is taken into account to create a fuzzy model along with the necessity measure to control the satisfaction level of the constraints. Weighted sum method

To solve the bi-objective optimization model, the WSM is employed, which is one of the most widely used and intuitive scalarization techniques in multi-objective optimization80,81. WSM transforms multiple conflicting objectives into a single aggregate function, enabling the efficient generation of Pareto-optimal solutions with relatively low computational complexity82,83. This simplicity makes WSM highly interpretable and easy to implement, which is especially advantageous in real-world MILP-based SC and energy models84. Furthermore, it facilitates sensitivity and trade-off analysis, which is crucial for informed managerial decision-making. Although more advanced techniques such as the ε-constraint85 and goal programming86 methods exist, WSM continues to be favored in many recent engineering and SC studies due to its practical applicability79,87–89. Therefore, integrating WSM with fuzzy programming supports the study’s goals of flexibility, computational efficiency, and practical relevance.

Discussion: theoretical vs. practical insights

The development of a bi-objective robust fuzzy MILP model contributes notably to the literature on sustainable SC optimization, particularly in industries with high environmental impact like cement production. This model advances current theoretical approaches by integrating sustainability into the design of SCs through microgrid-based energy management. The research extends the boundaries of multi-objective optimization models by incorporating both economic (profit maximization) and environmental (dust minimization) objectives into a unified framework. Furthermore, the use of fuzzy programming to address pervasive uncertainty adds depth to the RO literature, demonstrating how uncertainty in demand and SC operations can be handled effectively. In terms of energy sustainability, the developed model highlights the potential for integrating local renewable energy sources within cement manufacturing, which is considered as an energy-intensive industry. It bridges the gap between SC management and energy systems, offering a dual focus on reducing operational costs and environmental impact. This model also contributes to the theory of microgrid optimization by demonstrating how DG can be utilized to manage energy consumption while mitigating greenhouse gas emissions and dust pollution, issues that are particularly critical in the cement industry.

Microgrid-based CSC design comprises a significant step towards a more sustainable, efficient, and resilient industry. While practical challenges such as high initial costs, network integration complexities, and technical barriers exist, the accuracy of SC planning and network design leads to significant improvements in different directions, such as renewable energy adoption. The strategic implementation of microgrids can not only enhance operational efficiency and cost-effectiveness but also contribute notably to the global attempts to mitigate the carbon footprint of cement production. As discussed in this work and the proposed microgrid-based CSC, it is clear that sustainability plays a key role in the network, which leads to an efficient utilization of renewable local energy resources. The income ratio was demonstrated to be the most important factor controlling the system’s profitability directly, such that a 20% decrease in this factor will result in a negative total profit. Managers must seek for a practical way to enhance the market share and continuously follow up on this issue. Furthermore, defining logical minimum satisfaction levels must be taken into consideration to adjust the severity of the uncertainty and flexibility of the system. According to the results, 50% seems to be a normal value, leading to acceptable results.

The findings and sensitivity analyses in this work provide several actionable insights for managers seeking to configure a sustainable, microgrid-based CSC under uncertainty, as follows:

I.Prioritize microgrid deployment at high-consumption nodes.According to Tables 4, 5 and 6, suppliers account for 82.46% of the network’s total electricity consumption (6,596,464 kWh annually), followed by production plant stages (15.49%, 1,239,192 kWh) and distribution centers (2.05%, 163,801.6 kWh). This hierarchy is consistent across seasons. Installing microgrid systems powered by local renewable sources at supplier sites can therefore yield the largest reductions in annual grid electricity purchases. Managers should target these high-consumption nodes first to maximize the benefits of grid independence.

Conclusion and outlook

This study presented a novel bi-objective robust fuzzy MILP model for designing a microgrid-based CSC under uncertainty. The proposed model integrates local renewable energy sources into the SC to address both economic profitability and environmental responsibility, specifically minimizing dust emissions. Uncertainties in demand, emissions, and energy cost were addressed using fuzzy chance-constraint programming, and the WSM was used to solve the bi-objective structure.

An illustrative example demonstrated that the model could generate optimal decisions regarding energy source selection, material flow, and emission management in less than two seconds, showing high computational efficiency. The sensitivity analyses revealed two key findings, such that income ratio and minimum satisfaction level are the most influential parameters on both profit and environmental outcomes. Moreover, the use of local energy sources through microgrids not only reduces dependency on the main grid but also significantly lowers total emissions. Consequently, the main contributions of this study lies in the integration of three aspects:

Methodological: The combination of robust fuzzy programming with a bi-objective MILP in the context of a high-emission energy-intensive SC.
