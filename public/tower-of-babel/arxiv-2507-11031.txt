Rapid Mixing of Glauber Dynamics for Monotone Systems via Entropic Independence
Weiming Feng, Minji Yang

Abstract
We study the mixing time of Glauber dynamics on monotone systems. For monotone systems satisfying the entropic independence condition, we prove a new mixing time comparison result for Glauber dynamics. For concrete applications, we obtain O ~ ​ ( n ) \tilde{O}(n) mixing time for the random cluster model induced by the ferromagnetic Ising model with consistently biased external fields, and O ~ ​ ( n 2 ) \tilde{O}(n^{2}) mixing time for the bipartite hardcore model under the one-sided uniqueness condition, where n n is the number of variables in corresponding models, improving the best known results in [Chen and Zhang, SODA’23] and [Chen, Liu, and Yin, FOCS’23], respectively. Our proof combines ideas from the stochastic dominance argument in the classical censoring inequality and the recently developed high-dimensional expanders. The key step in the proof is a novel comparison result between the Glauber dynamics and the field dynamics for monotone systems.

Introduction

Sampling from high-dimensional distributions is a central problem in computing and data science.
A widely-used sampling technique is the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method.
The
Glauber dynamics
(also known as the
Gibbs sampling
) is one of the most fundamental sampling methods in MCMC. Given a distribution
\mu
over
\{0,1\}^{V}
, the Glauber dynamics starts from an arbitrary feasible
configuration

X\in\{0,1\}^{V}
and iteratively updates
X
as follows:

•

select a variable
v\in V
uniformly at random;

•

resample the value of
v
from
\mu_{v}^{X_{V\setminus\{v\}}}
, where
\mu_{v}^{X_{V\setminus\{v\}}}
is the marginal distribution of
\mu
on
v
conditional on the current configuration
X_{V\setminus\{v\}}
of other variables.

One central problem for the Glauber dynamics is to understand its convergence rate, which is captured by the notion of the
mixing time
. The mixing time is the number of iterations that the Glauber dynamics needs to make the distribution of the configuration
X
to be close to the target distribution
\mu
.
For various kinds of distributions, the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics was of great interest and extensively studied
[
29
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the case where the target distribution
\mu
is a
monotone system
.
Monotone systems are an important class of high-dimensional distributions and it is well-studied in the literature of sampling algorithms (e.g. see
[
26
,
33
,
31
,
34
,
32
,
35
,
22
,
24
]
).
For any
X,Y\in\{0,1\}^{S}
, where
S\subseteq V
, define the
partial ordering
:

\displaystyle X\preceq Y\text{ if and only if }X_{v}\leq Y_{v}\text{ for all }v\in S.

(1)

Definition 1.1

(Monotone systems)
.

A distribution
\mu
over
\{0,1\}^{V}
is said to be a monotone system if for
v\in V
, any feasible and comparable pinnings
\sigma,\tau\in\{0,1\}^{V\setminus\{v\}}
with
\sigma\preceq\tau
, the distribution
\mu^{\sigma}_{v}
is stochastically dominated by
\mu^{\tau}_{v}
.

In the definition,
\mu^{\sigma}_{v}
and
\mu^{\tau}_{v}
are marginal distributions of
\mu
on
v
conditional on
\sigma
and
\tau
respectively.
The marginal distribution
\mu^{\sigma}_{v}
is said to be stochastically dominated by
\mu^{\tau}_{v}
if
\mu^{\sigma}_{v}(1)\leq\mu^{\tau}_{v}(1)
. Equivalently, there exists a coupling
(X,Y)
of
\mu^{\sigma}_{v}
and
\mu^{\tau}_{v}
, where
X\sim\mu^{\sigma}_{v}
and
Y\sim\mu^{\tau}_{v}
, such that
X\leq Y
with probability 1.

Consider the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics starting from configuration
X_{0}\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle T^{\textnormal{GD}}_{\textnormal{mix-}X_{0}}(\mu,\varepsilon)=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}\mid d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({P^{t}(X_{0},\cdot)},{\mu}\right)\leq\varepsilon\}.

(2)

where
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({P^{t}(X_{0},\cdot)},{\mu}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}|P^{t}(X_{0},\sigma)-\mu(\sigma)|
denotes the total variation distance between two distributions
P^{t}(X_{0},\cdot)
and
\mu
.
In particular, for monotone systems, one often considers the case where
X_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{V}
is the maximum all-one configuration.

The
censoring inequality

[
34
,
21
]
is a powerful tool to study the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics on monotone systems, which states that extra updates do not delay mixing. Hence, to analyze the mixing time of Glauber dynamics, one can focus on updates in a local region of interest and ignore the updates outside the region. One typical application of the censoring inequality is to compare Glauber dynamics with
block dynamics
.
Given the current configuration
X
, the block dynamics samples a random subset
S\subseteq V
, where
S
is
independent
of
X
, and then updates
X_{S}
conditional on
X_{V\setminus S}
.
With the censoring inequality, proving the mixing of Glauber dynamics can be reduced to proving the mixing of block dynamics and the mixing of Glauber dynamics in local regions
S
.
This framework and its generalizations have achieved great success in many monotone systems
[
19
,
32
,
5
,
7
,
6
]
.

Recent advances in spectral independence
[
4
]
and entropic independence
[
2
]
have led to new techniques for analyzing the mixing time of Glauber dynamics. Many new mixing time results have been established through the analysis of
field dynamics

[
11
,
12
,
9
,
17
,
15
,
8
]
. In field dynamics, given the current configuration
X
, it samples a random subset
S\subseteq V
that is
adaptive
to
X
(i.e., the distribution of
S
depends on the current state), and then updates the configuration
X_{S}
according to a
tilted conditional distribution
. The key challenge in applying the censoring inequality to field dynamics arises from the
correlation
between the adaptive sampling of
S
and the current configuration
X
, combined with the specific update rule for
X_{S}
. Consequently, for some important monotone systems, while the optimal mixing time of field dynamics is understood, the best known mixing time bounds for Glauber dynamics remain sub-optimal.

In this paper, we develop a new comparison result between the Glauber dynamics and the field dynamics for monotone systems, which leads to improved mixing time results from entropic independence.
We first exhibit some concrete applications of the new comparison result in
Section

1.1
and then give our general and technical results in
Section

1.2
.

1.1
Applications

Random cluster model from ferromagnetic Ising model

The ferromagnetic Ising model is a fundamental spin system in statistical physics.
Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected graph with
n
vertices and
m
edges. Let
\beta\in(1,+\infty)^{E}
be the
edge activities
and
\lambda\in[0,1]^{V}
be the
external fields
. The ferromagnetic Ising model
\mu_{\beta,\lambda}^{\textnormal{Ising}}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\forall\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{V},\quad\mu_{\beta,\lambda}^{\textnormal{Ising}}(\sigma)\propto w_{\beta,\lambda}^{\textnormal{Ising}}(\sigma):=\prod_{e\in m(\sigma)}\beta_{e}\prod_{v\in V:\sigma_{v}=1}\lambda_{v},

where
w_{\beta,\lambda}^{\textnormal{Ising}}(\sigma)
denotes the weight of
\sigma
and
m(\sigma):=\{e=\{u,v\}\in E\mid\sigma_{u}=\sigma_{v}\}
is the set of monochromatic edges of
\sigma
.

In the ferromagnetic Ising model, neighboring vertices are more likely to have the same value. If the Glauber dynamics starts from all-ones configuration, then it may take exponential time to reach a configuration dominated by 0s. Alternatively, to draw random samples from the ferromagnetic Ising model, consider the following
random cluster model
.

Let
p\in[0,1]^{E}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]^{V}
be parameters.
For any subset of edges
S\subseteq E
, let
\kappa(V,S)
denote the set of connected components of subgraph
(V,S)
. The random cluster model defines a distribution
\mu_{p,\lambda}^{\textnormal{RC}}
such that for any subset of edges
S\subseteq E
,

\displaystyle\mu_{p,\lambda}^{\textnormal{RC}}(S)\propto w_{p,\lambda}^{\textnormal{RC}}(S):=\prod_{e\in S}\frac{p_{e}}{1-p_{e}}\prod_{C\in\kappa(V,S)}\left(1+\prod_{v\in C}\lambda_{v}\right).

(3)

Here,
C\in\kappa(V,S)
denotes a connected component of subgraph
(V,S)
and
C
denotes the set of vertices in this connected component.
By setting
p_{e}=1-\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}
for all
e\in E
,
one can transfer a random sample from the random cluster model
\mu_{p,\lambda}^{\textnormal{RC}}
to a random sample from the ferromagnetic Ising model
\mu_{\beta,\lambda}^{\textnormal{Ising}}
using a simple coupling in
[
26
,
20
]
.

The problem of sampling from random cluster models was extensively studied.
Assume all
p_{e}\in(0,1)
are constants.
Jerrum and Sinclair
[
26
]
gave the first polynomial time algorithm to sample from the random cluster model with
\lambda=\bm{1}
. Guo and Jerrum
[
22
]
further proved the
\tilde{O}(m^{3}n^{4})
mixing time of the Glauber dynamics for this case. Recently, many works
[
30
,
20
,
17
,
14
]
considered the random cluster model with consistently biased external fields, i.e. for all
v\in V
,
\lambda_{v}\leq 1-\delta_{\lambda}
for some constant
\delta_{\lambda}>0
. Feng, Guo and Wang
[
20
]
proved that the mixing time of Glauber dynamics is
\Delta^{O(\Delta^{2})}\cdot n\log n
, where
\Delta
is the maximum degree of the graph. The mixing time may not be polynomial when the maximum degree is large. Chen and Zhang
[
17
]
gave a
sampling algorithm
(not Glauber dynamics) in time
m\cdot(\log n)^{C}
, where the exponent
C
is a constant depending on
(p_{e})_{e\in E}
and
\delta_{\lambda}
.
By a standard comparison argument, the technique in
[
17
]
also leads to
\tilde{O}(m^{2})
mixing time of the Glauber dynamics.

It is well-known that the random cluster model is a monotone system.
Our first application is a near-linear mixing time of the Glauber dynamics on the random cluster model with consistently biased external fields.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\delta_{p},\delta_{\lambda}\in(0,1)
be constants.
For any graph
G=(V,E)
with
|V|=n
and
|E|=m
, any
p\in[\delta_{p},1)^{E}

starting from the empty set

satisfies

T^{\textnormal{GD}}_{\textnormal{mix-}\varnothing}(\mu_{p,\lambda}^{\textnormal{RC}},\varepsilon)\leq C(\delta_{p},\delta_{\lambda})\cdot m\cdot(\log n)^{O(1)}(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{2},

where
C(\delta_{p},\delta_{\lambda})
is a constant depending on
\delta_{p}
and
\delta_{\lambda}
.

Compared to the results in
[
20
,
17
]
, our mixing time is
\tilde{O}(m)
for all (possibly unbounded degree) graphs. In terms of a sampling algorithm, our result yields an algorithm with constant error in time

, where the exponent of

[

]
runs in time

Bipartite hardcore model

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with maximum degree
\Delta\geq 3
and
\lambda>0
be the external fields.
The hardcore model
\mu^{\textnormal{HC}}_{G,\lambda}
defines a distribution over all independent sets in graph
G
such that

\displaystyle\forall\text{ independent set }S\subseteq V,\quad\mu^{\textnormal{HC}}_{G,\lambda}(S)\propto\lambda^{|S|}.

The uniqueness threshold of the hardcore model is given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}(\Delta)\triangleq\frac{(\Delta-1)^{\Delta-1}}{(\Delta-2)^{\Delta}}\approx\frac{e}{\Delta}.

The famous computational phase transition result shows that if
\lambda\leq\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
, then the hardcore model can be sampled from in polynomial time
[
37
,
8
]
; if
\lambda>\lambda_{c}(\Delta)
, then unless
\textbf{NP}=\textbf{RP}
, a polynomial time sampler does not exist
[
36
]
.

If the graph
G=(V_{L}\uplus V_{R},E)
is bipartite, then the hardness result
[
36
]
can not apply.
The bipartite hardcore model is closely related to the #BIS problem (counting the number of independent sets in the bipartite graph), which is one of the most important open problems in sampling and approximate counting
[
18
]
.
The sampling problem of the bipartite hardcore model was extensively studied both in high-temperature (small
\lambda
) regime
[
27
,
15
]
and low-temperature (large
\lambda
) regime
[
23
,
16
,
28
,
13
,
25
]
.

For bipartite hardcore model, an important high-temperature regime is when the uniqueness condition is satisfied with respect to the degree on one side.
Let
\Delta_{L}
denote the maximum degree of vertices in the left side
V_{L}
. Suppose
\lambda\leq(1-\delta)\lambda_{c}(\Delta_{L})
for some constant
\delta>0
. Chen, Liu and Yin
[
15
]
proved that the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics for

is

\delta

Recently, Chen and Feng
[

]
considered the

\Delta_{R}
on the right side

to satisfy
\Delta_{R}=O(\Delta_{L})
.
For Glauber dynamics starting from the independent set consisting of all vertices in one side,

]
proved the
C(\lambda,\delta)\cdot n(\log n)^{O(1/\delta)}

It is well-known that the bipartite hardcore model is a monotone system.
We give the following mixing time result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\delta>0
be a constant. Let
\Delta_{L}\geq 3
and
\lambda>0
be two parameters satisfying
\lambda<(1-\delta)\lambda_{c}(\Delta_{L})

Compared to the result in
[

to
\tilde{O}(n^{2})

Compared to the result in
[
10

[
10
]
gave a faster

Remark 1.4

1.2
General and technical results

Monotone spin systems with entropic independence

Our general result is a comparison theorem, which works for monotone systems satisfying entropic independence. Let
\mu
be a distribution with support
\Omega\subseteq\{0,1\}^{V}
. For any distribution
\nu
over
\Omega
, define their KL-divergence as
\mathrm{D}_{\textnormal{KL}}(\nu\parallel\mu)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}\nu(\sigma)\log\frac{\nu(\sigma)}{\mu(\sigma)}
. The following notion was introduced in
[
2
]
.

Definition 1.5

(Entropic independence
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\alpha>0
. A distribution
\mu
with support
\Omega\subseteq\{0,1\}^{V}
is said to be
\alpha
-entropically independent if for any distribution
\nu
over
\Omega
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in V}\mathrm{D}_{\textnormal{KL}}(\nu_{i}\parallel\mu_{i})\leq\alpha\mathrm{D}_{\textnormal{KL}}(\nu\parallel\mu),

(4)

where
\mu_{i}
and
\nu_{i}
are the marginal distributions of
\mu
and
\nu
on variable
i\in V
respectively.

Our general result compares the mixing time of Glauber dynamics for
\mu
to the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics for a tilted distribution.

Definition 1.6

(Tilted distributions)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution with support
\Omega\subseteq\{0,1\}^{V}
and
\theta>0
be a real number. The tilted distribution
(\theta*\mu)
is defined as

\displaystyle\forall\sigma\in\Omega,\quad(\theta*\mu)(\sigma)\propto\mu(\sigma)\theta^{\left\|\sigma\right\|_{1}},

(5)

where
\left\|\sigma\right\|_{1}
is the number of 1’s in
\sigma
.

The tilted distribution
(\theta*\mu)
puts an external field on each vertex. For example, if
\mu
is a Hardcore model on graph
G=(V,E)
with parameter
\lambda
, then
(\theta*\mu)
is a hardcore model on
G
with parameter
\theta\lambda
.
For many natural distributions, it is easy to analyze the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics on
(\theta*\mu)
for a proper
\theta\in(0,1)
.
We next define the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics for tilted distributions with pinnings. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, define

\displaystyle T_{\textnormal{mix}}^{\textnormal{tilted}}(\mu,\theta,\varepsilon)\triangleq\max\left\{T_{\textnormal{mix}}^{\textnormal{GD}}((\theta*\mu)^{\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda}},\varepsilon)\mid\Lambda\subseteq V\text{ and }\mu_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda})>0\right\},

(6)

where
(\theta*\mu)^{\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda}}
is the distribution of
\theta*\mu
conditional on the values on
\Lambda
are all pinned as 1 and for any distribution
\nu
, we use
T_{\textnormal{mix}}^{\textnormal{GD}}(\nu,\varepsilon)
to denote the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics
P
on
\nu
starting from an
arbitrary
feasible configuration:

\displaystyle T_{\textnormal{mix}}^{\textnormal{GD}}(\nu,\varepsilon)=\max_{X\in\{0,1\}^{V}:\nu(X)>0}\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}\mid d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({P^{t}(X,\cdot)},{\nu}\right)\leq\varepsilon\}.

(7)

We remark that the mixing time in (
7
) is stronger than the mixing time in (
2
) and we use the stronger mixing time to define the mixing time of tilted distributions in (
6
).

Our general result is a comparison theorem between
T_{\textnormal{mix}}^{\textnormal{tilted}}(\mu,\theta,\varepsilon)
and
T_{\textnormal{mix-}\mathbf{1}_{V}}^{\textnormal{GD}}(\mu,\varepsilon)
when
\mu
is a monotone system satisfying entropic independence.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mu
be a monotone system with support
\Omega\subseteq\{0,1\}^{V}
. Let
\theta\in(0,1)
. Let
\alpha:[0,-\log\theta]\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be an integrable function.
Suppose for any
t\in[0,-\log\theta]
, for any
\Lambda\subseteq V
such that
\mu_{\Lambda}(\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda})>0
, the distribution
(e^{-t}*\mu)^{\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda}}
is
\alpha(t)
-entropically independent.
Then, the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics on
\mu
starting from the maximum all-1 state
\mathbf{1}_{V}
satisfies

\displaystyle T_{\textnormal{mix-}\mathbf{1}_{V}}^{\textnormal{GD}}(\mu,\varepsilon)\leq T\cdot T_{\textnormal{mix}}^{\textnormal{tilted}}\left(\mu,\theta,\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\right),

where

\displaystyle T=O\left(\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1/\theta}4\alpha(t)dt\right)\cdot\left(\log\log\frac{1}{\mu_{\min}}+\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\text{ and }\mu_{\min}=\min_{\sigma\in\Omega}\mu(\sigma).

In applications, by appropriately choosing the parameter
\theta
, we can guarantee that
(\theta*\mu)
is in a non-critical region so that
T_{\textnormal{mix}}^{\textnormal{tilted}}(\mu,\theta,\varepsilon)
is easy to bound. Typically,
T_{\textnormal{mix}}^{\textnormal{tilted}}(\mu,\theta,\varepsilon)=n\cdot\mathrm{polylog}\frac{n}{\varepsilon}
and
\mu_{\min}=e^{-\Theta(n)}
. Hence, if we can verify the entropic independence condition and show that
\exp\left(\int_{0}^{1/\theta}4\alpha(t)dt\right)\leq\mathrm{polylog}(n)
, then
Theorem

1.7
gives a near-optimal
n\cdot\mathrm{polylog}\frac{n}{\varepsilon}
mixing time for the Glauber dynamics starting from the maximum all-1 state
\mathbf{1}_{V}
.

Using field dynamics, previous works
[
11
,
12
,
9
]
compare the
spectral gap
and
modified log-Sobolev (mLS) constants
between the Glauber dynamics on the critical and non-critical regimes.
Our result
Theorem

1.7
directly compares the mixing time of Glauber dynamics on monotone systems.
The spectral gap may lead to a sub-optimal mixing time bound and the mLS constants are sometimes hard to analyze even in the non-critical regime.
Compared to previous works, our comparison result is easier to apply and can obtain the near-optimal mixing time bound for many distributions. However, our result only works for monotone systems.

In
[
10
]
, a mixing time comparison result is proved for Glauber dynamics in the critical and non-critical regimes on monotone systems.
However, their non-critical regime is defined in terms of certain conditional distributions induced from
\mu
while our non-critical regime is defined as the tilted distributions.
Due to this difference, our result works for many monotone systems for which it is unclear whether their result applies.
For example, our
Theorem

1.3
works for general bipartite graphs while their result only works for a special balanced bipartite graphs.
From a technical perspective, the result in
[
10
]
is an application of the standard censoring inequality
[
34
]
while our result is obtained through a new comparison argument between Glauber dynamics and field dynamics.

The mixing time of the Glauber dynamics for

Preliminaries: A general comparison technique

In the preliminaries,
we give and slightly reformulate the comparison technique developed by Fill and Kahn
[
21
]
, which will be used as basic tools in our proof.
Let
\mu
be a distribution with support
\Omega
.
Let
\preceq
be a partial order on
\Omega
.
Define the following class of
increasing
functions. Some literature also call them
non-decreasing
functions.

Definition 2.1

(Increasing function)
.

A non-negative function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is said to be an increasing function if for any
X,Y\in\Omega
with
X\preceq Y
, it holds that
f(X)\leq f(Y)
.

The following the stochastic dominance relation between distributions is well-known.

Definition 2.2

(Stochastic dominance)
.

Let
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
be two distributions over
\Omega
. The following two definitions of stochastic dominance (denoted by
\pi\preceq_{\text{sd}}\pi^{\prime}
) are equivalent.

•

There exists a coupling
(X,Y)
of
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, where
X\sim\pi
and
Y\sim\pi^{\prime}
, such that
X\preceq Y
with probability 1.

•

For any increasing function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}[f(X)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi^{\prime}}[f(Y)]
.

The equivalence of the two definitions in
Definition

2.2
is proved in
[
29
, Theorem 22.6]
.

Fill and Kahn
[
21
]
also introduced a relation between different Markov chains.
In this paper, we restrict our attention to
reversible
Markov chains.
A Markov chain with transition matrix
P
is reversible with respect to
\mu
if for any
\sigma,\tau\in\Omega
,
\mu(\sigma)P(\sigma,\tau)=\mu(\tau)P(\tau,\sigma)
.
Then
\mu
is a stationary distribution of
P
such that
\mu P=\mu
, where
\mu
is viewed as a row vector. We also view
P
as a linear operator acting on column vectors.

Definition 2.3

(Stochastically monotone chain)
.

A Markov chain
P
is said to be stochastically monotone if for any increasing function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
Pf
is also an increasing function.

In the above definition, a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is view as a column vector. The definition says that
P
, as a linear operator, preserves the increasing property of the function.
The following proposition gives two additional equivalent definitions of the stochastically monotone chain.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
29
, Prop. 22.7]
)
.

The following statements are equivalent.

•

P
is stochastically monotone.

•

for any two distributions
\nu\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\nu^{\prime}
,
\nu P\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\nu^{\prime}P
.

•

for any pair of comparable
\sigma,\tau\in\Omega
with
\sigma\preceq\tau
,
P(\sigma,\cdot)\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}P(\tau,\cdot)
.

Define the following family of Markov chains for distribution
\mu

\displaystyle\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}=\{\text{Markov chains that are reversible w.r.t. $\mu$ and stochastically monotone}\}.

(9)

Define the following comparison inequality relation
\preceq_{\text{mc}}
over the Markov chains space
\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}
.

Definition 2.5

(Comparison inequality relation
[
21
]
)
.

For any
P,Q\in\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}
, we say that
P\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}Q
iff for any distribution
\nu
such that the function
\frac{\nu}{\mu}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is increasing,
\nu P\preceq_{\text{sd}}\nu Q
.

The notation
\frac{\nu}{\mu}
denotes a function from
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined by
\frac{\nu}{\mu}(\sigma)=\frac{\nu(\sigma)}{\mu(\sigma)}
for any
\sigma\in\Omega
.
Definition

2.5
is different from the one in
[
21
]
, where they define
P\preceq_{\text{mc}}Q
iff for any two increasing functions
f,g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\langle Pg,f\rangle_{\mu}\leq\langle Qg,f\rangle_{\mu}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
is the weighted inner product defined by
\langle h,\ell\rangle_{\mu}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Omega}h(\sigma)\ell(\sigma)\mu(\sigma)
.
For reversible Markov chains, two definitions are equivalent, we give a proof in Appendix
A
.

It is straightforward to verify that the family
\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}
is closed under linear combination and composition, i.e., if
P,Q\in\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}
, then
\lambda P+(1-\lambda)Q\in\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}
for any
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
and
PQ\in\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}
.
Here,
PQ
is matrix multiplication of
P
and
Q
, which denotes the composition of the Markov chain that first applies the transition in
P
and then the transition in
Q
.
The following properties of the comparison inequality are proved in
[
21
]
.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
21
, Prop. 2.3, 2.4]
)
.

The following properties hold for Markov chains in
\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}
.

•

If
P_{0}\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}Q_{0}
and
P_{1}\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}Q_{1}
, then for any
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
,
\lambda P_{0}+(1-\lambda)P_{1}\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}\lambda Q_{0}+(1-\lambda)Q_{1}
.

•

If
P_{i}\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}Q_{i}
for any
1\leq i\leq\ell
, then
P_{1}P_{2}\ldots P_{\ell}\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}Q_{1}Q_{2}\ldots Q_{\ell}
.

The following comparison results are proved in
[
34
,
21
]
.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
34
, Thm. 2.5]
and
[
21
, Cor. 3.3]
)
.

For any two distributions
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, if
\frac{\pi}{\mu}
is an increasing function and
\pi\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\pi^{\prime}
, then
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\pi},{\mu}\right)\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\pi^{\prime}},{\mu}\right)
.

As a consequence, for two Markov chains
P,Q\in\mathcal{MC}_{\mu}
, if
P\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}Q
, then for any distribution
\pi_{0}
such that
\frac{\pi_{0}}{\mu}
is increasing, it holds that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\pi_{0}P},{\mu}\right)\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left({\pi_{0}Q},{\mu}\right)
.

Since
Definition

2.5
is an alternative (but equivalent) definition to the one in
[
21
]
, we prove
Lemma

2.6
and
Lemma

2.7
in Appendix
A
for completeness.

3.1
A simulation algorithm of the field dynamics

To prove

1.9
, we compare the Glauber dynamics with an algorithm that simulates the field dynamics. In the simulation algorithm, each resampling step of the field dynamics is implemented by running the Glauber dynamics for the tilted distribution.
To use the comparison technique in

2
, we need to ensure that the simulation algorithm forms a Markov chain.
To achieve this, we extend the configuration space from
\{0,1\}^{V}
to
\{0,1,\star\}^{V}

Define the following two basic operations.

•

Lifting Operation
: The

•

X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{alg}}
satisfies the following recurrence relation

\displaystyle\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{alg}}=\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{alg}}P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(t+1)},\text{ where }P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(t+1)}=\begin{cases}P_{\textnormal{cl}}P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}&\text{if }t\text{ mod }T_{2}=0,\\
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}&\text{if }t\text{ mod }T_{2}>0.\end{cases}

(12)

We remark that in
P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(t+1)}
,
(t+1)
is the time index of the transition matrix but not the exponent. We always use the bracket notation in upper index to denote the time index.

A modified Glauber dynamics

Recall that
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
is the transition matrix of the Glauber dynamics on
\pi
. The distribution
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
satisfies the following simple recurrence relation

\displaystyle\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}.

(13)

Note that two recurrences (
12
) and (
13
) are in the difference forms because
(P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(t)})_{t\geq 0}
is time-inhomogeneous but
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
is time-homogeneous. To do the comparison, we need to modify the Glauber dynamics. The modification is based on the following property of the chain
P_{\textnormal{cl}}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Recall that
\textsf{lift}(\mathbf{1}_{V})\sim\pi^{(0)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
and
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\pi^{(t-1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
is the sequence of distributions generated by the Glauber dynamics on
\pi
. Then for any
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}P_{\textnormal{cl}}=\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}.

The lemma states a very interesting property that contracting-lifting chain
P_{\textnormal{cl}}
has multiple stationary distributions and
every

\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
is a stationary distribution of
P_{\textnormal{cl}}
.
The lemma is proved in
Section

5
.
Using this lemma, we can write the following new recurrence relation for
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}

\displaystyle\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}P_{\pi\textnormal{-mGD}}^{(t+1)},\text{ where }P_{\pi\textnormal{-mGD}}^{(t+1)}=\begin{cases}P_{\textnormal{cl}}P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}&\text{if }t\text{ mod }T_{2}=0,\\
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}&\text{if }t\text{ mod }T_{2}>0.\end{cases}

(14)

The above recurrence relation gives a modified time-inhomogeneous Markov chain
(P_{\pi\textnormal{-mGD}}^{(t)})_{t\geq 1}
for
\pi
. For the transition from time
t
to time
t+1
, if
t\text{ mod }T_{2}=0
, then the transition matrix is
P_{\textnormal{cl}}P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
; otherwise, the transition matrix is
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
.
Thanks to
Lemma

3.4
, this modified Markov chain generates the same sequence of distributions
(\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}})_{t\geq 1}
as the Glauber dynamics on
\pi
. Now, the good property is that
(P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(t)})_{t\geq 1}
and
(P_{\pi\textnormal{-mGD}}^{(t)})_{t\geq 1}
are in the same form.

Comparison between two Markov chains

We now apply the general technique in
Section

2
to compare the two time-inhomogeneous Markov chains in (
12
) and (
14
).
Recall that by (
9
),
\mathcal{MC}_{\pi}
is the family of Markov chains that are reversible with respect to
\pi
and stochastically monotone, where the stochastic monotone property is defined in
Definition

2.3
.
The following properties for
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
,
P_{\textnormal{cl}}
, and
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}
can be verified by their definitions.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
\mu
is a monotone system, then
P_{\textnormal{cl}}\in\mathcal{MC}_{\pi}
,
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}\in\mathcal{MC}_{\pi}
, and
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}\in\mathcal{MC}_{\pi}
.

The proof of
Lemma

3.5
is deferred to

5
.

Now, we can use the comparison inequality relation
\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}
in

2.5
to compare the two Markov chains.
We prove the following lemma for two transition matrices.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
\mu
is a monotone system, then
P_{\pi\textnormal{-mGD}}^{(t)}\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(t)}
for all
t\geq 1
.

Using
Lemma

3.6
and the second property of
\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}
in

, we have

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 1,\quad P_{\pi\textnormal{-mGD}}^{(1)}P_{\pi\textnormal{-mGD}}^{(2)}\cdots P_{\pi\textnormal{-mGD}}^{(t)}\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(1)}P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(2)}\cdots P_{\textnormal{alg}}^{(t)}.

Note that
\pi^{(0)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\pi^{(0)}_{\textnormal{alg}}
are the distribution of the initial configuration
\textsf{lift}(\mathbf{1}_{V})
. One can verify that
\frac{\pi^{(0)}_{\textnormal{GD}}}{\pi}
is an increasing function (see

6.1

\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}
in

2.5
implies

3.3

.

Remark 3.7
.

In the above proof, we prove

in

3.3
via the comparison between the simulation algorithm and the modified Glauber dynamics in

for all

. The answer is no. A counterexample is given in Appendix
B
. Hence, although

is defined by the standard Glauber dynamics, when proving the stochastic dominance result for

, it is

necessary to apply the comparison argument to the standard Glauber dynamics. This idea may be of independent interest.

We give some high-level ideas for the proof of
Lemma

3.6
. By comparing (

) and (
14

P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}\preceq_{\textnormal{mc}}P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}
. In both

and

, the Markov chains first pick a vertex

uniformly at random, and then update the value of
i
according to their rules. Let
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}^{i}
and

denote the transition matrix conditional on picking vertex

. It suffices to show that

\nu
such that

is an increasing function. We need to show that

. One challenge is as follows. Suppose

. If

may update the value of

to

can update

to a larger configuration. However, we can still show

by using the fact that

is an increasing function. The intuition is that the increasing property suggests that

can also take the value

with certain probability. In case of

always keeps the value of

as

is given in

4.1
Correctness of the simulation algorithm

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

If we can perfectly simulate the field dynamics for
T_{1}
steps, then the total variation distance between the output and
\mu
is at most
\varepsilon/2
. In the simulation algorithm, every transition of the field dynamics is simulated by running the Glauber dynamics for
T_{2}
steps. By the definition of the mixing time of tilted distributions, for each transition, we can couple the perfect simulation with the simulation algorithm successfully with probability at least
1-\varepsilon/(2T_{1})
. By a union bound over
T_{1}
steps, the total variation distance between the output of the simulation algorithm and the output of the perfect simulation is at most
\varepsilon/2
. The lemma follows by the triangle inequality of the total variation distance.
∎

4.2
Comparison between Glauber dynamics on
\pi
and
\mu

We need a middle step to relate the Glauber dynamics on
\pi
with the Glauber dynamics on
\mu
.
Recall that
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
and
P_{\mu\textnormal{-GD}}
are the transition matrices of the Glauber dynamics on
\pi
and
\mu
, respectively.
Let
(X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\pi$-GD}})_{t\geq 0}
(resp.
(X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\mu$-GD}})_{t\geq 0}
) be the sequence generated by the Glauber dynamics for
\pi
(resp.
\mu
) starting from
X^{(0)}_{\textnormal{$\pi$-GD}}=\textsf{lift}(\mathbf{1}_{V})
(resp.
X^{(0)}_{\textnormal{$\mu$-GD}}=\mathbf{1}_{V}
).

Lemma 4.1
.

For any integer
t\geq 0
,
X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\pi$-GD}}
and
\textnormal{{lift}}(X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\mu$-GD}})
are identically distributed, and moreover,
\textnormal{{contr}}(X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\pi$-GD}})
and
X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\mu$-GD}}
are identically distributed.

Proof.

We first use induction to show that
X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\pi$-GD}}
and
\textnormal{{lift}}(X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\mu$-GD}})
are identically distributed for any integer
t\geq 0
.
Let
\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
be the distribution of
X^{(t)}_{\mu\textnormal{-GD}}
.
Recall that
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
denotes the distribution of
X^{(t)}_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
.
When
t=0
,
X^{(0)}_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}=\textnormal{lift}(\mathbf{1}_{V})=\textnormal{lift}(X^{(0)}_{\mu\textnormal{-GD}})
.

Assume
X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\pi$-GD}}
and
\textnormal{{lift}}(X^{(t)}_{\textnormal{$\mu$-GD}})
are identically distributed for some
t\in\mathbb{N}
. We prove the statement for
t+1
.
Let
\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}
be the distribution of
X^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{$\mu$-GD}}
conditional on
i\in V
is update by the Glauber dynamics. Then we have
\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in V}\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}
. Similarly we can define
\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}
and
\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in V}\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}
. Define two functions
C_{1},C_{\star}:\{0,1,\star\}^{V}\to\mathbb{N}
that count the number of 1s and
\star
s in a configuration. Formally, for any
\sigma\in\{0,1,\star\}^{V}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{1}(\sigma)=\left|\{i\in V\mid\sigma_{i}=1\}\right|;\\
C_{\star}(\sigma)=\left|\{i\in V\mid\sigma_{i}=\star\}\right|.\end{split}

(15)

Now, we fix a variable
i\in V
and fix a configuration
\sigma\in\Omega(\pi)\subseteq\{0,1,\star\}^{V}
. Define
\tau=\textnormal{contr}(\sigma)\in\Omega(\mu)
. We only need to consider feasible
\sigma,\tau
because Glauber dynamics stays in feasible space.
Define
\sigma^{x}
and
\tau^{y}
for
x\in\{0,1,\star\}
and
y\in\{0,1\}
by

\displaystyle\sigma^{x}_{i}=x,\quad\tau^{y}_{i}=y;

\displaystyle\sigma^{x}_{j}=\sigma_{j},\quad\tau^{y}_{j}=\tau_{j},\quad\forall j\in V\setminus\{i\}.

In words,
\sigma^{x}
and
\tau^{y}
are configurations obtained from
\sigma
and
\tau
by changing the value of
i
to
x
and
y
, respectively.
We can compute
(\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})(\sigma)
and
(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})(\tau)
using terms of
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
and
\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
, respectively. By the transition of Glauber dynamics, the value of
i
is resampled from the conditional distribution, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})(\sigma)&=\sum_{x\in\{0,1,\star\}}\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\sigma^{x})\frac{\pi(\sigma)}{\sum_{z\in\{0,1,\star\}}\pi(\sigma^{z})};\\
(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})(\tau)&=\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{y})\frac{\mu(\tau)}{\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}}\mu(\tau^{z})}.\end{split}

(16)

By definition of
\pi
,
\textsf{lift}(\mu)=\pi
and
\pi(\sigma)=\mu(\tau)\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma)}
. Hence,
\pi(\sigma^{1})+\pi(\sigma^{\star})
equals to
\mu(\tau^{1})\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}
. This is because,
\tau^{1}
is lifted
\sigma^{1}
or
\sigma^{\star}
if and only if every
j\neq i
with
\tau_{j}=1
are lifted correctly.
It holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\pi(\sigma)}{\sum_{z\in\{0,1,\star\}}\pi(\sigma^{z})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(\tau)\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma)}}{\mu(\tau^{0})\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}+\mu(\tau^{1})\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(\tau)\theta^{\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i}=1\}}(1-\theta)^{\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i}=\star\}}}{\mu(\tau^{0})+\mu(\tau^{1})},\quad\text{where $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ is the indicator function.}

By induction hypothesis,
\textsf{lift}(\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}})=\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
. By the definition of the lifting, we can compute the sum of
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\sigma^{z})
for
z\in\{0,1,\star\}
. Again, the sum of
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\sigma^{1})+\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\sigma^{\star})
equals to
\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{1})\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}
. We have the following equation

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\{0,1,\star\}}\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\sigma^{z})

\displaystyle=\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{0})\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}+\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{1})\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}

\displaystyle=(\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{0})+\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{1}))\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}.

Notice that
\theta^{\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i}=1\}}(1-\theta)^{\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i}=\star\}}\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}=\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma)}
. Combine above two equations and (
16
), we have

\displaystyle(\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(\tau)\theta^{\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i}=1\}}(1-\theta)^{\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i}=\star\}}}{\mu(\tau^{0})+\mu(\tau^{1})}(\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{0})+\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{1}))\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}

\displaystyle=\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma)}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}}\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}(\tau^{x})\frac{\mu(\tau)}{\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}}\mu(\tau^{z})}

\displaystyle=\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma)}\cdot(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})(\tau).

The above equation can be interpreted as follows: to draw a random sample
\sigma
from
\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}
, we first draw a sample
\tau\sim\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}
, and then apply the lifting operation to
\tau
. We slightly abuse the notation by using
\textsf{lift}(\nu)
to denote the distribution of
\textsf{lift}(X)
for
X\sim\nu
. The above equation shows
\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}=\textsf{lift}(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})
. We have

\displaystyle\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in V}\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in V}\textnormal{{lift}}(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})=\textnormal{{lift}}(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}),

where the last equality follows from
\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in V}\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i}
and it is straightforward to verify that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in V}\textnormal{{lift}}(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\mid_{i})=\textnormal{{lift}}(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}})
. Hence, the above equation shows that
X^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{$\pi$-GD}}\sim\pi^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
and
\textnormal{{lift}}(X^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{$\mu$-GD}})\sim\textsf{lift}(\mu^{(t+1)}_{\textnormal{GD}})
are identically distributed. This proves the induction step.

We next prove the second result. We slightly abuse the notation by using
\textsf{contr}(\nu)
to denote the distribution of
\textsf{contr}(X)
for
X\sim\nu
.
For any
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{V}
, it is easy to see
\tau=\textsf{contr}(\textsf{lift}(\tau))
.
Hence, for any distribution
\nu
over
\{0,1\}^{V}
,
\nu=\textsf{contr}(\textsf{lift}(\nu))
. Then for any integer
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{contr}}(\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}})=\textnormal{{contr}}(\textsf{lift}(\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}))=\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}},

where the first equality holds because of the first result
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}=\textsf{lift}(\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}})
of the lemma.
∎

4.3
Analysis of contracting operation

We also need the following lemma, which shows that the stochastic dominance relation is preserved by the
contr
operation. The following lemma gives the result for both the lifting and contracting operations. The result for the lifting operation will be used in later sections.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
be two distributions over
\{0,1\}^{V}
. Let
\nu_{L}
be the distribution of
\textsf{lift}(X)
for
X\sim\nu
and
\nu_{L}^{\prime}
be the distribution of
\textsf{lift}(Y)
for
Y\sim\nu^{\prime}
. If
\nu\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\nu^{\prime}
, then
\nu_{L}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\nu_{L}^{\prime}
.

Let
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
be two distributions over
\{0,1,\star\}^{V}
. Let
\nu_{C}
be the distribution of
\textsf{contr}(X)
for
X\sim\nu
and
\nu_{C}^{\prime}
the distribution of
\textsf{contr}(Y)
for
Y\sim\nu^{\prime}
. If
\nu\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\nu^{\prime}
, then
\nu_{C}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\nu_{C}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Consider two distributions
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
such that
\nu\preceq\nu^{\prime}
. By
Definition

2.2
, there exists a coupling
(X,Y)
where
X\sim\nu
,
Y\sim\nu^{\prime}
such that
X\preceq Y
. We use this
(X,Y)
to construct a coupling
(P,Q)
between
\nu_{L}
and
\nu_{L}^{\prime}
.
For every
i\in V
,
X_{i}\leq Y_{i}
. Since the lifting operation lifts each value of
i\in V
independently, we only need couple the lifting operation on each
i\in V
such that
\textsf{lift}(X)_{i}\leq\textsf{lift}(Y)_{i}
.
If
X_{i}=Y_{i}
, then
\textsf{lift}(X)_{i}=\textsf{lift}(Y)_{i}
can be coupled perfectly. If
X_{i}\neq Y_{i}
, then the only possible case is
X_{i}=0
and
Y_{i}=1
, and
\textsf{lift}(X)_{i}=0
, so
\textsf{lift}(X)_{i}<\textsf{lift}(Y)_{i}
because
\textsf{lift}(Y)_{i}\in\{1,\star\}
.

The contracting operation is a deterministic operation. The second property is easier to verify.
Consider
\nu,\nu^{\prime}
over
\{0,1,\star\}^{V}
satisfying
\nu\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\nu^{\prime}
.
There exists a coupling
(X,Y)
where
X\sim\nu
,
Y\sim\nu^{\prime}
such that
X\preceq Y
.
We apply the contracting operation to both
X
and
Y
to obtain a coupling between
\nu_{C}
and
\nu_{C}^{\prime}
.
For any
i\in V
, if
X_{i}=0
, then
\textsf{contr}(X)_{i}=0\leq\textsf{contr}(Y)_{i}
. If
X_{i}=1
or
X_{i}=\star
, then
Y_{i}=1
or
Y_{i}=\star
, which implies
\textsf{contr}(X)_{i}=\textsf{contr}(Y)_{i}=1
. Hence, there is a coupling between
\nu_{C}
and
\nu_{C}^{\prime}
such that
\textsf{contr}(X)\preceq\textsf{contr}(Y)
.
∎

4.4
Proof of the comparison result

To prove
Lemma

3.2
, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
P
be a reversible chain with respect to distribution
\mu
. For any distribution
\nu
,
P\frac{\nu}{\mu}=\frac{\nu P}{\mu}
, where
\frac{\nu}{\mu}
is a column vector such that
\frac{\nu}{\mu}(\sigma)=\frac{\nu(\sigma)}{\mu(\sigma)}
and
\nu,\mu
are row vectors.

Proof.

To verify, for any
x
, we have
(P\frac{\nu}{\mu})(x)=\sum_{y}P(x,y)\frac{\nu(y)}{\mu(y)}
. Since
P
is reversible, it can be written as
\sum_{y}P(y,x)\frac{\nu(y)}{\mu(x)}=\frac{(\nu P)(x)}{\mu(x)}
.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove
Lemma

3.2
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.2
assuming
Lemma

3.3
.

By
Lemma

3.3
,
\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{alg}}
for any
t\geq 0
. For any distribution
\nu
, we use
\textsf{contr}(\nu)
to denote the distribution of
\textsf{contr}(X)
for
X\sim\nu
. By
Lemma

4.1
,
\textsf{contr}(\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}})=\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}
.
Using
Lemma

4.2
, we have
\textsf{contr}(\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}})=\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\textsf{contr}(\pi^{(t)}_{\textnormal{alg}})
.

We next show that
\frac{\mu^{(t)}_{\textnormal{GD}}}{\mu}:\Omega(\mu)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is an increasing function.
Since
\mu
is a monotone system, by
Definition

1.1
, the third definition of stochastically monotone chain in
Proposition

2.4
, and the transition of Glauber dynamics, it is straightforward to verify that the Glauber dynamics

5.1
Stochastic monotonicity of Markov chains

We first show that
\pi
is a monotone system over
\Omega(\pi)\subseteq\{0,1,\star\}^{V}
.
We extend the definition of a monotone system in
Definition

1.1
to the case that the distribution is over
\{0,1,\star\}^{V}
. We say the lifted distribution
\pi
is a monotone system if for any
i\in V
, for any feasible and comparable configurations
\sigma\preceq\tau
with
\sigma,\tau\in\{0,1,\star\}^{V\setminus\{i\}}
, the conditional distribution
\pi^{\sigma}_{i}
is stochastically dominated by
\pi^{\tau}_{i}
, which means there exists a coupling
(x,y)
of
\pi^{\sigma}_{i}
and
\pi^{\tau}_{i}
such that
x\leq y
with probability 1, where we use the ordering
0<1<\star
.
By the monotonicity of the distribution
\mu
, we have the following property for the lifted distribution
\pi
, which implies that the Glauber dynamics
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
is stochastically monotone.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\mu
is a monotone system, then
\pi
is also a monotone system. This implies the Glauber dynamics
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
on
\pi
is stochastically monotone.

Proof.

Given any configuration
\sigma\in\Omega(\pi)
and any
i\in V
, for any
x\in\{0,1,\star\}
, let
\sigma^{x}
be the configuration obtained from
\sigma
by setting
\sigma_{i}=x
.
The conditional distribution of
\pi
is

\displaystyle\pi^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(0)=\frac{\pi(\sigma^{0})}{\pi(\sigma^{0})+\pi(\sigma^{\star})+\pi(\sigma^{1})}.

Note that
\pi(\sigma^{0})=\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{0}))\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{0})}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{0})}
, where
C_{1}
and
C_{\star}
are defined in (
15
), and
\pi(\sigma^{\star})+\pi(\sigma^{1})=\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{1}))\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma^{1})-1}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma^{1})}
. The second equation holds because to obtain
\sigma^{1}
or
\sigma^{\star}
from
\pi
, we first need to sample
X\sim\mu
such that
X=\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{1})
, and for any
j\neq i
with
X_{j}=1
, if
\sigma_{j}=1
, we need to keep the value of
X_{j}
in the lifting operation, which happens with probability
\theta
; if
\sigma_{j}=\star
, we need to lift the value of
X_{j}
to
\star
in the lifting operation, which happens with probability
1-\theta
. Combining these two equations, we have

\displaystyle\pi^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(0)=\frac{\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{0}))}{\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{0}))+\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{1}))}.

(17)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\pi^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)=\frac{\theta\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{1}))}{\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{0}))+\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{1}))},\quad\pi^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(\star)=\frac{(1-\theta)\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{1}))}{\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{0}))+\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma^{1}))}.

(18)

Hence, we can use the following process to sample from the distribution
\pi^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}
: (1) Sample
x\sim\mu_{i}^{\textsf{Contr}(\sigma)_{V\setminus\{i\}}}
; (2) If
x_{i}=0
, then output
0
; (3) If
x_{i}=1
, then output
1
with probability
\theta
and output
\star
with the rest probability
1-\theta
.

For
\sigma,\tau\in\Omega(\pi)
such that
\sigma\preceq\tau
, construct the following coupling of
\pi^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}
and
\pi^{\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}
.

1.

Since
\sigma\preceq\tau
, we have
\textsf{Contr}(\sigma)\preceq\textsf{Contr}(\tau)
. Since
\mu
is a monotone system, we can sample
(x,y)
from the coupling of
\mu^{\textsf{Contr}(\sigma)_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}
and
\mu^{\textsf{Contr}(\tau)_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}
such that
x\leq y
.

2.

If
x=0
, then keep
x=0
. Randomly lift the value of
y
if
y=1
. In this case,
0=x\leq y
.

3.

If
x=1
, then
1=y\geq x=1
. Use the perfect coupling to lift both
x
and
y
, so that
x=y
.

The above coupling of
\pi^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}
and
\pi^{\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}
satisfies that
x\leq y
with probability 1. Therefore,
\pi^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}\preceq\pi^{\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}
for any
i\in V
and any
\sigma\preceq\tau
.

Using the third definition in
Proposition

2.4
, it is straightforward to show that the Glauber dynamics
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
is stochastically monotone.
∎

Next, we show that the chain
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}
is stochastically monotone. We need the following lemma, which says the monotone property is preserved by tilted distributions.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
\mu
over

is a monotone system, then for any
\theta>0
, the tilted distribution

is a monotone system.

Proof.

Fix two feasible configurations
\sigma
and
\tau
in

,

\displaystyle(\theta*\mu)^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)=\frac{\theta\mu^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)}{\theta\mu^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)+\mu^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(0)}\text{ and }(\theta*\mu)^{\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)=\frac{\theta\mu^{\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)}{\theta\mu^{\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)+\mu^{\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(0)}.

(19)

The lemma follows from
\mu^{\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)\leq\mu^{\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)
by the monotonicity of

Lemma 5.3

If

5.2
Reversibility of Markov chains

Lemma 5.5
.

All Markov chains
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
,
P_{\textnormal{cl}}
and
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}
are reversible with respect to
\pi
.

Proof.

The reversibility of
P_{\pi\textnormal{-GD}}
is well-known. We prove the reversibility of
P_{\textnormal{cl}}
and
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}
with respect to
\pi
.
Note that starting from any feasible configuration, the two chains always stay in feasible space
\Omega(\pi)
. We only need to prove the detailed balance equation for any pair of configurations in
\Omega(\pi)
.

Fix two configurations
\sigma
and
\tau
in
\Omega(\pi)
. For
P_{\textnormal{cl}}
, we need to show that

\pi(\sigma)P_{\textnormal{cl}}(\sigma,\tau)=\pi(\tau)P_{\textnormal{cl}}(\tau,\sigma).

By the definition of the contracting and lifting operation, if
\{v\in V\mid\sigma_{v}=0\}\neq\{v\in V\mid\tau_{v}=0\}
, then
P_{\textnormal{cl}}(\sigma,\tau)=P_{\textnormal{cl}}(\tau,\sigma)=0
. Assume the two sets are the same, which means
\textsf{Contr}(\sigma)=\textsf{Contr}(\tau)
. Recall that
C_{1},C_{\star}
in (
15
) count the number of
1
s and
\star
s in the configuration. We have
P_{\textnormal{cl}}(\sigma,\tau)=\theta^{C_{1}(\tau)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\tau)}
and
P_{\textnormal{cl}}(\tau,\sigma)=\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma)}
. The reversibility is proved by noting that
\pi(\sigma)=\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma))\theta^{C_{1}(\sigma)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\sigma)}
and
\pi(\tau)=\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\tau))\theta^{C_{1}(\tau)}(1-\theta)^{C_{\star}(\tau)}
.

For
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}
, we need to show the following detailed balance equation.

\pi(\sigma)P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}(\sigma,\tau)=\pi(\tau)P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}(\tau,\sigma).

If
\sigma=\tau
, then the equality holds trivially. Assume
\sigma\neq\tau
. By the definition of
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}
, we only need to consider the case where there exists exactly one
i\in V
such that
\sigma_{i}\neq\tau_{i}
and the two values are
0
or
1
. Otherwise, the transition probabilities on both sides are 0. Assume
\sigma_{i}=0
and
\tau_{i}=1
. Let
\rho=\sigma_{V\setminus\{i\}}=\tau_{V\setminus\{i\}}
. The above equality is equivalent to

\pi^{\rho}_{i}(0)P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}(\sigma,\tau)=\pi^{\rho}_{i}(1)P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}(\tau,\sigma).

Using (
17
) and (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\pi^{\rho}_{i}(0)=\frac{\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma))}{\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma))+\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\tau))},\quad\pi^{\rho}_{i}(1)=\frac{\theta\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\tau))}{\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma))+\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\tau))},

(20)

By the definition of the Markov chain
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}
, using (
19
) and a simple normalization, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}(\sigma,\tau)&=(\theta*\mu)^{\textsf{Contr}(\sigma)_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(1)=\frac{\theta\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\tau))}{\theta\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\tau))+\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma))},\\
P_{\textnormal{s-GD}}(\tau,\sigma)&=(\theta*\mu)^{\textsf{Contr}(\tau)_{V\setminus\{i\}}}_{i}(0)=\frac{\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma))}{\theta\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\tau))+\mu(\textsf{Contr}(\sigma))}.\end{split}

(21)

Combining (
20
) and (
21
) proves the detailed balance equation.
∎

Appendix A
Proofs for comparison inequality relation

The following two observations follow directly from the
Definition

2.2
.

Observation A.1
.

The stochastic dominance relation is preserved under linear combination. If
\nu_{0}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\pi_{0}
and
\nu_{1}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\pi_{1}
, then for any
\lambda\in[0,1]

\lambda\nu_{0}+(1-\lambda)\nu_{1}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\lambda\pi_{0}+(1-\lambda)\pi_{1}

The stochastic dominance relation is transitive. If
\pi_{0}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{1}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\pi_{2}
, then
\pi_{0}\preceq_{\textnormal{sd}}\pi_{2}
.

The first result follows from the coupling definition of stochastic dominance and the second result follows from the expectation-based definition of stochastic dominance.
