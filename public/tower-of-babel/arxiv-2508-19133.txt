Existence and long-time behavior of global strong solutions to a nonlinear model of tumor growth
Jeffrey Kuan, Konstantina Trivisa

Abstract
In this manuscript, we study a nonlinear model of tumor growth, described by a coupled hyperbolic-elliptic system of partial differential equations. In this model, the compressible flow of tumor cells is modeled by a transport equation for the cell density, which takes into account transport via a background flow (given by a potential solving a Brinkman-type equation), and which has a source term modeling cell growth and death. In this manuscript, we show that for sufficiently large viscosity, the tumor growth system admits nontrivial global strong solutions for positive initial data having a gradient with sufficiently small norm. This illustrates the regularizing effects of the source term representing tumor cell growth and death on the resulting transport dynamics of the equation. Furthermore, we characterize the long-time behavior of global strong solutions to the tumor growth system using a level-set analysis, in which we analyze how level sets evolve as they are transported by the flow, in terms of expansion/contraction and accretion/depletion of cells. While there has been past work on global existence of weak solutions for this tumor growth system [ 41 , 42 ] , this manuscript opens the study of well-posedness in terms of more regular strong/classical solutions, which exist globally in time.



1
Description of the model

In this manuscript, we consider a model of tumor growth, in which cells are transported by a background flow given by a Brinkman type potential, and in which tumor cell proliferation effects are driven by internal pressure within the tumor, where the internal pressure is given by a constitutive relationship involving the cell density. We consider a tumor region
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(where
d=2,3
) which is a bounded connected domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. We model the cell density
n(t,x):[0,T]\times\Omega\to[0,\infty)
and potential
W(t,x):[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
by the equations:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}n-\text{div}(n\nabla W)=nG(p),\\
-\mu\Delta W+W=p,\end{cases}

where
G(p):[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a growth rate function and
p
is the internal pressure of the tumor. We assume that
G(\cdot)
is a
C^{1}
decreasing function with a unique positive zero
p_{0}
(referred to as the
homeostatic pressure
), which is motivated by physically relevant considerations
[
6
,
38
]
. The internal pressure
p(\rho)
is specified as a function of
\rho
via a constitutive relationship.

The first equation models the transport of tumor cells via a background flow, where the background velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
is given in terms of the potential as
\boldsymbol{u}:=-\nabla W
. It behaves as a continuity equation for the tumor, which resembles a compressible flow. The second equation for the potential
W
is a momentum equation, which is an elliptic equation of Brinkman type. As is common in compressible flow applications
[
25
,
33
]
, we assume a power law constitutive relationship for the internal pressure,
p(n)=an^{\gamma}
for some constant
a>0
and
\gamma\geq 1
. For concreteness, we also consider a specific form of the growth rate
G(n)
, given by

G(n):=\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta},

(1)

for positive constants
\theta>0
and
\alpha,\beta>0
, as is done in
[
41
]
. Therefore, the resulting model under consideration is the following coupled elliptic-hyperbolic system:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}n-\text{div}(n\nabla W)=\alpha n-\beta n^{\gamma\theta+1}\\
-\mu\Delta W+W=an^{\gamma}\end{cases}\quad\text{ on }\Omega,

(2)

for some adiabatic constant
\gamma\geq 1
,
\theta>0
, and positive constants
\alpha,\beta,a>0
. We prescribe Neumann boundary conditions for the potential
W
:

\nabla W\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

and we prescribe initial data:

n(0,\cdot)=n_{0},

where
n_{0}
is a smooth function in
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
that is nonnegative so that
\displaystyle\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x):=m\geq 0.

1.1
Summary of results

The goal of this manuscript is to show that the equations (
2
) admit nontrivial smooth global (classical) solutions. While past analysis for this equation has shown existence of global weak solutions for
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
nonnegative initial data
[
41
,
42
]
, we have the alternative goal in this manuscript of showing that these equations (
2
) admit unique global
strong solutions
for initial data with sufficiently small gradient, and furthermore, analyzing the long-time behavior of global strong solutions, when they exist. One can verify that the equations (
2
) admit trivial global smooth solutions where
n(t,x)
is constant for each time
t\geq 0
,
n(t,x):=\overline{n}(t)
, for
\overline{n}(t)
satisfying an ODE for arbitrary constant initial data
m\geq 0
:

\frac{d\overline{n}}{dt}=\alpha\overline{n}-\beta\overline{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}\overline{n}^{1+\gamma},\qquad\overline{n}(0)=m.

However, our goal in this manuscript is to show the existence of
nontrivial
(non-constant) global smooth solutions.

In particular, we have the following global existence result of unique smooth solutions, for strictly positive initial data
n_{0}>0
with
\|\nabla n_{0}\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}
sufficiently small, under the assumption of sufficiently large viscosity parameter
\mu
in the Brinkman equation.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n_{0}>0
be a smooth nonnegative function on
\overline{\Omega}
with
m:=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)>0
and
M:=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)>0
. Then, there exists a constant
C(M,a,\gamma)
such that if
\mu
is sufficiently large so that it satisfies

C(M,a,\gamma)\mu^{-1}<\frac{\alpha}{8}\min(\gamma\theta,1),

then there exists
\delta
depending on
m
,
M
,
a
,
\alpha
,
\gamma
,
\theta
, and
\mu
, so that there exists a unique global classical smooth solution
(n,W)
to (
2
) whenever
\|\nabla n_{0}\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}<\delta
. Furthermore, if the initial data satisfies
0<n(t,x)\leq n^{*}
where
n^{*}
is the unique positive zero of
G(n):=\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta}=0
, then the solution satisfies the bound

0\leq n(t,x)\leq n^{*},\qquad\text{ if }0\leq n_{0}\leq n_{*},

(3)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Otherwise, the solution satisfies:

0<n(t,x)\leq n^{*}+(M-n^{*})e^{-rt},

(4)

where
M:=\max_{\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}
and the constant
r>0
depends only on
\alpha
and
\beta
(and not on
M
).

We note that the structure of the growth rate term (
1
) introduces additional dissipation into the system (
2
), which helps to regularize the dynamics of the resulting system. Hence, this global existence result in Theorem
1.1
for initial data with small gradient is the analogue of a similar small data global existence result for the damped 3D compressible Euler equations, see Theorem 5.2 in
[
39
]
. We emphasize that in our result in Theorem
1.1
, there is no restriction on the
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
norm of the initial data; there is only a restriction on the size of the
L^{p}(\Omega)
norm of the
gradient
of the initial data.

After showing that nontrivial global smooth solutions exist for our system (
2
), our next goal is to
characterize the asymptotic behavior of these global strong solutions as
t\to\infty
. Let
n^{*}>0
be defined as the unique positive value for which

G(n^{*}):=\alpha-\beta(n^{*})^{\gamma\theta}=0.

(5)

From inspection, we see that there are two trivial stationary solutions to the system (
2
):

(n,W)=(0,0)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad(n,W)=(n^{*},a(n^{*})^{\gamma}),

which gives preliminary hints as to the potential asymptotic behavior of the system. It is clear that the system started at
n_{0}(x)=0
stays stationary at the first stationary solution
(n,W)=(0,0)
. We show that any global strong solution
(n(t,x),W(t,x))
with
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
for some
p>d
, converges as
t\to\infty
in an appropriate strong topology to the second stationary solution
(n^{*},a(n^{*})^{\gamma})
. In particular, we obtain the following result long-time behavior result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Any global strong solution
(n(t,x),W(t,x))
to (
2
) with the regularity
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for some
p>d
and for some smooth initial data
n_{0}\geq 0
, satisfies the following long-time behavior asymptotic estimates in time:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|n(t,x)-n^{*}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}=0,\qquad\lim_{t\to\infty}\|W(t,x)-a(n^{*})^{\gamma}\|_{W^{2,q}(\Omega)}=0,

for all
1\leq q<\infty
.

1.2
Literature review

The mathematical analysis of tumor growth has been widely studied in the mathematical literature. Such models often involve coupled systems of PDEs, and the questions that we study in the current manuscript, namely questions of global existence of solutions, uniqueness, stationary solutions, and long-time convergence to stationary states, are classical mathematical problems of interest in the study of tumor growth models throughout the years. We review some of the developments in the mathematical modeling of tumor growth.

One important class of models of tumor growth are free boundary models in which tumor cells are transported by a background flow, typically given as the gradient of an internal pressure via Darcy’s law, where the free boundary evolves according to the dynamics of the tumor, for example in relation to the internal pressure along the moving interface. Typically, one models the concentration of nutrients (and potentially other substances) and the movement of the boundary. In the case of spherical symmetry (which allows for the consideration of a 1D model), questions of global asymptotic stability to steady states and the mathematical analysis of such models has been carried out in
[
13
,
14
,
28
]
. Local well-posedness has also been studied in more general contexts for radially symmetric tumor growth models, for example in the case of a coupled cytokine-tumor cell system in
[
7
]
.

For higher-dimensional free boundary tumor growth models which do not assume radial symmetry, the analysis of non-radially symmetric steady states under certain conditions is performed in
[
29
,
30
]
for two dimensions and in
[
26
]
for three dimensions, where stationary solutions which break radial symmetry are analyzed (referred to as “symmetry-breaking bifurcations”). In such models, the movement of the boundary is driven by the internal pressure on the boundary, which must satisfy a condition of agreeing with the surface tension (proportion to the mean curvature) along the free boundary. The questions of local well-posedness
[
4
]
and global well-posedness/asymptotic stability for solutions sufficiently close to radially symmetric solutions under certain assumptions on the proliferation rate
[
3
]
have also been studied for this non-radially symmetric free boundary model of tumor growth.

There are also extensions to free boundary problems with mixed states, namely cells of various types where the total combined density is constant throughout the tumor. One such model (an
M3
model) consisting of proliferating, quiescent, and dead/necrotic cells is studied in
[
9
]
, in terms of local well-posedness (without any assumption of radial symmetry). For such models with multiple (two or three) possible states for the tumor cells, there are results on existence of unique stationary solutions
[
11
]
, and linear asymptotic stability of stationary solutions
[
8
]
. There are also global well-posedness results for such models in the case of radial symmetry, for an elliptic-hyperbolic system with a Brinkman-type equation for the nutrient concentration
[
12
]
, for a parabolic-hyperbolic system with a parabolic equation for the nutrient concentration
[
15
]
, and for a parabolic-hyperbolic system which also includes the effects of drug application
[
44
]
. For more information on free boundary tumor models, we refer the reader to the survey article
[
27
]
and the references therein.

There are extensions of these models in which there is
variable tumor cell density
throughout the tissue, which gives these free boundary models a compressible nature, as tumor cells can compress/expand while being transported by the background flow. Such a model is analyzed in
[
19
]
, in which there is a continuity equation for the cell density, and the background flow is given by a Brinkman-type (elliptic) equation, and global existence of weak solutions is established in this case. In this model, the Brinkman equation is instead used, which is a regularized form of Darcy’s law. These global existence results for weak solutions were extended in
[
20
]
to the case of tumor growth with drug application and also in
[
18
]
, where in addition to the continuity equation for the total density, there is a (parabolic) Forchhemier’s equation (which generalizes the Brinkman law). We remark that these works use a lot of the mathematical machinery developed for compressible Navier-Stokes flows, see
[
25
,
33
]
.

In contrast to the models discussed thus far, in the current manuscript, we consider a tumor growth model on fixed domain. This model is motivated by past works
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
, which consider the tumor as a medium in which cells are actively transported by a background flow and grow/deplete according to some given pressure law related to the density via a power law. Most closely related to the model in the current manuscript is the work of
[
36
]
, which uses a Brinkman equation for the potential. These works
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
are particularly interesting, as they relate such models to free boundary Hele-Shaw type models using a “stiff pressure law limit,” in which the power in the power law for the pressure goes to infinity. For such models of active transport and growth, particularly
[
36
]
, global existence of weak solutions and development of a convergent finite difference scheme is accomplished in
[
41
]
, and this is later extended to a coupled system with drug application and nutrient consumption in
[
42
]
, using methods of compressible flow dynamics
[
25
,
33
]
.

We remark that the past existence results on the model in consideration in this manuscript are for global existence of
weak solutions
(see
[
41
]
), but due to the dissipation present in (
2
), we anticipate a better result. This is in the spirit of the well-known work by
[
39
]
, where it is shown that the addition of damping to the 3D compressible Euler equations gives rise to global strong solutions for small initial data. Thus, the contribution of the current manuscript will be to study the well-posedness of (
2
) in terms of global
strong solutions
for appropriate initial data (with “small” gradient), and to develop methods for studying the asymptotic long-time behavior of strong solutions to the system. In contrast to the methods for weak solutions in the spirit of
[
25
,
33
]
for compressible flows, we use different methods here, for showing well-posedness in terms of strong solutions. We also introduce a new perspective of studying the equations (
2
), which involves a geometric level set analysis (see Section
5.2
) that has the advantage of providing direct hands-on information about how the tumor growth system evolves.

1.3
Outline

The first goal of this manuscript is to establish global existence and uniqueness, which depends on careful a priori estimates. Along these lines, we first begin by proving some essential
a priori estimates
and minimum/maximum principles in Section
2
that will be useful in our analysis.

In Section
3
, we show local well-posedness for the system with
strictly positive
initial data. This consists of deriving estimates for a linearized equation in Section
3.1
, and then applying the Schauder fixed point theorem to obtain a local solution as a fixed point of a map in Section
3.2
.

We then extend our local existence result for positive initial data to a global existence result for initial data with “small” gradient in Section
4
, hence proving Theorem
1.1
. The essential part of the analysis is showing an a priori dissipative estimate on the
L^{p}(\Omega)
norm of
\nabla n
in Proposition
4.1
, which hence gives global control of these norms in time for appropriate solutions to (
2
). We also show a weaker (non-dissipative) a priori estimate in Proposition
4.2
. We then obtain higher regularity results in Proposition
4.3
to show that local solutions are actually smooth. Then, in Section
4.2
, we combine all of these results to prove the global existence result in Theorem
1.1
.

Finally, since we now have that nontrivial strong solutions exist, we study the long-time asymptotics of global strong solutions to (
2
), when they exist in Section
5
. We begin by discussing a flow map
\Phi_{t}(x)
of the background flow
\boldsymbol{u}:=-\nabla W
and its associated properties in Section
5.1
. We then use a level-set analysis to show properties of the expansion/contraction of regions near vacuum in Section
5.2
. Finally, we use all of this information to prove the long-time behavior result in Theorem
1.2
. In the proof of this result, we use several results about the Green’s function of the Brinkman equation, which we establish in the appendix in Section
7
.

2
A priori estimates

We will derive some important Sobolev and maximum/minimum a priori estimates that will have an important role in our analysis. We derive a priori estimates under the assumption that
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
for some
p>d
. The first is an observation about the Brinkman equation, which quantifies the Calderón-Zygmund regularity properties of the elliptic equation for the potential
W
. We first recall some classical Calderón-Zygmund estimates for Neumann boundary problems, from
[
2
,
32
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
W
satisfies the Neumann boundary problem:

-\mu\Delta W+W=f\text{ on }\Omega,\qquad\qquad\nabla W\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

Then, for all positive integers
m
,

\|W\|_{W^{m+2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C(m,p)\Big(\|f\|_{W^{m,p}(\Omega)}+\|W\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\Big).

Proof.

To obtain the estimates for the case of
m=0
, we can appeal to Theorem 2.3.3.6 in
[
32
]
, and from here, we can obtain the general result for general positive integers
m\geq 1
using Theorem 15.2 in
[
2
]
.
∎

Next, we apply the Calderón-Zygmund estimates in Proposition
2.1
to obtain the following
a priori estimate
for the potential
W
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Consider the equation:

-\mu\Delta W+W=an^{\gamma}\quad\text{ on }\Omega,

(6)

with Neumann boundary conditions for a nonnegative
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
for
p>d
. Then, there exists a unique classical solution
W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
to (
6
) such that:

\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))}\leq C_{p}\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))}^{\gamma},

for a constant
C_{p}
depending on
p
that is independent of
T
. Also,
W\geq 0
is nonnegative.

Proof.

Note that the choice of
p
ensures that by Sobolev embedding,
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;C(\overline{\Omega}))
and hence,
n^{\gamma}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, so the existence of a unique weak solution
W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
is guaranteed by the Lax-Milgram theorem, see
[
21
]
. Testing the equation (
6
) by
W^{p-1}
and using Young’s inequality, we obtain:

\mu(p-1)\int_{\Omega}W^{p-2}|\nabla W|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}W^{p}\leq C\int_{\Omega}n^{p\gamma}\leq C\|n^{\gamma}\|^{p}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}\leq C\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))}^{\gamma p},

(7)

since
W^{1,p}(\Omega)\subset C(\overline{\Omega})
. Hence,

\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}\leq C_{p}\|n\|^{\gamma}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))}.

(8)

Since
\nabla(n^{\gamma})=\gamma n^{\gamma-1}\nabla n
and
\gamma\geq 1
and
W^{1,p}(\Omega)\subset C(\overline{\Omega})
since
p>d
, we see that
n^{\gamma}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
also. Therefore, by Calderón-Zygmund estimates (Theorem
2.1
) and the estimate in (
8
), we obtain the desired regularity estimate:

\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))}\leq C_{p}\Big(\|n^{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))}+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}\Big)\leq C_{p}\|n\|^{\gamma}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))}.

Finally, it remains to show that
W
is nonnegative. We test the equation (
6
) by
-\text{sgn}(-W)
, which is
-1
if
W\leq 0
and
0
if
W>0
. We integrate over
\Omega
to obtain:

\int_{\Omega}W^{-}=-\int_{\Omega}an^{\gamma}\text{sgn}(-W)+\mu\int_{\Omega}\Delta(-W)\text{sgn}(-W),

(9)

where
W^{-}:=-\min(W,0)
. By the result on pg. 64 of
[
24
]
, since
W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))\subset L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
with
\nabla W\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, we have
\displaystyle\mu\int_{\Omega}\Delta(-W)\text{sgn}(-W)\leq 0
. Hence, by
n\geq 0
and (
9
), we obtain
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}W^{-}\leq 0
, and thus
W^{-}
is identically zero, so
W(t,\cdot)\geq 0
for all
t
.
∎

Applying Hopf’s lemma to the elliptic equation for
W
, we can also obtain minimum and maximum bounds on the potential
W
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
W(x)
and
n(x)
be spatially smooth functions on
\overline{\Omega}
with
n\geq 0
, which satisfy

-\mu\Delta W+W=an^{\gamma},\qquad\text{ on }\Omega.

Then, for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
:

a\left(\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n(x)\right)^{\gamma}\leq W(x)\leq a\left(\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n(x)\right)^{\gamma}.

Proof.

This follows by using Hopf’s lemma, and we refer the interested reader to the complete proof provided in the proof of Lemma 3.2 in
[
41
]
.
∎

The next is an observation about the first equation, which is the continuity equation for the cell density. It is a maximum principle for the continuity equation, which will give uniform in time
\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}
bounds. In particular, the result shows that the solution is always bounded above by
n^{*}
if it is initially bounded above by
n^{*}
; else, the maximum value of the solution decays exponentially to
n^{*}
asymptotically as
t\to\infty
. We refer the reader to (
5
) for the definition of
n^{*}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
n
and
W
be spatially smooth functions on
\overline{\Omega}
for each time
t\geq 0
, which satisfy (
2
) with smooth initial data
n_{0}\geq 0
and Neumann boundary conditions
\nabla W\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. Recall the definition of
n^{*}
from Theorem
1.1
. Suppose that
\partial_{t}n(t,\cdot)
is also spatially smooth on
\overline{\Omega}
for all
t\geq 0
, and let
M=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)
.

•

If
0\leq M\leq n^{*}
, we have that the following upper bound holds:

0\leq n(t,x)\leq n^{*},\qquad\text{ for all $t\in[0,T]$ and $x\in\Omega$}.

•

If
M>n^{*}
, then there exists a positive constant
r>0
(independent of
M
, depending only on the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
) such that

0\leq n(t,x)\leq n^{*}+(M-n^{*})e^{-rt},\qquad\text{ for all $t\in[0,T]$ and $x\in\Omega$.}

Proof.

We will hence prove the second assertion, since the first assertion follows similarly. Since
n^{*}
is a constant, we obtain the following equation for
n-n^{*}
:

\partial_{t}(n-n^{*})-\text{div}((n-n^{*})\nabla W)=\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}+n^{*}\Delta W.

Setting
w:=e^{rt}(n-n^{*})
for
r>0
and using the second equation in (
2
), we obtain

\partial_{t}w-\nabla w\cdot\nabla W=e^{rt}(\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta})+\mu^{-1}(w+e^{rt}n^{*})(W-an^{\gamma})+rw.

Note that since
\alpha n^{*}-\beta(n^{*})^{1+\gamma\theta}=0
and the function
z\to\alpha z-\beta z^{1+\gamma\theta}
is concave down, there exists a positive constant
C
such that

\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}<-C(n-n^{*}),\qquad\text{ for all }n\geq n^{*},

so we can fix
r
to be any constant such that
0<r<C
.

We then claim that
w(t,x)\leq(M-n^{*})
for all
t\geq 0
and for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, where we note that
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}w(0,x)=M-n^{*}
by construction. To show this, we argue by contradiction and suppose that the strict maximum over
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
for arbitrary
T
is attained at a point
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
, so that
w(t_{0},x_{0})>M-n^{*}
. In this case, we have:

•

\nabla w(t_{0},x_{0})=0
(due to Neumann boundary conditions, this holds even if
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
).

•

Furthermore, since
w(t_{0},x_{0})>M-n^{*}
, we have that
n(t_{0},x_{0})>n^{*}
and hence

e^{rt}(\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta})+rw|_{(t_{0},x_{0})}\leq-(C-r)w(t_{0},x_{0})<0,

where
C-r>0
.

•

Finally, by Proposition
2.3
,
W(t_{0},x_{0})-a(n(t_{0},x_{0}))^{\gamma}\leq 0
since
n(t_{0},x_{0})=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n(t_{0},x)
. Note also that
w+e^{rt}n^{*}>0
.

Therefore, we conclude that
\partial_{t}w(t_{0},x_{0})<0
, but this contradicts that
w(t_{0},x_{0})>M-n^{*}
is the strict maximum in
[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}
. So we conclude that
w(t,x)\leq M-n^{*}
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, and hence,

0\leq n(t,x)\leq n^{*}+(M-n^{*})e^{-rt},\qquad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T],x\in\overline{\Omega}.

We defer the proof of
n(t,x)\geq 0
to the next proposition, which is about minimum principles.
∎

Finally, we conclude this section on a priori estimates by showing a corresponding lower bound for the solution away from vacuum if the initial data is initially bounded below away from vacuum. Since the proof of this lower bound result does not use Hopf’s lemma, we can establish this lower bound for lower regularity solutions.

Proposition 2.5
.

Consider smooth initial data
n_{0}\geq 0
such that
n_{0}\geq m\geq 0
for some nonnegative constant
m
. Then, a strong solution
(n,W)
to (
2
) with
n\in C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}n\in C(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
satisfies the uniform bound for all
t\in[0,T]
:

n(t,x)\geq\overline{n}(t),

where
\overline{n}
is the unique solution to the ODE initial value problem:

\frac{d\overline{n}}{dt}=\alpha\overline{n}-\beta\overline{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}\overline{n}^{1+\gamma},\qquad\overline{n}(0)=m.

As a consequence, for all
t\in[0,T]
:

n(t,x)\geq n_{*}:=\min(m,\eta),

where
\eta>0
is defined to be the unique solution to the equation:

\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}=0.

(10)

Proof.

Using the equation
-\mu\Delta W+W=an^{\gamma}
, we obtain from the continuity equation that

\partial_{t}n-\nabla n\cdot\nabla W=\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}+\mu^{-1}nW-a\mu^{-1}n^{1+\gamma}.

Since
W\geq 0
by Proposition
2.2
, we use the ODE for
\overline{n}
and the fact that
\overline{n}
is spatially constant to conclude that

\partial_{t}\overline{n}-\nabla\overline{n}\cdot\nabla W=\partial_{t}\overline{n}\leq\alpha\overline{n}-\beta\overline{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}+\mu^{-1}\overline{n}W-a\mu^{-1}\overline{n}^{1+\gamma}.

Subtracting the equations for
n
and
\overline{n}
and multiplying by
\text{sgn}(\overline{n}-n)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}

\displaystyle\leq\nabla(\overline{n}-n)^{+}\cdot\nabla W+\alpha(\overline{n}-n)^{+}-\beta(\overline{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}-n^{1+\gamma\theta})^{+}-a\mu^{-1}(\overline{n}^{1+\gamma}-n^{1+\gamma})^{+}+\mu^{-1}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}W

\displaystyle\leq\nabla(\overline{n}-n)^{+}\cdot\nabla W+\alpha(\overline{n}-n)^{+}+\mu^{-1}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}W.

This is justified because
\overline{n}-n\in H^{1}([0,T]\times\Omega)
by the regularity assumptions on
n
, and by pg. 64 in
[
24
]
, we have the identities
\text{sgn}(f)\partial_{t}f=\partial_{t}f^{+}
and
\text{sgn}(f)\nabla f=\nabla f^{+}
for
f\in H^{1}([0,T]\times\Omega)
. So integrating over
\Omega
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\overline{n}-n)^{+}\cdot\nabla W\right|+\alpha\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}+\mu^{-1}\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}W

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}\Delta W\right|+\alpha\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}+\mu^{-1}\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}W.

So since
W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,6}(\Omega))
by Proposition
2.2
and Sobolev embedding for all
T>0
, we obtain that for almost all
t\in[0,T]
:

\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}(t)\leq\Big(C(1+\mu^{-1})\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,6}(\Omega))}+\alpha\Big)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}(s)ds,

since
\overline{n}(0)=m\leq n_{0}
by definition so that
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\overline{n}-n)^{+}(0)=0
. So by Gronwall’s lemma (Lemma 1.4.2 in
[
5
]
),
(\overline{n}-n)^{+}(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
, and hence
n(t,x)\geq\overline{n}(t)>0
for all
t\geq 0
.

A simple stability analysis of the ODE shows that for
\overline{n}(0)=m
where
0<m<\eta
,
n(t)
is strictly increasing with
\lim_{t\to\infty}\overline{n}(t)=\eta
. If
m=0
, then
\overline{n}(t)=0
for all
t
and if
m\geq\eta
, then
\overline{n}(t)\geq\eta
for all
t
. This verifies the second claim, given the previous result that
n(t,x)\geq\overline{n}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
, since
\overline{n}(t)\geq n_{*}:=\min(m,\eta)
for all
t\geq 0
.
∎

Finally, we conclude this section by establishing the
uniqueness
claim in Theorem
1.1
on uniqueness of strong solutions
(n,W)
, with
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for some
p>d
, to (
2
).

Proof of uniqueness claim in Theorem
1.1
.

Consider
(n_{1},W_{1})
and
(n_{2},W_{2})
, which are both strong solutions to (
2
) for the same smooth initial data
n_{0}\geq 0
, with regularity
n_{i}\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W_{i}\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for some
p>d
and
i=1,2
. Then,

\partial_{t}(n_{1}-n_{2})-\nabla(n_{1}-n_{2})\cdot\nabla W_{1}-\nabla n_{2}\cdot\nabla(W_{1}-W_{2})\\
-(n_{1}-n_{2})\Delta W_{1}-n_{2}\Delta(W_{1}-W_{2})=(\alpha n_{1}-\beta n_{1}^{1+\gamma\theta})-(\alpha n_{2}-\beta n_{2}^{1+\gamma\theta})

(11)

-\mu\Delta(W_{1}-W_{2})+(W_{1}-W_{2})=an_{1}^{\gamma}-an_{2}^{\gamma}.

(12)

We test the first equation (
11
) by
n_{1}-n_{2}
. We compute that

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}\Delta W_{1}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(n_{1}-n_{2})\nabla n_{2}\cdot\nabla(W_{1}-W_{2})\\
-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{2}(n_{1}-n_{2})\Delta(W_{1}-W_{2})=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big((\alpha n_{1}-\beta n_{1}^{1+\gamma\theta})-(\alpha n_{2}-\beta n_{2}^{1+\gamma\theta})\Big)(n_{1}-n_{2}).

(13)

For the estimates, we define
n_{max,T}
to be any nonnegative number for which
n_{1}(t)\leq n_{max,T}
and
n_{2}(t)\leq n_{max,T}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. We estimate the resulting terms as follows:

•

For the first term, since
W_{1}\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for
p>d
,
\|\Delta W_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq C
.

•

For the next term, we estimate that
\|an_{1}^{\gamma}-an_{2}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(n_{max,T})\|n_{1}-n_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, so

\|\nabla(W_{1}-W_{2})\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\leq C\|W_{1}-W_{2}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(n_{max,T})\|n_{1}-n_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

by Calderón-Zygmund estimates applied to (
12
). Therefore, since
\|\nabla n_{2}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}\leq C
by assumption, we estimate:

\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(n_{1}-n_{2})\nabla n_{2}\cdot\nabla(W_{1}-W_{2})\right|\leq C(n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{1}-n_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

•

We compute

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{2}(n_{1}-n_{2})\Delta(W_{1}-W_{2})\right|

\displaystyle\leq n_{max,T}\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{1}-n_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|W_{1}-W_{2}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{1}-n_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

•

Finally, we can estimate that

|(\alpha n_{1}-\beta n_{1}^{1+\gamma\theta})-(\alpha n_{2}-\beta n_{2}^{1+\gamma\theta})|\leq C(n_{max,T})|n_{1}-n_{2}|.

Next, we test the equation for the potential (
12
) by
W_{1}-W_{2}
and obtain:

\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(W_{1}-W_{2})|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(W_{1}-W_{2})^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}a(n_{1}^{\gamma}-n_{2}^{\gamma})(W_{1}-W_{2}).

(14)

We estimate
|n_{1}^{\gamma}-n_{2}^{\gamma}|\leq C(n_{max,T})|n_{1}-n_{2}|
and hence,

\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}a(n_{1}^{\gamma}-n_{2}^{\gamma})(W_{1}-W_{2})\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(W_{1}-W_{2})^{2}+C(n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}.

So combining all of the estimates with (
13
) and (
14
), we obtain:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(W_{1}-W_{2})^{2}+\mu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(W_{1}-W_{2})|^{2}\leq C(n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}.

By Gronwall’s lemma, we conclude that
n_{1}(t)=n_{2}(t)
and
W_{1}(t)=W_{2}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

3
Local existence of a solution for positive initial data

Having proved the uniqueness claim in Theorem
1.1
at the end of Section
2
, it suffices to show existence of global smooth solutions for appropriate initial data (satisfying a “small” gradient assumption, as described in Theorem
1.1
). To show this global existence result, as a first step, we consider the
local existence of a solution for smooth positive initial data
, and then in the following Section
4
, we show that the local solution can be prolonged for all time (for appropriate initial data), using careful a priori estimates. In this section and the next, we consider strictly positive initial data
n_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, namely smooth initial data such that:

M\geq n_{0}(x)\geq m>0,\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\overline{\Omega},

where

m:=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x),\qquad M:=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x).

(15)

We will first establish local existence of a solution in an appropriate function space, up to some (sufficiently small) time
T^{*}>0
to the given system by using a Schauder fixed point argument, in the spirit of for example
[
43
]
. This will require first obtaining estimates on a linearized form of the given continuity equation, and then defining an appropriate map for which a fixed point of this map would be a solution to the system. In this section, we will hence analyze a linear equation related to (
2
), which will allow us to show local existence of solutions to (
2
) for positive smooth initial data.

3.1
Analysis of the linearized continuity equation

To set up the local existence proof for (
2
), in which we will express the solution to (
2
) as the fixed point of some abstract map, we first examine the linearized problem for the continuity equation, given by

\partial_{t}n-\nabla n\cdot\nabla W-n\Delta W=F,

(16)

where
F
is a given source term, and
W
is a given a priori known function. To set up a fixed point argument for (
2
) using the Schauder fixed point theorem, we need appropriate notions of existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence for the
linearized
equation (
16
). We have the following existence result and associated estimate for this linearized equation.

Proposition 3.1

(Existence and uniqueness for the linearized problem)
.

For any integer
m\geq 2
, given a function
W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))
and a source term
F\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))
, there exists a unique solution
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))
to (
16
). Furthermore, this solution satisfies the following energy estimate for almost every
t\in[0,T]
:

\|n(t)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\leq C_{0}\Big(\|n_{0}\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\Big)\text{exp}\Big(Ct(1+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))})\Big),

(17)

and
\partial_{t}n\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{m-1}(\Omega))
with the additional estimate:

\|\partial_{t}n\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m-1}(\Omega))}\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\\
+C\|W\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}\Big(\|n_{0}\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\Big)\text{exp}\Big(CT(1+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}\Big).

Remark 3.1
.

In the estimate (
17
), we label the constant by
C_{0}
since its actual value will play an important role in the Schauder fixed point estimates.

Proof.

Existence of a solution is standard. We prove an estimate for the norms of
n
and
\nabla n
, and then describe the general estimate for
D^{m}n
. We use that for
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))
,
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{m-2,\infty}(\Omega))
and
D^{m-1}n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))
by Sobolev embedding in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d=2,3
.

Estimate for
n
.
We test the PDE by
n
and integrate by parts in
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}n(\nabla n\cdot\nabla W)
to obtain:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|n(t)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|n_{0}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{2}\Delta W+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nF.

We estimate
\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nF\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\|n\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Big)
. Since
m\geq 2
, we use Sobolev embedding to obtain:

\|n(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\|n_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+C\int_{0}^{t}\|n(s)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big(1+\|W(s)\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}\Big)ds.

Estimate for
\nabla n
.
We consider
\partial_{j}n
by differentiating the equation, after which we obtain:

\partial_{t}n_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Big(n_{ij}W_{i}+n_{i}W_{ij}+n_{j}W_{ii}+nW_{iij}\Big)=F_{j}.

Next, we test by
n_{j}
and integrate by parts in
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}n_{ij}n_{j}W_{i}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{2}W_{ii}
to obtain:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{j}n(t)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{j}n_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(n_{i}W_{ij}+\frac{1}{2}n_{j}W_{ii}+nW_{iij}\Big)n_{j}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}F_{j}n_{j}.

Since
m+2\geq 4
, we obtain the estimate:

\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{j}n(t)|^{2}\leq\|n_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}\|n(s)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big(1+\|W(s)\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}\Big)ds.

Estimate for general
D^{m}n
for
m\geq 2
.
We differentiate the equation with a general multi-index
\partial_{\alpha}
with
|\alpha|=m
, and obtain:

\partial_{t}\partial_{\alpha}n-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Big(\partial_{i}\partial_{\alpha}n\partial_{i}W+\partial_{\alpha}n\partial_{i}^{2}W+R_{\alpha}\Big)=\partial_{\alpha}F,

where
R_{\alpha}
is a sum of terms of the form
\partial_{\beta}n\partial_{\gamma}W
where
0\leq|\beta|\leq m
and
|\gamma|=m+2-|\beta|
. We then test by
\partial_{\alpha}n
, and by integrating by parts, we obtain:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{\alpha}n(t)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{\alpha}n_{0}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{2}\Delta W+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}R_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}n+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{\alpha}F\partial_{\alpha}n.

Since
W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))
for
m\geq 2
, by Sobolev embedding,

\left|\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{2}\Delta W\right|\leq\|n\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}\|W\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}.

We obtain a similar estimate for the rest of the terms in
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}R_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}n
, which is the sum of terms of the form
\partial_{\beta}n\partial_{\gamma}W
for
0\leq|\beta|\leq m
and
|\gamma|=m+2-|\beta|
, using casework:

•

Case 1:
0\leq|\beta|\leq m-2
. In this case,
\partial_{\beta}n
is in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
by Sobolev embedding, so

\left|\int_{\Omega}\partial_{\alpha}n\partial_{\beta}n\partial_{\gamma}W\right|\leq\|\partial_{\alpha}n\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\partial_{\beta}n\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\partial_{\gamma}W\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|n\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}\|W\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}.

•

Case 2:
|\beta|=m
. In this case,
|\gamma|=2
, so since
m+2\geq 4
, by Sobolev embedding,

\left|\int_{\Omega}\partial_{\alpha}n\partial_{\beta}n\partial_{\gamma}W\right|\leq\|\partial_{\alpha}n\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\partial_{\beta}n\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\partial_{\gamma}W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|n\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}\|W\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}.

•

Case 3:
|\beta|=m-1
. In this case,
|\gamma|=3
, so by Sobolev embedding and the fact
m+2\geq 4
, we can estimate
\partial_{\gamma}W
in
L^{6}(\Omega)
. Similarly, we can estimate
\partial^{\beta}n
in
L^{6}(\Omega)
since
n\in H^{m}(\Omega)
and
|\beta|=m-1
. Hence,

\left|\int_{\Omega}\partial_{\alpha}n\partial_{\beta}n\partial_{\gamma}W\right|\leq C\|\partial_{\alpha}n\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\partial_{\beta}n\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\|\partial_{\gamma}W\|_{L^{6}(\Omega)}\leq C\|n\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|W\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}.

So we obtain the final estimate:

\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{\alpha}n(t)|^{2}\leq\|n_{0}\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}\|n(s)\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\Big(1+\|W(s)\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}\Big).

(18)

Conclusion of proof.
By adding the estimate (
18
) over all multi-indices
0\leq|\alpha|\leq m
:

\|n(t)\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|n_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|n(s)\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\Big(1+\|W(s)\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}\Big)\right),

for a constant
C
that does not depend on
T
. Therefore, by Gronwall’s lemma (Lemma 1.4.2 in
[
5
]
), we have the following bound for almost every
t\in[0,T]
:

\|n(t)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\Big(\|n_{0}\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\Big)\text{exp}\left(Ct\left(1+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}\right)\right).

Then, using the equation itself,
\partial_{t}n=\nabla n\cdot\nabla W+n\Delta W+F
, and by Sobolev embedding,

\|\partial_{t}n\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m-1}(\Omega))}\leq 2\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\|W\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}+\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))},

which implies the desired bound:

\|\partial_{t}n\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m-1}(\Omega))}\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\\
+C\|W\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}\Big(\|n_{0}\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|F\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}^{2}\Big)\text{exp}\Big(CT(1+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}\Big).

∎

Proposition 3.2

(Continuous dependence for the linearized problem)
.

Suppose that

W_{k}\to W\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega)),\qquad F_{k}\to F\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega)),

for some integer
m\geq 2
, and let
n_{k}
and
n
be the associated solutions to (
16
) with
W_{k}
and
F_{k}
, and
W
and
F
respectively. Furthermore, suppose that
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))
. Then,
n_{k}\to n
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

If we subtract the equations for
n
and
n_{k}
, we obtain:

\partial_{t}(n-n_{k})-\nabla n\cdot\nabla(W-W_{k})-\nabla(n-n_{k})\cdot\nabla W_{k}-n\Delta(W-W_{k})-(n-n_{k})\Delta W_{k}=F-F_{k},

with initial data
(n-n_{k})(0)=0
. By testing the equation and its spatial derivatives with
\partial_{\alpha}(n-n_{k})
for
0\leq|\alpha|\leq m
, we obtain an analogous estimate for the norms of the difference:

\|(n-n_{k})(t)\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\leq C\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\|n(s)\|_{H^{m+1}(\Omega)}\|(n-n_{k})(s)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|(W-W_{k})(s)\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}ds\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\|(n-n_{k})(s)\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|W(s)\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}ds+\int_{0}^{t}\|(n-n_{k})(s)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|(F-F_{k})(s)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}ds\Bigg).

Therefore,

\|(n-n_{k})(t)\|^{2}_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\leq C\Big(\|W-W_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}+\|F-F_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}\Big)\\
+C\int_{0}^{t}\Big(1+\|n(s)\|^{2}_{H^{m+1}(\Omega)}+\|W(s)\|_{H^{m+2}(\Omega)}\Big)\|(n-n_{k})(s)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}.

So by Gronwall’s inequality (Lemma 1.4.2 in
[
5
]
), for almost every
t\in[0,T]
:

\|(n-n_{k})(t)\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\Big(\|W-W_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))}+\|F-F_{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))}^{2}\Big)\\
\cdot\text{exp}\Big(Ct(1+\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))})\Big).

Therefore, the convergence of
n_{k}
to
n
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))
follows from the convergences
W_{k}\to W
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+2}(\Omega))
and
F_{k}\to F
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega))
, and the regularity assumption that
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{m+1}(\Omega))
.

∎

3.2
A fixed point argument

Next, we use the estimates on the linearized equations in Proposition
3.1
to establish the existence of a local solution, using the Schauder fixed point theorem. We consider the following solution space for
n
:

X=C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)),

and we define the following subset of
X
:

B_{R}:=\{n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega)):R^{-1}\leq n(t,\cdot)\leq R\text{ for $t\in[0,T]$},\\
n(0,\cdot)=n_{0},\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\leq R,\|\partial_{t}n\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq R\},

(19)

where we recall that the smooth initial data
n_{0}(x)
satisfies
n_{0}\geq m:=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)>0

We define a map

Proposition 3.3

(Claim 1A: Boundedness of solution norms)
.

There exists
R>0
sufficiently large and

sufficiently small such that for all
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
and for

\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\leq R,\qquad\|\partial_{t}n\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq R.

Proof.

To establish this estimate, we use the estimates in Proposition
3.1
. We hence will estimate the norm
\|\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}
for
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
; since it is possible that

, in order to estimate the

norm of the second and third spatial derivatives:

\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta})=\alpha\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\tilde{n}+\beta(1+\gamma\theta)\gamma\theta\tilde{n}^{\gamma\theta-1}\partial_{j}\tilde{n}\partial_{k}\tilde{n}+\beta(1+\gamma\theta)\tilde{n}^{\gamma\theta}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\tilde{n},

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}

\displaystyle(\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta})=\alpha\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\tilde{n}+\beta(1+\gamma\theta)\gamma\theta(\gamma\theta-1)\tilde{n}^{\gamma\theta-2}\partial_{j}\tilde{n}\partial_{k}\tilde{n}\partial_{l}\tilde{n}

\displaystyle+\beta(1+\gamma\theta)\gamma\theta\tilde{n}^{\gamma\theta-1}\Big(\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\tilde{n}\partial_{l}\tilde{n}+\partial_{j}\partial_{l}\tilde{n}\partial_{k}\tilde{n}+\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\tilde{n}\partial_{j}\tilde{n}\Big)+\beta(1+\gamma\theta)\tilde{n}^{\gamma\theta}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\partial_{l}\tilde{n},

we must use the lower bound

for

\|\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\leq C\left((1+R^{\gamma\theta})\|\tilde{n}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}+R^{|\gamma\theta-1|}\|\tilde{n}\|^{2}_{H^{3}(\Omega)}+R^{|\gamma\theta-2|}\|\tilde{n}\|^{3}_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\right).

\|\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\leq T^{1/2}\|\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\\
\leq CT^{1/2}\Big((1+R^{\gamma\theta})\|\tilde{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}+R^{|\gamma\theta-1|}\|\tilde{n}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}+R^{|\gamma\theta-2|}\|\tilde{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}^{3}\Big).

By the Calderon-Zygmund estimates in Propositions

and
2.2

\tilde{n}\in B_{R}

\|\tilde{W}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{5}(\Omega))}\leq CT^{1/2}\Big(\|\tilde{n}^{\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\Big)\leq CT^{1/2}\Big(R^{\gamma-1}\|\tilde{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\\
+R^{|\gamma-2|}\|\tilde{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}^{2}+R^{|\gamma-3|}\|\tilde{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}^{3}+\|\tilde{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}^{\gamma}\Big).

Recall the choice of
R
in (
22

, and by combining the definition of

(

\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\leq C\Big(\|n_{0}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}+\|\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\Big)\text{exp}\Big(CT(1+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{5}(\Omega))})\Big)\\
\leq C_{0}\left(\frac{R}{2C_{0}}+CT^{1/2}\left(R+R^{3+|\gamma\theta-2|}+R^{2+|\gamma\theta-1|}+R^{1+\gamma\theta}\right)\right)\\
\cdot\exp\left(CT(1+C(R^{\gamma}+R^{2+|\gamma-2|}+R^{3+|\gamma-3|})\right).

(23)

From Proposition

, we have the estimate:

\|\partial_{t}n\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\\
+C\|W\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{5}(\Omega))}\Big(\|n_{0}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}+\|\alpha\tilde{n}+\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))}\Big)\text{exp}\Big(CT(1+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{5}(\Omega))})\Big)

\|\partial_{t}n\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq CT^{1/2}\left(R+R^{3+|\gamma\theta-2|}+R^{2+|\gamma\theta-1|}+R^{1+\gamma\theta}\right)\\
+CT^{1/2}(R^{\gamma}+R^{2+|\gamma-2|}+R^{3+|\gamma-3|})\left(\|n_{0}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}+CT^{1/2}\left(R+R^{3+|\gamma\theta-2|}+R^{2+|\gamma\theta-1|}+R^{1+\gamma\theta}\right)\right)\\
\cdot\text{exp}(CT(1+C(R^{\gamma}+R^{2+|\gamma-2|}+R^{3+|\gamma-3|})).

(24)

Therefore, we can choose

\|\partial_{t}n\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq R
for all
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
. Since we chose

so that
\|n_{0}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\leq R/(2C_{0})
which is only crucial for the first term in (

) and (

) that does not have a factor of

to some positive power, the time

depends only on

.

Next, we have the following proposition which bounds the change in the spatial minimum and maximum of the solution
n
in time, to the linearized equation.

Proposition 3.4

(Claim 1B: Maximum and minimum bounds)
.

There exists
R
sufficiently large and
T>0
sufficiently small such that given
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
, the solution
n:=\Phi(\tilde{n})
defined by (
20
) and (
21
) satisfies
1/R\leq n\leq R
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Since this is a linear equation in
n
, the result will follow from a maximum/minimum principle argument. Let
m:=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)
and
M:=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)
be minimum and maximum values of the initial data. We claim that for all
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
such that
\tilde{n}
and
\partial_{t}\tilde{n}
are spatially smooth on
\overline{\Omega}
, we have that

me^{-bt}-bt\leq\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n(t,\cdot)\leq\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n(t,\cdot)\leq e^{Bt}(M+Bt),

for all
t\geq 0
, for
b>0,B>0
. It suffices to consider
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
with
\tilde{n},\partial_{t}\tilde{n}
spatially smooth, since the minimum/maximum inequality holds for general
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
by approximation.

Verification of maximum bound.
Note that we can rewrite the first equation as:

\partial_{t}n-\nabla n\cdot\nabla\tilde{W}=\alpha\tilde{n}-\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}+\mu^{-1}n(\tilde{W}-a\tilde{n}^{\gamma}).

(25)

By Proposition
2.3
and the fact that
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
,
0\leq\tilde{W}(t,\cdot)\leq a\left(\max_{t\in[0,T],x\in\overline{\Omega}}\tilde{n}\right)^{\gamma}\leq aR^{\gamma}
, and the function
\alpha z-\beta z^{1+\gamma\theta}
is uniformly bounded from above for
z\geq 0
. Hence, for our choice of
R
in (
22
), we can define
B:=\max\Big(a\mu^{-1}R^{\gamma},1+\max_{z\geq 0}(\alpha z-\beta z^{1+\gamma\theta})\Big)
which satisfies:

\alpha z-\beta z^{1+\gamma\theta}<B\ \ \text{ for all }z\geq 0,\qquad 0\leq\mu^{-1}\tilde{W}(t,x)\leq a\mu^{-1}R^{\gamma}\leq B\ \ \text{for all $t\in[0,T]$, $x\in\overline{\Omega}$}.

Therefore,
N:=e^{-Bt}n-Bt
satisfies
\partial_{t}N-\nabla N\cdot\nabla\tilde{W}<0
, and we observe that
N
is spatially smooth by regularity theory, since
\tilde{n}
is. Then,
N
cannot attain a strict maximum at
t>0
, since at such a strict maximum,
\nabla N\cdot\nabla\tilde{W}=0
and hence
\partial_{t}N<0
at this strict maximum, which gives a contradiction. Hence,
N(t,\cdot)\leq\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}N(0,\cdot)=M
, so that

n(t,x)\leq e^{Bt}(M+Bt),\qquad\text{ for all $t\geq 0$.}

(26)

Verification of minimum bound.
We consider (
25
) and note that
\tilde{W}\geq 0
by Proposition
2.2
. Since
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
, we have
1/R\leq\min_{t\in[0,T],x\in\overline{\Omega}}\tilde{n}\leq\max_{t\in[0,T],x\in\overline{\Omega}}\tilde{n}\leq R
.
Thus, there exists a constant
b>a\mu^{-1}R^{\gamma}
such that for all
z\in[0,R]
,
\alpha z-\beta z^{1+\gamma\theta}>-b
. Hence,
\overline{n}:=e^{bt}(n+bt)
satisfies
\partial_{t}\overline{n}-\nabla\overline{n}\cdot\nabla\tilde{W}>0
, and by a similar argument to the maximum principle, we deduce that
\overline{n}
cannot attain a strict minimum for
t>0
. Hence,
\overline{n}(t,x)\geq\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\overline{n}(0,\cdot)=m
, so

n(t,x)\geq me^{-bt}-bt.

(27)

Conclusion of the proof.
Since
R>\max(2m^{-1},2M)
by (
22
), we can choose
T>0
sufficiently small such that
1/R\leq me^{-bT}-bT\leq e^{BT}(M+BT)\leq R
, and then the desired result follows from (
26
) and (
27
). Note that the choice of
T>0
here depends on the minimum and maximum values
m
and
M
of the initial data
n_{0}(x)
.

∎

Finally, we establish continuity of the map
\Phi
, which will be a direct consequence of the result on continuous dependence for the linearized equation, Proposition

Proposition 3.5
.

The map
\Phi:B_{R}\to B_{R}
is a continuous map with respect to the topology of
X:=C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Suppose
\tilde{n}_{k}\to\tilde{n}
in
C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
, with
\tilde{n}_{k}\in B_{R}
for all
k
,
\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
. We want to show that the corresponding solutions
n_{k}\to n
in
C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
. We observe from Proposition
3.3
that
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{3}(\Omega))
. Therefore, it suffices to show that

\tilde{W}_{k}\to\tilde{W}\ \text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{4}(\Omega)),\qquad\alpha\tilde{n}_{k}-\beta\tilde{n}_{k}^{1+\gamma\theta}\to\alpha\tilde{n}-\beta\tilde{n}^{1+\gamma\theta},\ \text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)).

(28)

by Proposition
3.2
. For this purpose, it suffices to verify the claim that:

\tilde{n}_{k}^{p}\to\tilde{n}^{p},\qquad\text{ in }C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))\text{ for any }p\geq 1,

(29)

as this will directly imply (
28
), via Calderón-Zygmund estimates (Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
).

We hence complete the proof by verifying (
29
). We note that
\tilde{n}_{k}\to\tilde{n}
in
C(0,T;C(\overline{\Omega}))
,
\nabla\tilde{n}_{k}\to\nabla\tilde{n}
in
C(0,T;L^{6}(\Omega))
, and
\nabla^{2}\tilde{n}_{k}\to\nabla^{2}n
in
C(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
by Sobolev embedding. Therefore, we directly obtain by using the mean-value theorem and the fact that
1/R\leq\tilde{n}_{k}\leq R
,
1/R\leq\tilde{n}\leq R
, since
\tilde{n}_{k},\tilde{n}\in B_{R}
, that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\tilde{n}^{p}-\tilde{n}_{k}^{p}\|_{C(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}=0
. Similarly, we calculate
\nabla\tilde{n}_{k}^{p}=p\tilde{n}_{k}^{p-1}\nabla\tilde{n}_{k}
and
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(\tilde{n}_{k}^{p})=p(p-1)\tilde{n}^{p-2}_{k}\partial_{i}\tilde{n}_{k}\partial_{j}\tilde{n}_{k}+p\tilde{n}_{k}^{p-1}\partial^{2}_{ij}\tilde{n}_{k}
, and hence:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla(\tilde{n}_{k}^{p}-\tilde{n}^{p})\|_{C(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}=0,\qquad\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\nabla^{2}(\tilde{n}_{k}^{p}-\tilde{n}^{p})\|_{C(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}=0,

where we use the fact
1/R\leq\tilde{n}_{k}\leq R
and
1/R\leq\tilde{n}\leq R
. For estimating terms such as
|\tilde{n}^{p-1}-\tilde{n}_{k}^{p-1}|
and
|\tilde{n}^{p-2}-\tilde{n}_{k}^{p-2}|
, it is important that we have both a uniform lower bound
\tilde{n}_{k},\tilde{n}\geq 1/R
and upper bound
\tilde{n}_{k},\tilde{n}\leq R
, which will allow us to use the mean value theorem to estimate these terms, since
p-1
and
p-2
can be either positive or negative depending on
p
. This completes the proof of (
29
), and hence also the proof of the proposition.
∎

depends only on the norm

.

Let

, and define

and

4
Global existence of strong solutions

In this section, we use the local existence result in Proposition
3.6
to prove global existence of unique smooth solutions for smooth initial data
n_{0}(x)
with sufficiently small
L^{6}(\Omega)
norm. We note that the choice of
L^{6}(\Omega)
here is mostly for concreteness. We obtain global a priori estimates which show that a solution exists for all time, not just locally in time, which will allow us to prolong the time of existence of the solution.

To do this, we show a first a priori estimate where if the solution is sufficiently far away from vacuum (namely
n_{0}(x)\geq\eta-\epsilon
for
\eta
defined in Proposition
2.5
and
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small), the estimate for the first derivative norms is dissipative (see Proposition
4.1
), which gives global control of the norms of the gradient. This is in the spirit of results on global well-posedness for damped 3D compressible Euler equations for example, see Theorems 5.1 and 5.2 in
[
39
]
.

In the case where the smooth positive initial data
n_{0}(x)
takes values below
\eta-\epsilon
(so that the dissipative a priori estimate in Proposition
4.1
does not directly apply), we show using a more general (non-dissipative) a priori estimate in Proposition
4.2
that the solution exists on a time interval
[0,T]
for some
T>0
. By the fact that the minimum value increases according to the minimum principle involving
\overline{n}(t)
in Proposition
2.5
, we can choose
T
so that at time
T
,
n(T,\cdot)
is bounded below by
\eta-\epsilon
, which then allows us to apply the a priori bound in Theorem
4.1
to obtain global existence. Thus, the a priori bounds in Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
4.2
will be the main focus of this section, as they will naturally lead to a proof of global existence for initial data with small norm of the gradient in Theorem
1.1
.

4.1
Global a priori estimates for positive initial data

In this section, assume that the initial data satisfies
n_{0}(x)\geq m>0
, for some positive constant
m:=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)
. We already have local existence of
n\in C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
and
W\in C(0,T;H^{4}(\Omega))
for a sufficiently small
T>0
. We want to obtain global estimates on the norms of
n
, with the goal of proving Theorem
1.1
. We accomplish this by establishing the following result, where we recall that

is the spatial dimension

.

Proposition 4.1

(Dissipative estimate of
L^{p}
gradient norm for small data)
.

Suppose that
(n,W)
with regularity
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
,
\partial_{t}n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
, and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for some fixed even positive integer
p>d
, is a strong solution to (
2
) for smooth initial data
n_{0}(x)>0
, with
\min_{(x,t)\in[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}}n(t,x)\geq\eta-\epsilon
where
\epsilon>0
is any positive number for which

\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}\min(\gamma\theta,1)\qquad\text{ for all }n\geq\eta-\epsilon,

(30)

and
\eta
is defined in (
10
) as the unique solution to
\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}=0
. We also define
\max_{[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}}n(t,x):=n_{max,T}
. Then, there exists a constant
C(n_{max,T},a,\gamma)
depending only on those parameters listed, such that if
\delta
and
\mu>0
satisfy

C(n_{max,T},a,\gamma)(\delta+\mu^{-1})\leq\frac{\alpha}{8}\min(\gamma\theta,1),

and we have the a priori bound that

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{i}n\|_{C(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}^{p}\right)^{1/p}<\delta,

(31)

then the following inequality holds for all
t\in[0,T]
and
1\leq j\leq d
:

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(t))^{p}+\frac{\alpha}{8}\min(\gamma\theta,1)p\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(s))^{p}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}n_{0,j}^{p}.

Before proving this result, we show the following technical bound.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that a strictly positive
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
for a positive even integer
p>d
, with an upper bound
\max_{[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}}n(t,x):=n_{max,T}
, satisfies

-\mu\Delta W+W=an^{\gamma}\text{ on }\Omega,\qquad\nabla W\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

Then,

\|\nabla W\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C(p,n_{max,T},a,\gamma)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{j}n\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/p},

(32)

\|\nabla W\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(n_{max,T},a,\gamma)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{j}n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

(33)

Proof.

Let
n_{avg}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}n(t,x)dx
, and note that for
W_{avg}=a(n_{avg})^{\gamma}
,

-\mu\Delta(W-W_{avg})+(W-W_{avg})=an^{\gamma}-an_{avg}^{\gamma}\text{ on }\Omega,\qquad\nabla(W-W_{avg})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

Since
n-n_{avg}
has mean zero, by Poincaré’s inequality:

\|n-n_{avg}\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}\leq\|\nabla(n-n_{avg})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\|\nabla n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

and hence by Calderón-Zygmund estimates (Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
),

\displaystyle\|W-W_{avg}\|_{W^{3,p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}\|an^{\gamma}-an^{\gamma}_{avg}\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(p,n_{max,T},a,\gamma)\|n-n_{avg}\|_{W^{1,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(p,n_{max,T},a,\gamma)\|\nabla n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

Since
W_{avg}
is a constant, we directly obtain that
\nabla W=\nabla(W-W_{avg})
and hence:

\|\nabla W\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C(p,n_{max,T},a,\gamma)\|\nabla n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

To obtain the estimate (
33
) and eliminate the dependence on
p
, note that
p\geq 4
since it is even and
p>d
. Hence, we can use a specific
L^{4}(\Omega)
Calderón-Zygmund constant and obtain:

\|\nabla W\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\nabla W\|_{W^{2,4}(\Omega)}\leq C(n_{max,T},a,\gamma)\|\nabla n\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\leq C(n_{max,T},a,\gamma)\|\nabla n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

∎

Now, we have all of the ingredients needed to prove the essential a priori estimate in Proposition
4.1
.

Proof.

The proof will follow from
a priori estimates
on the first spatial derivatives of
n
. We note that for convenience, we assume that
p>d
is also an even positive integer so that
x^{p}
is always a nonnegative quantity for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Estimate on the gradient of the density.
Taking the gradient of the equation for the evolution of the density and the potential, we obtain

\partial_{t}n_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big(n_{ij}W_{i}+n_{i}W_{ij}+n_{j}W_{ii}+nW_{iij}\Big)=\alpha n_{j}-\beta(\gamma\theta+1)n^{\gamma\theta}n_{j},

(34)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}W_{iij}=\mu^{-1}\Big(W_{j}-a\gamma n^{\gamma-1}n_{j}\Big).

(35)

We test (
34
) by
pn_{j}^{p-1}
for
1\leq p<\infty
, and we estimate the right-hand side of the result:

\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(t))^{p}=\int_{\Omega}n_{0,j}^{p}+p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(n_{ij}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{i}+n_{i}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{ij}+n_{j}^{p}W_{ii}+nn_{j}^{p-1}W_{iij}\Big)\\
+\alpha p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(s))^{p}-\beta(\gamma\theta+1)p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma\theta}n_{j}^{p}.

(36)

We rewrite some of the terms on the right-hand side as follows, recalling the assumption on the choice of
\epsilon
in (
30
).

Terms 2 and 4.
By integration by parts,
\displaystyle p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{ij}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{i}=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}\Delta W
. Hence, combining this with the fourth term and using (
2
), we obtain:

\displaystyle p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(n_{ij}n_{i}^{p-1}W_{i}+n_{j}^{p}W_{ii}\Big)

\displaystyle=(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}\Delta W

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}W-a\mu^{-1}(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}n^{\gamma}.

Term 5.
We use the equation for
W_{iij}
in (
35
) to conclude that

p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn_{j}^{p-1}W_{iij}=\mu^{-1}p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn_{j}^{p-1}W_{j}-a\gamma\mu^{-1}p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma}n_{j}^{p}.

Hence, we can rewrite (
36
) as

\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(t))^{p}=\int_{\Omega}n_{0,j}^{p}+p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{i}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{ij}+p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta}-a\gamma\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}\Big)n_{j}^{p}\\
+\mu^{-1}(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}W+\mu^{-1}p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn^{p-1}_{j}W_{j}-a\mu^{-1}(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma}n_{j}^{p}-\beta\gamma\theta p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma\theta}n^{p}_{j}.

(37)

Using the assumption on
\epsilon
in (
30
), the fact that
n\geq\eta-\epsilon
for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, and the fact that
p>d\geq 2
is even and hence
\displaystyle\frac{2p}{3}<p-1
, we estimate:

p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\left(\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta}-a\gamma\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}\right)n^{p}_{j}\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}\min(\gamma\theta,1)p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}\qquad\text{(since $\gamma\geq 1$)},

\displaystyle-a\mu^{-1}(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma}n_{j}^{p}-\beta\gamma\theta p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma\theta}n_{j}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3}\min(\gamma\theta,1)p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(-a\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}-\beta n^{\gamma\theta})n^{p}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\alpha}{2}\min(\gamma\theta,1)p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{p}_{j}.

We thus obtain from (
37
) the following, after summing over
j=1
to
j=d
:

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(t))^{p}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}n_{0,j}^{p}+p\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{i}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{ij}+\mu^{-1}(p-1)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}W\\
+\mu^{-1}p\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn_{j}^{p-1}W_{j}-\frac{\alpha}{4}\min(\gamma\theta,1)p\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}.

(38)

Using (1) the a priori bound in (
31
) to estimate
\|n_{i}\|_{C(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}
in the term involving
n_{i}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{ij}
, (2) the estimate (
33
) in Lemma
4.1
, and (3)
0\leq n\leq n_{max,T}
and hence
W\leq C(n_{max,T})
by Proposition
2.1
and
2.2
, we conclude:

\displaystyle\Bigg|p\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{i}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{ij}+\mu^{-1}(p-1)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}W+\mu^{-1}p\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn^{p-1}_{j}W_{j}\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq p\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{i}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}\|\nabla W\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mu^{-1}p\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}+\|n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}\|\nabla W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C(n_{max,T},a,\gamma)p(\delta+\mu^{-1})\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}.

So assuming that
\delta
is sufficiently small and
\mu
is sufficiently large so that the resulting constant
\displaystyle C(n_{max,T},a,\gamma)(\delta+\mu^{-1})\leq\frac{\alpha}{8}\min(\gamma\theta,1)
, we conclude from (
38
) that for every
1\leq j\leq d
:

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(t))^{p}+\frac{\alpha}{8}\min(\gamma\theta,1)p\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(s))^{p}\right)ds\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}n_{0,j}^{p}.

∎

As a direct consequence of the previous estimates, we can obtain a less refined (non-dissipative) estimate on the
L^{p}
norm of the gradient in the case where we do not require the minimum
n_{min,T}:=\min_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}}n(t,x)
to be greater than or equal to
\eta-\epsilon
. This less refined estimate will be useful for showing global existence for
any
nonnegative initial data with small gradient and no vacuum.

Proposition 4.2

(General estimate on
L^{p}
norm of gradient)
.

Suppose that
(n,W)
has regularity
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for some fixed positive even integer
p>d
and
\min_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}}n(t,x):=n_{min,T}>0
. Define
n_{max,T}:=\max_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}}n(t,x)
. Then, if

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{i}n\|^{p}_{C(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}\right)^{1/p}<\delta,

then the following inequality holds for all
t\in[0,T]
and
1\leq j\leq d
:

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(t))^{p}dx\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}n_{0,j}^{p}dx+C(\delta,p,a,\alpha,\beta,\theta,\mu,\gamma,n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(s))^{p}dx\right)ds.

Proof.

From the previous proof in (
37
), we have the equality:

\int_{\Omega}(n_{j}(t))^{p}=\int_{\Omega}n_{0,j}^{p}+p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{i}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{ij}+p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta}-a\gamma\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}\Big)n_{j}^{p}\\
+\mu^{-1}(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{j}^{p}W+\mu^{-1}p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn^{p-1}_{j}W_{j}-a\mu^{-1}(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma}n_{j}^{p}-\beta\gamma\theta p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma\theta}n^{p}_{j}.

From the estimate (
32
) in Lemma
4.1
applied to the given
p>d
, we have the bound

\|\nabla W(t)\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C(n_{max,T},p,a,\gamma)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|n_{j}\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/p}.

Hence, we can estimate:

\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn_{j}^{p-1}W_{j}\right|\leq n_{max,T}\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}\|W_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C(n_{max,T},p,a,\gamma)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}.

Using the assumption that
\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\|\partial_{j}n\|^{p}_{C(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}\right)^{1/p}<\delta
, we can also estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{i}n_{j}^{p-1}W_{ij}\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{i}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}\|W_{ij}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{j}\|^{p-1}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\|W_{j}\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\delta,n_{max,T},p,a,\gamma)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}.

The result therefore follows from these estimates, the fact that
\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|W\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C(n_{max,T},p,a,\gamma)
by Calderón-Zygmund estimates, and the identity (
37
).
∎

Next, we improve the regularity of solutions as follows. While we have only shown local existence in Proposition
3.6
in
C(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
, we show that these solutions have higher regularity as a result of the fact that the initial data is actually smooth.

Proposition 4.3

(Regularity of local-in-time strong solutions)
.

Suppose that
(n,W)
with regularity
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
,
\partial_{t}n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
, and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for some positive even integer
p>d
is a strong solution to (
2
) for smooth initial data
n_{0}(x)>0
, with
\min_{(x,t)\in[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}}n(t,x):=n_{min,T}>0
. Define
\max_{[0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}}n(t,x):=n_{max,T}
. Then,
n(t,\cdot)
and
\partial_{t}n(t,\cdot)
are spatially smooth functions for each
t\in[0,T]
on
\overline{\Omega}
, with the estimates:

\|D^{k}n(t)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega))}^{p}\leq e^{C^{*}t}\Big(1+\|D^{k}n_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\Big),\qquad\|W(t)\|_{W^{k+2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C^{*}\|n(t)\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)},

for all positive integers
k
and for a constant
C^{*}
depending only on

C^{*}:=C(p,\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{k-1,p}(\Omega))},n_{min,T},n_{max,T}),

which is increasing as a function of
\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{k-1,p}(\Omega))}
.

Proof.

We show this through a priori estimates. Because we are already assuming that we have a solution on
[0,T]
, we can use bounds for lower derivatives, namely for
n
in
C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
, to help close the
a priori estimates
for the higher derivatives.

Estimates on the second derivatives of the density.
We take the second derivative
\partial_{j}\partial_{k}
of both equations and obtain:

\partial_{t}n_{jk}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big(n_{ijk}W_{i}+n_{ij}W_{ik}+n_{ik}W_{ij}+n_{i}W_{ijk}+n_{jk}W_{ii}+n_{j}W_{iik}+n_{k}W_{iij}+nW_{iijk}\Big)\\
=\alpha n_{jk}-\beta(\gamma\theta+1)\gamma\theta n^{\gamma\theta-1}n_{j}n_{k}-\beta(\gamma\theta+1)n^{\gamma\theta}n_{jk},

(39)

\mu\sum_{i=1}^{d}W_{iijk}=W_{jk}-a\gamma(\gamma-1)n^{\gamma-2}n_{j}n_{k}-a\gamma n^{\gamma-1}n_{jk}.

(40)

We test the first equation by
pn_{jk}^{p-1}
and recall that we so far have estimates for all
1\leq p<\infty
on
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
. After testing (
39
) with
pn_{jk}^{p-1}
, we obtain the following estimates:

•

First, we obtain after integration by parts:

\displaystyle p\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(n_{ijk}n_{jk}^{p-1}W_{i}

\displaystyle+n_{jk}^{p}W_{ii}\Big)\Big|=(p-1)\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{jk}^{p}\Delta W\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mu^{-1}(p-1)\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n_{jk}^{p}W\right|+a\mu^{-1}(p-1)\left|\int_{0}^{t}n_{jk}^{p}n^{\gamma}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}n|^{p}\right)\Big(\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))}^{\gamma}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C(p,n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}n|^{p}.

•

Next, we use (
40
) to estimate

\displaystyle\Big|p\sum_{i=1}^{d}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn_{jk}^{p-1}W_{iijk}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}nn_{jk}^{p-1}W_{jk}\right|+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma-1}n_{j}n_{k}n_{jk}^{p-1}\right|+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma}n_{jk}^{p}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(n^{p}|W_{jk}|^{p}+n^{(\gamma-1)p}|\nabla n|^{2p}\Big)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(1+n^{\gamma}\Big)|D^{2}n|^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(p,n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\|W\|^{p}_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla n\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{p}\|\nabla n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\Big)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}n|^{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(p,\|n\|_{C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))},n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(1+|D^{2}n|^{p}\Big),

using
p>d
along with the Sobolev embedding
W^{2,p}(\Omega)\subset W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
to estimate
\|\nabla n\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
and the assumption that
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))\subset C(0,T;W^{2,\infty}(\Omega))
. Note that by Calderón-Zygmund estimates (Proposition
2.1
and
2.2
),
\|W\|_{C(0,T;W^{2,p}(\Omega))}\leq C_{p}\|n\|_{C(0,T;L^{p}(\Omega))}
.

•

We estimate
\partial_{jk}(n^{\gamma})
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
as:

\displaystyle\|n^{\gamma-2}n_{j}n_{k}+n^{\gamma-1}n_{jk}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\Big(\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\|n_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|n_{jk}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C(n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\Big(\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\|n\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}+\|n_{jk}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C(n_{min,T},n_{max,T},\|n\|_{C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))})\Big(1+\|D^{2}n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\Big).

So by Calderón-Zygmund estimates (Proposition
2.1
and
2.2
),

\|W\|_{W^{3,\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|W\|_{W^{4,p}(\Omega)}\leq C(p,\|n\|_{C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))},n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\left(1+\|D^{2}n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\right).

Hence, we can estimate:

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\Big(n_{ij}n_{jk}^{p-1}W_{ik}+n_{ik}n_{jk}^{p-1}W_{ij}+n_{i}n_{jk}^{p-1}W_{ijk}+n_{j}n_{jk}^{p-1}W_{iik}+n_{k}n_{jk}^{p-1}W_{iij}\Big)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla n|^{p}|D^{3}W|^{p}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}n|^{p}\right)\Big(1+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,\infty}(\Omega))}^{p}\Big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla n\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{p}\|W\|_{W^{3,p}(\Omega)}^{p}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}n|^{p}\right)\Big(1+\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)}^{p}\Big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(p,\|n\|_{C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))},n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(1+|D^{2}n|^{p}\Big),

since
\|\nabla n\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{p}\|n\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}
and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))\subset C(0,T;W^{2,\infty}(\Omega))
with

\|W\|_{C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))}\leq C(p,n_{max,T})\|n\|_{C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))}.

•

Finally, we estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma\theta-1}n_{j}n_{k}n_{jk}^{p-1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\|n_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|n_{j}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\|n_{jk}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}dt

\displaystyle\leq C(\|n\|_{C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))},n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(1+|D^{2}n|^{p}\Big),

by the estimate
\|n_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\|n\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)}
. We also have the estimate:

\beta p(\gamma\theta+1)\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma\theta}n_{jk}^{p}\right|\leq C(n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}n|^{p}.

Conclusion.
Combining all of the above estimates, we obtain:

\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}n(t)|^{p}\leq\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}n_{0}|^{p}+C(p,\|n\|_{C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))},n_{min,T},n_{max,T})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(1+|D^{2}n(s)|^{p}\Big)ds,

so by Gronwall’s inequality and Calderón-Zygmund estimates, we obtain:

\|D^{2}n(t)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\leq e^{C^{*}t}\Big(1+\|D^{2}n_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\Big),\qquad\|W(t)\|_{W^{4,p}(\Omega)}\leq C^{*}\|n\|_{W^{2,p}(\Omega)},

for some constant
C^{*}
depending on the parameters
C^{*}:=C(p,\|n\|_{C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))},n_{min,T},n_{max,T})
.

Estimates on all higher derivatives.
We obtain estimates on higher order spatial derivatives by using an inductive argument, with an inductive assumption that for some positive integer
k
, we have established the following bound:

\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{k,p}(\Omega))}\leq C,\quad\text{ and hence, by Calder\'{o}n-Zygmund},\quad\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{k+2,p}(\Omega))}\leq C.

(41)

We then want to show that under this inductive assumption, we can obtain the estimates:

\|D^{k+1}n\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\leq e^{C^{*}t}\Big(1+\|D^{k+1}n_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\Big),\qquad\|W(t)\|_{W^{k+3,p}(\Omega)}\leq C^{*}\|n(t)\|_{W^{k+1,p}(\Omega)},

(42)

for some constant
C^{*}
, where for simplicity of notation, we will use this shorthand to indicate the following explicit dependence of constants on parameters for the remainder of the proof:

C^{*}=C(p,\|n\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{k,p}(\Omega))},n_{min,T},n_{max,T}).

(43)

To do this, we consider a general multi-index
\partial_{\alpha}
with
|\alpha|=k+1
and we take the derivative with respect to this multi-index of both equations. We obtain:

\partial_{t}\partial_{\alpha}n-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Big((\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{i}n)\partial_{i}W+(\partial_{\alpha}n)\partial_{i}^{2}W+n\partial_{\alpha}\partial^{2}_{i}W\Big)+R_{\alpha}=\alpha\partial_{\alpha}n-\beta(\gamma\theta+1)n^{\gamma\theta}\partial_{\alpha}n+T_{\alpha},

(44)

where:

•

R_{\alpha}
is comprised of terms
(\partial_{\beta}n)(\partial_{\gamma}W)
for multi-indices with
1\leq|\beta|\leq k+1=|\alpha|
and
|\gamma|=k+3-|\beta|
.

•

T_{\alpha}
is comprised of terms
n^{\gamma\theta-m}(\partial_{\alpha_{1}}n)(\partial_{\alpha_{2}}n)\cdot\cdot\cdot(\partial_{\alpha_{m+1}}n)
, for a positive integer
1\leq m\leq k
and multi-indices
1\leq|\alpha_{1}|\leq|\alpha_{2}|\leq...\leq|\alpha_{m+1}|\leq k-m+1
with
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}|\alpha_{i}|=k+1
.

We also have the equation for the potential:

\mu\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{\alpha}\partial^{2}_{i}W=\partial_{\alpha}W-a\partial_{\alpha}(n^{\gamma}).

(45)

We test (
44
) with
p(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{p-1}
and integrate over space, and estimate the resulting terms.

•

By integrating by parts and using the Neumann boundary condition on
W
:

p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big((\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{i}n)\partial_{i}W+(\partial_{\alpha}n)\partial^{2}_{i}W\Big)(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{p-1}=(p-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{p}\Delta W.

Since
k+2\geq 4
in the inductive assumption, we can estimate that
\|\Delta W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|W\|_{W^{4,p}(\Omega)}
, and hence by the boundedness of
W
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{k+2,p}(\Omega))
:

\left|p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big((\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{i}n)\partial_{i}W+(\partial_{\alpha}n)\partial^{2}_{i}W\Big)(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{p-1}\right|\leq C^{*}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{p},

where we recall the shorthand
C^{*}
from (
43
).

•

Next, using the equation for the potential (
45
), we estimate that

\left|p\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{p-1}\partial_{\alpha}\partial^{2}_{i}W\right|\leq C_{p}\left(\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{p-1}\partial_{\alpha}W\right|+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n(\partial_{\alpha}n)^{p-1}\partial_{\alpha}(n^{\gamma})\right|\right).

(46)

For the first term on the right-hand side of (
46
), using the inductive assumption on
W
in (
41
):

5
Long-time behavior of global strong solutions

Now that we have shown that nontrivial global strong solutions can exist in Theorem
1.1
, we study the long-time behavior of global strong solutions to the system (
2
) with general smooth initial data
n_{0}\geq 0
. In this case, we cannot obtain lower bounds on the density away from zero (see Proposition
2.5
) and we must therefore use a more refined analysis to study the long-time behavior of the system. The main idea will be to do a “level-set analysis”, which involves analyzing the equation through a geometric perspective. By Proposition
2.4
, we must have
0\leq n(t,x)\leq\max(n^{*},M)
, for all
t\geq 0
,
where
\displaystyle M:=\text{max}_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)
. Hence, consider the level set
\{n_{0}(x)=\xi\}
for some
0\leq\xi\leq\max(n^{*},M)
. Rewriting the equations using
\boldsymbol{u}:=-\nabla W
, we obtain:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}n+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla n=\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}n^{1+\gamma}+\mu^{-1}nW,\\
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})=a\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}-\mu^{-1}W.\end{cases}

(51)

Rewriting these equations in this form, we note that there are
three contributions
to the way in which this level set
\{n_{0}=\xi\}
for the initial data evolves over time:

•

Transport of the cell density
\boldsymbol{n}
via the flow
\boldsymbol{u}
.
The left-hand side of the first equation in (
51
) shows the transport of
n(t,x)
via the velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)
. Analyzing this transport phenomena involves understanding the flow map associated to the time-dependent vector field
\boldsymbol{u}:=-\nabla W
, which we will discuss in Section
5.1
.

•

Accretion and depletion of cells.
From the first equation, we see from the right-hand side that as the cells are transported by the fluid velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
, we either have accretion (increase) or depletion (decrease) of cell density along the flow lines of the velocity depending on the sign of the right-hand side. Note that for
\eta
defined as the solution to
an-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}=0
, we have that there is
always
(strictly positive) accretion of cells for level sets with
0<\xi<\eta
, due to the non-negativity of
W
.

•

Expansion and contraction of the level set.
Because the level set is transported by the fluid velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
, we have local expansion/contraction of the level set depending on the sign of
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})
. The magnitude of
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})
gives the time derivative of the local expansion or contraction of volume of the level set, according to
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})=\mu^{-1}(an^{\gamma}-W)
.

To make this level set analysis concrete, we introduce the
cumulative distribution function
for the cell density at each time
t
, given by
F_{t}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\text{meas}(\Omega)]
, defined as

F_{t}(\xi)=\text{meas}\{n(t,\cdot)\leq\xi\}.

(52)

For example, by the maximum principle discussed in Proposition
2.4
, we have that
F_{t}(\xi)=\text{meas}(\Omega)
for
\xi\geq\max(n^{*},M)
where
\displaystyle M:=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}n_{0}(x)
for smooth solutions. The main intermediate result about the dynamics of the tumor growth model is as follows:

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists
\nu\in(0,\eta)
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(\xi)=0
for all
0\leq\xi\leq\nu
.

To prove this result, we will first study the flow map of the vector field
\boldsymbol{u}:=-\nabla W
to analyze the transport portion of the first equation in (
51
) for the cell density, in Section
5.1
. Then, we establish Proposition
5.1
by showing that
W(t,x)\geq w>0
for some constant

, on a set of measure arbitrarily close to the full measure of
\Omega
. In this case, the second equation in (
51

\boldsymbol{u}
is strictly negative for almost the entire vacuum set

, so that the vacuum is essentially “exponentially shrinking” in time. Showing this lower bound on

will involve carefully analyzing the elliptic equation

5.1
The flow map

We assume that we have a global strong solution

Since

is uniformly Lipschitz on bounded time intervals, we claim that the flow map is globally well-defined, and furthermore, it is a bijective map.

Proposition 5.2

\boldsymbol{u}
is Lipschitz. (If
\Phi_{t}(x_{1})=\Phi_{t}(x_{2})
for two different points
x_{1},x_{2}\in\Omega
, this would contradict uniqueness of solutions to the flow map locally).

Surjectivity.
Given some fixed
t_{0}>0
, we can find for any
x\in\Omega
a corresponding
y\in\Omega
such that
x=\Phi_{t_{0}}(y)
, by considering the flow (after time
t_{0}
) of the reversed vector field
\boldsymbol{v}(t,x):=-\boldsymbol{u}(t_{0}-t,x)
starting at
x
, which would give the point
y
.

Lipschitz continuity of
\Phi_{t}(\cdot)
.
By integrating the differential equation (
53
), we obtain:

|\Phi_{t}(x)-\Phi_{t}(y)|\leq|x-y|+\int_{0}^{t}|\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x))-\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(y))|ds\leq|x-y|+L_{T}\int_{0}^{t}|\Phi_{s}(x)-\Phi_{s}(y)|ds,

where
L_{T}
is defined in (
54
). Thus by Gronwall’s inequality,
|\Phi_{t}(x)-\Phi_{t}(y)|\leq|x-y|e^{L_{T}t}
, for
x,y\in\Omega
, so
\Phi_{t}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant bounded above by
e^{L_{T}t}
.
∎

Since the flow
\Phi_{t}(x)
of the vector field
\boldsymbol{u}
is globally defined in time, we can use the method of characteristics to explicitly write the solution to the first equation in (
51
) as

n(t,x)=n_{0}(\Phi^{-1}_{t}(x))+\int_{0}^{t}(\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}n^{1+\gamma}+\mu^{-1}nW)(s,\Phi_{s}(\Phi^{-1}_{t}(x)))ds,

(56)

since by the chain rule, this equation is the same as

\frac{d}{ds}n(s,\Phi_{s}(x))=(\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}n^{1+\gamma}+\mu^{-1}nW)(s,\Phi_{s}(x)).

Since by Proposition
5.2
, the flow map
\Phi_{t}(x)
is Lipschitz continuous as a map from
\Omega
to itself, for each
t\geq 0
, we can establish a relationship between the rate of expansion/contraction of sets under the flow
\Phi_{t}(\cdot)
and the divergence of the vector field
\boldsymbol{u}
which generates the flow.

Proposition 5.3
.

The flow map
\Phi_{t}(\cdot):\Omega\to\Omega
satisfies the following differential equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\Big(\text{det}(\nabla\Phi_{t}(\cdot))\Big)=\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})(t,\Phi_{t}(\cdot))\text{det}(\nabla\Phi_{t}(\cdot)),\qquad\text{det}(\nabla\Phi_{0}(\cdot))=1.

(57)

Furthermore, for any measurable set
S\subset\Omega
,

\text{meas}(\Phi_{t}(S))=\int_{\Omega}1_{S}(x)\text{det}(\nabla\Phi_{t}(x))dx=\int_{\Omega}1_{S}(x)\text{exp}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x)))ds\right)dx.

(58)

In differential form,

\frac{d}{dt}\Big(\text{meas}(\Phi_{t}(S))\Big)=\int_{\Omega}1_{S}(x)\text{div}\Big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,\Phi_{t}(x))\Big)\text{exp}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\text{div}\Big(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x))\Big)ds\right)dx.

(59)

Proof.

We can verify that the Jacobian of the Lipschitz map
\Phi_{t}(\cdot):\Omega\to\Omega
satisfies the differential equation in (
57
) by direct computation using the chain rule, and furthermore,
\nabla\Phi_{0}(x)
is the identity matrix for all
x\in\Omega
. Note that if we integrate (
57
), we obtain

\text{det}(\nabla\Phi_{t}(x))=\text{exp}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x)))\right)ds.

(60)

The first equality in (
58
) follows from writing
\displaystyle\text{meas}(\Phi_{t}(S))=\int_{\Omega}1_{\Phi(t,S)}(x)dx
and then using a change of variables
x\to\Phi_{t}(x)
. The change of variables can be accomplished by using a change of variables formula, see Theorem 3.9 in
[
23
]
, which we can apply since
\Phi_{t}(\cdot)
is a Lipschitz continuous bijective map from
\Omega
to
\Omega
. The second equality in (
58
) follows by applying the explicit solution in (
60
).
∎

5.2
Level set analysis

To prove Proposition
5.1
, we carefully analyze the behavior of the system near vacuum. This will involve analyzing the relative balance between the effects of accretion/depletion and expansion/contraction on the level sets of the initial data, as the system evolves. In particular, we will use the formula in (
58
) which governs the expansion/contraction of measurable sets under the flow map of the time-dependent vector field
\boldsymbol{u}(t,x)
to perform a
level set analysis
, namely an analysis of how level sets of the initial data
\{n_{0}(x)=\xi\}\subset\Omega
evolve under the flow, both in terms of the change in the level itself
\xi
due to accretion/depletion of cells and also in terms of expansion/contraction due to the divergence of the flow
\boldsymbol{u}
.

From the first equation of (
51
), given an initial level set
E_{\xi}:=\{n_{0}(x)=\xi\}
, we can use the nonnegativity of
W
in Proposition
2.2
and the comparison principle to conclude that

n(t,x)\geq\overline{n}(t),\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\Phi_{t}(E_{\xi}),

(61)

where
\overline{n}(t)
(as just a scalar-valued function of time) satisfies the ODE:

\frac{d\overline{n}}{dt}=\alpha\overline{n}-\beta\overline{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}\overline{n}^{1+\gamma},\qquad\overline{n}(0)=\xi.

(62)

Using a stability analysis and the fundamental theorem on existence and uniqueness of differential equations (Theorem 2.1 in
[
40
]
), we make the following observations:

•

If
\xi=0
, then the solution to this ODE is constant,
\overline{n}(t)=0
. If
\xi=\eta
, then the solution to this ODE is also constant,
\overline{n}(t)=\eta
.

•

If
0<\xi<\eta
, then the solution to this ODE is globally defined and increasing in
t
, with
0<\overline{n}(t,x)<\eta
for all
t\in(-\infty,\infty)
.

•

If
\xi>\eta
, then
\overline{n}(t)
exists for all
t
, and
\overline{n}(t)
is decreasing with
\overline{n}(t)>\eta
for all
t\geq 0
.

These observations allow us to perform a careful level set analysis, which will analyze how level sets change under accretion/depletion of cells as they are transported by the flow. This will allow us to analyze how the measure of level sets change under the flow. Namely, the goal is to prove Proposition
5.1
, namely show that for some
\nu\in(0,\eta)
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{meas}\{n(t,\cdot)\leq\nu\}=0
. To do this, we using these intermediate steps proved in the next three lemmas:

•

Step 1.
We show that

\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(0)=r\qquad\text{for some constant $r\geq 0$}.

(63)

This shows that the
measure of the vacuum converges to some constant
, but since
r
could be positive,
this does not definitively show that the vacuum vanishes in long time
(which is what we would need for Proposition
5.1
).

•

Step 2.
We show that for some
\nu>0
sufficiently small,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{meas}\{x\in\Omega:0<n(t,x)<\nu\}=0.

(64)

Thus, to prove Proposition
5.1
, it remains to improve the result from Step 1 to show that the vacuum
\{n(t,x)=0\}
vanishes in long time.

•

Step 3.
In this step, we improve the result in Step 1 and show that
r=0
in (
63
), so that the
vacuum vanishes in long time
. To do this, we use the elliptic equation
-\mu\Delta W+W=an^{\gamma}
for
W
to show that for all
t\geq\tau
for
\tau
sufficiently large:

W(t,x)\geq w>0\qquad\text{for some positive constant $w$},

(65)

on a set of almost full measure on
\Omega
. This would allow us, using the second equation in (
51
), to conclude that the constant
r
in Step 1 is equal to zero. This would complete the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Lemma 5.1

(Step 1, see (
63
))
.

Let
n(t,x)\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for
p>d
solve (
2
) for nonnegative smooth initial data
n_{0}(x)\geq 0
. Then,
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(0)
exists and this limit is equal to
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(0):=r
, for some constant
0\leq r\leq\text{meas}\{n_{0}(x)=0\}
, where
F_{t}(\xi)
is defined in (
52
). Furthermore,
F_{t}(0)
is a decreasing function of time.

Proof.

Using (
61
) and the differential equation for
\overline{n}
in (
62
), we can observe that

\Phi_{t}^{-1}(V_{t}):=\Phi^{-1}_{t}\Big(\{x\in\Omega:n(t,x)=0\}\Big)\subset\{x\in\Omega:n_{0}(x)=0\}.

Therefore, using the identity in (
58
), we obtain that

\text{meas}(V_{t})=\int_{\Omega}1_{\Phi^{-1}_{t}(V_{t})}\text{exp}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x)))ds\right)dx.

(66)

Note that for
x\in\Phi_{t}^{-1}(V_{t})
,
n(s,\Phi_{s}(x))=0
for all
s\in[0,t]
by the properties of (
62
) (namely that the solution
\overline{n}
at any strictly positive initial condition remains strictly positive). Therefore, using the second equation in (
51
),
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})=a\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}-\mu^{-1}W
, which is non-positive whenever
n=0
by Proposition
2.2
, we conclude that
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{x}(s)))=-W(s,\Phi_{x}(s))\leq 0
for
x\in\Phi_{t}^{-1}(V_{t})
and for all
s\in[0,t]
. By differentiating (
66
) with respect to
t
, we conclude that
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\text{meas}(V_{t})\leq 0
, so that the measure
F_{t}(0)
of the vacuum set is decreasing in time. Therefore, because
F_{t}(0)
is nonnegative and decreasing in
t
, it must have a nonnegative limit,
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(0):=r\geq 0
.
∎

Lemma 5.2

(Step 2, see (
64
))
.

Let
n(t,x)\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W(t,x)\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for
p>d
be solutions to (
2
) for smooth initial data
n_{0}(x)\geq 0
. Then, there exists
\nu>0
sufficiently small such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{meas}\Big(\{x\in\Omega:0<n(t,x)\leq\nu\}\Big)=0
, and hence, for any
0<\xi\leq\nu
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(\xi)=r:=\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(0)
.

Proof.

Recall the comparison principle in (
61
) and the ODE for
\overline{n}
from (
62
). Choose
\nu\in(0,\eta)
, where we recall that
\eta
is the positive zero of
\alpha-\beta n^{\gamma\theta}-a\mu^{-1}n^{\gamma}=0
, such that
\displaystyle\beta\nu^{\gamma\theta}+a\mu^{-1}\nu^{\gamma}<\frac{\alpha}{2}
. Then, for
0<\xi\leq\nu
, any solution of (
62
) satisfying
\overline{n}(0)=\xi
is defined for all
t\in(-\infty,\infty)
, and furthermore, for all
t\leq 0
:

\frac{d\overline{n}}{dt}=\alpha\overline{n}-\beta\overline{n}^{1+\gamma\theta}-a\overline{n}^{1+\gamma}>\frac{\alpha}{2}\overline{n}>0.

So a solution
\overline{n}
to (
62
) with
\overline{n}(0)=\xi
for
0<\xi\leq\nu
satisfies the following decay estimate:
\overline{n}(t)\leq\xi e^{\alpha t/2}
, for
t\leq 0
. By shifting in time, we have that for any time
t_{0}\geq 0
,

\overline{n}(t_{0})=\xi\text{ for }0<\xi\leq\nu\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\overline{n}(t)\leq\xi e^{-\alpha(t_{0}-t)/2}\text{ for all }0\leq t\leq t_{0}.

(67)

So consider the level set
S_{t,\nu}:=\{x\in\Omega:0<n(t,x)\leq\nu\}
. Then, using (
58
), we obtain

\text{meas}(S_{t,\nu})=\int_{\Omega}1_{\Phi_{t}^{-1}(S_{t,\nu})}\text{exp}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x)))ds\right)dx.

(68)

By (
67
), we have that

\Phi_{t}^{-1}(S_{t,\nu})\subset\{x\in\Omega:0<n_{0}(x)\leq\nu e^{-\alpha t/2}\}.

(69)

Using the second equation of (
51
),
W\geq 0
from Proposition
2.2
, and the estimate that for
x\in\Phi_{t}^{-1}(S_{t,\nu})
,

0<n(s,\Phi_{s}(x))\leq\nu e^{-\alpha(t-s)/2}\quad\text{ for }s\in[0,t],

we obtain that

\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x)))\leq a\mu^{-1}\nu^{\gamma}e^{-\alpha\gamma(t-s)/2},\qquad\text{ for }x\in\Phi_{t}^{-1}(S_{t,\nu})\text{ and }0\leq s\leq t.

(70)

So applying (
69
) and (
70
) in (
68
), we obtain that:

\displaystyle\text{meas}\Big(\{x\in\Omega

\displaystyle:0<n(t,x)\leq\nu\}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\text{meas}\Big(\{x\in\Omega:0<n_{0}(x)\leq\nu e^{-\alpha t/2}\}\Big)\text{exp}\left(a\mu^{-1}\nu^{\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\alpha\gamma(t-s)/2}ds\right)

\displaystyle\leq\text{meas}\Big(\{x\in\Omega:0<n_{0}(x)\leq\nu e^{-\alpha t/2}\}\Big)e^{2a\mu^{-1}\alpha^{-1}\gamma^{-1}\nu^{\gamma}}.

The result follows once we note that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{meas}\Big(\{x\in\Omega:0<n_{0}(x)\leq\nu e^{-\alpha t/2}\}\Big)=0
.
∎

Finally, we complete the proof of Proposition
5.1
by showing the result of Step 3 in (
65
). Namely, we want to show that the vacuum set vanishes as
t\to\infty
. As of now, from Lemma
5.1
, we have that for a strong solution
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
with
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
and
p>d
to (
2
) for smooth nonnegative initial data
n_{0}
that is not identically zero,
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(0):=r
, for
0\leq r<\text{meas}(\Omega)
. We claim that
r=0
, by showing that
W(t,x)\geq w>0
for all
t\geq 0
on a subset of
\Omega
of arbitrarily large measure, so that by (
51
), we see that
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq-\mu^{-1}w<0
on almost the entire vacuum set so that the measure of the vacuum is essentially exponentially decreasing in time by (
58
).

To show the strict positivity of
W
away from zero, we analyze the elliptic equation

-\mu\Delta W+W=f,\qquad\nabla W\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

(71)

for a general source term
f
. Recall that the
Green’s function
G(x,y)
in the context of equation (
71
), is defined to be a function
G(x,y)
for
x,y\in\Omega
that satisfies:

-\mu\Delta_{x}G(x,y)+G(x,y)=\delta_{y},\qquad\nabla_{x}G(x,y)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

where
\delta_{y}
is the Dirac delta distribution centered at the point
y\in\Omega
. Given the Green’s function, the solution to the inhomogeneous problem (
71
) is:

W(x)=\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)f(y)dy,\qquad\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

(72)

We emphasize that the Green’s function is dependent on the specific geometry of
\Omega
. By studying properties of the Green’s function
G(x,y)
, we are able to obtain lower bounds on
W(t,x)
. To do this, we use standard properties of (
71
) and of the Green’s function, which we prove for the interested reader in the appendix, see Section
7
.

We will now prove the asymptotic vanishing of vacuum in Step 3 in (
65
) as follows. This improves the result from Step 1 in Lemma
5.1
by showing that the limiting measure of the vacuum set
r
is equal to
0
in long time.

Lemma 5.3

(Step 3, see (
65
))
.

Let
n(t,x)\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
for
p>d
be solutions to (
2
) for smooth nonnegative initial data
n_{0}(x)
that is not identically zero. Then,
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(0)=0
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
r=0
in Lemma
5.1
. We assume for contradiction that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{meas}\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}:=r>0,

(73)

where
r<\text{meas}(\Omega)
since the initial data
n_{0}(x)
is not identically zero. For the choice of
\nu\in(0,\eta)
in Lemma
5.2
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{meas}\left\{n(t,\cdot)>\nu\right\}=\text{meas}(\Omega)-r
. Thus, for some
\tau
sufficiently large,

\text{meas}\{n(t,\cdot)>\nu\}\geq\frac{\text{meas}(\Omega)-r}{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\text{meas}\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}\leq\frac{5r}{4},\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq\tau.

(74)

Define the following compact set
K^{\delta}_{y}\subset\Omega
for each
y\in\Omega
and
\delta>0
:

K^{\delta}_{y}:=\{x\in\Omega:|x-y|\geq\delta\text{ and }\text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\geq\delta\},

and let
K^{\delta}\subset\Omega\times\Omega
be the compact set:

K^{\delta}:=\bigcup_{y\in\Omega,\text{dist}(y,\partial\Omega)\geq\delta}\Big(\{y\}\times K_{y}^{\delta}\Big).

Choose
\delta
sufficiently small so that

\text{meas}(\Omega\setminus K_{x}^{\delta})\leq\min\left(\frac{\text{meas}(\Omega)-r}{4},\frac{r}{4}\right),\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\Omega^{\delta}:=\{x\in\Omega:\text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\geq\delta\}.

(75)

Because
K^{\delta}
is a compact subset of
\Omega\times\Omega
on which the Green’s function
G(x,y)
is continuous and strictly positive (see Proposition
7.1
), we obtain that for some positive constant
m
:

G(x,y)\geq m>0\qquad\text{ for $(x,y)\in K^{\delta}$}.

(76)

We will use (
76
) to derive a contradiction, by showing that most of the vacuum set (due to the positivity of the Green’s function) is exponentially shrinking, which will contradict the limit in (
73
) since
r>0
. By (
72
),
\displaystyle W(t,x)=a\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)n^{\gamma}(t,y)dy
. Observe that since
n(t,x)\geq 0
and
G(x,y)
is strictly positive on
\Omega\times\Omega
by Proposition
7.1
, we can obtain by (
76
) that:

W(t,x)\geq am\int_{K_{x}^{\delta}}n^{\gamma}(t,y)dy,\qquad\text{ for }x\in\Omega\text{ with }\text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\geq\delta.

By combining (
74
) and (
75
), we see that for all
t\geq\tau
and for all
x\in\Omega
with
\text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\geq\delta
:

\text{meas}\Big(\{n(t,\cdot)>\nu\}\cap K^{\delta}_{x}\Big)\geq\frac{\text{meas}(\Omega)-r}{4},

and hence, for all
t\geq\tau
:

W(t,x)\geq am\left(\frac{\text{meas}(\Omega)-r}{4}\right)\nu^{\gamma}:=w>0,\qquad\text{ for }x\in\Omega^{\delta}:=\{x\in\Omega:\text{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)\geq\delta\}.

Note that the condition (
75
) implies that
\text{meas}(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\delta})\leq r/4
, and thus,
W(t,x)\geq w>0
for all
x\in\Omega^{\delta}
, which is a set of measure at least
\displaystyle\text{meas}(\Omega)-\frac{r}{4}
. So since
\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}
has measure at least
r
for all
t
by Lemma
5.1
, we have that for all
t\geq\tau
:

\text{meas}\Big(\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}\cap\{W(t,\cdot)\geq w\}\Big)\geq\frac{3r}{4},

(77)

and on this set
\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}\cap\{W(t,\cdot)\geq w\}
, which has measure at least
3r/4
for all
t\geq\tau
,
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})=-\mu^{-1}W\leq-\mu^{-1}w
. Therefore, by the vacuum bound in (
74
) and (
77
), for all
t\geq\tau
:

\text{meas}\Big(\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}\cap\{W(t,\cdot)<w\}\Big)\leq\frac{5r}{4}-\frac{3r}{4}=\frac{r}{2},

on this set,
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})=-\mu^{-1}W\leq 0
by Proposition
2.2
. To summarize, for each
t\geq\tau
, the vacuum
\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}
consists of two disjoint sets:

•

A vacuum set
V_{1}(t):=\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}\cap\{W(t,\cdot)\geq w\}
on which
W
is positive and strictly bounded away from zero, with

\text{meas}(V_{1}(t))\geq 3r/4,\qquad\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq-\mu^{-1}w<0\text{ on }V_{1}(t).

(78)

•

A vacuum set
V_{2}(t):=\{n(t,\cdot)=0\}\setminus V_{1}(t)
of measure
\text{meas}(V_{2}(t))\leq r/2
, on which we only know that
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq 0
.

Let
V_{t}:=\{x\in\Omega:n(t,x)=0\}
be the vacuum. By properties of the ODE (
62
) and (
61
), we have that
\Phi_{t}(V_{s})=V_{s+t}
and since
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq 0
by (
51
) on the vacuum set, we conclude that
\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Big(\text{meas}(V_{t})\Big)\leq 0
. In addition, using (
59
), for
t\geq\tau
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\text{meas}(V_{t}))

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}1_{V_{\tau}}(x)\text{div}\Big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,\Phi_{t-\tau}(x))\Big)\text{exp}\left(\int_{0}^{t-\tau}\text{div}\Big(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x))\Big)ds\right)dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}1_{\Phi_{t-\tau}^{-1}(V_{1}(t))}(x)\text{div}\Big(\boldsymbol{u}(t,\Phi_{t-\tau}(x))\Big)\text{exp}\left(\int_{\tau}^{t}\text{div}\Big(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x))\Big)ds\right)dx,

since
\Phi_{t-\tau}^{-1}(V_{1}(t))\subset\Phi_{t-\tau}^{-1}(V_{t})=V_{\tau}
,
\Phi_{t-\tau}(V_{\tau})=V_{t}
, and
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq 0
on the vacuum set. Since
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq-\mu^{-1}w<0
on
V_{1}(t)
, we estimate that for all
t\geq\tau
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\text{meas}(V_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq-\mu^{-1}w\int_{\Omega}1_{\Phi_{t-\tau}^{-1}(V_{1}(t))}(x)\text{exp}\left(\int_{0}^{t-\tau}\text{div}\Big(\boldsymbol{u}(s,\Phi_{s}(x))\Big)ds\right)dx

\displaystyle=-\mu^{-1}w\cdot\text{meas}(V_{1}(t))\leq-\frac{3}{4}\mu^{-1}wr<0,

by (
78
). Since
\displaystyle-\frac{3}{4}\mu^{-1}wr
is a negative constant, this contradicts the initial assumption that
F_{t}(0):=\text{meas}(V_{t})\geq r
for all
t\geq 0
. This establishes the result that
r=0
.
∎

Combining Lemma
5.1
, Lemma
5.2
, and Lemma
5.3
establishes Proposition
5.1
, that there exists some
\nu>0
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(\nu):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{meas}\Big(\{x\in\Omega:0\leq n(t,x)\leq\nu\}\Big)=0
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2

Equipped with Proposition
5.1
, we can now establish the long-time behavior result.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By Theorem
5.1
, there exists
\nu>0
, such that
0<\nu<\eta
and

\lim_{t\to\infty}F_{t}(\nu):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{meas}\{x\in\Omega:n(t,x)\leq\nu\}=0.

(79)

Furthermore, by Proposition
2.4
, for each
\lambda>n^{*}
, there exists a corresponding
T_{\lambda}>0
for which
0\leq n(t,x)\leq\lambda
for all
t\geq T_{\lambda}
. (Though Proposition
2.4
is for smooth solutions, this maximum bound holds for strong solutions with
n\in C(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
and
W\in C(0,T;W^{3,p}(\Omega))
, which are unique, by an approximation argument, for example involving artificial viscosity as in
[
41
,
42
]
.)

Fix an arbitrary choice of
\lambda>n^{*}
and note that for
\hat{G}(n):=\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}
, where
\hat{G}(n)=nG(n)
for
G(n)
as defined in (
1
):

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)dx\right)=-\int_{\Omega}(\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta})=-\text{meas}(\Omega)\cdot\hat{G}(\lambda)+\int_{\Omega}\Big(\hat{G}(\lambda)-\hat{G}(n)\Big).

Then, there exists a positive constant
c_{\nu}
(independent of
\lambda
) such that for
\hat{G}(n):=\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}
:

0\leq c_{\nu}(\lambda-n)\leq\hat{G}(n)-\hat{G}(\lambda),\qquad\text{ for all }\nu\leq n\leq\lambda.

(80)

To see this, we observe that due to the fact that
\hat{G}(z):=\alpha n-\beta n^{1+\gamma\theta}
is concave down,

0\leq\frac{\hat{G}(\nu)}{n^{*}-\nu}(\lambda-n)=\frac{\hat{G}(\nu)-\hat{G}(n^{*})}{n^{*}-\nu}(\lambda-n)\leq\frac{\hat{G}(\nu)-\hat{G}(\lambda)}{\lambda-\nu}(\lambda-n)\leq\hat{G}(n)-\hat{G}(\lambda),\\
\qquad\text{ for all }\nu\leq n\leq\lambda,

so we can set
\displaystyle c_{\nu}:=\frac{\hat{G}(\nu)}{n^{*}-\nu}
. Then, we estimate that for
t\geq T_{\lambda}
:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)dx\right)\leq c_{\nu}\lambda F_{t}(\nu)-\text{meas}(\Omega)\cdot\hat{G}(\lambda)-c_{\nu}\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n).

(81)

We emphasize that
-\text{meas}(\Omega)\cdot\hat{G}(\lambda)>0
since
\hat{G}(\lambda)<0
.

We want to show that
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}(n^{*}-n)=0
. Thus, consider an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
, and choose
\lambda>n^{*}
sufficiently close to
n^{*}
such that

\max\Big(\text{meas}(\Omega)\cdot(\lambda-n^{*}),c_{\nu}^{-1}\text{meas}(\Omega)\cdot(-\hat{G}(\lambda))\Big)<\frac{\epsilon}{4}.

(82)

Then, given this choice of
\lambda
, we have an associated time
T_{\lambda}
for which the differential inequality (
81
) holds for all
t\geq T_{\lambda}
. By (
79
), we choose a time
T_{\epsilon}
sufficiently large such that

T_{\epsilon}\geq T_{\lambda},\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda F_{t}(\nu)<\frac{\epsilon}{4}\text{ for all }t\geq T_{\epsilon}.

(83)

Then, we can consider the differential inequality (
81
) from the initial time
T_{\epsilon}
, where we note that because
0\leq n(t,\cdot)\leq\lambda
for all
t\geq T_{\epsilon}\geq T_{\lambda}
, we have that
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)\geq 0
for all
t\geq T_{\epsilon}
. We hence obtain from (
81
), (
82
), and (
83
) that for all
t\geq T_{\epsilon}
:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)dx\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}c_{\nu}-c_{\nu}\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)dx.

By solving this differential inequality, we obtain that

0\leq\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)(t)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+Ce^{-c_{\nu}(t-T_{\epsilon})},\qquad\text{ for }t\geq T_{\epsilon},

for some constant
C
, depending on the initial data
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)(T_{\epsilon},\cdot)dx
at time
T_{\epsilon}
.
So for a sufficiently large time
\tau_{\epsilon}\geq T_{\epsilon}
, we have that

0\leq\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)(t)\leq\frac{3\epsilon}{4},\qquad\text{ for all }t\geq\tau_{\epsilon}\geq T_{\epsilon}\geq T_{\lambda}.

(84)

We have that for all
t\geq T_{\lambda}
, for which we recall that
0\leq n(t,x)\leq\lambda
:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}(\lambda-n)-\int_{\Omega}|n^{*}-n|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{n\geq n^{*}}\Big((\lambda-n)-(n-n^{*})\Big)\right|+\left|\int_{n<n^{*}}(\lambda-n^{*})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{n\geq n^{*}}(\lambda-n^{*})+\int_{n<n^{*}}(\lambda-n^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\text{meas}(\Omega)(\lambda-n^{*}),

so combining this with (
82
) and (
84
), we obtain that
\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{\Omega}|n^{*}-n(t,x)|\leq\epsilon
for
t\geq\tau_{\epsilon}
, which establishes that
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|n(t,x)-n^{*}|=0
. By using the upper bound from Proposition
2.4
that
0\leq n(t,x)\leq M
, we also have that:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|n(t,x)-n^{*}|^{q}=0,\qquad\text{ for all }1\leq q<\infty.

(85)

Consider arbitrary
1\leq q<\infty
. To show that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|W(t,x)-W^{*}\|_{W^{2,q}(\Omega)}=0
for
W^{*}=a(n^{*})^{\gamma}
, note that

-\mu\Delta(W-W^{*})+(W-W^{*})=a(n^{\gamma}-(n^{*})^{\gamma}),\qquad\nabla(W-W^{*})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

Since
n\to n^{*}
in
L^{q}(\Omega)
as
t\to\infty
, we have by the mean value theorem and the maximum principle (Proposition
2.4
) that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|a(n^{\gamma}-(n^{*})^{\gamma})\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}=0
and thus by Calderón-Zygmund estimates (Proposition
2.1
and
2.2
), we conclude that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|W-W^{*}\|_{W^{2,q}(\Omega)}=0
also, for all
1\leq q<\infty
.
∎

6
Conclusion

Finally, we summarize the results in this manuscript and mention some interesting directions for future investigation that arise naturally from our results. We have shown, for sufficiently large viscosity parameter
\mu
, the existence of nontrivial global strong (and even smooth) solutions for data with sufficiently small gradient, which can intuitively be thought of as initial data with small deviations, but with no restriction on the smooth initial data nonnegative
n_{0}(x)
itself (only on its gradient), other than that it is strictly positive (bounded away from vacuum). This is possible due to the additional dissipation arising from the effects of cell death in the growth rate function
G(p)
. While it was previously known that global weak solutions exist
[
41
,
42
]
for
n_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we present in this manuscript new results on global strong solutions. This is a delicate matter, in particular because it is well-known that transport equations may exhibit singularities and hence, in general are best understood in a weak (or even a renormalized weak) sense, see
[
10
,
16
]
. Even though it has been shown that the presence of damping may give rise to strong solutions for small data, for example to the 3D compressible Euler equations, even for these equations, singularities can form for arbitrary initial data, see the discussion in
[
39
]
for example.

It would be particularly interesting to characterize more specific conditions for which global strong solutions exist for (
2
). The fact that our result in Theorem
1.1
holds for sufficiently large viscosity
\mu
, initial data away from vacuum
n_{0}(x)>0
, and small gradient initial data, suggests future investigation of understanding whether nontrivial strong solutions exist for arbitrary viscosity parameters
\mu
, or in the case of vacuum. The difficulty for considering initial data with vacuum is that the dissipative term
\displaystyle-\beta(\gamma\theta+1)p\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}n^{\gamma\theta}n^{p}_{j}
in (
36
) vanishes when
n=0
. In addition, to make this dissipative term have “enough” dissipation to absorb the effects of the other nonlinear terms in the estimate, we require
\mu
to be sufficiently large. It would therefore be interesting to consider arbitrary
\mu
, and also the singular limit as
\mu\to 0
.

We have also shown a long-time convergence result for global strong solutions in Proposition
1.2
. It would be interesting to see if this result extends more generally to weak solutions, which always exist globally for
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
nonnegative initial data. In particular, the level set analysis in Section
5
relies primarily on the flow map
\Phi_{t}(x)
for the vector field
\boldsymbol{u}=-\nabla W
. Even at the weak solution level, we have that
n\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
, and hence,
W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,p}(\Omega))
for all
1\leq p<\infty
by Calderón-Zygmund estimates. Then,
\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})=-\Delta W=\mu^{-1}(an^{\gamma}-W)
is in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
. This is sufficient regularity to apply the transport theory of DiPerna and Lions
[
10
,
16
]
, and hence obtain a
generalized flow map
for the Sobolev vector field
\boldsymbol{u}:=-\nabla W\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p}(\Omega))
for all
1\leq p<\infty
. While the low (non-Lipschitz) regularity of the vector field
\boldsymbol{u}
and the generalized flow map may not be sufficient to directly obtain the explicit expansion/contraction of volume formulas in Proposition
5.3
for example, a similar argument of approximating/regularizing the equations (
2
) and passing to a limit, might give insight into long-time behavior of global weak solutions, via a result analogous to Theorem
1.2
.

7
Appendix: Analysis of an elliptic equation

In this appendix, we analyze the elliptic equation with Neumann boundary conditions:

-\mu\Delta W+W=F\text{ on }\Omega,\qquad\nabla W\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(86)

The goal of the analysis will be to deduce properties of the Green’s function (in particular, continuity and positivity properties) that will enable use to show that the vacuum vanishes in long time for global strong solutions to (
2
), in Lemma
5.3
. To do this, we first discuss the fundamental solution for the operator
(-\mu\Delta+I)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for
d=2,3
, and then use these results on the fundamental solution to deduce important properties of the Green’s function for (
86
), which we need for the proof of our long-time behavior result.

7.1
The fundamental solution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

First, we find the fundamental solution for the operator
(-\mu\Delta+I)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for
d=2,3
, which is a function
\Psi(x)
such that

-\mu\Delta\Psi+\Psi=\delta_{0}\qquad\text{ on }{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

in the distributional sense. We can find such a solution by noting that
-\mu\Delta\Psi+\Psi=0
away from the origin. Writing the equation in polar coordinates for a radially symmetric function,
\Psi(x)=w(\mu^{-1/2}r)
, we obtain for
r\neq 0
:

r^{2}w^{\prime\prime}(r)+(d-1)rw^{\prime}(r)-r^{2}w(r)=0.

Fundamental solution for
d=2
.
By the definition of Bessel functions, we obtain the fundamental solution in terms of a
modified Bessel function of the second kind
for a constant
c_{2}
:

w(r)=c_{2}K_{0}(r),\qquad\Psi(x)=c_{2}K_{0}(\mu^{-1/2}|x|),

where
K_{0}
is a modified Bessel function of the second kind, see Section 10.25 of
[
17
]
.

We can solve for
c_{2}
by considering an arbitrary test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, integrating by parts, and omitting a ball around the origin:

\displaystyle\phi(0)=-\mu\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\Psi(x)

\displaystyle\Delta\phi+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\Psi(x)\phi

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}(-\mu\Delta\Psi+\Psi)\phi+\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\Psi(x)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}-\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nu}\phi\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\Psi(x)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}-\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nu}\phi\right).

By (10.29.3), (10.30.2), and (10.30.3) in
[
17
]
,
K_{0}(r)\sim-\log(r)
and
K_{0}^{\prime}(r)\sim-1/r
, as
r\to 0
. Therefore, since
\nabla\phi
is globally bounded due to the compact support of
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\Psi(x)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}\right|\leq-c_{2}\Big(\log(\mu^{-1/2}\epsilon)\Big)\cdot(2\pi\epsilon)\max_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|\nabla\phi|\to 0,\qquad\text{ as }\epsilon\to 0,

(87)

-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nu}\phi=-c_{2}(\mu^{-1/2}\epsilon)^{-1}\cdot\phi(0)\cdot 2\pi\epsilon=-2\pi\mu^{1/2}c_{2}\phi(0).

So we conclude that

\phi(0)=-\mu\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\Psi(x)\Delta\phi+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\Psi(x)\phi=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\Psi(x)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}-\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nu}\phi\right)=-2\pi\mu^{1/2}c_{2}\phi(0).

Therefore,
\displaystyle c_{2}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mu^{1/2}}.

Fundamental solution for
d=3
.
In the case of
d=3
, we obtain by inspection
w(r)=e^{-r}/r
. Therefore, the fundamental solution for a dimensional constant
c_{3}
is:

\Psi(x)=c_{3}\mu^{1/2}\left(\frac{e^{-\mu^{-1/2}|x|}}{|x|}\right).

As in the case of
d=2
, we calculate the constant
c_{3}
by noting that

\phi(0)=-\mu\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\Psi(x)\Delta\phi+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\Psi(x)\phi=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\Psi(x)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}-\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nu}\phi\right).

(88)

We compute that

\left|\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\Psi(x)\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\nu}\right|\leq c_{3}\mu^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1}e^{-\mu^{-1/2}\epsilon}4\pi\epsilon^{2}\max_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla\phi(x)|\to 0,\qquad\text{ as }\epsilon\to 0,

-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B_{\epsilon}(0)}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\nu}\phi=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}c_{3}\mu^{1/2}\left(\epsilon^{-2}e^{-\mu^{1/2}\epsilon}+\mu^{-1/2}\epsilon^{-1}e^{-\mu^{1/2}\epsilon}\right)4\pi\epsilon^{2}\phi(0)=4\pi\mu^{1/2}c_{3}\phi(0).

(89)

Therefore, we conclude that
\displaystyle c_{3}=\frac{1}{4\pi\mu^{1/2}}
.

Conclusion.
Thus, we have that the fundamental solution is:

\Psi(x)=\begin{cases}-c_{2}K_{0}(\mu^{-1/2}|x|),\qquad\text{ for }d=2,\\
\displaystyle c_{3}\left(\frac{e^{-\mu^{1/2}|x|}}{|x|}\right),\qquad\text{ for }d=3,\end{cases}

(90)

where the constants
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
are given by
c_{2}=(2\pi)^{-1}\mu^{-1/2},c_{3}=(4\pi)^{-1}\mu^{-1/2}
.

7.2
Analysis of the Green’s function

We use the fundamental solution (
90
) to analyze the Green’s function for the operator
-\mu\Delta+I
posed on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with smooth boundary. We recall that the Green’s function
G(x,y):\Omega\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
associated to the Neumann problem for the operator
-\mu\Delta+I
on
\Omega
, is a function satisfying for each fixed but arbitrary
x\in\Omega
:

-\mu\Delta_{x}G(x,y)+G(x,y)=\delta_{y},\qquad\nabla_{x}G(x,y)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

Here,
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac delta distribution centered at the point
x\in\Omega
. The Green’s function is useful because it can be used to construct the (unique) solution to the inhomogeneous Neumann problem
-\mu\Delta W+W=f(x)
and
\nabla W\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
via the following integration:

W(x)=\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)f(y)dy.

Given a general bounded domain
\Omega
with smooth boundary, we can construct the Green’s function
G(x,y)
via the fundamental solution
K(x)
computed in (
90
) as follows. Define a
corrector function

\varphi(x,y)
as the solution to the Neumann problem:

-\mu\Delta_{x}\varphi(x,y)+\varphi(x,y)=0,\qquad\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,y)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|_{\partial\Omega}=\nabla_{x}\Psi(\cdot-y)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\Big|_{\partial\Omega}.

(91)

Then, the Green’s function can be expressed in terms of the fundamental solution as

G(x,y)=\Psi(x)-\varphi(x,y).

We can use this characterization of the Green’s function to establish some important properties of
G(x,y)
that will be useful in our analysis of the tumor growth model under consideration. Related to this is the positivity of the Green’s function, and to show this, we first show that the operator
(-\mu\Delta+1)W
in (
71
) has a strict positivity property:

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose that
W\geq 0
satisfies

-\mu\Delta W+W=0

(92)

on an open ball
B
, where
W\in C^{2}(B)\cap C(\overline{B})
. Then, either
W(x)>0
everywhere on
B
, or
W
is identically zero on
B
.

Proof.

By the strong maximum principle (cf. Theorem 4 on pg. 352 in
[
22
]
), if
W(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=0
at an interior point of
B
and is not identically zero on
B
, then
W
attains a nonpositive minimum on the interior of
B
and hence
W
must be constant. However, this is a contradiction, since any constant solution to the equation (
92
) must be identically zero.
∎

Now, we have the components needed to establish these properties of the Green’s function.

Proposition 7.1
.

The Green’s function
G(x,y)
for
-\mu\Delta+I
has the following properties:

•

Symmetry.

G(x,y)=G(y,x)
for
(x,y)\in(\Omega\times\Omega)-D
, where the diagonal
D:=\{(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega:x=y\}
.

•

Continuity off of the diagonal.

G(x,y)
is continuous as a function of
(x,y)\in(\Omega\times\Omega)\setminus D
. Furthermore,
\lim_{(x,y)\to(z,z)}G(x,y)=\infty
for any point
z\in\Omega
, where the limit is taken along points in
\Omega\times\Omega
.

•

Positivity of the Green’s function.

G(x,y)>0
for all
x,y\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Proof of symmetry.
Consider arbitrary points
y,z\in\Omega
such that
y\neq z
and let
B(x,r)
denote the open ball of radius
r
centered at the point
x
. Consider
r>0
sufficiently small so that
B(x,r)\cap B(z,r)=\varnothing
and apply Green’s identity to the set

U_{y,z}:=\Omega\setminus(B(y,r)\cup B(z,r))

to obtain:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{U_{y,z}}\Big(-\mu\Delta_{x}G(x,y)+G(x,y)\Big)G(z,y)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{U_{y,z}}\Big(-\mu\Delta_{x}G(x,z)+G(x,z)\Big)G(x,y)ds+\mu\int_{\partial B(y,r)\cup\partial B(z,r)}\Big(G(x,z)\nabla_{x}G(x,y)-G(x,y)\nabla_{x}G(x,z)\Big)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}dS(x)

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\partial B(y,r)\cup\partial B(z,r)}\Big(G(x,z)\nabla_{y}G(x,y)-G(x,y)\nabla_{y}G(x,z)\Big)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}dS(x),

(93)

where we used the definition of the Green’s function to conclude that
-\mu\Delta_{x}G(x,y)+G(x,y)=0
, along with integration by parts and the zero Neumann boundary condition on the Green’s function. Here, we emphasize that
\boldsymbol{n}
denotes the outer unit normal to the boundary of the balls
B(y,r)
and
B(z,r)
. Because
G(x,y)
for fixed
y
is smooth away from
x=y
, we can conclude that
\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\partial B(z,r)}G(z,y)\nabla_{y}G(x,y)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
and similarly,
\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\partial B(y,r)}G(x,y)\nabla_{y}G(x,z)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
, as in (
87
) and (
88
). The result will be established if we show that

\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\partial B(y,r)}G(x,z)\nabla_{x}G(x,y)=-G(y,z),\qquad\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\partial B(z,r)}G(x,y)\nabla_{x}G(x,z)=-G(z,y),

because then taking the limit as
r\to 0
in (
7.2
) gives the result that
G(y,z)=G(z,y)
.

So it suffices to show that

\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\partial B(y,r)}G(x,z)\nabla_{x}G(x,y)\\
=\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\partial B(y,r)}G(x,z)\nabla_{x}\Psi(x-y)+\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\partial B(y,r)}G(x,z)\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,y)=-G(y,z),

(94)

since an analogous calculation works for the same integral with
y
and
z
interchanged. By (
91
),
\varphi
is the solution to an elliptic equation with constant coefficients and smooth Neumann data on a smooth domain, so
\varphi(x,y)
for fixed
y
is a smooth function of
x
on
\Omega
. Thus, the second term on the right-hand side of (
94
) is zero. By the normalization of the fundamental solution
\Psi(x)
in (
89
), the first term on the right-hand side of (
94
) is
-G(y,z)
, which concludes the proof of (
94
) and hence, of the symmetry property.

Proof of off-diagonal continuity.
The goal is to show that
G(x,y):=\Psi(x-y)+\varphi(x,y)
is continuous as a function of
(x,y)\in(\Omega\times\Omega)\setminus D
. To see this, one can see from the explicit form of the fundamental solution in (
90
) that
\Psi(x-y)
is continuous on
(\Omega\times\Omega)\setminus D
. So it suffices to show that
\varphi(x,y)
is continuous on
\Omega\times\Omega
(and in fact, this is true, including the diagonal). Since
\lim_{(x,y)\to(z,z)}\Psi(x-y)=\infty
, this would also prove the assertion about the limit towards the diagonal being positive infinity since
\varphi(x,y)
would be a bounded in value along on the diagonal, and continuous on all of
\Omega\times\Omega
(including the diagonal).

So we show that
\varphi(x,y)
is continuous on
\Omega\times\Omega
. Fix
x_{0}\in\Omega
and
\epsilon>0
, and note that by smoothness properties of the fundamental solution
\Psi(x)
in (
90
) (away from the singularity at the origin), there exists some
\delta
sufficiently small with
B(x_{0},\delta)\subset\Omega
such that for all
x\in B(x_{0},\delta)
:

\|\nabla_{x}\Psi(x_{0}-y)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}-\nabla_{x}\Psi(x-y)\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\epsilon,\qquad\text{ for }0<\alpha<1.

Then, by Schauder estimates (Theorem 6.30 in
[
31
]
), we can show using the linearity of the equation (
91
) and the bound on the closeness of the Neumann boundary data, that for all
x\in B(x_{0},\delta)
:

\|\varphi(x_{0},\cdot)-\varphi(x,\cdot)\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\epsilon,

which shows that
\varphi(x,y)
is continuous on
\Omega\times\Omega
and hence completes the proof of the claim.

Proof of positivity.
Let
S:=\{(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega:G(x,y)>0\}
.
S
is nonempty since we proved that
\lim_{(x,y)\to(z,z)}G(x,y)=\infty
in the previous property. This statement about the limit towards the diagonal being infinity (along with the continuity of
\varphi(x,y)
on all of
\Omega\times\Omega
), along with the fact that
G(x,y)
is continuous off the diagonal show that
S
is an open set. Then, Proposition
7.1
also shows that
S
is a closed set, so since
\Omega\times\Omega
is connected, we conclude that
S=\Omega\times\Omega
, which completes the proof.

∎
