A synthetic oversampling-based customized ResNet51-Conv1D framework for early colorectal cancer prediction using structured clinical data from the PLCO screening trial
Authors: S. Thanga Prasath, M. M. Asha, B. Nagarajan, Rajkumar Yesuraj, K. Prathapchandran, Y. Sreeraman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Timely diagnosis of colorectal cancer (CRC) is crucial in reducing global cancer deaths. Physicians will benefit significantly by developing an automated prediction system using advanced technology to detect CRC at an early stage. Besides, existing AI-based diagnostics models primarily rely on imaging data, and their effectiveness is inconsistent when applied to clinical data. This study recommends a personalized one-dimensional residual network (ResNet51-Conv1D) structure adapted for structured clinical data analysis to learn hierarchical feature associations from a large-scale structured PLCO dataset. In this study, structured data were used, including nearly 1,54,892 participants aged 55 to 74, composed of 76,679 men and 78,213 women. The suggested model applies block segmentation to preserve the dependency between local features and employs two oversampling methods, SMOTE and ADASYN, in order to address class imbalance and improve representation of minority classes. The statistical effect of oversampling was determined by analyzing the model’s performance before and after oversampling. Before oversampling, the model’s CRC detection metrics were less accurate (recall = 1.83%; F1-score = 2.50%). When block segmentation was incorporated with a standard SMOTE and ADASYN oversampling methods, the sensitivity of CRC improved significantly. SMOTE with 40 and 50 segments performed best, with MCC values of 89.59% and 88.04%, balanced accuracy scores of 94.43% and 93.23%, and G-mean scores of 94.43% and 93.23%. Furthermore, ADASYN enhanced cancer detection robustness with MCC values of 83.33% and G-average scores of 90.41%. These results demonstrate that combining structured feature segmentation with imbalance-handling strategies improves model stability and minority-class detection, showing that the ResNet51-Conv1D framework is a reliable and efficient approach for early CRC detection using imbalanced structured clinical data.

Introduction

Cancer is a condition in which body cells not only abandon their function and grow irresponsibly, but also invade neighboring tissues, causing extensive damage to tissues [1, 2]. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies worldwide among men and women, and is classified as a digestive system disease [3]. CRC is the proliferation of cancerous cells in the colon and rectum and it ranks as the third major cause of cancer related deaths in the world [4, 5]. According to the International Agency of Research on Cancer (IARC), men have a greater likelihood of developing CRC with a lifetime risk of about 1 in 21 and women have a lifetime risk of about 1 in 24 [6]. The risk of CRC is increased by several factors like dietary behavior, such as an excessive intake of red meat or processed meat, alcohol, obesity, tobacco, genetic diseases, and a history of CRC in your family [7, 8]. Early diagnosis is critical as the five-year survival rate of early-stage CRC is approximately 91%, and with the increase in the disease stage, the survival rate declines to just 16% [9].

CRC is detected by several diagnostic methods, such as clinical examination, colonoscopy, sigmoidoscopy, ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and histopathological examination [10]. Of these, histopathological analysis is essential to diagnosing benign and malignant lesions through morphological analysis of cellular and tissue alterations [11]. Nevertheless, the subjectivity and human error can be introduced by the traditional diagnostic processes and the manual analysis of large medical data, which can diminish the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in most cases [12]. The World Health Organization has argued that late diagnosis has a huge impact on survival of patients and it adds to the preventable mortality in the world [13].

Computer-aided diagnostic systems based on deep learning have increasingly been used to assist clinicians in decision-making and minimize the number of errors in manual diagnostics [14–16]. The deep learning (DL) systems, especially multilayer artificial neural networks, have the ability to learn intricate patterns on large data sets and have proved successful on disease prediction problems [16]. Unlike traditional machine learning (ML) methods such as regression models, K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN), and Extreme Gradient Boosting, deep learning approaches can automatically learn hierarchical feature representations without extensive manual feature engineering [17].

The major objective of the study is to detect colorectal cancer patients using a Residual Network (ResNet) deep learning model. To prepare for this, the data were balanced through different oversampling approaches in order to increase the number of cancer samples. The original PLCO data had almost 98% healthy samples and fewer than 2% of the CRC samples. To solve this imbalance, oversampling methods like SMOTE and/or ADASYN were used to balance the CRC samples. In this work, a novel personalised ResNet architecture that uses one-dimensional convolutional neural networks (1D Conv) was designed to capture the data sequences more effectively. The classifier’s performance was evaluated by comparing the results obtained before and after applying oversampling techniques. The evaluation of classifier performance is crucial in our work, for which we used specialised imbalanced performance metrics in addition to general metrics.

The main contributions of the research to the early detection of CRC will be as follows:

i.A customized ResNet-51 architecture incorporating Conv1D layers is developed to learn patterns in high-dimensional, structured clinical variables to facilitate early colorectal cancer detection.ii.An artificial oversampling strategy is integrated to mitigate class imbalance, improving the robustness of the classification model when dealing with minority CRC cases.iii.A procedural experimental analysis will be performed before and after the use of block segmentation and oversampling that helps to conduct a complex evaluation of the proposed model in the absence of balanced clinical data.iv.Special metrics of imbalanced biomedical datasets, such as MCC and balanced performance measures, are used to evaluate the model performance along with the common classification metrics.

Related works and recent advances

ML and DL approaches in cancer prediction

In recent decades, the application of artificial intelligence in the sphere of medicine has increased significantly. In particular, the models representing the different fields of artificial intelligence are being utilized to detect severe chronic illnesses, including cancer and kidney disease. Much of the literature has explained ML- and DL-based approaches to identifying different types of cancers by examining a large body of medical data, such as food habits and radiology data. The prior works on the detection of colorectal cancer relied on conventional supervised learning algorithms [18, 19]. In 2025, Attallah O [20]. developed an integrated computer-aided diagnostic system which combines lightweight Convolutional Neural Networks with handcrafted features, utilizing the LC25000 dataset. The minimal redundancy maximum relevance and non-negative matrix factorization were used in feature extraction and selection. Such hybrid structure showed that it was 2.3% more sensitive and had an accuracy of 99.5%, which means that the hybridization of deep and handcrafting features in combination with feature selection can increase the accuracy of cancer diagnosis. The same year, Chen G. et al. [21] developed a dual-modality radiomics Vision Transformer (ViT) model to detect bone metastasis risk in CRC using a Radiomic features extraction and LASSO regression feature selection model. The study used a CT scan image dataset from a multicenter retrospective cohort of 155 patients (81 metastatic, 74 non-metastatic). The essential 1,316 features extracted and applied LASSO to select 27 key features. The ViT model performed better than the radiomics models, achieving an accuracy of 0.839, demonstrating superior risk prediction.

In recent decades, most researchers have used ML models as a proven approach to identify complex links between diet, lifestyle, and chronic disease risk. Eckart A. C. et al. (2025) [22] developed an XGBoost-based machine-learning framework to assess diet-related influences of diabetes (Type 2) using a large-scale public health dataset. The food information, like animal-sourced foods (ASFs) and plant-based foods (PBFs), was obtained from data collected between 2013 and 2016 (n = 2,746).

The diet-lifestyle factors were transformed with the help of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and were integrated with the initial variables. The optimised gradient-boosting classifier strong predictive performance of 83.4%, which revealed that demographical PCA scores were the most predominant predictors and it was found that diabetes risk was cut down by food sources that are rich in nutrientsEqually, other recent investigations also employed ML methods to identify various types of tumours. Koker G. et al. [23] developed a machine learning methodology with random forest algorithms to use dietary information of people who had undergone colonoscopy to predict the histopathological type of colon polyps in 2025. The results indicated that the SVM model identified more people who frequently consumed bulgur and red meat as having a higher risk of cancer and the model had the best classification performance (76.4) and precision (71.4) to recognize adenomatous polyps. Likewise, Ji X. et al. (2025) [24] designed a prediction model (neural network) to examine how fibre-rich enteral nutrition diet affects postoperative recovery in colorectal cancer patients (n = 164). Four trained ML algorithms were discussed in this paper (Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), neural network and SVM). The neural network showed the level of performance of 86% accuracy, which reveals that fibre-rich foods have the potential of improving immune responses.

Deep learning techniques have been broadly used in different fields of healthcare especially in the diagnosis of diseases to facilitate timely and right clinical decisions. Various hybrid DL models that are combined with various feature engineering methods have been designed and experimented to detect key patterns on large sequential and image medical data. Pal, A. et al. [25] introduced a DL framework in 2025 that combined a Vision Transformer with a Deformable CNN to classify cancers. The study utilised an image dataset of colon and lung tumours covering five categories: colon adenocarcinoma, normal colon tissue, lung adenocarcinoma, normal lung tissue, and lung squamous cell carcinoma. The ViT-DCNN exhibited good performance, achieving an accuracy of 94.24%, surpassing ResNet-152, EfficientNet-B7, SwinTransformer, and TransUNet. Supriya K et al. [26] introduced a DL approach in 2024 that combines long short-term memory networks with a rough–set–based fuzzy approximation model to improve stage-wise CRC detection using gene expression data. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) method was used to eliminate redundant and noisy data and fetch the predominant feature. The classification and prediction models were trained using both Unidirectional and Bidirectional LSTM models, with the Bidirectional LSTM achieving 98.5% accuracy. In 2025, Choudhary et al. [27] proposed the bidirectional vision transformer (OBVT) for CRC detection in images. The study used two colorectal histology datasets: NCT-CRC-HE-100 K and another with histopathological images. Capsule-assisted Res2Net (CAR2N) technology was employed to extract the required input features from the images, and a trimmed pixel density-dependent median filter (TPDMF) method was utilized to enhance the image quality. Furthermore, the OBVT serves as a classifier, with hyperparameters tuned via chaotic-order snake optimization (Ch-SOA). The proposed OBVT framework outperformed the others, achieving maximum accuracies of 97.11% and 97.01% for colorectal histology, respectively, on NCT-CRC-HE-100 K.

Due to the large size of the medical dataset, there is a chance of facing an imbalanced distribution among the classes. In such situations, resampling techniques were used to synthetically enhance and/or decrease the samples and address the imbalances [28]. Lin Y. et al. (2025) [29] developed a ResNet architecture with resampling techniques to analyse a Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) serum dataset comprising 3,551 samples (1,655 cancer cases and 1,896 controls) with seven different cancers. The SERS dataset was highly imbalanced, so the researchers attempted several resampling techniques, including SMOTE, ADASYN, BorderlineSMOTE, SMOTEENN, and SMOTETomek, to balance the samples. Among these, Borderline SMOTE performed as the superior of all resampling methods with an accuracy of 93.15%. Jia J. et al. (2025) [30] designed a machine learning approach for an early diagnosis of coal workers’ pneumoconiosis (CWP) using biochemical examination data from 283 participants (81 CWP cases and 202 controls). To resolve the severe imbalance across the samples, three major oversampling methods, SMOTE, ADASYN, and an integrated feature-selection-based sampling approach, were tested with the following ML classifiers: Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forest-Adaboost ensembles. The RF-Adaboost model, when integrated with SMOTE, demonstrated optimal performance, resulting in an F1-score of 0.8757. Xu Q. et al. (2025) [31] introduced a chronic heart failure (CHF) fatality estimation model with 489 patient samples from the Jackson Heart Study. The dataset was highly imbalanced, to address this, the study assessed five resampling methods including undersampling, Random Oversampling, SMOTE, BorderlineSMOTE, ADASYN, and SMOTE-ENN integrated with seven ML algorithms, such as combined with seven machine-learning models, including decision tree, RF, XGBoost, adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), SVM, naive Bayes (NB), and multilayer perceptron (MLP). The hybrid SMOTE-ENN with XGBoost outperforms traditional oversampling, achieving predictive performance with an accuracy of 81.58% and an F1 score of 0.86.

Although promising results have been achieved, the majority of studies consider image datasets and apply CNNs or Vision Transformers without considering structured clinical data. Materials and methods

Study design and population

The Prostate, Lung, Colorectal and Ovarian (PLCO) Cancer Screening Trial [32] is a randomised, controlled trial designed to evaluate the cancer screening methods reduce the mortality from PLCO cancer. Conducted between 1993 and 2001 across ten hospitals in ten U.S. states, the study enrolled nearly 1,54,892 individuals aged between 55 and 74 [33]. Participants have to answer the Baseline Questionnaire (BQ), Dietary Questionnaire (DQX), and Diet History Questionnaire (DHQ). The trial was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The human subjects review boards of the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and each screening center reviewed and approved this PLCO Screening Trial, and all methods were performed according to the appropriate regulations and guidelines. Furthermore, all individuals provided written informed consent.

Data collection and exposure assessment

Eligible individuals were assigned to either the intervention or the control arm. Participants were excluded if they had a prior diagnosis of PLCO cancers, were enrolled in another cancer-related trial, or had both ovaries removed (for female participants). The three standardized forms were used to collect the data, the Baseline Questionnaire (BQ) [34] was used to collect demographic and health related data (filled by 96.8% of participants), Diet History Questionnaire (DHQ) to measure habitual dietary intake (filled by 77%), and Dietary Questionnaire (DQX) filled by 82%, used in the intervention arm.

These standardized questions are part of the PLCO Screening Trial documentation and are available to approved researchers through the Cancer Data Access System (CDAS) following data use agreements (https://cdas.cancer.gov/datasets/plco/4/).

Data accessibility

The NCI and Cancer Data Access System (CDAS) provided the dataset for this study. The interested candidate in accessing the PLCO dataset mentioned in the study should apply via the CDAS website https://cdas.cancer.gov/learn/plco/instructions/?subtype=Data-Only).

Proposed methodology architecture

Residual networks (ResNet) architecture

In recent times, deep learning techniques have been effectively deployed in the medical field to accurately predict various diseases [35], allowing doctors to make swift decisions and start personalized treatment decisions. Deep learning models usually need time to train and are subject to layer restrictions. The Residual (shortcuts) convolutional neural network (ResNet) is a specific architecture of neural networks [36] built to address the gradient vanishing problem encountered in convolutional neural networks, particularly those with a large number of layers.

This is done by introducing skip connections. Skip connections are direct connections between convolutional layers, as seen in Fig. 1. Figure 2 depicts a five-stage research pipeline approach for predicting colorectal cancer risk based on dietary information from the PLCO database. During the initial phase, the dataset was preprocessed by identifying empty features, removing outliers and irrelevant data, addressing missing and inaccurate values by substituting them with “-1,” and eliminating duplicate entries and redundant characteristics. In Phase 2, the cleaned dataset is separated into healthy participants and colorectal cancer cases for feature selection and processing.

Phase 3 overcomes class imbalance by employing SMOTE, which finds the closest minority class neighbours via Euclidean distance and generates synthetic samples through interpolation. The artificially generated samples are appended to the original dataset, thereby creating a balanced dataset. In Phase 4, the balanced dataset is partitioned into training, testing, and validation subsets using the stratified data split method, and the customized ResNet-51 (Conv1D) classification model is trained to classify the samples as healthy or colorectal cancer. Finally, the model’s performance is evaluated in the last phase using both general metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, and F1-score) and imbalance-sensitive metrics (balanced accuracy, MCC, NPV, FDR, and G-mean), to comprehensively assess its predictive capabilities.

Proposed Resnet51-conv1D framework

The study employs the PLCO information, which includes 102 dietary variables and 154,892 participants. This extensive data allows us to examine the intricate connections among different food items and the hazards of CRC. Initially, the database contains massive noise; thus, during the preprocessing stage, erroneous values and redundant attributes are dropped to stimulate the training process and ensure accuracy. Next to that, dimensionality reduction was performed to streamline the database by extracting empty, null, and negative values, especially in food profiles frequently filled out as alphanumeric values, resulting in 83 dietary features.

After successfully eliminating the above data, a total of 135,886 samples were retained (133,827 Healthy and 2059 CRC) for the consecutive process. However, the predominance of healthy records makes the learning model biased towards majority class identification and creates challenges for CRC classification during implementation. To tackle this imbalance in class sample distribution, we utilise minority oversampling techniques.

Oversampling strategies

Application of SMOTE

Medical datasets have a skewed representation, where there is a minority class, which is generally underrepresented, resulting in an imbalanced dataset. These differences may have an impact on the performance of the learning model. This problem can be overcome using different resampling methods. Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) artificially creates new records by interpolating the existing cancer cases, therefore, increasing the representation of the minority classes [39]. In contrast, random down-sampling equalizes datasets by randomly eliminating samples from the dominant class [40].

From the 135,886 (133,827 Healthy and 2059 CRC) samples, we intend to generate a significantly larger number of synthetic CRC samples using SMOTE. We aim to create approximately 75,000 new synthetic CRC instances. This may appear excessive, but it is essential for achieving better balance in the dataset and enabling the learning model to more effectively identify and classify CRC cases.

SMOTE finds the k-nearest neighbors for each minority class instance and generates synthetic samples by interpolation among them [41]. Application of ADASYN

In contrast to conventional SMOTE, which evenly produces synthetic data, ADASYN selectively generates excess synthetic samples in regions where the minority class is underrepresented [43]. Model training and optimization

Dataset preparation and augmentation

From the original database, a subset of 135,886 samples was extracted, including 133,827 healthy individuals and 2,059 CRC cases. The healthy participants were evenly distributed across all blocks from the original population. To address the data imbalance problem, 75,000 synthetically generated CRC samples were added to the actual 2,059 cancer cases. The augmented CRC samples (in total 77,059) were equally distributed throughout all blocks.

The dataset was processed using two distinct approaches. In the first approach, the entire dataset, including the synthetically augmented samples, was directly forwarded to the classification model. In the second approach, batch segmentation was introduced to improve the learning stability and generalisation capability of the ResNet model under the highly imbalanced CRC distribution.

In this way, instead of training the model with the entire oversampled dataset at once, the samples were divided into balanced subsets forming 30, 40, and 50 block partitions, enabling a more consistent representation of both healthy and CRC samples of the ResNet model, thereby improving class-specific learning (Fig. 4).

Consequently, the 30-block batch comprised 4,461 healthy individuals and 2,569 CRC cases, resulting in a total of 7,030 samples. Likewise, the 40-block and 50-block batches contained 3,346 and 2,677 healthy individuals, respectively, along with 1,926 and 1,541 CRC cases, resulting in a total of 5,275 and 4,218 samples, respectively, as shown in Fig. 4; Table 2. In both methods, demographic details were removed from the dataset, and only dietary information was used.

Experimental setup of customized one-dimensional ResNet51

The proposed deep learning framework to analyze the PLCO data is a customized one-dimensional ResNet-51 model, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Unlike the traditional ResNet architecture, it begins with a modified input layer suitable for sequential data. An initial Conv1D with a LeakyReLU activation and batch normalisation is used to extract essential features from the input sequence.

Following this, the network is organised into four residual stages containing [3, 8, 10, 3] residual blocks, respectively. Each stage utilises different filter sizes to progressively learn higher-level feature representations while mitigating the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks through residual skip connections. Stage 1 and Stage 4 utilise 64 and 512 filters, respectively, while Stage 2 and Stage 3 employ 128 and 256 filters.

Within each block, Conv1D layers with kernel size 3 are used, and downsampling is performed using a 1 × 1 convolution with stride 2 when required. These residual connections allow efficient information propagation across layers and improve learning stability. This architecture helps the model capture complex sequential patterns while reducing overfitting.

Afterwards, the output from the residual blocks passes through an AveragePooling1D layer for dimensionality reduction. The resulting feature maps are then flattened into a vector representation and forwarded to a dense output layer with Softmax activation for binary classification. Stratified dataset split strategy

The preprocessed data was initially randomly shuffled and stratified to maintain the original class balance of the colorectal cancer (CRC) and the healthy samples. Stratified sampling is used to ensure the ratio of minority and majority classes is consistent in all subsets, which is especially significant with highly imbalanced biomedical data.

A 70:15:15 ratio was then used to divide the dataset into training, validation and testing datasets as demonstrated in Table 4. In particular, 70% of the samples were used in the training stage, 15% in the validation and 15% in the testing. The parameters of the customized ResNet51-Conv1D model were learned on the training subset and the model performance was monitored on the validation subset to avoid overfitting. Only after training, the independent test subset was applied to test the final generalization ability of the model on unseen data.

In addition to the whole dataset, segmented datasets generated through block segmentation (30, 40, and 50 batches) were also partitioned using the same stratified data-splitting strategy. Table 4 summarizes the distribution of samples in training, validation and test sets of the entire dataset as well as the segmented datasets.

Conclusion

The intention of this research is to develop a modernized deep learning model to detect CRC instances. In this study, we have shown that the oversampling technique is a solid method for achieving robust minority classification on unbalanced datasets. The current study recommends a batch-segmentation algorithm that would improve the generalizability of the suggested ResNet51 model. This technique minimized gradient instability throughout the deep networks and ensured that the model was not overfit to the majority samples and/or synthetic samples and thus produced more consistent performance in healthy and CRC prediction.

The two oversampling strategies tested during the research, SMOTE and ADASYN, offered the strongest and most consistent advancement on the PLCO data set because of its synthetic generation of samples. In some cases, ADASYN was found to be preferable to SMOTE; however, it was consistently surpassed by SMOTE in every block because of its unstable performance. SMOTE significantly improved overall performance measures such as the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) and Balanced Accuracy (BA), and proved that the proposed model’s convincingness in precisely predicting positive and negative classes. The findings of the research stress the significance of the combination of thorough evaluation measures and conventional accuracy measures to gain a deeper insight into the behaviour of the learning model in case of handling unbalanced data.

The progressive enhancement of CRC classification metrics such as specificity, NPV, and FDR, with increasing batch segmentation, proves the benefits of this training strategy. For instance, at 40 batch segments, the ResNet51 model reached a CRC recall of 96.20% and an F1-score of 90.80%, showcasing a significant improvement in its ability to detect the minority class. Our research confirms that combining SMOTE with batch-segmented training is a highly effective methodology for improving minority-class identification in an imbalanced medical dataset.

The limitations of the proposed deep learning model include relatively higher training time and sensitivity to class imbalance in extremely skewed datasets. Future work will focus on improving the computational efficiency of the customized ResNet51-Conv1D architecture and investigating different segmentation configurations to further optimize minority class detection. In addition, future research will investigate the integration of reinforcement learning–based feature selection methods for structured tabular clinical datasets to further enhance feature representation and improve minority class prediction performance.
