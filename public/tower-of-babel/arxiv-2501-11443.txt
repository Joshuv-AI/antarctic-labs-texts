Edoardo Giovanni Tolotti

Abstract

In the celebrated work of Friesecke, James and Müller ’06 the authors derive a hierarchy of models for plates by carefully analyzing the
\Gamma
-convergence of the rescaled nonlinear elastic energy. The key ingredient of their proofs is the rigidity estimate proved in an earlier work of theirs. Here we consider the case in which the elastic energy has a multi-well structure: this type of functional arises, for example, in the study of solid-solid phase transitions. Since the rigidity estimate fails in the case of compatible wells, we follow Alicandro, Dal Maso, Lazzaroni and Palombaro ’18 and add a regularization term to the energy that penalizes jumps from one well to another, leading to good compactness properties. In this setting we recover the full hierarchy of plate models with an explicit dependence on the wells. Finally, we study the convergence of energy minimizers with suitable external forces and full Neumann boundary conditions. To do so, we adapt the definition of optimal rotations introduced by Maor, Mora ’21.

Introduction

A large variety of elastic models for thin plates, i.e., slender bodies whose reference configuration has no intrinsic curvature, have been proposed in the mechanical literature (see for example
[
9
,
2
,
20
,
25
]
). The objective of this work is to rigorously derive some of these models as
\Gamma
-limits of three-dimensional nonlinear elasticity. We focus on elastic energy densities that are minimized on a finite number of copies of
\SO(3)
.
This setting is relevant, for example, in the modeling of solid-solid phase transitions (see
[
4
]
).

To be more precise, we consider a thin cylinder
\Omega_{h}=S\times(-\frac{h}{2},\frac{h}{2})
, where the mid-plane
S
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
h
is the small thickness of the plate. Its elastic energy takes the form

E_{h}(w_{h})=\int_{\Omega_{h}}W(\nabla w_{h})\,dx\,,

(2)

where
w_{h}:\Omega_{h}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a deformation of
\Omega_{h}
and

W
is the elastic energy density. We suppose that
W
is minimized on the set
K=\cup_{i=1}^{l}\SO(3)U_{i}
, where
U_{1},\dots,U_{l}
are symmetric and positive-definite matrices. In this work, we study the
\Gamma
-limit of
\frac{1}{h^{\alpha+1}}E_{h}
, as
h\to 0
, for
\alpha\geq 2
. Roughly speaking, this corresponds to the analysis of deformations whose energy per unit volume
V_{h}=h^{-1}E_{h}
scales like
h^{\alpha}
.

This problem has been extensively studied in the mathematical literature.
The regime
\alpha=0
, i.e.,
V_{h}\sim 1
, leads to the so-called membrane theory, rigorously derived for the first time by Le Dret and Raoult in
[
19
]
(see also
[
14
,
3
]
).
Here the hypotheses on
W
are compatible with a multi-well structure.
The scaling
0<\alpha<2
is largely unexplored and only partial results are available (see
[
10
]
). In the single-well setting (that is,
l=1
and
U_{1}=\Id
), the cases
V_{h}\sim h^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\geq 2
, have been studied by Friesecke, James, and Müller in
[
16
,
15
]
. When
\alpha=2
, they retrieve the so-called Kirchhoff-Love theory, first proposed by Kirchhoff in
[
18
]
. When
\alpha=4
, they obtain the Von Kármán model introduced in
[
25
]
, whereas in the case
2<\alpha<4
and
\alpha>4
they derive the so-called constrained and linearized Von Kármán model, respectively. The key ingredient of all the latter results is the celebrated rigidity estimate proved by the same authors in
[
15
]
. Indeed, a quantitative understanding of the closeness of deformations gradients to the set of rotations is needed in order to rigorously linearize the energy density near the identity.

Various extensions of the rigidity estimate are available in the multi-well case. For two strongly incompatible wells, the rigidity estimate has been proved by Chaudhuri and Müller in
[
6
]
(and later with a different proof by De Lellis and Székelyhidi in
[
12
]
).
In this setting the same authors also investigated the scaling
\alpha=1
in
[
7
]
. A further generalization is given by Chermisi and Conti in
[
8
]
, for a finite number of well-separated wells. However, it is well-known that the rigidity estimate fails without separation assumptions on the wells. Indeed, if two of the wells are rank-one connected, it is easy to construct continuous deformations with zero elastic energy, whose gradient oscillates between the two wells. In the absence of a rigidity estimate, energy bounds fail to provide the necessary compactness for deformations.

One possible way to overcome this issue is to singularly perturb the elastic energy by adding a higher order term of the form

h^{-1}\eta^{p}(h)\int_{\Omega_{h}}|\nabla^{2}w_{h}|^{p}\,dx\,,

(3)

where
\eta(h)\to 0
as
h\to 0
and
p>1
is a suitable exponent. This is a classical way of selecting preferred configurations in the Van der Waals–Cahn–Hilliard theory. Indeed, this additional term introduces a competition in the minimization problem: the free energy favors deformations with gradient in
K
, while the perturbation penalizes transitions between the wells.

Various analyses have been carried out in this setting. In the membrane scaling
\alpha=0
, a full description of the
\Gamma
-limit of the perturbed energy with
p=2
is obtained in
[
13
,
11
]
.
As in the non-perturbed case, the hypotheses on the energy density allow for
W
to have a multi-well structure. The expression of the
\Gamma
-limit depends on the behavior of
\eta(h)/h
. We also recall the work by Shu
[
23
]
, where an additional homogenization parameter is taken into account.

In our work we focus on the regimes
\alpha\geq 2
, and we do not assume any hypotheses of well-separation or connectedness of the wells.
We follow ideas from Alicandro, Dal Maso, Lazzaroni, and Palombaro in
[
1
]
, where the linearization of a multi-well elastic energy is studied.
We show that the perturbation coefficient
\eta(h)
can be chosen in such a way that, at the limit, deformation gradients are forced to fall into a single well and at the same time the (rescaled) perturbation term becomes negligible.
Under these scaling assumptions on
\eta(h)
, in
Section

3
we show that the
L^{2}
-norm of the distance of the deformation gradient from a specific well can be bounded by a suitable power of the perturbed elastic energy.
In particular, once such a well has been identified, the usual rigidity estimate can be applied to deduce compactness.

The hierarchy of plate models that we derive is similar to the one found in
[
16
,
15
]
for the single-well case. A key difference with respect to these works is the dependence of the limit models on the well around which we have to linearize. For
\alpha=2
, we retrieve the Kirchhoff model (see
Theorem

1.1
). Sequences of deformations with
V_{h}\sim h^{2}
converge to isometric embeddings of the mid-plane into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, for a flat metric depending on the well. The resulting model is similar in spirit to the one obtained in
[
5
]
, where the authors consider a prestrained model and the limit deformation realizes an isometric embedding of the mid-plane for a metric depending on the prestrain. For
2<\alpha<4
, the
\Gamma
-limit is given by the constrained Von Kármán model, where the Von Kármán equations now depend on the well and take the form

\nabla u^{T}+\nabla u+|U^{-1}_{i}e_{3}|^{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v=0\,.

(4)

Here
u
and
v
are the limiting in-plane and out-of-plane displacements with respect to a reference deformation depending on the well (i.e., of the form
x\mapsto U_{i}x
). Equation (
4
) means that
u
and
v
satisfy a matching isometry condition up to the second order, for a well-dependent flat metric (see
Section

1.2.2
). For
\alpha=4
and
\alpha>4
, we retrieve the Von Kármán and the linearized Von Kármán model, respectively. All the
\Gamma
-convergence results for
\alpha>2
are stated in
Theorem

1.3
. The Von Kármán model we obtain is similar to the one derived in
[
22
]
in the prestrained case.

Concerning the proofs, once compactness is established, the liminf inequality can be obtained arguing as in
[
16
,
15
]
. Since the penalty term requires additional regularity, the truncation argument used in
[
16
,
15
]
for the construction of the recovery sequences cannot be applied and is replaced by suitable approximation results. In particular, for
2\leq\alpha<4
we need to assume some higher regularity for the mid-plane
S
.

The last part of the paper is devoted to the convergence of (quasi-)minimizers for the pure traction problem. More precisely, we consider a sequence of dead loads
f_{h}
and add to the energy their work per unit volume given by

-h^{-1}\int_{\Omega_{h}}f_{h}\cdot w_{h}\,dx\,.

(5)

We assume
f_{h}
to be of order
h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+1}
. For
\alpha>2
we expect the limiting force term to depend only on the out-of-plane displacement (which is of order
h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-1}
) and not on the in-plane displacement (which is of order
h^{\alpha-2}
for
2\leq\alpha<4
,
h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
for
\alpha\geq 4
). However, since the plate is not clamped, we need to understand how the presence of the force term reduces the rotation invariance of the problem. To do so, we follow the approach by Maor and Mora
[
21
]
, where the notion of optimal rotations is introduced. These are rotations preferred by the forces, that can be different from the rotations selected by the rigidity estimate, around which linearization takes place.
We show how this concept can be adapted to the dimension reduction setting. In this framework, we deduce a minimization property for the limit of (quasi-)minimizing sequences. A precise statement of these results is given in
Theorems

1.5
and
1.7
.

The paper is organized as follows. In
Section

1
we introduce some notations, and we state the main results. After proving some preliminary lemmas, in
Section

3
we prove compactness of sequences with bounded perturbed energy. In
Section

4
we conclude the proof of the
\Gamma
-convergence results. Finally, in
Section

5
we study the minimizing sequences in presence of dead loads. The
Appendix

A
collects some results on isometries construction.

Notation and main results

1.1
Notation and functional setting

Consider a set of invertible matrices
U_{1},\ldots,U_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
with positive determinant and associate to each of them an energy well
K_{i}=\SO(3)U_{i}
. We assume the wells to be disjoint, i.e.,
U_{j}^{-1}U_{i}\notin\SO(3)
for any choice of
i\neq j
. We do not consider any restriction on the connectedness of the wells, in particular some
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
may be rank-one connected. By polar decomposition, we can suppose that
U_{1},\dots,U_{l}
are symmetric and positive definite. Let
K=\cup_{i=1}^{l}\SO(3)U_{i}
. We denote by
W\colon\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\to[0,+\infty]
the elastic energy density. We assume
W
to be Borel measurable and to satisfy the following hypotheses:

(W1)

W(F)=0\iff F\in K
,

(W2)

W
is
C^{2}
in a neighborhood of
K
,

(W3)

W
is frame indifferent, i.e.,
W(RF)=W(F)
for every
R\in\SO(3)
and for every
F\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
,

(W4)

W(F)\geq Cf_{q}(\dist(F,K))
for every
F\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
,

where
C>0
and
f_{q}(t)=t^{2}\land t^{q}
and
q\in[0,2]
. Note that far away from the set
K
the energy density may have linear or sublinear growth. We will denote by
Q_{j}
the quadratic form
D^{2}W(U_{j})
.

For
h>0
we consider a thin plate
\Omega_{h}=S\times(-\frac{h}{2},\frac{h}{2})=S\times hI
, where
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an open, connected and bounded set with Lipschitz boundary and
I=(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
. For some results we will need higher regularity of the boundary of
S
. More precisely, we will request that

\begin{gathered}\text{there is a closed subset }\Sigma\subset\partial S\text{ with }\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Sigma)=0\text{ such that}\\
\text{the outer unit normal }\vec{n}\text{ to }S\text{ exists and is continuous on }\partial S\backslash\Sigma\,.\end{gathered}

(6)

This property is called
condition
(\ast)
in
[
17
]
. We denote by
\Omega
the rescaled plate, that is
\Omega=\Omega_{1}
.

Given
\alpha\in[2,+\infty)
, set
\gamma=\frac{\alpha}{2}
. We choose
p>1
and
\eta\colon(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
such that for some constant
C>0
:

(P1)

\eta(h)\geq Ch^{\frac{\alpha}{3}}
for every
h>0
,

(P2)

\eta(h)h^{\gamma(1-\frac{2}{p})-1}\to 0
as
h\to 0
,

(P3)

p>\frac{6}{5}
, if
q<2
.

Conditions
(P1)
and
(P3)
ensure that the penalty term is strong enough to provide suitable compactness estimates (see
Proposition

3.1
) whereas condition
(P2)
guarantees that the penalty term is negligible at the limit.
Note that
(P1)
–
(P3)
are compatible, since for every
\alpha\geq 2
we have
1-\gamma(1-\frac{p}{2})<\frac{\alpha}{3}
for
p
large enough.

The symbol
\nabla_{h}y
denotes the rescaled gradient of
y
, while
\nabla_{h}^{2}y
is the rescaled Hessian of
y
. They are defined as follows:

\displaystyle(\nabla_{h}y)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\partial_{j}y_{i}&\text{if }j\neq 3\,,\\
\frac{1}{h}\partial_{3}y_{i}&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(7)

\displaystyle(\nabla_{h}^{2}y)_{ijk}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\partial_{jk}y_{i}&\text{if }j,k\neq 3\,,\\
\frac{1}{h}\partial_{3k}y_{i}&\text{if }j=3\,,k\neq 3\,,\\
\frac{1}{h}\partial_{j3}y_{i}&\text{if }j\neq 3\,,k=3\,,\\
\frac{1}{h^{2}}\partial_{33}y_{i}&\text{if }j=3\,,k=3\,.\\
\end{cases}

(8)

We set

\mathcal{H}_{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})=\{y\in W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})\colon\nabla^{2}_{h}y\in L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3\times 3})\}\,,

(9)

and for
\alpha\geq 2
we define the
\alpha
-rescaled energy
E^{\alpha}_{h}\colon W^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{3})\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

E^{\alpha}_{h}(y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla_{h}y)\,dx+\dfrac{\eta^{p}(h)}{h^{\alpha}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{h}^{2}y|^{p}\,dx\,\,&\text{if}\,y\in\mathcal{H}_{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,\\
+\infty\,\,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(10)

We explain now some useful matrix notation that we will use throughout the paper. Given a general matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
we will write
A^{\prime}
to denote the
2\times 2
submatrix obtained removing the third row and column. Similarly, we will often write
x^{\prime}
in place of
(x_{1},x_{2})
and
\nabla^{\prime}
instead of
\nabla_{x_{1},x_{2}}
. We will use the super(sub)scripts to denote submatrices of
A
in the following way: every missing subscript index is a removed row while every missing superscript index is a removed column. For example,
A^{1,2}
is the
3\times 2
submatrix given by the first two columns of
A
while
A_{1,2}
is the
2\times 3
submatrix given by the first two rows of
A
. Whenever we will sum or multiply matrices and vectors with different dimension we will imply that the smaller one is naturally embedded in the bigger space by adding zeros in the missing entries. For example, if
F\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
G\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
the expression
F+G
means
\iota(F)+G
where

\iota:\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\,,\quad F\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}F&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

(11)

We will denote by
I_{h}
the matrix

I_{h}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&h\end{pmatrix}\,.

(12)

1.2
Main results

The main objective of this paper is to extend the rigorous derivation of a hierarchy of plate models obtained by Friesecke, James, and Müller in
[
16
,
15
]
for a single well to the case of a multiwell elastic energy. In order to do so, we need to introduce the limiting models centered at a well
K_{j}
. We define

\bar{Q}_{j}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}\quad F\mapsto\min_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q_{j}(U_{j}^{-1}(\sym(F)+a\otimes e_{3}+e_{3}\otimes a))\,.

(13)

This is a quadratic form that generalizes the one defined in
[
15
, Equation (8)]
, taking into account that linearization may arise around a possibly nontrivial deformation gradient
U_{j}
. The minimum in
\bar{Q}_{j}
exists thanks to some coercivity property of
Q_{j}
that will be proved later (see
Lemma

2.2
).

1.2.1
The Kirchhoff regime
\bm{\alpha=2}

For
\alpha=2
, we will prove the
\Gamma
-convergence of
E_{h}^{\alpha}
to the Kirchhoff functional

E^{\KL}_{j}(y)=\dfrac{1}{24}\displaystyle\int_{S}\bar{Q}_{j}(\nabla y^{T}\nabla\nu)\,dx\,,

(14)

where
y\in W^{2,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{3})
satisfies the constraint
(\nabla y)^{T}\nabla y=(U_{j}^{2})^{\prime}
and
\nu
is the unique vector such that
(\nabla y,\nu)U_{j}^{-1}\in\SO(3)
a.e. in
S
(whose existence is guaranteed by
Lemma

3.6
). Note that
y
is an isometric immersion for the flat metric
(U^{2}_{j})^{\prime}
. The
\Gamma
-convergence result is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(
\Gamma
-convergence (
\alpha=2
))
.

Suppose that
S
satisfies (
6
).

1.

For any sequence
(y_{h})\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
that satisfies
E_{h}^{2}(y_{h})\leq C
for every
h>0
there exist
y\in W^{2,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
j\in\{1,\dots,l\}
such that

(a)

\nabla y^{T}\nabla y=(U_{j}^{2})^{\prime}
,

(b)

\nabla_{h}y_{h}\to(\nabla y,\nu)
in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3})
(up to a subsequence), where
\nu
is defined as in
Lemma

3.6
.

2.

For any sequence
(y_{h})\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
as in
1
it holds

\liminf\limits_{h\to 0}E_{h}^{2}(y_{h})\geq E^{\KL}_{j}(y)\,.

(15)

3.

For any
y\in W^{2,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
\nabla y^{T}\nabla y=(U_{j}^{2})^{\prime}
for some index
j\in\{1,\dots,l\}
, there exists a sequence
(y_{h})\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
1b
holds true and

\lim_{h\to 0}E_{h}^{2}(y_{h})=E^{\KL}_{j}(y)\,.

(16)

1.2.2
The Von Kármán regimes
\bm{\alpha>2}

When
\alpha>2
, the limiting models are expressed in terms of
u
and
v
, the limits of the rescaled in-plane and out-of-plane averaged displacements around a well
K_{j}
, called
u_{h}
and
v_{h}
, respectively. They are defined as follows:

\displaystyle u_{h}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}^{2}

\displaystyle x^{\prime}\mapsto\min\left\{h^{-\gamma},h^{2-2\gamma}\right\}\begin{pmatrix}w_{h}(x^{\prime})\cdot U_{j}e_{1}\\
w_{h}(x^{\prime})\cdot U_{j}e_{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(17)

\displaystyle v_{h}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle x^{\prime}\mapsto h^{1-\gamma}w_{h}(x^{\prime})\cdot U_{j}e_{3}\,,

(18)

where we recall that
\gamma=\frac{\alpha}{2}
and

w_{h}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad x^{\prime}\mapsto\int_{I}(y_{h}-U_{j}\Id_{h}x)\,dx_{3}\,.

(19)

Observe that
u_{h}
and
v_{h}
are the components of the averaged displacement
w_{h}
in the basis given by
\{U_{j}^{-1}e_{1},U_{j}^{-1}e_{2},U_{j}^{-1}e_{3}\}
. This may not look as a natural choice, since a basis of tangent vectors to the embedded midplane
U_{j}(S\times\{0\})
is given by
\{U_{j}e_{1},U_{j}e_{2}\}
and the normal direction is given by
U^{-1}_{j}e_{3}
.
However, since
\{U_{j}^{-1}e_{i}:i=1,2,3\}
is the dual basis of
\{U_{j}e_{i}:i=1,2,3\}
, this alternative simplifies both the statement and the computations, and gives a completely equivalent result (see
Remark

3.10
). When
2<\alpha<4
, we retrieve the
\Gamma
-convergence to the constrained Von Kármán functional, namely,

E^{\CVK}_{j}(v)=\dfrac{1}{24}\displaystyle\int_{S}\bar{Q}_{j}(\nabla^{2}v)\,dx\,,

(20)

whenever
v\in W^{2,2}(S)
is such that there exists
u\in W^{1,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{2})
that solves

\nabla u^{T}+\nabla u+|U^{-1}_{j}e_{3}|^{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v=0\,.

(21)

As in the single well case, this constraint means that
u
and
v
satisfy a matching isometry condition up to the second order; however, the metric now depends on the well, and it is not necessarily the Euclidean one. More precisely, if one defines for
\varepsilon>0

y_{\varepsilon}=U_{j}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\varepsilon U_{j}^{-1}e_{3}v+\varepsilon^{2}U_{j}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(22)

then (
21
) is equivalent to
\nabla y_{\varepsilon}^{T}\nabla y_{\varepsilon}=(U_{j}^{2})^{\prime}+O(\varepsilon^{3})
.

Remark 1.2
.

If
S
is simply connected, equation (
21
) implies in particular that
\det(\nabla^{2}v)=0
. For a proof of this result see
[
15
, Proposition 9]
.

The case
\alpha=4
corresponds to the Von Kármán model, that is,

\displaystyle E^{\VK}_{j}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{24}\displaystyle\int_{S}\bar{Q}_{j}(\nabla^{2}v)\,dx

(23)

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}\,\,+\dfrac{1}{8}\displaystyle\int_{S}\bar{Q}_{j}(\nabla u^{T}+\nabla u+|U^{-1}_{j}e_{3}|^{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v)\,dx\,,

(24)

for
v\in W^{2,2}(S)
and
u\in W^{1,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Lastly, if
\alpha>4
, we prove the
\Gamma
-convergence to the linearized Von Kármán model:

E^{\LVK}_{j}(u,v)=\dfrac{1}{24}\displaystyle\int_{S}\bar{Q}_{j}(\nabla^{2}v)\,dx+\dfrac{1}{8}\displaystyle\int_{S}\bar{Q}_{j}(\nabla u^{T}+\nabla u)\,dx\,,

(25)

for
v\in W^{2,2}(S)
and
u\in W^{1,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The following theorem summarizes these
\Gamma
-convergence results.

Theorem 1.3

(
\Gamma
-convergence (
\alpha>2
))
.

Suppose
\alpha>2
.

1.

For any sequence
(y_{h})\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
that satisfies
E_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h})\leq C
for every
h>0
there exist an index
j\in\{1,\dots,l\}
, two sequences
(\bar{R}_{h})\subset\SO(3)
,
(c_{h})\subset\mathbb{R}
, and two maps
v\in W^{2,2}(S)\,,u\in W^{1,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that, up to a subsequence, the following convergences hold:

(a)

u_{h}\rightharpoonup u
in
W^{1,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

(b)

v_{h}\to v
in
W^{1,2}(S)
,

where
u_{h}
and
v_{h}
are, respectively, the in-plane and out-of-plane displacements around the well
K_{j}
defined as in (
17
)–(
18
) for the roto-translated deformation

\tilde{y}_{h}=\bar{R}_{h}y_{h}+c_{h}\,.

(26)

Moreover, if
2<\alpha<4
, then

\nabla u^{T}+\nabla u+|U^{-1}_{j}e_{3}|^{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v=0\,.

(27)

2
Preliminary results

In this section we give a few preliminary results regarding the energy
W
. These properties are well known in the case of a single well elastic energy. Firstly, we explore the invariance properties of the second derivative
Q_{j}
that follow from frame indifference
(W3)
. Lastly, we prove that
(W4)
implies some coercivity for
Q_{j}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The following equality holds true for every
j=1,\dots,l
:

Q_{j}(A)=Q_{j}(\sym(AU_{j}^{-1})U_{j})=Q_{j}(U_{j}^{-1}\sym(U_{j}A))\quad\forall\,A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\,.

(63)

Proof.

Define
\tilde{W}(F)=W(FU_{j})
. By the properties
(W1)
–
(W3)
we deduce that
\tilde{W}
is frame indifferent, it is
C^{2}
in a neighborhood of
\SO(3)
, and it is minimized on
\SO(3)
. It is well known that any map satisfying these properties has symmetric second derivative at the identity, namely:

D^{2}\tilde{W}(\Id)[A]^{2}=D^{2}\tilde{W}(\Id)[\sym(A)]^{2}\,.

(64)

By some simple computation we get
D^{2}\tilde{W}(\Id)[A]^{2}=Q_{j}(AU_{j})
. Hence,

Q_{j}(A)=Q_{j}(AU_{j}^{-1}U_{j})=Q_{j}(\sym(AU_{j}^{-1})U_{j})\,.

(65)

To conclude we observe that by definition of symmetric part and the symmetry of
U_{j}
we have

\displaystyle Q_{j}(\sym(AU_{j}^{-1})U_{j})

\displaystyle=Q_{j}(\sym(U_{j}^{-1}U_{j}AU_{j}^{-1})U_{j})

(66)

\displaystyle=Q_{j}(U_{j}^{-1}\sym(U_{j}A))\,.

(67)

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists
\lambda>0
such that for every
j=1,\dots,l
and every
A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}

Q_{j}(U_{j}^{-1}\sym(A))\geq\lambda|\sym(A)|^{2}\,.

(68)

Proof.

By
(W4)
we have, for
\varepsilon\ll 1

W(U_{j}+\varepsilon U_{j}^{-1}A)\geq C\dist^{2}(U_{j}+\varepsilon U_{j}^{-1}A,K)=C\dist^{2}(U_{j}+\varepsilon U_{j}^{-1}A,K_{j})\,.

(69)

By the Taylor expansion of
W
at
U_{j}
and by
Lemma

2.1
we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}Q_{j}(U_{j}^{-1}\sym(A))+o(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle\geq C\dist^{2}(U_{j}+\varepsilon U_{j}^{-1}A,K_{j})

(70)

\displaystyle\geq C\dist^{2}(\Id+\varepsilon U_{j}^{-1}AU_{j}^{-1},\SO(3))

(71)

\displaystyle=C\varepsilon^{2}|\sym(U_{j}^{-1}AU_{j}^{-1})|^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{2})

(72)

\displaystyle=C\varepsilon^{2}|U_{j}^{-1}\sym(A)U_{j}^{-1}|^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{2})

(73)

\displaystyle\geq C\varepsilon^{2}|\sym(A)|^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

(74)

Dividing by
\varepsilon^{2}
and passing to the limit as

we conclude.
∎

It follows that the function
L_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\sym}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
that maps a matrix

into the vector

such that

(75)

is well-defined owing to the coercivity of

.
Moreover,

is linear.
Indeed,

can be represented by a fourth-order tensor

so that

(76)

In particular,
L_{j}(D)
is the unique vector that solves the linear optimality conditions

\mathbb{C}(D+x\otimes e_{3}+e_{3}\otimes x):(e_{i}\otimes e_{3}+e_{3}\otimes e_{i})=0,\qquad i=1,2,3.

(77)

3

In order to study the

In the case

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
U
be a symmetric and positive definite matrix. Let
y\in W^{2,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{3})
be such that
\nabla y^{T}\nabla y=(U^{2})^{\prime}
. Then there exists a unique function
\nu\in W^{1,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that

(\nabla y,\nu)U^{-1}\in\SO(3)\quad\text{a.e. in $S$}\,.

(96)

In particular,
\nu
is given by

\nu=\frac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}\left[\det(U^{-1})(\partial_{1}y\wedge\partial_{2}y)-\sum_{k=1}^{2}(U^{-1}e_{k}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})\partial_{k}y\right]\,.

(97)

Proof.

For the existence, it is enough to prove that
(\nabla y,\nu)^{T}(\nabla y,\nu)=U^{2}
and
\det(\nabla y,\nu)>0
. By the hypothesis on
y
we need to prove that

1.

\partial_{1}y\cdot\nu=(U^{2})_{13}
,

2.

\partial_{2}y\cdot\nu=(U^{2})_{23}
,

3.

\nu\cdot\nu=(U^{2})_{33}
.

For
j=1,2
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{j}y\cdot\nu=-\dfrac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}(U^{-1}e_{k}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})(U^{2})_{jk}+(U^{2})_{j3}=(U^{2})_{j3}\,.

(98)

To complete the proof we observe that

|Ue_{1}\wedge Ue_{2}|^{2}=|Ue_{1}|^{2}|Ue_{2}|^{2}-(Ue_{1}\cdot Ue_{2})^{2}=|\partial_{1}y\land\partial_{2}y|^{2}

(99)

and, since
Ue_{1}\land Ue_{2}=\cof(U)e_{3}

|Ue_{1}\wedge Ue_{2}|^{2}=|\deter(U)U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}=\deter^{2}(U)|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}\,.

(100)

We are now ready to conclude:

\displaystyle\nu\cdot\nu

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}+\dfrac{(U^{-1}e_{1}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{4}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}(U^{-1}e_{k}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})(U^{2})_{1k}

(101)

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}+\dfrac{(U^{-1}e_{2}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{4}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}(U^{-1}e_{k}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})(U^{2})_{2k}

(102)

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}-\dfrac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{3}(U^{-1}e_{k}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})(U^{2})_{3k}+(U^{2})_{33}

(103)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}-\dfrac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}+(U^{2})_{33}=(U^{2})_{33}\,.

(104)

To show that

, it is sufficient to note that

(\nabla y,\nu)=(\partial_{1}y,\partial_{2}y,\partial_{1}y\land\partial_{2}y)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-\frac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}(U^{-1}e_{1}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})\\
0&1&-\frac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}(U^{-1}e_{2}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})\\
0&0&\frac{\det(U^{-1})}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(105)

and that the determinant of both matrices in the right-hand side is positive.

To prove uniqueness we observe that, for any choice of two different rotations

. Indeed, given a vector

(106)

Since

and

if and only if

is parallel to the rotation axis, that is,

belongs to a

-dimensional subspace. In particular,
\rank(R_{1}-R_{2})=2
.
Suppose now that there is another vector
\tilde{\nu}
such that

(\nabla y,\tilde{\nu})U^{-1}\in\SO(3)\,\,\text{a.e..}

(107)

Then,

(0,\nu-\tilde{\nu})U^{-1}=(\nu-\tilde{\nu})\otimes U^{-1}e_{3}

(108)

coincides a.e. with the difference of two rotations and has rank 1 whenever

almost everywhere.

21
, Lemma 4.4]
we have
R_{h}e^{W_{h}}\in\mathcal{R}_{h}^{j}
. Thus, passing to the limit, we get by
Lemma

5.2
that
Re^{W}\in\mathcal{R}^{j}
, that is, by definition
Re^{W}\in\tilde{\mathcal{R}}^{j}
.
∎

The above results guarantee the well-posedness of the projection.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
(R_{h})\subset\SO(3)
be a sequence such that
R_{h}\to R
and
R\in\mathcal{R}^{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,l\}
. Then
P_{h}^{j}(R_{h})
is well-defined for
h\ll 1
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove that
\dist_{\SO(3)}(R_{h},\mathcal{R}_{h}^{j})\to 0
. By
Proposition

5.5
, there exists a sequence of rotations
\tilde{R}_{h}
such that
\tilde{R}_{h}\in\mathcal{R}_{h}^{j}
for every
h
and
\tilde{R}_{h}\to R
. Since

\dist_{\SO(3)}(Q^{\prime},Q)=|Q^{\prime}-Q|+O(|Q^{\prime}-Q|^{2})

(262)

for every
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\SO(3)
, we have

\dist_{\SO(3)}(R_{h},\mathcal{R}_{h}^{j})\leq\dist_{SO(3)}(R_{h},\tilde{R}_{h})=|R_{h}-\tilde{R}_{h}|+O(|R_{h}-\tilde{R}_{h}|^{2})\to 0\,.

(263)

∎

We are now ready to conclude the proof of
Theorem

1.7
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.7
.

By
Lemma

5.1
, we have
E_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h})\leq C
.

We prove that
\bar{R}^{T}U_{j}\in\mathcal{M}
. This will also show that
j\in\Lambda
. Indeed, let
RU_{k}\in K
and define

y_{h}^{\prime}(x^{\prime},x_{3})=RU_{k}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
hx_{3}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(264)

We get

\displaystyle J_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h})-\inf_{y}J_{h}^{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle\geq J_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h})-J_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h}^{\prime})

(265)

\displaystyle\geq-\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot y_{h}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot RU_{k}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot\bar{R}_{h}^{T}U_{j}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\max\{h^{\gamma},h^{2\gamma-2}\}u_{h}\\
h^{\gamma-1}v_{h}\end{pmatrix}\,dx

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot(RU_{k}-\bar{R}_{h}^{T}U_{j})\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,dx\,.

Multiplying by
h^{\gamma-1}
and passing to the limit we deduce that

\int_{\Omega}f\cdot(RU_{k}-\bar{R}^{T}U_{j})\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,dx\leq 0\,,

(266)

where we have used convergence (
29
).
Since
RU_{k}\in K
is arbitrary, we have
\bar{R}^{T}U_{j}\in\mathcal{M}
.

By
Proposition

5.6
, the projection
P_{h}^{j}(R_{h}^{T})
is well-defined for
h\ll 1
. Let
W_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\skw}
with
|W_{h}|=1
be such that

P^{j}_{h}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})W_{h}\in N\mathcal{R}_{h}^{j}{}_{P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})}

(267)

and
\bar{R}_{h}^{T}=P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})e^{d_{h}W_{h}}
, where
d_{h}=\dist_{\SO(3)}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T},P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T}))
. Then, by (
265
) with
RU_{k}=P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})U_{j}
and the fact that
F_{h}(P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})W_{h}U_{j})=0
we have, expanding the exponential map,

\displaystyle J_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h})-\inf_{y}J_{h}^{\alpha}(y)

\displaystyle\geq J_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h})-J_{h}^{\alpha}\left(P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})U_{j}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
hx_{3}\end{pmatrix}\right)

(268)

\displaystyle\geq-C-\dfrac{d_{h}^{2}}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})W_{h}^{2}U_{j}\,dx+O\left(\dfrac{d_{h}^{3}}{h^{\gamma-1}}\right)\,.

Clearly
P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})\to\bar{R}^{T}
. Moreover, up to subsequences,
W_{h}\to W
and by
Lemma

5.3
,
\bar{R}^{T}W\in N\mathcal{R}^{j}_{\bar{R}^{T}}
, thus
F(\bar{R}^{T}W^{2}U_{j})<0
. We deduce by (
268
) that
d_{h}^{2}=O(h^{\gamma-1})
. In particular, there exists
\beta\geq 0
such that
h^{1-\gamma}d_{h}^{2}\to\beta^{2}
and so

h^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T}-P^{j}_{h}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T}))=\dfrac{d_{h}}{h^{\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-1)}}P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})W_{h}+O\left(\dfrac{d_{h}^{2}}{h^{\frac{1}{2}(\gamma-1)}}\right)\to\beta\bar{R}^{T}W\,.

(269)

We are left to prove the minimality property. We show it for
2<\alpha<4
. The other cases can be proved analogously. Firstly, note that

\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot y_{h}\,dx-\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}F_{h}(P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})U_{j})\to\int_{S}f\cdot\bar{R}^{T}U_{j}^{-1}e_{3}v\,dx-F(\beta^{2}\bar{R}^{T}W^{2}U_{j})\,.

(270)

Indeed, we have the equality

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot y_{h}\,dx-F(P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})U_{j})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot\bar{R}_{h}^{T}U_{j}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}h^{2\gamma-2}u_{h}\\
h^{\gamma-1}v_{h}\end{pmatrix}\,dx

(271)

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot(P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})-\bar{R}_{h}^{T})U_{j}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,dx\,.

The first term behaves as follows:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot\bar{R}_{h}^{T}U_{j}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}h^{2\gamma-2}u_{h}\\
h^{\gamma-1}v_{h}\end{pmatrix}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{h^{\gamma+1}}f_{h}\cdot\bar{R}_{h}^{T}U_{j}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}h^{\gamma-1}u_{h}\\
v_{h}\end{pmatrix}\,dx

(272)

\displaystyle\to\int_{S}f\cdot\bar{R}^{T}U_{j}^{-1}e_{3}v\,dx\,.

(273)

On the other hand, since
F_{h}(P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})W_{h}U_{j})=0
, we have

\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}F_{h}(P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})U_{j})=\dfrac{d_{h}^{2}}{h^{\gamma-1}}\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{h^{\gamma+1}}f_{h}\cdot P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})W_{h}^{2}U_{j}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,dx+O\left(\dfrac{d_{h}^{3}}{h^{\gamma-1}}\right)\,,

(274)

that converges to
F(\beta^{2}\bar{R}^{T}W^{2}U_{j})
. Thus, by
Theorem

1.3
–
2
we have

\liminf_{h\to 0}(J_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h})+F(P_{h}^{j}(\bar{R}_{h}^{T})U_{j}))\geq J_{j}^{CVK}(v,\bar{R}^{T},\beta W)\,.

(275)

Take an admissible quadruplet
(i,v^{\prime},R^{\prime},W^{\prime})
and let
y_{h}^{\prime}
be the recovery sequence for
v^{\prime}
provided by
Theorem

1.3
–
3
. By
Proposition

5.5
, we can construct a sequence of rotations

converging to
R^{\prime}
such that
R_{h}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{h}^{i}
. Note that, by
(W3)
,

E_{h}^{\alpha}(y_{h}^{\prime})=E_{h}^{\alpha}(R^{\prime}_{h}y_{h}^{\prime})\,,

(276)

so that
E_{h}^{\alpha}(R^{\prime}_{h}y_{h})\to E_{i}^{CVK}(v^{\prime})
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f_{h}\cdot R^{\prime}_{h}y_{h}^{\prime}-\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}F_{h}(R^{\prime}_{h}U_{i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{h^{\alpha}}\int_{S}f_{h}\cdot R^{\prime}_{h}U_{i}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}h^{2\gamma-2}u_{h}^{\prime}\\
h^{\gamma-1}v_{h}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\,dx

(277)

\displaystyle\to\int_{S}f\cdot R^{\prime}U_{i}^{-1}e_{3}v^{\prime}\,dx\,,

(278)

where
u_{h}^{\prime},v_{h}^{\prime}
are defined as in (
17
)–(

y_{h}^{\prime}
and

, respectively. By hypothesis
(F1)

Lemma A.1
.

Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
be a symmetric and positive definite matrix. For
\varepsilon>0
let
y_{\varepsilon}\in W^{2,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a map of the form

y_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime})=U\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\varepsilon vU^{-1}e_{3}+\varepsilon^{2}U\begin{pmatrix}u_{\varepsilon}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,,

(285)

such that
\nabla y^{T}\nabla y=(U^{2})^{\prime}
, where
v\in W^{2,\infty}(S)
and
u_{\varepsilon}
is a bounded sequence in
W^{2,\infty}(S;\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Then, if
\nu_{\varepsilon}
is defined as in
Lemma

3.6
, we have

\nabla y_{\varepsilon}^{T}\nabla\nu_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon\nabla^{2}v+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

(286)

Proof.

First, we compute
\partial_{1}y_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{2}y_{\varepsilon}
. We have

\partial_{1}y_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{2}y_{\varepsilon}=Ue_{1}\wedge Ue_{2}+\varepsilon\left[Ue_{1}\wedge U^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\partial_{2}v\end{pmatrix}+U^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\partial_{1}v\end{pmatrix}\wedge Ue_{2}\right]+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

(287)

Given the identity
Ua\wedge Ub=\det(U)U^{-1}(a\wedge b)
, we easily deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{1}y_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{2}y_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=Ue_{1}\wedge Ue_{2}

(288)

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}\,\,+\varepsilon\det(U)U^{-1}\left[\partial_{2}ve_{1}\wedge(U^{-1})^{2}e_{3}+\partial_{1}v(U^{-1})^{2}e_{3}\wedge e_{2}\right]+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

(289)

Computing the cross products, we get

\partial_{1}y_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{2}y_{\varepsilon}=Ue_{1}\wedge Ue_{2}-\varepsilon\det(U)U^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}\partial_{1}v\\
|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}\partial_{2}v\\
-\sum_{k=1}^{2}(U^{-1}e_{k}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})\partial_{k}v\end{pmatrix}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

(290)

Let us set
q(x^{\prime}):=-\frac{1}{|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{2}(U^{-1}e_{k}\cdot U^{-1}e_{3})\partial_{k}v
. We have

\nabla(\partial_{1}y_{\varepsilon}\wedge\partial_{2}y_{\varepsilon})=-\varepsilon|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}\det(U)U^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla^{2}v\\
\nabla q\end{pmatrix}+O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

(291)

Now observe that for every triplet of indices
i,j,k=1,2
we have

\partial_{i}y_{\varepsilon}\cdot\partial^{2}_{jk}y_{\varepsilon}=O(\varepsilon^{2})\,.

(292)

Moreover,

\partial_{i}y_{\varepsilon}^{T}U^{-1}=e_{i}^{T}+O(\varepsilon)\,.

(293)

Combining the previous equations with the definition of
\nu_{\varepsilon}
, the thesis follows.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
be a symmetric and positive definite matrix and define
A=\sqrt{(U^{2})^{\prime}}
. Suppose that
S
is simply connected. Let
v\in W^{2,2}(S)
be such that
|U^{-1}e_{3}|\|\nabla vA^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}<1
. Then, there exists
\phi\in W^{2,2}(S;\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that the map

y(x^{\prime})=vU^{-1}e_{3}+U\begin{pmatrix}\phi\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(294)

satisfies
\nabla y^{T}\nabla y=(U^{2})^{\prime}
if and only if
\det(\nabla^{2}v)=0
. Moreover, if
v
satisfies the condition
|U^{-1}e_{3}|\|\nabla vA^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\frac{1}{2}
, then
\phi
can be chosen such that
u:=U(\phi-x^{\prime},0)^{T}
satisfies the following estimates:

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{2}v\|_{L^{2}}\,,

(295)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{2,2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla^{2}v\|_{L^{2}}+C\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\,.

(296)

Finally, if
v\in W^{2,\infty}(S)
, then
u\in W^{2,\infty}(S;\mathbb{R}^{2})
and the following inequality holds:

\|u\|_{W^{2,\infty}}\leq C(\|\nabla^{2}v\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2})\,.

(297)

Proof.

Observe that
y
of the form (
294
) satisfies
\nabla y^{T}\nabla y=A^{2}
if and only if

\nabla\phi^{T}A^{2}\nabla\phi+|U^{-1}e_{3}|^{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v=A^{2}\,.

(298)

Defining
\phi^{\prime}:=A\phi
and
v^{\prime}=|U^{-1}e_{3}|v
the previous equation is equivalent to solve

(\nabla\phi^{\prime}A^{-1})^{T}(\nabla\phi^{\prime}A^{-1})+(\nabla v^{\prime}A^{-1})^{T}\otimes(\nabla v^{\prime}A^{-1})^{T}=\Id_{2}

(299)

for the unknown
\phi^{\prime}
. Define now
\tilde{v}^{\prime}\in W^{2,2}(AS;\mathbb{R}^{3})
given by
\tilde{v}^{\prime}(Ax^{\prime})=v^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
. Solving the above equation for
\phi^{\prime}
is equivalent to solve

(\nabla\tilde{\phi}^{\prime})^{T}\nabla\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}+\nabla\tilde{v}^{\prime}\otimes\nabla\tilde{v}^{\prime}=\Id_{2}\,,

(300)

for
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}\in W^{1,2}(AS;\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Thus, we can conclude by applying
[
15
, Theorem 7]
. Note that the transformations
v\leadsto v^{\prime}\leadsto\tilde{v}^{\prime}
preserve the property of having null determinant of the Hessian, namely

\det(\nabla^{2}v)=0\iff\det(\nabla^{2}v^{\prime})=0\iff\det(\nabla^{2}\tilde{v}^{\prime})=0\,.

(301)

Moreover, they preserve the boundedness of the gradient in the
L^{\infty}
norm. Finally, define
\tilde{u}^{\prime}=\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}-x^{\prime}
. It is easy to show that

\displaystyle\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}=C\|\nabla\tilde{u}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\,,

(302)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{1,2}}=C\|\tilde{u}^{\prime}\|_{W^{1,2}}\,,

(303)

\displaystyle\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}}=C\|\nabla\tilde{v}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,,

(304)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}v\|_{L^{2}}=C\|\tilde{v}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\,.

(305)

Estimates (
295
)-(
296
) then follow from
[
15
, Equations (180)–(181)]
. We are left to prove (
297
). Note that it is sufficient to do the proof for
\tilde{u}^{\prime}
, so we can suppose
U=\Id
. Then, the result follows from
[
24
, Lemma 4.3]
.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
be a symmetric and positive definite matrix and let
A=\sqrt{(U^{2})^{\prime}}
. Suppose that
S
is simply connected and satisfies (
6
). Let
v\in W^{2,2}(S)
be such that

|U^{-1}e_{3}|\|\nabla vA^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}}<1

(306)

and
\det(\nabla^{2}v)=0
. Then there is a sequence
(v_{n})\subset C^{\infty}(\bar{S})
such that
\det(\nabla^{2}v_{n})=0

v_{n}\to v
in
W^{2,2}(S)
.

Proof.

Applying
Lemma

A.2
, we construct
\phi
such that

y(x^{\prime})=vU^{-1}e_{3}+U\begin{pmatrix}\phi\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(307)

satisfies
\nabla y^{T}\nabla y=(U^{2})^{\prime}
. Using the same notation as in the proof of
Lemma

A.2
, we note that
\phi
is defined in such a way that
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}:=A\phi(Ax)
gives an isometric embedding of

for the Euclidean metric, that is,
\nabla\tilde{y}^{T}\nabla\tilde{y}=\Id_{2}
, where
\tilde{y}=(\tilde{\phi}^{\prime},\tilde{v}^{\prime})^{T}
. Since (
6
) holds true also for the set

, by
[
17

(\tilde{y}_{n})\subset W^{2,2}_{\text{iso}}(AS;\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{AS};\mathbb{R}^{3})

\tilde{y}_{n}\to\tilde{y}

W^{2,2}_{\text{iso}}(AS;\mathbb{R}^{3})=\{y\in W^{2,2}(AS;\mathbb{R}^{3})\colon\nabla y^{T}\nabla y=\Id_{2}\}\,.

(308)

\tilde{v}_{n}=\tilde{y}_{n}\cdot e_{3}
