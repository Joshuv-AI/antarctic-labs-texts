The Cloud and Flock Polynomials of q q -Matroids
Heide Gluesing-Luerssen, and Benjamin Jany

Abstract
We show that the Whitney function of a q q -matroid can be determined from the cloud and flock polynomials associated to the cyclic flats. These polynomials capture information about the corank (resp., nullity) of certain spaces whose cyclic core (resp., closure) is the given cyclic flat. Going one step further, we prove that the Whitney function, and in fact the cloud-flock lattice, are determined by the configuration of the q q -matroid, which is the abstract lattice of cyclic flats together with the corank-nullity data. Furthermore, we show that the configuration and cloud-flock lattice behave well under duality and direct sums, whereas the Whitney function does not contain enough information to behave well under taking direct sums. As an aside we show that every configuration of a matroid arises as a configuration of a q q -matroid, whereas the converse is not true.



Keywords:

q
-matroid, Whitney function, cyclic flats, cloud polynomial, flock polynomial.

1
Introduction

In this paper we investigate certain (polynomial) invariants of
q
-matroids.
Since their introduction in 2018 in
[
20
]
,
q
-matroids have attracted a lot of attention due to their close relationship to rank-metric codes
[
20
,
17
,
22
,
11
,
19
,
12
]
.
The theory of
q
-matroids, despite its young age, has made substantial progress in the areas of cryptomorphisms
[
6
]
,
subspace designs
[
4
]
, and structural properties
[
18
,
1
,
8
,
15
]
.
In the notoriously hard area of representability some first results have been obtained in
[
13
,
2
]
, but unfortunately even questions with simple answers in matroid theory have proven to be extremely challenging.

It is well-known that for matroids the Whitney function, which is the bivariate generating function of the corank-nullity pairs over all subsets, is related to the Tutte polynomial via a simple variable shift.
The two polynomials were introduced independently for graphs by Tutte and count different invariants.
Crapo
[
9
]
generalized them to matroids and established their simple shift relation.
The Tutte polynomial (and thus the Whitney function) satisfies certain fundamental recursions with respect to matroid minors.
In fact, together with a normalization these recursions define the Tutte polynomial.

For
q
-matroids, the Whitney function can be defined exactly as for matroids.
However, it turns out that it does not satisfy the
q
-analogues of the above-mentioned recursions.
To be more precise, it is not a
q
-Tutte-Grothendieck invariant in the sense of
[
7
]
.
For this reason, in
[
7
]
the authors search for such a
q
-Tutte Grothendieck invariant for
q
-matroids.
They succeed in doing so under the assumption that the subspace lattice of the
q
-matroid admits a certain type of partition (which unfortunately is not always the case).
They further show that there exists a bijection in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
relating the Whitney function and the Tutte polynomial.
This bijection can be expressed as a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear substitution of the monomials
x^{i}y^{j}
, and thus differs substantially
from the simple shift relation in the matroid case.

In this paper we focus on the Whitney function and introduce further invariants for
q
-matroids.
A prominent role will be played by the collection of cyclic flats.
It is well known
[
1
,
15
]
that the collection
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
of cyclic flats of a given
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
together with the rank values of the cyclic flats fully determine
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Following the study of matroids in
[
21
,
10
]
, we introduce the cloud and flock polynomials associated with the cyclic flats of a
q
-matroid.
The cloud polynomial associated with
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
is the generating function for the corank of all flats whose cyclic core is
Z
, while the flock polynomial is the generating function for the nullity of all the spaces whose closure is
Z
.
Making use of the powerful properties of
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
, we show
in
4.10
that the collection of all cloud and flock polynomials determines the Whitney function.
This provides us with a
q
-analogue of the corresponding result for matroids
[
21
,
10
]
, but it takes a more complicated form than for matroids.
This has the consequence that, in general, the Whitney function of a direct sum
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
is not determined by the Whitney functions of
the summands
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
; see
9.2
.
This stands in striking contrast to the simple product formula for direct sums of matroids.
Moreover – as for matroids – the Whitney function does not determine the cloud and flock polynomials, and actually
it does not even determine the number of cyclic flats.

In addition, we introduce the configuration and cloud-flock lattice of a
q
-matroid (see
[
10
]
for configurations of matroids).
The former is defined by replacing in the lattice of cyclic flats each actual cyclic flat
Z
by its corank-nullity pair, while in the latter we replace
Z
by its pair of
cloud and flock polynomial.
Obviously, the configuration contains much less information than the lattice of cyclic flats and, unsurprisingly,
there exist non-equivalent
q
-matroids with the same configuration.
Since the degrees of the cloud and flock polynomial associated with
Z
are the corank and nullity of
Z
, the cloud-flock lattice trivially
determines the configuration.
It turns out that the converse is true as well: the configuration fully determines the cloud-flock lattice and thus the Whitney function.
This is worked out in two steps.
First, in
5.8
we establish the result for
full

q
-matroids (the
q
-analogue of loopless and coloopless matroids).
Thereafter, we make use of the fact that a non-full
q
-matroid can be decomposed into the direct sum of a full, a trivial, and a free
q
-matroid
[
15
]
.
With some lengthy, yet straightforward computations about the relevant invariants of direct sums involving a trivial or free summand, we are then able to prove the general case in
7.7
.

Just as for matroids, the converse of the just mentioned result is not true, that is, the Whitney function does not determine the configuration – simply because it does not even determine the number of cyclic flats.
However, as we show in
9.5
, some partial information about the cyclic flats can be retrieved from the Whitney function.

In Corollaries
5.10
and
7.8
we show that the configuration and the cloud-flock lattice of a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
fully determines the respective information for
the dual
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
. However, the duality relation is not as simply as the well-known identity for the Whitney function.
We discuss this at the end of
Section

5
.
In
7.9
we establish that the configurations and the cloud-flock lattices of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
determine the same
information for
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

In
Section

6
we take a brief excursion and show that every finite lattice arises as the lattice of cyclic flats of a
q
-matroid.
The proof will make use of the same result for matroids. This will allow us to show that every configuration of a matroid arises as the configuration of a
q
-matroid, while the converse is not true.

Finally, in
Section

8
we introduce condensed configurations.
They consist of data pertaining to specific partitions of the cyclic flats.
These partitions and their data capture information about the corank-nullity pairs of the cyclic flats and their containment relations.
This information is not sufficient to reconstruct the configuration.
But it is sufficient to fully determine the Whitney function; see
8.9
.
The converse is not true.
This section closely follows the matroid case developed in
[
10
]
.

{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}

For any finite-dimensional

-vector space

For a matrix

2
Basic Notions of
q
-Matroids

In this section we collect some basic notions and facts for
q
-matroids.

Definition 2.1
.

A
q
-matroid with ground space
E
is a pair
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
, where
E
is a finite-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector space
and
\rho:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 0}
is a map satisfying

(R1)

Dimension-Boundedness:
0\leq\rho(V)\leq\dim V
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
;

(R2)

Monotonicity:
V\leq W\Longrightarrow\rho(V)\leq\rho(W)
for all
V,W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
;

(R3)

Submodularity:
\rho(V+W)+\rho(V\cap W)\leq\rho(V)+\rho(W)
for all
V,W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.

We call
\rho(V)
the
rank of

V
and
\rho({\mathcal{M}}):=\rho(E)
the rank of the
q
-matroid.

Definition 2.2
.

Two
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i})
are
equivalent
, denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\approx{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
, if there exists an
{\mathbb{F}}
-isomorphism
\alpha:E_{1}\longrightarrow E_{2}
such that
\rho_{2}(\alpha(V))=\rho_{1}(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1})
.

The following notions are crucial in any study of
q
-matroids.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid.
A subspace
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
is
independent
if
\rho(V)=\dim V
and
dependent
otherwise.
A dependent space all of whose proper subspaces are independent is called a
circuit
.
The subspace
V
is a
flat
if it is inclusion-maximal in the set
\{W\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid\rho(W)=\rho(V)\}
, in other words
\rho(V)<\rho(V+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$})
for all
x\in E\setminus V
.
Moreover,
V
is
cyclic
(or
open
) if
\rho(W)=\rho(V)
for all
W\in\mbox{\rm Hyp}(V)
.
We set
{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})=\{F\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid F\text{ flat in }{\mathcal{M}}\}
and

{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})=\{O\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid O\text{ cyclic in }{\mathcal{M}}\}
and

{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})={\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\cap{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})=\{Z\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid Z\text{ cyclic flat in }{\mathcal{M}}\}.

We call
{\mathcal{M}}

full
if
0
and
E
are cyclic flats.

Note that a full
q
-matroid is the
q
-analogue of a loopless and coloopless matroid;
see also
[
15
, Rem. 7.8]
for a more detailed discussion.

It is well known that the collection of independent spaces uniquely determines the
q
-matroid.
The same is true for the flats, dependent spaces, open spaces, and circuits.
We refer to
[
20
, Thm. 8]
and
[
6
]
for the according cryptomorphisms.
Moreover, the cyclic flats together with their rank values also uniquely determine the
q
-matroid.
Indeed,

\rho(V)=\min_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})}\Big(\rho(Z)+\dim((V+Z)/Z)\Big)\ \text{ for all }V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E);

(2.1)

see
[
15
, Cor. 4.6]
.
The resulting cryptomorphism can be found in
[
1
, Cor. 4.12]
.

A large class of
q
-matroids are the representable ones.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
20
, Sec. 5]
)
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
be a field extension of
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and let
G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}
.
Define the map
\rho:{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{F}}^{n})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
via

\rho(\mbox{\rm rowsp}(Y))=\mbox{\rm rk}\,(GY\mbox{$\!{}^{\sf T}$})\ \text{ for all matrices $Y\in{\mathbb{F}}^{t\times n}$ and all $t\in\{0,\ldots,n\}$}

(where the rank is over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
).
Then
\rho
is well-defined and
{\mathcal{M}}_{G}:=({\mathbb{F}}^{n},\rho)
is a
q
-matroid. It is called the
q
-matroid
represented by
G
.
A
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
of rank
k
with ground space
E
is called
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
-representable
if
{\mathcal{M}}\approx{\mathcal{M}}_{G}
for some matrix
G\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}^{k\times n}
.

The row space of
G
is known as the rank-metric code generated by
G
.
Many invariants of the code, but not all, can be determined by the associated
q
-matroid.
We refer to
[
20
,
17
,
4
,
12
]
and Section 6 in the survey
[
16
]
for further details.

Example 2.5
.

Let
k\in[n]_{0}
.
The
uniform
q
-matroid of rank
k
with
n
-dimensional ground space
E
is defined to be
(E,\rho)
, where
\rho(V)=\min\{k,\dim V\}
for all

V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
It is denoted by
{\mathcal{U}}_{k,n}(E)
or simply
{\mathcal{U}}_{k,n}
(in which case the underlying field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
needs to be clear from the context).
Note that
{\mathcal{U}}_{0,n}
is represented by the
1\times n
-zero matrix and

{\mathcal{U}}_{n,n}
by the
n\times n
-identity matrix.
For
0<k<n
, the uniform
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{U}}_{k,n}
is representable over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
if and only if
m\geq n
; see
[
16
, Rem. 3.10]
.
We call
{\mathcal{U}}_{0,n}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{n,n}
the
trivial
and the
free

q
-matroid, respectively.

Just like for matroids, not every
q
-matroid is representable. Examples can be found in
[
12
, Sec. 4]
and
[
8
, Sec. 3.3]
. See also
2.12
(b) below.

Restriction and contraction of
q
-matroids are defined in the usual way.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid and
X\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
.
The
restriction of
{\mathcal{M}}
to
X
and the
contraction of
X
from
{\mathcal{M}}

are defined as the
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}|X=(X,\hat{\rho})
with
\hat{\rho}(V)=\rho(V)
for all
V\leq X
and

{\mathcal{M}}/X=(E/X,\tilde{\rho})
with
\tilde{\rho}(V/X)=\rho(V)-\rho(X)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
containing
X
, respectively.

Occasionally we will make use of the dual
q
-matroid.
The following can be found for instance in
[
20
, Def. 41, Thm. 42]
, where duality is based on a lattice involution,
or in
[
12
, Thm. 2.8]
based on a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on
E
.
In
[
12
]
it is also explained that, while
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
depends on the choice of the bilinear form, it is
unique up to equivalence.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\langle{\cdot}\!\mid\!{\cdot}\rangle
be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form (NSBF) on
E
, and for
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
set

V^{\perp}=\{w\in E\mid\mbox{$\langle{v}\!\mid\!{w}\rangle$}=0\text{ for all }v\in V\}
.
Given a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
. Then

\rho^{*}(V)=\dim V+\rho(V^{\perp})-\rho(E)

defines a rank function and thus
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}=(E,\rho^{*})
is a
q
-matroid, called the
dual
of
{\mathcal{M}}
with respect
to the chosen NSBF.
Furthermore,
{\mathcal{M}}^{**}={\mathcal{M}}
, where
{\mathcal{M}}^{**}=({\mathcal{M}}^{*})^{*}
is the bidual.

Remark 2.8
.

For all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
one has
V\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longleftrightarrow V^{\perp}\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}^{*})
; see
[
6
, Cor. 86]
.
As a consequence,
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}^{*})=\{Z^{\perp}\mid Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})\}
.

We now turn to the direct sum of
q
-matroids, which has been introduced in
[
8
]
.
Throughout the paper we use the following notation.

Notation 2.9
.

For any direct sum
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
of
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector spaces
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
we denote by

\pi_{i}:E\longrightarrow E_{i},\,i=1,2,
the corresponding projections.
Moreover, for any collections
{\mathcal{V}}_{i}
of subspaces in
E_{i}
we set

{\mathcal{V}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{V}}_{2}=\{V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\mid V_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{i}\}
.

Theorem 2.10

(
[
8
, Sec. 6]
and
[
14
, Ch. 5]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
be
q
-matroids and set
E=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}
.
Define
\rho^{\prime}_{i}:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\ V\longmapsto\rho_{i}(\pi_{i}(V))
for
i=1,2
.
Then
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}_{i}=(E,\rho^{\prime}_{i})
is a
q
-matroid.
Define

\rho:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\quad V\longmapsto\dim V+\min_{X\in{\mathcal{L}}(V)}\big(\rho^{\prime}_{1}(X)+\rho^{\prime}_{2}(X)-\dim X\big).

(2.2)

Then
{\mathcal{M}}:=(E,\rho)
is a
q
-matroid, called the
direct sum
of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
and denoted by
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

It is easy to see that

\rho(V_{1}\oplus V_{2})=\rho_{1}(V_{1})+\rho_{2}(V_{2})\ \text{ for all $V_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{i}),\,i=1,2$}.

(2.3)

In particular
\rho({\mathcal{M}})=\rho_{1}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})+\rho_{2}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})
.

Remark 2.11
.

The auxiliary
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}^{\prime}
appearing in
2.10

are in fact special cases of the direct sum.
Indeed,
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}^{\prime}\approx{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{U}}_{0,n_{2}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{\prime}\approx{\mathcal{U}}_{0,n_{1}}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

The direct sum of
q
-matroids behaves very different from that for matroids.
Some discrepancies are summarized in the following result.
We will see more differences later in this paper.
Recall
2.9
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
and
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

(a)

Similar to
2.3
, for a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{N}}
let
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{N}})
and
{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{N}})
denote the collection of independent spaces and circuits, respectively.
Then
{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})\subset{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{M}}),\ {\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})\subset{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}),\ {\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})\subset{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
,
and
{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\cup{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})\subset{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{M}})
. In general equality does not hold in any of these cases.
see
[
15
, Prop. 6.1]
. This stands in contrasts to the matroid case where one has equality for all collections.

(b)

{\mathcal{M}}
may be non-representable over any field extension of
{\mathbb{F}}
even if
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
are
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{m}}
-representable;
see
[
13
]
and
[
2
]
for further details.
Again, this differs strikingly from the matroid case.

On the positive side, the cyclic flats behave well with the direct sum.

Theorem 2.13

(
[
15
, Thms. 5.6 and 6.3]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E_{i},\rho_{i}),\,i=1,2,
and
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
with rank function
\rho
.
Then
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
. As a consequence (see (
2.1
)),

\rho(V)=\dim V+\min_{Z_{i}\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{i})}\big(\rho_{1}(Z_{1})+\rho_{2}(Z_{2})-\dim((Z_{1}\oplus Z_{2})\cap V)\big)

for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E_{1}\oplus E_{2})
.

3
The Whitney Function of
q
-Matroids

Throughout this section, let
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
E
be an
n
-dimensional
{\mathbb{F}}
-vector space.
Fix a
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
and let
\hat{\rho}=\rho(E)
be its rank.

The Whitney function of
{\mathcal{M}}
(also known as rank-generating function or corank-nullity function) is defined in the usual way; see also

[
7
, Def. 64]
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
Whitney function
or
corank-nullity function
of
{\mathcal{M}}
is

R_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{V\leq E}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(V)}y^{\dim V-\rho(V)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y].

The exponent
\hat{\rho}-\rho(V)
is called the
corank
of
V
and
\dim V-\rho(V)
is the
nullity
.

Clearly, the Whitney function is invariant under equivalence of
q
-matroids.

The coefficients of the Whitney function can easily be described.
A monomial
x^{i}y^{j}
appears in
R_{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if there exists a subspace
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
such that
\rho(V)=\hat{\rho}-i
and

\dim V=\hat{\rho}-i+j
.
Furthermore, in that case
j\leq n-\hat{\rho}
. Indeed,
choosing a subspace
W
such that
V\oplus W=E
, we obtain from submodularity

\hat{\rho}=\rho(E)\leq\rho(V)+\rho(W)\leq\rho(V)+\dim W=\hat{\rho}-i+n-\dim V=n-j
and thus
j\leq n-\hat{\rho}
.
All of this shows that the Whitney function is of the form

R_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{\rho}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-\hat{\rho}}\nu_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}

(3.1)

where

\nu_{i,j}=\big|\{V\leq E\mid\rho(V)=\hat{\rho}-i,\,\dim V=\hat{\rho}-i+j\}\big|.

(3.2)

Remark 3.2
.

The Whitney function determines various parameters of
{\mathcal{M}}
. First of all, we have

\hat{\rho}=\deg_{x}R_{{\mathcal{M}}},\quad n-\hat{\rho}=\deg_{y}R_{{\mathcal{M}}},\quad|{\mathcal{L}}(E)|=R_{\mathcal{M}}(1,1),

and
x^{\hat{\rho}}
and
y^{n-\hat{\rho}}
are monomials in
R_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Furthermore,
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\nu_{\hat{\rho}-i,k-i}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{n}{k}
for every
k\in[n]
and

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{M}}(0,0)

\displaystyle=\text{number of bases of }{\mathcal{M}},

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{M}}(1,0)

\displaystyle=\text{number of independent spaces of }{\mathcal{M}},

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{M}}(0,1)

\displaystyle=\text{number of spanning spaces of }{\mathcal{M}}

(see
[
6
]
for bases and spanning spaces).
Finally, if
R_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{\rho}}f_{i}x^{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-\hat{\rho}}g_{j}y^{j}
where
f_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[y]
and
g_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
, then

0\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longleftrightarrow f_{\hat{\rho}}=1\ \text{ and }\ E\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longleftrightarrow g_{n-\hat{\rho}}=1.

Indeed,
0\not\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
is equivalent to the existence of a space
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }\rho(V)=0
, and this in turn
is equivalent to
x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(V)}y^{\dim V-\rho(V)}=x^{\hat{\rho}}y^{\ell}
being a monomial in
R_{\mathcal{M}}
for some
\ell>0
.
Likewise,
E\not\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
is equivalent to the existence of some
W\in\mbox{\rm Hyp}(E)
such that
\rho(W)=\hat{\rho}-1
, and this is equivalent to

xy^{n-\hat{\rho}}
being a monomial in
R_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Later in
9.5
we will see how to extract partial information about the cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}
from the Whitney function.

The following is easy to see.

Example 3.3
.

Let
k\in[n]_{0}
and
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{U}}_{k,n}
. Then
R_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{n}{j}x^{k-j}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{n}{j}y^{j-k}
.

Just like for matroids, there exist non-equivalent
q
-matroids with the same Whitney function.
The following example is not hard to find. We will see more interesting cases later in
Examples
5.9
,
8.4
, and
9.1
.

Example 3.4
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
. In
E={\mathbb{F}}^{6}
define

V_{1}=\mbox{$\langle{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}}\rangle$},\ V_{2}=\mbox{$\langle{e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}}\rangle$},\ V_{3}=\mbox{$\langle{e_{3},e_{4},e_{5}}\rangle$}

and set
{\mathcal{V}}=\{V_{1},\,V_{2}\}
and
{\mathcal{W}}=\{V_{1},\,V_{3}\}
.
Define
\rho_{i}:{\mathcal{L}}(E)\longrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\,i=1,2,
via

\rho_{1}(V)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}2,&\text{if }V\in{\mathcal{V}},\\
\min\{\dim V,3\},&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right\}\qquad\rho_{2}(V)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}2,&\text{if }V\in{\mathcal{W}},\\
\min\{\dim V,3\},&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right\}

Then
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
are rank functions (see
[
12
, Prop. 4.6]
) and thus we have
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}=(E,\rho_{i})
.
It turns out that

R_{{\mathcal{M}}_{1}}=R_{{\mathcal{M}}_{2}}=x^{3}+y^{3}+63x^{2}+2xy+63y^{2}+651x+651y+1393.

The two
q
-matroids are not equivalent.
Indeed, the lattices of cyclic flats are given by
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{1})={\mathcal{V}}\cup\{0,{\mathbb{F}}^{6}\}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}_{2})={\mathcal{W}}\cup\{0,{\mathbb{F}}^{6}\}
,
and
V_{1}\cap V_{2}=0
, whereas
V_{1}\cap V_{3}\neq 0
.
Note that the abstract lattices of cyclic flats are isomorphic, and the isomorphism preserves the dimension and
rank values of the cyclic flats.
Later in
Section

5
we will describe this via the configurations of
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
.

Recall the dual
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
from
2.7
.
Obviously, the rank functions
\rho
and
\rho^{*}
satisfy

\dim V^{\perp}-\rho^{*}(V^{\perp})=\rho(E)-\rho(V)\ \text{ for all }V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E),

(3.3)

that is, the nullity of
V^{\perp}
in
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
equals the corank of
V
in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
This leads immediately to the following duality of the Whitney function; see also
[
7
, Prop. 92]
.

Theorem 3.5
.

R_{{\mathcal{M}}^{*}}(x,y)=R_{{\mathcal{M}}}(y,x)
.

We close this section with a short remark on the characteristic function.

Remark 3.6
.

The characteristic polynomial of
{\mathcal{M}}
is defined as

\chi_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{V\leq E}\mu(0,V)x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(V)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
, where
\mu
is the Möbius function on the subspace lattice
{\mathcal{L}}(E)
(see also

[
4
, Def. 23]
).
Thus,
\chi_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{\dim V=j}(-1)^{j}q^{\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{2}{j}{2}}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(V)}
.
With the notation from (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) we have

\chi_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{\rho}}\sum_{j=i}^{n}(-1)^{j}\nu_{\hat{\rho}-i,j-i}q^{\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{2}{j}{2}}x^{\hat{\rho}-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{\rho}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-\hat{\rho}}\nu_{i,j}(-1)^{\hat{\rho}+j-i}q^{\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{2}{\hat{\rho}+j-i}{2}}x^{i}.

In other words,
\chi_{\mathcal{M}}
is obtained from
R_{\mathcal{M}}
by substituting
(-1)^{\hat{\rho}+j-i}q^{\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{2}{\hat{\rho}+j-i}{2}}x^{i}
for
x^{i}y^{j}
.
This can be rephrased as substituting
q^{\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{2}{\hat{\rho}+j-i}{2}}(-x)^{i}(-1)^{j}
for
x^{i}y^{j}
in the polynomial
(-1)^{\hat{\rho}}R_{\mathcal{M}}
.
The latter may be regarded as the
q
-analogue of the well-known matroid identity
\chi_{M}(x)=(-1)^{\hat{r}}R_{M}(-x,-1)
, where
\hat{r}
is the rank of the matroid
M
.

4
The Cloud and Flock Polynomial

Following
[
21
,
10
]
on clouds and flocks for matroids, we introduce the cloud and flock polynomials of a
q
-matroid.
The cloud polynomial of a cyclic flat
Z
captures information about the corank of the flats whose cyclic core is
Z
, while the flock polynomial of
Z
contains information about the nullity of the spaces whose closure is
Z
.
The main result of this section shows that the collection of all cloud and flock polynomials of
{\mathcal{M}}
fully determine the Whitney function
R_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Throughout, let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid with
\rho({\mathcal{M}})=\hat{\rho}
. Recall the notation from
2.3
.
Closely related to the notion of flats and cyclic spaces are the closure and cyclic core.
For
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
we define the
closure
of
V
as

\text{cl}(V)=\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{1}{x\in E}{\rho(V+\langle x\rangle)=\rho(V)}}\hskip-16.99998pt\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$},

and the
cyclic core
as

\text{cyc}(V)=\{x\in V\mid\rho(W)=\rho(V)\text{ for all $W\leq V$ such that $W+\mbox{$\langle{x}\rangle$}=V$}\}.

Clearly,
\text{cl}(V)\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
and
\text{cyc}(V)\in{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{M}})
for all
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
; see also
[
15
, Thm. 3.6]
.
Properties of the closure can be found in
[
6
, Def. 15]
(see also the summary in
[
15
, Thm. 2.7]
), while the cyclic core has been studied in detail in
[
1
]
and
[
15
]
.
We will need the following results, which can be found in

[
15
, Prop. 3.10(a), Cor. 3.7, Lem. 4.1]
or
[
1
, Lemmas 3.17, 3.23, 3.22]
.

Remark 4.1
.

Let
V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
. Then

(a)

\dim V-\rho(V)=\dim\text{cyc}(V)-\rho(\text{cyc}(V))
;

(b)

\text{cl}^{*}(V^{\perp})=\text{cyc}(V)^{\perp}
, where
\text{cl}^{*}
is the closure in
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
;

(c)

F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\Longrightarrow\text{cyc}(F)\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.

Thus we obtain two well-defined operators

\left.\begin{array}[]{ccccrcc}\text{cl}:&\;{\mathcal{L}}(E)&\longrightarrow&{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}}),&\quad V&\longmapsto&\text{cl}(V),\\[2.15277pt]
\text{cyc}:&\;{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})&\longrightarrow&{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}}),&\quad F&\longmapsto&\text{cyc}(F).\end{array}\qquad\right\}

(4.1)

The following notions are the
q
-analogues of the cloud and flock for matroids as studied in
[
21
]
and
[
10
]
.
Note that
\text{cyc}^{-1}
and
\text{cl}^{-1}
below refer to the operators in (
4.1
), hence the spaces in
\text{cyc}^{-1}(Z)
are flats by definition.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
The
cloud
of
Z
and
flock
of
Z
are, respectively,

\text{cyc}^{-1}(Z)=\{F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\mid\text{cyc}(F)=Z\}\ \text{ and }\ \text{cl}^{-1}(Z)=\{V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid\text{cl}(V)=Z\},

Furthermore, the
cloud polynomial
and
flock polynomial
of
Z
are, respectively,

c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}=\sum_{F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(Z)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]\ \text{ and }\ f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}=\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)}y^{\dim V-\rho(V)}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[y].

Note the asymmetry in the above definition.
Whereas the cloud consists of the
flats
whose cyclic core is
Z
, the flock consists of
all
spaces whose closure is
Z
.
We will discuss this asymmetry later in
5.12
.

Remark 4.3
.

It is easy to see that
\deg c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}=\hat{\rho}-\rho(Z)
, which is the corank of
Z
, and
\deg f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}=\dim Z-\rho(Z)
, which is the nullity of
Z
(for the monotonicity of the nullity function see
[
1
, p. 99]
).
Moreover, if
{\mathcal{M}}
is full (see
2.3
), then
\text{cyc}^{-1}(E)=\{E\}
and
\text{cl}^{-1}(0)=\{0\}
and thus
c_{{\mathcal{M}},E}=1=f_{{\mathcal{M}},0}
.

The following simple descriptions of the cloud and flock will be very useful.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
. Then

\displaystyle\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)

\displaystyle=\{V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid V\leq Z\text{ and }\rho(V)=\rho(Z)\},

\displaystyle\text{cyc}^{-1}(Z)

\displaystyle=\{F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})\mid Z\leq F\text{ and }\dim F-\rho(F)=\dim Z-\rho(Z)\}.

Proof.

The statement about
\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)
is clear from the definition of the closure and the fact that
Z
is a flat.
For the cloud we use duality; see
2.7
and
4.1
.
Denoting by
\text{cl}^{*}
the closure in the dual
q
-matroid
{\mathcal{M}}^{*}
,
we obtain for any
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})

\displaystyle F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(Z)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow Z=\text{cyc}(F)\Longleftrightarrow Z^{\perp}=\text{cyc}(F)^{\perp}\Longleftrightarrow Z^{\perp}=\text{cl}^{*}(F^{\perp})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow F^{\perp}\leq Z^{\perp}\text{ and }\rho^{*}(Z^{\perp})=\rho^{*}(F^{\perp})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow Z\leq F\text{ and }\dim Z^{\perp}+\rho(Z)-\rho(E)=\dim F^{\perp}+\rho(F)-\rho(E)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow Z\leq F\text{ and }\rho(Z)-\dim Z=\rho(F)-\dim F,

where the fourth step follows from the first part.
∎

Example 4.5
.

Let
k\in[n]_{0}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{k,n}:={\mathcal{U}}_{k,n}(E)
be the uniform
q
-matroid of rank
k
on an
n
-dimensional ground space
E
.
The flats are

{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{U}}_{k,n})=\{F\leq E\mid\dim F\leq k-1\text{ or }F=E\}
and the cyclic spaces are

{\mathcal{O}}({\mathcal{U}}_{k,n})=\{V\leq E\mid\dim V\geq k+1\text{ or }V=0\}
, respectively.
Thus for
1\leq k\leq n-1
we have
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{U}}_{k,n})=\{0,E\}
, while
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{U}}_{0,n})=\{E\}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{U}}_{n,n})=\{0\}
;
see also
[
15
, Ex. 4.3]
.
Furthermore,

\text{cl}^{-1}(E)=\{V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid\dim V\geq k\}\quad\text{ for }\ 0\leq k\leq n-1

and

\text{cyc}^{-1}(0)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\{F\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid\dim F\leq k-1\},&\text{if }1\leq k\leq n-1,\\
{\mathcal{L}}(E),&\text{if }k=n.\end{array}\right.

Define for
k\in[n]_{0}

c_{k,n}=\delta_{k,n}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{n}{j}_{q}x^{k-j}\text{ and }\ f_{k,n}=\sum_{j=k}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{n}{j}_{q}y^{j-k},

where
\delta_{k,n}
is the Kronecker-
\delta
-function.
Then
c_{k,n}=c_{{\mathcal{U}}_{k,n},0}
for
k\in[n]
and
f_{k,n}=f_{{\mathcal{U}}_{k,n},E}
for
k\in[n-1]_{0}
.

The rest of this section is devoted to showing that the Whitney function is fully determined by the collection of cloud and flock polynomials.
To do so, we need the following simple lemma from Linear Algebra, whose proof can be found in the appendix.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
F,\,Z
be
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
-vector spaces with
\dim F=f
and
\dim Z=z
and
Z\leq F
.

(a)

Set
{\mathcal{W}}=\{W\in{\mathcal{L}}(F)\mid Z\oplus W=F\}
, that is,
{\mathcal{W}}
is the collection of all direct complements of
Z
in
F
. Then
|{\mathcal{W}}|=q^{(f-z)z}
.

(b)

Let
V\leq Z
and
\dim V=v
. Then the set
\{V\oplus W\mid W\in{\mathcal{W}}\}
has cardinality
q^{(f-z)(z-v)}
and each subspace
in
\{V\oplus W\mid W\in{\mathcal{W}}\}
is obtained
q^{(f-z)v}
times.

As a first step we establish a relation between the flock of a flat and the flock of its cyclic core.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
and
Z=\text{cyc}(F)
, thus
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
Let the collection
{\mathcal{W}}
be as in
Lemma

4.6
(a).
Then the map

\gamma:\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)\times{\mathcal{W}}\longrightarrow\text{cl}^{-1}(F),\quad(V,W)\longmapsto V\oplus W

is well-defined and surjective.
Moreover, for any
T\in\text{cl}^{-1}(F)
the fiber is of the form

\gamma^{-1}(T)=\{(T\cap Z,W)\mid W\in{\mathcal{W}}_{T}\},

for some subset
{\mathcal{W}}_{T}\subseteq{\mathcal{W}}
of size
|{\mathcal{W}}_{T}|=q^{(f-z)\dim(T\cap Z)}
.
In particular, if
T\in\text{cl}^{-1}(F)
then
T\cap Z\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)
and

\dim(T\cap Z)=\dim T-\dim F+\dim Z
.

Proof.

For any
W\in{\mathcal{W}}
we have
Z\oplus W=F
. Consider the restriction
\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}={\mathcal{M}}|_{F}
and denote its rank function again by
\rho
.
Then
[
15
, Prop. 7.5]
implies

\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}=\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}|_{Z}\oplus\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}|_{W}

(4.2)

and
\hat{{\mathcal{M}}}|_{W}
is the free
q
-matroid on the ground space
W
. Thus,
\rho(F)=\rho(Z)+\dim W
.

Well-definedness of
\gamma
:

We have to show that
\text{cl}(V\oplus W)=F
for all
V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)
and
W\in{\mathcal{W}}
.
Using (
2.3
) and the fact that
\text{cl}(V)=Z
we obtain from (
4.2
)

\rho(V\oplus W)=\rho(V)+\rho(W)=\rho(V)+\dim W=\rho(Z)+\dim W=\rho(F).

Since
V\oplus W\leq F
and
F
is a flat, this implies
\text{cl}(V\oplus W)=F
.

Surjectivity:

Let
T\in\text{cl}^{-1}(F)
. Set
V=T\cap Z
.
Furthermore, let
Y\leq T
be such that
T=V\oplus Y
.
Then there exists
W\in{\mathcal{W}}
such that
Y\leq W
.
(Indeed, let
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}
be such that
\beta_{1}
is a basis of
T\cap Z
, and
\beta_{2}
is a basis of
Y
, and

\beta_{1}\cup\beta_{3}
is a basis of
Z
.
Then
\beta_{1}\cup\beta_{2}
is a basis of
T
and
\beta_{1}\cup\beta_{2}\cup\beta_{3}
is a basis of
T+Z
.
In particular, the last set is linearly independent.
Hence
\beta_{2}
can be extended to a basis of a direct complement of
Z
.)
Now we obtain from (
4.2
) along with (
2.3
)

\rho(V)+\dim Y=\rho(T)=\rho(F)=\rho(Z)+\dim W.

Since
\dim Y\leq\dim W
and
\rho(V)\leq\rho(Z)
, this implies
\dim Y=\dim W
and thus
Y=W
.
Moreover,
\rho(V)=\rho(Z)
and thus
V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)
.
Now we have
T=\gamma(V,W)
and
\dim T=\dim(T\cap Z)+\dim F-\dim Z
.
This also shows that the fiber
\gamma^{-1}(T)
is of the stated form, and its size is a consequence of
Lemma

4.6
(b).
∎

The following is a consequence of
4.7
and
Lemma

4.6
(b).

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
and
Z=\text{cyc}(F)
, thus
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
. Moreover, let
\dim F=f
and
\dim Z=z
.
Then

\big|\{T\in\text{cl}^{-1}(F)\mid\dim T=v+f-z\}\big|=q^{(f-z)(z-v)}\big|\{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)\mid\dim V=v\}\big|.

Now we are ready to present a
q
-analogue of the cloud-flock formula for the Whitney function of matroids;
see
[
10
, Thm. 2.1]
or
[
21
, Prop. 4.6]
.
Note that – different from the matroid case – the Whitney function is not simply the sum of the products of
the cloud and flock polynomials over all cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Theorem 4.9
.

R_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})}\sum_{F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(Z)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)}q^{(\dim F-\dim Z)(\dim Z-\dim V)}y^{\dim V-\rho(Z)}.

Proof.

Let us first fix some
F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})
and let
Z=\text{cyc}(F)
. Let
\dim F=f
and
\dim Z=z
.
Then
f-\rho(F)=z-\rho(Z)
thanks to
4.1
(a).
Using
4.7
and
4.8
we first compute

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\text{cl}^{-1}(F)}y^{\dim T-\rho(T)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=0}^{z}\sum_{T\in\text{cl}^{-1}(F)\atop\dim(T\cap Z)=v}y^{v+f-z-\rho(F)}=\sum_{v=0}^{z}q^{(f-z)(z-v)}\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)\atop\dim V=v}y^{v+f-z-\rho(F)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=0}^{z}q^{(f-z)(z-v)}\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)\atop\dim V=v}y^{v-\rho(Z)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)}q^{(f-z)(z-\dim V)}y^{\dim V-\rho(Z)}.

From this we obtain

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(T)}y^{\dim T-\rho(T)}=\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})}\sum_{T\in\text{cl}^{-1}(F)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(T)}y^{\dim T-\rho(T)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}\sum_{T\in\text{cl}^{-1}(F)}y^{\dim T-\rho(T)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{F}}({\mathcal{M}})}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(\text{cyc}(F))}q^{(\dim F-\dim\text{cyc}(F))(\dim\text{cyc}(F)-\dim V)}y^{\dim V-\rho(\text{cyc}(F))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})}\sum_{F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(Z)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)}q^{(\dim F-\dim Z)(\dim Z-\dim V)}y^{\dim V-\rho(Z)}.\qed

It is not immediate from the above result that the Whitney function depends only on the cloud and flock polynomials of the cyclic flats of
{\mathcal{M}}
.
Fortunately, this is indeed the case as we can see from the following expression.

Corollary 4.10
.

For any
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
write

c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\deg c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}}c_{\alpha,Z}x^{\alpha}\ \text{ and }\ f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}=\sum_{\beta=0}^{\deg f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}}f_{\beta,Z}y^{\beta}.

Then

R_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\sum_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\deg c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}}\sum_{\beta=0}^{\deg f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}}q^{(\deg c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}-\alpha)(\deg f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}-\beta)}c_{\alpha,Z}f_{\beta,Z}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}.

Proof.

Consider
R_{\mathcal{M}}
as in
4.9
.
Let
Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
Thanks to Remarks
4.3
and
4.1
(a) we have for any
F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(Z)
and
V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(Z)

\displaystyle\dim F-\dim Z=\rho(F)-\rho(Z)=\hat{\rho}-\rho(Z)-(\hat{\rho}-\rho(F))=\deg c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}-(\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)),

\displaystyle\dim Z-\dim V=\dim Z-\rho(Z)-(\dim V-\rho(V))=\deg f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}-(\dim V-\rho(Z)).

Now the result follows from
4.9
and
4.2
.
∎

The last result can be expressed more elegantly by defining a suitable product between polynomials in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
and
{\mathbb{Z}}[y]
.

Remark 4.11
.

For
f=\sum_{i=0}^{N}f_{i}x^{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x]
and
g=\sum_{j=0}^{M}g_{j}y^{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[y]
define

f*g:=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{M}q^{(\deg f-i)(\deg g-j)}f_{i}g_{j}x^{i}y^{j}.

Then
4.10
reads as
R_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})}c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}*f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}
.

We close this section with the following example.

Example 4.12
.

Consider the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{2^{7}}
with primitive element
\omega
satisfying
\omega^{7}+\omega+1=0
.
Let

G=\begin{pmatrix}1&\omega^{90}&0&\omega^{10}&0&\omega^{4}\\
0&0&1&\omega^{7}&0&\omega^{90}\\
0&0&0&0&1&\omega^{32}\end{pmatrix}

and
{\mathcal{M}}={\mathcal{M}}_{G}=({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{6},\rho)
.
Using

well-defined bijection.
It remains to show that

is meet- and join-preserving.
Let

we have for any

\displaystyle F=Z^{\prime}\vee Z^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow F=\text{cl}(Z^{\prime}+Z^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow F\text{ is the inclusion-smallest flat of~${\mathcal{M}}|Z_{2}$ containing }Z^{\prime}+Z^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow F/Z_{1}\text{ is the inclusion-smallest flat of $\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}$ containing }(Z^{\prime}+Z^{\prime\prime})/Z_{1}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\phi(F)\text{ is the inclusion-smallest flat of $\widehat{{\mathcal{M}}}$ containing }\phi(Z^{\prime})+\phi(Z^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\phi(F)=\text{cl}(\phi(Z^{\prime})+\phi(Z^{\prime\prime}))=\phi(Z^{\prime})\vee\phi(Z^{\prime\prime}).

is the inclusion-largest cyclic space contained in

To do so, we need the following maps.

Definition 5.6
.

Define

\displaystyle d_{x}:{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}[x],\quad\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}f_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}\longmapsto\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}f_{ij}x^{i-j},

\displaystyle d_{y}:{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}[y],\quad\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}f_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}\longmapsto\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=i}^{m}f_{ij}y^{j-i}.

Note that
d_{x}(f)
is the truncation of the Laurent polynomial
f(x,x^{-1})
to the terms with positive exponents, while
d_{y}(f)
is the truncation of

f(y^{-1},y)
to the terms with non-negative exponents.
Both maps are
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear.

Recall
4.11
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a full
q
-matroid of rank
\hat{\rho}
on the
n
-dimensional ground space
E
.
Set
\widehat{{\mathcal{Z}}}({\mathcal{M}})={\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})\setminus\{0,E\}
and

\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\sum_{Z\in\widehat{{\mathcal{Z}}}({\mathcal{M}})}c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}*f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z}
.
Then

c_{\hat{\rho},n}=d_{x}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+c_{{\mathcal{M}},0}\quad\text{ and }\quad f_{\hat{\rho},n}=d_{y}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+f_{{\mathcal{M}},E},

where
c_{\hat{\rho},n}
and
f_{\hat{\rho},n}
are the cloud and flock polynomial of the uniform
q
-matroid given in
4.5
.
Hence
c_{{\mathcal{M}},0}
and
f_{{\mathcal{M}},E}
are fully determined by the collection

\{(c_{{\mathcal{M}},Z},f_{{\mathcal{M}},Z})\mid Z\in\widehat{{\mathcal{Z}}}({\mathcal{M}})\}
.

Proof.

First of all,
3.1
implies
d_{x}(R_{{\mathcal{M}}})=\sum_{j=0}^{\hat{\rho}-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{n}{j}_{q}x^{\hat{\rho}-j}=c_{\hat{\rho},n}
.
Next, since
{\mathcal{M}}
is full we have
\text{cl}(0)=0
and
\text{cyc}(E)=E
.
This implies that every
F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(0)
satisfies
\rho(F)<\hat{\rho}
.
Indeed, suppose
\rho(F)=\hat{\rho}=\rho(E)
.
Then, since
F
is a flat, this implies
F=\text{cl}(F)=E
. But then
0=\text{cyc}(F)
contradicts
\text{cyc}(E)=E
.
Hence
\rho(F)<\hat{\rho}
.
Moreover, every
V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(E)
satisfies
\dim V\geq\rho(V)=\hat{\rho}
.
All of this together with the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linearity of

leads to

\displaystyle c_{\hat{\rho},n}

\displaystyle=d_{x}(R_{{\mathcal{M}}})=d_{x}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+d_{x}(\sum_{F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(0)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}y^{0})+d_{x}(\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(E)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\hat{\rho}}y^{\dim V-\hat{\rho}})

\displaystyle=d_{x}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+\sum_{F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(0)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}+d_{x}(\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(E)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\dim V})

\displaystyle=d_{x}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+c_{{\mathcal{M}},0}+0,

where the third and fourth step follows from the facts that every exponent in the terms
x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}
is positive while every exponent in the terms

x^{\hat{\rho}-\dim V}
is non-positive.

Similarly,
d_{y}(R_{{\mathcal{M}}})=\sum_{j=\hat{\rho}}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{n}{j}_{q}y^{j-\hat{\rho}}=f_{\hat{\rho},n}
and we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\hat{\rho},n}

\displaystyle=d_{y}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+d_{y}(\sum_{F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(0)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\rho(F)}y^{0})+d_{y}(\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(E)}x^{\hat{\rho}-\hat{\rho}}y^{\dim V-\hat{\rho}})

\displaystyle=d_{y}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+d_{y}(\sum_{F\in\text{cyc}^{-1}(0)}y^{\rho(F)-\hat{\rho}})+d_{y}(\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(E)}y^{\dim V-\hat{\rho}})

\displaystyle=d_{y}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+0+\sum_{V\in\text{cl}^{-1}(E)}y^{\dim V-\hat{\rho}}=d_{y}(\widehat{R}_{{\mathcal{M}}})+f_{{\mathcal{M}},E},

where the third step follows from the fact that every exponent in
y^{\rho(F)-\hat{\rho}}
is negative and every exponent in
y^{\dim V-\hat{\rho}}
is non-negative.
This proves the two identities.
The last statement is clear.
∎

Lemma 6.2

\sigma(B)

(a,b)\leq(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\text{ if $a\leq a^{\prime}$ and $b\leq b^{\prime}$.}

(a,b)\wedge(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=(a\wedge a^{\prime},b\wedge b^{\prime})

(a,b)\vee(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=(a\vee a^{\prime},b\vee b^{\prime})

8
Condensed Configurations

In Examples
5.2
and
5.9
we have seen that all cyclic flats with the same corank-nullity pair play the
same role within the lattice
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
. Even more, they all have the same cloud and flock polynomial.
As one may expect, this is not the case in general.
In this section we will have a closer look at this question.
Our discussion follows very closely the matroid case in
[
10
, Sec. 5]
, and, in fact, the results do not differ from the matroid case.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid. A
condensation

{\mathcal{P}}
of
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
is a partition of
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
with the properties:

(1)

The elements in every block
B
of
{\mathcal{P}}
have the same corank-nullity pair (and thus the same rank and dimension).

(2)

For all blocks
B,B^{\prime}
of
{\mathcal{P}}
there exist an integer
\Gamma_{{\mathcal{P}}}(B,B^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that

|\{Z\in B\mid Z\leq Z^{\prime}\}|=\Gamma_{{\mathcal{P}}}(B,B^{\prime})\text{ for all }Z^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}.

For a condensation
{\mathcal{P}}
we define a partial order on its blocks via
B\leq B^{\prime}
if

\Gamma_{{\mathcal{P}}}(B,B^{\prime})>0
(that is, every cyclic flat in
B^{\prime}
contains at least one cyclic flat in
B
).

Note that every condensation
({\mathcal{P}},\leq)
is a poset with largest element
\{\text{cyc}(E)\}
and smallest element
\{\text{cl}(0)\}
.
One can find examples showing that
({\mathcal{P}},\leq)
is in general not a lattice.

Definition 8.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid and let
{\mathcal{P}}=(B_{0},\ldots,B_{t})
be a condensation of
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.
For a block
B
of
{\mathcal{P}}
let
\lambda(B)=(\rho(E)-\rho(Z),\dim Z-\rho(Z))
for any
Z\in B
, that is,

\lambda(B)
is the corank-nullity pair of any cyclic flat in
B
.
We define
\mbox{$\mbox{\rm Config}^{*}_{{\mathcal{P}}}$}({\mathcal{M}}):=(\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}},\,\Lambda_{\mathcal{P}})
, where

\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}}=\big(\Gamma_{{\mathcal{P}}}(B_{i},B_{j})\big)_{i,j\in\{0,\ldots,t\}}\ \text{ and }\ \Lambda_{\mathcal{P}}=\big(\lambda(B_{0}),\ldots,\lambda(B_{t})\big).

\mbox{$\mbox{\rm Config}^{*}_{{\mathcal{P}}}$}({\mathcal{M}})
is called the
condensed configuration
of
{\mathcal{M}}
with respect to
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Example 8.3
.

(a)

The trivial partition
{\mathcal{P}}=\big(\{Z\}\big)_{Z\in{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})}
is a condensation.
In this case
\Gamma_{\mathcal{P}}(Z,Z^{\prime})=1
iff
Z\leq Z^{\prime}
and zero otherwise.
In other words,
\Gamma_{{\mathcal{P}}}
is the adjacency matrix of the poset
{\mathcal{Z}}({\mathcal{M}})
.

(b)

Consider Examples
4.12
/
5.2

\displaystyle=\sum_{D\in[B,B^{\prime}]\setminus\{B,B^{\prime}\}}c_{{\mathcal{P}}}(D,B^{\prime})*f_{{\mathcal{P}}}(B,D)

\displaystyle=\sum_{D\in[B,B^{\prime}]\setminus\{B,B^{\prime}\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z^{\prime\prime}\in D\\

We now turn to the direct sum.
Recall that for classical matroids it is known that the Whitney function of a direct sum
M_{1}\oplus M_{2}
is the product of the Whitney
functions of the two summands.
One easily verifies that this is not the case for
q
-matroids, and it is also not true for the Tutte polynomials as defined in
[
7
]
.
In fact, as following example shows the situation is even worse: for
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
,
the Whitney function
R_{{\mathcal{M}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}}
is not a function of
R_{{\mathcal{M}}_{1}}
and
R_{{\mathcal{M}}_{2}}
.

Example 9.2
.

Consider
9.1
. The
q
-matroids
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
have the same Whitney function, yet taking the direct sum of
{\mathcal{M}}_{i}
with the uniform
q
-matroid

{\mathcal{U}}_{1,2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2})
leads to the Whitney functions

\displaystyle R_{{\mathcal{U}}_{1,2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2})\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{1}}

\displaystyle=x^{4}+(2y+255)x^{3}+(2y^{2}+159y+10793)x^{2}+(2y^{3}+159y^{2}+3469y+96996)x

\displaystyle\quad+y^{4}+255y^{3}+10793y^{2}+96996y+197316,

\displaystyle R_{{\mathcal{U}}_{1,2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2})\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}}

\displaystyle=x^{4}+(2y+255)x^{3}+(2y^{2}+159y+10793)x^{2}+(2y^{3}+159y^{2}+3475y+96996)x

\displaystyle\quad+y^{4}+255y^{3}+10793y^{2}+96996y+197310.

This shows that there is no
q
-analogue of the well-known matroid identity
R_{M_{1}\oplus M_{2}}=R_{M_{1}}R_{M_{2}}
.
In
[
7
]
the authors derive a notion of Tutte polynomial for
q
-matroids that admit a Tutte partition and establish a bijection between the Whitney function and the Tutte polynomial
[
7
, Prop. 67 and Cor. 68]
.
It can be expressed in terms of a rather technical (
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear) substitution for the monomials
x^{i}y^{j}
.
Thanks to this bijection, our example shows that there is also no
q
-analogue of
R_{M_{1}\oplus M_{2}}=R_{M_{1}}R_{M_{2}}
in terms of Tutte polynomials.
Finally, the direct sums
{\mathcal{U}}_{1,2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2})\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{1,2}({\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2})\oplus{\mathcal{M}}_{2}
also have different characteristic polynomials.
This follows immediately from the substitution rule given in
3.6
.
Hence the Whitney functions of two
q
-matroids do not even determine the characteristic polynomial of their direct sum.

Despite these negative results, there is some partial information about the cyclic flats that can be extracted from the Whitney function.
We need the notion of extremal terms in a polynomial; see also
[
21
, Rem. 4.11]
for matroids.

Definition 9.3
.

Let
f=\sum_{i=0}^{a}\sum_{j=0}^{b}f_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
with
\deg_{x}f=a
and
\deg_{y}f=b
. We define

\mbox{\rm M}_{f}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{0,0}&f_{0,1}&\ldots&f_{0,b}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
f_{a,0}&f_{a,1}&\ldots&f_{a,b}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{(a+1)\times(b+1)}.

A position
(\alpha,\beta)\in\{0,\ldots,a\}\times\{0,\ldots,b\}
is called
extremal
in
M_{f}
if

f_{\alpha,\beta}\neq 0,\quad f_{\alpha,j}=0\text{ for all }j>\beta,\quad f_{i,\beta}=0\text{ for all }i>\alpha.

In other words,
f_{\alpha,\beta}
is the rightmost nonzero entry in its row and the lowest nonzero entry in its column.
In this case we call
f_{\alpha,\beta}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}
an
extremal term
of
f
.

Before we turn to the extremal terms of the Whitney function, we show that its coefficient matrix is top-left aligned.

Proposition 9.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(E,\rho)
be a
q
-matroid of rank
\hat{\rho}
on the
n
-dimensional ground space
E
,
and let
R_{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{\rho}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-\hat{\rho}}\nu_{i,j}x^{i}y^{j}
.
We define the
Whitney matrix
of
{\mathcal{M}}
as

\mbox{\rm W}_{\mathcal{M}}:=\mbox{\rm M}_{R_{{\mathcal{M}}}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{(\hat{\rho}+1)\times(n-\hat{\rho}+1)}.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)
be such that
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}\neq 0
. Then

(a)

\nu_{\alpha,j}\neq 0
for all
j=0,\ldots,\beta
.

(b)

\nu_{i,\beta}\neq 0
for all
i=0,\ldots,\alpha
.

Hence the nonzero entries of
\mbox{\rm W}_{{\mathcal{M}}}
are top-left aligned.

Proof.

a) If
\beta=0
, there is nothing to show.
Thus let
\beta>0
.
By assumption there exists
V_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)
such that
\rho(V_{0})=\hat{\rho}-\alpha
and
\dim V_{0}=\hat{\rho}-\alpha+\beta
.
Then
V_{0}
is dependent and there exists a basis
A\lneq V_{0}
. Hence
\dim A=\rho(A)=\rho(V_{0})=\hat{\rho}-\alpha
.
This shows that
\nu_{\alpha,0}\neq 0
.
Moreover, for all
j\in\{0,\ldots,\beta\}
there exists a subspace
B:=A\oplus\mbox{$\langle{v_{1},\ldots,v_{j}}\rangle$}\leq V_{0}
, and it satisfies

\rho(B)=\hat{\rho}-\alpha
and
\dim B=\hat{\rho}-\alpha+j
. Thus
\nu_{\alpha,j}\neq 0
.

b) Thanks to
3.5
we have
\mbox{\rm W}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{*}}=(\mbox{\rm W}_{{\mathcal{M}}})\mbox{$\!{}^{\sf T}$}
and the result follows from (a).
∎

Now we arrive at the following
q
-analogue of
[
21
, Rem. 4.11]
.

Theorem 9.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a
q
-matroid of rank
\hat{\rho}
on an
n
-dimensional ground space and consider its Whitney matrix

\mbox{\rm W}_{\mathcal{M}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{(\hat{\rho}+1)\times(n-\hat{\rho}+1)}
.
Suppose
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}
is an extremal term of
R_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Then there exist exactly
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
cyclic flats of corank
\alpha
and nullity
\beta
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\{V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid\rho(V)=\hat{\rho}-\alpha,\,\dim V=\rho(V)+\beta\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\{V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid\rho(V)<\hat{\rho}-\alpha,\,\dim V=\rho(V)+\beta\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle=\{V\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\mid\rho(V)=\hat{\rho}-\alpha,\,\dim V>\rho(V)+\beta\}.

The extremality of
(\alpha,\beta)
implies
|{\mathcal{A}}|=\nu_{\alpha,\beta}>0
and
{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{C}}=\emptyset
.
Let
Z\in{\mathcal{A}}
. We have to show that
Z
is a cyclic flat.
Since
{\mathcal{C}}=\emptyset
, there are no spaces properly containing
Z
with the same rank, and thus
Z
is a flat.
Suppose now that
Z
is not cyclic. Then there exists
W\in\mbox{\rm Hyp}(Z)
such that
\rho(W)=\rho(Z)-1
.
Hence
\dim W-\rho(W)=\dim Z-\rho(Z)=\beta
and
\rho(W)=\hat{\rho}-\alpha-1
.
Thus
W\in{\mathcal{B}}
, which is a contradiction.
Finally, any cyclic flats of corank
\alpha
and nullity
\beta
has to be an element of
{\mathcal{A}}
, showing that there are exactly
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}

such cyclic flats.
∎

The above result does not account for all cyclic flats.

(0,3),\,(1,2),\,(2,1)
, and

10
Further Question

Our investigation gives rise to the following questions, which we have to leave to future research.

(a)

The configuration fully determines the Whitney function. The proof, however, relies on induction and is not explicit.
Can one give an explicit expression of the Whitney function in terms of the configuration?
The same question applies to condensed configurations.

(b)

The Whitney function guarantees, via its extremal terms, the existence of certain cyclic flats.
Can these cyclic flats be distinguished from the remaining cyclic flats other than via their ‘extremality’?
