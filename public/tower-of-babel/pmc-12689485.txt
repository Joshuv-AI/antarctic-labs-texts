A Novel Neurocosmetic Postprocedure Cream for Optimal Recovery and Tolerability From Energy‐Based Devices
Authors: Nathan M Weir, A J Burns, Tatiana Kononov, Alisar S Zahr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesA neurocosmetic post‐procedure cream (NPPC) was developed as a semi‐breathable topical to provide relief and recovery following energy‐based device treatments. The NPPC was investigated following hybrid fractional and fractional ablative laser resurfacing to assess tolerability, safety, and patient compliance and retention, as well as compare against a conventional post‐procedure topical routine.MethodsTwo randomized, double‐blinded, split‐face, controlled, procedure‐pairing studies were conducted with healthy females aged 35–65 years with Fitzpatrick Skin Types I–III and moderate to severe photodamage. Subjects were randomized to apply the NPPC and a comparator moisturizer (CM) or comparator anhydrous formulation (CAF) three times daily for 7 days post‐hybrid fractional laser (HFL) or 14 days post‐fractional ablative CO2 laser (FACL), respectively. Tolerability evaluations, questionnaires, and clinical photography were conducted at baseline and post‐baseline timepoints.ResultsSixteen and twenty‐two healthy female subjects completed the HFL and FACL studies, respectively. The NPPC was effective at improving erythema, edema, dryness, burning, itching, stinging, and discomfort as evidenced by tolerability evaluations, clinical photography, and questionnaires. There were no significant adverse events. Day 1 post‐FACL treatment, 91% of subjects favorably agreed “the product calmed my skin.” Patient relief and recovery were achieved with the NPPC. Across the two studies, the NPPC performed similarly or better than the conventional postprocedure topical routine investigated. The studies are limited to a small sample size, and the lack of a vehicle‐only or untreated control arm is unethical.ConclusionsAn NPPC developed to soothe skin following energy‐based device procedures demonstrated patient tolerability, safety, and improved healing time following hybrid and fractional ablative laser treatments. Patients indicated favorable responses to the treatments, and there was high agreement that they would return for a second treatment, denoting patient retention. Introduction

The use of energy‐based devices (EBDs) in cosmetic procedures has surged in recent years due to their ability to effectively address a range of skin concerns [1, 2]. These EBDs, such as CO2 and Erbium lasers, work by delivering infrared energy to targeted areas and depths of the skin, resulting in ablation, coagulation, and thermal injury. Fully ablative lasers provide marked results for reduction in rhytides, pigmentation, skin laxity, actinic damage, and other signs of photoaging [1, 3, 4]. Unfortunately, prolonged healing and persistent erythema, along with safety concerns, limited widespread use [5]. The evolution of fractionated lasers to target a small percentage of the skin allows for improved safety and tolerability but requires multiple treatments to attain more dramatic results [3, 6]. Despite advancements in EBD technology, postprocedure pain, discomfort, and recovery time are the most common reasons for patients not completing an EBD series [7].

Most EBD protocols use a postprocedure ointment (OIN) to occlude the compromised skin barrier. Petrolatum‐based OINs are superior in soothing skin as well as decreasing TEWL and infection [8, 9, 10, 11]. However, OINs are often perceived as greasy and less cosmetically desirable [8]. Addressing postprocedure discomfort often involves cool compresses, acetic acid soaks, and oral analgesics [11]. Few products in the market suitable for postprocedure skin directly attenuate discomfort at the site of injury. The Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid (TRPV1) skin nociceptor is known for its role in pain perception, and its modulation could lead to enhanced tolerability of cosmetic treatments, especially for individuals with sensitive skin [12].

A postprocedure cream, formulated as a semi‐occlusive, gas‐permeable, and skin lipid‐based delivery system containing neurocosmetic technology, microbiome technology, as well as lipophilic and hydrophilic emollients, was developed to reduce discomfort and enhance skin recovery [13]. Directly targeting communication between the skin and nervous system, a blend of botanical actives (neurocosmetics) including Albatrellus Confluens Extract, Saccharomyces Cerevisiae Extract, and Opuntia Ficus‐Indica Callus Culture Extract was formulated into the postprocedure cream to downregulate TRPV1 and upregulate the well‐being molecule β‐endorphin, thus comforting skin following EBD treatment [13, 14]. Therefore, neurocosmetics are topical products containing botanical actives or synthetic active such as peptides that enhance the communication between the skin and the brain, when the skin is stressed. By targeting receptors on both sensory neurons and keratinocytes, neurocosmetics modulate inflammation and stress.

This neurocosmetic postprocedure cream (NPPC) has demonstrated enhanced skin recovery post‐radiofrequency microneedling but has not been investigated for its tolerability or benefit on more aggressive laser treatments [15]. It was hypothesized that the NPPC would reduce discomfort postprocedure, accelerate skin recovery, and minimize downtime following EBD treatments, including hybrid fractional ablative/non‐ablative laser (hybrid fractional laser [HFL]) and fractional ablative CO2 laser (FACL), in healthy women with moderate to severe facial photodamage. The first objective was to investigate the safety, tolerability, patient compliance, and retention of NPPC versus CM following HFL. The second objective was to investigate safety, tolerability, patient compliance, and retention of NPPC versus CAF following FACL.

Materials and Methods

HFL Study

An institutional review board (IRB)‐approved 14‐day multicenter, randomized, double‐blinded, split‐face, and controlled case study was conducted involving 20 healthy female subjects aged 35–65 years with Fitzpatrick Skin Types (FSTs) I–IIV and moderate to severe global facial photodamage (score of 4–7 on the Modified Griffiths' 10‐point scale, where 0 corresponds to no photodamage and 9 is severe photodamage). Site 1 and Site 2 were medical practices located in Atlanta, Georgia, and Dallas, Texas, respectively. Twenty (20) subjects (10 per site) were recruited to receive one HFL treatment. Following treatment and based on randomization, subjects were to apply the NPPC to one side of the face and a comparator moisturizer (CM) to the other side for 7 days postprocedure. The CM was selected as a basic moisturizer considered to be a conventional postprocedure product. Patients were blinded by receiving both the NPPC and comparator in identical white airless bottles labeled “postprocedure cream,” despite the products being visually and texturally distinct. Supporting Material provides details on the skincare regimen.

All subjects at both sites received an HFL treatment (HALO®, Sciton®, Palo Alto, California, the United States) under the following settings: 1470 nm non‐ablative diode at 350 µm and 20% density and 2940 nm ablative erbium/yttrium aluminum garnet (YAG) diode at 20 µm and 18% density (350/20/20/18). These settings are low in depth but aggressive to achieve heat and swelling to test the safety and tolerability of the NPPC and acceptable for all FST I–IV. Board‐certified plastic surgeons oversaw the study, and laser specialists performed the treatment. Subjects numbed for 1 h with benzocaine/lidocaine/tetracaine (20%/6%/4%) before treatment.

FACL Study

An IRB‐approved 28‐day, single‐center, randomized, double‐blinded, split‐face, and controlled case study involving 23 healthy female subjects, 35–65 years of age with FST I–III and moderate to severe overall facial photodamage (score of 4–9 on the Modified Griffiths' scale). The study was conducted in Blue Ash, Ohio, by a board‐certified dermatologist. Twenty‐three (23) subjects were recruited to receive one FACL treatment. After the procedure and as per randomization, subjects were to apply to the NPPC and a comparator anhydrous formulation (CAF) split‐face for 14 days; the comparator side also required a basic OIN for the first 3 days postprocedure. The CAF was chosen as a conventional physician‐dispensed postprocedure product formulated as an anhydrous gel containing peptides, arnica, and additional botanical extracts, marketed to support EBD procedures. Patients were blinded by receiving both the NPPC and comparator in identical white airless bottles labeled “postprocedure cream,” despite the products being visually and texturally distinct. Supporting Material provides details on the skincare regimen.

All subjects received an FACL treatment (DEKA SmartXide Tetra CO2, Cartessa Aesthetics, Melville, New York, the United States) performed by the principal investigator (PI), a board‐certified dermatologist. The treatment was set at power 20 W with spacing 500 µm (13% density), dwell time 1200 µs pulse duration, spray mode on, and single pass with 10% overlap. A medical assistant monitored smoke ventilation, and a cryotherapy device (Cryo 7, Zimmer Medizin Systems, Irvine, California, the United States) was used for tissue cooling during the procedure. Before the treatment, subjects were numbed for 1 h under occlusion with lidocaine 23% and tetracaine 7% by SKNV (Pompano Beach, Florida, the United States).

Eligibility Criteria

For both studies, patients were included if they were willing to discontinue all active topical facial products and to refrain from all facial skin rejuvenation treatments during the study. Exclusion criteria included subjects who had experienced a previous hypersensitivity reaction to any of the ingredients in the study products, and those who were pregnant, breastfeeding, or planning a pregnancy during the study. Subjects who had undergone chemical peels or microneedling within 6 months and/or any laser resurfacing treatments within 12 months were ineligible.

Ethical Considerations

The protocol for each study was approved by Advarra IRB (Columbia, Maryland, the United States). Both studies were conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. Written informed consent was provided, by which the patients agreed to the use and analysis of their data and clinical photography.

Evaluations

Tolerability grading, self‐assessment questionnaires (SAQs), and standardized clinical photography were completed on procedure day and postprocedure days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14. Objective tolerability parameters (dryness, edema, and erythema) and subjective (burning, stinging, and itching) were graded according to a 4‐point scale ranging from 0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe. Subjects were also asked to complete SAQs relating to product effectiveness and acceptability using the following scale: 1 = Completely Disagree, 2 = Slightly Disagree, 3 = Neither Agree nor Disagree, 4 = Slightly Agree, and 5 = Completely Agree. Clinical photographs were used for image analysis to further evaluate skin recovery (Supporting Material).

Statistical Analysis for Both Studies

The Wilcoxon Signed‐Rank Test was used for data analysis, and statistical significance was set at the *p ≤ 0.05 level. Postprocedure was considered baseline for the investigator and subjective tolerability. Additionally, for the HFL study, the baseline for investigator tolerability was postprocedure product application. For the SAQ, ratings of 4 (Slightly Agree) and 5 (Completely Agree) were considered favorable agreement. The sample size determination was calculated for both studies with an 80% power and a significance level of 5%. For both the HFL and FACL studies, a total sample size of 18 was required (Supporting Material).

Results

The first and second objectives were achieved in both HFL and FACL studies. The safety, tolerability, patient compliance, and retention between the NPPC and comparator products were compared.

Across both sites, the NPPC was effective in alleviating investigator and subjective tolerability parameters, and results indicate that the NPPC promotes skin recovery and relief post‐HFL procedure. Due to the small sample size, there was a trend in the NPPC performing better than the CM; however, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups.

Two nonserious AEs were reported during the study (Atlanta, Georgia). One subject experienced a moderate reaction with irritation resolving in time; the subject continued using the product 3× a day and completed the study. The second AE was moderate contact dermatitis; the subject was withdrawn from the study. The product completed safety testing (Human Repeat Insult Patch Testing under occlusion on intact skin) on subjects with self‐perceived sensitive skin. Results indicated that the product did not elicit dermal irritation or induce sensitization. The product does not contain any known allergens; however, botanical‐derived extracts, including Daucus carota, apple, watermelon, cactus, and mushroom, could have possible contact sensitizers in susceptible people, especially with repeated exposure.

SAQ

Overall, the NPPC showed strong benefits versus the CM [16]. On Day 1 postprocedure, the NPPC outperformed the CM on all statements related to discomfort and relief. FACL Study

Subjects

Twenty‐two subjects aged 42–64 (average 50.7 years) with FST I–III completed the study (Table 1). One subject withdrew from the study due to an AE. Subjects were required to bring their diaries and products to the clinical site at each visit to be reviewed by the non‐blinded coordinator to assure adherence to the protocol.

Tolerability and Safety

The NPPC side saw improvements in dryness, edema, and erythema by Day 14 postprocedure. Dryness decreased over the course of the study, and by Day 7 postprocedure was resolved [17]. Edema and erythema increased at Day 1 postprocedure, remained stable for 2 days, and then fully resolved by Day 14 (*p ≤ 0.05). The NPPC provided subject relief postprocedure. There were no changes in burning or stinging postprocedure, product application compared to postprocedure, and both parameters decreased by a median percent of −100% by Day 1 postprocedure which was sustained to Day 14 postprocedure (*p ≤ 0.05). Although itching was not fully resolved by Day 14, a significant decrease of 50% was determined (*p ≤ 0.05).

Overall, the NPPC and comparator products were similarly efficacious in alleviating tolerability parameters as assessed by both investigator and subjects' postprocedure [17]. Findings indicate that the NPPC can be used as a stand‐alone postprocedure treatment, whereas the CAF requires an OIN for the first 3 days postprocedure.

Two nonserious AEs were reported. One subject experienced moderate irritant dermatitis/eczema related to the washout of the facial moisturizer and withdrew before baseline. The second subject developed moderate urticaria that was possibly related to starting a new oral medication; the subject completed the study. The second subject continued using both the NPPC and CAF as the moderate urticaria resolved.

The SAQ results show the efficacy of the NPPC in providing relief to the skin postprocedure. Discussion

The advent of fractional ablative and non‐ablative laser resurfacing has improved safety and decreased recovery and social downtime [18, 19, 20]. Similar results may be obtained to those of fully ablative CO2 and erbium lasers, but require multiple treatments [18, 19]. Optimizing patient comfort and recovery not only improves cosmetic outcome while decreasing risk of infections and scarring, but also ensures the patient is more likely to return and complete the recommended treatment series [21, 22]. While there is not one standard postprocedure protocol for EBDs, it is commonly accepted to use a non‐occlusive barrier following non‐ablative fractionated laser as the skin barrier is intact and dissipation of heat helps with comfort immediately after the treatment [8, 20, 21]. Conversely, fractionated ablative lasers vaporize columns of skin, leaving channels through the epidermis allowing water and serum to escape and bacteria to enter [22, 23]. Exposure of the dermis leads to increased pain and pruritus [18, 22]. Creams or gels are often employed for non‐ablative procedures, while OINs are conserved for ablative laser treatments [8, 21, 22]. The greasy nature of OINs makes them less cosmetically desirable with potential for noncompliance among users [9]. The duration of using creams, gels, and OINs post‐EBD procedures is dependent on the intensity of the EBD and the downtime typical of EBD. As there is not one‐standard postprocedure protocol for EBDs, application of such products ranges from 5 to 28 days postprocedure.

The pre‐procedure and postprocedure benefits of the CAF with ablative and non‐ablative lasers have shown statistically significant decreases in discomfort and healing time, but only when used in conjunction with a proprietary petrolatum or dimethicone‐based OIN [24, 25, 26, 27]. A few smaller pilot studies investigate the CAF as a pre‐ and postprocedure product to enhance the efficacy of the treatment, but largely neglect parameters of tolerance, pain, and healing in the first week following any EBD treatment [24, 25]. Most publications demonstrating the CAF efficacy have focused on enhanced cosmetic results following EBD procedures, such as improvements in photoaging, collagen, and elastin [24, 25, 27]. These CAF studies also use the entire postprocedure system, which includes a gentle cleanser, anhydrous serum, proprietary OIN, and mineral sunscreen [27]. There are no current studies with the CAF as a postprocedure monotherapy treatment.

In comparison, the development of a breathable, semi‐occlusive, and skin lipid‐based postprocedure cream containing neurocosmetic technology allows flexibility of a solitary product across a range of EBDs [13, 15]. The NPPC delivery system is a water‐in‐oil emulsion composed of an external phase containing phospholipids, cholesterol, phytosphingosine, and ceramides and an internal phase containing the neurocosmetic ingredients, Helichrysum Italicum Extract, Daucus Carota Sativa Root Extract, Albatrellus Confluens Extract, Saccharomyces Cerevisiae Extract, and Opuntia Ficus‐Indica Callus Culture Extract. Additionally, the NPPC is formulated with THD ascorbate and microbiome technology has been previously tested preclinically and clinically [13, 15]. In an ex vivo model, the NPPC attenuated both keratinocyte and sensory neuron activation by boosting β‐endorphin and downregulating TRPV1 (Supporting Material) [13]. Activation of TRPV1 contributes to the sensation of heat and inflammation, which are important early mediators in wound healing and defense [28]. For example, TRPV1 activation in dermal neurons and nearby dendritic cells potentiates IL‐23 and subsequently Th17 to defend against Candida and Staphylococcus aureus infections [12]. In a clinical study, NPPC application post‐RF microneedling showed improvement in erythema and tolerability across a series of treatments, directionally superior to the CAF [15]. This study extends the application of NPPC for use in both ablative and non‐ablative laser treatments to protect the compromised skin barrier and focuses on clinical outcomes, tolerability, and patient comfort.

Notably, our collective data does not demonstrate signs of delayed healing but supports shortened healing time with decreased perceived downtime. Furthermore, no subjects experienced bacterial, viral, or fungal infections. In the split‐face trial using FACL, the most intensive EBD used across the clinical studies demonstrated that the NPPC was able to improve hydration and decrease erythema, edema, and pain similar to the CAF with OIN. Although the NPPC was used for a short duration postprocedure (7–14 days), early signs of post‐inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) were not observed by clinician graders or captured by the imaging systems. We anticipate that in real‐world use, patients will transition into their daily skincare regimen, which may include brightening agents for those susceptible to hyperpigmentation. This sequential approach may help reduce the risk of PIH by supporting skin barrier recovery and minimizing ongoing inflammation during the critical early healing phase.

Patient opinion was positive of the NPPC, and subjects preferred it over the CAF for both relief in pruritus and head‐to‐head preference. Furthermore, more than three‐quarters of participants said they would repeat the procedure using the NPPC. Previous publications reported that the CAF decreased erythema and healing time when paired postprocedure with HFL treatment [24, 26, 27]. Though we did not have an untreated control arm, the NPPC performed as well as the CAF in all measured parameters, including healing time and patient satisfaction. The NPPC was evaluated using the Human Repeat Insult Patch Test and was found not to cause dermal irritation or induce sensitization. However, one case of contact dermatitis was reported; therefore, patch testing is recommended before use. The NPPC offers a safe, effective, and simplified option for patients following laser resurfacing.

Although comparator products (CAF and CM) were included, the studies are limited to the small sample size and the lack of a vehicle‐only or untreated control arm. This omission limits the ability to accurately assess the NPPC's relative efficacy.

Conclusion

A NPPC was developed for patients receiving skin rejuvenation procedures. This topical treatment was investigated with hybrid fractional (HFL) and fractional ablative (FACL) lasers to provide relief and recovery post‐procedure. For the HFL, both treatments demonstrated comparable outcomes with no statistical significance found at the end of the study. For the more intensive FACL, it is notable that although the healing time, erythema, pain, and pruritus results were similar in both treatment arms, the CAF was paired with a barrier OIN in a two‐step process to achieve similar results to the one‐step NPPC. Subject questionnaires demonstrated excellent tolerability and favorability with the NPPC, with a majority of patients expressing interest in returning for a second treatment. Overall, subjects reported a high degree of satisfaction and interest in further treatments with the NPPC. Both studies were limited to the small sample size and the lack of a vehicle‐control or untreated arm.
