Clinical Implementation and Validation of the Arabic Face‐Q Rhinoplasty Module
Authors: Hani Alanazi, Almaha A. Alqabbani, Badi F. Aldosari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimThe Face‐Q is a patient‐reported outcomes instrument with 43 independent scales; its rhinoplasty module measures patient satisfaction with nasal appearance and adverse effects following aesthetic rhinoplasty. However, no validated Arabic version exists for Middle Eastern populations. This study aimed to validate and culturally adapt the Arabic FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module following COSMIN guidelines.MethodsA prospective validation study was conducted at King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Riyadh, between November 2019 and March 2020. Fifty Arabic‐speaking patients (mean age 30.3 ± 6.8 years; 54% male) undergoing primary rhinoplasty completed the Arabic FACE‐Q preoperatively and 3 months postoperatively. We assessed structural validity (confirmatory factor analysis), convergent validity (Average Variance Extracted), discriminant validity (Heterotrait–Monotrait ratio), internal consistency (Cronbach's α, McDonald's ω), test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient), responsiveness (effect sizes), and measurement error (Standard Error of Measurement, Smallest Detectable Change).ResultsThe three‐factor structure was confirmed with acceptable fit (CFI = 0.924, TLI = 0.910, RMSEA = 0.081, SRMR = 0.087). The Arabic module demonstrated strong internal consistency (α: Nose = 0.77, Nostrils = 0.94, Adverse Effects = 0.77; ω: Nose = 0.933, Nostrils = 0.944, Adverse = 0.639) and good test–retest reliability (ICC: Nose = 0.74, Nostrils = 0.77, Adverse Effects = 0.71; all p < 0.001). Convergent validity was confirmed (AVE: Nose = 0.503, Nostrils = 0.809) with good discriminant validity (HTMT ratios < 0.60). Responsiveness analysis showed very large effect sizes (Nose r = 0.871, Nostrils r = 0.788) with significant improvements in satisfaction scores postoperatively (p < 0.001). Smallest Detectable Change values were 6.34 points (Nose) and 2.70 points (Nostrils). However, substantial ceiling effects were observed (Nose 48%, Nostrils 74%).ConclusionThe Arabic FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module demonstrates good structural validity, excellent reliability, and exceptional responsiveness to surgical change. Despite ceiling effects limiting detection of further improvements, the instrument is suitable for clinical and research use in Arabic‐speaking populations.

Introduction

The rhinoplasty procedure has been one of the most commonly performed aesthetic surgeries, worldwide, in the last decade [1]. Of note, the frequency of rhinoplasty has been increasing among the Saudi population, particularly among those aged between 19 and 34 years old [2].

The nasal anatomy and its subunits constitute the basis of facial analysis before and after rhinoplasty. Saudi and Middle Eastern populations share some nasal subunit features, such as an over‐projected osseocartilaginous vault and radix, a dorsal hump, thick skin, tip asymmetry, and nasal deviation [3].

Evaluation of postsurgical nasal appearance involves clinical outcome evaluation tools such as observer ratings of pre‐ and postoperative photographs. However, as appreciation of facial appearance is subjective, the assessment of patients' perspectives regarding their own facial appearance is essential. Therefore, several scales and outcome measures have been used to assess satisfaction levels among individuals who undergo cosmetic procedures [4]. The FACE‐Q is a patient‐reported outcomes instrument that consists of 43 independent scales and checklists. The FACE‐Q was recently validated for the assessment of facial appearance and social and psychological health among patients who undergo facial aesthetic surgery [5, 6, 7].

The Face‐Q rhinoplasty module was developed by Klassen in 2016 as a patient‐reported outcomes instrument specifically designed for aesthetic rhinoplasty, to assess adverse effects and patient satisfaction with nasal appearance and nostrils. The Face‐Q rhinoplasty module was developed by Klassen in 2016 to assess adverse effects and patient satisfaction when it comes to the appearance of the nose and the nostrils following rhinoplasty. In the Face‐Q rhinoplasty module, different aspects of the nasal appearance are analyzed, including the size of the nose and of the nostrils and overall shape and appearance. These assessments involve examination in the mirror and in photographs. Moreover, this tool allows patients to express their levels of satisfaction with individual nasal anatomical subunits. It also contains a checklist of adverse effects, including skin thickening, tenderness, difficulty in breathing, or unnatural appearance of the nose [8]. Thus, the FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module is a useful tool that measure post‐rhinoplasty adverse effects and patients' satisfaction before and after rhinoplasty. This, in turn, facilitates communication, decision‐making, and favorable surgical outcomes among patients undergoing rhinoplasty [7, 8]. Notably, the FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module was utilized in different countries and is in use in multiple languages including English, Finnish, French, Brazilian, Portuguese, Italian, and Norwegian [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. As there is no validated Arabic form of the FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module to assess patient satisfaction and adverse effects in aesthetic rhinoplasty among Arabic‐speaking populations, our study aimed to provide a validated and culturally adapted Arabic FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module for aesthetic surgery.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional validation study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board, College of Medicine, King Saud University (Reference: 20/0329/IRB). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Due permission was acquired from the original researcher who developed the original scale to undergo this study [8].

Measurement Properties Framework

This validation study followed COSMIN taxonomy, assessing: internal consistency (Cronbach's α, McDonald's ω), test–retest reliability (ICC), structural validity (CFA), convergent validity (AVE), discriminant validity (HTMT), responsiveness (pre–post comparisons with effect sizes), content validity (translation/adaptation process), and measurement error (SEM, SDC). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to evaluate the structural validity of the Arabic FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module following COSMIN guidelines. We tested the theoretical three‐factor model comprising Nose (10 items), Nostril (5 items), and Adverse Effects (4 items) domains using maximum likelihood estimation. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: χ 2 test (p > 0.05, though acknowledging its sensitivity to sample size), Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.90 acceptable, ≥ 0.95 good), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI ≥ 0.90 acceptable, ≥ 0.95 good), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA < 0.10 acceptable, < 0.06 good), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR < 0.10 acceptable, < 0.08 good) [15].

Convergent validity was assessed using Average Variance Extracted (AVE ≥ 0.50) [16] and composite reliability (McDonald's ω ≥ 0.60 for preliminary research) [17]. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the Heterotrait–Monotrait (HTMT) ratio, with values below 0.85 indicating good discriminant validity between factors [18]. Factor loadings were considered acceptable at ≥ 0.40 and good at ≥ 0.70. Modification indices above 10 were examined to identify potential model improvements.

Measurement error indices were calculated using postoperative data. Standard Error of Measurement (SEM) was calculated as SD × √(1 − ICC), where SD was derived from postoperative scores (n = 50) and ICC from postoperative test–retest reliability (n = 20). The Smallest Detectable Change (SDC) was calculated as 1.96 × √2 × SEM, representing the minimum change beyond measurement error at the 95% confidence level [19].

Study Questionnaire

The FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module is part of the FACE‐Q scale. It consists a set of validated questions divided into three parts to measure patient‐reported outcomes following rhinoplasty. The first section concerns patient satisfaction with the nose, which involves 10 items, indicating the degree to which patients are satisfied or dissatisfied with their appearance before and after rhinoplasty. The second section contains five items which aim at rating the level of patients' satisfaction and dissatisfaction with their nostrils. The third set of questions is designed to report any adverse events that patients may experience after rhinoplasty. Each satisfaction item uses a 4‐point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 2 = somewhat dissatisfied, 3 = somewhat satisfied, 4 = very satisfied). The nose satisfaction scale scores range from 10 to 40, nostril satisfaction scores range from 5 to 20. Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction with appearance.

Translation Process

The translation process was adapted according to the international translation guidelines and translation recommendations by Q‐Portfolio and [20, 21] hree bilingual rhinoplasty surgeons who are fluent in English and Arabic languages participated in the translation process. The original English FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module was forward translated into Arabic by two individuals. Then, the Arabic version was back‐translated into English by the third translator who did not review the original English version of the Face‐Q rhinoplasty module. After that, the English translated version was compared to the original version by two qualified professional translators who were familiar with American English and Arabic. The investigators were subsequently provided the opportunity to study and remark on the back translation. Following that, after acquiring written consent, pilot testing of the Arabic version was undertaken on ten Saudi patients who underwent rhinoplasty. After examining the pilot results, the Arabic version was modified in accordance with their suggestions (Figure 1).

Subjects and Study Setting

This cross‐sectional study was performed between November 2019 and September 2020 at King Abdulaziz University Hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. We invited 63 patients who underwent rhinoplasty by the same senior rhinoplasty surgeon to participate in our study. Fifty patients responded and were included in our study, and 13 patients were excluded. One week before the rhinoplasty surgery and in outpatient clinic, patients were asked to fill the Arabic Face‐Q rhinoplasty module. Also, patients were asked to complete the scale 3 months after rhinoplasty.

Our inclusion criteria were Arabic patients who were 18 years old and above, who underwent primary open rhinoplasty surgery for functional and cosmetic purposes, and had provided written consent. The exclusion criteria included all patients who refused to participate in the study, were not Arabic speakers, and were lost to follow‐up.

Data Analysis

The study data were not normally distributed, as per outcomes of the data exploration. Accordingly, nonparametric tests were used upon further statistical analysis. The Arabic Face‐Q module results were compared between groups using the paired samples Wilcoxon test. Statistical significance was defined as having a p < 0.05, with a confidence level of 95%. The internal consistency of the Arabic Face‐Q scale was measured using Cronbach's α. A score of 0.7–0.8 was considered “satisfactory,” that of 0.8–0.9 was considered “strong,” and a score of > 0.9 was considered “excellent.” The two‐way mixed‐effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) model was used to measure the test–retest reliability of the total Arabic Face‐Q score across domains. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare mean values of the Face‐Q outcomes between items of the questionnaire as well as between the different age groups. Effect sizes for pre–post comparisons were calculated using r = Z/√n from the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, with values of 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively. The responsiveness of the Arabic Face‐Q module was tested by comparing the scores of the participants' preoperative and postoperative results. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, Chicago, IL, USA) except for CFA where R v 4.3 was used.

Results

The study included 50 consecutive patients (27 males, 54%; 23 females, 46%) with a mean age of 30.3 ± 6.8 years (range: 16–47 years) who underwent primary open rhinoplasty between November 2019 and March 2020. The majority of patients (47/50, 94%) were medically fit without comorbidities, while 2 patients (4%) had controlled hypertension and 1 (2%) had systemic lupus erythematosus in remission. All procedures were performed by a single senior surgeon. The responsiveness of the Arabic Face‐Q rhinoplasty module was assessed by comparing the scores among the study groups. The study showed significant difference between the preoperative and postoperative results in all the 15 items of the nose and nostril domains of the Arabic version of the FACE‐Q (p < 0.001, Table 1). In addition, item‐level analysis revealed uniformly large effect sizes across all 15 items (r = 0.738–0.881), confirming that each aspect of nasal appearance measured by the Arabic FACE‐Q is highly responsive to rhinoplasty outcomes. Notably, there was good test–retest reliability in the nose domain (ICC = 0.74, p = 0.001), the nostrils domain (ICC = 0.77, p = 0.001), and the adverse events domain (ICC = 0.71, p = 0.001) of the Arabic FACE‐Q scale (Table 2).

The three‐factor CFA model (Figure 2) demonstrated acceptable fit to the data (χ 2 = 190.69, df = 144, p = 0.006; CFI = 0.924; TLI = 0.910; RMSEA = 0.081 [90% CI: 0.045–0.110]; SRMR = 0.087). While the χ 2 test was significant, likely due to our small sample size (n = 50), all other fit indices met acceptable thresholds, supporting the hypothesized three‐factor structure (Figure S1).

Standardized factor loadings for the Nose domain ranged from 0.432 to 0.843, with 8 of 10 items showing loadings above 0.60. Item 8 (“How your nose looks in photos”) had the lowest loading (0.432), while Items 4 (“How well your nose suits your face,” 0.838) and 7 (“The shape of your nose in profile,” 0.843) showed the strongest loadings. The Nostril domain demonstrated consistently strong loadings ranging from 0.791 to 0.980, with all items exceeding the 0.70 threshold. The Adverse Effects domain showed more variable loadings (0.315–0.874), with Item 2 (“Tenderness when wearing sunglasses”) showing the highest loading (0.874) and Item 3 (“Difficulty breathing through your nose”) showing the weakest (0.315).

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Convergent validity was supported by high composite reliability values (McDonald's ω) for the Nose (0.933) and Nostril (0.944) domains, both exceeding the 0.70 threshold. The Adverse Effects domain showed lower but acceptable reliability (0.639). Average Variance Extracted values were 0.503 for Nose and 0.809 for Nostril, both meeting or exceeding the 0.50 criterion for convergent validity. The Adverse Effects domain AVE was 0.374, below the threshold, suggesting some items may not strongly represent this construct. The Adverse Effects domain was retained as its reliability (ω = 0.64) exceeds the accepted threshold for scale validation (≥ 0.60 [15]), and it measures a clinically essential construct—postoperative complications—that is conceptually distinct from satisfaction measures and critical for comprehensive safety assessment, consistent with the original FACE‐Q structure enabling cross‐cultural comparisons. Discriminant validity was confirmed through HTMT analysis, with all ratios well below the 0.85 threshold. The inter‐factor correlations further supported this distinction, with the Nose and Nostril domains showing moderate positive correlation (r = 0.648, p = 0.002), while neither showed significant correlation with the Adverse Effects domain (Nose‐Adverse: r = −0.136, p = 0.411; Nostril‐Adverse: r = −0.158, p = 0.363).

Moreover, the Arabic version of the FACE‐Q module showed strong internal consistency in the nostrils domain of the scale with a Cronbach's α of 0.94, while there was satisfactory internal consistency in the nose and adverse effects domain of the scale. A Cronbach's α of 0.77 was achieved in both domains.

Measurement error analysis using postoperative data revealed acceptable precision across all domains (Table 3). The Nose domain demonstrated an SEM of 2.29 points with an SDC of 6.34 points, indicating that individual changes exceeding 6 points (21.1% of the scale range) can be considered beyond measurement error. The Nostril domain showed the best measurement precision with an SEM of 0.97 and SDC of 2.70 points (18.0% of scale range), reflecting its excellent reliability (ICC = 0.77).

Substantial ceiling effects were observed postoperatively, with 48.0% of patients achieving maximum satisfaction scores (40/40) in the Nose domain and 74.0% achieving maximum scores (20/20) in the Nostril domain. Conversely, the Adverse Effects domain showed a floor effect, with 38.0% of patients reporting minimal symptoms (score of 4), which represents the best possible outcome for this reverse‐scored domain. No ceiling effect was observed for adverse effects, indicating no patients reported maximum symptom severity postoperatively.

The coefficient of variation was low for both satisfaction domains (Nose: 6.2%, Nostril: 5.1%), indicating good relative precision. The Adverse Effects domain showed higher variability (CV = 18.4%), consistent with its lower reliability and the heterogeneous nature of postoperative symptoms. Discussion

The FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module was developed by Klassen in 2016. The aim of this tool is to specifically measure the esthetic and socio‐psychological aspects among rhinoplasty patients [8]. Our study showed that the Arabic version of the FACE‐Q module is reliable, as demonstrated by its good test–retest reliability, with an ICC > 0.7 for all domains. In addition, it had strong and satisfactory internal consistency in the nostrils domain of the scale with a Cronbach's α of 0.94 and a satisfactory level of internal consistency in the nose and adverse effects domains with a Cronbach's α of 0.77 therefore. In the original report by Klassen and colleagues, their results showed strong internal consistency with Cronbach's α level of 0.96 for the satisfaction with nostrils domain, which was similar to the findings in our study; however, on the nose scale, they had a higher internal consistency with a Cronbach's α level of 0.96 [8]. Our findings are also similar to the findings of previously translated versions of the FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. A report by Homsy and colleagues involving a larger sample of Finnish patients (n = 240) showed that the Finnish version of the FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module had a high internal consistency for most of the FACE‐Q domains, however for the adverse events domain, it had a poor internal consistency score [12].

In the Dutch FACE‐Q module, significant internal consistency between different questions in the nose and nostril questionnaires was noted. Further examination revealed a strongly significant test–retest reliability score and significant improvement between preoperative and postoperative patient satisfaction for the nose and nostrils domains [14].

Similarly, the French version of the module provided by Radulesco and colleagues showed high reliability in the nose and nostrils questions [11]. The Arabic FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module is able to differentiate between preoperative and postoperative patient satisfaction. The study showed a significant difference between preoperative and postoperative patient satisfaction in all questions of the nose and nostril domains (p < 0.001) which confirms the responsiveness of the Arabic Face‐Q. Comparable to the results of the original study by Klassen and colleagues, patient satisfaction with the nose and nostrils was also significantly higher after rhinoplasty (p < 0.001) [8]. Schwitzer and colleagues were the earliest to utilize the FACE‐Q module to evaluate postoperative patients' satisfaction with the overall facial appearance, nose appearance, and quality of life post‐rhinoplasty in their study. This study revealed a considerable improvement in pre‐ and postoperative FACE‐Q scores in terms of overall facial appearance and psychological function. In addition, the participants in the study by Schwitzer and colleagues demonstrated marked satisfaction in all of the FACE‐Q questions related to their nasal appearance following rhinoplasty [7]. Our study presented the first validated Arabic form of the FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module, which can be used among rhinoplasty patients in different Arab countries. In addition, this paper presents the first Arabic tool that assesses patients' self‐perception and satisfaction about different anatomical subunits of the nose and possible adverse effects after rhinoplasty.

Study Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. Our study was performed at a single center with a relatively small sample size. Also, patients were asked to complete the scale 3 months after rhinoplasty, which may not have been sufficient for adequate follow‐up of surgical outcomes. However, compared to the original study by Klessan and colleagues, our follow‐up period was in the range of the follow‐up period they used, which was 0.5–36 months postsurgery [8]. Furthermore, this study aimed to validate the Arabic version instead of assessing the rhinoplasty surgery results. Although written Arabic is well understood by almost all Arabic‐speaking populations, regardless of dialect or origin, future multicenter studies are recommended to confirm the validity of the Arabic Face‐Q rhinoplasty module for aesthetic rhinoplasty across different Arabic dialects. The sample size of 50 patients is at the lower boundary for CFA, which may affect parameter stability. The Adverse Effects domain showed suboptimal internal consistency and convergent validity, suggesting this subscale may need refinement for Arabic populations. Additionally, we could not assess criterion validity as no gold‐standard Arabic rhinoplasty PROM exists for comparison.

Conclusion

The Arabic version of the FACE‐Q rhinoplasty module has been proven as a valid tool with good reliability in assessing patients undergoing rhinoplasty procedures. It is a patient‐reported outcome tool that is used to evaluate adverse effects and patients' satisfaction with nasal appearance and nostrils after rhinoplasty. This tool can be included in clinical practice and research studies to measure patients' self‐perception of rhinoplasty outcomes. Future multicenter studies are recommended to confirm the validity of the Arabic Face‐Q rhinoplasty module across different dialects.
