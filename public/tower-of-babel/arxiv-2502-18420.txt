Trotter error and gate complexity of the SYK and sparse SYK models
Yiyuan Chen, Jonas Helsen, Maris Ozols

Abstract
The Sachdev–Ye–Kitaev (SYK) model is a prominent model of strongly interacting fermions that serves as a toy model of quantum gravity and black hole physics. In this work, we study the Trotter error and gate complexity of the quantum simulation of the SYK model using Lie–Trotter–Suzuki formulas. Building on recent results by Chen and Brandão [ 6 ] – in particular their uniform smoothing technique for random matrix polynomials – we derive bounds on the first- and higher-order Trotter error of the SYK model, and subsequently find near-optimal gate complexities for simulating these models using Lie–Trotter–Suzuki formulas. For the k k -local SYK model on n n Majorana fermions, at time t t , our gate complexity estimates for the first-order Lie–Trotter–Suzuki formula scales with 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n k + 5 2 ​ t 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{k+\frac{5}{2}}t^{2}) for even k k and 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n k + 3 ​ t 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{k+3}t^{2}) for odd k k , and the gate complexity of simulations using higher-order formulas scales with 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n k + 1 2 ​ t ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{k+\frac{1}{2}}t) for even k k and 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n k + 1 ​ t ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{k+1}t) for odd k k . Given that the SYK model has Θ ⁡ ( n k ) \Theta(n^{k}) terms, these estimates are close to optimal. These gate complexities can be further improved upon in the context of simulating the time evolution of an arbitrary fixed input state | ψ ⟩ \ket{\psi} , leading to a 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 ) \mathcal{O}(n^{2}) -reduction in gate complexity for first-order formulas and 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}) -reduction for higher-order formulas. We also apply our techniques to the sparse SYK model, which is a simplified variant of the SYK model obtained by deleting all but a Θ ⁡ ( n ) \Theta(n) fraction of the terms in a uniformly i.i.d. manner. We find the average (over the random term removal) gate complexity for simulating this model using higher-order formulas scales with 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n 1 + 1 2 ​ t ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{1+\frac{1}{2}}t) for even k k and 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n 2 ​ t ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}t) for odd k k . Similar to the full SYK model, we obtain a 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}) -reduction simulating the time evolution of an arbitrary fixed input state | ψ ⟩ \ket{\psi} . Our results highlight the potential of Lie–Trotter–Suzuki formulas for efficiently simulating the SYK and sparse SYK models, and our analytical methods can be naturally extended to other Gaussian random Hamiltonians.

Additionally, we study quantum simulation of the

. Moreover,

Previous work

The second case applies to random

G\approx\Gamma\norm{H}_{\mathrm{local},2}t.

(8)

Contrary to the previous case, this result does not require each local term
g_{i}K_{i}

g_{i}

[
6
]

However, Chen and Brandão’s results
[
6
]
are not directly applicable to the SYK model, because their theorems are derived in the context of local qubit Hamiltonians while the SYK models is a fermionic Hamiltonian. In particular, the Majorana fermions obey a different algebra than qubits. Contrary to qubits, Majorana fermions anti-commute when they do not collide. Mapping Majorana fermions to qubit operators (through e.g. the Jordan–Wigner transform) incurs inevitable locality overheads that make the bounds in
[
6
]
suboptimal. Still,
[
6
]
contains useful theorems and tools, such as uniform smoothness and Rademacher expansions, which apply in a broader context for operators as opposed to scalars. Using these results directly in the fermionic setting we can study the Trotter error of the SYK and the sparse SYK model.

There are other works studying the Trotter error of the SYK model. García-Álvarez et al.
[
11
]
gave a rough estimate of the gate complexity for simulating the SYK model with
k=4
using the first-order product formula. Their result estimated that the gate complexity scales as
\mathcal{O}(n^{10}t^{2})
. Asaduzzaman et al.
[
2
]
found an improved gate complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{5}t^{2})
by using a graph-coloring algorithm to minimize the number of commuting clusters of terms in the qubitized Hamiltonian, although this result seems to be formulated under a norm assumption we could not verify. Both of these results only consider the first-order product formula specifically applied to the SYK model with
k=4
. For the SYK model with general
k
, Xu, Susskind, Su, and Swingle
[
32
]
give a gate complexity estimate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2k-1+(k+1)/l}\log(n)t^{1+1/l})
for simulations using higher-order product formulas (where
l
is the degree of the Trotter formula). They have also given a gate complexity estimate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{1+1/l}\log(n)t^{1+1/l})
for simulating the sparse SYK model using higher-order product formulas. All the works above evaluate the complexity scaling under an average case statement, i.e. they compute the gate complexity for which the average Trotter error of the corresponding simulation is sufficiently small. In this work, we present an improved result in gate complexity using higher-order product formulas for both the SYK model and the sparse SYK model with general
k
, formulated under a more precise probability statement to characterize sufficiently small Trotter error.

Product formulas are not the only way to simulate the SYK model. For instance, asymmetric qubitization allows one to reach a gate complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{7/2}t+n^{5/2}t\cdot\mathrm{polylog}(n/\epsilon))

1.3
Summary of results for the sparse SYK model

We also extend our analysis to the sparse SYK model. The probabilistic removal of terms for obtaining the sparse SYK model can be equivalently achieved by attaching a Bernoulli variable
B_{\bm{i}}\in\{0,1\}
to each term of the SYK model, such that every
B_{\bm{i}}
takes the value 1 with a certain probability
p_{B}
and the value 0 with probability
1-p_{B}
. The value of
p_{B}
is tuned in such a way that on average, there are
\mathcal{O}(n)
terms in the sparse SYK model.

Due to the probabilistic removal, the number of remaining terms in the sparse SYK model depends on the specific sample of the Bernoulli variables. Hence, both the Trotter error and the corresponding gate complexity will also depend on the specific sample of the Bernoulli variables. Therefore, we study the sparse SYK model in the following setting. First, we treat the Bernoulli variables as coefficients and compute the Trotter error and the gate complexity as functions depending on these coefficients. Then, by averaging over these Bernoulli coefficients, we end up with average-case statements for both the Trotter error and gate complexity of the sparse SYK model.

We first state the following theorem bounding the average Trotter error:

\Big\langle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{D}(\bm{B})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\Big\rangle:=\Big\langle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e^{iH_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B})t}-S_{l}(t/r)^{r}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\Big\rangle,

(17)

Preliminaries

In this section, we formally describe the problem, provide sufficient mathematical background and introduce some key definitions and notation that will be used throughout.

2.1
The SYK and sparse SYK models

The
SYK model
is defined as a random
k
-local Majorana fermionic Hamiltonian
5
5

5

For qubit Hamiltonians the underlying
n
-qubit Hilbert space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
has tensor product structure, and a term is “
k
-local” if it acts as identity on all but
k
qubits. For fermionic Hamiltonians the underlying Hilbert space need not have such structure, and the notion of locality is different – it refers to the number of Majoranas involved in a given term.

H_{\text{SYK}}=i^{k(k-1)/2}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}J_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\chi_{i_{1}}\cdots\chi_{i_{k}},

(26)

consisting of (even)
n
Majorana fermions. The coefficients
J_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}
are i.i.d. Gaussian variables such that

\E(J_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}})=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\E(J_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}^{2})=\frac{(k-1)!\mathcal{J}^{2}}{kn^{k-1}}=:\sigma^{2}

(27)

with a certain energy constant
\mathcal{J}<\infty
.
The
\chi_{i}
’s are the Majorana fermions that obey the following anti-commutation relation:

\{\chi_{i},\chi_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}.

(28)

The
sparse SYK model
is a modified version of the SYK model proposed by Xu, Susskind, Su, and Swingle
[
32
]
, that requires fewer resources to simulate, but still captures several essential properties
[
32
,
26
]
. The model is obtained by removing each local interaction in the SYK model with probability
1-p_{B}
, where
p_{B}
is usually defined as

p_{B}=\frac{\kappa n}{\binom{n}{k}}

(29)

with a constant
\kappa\geq 0
(not to be confused with locality
k
) controlling the sparsity of the model. According to
[
32
,
26
]
, gravitational physics emerges for
\kappa
between
\frac{1}{4}
and 4 at low temperatures.

Removing the local terms in the SYK model is equivalent to attaching a Bernoulli variable
B_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}}\in\{0,1\}
to each term such that
\Prob(B_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}}=1)=p_{B}
. Hence, the sparse SYK model can be written as

H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}=i^{k(k-1)/2}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}B_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}}J_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}}\chi_{i_{1}}\cdots\chi_{i_{k}}.

(30)

To ensure that this model still has extensive energy, we renormalize the variance of the Gaussian couplings
J_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}
as

\sigma^{2}\longmapsto\frac{\sigma^{2}}{p_{B}},

(31)

to keep the model extensive.

2.2
Representation and norm of Majoranas

Evaluating the norm of the Trotter error of the SYK model inevitably requires evaluating the norm of the Majorana fermions
\chi_{i}
appearing in equation (
26
). For even
n
, the Majoranas
\chi_{1},\dotsc,\chi_{n}
can be understood as generators of the Clifford algebra
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n/2}).
However,
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n/2})
is inherently not a normed space. Hence, when computing the norm of any element of this algebra, we implicitly compute the norm of its representation in
\mathrm{GL}(2^{n/2},\mathbb{C}).
A concrete representation we have in mind here is the Jordan--Wigner transformation
6
6

6

One can choose another representation, as long as it satisfies the two properties in equation (
33
).

[
25
]

\rho_{\mathrm{JW}}:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n/2})\longrightarrow\mathrm{GL}(2^{n/2},\mathbb{C}).

(32)

It is a representation of
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n/2})
because it preserves anti-commutativity and normalization of the generators
\chi_{i}
:

\displaystyle\rho(\{\chi_{i},\chi_{j}\})

\displaystyle=\{\rho(\chi_{i}),\rho(\chi_{j})\},

\displaystyle\rho(\chi_{i})\rho(\chi_{i})

\displaystyle=I_{2^{n/2}}

(33)

for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
. From now on, when we write the norm
\norm{\chi}
for any
\chi\in\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n/2})
, we implicitly mean the norm
\norm{\rho_{\mathrm{JW}}(\chi)}
of its Jordan–Wigner representation. In other words, we have introduced a norm on
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n/2})
through a norm on
\mathrm{GL}(2^{n/2},\mathbb{C}).

2.3
Notation for local Hamiltonians

The summation indices of the SYK and sparse SYK models can be cumbersome when computing product formula approximations of these models. Hence, in this section we introduce an alternative way of indexing terms in a local Hamiltonian by assigning an arbitrary ordering to hyperedges so that we can index terms by integers instead of subsets.

Let
H
be a
k
-local Hamiltonian acting on
n
particles labeled by
V=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
. We can write
H
as

H=\sum_{\eta\in E}H_{\eta},

(34)

where
E\subseteq 2^{V}
is some set of size-
k
subsets of
V
, and each
H_{\eta}
is a local interaction on particles
\eta\subseteq V
.
This notation naturally induces a hypergraph
(V,E)
representing the local terms of
H.
The particles
V
are the vertices of the hypergraph, while
E
are the hyperedges. Each hyperedge denotes the particles on which the corresponding local interaction acts on. As we are using the hyperedges as labels for the local terms in the Hamiltonian, we denote the total number of local terms as
\Gamma:=|E|.

Throughout this paper, we will encounter situations where we need to partially sum over hyperedges in
E
, or sum over pairs of hyperedges in certain ordering. In those cases, the above notation can be cumbersome. To get a clearer overview for bookkeeping, we choose an arbitrary bijection

\phi:\{1,\dotsc,\Gamma\}\to E

(35)

to identify each hyperedge with a number (the explicit definition of
\phi
does not matter). Observe that
\phi
induces an ordering on
E
, such that for
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in E
,

\eta_{1}>\eta_{2}\iff\phi^{-1}(\eta_{1})>\phi^{-1}(\eta_{2}).

(36)

In certain situations, we need to repeatedly sum over
E
. Hence, we can periodically extend
\phi
to
\mathbb{N}
by considering the map

\gamma:\mathbb{N}\to E,\qquad\gamma(r+q\cdot\Gamma):=\phi(r)

(37)

with
1\leq r\leq\Gamma
and
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We will refer to this map
\gamma
as the
ordering map
.
For example, if
f:E^{4}\to\mathbb{C}
and
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, the ordering map allows us to write down sums such as

\sum_{i=1}^{3\Gamma}\sum_{j=i+1}^{4\Gamma}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,l=1\\
k+l\leq\Gamma\end{subarray}}^{\Gamma}f(H_{\gamma(g(i))},H_{\gamma(j)},H_{\gamma(g(k))},H_{\gamma(l)}),

(38)

which would be quite cumbersome if we used summation over hyperedges. More importantly, we can write the SYK model in this notation as

H_{\mathrm{SYK}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma}H_{\gamma(i)},

(39)

where
\gamma
is any ordering map of our choice. The hyperedge
\gamma(i)=\{\gamma(i)_{1},\dotsc,\gamma(i)_{k}\}\in E
denotes the Majorana fermions acted upon by the local term

H_{\gamma(i)}=i^{k(k-1)/2}J_{\gamma(i)}\left(\chi_{\gamma(i)_{1}}\cdots\chi_{\gamma(i)_{k}}\right)_{\text{lex}},

(40)

where the product of Majorana fermions is ordered lexicographically to match the ordering of indices in the original definition of the SYK model in equation (
26
).

2.4
Trotter error and product formulas

Simulating the SYK model on a quantum computer means implementing its time-evolution operator
e^{itH_{\mathrm{SYK}}}
for any choice of the random couplings.
In general, it is unclear how to exactly implement this matrix exponential, but there are several efficient approximation methods, one of them being product formulas. In essence, product formulas allow us to decompose an exponential of a sum of matrices into products of exponentials. Suppose
H
is a local qubit Hamiltonian defined as

H=\sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma}H_{\gamma(i)},

(41)

with a certain ordering map
\gamma
defined in equation (
37
).
Then we can decompose the unitary time evolution as a product plus some error

e^{iHt}=e^{iH_{\gamma(1)}t}e^{iH_{\gamma(2)}t}\cdots e^{iH_{\gamma(\Gamma)}t}+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}).

(42)

The product

S_{1}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{\Gamma}e^{itH_{\gamma(i)}}

(43)

is called the
first-order product formula
. The error term
\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
associated with this formula is called the
first-order Trotter error
.

We can also write down product formulas of higher orders – the most prominent family of examples being the
Lie–Trotter–Suzuki formulas

[
29
]
. The
second-order formula
is defined as

S_{2}(t)=\prod_{i=\Gamma}^{1}e^{iH_{\gamma(i)}t/2}\prod_{j=1}^{\Gamma}e^{iH_{\gamma(j)}t/2},

(44)

and
higher-order formulas
are defined recursively:

S_{2p}(t)=S_{2p-2}(q_{p}t)^{2}\cdot S_{2p-2}((1-4q_{p})t)\cdot S_{2p-2}(q_{p}t)^{2}

(45)

with
q_{p}=1/(4-4^{1/(2p-1)})
. This recursive structure only defines product formulas of even order. Therefore, when we refer to higher-order product formulas in this work, we only consider even orders. In general, an
l
-th order product formula has Trotter error of leading order
\mathcal{O}(t^{l+1}).

Another way to write down the
l
-th order product formula is to factor the recursive equation (
45
) into a product of
\Upsilon

stages
:

S_{l}(t)=\prod_{a=1}^{\Upsilon}\prod_{b=1}^{\Gamma}e^{ic(a,b)H_{\gamma(d(a,b))}t}

(46)

where
c(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}
and
d(a,b)
are chosen such that the above equals equation (
45
). By the recursive definition of
l
-th order product formulas, the number of stages is
\Upsilon=2\cdot 5^{\frac{l}{2}-1}
. We can write this even more compactly with
J=\Upsilon\cdot\Gamma
:

S_{l}(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}e^{ia_{j}H_{\gamma(b_{j})}t}

(47)

where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
b_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
are chosen such that the above expression is the same as equation (
45
).

In general, one uses
S_{l}(t/r)^{r}
to approximate the Hamiltonian, instead of just
S_{l}(t).
We refer to the
number of rounds

r
of applying the product formulas as
Trotter number
for short. For further reading on this topic, we refer the reader to
[
6
,
8
,
29
]
.

2.5
Probability statements and concentration inequalities

Because the SYK and sparse SYK models are random Hamiltonians, there are many ways to characterize what sufficiently small Trotter error means. The first one, appearing in
[
2
,
32
,
11
]
, is the average case statement

\E\left(\norm{e^{iHt} - S_l(t/r)^r}\right)<\epsilon,

(48)

where the expectation is over the randomness in
H
. Because this formulation of small Trotter error only covers the average case, we use the following two more precise statements given by
[
6
]
. The first one is stated using the operator norm:

\Prob\left(\norm{e^{iHt} - S_l(t/r)^r}\geq\epsilon\right)<\delta.

(49)

The other one quantifies small Trotter error by looking at the difference between the actual time-evolution of an arbitrary fixed input state
\ket{\psi}
and its approximated time evolution in
l^{2}
-norm:

\Prob\left(\norm{(e^{iHt} - S_l(t/r)^r)\ket{\psi}}_{l^{2}}\geq\epsilon\right)<\delta.

(50)

In addition to yielding more precise characterization of small Trotter error, these two probability statements also connect to the following two norms for random-valued operators
A
on a given finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}A\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}

\displaystyle:=\Big\lparen\E\big\lparen\norm{A}_{p}^{p}\big\rparen\Big\rparen^{\frac{1}{p}},

(51)

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}A\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{\mathrm{fix},p}

\displaystyle:=\sup\left\{\Big\lparen\E\big\lparen\norm{A\ket{\phi}}_{l^{2}}^{p}\big\rparen\Big\rparen^{\frac{1}{p}}:\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{H},\norm{\ket{\phi}}_{l^{2}}=1\right\}.

(52)

These norms obey certain uniform smoothness properties, which lead to better scaling in the Trotter error bounds and the corresponding gate complexities. Structuring our proofs around these properties ensures that the results that we derive in terms of
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\cdot\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}
also hold in terms of
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\cdot\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{\mathrm{fix},p}
. Hence, following the notation in
[
6
]
, we will introduce the notation
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\cdot\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}
7
7

7

The notation used in
[
6
]
is
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*}
which hides the

-dependence. Here, we will denote this dependence explicitly.
as a placeholder for both of these norms so that we can formulate our results more concisely. Whenever we state a result in the form

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}A\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\leq C\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}B\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

for

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\cdot\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}A\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}\leq C\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}B\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}A\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{\mathrm{fix},p}\leq C\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}B\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{\mathrm{fix},p}

Lemma 2.1

(Concentration inequality for operator norm)
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian on a Hilbert space. Let
l\in\{1\}\cup 2\mathbb{N}
be the order of the product formula approximating the Hamiltonian.
If the Trotter error of this product formula is bounded by a positively valued function
\lambda(p,r)
, in the sense that

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e^{iHt}-S_{l}(t/r)^{r}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}\leq p\lambda(p,r)\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}

(55)

for all
p\geq 2
, then choosing the Trotter number
r
so that it obeys the inequality

\frac{\lambda\left(\log(e^2 \opnorm{I}_p^p/\delta),r\right)}{\epsilon}\leq\frac{1}{e\cdot\log(e^2 \opnorm{I}_p^p/\delta)}

(56)

ensures that

\Prob\left(\norm{e^{iHt} - S_l(t/r)^r}\geq\epsilon\right)<\delta.

(57)

Lemma 2.2

(Concentration inequality for fixed input state)
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian on a Hilbert space of dimension
D
and
\ket{\psi}
an arbitrary fixed input state in this space. Let
l\in\{1\}\cup 2\mathbb{N}
be the order of the product formula approximating the Hamiltonian.
If the Trotter error of this product formula is bounded by a positively valued function
\lambda(p,r)
, in the sense that

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e^{iHt}-S_{l}(t/r)^{r}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{\mathrm{fix},p}\leq p\lambda(p,r)

(58)

for all

\frac{\lambda\left(\log(e^2/\delta),r\right)}{\epsilon}\leq\frac{1}{e\cdot\log(e^2/\delta)}

(59)

\Prob\left(\norm{(e^{iHt} - S_l(t/r)^r)\ket{\psi}}_{l^{2}}\geq\epsilon\right)<\delta.

(60)

The proof of these lemmas can be found in Appendix

2.6
Gate complexity

After finding the Trotter number
r
through the concentration inequalities (depending on the probability statement we choose), we can compute the number of local exponentials in the corresponding approximation
S_{l}(t/r)^{r}
as

C=\Upsilon\Gamma r,

(61)

where
\Upsilon
is the number of stages and
\Gamma
the number of local terms, see equation (
46
).
However,
C
is not the actual gate complexity
8
8

8

We defined gate complexity as the number of gates required to simulate the Hamiltonian such that the Trotter error is sufficiently small, as characterized by either equation (
49
) or (
50
).
of simulating a fermionic Hamiltonian. As we mentioned earlier, mapping fermions to qubits inevitably introduces extra overhead for the implementation of each local exponential. For instance, one could consider the standard Jordan–Wigner transform that gives each local exponential an
\mathcal{O}(n)
overhead. In that case, the actual gate complexity should scale with

G=\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\Upsilon\Gamma r).

(62)

Fortunately, using the ternary tree mapping
[
18
]
incurs only a
\log(n)
overhead for each local exponential, giving a total gate complexity that scales with

G=\mathcal{O}(\log(n)\cdot\Upsilon\Gamma r).

(63)

Because
\log(n)
is a slow-growing function, we opt to omit it when writing down the gate complexity. To distinguish from the actual gate complexity, our result will be reported as

G=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\Upsilon\Gamma r),

(64)

but the reader should always keep in mind the overhead from fermion-to-qubit mapping.

2.7
Error representation

We will need a more nuanced error representation to obtain better bounds on the Trotter error
\norm{e^{iHt} - S_l(t/r)^r}
.
Instead of measuring the error by a single number, we will consider an operator
\mathcal{E}(t)
that captures the deviation from the exact evolution
e^{iHt}
at any given time
t
.
More specifically, we will use the exponential form of the error from
[
8
,
6
]
to capture the deviation of the product formula (
47
) at any given
t
:

S_{l}(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}e^{ia_{j}H_{\gamma(b_{j})}t}=\mathrm{exp}_{\mathcal{T}}\left(i\int(\mathcal{E}(t)+H)dt\right),

(65)

where the right-hand side involves an ordered exponential. Hence, we can think of every product formula as the time evolution of our Hamiltonian
H
with some time-dependent error

added to it. We call
\mathcal{E}(t)
the

, and it takes the following form

,
8
]

\mathcal{E}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{J}e^{a_{k}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(b_{k})}t}[a_{j}H_{\gamma(b_{j})}]-a_{j}H_{\gamma(b_{j})}\right),

(66)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j)}
sends an operator

to the commutator with

:

\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j)}[O]:=i[H_{\gamma(j)},O].

(67)

This error generator will be our primary target when deriving bounds for the Trotter error.

2.8
Random matrix polynomials

A very useful method for the study of higher-order error generator is to view it as
random matrix polynomials
of local terms in the Hamiltonian. Hence, we introduce some tools for treating random matrix polynomials in this subsection.

We define a degree-
g
Gaussian random matrix polynomial of
m
matrix variables
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{m}
as

F(X_{m},\dotsc,X_{1})=\sum_{\bm{i}}T_{\bm{i}}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{g}}

(68)

where
\sum_{\bm{i}}
is the abbreviated notation for the summation

\sum_{\bm{i}}:=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{m}\dotsc\sum_{i_{g}=1}^{m},

(69)

and
T_{\bm{i}}:=T_{(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{g})}
are scalar coefficients. For notation here, we will consistently use bold text to denote vectors. In this case
\bm{j}
is the vector of the summation indices
(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{g}).
For simplicity, we pose that the variables
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{m}
are independent from each other, and each
X_{i}
can be written as

X_{i}=g_{i}K_{i}

(70)

with
g_{i}
a standard Gaussian variable and
K_{i}
a deterministic matrix with bounded norm
\norm{K_i}\leq\sigma_{i}
. We refer to the set that collects the subscripts of the random variables
F
depends on as the index set
\mathcal{I}
. In this case, we have

\mathcal{I}:=\{1,2,3,\dotsc,m\}=[m].

(71)

To study the concentration bounds on these polynomials,
[
6
, Proposition VII.2.1]
proposed the following (sum of squares) uniform smoothing inequality:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
F(X_{i_{m}},\dotsc,X_{i_{1}})
be a random matrix polynomial, with index set
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{m}\}
and each
X_{i}
is independent. Then, for
p\geq 2
and
C_{p}=p-1
,

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}F\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}^{2}\leq\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{I}}C_{p}^{|S|}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}[F]_{S}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}^{2}

(72)

where

[F]_{S}=\prod_{s\in S}(1-\mathbb{E}_{s})\prod_{s^{\prime}\in S^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}}[F],

(73)

with
\mathbb{E}_{s}
being the entry-wise expectation applied only on
X_{s}.
The notation
S^{c}
is the complement
\mathcal{I}\backslash S.

We refer to
[\cdots]_{S}
as the smoothing operator. This operator has a central part given by

(1-\mathbb{E})_{S}[\;\cdot\;]:=\prod_{s\in S}(1-\mathbb{E}_{s})[\;\cdot\;]

(74)

and an outer part given by

\mathbb{E}_{S^{c}}[\;\cdot\;]:=\prod_{s^{\prime}\in S^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}}[\;\cdot\;].

(75)

Using our notation, the smoothing operator could be written as

[\;\cdot\;]_{S}=(1-\mathbb{E})_{S}\circ\mathbb{E}_{S^{c}}[\;\cdot\;].

(76)

We would like to apply Theorem
2.3
to the random matrix polynomial
F
defined in equation (
68
). This requires us to identify, for each subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, all terms
T_{\bm{i}}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{g}}
in the polynomial
F
such that the corresponding smoothing operator is non-vanishing, i.e.

\left[T_{\bm{i}}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{g}}\right]_{S}\neq 0.

(77)

Notice that the Gaussian matrices
X_{i}
can appear with powers up to
g
in
F
, which makes them difficult to handle when applying the smoothing operator. To address this, we first expand each
X_{i}
into a product of Rademacher variables.

2.8.1
Rademacher expansion

Recall that the central limit theorem tells us that a standard Gaussian variable
g_{i}
can be written as the limit of a sum of i.i.d. Rademacher variables:

g_{i}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{i,j}}{\sqrt{N}},

(78)

where
\epsilon_{i,j}\in\{\pm 1\}
are Rademacher variables. Taking
N
to be a sufficiently large integer motivates us to express each
X_{i}
as a sum of Rademacher variables
Y_{i,j}
:

X_{i}\approx\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{i,j}}{\sqrt{N}}K_{i}=:\sum_{j=1}^{N}Y_{i,j}.

(79)

Under this expansion, we regard
F
as a polynomial in the variables

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=F(Y_{1,1},Y_{1,2},\dotsc,Y_{1,N},\dotsc,Y_{m,1},Y_{m,2},\dotsc,Y_{m,N})

(80)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\bm{i}}\sum_{\bm{j}}T_{(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{g})}Y_{i_{1},j_{1}}Y_{i_{2},j_{2}}\dotsc Y_{i_{g},j_{g}}.

Although this representation makes
F
appear more complicated, it offers a key simplification: the non-subleading terms of
F
in the large-
N
limit contain Rademacher variables with exponents only in
\{0,1,2\}
, rather than arbitrary powers up to
g
. This significantly simplifies the application of the smoothing operator. To illustrate this subleading argument, we consider the following example.

For the first non-trivial case
m=3
(the analysis for
m>3
is identical), we have

\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{N}\sum_{j_{2}=1}^{N}\sum_{j_{3}=1}^{N}Y_{1,j_{1}}Y_{1,j_{2}}Y_{1,j_{3}}.

(81)

How many terms of the form
Y_{1,j_{i}}^{3}
are present in this sum? The answer is
N
, corresponding to
j_{1}=j_{2}=j_{3}
. Summing over these terms gives

\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{N}Y_{1,j_{1}}^{3}=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{1,j_{1}}^{3}}{N^{3/2}}K_{1}^{3}.

(82)

Since the Rademacher variable
\epsilon^{3}_{1,j_{1}}
only takes values
\pm 1
, we obtain the inequality

\bigg|\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{1,j_{1}}^{3}}{N^{3/2}}\bigg|\leq\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N^{3/2}}.

(83)

Simplifying yields

\bigg|\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{N}\frac{\epsilon_{1,j_{1}}^{3}}{N^{3/2}}\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{N^{1/2}}.

(84)

Clearly, this expression vanishes as
N\to\infty
. Hence, each Rademacher variable in a non-subleading term of
F
, say
Y_{i,j_{i}}
, may only have exponent
0
,
1
, or
2
.

2.8.2
Resummation by subsets

While this provides useful restrictions on the exponents of the Rademacher variables, it also introduces some complications: the index set of
F
becomes
\mathcal{I}=[m]\times[N]
, rather than simply
[m]
, because we now regard
F
as a polynomial depending on the variables
Y_{1,1},\dotsc,Y_{m,N}
. Consequently, the subset
S
in the smoothing operator
[\cdots]_{S}
must now be taken from the Cartesian product
[m]\times[N]
. Given a subset
S\subseteq[m]\times[N]
, we can rewrite
F
as follows:

\displaystyle F=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x+y=g\\
x,y\in\{0,\dotsc,g\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s_{1},\dotsc,s_{x}\in S}\sum_{s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,s^{\prime}_{y}\in S^{c}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}\big/\mathcal{S}_{x}\times\mathcal{S}_{y}}T_{\pi\left[\bm{\pr}_{1}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{x},s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,s^{\prime}_{y})\right]}\pi\left[Y_{s_{1}}\cdots Y_{s_{x}}Y_{s^{\prime}_{1}}\dotsc Y_{s^{\prime}_{y}}\right].

(85)

Let us unpack the notation. Each term in
F
takes the form

\pi\left[Y_{s_{1}}\cdots Y_{s_{x}}Y_{s^{\prime}_{1}}\dotsc Y_{s^{\prime}_{y}}\right],

(86)

where
s_{1},\dotsc,s_{x}\in S
and
s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,s^{\prime}_{y}\in S^{c}
. The sums over
x
and
y
specify how many terms in the product
Y_{s_{1}}\cdots Y_{s_{x}}Y_{s^{\prime}_{1}}\dotsc Y_{s^{\prime}_{y}}
have subscripts in
S
and
S^{c}
, respectively. The permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}
reorders the positions of the Rademacher variables in the product to account for non-commutativity of the random variables. To avoid double counting, we exclude permutations that only reorder
Y_{s_{1}},\dotsc,Y_{s_{x}}
among themselves and/or
Y_{s^{\prime}_{1}},\dotsc,Y_{s^{\prime}_{y}}
among themselves, since such symmetries are already present in the summations over
s_{1},\dotsc,s_{x}
and
s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,s^{\prime}_{y}
. Thus, we sum over the quotient set
\mathcal{S}_{g}\big/\mathcal{S}_{x}\times\mathcal{S}_{y}
, which removes redundant symmetries from
\mathcal{S}_{g}
. We also extend the definition of the coefficients
T
so that they vanish whenever their subscript
\pi\left[\bm{\pr}_{1}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{x},s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,s^{\prime}_{y})\right]
does not correspond to a nonzero term in
F
.

To match the subscripts of coefficients
T_{(\cdots)}
, we introduce the projection from a direct product to the first set
\pr_{1}:A\times B\to A
defined by

\pr_{1}(i,j)=i\in A.

(87)

The bold symbol
\bm{\pr}_{1}
denotes a vector of projectors
(\pr_{1},\dotsc,\pr_{1})
with
g
entries. Hence,

\bm{\pr}_{1}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{x},s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,s^{\prime}_{y})=\left(\pr_{1}s_{1},\dotsc,\pr_{1}s_{x},\pr_{1}s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,\pr_{1}s^{\prime}_{y}\right).

(88)

Because we have separated the subscripts in parts that belong to
S
and parts that belong to
S^{c}
, the action of the smoothing operator is simply

[F]_{S}=\sum_{x+y=g}\sum_{s_{1},\dotsc,s_{x}\in S}\sum_{s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,s^{\prime}_{y}\in S^{c}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}\big/\mathcal{S}_{x}\times\mathcal{S}_{y}}T_{\pi\left[\bm{\pr}_{1}(s_{1},\dotsc,s_{x},s^{\prime}_{1},\dotsc,s^{\prime}_{y})\right]}\pi\left[(1-\mathbb{E})_{S}\Big[Y_{s_{1}}\cdots Y_{s_{x}}\Big]\mathbb{E}_{S^{c}}\Big[Y_{s^{\prime}_{1}}\dotsc Y_{s^{\prime}_{y}}\Big]\right]

(89)

Evaluating the above summations and combining it with Theorem
2.3
, we get the following result

Theorem 2.4
.

For
p\geq 2
and
C_{p}=p-1
,

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}F\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2}\leq g^{g}C_{p}^{g}\sum_{\bm{w}}\Bigg(\sum_{\bm{v}}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\left|T_{\pi\left[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w},2\bm{v})\right]}\right|\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}^{w_{i}+2v_{i}}\Bigg)^{2}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2},

(90)

where
\bm{w},\bm{v}\in\{0,\dotsc,g\}^{m}
are such that
|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g
.

9

9

If
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\dotsc,u_{m})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m}
is a vector then
|\bm{u}|:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|u_{i}|

The unary encoding

\bm{\eta}(\bm{a},\bm{b}):=(\underbrace{1,\dotsc,1}_{a_{1}\text{ times}},\underbrace{2,\dotsc,2}_{a_{2}\text{ times}},\dotsc,\underbrace{m,\dotsc,m}_{a_{m}\text{ times}},\underbrace{1,\dotsc,1}_{b_{1}\text{ times}},\underbrace{2,\dotsc,2}_{b_{2}\text{ times}},\dotsc,\underbrace{m,\dotsc,m}_{b_{m}\text{ times}})

Since the SYK model is Gaussian random, we will use this result to evaluate the expected Schatten norm of higher-order Trotter errors as we are going to see in the next section.

3

4
Higher-order Trotter error of the SYK model

In this section, we will extend our first-order Trotter error results from the Section
3
to higher-order Trotter error. As our main tool we will use the uniform smoothness Theorem
2.4
to evaluate the error generator as random matrix polynomials. Based on the error generator, we will then derive bounds for higher-order Trotter error, from which we will derive the gate complexity for simulating the SYK Hamiltonian using higher-order product formulas.

In equation (
65
) in Section
2.7
, we introduced a general exponential representation of the Trotter error, where we wrote the product formula as

\prod_{j=1}^{J}e^{ia_{j}H_{\gamma(b_{j})}t}=\mathrm{exp}_{\mathcal{T}}\left(i\int(\mathcal{E}(t)+H)dt\right),

(136)

\mathcal{E}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{J}e^{a_{k}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(b_{k})}t}[a_{j}H_{\gamma(b_{j})}]-a_{j}H_{\gamma(b_{j})}\right).

(137)

Here
\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j)}[O]:=i[H_{\gamma(j)},O]
, see equation (
67
), and
J=\Gamma\cdot\Upsilon
with
\Gamma
the number of terms in the
k
-local Hamiltonian and
\Upsilon
the number of stages of the product formula.

Following the approach in
[
6
]
, we can do a Taylor expansion on the error generator

H_{\gamma(1)},\dotsc,H_{\gamma(\Gamma)}
, which leads to

\mathcal{E}(t)=\sum_{g=l+1}^{g^{\prime}-1}\mathcal{E}_{g}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}(t),

(138)

where
g^{\prime}
is some cutoff and the sum starts at

l
-th order product formula is exact up to order

g
-th order error generator given by

\mathcal{E}_{g}(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{g_{J}+\dotsb+g_{j+1}=g-1}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(J)}^{g_{J}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j+1)}^{g_{j+1}}[H_{\gamma(j)}]\frac{t^{g-1}}{g_{J}!\dotsm g_{j+1}!},

(139)

and
\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{m=j+1}^{J}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(J)}t}\cdots e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m+1)}t}

(140)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}d\xi\sum_{g_{m}+\cdots+g_{j+1}=g^{\prime}-1,g_{m}\geq 1}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}\xi}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}^{g_{m}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j+1)}^{g_{j+1}}[H_{\gamma(j)}]\frac{(t-\xi)^{g_{m}-1}t^{g^{\prime}-g_{m}-1}}{(g_{m}-1)!g_{m-1}!\cdots g_{j+1}!}.

4.1
Error bound

We will proceed by separately evaluating the norms of

\Delta_{l}:=\frac{\mathcal{C}(l)\sqrt{p}\sigma t}{\sqrt{Q(n,k)}}\left(\binom{n}{k}\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}+\binom{n}{k}^{2}\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\right),

Proof.

When proving this result, we assume that

4.1.1

-th order error generator

In this subsection, we will upper bound

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}=\mathopen{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{g_{J}+\dotsb+g_{j+1}=g-1}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(J)}^{g_{J}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j+1)}^{g_{j+1}}[H_{\gamma(j)}]\frac{t^{g-1}}{g_{J}!\dotsm g_{j+1}!}\mathclose{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}_{*,p}

As the summation indices are difficult to work with here, we consider the following symmetrization:

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}=t^{g-1}\mathopen{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}\sum_{j_{g-1}=1}^{J}\cdots\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J}\tilde{\theta}_{\bm{j}}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j_{g-1})}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j_{1})}[H_{\gamma(j_{0})}]\mathclose{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}_{*,p},

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}F_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2}\leq C_{p}^{g}g^{g}\sum_{\bm{w}:|\bm{w}|\leq g}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\sum_{\bm{v}:|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g}\left|T_{\pi[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w},2\bm{v})]}\right|\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sigma^{w_{i}+2v_{i}}\right)^{2}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2},

(156)

where
\bm{w},\bm{v}\in\{0,\dotsc,g\}^{\Gamma}
such that
|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g
. The coefficients
T_{\pi[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w},2\bm{v})]}
are inherited from the corresponding
T_{\bm{j}}
in equation (
151
) by replacing the vector
\bm{j}
with
\pi[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w},2\bm{v})]
where
\bm{\eta}
is the unary encoding function defined in equation (
91
). By the bound in equation (
152
),

\left|T_{\pi[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w}+2\bm{v})]}\right|\leq g^{g}\ind(\pi[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w},2\bm{v})]),

(157)

where the indicator
\ind(\bm{a})
is defined in equation (
153
) on all vectors
\bm{a}\in[\Gamma]^{g}
.
Since
|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g
by construction,
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\sigma^{w_{i}+2v_{i}}=\sigma^{g}
and hence equation (
156
) simplifies to

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}F_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2}\leq C_{p}^{g}g^{3g}\sigma^{2g}\sum_{\bm{w}:|\bm{w}|\leq g}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\sum_{\bm{v}:|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g}\ind(\pi[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w},2\bm{v})])\right)^{2}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2}.

(158)

Let us split the sum over
\bm{w}
as

\sum_{\bm{w}:|\bm{w}|\leq g}=\sum_{w=0}^{g}\sum_{\bm{w}:|\bm{w}|=w}

(159)

and rewrite equation (
158
) as

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}F_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2}\leq C_{p}^{g}g^{3g}\sigma^{2g}\sum_{w=0}^{g}G_{w}(H)\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2}

(160)

where

G_{w}(H):=\sum_{\bm{w}:|\bm{w}|=w}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\sum_{\bm{v}:|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g}\ind(\pi[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w},2\bm{v})])\right)^{2}.

(161)

We can then fix a
w\in\{0,\dotsc,g\}
and focus on upper bounding
G_{w}(H)
. We can do this using the following lemma:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
H=\sum_{i=1}^{m}H_{\gamma(i)}
be a Hamiltonian of
m
terms with arbitrary ordering
\gamma
, such that for all
1\leq i<j\leq m
,
H_{\gamma(i)}
and
H_{\gamma(j)}
either commute or anti-commute. Fix
g\geq 1
and
w\in\{0,\dotsc,g\}
, and consider the
H
-dependent sum

G_{w}(H):=\sum_{\bm{w}:|\bm{w}|=w}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}}\sum_{\bm{v}:|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g}\ind(\pi[\bm{\eta}(\bm{w},2\bm{v})])\right)^{2},

(162)

where
\bm{w},\bm{v}\in\{0,\dotsc,g\}^{m}
such that
|\bm{w}|=w
and
|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g
. The permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}
reorders the entries of vectors in
[m]^{g}
. The unary encoding
\bm{\eta}
and the indicator
\ind
are defined on vectors
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\{0,\dotsc,g\}^{m}
and
\bm{j}\in[m]^{g}
as follows:

\bm{\eta}(\bm{a},\bm{b}):=(\underbrace{1,\dotsc,1}_{a_{1}\text{ times}},\underbrace{2,\dotsc,2}_{a_{2}\text{ times}},\dotsc,\underbrace{m,\dotsc,m}_{a_{m}\text{ times}},\underbrace{1,\dotsc,1}_{b_{1}\text{ times}},\underbrace{2,\dotsc,2}_{b_{2}\text{ times}},\dotsc,\underbrace{m,\dotsc,m}_{b_{m}\text{ times}}),

(163)

and

\ind(\bm{j}):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j_{g})}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j_{2})}[H_{\gamma(j_{1})}]\neq 0,\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(164)

Then

G_{w}(H)\leq g^{3g-2}m^{2}Q_{\max}(H)^{g-2}

(165)

where

Q_{\max}(H):=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\left|\left\{j\in[m]:[H_{\gamma(j)},H_{\gamma(i)}]\neq 0\right\}\right|.

(166)

The proof of this lemma can be found in Appendix
D
. For the SYK model, we have
m=\binom{n}{k}
and

Q_{\max}(H)=Q(n,k)
.
Hence, by Lemma
4.2

G_{w}(H)\leq g^{3g-2}\binom{n}{k}^{2}Q(n,k)^{g-2}.

(167)

Plugging this back into equation (
160
) gives us

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}F_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2}\leq C_{p}^{g}g^{3g}\sigma^{2g}\cdot(g+1)\cdot g^{3g-2}\binom{n}{k}^{2}Q(n,k)^{g-2}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}^{2}

(168)

where the factor
g+1
comes from the sum over
w
.
Taking the square root of both sides we get

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}F_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\leq C_{p}^{\frac{g}{2}}(g+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{3g-1}\sigma^{g}\binom{n}{k}Q(n,k)^{\frac{g}{2}-1}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}.

(169)

Plugging this back into equation (
145
) gives us

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\leq\Upsilon^{g}t^{g-1}C_{p}^{\frac{g}{2}}(g+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{3g-1}\sigma^{g}\binom{n}{k}Q(n,k)^{\frac{g}{2}-1}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}.

(170)

4.1.2
Beyond
g
-th order

Next, we want to upper bound the residual error term defined in equation (
140
):

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}=\mathopen{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{m=j+1}^{J}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(J)}t}\cdots e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m+1)}t}

(171)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}d\xi\sum_{g_{m}+\cdots+g_{j+1}=g^{\prime}-1,g_{m}\geq 1}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}\xi}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}^{g_{m}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j+1)}^{g_{j+1}}[H_{\gamma(j)}]\frac{(t-\xi)^{g_{m}-1}t^{g^{\prime}-g_{m}-1}}{(g_{m}-1)!g_{m-1}!\cdots g_{j+1}!}\mathclose{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}_{*,p}.

We would first like to remove the exponentials inside the expected Schatten norm. This is hindered by the fact that they depend on the summation index
m
. The workaround is to replace the triangular double summation by

\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{m=j+1}^{J}\mapsto\sum_{m=2}^{J}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}.

(172)

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}=\mathopen{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}

\displaystyle\sum_{m=2}^{J}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(J)}t}\cdots e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m+1)}t}

(173)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}d\xi\sum_{g_{m}+\cdots+g_{j+1}=g^{\prime}-1,g_{m}\geq 1}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}\xi}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}^{g_{m}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j+1)}^{g_{j+1}}[H_{\gamma(j)}]\frac{(t-\xi)^{g_{m}-1}t^{g^{\prime}-g_{m}-1}}{(g_{m}-1)!g_{m-1}!\cdots g_{j+1}!}\mathclose{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}_{*,p}.

By triangle inequality (and taking the integral outside the sum),

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\leq\sum_{m=2}^{J}\int_{0}^{t}d\xi\mathopen{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(J)}t}\cdots e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m+1)}t}

(174)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{g_{m}+\cdots+g_{j+1}=g^{\prime}-1,g_{m}\geq 1}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}\xi}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}^{g_{m}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j+1)}^{g_{j+1}}[H_{\gamma(j)}]\frac{(t-\xi)^{g_{m}-1}t^{g^{\prime}-g_{m}-1}}{(g_{m}-1)!g_{m-1}!\cdots g_{j+1}!}\mathclose{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}_{*,p}.

Since the exponentials inside the expected Schatten norm do not dependent on the summations over
j
and
g_{i}
’s, we can pull them in front of both sums and then by unitary invariance of the norm remove altogether:

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=2}^{J}\int_{0}^{t}d\xi\mathopen{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\sum_{g_{m}+\cdots+g_{j+1}=g^{\prime}-1,g_{m}\geq 1}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(m)}^{g_{m}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j+1)}^{g_{j+1}}[H_{\gamma(j)}]\frac{(t-\xi)^{g_{m}-1}t^{g^{\prime}-g_{m}-1}}{(g_{m}-1)!g_{m-1}!\cdots g_{j+1}!}\mathclose{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}_{*,p}.

(175)

Consider the same type of symmetrization as in the previous section:

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=2}^{J}\int_{0}^{t}d\xi\mathopen{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}\sum_{j_{g^{\prime}-1}=1}^{J}\cdots\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{J}\tilde{\theta}_{\bm{j}}\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j_{g^{\prime}-1})}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{\gamma(j_{1})}[H_{\gamma(j_{0})}]\mathclose{\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|\mkern-1.5mu\Bigg|}_{*,p},

(176)

where we have absorbed
\frac{(t-\xi)^{g_{m}-1}t^{g^{\prime}-g_{m}-1}}{(g_{m}-1)!g_{m-1}!\cdots g_{j+1}!}
into the coefficients
\tilde{\theta}_{\bm{j}}
, which are set to zero as needed to reduce this sum to the previous one. Compared to the previous section where
\tilde{\theta}_{\bm{j}}\in[0,1]
, the coefficients
\tilde{\theta}_{\bm{j}}
are now bounded by

|\tilde{\theta}_{\bm{j}}|\leq t^{g^{\prime}-2}

(177)

due to the extra factor of
(t-\xi)^{g_{m}-1}t^{g^{\prime}-g_{m}-1}
in their definition.

Similar to our strategy for the
g
-th order terms in equation (
147
), we can write the sums inside the norm in equation (
176
) as a random matrix polynomial with coefficients
T_{\bm{j}}
:

F_{g^{\prime}}(H_{\gamma(\Gamma)},\dotsc,H_{\gamma(1)}):=\sum_{j_{g^{\prime}-1}=1}^{\Gamma}\cdots\sum_{j_{0}=1}^{\Gamma}T_{\bm{j}}H_{\gamma(j_{g^{\prime}-1})}\cdots H_{\gamma(j_{0})}

(178)

where the coefficients
T_{\bm{j}}
are now bounded by

|T_{\bm{j}}|\leq(g^{\prime})^{g^{\prime}}t^{g^{\prime}-1}\ind(\bm{j}),

(179)

where the indicator function
\ind(\bm{j})
is defined for all vectors
\bm{j}\in[J]^{g^{\prime}}
in the same way as in equation (
153
).
By the same argument as in the previous section, we get the following bound that follows directly from equation (
169
) with the updated upper bound of the coefficients
T_{\bm{j}}
in equation (
179
):

\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}F_{g^{\prime}}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\leq C_{p}^{\frac{g^{\prime}}{2}}(g^{\prime}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}(g^{\prime})^{3g^{\prime}-1}\sigma^{g^{\prime}}\binom{n}{k}Q(n,k)^{\frac{g^{\prime}}{2}-1}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}.

(180)

Plug this back into equation (
175
) (the integral over
\xi
simply becomes a factor
t
), we get

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

\displaystyle\leq\Upsilon^{g^{\prime}+1}t^{g^{\prime}-1}C_{p}^{\frac{g^{\prime}}{2}}(g^{\prime}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}(g^{\prime})^{3g^{\prime}-1}\sigma^{g^{\prime}}\binom{n}{k}^{2}Q(n,k)^{\frac{g^{\prime}}{2}-1}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}.

(181)

4.1.3
Total error

Now, we can finally put everything together. For an
l
-th order
\Upsilon
-stage product formula, the norm of the error generator
\mathcal{E}
of the SYK model can be bounded by applying the triangle inequality in equation (
138
) and then using the bounds from equations (
170
) and (
181
):

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{g=l+1}^{g^{\prime}-1}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{g}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}+\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}_{\geq g^{\prime}}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

(182)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\sum_{g=l+1}^{g^{\prime}-1}\Upsilon^{g}t^{g-1}C_{p}^{\frac{g}{2}}(g+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{3g-1}\sigma^{g}\binom{n}{k}Q(n,k)^{\frac{g}{2}-1}

(183)

\displaystyle+\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\Upsilon^{g^{\prime}+1}t^{g^{\prime}-1}C_{p}^{\frac{g^{\prime}}{2}}(g^{\prime}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}(g^{\prime})^{3g^{\prime}-1}\sigma^{g^{\prime}}\binom{n}{k}^{2}Q(n,k)^{\frac{g^{\prime}}{2}-1}.

For
g^{\prime}=l+2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\mathcal{A}(l)\left(t^{l}\left[\sqrt{C_{p}}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\right]^{l+1}\binom{n}{k}Q(n,k)^{-1}+t^{l+1}\left[\sqrt{C_{p}}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\right]^{l+2}\binom{n}{k}^{2}Q(n,k)^{-1}\right),

(184)

where
\mathcal{A}(l)=\Upsilon^{l+3}(l+3)^{\frac{1}{2}}(l+2)^{3(l+2)-1}.

As we divide the total evolution time
t
into
r
rounds, with each round having duration
\frac{t}{r}
, the error per round can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e^{iHt/r}-S_{l}(t/r)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\frac{t}{r}}\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{E}(\tau)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}d\tau

(185)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\frac{\mathcal{A}(l)}{l+1}\left(\frac{t^{l+1}}{r^{l+1}}\left[\sqrt{C_{p}}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\right]^{l+1}\binom{n}{k}Q(n,k)^{-1}\right.

(186)

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\quad\;{}+\frac{t^{l+2}}{r^{l+2}}\left[\sqrt{C_{p}}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\right]^{l+2}\binom{n}{k}^{2}Q(n,k)^{-1}\right).

Using
C_{p}<p
, the total error is then bounded as

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e^{iHt}-S_{l}(t/r)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

\displaystyle\leq r\cdot\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e^{iHt/r}-S_{l}(t/r)\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}

(187)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\mathcal{C}(l)\sqrt{p}\sigma Q(n,k)^{-\frac{1}{2}}t

(188)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\left(\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}\binom{n}{k}+\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\binom{n}{k}^{2}\right)

with
\mathcal{C}(l)=\frac{\mathcal{A}(l)}{l+1}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

4.2
Gate complexity

Corollary 4.2.1
.

Simulating a
k
-local SYK model with (even)
l
-th order product formula, the gate complexity

G=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left[n^{g_{1}(k)}\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\mathcal{J}t\cdot\left(\left[n^{g_{2}(k)}\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{J}t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}+\left[n^{g_{3}(k)}\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{J}t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\right)\right],

(189)

where

\left(g_{1}(k),\;g_{2}(k),\;g_{3}(k)\right):=\begin{cases}\left(k,\;1,\;k+1\right)&\text{if $k$ is even},\\
\left(k+\frac{1}{2},\;\frac{1}{2},\;k+\frac{1}{2}\right)&\text{if $k$ is odd},\end{cases}

(190)

ensures the probability statement

\Prob\left(\norm{e^{iHt} - S_l(t/r)^r}\geq\epsilon\right)<\delta.

(191)

When
l
is sufficiently large, this gate complexity scales in
n
as

G_{l\gg 2}\sim\begin{cases}n^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{J}t&\text{if $k$ is even},\\
n^{k+1}\mathcal{J}t&\text{if $k$ is odd}.\end{cases}

(192)

Proof.

To derive the
l
-th order complexity considering the probability statement in (
49
), we first bound the
l
-th Trotter error using Theorem
4.1
:

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e^{iHt}-S_{l}(t/r)^{r}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}\cdot p\cdot\lambda(p,r),

(193)

where

\lambda(p,r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\mathcal{C}(l)\sigma Q(n,k)^{-\frac{1}{2}}t\left(\binom{n}{k}\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}+\binom{n}{k}^{2}\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\right).

(194)

This gives us an explicit expression for the concentration inequality (
55
). By Lemma
2.1
, finding the Trotter number
r
that ensures the probability statement (
49
) is equivalent to finding an
r
that solves the inequality

e\sqrt{p_{D}}\mathcal{C}(l)\sigma Q(n,k)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{t}{\epsilon}\left(\binom{n}{k}\left[\sqrt{p_{D}}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}+\binom{n}{k}^{2}\left[\sqrt{p_{D}}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\right)\leq 1,

(195)

where
p_{D}:=\log(e^2D/\delta)
with
D:=\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}^{p}.

Hence,

\displaystyle r\geq\sqrt{\log(e^2D/\delta)}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}t

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\left[2e\sqrt{\log(e^2D/\delta)}\mathcal{C}(l)\binom{n}{k}\sigma Q(n,k)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}\right.

(196)

\displaystyle\;+\left.\left[2e\sqrt{\log(e^2D/\delta)}\mathcal{C}(l)\binom{n}{k}^{2}\sigma Q(n,k)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\right).

Recall that the norm
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}
should be interpreted as the Schatten
p
-norm on
\mathrm{GL}(n/2,\mathbb{C})
because we have (even)
n
Majoranas. Hence,
D=\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}^{p}=2^{\frac{n}{2}}.
Substituting
D=2^{n/2}
and multiply
r
with the number of local terms
\Gamma
and the number of stages
\Upsilon=2\cdot 5^{\frac{l}{2}-1}
, the
l
-th order gate complexity scales with

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\Upsilon\sqrt{\frac{\log(2)}{2}n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\sigma\binom{n}{k}\sqrt{Q(n,k)}t\cdot\left(\left[2e\sqrt{\frac{\log(2)}{2}n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\mathcal{C}(l)\binom{n}{k}\sigma Q(n,k)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}\right.\right.

(197)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\;+\left.\left.\left[2e\sqrt{\frac{\log(2)}{2}n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\mathcal{C}(l)\binom{n}{k}^{2}\sigma Q(n,k)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\right)\right).

For fixed
k
and
l
, the
k
and
l
dependent factors can be removed from the big-
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
notation. Asymptotically, we have
\binom{n}{k}=\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
, and

for odd

(198)

where
g_{1}(k)
,
g_{2}(k)
,

are defined in equation (

).
This concludes the proof.

where

ensures the probability statement

5
Higher-order Trotter error of the sparsified SYK model

Our methods for evaluating higher-order Trotter errors also work in the case of the
sparse SYK model
which is defined as the regular SYK model (
26
) but with an additional Bernoulli variable
B_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}}\in\{0,1\}
attached to each term:

H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}B_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}}J_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{k}}\chi_{i_{1}}\cdots\chi_{i_{k}}.

(203)

Similar to our treatment for the regular SYK model in equation (
39
), we write the sparse SYK model in the local Hamiltonian form with an arbitrary ordering
\gamma

H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma}B_{i}H_{\gamma(i)}.

(204)

The sparse SYK model has two sources of randomness, one from the Gaussian variables
J_{\gamma(i)}
within
H_{\gamma(i)}
as in the regular SYK model, the other from the Bernoulli variables
B_{i}
. We pose that the Bernoulli variables are independent of each other and also independent of the Gaussian variables
J_{\gamma(i)}
. For simplicity, each Bernoulli variable
B_{i}
is equal to
1
with probability
p_{B}
and equal to
0
with probability
1-p_{B}
for some
p_{B}\in[0,1]
. Usually, we set

p_{B}=\frac{\kappa n}{\Gamma}

(205)

where
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the degree of sparsity, and relative to equation (
27
) we renormalize the variance of the Gaussian variables
J_{\gamma(i)}
to

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{p_{B}}\frac{(k-1)!\mathcal{J}^{2}}{kn^{k-1}}.

(206)

When evaluating the expected Schatten norm of the Trotter error of the sparse SYK model, we will deal with these two types of randomness separately. We will write
\bm{B}
to denote the Bernoulli variables
B_{1},\dotsc,B_{\Gamma}
. When we want to specify that this series of random variables takes a certain configuration
\bm{b}
, we will write
\bm{B}=\bm{b}
where
\bm{b}
is a fixed vector in
\{0,1\}^{\Gamma}
. This is equivalent to
B_{1}=b_{1},

B_{2}=b_{2}
,
\dotsc,

B_{\Gamma}=b_{\Gamma}.

Using this notation, we can write the Trotter error given a specific sample
\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{\Gamma}
as

\mathcal{D}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}):=e^{iH_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b})t}-S_{l}(t/r)^{r},

(207)

indicating that the Trotter error depends on the specific sample
\bm{b}
of the Bernoulli variables.

5.1
Average Trotter error

Let
b:=|\bm{b}|=\sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma}b_{i}
denote the number of ones in the sample
\bm{b}
of the Bernoulli variables
\bm{B}
. When the Bernoulli variables take the value of this sample, we get a configuration of the sparse SYK model where we treat the sample values as coefficients:

H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b})=\sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma}b_{i}H_{\gamma(i)},

(208)

which contains exactly
b
terms. Of course, this configuration is still a
k
-local Hamiltonian, although it becomes more difficult to count the anti-commuting terms, since the all-to-all connected symmetry in the full SYK model is broken. Therefore, we have to resort to average statements about both the Trotter error and gate complexity of these sparse models. The following theorem bounds the Trotter error averaged over the Bernoulli variables, defined as

G_{w}(H)

G_{w}(H)\leq g^{3g-2}m^{2}Q_{\max}(H)^{g-2}

(237)

in Lemma
4.2
. Since we perform the sparsification by attaching i
.
i
.
d
.
Bernoulli variables to the summands of the Hamiltonian, and the average of each Bernoulli variable is
p_{B}
, we could expect the average
\Big\langle G_{w}(H(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle
to scale with some polynomial (of utmost degree
g
)

\Big\langle G_{w}(H(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle\sim\sum_{s}a_{s}(p_{B}Q_{\max}(H))^{s}.

(238)

To illustrate this, let us naively forget the sum of squares in
G_{w}
and pretend it scales with the following sum of commutator chains (each commutator chain has weight 1):

G_{w}(H(\bm{B}))\sim\sum_{\pi}\sum_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{g}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{g}B_{i_{j}}\right)\ind(\pi(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{g})).

(239)

If we take the expectation now, we get

\Big\langle G_{w}(H(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle\sim\sum_{\pi}\sum_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{g}}\Big\langle\prod_{j=1}^{g}B_{i_{j}}\Big\rangle\ind(\pi(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{g})).

(240)

Note that the average of the product
\prod_{j=1}^{g}B_{i}
is not
p_{B}^{g}
, because we do not know how many distinct elements there are in the product. Therefore, we need to write our summation more carefully to control the distinct elements:

G_{w}(H(\bm{B}))\sim\sum_{\pi}\sum_{s=1}^{g}\sum_{\phi:\text{ surj}}\sum_{\text{distinct }i_{1},\dotsc,i_{s}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{s}B_{i_{j}}\right)\ind(\pi(i_{\phi(1)},\dotsc,i_{\phi(g)})),

(241)

where we now sum over
s
distinct elements
i_{1},\dotsc,i_{s}
. We use surjective functions
\phi:\{1,\dotsc,g\}\to\{1,\dotsc,s\}
to distribute
i_{1},\dotsc,i_{s}
over the indicator. In this way, the average becomes:

\Big\langle G_{w}(H(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle\sim\sum_{\pi}\sum_{s=1}^{g}\sum_{\phi:\text{ surj}}\sum_{\text{distinct }i_{1},\dotsc,i_{s}}p_{B}^{s}\ind(\pi(i_{\phi(1)},\dotsc,i_{\phi(g)})),

(242)

The right-hand side is upper-bounded by

\sum_{\pi}\sum_{s=1}^{g}\sum_{\phi:\text{ surj}}\sum_{\text{distinct }i_{1},\dotsc,i_{s}}p_{B}^{s}\ind(\pi(i_{\phi(1)},\dotsc,i_{\phi(g)}))\leq g!\cdot g^{g}mQ_{\max}(H)^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{g}(p_{B}Q_{\max}(H))^{s}

(243)

where
m
denotes the total number of terms in the Hamiltonian. We use
g!
to bound the number of permutations
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{g}
and
g^{g}
to bound the number of surjective functions from
\{1,\dotsc,g\}\to\{1,\dotsc,s\}
. The above bound in equation (

) is indeed a polynomial as expected, and resembles the bound in Lemma
5.2
apart from additional sums there. In reality, we cannot simply ignore the sum of squares in
G_{w}
as it plays a crucial role in capturing the scaling behavior of the Trotter error. To get the correct bound, one needs to expand the sum of squares and treat the overlapping summations carefully. (This is taken care of by the additional summations over
x
,
q
and
q^{\prime}
in Lemma
5.2
.) For details of this treatment, we refer the reader to the proof in Appendix
E
.

To apply Lemma
5.2
, let us first put expectations on both side of equation (
230
) and apply Jensen’s inequality:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{D}(\bm{B})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\Big\rangle\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\alpha(l)\sqrt{p}\sigma t\cdot\Bigg(

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+1}\Big\langle G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle}

(244)

\displaystyle+\Gamma\cdot

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}\Big\langle G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle}\Bigg).

For the SYK model, we have
Q_{\max}=Q(n,k)
, and therefore, by Lemma
5.2
, we get

\displaystyle\Big\langle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{D}(\bm{B})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\Big\rangle\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\alpha^{\prime}(l)\frac{\Gamma\sqrt{p}\sigma t}{Q(n,k)}\cdot\Bigg(

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+1}\sum_{s=0}^{w}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}=0}^{(l+1-w)/2}\sum_{c=0}^{\min(q,q^{\prime})}\left(p_{B}Q(n,k)\right)^{s+q+q^{\prime}-c}}

(245)

\displaystyle+\Gamma\cdot

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}\sum_{s=0}^{w}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}=0}^{(l+2-w)/2}\sum_{c=0}^{\min(q,q^{\prime})}\left(p_{B}Q(n,k)\right)^{s+q+q^{\prime}-c}}\Bigg),

where

\alpha^{\prime}(l):=(l+2)^{2(l+2)}\cdot\frac{\Upsilon^{l+3}(l+2)^{3(l+2)/2}}{l+1}.

(246)

We can simplify the expression above depending on how we tune the probability
p_{B}
. If
p_{B}Q(n,k)\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{w}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}=0}^{(g-w)/2}\sum_{c=0}^{\min(q,q^{\prime})}\left(p_{B}Q(n,k)\right)^{s+q+q^{\prime}-c}

\displaystyle\leq(p_{B}Q(n,k))^{g}\sum_{s=0}^{w}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}=0}^{(g-w)/2}\sum_{c=0}^{\min(q,q^{\prime})}1

(247)

\displaystyle\leq(p_{B}Q(n,k))^{g}(w+1)\left(\frac{g-w}{2}+1\right)^{3}

\displaystyle\leq(p_{B}Q(n,k))^{g}\cdot(g+1)^{4}.

Plugging these into the expression above, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\Big\langle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{D}(\bm{B})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\Big\rangle}{\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}}\leq\beta(l)\frac{\Gamma\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{p_{B}}t}{\sqrt{Q(n,k)}}\cdot\Bigg(\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{p_{B}Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}+\Gamma\cdot\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{p_{B}Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\Bigg),

(248)

where

\beta(l)=(l+3)^{\frac{5}{2}}(l+2)^{2(l+2)}\cdot\frac{\Upsilon^{l+3}(l+2)^{3(l+2)/2}}{l+1}.

(249)

If
p_{B}
is tuned such that
p_{B}Q(n,k)\leq 1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{w}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}=0}^{(g-w)/2}\sum_{c=0}^{\min(q,q^{\prime})}\left(p_{B}Q(n,k)\right)^{s+q+q^{\prime}-c}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=0}^{w}\sum_{x=0}^{(g-w)/2}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}=0}^{(g-w)/2}1

(250)

\displaystyle\leq(g+1)^{4}.

This gives us

\displaystyle\frac{\Big\langle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{D}(\bm{B})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\Big\rangle}{\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}}\leq\beta(l)\frac{\Gamma\sqrt{p}\sigma t}{Q(n,k)}\cdot\Bigg(\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}+\Gamma\cdot\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\Bigg).

(251)

This proves the statement.

5.2
Average gate complexity

As we have seen in equation (
230
), the Trotter error depends on the specific sample
\bm{b}
of the Bernoulli variables
\bm{B}
, so will the gate complexity. If the support of
\bm{b}
happens to have size
\Gamma
, we recover the full SYK Hamiltonian. If the support of
\bm{b}
is much smaller than
\Gamma
, we get a Hamiltonian with much fewer terms that requires much fewer gates to simulate. It is therefore tricky to derive one gate complexity that covers all the cases. Moreover, due to the difficulty of evaluating
G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))
for specific sample
\bm{b}
in equation (
230
), it is also difficult to formulate a gate count specific to each sample. Hence, we decided on the following approach, leading us to a notion of average gate complexity. Let

\mathcal{G}:=\{G(\bm{B}=\bm{b}):\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{\Gamma}\}

(252)

be the set of gate complexities for all configurations
\bm{b}
, where each
G(\bm{B}=\bm{b})
is the gate complexity of the
l
-th order product formula for approximating the configuration
H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b})
and ensuring the probability statement in equation (
49
) or (
50
). Each gate complexity
G(\bm{B}=\bm{b})
is the minimal gate complexity derived from the Trotter error of the corresponding sample
\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{D}(\bm{B}=\bm{b})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}
. If we take the expectation of
\mathcal{G}
over all samples Bernoulli variables, the quantity

\overline{G}:=\Big\langle G(\bm{B})\Big\rangle

(253)

yields a measure for he average gate complexity when simulating the sparse SYK model. We shall formulate our result in the following corollary:

Corollary 5.2.1
.

Simulating the
k
-local
\kappa
-sparse SYK model using (even)
l
-th order product formula has average gate complexity

\overline{G}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n\left(\sqrt{p_{B}Q(n,k)}\right)^{1+\frac{2}{l}}\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\mathcal{J}t\cdot\Bigg(\left[\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{J}t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}+\left[n^{k}\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{J}t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\Bigg)\right),

(254)

in the sense that
\overline{G}
upper bounds the average of the set

\mathcal{G}=\{G(\bm{B}=\bm{b}):\;\bm{b}\in[0,1]^{\Gamma}\},

(255)

where
G(\bm{B}=\bm{b})
is the minimal gate complexity of
S_{l}(t/r)^{r}
approximating the configuration
H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b})
that ensures the probability statement

\Prob\left(\norm{e^{iH_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b})t} - S_l(t/r)^r}\geq\epsilon\right)<\delta.

(256)

The term
Q(n,k)
is given by equation (
96
). When
l
is sufficiently large, the average gate complexity scales in
n
as

\overline{G}\sim\begin{cases}n^{1+\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{J}t&\text{if $k$ is even},\\
n^{2}\mathcal{J}t&\text{if $k$ is odd}.\end{cases}

(257)

This result is irrespective of the size of locality
k
, but rather its parity.

Proof.

Given a sample
\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{\Gamma}
, let us start from equation (
230
)

\displaystyle\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}\mathcal{D}(\bm{B}=\bm{b})\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\leq\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{*,p}\cdot p\cdot\lambda(p,r)

(258)

where

\displaystyle\lambda(p,r):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\alpha(l)\sigma t\cdot\Bigg(

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+1}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))}

(259)

\displaystyle+\Gamma\cdot

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))}\Bigg).

By Lemma (
2.1
), finding the Trotter number
r
to ensure the probability statement

\Prob(\norm{\mathcal{D}(\bm{B}=\bm{b})}\geq\epsilon)\leq\delta,

(260)

is equivalent to finding
r
such that

\displaystyle 1\geq e\sqrt{p_{D}}\alpha(l)\sigma\frac{t}{\epsilon}\cdot\Bigg(

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{p_{D}}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+1}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))}

(261)

\displaystyle+\Gamma\cdot

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{p_{D}}\sigma\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))}\Bigg).

where
p_{D}=\log(e^2D/\delta)
with
D=\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}^{p}_{p}
. Hence, we find that

\displaystyle r\geq\sqrt{p_{D}}\sigma t\cdot\Bigg(

\displaystyle\left[2e\sqrt{p_{D}}\alpha(l)\sigma\frac{t}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+1}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}

(262)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[2e\sqrt{p_{D}}\alpha(l)\sigma\frac{\Gamma t}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\Bigg).

For simplicity, we can move the sum over
G_{w}
out of the square brackets by considering a higher
r
:

\displaystyle r\geq\left(\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))}\right)^{\frac{1}{l}}\cdot R(n)

(263)

with
R(n)
defined as

R(n):=\sqrt{p_{D}}\sigma t\cdot\Bigg(\left[2e\sqrt{p_{D}}\alpha(l)\sigma\frac{t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}+\left[2e\sqrt{p_{D}}\alpha(l)\sigma\frac{\Gamma t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\Bigg)

(264)

which is a coefficient that appears in the Trotter number of all configurations.

The gate complexity specific to this configuration is then upper bounded by

G(\bm{B}=\bm{b})\leq|\bm{b}|\cdot r=R(n)\cdot|\bm{b}|\left(\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}=\bm{b}))}\right)^{\frac{1}{l}}.

(265)

It is an upper bound because we did not consider the minimal
r
.

Averaging over all Bernoulli variables gives us

\Big\langle G(\bm{B})\Big\rangle\leq R(n)\left\langle|\bm{B}|\left(\sqrt{\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}))}\right)^{\frac{1}{l}}\right\rangle.

(266)

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and applying Jensen’s inequality for the square root term, we get

\Big\langle G(\bm{B})\Big\rangle\leq R(n)\sqrt{\Big\langle|\bm{B}|^{2}\Big\rangle}\sqrt{\left(\sum_{w=0}^{l+2}\Big\langle G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle\right)^{\frac{1}{l}}}.

(267)

The first expectation can be computed from the second moment of the binomial distribution:

\displaystyle\Big\langle|\bm{B}|^{2}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=0}^{\Gamma}b^{2}\binom{\Gamma}{b}p_{B}^{b}(1-p_{B})^{\Gamma-b}

(268)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\Gamma-1)p_{B}^{2}+\Gamma p_{B}.

For the second expectation, we get

\Big\langle G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle\leq(p_{B}Q(n,k))^{g}\cdot(g+1)^{4}

(269)

if
p_{B}Q(n,k)\geq 1
, and

\Big\langle G_{w}(H_{\mathrm{SSYK}}(\bm{B}))\Big\rangle\leq(g+1)^{4}

(270)

if
p_{B}Q(n,k)\leq 1
.

We can finally put everything together. If
p_{B}Q(n,k)\geq 1
, we get

\Big\langle G(\bm{B})\Big\rangle\leq A(n)\sqrt{\Gamma(\Gamma-1)p_{B}^{2}+\Gamma p_{B}}\left(\sqrt{p_{B}Q(n,k)}\right)^{1+\frac{2}{l}}

(271)

where

A(n):=(l+3)^{\frac{5}{2l}}R(n)=(l+3)^{\frac{5}{2l}}\sqrt{p_{D}}\sigma t\cdot\Bigg(\left[2e\sqrt{p_{D}}\alpha(l)\sigma\frac{t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}+\left[2e\sqrt{p_{D}}\alpha(l)\sigma\frac{\Gamma t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\Bigg).

(272)

If
p_{B}Q(n,k)\leq 1
, we get

\Big\langle G(\bm{B})\Big\rangle\leq A(n)\sqrt{\Gamma(\Gamma-1)p_{B}^{2}+\Gamma p_{B}}.

(273)

Since we want to study the behavior of the gate complexity asymptotic in
n
, while fixing other parameters such as the sparsity
\kappa
, locality
k
, and evolution time
t
, we can safely assume that

p_{B}Q(n,k)\geq 1\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad(p_{B}\Gamma)^{m}\geq p_{B}\Gamma

(274)

for all
m\geq 1
. Since
D=\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}^{p}=2^{n/2}
, and
\sigma=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{J})
, suppressing all
l
- and
k
-dependent factors gives us

A(n)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\mathcal{J}t\cdot\Bigg(\left[\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{J}t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}+\left[n^{k}\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{J}t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\Bigg)\right).

(275)

Using this, we get the following asymptotic average gate complexities:

\overline{G}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(n\left(\sqrt{p_{B}Q(n,k)}\right)^{1+\frac{2}{l}}\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\mathcal{J}t\cdot\Bigg(\left[\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{J}t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l}}+\left[n^{k}\sqrt{n+\log(e^2/\delta)}\frac{\mathcal{J}t}{\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{l+1}}\Bigg)\right),

(276)

As for
Q(n,k)
, it’s behavior depends on the parity of locality

Suppressing all constants other than

\overline{G}\sim\begin{cases}n^{1+\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{J}t&\text{if $k$ is even},\\
n^{2}\mathcal{J}t&\text{if $k$ is odd}.\end{cases}

(277)

This proves the statement.

6.4
Outlook and open questions

Although we have derived bounds for several orders of Trotter error and found the near-optimal complexity for the product formula simulation of both the SYK and sparse SYK models, there are still some open questions left.

The first one concerns the error bound
\Delta_{l}.
Recall that by Theorem
4.1
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}e^{iHt}-S_{l}(t/r)^{r}\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}}{\mathopen{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}I\mathclose{|\mkern-1.5mu|\mkern-1.5mu|}_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{l}(n,k,t,r,p),

(292)

where

\Delta_{l}:=\frac{\mathcal{D}(l)\sqrt{p}\sigma t}{\sqrt{Q(n,k)}}\left(\binom{n}{k}\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l}+\binom{n}{k}^{2}\left[\sqrt{p}\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}\frac{t}{r}\right]^{l+1}\right).

(293)

Asymptotically for even
k
, note that
\sigma=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{k-1}{2}})
and
Q(n,k)=\mathcal{O}(n^{k-1})
. Hence,
\sigma\sqrt{Q(n,k)}=\mathcal{O}(1).
Therefore, for any even

in

k,t,r,
and
p
, then for any even
l>1
,
\Delta_{l}
will have the following asymptotic scaling in
n

\Delta_{l}(n,k,t,r,p)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{Q(n,k)}}\binom{n}{k}^{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{k+1}\right),

(294)

which is independent of
l
. This behavior is illustrated in Figure
5
, where the
\Delta_{l}

l

n_{0}
(in Figure

n
.

The second question addresses the gate complexity of the sparse SYK model. The quantity we provide does not yield the full picture, as it only gives a measure for the gate complexity averaged over all samples of the Bernoulli variables. To obtain a fuller picture, one could consider computing higher-order moments of the gate complexity, such as the variance, and give a probabilistic description via Chebyshev’s inequality in the form

\Prob\left(|G(\bm{B})-\overline{G}|\geq c\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(G(\bm{B}))}\right)\leq\frac{1}{c^{2}},

(295)

where
G(\bm{B})
is the gate complexity dependent on the Bernoulli variables,
\overline{G}
is the average gate complexity we computed, and
c
is a constant strictly larger than 1. Alternatively, one could define a typical set for the samples of the Bernoulli variables, and provide a statement about gate complexities for typical samples. We leave both approaches for future research.

Lastly, inspired by
[
2
]
, we could consider a graph-coloring optimization to reduce the number of gates per round in a product formula. This idea is based on the fact that we can decompose the SYK Hamiltonian into clusters of mutually commuting terms. If one considers the anti-commutation graph
A
of the SYK Hamiltonian, i.e. the graph obtained by placing each local term as a vertex, and an edge between two vertices if the corresponding local terms anti-commute, then the chromatic number
\chi_{A}
is the least number of commuting clusters we can decompose the SYK Hamiltonian into. The commuting clusters have the advantage that they can be simultaneously diagonalized. Implementing each commuting cluster as Pauli strings requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
gates, coming from the simultaneous diagonalization of the cluster
[
24
,
31
,
22
]
. This leads to a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\chi(A))
gates per round. Because the anti-commutation graph
A
is a
Q(n,k)
-regular graph, its chromatic number
\chi_{A}
is upper bounded by
Q(n,k)+1.
Hence, using this approach, the gate complexity per round would scale at most with
\mathcal{O}(n^{k+1})
, which is the same as the gate complexity where we use the Jordan–Wigner transformation to map each Majorana fermion to a Pauli string instead of the ternary tree mapping. However, the chromatic number
\chi_{A}
can potentially exhibit a better scaling than
Q(n,k)
, as we can see in Figure

Proof.

\bm{b}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
be a sample of the Bernoulli variables. When the Bernoulli variables take the values of this sample, we get a configuration of the sparse Hamiltonian where we can treat
\bm{b}
as coefficients

(377)

G_{w}
for general Hamiltonians, we now consider the configuration specific sum:

which looks slightly more complicated than the original

due to the extra coefficients

. The other summations over vectors

work the same as in

|\bm{w}|+2|\bm{v}|=g.

\ind
