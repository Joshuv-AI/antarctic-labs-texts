Establishing validated standards for Home and Work location Detection
Silvia de Sojo, Lorenzo Lucchini, Ollin D. Langle-Chimal, Samuel P. Fraiberger, Laura Alessandretti

Abstract
Smartphone location data have transformed urban mobility research, providing unprecedented insights into how people navigate and interact in cities. However, leveraging location data at scale presents methodological challenges. Accurately identifying individuals’ home and work locations is critical for a range of applications, including commuting analysis, unemployment estimation, and urban accessibility studies. Despite their widespread use, home–work detection methods lack a standardized framework that accounts for differing data quality and that is validated against ground-truth observations. This limits the comparability and reproducibility of results across studies and datasets. In this paper, we present HoWDe, a robust algorithm for identifying home and work locations from mobility data, explicitly designed to handle missing data and varying data quality across individuals. Using two unique ground-truth datasets comprising over 5,100 5,100 individuals from more than 80 80 countries, HoWDe achieves home and work detection accuracies of up to 97 % 97\% and 88 % 88\% , respectively, with consistent performance across countries and demographic groups. We examine how parameter choices shape the trade-off between accuracy and user retention, and demonstrate how these methodological decisions influence downstream applications such as employment estimation and commuting pattern analysis. By supporting in-house pre-processing through a transparent and validated pipeline, HoWDe also facilitates the sharing of privacy-preserving mobility data. Together, our tools and findings establish methodological standards that support more robust, scalable, and reproducible mobility research at both individual and urban scales. Keywords: GPS data, Home and work detection, Human Mobility, Stop-location sequences, Mobility algorithms, Missing data, Urban analytics

Unveiling temporal patterns of home-work visits

We analyze the regularity of home and work visits by measuring how often and when individuals are recorded at their annotated locations.

Our analysis suggests that work location is best determined using weekday daytime hours, while home location can be identified using night hours, regardless of weekdays or weekends, similar to previous approaches

Most users’ daily patterns can be grouped into six behavioral profiles based on home and three on workday visits (see Methods for details on how behavioral profiles are obtained).

2.2
HoWDe: a Home and Work Detection algorithm

In this section, we introduce
HoWDe
, a dynamic home and work detection algorithm that can capture residential moves and job changes over time through a sliding window approach.
A key feature of
HoWDe
is the identification of home and work locations based on the proportion of time spent at a given location, rather than the absolute amount of time as approached in previous literature
[
29
,
28
,
14
,
15
,
33
,
34
]
.
This enables
HoWDe
to accommodate users with varying amounts of missing data over time (see Figure
1
c-f), through tunable parameters that set data-quality filters.
We share an open-source implementation of the algorithm in PySpark
[
37
]
.

2.2.1
HoWDe workflow

HoWDe takes as input a user’s pre-processed sequence of stops, where each stop is fully defined by a user-location unique ID and a start and end timestamp.

First, we perform a temporal aggregation of the stop sequences into hourly-bin sequences.
In each hourly bin, we retain the location where the individual has spent most of the time.
This aggregation limits sequences to a maximum of 24 locations per day, boosting computational efficiency and making
HoWDe
faster than previous approaches based on sliding windows, with only limited information loss
[
14
,
15
]
.

Second, we exclude days with insufficient hourly bins with data (this step is controlled by the tunable parameter
dhn
, see Table
2
).
This is done separately for work detection (where we consider business hours) and home detection (where we consider night hours, see Figure
2
b).
Hence, it is possible that one day is retained only for the home detection or for the work detection.

Third, for each day
t
, we calculate the
fraction of hourly-bins
spent at each location during nighttime (24 pm to 6 am for home detection), and typical business hours (9 am to 4 pm on weekdays, for work detection).
To compute such a fraction, we use as a denominator the number of hourly bins with records (Figure
2
.b).
Unlike previous approaches that rely on absolute time spent at each location, considering fractions can better accommodate users with varying amounts of missing data over time.

Fourth, for each day
t
, we consider a
window
of size
\Delta_{T}+1
, covering the range included in
t\pm\Delta_{T}/2
days (see Figure
2
.c).
This window acts as a memory component for the algorithm to better detect behavior in the period before and after a specific day.
In each window, we average the fraction of hours a location was visited (see Figure
2
c).

Thus, for each window, we obtain a collection of potential home and work locations.
Among these, we retain only those that meet minimum requirements with respect to the proportion of visits within and across days.
These requirements are controlled by parameters
dn_{H,W}
,
hf_{H,W}
,
df_{H,W}
(see Table
2
).
If no location meets the requirements, no home (or work) is assigned for that specific window.

Finally, we sort the home and work collections in two different ways.
For home, we sort based on the typical fraction of hourly bins the location is visited within a day.
For work, we sort based on the typical fraction of days the location is visited within the window.
If no work location can be selected based on the fraction of days, the sorting is based on the fraction of hourly bins.
The top location from these two lists is then assigned as home (or work).
By prioritizing the fraction of days over the fraction of hours for work detection, we are emphasizing work as a location defined by regular visits throughout the workweek (see Sec.SI-6).

In summary, the algorithm allows the control of two aspects.
It enables the user to specify the criteria for determining when there is enough data to attempt home and work detection.
Also, it enables to control of when a location can be considered as a candidate home or work location, all while accounting for the fact that users can have different levels of missing data.

2.3
Validation against ground truth data

To assess how well the procedure described above can capture individuals’ home and work locations, we measure i) the accuracy of the algorithm as the fraction of home/work locations that are correctly identified, and ii) the fraction of not-detected users, measured as the number of users where a home/work location was not detected against the number of users for which a home/work location was reported in the ground truth data (see Methods for more details).
We run the validation using the ground-truth datasets D1 and D2, described in Methods.
In dataset D2, annotators reviewed complete stop sequences; therefore, the temporal dimension (changes of home and work locations) can not be considered.
For this reason, for D2, we consider a single window covering the entire data period (see Sec.SI-7 for parameter details).

2.4
Robustness and biases across configurations

HoWDe
’s accuracy comes at the cost of user retention.
The exclusion of users may introduce biases toward specific demographic groups.
To assess these biases, we measure accuracy and user retention for different groups based on age (self-reported), gender (self-reported), urbanization levels, and country GDP.
Results are obtained for dataset D1, using the minimum and maximum parameter configurations (see Sec.SI-7).

The largest accuracy gaps appear in suburban areas and middle-income countries: home detection drops by
3.39
points in suburban areas (compared to urban areas) and work detection by
9.13
in middle-income countries (compared to high-income countries).
Despite accuracy deviations, home detection accuracy remains within
90\%

99\%

-

Nevertheless, even minor detection errors can propagate, compromising the overall reliability of the results.
To address this potential issue, we examine how tuning the detection algorithm parameters impacts results in two downstream applications: (i) employment rate estimates in Europe and (ii) commuting pattern characteristics.

3
Discussion

In this work, we contributed to the development of accurate and robust methods for home and work detection.
Using two datasets (D1, D2) covering
\sim 5,100
individuals from over
80
countries, we characterized home/work visit patterns.
We found that individuals are most likely at home between midnight and 6 AM (
\sim 75\%
probability) and at work between 9 AM and 4 PM on workdays (
\sim 50\%
).
However, visit patterns and missing data vary widely across users and over time, and even within individual users’ routines.

Building on these insights, we provided an open-source implementation of
HoWDe
, a home/work detection algorithm that integrates existing approaches while improving robustness against missing data.
We validated
HoWDe
against ground truth datasets, achieving up to
\sim 97\%
accuracy in D1 and
\sim 88\%
in D2 for home detection, and up to
\sim 88\%
accuracy in D1 and
\sim 82\%
accuracy in D2 for work detection.

Our analysis highlights the trade-off between accuracy and retaining users with low-quality data.
We identified two key parameters affecting performance: the window length considered and the minimum visit proportion required for classification.
A window of at least
28
days ensures a reliable home estimation, while work windows longer than
42
days reduce user retention without significant accuracy gains.

We also revealed how the algorithm’s accuracy varies across population groups, with the largest gaps appearing between urban and suburban areas (for home detection), and between high- and middle-income countries (for work detection).
Although these accuracy gaps persist across configurations, two case studies show that prioritizing accuracy over user retention is preferable for individual-level tasks, whereas maximizing retention may be more valuable for population-level analyses.

The insights gained from this study, along with a formalized algorithm, can support research on commuting
[
15
,
16
]
, social contact networks
[
6
]
, and access to amenities
[
14
]
, with implications for areas such as computational social science, urban planning, and public health
[
41
,
17
]
.
Improving location data processing will enhance our understanding of both universal mobility laws and micro-level social dynamics
[
42
]
.

Despite its strengths,
HoWDe
has key limitations.
While it allows customization of detection windows and thresholds, more advanced methods may be required to fully capture mobility patterns that are collected by diverse GPS-equipped devices
[
43
]
.
Its performance may also vary with lower temporal resolution or geographically limited datasets, particularly in underrepresented low-income regions with sparser mobile data.
Future work should assess its applicability in rural and less densely populated areas
[
44
]
.

Detecting home and work locations raises important ethical concerns, particularly regarding privacy
[
45
,
46
]
, as the geographical coordinates of these locations are highly identifying and can enable re-identification of individuals in mobility datasets.
Currently, many data holders apply in-house heuristics to detect home and work, but these methods are often opaque, introducing methodological uncertainty.
By providing a validated and open-source detection framework, our approach enables data holders to perform this sensitive step internally using a standardized and transparent pipeline.
We hope this will facilitate the sharing of privacy-preserving datasets—for example, through the release of obfuscated or aggregated representations—while ensuring methodological consistency and scientific utility for downstream research.

In summary, by advancing methods for home and work detection, this study contributes to a more robust and scalable foundation for mobility research.
By providing an open-source, validated solution, we hope to facilitate more reliable and ethical research, contributing to a deeper understanding of human mobility and its implications for society.

4.3
Home and Work locations detection algorithms: a primer

Smartphones use an in-built GPS sensor to estimate the device’s latitudes and longitude coordinates over time, at a frequency that varies depending on device usage.
The device position is then collected by various location-based apps, such as navigation or social media apps.
These data may then be used by location intelligence companies or researchers to study human mobility and its regularities, both at the individual and at the population level.
Raw location data utilized for mobility analysis consists of timestamped sequences of
(latitude, longitude)
records associated with a unique user ID.

The first pre-processing step involved in the analysis is the so-called “stop-location detection”
[
47
,
49
]
.
Stop location detection consists of (i) identifying records that are captured when individuals stay within a small area for a sufficient amount of time (e.g. when they are at home, at the office, or in a restaurant); (ii) applying spatial clustering to all these “static records”, to identify a set of unique locations, i.e. those places where people spend time.
After detecting the individuals’ stops-locations, individual trajectories can be summarized as sequences of stays.
Each stay is associated with a start time (when the stay started), a duration (how long the stay lasted), and a location ID (where the stay happened).

A second critical processing step often involved in mobility studies is to identify key locations, such as individuals’ home and work locations, starting from the sequences of stays.
To achieve this, researchers and practitioners often define heuristics based on the assumption that visits to home and work locations follow specific daily and weekly patterns.
While a standard practice does not exist, in this work, we focus on two distinct and widely used state-of-the-art methods that exploit travel circadian rhythms and pattern regularities. These methods were designed and tested over the past decade with different approaches, in a collective scientific effort, separately leveraging diverse sets of mobile-phone-based data (e.g. Call Detail Records (CDRs), eXtended Detail Records, (XDRs), or GPS records)
[
21
,
50
,
51
,
35
]
as well as activity-based travel surveys
[
36
]
.
The main rationale is to rank locations based on the day of the week (weekdays vs. weekends) and the time of day (daytime vs. nighttime) when visits occur.

4.3.1
The
Atlas
approach

The method used in
[
2
,
6
,
52
,
28
]
(hereinafter “Atlas” method) defines a home location as the most probable location visited on weekdays between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m., i.e. the stop location where a device was found spending most of the weekday nighttime. While the Atlas methods mostly focuses on home locations, its definition can be easily generalized to the case of work locations. Following a similar rationale, we define an Atlas work location as the most probable stop location visited during weekdays daytime.

4.3.2
The
Time-Geo
approach

In contrast with the definition introduced by the Atlas method, more complex heuristics are adopted in Jiang et al.
[
29
]
(hereinafter referred to as the “Time-geo” method), in particular for the case of identifying work locations.
Time-geo ranks stop locations based on the frequency of visits during weekday nights (between 7 p.m. and 8 a.m.) and weekends and picks the most visited location as an individual’s home.
The same logic is applied when detecting the work location, but now focusing on weekday work time boundaries (between 8 a.m. and 7 p.m.) and including additional requirements:
i) the candidate’s work location is visited at least three times across the study period, and ii) the distance between the work candidate and an individual’s home location is greater than
500
meters.

Note that both methods rely on the definition of stop location. Stop location definition might vary depending on the type of data analyzed and the specific characteristics of the data points, e.g. whether you use Call Detail Records (CDR), eXtended Detail Records (XDR), or GPS points. However, the principled choices these algorithms are founded on can naturally be extended to any of these different cases, making them easily generalizable.

In this work, stop locations are defined as spatiotemporally close sets of GPS points. All comparisons between home-work detection methods are always drawn from a set of stop locations constructed following identical procedures. For more information about stop location processing, we refer to the Supplementary Information (SI) Sec.SI-2 and the existing literature
[
47
,
14
]
.

4.4
Home and work visits behavioral clusters

Section
2.1
focused on the temporal attributes of users’ daily mobility patterns. To better highlight groups of users with similar behavioral features, we reported the results in Figure
1
.c-f, sorting daily patterns based on the visit hour of the first home visit. behavioral groups were generated using the K-Modes clustering algorithm, a categorical adaptation of the traditional K-Means algorithm
[
53
]
. Here, we use daily sequences of 24 hourly bins with three possible stop categories. For example, in the case of home patterns, each sequence consists of either a home (category 1), a visit to “a different location”, e.g., work or recreational locations (category 2), or an hour with missing data (category 3). A similar approach is used for work patterns, where visits to work location are considered as “category 1”, while visits to home location are considered as “a different location” (category 2). The K-Modes algorithm requires as a parameter, the number of desired clusters to compute. In Figure
1
.c,e we reported 6 clusters. In Figure
1
.d,f we reported 3 and 4 clusters, respectively. Note that, while the number of clusters is the result of the selection of cluster number following the elbow method for each of the four plots, our purpose is to simply highlight the main different visit patterns. Following this idea, in Sec.SI-3, where we present results for the smaller D2 dataset, we manually selected three as the number of clusters used to report the hourly day-visit patterns.

4.5
Behavioral profile entropy

We quantify the degree to which users transition between behavioral profiles measuring the normalized Shannon entropy
[
38
]
, treating each behavioral cluster as a distinct state, following:

H_{\text{norm}}=-\frac{1}{\log{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\log{p_{i}},

(1)

where
k
is the total number of possible clusters, and
p_{i}
is the probability of occurrence of each state, calculated as:
p_{i}=\frac{n_{i}}{N}
where
n_{i}
are the total days classified in cluster
i
and
N
the total number of days for a given user.
Normalizing by the maximum entropy
\frac{1}{\log{k}}
, ensures that entropy remains comparable across cases (weekend vs. weekday and home vs. work), staying within the same range from 0 (when there is only one profile) to 1 (when there are multiple profiles, and all are equally important).

4.7
Accuracy and “fraction of not detected” definition

In this work, we report the performance of the algorithm in terms of two main quantities: “accuracy” and “fraction of not detected”.
Here, we provide the definitions of these two concepts.
The definitions below are explained in the case of the home detection, and they apply in the same way for the work detection.

The fraction of ‘not detected’ indicates cases where a home location was labelled, but the algorithm couldn’t assign a home due to the thresholds on data quality or minimum fraction of visits.
In the case of D1, the dataset consists of a set of user-weeks, corresponding to users that self-reported living in a specific home (the “ground truth home”) in a given week.
For some of these user-weeks, our algorithm identifies a “detected home”.
We evaluate the performance of the algorithm by comparing pairs of “ground truth home” and “detected home” across user-weeks.
The “fraction of not detected” is thus computed as
f_{ND}=1-n_{\bar{H}}/n_{H}
, where
n_{\bar{H}}
is the number of user-week pairs with a detected home, and
n_{H}
is the number of user-week pairs with a ground truth home.
In the case of D2, where ground truth information is not referring to a specific week,
n_{\bar{H}}
corresponds to the number of users with a detected home, and
n_{H}
corresponds to the number of users with a ground truth home.

The “accuracy” captures the fraction of correctly labelled home locations among the set of detected home locations.
Therefore, we define accuracy as
Acc=m_{\bar{H}}/n_{\bar{H}}
, where
m_{\bar{H}}
is the number of user-weeks (for D1) or users (for D2) for which the detected home location matches the ground truth home location.
In dataset D2, if annotators identified multiple homes for an individual, we considered it a match if the individual’s detected home location corresponded to at least one of the annotated ground truth home locations.
Note that our definition does not correspond exactly to the traditional definition of accuracy because we separately assess the algorithm performance in terms of the detection/non-detection of a home (captured by
f_{ND}
) and the detection of the correct home (captured by
Acc
).

The definitions above are also used in the case of work detection, where only users with a reported work location,
n_{W}
, are included in the denominator of
f_{ND}
, and only those with a detected and a matching work location are counted in the nominator and in the accuracy computation, as
n_{\bar{W}}
and
m_{\bar{W}}
, respectively.
