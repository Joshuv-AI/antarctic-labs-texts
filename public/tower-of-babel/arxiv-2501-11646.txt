CDMA/OTFS Sensing Outperforms Pure OTFS at the Same Communication Throughput
HUGO HAWKINS \authorrefmark 1, GRADUATE STUDENT MEMBER, IEEE, CHAO XU \authorrefmark 1, SENIOR MEMBER, IEEE, LIE-LIANG YANG \authorrefmark 1, FELLOW, IEEE, AND LAJOS HANZO \authorrefmark 1, LIFE FELLOW, IEEE

Abstract
There is a dearth of publications on the subject of spreading-aided Orthogonal Time Frequency Space solutions, especially for Integrated Sensing and Communication, even though Code Division Multiple Access assisted multi-user Orthogonal Time Frequency Space (CDMA/OTFS) exhibits tangible benefits. Hence, this work characterises both the communication Bit Error Rate and sensing Root Mean Square Error performance of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, and contrasts them to pure Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. Three Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space configurations are considered: Delay Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, and Delay Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, which harness direct sequence spreading along the delay axis, Doppler axis, and DD domains respectively. For each configuration, the performance of Gold, Hadamard, and Zadoff-Chu sequences is investigated. The results demonstrate that Zadoff-Chu Delay Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space and Delay Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space consistently outperform pure Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing, whilst maintaining a similar communication performance at the same throughput. The extra modulation complexity of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is similar to that of other Orthogonal Time Frequency Space multi-user methodologies, but the demodulation complexity of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is lower than that of some other Orthogonal Time Frequency Space multi-user methodologies. Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing can also consistently outperform Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing whilst not requiring any additional complexity for target parameter estimation. Therefore, Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is an appealing candidate for implementing multi-user Orthogonal Time Frequency Space ISAC.

, as the number of wireless devices is expected to drastically increase. Since the Orthogonal Time Frequency Space concept was first introduced
[

, its employment for Integrated Sensing and Communication
[

has been a topic of interest. This is due in part to Orthogonal Time Frequency Space being less affected by Doppler shift than Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing, and to the Delay-Doppler Domain channel being defined by the delay and Doppler shifts of the propagation paths. This leads to the Delay-Doppler Domain channel fluctuating at a slower rate than its Time Frequency Domain and Time Domain counterparts. When the delay and Doppler shifts of the propagation paths are perfectly synchronous with the system’s sampling grid, the channel can be modelled using a sparse matrix. This can simplify the associated target parameter estimation algorithms
[
8
]
.

However, in practice, the delay and Doppler shifts are rarely integer multiples of the system resolutions, hence more complex detection algorithms are required for accurate target estimation. The full-search based Maximum Likelihood attains the best performance at the highest complexity. Hence, Gaudio et al.
[
9
]
compare the sensing performance of Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing and Orthogonal Time Frequency Space employing Maximum Likelihood detection applied to the entire possible set of delay and Doppler indices, at an unspecified resolution. At low velocities, where the Doppler shift is 0.28% of the subcarrier spacing, Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing Maximum Likelihood sensing approaches the Cramér-Rao Bound at a lower Signal to Noise Ratio than Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. This suggests that at low velocities, Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing is more suitable for sensing than Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. However,
[
9
]
does not compare the two waveforms at high velocities.

In
[
10
,
11
]
, the authors conceive a two dimensional correlation-based method for Orthogonal Time Frequency Space integer delay and Doppler index estimation. A generalised likelihood ratio test is developed to estimate the number of targets. The fractional indices of the estimated targets are determined by comparing the power-ratio of the indices surrounding the estimated integer indices in the imaging matrix. This method requires each target to have unique integer delay and Doppler indices. The authors observe that the correlation-based method results in an error floor at high Signal to Noise Ratios due to the inter-symbol interference caused by the multiple propagation paths in the channel. Tang et al.
[
12
]
propose a two-step sensing methodology to estimate the integer and fractional delay and Doppler indices of the targets for Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing. The first step employs a matched filter-based method to determine the integer indices. The second step utilises a Fibonacci-search based algorithm to iteratively determine the fractional indices. Unlike other parameter estimation methods, which reach an error floor at high Signal to Noise Ratios, the estimation error of the Fibonacci-search based algorithm continues to decrease as the Signal to Noise Ratio increases.

In
[
13
]
, Muppaneni et al. design a two-step method for sensing the target parameters of Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. Firstly, the integer indices are estimated by maximising a cost function for a fixed number of propagation paths. Secondly, the fractional indices are estimated using a matrix of all the integer indices and the previously estimated fractional indices. For the first path, only integer indices of the other paths are utilised to estimate the first path fractional indices. Once the fractional indices for a path have been estimated, the channel attenuation of the associated path is then estimated. After the fractional indices of a path are estimated, the difference between the received signal and the reconstructed received signal from the estimates is calculated. If this difference becomes smaller than a specific threshold, the sensing procedure is terminated, and path estimation is concluded. Otherwise, the algorithm continues until the fixed number of propagation path variables has been estimated. This method is also harnessed for communication channel estimation using pilot frames. The results presented in
[
13
]
show that the proposed algorithm leads to a lower sensing estimation error and communication Bit Error Rate than the modified Maximum Likelihood benchmark used for comparison.

A low-complexity variant of Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, known as Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing, was first introduced by Thaj et al.
[
14
]
. This waveform modulates the symbols in the delay-sequency (sic) domain, as opposed to the Delay-Doppler Domain of Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. This is achieved by replacing the Inverse Discrete Fourier Transform along the Doppler axis by the Walsh-Hadamard transform. Since this is a purely real-valued transform, a real-valued transmitted signal can be generated by employing a real-valued modulation scheme. The Hadamard transform is also more computationally efficient than the Discrete Fourier Transform and the Inverse Discrete Fourier Transform used for Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. However, since the modulated waveform is constructed in the delay-sequency domain, not the Delay-Doppler Domain, the propagation paths with identical delays cannot be separated without more complex detection methods, even if they have different Doppler shifts. Thaj et al.
[
14
]
show that Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing has a similar Bit Error Rate performance to Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, but it is computationally more efficient due to the use of the Hadamard transform. The pilot power and Peak to Average Power Ratio of Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing is reduced by Neelam and Sahuin
[
15
]
, by superimposing the pilot symbols on the data, and employing a low complexity iterative channel estimator. Moreover, the Bit Error Rate upper bound of Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing is determined by Sui et al.
[
16
]
, and a novel vector approximate message passing-based expectation-maximization detector is developed to improve the Bit Error Rate performance of Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing.

Doosti-Aref et al.
[
17
]
harness Index Modulation in the sequency domain, to improve both the spectral efficiency and Bit Error Rate of Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing. This method is further developed in
[
18
]
, where pairs of sequence indices of the sequency domain are determined by Index Modulation, as opposed to detecting individual indices. This improves the energy efficiency of the system, and reduces the error propagation imposed by erroneous activated index estimation.

The estimation and compensation of the In-phase and Quadrature-phase imbalance in the received signal caused by the hardware at high frequencies is investigated for Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing and Orthogonal Time Frequency Space by Neelam and Sahu
[
19
]
. The estimation is achieved by placing two pilot symbols in the delay-sequency domain transmitted signal, and then estimating the imbalance based on these symbols in the delay-time domain. The addition of a second pilot symbol decreases the number of indices available for data, since guard bands are also required between the pilot symbols, in addition to the guard band between the pilot symbols and the data. The In-phase and Quadrature-phase compensation is implemented in the delay-time domain. The compensation of the imbalance is improved as the pilot power is increased. This work is then also extended to determine both the carrier frequency offset and the channel parameters by Neelam and Sahu
[
20
]
, where both integer delay and fractional Doppler indices are considered.

The work in
[
19
]
is further extended to transmitter and receiver imbalance estimation and compensation as well as channel estimation by the same authors
[
21
]
. A complex pseudo noise-based training sequence is placed in the indices allocated to the pilot symbols. The imaginary part of the sequence is a cyclic shift of the real part of the sequence. The channel is estimated at discrete intervals, where the initial estimations are further refined by linear interpolation. An iterative algorithm is conceived for estimating and compensating the In-phase and Quadrature-phase imbalance.

Singh et al.
[
22
]
implement a deep learning-based detector for Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing to compensate for hardware impairments. This deep learning-based detector involves a convolutional neural network, which is trained on the transmitted, received, and Minimum Mean Square Error detector outputs. A data augmentation scheme is then developed to further enhance the input data to the neural network. The resultant detector has a slightly higher complexity than an Minimum Mean Square Error detector, whilst reducing the Bit Error Rate.

Reddy et al.
[
23
]
design a multi-user uplink method based on upsampled and circularly shifted Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing. These signals, when combined at the base station, are equivalent to single-user Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing, or block-based single carrier signals. This is equivalent to a system that allocates delay indices to users, where the secondary domain of the data can be selected simply by interchanging the matrix multiplying the modulated data. Specifically, the system may opt for the Inverse Discrete Fourier Transform for Orthogonal Time Frequency Space/Zak-Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, the Hadamard transform for Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing, or the identity matrix for single carrier Time Domain schemes. The Bit Error Rate performance of the Orthogonal Time Sequency Multiplexing method is comparable to that of the Orthogonal Time Frequency Space method.

Another method applying a real-valued transform to Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is proposed by Kalpage et al.
[
24
]
. A modified Zak-Orthogonal Time Frequency Space method relying on the Discrete Cosine Transform is developed to reduce the Peak to Average Power Ratio of Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. Replacing the Discrete Fourier Transform by the Discrete Cosine Transform reduces both the Peak to Average Power Ratio and the complexity of the system, without eroding the Bit Error Rate for the conditions considered by the authors.

Surabhi et al.
[
25
]
design a multi-user Orthogonal Time Frequency Space system that splits the available Delay-Doppler Domain indices into multiple sub-groups, each of which is allocated to a user. The sub-groups can be along the delay axis, the Doppler axis, or both axes. The Bit Error Rate of the schemes relying on index groups along the delay or Doppler axis is lower than that of the schemes that allocate indices for a user along two axes. The Bit Error Rate of this system using Maximum Likelihood or message passing-based detection is lower than that of Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access and Single Carrier Frequency-Division Multiple Access.

Khammammetti and Mohammed
[
26
]
propose a Delay-Doppler Domain index allocation method for multi-user communications similar to that of
[
25
]
, but place guard bands between the indices assigned to users, to minimise the inter-user interference. This leads to a simpler detector, at the cost of an eroded throughput.

Ge et al.
[
27
]
apply the Delay-Doppler Domain matrix partitioning method to uplink communication, separating stationary and mobile devices. The stationary devices modulate along the delay axis, and the mobile devices modulate along the Doppler axis. As the base station is static, the stationary users do not experience Doppler shift. A successive interference cancellation aided iterative turbo receiver utilising soft inputs and outputs is developed for detecting the uplink signals. The stationary user signals are decoded, followed by the information arriving from the mobile devices.

1.1
Contributions

Again, there is a paucity of publications on the subject of dense sequence spreading Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, especially for Integrated Sensing and Communication. Hence, this work analyses the communication Bit Error Rate and sensing Root Mean Square Error performance of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, and contrasts them with pure Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. Table
1
explicitly juxtaposes the novelties of the proposed system to the existing literature, which are detailed below:

•

A detailed analysis of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space in the context of Integrated Sensing and Communication is provided, where both fractional delay indices and fractional Doppler indices are considered.

•

An in-depth analysis of the communication Bit Error Rate and sensing Root Mean Square Error performance of delay only, Doppler only, and delay-Doppler sequence spreading for Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space.

•

This work demonstrates that Zadoff-Chu Delay Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space and Delay Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space are the configurations that consistently outperform pure Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing, whilst maintaining a similar communication performance at the same throughput.

Channel Model

In this section, the channel models are introduced. The generalised Time Domain, Time Frequency Domain, and Delay-Doppler Domain channel models are described in Section
3.1
. The communication channel parameters are presented in Section
3.2
, while the sensing channel parameters are discussed in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Generalised Channel Model

It is assumed that there is no external interference during transmission. The transmitted signal is passed through a time-varying and frequency-selective fading channel, as modelled in
[
37
]
. The Delay-Doppler Domain representation of the fading channel is:

\tilde{h}(\tau\textnormal{, }\nu)=\sum^{P-1}_{p=0}\tilde{h}_{p}\delta(\tau-\tau_{p})\delta(\nu-\nu_{p})\textnormal{ ,}

(14)

where
\tau
is the delay,
\nu
is the Doppler shift,
p=[0
,
1
, …,
P-1]
is the propagation path index, and
P
is the total number of propagation paths. Furthermore,
\tau_{p}
is the delay associated with the
p
th
path,
\nu_{p}
is the Doppler shift associated with the
p
th
path,
\tilde{h}_{p}
is the fading gain and path loss associated with the
p
th
path, and
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function.

When sampled in the Delay-Doppler Domain, the channel can be represented by the time-invariant parameter
\tilde{h}_{p}
, the delay index
\tau^{i}
, and the Doppler index
\nu^{i}
. The delay and Doppler indices are defined as:

\displaystyle\tau^{i}

\displaystyle=(\Delta fM)\tau\textnormal{ ,}

(15)

\displaystyle\nu^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{\Delta f}\nu\textnormal{ ,}

(16)

where
\Delta f
is the subcarrier spacing.

The Time Domain representation of the fading channel is:

h_{m\textnormal{, }n\textnormal{, }p}=\tilde{h}_{p}e^{j2\pi\nu^{i}_{p}\frac{nM+m-\tau^{i}_{p}}{MN}}\textnormal{ ,}

(17)

where
j=\sqrt{-1}
,
\tau^{i}_{p}
is the delay index associated with the
p
th
propagation path, and
\nu^{i}_{p}
is the Doppler index associated with the
p
th
propagation path.

The Time Domain channel matrix
\boldsymbol{H}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
is:

\boldsymbol{H}_{n}[m\textnormal{, }\lfloor m-\tau^{i}_{p}\rfloor_{M}]=\sum^{P-1}_{p=0}h_{m\textnormal{, }n\textnormal{, }p}\textnormal{ ,}

(18)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor_{M}
is the modulo
M
operator.

When the delay indices are assumed to be integers, the Time Domain received signal
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}
is

\boldsymbol{y}_{n}=\boldsymbol{H}_{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}+\boldsymbol{z}\textnormal{ ,}

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}
is the Time Domain transmitted signal, and
\boldsymbol{z}
is the complex Time Domain Additive White Gaussian Noise, with mean
\mu_{z}=0
and variance
\sigma_{z}^{2}
, expressed as
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{z}\textnormal{, }\sigma_{z}^{2})
.

When the integer delay index assumption is discarded,
\left(m-\tau^{i}_{p}\right)
is not an integer for
m=(0
,
1
, …,
M-1)
. As matrices do not have fractional indices, the channel must to be modelled differently. A portion of the fading channel is modelled in the Time Frequency Domain and then converted to the Time Domain as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{n}[m]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\sum^{P-1}_{p=0}h_{m\textnormal{, }n\textnormal{, }p}\sum^{M-1}_{\bar{m}=0}\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}_{n}[\bar{m}]e^{j2\pi\frac{\left(m-\tau^{i}_{p}\right)\bar{m}}{M}}

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{z}_{n}[m]\textnormal{ ,}

(20)

where
\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
is the Time Frequency Domain transmitted signal, and
\bar{m}=[0
,
1
, …,
M-1]
.

The Time Frequency Domain channel matrix
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
is:

\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{n}=\mathcal{F}_{M}\boldsymbol{H}_{n}\mathcal{F}_{M}^{H}\textnormal{ ,}

(21)

where
\mathcal{F}_{M}
is the
M
-point Discrete Fourier Transform and
\mathcal{F}_{M}^{H}
is the
M
-point Inverse Discrete Fourier Transform.

The Delay-Doppler Domain channel matrix
\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN\times MN}
is:

\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}=\left(\mathcal{F}_{N}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{M}^{H}\right)\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{X}\left(\mathcal{F}_{N}^{H}\otimes\mathcal{F}_{M}\right)\textnormal{ ,}

(22)

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product, and

\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{X}=diag\left(\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{n}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{0}&\boldsymbol{0}_{M\times M}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{0}_{M\times M}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{M\times M}&\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{1}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{0}_{M\times M}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{M\times M}&\boldsymbol{0}_{M\times M}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{N-1}\end{bmatrix}\textnormal{ .}

(23)

3.2
Communication Channel Parameters

Integer delay indices, fractional Doppler indices, and Rician fading are assumed for communication. There are
P_{com}
propagation paths. The first path
p_{com}=0
is the Line of Sight path, and the remaining
P_{com}-1
paths are Non-Line of Sight paths. The fading gain
\tilde{h}_{p\textnormal{, }com}
of the
p_{com}
th
propagation path is:

\tilde{h}_{p\textnormal{, }com}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{com}}{\kappa_{com}+1}}\textnormal{, }&\textnormal{if }p_{com}=0\\
\sqrt{\frac{1}{(\kappa_{com}+1)(P_{com}-1)}}\zeta_{p\textnormal{, }com}\textnormal{, }&\textnormal{if }p_{com}>0\textnormal{ ,}\end{cases}

(24)

where
\kappa_{com}
is the Rician K factor, and
\zeta_{p\textnormal{, }com}
is a complex Gaussian random variable with mean
\mu_{com}=0
and variance
\sigma_{com}^{2}=1
, expressed as
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{com}\textnormal{, }\sigma_{com}^{2})
.

The integer delay index
\tau^{i}_{p\textnormal{, }com}
is:

\tau^{i}_{p\textnormal{, }com}=\begin{cases}0\textnormal{,}&\textnormal{if }p_{com}=0\\
p_{com}\mathbin{\%}L_{com}\textnormal{,}&\textnormal{if }p_{com}>0\textnormal{ \& }P_{com}\geq L_{com}\\
\lfloor L_{com}\eta_{\tau\textnormal{, }com}\rceil\textnormal{,}&\textnormal{if }p_{com}>0\textnormal{ \& }P_{com}<L_{com}\textnormal{,}\end{cases}

(25)

where
L_{com}
is the number of delay taps,
p_{com}=[0
,
1
, …,
P_{com}-1]
,
\mathbin{\%}
is the remainder or modulus operator,
\eta_{\tau\textnormal{, }com}
is a random variable following a uniform distribution between 0 and 1, and
\lfloor\cdot\rceil
is the rounding function.

If
P_{com}<L_{com}
, no pair of propagation paths will have the same delay index, yielding:
\tau^{i}_{p_{1}\textnormal{, }com}\neq\tau^{i}_{p_{2}\textnormal{, }com}
, where
p_{1}=[0
,
1
, …
P_{com}-1]
,
p_{2}=[0
,
1
, …
P_{com}-1]
, and
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
.

The fractional Doppler index
\nu^{i}_{p\textnormal{, }com}
is:

\nu^{i}_{p\textnormal{, }com}=\begin{cases}\nu^{i}_{com\textnormal{, }max}\textnormal{, }&\textnormal{if }p_{com}=0\\
\lfloor 2\nu^{i}_{com\textnormal{, }max}\left(\eta_{\nu\textnormal{, }com}-0.5\right)\rceil\textnormal{, }&\textnormal{if }p_{com}>0\textnormal{ ,}\end{cases}

(26)

where
\eta_{\nu\textnormal{, }com}
is a random variable following a uniform distribution between 0 and 1, and
\nu^{i}_{com\textnormal{, }max}
is the maximum integer Doppler index:

\nu^{i}_{com\textnormal{, }max}=\lceil\frac{f_{c}NV_{com}}{\Delta fc_{0}}\rceil\textnormal{ ,}

(27)

with
V_{com}
representing the velocity of the communication receiver,
f_{c}
is the carrier frequency,
c_{0}
is the speed of light, and
\lceil\cdot\rceil
is the ceiling function.

3.3
Sensing Channel Parameters

The delay and Doppler indices for sensing,
\tau^{i}_{p\textnormal{, }sen}
and
\nu^{i}_{p\textnormal{, }sen}
respectively, are:

\tau^{i}_{p\textnormal{, }sen}=\frac{2\Delta fMR_{p\textnormal{, }sen}}{c_{0}}\textnormal{ ,}

(28)

\nu^{i}_{p\textnormal{, }sen}=\frac{2f_{c}NV_{p\textnormal{, }sen}}{\Delta fc_{0}}\textnormal{ ,}

(29)

where
R_{p\textnormal{, }sen}
is the range of the
p_{sen}
th
path,
V_{p\textnormal{, }sen}
is the velocity of the
p_{sen}
th
path,
p_{sen}=[0
,
1
, …,
P_{sen}-1]
, and
P_{sen}
is the number of sensing propagation paths.

As monostatic sensing is assumed, the transmitted signal is reflected from the target to the sensing receiver attached or adjacent to the transmitter, hence a factor of 2 is present in (
28
) and (
29
). The first
P_{t}
propagation paths are Line of Sight paths associated with each sensing target, and the remaining
P_{n}
paths are Non-Line of Sight paths. The total number of sensing propagation paths
P_{sen}
is:

P_{sen}=P_{t}+P_{n}\textnormal{ .}

(30)

The fading gain
\tilde{h}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}
of the
p_{t}
th
target is:

\tilde{h}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{sen}}{\kappa_{sen}+1}}\sqrt{\alpha_{p_{t}}}\textnormal{ ,}

(31)

where
\kappa_{sen}
is the sensing Rician K factor, and
\alpha_{p_{t}}
is the power gain associated with the
p_{t}
th
Line of Sight path, defined as:

\alpha_{p_{t}}=\frac{c_{0}^{2}\sigma_{p_{t}}}{(4\pi)^{3}f_{c}^{2}R_{p\textnormal{, }sen}^{4}}\textnormal{ ,}

(32)

where
\sigma_{p_{t}}
is the radar cross-section of the
p_{t}
th
target.

The fading gain
\tilde{h}_{p_{n}\textnormal{, }sen}
of the
p_{n}
th
Non-Line of Sight path is:

\tilde{h}_{p_{n}\textnormal{, }sen}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{P_{n}(\kappa_{sen}+1)}}\zeta_{p_{n}\textnormal{, }sen}\min_{\forall p_{t}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{p_{t}}}\right)\textnormal{ ,}

(33)

where
\zeta_{p_{n}\textnormal{, }sen}
is a complex Gaussian random variable with mean
\mu_{sen}=0
and variance
\sigma_{sen}^{2}=1
, expressed as
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{sen}\textnormal{, }\sigma_{sen}^{2})
.

The Non-Line of Sight power is set relative to the smallest value of
\alpha_{p_{t}}
, which is associated with the weakest target signal. The smallest value of
\alpha_{p_{t}}
is used to ensure that no Non-Line of Sight paths have an average power higher than
\sqrt{\frac{1}{P_{n}(\kappa_{sen}+1)}}
relative to any of the targets. As the system is operating in the mmWave band, the Non-Line of Sight reflections are assumed, on average, to be weaker than the Line of Sight signals.

The range and velocity of the Non-Line of Sight paths,
R_{p_{n}\textnormal{, }sen}
and
V_{p_{n}\textnormal{, }sen}
, are:

R_{p_{n}\textnormal{, }sen}=R_{n\textnormal{, }max}\eta_{\tau\textnormal{, }sen}\textnormal{ ,}

(34)

V_{p_{n}\textnormal{, }sen}=2V_{n\textnormal{, }max}\left(\eta_{\nu\textnormal{, }sen}-0.5\right)\textnormal{ ,}

(35)

where
\eta_{\tau\textnormal{, }sen}
and
\eta_{\nu\textnormal{, }sen}
are random variables following a uniform distribution between 0 and 1, and:

R_{n\textnormal{, }max}=\sqrt[4]{\kappa_{sen}}\max_{\forall p_{t}}\left(R_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}\right)\textnormal{ ,}

(36)

V_{n\textnormal{, }max}=\frac{\Delta fc_{0}}{4f_{c}}\textnormal{ ,}

(37)

The fourth root of
\kappa_{sen}
is present in (
36
) as the power gain is inversely proportional to
R^{4}
, as seen in (
32
). A maximum range is fixed, because the reflected signals having Non-Line of Sight paths associated with delays larger than the maximum in (
36
) are assumed to not significantly interfere, due to the high attenuation associated with a greater range.

4
Received Signal Processing

4.1
Communication Data Detection

The Delay-Doppler Domain received signal
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN\times 1}
can be represented as:

\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}=\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\textnormal{ ,}

(38)

where and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}
is the complex-valued Additive White Gaussian Noise in the Delay-Doppler Domain.

Minimum Mean Square Error demodulation is applied at the receiver, with perfect channel estimation assumed. The vector of estimated symbols
\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{s}\times 1}
is formulated as:

\boldsymbol{\hat{s}}=\boldsymbol{\tilde{G}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}\textnormal{ ,}

(39)

where
\boldsymbol{\tilde{G}}
is:

\boldsymbol{\tilde{G}}=\left(\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}\mathfrak{C}\mathfrak{C}^{H}\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}^{H}+N_{0}\boldsymbol{I}_{MN\times MN}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tilde{H}}\mathfrak{C}\textnormal{ ,}

(40)

where
N_{0}
is the Additive White Gaussian Noise power, and
\boldsymbol{I}_{MN\times MN}
is the
MN\times MN
identity matrix. The estimated symbols are then demodulated to obtain the estimated bits.

4.2
Sensing Target Parameter Estimation

For sensing, it is assumed that the number and directions of the targets are known. A two step sensing method is applied. The first step utilises a data cancellation-based method to estimate the integer indices of the targets. The accuracy of this step is limited by the system parameters. The second step employs Maximum Likelihood detection to estimate the fractional component of the indices estimated in step 1. The resolution of the fractional index estimation is a separate system parameter
N_{ML}
.

The first step is a modified data cancellation method adapted from
[
8
]
. The Delay-Doppler Domain received signal can be represented as in (
38
), or as:

\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}=\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}_{X}\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}\textnormal{ ,}

(41)

where
\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN\times 1}
is the vector containing the propagation path gains at the associated delay-Doppler indices, and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}_{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{MN\times MN}
is an expanded matrix of the Delay-Doppler Domain transmitted signal matrix
\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}
shown in (
42
),

\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}_{X}[\tau^{i}_{1}+M\nu^{i}_{1}\textnormal{, }\tau^{i}_{2}+M\nu^{i}_{2}]=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}[\tau^{i}_{1}-\tau^{i}_{2}+M\textnormal{, }\nu^{i}_{diff}]e^{\frac{j2\pi\nu^{i}_{2}\left(\tau^{i}_{1}-\tau^{i}_{2}\right)}{MN}}\textnormal{, }&\textnormal{if }\tau^{i}_{1}-\tau^{i}_{2}<0\\
\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}[\tau^{i}_{1}-\tau^{i}_{2}\textnormal{, }\nu^{i}_{diff}]e^{\frac{j2\pi\nu^{i}_{2}\left(\tau^{i}_{1}-\tau^{i}_{2}\right)}{MN}}\textnormal{, }&\textnormal{if }\tau^{i}_{1}-\tau^{i}_{2}>0\textnormal{ ,}\end{cases}

(42)

where
\tau^{i}_{1}
and
\tau^{i}_{2}=[0
,
1
, …,
M-1]
,
\nu^{i}_{1}
and
\nu^{i}_{2}=[0
,
1
, …,
N-1]
, and:

\nu^{i}_{diff}=\begin{cases}\nu^{i}_{1}-\nu^{i}_{2}+N\textnormal{, }&\textnormal{if }\nu^{i}_{1}-\nu^{i}_{2}<0\\
\nu^{i}_{1}-\nu^{i}_{2}\textnormal{, }&\textnormal{if }\nu^{i}_{1}-\nu^{i}_{2}>0\textnormal{ .}\end{cases}

(43)

The estimated channel parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\hat{h}}
is calculated as follows:

\boldsymbol{\hat{h}}=\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}_{X}^{H}\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}\textnormal{ .}

(44)

The indices at which the
P_{t}
peak amplitudes of
||\boldsymbol{\hat{h}}||^{2}
occur are the estimated integer delay and Doppler indices
\hat{\tau}^{dc}_{p_{t}}
and
\hat{\nu}^{dc}_{p_{t}}
of the first step, where
||\cdot||
is the Euclidean norm.

The Maximum Likelihood second step used for fractional index estimation applies Maximum Likelihood estimation to the indices adjacent to the
P_{t}
delay and Doppler integer indices gleaned from the first step. The delay indices
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{ml}_{p_{t}}
and Doppler indices
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{ml}_{p_{t}}
considered for the
p_{t}
th
target are:

\boldsymbol{\tau}^{ml}_{p_{t}}=\hat{\tau}^{dc}_{p_{t}}\boldsymbol{1}_{(2N_{ML}+1)\times 1}+\frac{\boldsymbol{n_{\tau}}}{N_{ML}}\textnormal{ ,}

(45)

\boldsymbol{\nu}^{ml}_{p_{t}}=\hat{\nu}^{dc}_{p_{t}}\boldsymbol{1}_{(2N_{ML}+1)\times 1}+\frac{\boldsymbol{n_{\nu}}}{N_{ML}}\textnormal{ ,}

(46)

where
\boldsymbol{1}_{(2N_{ML}+1)\times 1}
is a
\left[(2N_{ML}+1)\times 1\right]
vector of 1,
\boldsymbol{n_{\tau}}=\big[-N_{ML}
,
-N_{ML}+1,
…,
0
, …,
N_{ML}-1
,
N_{ML}\big]
,
\boldsymbol{n_{\nu}}=\big[-N_{ML}
,
-N_{ML}+1
, …,
0
, …,
N_{ML}-1
,
N_{ML}\big]
, and
N_{ML}
is the interpolation or resolution refinement factor.

The Maximum Likelihood algorithm creates a set of equivalent channels
\boldsymbol{\hat{H}}_{\tau^{ml}_{p_{t}}\textnormal{, }\nu^{ml}_{p_{t}}}
, with a gain of 1, for the sets
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{ml}_{p_{t}}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{ml}_{p_{t}}
. The gain is set to 1 since no channel gain estimation is performed. The algorithm calculates the peak associated with each combination, and determines the indices
\hat{\tau}^{ml}_{p_{t}}
and
\hat{\nu}^{ml}_{p_{t}}
estimated by selecting the combination with the largest peak, following
[
38
]

(\hat{\tau}^{ml}_{p_{t}}\textnormal{, }\hat{\nu}^{ml}_{p_{t}})=\arg\max\limits_{\forall\tau^{ml}_{p_{t}}\textnormal{, }\nu^{ml}_{p_{t}}}\frac{||\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\hat{H}}_{\tau^{ml}_{p_{t}}\textnormal{, }\nu^{ml}_{p_{t}}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}||^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\hat{H}}_{\tau^{ml}_{p_{t}}\textnormal{, }\nu^{ml}_{p_{t}}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\hat{H}}_{\tau^{ml}_{p_{t}}\textnormal{, }\nu^{ml}_{p_{t}}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{x}}}\textnormal{ ,}

(47)

where
\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}
is the Delay-Doppler Domain received signal vector.

The indices
\hat{\tau}^{ml}_{p_{t}}
and
\hat{\nu}^{ml}_{p_{t}}
are then used to calculate the target range
\hat{R}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}
and velocity
\hat{V}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}
, following
[
36
]

\hat{R}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}=\frac{\hat{\tau}^{ml}_{p_{t}}c_{0}}{2Mf_{s}}\textnormal{ ,}

(48)

\hat{V}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}=\frac{\hat{\nu}^{ml}_{p_{t}}\Delta fc_{0}}{2Nf_{c}}\textnormal{ .}

(49)

5
Cramér-Rao Bound

Following
[
39
]
, the average unbiased estimator Cramér-Rao Bounds for the associated range and velocity estimation are defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{l\textnormal{, }R}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N_{0}}{P_{avg}|\tilde{h}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}|^{2}\pi^{2}MN(M-1)^{2}}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{2\Delta f}\right)^{2}\textnormal{ ,}

(50)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{l\textnormal{, }V}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N_{0}}{P_{avg}|\tilde{h}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}|^{2}\pi^{2}MN(N-1)^{2}}\left(\frac{c_{0}\Delta f}{2f_{c}}\right)^{2}\textnormal{ ,}

(51)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{l\textnormal{, }R}^{2}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{l\textnormal{, }V}^{2}
are the variance of the target range and of the velocity estimation errors, respectively, while
P_{avg}
is the average power of the transmitted signal.

As the Root Mean Square Error is the metric used for sensing, the standard deviation is utilised:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{l\textnormal{, }R}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{N_{0}}{P_{avg}|\tilde{h}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}|^{2}\pi^{2}MN(M-1)^{2}}}\frac{c_{0}}{2\Delta f}\textnormal{ ,}

(52)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{l\textnormal{, }V}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{N_{0}}{P_{avg}|\tilde{h}_{p_{t}\textnormal{, }sen}|^{2}\pi^{2}MN(N-1)^{2}}}\frac{c_{0}\Delta f}{2f_{c}}\textnormal{ ,}

(53)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{l\textnormal{, }R}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{l\textnormal{, }V}
are the standard deviations of the target range and velocity estimation errors, respectively.

It is important to note that this Cramér-Rao Bound is an average Cramér-Rao Bound, not a true lower bound.

6
Computational Complexity Analysis

This section quantifies the additional complexity introduced by Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space to the modulation and communication demodulation compared to Orthogonal Time Frequency Space, and the complexity of the target parameter estimation methods.

6.1
Additional Modulation and Demodulation Complexity Compared to OTFS

In (
3
), the modulation relies on a multiplication of an
(MN\times N_{s})
matrix by an
(N_{s}\times 1)
vector. The computational complexity of this operation is on the order of
O(MNN_{s})
, for all the Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space schemes.

For Delay Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space,
N_{s}=N_{mult}N
,
1\leq N_{mult}\leq M
, hence the complexity at the minimum and maximum throughputs is
O(MN^{2})
and
O(M^{2}N^{2})
, respectively.

For Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space,
N_{s}=N_{mult}M
,
1\leq N_{mult}\leq N
, hence the complexity at the minimum and maximum throughputs is
O(M^{2}N)
and
O(M^{2}N^{2})
, respectively.

For Delay Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space,
N_{s}=N_{mult}
,
1\leq N_{mult}\leq MN
, hence the complexity at the minimum and maximum throughputs is
O(MN)
and
O(M^{2}N^{2})
, respectively.

The computational complexity of all the Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space schemes is the same for the same throughput. Modulating using (
4
) for Delay Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space and (
9
) for Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space leads to the same complexity.

For Minimum Mean Square Error demodulation, there is an additional matrix multiplication required in (
40
), where the channel matrix
(MN\times MN)
is multiplied by the spreading matrix
(MN\times N_{s})
. This multiplication can be performed once, with the resulting matrix used three times in (
40
). The complexity of the multiplication is on the order of
O(M^{2}N^{2}N_{s})
.

6.2
Complexity of the Sensing Methods

There are
MN\times MN
operations required for the creation of the expanded matrix of the Delay-Doppler Domain transmitted signal (
42
), hence the complexity is
O(M^{2}N^{2})
. The complexity of the matrix multiplication of the data cancellation method (
44
) is
O(MN\times MN\times 1)=O(M^{2}N^{2})
, since an
(MN\times MN)
matrix is multiplied by an
(MN\times 1)
vector. The total complexity of this algorithm is therefore
O(2M^{2}N^{2})
.

The computational complexity of (
47
) for fractional index estimation is
O(2MN)
. This operation is repeated
(2N_{ML}+1)^{2}
times, hence the total complexity of the Maximum Likelihood fractional index estimation is
O(2MN(2N_{ML}+1)^{2})
.

6.3
Discussions

The additional complexity imposed on the modulation by Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is comparable to that of Sparse Code Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space schemes, as both involve the spreading of symbols across the available resources. There exist certain matrix multiplication methods having reduced complexity for sparse matrices, so the additional complexity of Sparse Code Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space may be reduced, depending on the spreading matrix structure. Other multi-user methods, such as directly allocating Delay-Doppler Domain indices to users
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, may have a lower modulation complexity, depending on their modulation structure.

The additional demodulation complexity of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is much lower than that of Delay-Doppler Domain index allocation, because the properties of the dense sequences chosen for Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space allow for a relatively simple demodulation, due to their desirable cross-correlation properties. Although no correlation operations are harnessed for communication detection and demodulation, the low cross-correlation of the sequences mitigates inter-user/inter-symbol inteference. This trend becomes more pronouced when perfectly orthogonal sequences having zero-cross-correlation are employed. Delay-Doppler Domain index allocation requires more complex demodulation methods, for example, iterative methods such as successive interference cancellation, in order to mitigate the inter-symbol and inter-user interference.

Three different configurations of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space were introduced. The multi-user communication performance of Zadoff-Chu Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is similar to that of single user Orthogonal Time Frequency Space at an equal throughput. When fewer users are present, the multi-user throughput is diminished, and Zadoff-Chu Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space has a lower Bit Error Rate than single user Orthogonal Time Frequency Space. The communication performance of Zadoff-Chu sequences is similar for all three Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space spreading configurations. Gold and Hadamard sequences do not consistently outperform single user Orthogonal Time Frequency Space communication. The communication performance of the different configurations relative to Orthogonal Time Frequency Space at the normalised half and full load are summarised in Tables
5
and
6
, respectively.

The three Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space spreading configurations outperform pure Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing for all the velocities considered. Hadamard sequences lead to a superior sensing performance for Delay Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space at high velocities, but the three sequences have a similar performance at lower velocities. Gold sequences increase the sensing performance of Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space at high velocities, whereas Zadoff-Chu sequences lead to the similar sensing performance as pure Orthogonal Time Frequency Space at lower velocities. The sensing performance of Delay Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is similar for all three sequence types at high velocities, but Gold sequences lead to an inferior sensing performance at low velocities. The sensing performance of the different configurations relative to Orthogonal Time Frequency Space at full load are summarised in Tables
7
and
8
, for target velocities of
200
m/s and
110
m/s respectively.

Following these results, Zadoff-Chu Delay Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space and Delay Doppler Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space are the configurations that consistently outperform pure Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing, whilst maintaining a similar communication performance at the same throughput.

The added modulation complexity of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is similar to other Orthogonal Time Frequency Space multi-user methodologies, but the demodulation complexity of Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is lower than that of some other Orthogonal Time Frequency Space multi-user methodologies. Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing can also consistently outperform Orthogonal Time Frequency Space sensing whilst not requiring any additional complexity for target parameter estimation. Hence, Code Division Multiple Access Orthogonal Time Frequency Space is a computationally more attractive multi-user approach for Orthogonal Time Frequency Space Integrated Sensing and Communication than the alternatives in the literature.
