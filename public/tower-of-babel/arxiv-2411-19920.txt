Geometry of the fibers of the multiplication map of deep linear neural networks
Simon Pepin Lehalleur, Richárd Rimányi

Abstract
. We study the geometry of the algebraic set of tuples of composable matrices which multiply to a fixed matrix, using tools from the theory of quiver representations. In particular, we determine its codimension C C and the number θ \theta of its top-dimensional irreducible components. Our solution is presented in three forms: a Poincaré series in equivariant cohomology, a quadratic integer program, and an explicit formula. In the course of the proof, we establish a surprising property: C C and θ \theta are invariant under arbitrary permutations of the dimension vector. We also show that the real log-canonical threshold of the function taking a tuple to the square Frobenius norm of its product is C / 2 C/2 . These results are motivated by the study of deep linear neural networks in machine learning and Bayesian statistics (singular learning theory) and show that deep linear networks are in a certain sense “mildly singular”.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we study the possible ways for a tuple of matrices to multiply to zero (or more generally a fixed matrix). This elementary linear algebra problem leads to some rich geometry and combinatorics, and has applications to deep linear neural networks in statistics and machine learning.

1.1.
Mathematical setting

Fix nonnegative integers
d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}
and consider the vector space
\Rep=\Rep_{\underline{d}}
, consisting of tuples of matrices
A_{1}\in k^{d_{1}\times d_{0}}
,
A_{2}\in k^{d_{2}\times d_{1}},

\ldots,

A_{N}\in k^{d_{N}\times d_{N-1}}
, where
k
is an arbitrary field (say,
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
for the purpose of this introduction). Define
\Sigma=\Sigma^{0}_{\underline{d}}
as the subvariety of
\Rep
consisting of tuples whose product is zero:

\Sigma=\{(A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{N})\in\Rep\ :\ A_{N}A_{N-1}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}=0\}.

The variety
\Sigma
generally has many irreducible components. For example, when
d_{0}=d_{1}=d_{2}=1
, we have two components:
\{A_{1}=0\}
and
\{A_{2}=0\}
. For more complex sequences of
d_{i}
, the variety
\Sigma
typically has numerous components of varying dimensions (see Example
4.3
).

Describing
\Sigma
’s components or even the multiset of their dimensions seems to be a challenging combinatorial problem. In this paper, we determine the codimension
C=\codim_{\Rep}\Sigma
of
\Sigma
in
\Rep
—or equivalently the codimension of its largest dimensional irreducible components—and the number
\theta
of such top-dimensional components.

To study
\Sigma
, we borrow ideas and results from the theory of quiver representations.
The vector space
\Rep
has natural symmetries coming from change of bases, which yields an action of the group
G=\prod_{i=0}^{N}\GL_{d_{i}}
. The starting point of our proofs is that this action of
G
on
\Rep
has finitely many orbits and that the orbits are classified by simple combinatorial data called
Kostant partitions
(Corollary
2.9
). This is a special case of Gabriel’s theorem on representations of Dynkin quivers. Moreover,
\Sigma
is
G
-invariant and so it is the union of some of those orbits. Our main task is thus to understand the geometry and combinatorics of which
G
-orbits occur in
\Sigma
.

In fact, throughout the paper we study and solve the same questions for the variety
\Sigma^{r}_{\underline{d}}
of tuples whose product has rank equal to a given number
r
, as well as for the variety
\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)
of tuples whose product is a fixed matrix
B
. Here,
\mathrm{mult}
is the multiplication map.

\mathrm{mult}:\Rep_{\underline{d}}\to\Mat_{d_{N},d_{0}},(A_{1},\ldots,A_{N})\mapsto A_{N}\cdot A_{N-1}\ldots A_{1}

Those problems easily reduce to the case
r=0
and the variety
\Sigma
above (Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
).

1.2.
The results

The main results of the paper are formulas for
C
and
\theta
. We have three in the main body of the paper, (Theorems
5.5
,
6.1
, and
7.10
) and one in the appendix (Theorem
A.1
).

Theorem
5.5
uses Poincaré series calculations in equivariant cohomology, and produces an explicit formal power series in
q
whose lowest-degree term is
\theta q^{C}
. The resulting formula implies, in particular, that neither
C
nor
\theta
depend on the ordering of the integers
d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}
. This is a priori surprising, since reordering the
d_{i}
’s can change the dimension of
\Rep
and drastically alter both the number of components of
\Sigma
and the multiset of their codimensions. We expect there should be an elementary algebraic proof of this invariance, without topology. However, we do not provide such a proof here, relying instead on Theorem
5.5
(an alternative argument, still relying on equivariant cohomology, is given in Appendix
A
). A key component of our Poincaré series calculation is a remarkable result from Gabriel’s theory of quivers: the indecomposable module corresponding to the longest root in equioriented type A quivers is both projective and injective.

In Theorem
6.1
, we present
C
as the optimal solution of a quadratic integer program and
\theta
as the number of such optimal solutions. The proof relies on the combinatorics and algebra of quivers, as well as the crucial invariance of
C
and
\theta
with respect to the ordering of the integers
d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}
, as discussed earlier.

In Theorem
7.10
, we provide our most explicit formulas for
C
and
\theta
, which are well-suited for asymptotic analysis. The proof hinges on reinterpreting the quadratic integer program as a minimal distance problem: finding the closest lattice point in a simplex to a given point in Euclidean space. Central to this problem is the solution to the closest vector problem for type A root lattices, where the Voronoi cells of these lattices have the expected structure.

1.3.
Deep linear networks in Bayesian statistics and Machine Learning

One motivation for studying the geometry of composable tuples of matrices is
deep linear (neural) networks
and specifically their statistical properties in Bayesian statistics. We give a quick overview here and refer to the introduction of Section
8
for more context.

Deep linear networks (DLNs) are obtained from standard (feedforward, fully-connected) deep neural networks by replacing their non-linear activation functions by identity maps. Despite their simplicity, they are a useful “toy model” in modern deep learning theory (see Section
1.4
). By definition, the weights of a DLN form a tuple of composable matrices, and the function computed by the network is their product, so the parameter space of the model is our friend
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
where
\underline{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{N+1}
records the widths of the layers and
N
is the depth of the network.

A fundamental problem in statistics and machine learning is
density estimation
: given a fixed parametric statistical model and data generated from an unknown “true” probability distribution, infer the “optimal” parameter(s), for which the model is as close as possible to the true distribution. A natural way to measure the quality of this approximation is the relative entropy (or Kullback-Leibler divergence)
K(w)
between the true distribution and the model, considered as a function of the parameters
w
. For regression models such as neural networks,
K(w)
is the
mean square error population loss function
. Classical statistical learning theory often focuses on
regular
statistical models, in which there is a unique optimal parameter
w^{*}
where
K(w)
has a unique non-degenerate minimum. However, deep neural networks (linear or not) and other parametric models in modern machine learning are almost always
singular
. For a DLN where the true distribution is given by a matrix
B
, we write
K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B}:\Rep\to\mathbb{R}
for the corresponding relative entropy function:

K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B}(A_{*})=\|\mathrm{mult}(A)-B\|^{2}_{2}.

The set of optimal parameters is
(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})^{-1}(0)=\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)
, so the results of this paper precisely describe the geometry of the bottom of the loss landscape of DLNs.

Singular Learning Theory, a theory established by S. Watanabe
[
Wat09
,
Wat18
,
Wat24
]
, studies the asymptotic performance of (real analytic) singular models for Bayesian density estimation. A key role is played by the
real log-canonical threshold

\mathrm{rlct}(K)
of the real analytic function
K
at its optimal parameters. Singular learning theory also provides a tool to
estimate

\mathrm{rlct}(K)
from data, the
local learning coefficient

[
LFW+24
]
. The local learning coefficient has recently lead to very interesting applications to developmental interpretability of deep learning models throughout training
[
CLM+23
,
HWFR+24
,
WHvW+24
]
.

The
exact, theoretical
computation of
\mathrm{rlct}(K)
in realistic machine learning scenarios is generally impossible. However, for deep linear networks,
\mathrm{rlct}(K)
has been calculated by M. Aoyagi
[
Aoy24
]
and this was used in
[
LFW+24
]
to calibrate the accuracy of the local learning coefficient. The original formula of Aoyagi is complicated, and we started this project in order to understand it better. Combined with our results, it takes a rather simple form (Theorem
8.6
):

\mathrm{rlct}(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})=\frac{\codim(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})^{-1}(0)}{2}=\frac{\codim\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)}{2}.

For a general real analytic function
F
, we only have the inequality

\mathrm{rlct}(F)\leq\frac{\codim F^{-1}(0)}{2}.

This shows that the singularities of
K
are in some sense quite mild. Statistically speaking, this means that deep linear networks are “mildly singular”.

1.4.
Related works

The geometry and combinatorics of representations of Dynkin quivers, and especially equioriented type A quivers, is a rich topic in combinatorial algebraic geometry and representation theory
[
AD85
,
ADK81
,
AD80
,
FR02
,
BFR05
,
RWY18
]
, including connections with Schubert varieties and
standard monomial theory

[
Zel85
,
LM98
,
KR15
,
KMS06
]
. These works mainly focus on the geometry of individual orbit closures and not on possibly reducible unions of orbits like
\Sigma
. Interesting exception are the papers
[
CS81
,
MS83
]
on the
variety of complexes
, consisting of tuples of matrices such that the product of any two consecutive matrices is
0
.

Deep linear networks have been studied extensively in machine learning. The papers
[
TKB20
]
and
[
SB24
]
are closely related to ours. In
[
TKB20
]
, the authors start the study of the geometry of the fibers
\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)
. The paper
[
SB24
]
goes much further, including proofs using linear algebra of a number of results on the geometry of
\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)
which in the perspective of this paper are part of the basic representation theory of the quiver
Q
(which we collected in Sections
2
,
3
,
4
).

A large part of the literature on DLNs is concerned with other critical points of the loss functions
[
Kaw16
,
LK17
,
AMG24
]
and with the training dynamics by gradient descent
[
ZLM22
,
JT19
,
DAP+24
,
ACGH19
,
SMG14
,
JGc+22
,
ML24
]
. Our results do not directly bear on these problems, but we hope that the quiver representation approach will prove helpful to study them.

2.2.
Quiver geometry

Let
k
be a field—we are mostly interested in
k=\mathbb{R}
and
k=\mathbb{C}
. For our quiver
Q
and a fixed
dimension vector

\underline{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{N})\in\mathbb{N}^{N+1}
, define

\Rep_{\underline{d}}(k)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Mat_{d_{i},d_{i-1}}(k)=\prod_{a}\Hom(k^{t(a)},k^{h(a)})

where
\Mat_{d,d^{\prime}}(k)
is the vector space of
d\times d^{\prime}
matrices with entries in
k
and
a=(t(a)\to h(a))
runs through the arrows of
Q
.

We consider
\Rep_{\underline{d}}(k)
as an algebraic variety over
k
, or, more precisely, as the set of
k
-points of the
k
-algebraic variety
\Rep_{\underline{d}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Mat_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}
, which is isomorphic to the affine space
\mathbb{A}^{D}_{k}
with

(1)

\dim_{k}\Rep_{\underline{d}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i-1}d_{i}.

When the role of the field
k
is not important we will simply write
\Rep_{\underline{d}}=\Rep_{\underline{d}}(k)
.

Remark 2.2
.

In general, when
X
is an algebraic variety defined over
\mathbb{R}
(or over any non algebraically closed field), it is important to distinguish between
X
and the
real algebraic set

X(\mathbb{R})
of real points of
X
. For instance,
X(\mathbb{R})
may well be empty. Think of
X=\{x^{2}=-1\}\subset\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
However, in the situation of interest in this paper, this distinction will be mostly immaterial, in a sense that is made precise in Section
3.2
(see however Remark
4.7
). For this reason, we encourage readers less familiar with algebraic geometry over non-closed fields to ignore this distinction at first pass.

The algebraic matrix group
G=G_{\underline{d}}:=\prod_{i=0}^{N}\GL_{d_{i}}
acts on the representation space
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
as follows: for
g=(P_{0},\ldots,P_{N})\in G
and
A_{*}=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})\in\Rep_{\underline{d}}
, we have

g\cdot A_{*}:=(P_{1}A_{1}P_{0}^{-1}\ ,\ P_{2}A_{2}P_{1}^{-1}\ ,\ldots,\ P_{N}A_{N}P_{N-1}^{-1}).

Remark 2.3
.

The group
G
has two natural subgroups

G_{\mathrm{in}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}\GL_{d_{i}}\subset G\qquad\qquad G_{\mathrm{out}}:=\GL_{d_{0}}\times\GL_{d_{N}}.

We have
G=G_{\mathrm{in}}\times G_{\mathrm{out}}
and we write
\pi_{\mathrm{out}}:G\to G_{\mathrm{out}},(P_{0},\ldots,P_{N})\mapsto(P_{0},P_{N})
for the projection onto the outer factors. The action of
G_{\mathrm{in}}
corresponds to change of bases of the vector spaces
k^{d_{i}}
. Note that
G_{\mathrm{out}}
also naturally acts on
\Mat_{d_{N},d_{0}}
by

(P_{0},P_{N})\cdot B:=P_{N}BP_{0}^{-1}.

2.3.
Quiver algebra

A
Q
-module, or
Q
-representation, is a chain of finite dimensional vector spaces and linear maps

V_{0}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}V_{1}\xrightarrow{f_{2}}V_{2}\xrightarrow{f_{3}}\ldots\xrightarrow{f_{N-1}}V_{N-1}\xrightarrow{f_{N}}V_{N}.

Its dimension vector is defined to be
\underline{d}=(\dim(V_{i}))_{i=0,\ldots,N}
. In other words,
Q
-modules are to elements of
\Rep
what linear maps between finite dimensional vector spaces are to matrices.

A morphism between
Q
-modules
(V_{*},f_{*})
and
(V_{*}^{\prime},f_{*}^{\prime})
is a collection of linear maps
\phi_{i}:V_{i}\to V^{\prime}_{i}
that commute with
f_{*}
and
f^{\prime}_{*}
:

f^{\prime}_{i}\circ\phi_{i-1}=\phi_{i}\circ f_{i}.

Many standard notions of linear algebra (of vector spaces and modules over rings) extend to
Q
-modules and their morphisms, such as: exact sequence, isomorphism, direct sum, subspace, irreducibility (no non-trivial subspace), indecomposability (not a direct sum in a non-trivial way),
\Ext(-,-):=\Ext^{1}(-,-)
, injective module, projective module. Moreover,
\Ext
, injective and projective behave quite simply (compared e.g. to modules over general rings).

In more abstract terminology, the category of
Q
-modules is a
k
-linear abelian category which is Hom-finite, hereditary (no higher Ext). Injective and projective objects can also be completely classified (we will not need this).

The reader may develop familiarity with these concepts by verifying

•

that
k\xrightarrow{\id}k
is indecomposable but not irreducible;

•

\Ext((k\to 0),(0\to k))=k
; and

•

V_{i}=k
,
A_{i}=\id
(
\forall i
) is both an injective
Q
-module and a projective
Q
-module.

The Krull–Schmidt theorem holds for
Q
-modules: every
Q
-module is the direct sum of indecomposable ones, in a unique (up to permutation) way.

2.4.
Quiver geometry vs algebra. Gabriel’s theorem

Notice that elements of
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
can be made into
Q
-modules with
V_{i}=k^{d_{i}}
. We call the following well known and easy statement a theorem because of its importance.

Theorem 2.4
.

•

Every
Q
-module is isomorphic with an element of
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
for some
\underline{d}
.

•

Two elements of
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
are isomorphic as
Q
-modules if and only if they are in the same
G
-orbit.

Hence, listing the
G
-orbits of
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
is the same as listing the isomorphism types of
Q
-modules with dimension vector
\underline{d}
.

For a general quiver, this set of isomorphism types is infinite; for example, for the Jordan quiver with one vertex and one loop, this is precisely the set of normal forms for matrices under conjugation. However, for Dynkin quivers, this set is finite and can be described explicitly. This is the content of Gabriel’s theorem which we state in our special case:

Theorem 2.5
.

For
0\leq i\leq j\leq N
let
M_{ij}
be the
Q
-module

0\to 0\to\ldots 0\to k\xrightarrow{\id}k\xrightarrow{\id}\ldots\xrightarrow{\id}k\xrightarrow{\id}k\to 0\to 0\ldots\to 0,

where the first and last
k
’s are
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
. The
M_{ij}
’s form a complete set of indecomposable
Q
-modules (up to isomorphism).

Definition 2.6
.

Define

\mathcal{M}_{\underline{d}}:=\{\underline{m}=(m_{ij})_{0\leq i\leq j\leq N}\ |\ \forall i\leq j,\ m_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }\forall k,\ d_{k}=\sum_{i\leq k\leq j}m_{ij}\},

\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}:=\{\underline{m}=(m_{ij})_{0\leq i\leq j\leq N}\ |\ \forall i\leq j,\ m_{ij}\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\forall k,\ d_{k}=\sum_{i\leq k\leq j}m_{ij}\}.

Elements of the
\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}
are called Kostant partitions for
\underline{d}
. (We think of an
\underline{m}
as an upper triangular matrix whose rows and columns are indexed by
0,\ldots,N
.). Note that the set
\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}
of Kostant partitions is finite (unlike
\mathcal{M}_{\underline{d}}
).

We also use the notation
\underline{m}\vdash\underline{d}
for “
\underline{m}
is a Kostant partition of
\underline{d}
”.

Example 2.7
.

The set
\mathcal{M}^{+}_{(2,2,2)}
has 6 elements:

\begin{pNiceArray},\begin{pNiceArray},\begin{pNiceArray},\begin{pNiceArray},\begin{pNiceArray},\begin{pNiceArray}.

Remark 2.8
.

There are alternative terminologies for Kostant partitions in the literature on quiver representations:
lace arrays
,
multiplicity patterns
.

Corollary 2.9
.

The sets of the following objects are in bijection:

•

isomorphism classes of
Q
-modules with dimension vector
\underline{d}
;

•

G
-orbits of
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
;

•

Kostant partitions for
\underline{d}
.

Explicitly, the Kostant partition
\underline{m}
corresponds to the isomorphism class of the
Q
-module
\bigoplus_{0\leq i\leq j\leq N}m_{ij}M_{ij}
, and to the
G
-orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}}\subset\Rep_{\underline{d}}
consisting of all representations isomorphic to this
Q
-module.

Proof.

Follows from the Krull-Schmidt theorem for
Q
-modules together with Theorems
2.4
,
2.5
.
∎

To make this corollary concretely useful, we need to solve two basic problems:

•

Given a Kostant partition
\underline{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}
, construct explicitly tuples in the
G
-orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}}\subseteq\Rep_{\underline{d}}
.

•

Given
A_{*}\in\Rep_{\underline{d}}
, determine the Kostant partition corresponding to the orbit
G\cdot A_{*}
.

We solve the first one in the next section and the second in Section
3
.

Kostant partitions can be thought of as equivalence classes of lace diagrams:

Lemma 2.10
.

The map
\underline{m}(-):\mathrm{Lace}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}
is invariant under the action of
S_{\underline{d}}
and induces a bijection

S_{\underline{d}}\backslash\mathrm{Lace}_{\underline{d}}\simeq\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}.

Proof.

The map
\underline{m}(-)
is clearly invariant under the action and thus induces a map

S_{\underline{d}}\backslash\mathrm{Lace}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}.

Given a Kostant partition, one can construct a lace diagram by adding inductively intervals of length
m_{ij}
to the diagram in any order, using the first dots still available in each column. This shows that the map is surjective. Given two lace diagrams with the same Kostant partition, one can first choose a length-preserving bijection between their intervals, and then permute the dots according to that bijection. This shows that the map is injective.
∎

We can interpret the choice of a lace diagram as the choice of a base point for the associated orbit.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
L
be a lace diagram. Identify the
N+1
columns of dots of
L
as the standard basis vectors in the
N+1
vector spaces
k^{d_{0}},\ldots,k^{d_{N}}
. The line segments of
L
then encode linear transformations between these spaces by specifying how a basis vector is mapped to another basis vector to the right. If a dot has no segment coming out of it to the right, then that basis vector is mapped to 0. The corresponding matrices yield an element
A_{*}(L)\in\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}(L)}\subset\Rep_{\underline{d}}
.

Note that the resulting matrices are very special: they are
partial permutation matrices
:

•

all coefficients are
0
or
1
, and

•

every line and column has at most one
1
.

The combinatorics of lace diagrams are quite rich (see for instance
[
BFR05
,
KMS06
,
RWY18
]
). In this paper, we use them in an elementary way in the proof of Theorem
6.1
, see in particular Lemma
6.4
and Figure
1
.

3.
Rank patterns and combinatorics of orbits

3.1.
Rank patterns

Kostant partitions are the natural combinatorial codes for
G
-orbits of
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
—in fact this holds for an arbitrary oriented Dynkin quiver if we replace “intervals” with “positive roots of the same named root system” in Theorem
2.5
. However, for equioriented type A quivers there is another natural combinatorial code: rank patterns. Define

\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}:=\{\underline{r}=(r_{ij})_{0\leq i\leq j\leq N}\ |\ \forall i\leq j,r_{ij}\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }r_{ii}=d_{i}\}

to be the set of upper triangular arrays of size
N+1
with non-negative integer entries, whose diagonal encodes our fixed dimension vector. We call elements
\underline{r}\in\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}

rank patterns
.

Proposition 3.1
.

•

The maps

\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{M}_{\underline{d}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}
defined by

\begin{array}[]{ll}m_{ij}(\underline{r})=&r_{ij}-r_{i,j+1}-r_{i-1,j}+r_{i-1,j+1}\\
&(\text{with the convention }r_{ij}=0\text{ if }i<0\text{ or }j>N),\\
r_{ij}(\underline{m})=&\sum_{k\leq i\leq j\leq l}m_{kl}\end{array}

are well defined, and are inverses of each other.

•

Let
\underline{m}
and
\underline{r}
correspond to each other at the bijection above, and assume that
\underline{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\underline{d}}^{+}
. The
G
-orbit in
\Rep_{\underline{d}}(k)
corresponding to
\underline{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}
is

\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}:=\{(A_{1},\ldots,A_{N})\in\Rep_{\underline{d}}\ |\ \forall\ 0\leq i<j\leq N,\ \mathrm{rank}(A_{j}\cdots A_{i+1})=r_{ij}\}.

That is, the
G
-orbits of
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
are determined by rank conditions of the possible compositions in
Q
, and the rank conditions which occur are exactly the ones that correspond to
\mathcal{M}_{\underline{d}}^{+}
under the bijection. We leave the easy combinatorial proof to the reader. Write

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{orb}}_{\underline{d}}=\{\underline{r}\in\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}\ |\ \forall i,j,\ r_{ij}-r_{i,j+1}-r_{i-1,j}+r_{i-1,j+1}\geq 0\}

for the set of rank patterns corresponding to Kostant partitions and hence giving an alternative parametrization of orbits.

The characterization of the rank in terms of vanishing of minors, which are polynomials in the coefficients, implies that the rank is lower semi-continuous in the Zariski topology and so that
\mathcal{R}_{\underline{r}}
is a Zariski locally closed subvariety of
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
. Note that the condition defining
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
does not involve the diagonal
r_{ii}=d_{i}
, which is only used to encode the dimension vector
\underline{d}
into
\underline{r}
and to make some formulas cleaner.

Example 3.2
.

The rank pattern corresponding to the lace diagram (
2
) and the Kostant partition in Section
2.5
is

\underline{r}=\begin{pNiceArray}_{0\ldots 3,0\ldots 3}.

The orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
is not empty, of dimension
72
(as follows from Equation
4
below). If we changed
r_{13}=2
to
r^{\prime}_{13}=1
in this rank pattern (recall that the row and column indices run from
0
to
3
), then it would not correspond to a Kostant partition since we would then have

r_{22}-r_{23}-r_{12}+r^{\prime}_{13}=5-4-3+1<0

and hence we would have
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}=\emptyset
.

Notation.
Since Kostant partitions and corresponding rank patterns are equivalent combinatorial codes; both parametrize orbits. When we name an orbit with its rank pattern, we use the notation
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
, when we name an orbit with the Kostant partition
\underline{m}
we will simply call it
\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}}
.

3.2.
The choice of base field

We clarify Remark
2.2
about the choice of base field
k
, and show in particular that the geometry we are interested is essentially the same over
k=\mathbb{R}
and
k=\mathbb{C}
(see however Remark
4.7
for an interesting difference). In this section only, we let
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
(resp.
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
) stand for the corresponding
k
-variety and distinguish it from its set of rational points
\Rep_{\underline{d}}(k)
(resp.
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}(k)
).

Theorem 3.3
.

(1)

For any field
k
and
\underline{r}\in\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}
, we have

\underline{r}\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{orb}}_{\underline{d}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}\neq\emptyset\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}(k)\neq\emptyset.

(2)

For every
\underline{r}\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{orb}}_{\underline{d}}
, the
k
-rational points
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}(k)
are Zariski dense in
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
.

(3)

When
k=\mathbb{R}
(resp.
k=\mathbb{C}
), the real algebraic set
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}(\mathbb{R})
is a real analytic (resp. complex analytic) manifold of dimension equal to the dimension of
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
as an algebraic variety.

Proof.

We prove Claim (
1
). The implication
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}\neq\emptyset\Leftarrow\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}(k)\neq\emptyset
is obvious. If
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}(\bar{k})\neq\emptyset
so by Proposition
3.1
applied over
\bar{k}
we have
\underline{r}\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{orb}}_{\underline{d}}
. Finally, if
\underline{r}\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{orb}}_{\underline{d}}
, then
\underline{m}(\underline{r})\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\underline{d}}
by Proposition
3.1
. Then by choosing a lace diagram
L
encoding
\underline{m}(\underline{r})
and following Section
2.5
, we obtain a collection of partial permutation matrices
A_{*}(L)
in the
G
-orbit corresponding to
\underline{m}(\underline{r})
. Since
0,1\in k
, this shows
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}(k)=\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}(\underline{r})}(k)\neq\emptyset
.

Claim (
1
) then implies that
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}\simeq G/H
as
k
-varieties, where
H
is the stabilizer group of the orbit. This implies the other statements and finishes the proof.
∎

3.3.
Voight lemma and the codimension of orbits

[
Voi77
]
.
Another crucial relation between the geometry and algebra of quivers is

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
M
, considered as a
Q
-module, be an element of a
G
-orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset\Rep_{\underline{d}}(k)
. Then a normal slice to
\mathcal{O}
at
M
in
\Rep_{\underline{d}}(k)
is isomorphic to
\Ext(M,M)
.

While the isomorphism holds as an isomorphism of representations of the
G
-stabilizer group of
M
, we will only use the statement as isomorphism of vector spaces.

The first consequence of Lemma
3.4
is that a formula for the codimension of an orbit is a (non-symmetric) bilinear form, in the components of the Kostant partition.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\underline{m}\in\mathcal{M}_{\underline{d}}^{+}
be a Kostant partition and
\underline{r}=\underline{r}(\underline{m})\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{orb}}_{\underline{d}}
the corresponding rank pattern. Then

(4)

\codim_{\Rep}(\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq u\leq j\leq v\leq N}m_{(i-1)(j-1)}m_{uv}

Proof.

Let
M
be a
Q
-module in
\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}}
then by Lemma
3.4
we have

\codim_{\Rep}(\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}})=\dim\Ext(M,M)=\dim\Ext(\oplus_{ij}m_{ij}M_{ij},\oplus_{uv}m_{uv}M_{uv})\\
=\sum_{ij}\sum_{uv}m_{ij}m_{uv}\dim\Ext(M_{ij},M_{uv}),

where an easy calculation (cf.
[
FR02
, Lemma 4.4]
) gives

(5)

\dim\Ext(M_{ij},M_{uv})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i+1\leq u\leq j+1\leq v,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The formula in terms of
\underline{r}=\underline{r}(\underline{m})
follows from the definition of
\underline{r}(-)
(Proposition
3.1
).
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Alternatively, one can explicitly compute the Lie algebra of the stabilizer group of a point in
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
and deduce equation (
4
)
[
ADK81
, Lemma 3.2]
.

3.4.
The effect of additional longest roots

For a dimension vector
\underline{d}
, or a rank pattern
\underline{r}
let
\underline{d}+p
,
\underline{r}+p
be defined by adding
p
to every component. Consider
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}\subset\Rep_{\underline{d}}
,
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}+p}\subset\Rep_{\underline{d}+p}
. Note that the Kostant partition corresponding to
\underline{r}+p
is obtained from the Kostant partition
\underline{m}
of
\underline{r}
by adding
p
to
m_{0N}
and not changing the other components. In terms of lace diagrams, this consists of adding
p
longest intervals.

Theorem 3.7
.

The normal slices of
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}+p}
are isomorphic. In particular, the codimensions of these two orbits (in different representation spaces) are equal.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.4
, we have that the normal slice of
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}+p}
is

(6)

\Ext(M\oplus pM_{0N},M\oplus pM_{0N})\\
=\Ext(M,M)\oplus p\Ext(M,M_{0N})\oplus p\Ext(M_{0N},M)\oplus p^{2}\Ext(M_{0N},M_{0N})\\
=\Ext(M,M),

which is the normal slice to
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
.
The last equality holds because
M_{0N}
is both an injective and a projective
Q
-module
[
FR02
]
.
∎

3.5.
Orbit hierarchy

We equip
\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}
with the partial order induced by entry-wise comparison: for
\underline{r},\underline{s}\in\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}
, we write

\underline{s}\leq\underline{r}\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{\Leftrightarrow}}\qquad\forall\ i\leq j,\ s_{ij}\leq r_{ij}.

In particular,
\underline{0}
is the minimal element of
\mathcal{R}_{\underline{d}}
for this partial order. The lower semi-continuity of the rank implies that
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{s}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\rk=\underline{r}}\Rightarrow\underline{s}\leq\underline{r}
. The converse also holds, and can be established using the combinatorics of lace diagrams:

Theorem 3.8
.

[
AD80
]

If
\underline{r},\underline{s}\in\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{orb}}_{\underline{d}}
, then
\mathcal{O}_{\rk=\underline{s}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
if and only if
\underline{s}\leq\underline{r}
.

Remark 3.9
.

Theorem
3.8
is also proven in
[
SB24
]
.
It is generalized to the case of non equioriented type A quivers in
[
AD85
]
and to equioriented type D quivers in
[
AD84
]
. The remaining Dynkin cases seem to be open.

The algebraic geometry and the singularities of the orbit closures
\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{\rk=\underline{r}}
have been studied extensively, see Section
1.4
for references.

4.
Multiplication map and related loci

Consider the multiplication map

\mathrm{mult}:\Rep_{\underline{d}}\to\Mat_{d_{N},d_{0}},(A_{1},\ldots,A_{N})\mapsto A_{N}\cdot A_{N-1}\ldots A_{1}

We are interested in the geometry of the map
\mathrm{mult}
and its fibers. Note that, for
g=(P_{0},\ldots,P_{N})\in G
and
A_{*}\in\Rep_{\underline{d}}
, we have

(7)

5.
Poincaré series

In this section we present a Poincaré series calculation that results in calculating the dimension and the number of top-dimensional components of
\Sigma_{\underline{d}}^{r}
. We will use freely some tools from algebraic topology, namely equivariant cohomology of topological spaces acted upon by Lie groups. Those are standard tools in the study of quiver representations
[
Rei10
,
Rim13
]
.

5.1.
Pochammer symbols and the main statement

Definition 5.1
.

For a non-negative integer
s
define

\mathcal{P}_{s}=\frac{1}{(1-q)(1-q^{2})\cdots(1-q^{s})},

the inverse of the
q
-Pochhammer symbol. We will identify this function with its
|q|<1
formal power series. For a multiset
\underline{h}=\{h_{1},h_{2},\ldots\}
of nonnegative integers (i.e., a dimension vector or a Kostant partition), define

\mathcal{P}_{\underline{h}}=\prod_{i}\mathcal{P}_{h_{i}}.

Given an evenly graded vector space
V=\oplus_{n}V_{2n}
, its Poincaré series is the formal power series
P(V)=\sum_{n}\dim(V_{2n})q^{n}
. The inverse Pochammer symbol has an interpretation the generating series of the number of partitions with at most
s
parts, and consequenctly as a Poincaré series in equivariant cohomology:

\mathcal{P}_{s}=\sum_{\mu_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\mu_{s}\geq 0}q^{|\mu|}=P(\mathbb{C}[c_{1},\ldots,c_{s}])=P(H^{*}(B\!\GL_{s}(\mathbb{C})))=P(H^{*}_{\GL_{s}}(\pt)).

Here we have
\deg(c_{i})=2i
.

For later purposes we recall the infinite series expansion of the infinite Pochhammer symbol
(x;q)_{\infty}=\prod_{s=1}^{\infty}(1-xq^{s})
and its inverse
[
AAR99
, Cor. 10.2.2]
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(x;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{s}x^{s}

(8)

\displaystyle(x;q)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{s}q^{\binom{s}{2}}\mathcal{P}_{s}x^{s}.

We encode the codimensions of the orbits into a power series as follows:

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\underline{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{N+1}
and
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

Q_{\underline{d}}^{r}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{m}\vdash\underline{d}\\
m_{0N}=r\end{subarray}}q^{\codim(\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}})}\mathcal{P}_{\underline{m}}

The role of
Q_{\underline{d}}^{r}
in our study is clarified by:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
0\leq r\leq\min\underline{d}
, let
C
be the codimension of
\overline{\Sigma}^{r}_{\underline{d}}\subset\Rep_{\underline{d}}
and
\theta
the number of its top-dimensional irreducible components. Then

Q^{r}_{\underline{d}}=\theta q^{C}+\text{higher order terms}.

Proof.

Let the lowest degree term of
Q^{r}_{\underline{d}}
be
nq^{c}
(temporary notation). Since
\mathcal{P}_{\underline{m}}=1+
higher degree terms, the definition of
Q^{r}_{\underline{d}}
implies that
c
is the codimension of

\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{m}\vdash\underline{d}\\
m_{0N}=r\end{subarray}}\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}}
. As we saw in Section
4
, this union is precisely
\Sigma^{r}_{\underline{d}}
, and we have moreover
\codim\Sigma^{r}_{\underline{d}}=\codim\overline{\Sigma}^{r}_{\underline{d}}
. Moreover,
n
is the number of orbits whose codimension is minimal. However, if an orbit closure is not a component, then it cannot have minimal codimension among orbit closures. Therefore,
n
is also the number of the minimal codimensional
irreducible components
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

In the theory of characteristic classes of singularities—not discussed in this paper except in Appendix
A
—the sum
\sum_{s=0}^{r}Q^{s}_{\underline{d}}
is the Poincaré series of the ideal of cohomology classes “universally supported” on
\overline{\Sigma}_{\underline{d}}^{r}
.

The main result of this section is then a purely algebraic formula for
Q^{r}_{\underline{d}}
.

Theorem 5.5
.

We have

(9)

Q^{r}_{\underline{d}}=\mathcal{P}_{r}\cdot\sum_{s=0}^{\min\underline{d}-r}(-1)^{s}q^{\binom{s}{2}}\mathcal{P}_{s}\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}-r-s}.

We will prove Theorem
5.5
in Section
5.3
.
Combined with Lemma
5.3
, this Theorem provides formulas for

and
\theta
which are not easy to write in closed form but can be implemented efficiently in a computer algebra system.

Example 5.6
.

The series

Q^{0}_{(2,2,2)}=q^{3}+6q^{4}+\ldots,\qquad Q^{0}_{(2,3,2)}=2q^{4}+7q^{5}+\ldots,\qquad Q^{0}_{(2,4,2)}=q^{4}+4q^{5}+\ldots

correspond to the special cases discussed in Example

.
We have

and hence

has 2 top-dimensional components, of codimension

(and hence, dimension

5.2.
The spectral sequence

The essence of the proof of Theorem
5.5
in Section
5.3
is the understanding of a spectral sequence. We introduce this spectral sequence and the relevant arguments in the following known easier setting.

Theorem 5.7
.

We have

(10)

\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}=\sum_{\underline{m}\vdash\underline{d}}q^{\codim(\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}})}\mathcal{P}_{\underline{m}}.

This innocent-looking result is a special case of general combinatorial identities in the Donaldson-Thomas theory of arbitrary finite quivers
[
Rei10
, Theorem 2.1]
,
[
Rim13
,
RWY18
]
. For
N=1
(i.e.
Q=(\bullet\to\bullet)
), it is equivalent to the well-known
Durfee square identity
, or, the
pentagon identity of quantum dilogarithms
.

Proof of Theorem
5.7
, following
[
Rim13
]
.

We saw in Section
2.3
that the group
G_{\underline{d}}=

\prod_{i=0}^{N}\GL_{d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
acts on
\Rep_{\underline{d}}(\mathbb{C})
with finitely many orbits
\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}}
indexed by Kostant partitions. Let
F_{i}
denote the union of orbits with codimension at most
i
. Let us apply the Borel construction (
B_{G}X=BG\times_{G}X
) to the filtration

F_{0}\subset F_{1}\subset F_{2}\subset\ldots\subset F_{\dim(\Rep_{\underline{d}})}=\Rep_{\underline{d}}(\mathbb{C}).

We will study the (
\mathbb{Z}
-coefficient) cohomology spectral sequence associated with this filtration
[
AB83
,
Kaz97
,
Rim13
]
.

Stabilizer subgroups of different points in the same orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}}
are conjugate, hence isomorphic. It is known (
[
FR02
, Prop 3.6]
) that these stabilizers are homotopy equivalent to
G_{\underline{m}}=\prod_{ij}\GL_{m_{ij}}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 5.8
.

[
Kaz97
,
Rim13
]

The spectral sequence
E_{*}^{pq}
degenerates at
E_{1}
, it converges to
H^{*}(BG_{\underline{d}})
, and

E_{1}^{pq}=\bigoplus_{\codim_{\Rep,\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}}=p}H^{q}(BG_{\underline{m}}).

(where
\codim_{\mathbb{R}}=2\codim_{\mathbb{C}}
is the real codimension).

Proof of the Lemma.

The convergence claim follows from the fact that
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
is (equivariantly) contractible. The formula for
E_{1}^{pq}
follows from the usual description of the
E_{1}
page as relative cohomologies, if one applies excision and the Thom isomorphism to these relative cohomologies, see details in
[
Kaz97
,
Rim13
]
. The degeneration claim follows from the fact that all orbits have even real codimension and that

has no odd cohomology—hence all differentials of the spectral sequence are 0.
∎

Lemma
5.8
implies that

\rk\left(H^{2n}(BG_{\underline{d}})\right)=\sum_{p+q=2n}\rk(E_{1}^{pq}).

Multiplying this identity with

and summing for all

—using Lemma

—we obtain

(11)

P(H^{*}(BG_{\underline{d}}))=\sum_{\underline{m}\vdash\underline{d}}q^{\codim(\mathcal{O}_{\underline{m}})}P(H^{*}(BG_{\underline{m}})),

and the statement of Theorem

∎

In Appendix

Remark 5.11
.

One reason that Corollary

is surprising is that the geometry of

undergoes significant changes when the components of

are permuted. First of all, it is clear from equation (

) that the

of

is not permutation invariant, so that it is only the codimension of

which is invariant and not its dimension. For example,

\dim(\Rep_{(2,2,3)})=10\neq 12=\dim(\Rep_{(2,3,2)}).

Moreover, the

of irreducible components of

is not permutation invariant. Using Corollary

has 2 irreducible components (both of codimension

) while
\Sigma_{(2,2,3)}^{0}
and
\Sigma_{(3,2,2)}^{0}
have 3 irreducible components (2 of codimension 4 and 1 of codimension 6).

While Theorem

to a quadratic integer program. The argument is
almost
independent of Section

6.1.
Top-dimensional components of
\Sigma^{0}_{\underline{d}}
and a QIP

For a dimension vector
\underline{d}=(d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{N})
let
\underline{d}^{\prime}=(d^{\prime}_{0}\leq d^{\prime}_{1}\leq\ldots\leq d^{\prime}_{N})
be the same multiset, but arranged (weakly) increasingly. Consider
N
-tuples of non-negative integers
\underline{e}=(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{N})
and the following quadratic integer program for them:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\min&\displaystyle G_{\underline{d}}(\underline{e})=\sum_{1\leq j\leq i\leq N}e_{i}(e_{j}+d^{\prime}_{j}-d^{\prime}_{j-1})\\[7.22743pt]
\text{s.t.}&\displaystyle\ e_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}=d^{\prime}_{0}.\end{array}

Theorem 6.1
.

The codimension of the top-dimensional components of
\Sigma^{0}_{\underline{d}}
is the minimal value of (
6.1
). The number of top-dimensional components of
\Sigma^{0}_{\underline{d}}
is the number of times (
6.1

Assume that the dimension vector

Proof.

Induction on
N
(the number of columns).∎

Let

7.
Explicit formulas for the codimensions of loci

In this section, we make use of the rather simple structure of (

) and give an explicit solution to it. The final result is stated in Theorem
7.10

Lemma 7.1
.

The function

l\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}\mapsto\left(\sum_{i=0}^{l}d^{\prime}_{i}\right)-ld^{\prime}_{l}

is weakly decreasing and positive for
l=1
.

Proof.

This follows from

\left(\sum_{i=0}^{l}d^{\prime}_{i}\right)-(l+1)d^{\prime}_{l+1}-\left(\left(\sum_{i=0}^{l}d^{\prime}_{i}\right)-ld^{\prime}_{l}\right)=l(d^{\prime}_{l}-d^{\prime}_{l+1})\leq 0

since
\underline{d}^{\prime}
is weakly decreasing. The value for
l=1
is
d^{\prime}_{0}>0
.
∎

Depending of
\underline{d}
, this function may or may not become negative for
l=N
. We can, however, always make the following definition.

Definition 7.2
.

Define

m=\max\left\{l\ |\ \sum_{i=0}^{l}d^{\prime}_{i}\geq ld^{\prime}_{l}\right\}\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}.

To solve (
6.1
), we first define an integer
D
and vectors
\underline{s},\hat{\underline{s}}
associated with the weakly increasing dimension vector
\underline{d}^{\prime}=(d^{\prime}_{0}\leq d^{\prime}_{1}\leq\ldots\leq d^{\prime}_{N})
. The reader may find Figure
2
and Example
7.7
useful to understand these definitions.

Definition 7.3
.

Consider the points

\begin{array}[]{l}\underline{s}:=\left(d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{2},d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{3},\ldots\ldots\ldots,d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{N}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{N},\\
\hat{\underline{s}}:=\left(d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{2},d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{3},\ldots,d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{m}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},\end{array}

and let
\hat{\underline{v}}_{1},\hat{\underline{v}}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\underline{v}}_{k}
be the set of integer points in the hyperplane
\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}=d^{\prime}_{0}\}
closest to
\hat{\underline{s}}
in the Euclidean distance.

Definition 7.4
.

Define

\hat{D}:=\|\hat{\underline{s}}-\hat{\underline{v}}_{i}\|^{2}

(which is independent of
i
by definition). Define
\underline{v}_{i}
to be the image of
\hat{\underline{v}}_{i}
under the standard embedding
\mathbb{Z}^{m}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{N}

By construction we have

(18)

D=\hat{D}+\sum_{i=m+1}^{N}(d^{\prime}_{i}-d^{\prime}_{0})^{2}.

Theorem 7.5
.

For
\underline{d}^{\prime}=(d^{\prime}_{0}\leq d^{\prime}_{1}\leq\ldots\leq d^{\prime}_{N})
the solution of the quadratic integer program (
6.1
) (ie. the codimension of
\Sigma^{0}_{\underline{d}}

(19)

\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{D}+{d^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}(d^{\prime}_{i}-d^{\prime}_{0})^{2}\right),

Proof.

Consider the (

\sum_{j\leq i}e_{i}(e_{j}+d^{\prime}_{j}-d^{\prime}_{j-1})

\displaystyle 2G_{\underline{d}}(\underline{e})-{d^{\prime}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2G_{\underline{d}}(\underline{e})-\left(\sum e_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\leq i}e_{i}(d^{\prime}_{j}-d^{\prime}_{j-1})+\sum_{i}e_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}2e_{i}(d^{\prime}_{i}-d^{\prime}_{0})+e_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i}(e_{i}-(d^{\prime}_{0}-d^{\prime}_{i}))^{2}\right)-\sum_{i}(d^{\prime}_{i}-d^{\prime}_{0})^{2}

Therefore, using the notations from Definitions
7.2
-

, and the optimal solution of (

. We conclude that

has 3 top-dimensional components, each of codimension

.

It remains to compute
\hat{D}
more explicitly. We write
\left\{x\right\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor
for the fractional part of

.

Lemma 7.8
.

If
\delta\geq 0
, we have

\delta=m\left\{\frac{S}{m}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lfloor\frac{S}{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor=\frac{S}{m}-\left\{\frac{S}{m}\right\}.

If

\delta=m\left\{\frac{S}{m}\right\}-m\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lfloor\frac{S}{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor=\frac{S}{m}-\left\{\frac{S}{m}\right\}+1.

Proof.

Write

with

and

. Then by equation (

), we have

\delta\geq 0\Leftrightarrow\bar{S}<\frac{m}{2}.

Moreover,

which together with equation (

) concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 7.9
.

We have

\hat{D}=m\left\{\frac{S}{m}\right\}\left(1-\left\{\frac{S}{m}\right\}\right)+\frac{S^{2}}{m}+md_{0}^{2}-2d_{0}S

Proof.

We only treat the case
\delta\geq 0

\displaystyle\hat{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\hat{\underline{s}}-\hat{\underline{v}}_{1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\left(d_{0}-\left\lfloor\frac{S}{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right)^{2}+(m-\delta)\left(d_{0}-\left\lfloor\frac{S}{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor\right)^{2}

∎

We have finally arrived at the explicit formulas.

.

Let
\underline{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{N+1}

+\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq m}(d^{\prime}_{i}-r)(d^{\prime}_{j}-r)+r(d_{0}+d_{N}-r).

By Lemmas

Remark 7.11
.

It is not immediate from the formulas that those codimensions are integers. However one can easily show that, for any positive integers
\tilde{S},m
, the quantity

Example 7.12
.

Let

. We have

8.
Real-log canonical threshold of deep linear networks

The initial motivation of this work was to better understand the work of Aoyagi
[
Aoy24
]
and to relate it to the theory of quiver representations. Aoyagi’s work takes place in the context of
singular learning theory
, the Bayesian statistics of singular statistical models
[
Wat09
,
Wat18
,
Wat24
]
. In singular learning theory, the
real log-canonical threshold (rlct)
, a geometric invariant of singularities of real analytic functions, plays a central role because it controls the asymptotic performance of Bayesian inference.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
X
be a real analytic manifold and
F:X\to\mathbb{R}
a real analytic function.

(i)

The
real log canonical threshold
(RLCT) of
F
is

\mathrm{rlct}(F):=\sup\{s\in\mathbb{R}\ |\ |F|^{-s}\text{ is locally integrable }\}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

(ii)

Let
x\in X
. The
local real log canonical threshold
of
F
at
x
is

\mathrm{rlct}_{x}(F):=\sup\{s\in\mathbb{R}\ |\ |F|^{-s}\text{ is locally integrable at $x$}\}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}.

The rlct comes with a secondary invariant, the
real log-canonical multiplicity
(rlcm), which we only define in the local case for simplicity. This is an opportunity to introduce archimedean zeta functions, which play an important role in singularity theory and singular learning theory.

Proposition-Definition 8.2
.

Let
X
be a real analytic manifold,
F:X\to\mathbb{R}
a real analytic function and
x\in X
. Fix a volume form
\mathrm{dvol}
on
X
and a relatively compact open neighbourhood
U
of
x
The
local archimedean zeta function

\zeta_{F,U}(s):=\int_{U}|F|^{s}\mathrm{dvol}

which is a priori defined for
\mathrm{Re}(s)\gg 0
extends to a meromorphic function. The poles of
\zeta_{F}(s)
are independent of
U
small enough and are negative real numbers. The largest pole is
-\mathrm{rlct}_{x}(F)
, and we define the
real log-canonical multiplicity

\mathrm{rlcm}_{x}(F)\in\mathbb{N}
to be the order of that pole.

This proposition goes back to Atiyah
[
Ati70
]
and the proof is based on (real analytic) resolution of singularities.

Remark 8.3
.

As the name suggests, the rlct and the rlcm are the real counterparts of the
log-canonical threshold
(lct) and the associated multiplicity in complex (algebraic and analytic) geometry. The lct plays an important role in complex singularity theory and birational geometry, and has been studied much more intensively than its real counterpart (see
[
Mus11
,
Kol97
]
.

Here are some fundamental properties of the rlct which we need to put our results in context. We refer to
[
Lin11
]
and to the upcoming paper
[
Leh24
]
for a comprehensive treatment.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
X
be a real analytic manifold,
F:X\to\mathbb{R}
a real analytic function and
x\in X
.

(i)

0<\mathrm{rlct}_{x}(F)<\infty\ \Leftrightarrow\ F(x)=0
and
\mathrm{rlct}(F)<\infty\ \Leftrightarrow\ F^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset
.

Moreover, when
F(x)=0
, we have

(23)

\mathrm{rlct}_{x}(F)\in\left(0,\frac{\codim_{X,x}F^{-1}(0)}{2}\right]\cap\mathbb{Q}.

(ii)

The function
x\mapsto\mathrm{rlct}_{X,x}(F)
is lower semi-continuous.

(iii)

\mathrm{rlct}(F)=\inf_{x\in X}\mathrm{rlct}_{x}(F)

This inf is not always attained in the general real analytic case because of potential issues “at infinity”, but it is in many cases f.(e.g., when
X
is compact or when
X,F
are algebraic). When it is attained, we have

(24)

\mathrm{rlct}(F)\in\left(0,\frac{\codim_{X}F^{-1}(0)}{2}\right]\cap\mathbb{Q}.

(iv)

Let
G:X\to\mathbb{R}
be another real analytic function. Then we can consider
F+G
and
F\cdot G
as functions on
X\times Y
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{rlct}(FG)=

\displaystyle\min(\mathrm{rlct}(F),\mathrm{rlct}(G))\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathrm{rlcm}(FG)=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\mathrm{rlcm}(F)&\text{if}\ \mathrm{rlct}(F)<\mathrm{rlct}(G)\\
\mathrm{rlcm}(G)&\text{if}\ \mathrm{rlct}(F)>\mathrm{rlct}(G)\\
\mathrm{rlcm}(F)+\mathrm{rlcm}(G)&\text{if}\ \mathrm{rlct}(F)=\mathrm{rlct}(G).\end{array}\right.

Assume
F,G\geq 0
. Then

\mathrm{rlct}(F+G)=\mathrm{rlct}(F)+\mathrm{rlct}(G)\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{rlcm}(F+G)=\mathrm{rlcm}(F)+\mathrm{rlcm}(G)-1

Example 8.5
.

The definition immediately implies, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\mathrm{rlct}(x^{n})=\frac{1}{n}.

Using Statement
(iv)
above, we deduce that, for
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
e\leq d

\mathrm{rlct}(x_{1}^{n_{1}}\ldots x_{d}^{n_{e}})=\min\frac{1}{n_{i}}

and

\mathrm{rlct}(x_{1}^{2n_{1}}+\ldots+x_{e}^{2n_{e}})=\frac{1}{2n_{1}}+\ldots+\frac{1}{2n_{e}}

In particular, if
F=x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{e}^{2}
then

\mathrm{rlct}(F)=\frac{e}{2}=\frac{\codim_{X}F^{-1}(0)}{2}

saturates the inequality (
24
).

In singular learning theory, the rlct and the rlcm of a certain function
K
control the asymptotic performance of Bayesian inference in a large class of statistical models as the size of the dataset increases. More precisely,
K
arises as the
relative entropy (or Kullback-Leibler divergence) between the true distribution and the model
, considered as a function of model parameters.

In
[
Aoy24
]
, Aoyagi computes the real log-canonical threshold of the relative entropy function of
deep linear neural networks
. Deep linear networks are obtained from standard (feedforward, fully-connected) deep linear networks by replacing their non-linear activation functions by linear maps. Despite their simplicity, they are a useful “toy model” in modern deep learning theory, as we discussed in Section
1.4
. By definition, the weights of a deep linear networks are a tuple of composable matrices, and the function computed by the network is their product, so the parameter space of the model is
\Rep_{\underline{d}}
where
\underline{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{N+1}
records the widths of the layers and
N
is the depth of the network. We also need a “true distribution” generating the data, or rather in this context a “true function”. Aoyagi makes the assumption that this true function is linear and given by some
B\in\Mat_{d_{N},d_{0}}^{\rk=r}
with
0\leq r\leq\min{\underline{d}}
. This condition on the rank emsures that
B\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathrm{mult})
; in statistical terminology, we are in the
realisable
or
well-specified
case.

We now define directly the function
K=K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B}
for our deep linear model, referring to
[
Aoy24
]
for a derivation of how it arises as a relative entropy. As it turns out, in this case,
K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B}
is algebraic:

(26)

K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B}(A_{*}):=\|\mathrm{mult}(A)-B\|^{2}_{2}=\mathrm{Tr}((\mathrm{mult}(A)-B)^{t}(\mathrm{mult}(A)-B)))

We have
K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B}(A_{*})\geq 0
and

(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})^{-1}(0)=\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)

so that by Equation
23
we find

\mathrm{rlct}(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})\leq\frac{\codim\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)}{2}.

The main result of
[
Aoy24
]
is then the computation of
\mathrm{rlct}(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})
, which thanks to our results we can reformulate cleanly as

Theorem 8.6
.

\mathrm{rlct}(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})=\frac{\codim\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)}{2}.

and, with the notations of Section
7
,

\mathrm{rlcm}(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})=m^{2}\left\{\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\}\left(1-\left\{\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\}\right)

Proof.

This follows from a comparison between Formula (
22
) in Theorem
7.10
and
[
Aoy24
, Theorem 1]
. To see this requires some translations between our notations and the ones in loc.cit (Note the shift by one and reverse ordering of the dimensions):

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}&\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{aoyagi}{}{}]}}&\text{This paper}\\
\hline\cr\text{Dimension vector}&(H^{(L+1)},\ldots,H^{(1)})&(d_{0},\ldots,d_{N})\\
\text{Reduced dimension vector}&(M^{L+1},\ldots,M^{(1)})&(d_{0}-r,\ldots,d_{N}-r)\\
\text{Number of ``relevant" dimensions}&l&m\\
\text{Set of ``relevant" reduced dimensions}&(M^{(S_{1})},\ldots,M^{(S_{l+1})})&(d^{\prime}_{0}-r,\ldots,d^{\prime}_{m}-r)\\
\end{array}

In particular, the integers
M
and
a
defined in
[
Aoy24
, Theorem 1]
translate in our notation to

M=\left\lceil\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\rceil

and

a=\tilde{S}-m\left\lceil\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\rceil+m

so that

\frac{a(l-a)}{l}=m\left(1+\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}-\left\lceil\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\rceil\right)\left(-\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}+\left\lceil\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\rceil\right)

Now we make the following elementary observation about ceiling and floor functions: for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(\left\lceil x\right\rceil-x)(1+x-\left\lceil x\right\rceil)=\left\{x\right\}(1-\left\{x\right\}).

and conclude that

\frac{a(l-a)}{l}=m\left\{\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\}\left(1-\left\{\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\}\right).

Using this, we now recognize that Formula (
22
) in Theorem
7.10
is exactly (twice) the first formula for
\lambda=\mathrm{rlct}(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})
in
[
Aoy24
, Theorem 1]
. There is also a formula for the multiplicity in
[
Aoy24
, Theorem 1]
, which we simply translate into our notations.
∎

Remark 8.7
.

As far as we now, there is no simple relationship between
\mathrm{rlcm}(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})=m^{2}\left\{\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\}\left(1-\left\{\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}\right\}\right)
and the number
k=\binom{m}{\tilde{S}-m\left\lfloor\frac{\tilde{S}}{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor}
of irreducible components of
\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)
.

We expect that the formula of Theorem for global rlcts also extends to
local
rlcts and codimensions, i.e., that for every
A_{*}\in\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)
we have
\mathrm{rlct}_{A_{*}}(K^{\mathrm{DLN}}_{B})=\frac{\codim_{A_{*}}\mathrm{mult}^{-1}(B)}{2}.
We plan to come back to this question in future work.
