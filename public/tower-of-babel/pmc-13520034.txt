Co-Incineration of Real Municipal Sewage Sludge in a Fluidized Bed: Effects of the Coexistence of P, Fe, and Na on Agglomeration and Heavy Metal Emissions
Authors: Zhen-Shu Liu, Hong-Jia Wang, Zhang-Wei Yan, Chiou-Liang Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of the coexistence of P, Fe, and Na during the fluidized bed incineration of municipal sewage sludge on agglomeration, defluidization, and heavy metal emissions. The results indicate that increasing the temperature promotes the melting of eutectic compounds, promoting agglomeration and accelerating defluidization. Conversely, using smaller bed material particle sizes or increasing the operating gas velocity enhances particle mobility and mixing, delaying agglomeration. A comparison of the roles of P and Fe revealed that an increase in their content generally extended the defluidization time. However, high P concentrations weakened the inhibitory effect of Fe, leading to a shorter defluidization time for the P/Fe/Na ratio of 0.6/5/1 than for 0.4/5/1. Differences in heavy metal emission concentrations were negligible before defluidization; however, emissions gradually increased once agglomeration began, with Cu and Pb concentrations increasing significantly after defluidization. The Cu emission concentration was lower than that of Pb because of its higher melting and boiling points. Experimental results show that Fe reacts with P to form high-melting-point FePO4, extending fluidized bed stability. Low Fe content allows P to form low-melting-point compounds like NaPO3, increasing agglomeration risks. Therefore, to ensure long-term stability during cocombustion, operators must maintain an excess Fe/P ratio (Fe > P).

Introduction

In recent years, the progressive increase in sewerage connection rates across various municipalities in Taiwan has led to a significant increase in sewage treatment volumes and the subsequent production of sewage sludge. Consequently, rapid and efficient sludge disposal has become critical. Among various disposal methods, incineration is considered a high-capacity approach that offers rapid processing. Furthermore, the flue gas generated during incineration can be utilized for energy recovery via cogeneration (combined heat and power), whereas the resulting bottom ash can be recycled as civil engineering materials, such as sand or aggregates. However, municipal sewage sludge contains elements such as P, Na, and Fe, which can induce bed material agglomeration or even defluidization after fluidized bed incineration, compromising treatment efficiency and potentially leading to secondary pollution.

Elements such as S, Cl, Si, P, Fe, K, Na and alkaline earth metals, have been reported to significantly affect agglomeration. − The gradual accumulation of these agglomerates during operation can result in bed defluidization, causing unscheduled shutdowns, − which can lead to incomplete combustion and the release of secondary pollutants. , Researchers posit that agglomeration stems from the intrinsic tackiness of the bed material combined with interparticle collisions. Accordingly, Sevonius et al. categorized bed particle aggregation mechanisms into three distinct types.Type I: melt-induced agglomeration: some inorganic salts in the ash melt and form “necks” that bind particles into clusters. No chemical reactions occur between the inorganic salt species and the bed material. − Type II: coating-induced agglomeration: bed particles are rendered sticky by a viscous coating generated from reactions involving inorganic ash species. Subsequently, these particles adhere to agglomerates during collisions. ,,, Type III: alkali metals within the melt react with silica to form molten silicates, further facilitating particle aggregation. ,

Existing research has focused on ash agglomeration during coal combustion, estimating the influence of various elements on agglomeration and defluidization by comparing the elemental composition of postcombustion agglomerates with that of the raw feedstock. However, a definitive correlation between specific metal compositions and agglomeration has yet to be established because of significant origin-based variation in the complex metal composition of coal. Given that sludge has a high ash content and a complex matrix containing P, Si, Fe, Ca, Al, and S, it is highly susceptible to agglomeration and defluidization during thermal treatment. The coincineration of sludge with more complex waste streams can further exacerbate these issues.

Therefore, this study evaluates the specific impacts of key inorganic constituents (P, Fe, and Na) on the agglomeration-induced defluidization process in coincineration systems. This study expands upon the scope of the study conducted by Lin et al. They established the effects of P, Na, and Fe using simplified waste matrices. Although simulated waste reduces interference from complex matrices, real municipal sewage sludge contains intricate matrices, heavy metals, and a high ash content, which can result in defluidization behaviors distinct from those observed with simulated waste alone. Previous investigations utilizing synthetic matrices have provided fundamental insights into the chemical mechanisms of Na–P–Fe interactions; however, the behavior of these elements in actual municipal sewage sludge remains poorly understood because of its intricate ash chemistry and high trace metal content. Consequently, this study validated the effects of P, Na, and Fe coexistence during the coincineration of municipal sewage sludge and simulated waste. Furthermore, the volatilization and emission behaviors of heavy metals, such as Cu and Pb, during the process were explored, providing valuable guidance for industrial-scale sewage sludge incineration.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Equipment and Feedstock Composition

Municipal sewage sludge sourced from a wastewater treatment plant in southern Taiwan was used as the primary matrix. Following sampling, the sludge was dried at 105 °C and stored. The baseline concentrations of P, Na, and Fe were determined using inductively coupled plasma (ICP) spectrometry following microwave-assisted digestion of the sludge samples. Further, phosphoric acid (H3PO4), sodium nitrate (NaNO3), and ferric nitrate (Fe­(NO3)3) were employed as the respective sources to adjust the elemental ratios of P, Na, and Fe within the sludge.

The incineration feedstock was composed of a dried sludge matrix mixed with wood chips and polypropylene (PP) pellets, which were packaged in polyethylene (PE) bags with a total weight of 2.45 g. Table presents the elemental analysis results for the sludge, wood chips, PP pellets, and PE bags. The dosages required to adjust the P/Na/Fe ratios were calculated based on the sludge weight per bag. Aqueous solutions containing NaNO3, Fe­(NO3)3, and H3PO4 were prepared using deionized water. Subsequently, 0.5 mL aliquots of the resulting solution were added to each sludge package. Cu and Pb were added to the feedstock in the form of nitrate solutions to investigate the emission behavior of heavy metals during coincineration and defluidization. The experimental variables included the incineration temperature, gas velocity, bed particle size, and P, Na, and Fe ratios, as detailed in the experimental matrix in Table . The incineration was conducted in a laboratory-scale fluidized bed reactor, and the detailed configuration and system are presented in our previous work.

Experimental Procedures

The pressure drop across the bed and gas velocity were measured using a water manometer prior to the tests to determine the minimum fluidization velocity (U mf) for the experimental process. Further, all experiments were conducted at an operating velocity of 1.3× U mf to maintain a stable fluidization state. Silica sand was utilized as the bed material, and its density was approximately 2600 kg/m3. The silica sand was sieved using ASTM standard sieves, with a primary particle size of 650 μm. The incineration temperature was maintained between 800 and 900 °C, as shown in Table .

Prior to each test, the incinerator bed was preheated to the target temperature and the blower was activated to provide the necessary air supply. The flow rate was regulated using a rotameter to maintain a constant fluidization velocity of 1.3 U mf. Once the bed temperature stabilized, feeding was started at a rate of one package every 20 s. Agglomeration was monitored through visual observation and by tracking pressure fluctuations using a manometer to identify the onset of defluidization.

After agglomeration and defluidization, the fluidization quality of the sand bed decreases, which may lead to poor combustion performance and increased heavy metal release. Therefore, in this study, two metals commonly found in waste, Cu and Pb, were added to the feedstock to investigate the release behavior of heavy metals during agglomeration and defluidization. In this study, the “emission concentration” represents the mass concentration of heavy metals found in fly ash. The total mass concentration of heavy metals in the flue gas stream included both particulate-bound metals captured on the quartz fiber filter and vapor-phase/gaseous metals captured downstream in the acid impinger solutions. However, the concentration of gaseous heavy metals in flue gas is relatively low, while most heavy metals are distributed in fly ash through adsorption and nucleation processes. Therefore, the analysis of heavy metal dispersion focuses only on heavy metals in fly ash to discuss the extent of heavy metal emissions. The fly ash sampling procedure was primarily based on the U.S. EPA sampling standard method 5. The emission concentration was calculated as the heavy metal mass per gram of fly ash.

Fly ash was collected from the flue-gas sampling port every 4 min, with each sampling interval lasting 2 min. Once defluidization occurred, the event time was noted, and the bed materials were collected after the furnace had cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, the collected fly ash was subjected to microwave digestion and analyzed to determine the Cu and Pb concentrations using inductively coupled plasma spectrometer (ICP). The agglomerate samples were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) to characterize the morphology and elemental distribution of the agglomerates.

Results and Discussion

Effects of Different Fluidization Parameters on Agglomeration/Defluidization

Figure illustrates the defluidization time of the feedstock containing P, Fe, and Na (0.4:3:1) under varying operating temperatures, and Table S1 presents the raw defluidization time data. The results indicated that the defluidization time was inversely proportional to the operating temperature in the presence of P, Fe, and Na. Notably, the minimum defluidization time was recorded at 900 °C, suggesting accelerated agglomeration at higher temperatures.

Effect of operating temperature on agglomeration/defluidization time.

The melting fraction of eutectic compounds increases as the bed temperature rises from 800 to 900 °C. Furthermore, the degree of melting of low-melting-point substances increases at higher temperatures, leading to the generation of more liquid-phase matter. , These liquid substances adhere to the surface of the bed material via interparticle collisions. When sticky bed particles collide, the viscous material forms “necks”, which bind the particles into clusters. As the viscous material accumulates, the particle size increases, causing the bed material to transition into the Geldart Group D category. Under a fixed fluidization velocity, the interparticle adhesive forces exceed the aerodynamic forces exerted by the fluid motion, leading to defluidization.

Figure shows the SEM/EDS analysis results for the silica sand and agglomerates. As shown in Figure b, the SEM analysis identified a characteristic neck-like linkage connecting the two particles. The formation of this bridge was attributed to cooling and subsequent solidification of the liquid-phase substance, providing direct evidence of surface adhesion during the agglomeration process. From the EDS images, Na, P, and Fe were all present in the agglomerate neck structures. Table presents the EDS analysis results for the primary elements on the silica sand surface and within the neck structure. The EDS elemental distribution shows that Na is abundant in the neck structures, whereas the proportions of P and Fe are significantly higher than those in the silica sand. Therefore, Na, P, and Fe were the predominant components in the generated agglomerates, suggesting that they significantly affected the formation of eutectic substances.

Adjusting the excess air ratio during fluidized bed incineration requires varying the primary airflow. However, this can alter the operating gas velocity, which is a key fluidization parameter that significantly affects agglomeration/defluidization. Figure a illustrates the defluidization time distribution under different operating gas velocities (1.1, 1.3, and 1.5× U mf). The results indicated that increasing the operating gas velocity significantly extended the defluidization time.

Effects of operating gas velocity and bed material particle size on defluidization time: (a) operating gas velocity; (b) particle size of bed material.

Increasing the gas velocity promotes the mixing and mobility of the bed particles. Iwadate and Horio and Zhou and Li reported that higher fluidization velocities generate greater interparticle collision forces (fragmentation forces), facilitating the breakage of agglomerates and reducing their size. Similarly, Xu and Zhu observed that increasing the gas velocity minimized the agglomerated particle size. Therefore, a higher gas velocity contributes to the disruption of agglomerates, reducing the potential for defluidization.

Figure b shows the defluidization time for three different bed material particle sizes. An increase in the bed material particle size led to a corresponding decrease in the defluidization time. Given that several parameters were fixed in this study, 650 μm was selected as the primary particle size, and the gas velocity was set to 1.3× U mf for the 650 μm bed at 800 °C.

The 775 μm bed particles are the largest and therefore require a highest minimum fluidization velocity. At the operating gas velocity 1.3× U mf, the 775 μm particles can be fluidized; however, their movement is slow, which results in a lower momentum. As viscous substances accumulate and the particle size gradually increases, the bed particles transition into the larger Geldart Group D category. At a fixed fluidization velocity, the interparticle adhesive forces exceed the aerodynamic forces exerted by fluid motion, leading to defluidization.

A comparison of the results shown in Figures and with the findings reported by Lin et al. indicates that the defluidization times in the previous study for P/Na, Fe/Na, or P/Fe/Na coexistence were consistently longer than those observed in the current experiments. This discrepancy is attributed to the feedstock composition. In the previous experiment, the feedstock consisted primarily of simulated wastes. In contrast, the present study used real municipal sewage sludge to investigate the cocombustion conditions. Real municipal sewage sludge possesses a significantly more complex matrix; in addition to P, Fe, and Na, the sludge contains 31% ash, which includes elements such as Cu, Cr, Zn, Ni, As, Pb, and Fe. This sludge likely contained more low-melting-point substances than the wood-chip-based simulated waste, accelerating the defluidization process. Consequently, during the cocombustion of municipal sewage sludge and waste, agglomeration/defluidization is more likely to occur than during the incineration of simulated waste alone.

Effects of P/Fe/Na Ratios on Agglomeration/Defluidization

Figure a,b illustrate the defluidization time during sludge incineration under varying ratios of P, Fe, and Na. As shown in Figure a, the Fe concentration was held constant, and as shown in Figure b, the concentration of P was fixed. The results in Figure a indicate that a higher P content in the sludge extended the defluidization time; however, when the proportion of P exceeded 0.4, there was no sustained increase in defluidization time.

Effects of various P/Fe/Na ratios on defluidization time: (a) P/Na ratio; (b) Fe/Na ratio; (c) P/Fe/Na ratio.

The results in Figure b indicate that the maximum defluidization time, and thus the longest operational stability period, occurred when P was 0.4. Adjusting the Fe concentration revealed that the defluidization time increased with the Fe level. These findings confirm that increasing the Fe concentration helps extend the defluidization time, thereby enhancing the operational stability of fluidized bed incineration.

Figure c shows the effects of simultaneously increasing the P and Fe concentrations on the defluidization time. The results indicated that the defluidization time increased with increasing P and Fe concentrations. However, as noted previously in Figure a, when Fe is fixed, a P concentration of 0.6 leads to a slight decrease in defluidization time. Although Figure b indicates that increasing the Fe concentration typically extends the defluidization time, the presence of high P concentrations counteracts this benefit. A P/Fe/Na ratio of 0.6/5/1 resulted in a shorter defluidization time than a ratio of 0.4/5/1. This implies that the effect of high P concentrations weakens or overcomes the stabilizing effect of high Fe concentrations.

Lin et al. reported that increasing the P/Na ratio extended the defluidization time, suggesting that P exerted an inhibitory effect on agglomeration. Conversely, the defluidization time decreased with an increase in the Fe/Na ratio, implying that Fe might compromise the operational stability. When all three elements coexisted, the effect of P outweighed that of Fe, resulting in defluidization times longer than those observed with Na alone.

In contrast, the results of the present study regarding the coexistence of these three elements indicate that higher contents of both P and Fe in the sludge extended the defluidization time. The impact of Fe observed in this study deviates from previous findings. While increasing both the P and Fe concentrations typically leads to a gradual increase in the defluidization time, P diminishes the stabilizing effect of Fe when both are maintained at high concentrations. High-temperature thermodynamic studies by Horiguchi et al. and Grimm et al. demonstrated that the addition of Fe preferentially captures reactive P species to form stable, high-melting-point iron phosphate compounds (such as FePO4, melting point of ∼1050 °C), thereby significantly suppressing the melt formation and adhesiveness of alkali phosphates. As given by the following equation, Fe preferentially captures volatile P2O5 to form high-melting-point FePO4. Fe2O3+P2O5→2FePO4

When P is in stoichiometric excess (e.g., at a ratio of 0.6/5/1), the surplus P overflows the iron-binding capacity. The excess P first reacts with K and then with Na to form highly viscous low-melting-point metaphosphates (e.g., KPO3 and NaPO3) and form liquid bridges (“necks”) that override the structural stabilization of Fe.

Effects of Different Parameters on Heavy Metal Emission during Incineration

Figures – present the emission profiles of Cu and Pb during sludge incineration at various temperatures, bed material particle sizes, and gas velocities. Tables S2 and S3 present the raw concentration data for Cu and Pb under different parameters. The results across various operating conditions indicated that the emission concentrations fluctuated before the onset of defluidization; however, the fluctuations remained relatively minor. After a period of operation, more drastic oscillations in the emission concentrations were observed, suggesting that the initiation of agglomeration significantly affected the fluidization quality, indirectly affecting heavy metal emissions. As the system approached defluidization, the heavy metal concentrations gradually increased, with a sharp increase in the emission levels observed after defluidization. These results confirm that bed material agglomeration and subsequent defluidization not only compromise incineration efficiency but also exacerbate heavy metal emissions, thereby leading to potential pollution. This is attributed to the increase in the bed particle size during agglomeration, which reduces the particle mobility at a constant gas velocity. The impaired fluidity hinders the contact between the bed particles and heavy metals, reducing the adsorption efficiency. Furthermore, diminished fluidity prevents the uniform distribution of heat generated from sludge and waste combustion once defluidization occurs, potentially causing localized hotspots that promote heavy metal volatilization.

Effect of operating temperature on heavy metal emissions.

Figure shows the effect of various temperatures on heavy metal emissions. The results indicate that higher temperatures (e.g., 900 °C) yield slightly lower emission concentrations than 800 and 850 °C. Liu et al. found that the mechanisms influencing heavy metal emissions during incineration include bed material adsorption and the entrapment of heavy metals by liquid eutectics. The volume of the molten eutectic compounds increases with temperature. During sludge incineration, these liquid eutectics come into contact with the sludge, allowing the heavy-metal-containing ash residues to become embedded within the molten species and reduce the volatilization of heavy metals. However, once defluidization occurs, the contact probability between the bed material and sludge decreases, increasing heavy metal emissions.

Figure shows the heavy metal emission results at various gas velocities. At 1.5 U mf, the increased mobility of the bed material enhanced the particle momentum. This elevated gas velocity reduces the likelihood of particle adhesion, thereby extending operational stability and suppressing heavy metal emissions until the onset of defluidization. Figure shows the results for various bed material sizes. Smaller bed particles (545 μm) provide better fluidity at the same gas velocity, which results in superior operational stability and a longer defluidization time; this enables a more extensive adsorption of heavy metals. Thus, during prolonged operation, emission concentrations exhibit large fluctuations. In contrast, the 775 μm bed material with poorer fluidity than the 545 μm particles initiated agglomeration sooner, leading to higher heavy metal emission concentrations shortly after the start of operation.

A comparison of the emission profiles of Cu and Pb under various conditions in Figures – reveals that the emission trends are similar across different parameters despite variations in the specific metal concentrations. The concentration of Pb emitted from the flue gas was higher than that of Cu. This observation aligns with the physical properties of the two metals: Cu has melting and boiling points of 1083.5 and 2595 °C, respectively, while they are 327.6 and 1740 °C for Pb. Therefore, the experimental results were consistent with the relative melting and boiling points of these metals.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the synergistic effects of Na, P, and Fe during the fluidized-bed incineration of municipal sewage sludge and simultaneously analyzed heavy metal emission behavior. The results demonstrated that higher temperatures accelerate agglomeration by promoting the formation of low-melting-point eutectics, whereas smaller bed particles and higher gas velocities delay defluidization by enhancing particle momentum and mixing. The coexistence of P and Fe inhibits agglomeration and prolongs the operational duration. However, P and Fe also influence each other. Although increasing the Fe concentration typically extends the defluidization time, a P/Fe/Na ratio of 0.6/5/1 shortens the operational stability because of the interfering effect of high P levels. P may interact with Na to form highly viscous, low-melting-point phosphates that increase agglomeration. Heavy metal emission analysis revealed that Cu and Pb concentrations exhibited limited variation prior to defluidization, increased significantly following the onset of agglomeration, and increased drastically after defluidization. The Pb emissions were consistently higher than those of Cu, which was consistent with its lower melting and boiling points.

The experimental results indicate that when sludge contains P, the presence of Fe can lead to the formation of high-melting-point FePO4, which helps extend the stability of fluidized bed operation. If the Fe content is too low, P may form low-melting-point compounds such as NaPO3, increasing the risk of agglomeration. Therefore, to maintain long-term fluidization stability during the cocombustion of sludge and waste, operators should focus on the Fe/P ratio in the sludge and waste, ensuring that the amount of Fe consistently exceeds that of P.
