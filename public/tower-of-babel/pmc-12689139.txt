Vitamin A deficiency: eye manifestations, knowledge, and nutritional assessment among Khalwa (Quran boarding school) individuals, Khartoum state, Sudan, 2023: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Elkhidir Bashir Elkhidir Ali, Mohammedalmujtaba Gamar Abdallah Imamaldin, Anas Babiker Mohammed Elamin, Shafee S. Almahi, Mohammed Ahmed Mahjoub Osman Mohammed, Siham Ahmed Balla Musa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Vitamin A deficiency (VAD) is highly prevalent in developing countries, especially among malnourished children, with complications like blindness and deaths occurring in about half of those deficient in vitamin A. In Sudan, Quranic schools (Khalwa) have a large number of students, many of whom receive poor nutritional support. This study aims to highlight VAD prevalence and knowledge and to assess the associated dietary habits among this community.Methods:This was a cross-sectional study that used cluster random sampling and was conducted in a district of Khartoum state, with 332 participants included. Data were collected by a self-structured questionnaire, presented using descriptive statistics, and analyzed by SPSS v26.Results:Almost half of the students in this study are between the ages of 6 and 17 years. Most of their fathers were farmers, and the average daily income was low. Only 30.1% reported eating at least three types of food, with an overall lack of knowledge about vitamin A. Fifty-five (16.5%) had night blindness, of which 30 had conjunctival xerosis, 20 had Bitot’s spots, and 7 had conjunctival congestion. Eighty (24.1%) and 65 (19.6%) students had respiratory disease and diarrhea in the last two weeks, respectively. Local data show almost the same prevalence of night blindness in this group.Conclusion:Poor vitamin A knowledge and poor dietary habits are common among the residents of Quran-boarding-schools, with about one-sixth of them having night blindness. This necessitates health education and nutritional support for this community.

Introduction

Vitamin A is an important micronutrient that is involved in the maintenance of the mucosal tissue integrity, immune system, hematopoiesis, skeletal growth, fertility, gene expression, and embryogenesis[1–5]. Vitamin A deficiency (VAD) is an important micronutrient deficiency that may be due to malnutrition or abnormal transport of vitamin A[6]. The daily requirements of vitamin A for adult women and men are 2667 (IU)/day and 3300 IU/day, respectively[7]. Children, adolescents, pregnant, and lactating women are more susceptible to VAD, and this is further exaggerated by the low socioeconomic status, especially in developing countries such as Sudan[6].HIGHLIGHTSAccording to previous studies, Khalwa students in Sudan have a high prevalence of vitamin A deficiency (VAD).It is essential to assess the current status of VAD among the students and teachers, their knowledge of vitamin A, and their related food practices.This project gives the stakeholders an updated view of the problem to enable them to carry out the appropriate health interventions for this group.

VAD increases morbidity, mortality, and disability; it is an avoidable cause of blindness[8]. Night blindness is a sensitive and specific indicator of low serum retinol levels, which is the marker of vitamin A status in the body[9,10].

Clinical manifestation of VAD ranges from night blindness to complete blindness, and in this spectrum, there is conjunctival xerosis, Bitot’s spots, corneal xerosis, corneal ulcer, and xerophthalmic fundus. On the contrary, VAD may be subclinical, which may outweigh the number of the symptomatic cases[11,12].

In history, VAD was treated with liver supplements and oil to the eyes; now vitamin A supplementation, fortification, and dietary diversification are the methods that are used to eliminate VAD[13].

In the developing world, 250000–500000 of malnourished children become blind from VAD each year, and half of them die within the next 12 months. In Ethiopia, VAD was moderate, affecting 61% of preschool children, with pooled serum retinol binding globulin levels <70 μmol/L[12,14].

Traditional Quran centers (Khalawi) students come from within and outside Sudan to study in very crowded dorms with poor dietary diversification, which is mostly made of sorghum flour porridge and a stew made of dry okra (weika), onion, and oil, which predisposes them to micronutrient deficiencies such as VAD[15].

Several studies were conducted in Sudan to assess VAD in traditional Quranic schools. Two of them were conducted in Khartoum state in Oomdowanban, East Nile district, 2012, and Alsayem Deema Khalwa in Umbada district, 2014, in which night blindness, conjunctival xerosis, and Bitot’s spots were observed in 24%, 12.5%, and 1% of students, respectively, in the first study and 7.5% had VAD in the second study[15,16].

Another study was conducted in Al‑Gezira state, Sudan, in which the prevalence of night blindness, conjunctival xerosis, Bitot’s spots, and corneal xerosis was 17.3%, 20.3%, 0.7%, and 0.2%, respectively, which was associated with the duration of the child’s stay in the school[17].

Vitamin A knowledge was poor as reported by several community-based studies in Sub-Saharan Africa, but the status of Vitamin A knowledge among this particular risk group is not known[18,19].

If we provide the health authorities and the international charity organizations with updated data, this will enable them to visualize the problem and identify the necessary interventions. This study aims to assess VAD among the residents of multiple traditional Quran centers in one province in Khartoum state.

Methods

This is a community-based cross-sectional study that was conducted in East Nile Province, Khartoum State. Cluster random sampling was used. Only centers with more than 100 individuals were included, and only permanent residents in the centers were selected. Those who were not residents of the school dormitory were excluded from the study.

From a total of 55 centers, nine centers had more than 100 patients, and from those nine, four clusters were chosen randomly. Simple random sampling was used to randomize the clusters. All of the eligible residents in the selected school dormitory were included. Data were collected between 1 November 2022 and 1 December 2022. With 332 participants, data were collected using a structured interviewing questionnaire, which consisted of four sections: sociodemographic data, VAD risk factors, knowledge, and complications. Local terms were used to inquire about the symptoms, such as night blindness. All those who had night blindness underwent an eye examination, which was performed by an ophthalmologist using a torch and a funduscopy. Data were analyzed using SPSS v26. Frequencies, percentages, mean, median, inter-quartile range, standard deviation, tables, and figures were used to present the data. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Department of Community Medicine, University of Khartoum. Its unique ID number is COMMED 2022-94-14. Permission was obtained from the head of traditional Quran centers. Consent was taken from all participants or their guardians. The study was registered online on ClinicalTrials.gov. This cross-sectional study has been reported in line with the STROCSS guidelines[20].

Discussion

This study identified the prevalence of night blindness knowledge and nutritional status among the residents of Quranic boarding schools in the East Nile locality of Khartoum State. Most students in this study were above 16 years, which seems slightly different from other studies, where, e.g., the most common age group in a study in Algezira State in Sudan in 2019 was 5-15 years[17]. Another two studies in 2023 in Khartoum had the same mean age of 13.7, with students age ranging from 7 to 24 years[17,21]. Farming was the most common job pursued by the students’ fathers, and most mothers were housewives, which, in the setting of Sudan, indicates a low socioeconomic status, which is a risk factor for a low literacy level and poor nutritional knowledge. In a study conducted in rural Rufaa, Algezira State, 85.6% of students in one Quranic school belonged to poor families. This finding is consistent with the results of our study. The number of family members was similar to our study, where most had 5–10 members. In this study, the most common age group was 3-7 years[22]. In a large study including 1000 school children in India, they found that low socioeconomic status is associated with VAD [23].

More than 90% of students in this study had poor or no knowledge about vitamin A. This is consistent with the results of Gebre’s Ethiopian research, which investigated farmers’ knowledge of malnutrition as general, yet the results of our study were astonishing, as just 5.7% of the participants were able to answer just two out of 15 questions, which were about vitamin A benefits and deficiency complications[24]. This finding is logical because most of those who are in this group are not previously involved in formal education.

Less than one-third (30%) of the residents were taking at least three of the food items from outside the school, a very similar result reported by Noureddin AA et al, who conducted the study in the same locality, but focused on anemia rather than Vitamin A. This finding reflects the poor food diversity among them, which is most likely due to the lack of sufficient knowledge about dietary diversification, which is important in preventing micronutrient deficiencies, including VAD. Just 29.5%, 19.9%, and 28.9% of students in this study reported consuming meat, eggs, and fruits, respectively. These findings are worse than those of a previously reported study in a school in the same locality in which 20.6% and 44.9% didn’t consume milk and fruit, respectively[25]. In a survey in five rural areas in Khartoum and Algezira, children showing signs and symptoms of VAD were less likely to have consumed dairy products or non-leafy vegetables than those who did not exhibit these symptoms[26].

We found that 16% of the residents of these schools complained of night blindness, which is close to the results of Mohamed ZD et al’s study in AlGazira State (17.4%)[17], but slightly lower than Khier et al’s study in Khartoum (23.6%)[16], and higher than Umbada research (7.5%)[15] The results of all of the previously mentioned studies are far higher than the number reported by the Nutrition Department, Federal Ministry of Health, Sudan, 2005, which was just 0.9%.

In this study, conjunctival xerosis was the most frequent eye manifestation, followed by Bitot’s spot. This is different from VAD pattern reported by a Nigerian study screening for VAD where they found no cases conjunctival xerosis among the children, with corneal xerosis as most common manifestation, while Bitot’s spot was the second ocular manifestation, which is similar to our study results[27]. This is in contrast to the Weld study, where Bitot’s spot was the commonest eye manifestation in VAD children[26]. This low prevalence of eye manifestations may be due to the chronicity of ocular diseases in VAD, which may not have developed yet. However, other systemic complications may be suggested, as one-fifth and one-quarter of the students reported being sick with diarrhea and respiratory tract infections within the 2 weeks prior to data collection, respectively. A 2022 survey by Abdelkader et al in Saudi Arabia found that low vitamin A levels were associated with recurrent respiratory tract infections[28]. This association between VAD and recurrent infections is a suggested topic for future studies. In our study, we found that students who consumed food from outside the school had a lower chance of getting night blindness. This is plausible as the food, which was delivered there, lacked dietary diversification.

We recommend the introduction of vitamin A-containing foods such as the invented vitamin A-rich porridge by Swar et al to prevent VAD in this vulnerable group. Raising the awareness of this preventable cause of blindness among the residents of these schools is a top priority. Health education campaigns must be implemented among this risk group throughout Sudan and the nearby Sudan-belt countries. These campaigns should use social marketing, which was found to be an effective strategy to improve vitamin A knowledge, attitude, and practice in Tanzania[28].

Strength and limitations

Our value of VAD may be underestimating the actual VAD status, as many cases may be subclinical, i.e., not showing any signs or symptoms of the disease. This is because the diagnosis was based only on the symptoms and signs, rather than the laboratory results of pooled serum retinol binding globulin, which can detect the subclinical cases, due to the lack of external funding. A result from the neighboring Ethiopia is shocking: about two-third (61%) of the preschool children have VAD according to the Serum Retinol Binding Globulin test results[12,13]. In addition, this study design focused on the residents of the dormitories, but broader studies are needed to assess the magnitude of the problem among the other students, who are non-dormitory residents, and among the other students outside this locality.

This study adds to the previous literature, besides the high prevalence of VAD, the poor dietary knowledge and habits among this group.

Conclusion

The residents of the dormitories of Quran-boarding-schools in East-Nile Province have low knowledge of VAD, and most of them have poor dietary habits. About one-sixth of them suffer from night blindness, which necessitates health education and nutritional support for this community.
