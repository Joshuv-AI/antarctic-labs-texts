From burnout to growth: how job burnout links to posttraumatic growth in healthcare workers
Authors: Miao Lv, Jiayin Liu, Yinan Wang, Dexiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare workers face unprecedented psychological challenges due to relentless exposure to traumatic events, from global pandemics to workplace violence. Despite these adversities, some individuals experience posttraumatic growth (PTG)—a phenomenon where struggle leads to profound personal transformation. Given this concerning reality, understanding the current status and underlying mechanisms of PTG among healthcare workers becomes crucial.MethodsDrawing on a large-scale cross-sectional survey involving 1375 healthcare workers from two Chinese cities, this research examined the relationship between job burnout and PTG. Specifically, it explored how psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs serve as potential chain mediators in this relationship.ResultsThe results demonstrated that job burnout was a negative predictor of PTG. Furthermore, both psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs were found to mediate the relationship between job burnout and PTG, with a significant sequential chain mediation effect observed.ConclusionJob burnout was found to be associated not only with lower levels of PTG among healthcare workers, but also with reduced psychological resilience and less positive stress beliefs, which were related to lower PTG. This research enriched the theoretical understanding of posttraumatic growth among healthcare workers by revealing how job burnout diminished the likelihood of posttraumatic growth. Simultaneously, the research contributed a novel perspective to job burnout theory by highlighting its negative impact on individuals’ positive psychological transformation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03610-2.

Introduction

Healthcare workers continuously face high-risk, high-pressure working conditions during recurrent global health crises (e.g., COVID-19, H1N1 pandemics) and natural disasters (e.g., earthquakes). These environments are typically marked by resource limitations, organizational instability, and excessive workload demands [1–4]. These extreme stressors have triggered a global mental health crisis among healthcare professionals, as demonstrated by markedly elevated rates of burnout, posttraumatic stress disorder, depression, and anxiety disorders [5–7]. In China, the prevalence of burnout among healthcare professionals ranges from 66.5% to 87.8%, indicating a severe occupational health concern. Prolonged exposure to emotional exhaustion, workplace violence, and role conflicts contribute to a psychological burden characterized by cumulative trauma [8–10]. The psychological impact often manifests as acute fear, persistent helplessness, or profound despair, particularly following critical incidents including patient mortality, adverse medical events, or workplace violence [11]. These factors have led to the development of chronic psychological trauma and emotional fatigue in many healthcare workers, making it imperative to explore mechanisms for psychological recovery and resilience [12–14].

Beyond mitigating distress caused by trauma, posttraumatic growth (PTG) fosters positive emotional states and strengthens coping capacity in individuals [15]. Therefore, the growth of healthcare workers in traumatic environments may gradually take effect through changes in their intrinsic psychological mechanisms, including resilience, belief systems, and adaptive cognitive processing. Therefore, scrutinizing how healthcare workers respond developmentally to sustained occupational trauma is crucial for illuminating how cumulative stress shapes their capacity for posttraumatic growth. Further research is warranted to elucidate the psychological mechanisms that drive posttraumatic growth, facilitate the reconstruction of personal psychological resources and beliefs, and identify key factors that foster self-directed growth in high-stress professional settings. Advancing this line of inquiry holds significant implications for strengthening the psychological well-being of healthcare workers, refining organisational support systems, and enhancing their preparedness for future high-impact events, including large-scale public health emergencies [16]. In summary, healthcare workers face significant job burnout in chronically high-pressure and traumatic work environments, while posttraumatic growth may serve as a breakthrough to alleviate their psychological depletion. This research therefore seeks to investigate the psychological pathways linking job burnout and posttraumatic growth, with a specific focus on the mediating roles of psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs.

Job burnout and posttraumatic growth

Posttraumatic growth was conceptualized as the positive psychological transformation that can occur as individuals attempt to cope with highly challenging or traumatic life experiences [17–19]. It refers to the positive psychological changes individuals undergo after grappling with traumatic events or situations, which emerge from their struggle with these events [20, 21]. These changes are not directly caused by the traumatic events themselves, but rather emerge from the individual’s struggle with those events. Posttraumatic growth is commonly conceptualized across five dimensions: Appreciation of Life (AL), Personal Strength (PS), New Possibilities (NP), Relating to Others (RO), and Spiritual Change (SC)—reflecting multidimensional growth in values, perceptions, and relationships. While existing research has largely focused on posttraumatic growth among survivors of natural disasters or acute illness, limited attention has been given to healthcare workers, who have faced prolonged and cumulative exposure to trauma during the COVID-19 pandemic and other health crises [22–26].

Job burnout refers to a psychological syndrome of excessive physical and mental consumption and energy exhaustion caused by an extended stressful work environment with high job requirements and low resources [17, 27]. It includes the three dimensions of emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and low sense of achievement, with emotional exhaustion as the core component [28]. It is evident that with a prevalence of about 50%, healthcare workers belong to the occupational groups that are particularly frequently affected by job burnout [29]. The job demands resources (JD-R) model provides a theoretical framework for explaining the occurrence of job burnout and its influence on the psychological adaptation of healthcare workers [3]. According to this model, job demands deplete energy, while job resources buffer stress and enhance resilience [30]. Job demands include factors that require continuous effort and consume physical and mental energy for work, such as work-family conflict, workload, etc. In comparison, job resources refer to resources that can maintain and promote the physical and psychological development of individuals, such as social support and positive feedback [3, 31, 32]. When job demands chronically outweigh available resources, individuals experience psychological depletion, heightening vulnerability to burnout, depressive symptoms, and diminished coping capacity [33, 34]. The high-intensity and uncertain work environment, along with resource constraints, has led to a continuous drain on healthcare workers [15]. Consequently, job burnout may erode the internal psychological resources required for adaptive processing, thereby obstructing the development of posttraumatic growth in healthcare workers.

H1: Job burnout is negatively associated with posttraumatic growth among healthcare workers.

Job burnout and posttraumatic growth: the mediating role of psychological resilience

Psychological resilience is an individual’s capacity to effectively adapt and recover in the face of adversity and stress [35, 36], which is a core component of psychological capital. As a broader construct, psychological capital facilitates individuals’ efforts to pursue meaning, purpose, and transformation following adversity, ultimately enhancing their perceived value of life and well-being [18, 37–39]. According to the JD-R model, psychological resilience—as a key job resource—is negatively associated with job burnout and other psychological demands [40], while positively correlated with adaptive psychological outcomes under stress [19, 41]. Psychological resilience serves as a critical antecedent of posttraumatic growth, enabling individuals not only to recover from adversity but also to exceed their previous levels of functioning in specific psychological domains after traumatic exposure [42]. Empirical studies consistently support a positive association between psychological resilience and posttraumatic growth, indicating that individuals with higher resilience are more likely to cope constructively with trauma and derive meaningful growth from such experiences [21, 28, 43]. Accordingly, job burnout may undermine psychological resilience in healthcare workers, thereby limiting their capacity to achieve posttraumatic growth.

H2: Psychological resilience plays a mediating role in the relationship between job burnout and posttraumatic growth among healthcare workers.

Job burnout and posttraumatic growth: the mediating role of positive stress beliefs

Stress and coping theory underscores that individuals’ stress perception and their relationship with stress and the environment are crucial factors influencing their perception and coping ability [32]. Stress beliefs refer to individuals’ perceptions of the impact of stress on their health (such as positive or negative effects) and their ability to manage the consequences of stress. These beliefs encompass three types: positive stress beliefs, negative stress beliefs, and stress controllability beliefs [44]. Of particular note, positive stress beliefs refer to the perception that stress can have enhancing or motivating effects—serving as a catalyst for adaptive behavior and emotional regulation rather than simply a threat [34, 45, 46]. Individuals with positive stress beliefs are more likely to interpret stressful experiences as challenges rather than threats, thereby engaging in active, problem-focused coping strategies [36, 37]. This belief system contributes positively to both mental health outcomes and behavioral functioning under pressure [31]. Individuals with positive stress beliefs adapt more effectively to negative events [47, 48]. Consequently, job burnout is associated with lower positive stress beliefs of healthcare workers, thereby related to lower posttraumatic growth.

H3: Positive stress beliefs play a mediating role in the negative relationship between job burnout and posttraumatic growth among healthcare workers.

Job burnout and posttraumatic growth: The chain mediating role of psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs

Job burnout has the potential to diminish positive stress beliefs, weakening healthcare workers’ ability to reinterpret and manage trauma adaptively. In contrast, psychological resilience strengthens positive coping tendencies and reduces maladaptive responses [40, 41, 49]. As a positive psychological quality, psychological resilience can reduce negative feelings by increasing successful beliefs and coping strategies [50–52]. In stressful contexts, individuals with high psychological resilience tend to hold positive stress beliefs, viewing stress as a challenge rather than a threat, and are more likely to derive growth from adversity [53]. Thus, job burnout may be associated with lower levels of psychological resilience and, in turn, with diminished positive stress beliefs—together reflecting a potential pattern of psychological disruption that correlates with reduced posttraumatic growth among healthcare workers.

H4: Psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs play a chain mediating role between job burnout and posttraumatic growth among healthcare workers.

Overview of present research

Building on the theoretical framework and empirical evidence discussed above, the present research seeks to examine the relationship between job burnout and posttraumatic growth among healthcare workers, as well as the mediation effects of psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs.

Methods

Participants

This research sample consisted of 1375 Chinese healthcare workers aged 20 to 60 years. The sample comprised 336 males and 1039 females, across various positions: 389 doctors, 112 medical technicians, 750 nurses, and 124 administrative and logistics staff. Marital status distribution was 460 unmarried, 897 married, 17 divorced, and 1 widowed. Educational levels included 89 with technical secondary school or below, 587 with college degrees, 618 with bachelor’s degrees, and 81 with master’s degrees or above.

Monte Carlo power analysis [54] indicated that, with 2000 replications (20000 Monte Carlo draws per Rep) and our final N = 1375, both simple mediation effects and chain mediation effects achieved power >0.95 for the full PTG scale and five PTG subscales.

Procedure

We recruited healthcare personnel in two Chinese cities and collected data on job burnout, posttraumatic growth, psychological resilience, and positive stress beliefs. After providing informed consent, participants completed the survey either through a secure online platform or via a printed questionnaire. All English items underwent forward–backward translation to guarantee linguistic and conceptual equivalence. Immediately after completing the measures, participants entered standard demographic details. This research was in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Weifang Medical University Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects.

Measures

Job burnout

The 15-item Maslach Burnout Inventory-General Survey (MBI-GS) was used to assess participants’ job burnout [55]. For example, “Working all day is really a strain for me”, “I have become less enthusiastic about my work”. The scale uses a seven-point Likert scale, from 1 for “never” to 7 for “every day”. Averaging all the questions, a higher score indicated higher job burnout. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficient of job burnout scale was 0.850.

Posttraumatic growth

The Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI) assesses positive outcomes following traumatic events [22, 56]. The scale consists of 21 items divided into five dimensions: Appreciation of Life (e.g., I changed my priorities about what is important in life), Personal Strength (e.g., I discovered that I’ m stronger than I thought I was), New Possibilities (e.g., I’ m able to do better things with my life), Relating to Others (e.g., I better accept needing others) and Spiritual Change (e.g., I have a better understanding of spiritual matters). The scale used a seven-point scale ranging from 1 (“I did not experience this change as a result of my crisis”) to 7 (“I experienced this change to a very great degree as a result of my crisis”), with higher scores indicating more posttraumatic growth. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficient of the PTGI scale was 0.959. Cronbach’s α for the five subscales were: Relating to Others = 0.919 (7 items), New Possibilities = 0.876 (5 items), Personal Strength = 0.881 (4 items), Spiritual Change = 0.783 (2 items), and Appreciation of Life = 0.540 (3 items).

Psychological resilience

The psychological resilience was measured using 7 items from the Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PPQ) (comprising 26 items) [46]. The PPQ is composed of four dimensions: self-efficacy, hope, psychological resilience, and optimism. We adopted the psychological resilience dimension, including such as “I can recover quickly when I encounter setbacks”. The scale used a seven-point Likert scale, with 1 = “completely inconsistent” and 7 = “completely consistent”. Average scores were calculated for all the questions, with higher scores indicating higher levels of psychological resilience. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficient of the psychological resilience scale was 0.843.

Positive stress beliefs

The positive stress beliefs were measured using 4 items from the Beliefs About Stress Scale (BASS) (comprising 15 items) [37]. The BASS is composed of three dimensions: positive stress beliefs, negative stress beliefs, and stress controllability beliefs. The positive stress beliefs dimension was adopted, including such as “Stress makes me more productive”. The scale used a seven-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “strongly disagree” and 7 indicating “strongly agree”. The average score of all the questions was calculated. The higher the score, the higher the level of positive stress beliefs. In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficient of the positive stress beliefs scale was 0.891.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics 27.0 software was used for statistical analyses, including correlation analyses and linear regression analyses. To further test the mediation effect, the bootstrap method was applied, and the PROCESS Model 6 was used to verify the mediation model [57].

Results

Common method variance

Common method variance was assessed via Harman’s single-factor test [58]. The results showed seven factors with eigenvalues >1. The first factor explained 36.09% of the variance, below the 40% threshold, indicating no significant common method bias in the study data.

Descriptive statistics and correlational analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated for job burnout, psychological resilience, positive stress beliefs, and posttraumatic growth. Results showed that healthcare workers had mean scores of M = 2.33 (SD = 0.75) for job burnout, M = 3.17 (SD = 0.62) for psychological resilience, M = 2.76 (SD = 0.59) for positive stress beliefs, and M = 3.28 (SD = 0.79) for posttraumatic growth. Job burnout was negatively correlated with psychological resilience (r = − 0.609, p < 0.001), positive stress beliefs (r = − 0.346, p < 0.001), and posttraumatic growth (r = − 0.452, p < 0.001); posttraumatic growth was positively correlated with psychological resilience (r = 0.510, p < 0.001) and positive stress beliefs (r = 0.363, p < 0.001); and psychological resilience was positively correlated with positive stress beliefs (r = 0.362, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Testing the hypothesized chain mediation model

A chain mediation model was tested using PROCESS Model 6 to investigate the indirect relationship between job burnout and posttraumatic growth, with psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs specified as chain mediators. Gender, age, marital status, education level, unit attribute, and job attribute were included as covariates. Analyses were conducted using 5000 bias-corrected bootstrap resamples, with 95% confidence intervals determining significance.

As shown in Fig. 1, job burnout was associated with lower levels of posttraumatic growth (B = − 0.460, Boot SE = 0.028, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [− 0.515, − 0.406]), and the effect remained significant after accounting for mediators (B = − 0.204, Boot SE = 0.033, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [− 0.267, − 0.141]), indicating partial mediation effect. All three indirect pathways were statistically significant: via psychological resilience alone (B = − 0.194, Boot SE = 0.023, 95% CI = [− 0.241, − 0.152]), via positive stress beliefs alone (B = − 0.037, Boot SE = 0.010, 95% CI = [− 0.061, − 0.022]), and via the sequential pathway through both mediators (B = − 0.025, Boot SE = 0.007, 95% CI = [− 0.039, − 0.014]) (Table 2).Fig. 1Path diagram of the total mediation effect. ***p < 0.001

Testing alternative models

To further validate the robustness and uniqueness of the hypothesized chain mediation model, we tested four alternative models by reconfiguring the functional roles of core variables (Fig. 2). Alternative model a: exchange of two mediators

Individuals’ cognitive appraisals of stress (e.g., positive stress beliefs) may shape their psychological resources (e.g., psychological resilience) [59, 60]. For example, individuals with a stress-is-enhancing mindset (aligned with positive stress beliefs) experience less psychological resilience depletion under high stress. Translating this to our context, job burnout may first erode healthcare workers’ positive stress beliefs, and this diminished cognitive framing could further weaken their psychological resilience, ultimately hindering posttraumatic growth [59, 61]. Therefore, the negative impact of job burnout on posttraumatic growth may be achieved by first weakening individuals’ positive stress beliefs and consequently reducing their psychological resilience. Alternative model A was tested by changing the order of the mediators, psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs. The results of alternative model A did produce a significant total indirect effect, B = − 0.256, SE = 0.024, 95% CI = [− 0.306, − 0.213], yet the path of its chain mediation was smaller (B = − 0.018, SE = 0.004, 95% CI = [− 0.026, − 0.012]) than the corresponding path in the hypothesized model (B = − 0.025, SE = 0.007, 95% CI = [− 0.039, − 0.014]). These findings suggest that the hypothesized sequence of mediators provides a more robust explanation for the underlying mechanism.

Alternative model b: exchange of independent and dependent variables

A study involving 138 pediatric nurses found a direct correlation between posttraumatic growth and job burnout. Additionally, posttraumatic growth exerts an indirect influence on job burnout through the mediating effect of secondary traumatic stress [62]. Therefore, posttraumatic growth may alleviate job burnout among medical staff either directly or indirectly. Alternative Model B was specified by reversing the positions of the independent variable (Job burnout) and the dependent variable (Posttraumatic growth). Compared to the hypothesized model (R = 0.482, R² = 0.233, F = 54.665, t = − 16.495, p < 0.001, B = − 0.460, SE = 0.028, 95% CI = [− 0.515, − 0.406]), alternative model B was inferior in terms of both total effect and model fit (R = 0.467, R² = 0.218, F = 48.795, t = − 16.712, p < 0.001, B = − 0.422, SE = 0.025, 95% CI = [− 0.572, − 0.373]). These findings lend greater support to the direction proposed in the hypothesized model.

Alternative model c: positive stress beliefs moderating the mediation path of psychological resilience

We also tested some moderation models to assess whether the hypothesized mediators might more appropriately function as moderators. Stress beliefs significantly moderate the relationship between adverse events and individual adjustment, including strengthening the protective role of resilience resources [63]. Specifically, individuals holding positive stress beliefs exhibited a greater capacity to sustain their psychological resilience levels under high-stress conditions, whereas those with negative stress beliefs experienced a significant decline in psychological resilience as stress intensity increased [59], and the positive stress beliefs enable individuals to construct the positive meaning of perceived work stress [64]. It thus seems reasonable to consider that positive stress beliefs can be a moderating variable to buffer the weakening effect of job burnout on psychological resilience. Alternative Model C posits that positive stress beliefs moderate the relationships among job burnout, psychological resilience and posttraumatic growth. The interaction effect of positive stress beliefs × job burnout on psychological resilience was not significant, B = − 0.044, SE = 0.037, t = − 1.202, p >0.05, 95% CI = [− 0.117, 0.028]. Consequently, treating positive stress beliefs as a second-stage mediator rather than as a moderator yields the more persuasive account.

Alternative model d: psychological resilience moderating the mediation path of positive stress beliefs

Resource-oriented perspectives posit that resilient individuals are better positioned to translate adaptive cognitions into growth outcomes. Theoretical research on resilience enhancement indicates that psychological resilience, as a core personal resource, can strengthen the stability and executive effectiveness of cognitive beliefs [19]. Taft et al. found that high psychological resilience can amplify belief-based behavioral effects [65]. Empirically, individuals with high psychological resilience are more likely to maintain positive stress beliefs, and the impact of their beliefs on emotional outcomes is more enduring. Thus, psychological resilience may also function as a moderating variable in the relationship between job burnout and positive stress beliefs. Alternative Model D treats psychological resilience as a moderator on the relations among job burnout, positive stress beliefs and posttraumatic growth. However, the interaction effect of psychological resilience × job burnout on positive stress beliefs was not significant, B = 0.040, SE = 0.030, t = 1.368, p >0.05, 95% CI = [0.172, − 0.017]. These results corroborate that psychological resilience functions as a precursor mediator that shapes positive stress beliefs, rather than as a boundary condition that amplifies their effect.

Overall, the hypothesized model aligns more closely with our research question and provides a better fit to both theoretical and empirical explanations.

Discussion

This research examines associations between job burnout and posttraumatic growth among healthcare workers, as well as the mediating role of psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs. Model estimates indicated that job burnout was negatively associated with posttraumatic growth and, moreover, was linked to lower levels of growth through associations involving reduced psychological resilience and diminished positive stress beliefs. These findings suggested that job burnout may impede posttraumatic growth by attenuating individuals’ psychological resilience and diminishing their positive cognitive evaluations of stress.

The relationship between job burnout and posttraumatic growth

Posttraumatic growth is widely recognized as a constructive developmental response to trauma, yet job burnout correlates with reduced positive outcomes in healthcare settings [66]. The JD-R model posits that healthcare workers not only fulfill routine clinical duties but also bear substantial responsibility for public health emergency response, often under conditions of inadequate support [67, 68]. Such roles expose them to doctor–patient conflicts and workplace violence, correlating with occupational stress. Numerous past studies have highlighted that vicarious trauma and job burnout are common work-related outcomes for healthcare workers [69–71]. Persistent high job demands coincide with depleted emotional resources, reduced work engagement and self-efficacy, and elevated risks for depression, burnout, and disengagement [72, 73]. Moreover, the abrupt and unpredictable nature of medical emergencies often constrains the availability of external resources for healthcare workers. Excessive job demands, extended working hours, and limited institutional support correspond to significant pressure on healthcare workers, associated with job burnout and a range of psychological symptoms. Chronic work-related stress frequently coincides with job burnout [68, 74], which is negatively associated with healthcare workers’ capacity to achieve posttraumatic growth.

The erosion of posttraumatic growth by work burnout is not merely a manifestation of cumulative psychological discomfort, rather, it suppresses the generation and expression of various dimensions of through loss of resources [75]. Specifically, high levels of occupational burnout are associated with a reduction in the enhancement of posttraumatic intimacy among healthcare professionals, which may be attributed to emotional exhaustion that weakens empathetic responses following trauma. Burnout limits healthcare workers’ future perspectives and reinforces learned helplessness, potentially decreasing their willingness and ability to transform trauma into new pathways. Regarding personal strength, burnout diminishes healthcare professionals’ experiences of success and internal attribution, making it difficult for them to establish a self-efficacy belief. Similarly, burnout reduces the perception of positive emotions and narrows the focus of attention, hindering the re-prioritization of life events. Additionally, burnout occupies individuals’ reflective resources and obstructs the construction of meaning, thereby preventing the trauma from triggering updates to beliefs or value systems [30].

The Spiritual Growth subscale of the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory (PTGI) captures positive changes in life philosophy and existential understanding [22], exemplified by items such as “I have become stronger in my faith” and “I have developed a deeper understanding of spiritual matters” [76]. Although the subscale has been routinely retained in Chinese samples [76], the literal wording of several items—especially those invoking explicit religious language—frequently exhibits sub-optimal cultural fit [77, 78]. Extant work suggests that, within the Chinese cultural milieu, people tend to construe spiritual growth less as a reinforcement of doctrinal belief and more as a heightened sense of life meaning and secular wisdom. Just as individuals in the context of Chinese culture may demonstrate a greater tendency to reposition themselves in relation to self, family, or social relationships after experiencing trauma, rather than a direct appreciation of life and spiritual changes [79–81]. Consistent with this line of reasoning, the present research preserved the conceptual core of the subscale while omitting overt references to deity or organized religion. Contrary to prior reports of poor item performance, we observed no discernible cultural dissonance. We speculate that the discrepancy arises, at least in part, from our recruitment base in southern Fujian—an area where folk spiritual traditions (e.g., Mazu worship, ancestral veneration) are deeply woven into everyday life.

The chain mediating role of psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs

Previous research on posttraumatic growth has predominantly focused on high-risk populations, while relatively little attention has been paid to identifying protective factors that might buffer against trauma [82]. Both intrapersonal resources—such as psychological resilience—and external supports—such as social connectedness—are increasingly recognized as key determinants of positive mental health outcomes [83]. The present findings confirm that job burnout is negatively associated with posttraumatic growth, whereas psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs exhibit a positive association. According to the psychological resilience-in-action framework, the fulfillment of individuals’ basic psychological needs through familial, peer, and societal support correlates with the presence of resilience-related traits. Psychological resilience, as a critical internal resource, enables individuals to adaptively manage adversity and regulate emotional responses in high-stress environments. In parallel, positive stress beliefs—characterized by proactive and constructive interpretations of stressors—relate to adaptive coping and psychological well-being. Together, resilience and positive stress beliefs are associated with reduced psychological reactivity to traumatic stressors, attenuate anxiety and cognitive distortions, and may be positively associated with posttraumatic growth through cognitive reappraisal and emotional regulation. Posttraumatic growth represents a positive psychological response to trauma. However, job burnout constitutes a significant obstacle to the realization of such growth in healthcare workers. Resilience-related traits are more likely to emerge when an individual’s psychological needs are supported by family, peers, and broader social systems [84].

Consistent with the JD-R model, healthcare workers are tasked with both routine clinical responsibilities and emergent public health duties, which significantly tax their cognitive and emotional bandwidth. Moreover, the sudden onset and unpredictability of public health crises further constrain access to external support systems, corresponding to exacerbated stress and reducing opportunities for psychological recovery. High job demands combined with insufficient work-related resources—especially in emergency or critical care scenarios—are strongly associated with job burnout and a broad spectrum of psychological symptoms [49]. Psychological resilience and positive stress belief systems act as internal buffers, correlating with reduced maladaptive stress responses to traumatic events such as pandemics. These psychological resources may associate with enhanced cognitive reappraisal, mitigate anxiety and fear, and support posttraumatic growth by fostering constructive meaning-making.

Therefore, a multi-level response involving governmental, institutional, and community-level social psychological service systems is essential for addressing healthcare workers’ burnout and psychological distress [85]. Policy-makers and hospital administrators should ensure the availability of adequate material, logistical, and psychological support systems to mitigate excessive work-related stress [86]. Such coordinated support is associated with reduced job burnout, enhanced resilience and constructive belief systems, and the promotion of posttraumatic growth by mobilizing both external resources and internal psychological capacities [87]. Furthermore, family and social support correlate with purposeful rumination, enabling trauma-exposed healthcare workers to reinterpret adverse experiences constructively and achieve posttraumatic growth [88]. Healthcare workers should be encouraged to proactively seek professional psychological support when necessary. Intervention strategies such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness training, and stress management techniques have showed correlations with reduced burnout-related symptoms [47, 71].

Limitations and future directions

This research has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design restricts the ability to examine the dynamic evolution of job burnout and posttraumatic growth across different periods [84]. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are essential to clarify the temporal sequence of job burnout, psychological resilience, positive stress beliefs, and posttraumatic growth. Such research should test the hypothesized mechanisms and identify critical intervention windows through multi-wave designs that capture within-person changes over time. Second, the lack of behavioral or physiological indicators limits the objectivity of the current findings. To achieve a more comprehensive and ecologically valid understanding of how burnout manifests and influences psychological outcomes, future studies should incorporate behavioral data and biomarkers. Finally, although this study examined the mediating roles of psychological resilience and positive stress beliefs, it did not explore potential moderators such as social support, personality traits, or organizational factors. These variables may influence the strength or direction of observed relationships and should be integrated into future models to enhance their explanatory power.

Conclusions

This research demonstrates that job burnout among healthcare workers is associated with diminished posttraumatic growth, manifesting both directly and indirectly through reduced psychological resilience and weakened positive stress beliefs. Crucially, these negative associations extend across all five posttraumatic growth dimensions. Practically, resilience-training programs and cognitive-behavioral interventions targeting stress belief reframing may mitigate burnout’s impact on posttraumatic growth in clinical settings.
