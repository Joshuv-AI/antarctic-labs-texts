The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on pediatric bloodstream infections and alteration in antimicrobial resistance phenotypes in Gram-positive bacteria, 2020–2022
Authors: Fatemeh Fallah, Abdollah Karimi, Leila Azimi, Ghazale Ghandchi, Zari Gholinejad, Nafiseh Abdollahi, Nazanin Ahari Oskooie, Hannan Khodaei, Shahnaz Armin, Azita Behzad, Seyedeh Masumeh Hashemi, Seyedeh Narjes Ahmadizadeh, Masoud Alebouyeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlteration in the etiology of pediatric bloodstream infections (BSIs) and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is not well known during the Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. This study aimed to investigate the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on pediatric BSIs and alteration in antimicrobial resistance phenotypes in Gram-positive bacteria.MethodsThe frequency of BSIs among children under 18 years old was retrospectively recorded in a tertiary children’s hospital in Tehran, Iran from February 2020 to December 2022. The status of COVID-19 infection using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, bacteremia/fungemia according to BACTEC 9120 Culture System results, characterization of bacteria using biochemical tests, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns for Gram-positive bacterial isolates using disk diffusion method were determined. Statistical analysis was done to measure the correlation of COVID-19 infection with BSIs and AMR.ResultsOut of 13,345 COVID-19 tests and 4,194 BACTEC blood culture requests, bacteremia/fungemia were confirmed in 10.37% (435/4,194) of the patients who requested both tests simultaneously. The COVID-19 infection was confirmed in 25.3% (110/435) of the patients with bacteremia/fungemia. The infection with characterized Gram-positive bacteria (GPB) and fungi was detected in 32.3% (140/433) and 8.31% (36/433) of the cases, respectively. Coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CNS, 72, 16.62%), S. aureus (36, 8.3%), and Enterococcus spp. (22, 5%) were among the common isolates. Candida spp. and non-Candida yeasts were detected in 6.7% and 13.4% of the cases, respectively. A positive correlation was shown between the CNS bacteremia and COVID-19 infection (p-value = 0.019). Antibiotic susceptibility testing results showed the highest frequency of resistance to azithromycin among CNS, azithromycin and tetracycline among S. aureus and tetracycline among Enterococcus spp. Methicillin-resistance phenotype in the S. aureus (MRSA) and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus spp. (MR-CNS) was detected in 40% and 61.5% of the strains, respectively and the Enterococci were resistant to vancomycin in 33.3% of the isolates.ConclusionA decline in the trend of BSIs by GPB and an increase in AMR was shown in children during the COVID-19 pandemic. Increasing antibiotic resistance is a concern; however, chloramphenicol, linezolid, and vancomycin remain active against common causes of GPB-BSIs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-05146-7.

Introduction

It is estimated that bloodstream infections (BSIs) may be responsible for 11% of deaths annually among children in high-income countries [1]. However, due to the lack of surveillance programs in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), it is not possible to accurately estimate the incidence and mortality of community-acquired and hospital-acquired BSIs, especially among children. According to available data, the global incidence rate of BSIs is 140 and 160 cases per 100,000 per year [2]. Although little is known about the impact of risk factors associated with the incidence of BSIs and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among children in LMICs, changes in the epidemiology of microbial agents in hospital and community settings through improvements in public health systems, the introduction of new antimicrobial drugs, and the implementation of infection prevention and control programs can be expected over time.

Although Gram-negative bacteria, as in adults, are the main cause of hospital acquired-BSIs (HABSIs) in pediatrics, yeasts and Gram-positive bacteria are also responsible for severe infections in this group of patients [3]. Among yeasts, Candida albicans and non-albicans species also play an important role in HABSIs in children [4, 5]. Candida spp. are the first cause of fungemia in hospitalized pediatrics, which can occur in both single-type or mixed-type infections with other Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria [6]. A higher risk of subsequent bacteremia and a relatively higher candidemia-attributable mortality rate were shown in patients with mixed-type Candida/bacterial BSIs, which highlights its management in children, mainly those with underlying diseases [6].

There are increasing reports about the emergence of methicillin- and vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA and VRSA, respectively), methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococcus spp. (MR-CNS), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus spp. (VRE), macrolides- and fluoroquinolone-resistant Streptococcus spp. (Group A and non-Group A) and their involvement in HABSIs and sepsis [7, 8]. Transmission of mobile genetic resistance markers at inter- and intraspecies levels contributes to the emergence of new variants presenting multi-drug resistance (MDR) phenotypes, which should be considered by clinicians in regions with a high prevalence [9]. COVID-19 pandemic, as the leading cause of infections during 2019–2022, favored the emergence of AMR, probably through an increase in the length of hospital stay and prescription of antibiotics [10]. There is little data on alteration in the etiology of HABSIs and resistance phenotypes after the pandemic. To reduce mortality in children with BSIs, identify responsible microbial agents, and characterize their resistance phenotypes to antimicrobials, new surveillance studies, especially in LMICs are needed. In the current study, we investigated the frequency of Gram-positive bacteria and fungi in suspected children of bacteremia during 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic in a referral Children’s hospital in Tehran, Iran. We also investigated AMR of Gram-positive bacteria and the association between bacteremia/fungemia and COVID-19 infection.

Materials and methods

Patients and Samples

This retrospective study was performed in a tertiary pediatric hospital in Tehran, Iran during the COVID-19 pandemics from February 2020 to December 2022. Blood samples of children who were clinically suspected of BSIs were included to this study. The blood samples were obtained by nurses and inoculated directly into BD BACTEC™ Peds Plus/F culture vials (Becton and Dickinson Microbiology System, Sparks, MD, USA), according to the manufacturer protocol. After reception in the laboratory, the vials were incubated in Becton Dickinson Diagnostic Instrument Systems (BACTEC 9120 automated Blood Culture System, BD, Microbiology System, Sparks, MD, USA). The samples belonged to < 18 years old children who were admitted to different wards of the tertiary pediatric hospital, including Bone Marrow Transplantation, Emergency Department, Gastroenterology, Infectious diseases, Pulmonary, Nephrology, Neurology, Oncology, Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), Rheumatology, and Surgery. All of the patients were tested for COVID-19 using real time polymerase chain reaction according to the hospital regulations, as described below. This study was approved and registered by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1401.060). No additional sampling and medical interventions were done for the patients during the study period. To analyze the laboratory and demographic data, the guardians or parents of children signed an informed consent form at admission time according to the teaching hospital’s regulations. Records of patient demographics in the patients’ hospital files were used in this study [11].

Microbial characterization, anti-microbial susceptibility testing and detection of multi-drug resistant phenotypes

The inoculated pediatric BD BACTEC™ Peds Plus/F bottles were incubated in BACTEC 9120 automated Blood Culture System and followed up for 7 days until a positive alarm. The positive vials were subjected to subculture onto conventional and differential culture media, including Sheep Blood agar (BA) and MacConkey agar (Condalab, Madrid, Spain), at aerobic conditions at 37 °C. In the case of Gram-positive bacteria and yeasts, the colony growth was examined on the BA plates after 24 h. The grown colonies were characterized according to standard protocols based on colony morphology, Gram staining, oxidase, catalase and biochemical reactions, susceptibility to bacitracin and optochin, and the production of α, β, Ɣ hemolysis on blood agar [12]. In conditions where no colonies were grown in the culture media, 2 ml of each bottle was centrifuged (4000 g, 10 min) and used for the Gram- and methylene blue staining. Upon recording a positive smear for fungi and yeasts, 200 µl of the sediment was subcultured on HiCrome™ Candida Differential Agar (HiMedia Laboratories, India) and Sabouraud dextrose agar (Merck, Germany) media. Susceptibility of the strains to antibiotics was determined for Coagulase-negative Staphylococcus spp. (CNS), S. aureus, Enterococcus spp., and Non-Group A Streptococcus spp. using antibiotic discs (MAST Group, UK) according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute criteria (www.clsi.org) [13]. Isolation of Corynebacterium spp. in the absence of clinical signs of BSIs was considered environmental contamination that indicates no antibiogram need. Resistance to three and more classes of antibiotics, defined as multidrug-resistant phenotype (MDR), was determined for each isolate according to Magiorakos et al. [14].

Detection of COVID-19

Nasopharyngeal swab samples from all the patients were obtained due to hospital regulation during the COVID-19 pandemic. Laboratory diagnosis of COVID-19 were performed using viral nucleic acid extraction kits (BEHGEN and SIMBIOLAB companies, Iran), real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qrtPCR) and IVD-approved molecular kit (PishtazTeb Diagnostics, Iran) according to manufacturer’s instructions as described before [15, 16].

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the package IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 26) (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and the Chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test was used for analyzing the data. A p-value < 0.05 was considered as significant. To check normality of each variable, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used. The Chi-square test was used for the categorical comparison of groups with a threshold of significance (p-value < 0.05). Fisher’s exact tests were used for cell sizes < 5. Phi and Cramer’s V, and Eta were considered as the measure of association between independent variables and outcome. Binary logistic regression was used to measure association of each infection with the defined age groups.

Results

Analysis of the automated BACTEC blood culture results confirmed bacteremia/fungemia in 10.37% patients (435/4,194). The positive COVID-19 test was detected in 25.3% of the patients with bacteremia/fungemia (110/435). Coincidence of bacteremia/fungemia and COVID-19 was detected in 10.4% (46/435) of the patients. The infection with Gram-negative bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria, and fungi was detected in 59.3% (257/433), 32.3% (140/433), and 8.31% (36/433) of the cases, respectively. Out of 433 blood culture samples, except for two Gram-positive bacterial isolates that were not characterized, CNS (72, 16.62%), S. aureus (36, 8.3%), Enterococcus spp. (22, 5%) and Enterobacter (12, 2.7%) were among the common isolates linked to BSIs in children. In this study, the Micrococcus spp. blood isolates (3, 1.69%) were not considered a causative agent for BSI, due to their presence as a common member of the skin microbiota. Details about types of the Gram-negative isolates, their antibiotic susceptibility, and their correlation with COVID-19 were presented in another study [11]. Among the patients with Gram-positive bacterial BSIs (BSI-GPB) or fungemia (BSI-F), no significant association was found between the microorganisms and underlying diseases (p-value = 0.685), gender (p-value = 0.678) and age groups (Table 1). However, a significant difference was measured among different wards of the hospital (p-value = 0.01). Accordingly, the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit, Emergency Room and Bone Marrow Transplantation wards showed the highest frequency of bloodstream infection with GPB, respectively (30.2%, 21.2% and 14%, respectively) (Table 2) (Fig. 1, Additional file 1). S. epidermidis and S. aureus were common causes of BSIs-GPB in all the hospital wards (Fig. 2, Additional file 1). Enterococcus was the main cause of infection in the Oncology ward (Two-fold higher than S. aureus) and the third cause of BSIs-GPB in most hospital wards. Candida spp. were detected in a frequency of 6.7%, which was lower than the frequency of the infection with non-Candida yeast and fungi infections (13.4%). Candida spp. were mainly isolated from the patients in the Infectious Diseases and Surgery wards. Fungemia, regardless to types of fungi, were mainly detected in patients in the PICU and Gastroenterology wards (Table 2) (Fig. 1, Additional file 1).

Discussion

In light of the globally increasing AMR in neonates and pediatrics [7, 17], mainly in developing countries, regional studies for the detection of the etiology of BSIs and AMR are necessary. COVID-19 is considered as a risk factor for hospital-acquired infection [18]. Our study analyzed the status of COVID-19 RT-qPCR in patients with positive BACTEC results. Due to the lack of related data for all patients about the co-incidence of COVID-19 and BSIs during the study period, we were unable to analyze the risk of COVID-19 infection in the occurrence of community or hospital-acquired BSIs. Such a correlation was reported by other studies both in adults and children [19–22]. The correlation could be attributed to immunosenescence, organ dysfunction, early mechanical ventilation and use of other medical devices, ICU admission, longer length of hospital stay, and administration of immunosuppressive medications [20, 21]. An increase in the incidence of secondary infections, mainly by Candida spp., S. aureus, Klebsiella spp. and P. aeruginosa, in admitted patients with COVID-19 was reported in some other studies [19, 23, 24]. In a recent retrospective study in China, no significant change in the frequency of BSIs by the Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria was reported in comparison to the pre-pandemic period [25]. To understand the impact of COVID-19 on the etiology of BSIs-GPB and AMR phenotypes among our hospitalized children during the pandemic, we also compared our findings with data at the pre-COVID-19 period in our hospital. Accordingly, in a retrospective study in our hospital in 2013–2018, GPB was considered as the cause of BSIs in 45% of the cases with positive BACTEC results, which was relatively higher than its frequency in the current study (32.3%). This difference was mainly attributed to an increase in the frequency of Klebsiella spp. (8.8% vs. 20.2%) and a decrease in the frequency of CNS (32.5% vs. 15.8%) [26]. Although consistent with our results, CNS and S. aureus, as common members of the skin microbiota, and Enterococci, as a common member of the intestine, were among the most frequent GPB in both periods, the improvement in hand hygiene by healthcare staff during the COVID-19 pandemic may explain the reduction of BSIs by CNS. A decrease in the trend of BSIs by CNS, mainly S. epidermidis and S. hominis, in children from 2018 to 2021 was similarly reported by a study in China, which indicated a significant impact of COVID-19 on the etiology of BSIs [27]. To prevent bias in overestimating the involvement of skin microbiota in the onset of BSIs before the COVID-19 pandemic or decrease in their involvement in the occurrence of BSIs after the pandemic, differentiation of real infection from false-positive results is necessary. Some of the main criteria for differentiation of real infection and contamination include the resistance profile of isolates, a time to positivity of ≤ 16 h, reports of at least two independent positive blood cultures within 5 days, or one positive blood culture if the clinical symptoms are suggestive of an infection, and predisposing conditions, like neutropenia, the presence of a central venous catheter, and absence of another likely infection at the time of blood culture [28]. Further studies are needed to unravel this correlation.

The etiology of BSIs differs by region, age group, and the epidemiological status of the responsible pathogens in the community and hospital environment [29]. In a 7-year prospective study in a Children’s hospital in England, Gram-positive bacteria accounted for more BSIs episodes (66.2%), compared with Gram-negative bacteria and yeasts (31.3% and 2.2%, respectively) [30]. A relatively two-fold higher crude frequency of Gram-negative bacteria in pediatric-BSIs (60% vs. 33% Gram positives and 7% fungal) was reported in a referral South African Children’s hospital; however, considering the source of infection, Gram-positive pathogens showed higher prevalence in community-acquired BSIs, while in hospital-acquired sepsis Gram-negative and fungal pathogens were more prevalent [31]. Consistent with the measured frequency of GBP-BSIs in this study and those for Gram-negative bacteria in our hospital [11], in a retrospective study in a pediatric hospital in Iran from 2015 to 2019, BSIs with Gram-negative bact Consistent with the results of GBP-BSIs in this study and those for Gram-negative bacteria in our hospital, in a retrospective study in a pediatric hospital in Iran from 2015 to 2019, BSIs with Gram-negative bacteria were relatively higher than Gram-positive bacteria (46% and 54%, respectively) [3]. Their results showed CNS, Streptococcus spp. and S. aureus as leading causes of BSIs; however, in total, Gram-negative bacteria were more abundant than Gram-positive bacteria. The involvement of CNS and S. aureus as a common cause of BSIs-GPB was similarly reported by other studies [32]. In a European point prevalence survey on children and neonates, although Enterobacteriaceae was detected as the most isolated microorganism (28.8%), followed by CNS (20.9%) and S. aureus (10.5%), CNS was reported as the most common bacteria in neonates and infants < 12 months of age [33]. In our study, BSIs-GPB were mainly detected in the PICU, emergency room, and BMT wards. Among the characterized Gram-positive bacteria, CNS and S. aureus were dominant in most wards of the hospital, while Enterococcus spp. were the main cause of BSIs-GPB in the Oncology ward, which was statistically significant. In a recent study in our hospital, intestinal carriage and endogenous transmission of Enterococcus spp. to the bloodstream was established in children at oncology and BMT wards [34, 35]. The increased permeability of the intestine through the administration of chemotherapeutic drugs and infection control issues are assumed as main risk factors for the Enterococcus spp. mediated bacteremia [36]. The higher prevalence of CNS-BSIs among our patients in the Nephrology, PICU, BMT and Neurology wards could be explained by the prolonged stay and underlying conditions [33]. CNS and Corynebacterium spp. are considered members of the bacterial skin microbiota, which could cause BSIs upon the use of invasive medical device. Children with hematologic, oncologic, nephrological, and neurological diseases are at increased risk for BSIs by these opportunistic bacteria, which can be due to immunosuppression following neutropenia, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and mucosal barrier disruption [37, 38]. Differentiation of true bacteremia from contamination in these infections is main concern for the management of diseases, mainly in immunocompromised pediatric patients [39].

Most of the GNB strains showed MDR phenotype during the study period and were resistant to a wide range of antibiotics. Details about the resistance profiles for GNB are presented and discussed before [11]. Among the GPB, all the Enterococcus spp. isolates in the blood samples were susceptible to linezolid and chloramphenicol and 66.6% of them were susceptible to vancomycin, which were relatively higher in comparison to the results of previous survey in our hospital during 2013–2018 (93.2%, 60%, and 46.4%, respectively) [26]. The highest proportion of resistance in the Enterococcus isolates was related to tetracycline (81.2%), that was consistent with the pre-COVID-19 status [26]. In the same comparative view, the CNS isolates showed a relatively similar susceptibility to linezolid (91.5% vs. 95.3%) and vancomycin (93.1% vs. 96.4%), but higher susceptibility to chloramphenicol (89.8% vs. 70%). In the current study, methicillin resistance was measured according to resistance phenotype to cefoxitin, while data of the pre-pandemic period for methicillin were according to the oxacillin test (MRSA, 67.2% and MR-CNS, 85.8%). The higher frequency of MR-CNS strains compared with MRSA (61.5% vs. 40%) was similarly reported by other studies on pediatrics [3, 40]. In our study, the S. aureus isolates showed a relatively higher frequency of AMR to most classes of the antibiotics. The difference in susceptibility was meaningfully higher for azithromycin (50% vs. 5.8%), tetracycline (66.6 vs. 9%), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (41.5% vs. 25%), and clindamycin (51.9% vs. 26.3%) in 2013–2018 vs. 2020–2022. In line with our findings, a very low frequency of resistance to vancomycin was reported among the blood S. aureus isolates in children (95.5-100% susceptibility), while a controversy was presented in the frequency of the susceptibility to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (87%), clindamycin (55%) [3, 26]. A pooled prevalence of 63% (45–78%) for MRSA in hospitalized children with BSIs was reported in a meta-analysis in Iran, which was higher than its frequency in the current study (40%) and those reported by Moghaddam et al. (34%) [3, 41]. In our study, S. aureus isolates showed high resistance to azithromycin, which could be attributed to changes in the AMR frequency after COVID-19 pandemic [42]. The overuse of antibiotics to treat secondary bacterial infections in COVID-19 patients was proposed as a contributing factor in the increase in antibiotic-resistance in bacterial strains [43, 44]. In the case of BSIs caused by Staphylococcus and Enterococcus spp., while results of a meta-analysis showed that COVID-19 pandemic was not associated with changes in the incidence and the proportion of MRSA and VRE [45], there are conflicting results in other studies that support the impact of COVID-19 on increase of the incidence of Gram-positive bacteremia [46, 47]. According to findings of current study, it seems that chloramphenicol, linezolid, and vancomycin remain active against common causes of GPB-BSIs in the studied population during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although our results showed low frequency of probable-pandrug resistance strains of CNS, high frequency of the MDR phenotype among the S. aureus and Enterococcus strains associated with pediatric BSIs highlights the need for the implementation of infection control programs in pediatric hospitals, mainly in low and middle-income countries.

A higher rate of mortality after Candidemia had been reported among neonates compared with pediatrics, which particularly could increase when co-infection with bacteria exists [6]. Our study detected no significant difference in the infection with yeasts and Candida spp. between the defined age groups. Although fungemia showed higher frequency in PICU, Candida spp. were mainly detected in the Infectious Diseases and Surgery wards. High rate of colonization due to an increase in days of hospitalization and medical interventions like the use of central venous line and mechanical ventilation, parenteral nutrition, bacterial infection, immunocompromised status, surgery, underlying diseases like dialysis, and antibiotic therapy seem to be the main cause of infection with yeasts in these units [48]. No mixed-type Candida/bacterial BSIs was detected in our patients, however, in a study by Lee et al. on pediatric-BSIs, S. aureus showed the highest frequency of co-infection (19%), followed by Klebsiella pneumonia (14.3%) and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (11.9%) [6].

Conclusions

A decline in the trend of BSIs by GPB and an increase in resistance to antibiotics was shown in children during the COVID-19 pandemic. CNS, S. aureus and Enterococci remain as main cause of BSIs-GPB in all hospital wards, while Candida spp. and yeasts play a major role in specific wards. The increase in antibiotic resistance could be described by the overuse of antibiotics, like azithromycin, in COVID-19 patients; however, differences in types of interventions in the hospital wards may explain the measured difference in the etiology of BSIs-GPB. This highlights the importance of well-designed prophylactic regimens and infection prevention and control programs for each ward.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, a causal relationship between COVID-19 infection and BSIs in children was not establish due to its retrospective nature. Second, owing to unknown confounding factors, the generalization of results of this single-center study to broader population may be restricted. Third, considering the high frequency of coagulase-negative Staphylococci and other bacteria and yeasts that are linked to the skin and gut microbiota, it is challenging to differentiate between community-acquired and hospital-acquired BSIs. Moreover, due to a lack of data about the length of hospital stay, time of BACTEC-positivity after admission, and death rate the estimation of false-positive test results is not possible and attention should be paid about actual involvement of these bacteria in BSIs in pediatrics. Fourth, the characterization of the isolated GPB and yeast isolates were not done to the species level. Fifth, underestimation of fastidious and anaerobic bacteria or fungi, and their role in the pediatric bacteremia/fungemia may have occurred due to incubation time (7 days vs. 10–14 days), unavailability of BD BACTEC™ Plus Anaerobic or BACTEC Mycosis IC/F bottles in the studied hospital. Sixth, although MIC test is necessary for detection of susceptibility to vancomycin for all isolates of Staphylococci, this test was not done in our hospital and the actual resistance rate to this antibiotic may be lower than the reported frequency in this study. Future studies are needed to improve our understanding of the impact of COVID-19 pandemic on BSIs by different types of bacteria and fungi in children.
