Challenges in tracer development for tumor microenvironment (TME) imaging
Authors: Sebastian Martin, Lennard Wendlinger, Hélène Koch, Ines Lopez-Martinez, Gianluca Corsanici, Giulia Battistini, Yaileen Bonvin, Judith Delage, David Viertl, Radmila Faizova, Alexandra Litvinenko, Margret Schottelius
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular imaging of the tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as a powerful approach to non-invasively interrogate immune cell composition, activation state and functional dynamics, thereby addressing key limitations of biopsy-based biomarkers in immuno-oncology. Early clinical successes, particularly in immune checkpoint and CD8⁺ T-cell imaging, underscore the translational potential of this strategy. However, the development of robust and clinically relevant TME-targeted radiopharmaceuticals is confronted with a distinct set of biological, technical and methodological challenges that fundamentally differentiate it from classical tumor imaging. These include low and dynamic target expression, tight coupling between tracer affinity, molar activity and receptor occupancy, limited suitability of standard in vitro assays, increasing complexity of in vivo models, and heightened vulnerability to pitfalls in image interpretation and quantification.This review provides a comprehensive analysis of these challenges across the tracer development pipeline, spanning target selection, molecular format choice, radiochemistry, preclinical evaluation and data analysis. Rather than representing prohibitive obstacles, these limitations delineate the biological and technical boundary conditions within which TME imaging strategies must be developed and interpreted. Emerging solutions include the growing role of peptides and engineered antibody fragments, stepwise and question-driven evaluation workflows, harmonized preclinical models and integrated targeting concepts that align biological relevance with technical feasibility. Together, these considerations provide a framework for sustainable and translational progress in TME imaging.

Introduction

Undisputedly, immunotherapies based on checkpoint inhibitors, cytokines, and vaccines as well as cell-based therapies such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy have led to remarkable success in the treatment of specific solid and hematological cancers. But despite their great promise, immunotherapies are compromised by relatively low objective response rates [1], severe immune-related toxicities as well as primary and secondary resistance to therapy [2].

There is general consensus that one of the biggest challenges for improving the outcomes of immunotherapies lies in identifying patients who may have a response and to exclude those with a low possibility to respond. Intense efforts have therefore been directed towards the search for reliable predictive factors. These factors can be host-associated (e.g. age, sex, body mass index, comorbidities, nutrition, gut microbiome composition), immune system activation-related (e.g. IFN-gamma gene expression profile, circulating immune cell subsets), and tumor-related (e.g. checkpoint expression, tumor mutation burden, microsatellite instability, mismatch repair system deficiency, composition of the tumor microenvironment), and all contribute to the success or failure of immunotherapy via intricately interwoven crosstalk [3]. Many of the above biomarkers have been shown to be associated with response to immunotherapy, but they fail to predict tumor responses effectively and accurately [4], largely due to their complex and dynamic interplay.

Amongst others, the immune status of the tumor microenvironment (TME) before and during treatment has also emerged as a central determinant for the outcome of immunotherapies. However, biopsy-based biomarker analyses cannot capture the fluctuating dynamics of the continuously evolving TME across primary tumor and metastases. In contrast, molecular imaging can provide real-time, systemic information, not only on the expression levels of immune checkpoints as therapeutic targets, but also on the spatial distribution, abundance and even activation state of specific immune cell subsets. Several recent clinical studies have demonstrated the prognostic value of PD-L1- and PD-1 checkpoint imaging, as summarized in an excellent comprehensive review [5]. Furthermore, PET imaging of the spatial and temporal whole-body distribution of CD8 + cytotoxic T-cells has emerged as a valuable tool for patient stratification, early response assessment, characterization of CD8-mediated immune-related adverse events (irAEs), and prediction of immunotherapy outcome [6]. Similarly, PET imaging of T cell activation via quantification of Granzyme B release ([68Ga]Ga-Grazytracer [7]) or of T-cell specific metabolism (18F-labeled Arabinofuranosyl guanine; [18F]FAraG [8]) also has shown considerable potential for response assessment ([68Ga]Ga-Grazytracer, targeting Granzyme B) or the pretherapeutic prognosis of immunotherapy outcome ([18F]FAraG, targeting Deoxycytidine kinase). Beyond immune cell–focused approaches, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) represent another major regulatory component of the TME with potent immunosuppressive properties, and fibroblast activation protein (FAP) has consequently emerged as a prominent imaging and theranostic target. However, as FAP-directed tracer development has been comprehensively reviewed elsewhere [9–11], it will not be covered in detail in this review.

The above developments collectively illustrate that TME imaging can interrogate different, complementary dimensions of the antitumor immune response. A useful distinction in this context is between phenotypic and functional imaging. Phenotypic approaches assess the presence and density of defined cell populations or marker expression (e.g. CD8, macrophages, PD-L1, TIGIT), whereas functional imaging reflects cellular activation or effector activity (e.g. Granzyme B release or T-cell–specific metabolism with 1⁸F-FAraG). These strategies address complementary but fundamentally different clinical questions and differ in signal dynamics as well as in their requirements for tracer design and imaging protocols, as discussed in the following sections.

Despite conceptual and clinical progress, however, the field of TME imaging is still in its infancy. On the one hand, this is due to the still incomplete understanding of the biological roles of individual immune cell subsets and their dynamic interactions with each other and with tumor cells in shaping response, resistance, and failure of immunotherapy. Beyond these biological constraints, additional challenges arise in the practical development of TME-targeted tracers. Classical “tumor cell imaging” addresses abundant numbers of target cells, which in turn strongly overexpress the molecular target (at copy numbers of app. 500.000 per cell), and suitable in vitro and in vivo models are usually well established. In contrast, TME imaging aims to visualize low numbers of cells with usually low expression levels of the molecular target (about 5.000 -60.000 copies per cell) in a complex, fluctuating environment. This and a lack of readily available, standardized models for in vitro and in vivo evaluation represent significant challenges for moving TME-targeted tracers efficiently along the translational pipeline. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the inherent complexity of TME-targeted tracer development. It specifically highlights how molecular format, labeling technology, affinity, and pharmacokinetics determine imaging performance and translational potential, and how these constraints can be mitigated or integrated into sound image interpretation.

Challenge: selection of relevant targets

In the context of classical tumor imaging, the sensitive detection and visualization of tumor cells with high contrast to the surrounding healthy tissue is the primary objective. There is an established set of prerequisites that need to be met by molecular targets to qualify as suitable for targeting with radiopharmaceuticals and to provide clinically relevant imaging information [97]. Ideally, the expression level of a molecular target on tumor cells should also correlate with the severity of the disease, e.g. tumor stage, disease prognosis and therapy outcome. While this is not an absolute prerequisite, it provides additional information on tumor biology and pathophysiology, strengthening the relevance of targeted imaging for diseases prognosis and management [99–101].

Overall, in the context of tumor cell imaging, a molecular target is considered clinically relevant, if it fulfils the above requirements, and if the imaging information gained by targeting it with dedicated radiopharmaceuticals has notable impact on treatment planning and outcomes for patients. Especially the criterion of reliably providing relevant biological information has become a highly important benchmark for differentiating truly translational targeting concepts from the abundance of imaging approaches that may be valuable for basic science investigations but have not progressed beyond proof-of-concept studies.

When it comes to TME imaging, it is much more complicated to define criteria that qualify a specific biomarker as a clinically relevant imaging target. Firstly, virtually none of the molecular targets addressed for TME imaging so far (Table 1), is TME-specific or exclusively expressed by only one cell population within the TME (e.g. PD-L1). On the contrary, immune cells, their systemic distribution, their presence in the TME, and their activation state are at the center of attention in TME imaging. Thus, TME imaging almost exclusively addresses physiological molecular targets of the immune system at endogenous expression levels. Thus, neither “specificity for the disease” nor “abundance” (see points 1. and 2. above) are applicable criteria for target selection in this context. Secondly, the currently available data (Table 1) suggest, that virtually all imaging targets addressed so far, both clinically and preclinically, are biologically relevant, either as predictive (chose treatment) or prognostic (forecast outcome) biomarkers.

Thus, the parameters defining what qualifies a biomarker as a clinically relevant molecular target for TME imaging need to be re-evaluated and adapted. Generally, the clinical utility of a targeting concept in molecular imaging is intrinsically linked to the in vitro and in vivo characteristics of the imaging probe used. Therefore, the assessment of the potential relevance of a TME imaging target for impacting patient management should encompass the entire “targeting concept”, i.e. the molecular target as well as the radiopharmaceutical format and its characteristics.

In this context, the guiding question “Are we answering the right question—with the right tools?” may serve as a highly useful criterion for the combined validation of molecular targets and tracers in TME imaging. By ensuring compatibility between the molecular target (e.g., expression level and dynamics of up- and downregulation), tracer characteristics (e.g., affinity and pharmacokinetics), and the sensitivity of the available imaging devices, reproducible and reliable preclinical imaging data can be obtained. Such data provide a robust basis for assessing the relevance of a given targeting concept in addressing a specific clinical question.

PD-L1 imaging, for instance, enables non-invasive whole-body assessment of immune checkpoint expression, thereby overcoming the well-recognized sampling bias of single-site biopsies. Clinical studies have demonstrated associations with response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, supporting its primary role in baseline patient stratification [5, 12]. CD8⁺ T-cell imaging addresses a complementary aspect—namely the presence and distribution of cytotoxic effector cells within the tumor. Recent data indicate that PET-derived tumor-to-blood ratios may outperform established biomarkers, including PD-L1 immunohistochemistry, Ki-67, and circulating CD8 counts, in predicting lesion-level response [105]. In contrast, Granzyme B imaging reflects a different biological layer by reporting on effector function rather than cell presence, making it particularly suited for early on-treatment response assessment, where it has shown superior predictive performance compared with CT (RECIST 1.1) and [1⁸F]FDG PET (PERCIST) for progression-free survival [7]. At the opposite end of the spectrum, imaging of immunosuppressive TME components, such as CD206⁺ macrophages [65] or tumor hypoxia [83, 84] addresses the question of whether the microenvironment itself is likely to impede an effective antitumor immune response, with potential implications for therapy adaptation and combination strategies [83].

Together, these examples illustrate that TME imaging does not address a single uniform clinical objective. Rather, the relevance of a given targeting approach depends on therapeutic context, treatment phase, and the specific immune process under investigation. Notably, several imaging strategies may inform more than one clinical question—for example, PD-L1 imaging in both baseline stratification and treatment monitoring—emphasizing the importance of defining the intended clinical application a priori when assessing the translational potential of a TME imaging approach.

In the following sections, the complex interplay between target density and dynamics, tracer affinity and molar activity, as well as tracer pharmacokinetics and in vitro/in vivo study design is discussed in more detail, highlighting the specific challenges associated with tracer development for TME imaging.

Challenge: low target density

The accumulation of radiopharmaceuticals in the target tissue, i.e. the tumor and its metastases, and thus the achievable imaging contrast are largely determined by two major factors: on the one hand, by the radiopharmaceutical itself with its specific characteristics (e.g. target affinity, pharmacokinetics, tissue penetration etc.), and on the other hand, by the expression density of the molecular target in the tumor. It has been demonstrated that for a given radiopharmaceutical with defined affinity and pharmacokinetics, its uptake in the target tissue will linearly correlate with the number of available binding sites [106].

The maximum number of available binding sites in a specific tissue (= Bmax tissue) is defined by two parameters: first, by the number of cells expressing the biomarker, and second, by the maximum number of binding sites/cell (= Bmax cell).

Of note, the absolute cell numbers presented above are derived from a murine syngeneic tumor model and should be extrapolated to human tumors with caution. Human solid tumors typically display considerably larger volumes, more heterogeneous immune infiltration patterns and a substantially different stromal architecture compared to subcutaneous murine allografts [114, 115]. Nevertheless, the relative proportions and the orders of magnitude of immune cell populations within the TME are broadly conserved across species and thus serve the purpose of illustrating the general challenges of TME imaging discussed herein.

Although the CAF content of human tumors varies considerably, even within a single tumor entity (e.g. 12–58% in human head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [116]), the combination of a high proportion of FAP-positive cells in the TME and very high FAP expression levels on CAFs (~ 450,000 copies per cell [117]) results in a robust PET signal (SUVmax ~ 8) and high tumor-to-background ratios [9, 118]. Nevertheless, SUVmax values achieved in clinical tumor-cell imaging, for example with [68Ga]PSMA-11 (SUVmax ~ 20–30), remain substantially higher [119].

As FAP, PD-L1 is also abundantly expressed in the TME, both by tumor cells (Fig. 2) and by immunosuppressive immune cell subtypes such as macrophages and monocytes [120]. Therefore, this target also provides the required density for successful high-contrast molecular imaging (Table 1).

Other examples, where both cell abundance in the TME and the cellular target expression level are high enough to allow for specific imaging, but for which low tumor/background ratios may already complicate image interpretation, are e.g. CD8 + T cells and CD206 + macrophages (Table 1, Figs. 1 and 2). Here, the observed SUVmax values in clinical imaging are comparably low (≤ 5), even when antibody fragments with long circulation times, providing high absolute accumulation in the target cells, are used [39, 40]. For tracers with faster clearance kinetics such as the CD8-targeted nanobody [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-SNA006 [44] or an anti-CD206-single domain antibody [65], even lower SUVmax values in the range of 2–4 were found, even for tumors which were highly infiltrated with the respective target cell population.

In summary, there appears to be a minimum threshold in the number of available binding sites per tumor volume that is required to achieve sufficient tracer uptake for an unambiguous distinction between the presence and absence of a given target cell population in the TME (i.e. specific versus non-specific uptake). Based on clinical imaging data and the estimates summarized in Figs. 1 and 2 (see also Fig. 4 for quantitative immune cell infiltration in syngeneic tumor models), this threshold can be approximated at 50.000–100.000 million available binding sites per 500 mm3 tumor, using CD8⁺ T cells as a representative example. This estimate is inherently dependent on tracer properties, particularly target affinity and pharmacokinetics, but appears realistic for most TME-targeted radiopharmaceuticals, which typically exhibit low-nanomolar to picomolar binding affinities. Although clearly approximate, this threshold may serve as a practical reality check for radiopharmaceutical development: if flow cytometry and binding data indicate that the density of a novel TME target lies orders of magnitude below this range, the likelihood of successful in vivo imaging may be severely limited.

Challenge: affinity, molar activity and receptor occupancy

The core element of the tracer principle, first published by Georg de Hevesy in 1923 [121], is the finding that radioactive isotopes can be used to track biological processes without interfering with them. Intrinsically, this implies that the isotope or the targeted molecule labelled with the isotope (“tracer”) is present at very low concentrations, preventing pharmacological effects and thus alterations in the system, such as e.g. receptor activation and thus potential toxicities.

The Langmuir binding isotherm (Fig. 3) is derived from the law of mass action [122, 123] and describes the fractional occupation of a given saturatable molecular target (epitope, receptor, enzyme) as a function of the affinity of the receptor ligand (KD in nM) and its concentration [nM]. For classical drugs, receptor saturation and thus a maximal pharmacological effect are desirable. Given that the drug is non-toxic at higher doses, this can even be safely achieved for compounds with affinities in the μM range (grey oval in Fig. 3). In contrast, adherence to the tracer principle requires low receptor occupancy at low ligand doses – and this can only be achieved for ligands with high affinity (low nM to pM affinities, blue oval in Fig. 3).Fig. 3Langmuir isotherms for different fractional receptor occupancies [122, 123]; examples (clinical dosing in 70 kg patient): red star: 89Zr-trastuzumab [124], blue star: [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE [125, 126], green star: 18F-rhPSMA-7.3 [127, 128], red dot: [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-WL12 [129], green dot: [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-DTBP-3 [36]; representative preclinical example (25 g mouse): blue dot and open blue dot: [.64Cu]Cu-anti-CXCR3 [130]

For radiopharmaceuticals with a defined affinity, the injected ligand dose (and thus the initial blood concentration) is determined by two factors: (1) the required activity for efficient imaging, and (2) the molar activity of the tracer (in GBq/μmol or MBq/nmol). The latter is in turn determined by the radionuclide and the radiolabeling strategy [131]. To illustrate this, Fig. 3 shows the relative positioning of the clinically used peptide-based tracers [68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE (blue star) [125, 126] and [18F]F-rhPSMA-7.3 (green star) [127, 128] and of the HER2-targeted antibody [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab (red star) [124] as representative examples for ligands with low molar activity (40 GBq/μmol) and very high affinity ([68Ga]Ga-DOTATATE), high molar activity (320 GBq/μmol) and high affinity ([18F]F-rhPSMA-7.3) as well as very low molar activity (0.11 GBq/μmol) and high affinity ([89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab). Of note, this positioning is based on the calculated ligand concentration at the time point of injection into a 70 kg patient with a plasma volume of 3.5 L. Although this is a simplistic approach that neglects pharmacokinetic effects, it nevertheless serves to provide illustrative comparative values for the different levels of receptor occupancy achieved by these tracers.

As shown above, and with only few exceptions, peptide radiopharmaceuticals usually are truly administered at “tracer level”, leading to maximum receptor occupancies of app. 50%. In contrast, high contrast imaging using antibody-based radiopharmaceuticals oftentimes requires co-injection of significant amounts of unlabeled antibody to saturate non-specific Fc-mediated tracer accumulation [132, 133] and/or to reduce specific accumulation in non-target organs (“antigen sink”) [134]. Of note, Fc-mediated, non-specific tracer accumulation can be efficiently reduced by either using pretreatment with non-targeted immunoglobulins (IgG) [135] or Fc-engineered/deglycosylated antibodies [136, 137]. In contrast, the antigen sink effect can only be circumvented by coadministration of cold, targeted antibody. Consequently, molecular imaging with radiolabeled antibodies is often performed at dose levels close to receptor saturation. This is of particular importance in preclinical studies and becomes crucial when targeting epitopes/receptors with low expression, such as targets in the TME.

Thus, several studies have employed careful dose titration to identifying the maximum applicable dose that still allows imaging of sparsely expressed TME targets such as e.g. PD-1 [138] or CXCR3 [130] (also see Table 3). For these epitopes, specific PET detection of target expression was only possible at doses as low as 10 pmol/mouse. A similar observation was reported for the PD-L1 targeted antibody [111In]In-PD-L1.3.1, for which target saturation was observed at doses > 1μg (7 pmol) [139], and for IL-2R targeting using the native IL2 protein [56]. However, Table 3 also illustrates cases where much higher antibody doses were employed, which still provided a target-specific imaging signal. In the case of [89Zr]Zr-Pembrolizumab, this was due to the aforementioned antigen sink effect. At a dose of 67 pmol/animal, the particularly high affinity of [89Zr]Zr-Pembrolizumab to PD-1 led to trapping of most of the injected activity in the lymphoid system before it was able to reach the tumor. Only a ten-fold increase in antibody dose allowed for efficient visualization of PD-1 expressing T cells in the TME [124]. Similar effects were reported for nanobody- [42] and minibody-based tracers [38] targeting CD8. Overall, these combined findings highlight both the complexity and necessity of finding a fine-tuned balance between ligand affinity, ligand dose (as a function of molar activity) and systemic target expression to achieve effective, quantitative visualization of immune cell infiltration in the TME using antibody-based tracers.

As also shown in Table 3, the typical dosing of TME-targeted peptide tracers is in a similar range as that of antibody-based probes, for the same reasons (balance between avoiding target saturation in TME and preventing a sink effect in other target-expressing tissues/cells). However, it is important to note that for peptide radiopharmaceuticals, higher doses (around 100 pmol/animal) are usually acceptable due to their – usually – substantially lower target affinity compared to antibody-based tracers (Table 3, Fig. 3). Nevertheless, similar opposed effects of ligand concentration on on-target-off-site accumulation and tumor accumulation have been observed for peptide radiopharmaceuticals targeting e.g. PSMA (as a paradigmatic tumor target with variable expression) [144–146]. That excretion-related non-specific tissue accumulation can also be repressed by co-injection of an excess of unlabeled ligand has been confirmed for the PD-L1 targeted peptide [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-WL12 [23]. However, the effect of cold ligand co-injection on tumor SUV in patients was not reported and may be a factor to consider.

In contrast to antibody-based tracers targeting the TME, peptides represent the great advantage of allowing relatively straightforward chemical modification. This allows to circumvent approaches like the above (co-injection of cold WL-12 to repress excretion-related liver uptake [23]) that may lead to target saturation in the TME, e.g. by introducing alternative, hydrophilic chelators such as TRAP [21]. TRAP can be labelled with 68Ga at very high molar activities [147], and this alongside its utility as a pharmacokinetic modifier lead to substantially reduced liver uptake and improved background clearance of [68Ga]Ga-TRAP-WL12 compared to [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-WL12, with unchanged tumor uptake and retention.

Generally, the choice of the radionuclide and the radiolabeling/chelation method determine the achievable molar activity, which in turn isa major determinant for the success of high-sensitivity TME imaging. A high molar activity guarantees the injection of sufficient activities for high count rates at very low administered doses. This is key for detecting weakly expressed targets in the TME (Fig. 2) [148]. Peptide tracers are generally labelled site-specifically and with only one radioactive atom/molecule, and thus, their molar activity is highly dependent on the specific radiolabeling strategy used. While for 18F, molar activites up to > 1TBq/μmol can be reached [56], radiometal chelation usually results in lower molar activities due to (radio)metal contaminations (production-related, buffers, precursor). Typical molar activities for [68Ga]Ga-NOTA/DOTA-radiopharmaceuticals (using generator-produced 68Ga) range from 40–80 GBq/μmol, but may be increased to up to 3000 GBq/μmol by using highly efficient metal chelators such as TRAP [147]. For 89Zr- and 64Cu-labeled tracers, molar activities usually reach up to 60 and 300 GBq/μmol, respectively. This inherently limits the downward range of dose reduction for high-sensitivity TME imaging (Fig. 3). Consequently, the choice of an appropriate radionuclide for imaging low-level expression of a given TME target should not solely be determined by radionuclide availability and the ease of radiolabeling, but also on the achievable molar activity.

In the case of larger biovectors such as proteins and antibody-based tracers, this limitation can be circumvented by increasing the number of radiolabels per biomolecule. This involves either conjugation with > 1 pre-labeled prosthetic groups (e.g. [18F]Fluorobenzoate [56, 149]) or functionalization with > 1 chelator/molecule, i.e. increasing the so-called average degree of conjugation (DoC). This allows to increase the apparent molar activity by a factor of 2 and more. This has been exemplified by a CD8-targeted cys-diabody, which was designed to contain two accessible thiols for site specific chelator conjugation [38]. However, even in full-sized antibodies, high DoCs (≥ 3) may lead to substantial reduction of tumor accumulation and increased non-specific organ uptake [150], and thus the need for high molar activity must be carefully balanced with unwanted effects of chelator conjugation on general pharmacokinetics and targeting.

Challenge: investigational vs translational tracers

Undoubtedly, antibody-based tracers have pioneered the field of TME imaging and have provided important first clinical evidence of the predictive and prognostic value of TME imaging in the context of immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) therapies [12, 27–29]. However, there is also no doubt that radiolabeled full-sized antibodies are facing severe challenges on several levels that – if the usual criteria for “suitable” or “promising” imaging agents are applied—disqualify them for clinical translation.

These challenges include 1) their highly complex and expensive (bio)production, requiring advanced infrastructure, 2) the need for specifically adapted, biocompatible conjugation and radiolabeling techniques [151], 3) their extremely slow clearance and accumulation kinetics, ultimately resulting in prolonged imaging delays and thus, due to the necessity to use radionuclides with adapted half-lives (min. 12h), considerable radiation exposure of patients (Table 4). For example, CD8-targeted PET imaging using [89Zr]Zr-Df-IAB22M2C (Tables 1 and 4) delivers a dose of 0.8 mGy/MBq to the radiation sensitive red bone marrow [39]. This dose exceeds by far typical bone marrow doses delivered by radioligand therapy using [177Lu]Lu-DOTATATE (0.012 mGy/MBq) or [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 (0.012 mGy/MBq) [152] and thus deserves critical consideration. Furthermore, since, as mentioned previously (Table 3), comparably high antibody doses are oftentimes administered to reduce antigen sink effects and Fc-mediated non-specific accumulation, there is a non-negligible risk of an anti-drug immune response, precluding repetitive imaging during the course of therapy [153]. Of course, despite these challenges, (radio)labeled antibodies are invaluable and indispensable tools for early target validation. Antibodies can be raised against virtually any molecular target, even “elusive” targets in the TME for which no known endogenous ligands, which may serve as lead structures for radiopharmaceutical development, are known or available (e.g. PD-L1, TIGIT, CD8, CD206, CD163). This and the high target affinity and specificity of antibodies constitutes the basis of using (radio)labeled antibodies as investigational tracers for the preclinical assessment of the abundance as well as biological and potential clinical relevance of a given molecular target in the TME (see section on “selection of relevant targets”).

Moreover, antibody-based tracers remain essential for in vivo target-engagement studies, where their ability to directly compete with or report on the binding of therapeutic antibodies provides information that cannot easily be obtained with alternative molecular formats [12, 27]. It is therefore important to distinguish between the role of antibody-based tracers as investigational tools — where their unique advantages are undisputed — and their suitability as translational imaging agents intended for routine clinical use, where the challenges outlined above impose significant constraints.

Beyond the biological and technical challenges discussed above, the clinical translation of TME-targeted tracers is further shaped by regulatory and practical constraints. The production of immune-targeting tracers, particularly antibody fragments and engineered proteins, requires GMP-compliant manufacturing processes that are often substantially more complex and costly than those established for small-molecule or peptide radiopharmaceuticals [158]. In addition, toxicological requirements for tracers directed at immune targets may differ from those for classical tumor-targeting agents, as even sub-pharmacological quantities of immune-modulating ligands could theoretically induce biological effects and therefore necessitate careful preclinical safety evaluation. Finally, the feasibility of repeated imaging, which is of particular importance in TME imaging given the dynamic immune response during therapy, is constrained not only by radiation exposure (Table 4), but also by immunogenicity (especially for antibody-based tracers) and by practical considerations such as cost, tracer availability, and patient compliance.

Addressing these combined biological, technical, and regulatory constraints represents the next key step in tracer development. In particular, there is a need for targeted tracers with improved pharmacokinetic profiles that enable high-contrast imaging at early time points. One possible strategy that has been exploited extensively (Tables 1 and 4) is the use of small antibody fragments, with a strong focus on nanobodies [15, 35, 42, 43, 71, 159–162]. They combine the high affinity and selectivity of full-sized antibodies with favorable pharmacokinetics (due to their tenfold lower molecular weight compared to full-sized antibodies) and a relatively straightforward, scalable production process [163], all of which contribute to their relatively rapid translation into first-in-man and clinical studies [15, 44, 65, 66, 164].

However, spearheaded by the development of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-WL12 as the first peptide-based imaging agent targeting PD-L1 [165], more and more peptide (8–20 amino acids) radiopharmaceuticals addressing TME targets are emerging (Table 1), and due to the relative ease of chemical modification of short peptide sequences, progressively improved targeting and pharmacokinetic properties have been achieved. For example, substitution of DOTA in [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-WL12 by TRAP led to substantially reduced lipophilicity and thus dramatically improved imaging quality due to improved overall pharmacokinetics [21], as did the introduction of a Glu-for-Ile exchange in the peptide sequence, combined with the introduction of a PEG spacer for NOTA-conjugation, leading to [18F]AlF-NOTA-PCP2 [26]. Modifications in the cyclisation strategy, methylation pattern and amino acid sequence of WL-12 provided [18F]AlF-NOTA-iPD-L1 [166], which showed superior PD-L1 targeting in vitro. This specific example highlights the tremendous importance of the identification of a suitable first-in-kind lead structure such as WL12 as well as the power of relatively small chemical modifications in finetuning tracer performance. Well combined, both may ultimately lead to peptide radiopharmaceuticals that outperform nanobody-derived tracers in terms of imaging quality and targeting specificity, despite higher target affinity of nanobodies [161].

Generally, identification of a lead structure as a starting point for peptide tracer development for TME imaging still represents a major hurdle. However, in addition to the progressive downsizing of known protein ligands (e.g. PD-1 in the case of WL12), advanced phage display techniques [167, 168] have spawned lead structures for high-affinity peptide ligands for TME targets, including the TIGIT ligand [68Ga]Ga-GP12 [103], the CD263 targeted peptide [64Cu]Cu-ICT-01 [72], the CD206-binding UNO peptide [169] as well as [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-C25 [104, 170], a novel LAG3 targeted peptide tracer with excellent in vivo imaging capacities. These developments mark important progress toward expanding the repertoire of peptide-based tracers for TME imaging, although comprehensive optimization of lead candidates remains essential to achieve clinical translation.

Taken together, current evidence indicates that lower-molecular-weight tracers, particularly peptides and small engineered fragments, hold the greatest promise for clinical TME imaging. Their favorable pharmacokinetics, lower radiation burden, and early high-contrast imaging clearly outweigh the limitations of full-sized antibodies. Until now, besides their application for target visualization and quantification, radiolabeled antibodies have also been used to visualize target engagement of therapeutic antibodies in vivo [27, 171–173], but even this role is now increasingly challenged by peptide-based tracers that can probe the same binding sites with higher temporal resolution and superior imaging properties, as demonstrated for the PD-L1 targeted peptides [18F]AlF-NOTA-PCP1 [174] and [64Cu]Cu-WL12 [175].

Although the identification and optimization of suitable peptide structures remain demanding, these efforts are anticipated to provide a more sustainable advancement of clinically viable tracers than the premature translation of antibody-based constructs. Ultimately, however, the successful development of clinically relevant TME imaging agents will not only depend on improved tracer design but also on the availability of suitable experimental models that allow their accurate preclinical evaluation.

While the determination of physicochemical parameters is independent from specific assay systems and thus generally applicable for any new radiolabeled molecule, the assessment of the targeting properties of a novel radiopharmaceutical as well as the reliability of the obtained data are highly dependent on the specific in vitro assay system used.

In general, for novel radioligands targeting highly expressed cancer cell targets (see Fig. 2), all requirements for successfully characterizing the core parameters summarized in Table 5 are usually met: a cell line (or tissue sections [178]) with stable and high endogenous target expression is available, with the functional targeting being expressed in its natural context, the new radiotracer has sufficient affinity (usually < < 1 μM) to provide detectable, statistically significant specific binding and internalization, and the accessibility of known reference radioligands (ideally labeled with 125I) allow performing comparative competition binding studies (IC50) to assess structure activity relationships.

In addition to providing all relevant information on a tracer’s targeting and retention properties, this “standard toolkit of the radiopharmaceutical developer” is highly cost-effective, solely relying on radioactivity quantification using a gamma counter, and requiring a minimum of optional, non-standard consumables and instrumentation (such as a cell harvester or autoradiography instrument). However, despite these advantages, this standard toolkit is often either not accessible or not sufficient for the characterization of TME-targeted radiopharmaceuticals, particularly of peptide- and small molecule-based tracers.

By contrast, and as discussed previously, antibodies and antibody fragments, by nature of the underlying screening and selection processes, always display very high (nM to sub-nM) target affinities and specificities. Thus, the in vitro evaluation of antibody-(fragment)-based tracers is mostly limited to reconfirming the immunoreactivity and binding specificity of their radiolabeled analogs using cellular binding or flow cytometry methods, with only few studies providing additional quantitative binding data using complementary methodologies such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR, Table 5) [35, 42, 66, 71, 162, 179]. Furthermore, their constitutively high affinity even allows the sensitive detection of TME targets with low expression levels (see Fig. 2) and thus their evaluation using immune cells subsets with physiological target expression (e.g. upon isolation/enrichment from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) or splenocytes [34, 42, 46, 47]). Nevertheless, despite this relative robustness, antibody-based tracer evaluation for TME targeting is not without experimental complexity. Given the activation-/polarization-dependent regulation of the surface expression of many immune cell markers, lengthy and labor-intensive cell isolation and activation protocols are often required to obtain a suitable population for tracer evaluation. This demands extensive expertise in cell biology and immunology and may already represent a serious challenge for more chemically oriented tracer development labs.

Even more challenges are encountered in the in vitro characterization of peptide- and small molecule-based radioligands targeting TME components. In many instances, almost none of the requirements for successful in vitro evaluation using the classical radioactivity-based assay systems (Table 4) are met. While the inherent problem of low endogenous target expression may be relatively easily overcome by using stably transduced cell lines [34, 70, 130], other tracer-related factors (low affinity, fast koff) or the lack of reference radioligands still may represent major obstacles for an accurate determination of tracer affinity (KD, IC50) and specificity using standard radioactivity-based assays.

This has led to two divergent developments – either the omission of extensive in vitro tracer characterization beyond confirmatory radioligand binding (target binding in % of added dose) in favor of direct translation into in vivo studies [18, 21, 24, 25, 46, 51], or the exploration of alternative, technologically advanced techniques for affinity determination. Close-up analysis of all studies on antibody fragment/nanobody- and peptide-based tracer studies cited in Table 1 clearly indicates that, still, the majority of studies (41%) use radioligand-based assays for first-line tracer validation. However, only 23% of studies exclusively rely on radioligand-based affinity determinations (KD or IC50), while more sophisticated methodologies such as SPR [180], which also allow an assessment of binding kinetics, are used in 32% of studies, followed by complementary flow cytometry methods (23%) and ELISA-based assays (18%) (Table 6). Of note, more and more studies use multiple complementary methods to determine and confirm the targeting characteristics of novel TME targeted tracers [26, 35, 42, 71, 162], with confirmatory radioligand binding studies often constituting the last step in the evaluation workflow. Generally, the increasing implementation of technologically advanced in vitro evaluation methodologies represents a highly positive and, in many cases, necessary evolution in TME-targeted tracer development. The use of complementary, non-radioactivity-based techniques enables a more reliable and nuanced assessment of affinity, specificity and binding kinetics, thereby strengthening confidence in tracer performance prior to in vivo translation. At the same time, this shift also exposes structural challenges within the field. The high acquisition and maintenance costs of advanced instrumentation (Table 6), alongside the substantial expertise required for their operation and data interpretation, create a growing disparity between methodological expectations (by journals and peers) and the practical capabilities of many radiopharmaceutical development laboratories. These approaches will increasingly define the emerging “gold standard” for in vitro TME tracer characterization, and the field must actively address issues of accessibility, standardization and training. In this context, the facilitation of transdisciplinary collaborations between radiochemistry- and pharmacy-oriented groups and fundamental cell biology and immunology laboratories offers a particularly promising path forward. Such collaborative frameworks not only allow shared access to sophisticated technologies and biological expertise but also foster a more integrated understanding of TME biology, ultimately accelerating the rational development of robust, biologically informed imaging tracers for clinical translation.

Challenge: increasing complexity in in vivo evaluation

By nature, since in TME imaging different immune cell subpopulations and/or their activation state are at the center of attention, in vivo tracer evaluation is predominantly performed in mouse models based on wild type mice with an intact immune system, i.e. syngeneic tumor models. They are increasingly complemented by more advanced in vivo systems such as genetically modified (Knock-in, Knock-out) models or humanized mice (humanized immune system with or without CDX/PDX). Of course, “classical”, non-transduced xenograft models (cells expressing the human target, implanted into immunocompromised mice) are also used, but their use is restricted to the evaluation of PD-L1 targeted tracers [15, 18, 20, 21, 25, 26, 35, 157], exploiting the high endogenous PD-L1 expression in various human cancer cell lines, or to the first-line validation of the targeting efficiency and -specificity of novel radiopharmaceuticals. In some studies, cell lines transduced with the human surface marker of interest are also used for tumor inoculation and initial evaluation of targeting efficiency, before cross-validation in models with (realistically low) endogenous target expression levels [15, 34].

The relative advantages and limitations of the different animal models with respect to preclinical tracer evaluation and immunology research in general have been extensively discussed in excellent recent reviews [176, 177, 182, 183] and will thus not be recapitulated here. Specific focus will rather be directed towards the challenges researchers are facing in the development of TME-targeted tracers, as opposed to “classical” tracer development for cancer theranostics, and towards raising awareness for the additional layers of complexity associated with developing and evaluating tracers for TME imaging, which, as discussed for the in vitro evaluation, entails the need for advanced and sophisticated in vivo models.

As opposed to imaging of classical tumor targets with high and permanent, i.e. “static”, expression on the tumor cells with very little variation during tumor growth, the tumor microenvironment (except for tumor-cell expressed immune checkpoints) is a dynamic, constantly fluctuating system. Not only does the general composition of the TME (tumor vs stroma vs immune infiltrate) constantly evolve during tumor growth, but so does the activation state and polarization of different immune cell subsets, making the TME a “moving target” for imaging. Furthermore, there are marked differences between the immune systems of mice and men, both in terms of abundance of different immune cell populations and in terms of surface marker expression levels and immune signaling [183, 184]. This has a variety of practical and translational consequences for the evaluation of TME targeted tracers and comes with substantial experimental challenges, which will be discussed exemplarily in the context of imaging of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells as key players in antitumor immunity.

A first and major problem in the development and evaluation of novel tracers targeted towards CD8+ or CD4+ lies in the limited species homology between human and mouse CD4 (only 51% amino acid sequence identity [185]) and CD8 (alpha: only 49% amino acid sequence identity [186]), which lies notably below the genome-wide average of 85% for orthologous proteins. This limitation has been addressed by applying various, complementary experimental strategies, depending on the ultimate goal of the preclinical studies.

Usually, in vivo tracer characterization is deemed sufficiently comprehensive and conclusive, when targeting efficiency and specificity of the novel radiopharmaceutical can be clearly demonstrated. The most straightforward way to obtain these pivotal data is the use of xenograft models with high hCD4 or hCD8 expression (either human cancer cell line or murine cell line transduced with the human antigen) alongside a negative control cell line [42, 43, 47, 187, 188]. However, since the intended application of immune-cell targeted tracers is the in vivo imaging of immune cells and their dynamic tissue distribution over time (not a bulk of target-positive tumor cells), there is an increasing trend towards using more sophisticated models such as hCD4-knock in (replacement of mouse protein by human protein) [47, 188] or hCD8 transgenic models (co-expression of hCD8 with endogenous mCD8) [42] to additionally corroborate the target specificity of the tracer and its capacity to detect the inherently low endogenous target expression in the immune system. Of note, these models are particularly well suited to perform functional imaging studies reaching far beyond tracer evaluation itself, such as longitudinal imaging of tumor infiltration with T cells during tumor progression [42] or as a response to immunotherapy [47]. Alternatively, humanized mice bearing human (patient derived) xenografts have been used to assess progressive T-cell infiltration of the tumor [46]. Overall, both transgenic and knock-in models provide the advantage of human target expression, providing results with potentially higher translational value, but require high upfront costs, long development times, and complex breeding and validation. Humanized immune system models add even greater expense, technical burden, and biological variability due to incomplete immune reconstitution [182]. Of note, these added complexities do not necessarily improve the scientific validity of a tracer evaluation that, in the first place, requires robust demonstration of efficient targeting and target specificity, alongside the characterization of general tracer pharmacokinetics.

It is thus questionable to what extent a solid in vivo evaluation of a novel TME-targeted tracer needs to be linked to sophisticated functional tumor immunology studies, or if there would even be added value (in terms of cost-effectiveness and reduced complexity) in separating fundamental tracer evaluation from subsequent functional imaging studies in a biologically more relevant system such as syngeneic mouse models. Not only are syngeneic tumor models rapid, scalable, and cost-efficient, but even more importantly, they preserve a fully intact and functional immune system and thus a native tumor microenvironment. Of note, immune-related functional imaging studies in syngeneic models, assessing e.g. response to immunotherapy or chemotherapy [47, 53, 54] as well as kinetic tracing of immune cell redistribution and homing after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [189] or adoptive T cell transfer [48] require the (parallel) development of dedicated, mouse-specific probes. These efforts, however, are rewarded by providing translationally relevant information on tumor biology and tumor immunology, which then facilitate the clinical translation of the respective human-specific probes without the need for additional functional studies beyond basic tracer evaluation [7].

Of course, there are also many TME targets beyond CD4 and CD8, where species homology is largely conserved, e.g. immune checkpoints such as TIGIT [36], the M2 macrophage markers CD206 [66, 68–70, 190] and CD163 [71, 72], T cell metabolism [52, 191] or hypoxia-induced surface markers [86], to name only a few. In these cases, tracer evaluation is logically performed in syngeneic models and, due to the ease of using one and the same model, mostly combined with functional studies, providing direct translational evidence alongside tracer characterization. Nevertheless, even for those “uncomplicated” cases, additional in vivo evaluation in Knock-out models may be necessary to establish target specificity [66, 70, 71].

However, although widely used for immuno-oncological studies, the validity of data generated using syngeneic models also needs to be viewed with scrutiny. One major drawback, particularly in the context of TME tracer development and the inherent need for data comparability, is the lack of reliable, quantitative information on the exact composition of their TME. There is increasing general evidence that e.g. the MC38 colon cancer model is one of the most immunologically hot, i.e. highly immune infiltrated models [108, 110, 111, 192], while 4T1 mammary tumors tend to be relatively macrophage rich [108, 111, 192], with CT-26 colon cancer and B16-F10 melanoma models being classified as poorly infiltrated [108, 110, 112, 193]. Taken together, the in vivo evaluation of TME-targeted radiopharmaceuticals has evolved from a comparatively straightforward confirmation of tumor uptake and target specificity into a highly complex, multi-layered endeavor that increasingly merges tracer validation with sophisticated functional immunology studies. While advanced models—such as transgenic, knock-in or humanized mice—undoubtedly provide valuable biological insight and higher apparent translational relevance, their routine use risks blurring the boundary between what is necessary for robust tracer evaluation and what is desirable for mechanistic exploration of tumor-immune interactions. In this sense, radiopharmaceutical development is in danger of being overwhelmed by escalating expectations for ever more elaborate in vivo study designs, with substantial implications for cost, timelines and accessibility, particularly for early-stage or chemistry-driven laboratories. A pragmatic way forward mirrors the conclusions drawn for advanced in vitro characterization: a deliberate decoupling of fundamental tracer evaluation from downstream functional studies. Systematic, standardized in vivo workflows that prioritize clear demonstration of targeting efficiency, specificity and pharmacokinetics—ideally in well-defined, harmonized syngeneic models—should constitute the core requirement for tracer development. More complex functional imaging studies can then be pursued selectively, guided by specific biological questions rather than as an implicit benchmark for tracer quality. Such a stepwise, incremental strategy, combined with stronger standardization and collaborative frameworks, would help contain experimental complexity while preserving scientific rigor, ultimately enabling sustainable and rational progress in TME imaging without overburdening radiopharmaceutical development pipelines.

Challenge: pitfalls in image interpretation and data analysis

In TME imaging, image analysis and data interpretation represent an additional, often underappreciated source of uncertainty. Because immune cell populations are typically sparse, heterogeneously distributed and dynamically regulated, PET/CT signal differences are frequently small and close to the technical limits of detectability. Under these conditions, analytical choices in image acquisition, reconstruction and quantification can exert a disproportionate influence on biological conclusions, particularly when subtle uptake differences are interpreted as changes in immune cell abundance or activation.

A major contributor to this problem is the limited standardization of preclinical imaging workflows. In contrast to clinical PET, acquisition and analysis protocols in preclinical PET/CT studies remain highly heterogeneous. Although general reporting frameworks such as the ARRIVE and PREPARE guidelines [196, 197] and nuclear imaging–specific recommendations such as the AQARA principle [198] aim to improve rigor and comparability, their implementation in TME imaging is inconsistent. Importantly, standardized acquisition and reconstruction protocols have been shown to markedly reduce quantitative variability in multicenter preclinical PET/CT studies [199], highlighting the particular relevance of harmonization for immune imaging applications characterized by small effect sizes.

Intrinsic technical limitations of PET further complicate interpretation in the context of the TME. Finite spatial resolution, positron range effects and resulting partial volume effects systematically bias signal quantification in small or diffusely infiltrated immune cell populations [200–202]. Apparent differences in tracer uptake may therefore reflect voxel sampling and resolution effects rather than true biological variation. While partial volume correction strategies exist, the large correction factors involved can introduce additional uncertainty and should be applied with caution, ideally supported by orthogonal validation such as ex vivo gamma counting [201].

Quantitative readouts are additionally influenced by the choice of uptake metrics and ROI/VOI definition. Semi-quantitative measures such as %ID/mL or SUV are highly sensitive to segmentation strategy and background definition, with manual ROI delineation introducing user-dependent bias and automated approaches remaining strongly parameter-dependent [201–203]. These effects are amplified in TME imaging due to low target-to-background ratios and spatial heterogeneity, underscoring the need for transparent reporting and sensitivity analyses.

In this context, the selection of the quantitative metric itself warrants careful consideration. SUV-based parameters, although widely used due to their simplicity and clinical practicality, are influenced by multiple factors including injected dose, body weight, uptake time, and scanner calibration, and may therefore insufficiently reflect the subtle uptake differences often encountered in TME imaging [204]. Ratio-based metrics, such as tumor-to-background or tumor-to-blood ratios, can partially reduce these confounding effects and have demonstrated superior performance compared with SUV-derived parameters for predicting clinical response in the context of CD8 imaging [105]. Where technically and logistically feasible, kinetic modeling based on dynamic acquisition protocols may provide more robust quantitative descriptors; however, the increased methodological complexity must be balanced against practical constraints related to clinical workflow and patient compliance.

Importantly, the routine use of dynamic imaging and kinetic modeling in early-stage TME tracer evaluation is not always warranted. Applied to low-count or noisy datasets, overly complex models risk producing an appearance of quantitative precision without corresponding biological validity [205]. Kinetic analyses should therefore be hypothesis-driven and matched to data quality rather than adopted as a default standard.

Conclusion

Tumor microenvironment imaging holds great promise as a non-invasive tool to dynamically visualize and quantify biological determinants of immunotherapy response, yet its successful clinical implementation is confronted with a unique convergence of biological, technical and methodological challenges. Unlike classical tumor imaging, TME imaging operates at the limits of target abundance, tracer sensitivity and model validity, necessitating a fundamental rethinking of what constitutes a “relevant” target, a “suitable” tracer format and a “sufficient” level of experimental validation. As discussed throughout this review, low target density, dynamic regulation of immune cell populations, tight coupling between affinity, molar activity and receptor occupancy, and the limited availability of standardized evaluation models collectively impose substantial constraints. Importantly, however, these constraints do not represent insurmountable barriers but rather define the realistic space in which TME-targeted imaging approaches must be developed and interpreted.

A recurring theme emerging from the analysis of tracer design, in vitro characterization, in vivo evaluation and image interpretation is that increasing biological complexity is often accompanied by a disproportionate escalation of methodological demands. Advanced biological models, high-end analytical techniques and functional imaging paradigms undoubtedly provide deeper mechanistic insight. However, their routine use as implicit benchmarks for tracer quality risks overburdening radiopharmaceutical development pipelines and widening the gap between methodological expectations and practical feasibility. Addressing this challenge will require a strategic rebalancing: decoupling fundamental tracer validation from downstream functional studies, adopting stepwise and question-driven evaluation workflows, and aligning scientific rigor with biological relevance rather than with technical possibility.

Looking ahead, progress in TME imaging will depend less on addressing individual challenges in isolation than on integrating them into coherent targeting concepts. Such concepts should jointly consider target biology, tracer format, labeling chemistry, achievable molar activity, and imaging modality sensitivity. Within this framework, lower molecular weight tracers, particularly peptides and engineered antibody fragments, offer concrete opportunities to overcome several of the identified limitations. Their success, however, will depend on the use of harmonized preclinical models, proportionate validation strategies, and transparent reporting standards. Ultimately, the impact of TME imaging will be defined not by the absence of challenges, but by the field’s ability to address them through biologically informed, technically balanced, and translationally focused approaches.
