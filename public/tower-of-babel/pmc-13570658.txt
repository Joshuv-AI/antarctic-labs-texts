Disaster Preparedness Among Physicians in Underdeveloped Regions of Southwestern China: A Cross-Sectional Study of Disaster Response Competencies, Training Needs, and Policy Implications
Authors: Yeyao Hu, Min Tian, Yanyan Yu, Yongqin Qian, Hongli Deng, Dingying Wang, Yishuang Li, Rui Xia, Qiong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDisaster preparedness among physicians is critical for effective medical response during emergencies, especially in underdeveloped regions with limited healthcare resources. However, evidence from such contexts remains scarce. This study was conducted in Guizhou Province, a representative underdeveloped region in southwestern China, to assess physicians’ disaster response competencies and training needs, and to provide evidence for targeted healthcare policy and capacity-building strategies.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from June to August 2023 among physicians working in hospitals at various levels across Guizhou Province. The questionnaire included demographic and professional background information, a 46-item self-assessment of disaster response competencies, and a 51-item self-assessment of training needs related to disaster medicine education and training. All items were rated using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Mann–Whitney U-tests, Kruskal–Wallis tests, and multivariable linear regression.ResultsA total of 760 physicians completed the survey. The disaster response competency total score ranged from 46 to 230, with a median of 141 (IQR 131–181), indicating a low-to-moderate level of disaster preparedness. The training needs total score ranged from 51 to 255, with a median of 172 (IQR 153–243), reflecting moderate-to-high training needs. Multivariable linear regression showed that location of practice, hospital type, clinical department, and institutional support (presence of an in-hospital disaster rescue team and existence of a comprehensive emergency plan) were associated with physicians’ disaster response competency scores (P < 0.05).ConclusionPhysicians in underdeveloped regions of southwestern China demonstrate notable gaps in disaster response competencies alongside substantial training needs. Tailored, context-specific disaster medicine education and training, supported by institutional support, should be integrated into healthcare policy and hospital management to strengthen system resilience and improve emergency medical response capacity in underdeveloped regions.

Introduction

Natural disasters are increasing in frequency and severity worldwide, posing substantial threats to public health, disrupting medical services, and straining the capacity of physicians who provide frontline care.1 According to the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT), natural disasters affected over 185 million people globally in 2022, causing more than 30,000 deaths and economic losses exceeding USD 140 billion.2 The United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) defines a disaster as “a serious disruption of the functioning of a community or a society at any scale, due to hazardous events interacting with conditions of exposure, vulnerability, and capacity, leading to human, material, economic, and environmental losses and impacts”.3 In the medical context, this definition underscores that the impact of disasters depends not only on the nature of the hazard but also on disaster preparedness and disaster response competencies of healthcare providers—particularly physicians—who play a decisive role in saving lives during such events,4,5 This aligns with the WHO Health Emergency and Disaster Risk Management (Health EDRM) framework, which emphasizes healthcare workers’competencies as a core element of disaster preparedness.6

China is among the countries most affected by natural disasters, with frequent occurrences of floods, earthquakes, landslides, and extreme weather events.7 Guizhou Province—an underdeveloped region in southwestern China—serves as a representative example of resource-limited settings worldwide. In 2024 alone, disasters in this province affected approximately 4.15 million people and caused direct economic losses of 7.46 billion yuan,8 placing substantial pressure on hospitals and highlighting the urgent need to strengthen physicians’ disaster preparedness. Physicians, as frontline responders, play critical roles in disaster planning, triage, emergency medical services, and post-disaster care. Their competencies directly influence patient safety, health outcomes, and the overall resilience of healthcare systems.9–11

Previous studies have shown that physicians’disaster preparedness is shaped by targeted training, prior emergency experience, institutional resources, and coordinated disaster management plans.12 Evidence from high-income countries suggests that structured disaster medicine education and simulation-based training can significantly enhance physicians’ capabilities in triage, emergency care, and post-disaster recovery.13 In contrast, studies from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have reported that less than 40% of physicians receive formal disaster training, and fewer than one-third participate in emergency drills.14,15 This disparity reflects not only limited training resources but also insufficient integration of disaster preparedness into clinical practice and institutional policy.16 In China, most disaster preparedness research has examined nurses, medical students, or inter-professional healthcare teams,17–19 leaving limited evidence on practising physicians’disaster competencies in underdeveloped regions. In addition, the contribution of institutional support—such as hospital type, disaster rescue teams, and comprehensive emergency plans—remains insufficiently quantified in relation to physicians’ disaster preparedness.

Against this backdrop, the present study aims to evaluate disaster response competencies and training needs among physicians in Guizhou Province, China. Using a cross-sectional survey, we assessed self-reported competencies across key domains of disaster medicine, including emergency medical services, field first aid, disaster-specific technical skills, and theoretical knowledge. The findings will provide an evidence base for competency-oriented education programs tailored to physicians in developing countries and inform healthcare policy to strengthen physician-led disaster preparedness in resource-limited settings. Given the global rise in disaster risk and the shared challenges faced by underdeveloped regions, the insights from this study have implications that extend beyond China to similar healthcare systems worldwide.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Sample

A cross-sectional online survey was conducted from June to August 2023 among physicians working in hospitals at different levels in Guizhou Province, southwestern China. Data were collected using Questionnaire Star.

Participants and Sampling

Eligible participants were practising physicians aged ≥20 years who worked in hospitals in Guizhou Province and were able to complete the online questionnaire independently. Participation was voluntary. Physicians who were not practising in hospitals in Guizhou Province were excluded.

A convenience sampling approach was used. During the study period, members of the research team visited participating hospitals across Guizhou Province and invited eligible physicians to take part. Physicians completed the questionnaire anonymously on their own mobile phones by scanning a QR code via Questionnaire Star.

In total, 772 questionnaires were distributed; 12 were excluded during data cleaning, and 760 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis. The completion rate (98.45%) was calculated based on distributed questionnaires.

Sample Size Estimation

Sample size was estimated using an item-to-respondent ratio approach, which commonly recommends 5–10 participants per questionnaire item for scale-based surveys.20,21 The questionnaire comprised 97 items (46 assessing disaster response competencies and 51 assessing training needs). Therefore, the minimum sample size was 485 (97×5); allowing for up to 20% invalid responses, the minimum target sample size was 582. We recruited 760 physicians, exceeding the minimum target and supporting stable subgroup comparisons across hospital tiers.

Survey Instrument

General Information Form

A self-designed form included 21 items on demographic and professional characteristics (eg, location of practice, hospital level, department, and prior participation in disaster-related activities). Selected items used “yes/no/don’t know” response options.

Disaster Response Competencies and Training Needs Scale

The Disaster Rescue Capability and Training Needs Scale was developed by the research team based on the International Council of Nurses (ICN) Disaster Nursing Core Competencies 2.0 framework and adapted to the physician context. The final instrument included (1) a 46-item self-assessment of physicians’ disaster response competencies, and (2) a 51-item assessment of training needs across four domains: commonly used on-site first aid techniques, on-site disaster rescue knowledge, disaster rescue-related techniques, and disaster rescue-related knowledge. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores indicate higher self-rated disaster response competencies or greater training needs related to disaster medicine education and training. The full questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary Figure S1.

Questionnaire Validity and Reliability

Item content was refined through two rounds of expert consultation (n = 15). Expert authority coefficients indicated high credibility (judgement coefficient 0.97; familiarity coefficient 0.89; authority coefficient 0.93). Kendall’s coefficients of concordance suggested improving agreement across rounds (round 1: W = 0.445, P < 0.001; round 2: W = 0.755, P < 0.001), and all expert suggestions were incorporated. A pilot survey among 80 medical personnel was conducted to evaluate feasibility, clarity, and preliminary item performance; this pilot sample size is commonly used in scale development to support item analysis and initial reliability assessment, rather than hypothesis testing. Item analysis results were acceptable and no items were removed.21–23

Psychometric testing during instrument development supported structural validity and reliability (KMO = 0.991; Bartlett’s test P < 0.01; explained variance = 75.61%; factor loadings = 0.577–0.914; Cronbach’s α = 0.827; split-half reliability = 0.726; content validity index = 0.812).

Data Collection Procedure and Quality Control

Data were collected using an online questionnaire administered via Questionnaire Star. During the study period, members of the research team visited participating hospitals in Guizhou Province and invited eligible physicians to take part. After receiving a brief explanation of the study purpose and completion instructions, physicians completed the questionnaire independently on their own mobile phones by scanning a QR code and submitting their responses online. The survey was anonymous.

To enhance data quality, the questionnaire was configured to allow only one submission per device/IP address and required completion of all items before submission. Two researchers reviewed the dataset and excluded invalid questionnaires. In total, 772 questionnaires were distributed and 760 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis; the completion rate (98.5%) was calculated based on distributed questionnaires.

Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 was used for analysis. Normality was assessed using histograms and Q-Q plots. Outcome scores were skewed; therefore, disaster response competencies and training needs were summarised as median (IQR). Group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U-test (two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (three or more groups). Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses were entered into multivariable linear regression models. Model assumptions were checked using residual diagnostics, and bootstrapped confidence intervals were used as a sensitivity analysis. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (KLL-2022-842). According to Article 30(2) of the Measures for Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving the Human Body in China, the ethics committee approved a waiver of informed consent because the study was an anonymous questionnaire survey and did not involve personal privacy or commercial interests.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 760 physicians from nine prefecture-level areas in Guizhou Province were included. Most participants were male (59.6%). The largest age group was ≥40 years (56.2%), and 65.7% had >10 years of practice. Regarding institutional context, 83.9% reported that their institution had an emergency response plan, and 69.5% reported the presence of an organized disaster response team. Discussions

In this province-wide survey of 760 physicians, the median disaster response competency score was 141 (IQR 131–181), corresponding to an item-level median of 3.4 on the 1–5 scale. This indicates low-to-moderate competency and is consistent with reports that physicians often feel underprepared for major incidents, particularly high-complexity hazards such as CBRNE events.24 These findings again highlight the gap between theoretical awareness and operational readiness across settings.25

Our results emphasise the importance of structured disaster medicine education and training and institutional support. A national survey from Türkiye reported clear training needs for residency-based disaster education and regular drills, and showed that systematic, practice-oriented training and functional rescue structures are linked to better preparedness-related outcomes.26 Meta-analytic evidence also supports the effectiveness of disaster-focused education, while underscoring that impact depends on instructional design, simulation fidelity, and integration with institutional protocols.27

The competency gaps in procedure-specific skills were most apparent in low-frequency, high-stakes tasks, including advanced airway interventions such as cricothyrotomy. Similar deficits have been described internationally, particularly for technical procedures and hazard-specific decision-making that are rarely practised in routine care.28 Without scheduled retraining, performance in such critical skills may deteriorate within months, supporting the need for periodic refreshers rather than one-off teaching.29,30 High-fidelity simulation and stress-exposure approaches can improve performance under pressure but remain inconsistently implemented, especially outside tertiary settings.31 Addressing these skill gaps requires embedding recurrent, scenario-based training into institutional schedules and ensuring equitable access across all facility levels.

Marked geographical differences were observed, with lower competency levels in resource-constrained prefectures than in the provincial capital. Comparable regional gradients have been reported elsewhere, where limited funding, fewer training opportunities, and weaker health infrastructure constrain disaster readiness.32 Cross-facility collaboration models—such as joint exercises, mentorship, and resource-sharing between tertiary centres and county-level facilities—may help reduce these gaps.33

Physicians working in tertiary comprehensive hospitals demonstrated higher disaster response competency scores, which likely reflects differences in institutional capacity across hospital types. Tertiary comprehensive hospitals are more likely to have structured emergency governance (eg, incident command arrangements), routine drills, and integrated emergency plans, all of which are key components of hospital-level preparedness frameworks.34 In contrast, lower-level or resource-constrained facilities often have gaps in disaster committees/plans and surge-related capacities, which may limit opportunities for skills maintenance and coordinated response.35 These findings support a tiered provincial preparedness approach, with tertiary centers providing standardized training, joint exercises, and implementation support for lower-level facilities.

Early-career physicians (<30 years) showed stronger competency and greater enthusiasm for training, consistent with evidence that younger clinicians may benefit more from simulation and e-learning modalities.36 Clinicians from high-exposure departments (eg, emergency and prehospital care) also tended to perform better than outpatient-oriented specialties, echoing observations during the COVID-19 pandemic that exposure and task proximity shape perceived readiness and performance.37 Extending mass-casualty triage and incident-command training to outpatient staff using specialty-adapted scenarios may therefore improve system-wide competency rather than concentrating preparedness only within emergency-facing units.38

Higher educational attainment and prior disaster training experience were associated with better competency, aligning with evidence that recurrent, simulation-based education embedded in all-hazards planning can improve knowledge retention, technical performance, and decision-making confidence.39,40 Training needs remained substantial in this cohort, reflecting a broader pattern of unmet disaster training needs reported across healthcare professions.10

A further challenge is the absence of standardised, competency-based curricula in undergraduate and postgraduate medical education, which limits consistent competency development across career stages.41 Best-practice recommendations increasingly favour tiered and context-adapted training pathways, including immersive approaches (eg, VR and mixed reality) to support decision-making and procedural performance in high-stress environments.42,43 Without institutional mandates for periodic refresher training, even well-designed programmes may be vulnerable to rapid skill attrition.44

In summary, improving disaster response competency in Guizhou Province will require coordinated strategies that combine structured, recurrent training with strengthened institutional support across all healthcare tiers, while addressing geographical inequities in access to preparedness resources.45,46

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be considered. Although the survey included a substantial sample of 760 physicians, it may not fully represent all medical professionals in Guizhou Province, particularly those working in rural clinics or specialized non-emergency departments. Broader inclusion could provide a more comprehensive view of preparedness across the healthcare system. The reliance on self-administered questionnaires introduces potential self-reporting bias, as physicians may overestimate their preparedness or provide responses they perceive as professionally desirable. This is especially relevant for rarely performed procedures such as cricothyrotomy, where actual skill may diverge from reported confidence.

The cross-sectional design captures preparedness levels at a single point in time, limiting the ability to assess temporal trends or the impact of specific interventions. Moreover, the study assessed knowledge and self-perceived preparedness without directly measuring practical performance in real or simulated disaster scenarios, restricting conclusions about the translation of knowledge into action. Geographical and institutional contexts—unique to Guizhou—may also limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions with different healthcare structures and resources. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs, incorporate objective skills assessments, and explore determinants of preparedness disparities in greater depth.

Recommendations

Based on the findings, several actions are recommended. First, targeted, competency-based training programs should be implemented to address gaps in critical skills, particularly advanced airway management and CBRNE response. Such training should be recurrent, context-adapted, and accessible across all facility levels. Second, disaster preparedness education should be embedded in both undergraduate medical curricula and ongoing professional development, ensuring continuous knowledge reinforcement. Third, multidisciplinary collaboration should be fostered through joint drills and simulation exercises involving physicians, nurses, allied health workers, and administrative staff.

Simulation-based training should replicate high-stress, resource-limited environments to strengthen decision-making and procedural performance. Policy interventions should ensure equitable resource allocation, particularly to under-resourced prefectures, and establish province-wide networks linking tertiary centers with lower-level facilities for training and support. Lastly, systematic monitoring and evaluation mechanisms should be adopted to measure preparedness improvements over time and integrate lessons learned into updated protocols. Building a culture of readiness through integrated training, policy support, and inter-institutional collaboration is essential for enhancing the province’s resilience to future disasters.

Conclusion

This province-wide survey assessed disaster response competencies and training needs among practising physicians in Guizhou Province, China. Physicians demonstrated low-to-moderate disaster response competencies alongside moderate-to-high training needs. Competency varied by geography, hospital type, and clinical department, and was associated with practical exposure (participation in on-site disaster response and drills) and institutional support (eg, the presence of an organized in-hospital disaster rescue team and comprehensive emergency planning). Importantly, the most prominent competency gaps were concentrated in advanced, low-frequency but high-stakes procedures (eg, advanced airway interventions such as cricothyrotomy) and disaster situation assessment/decision-making. These gaps directly inform intervention priorities: competency-based disaster medicine education and training should be strengthened through recurrent, scenario-based simulation and drills, supported where feasible by technology-enhanced methods (eg, virtual simulation) to improve procedural performance and decision-making under pressure.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted in a single province using a cross-sectional, self-reported design, which limits causal inference and the assessment of temporal changes in competencies. Future research should include multi-province or national samples and longitudinal designs, and incorporate objective performance measures to evaluate the sustainability and real-world impact of tiered training models and technology-enhanced approaches. In line with the WHO Health Emergency and Disaster Risk Management (Health EDRM) framework, integrating disaster medicine education and training into continuing professional development—together with strengthened institutional support—may improve physicians’ capacity to respond effectively to complex and evolving disasters.
