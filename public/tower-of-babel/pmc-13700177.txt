Single-cell transcriptomic atlas of gastric cancer reveals malignant cell heterogeneity and nominates EMB as a candidate prognostic biomarker
Authors: Zheng Ma, Na Huang, Bingjie Ren, Tianran Wang, Liping Dai, Erping Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) shows marked cellular heterogeneity and a complex immune microenvironment, which limits reliable biomarkers and therapeutic targets. We constructed a primary GC single-cell atlas to resolve malignant cell states linked to prognosis and to identify candidate targets.MethodsSingle-cell transcriptomes from tumor, adjacent/normal, and premalignant gastric tissues were integrated. After quality control and doublet removal, data were batch-corrected, clustered, and annotated. Malignant epithelial cells were inferred from large-scale copy-number variation patterns and re-clustered. A prognostic signature was constructed by using the top marker genes of the low-differentiation ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial state as a biologically informed candidate pool, followed by univariable Cox regression and LASSO Cox modeling in the TCGA stomach adenocarcinoma cohort. Survival was evaluated by Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank testing, and discrimination was assessed by time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis. Immune context was estimated by computational deconvolution and Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion scoring. Cell-cell communication was inferred from ligand-receptor expression. EMB expression across GC cell lines was assessed using CCLE/DepMap data, and EMB knockdown efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR. EMB function was tested by shRNA knockdown followed by wound-healing and Matrigel Transwell invasion assays and immunoblotting of epithelial-mesenchymal transition markers.ResultsThirteen major cell populations spanning epithelial, immune, and stromal lineages were identified. Copy-number analysis separated malignant from non-malignant epithelium and resolved 8 malignant epithelial clusters. An ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial state showed the lowest differentiation state and highest stem-like potential. A seven-gene signature stratified patients into high- and low-CSL groups; the high-CSL group had worse overall survival and showed bulk-level immune features consistent with an inflammatory but impaired immune context, including an increased CIBERSORT-estimated M2 macrophage fraction and higher dysfunction/exclusion scores. EMB was prioritized as a candidate prognostic hub gene, and EMB-high cells within the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster showed inferred ligand-receptor interactions with monocytic/macrophage-related immune populations. EMB knockdown reduced gastric cancer cell migration and invasion and shifted epithelial–mesenchymal transition markers, increasing E-cadherin and decreasing N-cadherin.ConclusionsThis primary GC single-cell atlas identifies a low-differentiation ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial state and uses its marker genes to derive a seven-gene CSL signature associated with poor prognosis and bulk tumor-level immune features. EMB emerges as a candidate biomarker and functional mediator of GC cell migration and invasion, warranting further mechanistic validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16551-9.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Single-cell RNA sequencing, EMB

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide, characterized by high histological and molecular heterogeneity [1, 2]. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, the prognosis for advanced GC remains poor, with five-year survival rates under 30% in many cases [1]. Traditional classifications of GC, such as Lauren’s diffuse vs. intestinal types, capture some aspects of tumor heterogeneity but do not fully explain differences in clinical outcomes and therapy responses [3]. Tumors are complex ecosystems composed of malignant cells coexisting with various stromal and immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME). This cellular milieu plays crucial roles in cancer progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy.

Notably, the immune component of the TME in GC is highly variable and can exert both pro-tumor and anti-tumor influences [4–8]. Different immune cell subsets infiltrate GC to varying degrees, and their spatial organization and functional states can dramatically impact disease course and treatment efficacy [9, 10]. For example, tumors with abundant T and NK cells or organized tertiary lymphoid structures may respond better to immunotherapy, whereas those dominated by immunosuppressive myeloid cells and exhausted T cells often exhibit immune evasion and poor outcomes [11]. Although programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and microsatellite instability (MSI), which reflects defective DNA mismatch repair, have been used as biomarkers for immunotherapy stratification, neither alone is sufficient to accurately predict benefit in gastric cancer (GC), likely because of the complex and dynamic nature of TME [12]. Thus, comprehensive profiling of the GC microenvironment at single-cell resolution is critical for uncovering the interactions between malignant and immune cells that drive tumor behavior.

scRNA-seq has emerged as a powerful approach to dissect the intra-tumoral heterogeneity of GC and its TME [13–15]. Recent scRNA-seq studies have identified diverse cell lineages in GC and revealed previously unrecognized subpopulations linked to disease progression [16]. For instance, one study described a unique epithelial subpopulation with an epithelial-myofibroblast transition phenotype associated with poor prognosis, while another uncovered an immune-related malignant cell subgroup in EBV-positive tumors with high expression of antigen presentation genes [17]. Similarly, single-cell analyses of metastatic GC have demonstrated that tumor cells can exist in multiple transcriptional states and that the composition of these states correlates with patient survival [18]. Moreover, single-cell profiling has highlighted dynamic TME changes during disease progression, for example, the expansion of monocyte-like dendritic cells and highly plastic cancer cell clusters in peritoneal metastases, which contribute to immune evasion via autophagy programs [18, 19]. These insights underscore the value of high-resolution single-cell data in identifying key cellular players and interactions in GC.

In this study, we performed an integrative scRNA-seq analysis of GC by combining four datasets (GSE134520, GSE163558, GSE167297, and GSE183904) encompassing 43 patients. Our goals were to (1) map the cellular landscape of GC and adjacent gastric tissue, (2) distinguish malignant epithelial cells from non-malignant epithelial cells and characterize their molecular features, (3) resolve intra-tumoral heterogeneity among malignant epithelial cells and identify low-differentiation malignant states, (4) construct a prognostic signature using marker genes from a stem-like malignant epithelial state, and (5) explore tumor-level immune associations and candidate functional genes. By integrating single-cell transcriptomes with TCGA bulk clinical data and in vitro assays, we identified an ARHGDIB⁺ low-differentiation malignant epithelial state and prioritized EMB as a candidate prognostic hub gene associated with GC cell migration and invasion.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

scRNA-seq datasets of GC and related gastric tissues were aggregated from public repositories, including GSE134520, GSE163558, GSE167297, and GSE183904 [16, 20–22]. After sample selection, the integrated atlas included 71 samples, comprising 43 tumor samples, 16 adjacent/normal gastric tissue samples, and 12 premalignant lesion samples. The premalignant lesion samples included non-atrophic gastritis (NAG), chronic atrophic gastritis (CAG), and intestinal metaplasia (IM) samples from GSE134520. Detailed sample metadata and inclusion information are summarized in Table S1. Count matrices were analyzed primarily in Scanpy v1.9.1 [23]. Doublets were flagged using Scrublet with default parameters and removed from further analyses [24]. Bulk RNA‑seq profiles with clinical annotations for GC were collected from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, accessed 23 May 2025). Gene expression data in FPKM format were used for downstream expression-related analyses, whereas raw count data were used for differential expression analysis. FPKM values were log2-transformed as log2(FPKM + 1) before expression-related analyses. Raw count data were used for DESeq2-based differential expression analysis [25]. TCGA clinical information was matched to expression profiles by sample barcode, and patients without complete overall survival information were excluded from survival analyses.

Quality Control (QC)

QC was performed per sample in Scanpy. Cells were retained if they met the following criteria: (i) detected genes per cell ≥ 200; (ii) total UMI counts per cell ≥ 500; and (iii) mitochondrial transcript fraction ≤ 10%. Genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells were removed. Doublets were identified using Scrublet and predicted doublets were removed before downstream analyses. The distributions of major QC metrics are shown in Fig. S1.

scRNA‑seq clustering, visualization, and cell annotation

After QC and normalization, 3000 highly variable genes were selected using sc.pp.highly_variable_genes in Scanpy. The number of principal components (PCs) used for downstream analysis was determined by inspecting the PCA explained-variance elbow plot. As the variance curve plateaued around PC40, the top 40 PCs were retained for Harmony batch correction, using sample ID as the batch variable [26]. Clusters were identified using the Leiden algorithm (resolution = 0.2) [27] and visualized with UMAP to summarize cellular heterogeneity. Annotation combined literature‑curated marker genes with de novo differential expression analysis [13, 18]. For the latter, sc.tl.rank_genes_groups (Wilcoxon rank‑sum test) was applied to each cluster to define signature genes distinguishing that cluster from all others, which guided final cell‑type labels.

InferCNV analysis

Large‑scale somatic chromosomal copy‑number variations (CNVs) in epithelial cells were inferred with inferCNVpy v0.3.0 (https://github.com/icbi-lab/infercnvpy). Inputs included raw counts, cell annotations, and gene‑to‑chromosome position files prepared per package specifications. B and T cells served as reference “normal” populations; default parameters were used. Epithelial cells were stratified by per‑cell CNV score, with scores above the cohort median classified as malignant and those below as non‑malignant. Malignant epithelial cells were subsequently re‑clustered with Leiden to resolve intratumoral subpopulations.

GSEA and gene ontology enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment was performed with clusterProfiler [28], emphasizing Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process terms to interpret cellular programs and pathways associated with the gene sets.

CytoTRACE2 analysis

CytoTRACE2 was used to estimate cellular differentiation potential from single-cell RNA-seq data, based on the observation that the number of detected genes per cell decreases as cells differentiate [29]. For each sample, the post-QC raw UMI count matrix was used as input and analyzed with default parameters. CytoTRACE scores were computed for each cell, with higher values indicating greater stemness.

Transcription factor regulon analysis

Regulatory network inference used pySCENIC v1.1.3 with default settings [30]. For computational tractability, 300–500 cells were randomly sampled from each cluster to construct a gene‑by‑cell matrix. Motif scanning was restricted to ± 10 kb around transcription start sites (TSSs) or to the 500 bp upstream region of TSSs. Cluster‑specific transcription factors (TFs) were defined as the top 10–15 most enriched regulons (Wilcoxon rank‑sum test) relative to all other clusters, and TF networks were visualized.

Construction and validation of the ARHGDIB+ malignant-state-derived prognostic model

Because the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster showed the lowest differentiation state and the highest stem-like potential in CytoTRACE2 analysis, we used its marker genes as a biologically informed candidate pool for prognostic gene selection. The top 200 marker genes of this subcluster were selected according to differential expression ranking. This number was chosen to retain a sufficiently broad set of subcluster-associated genes for survival screening while reducing noise from lower-ranking genes.

These candidate genes were then mapped to the TCGA-STAD bulk RNA-seq cohort. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was first performed to identify genes associated with overall survival. Genes with P < 0.05 were further subjected to LASSO Cox regression to reduce dimensionality and mitigate overfitting. For each patient, a risk score was computed as a weighted sum of the expression values and their corresponding LASSO coefficients: Risk score = (β1 × Expr1) + (β2 × Expr2) + … + (βn × Exprn). Patients were stratified into high- and low-CSL groups according to the median risk score. Model performance was assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis. The TCGA-based analysis was used to evaluate the patient-level prognostic value of genes selected from the ARHGDIB+ malignant-state marker pool. It was not intended to directly estimate the abundance of ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial cells in bulk samples. To validate the relationship between the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster and the seven CSL model genes at the single-cell level, UMAP feature plots were generated for representative malignant subcluster marker genes and for the seven CSL genes. Spearman correlation analysis was performed between ARHGDIB and each CSL gene using normalized single-cell expression values from malignant epithelial cells.

Immune infiltration analysis and functional enrichment

CIBERSORT was used to estimate immune cell composition in the high‑ and low‑CSL groups and to examine associations between immune‑infiltrating populations and prognostic genes [31]. Tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) scores were calculated for each sample, and expression of immune checkpoint–related genes was compared between CSL groups using the Wilcoxon rank‑sum test. To characterize transcriptional differences, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified with DESeq2 [25]. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with an adjusted P < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|) > 1 were considered as DEGs. Because these analyses were performed using TCGA bulk RNA-seq data, immune infiltration, TIDE, and pathway enrichment results were interpreted as tumor-level associations with the CSL risk score rather than as cell-state-specific mechanisms of the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster.

Gene mutation profiling

Somatic mutation data for GC were retrieved from TCGA. The maftools R package [32] was used to summarize mutational landscapes and to calculate tumor mutational burden (TMB) for each sample. Associations between TMB and the risk score were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation. Samples were further divided into high‑ and low‑TMB cohorts by the median TMB, and Kaplan Meier analyses were performed to compare survival outcomes between the strata.

Definition of EMB-high and EMB-low cells within the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster

EMB was prioritized from the ARHGDIB⁺ malignant-state-derived CSL signature. Therefore, EMB-high and EMB-low cells were defined within the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster to maintain consistency with the subcluster-centered analytical framework. Cells within this subcluster were stratified according to normalized EMB expression. EMB-high and EMB-low cells were defined as the top and bottom quartiles of EMB expression, respectively, and intermediate cells were excluded from the interaction comparison to increase contrast.

Cell-cell interaction analysis

Cell-cell communication was inferred with CellPhoneDB (database v5.0.0) [33]. For the EMB-high/low comparison, CellPhoneDB analysis was performed between EMB-high or EMB-low cells within the ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial subcluster and monocytic/macrophage-related immune populations. Therefore, this analysis was designed to explore predicted tumor–myeloid communication rather than to compare interactions across all immune cell types. Interaction strength was estimated from ligand-receptor expression, with significance assessed by permutation testing. Ligand-receptor pairs with P < 0.001 were retained. CellPhoneDB analysis was used as an exploratory and hypothesis-generating approach. Predicted ligand-receptor interactions were not interpreted as experimentally validated cell-cell communication mechanisms. No additional immune subcluster-level annotation was introduced for this analysis; the immune cell identities were based on the annotated immune populations from the main single-cell atlas and their marker gene profiles shown in Fig. 2B and C.

The workflow of the study

CCLE/DepMap analysis, cell culture, EMB knockdown, RT-qPCR validation, and wound-healing assay

To evaluate EMB expression across GC cell lines, EMB mRNA expression data were obtained from the CCLE/DepMap resource. GC cell lines were selected according to lineage annotation, and EMB expression levels were compared across available cell lines. MKN45 cells showed relatively high EMB expression and were therefore selected for EMB loss-of-function experiments. MKN45 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. EMB knockdown was achieved with shRNAs cloned into pLKO.1‑TRC (sh‑EMB‑a: CCTGTTGGTGTTCAAATGAAT; sh‑EMB‑b: GAACATTCTAGTATGCCAGTA). Constructs were transformed into E. coli for plasmid amplification, purified, and transfected into 293T cells for lentiviral packaging. Viral supernatants collected at 48 h were filtered and used to infect target cells, followed by puromycin selection to establish stable EMB‑depleted lines. Knockdown efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR. Relative EMB expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH. The EMB primer sequences were: forward, GGCCACCATTCTGCTTTGTG; reverse, TCACTGGCCCAGAGACTCAT.

For wound‑healing assays, transfected cells were seeded in 6‑well plates and grown to 80% confluence. A linear scratch was generated using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing with PBS. Fresh medium was then added, and images were captured at 0 h and 24 h from the same marked field under identical magnification. Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ by measuring the initial wound area at 0 h and the remaining wound area at the indicated time point. Cell migration was expressed as the wound-healing rate, calculated as: Wound-healing rate (%) = (A0h − At) / A0h × 100%, where A0h is the initial wound area and At is the residual wound area at the indicated time point.

Transwell invasion assays

Cell invasion was assessed using Transwell chambers with 8.0-µm pore membranes (Corning, USA). For invasion assays, the inserts were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, 356234). Cells (2.0 × 105 cells/mL) were suspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chambers, while the lower chambers were filled with complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum. After 24 h, cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R v4.4.3 and Python. Non-normally distributed data were compared using the Wilcoxon test. Survival differences were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess associations between gene expression values or scores. For in vitro experiments involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by multiple-comparison testing was used unless otherwise specified. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell atlas of gastric tumors and adjacent tissues

After stringent quality control and doublet removal, we obtained a total of 184,185 high-quality single cells from 71 samples (Fig.1 and Fig S1A–D). These samples included 43 tumor samples, 16 adjacent/normal gastric tissue samples, and 12 premalignant lesion samples, as summarized in Table S1. We performed unsupervised clustering of the integrated single-cell data using the Leiden algorithm, which yielded 13 distinct cell clusters (clusters 0–12) on the UMAP projection (Fig. 2A). Based on canonical marker genes and lineage-specific transcripts, we annotated these clusters into three major lineages: epithelial cells, immune cells, and stromal cells (Fig. 2B). Specifically, the epithelial lineage comprised four clusters, Gastric epithelial cells (cluster 1, marked by EPCAM, KRT19), Gastric chief cells (cluster 7, LIPF, PGC markers), Gastrointestinal neuroendocrine cells (cluster 8, expressing CHGA), and highly proliferative cells (cluster 12, enriched for proliferation markers like MKI67). The immune lineage included T cells (cluster 0 and 11, CD3D, CD3E), Plasma cells (cluster 2, MZB1, IGHG), Monocytes (cluster 3, CD14, LYZ), B cells (cluster 5, CD79A, MS4A1), and Mast cells (cluster 9, TPSAB1, CPA3). The stromal lineage comprised Fibroblasts (cluster 4, COL1A1, DCN), Endothelial cells (cluster 6, VWF, FLT1), and Smooth muscle cells (cluster 10, TAGLN, ACTA2). These assignments were supported by a dot plot of marker gene expression (Fig. 2C) and are consistent with cell type profiles from other single-cell GC studies [34, 35].

C Dot plot of representative marker genes across cell types (dot size, fraction of cells in group; color, mean expression in group). D Stacked bar plot showing relative cell-type abundance in tumor and non-tumor samples. The non-tumor group included adjacent/normal gastric tissues and premalignant lesion samples. E Relative cell-type abundance by sex (Female vs. Male)

Next, we descriptively compared the cellular composition of tumor and non-tumor gastric samples. Tumor samples showed visually higher proportions of several immune populations, including T cells, plasma cells, and monocytes, compared with non-tumor samples (Fig. 2D). T cells represented one of the major cell populations in tumor tissues, suggesting active immune infiltration. This observation is consistent with previous single-cell studies reporting enrichment of lymphoid and myeloid populations in gastric tumors [34]. Together, these findings indicate that gastric tumors contain a complex immune microenvironment with increased immune cell representation. We also examined cell-type composition according to patient sex. Male and female samples showed broadly similar overall lineage distributions in the descriptive composition plot (Fig. 2E). Although small visual differences were noted in some immune cell populations, such as monocytes, no formal statistical testing was performed for this comparison; therefore, these results are presented as descriptive observations only.

In summary, our integrated single-cell analysis provides a comprehensive map of GC at single-cell resolution. We delineated all major cell types present in GC and adjacent tissue, confirming an intricate cellular ecosystem. The TME is enriched for diverse immune cells, particularly T cells and monocytes, indicating an immune-reactive yet possibly dysregulated context within tumors. This atlas establishes the foundation for deeper investigation into malignant and immune cell interactions in GC.

Identification of malignant epithelial cells and their pathways

We applied an inferCNV analysis to infer large-scale CNV profiles of single cells. Cells with significant CNV signal across multiple chromosomal regions were classified as putative malignant epithelial cells. We set a CNV score threshold of 0.022 to segregate epithelial cells into malignant and non-malignant cells (Fig. 3A, B).

A inferCNV heatmap showing large‑scale copy‑number variation patterns across single cells (genes ordered by chromosomal position; red, relative gain; blue, relative loss; T and B cells used as reference). B UMAP of epithelial cells colored by inferCNV‑inferred malignant status. C Hallmark GSEA plots of pathways enriched in malignant versus non‑malignant epithelial cells. D Top GO biological process terms enriched for genes upregulated in malignant epithelial cells (bar length, −log10 adjusted P value)

Having defined the malignant epithelial cell population, we compared the transcriptomic profiles of malignant and non-malignant epithelial cells to identify cancer-associated biological programs. Hallmark GSEA based on the ranked differential expression profile showed positive enrichment of several metabolic and hormone-response gene sets in malignant epithelial cells, including Xenobiotic metabolism, Cholesterol homeostasis, Androgen response, and Estrogen response early and late gene sets (Fig. 3C). These findings indicate pathway-level enrichment in malignant epithelial cells, rather than direct evidence that all genes within these pathways were uniformly upregulated. The enrichment of these programs suggests that malignant epithelial cells exhibit altered metabolic and hormone-response transcriptional states. Although GC is not classically considered a hormone-driven cancer, the positive enrichment of estrogen- and androgen-response gene sets may reflect broader transcriptional remodeling of malignant epithelial cells or interactions with the TME. Consistent with our findings, a recent single-cell study on gastric signet-ring cell carcinoma also reported aberrant activation of MAPK and estrogen signaling pathways in diffuse-type tumor cells [36]. Conversely, pathways associated with immune surveillance were downregulated in malignant cells, notably, the Allograft rejection pathway (which includes antigen presentation and T-cell activation genes) was suppressed in cancer cells. This immune evasion signature is in agreement with the observation that some GC cells reduce antigen presentation to avoid T cell recognition.

We performed GO enrichment analysis on the top 200 genes upregulated in malignant epithelial cells. The most significant GO BP terms for malignant cell genes were Cytoplasmic translation, Peptide biosynthetic process, and Macromolecule biosynthetic process (Fig. 3D). These terms reflect the heightened protein synthesis and anabolic activity in cancer cells, which have increased ribosomal protein expression and active translation to support rapid growth.

Together, these analyses indicate that GC cells (malignant epithelium) can be distinguished from normal gastric epithelium by pervasive changes in their transcriptome. GC cells upregulate pathways for metabolism and biomass production, while downregulating antigen presentation and immune signaling, changes that likely confer growth advantages and immune evasion capabilities. This is in line with prior evidence of metabolic reprogramming and immune escape mechanisms in gastric tumors [37]. Having confirmed the identity and unique features of the malignant epithelial population, we proceeded to dissect the heterogeneity within these cancer cells.

Heterogeneity of malignant epithelial subpopulations

We re-clustered the malignant epithelial cells to explore intra-tumoral heterogeneity and identify distinct subpopulations of cancer cells. This sub-clustering analysis yielded 8 malignant epithelial subclusters (Fig. 4A). We assigned labels to these malignant epithelial subclusters based on their top-ranked marker genes identified by differential expression analysis (Scanpy rank_genes_groups, Wilcoxon test). Each subcluster was named after the most differentially expressed gene compared with all other malignant epithelial cells, yielding SPINK1⁺ Epi, MUC5AC⁺ Epi, CLDN4⁺ Epi, SCG5⁺ Epi, CD74⁺ Epi, PGC⁺ Epi, ARHGDIB⁺ Epi, and CKB⁺ Epi (Table S2).

B CytoTRACE2 potency scores across malignant epithelial subclusters (higher scores indicate less differentiated states). C UMAP colored by subcluster identity. D UMAP showing CytoTRACE2 potency scores. E CytoTRACE2‑derived relative differentiation order. F Heatmap of representative transcription factor regulon activities inferred by pySCENIC across malignant epithelial subclusters (values scaled per regulon)

The existence of these distinct malignant cell subtypes underscores the intra-tumoral heterogeneity in GC. We next sought to functionally characterize these subclusters. Using CytoTRACE2 analysis, we found notable differences in developmental potential across the 8 subpopulations. The ARHGDIB+ Epi cluster consistently ranked highest in stemness, with a high CytoTRACE score indicating a less differentiated, more stem-like state (Fig. 4B). This subpopulation also had the lowest expression of differentiation markers, and the highest expression of genes associated with cell cycle and proliferation. We also examined CytoTRACE2-inferred differentiation ordering (Fig. 4C–E). ARHGDIB⁺ Epi cells occupied a relatively low-differentiation position among malignant epithelial states, supporting the interpretation that this cluster represents a stem-like malignant epithelial state. However, because CytoTRACE2 infers differentiation potential from transcriptomic features, this analysis does not establish direct lineage relationships among malignant epithelial states. Notably, a previous scRNA-seq study identified a subset of proliferative, stem-like GC cells associated with poor prognosis, which parallels our findings for the ARHGDIB+ subcluster [18].

We then performed TF regulatory network analysis on each malignant subcluster. Each subpopulation showed enrichment of distinct master TFs, reflecting their lineage bias. The ARHGDIB+ Epi cluster, on the other hand, showed high activity of FLI1, an ETS-family TF involved in hematopoietic development and possibly angiogenesis. FLI1 upregulation in ARHGDIB+ tumor cell is intriguing and could suggest an endothelial or mesenchymal trans-differentiation tendency, as FLI1 is normally expressed in endothelial cells and certain immune cells. Further investigation is needed, but differential TF activity reinforces that these malignant subpopulations are not only transcriptionally distinct but may also be regulated by different gene programs.

In summary, we identified 8 transcriptionally distinct subpopulations of malignant epithelial cells in GC. The ARHGDIB+ subcluster emerged as a particularly interesting group with stem-like features and potential clinical relevance, prompting us to investigate its prognostic significance.

Prognostic risk signature derived from a stem-like malignant subcluster

CytoTRACE2 analysis indicated that the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster showed the lowest differentiation state and the highest stem-like potential among the malignant epithelial subclusters. We therefore used this subcluster as a biologically informed source for candidate prognostic genes. Specifically, the top 200 marker genes of the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster were selected as a stem-like malignant-state-derived candidate gene pool. This strategy was intended to prioritize prognosis-related genes associated with a low-differentiation malignant cell state, rather than to directly quantify the abundance of ARHGDIB⁺ cells in bulk TCGA samples.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of this single-cell-derived candidate pool, we mapped these genes to the TCGA-STAD bulk RNA-seq cohort and performed univariable Cox regression for overall survival. Prognosis-associated genes were further subjected to LASSO Cox regression to reduce dimensionality and construct a parsimonious prognostic model. This analysis identified a seven-gene signature consisting of VIM, RGS1, NUDT1, ALOX5AP, OSTF1, STK17A, and EMB. We defined this model as a cancer stem-like (CSL) signature because it was derived from marker genes of the low-differentiation ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial state. To further support the relationship between the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster and the seven-gene CSL signature, we performed additional single-cell-level validation. UMAP feature plots showed the expression patterns of representative marker genes for the eight malignant epithelial subclusters, including ARHGDIB for the ARHGDIB+ subcluster (Fig. S2A). We then visualized ARHGDIB and the seven CSL model genes in malignant epithelial cells. These genes were detectable in malignant epithelial cells and showed partially overlapping expression patterns with the ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial state to varying degrees (Fig. S2C).

Spearman correlation analysis further showed positive associations between ARHGDIB and the seven CSL genes in malignant epithelial cells (Fig. S2B). These results support the interpretation that the seven-gene CSL signature represents a prognostic transcriptional footprint derived from the ARHGDIB⁺ low-differentiation malignant epithelial state.

LASSO Cox regression selected the final seven-gene CSL model from the ARHGDIB⁺ candidate gene pool (Fig. 5A). For each TCGA-STAD patient, a CSL risk score was calculated as the weighted sum of the expression levels of the seven genes and their corresponding LASSO coefficients. Patients were then divided into high-CSL and low-CSL groups according to the median risk score. After excluding patients without complete survival information, Kaplan–Meier analysis included 363 TCGA-STAD patients, with 182 patients in the high-CSL group and 181 patients in the low-CSL group. The high-CSL group showed significantly worse overall survival than the low-CSL group (Fig. 5B). The median overall survival was 19.0 months in the high-CSL group and 56.2 months in the low-CSL group. Time-dependent ROC analysis yielded AUCs of 0.62, 0.67, and 0.70 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, respectively, indicating moderate prognostic performance (Fig. 5C).

B Kaplan–Meier overall survival curves for the high‑CSL and low‑CSL groups stratified by the median risk score (log‑rank test). C Time‑dependent ROC curves for 1‑, 3‑, and 5‑year survival. D Multivariable Cox regression of model genes shown as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals

To further evaluate the contribution of individual model genes, we examined their associations with overall survival using Cox regression (Fig. 5D). VIM and NUDT1 retained independent associations with poorer overall survival, whereas the remaining genes showed weaker individual effects. These results suggest that the seven-gene CSL signature captures a patient-level prognostic transcriptional pattern derived from the ARHGDIB+ low-differentiation malignant epithelial state. Importantly, because TCGA-STAD bulk RNA-seq does not resolve single-cell states, the CSL score should be interpreted as a bulk-level prognostic projection of the ARHGDIB+ malignant-state-derived gene program, rather than a direct measurement of ARHGDIB+ cell abundance.

Immune and genomic differences between high-CSL and low-CSL groups

To characterize the biological features associated with the CSL risk score, we compared transcriptomic, immune, and genomic profiles between high-CSL and low-CSL tumors in the TCGA-STAD cohort. GSEA of GO biological process terms showed that high-CSL tumors were enriched for multiple immune-related processes, including myeloid leukocyte activation, positive regulation of T cell activation, regulation of leukocyte-mediated immunity, and response to chemokine (Fig. 6A). These results indicate that high-CSL tumors exhibit increased immune- and inflammation-related transcriptional activity at the bulk tumor level. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further supported this observation. High-CSL tumors were enriched for pathways related to immune and inflammatory responses, including chemokine signaling, hematopoietic cell lineage, leishmaniasis, and toxoplasmosis pathways (Fig. 6B). Although some of these pathway names are infection-related, their enrichment largely reflects the upregulation of immune-response, cytokine/chemokine, and myeloid-associated genes in the high-CSL group. We next used CIBERSORT to estimate immune cell composition in the two CSL groups. The high-CSL group showed an increased fraction of M2 macrophages, whereas the fraction of regulatory T cells was lower than that in the low-CSL group (Fig. 6C). The increased CIBERSORT-estimated M2 macrophage fraction suggests macrophage-associated immune features at the bulk tumor level in high-CSL tumors. The lower Treg fraction indicates that the immunosuppressive phenotype associated with the CSL score may not be primarily driven by Treg enrichment. Therefore, rather than interpreting high-CSL tumors as simply “immune activated” or “immune suppressed,” these findings suggest a more complex immune contexture.

We then compared somatic mutation landscapes between the two CSL groups. In the high-CSL group, the most frequently mutated genes included TTN, TP53, and ARID1A (Fig. 6D), whereas in the low-CSL group, the most frequently mutated genes included TTN, TP53, and MUC16 (Fig. 6E). ARID1A mutation appeared more frequent in the high-CSL group. In the TCGA molecular classification of gastric adenocarcinoma, gastric cancer is divided into EBV-positive, microsatellite instability (MSI), genomically stable (GS), and chromosomal instability (CIN) subtypes [38]. ARID1A alterations have been reported across several molecular contexts, including EBV-positive tumors, hypermutated/MSI-related tumors, and genomically stable tumor [38]. Therefore, the enrichment of ARID1A mutation in the high-CSL group may suggest a possible association between the CSL risk score and the molecular/genomic background of gastric cancer. However, because molecular subtype annotation was not directly integrated into the current analysis, this observation should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as evidence that the CSL model defines a specific molecular subtype.

Finally, we evaluated immune evasion-related features using TIDE. High-CSL tumors showed higher TIDE scores, with increased dysfunction and exclusion scores compared with low-CSL tumors (Fig. 6F). These results suggest that despite enrichment of immune activation-related transcriptional programs, high-CSL tumors may exhibit impaired antitumor immune function. The high-CSL group also showed differences in MSI-related scores in the TIDE analysis (Fig. 6F), further indicating that immune response prediction in gastric cancer is multifactorial. This is consistent with previous work showing that immunotherapy response in gastric cancer cannot be fully explained by a single biomarker such as PD-L1, MSI, TMB, or immune infiltration alone, but instead requires multidimensional evaluation of the tumor immune microenvironment [12].

Collectively, high-CSL tumors showed enrichment of immune- and inflammation-related transcriptional programs, increased M2 macrophage infiltration, elevated TIDE dysfunction and exclusion scores, and distinct somatic mutation patterns. These findings suggest that the CSL risk score is associated with an inflammatory yet functionally impaired immune context with M2 macrophage-associated bulk features. Because these analyses were based on TCGA bulk data, these results should be interpreted as tumor-level associations rather than direct evidence of a myeloid-skewed tumor microenvironment or ARHGDIB+ cell-state-specific immune mechanisms.

Prioritization of EMB as a candidate prognostic hub gene

To further prioritize genes within the seven-gene CSL signature, we applied a random forest model to rank the relative contribution of each gene to CSL group classification. EMB showed the highest feature importance among the seven genes, suggesting that EMB expression contributed strongly to distinguishing high-CSL from low-CSL tumors (Fig. 7A). Because feature importance from machine learning does not establish causality, we interpreted EMB as a candidate prognostic hub gene for further exploratory analysis rather than as a validated driver gene.

We next examined the clinical relevance of the top-ranked genes from the random forest analysis. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with EMB-high tumors had significantly poorer overall survival than those with EMB-low tumors (Fig. 7B). For VIM, patients in the VIM-high group showed a trend toward poorer survival, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 7C). In contrast, OSTF1-high and OSTF1-low groups showed no significant survival difference (Fig. 7D). We further compared gene expression between tumor and normal tissues in TCGA-STAD. EMB was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (Fig. 7E), whereas VIM and OSTF1 did not show significant tumor-versus-normal differences (Fig. 7F, G). Together, these analyses supported EMB as the most clinically relevant candidate among the top-ranked CSL genes and provided the rationale for subsequent functional validation.

Therefore, we selected EMB for functional follow-up experiments. Importantly, the current evidence supports a potential role of EMB in gastric cancer cell migration and invasion but does not by itself establish EMB as a definitive therapeutic target. Further mechanistic and in vivo studies will be needed to validate the therapeutic relevance of EMB.

Inferred interactions between EMB-high ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial cells and monocytic/macrophage-related immune populations

Because EMB was prioritized from the ARHGDIB+ malignant-state-derived CSL signature, we further stratified cells within the ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial subcluster into EMB-high and EMB-low groups. EMB-high and EMB-low cells were defined using the upper and lower quartiles of normalized EMB expression, respectively, and intermediate cells were excluded from the interaction analysis. UMAP visualization showed the distribution of EMB-high and EMB-low cells within the ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial subcluster, and EMB expression was visualized in the same embedding (Fig. S2D, E).

We next used CellPhoneDB to compare ligand-receptor interaction patterns between EMB-high or EMB-low ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial cells and monocytic/macrophage-related immune populations. CellPhoneDB analysis predicted that EMB-high ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial cells had a greater number and overall strength of ligand-receptor interactions with monocytic/macrophage-related immune populations than EMB-low ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial cells (Fig. S3A). Representative predicted ligand-receptor pairs are shown in Fig. S3B. These included CCL2–CCR2 and CSF1–CSF1R, which are associated with monocyte recruitment and macrophage maintenance, as well as VCAM1/ICAM1–integrin interactions, which may reflect adhesion-related tumor–myeloid communication. In addition, predicted immunoregulatory interactions, including CD47–SIRPA, CD24–SIGLEC10, HLA-G–LILRB2, and TGF-β-related ligand-receptor pairs, suggested potential communication patterns between EMB-high ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial cells and monocytic/macrophage-related immune populations.

These findings suggest that EMB-high cells within the ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial subcluster may have stronger inferred interactions with monocytic/macrophage-related immune populations. However, because CellPhoneDB analysis is based on ligand-receptor expression inference, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating and do not establish experimentally validated EMB-dependent tumor–macrophage mechanisms.

Functional validation of EMB in GC cell migration and invasion

To strengthen the rationale for cell line selection, we first examined EMB expression across gastric cancer cell lines using CCLE/DepMap data (Table S3 and Fig. S4A). MKN45 cells showed relatively high EMB expression among GC cell lines and were therefore selected for loss-of-function experiments. We then used two independent shRNAs to silence EMB in MKN45 cells. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed significant reduction of EMB transcript levels after shRNA-mediated knockdown, validating the efficiency of EMB depletion (Fig. S4B).

We next tested whether EMB contributes to the aggressive phenotype of GC cells. Using two independent shRNAs, we silenced EMB in MKN45 cells and assessed motility by scratch wound‑healing assays. Compared with controls, EMB‑depleted cells exhibited markedly delayed wound closure, corresponding to an ~ 40–50% reduction in migration at 24 h (Fig. 8A). Consistently, in Matrigel‑coated Transwell assays, EMB knockdown led to a pronounced loss of invasive capacity, with more than a 50% decrease in the number of cells penetrating the matrix (Fig. 8B). In line with these phenotypes, immunoblotting showed increased E‑cadherin and decreased N‑cadherin upon EMB knockdown (Fig. 8C). These results suggest that EMB is functionally associated with migration and invasion in GC cells, consistent with its prioritization as a candidate hub gene within the CSL model.

Together with its prioritization in the CSL model, EMB may represent a candidate prognostic biomarker and functional mediator of invasive phenotypes in GC cells.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq data from four GC cohorts to construct a single-cell atlas of the GC tumor microenvironment. We recovered the major epithelial, immune, and stromal compartments and observed increased immune representation in tumors, together with immune dysfunction-associated features in high-CSL tumors, in line with prior GC single-cell studies [39, 40].

A key finding is the marked heterogeneity within malignant epithelial cells. We resolved eight malignant epithelial states and identified an ARHGDIB⁺ subpopulation with a lower differentiation state and higher stem-like potential, suggesting a stem-like malignant epithelial program. This observation is consistent with the concept that poorly differentiated malignant states are linked to aggressive behavior and adverse outcomes in GC and related subtypes [17, 18, 34]. Importantly, genes derived from the ARHGDIB+ malignant-state marker pool enabled construction of a seven-gene CSL signature that stratified patient survival, suggesting patient-level prognostic relevance of this low-differentiation malignant epithelial program.

High-CSL tumors exhibited an immune phenotype that can be best described as “inflamed but impaired.” Enrichment of immune-related GO terms, including T cell activation-related processes, indicates increased immune/inflammatory transcriptional activity at the bulk tumor level. However, this does not necessarily represent effective antitumor immunity. The higher TIDE dysfunction and exclusion scores suggest that immune activity in high-CSL tumors may be functionally impaired. In addition, CIBERSORT suggested an increased M2 macrophage fraction in high-CSL tumors, indicating M2 macrophage-associated bulk immune features. Although Treg fractions were lower in high-CSL tumors, this finding does not rule out immunosuppression; instead, it suggests that the CSL-associated immune phenotype may not be primarily driven by Treg enrichment. These interpretations remain bulk-level associations and do not establish a myeloid-skewed tumor microenvironment at single-cell resolution.

An important consideration in this study is the cross-scale integration of scRNA-seq-defined malignant cell states with bulk TCGA transcriptomes. Our purpose was not to use bulk RNA-seq to resolve the ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial subcluster at single-cell resolution. Instead, scRNA-seq was used to identify a biologically meaningful, low-differentiation malignant epithelial state and to define its marker genes as a candidate pool for prognostic screening. TCGA-STAD bulk RNA-seq was then used to determine which genes from this candidate pool had patient-level prognostic value. Therefore, the resulting CSL score should be interpreted as a bulk-level prognostic transcriptional signature derived from an ARHGDIB+ stem-like malignant cell state, rather than as a direct estimate of ARHGDIB+ cell abundance.

These immune features may have implications for immunotherapy stratification. High-CSL tumors showed immune/inflammatory transcriptional enrichment together with higher TIDE dysfunction and exclusion scores, suggesting that immune activation-related signals may coexist with impaired antitumor immune function. The increased CIBERSORT-estimated M2 macrophage fraction further suggests macrophage-associated suppressive features at the bulk tumor level. Because macrophage-rich immune contexts can contribute to T cell dysfunction, immune exclusion, and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade, future studies should evaluate whether CSL-high tumors may benefit from therapeutic strategies combining checkpoint blockade with approaches that modulate TAM/macrophage-associated suppression. This interpretation remains hypothesis-generating and requires validation in immunotherapy-treated cohorts.

We further prioritized EMB as a candidate prognostic hub gene within the CSL signature. EMB expression was associated with poor prognosis, and EMB knockdown reduced GC cell migration and invasion in vitro. These findings support a functional association between EMB expression and invasive phenotypes in GC cells. However, random forest ranking and in vitro loss-of-function assays do not establish EMB as a validated therapeutic target, and further mechanistic, rescue, overexpression, and in vivo studies are required.

Mechanistically, the increase in E-cadherin and decrease in N-cadherin after EMB knockdown suggest that EMB may be associated with epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity in GC cells. However, the downstream pathways linking EMB to migration and invasion remain to be defined. Several metastasis-related mechanisms may be relevant to this hypothesis. Matrix remodeling is a central component of tumor invasion, and recent studies have linked MMP2 regulation to cancer cell migration and metastatic behavior, including transcriptional control of MMP2 and activation of MMP2-associated signaling pathways in invasive tumor models [41, 42]. MMP1-positive malignant states have also been associated with metastatic potential and tumor–immune interactions in single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses, and MMP1-related signaling has been implicated in cell migration and invasion [43, 44]. In addition, TGF-β signaling is a canonical regulator of EMT, invasion, immune evasion, and tumor progression [45]. Structural features of invasion, such as invadopodia-mediated extracellular matrix degradation, biomechanical adaptation to confined tumor microenvironments, and pre-metastatic niche formation, may further cooperate with tumor-intrinsic motility programs [46–48]. Therefore, EMB may participate in invasion-associated epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity, potentially intersecting with ECM remodeling and TGF-β-related programs. Nevertheless, we did not directly test whether EMB regulates MMP1, MMP2, TGF-β signaling, invadopodia formation, biomechanical invasion programs, or metastatic niche remodeling. These possibilities should be investigated in future mechanistic studies.

The predicted interactions between EMB-high ARHGDIB+ malignant epithelial cells and monocytic/macrophage-related immune populations should be considered hypothesis-generating. CellPhoneDB infers potential communication based on ligand-receptor co-expression and does not demonstrate physical interaction, signaling activity, or causal regulation. Therefore, although the predicted CCL2–CCR2, CSF1–CSF1R, CD47–SIRPA, CD24–SIGLEC10, HLA-G–LILRB2, TGF-β-related, and MIF–CD74 interactions suggest possible tumor–myeloid communication patterns, direct validation will require tumor–macrophage co-culture systems, macrophage polarization assays, cytokine profiling, spatial validation, and in vivo studies.

This study has several limitations. First, the scRNA-seq atlas was generated by retrospectively integrating public datasets; although batch correction was applied, residual cohort- or platform-related effects may remain. In addition, malignant epithelial cells were inferred from transcriptomic CNV patterns rather than validated using matched genomic data. Second, the TCGA-based prognostic, immune infiltration, TIDE, and mutation analyses were performed using bulk data and therefore should be interpreted as tumor-level associations with the CSL score rather than cell-state-specific mechanisms of ARHGDIB⁺ malignant epithelial cells. Third, CellPhoneDB analysis provides ligand–receptor predictions based on transcriptomic co-expression and does not establish physical interaction or causal signaling. Thus, the inferred EMB-high tumor–monocytic/macrophage communication requires validation using co-culture systems, macrophage polarization assays, cytokine profiling, spatial approaches, or in vivo models. Finally, although two independent shRNAs were used and RT-qPCR confirmed EMB knockdown efficiency, rescue, overexpression, and in vivo experiments were not performed. Therefore, the causal role of EMB and its potential regulation of downstream invasion-related programs, including EMT, MMP1/MMP2 activity, TGF-β signaling, invadopodia formation, biomechanical invasion, and metastatic niche remodeling, remain to be determined.

Conclusions

By integrating scRNA-seq datasets across four cohorts, we delineated a GC single-cell atlas and identified an ARHGDIB+ low-differentiation malignant epithelial state. Using the top marker genes of this state as a biologically informed candidate pool, we derived a seven-gene CSL signature with patient-level prognostic relevance in TCGA-STAD. We further prioritized EMB as a candidate prognostic hub gene and showed that EMB knockdown reduced gastric cancer cell migration and invasion. Inferred EMB-high tumor–monocytic/macrophage communication patterns suggest a potential association between EMB-high malignant states and macrophage-associated immune features, but these mechanisms require further experimental validation.
