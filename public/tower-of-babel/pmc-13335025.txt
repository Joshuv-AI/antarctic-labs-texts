Successful treatment by high dose glucocorticoid of miliary tuberculosis complicated with acute respiratory distress syndrome: a case report
Authors: Feishen Lin, Xiaoli Tang, Xianshu Fei, Jing Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 25-year-old previously healthy man presented with a productive cough for 2 months and progressive dyspnea for 1 week. He did not respond to initial anti-infective treatment at a local hospital. Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated diffuse miliary nodules, ground-glass opacities throughout both lungs, and bilateral pleural effusion (more prominent on the right). Laboratory tests revealed type I respiratory failure, elevated inflammatory markers, hyponatremia, hypoproteinemia, and raised tumor markers (NSE, CYFRA21-1, SCC, CA125). Sputum acid-fast bacilli smears were negative, but pleural fluid analysis showed elevated adenosine deaminase and interferon-gamma, suggestive of tuberculous pleurisy. Percutaneous lung biopsy confirmed epithelioid granulomas with positive acid-fast staining, establishing the diagnosis of miliary tuberculosis, tuberculous pleurisy, severe pulmonary infection, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Anti-tuberculosis therapy was withheld initially due to elevated liver enzymes and was initiated once liver function normalized. High-dose methylprednisolone (160 mg/day) combined with noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) was added, yielding initial improvement. However, complications arose after 8 days of NIPPV, including pneumothorax, subcutaneous and mediastinal emphysema, and suspected disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). NIPPV was discontinued, subcutaneous incision and xiphoid puncture drainage performed, and supportive measures (cryoprecipitate, immunoglobulin, continued glucocorticoids) instituted. Symptoms and imaging gradually resolved. After 1 year of anti-tuberculosis treatment, the patient achieved complete clinical and radiological recovery. This case highlights that, alongside potent anti-tuberculosis therapy, high-dose glucocorticoids combined with NIPPV (with cautious management of barotrauma risks) can effectively control life-threatening miliary tuberculosis complicated by ARDS.Keywords: High-dose glucocorticoid therapy, Miliary tuberculosis, ARDS, NIPPV

Introduction

Miliary tuberculosis is a fatal disease that is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which enters the blood flow through a rupture. Circulation can bring disseminated tuberculosis foci in the lung, and even spread to multiple organs. It can be divided into acute, subacute, and chronic miliary tuberculosis. Acute miliary tuberculosis is mainly caused by the short-term entry of a large number of Mycobacterium tuberculosis into the blood circulation, which often occurs in immunocompromised people, but adults without immunocompromised factors can also suffer from it [1]. The onset of acute miliary tuberculosis is rapid, with acute symptoms such as high fever, cough, chest tightness, shortness of breath, and so on. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) rarely occurs [2], with an incidence of 0.15%-10% [3]. However, patients with ARDS will die soon if they are not treated in time [4, 5]. Therefore, early identification and proper treatment can save their lives [6]. In terms of treatment, antituberculosis therapy and life support are the main measures, but the application of glucocorticoids, especially high-dose glucocorticoids, is still controversial. Some studies have shown that glucocorticoid pulse therapy can improve the prognosis of ARDS patients with miliary tuberculosis [7], but the mechanism remains unclear. For patients with acute progression, glucocorticoids may play a role in inhibiting the inflammatory response in addition to anti-tuberculosis therapy, thereby preventing further progression of the disease.

This report describes a previously healthy young adult with miliary tuberculosis complicated by acute respiratory distress syndrome who exhibited rapid clinical deterioration despite initial anti-tuberculosis therapy. The patient demonstrated significant improvement following adjunctive high-dose glucocorticoid therapy combined with noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, although treatment was complicated by barotrauma. This case highlights the potential role and challenges of high-dose glucocorticoids in the management of life-threatening miliary tuberculosis with ARDS and contributes additional clinical evidence to this controversial therapeutic approach.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old man was admitted to our hospital with white phlegm, fever for 2 months, and progressive dyspnea for 1 week. The highest temperature of the patient was 38.5℃, and the treatment for cough and fever was ineffective. A week ago, he had difficulty breathing, and a chest CT at a local hospital revealed exudative lesions in both lungs with bilateral pleural effusion. The patient’s symptoms worsened after 3 days of treatment with cefazoxime sodium, and arterial blood gas analysis showed respiratory failure. Subsequently, the patient was given noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), moxifloxacin, methylprednisolone (40 mg/ day × 3 days), ribavirin, oseltamivir, and other treatments, but the symptoms still did not improve, so he was admitted to the tuberculosis department of our hospital. The patient had no special medical history, and the physical examination displayed that the temperature was 38.1℃, pulse was 146 bpm, breathing was 28 bpm, and blood pressure was 116/91 mmHg. The doctor’s auscultation showed reduced breathing sounds, no heart murmur, and no edema of the lower extremities. Laboratory examinations showed abnormal liver function, lactic dehydrogenase (LDH) and D-dimer were elevated, hyponatremia, hypochloremia, hypoproteinemia, and neuron-specific enolase (NSE), cytokeratin fragment antigen 21 − 1 (CYFRA21-1), squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC); sugar antigen 125 (CA125) were higher than normal levels. Blood gas analysis showed that PH was 7.47, PaO2 was 51 mmHg, and PaCO2 was 25 mmHg, indicating type I respiratory failure. Chest CT upon admission demonstrated diffuse small nodules (miliary pattern) and ground-glass opacities throughout both lungs, along with bilateral pleural effusions (predominantly right-sided) (Fig. 1A and F). While these findings are nonspecific and can overlap with other diffuse parenchymal processes (e.g., viral pneumonia, atypical infections, pulmonary edema, or hypersensitivity pneumonitis), the uniform distribution of nodules, absence of alternative microbiologic evidence, and supportive pleural fluid markers (elevated ADA and IFN-γ) raised strong suspicion for miliary tuberculosis. Percutaneous lung biopsy revealed epithelioid granulomas with central necrosis in some areas, consistent with caseating granulomatous inflammation (Fig. 2A). Acid-fast staining was positive for bacilli (Fig. 2B), confirming the presence of mycobacteria within the granulomatous lesions. These histopathological features necrotizing epithelioid granulomas with acid-fast positivity—are characteristic of pulmonary tuberculosis and were pivotal in establishing the diagnosis of miliary tuberculosis, distinguishing it from noninfectious granulomatous diseases (e.g., sarcoidosis) or other infections lacking AFB positivity.

Lung puncture biopsy and acid-fast staining results.: (A) A few granulomatous changes could be seen in the lung puncture pathology.: (B) Acid-fast staining results, and the arrow in Figure B showed positive acid-fast staining

After admission, the patient underwent sputum acid-fast staining three times, and the results were negative. The results of thoracic drainage showed adenosine deaminase was 50.1 U/L (normal pleural fluid < 40 U/L), the interferon-gamma was 555 ng/L (normal pleural fluid < 140 ng/L), and total protein was 49 g/L, suggesting that the patient may have tuberculous pleuritis. A lung puncture biopsy showed epithelioid granuloma lesions, and acid-fast staining showed (+) (Fig. 2A and B).

Due to significant elevation of aminotransferases after admission, all anti-tuberculosis drugs were temporarily withheld because of concern for drug-induced hepatotoxicity. During this period, the patient received only liver-protective therapy, electrolyte supplementation, and nutritional support. After approximately 7 days of supportive management, liver function tests returned to near-normal levels, and standard anti-tuberculosis therapy with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol was promptly initiated (H: 0.3 g qd; R: 0.45 g qd; Z: 0.5 g tid; E: 0.75 g qd). Combination therapy was resumed to minimize the risk of drug resistance and ensure appropriate treatment of suspected miliary tuberculosis. After 13 days, the patient experienced sudden chest tightness, low breathing sounds in the left lung, and moist crackles in the right lung. Peripheral oxygen saturation was 82%, and blood gas analysis results indicated that PaO2 was 47.0 mmHg, and lactic acid was 2.00 mmol/L, indicating respiratory alkalosis. Besides, the blood neutrophil ratio was 0.88 (reference range: 0.40–0.75), potassium was 3.0 mmol/L, sodium was 131.0 mmol/L, chlorine was 99.0 mmol/L, LDH was 3066 U/L (normal range: 120–250 U/L), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) was 126.00 pg/mL (normal range: <100 pg/mL), serum troponin I was 0.010 ng/mL (normal range: <0.04 ng/mL), and D-dimer was 1.6 mg/L (normal range: <0.5 mg/L). The chest radiograph showed diffuse consolidation of both lungs and a significantly enlarged heart. The patient was immediately given a mask, oxygen, and enhanced antimicrobial therapy with imipenem-cilastatin and amikacin.

Chest radiography demonstrated new diffuse bilateral pulmonary consolidation, consistent with progression to ARDS, along with apparent cardiomegaly. Although secondary pulmonary infection could not be completely excluded, the absence of focal lobar consolidation and the rapid bilateral radiographic progression favored ARDS secondary to severe miliary tuberculosis rather than new-onset pneumonia. The enlarged cardiac silhouette was considered most likely related to acute volume overload and hypoalbuminemia-associated fluid redistribution rather than intrinsic cardiac dysfunction. The patient was treated with oxygen therapy, short-term broad-spectrum antimicrobials, and supportive management, including fluid restriction, diuretics, and albumin supplementation. Following these interventions, high-dose methylprednisolone (160 mg/day) and noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation were initiated, resulting in gradual improvement in oxygenation and clinical symptoms. Despite these measures, hypoxemia persisted, necessitating escalation to high-dose methylprednisolone and noninvasive positive pressure ventilation. After controlling infusion volume, appropriate diuresis, electrolyte, and albumin supplementation, the patient still had dyspnea and hypoxemia. Subsequently, the patient was given glucocorticoid (methylprednisolone 160 mg/day) and NIPPV, and the patient’s symptoms improved. However, after 8 days of intermittent use of NIPPV, the patient again experienced increased dyspnea, and chest CT examination revealed mediastinal emphysema and subcutaneous emphysema (Figs. 3A and 4B).

Adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy consisted of intravenous methylprednisolone administered at a dose of 160 mg/day, corresponding to approximately 2.5 mg/kg/day based on the patient’s body weight. This regimen was initiated in response to refractory hypoxemia and radiological progression consistent with ARDS despite optimized anti-tuberculosis therapy and supportive care. The dose was classified as high-dose glucocorticoid therapy according to commonly used definitions in ARDS and severe inflammatory lung injury, where methylprednisolone doses ≥ 1–2 mg/kg/day or pulse-equivalent regimens have been described. High-dose methylprednisolone was maintained for 10 days, followed by gradual tapering based on clinical improvement, oxygenation parameters, and radiological resolution to minimize the risk of rebound inflammation. The use of adjunctive glucocorticoids was guided by prior reports and observational studies suggesting potential benefit in miliary tuberculosis complicated by ARDS through modulation of excessive inflammatory responses, rather than direct antimycobacterial effects. The patient developed recurrent dyspnea accompanied by extensive subcutaneous emphysema and progressive mediastinal emphysema on chest computed tomography. Although no overt hemodynamic instability or sustained hypotension was documented at that time, the patient exhibited worsening respiratory distress with increasing intrathoracic pressure, raising concern for impending ventilatory compromise and potential tension physiology. Given the rapid radiological progression and clinical deterioration, noninvasive ventilation was discontinued. As a preventive decompressive measure, subcutaneous incision combined with puncture below the xiphoid process was performed to evacuate mediastinal air and reduce intrathoracic pressure. Following decompression, the patient received supportive management including high-dose methylprednisolone, cryoprecipitate, and immunoglobulin therapy. Subsequently, respiratory symptoms gradually improved, laboratory abnormalities stabilized, and follow-up imaging demonstrated progressive resolution of mediastinal and subcutaneous emphysema. At the same time, platelet count and fibrinogen were decreased. Considering that pressure injury and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) could not be ruled out, the patient discontinued NIPPV and continued with mask oxygen, and subcutaneous incision and puncture below the xiphoid process for drainage were performed. Meanwhile, the patient was infused with cryoprecipitation and globulin and treated with methylprednisolone (160 mg/ day) for 10 days. As a result, the patient’s platelet counts and fibrinogen increased gradually, and the patient’s symptoms gradually improved, and chest CT indicated obvious absorption of the lesion. After discharge, the patient continued to receive anti-tuberculosis treatment and local follow-up. Sputum acid-fast staining and culture were negative. One year after discharge, the patient was cured and stopped the anti-tuberculosis therapy (Fig. 4A-F).

Discussion

Miliary tuberculosis is an acute tuberculosis infection that develops from primary tuberculosis [9]. It is caused by the rapid entry of Mycobacterium tuberculosis into the bloodstream and may also be caused by cheesy lesions that enter the bloodstream, and is common in children, the elderly, and immunosuppressed patients. Its imaging manifestation is relatively uniform in distribution, size, and chronological order, like millet [10]. It can cause ARDS, but it often occurs in immune-impaired people, which is a rare cause of ARDS [11].

In our study, this patient was a young male with normal immune function and no latent disease. The onset of the disease was “productive cough”, followed by fever, and rapidly progressed to dyspnea in the later stage. The patient was diagnosed with miliary tuberculosis, tuberculous pleurisy, severe pulmonary infection, and ARDS according to laboratory examinations and lung puncture biopsy.

The radiological diagnosis of miliary tuberculosis relies on the classic pattern of uniformly distributed 1–3 mm nodules on HRCT, often with associated ground-glass opacities in advanced or complicated cases. However, these features can be nonspecific, particularly when ground-glass predominates or nodules are ill-defined, leading to overlap with conditions such as viral pneumonias, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, or hypersensitivity pneumonitis. In our patient, the definitive diagnosis rested on integrative evidence: clinical progression, pleural fluid biochemistry suggestive of tuberculous pleurisy, and crucially, lung biopsy showing epithelioid granulomas with positive acid-fast staining hallmarks of Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection that exclude most noninfectious differentials. This underscores the importance of invasive tissue confirmation in atypical or severe presentations of disseminated TB.

Miliary tuberculosis combined with ARDS is one of the fatal diseases of tuberculosis patients [12]. Early anti-tuberculosis therapy plays an important role in the prognosis of patients with miliary tuberculosis combined with ARDS [13]. In our study, the patient developed respiratory alkalosis 13 days after anti-tuberculosis therapy. The acute deterioration observed after initiation of anti-tuberculosis therapy was considered to represent progression of ARDS rather than a distinct episode of bacterial pneumonia. This interpretation was supported by the diffuse bilateral pattern of pulmonary consolidation, rapid onset of hypoxemia, and elevated inflammatory markers. In addition, the transient cardiomegaly observed on chest radiography was most likely related to volume overload and hypoalbuminemia during critical illness, rather than primary cardiac pathology. Similar findings have been reported in severe systemic infections and ARDS, where fluid redistribution and capillary leak contribute to transient cardiac silhouette enlargement. The patient was immediately given mask oxygen and enhanced antimicrobial therapy, but the patient still had dyspnea and hypoxemia. Studies have shown that the timely use of glucocorticoids has achieved positive effects in patients with miliary pulmonary tuberculosis complicated with respiratory failure [7, 14]. High-dose glucocorticoids were administered as adjunctive therapy to mitigate excessive inflammatory lung injury in the setting of ARDS, rather than as a substitute for definitive anti-tuberculosis treatment.

However, after 8 days of intermittent use of NIPPV, the patient again experienced increased dyspnea. Subsequently, the patient discontinued NIPPV and continued with mask oxygen, and subcutaneous incision and puncture below the xiphoid process for drainage were performed. Meanwhile, the patient was infused with cryoprecipitation and globulin and treated with methylprednisolone (160 mg/ day) for 10 days. As a result, the patient’s symptoms gradually improved.

The patient completed a total of 12 months of anti-tuberculosis therapy (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide, with appropriate dose adjustments and monitoring for hepatotoxicity). Although standard guidelines for drug-susceptible miliary/disseminated tuberculosis without central nervous system involvement recommend a 6-month regimen [15], prolongation to 12 months was elected in this case due to the exceptional severity of presentation including ARDS with refractory hypoxemia, barotrauma complications (pneumothorax, mediastinal/subcutaneous emphysema), suspected disseminated intravascular coagulation, and tuberculous pleurisy with persistent bilateral effusions. Serial imaging demonstrated gradual but delayed resolution of consolidations, cystic lesions, and residual fibrotic bands over 6–12 months, with no evidence of relapse or resistance on follow-up sputum examinations. Extended therapy was continued to ensure microbiological and clinical cure in the context of life-threatening complications and slow response, consistent with individualized extensions reported in severe miliary TB cases with ARDS or multiorgan involvement [1, 6].

TB-associated ARDS, particularly in miliary dissemination, carries high mortality (often 50–70% in critically ill cohorts), driven by overwhelming inflammation, diffuse alveolar damage, and multiorgan failure [13, 16]. Early anti-TB therapy remains cornerstone treatment, but adjunctive strategies to modulate inflammation are debated. High-dose glucocorticoids have shown promise in select severe cases, with case series and retrospective analyses reporting improved short-term oxygenation, reduced inflammatory markers, and lower 90-day mortality in critically ill pulmonary TB patients with acute respiratory failure [3, 4, 7, 17]. For miliary TB complicated by ARDS specifically, steroid pulse or high-dose regimens (e.g., methylprednisolone equivalents) have been linked to better prognosis through suppression of cytokine storm and prevention of further lung injury [18].

However, systematic reviews and meta-analyses of adjunctive corticosteroids in pulmonary or disseminated TB (excluding meningeal/pericardial forms with established benefit) frequently demonstrate no significant overall mortality reduction, with short-term clinical/radiographic gains often not sustained long-term [19, 20]. Evidence quality remains low to moderate, limited by observational designs, heterogeneity, and small sample sizes in ARDS-specific subgroups. Risks of high-dose glucocorticoids in active TB are substantial and include increased susceptibility to secondary bacterial/fungal infections, potential exacerbation of dissemination (e.g., progression of cryptic miliary TB to overt ARDS), delayed mycobacterial clearance, and immunosuppression-related complications such as opportunistic infections or hyperglycemia [2, 21]. These concerns are particularly relevant in immunocompetent patients with high mycobacterial burden, as in our case.

NIPPV was initially beneficial in our patient for managing hypoxemia and avoiding immediate intubation, consistent with its role in selected ARDS cases to reduce ventilator-associated complications [22]. However, prolonged or intermittent use contributed to barotrauma (pneumothorax, mediastinal/subcutaneous emphysema), likely exacerbated by underlying lung fragility from miliary nodules and cystic changes. This necessitated prompt discontinuation and drainage, highlighting the need for cautious application in TB-related ARDS, where lung compliance is reduced and barotrauma risk elevated compared to non-infectious ARDS [23]. In our patient, clinical and radiological improvement temporally coincided with high-dose methylprednisolone initiation alongside anti-TB therapy and supportive adjustments, aligning with reports of short-term benefit in severe inflammatory TB-ARDS. Nonetheless, this single-case observation does not prove causality, as multifactorial contributions (e.g., optimized anti-TB regimen, NIPPV weaning, drainage, nutritional support) cannot be disentangled. Larger prospective studies or RCTs are essential to clarify efficacy, optimal dosing, timing, and risk mitigation for adjunctive high-dose glucocorticoids in this setting. In select cases of severe miliary TB with ARDS, high-dose glucocorticoids (e.g., methylprednisolone pulse or equivalent) have been associated with short-term benefits, such as improved oxygenation, reduced inflammatory burden, and better survival in small series [7, 24]. For instance, steroid pulse therapy has shown potential to control overwhelming inflammation and improve prognosis in miliary TB-ARDS [7]. Similarly, some propensity-adjusted analyses in critically ill pulmonary TB patients with acute respiratory failure suggest adjunctive corticosteroids may reduce mortality in severe cases [17].

The role of adjunctive corticosteroids, particularly high-dose regimens, in miliary tuberculosis complicated by ARDS remains controversial. Limited evidence from case series and observational studies suggests that high-dose or pulse corticosteroid therapy may improve short-term outcomes, including oxygenation and survival, by attenuating excessive inflammatory responses in severe disease. However, broader evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses in pulmonary and disseminated tuberculosis has not consistently demonstrated a significant mortality benefit, and long-term outcomes remain uncertain. In addition, corticosteroid use carries potential risks, including secondary infections, delayed mycobacterial clearance, and immunosuppression-related complications. In this context, the clinical improvement observed in our patient following high-dose methylprednisolone likely reflects a combination of anti-inflammatory effects and comprehensive supportive care rather than a direct causal effect. Therefore, adjunctive corticosteroids may be considered on an individualized basis in life-threatening miliary TB with ARDS, particularly when hyperinflammation predominates, while carefully balancing potential benefits against associated risks [19, 20]. Routine use does not consistently reduce mortality in pulmonary TB [19], and in some forms (e.g., pericardial or pleural TB), adjunctive steroids fail to show survival advantages [25]. Potential risks include exacerbation of dissemination in cryptic or occult miliary TB, increased susceptibility to secondary infections, immunosuppression-related complications, delayed microbiological clearance, or barotrauma amplification in ventilated patients [2, 26]. High-dose regimens carry additional concerns such as hyperglycemia, gastrointestinal bleeding, and opportunistic infections [27].

In this context, the temporal association of clinical and radiological improvement with high-dose methylprednisolone in our patient (combined with potent anti-TB therapy and supportive care) aligns with reports of short-term benefit in severe inflammatory presentations but does not establish causality, given the single-case nature and confounding factors (e.g., NIPPV adjustments, drainage, nutritional support). A balanced interpretation supports individualized use of adjunctive high-dose glucocorticoids in life-threatening miliary TB-ARDS when inflammation predominates, weighed against risks, pending higher-quality evidence from RCTs. This report is limited by its single-patient design and the inability to establish a causal relationship between high-dose glucocorticoid therapy and clinical improvement. Additionally, invasive hemodynamic monitoring was not performed, and secondary infection could not be completely excluded during acute deterioration. Nevertheless, this case provides valuable insight into the multidisciplinary management of life-threatening miliary tuberculosis complicated by ARDS.

The clinical course is presented chronologically to clarify diagnostic reasoning, therapeutic decision-making, and outcomes. Although a temporal association was observed between adjunctive high-dose glucocorticoid therapy and clinical improvement, a causal relationship cannot be established due to the single-patient nature of this report. High-dose glucocorticoids were administered as adjunctive therapy to attenuate excessive inflammatory lung injury in the setting of ARDS, rather than as a substitute for definitive anti-tuberculosis treatment. Secondary infection during clinical deterioration could not be entirely excluded; however, the overall clinical, laboratory, and radiological features supported ARDS progression associated with severe miliary tuberculosis. This case highlights practical challenges in managing life-threatening tuberculosis complicated by respiratory failure while acknowledging inherent methodological limitations.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates that miliary tuberculosis complicated by ARDS can be successfully managed with potent anti-tuberculosis therapy combined with high-dose glucocorticoids (methylprednisolone 160 mg/day) and cautious NIPPV. Despite initial deterioration, complications including pneumothorax, barotrauma-related emphysema, and suspected disseminated intravascular coagulation, timely discontinuation of NIPPV, surgical drainage, supportive care, and continued glucocorticoids led to progressive radiological and clinical resolution. The patient achieved complete recovery after 1 year of treatment. These findings support adjunctive high-dose glucocorticoids in controlling overwhelming inflammation in severe miliary TB-ARDS, improving outcomes in selected cases when balanced against risks such as barotrauma.
