A case report of femorocele mimicking a spermatic hydrocele in a male patient: the first laparoscopic repair
Authors: Koki Miya, Fumitaka Yagi, Takeshi Kato, Takashi Saitoh, Katsuhiko Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Femorocele, defined as fluid accumulation within a femoral hernia sac, is an extremely rare condition; to our knowledge, previously reported cases have predominantly occurred in female patients, and laparoscopic management has rarely been described.Case presentation:A 75-year-old man presented with a gradually enlarging, painless swelling in the left groin. Computed tomography revealed a cystic lesion that was initially diagnosed as a spermatic hydrocele. In response to rapid enlargement and onset of pain, elective laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair was performed. Intraoperatively, the hernial orifice corresponded to the femoral ring, and the neck of the hernial sac was constricted by thickened preperitoneal fat with minimal communication with the peritoneal cavity. The sac contained only clear fluid without bowel or omentum, leading to a diagnosis of a primary femorocele. Mesh repair was successfully completed; the postoperative course was uneventful.Discussion:Given their rarity and nonspecific imaging findings, femoroceles are frequently misdiagnosed preoperatively. To our knowledge, this appears to be the first reported case of a femorocele being treated laparoscopically. Laparoscopic TAPP repair provides excellent visualization of the femoral ring and surrounding anatomical structures, thereby enabling both accurate intraoperative diagnosis and definitive treatment. Compared with open or robotic approaches, laparoscopic repair is widely available, cost-effective, and particularly suitable for use in community hospital settings.Conclusion:Femoroceles can occur in male patients and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of cystic groin masses. Laparoscopic TAPP repair demonstrates the feasibility that offers combined diagnostic and therapeutic advantages for this rare condition.

Introduction

Femoral hernias originate from the femoral canal below the inguinal ligament and constitute approximately 5% of all groin hernias. The accumulation of fluid within a femoral hernia sac, referred to as a “femorocele,” is an extremely rare condition, with only 17 reported cases to date (Table 1)[2–15], all occurring in female patients. Treatment mainly consists of open repair, although more recently a single case of robot-assisted repair has been described. To the best of our knowledge, however, no cases of laparoscopic treatment of this condition have been reported[2–15]. Herein, we report the occurrence of a femorocele in a male patient who was initially misdiagnosed as having a spermatic hydrocele and underwent successful laparoscopic transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair. Presentation of the case

The patient was a 75-year-old man who presented to our department with a painless swelling in the left groin that had gradually enlarged over the previous 3 years. He had a medical history that included well-controlled hypertension and prior brachytherapy, but no diabetes, cardiac disease, or hepatic disease, and he had never undergone abdominal surgery.

On initial examination, a well-defined, elastic, soft mass measuring approximately 3 cm in diameter was palpable in the left groin. The surface was smooth, with no tenderness, skin changes, or erythema, although the mass was not readily reducible. The patient’s general condition and vital signs were stable; laboratory investigations revealed normal levels of white blood cells and C-reactive protein.

Noncontrast abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a cystic lesion (40 mm × 40 mm) in the left groin, which was initially interpreted as a spermatic hydrocele (Fig. 1). Ultrasonography showed similar findings, and the ultrasound report also described the lesion as a spermatic hydrocele. Because both CT and ultrasonographic findings were interpreted as consistent with a spermatic hydrocele and no signs of bowel incarceration or ascites were observed, conservative management with outpatient observation was selected at that time. In retrospect, careful assessment of the anatomical relationship between the lesion and the femoral vessels might have suggested the possibility of a femoral hernia. Three months later, however, the patient revisited our department with complaints of progressive swelling and pain in the same area. During the intervening period, the mass had enlarged to approximately 8 cm in diameter and had become slightly firmer and more tender. Given the appreciable increase in size compared with the previous examination, an incarcerated inguinal hernia was suspected. However, manual reduction was successful, reducing the swelling, and the patient’s symptoms subsequently improved. Thereafter, he was admitted for observation, and elective laparoscopic TAPP hernia repair was performed 2 weeks later.

The operation was performed under general anesthesia at a community hospital by a senior surgeon experienced in laparoscopic hernia repair. The laparoscopic TAPP procedure was performed using a standard three-port technique. To access the preperitoneal space, the peritoneum was incised anterior to the hernia orifice, and the hernia sac was carefully dissected and reduced by gentle traction, thereby ensuring complete separation from the surrounding preperitoneal fat and femoral vessels. The preperitoneal space was then fully developed, and a BARD 3D Max Light Mesh size M (C. R. Bard, Inc., Murray Hill, NJ, USA) was placed to cover the femoral ring and the entire myopectineal orifice. The mesh was fixed using a BARD Capsure (C. R. Bard, Inc., Murray Hill, NJ, USA) with six fixation points. Hemostasis was confirmed, and the peritoneum was closed with continuous absorbable sutures. The operative time was 106 minutes, and estimated blood loss was minimal. Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis with cefazolin was administered, and thromboembolism prophylaxis consisted of intermittent pneumatic compression and subcutaneous heparin.

Intraoperatively, the hernial orifice corresponded to the femoral ring. The neck of the hernial sac was constricted by thickened preperitoneal fat, and communication with the peritoneal cavity was minimal. Although the sac contained no bowel or omentum, it was filled with clear fluid, which was confined within the sac; no free ascitic fluid was observed in the peritoneal cavity. On the basis of these contents and anatomical findings, the lesion was diagnosed as a primary femorocele, representing an accumulation of fluid within the femoral hernial sac (Fig. 2). The postoperative course was uneventful (Clavien–Dindo grade 0). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 3. During the 6-month follow-up period, no clinical recurrence was identified on outpatient follow-up, and no additional treatment was required.

Discussion

Femorocele is an extremely rare condition. A literature search was conducted in PubMed using the keywords “femorocele” and “femoral hydrocele.” The final search was performed on 15 March 2026, and no restrictions were applied regarding publication period or language. A total of 39 articles were initially identified. After reviewing the titles and abstracts, six publications describing femorocele were selected. In addition, the reference lists of these articles were examined to identify further relevant reports. As a result, a total of 17 cases of femorocele reported in 14 articles were identified in the literature (Table 1)[2–15]. To the best of our knowledge, this case may represent the first reported male femorocele treated laparoscopically in the available literature. Femoroceles can be classified into either primary or secondary types. Primary femoroceles are believed to arise from obstructions of the sac neck or entrapment of the omentum, which can lead to isolation of fluid within the sac. The femorocele described herein corresponds to this type. In contrast, secondary femoroceles occur when ascitic fluid associated with conditions such as cirrhosis or heart failure enters the femoral hernia sac[13]. A review of previously reported cases reveals several characteristic features. All previously reported cases occurred in female patients, whereas the present case represents the first reported male case. Reported lesions tended to occur more frequently on the right side. These findings may be consistent with the general epidemiological characteristics of femoral hernias, which are known to occur more commonly in women and often on the right side[1]. Only a minority of cases were associated with ascites, suggesting that primary femorocele is more common than the secondary form associated with ascites. Regarding surgical management, most cases were treated using open repair techniques, including sac ligation and suture repair, whereas minimally invasive approaches have rarely been reported. These findings highlight the rarity of this condition and support the feasibility of laparoscopic management in selected cases.

Clinically, femoroceles present with groin swelling, although an accurate preoperative diagnosis is rare; even in cases in which these are depicted as cystic lesions on ultrasonography or CT, they are often misdiagnosed as a hydrocele of the canal of Nuck or a spermatic hydrocele[14]. To date, only two cases have been reported in which this condition has been correctly diagnosed preoperatively[2,14], whereas most have been identified intraoperatively, thereby highlighting the diagnostic difficulty. Generally, femoral hernias have narrow necks and are prone to obstruction or strangulation[1], and, consequently, recognizing femoroceles as a possible differential diagnosis and performing detailed imaging evaluations are essential for preventing incarceration. In two previous cases[2,14], preoperative diagnoses were based on CT findings, emphasizing their diagnostic utility. In this regard, Vagholkar has highlighted five key CT findings considered important for diagnosis: (1) location of the sac, (2) relation to femoral vessels, (3) extent of the sac, (4) nature of sac contents, and (5) presence or absence of intraperitoneal ascites[14]. In the present case, although the lesion was preoperatively interpreted as a spermatic hydrocele, careful intraoperative evaluation of the anatomical relationship between the hernial sac and femoral vessels might have suggested its femoral origin. Dagur et al[17] have also noted that hydroceles of the pelvis and groin represent a heterogeneous group of lesions and that appropriate classification and differential diagnosis are essential.

Historically, the treatment of femoroceles has involved high ligation of the sac and closure of the femoral ring, and, owing to the widespread adoption of mesh, open-mesh repair has become the standard approach. More recently, robot-assisted repair has been reported to have favorable outcomes in terms of safety and efficacy[2]. Kafadar et al reported that laparoscopic TAPP repair can be safely applied even in complicated femoral hernia cases, providing good visualization of the incarcerated bowel and allowing safe repair[1]. Laparoscopic surgery provides magnified visualization, thereby enabling the precise identification of anatomical structures, which, in common with robotic surgery, has the combined advantages of accurate diagnosis and safe treatment. Moreover, compared with robotic surgery, laparoscopic repair has certain practical advantages, including lower equipment and operating costs, wider availability in regional or community hospitals, and shorter setup time, making it a more accessible minimally invasive option. In the present case, although the lesion was initially misdiagnosed as a spermatic hydrocele, laparoscopic TAPP repair enabled a definitive diagnosis and successful repair.

This report has several limitations, including its single-case design and the relatively short follow-up period.

Conclusion

We report a case of groin swelling initially diagnosed as a spermatic hydrocele, which was intraoperatively identified as a primary femorocele and successfully treated using laparoscopic TAPP repair. This case highlights that femoroceles can also occur in male patients and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of cystic groin lesions. Although preoperative diagnosis may be challenging, careful evaluation of CT findings and anatomical relationships can aid in diagnosis. This case suggests that laparoscopic TAPP repair may be a feasible, minimally invasive option with diagnostic and therapeutic advantages in selected cases.
