Exploring barriers to mental health care access among rural adolescents in Gondar Zuria, Ethiopia: a qualitative study
Authors: Assefa Kebad Mengesha, Habtamu Semagne Ayele, Alemante Tafese Beyna, Solomon Asmamaw Tadesse, Demis Getachew, Melshew Fenta Misker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health issues are increasingly prevalent among adolescents, particularly in rural areas of low-income countries where resources are limited and cultural norms often discourage help-seeking. This study explored barriers to accessing mental health care among rural adolescents in Gondar Zuria, Ethiopia.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study design was used. Purposive sampling ensured variation across gender, residence, school attendance, and socioeconomic background. A total of 18 adolescents and 6 key informants participated in individual interviews, and 3 focus group discussions were conducted for validation. Data were collected using semi-structured guides and analyzed through inductive thematic analysis.ResultsFour major themes were identified: (1) limited awareness and misconceptions about mental health, (2) family-level barriers including low parental support and financial constraints, (3) community and societal barriers such as stigma and reliance on traditional practices, and (4) health system barriers including lack of services and inadequate training of health workers. These multi-level factors collectively constrained adolescents’ access to mental health care.ConclusionsRural adolescents in Gondar Zuria face substantial structural, socio-cultural, and individual-level barriers to mental health care. Policy-makers should prioritize integration of adolescent mental health services into schools and primary health care, strengthen the capacity of health workers, and implement culturally sensitive awareness and anti-stigma initiatives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25699-1.Keywords: Barriers to care, Ethiopia, Mental health access, Qualitative study, Rural adolescents, Adolescent health, Rural health

Introduction

Adolescence is a critical developmental period marked by profound physical, psychological, and social transitions. Globally, mental health disorders account for approximately 16% of the total burden of disease and injury among individuals aged 10–19 years, and an estimated 70% of lifelong mental disorders begin during this stage [1, 2]. Despite this high burden, adolescents often face significant barriers in accessing mental health services, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where resources are limited and cultural norms frequently discourage help-seeking [3, 4].

In sub-Saharan Africa, evidence indicates that adolescent mental health needs remain largely unmet due to low awareness, stigma, and a shortage of trained professionals [5, 6]. In Ethiopia, depression, anxiety, and psychological distress have been reported among 22–34% of adolescents [7, 8]. However, service utilization is extremely low. For instance, a recent study in southern Ethiopia found that only 6% of adolescents with depressive symptoms accessed any form of mental health care [9]. Multiple contextual and structural factors contribute to this treatment gap, including limited availability of services outside major cities, long travel distances to referral hospitals, cultural attribution of mental illness to spiritual causes, and low levels of mental health literacy within families and communities [10–12].

Most studies in Ethiopia have concentrated on the prevalence and associated factors of adolescent mental health problems [7–9]. In contrast, relatively few qualitative inquiries have examined barriers to accessing services among adolescents in rural areas. Understanding adolescents’ perspectives is essential for designing culturally appropriate and context-specific interventions to improve access. Gondar Zuria Woreda, a predominantly rural district in the Central Gondar Zone, has no dedicated mental health services at the primary health care level. Adolescents in this setting may face unique barriers due to geographic isolation, limited mobility, and socio-cultural norms. However, to date, no qualitative study has explored adolescents’ experiences and perceived barriers to mental health care access in this region.

The objective of this study was therefore to explore the barriers to mental health care access among rural adolescents in Gondar Zuria, Ethiopia. Specifically, the study sought to answer the research question: What structural, socio-cultural, and individual-level factors hinder rural adolescents in Gondar Zuria from accessing mental health care?

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a descriptive qualitative study to explore the barriers and facilitators to adolescent mental health care in Gondar Zuria Woreda, located in the Central Gondar Zone of Ethiopia. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) to ensure transparency and rigor in the reporting process [13].

Gondar Zuria is a predominantly rural Woreda with limited access to specialized mental health services; consequently, primary health care centers and community health extension workers (HEWs) serve as the main providers of mental health care in the area. Data collection was conducted in the Woreda between March and May 2024.

Study population and inclusion criteria

Study population and eligibility

Adolescents aged 15–19 years who had lived in Gondar Zuria for at least six months were eligible. Adolescents with severe physical illnesses that prevented participation were excluded. For participants under 18 years, written parental/guardian consent and adolescent assent were obtained. In addition to adolescents, we purposively recruited six key informants from three stakeholder groups: HEWs (n = 2), school teachers trained in student counselling (n = 2), and local administrators responsible for social and health affairs (n = 2). These key informants were selected because of their roles in adolescent welfare and mental-health–related activities in the Woreda.

Sampling strategy and recruitment procedures

A purposive, maximum-variation sampling approach was employed to capture diverse adolescent perspectives across gender, place of residence, school attendance status, and socioeconomic background. Recruitment was conducted in close collaboration with health extension workers (HEWs), school administrators, and community leaders to ensure representation from different segments of the community.

HEWs from all 43 kebeles were first asked to prepare shortlists of adolescents who met the study’s eligibility criteria and could speak knowledgeably about mental health issues. These shortlists served solely as a means of facilitating contact and did not determine final inclusion. The research team made the final purposive selections based on predefined stratification criteria.

The stratification criteria included: (1) gender (male or female), (2) place of residence (urban vs. rural kebele), (3) school attendance status (currently enrolled vs. out of school), and (4) perceived socioeconomic background (low, middle, or high), as assessed collaboratively by HEWs and community leaders. From each sub-stratum, two to three adolescents were purposively invited to participate, with selection prioritizing both willingness to participate and heterogeneity of lived experience—for instance, differences in health-seeking behaviors or household conditions. Community leaders and school directors assisted in introducing participants and contacting out-of-school adolescents, while eligibility was verified during the initial point of contact.

In addition to adolescents, key informants were purposively selected by the research team in consultation with local authorities based on their roles, professional experience, and capacity to provide system-level insights into adolescent mental health care.

Sample size determination and operationalization of saturation

An iterative recruitment process was employed, guided by the principle of theoretical saturation [14]. The initial plan was to begin with 15 adolescent interviews and 4 key informant interviews, with emerging codes reviewed after every three interviews to monitor thematic development. Throughout the analysis, the research team continuously assessed the data, defining saturation operationally as the point at which two consecutive interviews yielded no new codes or themes.

Based on this criterion, saturation was achieved after 18 adolescent interviews and 6 key informant interviews. To further triangulate and validate the findings, three focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with adolescents. Each FGD included 5–7 participants (total FGD participants = 17), recruited from the same stratified pool but not overlapping with individual interview participants. The FGDs served to confirm and elaborate on existing categories; since they did not generate any novel themes, no additional data collection was deemed necessary.

In total, the study included 24 individual interviews (18 adolescents and 6 key informants) and 3 FGDs (17 adolescents), yielding an overall sample of 41 participants.

Data collection instruments and procedures

The interview and focus group discussion (FGD) guides were developed based on the World Health Organization’s Social Determinants of Health Framework and existing qualitative literature on adolescent mental health with minor modification [15–20]. The semi-structured guides, initially drafted in English, explored four main domains: (1) knowledge and perceptions of mental health, (2) perceived need for mental health services, (3) barriers to care at the individual, family, community, and health-system levels, and (4) recommendations to improve service access. Example prompts included: “Can you describe any challenges you or your peers have faced when trying to get mental health support?” and “What would make it easier for adolescents in your community to seek help?”

The guides were pilot-tested with three adolescents from a neighboring Woreda to evaluate clarity, comprehension, and cultural relevance. Minor wording adjustments were made prior to formal data collection. Individual interviews lasted approximately 45–60 min, while FGDs ranged from 60 to 90 min.

All data were collected in Amharic by two experienced qualitative researchers fluent in both Amharic and English. Before commencing fieldwork, the research team participated in a two-day training covering qualitative interviewing techniques, field procedures, informed consent, confidentiality, and reflexivity. Interviews and FGDs were audio-recorded with participants’ permission, and detailed field notes and reflexive memos were prepared immediately after each session to capture contextual insights and researcher reflections.

Focus group composition and conduct

Focus group discussions (FGDs) were organized to encourage open dialogue and minimize participant inhibition. Where appropriate, groups were segregated by gender or school attendance status to maintain homogeneity along dimensions likely to influence comfort and disclosure. Each FGD comprised 5–7 participants and was co-facilitated by a moderator and a note-taker to ensure smooth discussion and accurate documentation. The FGDs followed the same semi-structured guide used for individual interviews to ensure consistency in data collection.

Preliminary findings from the in-depth interviews (IDIs) were summarized and shared during the FGDs to facilitate member checking and invite participants to confirm, clarify, or expand upon emerging themes. This process helped enhance the credibility and depth of the qualitative findings.

Transcription, translation, and data management

All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Amharic by trained transcribers. The resulting transcripts were then translated into English by bilingual experts. To ensure translation accuracy and semantic equivalence, 20% of the transcripts were back-translated into Amharic by an independent bilingual translator. Any discrepancies identified during this process were reviewed and reconciled through discussion among the research team.

All transcripts, field notes, and reflexive memos were securely stored on password-protected computers and encrypted external drives accessible only to authorized study personnel. NVivo 14 software was used to facilitate data organization, coding, and management throughout the analysis process.

Analytic approach and coding procedures

Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun & Clarke’s approach [21–23]. The six analytic phases — (1) familiarization with the data, (2) generation of initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) producing the report — were followed iteratively.

Two researchers independently familiarized themselves with the data and conducted line-by-line coding to capture key concepts and patterns. A codebook was developed iteratively through a combined inductive–deductive approach: inductive codes emerged directly from participants’ narratives, while deductive codes were derived from the WHO Social Determinants of Health Framework [24].

After independently coding an initial subset of transcripts, the coders met regularly to compare, refine, and consolidate codes, clarifying definitions and organizing them into a coherent hierarchical structure. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, and when necessary, a third senior investigator adjudicated unresolved differences.

Throughout the analytic process, reflexive memos were maintained to document analytic decisions, emerging hypotheses, and reflections on potential researcher biases. An audit trail—including successive codebook versions, meeting notes, and coding logs—was preserved to enhance the dependability and transparency of the analysis. NVivo 14 software was used to facilitate code application, data retrieval, and audit-trail management.

Trustworthiness and rigor

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, strategies were implemented in alignment with the four established qualitative criteria: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Credibility was enhanced through methodological triangulation, combining data from in-depth interviews (IDIs), focus group discussions (FGDs), and key informant interviews, as well as through member checking during FGDs, where participants reviewed and elaborated on preliminary findings. Transferability was supported by providing rich, contextual descriptions of the study setting, sampling procedures, and participant characteristics, enabling readers to assess the applicability of findings to other contexts. Dependability was maintained through a comprehensive audit trail that documented analytic decisions, codebook iterations, and meeting notes throughout the research process. Finally, confirmability was strengthened through peer debriefing, the systematic use of reflexive memos, and transparent documentation of coding and analytic procedures, ensuring that findings reflected participants’ perspectives rather than researcher bias.

Results

A total of 41 participants took part in the study, comprising 24 individual interviews (18 adolescents and 6 key informants) and three focus group discussions (FGDs) involving 17 adolescents. Among the 18 adolescents who participated in individual interviews, 10 were female and 8 were male, representing both urban (n = 7) and rural (n = 11) kebeles. Six adolescents were out of school at the time of data collection. The key informants included two health extension workers, two high school teachers trained in counselling, and two local social affairs officers (see Table 1).

Derivation of themes and analytic transparency

Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework. Two researchers independently coded the English translations of all transcripts. A codebook was developed iteratively using a combined inductive–deductive approach: initial codes were generated inductively from participant narratives and subsequently organized deductively using the WHO Social Determinants of Health Framework as a sensitizing lens.

To ensure coding consistency, 20% of transcripts were double-coded, and any differences were discussed during regular analytic meetings. Unresolved discrepancies were adjudicated by a third senior investigator. The codebook was versioned and archived, and all coding decisions and revisions were documented in an audit log to support transparency and dependability.

Emergent themes were triangulated across individual interviews, FGDs, and key informants. Representative quotations were purposively selected to reflect variation in gender, place of residence, school attendance, and participant type. The final codebook and a coding audit summary are available from the authors upon request.

Overview of themes

Analysis generated four overarching themes and nine sub-themes capturing multi-level barriers to accessing adolescent mental health care: (1) Limited awareness and misconceptions, (2) Family-level barriers, (3) Community and societal barriers, and (4) Health system barriers. Representative quotations are provided alongside contextual participant identifiers (participant ID, age, gender, and place of residence) to enhance transparency and traceability of the findings.

Theme 1: limited awareness and misconceptions

Sub-theme 1.1 — Perceived lack of knowledge

Adolescents frequently expressed limited understanding of mental health and were unsure where to seek care.

“Before this discussion, I never thought anxiety or sadness is a kind of health problem… I thought mental illness meant running on the street.” — IDI_A05; 16-year-old female; rural.

“I don’t know any place in this area that deals with this type of problem. We only have a health post for fever and vaccination.” — FGD1_P03; 17-year-old male; rural FGD participant.

Sub-theme 1.2 — Stigma and cultural misinterpretation

Participants described spiritual and moral explanations for mental illness that discouraged formal help-seeking.

“People in our community believe it is God who can heal these problems. If someone goes to a hospital, they say that the person has no faith.” — IDI_A12; 18-year-old male; rural.

Theme 2: Family-Level barriers

Sub-theme 2.1 — Limited parental support

Adolescents reported that parents often minimized emotional symptoms and prioritized visible physical problems.

“When I said I feel heavy in my heart, my father told me I am becoming lazy and that such thoughts don’t exist.” — IDI_A02; 17-year-old female; urban.

Sub-theme 2.2 — Financial constraints

Costs of transport and treatment were repeatedly cited as barriers to accessing services in Gondar town.

“My mother wanted to take me, but transport is expensive and we have other household needs. So, she told me we will go only if it becomes worse.” — FGD2_P05; 16-year-old male; rural FGD participant.

Theme 3: community and societal barriers

Sub-theme 3.1 — Social norms and fear of disclosure

Fear of ridicule and social exclusion led adolescents to conceal symptoms.

“You cannot tell such things; they will laugh and say you are crazy or weak.” — IDI_A09; 17-year-old female; urban.

Sub-theme 3.2 — Preference for traditional/religious practices

Religious healing and traditional practices were described as the community’s first recourse.

“People first go to the holy water. Only after many days, if nothing changes, they may go to the hospital.” — KI_SA01; Local social affairs officer (key informant).

Theme 4: health system barriers

Sub-theme 4.1 — Low availability and accessibility of services

Participants emphasized a lack of local mental health services and the burden of travel.

“There is no mental health clinic here. If you want help, you must travel to Gondar, which is far and expensive.” — KI_HEW01; Health extension worker (key informant).

Long waiting times at the referral facility were also noted as a deterrent.

Sub-theme 4.2 — Inadequate health worker training

Frontline workers reported limited training and confidence in managing adolescent mental health concerns.

“We only had one session during our training. We don’t know how to advise youths; we usually just tell them to pray or try to relax.” — KI_HEW02; Health extension worker (key informant) (see Table 2).

Discussion

The present study explored barriers to mental health care access among adolescents, identifying structural, socio-cultural, and individual-level factors as key determinants of service utilization. Our findings demonstrated that limited availability of mental health facilities, shortages of trained professionals, and inadequate awareness were the most frequently reported obstacles. These results are broadly consistent with previous studies conducted in other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). For instance, a cross-sectional survey in Kenya found that more than two-thirds of adolescents reported lack of nearby mental health services and inadequate trained personnel as primary barriers to seeking help [25]. Similarly, a community-based study in Nigeria highlighted structural barriers as the most significant impediment to adolescent mental health service access [26].

While these patterns resonate globally, they take on specific dimensions in Ethiopia’s decentralized health system, where mental health remains under-integrated within the primary health care structure. In our study area, health posts and centers—the first points of contact—were primarily focused on communicable diseases and maternal–child health, with no dedicated mental health personnel or referral mechanisms for adolescents. This reflects national challenges, as Ethiopia’s Health Sector Transformation Plan IV (HSTP-IV) still lists mental health as an emerging, cross-cutting issue rather than a core service component. The absence of adolescent-focused mental health programs and limited referral linkages between schools and health facilities exacerbate these gaps, particularly in rural settings [27].

Importantly, our study also found that stigma and cultural beliefs remain major socio-cultural barriers, with more than half of the respondents reporting that fear of being labelled or judged prevented them from seeking mental health care. This concurs with the results of a population-based study in India, in which adolescents endorsed high levels of stigma and fear of social consequences as deterrents to mental health service utilization [28]. The finding is also in line with a qualitative study conducted in Nepal, which demonstrated that cultural norms and traditional beliefs often discourage adolescents from discussing mental health problems or seeking professional help [29]. However, the nature of stigma in Ethiopia is deeply interwoven with religious and communal worldviews. Adolescents in our study frequently described mental distress as a sign of weak faith, demonic possession, or divine punishment. The strong cultural reliance on holy water treatment and prayer, while providing a sense of spiritual support, may inadvertently delay formal help-seeking. Moreover, in rural Amhara communities where collectivist values dominate, conformity and fear of social exclusion strongly influence behavior, reinforcing the silence around mental illness. Addressing stigma, therefore, requires culturally grounded approaches that engage faith leaders and community elders as allies in promoting mental health awareness.

Another notable finding of our study was the low level of knowledge and awareness about mental health issues among adolescents. This is in line with evidence from a nationwide survey in South Africa, which reported that a majority of adolescents were unable to recognize common mental health problems and were unaware of available mental health services [30]. Likewise, a recent study in Ghana found that adolescents with better mental health literacy were more likely to seek help from health professionals [31]. Within Ethiopia, mental health literacy remains low among both adolescents and caregivers, partly due to limited integration of psychosocial education in school curricula and scarce youth-friendly health information platforms [32]. Health extension workers—who serve as the frontline in rural health delivery—reported minimal training on adolescent mental health, often resorting to informal advice such as prayer or relaxation. Expanding their training and including mental health content in the Health Extension Program (HEP) could represent a cost-effective strategy for improving community awareness [33].

The current study also revealed that family support and peer encouragement positively influenced mental health-seeking behavior. This aligns with previous evidence emphasizing the role of social support. A recent study in the United Kingdom reported that adolescents who received emotional encouragement from their peers were more likely to access mental health services [34]. Furthermore, a systematic review involving 22 studies highlighted the importance of supportive family environments in enhancing mental health service use among adolescents [35]. In the Ethiopian context, family attitudes often determine care-seeking, as parents and guardians act as gatekeepers to health decisions. Yet, as our findings showed, many parents dismiss emotional distress as laziness or spiritual weakness. In rural households facing economic strain, financial priorities and transport costs further limit help-seeking even when mental distress is recognized. These intersecting structural and cultural pressures highlight the importance of family-centered mental health education and school-based awareness programs that can reach both adolescents and their caregivers.

Taken together, our findings provide valuable insights for policymakers and health service planners. In particular, interventions aimed at increasing the availability of mental health services in schools and communities, strengthening the workforce through targeted training, and improving mental health literacy among adolescents should be prioritized. Furthermore, culturally sensitive anti-stigma initiatives may play a critical role in addressing socio-cultural barriers and supporting adolescents in seeking help. At the policy level, aligning these strategies with Ethiopia’s National Adolescent and Youth Health Strategy (2021–2025) and the National Mental Health Strategy would enhance sustainability [36]. Integrating adolescent mental health into existing school health programs, leveraging the Health Extension Workers’ community platform, and collaborating with religious institutions and youth associations could provide locally acceptable and sustainable entry points [37]. Future research in Ethiopia should move beyond cross-sectional and descriptive designs to include implementation and intervention studies assessing scalable, culturally adapted models—such as school-based counselling, digital peer-support interventions, and task-shifting to trained lay counsellors within rural communities. Longitudinal research could further illuminate how changing social norms and policy investments shape mental health service access over time.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as it was conducted in a single rural district, the findings may not be generalizable to other regions of Ethiopia or similar LMIC settings. Second, data were based on self-reported experiences, which may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Third, we did not include clinical assessments of adolescents’ mental health status, which limits the ability to link perceived barriers with actual mental health conditions. Fourth, the purposive sampling strategy may have introduced selection bias, as participants willing to discuss mental health issues could differ from those who declined. Finally, although transcripts were translated and back-translated, subtle meanings may have been lost in translation. Despite these limitations, the study provides important context-specific insights into barriers to adolescent mental health care in rural Ethiopia.

Conclusion

Rural adolescents in Gondar Zuria face significant barriers to accessing mental health care, including limited awareness, cultural stigma, and a shortage of trained professionals. Addressing these challenges requires moving beyond general policy calls to concrete strategies. Specifically, integrating adolescent-focused mental health services into existing school health programs and primary health care facilities could improve accessibility. Training health workers in adolescent mental health and deploying community-based support initiatives would further strengthen local capacity. In parallel, culturally sensitive awareness campaigns are needed to reduce stigma and encourage early help-seeking. Together, these approaches can help bridge the treatment gap and promote equitable mental health care for rural adolescents.
