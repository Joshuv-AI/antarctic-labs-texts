Immunology of Biliary Atresia
Authors: Phoebe N Miller, Suruthi Baskaran, Amar Nijagal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiliary atresia is a progressive neonatal cholangiopathy that leads to liver failure. Characterized by inflammation-mediated liver injury, the immune system plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of this disease. Though several types of immune cells and mediators have been implicated in animal models of biliary atresia, emerging literature reflects the complex interplay of components of the immune response that contributes to disease progression in humans. Novel therapies targeting the immune system are needed to mitigate the devastating effects of biliary atresia. This review highlights the current literature on the components of the immune system that have been in implicated in biliary atresia and the rich interplay between the major arms of the immune system- innate and adaptive immunity- to cause the highly morbid consequences of this disease.Keywords: Biliary atresia, Liver inflammation, Pediatric liver disease

Introduction

Biliary Atresia (BA) is a progressive neonatal cholangiopathy that is characterized by inflammation and fibrosis, ultimately leading to extrahepatic bile duct obstruction. If untreated, BA leads to end-stage liver disease. As a result, BA is the most frequent indication for pediatric liver transplantation.1 The most effective treatment for patients with BA has been the Kasai portoenterostomy.2,3 After restoration of bile flow with this procedure, the majority of patients have temporary relief of their obstructive cholangiopathy, although between 50 and 65% require liver transplantation by adulthood.4–7 Attempts to limit inflammation using corticosteroids have not resulted in improved bile drainage in randomized clinical trials, though it remains unclear if these therapies could benefit select patients.8

Pathologic examination of human BA specimens provides important insight about disease pathogenesis. Findings consistent with BA include periportal edema and fibroblast proliferation with ductular proliferation, as well as the presence of inflammatory cells. The principle findings are injury to the large duct and extrahepatic biliary tract through fibrous obliteration, but destruction of small intrahepatic bile ducts occurs later in disease. “Ductopenia” has been described as being key to the progression of liver dysfunction and ultimately end stage liver failure,9 though bile duct loss is heterogeneous in BA explant specimens.9 Bile “lakes” are another feature of disease progression – these are xanthogranulomatous inflammatory reactions composed of sheets of pigment laden histiocytes and multinucleated giant cells with fibrous walls.10,11 These data suggest that there are likely two distinct phases of BA: inflammatory and fibrotic.12

Given the inflammatory nature of BA, our review focuses on the role of the immune system in BA development and pathogenesis. We will cover the prevailing hypotheses about the etiology of BA and the animal models being used to study its pathogenesis. This review primarily highlights the role of the innate and adaptive immune system in BA progression and concludes with potential immunologic targets for novel therapeutics for this disease.

Classification of BA

Syndromic vs non-syndromic

Patients with BA exhibit two classical phenotypic patterns: non-syndromic and syndromic.9 The majority of patients with BA have the non-syndromic form, also known as the perinatal form.9,13 In non-syndromic BA, neonates remain jaundice-free after birth and symptoms develop within the first several weeks of life. Though this type of BA is generally considered an acquired form resulting from a perinatal or postnatal insult,12 there is evidence that BA has prenatal origins.14 Unlike non-syndromic/perinatal BA, the syndromic form is considered to be embryonic in origin or congenital; 15% of infants with BA present with jaundice at birth and often have other congenital malformations. There are defined syndromes for which BA is a central component of the constellation of diseases involved (e.g., Biliary Atresia Splenic Malformation, BASM).15 As with BASM, there are many associated abnormalities with biliary atresia including polysplenia, cardiac malformations, azygos continuation, malrotation, and situs inversus.16 BA is also associated with other congenital diseases including Hirschsprung disease, and less commonly, Zimmerman-Laband syndrome (cranio-facial, spinal, and oral developmental delay), Kabuki syndrome (characteristic facial features, skeletal development delay), and Kartagener syndrome (ciliary motility pathology causing situ inversus, chronic sinusitis and bronchiectasis).17 A recent meta-analysis found that patients with BASM tend to present earlier in life than non-syndromic patients and therefore undergo Kasai at younger ages.18 BA with cystic malformations is considered congenital but tends to present in a more delayed fashion.15 These three distinct patterns of BA suggest there are multiple factors that contribute to BA pathogenesis.

Etiology and contributors to BA pathogenesis

Common hypotheses for the cause of BA are (1) environmental or toxin-mediated, (2) a fetal or prenatal disposition, (3) a defect in the morphogenesis of the biliary tree, (4) viral-mediated, and (5) autoimmune.

Environmental

There are few examples to indicate that environmental factors can lead to the development of BA. The most extensive work to identify an environmental cause for BA is when lambs and calves in Australia developed jaundice and bile duct obstruction.19 Investigators identified a phytosterol, biliatresone, which selectively destroyed extrahepatic bile ducts in zebrafish larvae in both a dose- and time-dependent manner.20 Although a seasonal quality to BA has been shown, biliatresone is the only widely reported environmental toxin identified.21

Genetic predisposition

There is evidence to suggest that BA develops prenatally.22 Several genetic alterations have been identified in patients with BA. One study reported an association with a DNA variant encompassing the ADD3 genes, which is involved in cell-to-cell contact in epithelial tissues including the biliary tract.23 The heterozygous deletion of glypican 1 (GPC1), a heparan sulfate proteoglycan that regulates Hedgehog signalling and inflammation, was associated with BA in studies that showed levels of cholangiocyte GPC1 were lower than in healthy individuals.24,25 Hedgehog signalling has been implicated in the restoration of biliary architecture after injury and modulates repair in a coordinated fashion via macrophages.26

Defect in morphogenesis of the biliary tract

Malformation during the development of the biliary tract may play a role in BA, although evidence is inconclusive. During embryogenesis, the ductal plate ought to disappear, giving rise to bile ducts. Ductal plate malformations likely reflect a primary ciliary defect causing defective tubogenesis. Insults to the intrahepatic tubules lead to congenital fibrosis. These malformations have been implicated in the development of BA, although so far only in theory: ductal malformation was only identified in 10% of the portal tracts in a study of eight infants with BA. The presence of ductal plate malformations at the time of diagnosis may indicate further heterogeneity in the genesis of BA.27,28

Viral

Viral infection is a frequently cited etiologic agent for BA and was first proposed by Benjamin Landing in 1974.29 The viruses most commonly implicated include members of the herpesviridae family (cytomegalovirus and Epstein-Barr virus) and of the reoviridae family (reovirus and rotavirus).

Cytomegalovirus has been measured in anywhere from 0 to 76% of patients with BA30 with some studies subclassifying BA into BA with CMV.15,16 A Chinese cohort study of infants with BA found >60% of the livers had CMV DNA or protein.31 A British study noted poor drainage after Kasai amongst CMV-positive patients and a higher risk of cholangitis, a tendency to have higher transaminase levels, larger spleens, and overall poorer outcomes including higher risk of death.16 However, none of the liver biopsies in this series stained positively for CMV.15 CMV infections have been suggested to be short lived, which may make it even more challenging to detect them consistently. One study found that the presence of CMV infection in children with BA led to increased CD8+cell expression in bile ducts compared to neonates with EBV.32

Evidence to support EBV infection as a causative agent of BA has also been inconclusive. EBV in patients with BA ranges from 0 to 40%.30 Although EBV has been implicated in other autoimmune condition like rheumatoid arthritis, as well as other congenital abnormalities, its link to BA has been less clear. Reovirus, on the other hand, was detected in the hepatobiliary tissues of 55% of patients with BA and 78% of patients with choledochal cysts.33 However, the largest US study to date, which included 40 patients with BA, concluded that reovirus infection in the first six months is common in neonates with any form of cholestasis and likely lacked any role in the pathogenesis of BA specifically.34

Rotavirus has been particularly useful in mouse models; the periportal inflammation of BA has been replicated in mouse models using a rhesus strain of rotavirus. After infection, RRV can be detected in cholangiocytes of intra- and extra-hepatic ducts, macrophages and dendritic cells. RRV seems to initiate a response by both innate and humoral immunity, attracting T-killer cells to the duct epithelium, initiating a cascade of events that replicate the pathologic findings observed in human BA.35 Although rotavirus is frequently evoked as an etiology of non-syndromic BA, a nationwide study in Korea comparing data from before and after the introduction of a rotavirus vaccine found no difference in BA rates, while rotavirus infection rates decreased dramatically.36

Studying viruses in BA patients poses many challenges. The markers and serologic tests used are inconsistent across studies, and maternal serologies have not been consistently evaluated. Some of these markers studies in BA include matrix metallopeptidase-7 (MMP-7), interleukin 18 (IL18), interleukin 33 (IL33), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRi).37 One meta-analysis demonstrated that of said markers, the sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of BA were highest for MMP-7 (96 and 91% respectively) followed by GGT.37 Furthermore, infants clear viruses rapidly, suggesting the possibility of missing the inciting infection.

Autoimmune

The events that trigger BA remain an area of active investigation, but the subsequent immune reaction is well described. An initiating viral or toxic insult to the biliary epithelium leads to the expression of neoantigens on the bile duct epithelial surface. These are presented by macrophages to naïve T lymphocytes, which organize a broader immune response, causing cytotoxic T cells and proinflammatory cytokines to injure, scar and, ultimately, obliterate the bile duct. The robust autoimmune response to self-antigens may be at the root of this disease. Auto-antibodies have been found to be elevated in BA patients, including elevated IgM-ANCA38 and anti-Rho.38 Although no large multi-center study of HLA genotyping has been done, a cohort study in the U.K. found no HLA-associated loci in 101 patients with BA.39 In the largest study done in China, no HLA genes were strongly associated with BA in 336 infants.40 The findings from these studies indicate that an autoimmune response may contribute to BA pathogenesis, but further work is needed to define the nature of the autoimmune response in BA patients.

The immune system in BA

The immune system plays a pivotal role in BA. The focus of this review is on the two major arms of immunity: the innate and adaptive immune systems. Innate immunity is characterized by nonspecific immune responses to broad classes of damage- and pathogen-associated molecules. The major cells of the innate immune system includes neutrophils, macrophages, natural killer cells (NK cells), dendritic cells, eosinophils, basophils, mast cells, and innate lymphoid cells. In contrast to the innate immune system, the adaptive immune system is characterized by a highly specific immune response coordinated by T- and B-lymphocytes. Below, we discuss the role of the innate immune system first, and then the adaptive immune system, breaking down individual cell types and attempting to weave their complex interactions together in BA.

Neutrophils

Inflammation is a key component in the pathogenesis of BA12 and neutrophils are key components in the initial inflammatory response to tissue injury. Neutrophils infiltrate the portal tracts and the liver parenchyma in patients with BA.41 Human livers with BA stain positive for high levels of the antimicrobial inflammatory peptide CAP37, found in neutrophil granules.41 Similarly in RRV-induced BA, neonatal mice have elevated CD177+ cells, a subset of neutrophils that have higher expression of interferon-stimulated and neutrophil degranulation genes.42 These CD177+ cells perpetuate bile duct injury by generating neutrophil extracellular traps associated with high levels of mitochondrial activity and reactive oxygen species. Notably, the use of N-acetylcysteine to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) in patients with BA undergoing surgical biliary diversion significantly lowered total bile acid and direct bilirubin levels.42 In mice, depleting neutrophils by using anti-Ly6G antibodies improved the sequelae of BA, including less jaundice and improved weight.42

Cytokines and chemokines have a recognized role in regulating neutrophil function. In particular, high levels of IL-8 and CXCL-1 are implicated in biliary epithelial-cell damage by inducing neutrophil chemoattraction.43 Elimination of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-12, responsible for T-lymphocyte and natural killer cell function, led to a change in the inflammatory response from an IFN-gamma predominant response to a neutrophil-predominant infiltration with increased levels of TNF alpha, IFN alpha and IFN beta. These findings highlight the interplay of different immune pathways in BA that may act through modulating neutrophil infiltration and function.44 One such pathway that promotes neutrophil chemotaxis in RRV-induced BA is the release of Mip2/Cxcl2 by RRV-infected hepatic macrophages leading to bile duct injury.45 These results suggest that neutrophils play an important role in the inflammatory response in BA, as they coordinate with other cells involved in innate and adaptive immunity to potentiate bile duct injury and obstruction.

Macrophages and monocytes

Macrophages play important roles in tissue inflammation and fibrosis and are classically categorized as proinflammatory (“M1”) and pro-reparative/pro-fibrotic macrophages (“M2”). A study on human BA samples showed greater increase of M2 macrophage infiltration than of M1 macrophage infiltration. A high ratio of M2 to M1 macrophages corresponded with increased liver fibrosis and elevated conjugated bilirubin in children with BA.46 However, a decreased proportion of M2 macrophages was associated with an increased rate of postoperative cholangitis after Kasai procedure, suggestive of additional pathways governing fibrosis in BA.47 Apart from the classical inflammation and fibrosis pathway, macrophages also contribute to the pathogenesis of BA by undergoing necrotic programmed cell death or necroptosis, a higher rate of which correlates with worse prognosis.48

Although the classification of M1 and M2 macrophages helps to broadly define inflammatory and reparative macrophages, macrophage phenotype and function are far more nuanced than this dichotomous classification would suggest. Single-cell RNA sequencing has helped advance what is understood about macrophage function in cholestatic BA livers as compared to heathy human controls,49 as well as to mice with BA.50 Diseased macrophages have more heterogeneity and fewer immune regulatory genes compared to healthy macrophages, further emphasizing the dynamic role of different macrophage subsets in the pathogenesis of BA. Macrophages cluster in fibrotic niches of BA livers and exert individual effects, interacting with other cell types such as NK cells, transitional B cells, and neutrophils.51 Macrophages react to RRV-mediated BA by secreting chemokines. One example is the Mip2/Cxcl2 pathway, which induces chemotaxis of neutrophils and promotes inflammation.45 Conversely, high levels of cytokines such as macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) are found in BA and are hypothesized to contribute to the retention of macrophages in inflammatory foci, synthesis of proinflammatory cytokines, and T cell activation, among other functions.52,53 Thus, macrophage subsets are heterogeneous in their function, with certain subsets exhibiting pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic functions in BA whereas other macrophage subsets possess pro-reparative capability.

Monocytes may also play an important role in the progression of liver inflammatory diseases like BA. The aggressive nature of BA in neonates compared to the pathogenesis of inflammatory liver diseases in adults suggests that the mechanisms responsible for restoring tissue homeostasis following inflammation are impaired in affected infants. Recent RRV-infected murine models have suggested a role for Ly6cLo and Ly6cHi monocyte sub-types in perinatal liver inflammation. In a murine model of perinatal hepatic inflammation, Ly6cLo non-classical monocytes expressed a pro-reparative transcriptomic profile and the relative abundance of Ly6cLo monocytes promoted resolution of perinatal liver inflammation, resulting in resistance to disease for neonatal mice.54,55

Dendritic cells and natural killer cells

Dendritic cells (DCs) are important antigen presenting cells that interact with CD4 and CD8 T-lymphocytes, and NK cells to promote inflammation.56 DCs can be classified into plasmacytoid and conventional (myeloid and lymphoid DCs) DCs. RRV-induced BA mice (as compared to control mice) express a higher number of activated plasmacytoid DCs in proximity to the biliary ductal epithelium in the early stages of the disease. Eradication of plasmacytoid DCs has been shown to reduce the degree of ductal obstruction and improve survival.57 These DCs secrete IL-15 and activate NK cells, which leads to the lysis of cholangiocytes through the release of granzymes and perforin granules.58 Lysis is also achieved through a NKg2d-dependent pathway.59 NK cells are also activated by alternate pathways like HMGB1 (high mobility group box-1) protein released from injured cholangiocytes to effect further biliary epithelial damage.60 The hepatic DCs and NK cells from murine models of BA also express high levels of the proinflammatory gene TNF alpha and its receptors TNF1 and 2. The TNF alpha-TNF2 pathway facilitates the lysis of bile duct epithelium and is another potential target for modifying the NK cell response in BA.61 Notably, NK cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes form an important part of the inflammatory milieu in BA.62 Hence, manipulations of DCs and NK cells have the potential to be a vital aspect of BA management.

Adaptive immunity

T lymphocytes

The role of adaptive immunity in BA is supported by the presence of T-cell-mediated bile duct injury. The classic pathology of BA is lymphocytic invasion into the portal area, not unlike acute cellular rejection or graft vs host disease.63 Ohya et al. studied liver specimens of 31 patients who had the Kasai procedure and found that all had both degeneration of their intrahepatic bile ducts and lymphocytic infiltration into biliary epithelial cells, suggesting the possibly unique role of a cellular immune response in the pathogenesis of BA.63 Through immunohistochemistry analysis of BA tissues, Mack et al. demonstrated an increase in CD8, CD4 T-cells and Kupffer cells (CD68). Reverse transcription PCR analysis of BA tissue indicated expression of Th1 cytokines including IL-2, IFN-gamma, TNF, and IL-12.64 These findings suggest a Th-1 mediated portal tract inflammatory response. Mack et al. also described oligoclonal expansion of T cells in BA, suggesting that these T-cells are proliferating in response to a specific set of antigens.38 In murine models infected with RRV, PCR-array analysis has demonstrated specific genes related to the proliferation of T-cells including Jagged2 and NICD (Notch receptor fragments), which may suggest additional signaling pathways by which these inflammatory networks are activated (see-2.2.2).65

While no viral agent has been consistently associated with BA in human models, the proposed mechanism of viral injury followed by T-cell mediated tissue damage has been supported by studies in RRV-infected murine models where RRV has a unique tropism for biliary epithelial cells and results in progressive inflammation and obstruction of the extrahepatic biliary system by two weeks of age. This inflammation is mediated by the production of IFN-gamma by CD4+ and CD8+ cells.59 In IFN-gamma knock-out murine models, the sequential switch to a lymphocyte-based hepatic inflammation did not occur, and ductular fibrosis and biliary obstruction was prevented.

In adults, T-regulatory (Tregs) cells have been shown to play an important role in immune hemostasis. The depletion of Tregs is associated with severe autoimmune and inflammatory conditions in murine models and it is thought that this regulatory suppression is mediated by the expression of FOXP3. Grendebacke et al. demonstrated that CD4 FOXP3 T cell numbers increased rapidly in the circulation during the first days of life, which suggests a conversion to suppressive Tregs from CD25high Treg precursors.66 Thus, even in neonates, Tregs seem to play a formative role in immunoregulation. Brindley et al. demonstrated a significant decrease in Tregs in peripheral blood for patients with BA, and those with a history of CMV infection had notably greater Treg deficits, which may contribute to a decrease in autoregulation and therefore an increased risk of BA formation and its progression.67 Liu et al. identified a large subset of Th17 cells and found that by depleting the Th17 cells, the BA symptoms (onset of jaundice, acholic stools and retarded growth) were attenuated, and the survival rate was improved.68 In the same study, they also found that healthy adult Treg cells introduced into murine models decreased Th17 cell activity. They identified that dendritic cell production of IL-6 paralyzed the Treg suppressive effect on Th17 cells, resulting in an increase in Th17, which contributed to bile duct injury.68 Similarly Luo et al. found that Treg augmentation in the murine RRV models led to increased survival, decreased direct bilirubin levels and liver inflammation, and expansion of resident macrophages.69 One potential therapeutic approach is to maximize optimal Treg function, which could play a therapeutic role in BA pathogenesis.70

B lymphocytes

In 1981, Hadchoel et al. described IgM and IgG immunoglobulin deposits along the basement membrane of bile duct epithelial cells in the remnant of fewer than half of infants with BA tissue available for analysis.71 However, the concept of natural auto-antibodies has been posited whereby select neonatal IgM antibodies react to self-antigens that might be secondary to lapse in the regulation of a form of natural auto-immunity. One cause of this may be environmental or viral, in keeping with prior suggested mechanisms for BA.72 In an effort to identify the auto-antigens recognized by serum antigens in RRV-induced murine models, Lu et al. identified auto-antibodies against alpha-enolase with cross-reactivity to RRV. Serum samples from patients with BA had increased levels of anti-enolase IgM and IgG suggesting a form of molecular mimicry as well as a role for humoral immunity in the pathogenesis of BA.73

Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells and myeloid progenitors

The role of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and myeloid progenitors in the pathogenesis of BA is an emerging concept. HSPCs serve as hubs of inflammation and respond through proliferation and myeloid differentiation.74 Interestingly, our studies in mice highlight the limited role of adaptive (lymphoid) immunity in the pathogenesis of BA by demonstrating similar survival after RRV infection in control mice and in mice deficient in T and B lymphocytes.75 However, innate (myeloid) immune populations such as neutrophils and monocytes play a crucial role in the propagation of hepatic injury following RRV-induced BA.76 When examining myeloid progenitors, we found that neonatal hepatic myeloid progenitors expand in response to RRV, leading to further damage to bile duct epithelium. In fact, the eradication of these myeloid progenitors led to improved survival, weight gain, and reduced inflammation of liver tissues55 in RRV-induced BA. Randomized controlled trials to manipulate HSPCs using G-CSF (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) are currently underway77 and demonstrate a 12-fold increase in hematopoietic stem cell population as well as an overall decrease in cholangitis at 6 months in Phase I trials.78

Autoimmunity

In humans, the progressive nature of BA has often raised the question of an autoimmune process. The inflammatory process identified in murine models, including the oligoclonic expansion of T-cells, suggests the presence of a specific antigen. Mack et al. showed the presence of bile-duct epithelial antigen-specific T cells from livers and spleens of RRV-diseased mice and demonstrated adoptive transfer of T cells from RRV-induced BA mice into recipients with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) resulted in periductal inflammation composed of donor T cells and recipient macrophages.79 Moreover, CD8 T-cells play a unique role in the recognition of RRV-infected cholangiocytes, lysing and clearing the RRV-infected cholangiocytes as well as all the uninfected ones, extending the epithelial injury in an autoimmune type fashion.59 While there are no similar studies in human tissues, HLA-DR has been shown to be highly expressed in the central portal duct cells of human BA tissues as compared to healthy liver tissues.80 Although some of the evidence remains conflicting, a European study of 101 children with BA reported no significant association between BA and HLA genotypes.39 In contrast, a Japanese study of 392 children with BA reported a strong association between BA and HLA-DR2.

Therapeutics and future directions

Given the devastating impact of BA and the lack of efficacious therapies, novel treatments are needed. Several promising treatments are currently being studied to mitigate the devastating progression of BA. Wang et al. demonstrated that hepatic B-cell lymphopoiesis does not cease in RRV-infected mice, is associated with accumulation of IgG-autoantibody, and that depleting B-cells or blocking antigen presentation reduced liver damage.81 In a small clinical trial, rituximab, a B-cell-modifying therapy, resulted in a reduction of B cells and a restoration of macrophages though no clinical outcomes were recorded.81 Other immune targets also hold promise. Kim et al. demonstrated higher peripheral blood NK cells and IL-8, and lower regulatory T cells correlated with more aggressive forms of BA. Therapies targeting these pathways may protect the biliary tree from ongoing damage.82 Additional pathways modifying innate immune mechanisms could also be used to treat BA. For example, manipulating HSPCs using G-CSF (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) may slow disease progression77 and has shown promising findings in Phase I clinical trials.78 Though no single therapeutic agent has shown efficacy in patients with BA, blocking unmitigated inflammation or enhancing reparative/regulatory functions of immune-mediated pathways in BA are important opportunities to explore in order to mitigate the devastating consequences of BA.

Conclusion

BA remains a life-threatening condition for neonates. While the pathogenesis of BA is an evolving area of research, the multifaceted role of the immune system in biliary injury and fibrosis is increasingly clear. There are many avenues through which immune dysregulation and disequilibrium lead to the pathogenesis of BA. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms by which adaptive immune cells and myeloid populations contribute to the immune dysregulation that occurs in BA will lead to fruitful avenues for therapeutic development.
