Pharmacokinetics and brain uptake of sodium selenate and selenium in naïve rats and a lateral fluid percussion injury rat model
Authors: Chenxu Li, Pablo M Casillas‐Espinosa, Patricia Grandizoli Saletti, Tina Chi, Glenn Yamakawa, Juliana Silva, Matt Hudson, Wei Liu, Nigel C Jones, Sandy R Shultz, Idrish Ali, Usha Mishra, James C Cloyd, Solomon L Moshe ́, Aristea S Galanopoulou, Terence J O'Brien, Lisa D Coles
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost‐traumatic epilepsy (PTE) is a life‐long complication of traumatic brain injury (TBI). The development of PTE is associated with neurological morbidity and increases the risk of mortality. An aim of EpiBioS4Rx (Epilepsy Bioinformatics Study for Antiepileptogenic Therapy) was to test potential therapies to prevent the development of PTE in the lateral fluid percussion injury (LFPI) rat model of TBI, in which rats were subjected to injury at the left parietal cortex. Sodium selenate has been reported to be antiepileptogenic post‐TBI in rodent models by activating protein phosphatase 2A and reducing phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) protein. We aimed to characterize the pharmacokinetics (PK) and brain uptake of sodium selenate using naïve control and LFPI rats. Rats received either a single bolus dose or a single bolus dose followed by a 7‐day subcutaneous minipump infusion of sodium selenate. Sodium selenate and selenium concentrations in plasma and brain were analyzed and used for PK estimation and brain exposure assessment. Selenium concentrations rapidly increased after sodium selenate administration, demonstrating biotransformation from sodium selenate to selenium. Sodium selenate and selenium PK parameters were estimated using non‐compartmental analysis. Sodium selenate clearance (CL/F) and volume of distribution (V d/F) varied by dose and route of administration, suggesting differences in bioavailability and nonlinear pharmacokinetics at the doses tested. Brain‐to‐plasma partition coefficients (AUCbrain/AUCplasma) for sodium selenate and selenium were found to be 0.7–1.3 and 0.1–0.3 following single‐dose injection, respectively, indicating active transport of sodium selenate across the blood–brain barrier (BBB).Keywords: antiepileptogenesis, blood–brain barrier, pharmacokinetics, post‐traumatic epilepsy, traumatic brain injury

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is caused by external mechanical forces on the brain and is a major cause of morbidity and mortality globally. 1 , 2 TBI is commonly accompanied by long‐term complications including cognitive impairment, sensory processing issues, and epilepsy. Post‐traumatic epilepsy (PTE) develops in 2%–50% of patients following a TBI, with the greatest incidence after moderate‐to‐severe TBI. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 PTE is defined as recurrent, unprovoked seizures occurring between 7 days to years after a TBI and is thought to be causally related to the trauma itself. 7 A number of risk factors are associated with the development of PTE including the occurrence of early seizures, intracranial hemorrhage, skull fracture, and the severity of brain injury. 8 , 9 Furthermore, the development of PTE is associated with worse functional outcomes, neurobehavioral disorders, and an elevated risk of mortality. 9 , 10 As such, implementing therapeutic interventions that can mitigate the sequelae of TBI and prevent the development of PTE in those at risk would be a significant advance in care.

At present, there are no FDA‐approved therapies targeting epileptogenesis post‐TBI. Furthermore, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms behind PTE disease causation and progression are not fully characterized, hindering the development of effective therapies. 2 Increasing evidence suggests the involvement of many different pathophysiological processes in post‐traumatic epileptogenesis including increased phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) forms, hemorrhages, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 P‐tau‐based mechanisms have been proposed as significant contributors to the neuropathological changes observed after TBI and possibly PTE and, more generally, epilepsy. 15 Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of tau phosphorylation decreases the risk of seizure induction following epileptogenic brain injury. 12 , 16 , 17 The protein expression and activity of PR55, the regulatory B‐subunit of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) which is the primary enzyme that regulates tau phosphorylation, were reported to be significantly reduced following TBI. 18 , 19 , 20 Earlier studies showed increased p‐tau after TBI in various rat models of epileptogenesis. 18 , 20

In the lateral fluid percussion injury (LFPI) model of TBI, increased p‐tau was observed post‐TBI following a phosphorylation site‐ and time‐dependent pattern. 11 Sodium selenate, an inorganic less toxic form of selenium, has been reported to be an antiepileptogenic therapy in rat models of PTE and other forms of acquired epilepsy, presumably based on its ability to target tau phosphorylation. 18 , 20 , 21 Sodium selenate treatment in PTE or kainic acid status epilepticus rat models activates PP2A activity, enhances PR55 protein expression, attenuates tau phosphorylation, improves outcomes, and mitigates the progression and severity of epilepsy. 11 , 18 , 20 , 21 , 22 In our prior studies, 1‐week treatment of LFPI rats given the maximal tolerated sodium selenate dose reduced p‐tau at Ser202/Thr205 [AT8‐immunoreactivity (AT8‐ir)] over total tau ratio and increased PP2A activity in cortical extracts at 2 days post‐LFPI. 11 However, it failed to alter the AT8‐ir, AT180‐ir (p‐tau‐Thr231‐ir), or PR55‐ir expression in the cortical neurons, suggesting that additional mechanisms may underlie the effects of sodium selenate in post‐TBI pathologies. 11 Selenium also displays antioxidant properties that could attenuate oxidative damage following TBI in both brain‐derived cells and mouse models. 23

Understanding the pharmacokinetics (PK) of sodium selenate is critical to optimizing dosing strategies for animal models and for treatment in humans. The Epilepsy Bioinformatics Study for Antiepileptogenic Therapy (EpiBioS4Rx) project is a multidisciplinary program aimed at identifying biomarkers of epileptogenesis and testing potential antiepileptogenic therapies using a rat model of PTE. PK sampling was included in the design of these studies. The work reported herein aimed to characterize the PK and brain uptake of sodium selenate and selenium in both naïve and brain‐injured animals (LFPI model) following: (1) Single subcutaneous (s.c.) or intraperitoneal (i.p.) doses in naïve rats or (2) Single s.c. loading doses followed by 7‐day s.c. infusions in naïve and LFPI rats. The single‐dose PK data were used for screening and guiding dose selection in the longer‐term studies. Osmotic minipumps were used to continuously deliver a constant dose of sodium selenate subcutaneously in order to maintain target concentrations. An important attribute of the EpiBioS4Rx project is to increase the rigor and reproducibility of preclinical research by conducting the same studies at different sites. Therefore, the secondary objective of this project was to verify consistency in dose formulations and administrations by comparing drug exposure among different study sites.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study sites

Three sites from EpiBioS4Rx were involved in the present study as part of the target engagement and modification preclinical PTE project. These sites include Albert Einstein College of Medicine (Einstein, NY, USA), Monash University and the University of Melbourne (Melbourne, Victoria, Australia), and the University of Minnesota Twin Cities (Minneapolis, MN, USA). Animal studies were conducted at Albert Einstein College of Medicine and the Melbourne, Australia Site (University of Melbourne and Monash University). Bioanalysis and pharmacokinetic analyses were completed at the University of Minnesota. All procedures and data collection were harmonized by using standard operating procedures, common data elements (CDEs), and case report forms (CRFs). 19 , 24 , 25 , 26

Preparation of sodium selenate and osmotic pumps

Sodium selenate was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (Einstein: S8295‐25G Sigma Aldrich, USA, Melbourne: 71948‐100G, Sigma‐Aldrich, Australia). For single bolus injection studies, 1 mg/mL sodium selenate solution was prepared by dissolving the drug in 0.9% sterile saline. The formulation was then injected into rats or further diluted according to the dose (0.04 mg/kg or 0.2 mg/kg). Furthermore, subcutaneously implanted osmotic minipumps (2ML1, delivery rate 10 μL/h, Alzet, Cupertino, CA, USA) were used to deliver a constant amount of drug over a 7‐day period for the assessment of drug tolerability. 21 , 27 The required concentration of the drug formulation in the pumps was calculated based on the body weights of the animals in order to continuously administer a 1 mg/kg/day dose. All minipumps then underwent pre‐incubation in sterile saline for 24 h at 37°C for priming to ensure immediate delivery of the drug following implantation. 27

Animal studies

The single‐dose PK studies were conducted at the Melbourne, Australia Site (University of Melbourne and Monash University), while the minipump infusion PK studies were conducted at both Albert Einstein College of Medicine and the Melbourne sites. Approval was obtained through the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Albert Einstein College of Medicine (IACUC #20170107) and The Florey Animal Ethics Committee (protocol # 17–049 UM) prior to study initiation. Male, 10–11‐week‐old Sprague Dawley rats (300–350 g) were used as naïve controls or following LFPI induction at the left parietal region, using a 5 mm circular craniectomy and fluid percussion device (AmScien Instruments, Richmond, VA, USA) to induce moderate/severe TBI with a mortality of about 30% as previously described. 11 , 28 , 29 , 30 The study design is shown in Figure 1.

Single‐dose injection pharmacokinetic study

Single s.c. bolus injection of 0.04 or 0.2 mg/kg or single i.p. bolus injection of 1 mg/kg sodium selenate was administered to naïve rats. Blood samples were collected from the lateral tail vein using a 23 G butterfly needle (#367284, BD Vacutainer, BD Biosciences, Australia). Blood was collected into a 0.5 mL K2EDTA tube (#365975, BD Microtainer, BD Biosciences, Australia) at room temperature and was immediately mixed with EDTA anticoagulant. 31 Samples were then promptly centrifuged at 1300×g (Centrifuge 5415R Eppendorf Biotools, CA) for 10 min at 4°C to separate plasma from blood and the resulting plasma was stored at −80°C until analysis. Blood samples were collected prior to dosing, and at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 24 h following the administration (n = 1 to 5/group/time point). Parietal cortical samples were harvested at 2, 4, 6, and 24 h following dosing (n = 2 to 3/group/time point). The left cerebral cortex (LCCX) and right cerebral cortex (RCCX) were dissected and flash frozen, and then stored at −80°C until analysis. Brain and plasma samples were analyzed for sodium selenate and selenium concentrations as described below.

Loading dose +7‐day continuous infusion pharmacokinetic study

Single s.c. injection of 0.04 mg/kg sodium selenate was administered in naïve rats or immediately after LFPI followed by s.c. minipump placement (ALZET 2ML1) 1 h later. The loading dose was administered in order to rapidly achieve steady‐state concentrations of sodium selenate. The stock concentration of sodium selenate in the minipump was 1 mg/mL. Sodium selenate was then dosed at 1 mg/kg/day for 7 days after which time the minipump was removed. Blood samples were collected prior to dosing, at 0.5, 1, and 2 h following the bolus administration, at the time of minipump removal, and at 0.5, 2, and 4 h following removal using the method as mentioned above (n = 1 to 4/group/time point). Immediately after sample collection, plasma was separated from blood and was stored at −20°C until analysis. Parietal cortical samples were harvested at 2, 120, and 172 h following the loading dose (n = 2 to 3/group/time point). The LCCX and RCCX were dissected and flash frozen, and then stored at −80°C until analysis. Brain and plasma samples were then analyzed for sodium selenate and selenium concentrations.

Analysis of sodium selenate and selenium

Chemicals and reagents

Sodium selenate and Selenium were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Methanol (HPLC grade), nitric acid, and PBS were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Blank plasma was purchased from Biological Specialty (Colmar, PA, USA). Deionized water of 18.5ΩM‐cm resistance from the Barnstead Nano pure water purification system. The zirconium beads were purchased from VWR (Radnor, PA, USA).

Analysis of selenate (SeO4 ) using ion chromatography

Standard preparation

The sodium selenate stock standard was prepared by dissolving approximately 50 mg of sodium selenate into 5 mL of deionized water to make a 10 mg/mL (10 000 ppm) solution. The sodium selenate stock solution was then diluted with deionized water to prepare the highest calibration standard concentration of 2000 ppm. This sub‐stock solution was further diluted 50:50 to obtain 12.5 to 2000 ppm calibration standard concentration solutions. Plasma samples with sodium selenate concentrations ranging from 6.25 to 1000 ppm were achieved by adding 25 μL of each calibration standard to 50 μL of plasma. The calibration standard spiked plasma samples were deproteinated by the addition of 1 mL of methanol and vortexed for 30 s. The samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant of each sample was collected into a clean tube and evaporated under nitrogen in a water bath at 37°C. Finally, the samples were reconstituted in 200 μL of deionized water and transferred into analysis vials.

Brain sample preparation

Rat brain lysate was prepared in microcentrifuge tubes containing 50–150 mg of brain tissue, 100 mg of zirconium beads, and 200 μL of cold PBS. Each brain sample was homogenized using the Bullet Blender (Next Advance Inc., Troy, NY, USA) twice for 3 min at a speed setting of 8. The homogenized tissue samples were then sonicated for 1 min and centrifuged for 2.5 min at 15000×g. The supernatant was transferred to a clean microcentrifuge tube and the brain tissue lysate was stored at −80°C for analysis. The brain tissue lysate was thawed before analysis. 0.05 mL of the brain lysate was pipetted into a clean test tube and precipitated with 1 mL of methanol. Each sample was centrifuged for 10 min at 2500 rpm, and the supernatant was collected and evaporated under nitrogen at 37°C water bath. The sample was reconstituted in 0.2 mL of deionized water into analysis vials.

Instrument

The selenate was detected using Thermo Scientific Dionex ICS 5000 ion chromatography system (UMN dept. of Earth Science) with GP40 gradient Pump, CD20 Conductivity detector, and Chromeleon 7 software. Selenate was separated using Dionex Ion Pac AS7 anion‐exchange column (4 mm ID × 250 mm length) and AG7 guard column (4 mm ID × 50 mm length). The mobile phase consisted of 100 mM sodium hydroxide and deionized water. The lower limit of quantification of this analytical method was 6.25 μg/mL, and the linear range was 6.25 to 1000 μg/mL.

Analysis of selenium using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS)

Plasma and brain sample preparation

Rat plasma samples were prepared by digesting 20 μL of plasma samples in 280 μL of 10% HNO3 for 24 h and centrifuged for 3 min at 13000×g. The supernatant was collected for ICP‐MS analysis. The brain samples were digested by adding 20 μL of brain lysate in 280 μL of 10% HNO3 and digested for 24 h for selenium analysis. The samples were then centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected into a clean vial for ICP analysis.

Standard preparation

The stock standard was prepared at 1 mg/mL (1000 ppm) by dissolving the 5 mg of selenium in 5 mL of 10% HNO3. The stock solution of selenium was diluted to 10 ppm with deionized water and the sub‐stock solution was further diluted to 1 ppm to prepare calibration standard solutions of concentrations 46.87 to 1500 ppb, and the final concentrations of selenium ranged from 46.87 to 750 ppb of selenium when 20 μL of standard was added to 280 μL 10% HNO3.

Instrument

The selenium was analyzed using Thermo Scientific Xseries‐2 ICP‐MS (UMN Earth Science Dept), CCTKED mode, and operated in CRC (chemical reaction cell) mode. The ICP‐MS had CETAC ASX‐520 autosampler and PFA‐ST Microflow Nebulizer. Selenium (Se‐78) was detected using Krypton (Kr‐83) and Yttrium (Y‐83) as internal standards. The lower limit of quantification of this analytical method was 46.87 ng/mL, and the linear range was 46.87 to 750 ng/mL.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Sodium selenate and selenium plasma concentration–time data following different single loading doses were imported into Phoenix™ (Certara, V8, NC, USA), and pharmacokinetic parameters of both compounds were estimated using non‐compartmental analysis (NCA) with the naïve pooled data (NDP) approach. 32 Maximum concentration (C max), time to maximum concentration (T max), area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), and terminal elimination half‐life (t 1/2) were estimated for both compounds. Total body clearance (CL/F) and volume of distribution (V d/F) were also estimated for sodium selenate following the three doses. Standard errors (SE) were calculated for C max and AUC.

Clearance of sodium selenate at steady‐state (CLss) was calculated using the data from the 7‐day minipump infusion study. In multiple‐dose and infusion studies, a steady‐state is reached when the rate of drug infusion equals the rate of drug elimination. Brain concentrations of sodium selenate and selenium were used to calculate the AUCbrain. Brain‐to‐plasma concentration ratios (C b/C p) were calculated by dividing the brain concentration by the plasma concentration at given time points (2, 4, 6, and 24 h post‐administration), and brain‐to‐plasma partition coefficient (AUCbrain/AUCplasma) were calculated by dividing the brain area under the curve by the plasma area under the curve.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were plotted, and statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism, Version 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA). Two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare the brain‐to‐plasma concentration ratios between the LCCX in naïve and LFPI groups, as well as between the LCCX and RCCX within the LFPI group for sodium selenate and selenium, respectively. Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was performed to correct for multiple comparisons. Two‐sample t‐tests were conducted to compare the concentrations of sodium selenate and selenium obtained from LFPI rats among study sites. A p‐value threshold of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Single‐dose injection pharmacokinetic study

The concentration–time profiles of sodium selenate and selenium in plasma following single i.p. or s.c. bolus administrations are shown in Figure 2. The baseline selenium concentrations were found to be approximately 150 ng/mL, which is consistent with literature‐published values. 34 , 35 , 36 Following dosing, maximum plasma concentrations of sodium selenate and selenium occurred at ~1 h and the decline in plasma concentrations appeared bi‐exponential. The concentration–time profiles of selenium were similar to those of sodium selenate and increased with dose, suggesting rapid biotransformation between the two compounds. While administration of sodium selenate resulted in quantifiable increases in both sodium selenate and selenium concentrations in plasma, these increases in plasma concentration were not proportionate with dose increase. The concentrations of sodium selenate in the 1 mg/kg i.p. group were similar to those in the 0.2 mg/kg s.c. groups, indicating that the bioavailability following i.p. dosing could be substantially lower than that of s.c. dosing.

NCA estimated PK parameters of sodium selenate and selenium are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. The PK parameters were estimated without using the 24‐h data points due to a limited number of samples above the lower limit of quantification. The maximum concentrations and AUCs of both sodium selenate and selenium were observed not to increase proportionally with the dose. The CL/F of sodium selenate in the 1 mg/kg i.p. group was much higher than those in the 0.04 and 0.2 mg/kg s.c. groups, indicating differences in bioavailability in the two routes of administration. Moreover, the estimated CL/F in the 0.2 mg/kg s.c. group was also greater than that in the 0.04 mg/kg s.c. group, demonstrating that elimination kinetics may not be linear and there may exist an absorption–limitation of sodium selenate via s.c. administration. Similar patterns were observed in the estimated V d/F of sodium selenate. The estimated t 1/2 of sodium selenate were observed to be the shortest in the 1 mg/kg i.p. dosing group, while the longest was seen in the 0.2 mg/kg s.c. dosing group. The estimated t 1/2 of selenium ranged from 4 to 5 h in the 0.2 mg/kg s.c. and 1 mg/kg i.p. dosing groups. Selenium t 1/2 in the 0.04 mg/kg s.c. dosing group was not reported because the concentrations were close to the endogenous levels.

The concentration–time profiles of sodium selenate and selenium in the brain are shown in Figure 3. Brain concentrations of both compounds generally follow similar patterns as in plasma with the T max of about 2 h post‐administration. Sodium selenate and selenium brain concentrations also increased with dose but not proportionally. The mean brain‐to‐plasma concentration ratios of sodium selenate and selenium at specific time points are depicted in Figure 4. The brain‐to‐plasma ratios of sodium selenate were greater than 1 at later time points, whereas the brain‐to‐plasma ratios of selenium approached 0.2. This information suggests a lower uptake of selenium in the brain as compared to sodium selenate.

The brain AUCs and the corresponding brain‐to‐plasma partition coefficients of sodium selenate and selenium are summarized in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. For both sodium selenate and selenium, the brain AUCs were comparable between the left and right cerebral cortex. Similar to plasma AUCs, an increased s.c. dose resulted in elevated AUCs in both hemispheres of the brain; however, the increases were not proportional to the doses. Furthermore, the dose‐normalized AUCs in the brain after i.p. dosing were much lower than those in s.c. dosing groups, which was consistent with the plasma exposures. The brain‐to‐plasma partition coefficients of sodium selenate and selenium were calculated as a ratio of AUC (AUCbrain/AUCplasma) and were used to evaluate the brain‐targeting efficiency of both compounds. The brain‐to‐plasma partition coefficients of sodium selenate were ranged from 0.67 to 1.26, whereas those of selenium were ranged from 0.12 to 0.25. The partition coefficients of sodium selenate were about 5‐fold greater than that of selenium. The combined information indicates a much higher brain uptake of sodium selenate than that of selenium.

Loading dose +7‐day continuous infusion pharmacokinetic study

Sodium selenate and selenium plasma concentrations after a single 0.04 mg/kg dose s.c. injection followed by 7‐day 1 mg/kg/day s.c. infusion are shown in Figure 5. Following the same loading dose, the time courses of sodium selenate exposure in plasma were similar in both naïve rats and LFPI rats. Similarly, the plasma concentrations of selenium were quite comparable between the two groups. Furthermore, the steady‐state concentrations of sodium selenate were reached ~1 h after minipump placement and maintained at about 50 000 ng/mL during the s.c. minipump dosing. In addition, the steady‐state concentrations of sodium selenate were slightly higher in the naïve group than in the LFPI group. Selenium concentrations were maintained around 350 ng/mL during steady state. The CLss of sodium selenate in naïve rats and LFPI rats are found to be 0.5708 and 0.8926 mL/h/kg, respectively. These findings were comparable with the results obtained through NCA following single‐dose s.c. administrations.

DISCUSSION

To date, there is no antiepileptogenic therapy available that is known to prevent or mitigate PTE in humans. The current standard of care for moderate‐to‐severe TBI typically involves prescribing antiseizure medications for short term seizure prophylaxis, although these do not prevent the development of PTE. 37 , 38 , 39 Preclinical evidence shows that sodium selenate has the potential to attenuate neuronal degeneration, improve sensorimotor and neurobehavioral comorbidities, and decrease the frequency of seizures. 16 , 18 , 20 , 21 , 40 , 41 The potential of sodium selenate as a disease‐modifying therapy for the prevention or mitigation of PTE in the LFPI model is currently being tested in Project 2 of EpiBioS4Rx in a multicenter preclinical trial. However, the current knowledge of sodium selenate PK in both animals and humans is limited, necessitating further investigation. Here, we have described sodium selenate PK, biotransformation to selenium, and its brain uptake in naive rats and the LFPI‐induced rat model of moderate–severe TBI.

To our knowledge, this report presents the first documented investigation of sodium selenate PK in rodents. Generally, s.c. and i.p. administration resulted in increased systemic exposure of sodium selenate and biotransformation to selenium. We found that plasma concentrations of both sodium selenate and selenium exhibited a bi‐exponential decline, suggesting very rapid distribution into the central compartment followed by a slower distribution into the peripheral compartment. This finding aligns with the observation of increased concentrations in the brain relative to plasma until distribution equilibrium is reached, indicating a distributive phase of both compounds into the brain (i.e., the peripheral compartment). A nonlinear pattern in the PK profile of sodium selenate was observed as evidenced by the less‐than‐proportional increase in AUC in relation to the dose. This resulted in a dose‐dependent increase in the estimated apparent clearance, that is, CL/F. This change in apparent clearance could be due to differences in bioavailability rather than elimination mechanisms, but this can only be determined with an intravenous administration study. Specific factors that could explain the PK nonlinearity include inadequate solubility of one or both of the formulations, or alteration in absorption kinetics. Of note, the t 1/2 we have found for sodium selenate after a single‐dose injection in rats of 2–5 h is similar to the 1–3 h reported in the only study that has investigated the PK properties of sodium selenate in humans. 42 Prolonged absorption from the injection site in rats may have resulted in longer terminal phase t 1/2. Importantly, this prolonged t 1/2 is governed by absorption rather than elimination, a phenomenon also known as flip‐flop kinetics. 43

Notably, much lower exposures were observed following i.p. dosing, which is likely due to differences in the bioavailability between the two routes of administration. This may be attributed to significant first‐pass metabolism following i.p. injection, given that systemic bioavailability is determined by the fraction of drug absorbed times the fraction that escapes first‐pass metabolism. Moreover, the hydrophilic property of sodium selenate may also hinder its absorption from the peritoneal cavity to the portal vein compartment. 44 Lastly, the solubility of the formulation in the peritoneal cavity may have resulted in precipitation of the study drug. While further investigation is still warranted, these findings would have practical implications for establishing appropriate dosage regimens for long‐term studies.

The implanted subcutaneous minipump was well tolerated and able to achieve consistent exposures of both sodium selenate and selenium. Baseline selenium concentrations of around 150 ng/mL were observed in our study, which is consistent with concentrations of 100–600 ng/mL that have been previously reported. 34 , 35 , 36 The administration of sodium selenate results in reproducible increases in selenium that were directly related to sodium selenate concentration–time profiles. Following dosing, selenate can be reduced to selenite by thioredoxin reductase and thioredoxin, and then further metabolized into selenophosphate and selenoproteins. 45 The liver is considered an important site for selenium metabolism, facilitating the synthesis of selenoproteins and their transport into the circulatory system. 46 In our study, selenium from the selenoproteins was detected by digesting the selenoproteins with nitric acid. However, limited information is available regarding the rate‐limiting steps of selenate metabolism, indicating a need for further investigation to better explain the underlying mechanisms. The magnitude of selenium increase was found to be much lower than that of selenate, aligning with a previous finding of minor metabolism from selenate to selenium. 47 Steady‐state appeared to be reached after 7 days of minipump placement, with stable sodium selenate and selenium plasma concentrations of about 50 000 and 350 ng/mL, respectively. Here, we characterized the elimination kinetics by determining the clearance at steady state, which was consistent with the estimations obtained from NCA. Given that the plasma concentration–time profile of selenium closely resembles that of sodium selenate and measurement of selenium is considerably easier than that of sodium selenate, the use of selenium might be a reasonable surrogate for sodium selenate exposure in plasma. Moreover, the metabolic pathway of selenium is complex. To completely understand the pharmacological effects of sodium selenate, the contributions of other compounds such as selenoproteins may need to be taken into account.

An important factor that needs to be considered for a CNS‐acting drug is its ability to reach the site of action at a therapeutic concentration. In this study, we use brain‐to‐plasma AUC ratios as a measurement of brain permeability. Interestingly, we noticed differences in the brain uptake of sodium selenate and selenium, despite the high hydrophilicity of both compounds. The brain uptake of selenium was low, with its total exposure accounting for only about 20% of that in the plasma. In contrast, sodium selenate did cross the BBB well with brain concentrations comparable to those in plasma. These findings imply that specific proteins may engage in the transport of sodium selenate across the BBB. A recent review pointed out that the brain uptake of selenate may be mediated by sulfate transporters, which are inorganic anion transporters, because of the isomorphism of selenate to sulfate. 48 Mechanistic studies are needed in the future to identify and verify the potential transporters. Taken together, the data reported here suggests good penetration of sodium selenate into the brain, and its brain accumulation might be the key to targeting epileptogenesis in PTE.

A key aspect of the EpioBios4Rx project is increasing the reproducibility of study results by conducting the same studies at multiple sites. Comparing drug concentrations obtained from two sites allowed us to determine if drug formulations and dosing protocols were similar. We found that sodium selenate and selenium concentrations collected from two sites analyzed on different occasions were relatively comparable. Such information permits the comparison of efficacy outcomes across different locations.

The limitation of our investigation is the relatively small number of animals studied. Given the limited number of samples per animal, a naïve‐pooled non‐compartmental modeling approach was used, which allowed us to only estimate the variance for AUC and C max. In addition, we were not able to distinguish between subject variability and residual unexplained variability when estimating the PK parameters of sodium selenate and selenium. Limited data in early time points precluded full characterization of the absorption process. Despite these limitations, this work did provide insight into drug exposures at doses that have shown biological promise. Our findings provide initial exposure characteristics of sodium selenate for use in future PK/PD modeling designs.

In conclusion, sodium selenate is considered a promising, potential disease‐modifying therapy in preventing or mitigating acquired epilepsy and its comorbidities, including PTE. Obtaining PK information early in the screening allows for the development of PK/PD models and simulations of the effect of drugs on efficacy and safety measurements, which can guide the design of optimal treatment protocols in the EpiBioS4Rx project. To our knowledge, this is the first study that investigated sodium selenate PK parameters in rodents. Selenium concentration–time profiles mimic those of sodium selenate, suggesting rapid biotransformation. Changes in the estimated CL/F suggest nonlinear elimination kinetics of sodium selenate in rats, but further studies are required to confirm this observation. The potential flip‐flop kinetics observed suggest delayed absorption following s.c. injection dosing. These characterizations of sodium selenate PK will be critical for the design of future studies.
