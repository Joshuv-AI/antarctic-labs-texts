Introduction: Beef and the Five Principles of Circularity

Circularity is a model of production and consumption that prioritizes keeping resources in use for as long as possible through avoidance of use, reuse, recycling, and regeneration of materials or products while minimizing environmental impact (Muscat et al., 2021). Enhancing the circularity of food systems has been proposed as a holistic approach for sustainable intensification of agriculture (Mcallister et al., 2025). Agricultural systems have been pivotal to enhancing circularity, from byproduct utilization, manure management, and bioenergy, all contributing to the five principles of circularity (Muscat et al., 2021; McAllister et al., 2025). Beef cattle production systems contribute extensively to the five principles of circularity; however, the contribution from beef production to circularity is often undervalued or inaccurately represented, especially from ecological and carbon accounting perspectives (Dillon et al., 2021; Oerly et al., 2022). With an anticipated 13% increase in global beef consumption in the next decade (OECD/FAO, 2025), the purpose of this article is to identify the key mechanisms by which beef production systems contribute to the five principles of circularity (Figure 1) and serve as a key opportunity for sustainable advances in agriculture.

The five principles of circularity (Muscat et al., 2021) applied to beef production systems, focusing on circular components of extensive grazing production systems, intensive cattle feeding systems, and products of the rendering process.

Grazing Systems: Land-Based Circularity

Extensive grazing operations are a critical component of circularity in beef production systems. Grazing domestic livestock, like wild ruminants, are drivers of nutrient circularity and pivotal for the maintenance and regeneration of grassland ecology, yet these ecosystem services are often unrecognized for the role they play in circularity. While concerns over mismanagement of grazing lands, such as overgrazing, and potential impacts on the environment need to be addressed, well managed grazing operations support: the safeguarding of the world’s most critical habitats; water, carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycles; fire suppression; and upcycling of low-quality forage into high quality protein (Mottet et al., 2017; Van Zanten et al., 2019; Place, 2024; Van Eenennaam, 2024). Most cattle graze on marginal lands–lands too steep, dry, low in soil quality, or remotely located to grow crops or support other agricultural uses (Mottet et al., 2017; Van Zanten et al., 2019). By grazing these lands, cattle produce a valuable food source that otherwise could not be generated (Figure 1).

Grazing lands may also have the potential to support development of alternative forms of energy through installation of wind turbines and photovoltaic solar panels (Figure 1). Wind turbines are commonly used across grazing lands, and ongoing research continues to refine the placement and design of turbines to minimize impacts on bird populations through risk of collision (Wulff et al., 2016; May et al., 2020; McClure et al., 2021, 2022; REWI, 2025). Solar grazing has been most feasible and popular with grazing sheep (Andrew et al., 2025), and is currently in the initial stages of adapting the practice for cattle. Pilot studies have shown that by raising the solar panels and taking other measures to prevent equipment damage, grazing cattle beneath solar panels can be possible (Schoeck, 2023; Vaughan et al., 2023). However, many of these solar systems do not allow for grazing and can result in the conversion of natural landscapes into energy markets which poses a risk to increased conversion of grazing lands (Diffendorfer et al., 2024; Blaydes et al., 2025). Balancing alternative energy markets while maintaining grasslands will be critical for food security and environmental well-being in the future.

One of the most underrecognized benefits of cattle grazing is carbon storage (Figure 1). Grasslands are integral for terrestrial carbon storage, accounting for ∼20% of the world’s terrestrial carbon and contributing to ∼18% of the world’s annual terrestrial carbon sink (MacDougall et al., 2026). When managed effectively, cattle grazing can help to maintain and, in some cases, improve soil carbon storage in grasslands, particularly in temperate regions (Chen et al., 2015). This can be achieved with balancing carbon and nitrogen from cattle manure and stimulating photosynthesis and root growth from compaction and grazing. However, maintaining grasslands with cattle grazing is becoming increasingly financially and sociologically challenging. While carbon programs exist to incentivize grazing practices to realize this carbon storage potential, participating producers face challenges demonstrating the permanence of stored carbon, and non-participating producers find that the carbon is not sufficiently valued to justify the practice change (Wang et al., 2026).

Intensive Feeding Operations: Upcycling Byproducts and Waste

Feedyards contribute to circularity through two key mechanisms: the incorporation of coproducts and byproducts in their feeds and the creation of manure for use as an alternative to synthetic fertilizers. Compared to other livestock systems, cattle feedlots require the highest ratio of human-edible feed to produce a kg of protein (Mottet et al., 2017). However, most beef cattle spend more than two-thirds of their lives consuming forage during the grazing phase of production and the requirement for human-edible feed during the finishing phase is diluted over the animal’s lifetime such that, on average, ruminants require 12.5% less human-edible feed compared to monogastrics to produce the same amount of meat (Mottet et al., 2017). Byproducts and coproducts make up a substantial portion of feedlot rations, accounting for more than one-third of typical diets (IFEEDER, 2025). Common ingredients include crop-processing residues such as oil cakes and meals, as well as coproducts from industrial processes like ethanol production, where feeds such as dried distillers grains are generated alongside fuel (Place, 2024; IFEEDER, 2025). Byproducts are materials produced during processing that are not the primary intended output and are generally of lower economic value than the main product, whereas coproducts are co-generated within the same system and hold comparable economic value (FAO, 2025). Incorporating these materials into feedlot diets enhances resource efficiency and circularity in beef production by utilizing otherwise underused outputs while contributing valuable nutrients to cattle rations and ultimately producing a high-quality protein source supporting human dietary needs.

In addition to upcycling human-inedible coproducts and byproducts, feedyard manure can be upcycled to high-quality fertilizer. Unlike grazing systems, where cattle deposit their manure over large areas of land, feedyards produce manure in a concentrated area and rely on manure management techniques to process and store manure before it is applied to land. Most large feedyards stockpile and compost manure. Composted manure is a nutrient-rich fertilizer that can be applied to nearby row-crop systems or even be utilized by niche market producers (i.e., organic) who are prohibited from using synthetic fertilizers. Beyond composted manure, stockpiled manure is also utilized for direct land application. Manure provides nutrients (e.g., nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) that can be recycled back into cropping systems (FAO, 2025; Kebreab et al., 2026). However, effective use requires matching nutrient application rates to crop demand and implementing best management practices to ensure appropriate distribution and prevent nutrient accumulation, particularly in regions with high livestock density.

Anaerobic digesters have been proposed as a method for upcycling manure into a source of renewable energy, though adapting digesters for use with feedlot manure remains challenging. Digesters require consistent feedstock, which works well for dairy and swine manure systems that continually collect liquid slurry (e.g., a mix of manure and urine) from flush and scrape barns. In contrast, manure from feedyards is scraped from dirt pens once or twice a year, resulting in low-moisture, low-substrate waste that is not compatible for digesters. Ongoing research for dry batch anaerobic digestors, or “dry digesters”, in Europe and North America aims to address these challenges (Wedwitschka et al., 2022).

Coproduct Utilization: Rendering as One of Beef’s Prime Circularity Engines

In beef cattle production systems, the main output of the system is beef. On average, a 600-kg live animal will yield 47% (286 kg) of primal cuts (main sections first separated from a carcass) and trim (fat and lean meat removed during fabrication). Of the remaining 53%, 5% is hide, 5% is offal, and 43% goes to rendering. From a circular economy standpoint, rendering is critical and prevents around 60 billion pounds of material from ending up in landfills, adding over $10 billion USD to the economy annually (NARA, 2026). Rendering products include but are not limited to tallow, blood meal, meat meal, meat and bone meal, gelatin, and collagen. As important as rendering is to the circular nature of beef production, the positive contribution of rendering is often undervalued, and not well understood by stakeholders and consumers alike, especially in hides, tallow, and pet food.

Every year over 28 million hides from beef and dairy animals are produced (NARA, 2026). Leather products, the final processing of cattle hides, are considered investment pieces, lasting anywhere from 10 to 30 years (NARA, 2026). Due to the endurance of these products and their associated production costs, clothing items from hides are the antithesis of “fast fashion”. Fast fashion consists of mass-produced textiles, including synthetic leathers generally made from synthetic materials (e.g., polyethene) from the petrochemical industry and available at lower costs than traditional leather goods. Synthetic leather is considered a disposable product and lasts 2 to 5 years before rapidly degrading (NARA, 2026). Furthermore, laundering and end of life disposal of synthetic fibers have been linked to release of microplastics into the environment (Acharya et al., 2021; Lovett et al., 2025).With the rise of fast fashion, the fashion industry is now responsible for 10% of global greenhouse gas emissions (NARA, 2026). While traditional leather is a high-quality and durable textile, many sustainability indices have labeled traditional leather as a less sustainable textile compared to synthetic leathers (NARA, 2026). There are economic and environmental tradeoffs across all textiles, but to effectively move sustainability efforts forward, sustainability indexes for textiles, especially leather and its synthetic counterparts, will need to be holistically evaluated and that the circular nature of leather recognized (Figure 1).

Since the bronze age, tallow has been used as the principal fat source to make soaps, candles, and skincare products (Sataray-Rodriguez et al., 2025). During the late 20th century, tallow was largely replaced by palm and other vegetable oils in mass-produced soaps and skincare products. Due to the power of the social media influencer omnipresence, tallow has once again been labeled as an excellent emulsifier for beauty products (Figure 1). Environmental concerns associated with oils such as palm and soybean have contributed to renewed interest in tallow (Sataray-Rodriguez et al., 2025). In regions with feedlot finished cattle, tallow has also historically been used as a fat source in feeder cattle rations. However, in recent years, tallow has been classified as a high-efficiency feedstock for producing sustainable aviation fuel (SAF; Calderon et al., 2024) and thus the market for tallow has skyrocketed (Figure 1). This market has displaced tallow from feeder cattle rations into SAF. Additionally, this increased demand for tallow as a sustainable fuel source has resulted in complexities around carbon accounting, with carbon credits going to companies utilizing SAF while the contribution of animal agriculture to the reduction in fossil fuel use remains largely unrecognized. While tallow currently has high market value, the circular nature of tallow is often unrecognized, even though it has held value to society for centuries.

Globally, the pet care industry is over $346 billion USD in market size, with North America as the largest market, representing a 41% share of the global pet care market (Precedence Research, 2025). In the US, beef and beef byproducts are in the top 5 commodities for pet food, constituting 14% (1.3 million tons) of pet food ingredients (IFEEDER, 2025). Rendered products used in pet food are a form of nutritional upcycling. Rendered beef items that are upcycled into pet food would otherwise be waste. While a less traditional means of considering circularity, rendered beef products contribute to pet food production and serve as a key means of avoiding non-essential products and limiting waste (Figure 1). Although the pet-food industry upcycles byproducts into high quality pet food, this upcycling is not currently recognized in product carbon footprint accounting.

Conclusion

Beef production systems contribute to circularity through multiple, interconnected pathways across grazing, feeding, and processing stages of production. The work herein has highlighted the key mechanisms by which beef systems align with the five principles of circularity. Grazing cattle enable the conversion of human-inedible biomass on marginal lands into nutrient-dense food while supporting ecosystem processes such as nutrient cycling, carbon storage, and biodiversity. In intensive feedyard systems, the incorporation of coproducts and byproducts into feedlot rations and the recycling of manure nutrients into cropping systems further enhance resource efficiency. Beyond the farmgate, rendering extends circularity by transforming non-edible portions of the animal into valuable inputs for many other products, including the production of leather, multiple uses of tallow, and pet food, ensuring that a substantial proportion of the animal is retained within productive use.

Collectively, these pathways demonstrate that beef production is not a linear system, but rather a highly integrated network that upcycles biomass, redistributes nutrients, and generates value across multiple sectors. In the context of projected increases in global beef demand, these circular mechanisms represent an important opportunity to support sustainable food production by maximizing the use of available resources and reducing waste across the system.

Despite these contributions, several elements of circularity within beef systems remain incompletely recognized within current sustainability assessment frameworks. Improving the recognition of circularity in beef systems represents a key opportunity to better align sustainability assessments with the biological and industrial realities of beef production. In particular, more holistic accounting approaches that account for co-product flows, nutrient recycling, and cross-sector linkages would provide a more comprehensive representation of circularity in beef systems. Recognizing and appropriately accounting for these circular pathways will be essential for accurately evaluating the environmental performance of beef systems and identifying opportunities to further enhance their contribution to sustainable food production.
