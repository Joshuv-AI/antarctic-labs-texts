A Deep Learning Framework for TTV Characterization: A Case Study of the Kepler-88 System
Chen Chen, Jiapeng Gao, Gongjie Li, Lingkai Kong, Molei Tao

Abstract
Transit timing variation (TTV) provides rich information about the mass and orbital properties of exoplanets, which are often obtained by solving an inverse problem via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). In this paper, we design a new data-driven approach, which potentially can be applied to problems that are hard to traditional MCMC methods, such as the case with only one planet transiting. Specifically, we use a deep learning approach to predict the parameters of non-transit companion for the single transit system with transit information (i.e., TTV, and Transit Duration Variation (TDV)) as input. This approach is constructed using the Transformer -based architecture that can extract long-range interactions from TTV sequential data. We focus on two-planet systems based on Kepler-88 as a starting point, with the inner planet fixed to the properties of Kepler-88 and the mass and orbital elements of the outer planet varied. Applying to Kepler-88, we obtained an overall fractional error of ≲ \lesssim 10% on semi-major axis and eccentricity, and ∼ 10 − 30 % \sim 10-30\% on planet mass (assuming that the stellar and transiting planet parameters are known), based on a training set of a million simulations. When applied to the test set, our model achieves low fractional errors of less than a few percent for systems with TTV semi-amplitudes ranging from ∼ 0.3 \sim 0.3 s to ∼ 5 \sim 5 min. Extending our approach to a broader range of planetary architectures, including systems with substantially weaker or stronger TTV signals and those containing additional planets or stellar companions, remains an important direction for future work.

A Bayesian inverse problem approach is often used to derive the masses and orbital parameters of exoplanets from TTV
[
44
,
27
,
34
,
29

. The idea is to postulate a prior distribution on the parameters, construct a likelihood function based on the planetary dynamics that quantifies the likelihood of observing the TTV data given one set of parameter values, then correct the prior using the observations, i.e. construct a posterior distribution conditioned on the observations, and finally, sample from the posterior to statistically estimate the planetary parameters. However, this approach is computationally expensive, sensitive to the prior distribution, and not always accurate. For example, statistically speaking, the posterior distribution is often multimodal and algorithmically challenging to sample from. Moreover, the likelihood function may not be continuously differentiable, e.g., due to resonant planetary dynamics in our case (see e.g., Fig.
1
), which renders popular gradient-based samplers such as HMC/NUTS
[
24
]
no longer applicable; affine invariant ensemble sampler
[
22
]
may help, but mixing could be slow and thus the posterior sample quality may not be ideal.

In addition, physically speaking, when the system has only one transiting planet, the properties of non-transit planets are even harder to obtain, since the unknown planets require an even larger parameter space to fit for the TTVs.

On the other hand, deep neural networks demonstrated impressive performance across a wide range of application domains. In astronomy, and particularly for the detection of exoplanets, machine learning approaches have been developed to assist the search for transiting exoplanets based directly on transit light curves

2

46

recently extended this approach (mainly) by changing the training data set to those produced by Rebound and TTVFast.

In this paper, we leverage the
Transformer

[

which is more recent than CNN. Transformer can capture long-range dependence in a sequence, specially when the training dataset is large
[
7

Moreover, our method is built such that it uses a synthetic (as opposed to observational) training set (see Sec.
II

The neural network approach is plausible, because we leverage the universal approximation capability, even for less smooth functions, of deep learning models, and indeed promises are demonstrated in finding a trained neural network model that maps TTV, as well as TTV+TDV sequence to planetary parameters.

II
Method

In this work, we focus on two-planet systems, in which one planet transits while the other does not.
The goal is to predict the non-transit planet mass and orbital parameters (
a, e, i
) with the known transit information (transit time, TTV, TDV, average duration, and average orbital period) of the transiting planet as the input for the deep learning model.

To this end, we adopt the following work flow: we first enumerate possible values of the unknown parameters, and calculate the TTV, TDV, average duration and period of the transiting planet corresponding to each parameter set, using the high accuracy simulation package Gravitational Rigid-body Integrators (GRIT)
[
10
]
(note it works for both N-body (point masses) and N-rigid-bodies (planets with nonzero volumes)). The transit information is calculated based on the position of the planet and the size of the star. The result constitutes our training data set, which is however not large enough to contain the actual observed TTV sequence. Then we construct a specialized neural network model, by incorporating the attention mechanism from a powerful deep learning architecture known as Transformer with another popular architecture known as Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU
[
11
]
, which is a variant of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)). The idea is to use GRU to encode the initial input, so that the downstream transformer component has input with better processed information; see Section
II.2
for more details as well as why this hybrid architecture.
Afterward, we train this model with transit information being the input and planetary properties being the output.
Once the model is trained, we provide it with input being the TTV observation data (no longer synthetic), without denoising, and collect the model’s output as the predicted planetary parameters. The error on the testing set (i.e., the data we prepare and not involved in the training process) is shown to demonstrate our model performance (see Section
III.2
).

We discuss the details of data preparation, deep learning model, and training and tuning process in Section
II.1
-
II.3
, respectively.

II.2
Deep Learning Model

II.2.1
Overall Model Architecture

TTVs are sequential data, and some of the most popular models designed for sequential data are Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) and Transformer. Both models have their own benefit: RNN is easier to train at least in terms of hyperparameter tuning, and less data is required. However, it may have difficulty in capturing long term dependencies in the sequence even with improvements such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU). Transformer is more expressive and achieving the state of the art performance in many tasks, e.g., machine translating
[
47
]
. However, it is computationally expensive to train, and performs worse than less complicated models empirically when less data is provided. What’s more, training Transformers can be tricky; for example, it is sensitive to warmup
[
53
]
.
Instead of using just transformer or just RNN, we create a hybrid architecture that combines both, due to 1) relevant prior research, 2) contemporary deep learning intuition and empiricism, and 3) experimental results specific to our task. We will first describe the architecture in the following paragraph, and then elaborate these three reasons a little more.

Our model architecture is illustrated in Figure
3
, which is a multi-task model with multiple decoders. It is mainly constructed by gated recurrent unit (GRU), feed-forward layer (FF), and transformer encoder.
This architecture is inspired by
[
36
]
, in which they constructed a deep learning model for earthquake detection and phase picking. However, we revise their architecture to better fit our problem. Specifically, CNN is not included in our model while we keep the idea of using RNN before transformer. Instead of using LSTM as in
[
36
]
, we replaced it with GRU because Transformer+LSTM generally underperformed the Transformer+GRU architecture in our hyperparameter search, while also requiring slightly greater training resources and a larger model size. The way of processing output from the transformer is also different. Instead of taking the whole sequence, we only select one element from the output sequence of the transformer and then send it to the decoders, which is based on the idea from Vision Transformer
[
14
]
. The transformer encoder is then followed by feed forward layers, which serve as decoders as commonly used in scientific time-series forecasting.
The details of our model architecture are further explained in the following paragraph.

The reasons for our design of this specific hybrid architecture, as briefed above, are: 1)
[
36
]
demonstrated a hybrid architecture successful for earthquake time series data, which shared similarities to the TTV data considered here.
2) Transformer is good at capturing long-range dependencies, but it has weak inductive bias for temporal continuity, is data-hungry, and can be unstable for long, noisy physical signals
[
51
,
54
, e.g.,]
. In contrast, GRU has a strong temporal inductive bias that excels in capturing short-range dependencies. Since planetary dynamics are across multiple timescales, having both local dynamics and global dependencies, a hybrid model like ours would split the job, where GRU handles local temporal evolution and transformer captures long-range/global structure.
In fact, at the initial submission of this paper, (pure) transformer was still arguably the most popular architecture for sequential data; however, as for now (a couple of years later), the machine learning community has gradually shifted to hybrid models
[e.g.,
6
, which is largely based on Mamba+transformer - note Mamba, or state space model in general, is a modern replacement of RNN (including GRU) that was not popularized yet when this paper was submitted]
.
3) We empirically tested pure transformer, transformer+LSTM (a variant of RNN), and transformer+GRU below.

The results are shown in Figure
2
. The pure Transformer performs the worst, distinguishing it from the other two, Transformer+GRU and Transformer+LSTM. The MSE calculated on the validation set is
6.130\times 10^{-5}
for Transformer+GRU, slightly better than LSTM which gives
6.241\times 10^{-5}
. The Transformer+LSTM result we show here is already the best-performing model after searching nearby values of hyperparameters, none of them outperforms the Transformer+GRU model. Moreover, the Transformer+LSTM architecture requires larger training resources and model size (7.32M vs 6.85M parameters) to achieve comparable performance to the Transformer+GRU architecture. Therefore, the Transformer+GRU architecture is more favorable.

II.2.2
Background Information: Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) and Transformer Encoder

In this section, we briefly overview the concepts of GRU and attention mechanism. More details can be found in, e.g.,

The GRU
[
11
]
is a type of recurrent neural network (RNN) that processes the sequential data. The basic idea of an RNN is that it reads the data sequentially and the output at

(t-1)^{th}
step. The GRU is an improved version of the basic RNN (and a simplified version of LSTM
[

]

h

1
.

h_{t}&=(1-z_{t})n_{t}+z_{t}h_{t-1}\end{split}

II.3
Training and Tuning

In order to optimize the hyperparameters of our model, we conduct a grid search and choose hyperparameters that yield a relatively low validation error, defined as the model error calculated on a validation dataset that is held out from the training process. This validation dataset is independent of both the training dataset and the final evaluation dataset and is used only for hyperparameter selection, thereby preventing data leakage between model training, hyperparameter optimization, and final evaluation. We use AdamW
[
32
]
as the optimizer for the training. The learning rate scheduler is linear schedule with warm-up, and the warm-up steps is set to 6% of the total steps. Specifically, the learning rate first linearly increases from zero to a maximum value and then linearly decreases to nearly zero. The loss function we use is mean squared error (MSE) as it is a regression problem.
We train the models with and without TDV separately. For the model without TDV, the model does not predict inclination but still outputs semi-major axis, eccentricity, and mass. The hyperparameters we chose for both models are described in the following.

Regarding the model with TDV, the number of GRU layer (
N_{G}
) is 4, the number of FF for encoding
Input 2
(
N_{F}
) is 2, the number of transformer encoder layer (
N_{T}
) is 3 with 4 heads, and the number of decoder layer (
N_{FD}
) is 4. The feature size is 256 for GRU and transformer encoder. For the FF encoder of
Input 2
, the feature size of first layer is 128, and the second one is 256. For the decoders, the feature size of the first layer is 256, and then each layer is reduced to half of that of previous layer.
We choose a batch size of 64 and train 200 epochs with a maximum learning rate of 0.0008. The weights of FF layers are initialized by uniform Xavier
[
21
]
, and those of GRU layers are by orthogonal initialization. The bias of all layers is initialized as zero.

For the model without TDV, it has 3 layers of GRU (
N_{G}
), 2 layers of FF (
N_{F}
) for the encoder of
Input 2
, 3 layers of transformer encoder (
N_{T}
) with 16 heads, and 3 layers of FF (
N_{F}D
) in each decoder. The feature size of GRU and transformer is 64. Similar to the model with TDV, the first layer of FF encoder for
Input 2
is 32, and the second one is 64. Within the decoders, the first layer has a feature size of 64, and the subsequent layers have a feature size of half of that of their previous layer. We choose the batch size of 64 and train 80 epochs with a maximum learning rate of 0.0008. The layer initialization is the same as the model with TDV. Overall, it takes us one day to tune the hyperparameters and train our model.

III.1
Prediction on Kepler-88

III.1.2
Prediction With Noise

In this section, we consider results including noises in observational data. Assuming that the noise reflects timing measurement uncertainties induced by photometric noise and propagated through the transit fit, we draw the noise independently from a Gaussian distribution for each transit. Specifically, we select data from total dataset with a TTV amplitude larger than 1 minute, and add noise which is randomly generated from a standard normal distribution with a 1 minute standard deviation, which matches the measured per-transit timing precision of Kepler-88. The magnitude of the noise is set primarily by the host-star brightness, the transit depth, and the cadence.

We focus on the model without TDV in the input. The training procedure and the model set-up are the same as that described in Section
II.3
but with different hyperparameters, based on the results of a grid search for the hyperparameters. We train the model for 400 epochs with a batch size of 256 and a maximum learning rate of 0.0008, and select the one with a relatively low error on the evaluation set. The selected model has 4 layers of GRU, 3 layers of transformer encoder with 8 heads, and 4 layers of decoder. The feature size of GRU and transformer encoder is 256.

Table

is 194.9

\sim 12

\sim 0.1\%
) perturbation on the signal.

We note that our model assumes noise sampled independently from a Gaussian distribution and has not been tested against non-Gaussian or correlated noise. In addition, the model’s noise invariance is demonstrated only for the high signal to noise system (Kepler-88), where the timing uncertainties remain substantially smaller than the TTV amplitudes. Many observed systems may have considerably lower signal-to-noise ratios, for which the robustness of the method is not guaranteed. We therefore recommend the ‘noise-free’ model for general applications, while leaving a systematic investigation of low-SNR systems and non-Gaussian, correlated noise to future work.

II.1
, we generate data by running N-body simulation, and the testing set is 1% of total data generated and is not involved in the training process.

IV
Limitations and Scope of Applicability

In this work, we use Kepler-88 as a representative example. Specifically, we fix the inner planet to have the same properties as Kepler-88b, a Neptune-sized planet at
\sim 0.1
AU from a
\sim 1M_{\odot}
star, while varying the properties of the outer planet, with its mass ranging from approximately a Neptune mass to
10,M_{\rm J}
, and its semi-major axis spanning
\sim 0.1
–
5
AU (see Table
1
). The overall dynamics of the planetary system can be scaled with the planetary masses and semi-major-axis separations, and this range of outer planet properties is broad. Thus, the method can be well applied to a wide range of systems. Applying to our testing sample, we find that the fractional error of our prediction generally lies below a few percent for systems with TTV semi-amplitude between
10^{-4}-0.1
hr (
\sim 0.3
sec to
\sim 5
min).

Nevertheless, there are limitations on the applicability of our method. Our analysis assumes a two-planet system orbiting a single star, whereas additional planets or stellar companions can produce more complex TTV signatures. In addition, because we draw the outer planet’s parameters from uniform distributions, our training set does not specifically target systems in or near mean-motion resonances. Consequently, the applicability of our method to planetary systems with architectures substantially different from those considered here is not guaranteed.

Accounting for the full range of possible planetary architectures, including systems with additional planets, planets in or near mean-motion resonances, or stellar companions, would substantially expand the parameter space and make both the system modeling and the generation of training data sets computationally challenging. We therefore restrict our analysis to two-planet systems in this paper with TTV semi-amplitude of
\sim 0.3
sec to
\sim 5
min. Future studies could build on our framework, potentially using transformer-based models, by extending the training set to include a broader range of planetary architectures or focusing on specific targeted systems across different dynamical regimes.

V
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we develop a deep learning model that predicts the parameters (a, mass, e and i) of non-transit planet directly from transit information. We focus on two-planet systems with one planet transiting, although there is no fundamental difficulty in extending the method to cases with more transiting and/or non-transiting (i.e. hidden) planets. A significant part of our demonstration is based on Kepler-88. The predictions on Kepler-88 give a fractional error of 6.5% for
a
, 19.6% for
mass
, 11.5% for
e
and 24.2% for
i
, when the model has TDV in the input. The model without TDV has a slightly larger fractional errors on the predictions of
a
(7.22%) and
mass
(29.5%) while a lower fractional error on
e
(2.81%). On the testing set, our model shows an accuracy with fractional errors less than a few percent for most (
\sim 90\%
) samples with TTV semi-amplitude between
\sim 0.3
sec and
\sim 5
min.
To further illustrate the performance, three specific examples are selected and shown an overall small fractional error of
\sim
1% when the outer companion is massive (1 and 5
M_{J}
) at a further orbital distance of 3 AU. When the outer companion is less massive (20
M_{E}
) and much closer to the host star (0.2 AU), the fractional errors are below
\sim
10% but still considered low.

When the non-transit planet has a further orbital distance and higher mass, the fractional error almost always stays low. However, when the orbital distance is much closer, and mass is lower, the fractional error goes up. It is natural to have this trend using MSE loss function in our training, which leads to similar absolute error across different systems and causes the fractional error to be larger for systems with lower true values. In addition, the dynamics are more complex when the outer planet is closer, making the training harder. Moreover, we note that there are more systems that allow the non-transiting planet to transit when the non-transiting planet is located closer to the host star. Thus, more systems are filtered out when we prepare for the the training sample, and the corresponding sample size is in turn smaller for closer-in non-transiting planets. This also makes it harder to train the model and leads to larger error for non-transiting planets that are located closer to the host star.

Due to these facts, if the system has small values of parameters, it could lead to large error with respect to its true value. To mitigate this situation, several ways could address it.
Firstly, we can generate more data to train our model and (or) desample the data to make it more balanced on the distribution over
a
.
Alternatively, we can narrow down the parameter space given previous results. For instance, if initially we use a large parameter space but the models consistently give a small value, then we could prepare a new training set with a smaller range to fine-tune the model.
Additionally, we could also log-scale the parameters which have a large range, and thus the model would pay more attention to the small value.

One note of the current work is that we fix the parameters of the transit planet for simplicity. In future work, we could try to vary the parameters of transit planet in a small range when preparing the training data set to potentially boost adaptability and enhance accuracy.

We note that different from the traditional MCMC approach that works on individual systems separately, the machine learning approach can be applied to a large number of systems altogether. Moreover, the performance of our method is evaluated via a statistical estimate of the overall accuracy of our predictions for an ensemble of systems. It is a limitation that it is unclear yet, as for most deep learning based approaches, how to obtain a guarantee on prediction accuracy for a specific system. Thus, different from the MCMC approach, our model does not provide a posterior distribution of the parameters. Instead, our model can obtain an initial guess on the hidden planet parameters, which can be useful for following up MCMC analysis.
