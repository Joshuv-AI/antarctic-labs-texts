Rational Adversaries and the Maintenance of Fragility: A Game-Theoretic Theory of Rational Stagnation
Daisuke Hirota

Abstract
Cooperative systems often remain in persistently suboptimal yet stable states. This paper explains such “rational stagnation” as an equilibrium sustained by a rational adversary whose utility follows the principle of potential loss , u D = U ideal − U actual u_{D}=U_{\text{ideal}}-U_{\text{actual}} .Starting from the Prisoner’s Dilemma, we show that the transformation u i ′ = a ​ u i + b ​ u j u_{i}^{\prime}=au_{i}+bu_{j} and the ratio of mutual recognition w = b a w=\frac{b}{a} generate a fragile cooperation band [ w min , w max ] [w_{\min},w_{\max}] where both (C,C) and (D,D) are equilibria. Extending to a dynamic model with stochastic cooperative payoffs R t R_{t} and intervention costs ( C c , C m ) (C_{c},C_{m}) , a generalized Bellman equation yields three strategic regimes— immediate destruction , rational stagnation , and intervention abandonment . The appendix further generalizes the utility into a reference-dependent nonlinear form and proves its stability under reference shifts, ensuring robustness of the framework. Applications to social-media algorithms and political trust illustrate how adversarial rationality can deliberately preserve fragility.

1
Introduction

1.1
Problem Statement and Research Motivation

Modern cooperative systems—from digital networks to political institutions—often exhibit a paradoxical stability: they neither collapse nor achieve evolutionary progress, but instead remain in persistently suboptimal states. This paper argues that such sustained stagnation is not a symptom of irrationality or system malfunction; rather, it is the consequence of a stable equilibrium that is amenable to formal modeling. It further contends that this equilibrium is actively maintained by a form of
strategic rationality
that has hitherto lain outside the formal scope of game theory.

This study models the phenomenon around the concept of the
rational adversary
. A rational adversary is an agent who derives
subjective utility
not from maximizing her own within-game payoff, but from minimizing the aggregate social welfare that other players could otherwise attain. The optimal strategy of such an agent can take diverse forms, ranging from
immediate destruction
of the system to the strategic
maintenance of fragility
.

1.2
Central Research Question

Classical game theory provides a robust framework for analyzing strategic interaction under fixed rule sets. However, this framework typically does not account for agents whose primary utility derives from degrading the cooperative potential of the system itself. This observation motivates the central research question:

How does the introduction of a
rational adversary
—whose payoff is defined by the society-wide
potential loss
—transform the equilibrium and temporal dynamics of
cooperative systems
?

This paper addresses this question by formally modeling what I term
adversarial rationality
. It treats the suppression of cooperation not as an aberration, but as a well-defined equilibrium strategy amenable to formal analysis.

1.3
Contributions and Positioning of This Paper

This paper advances the study of strategic interaction on three levels.

First, a new formalization of adversarial rationality:

This paper introduces a novel category of rational agents, the
suppressive adversary
, whose utility is defined by the
principle of potential loss
(
u_{D}=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
). In this formulation, the agent derives utility precisely from preventing the realization of the system’s latent capacity. This rationality has not been explicitly formalized in prior work. It is distinct in both motivation and strategy from the “value-extractive adversary” in security games and from “evasive adversaries” in adversarial learning.

Second, a new mechanism of sustained stagnation:

Within this theoretical framework, we derive a distinctive dynamic equilibrium termed
rational stagnation
. In this regime, the adversary deliberately forgoes
immediate destruction
in order to harvest larger future “potential loss,” thereby pursuing a forward-looking, speculative strategy. This mechanism marks a clear departure from existing explanations that attribute stagnation to emergent failures of cooperation (e.g., poverty traps) or to conservative strategies aimed at protecting existing benefits (e.g., vested interests). It shows that stagnation can be not merely a system “failure,” but a product of adversarial “governance.”

Third, foundations for a new research program:

By inverting the standard mechanism-design problem, this paper lays the theoretical groundwork for
adversarial mechanism design
. Rather than maximizing social welfare, the question here is how an adversary would design the rules of the game to maximize future manipulability and fragility. This perspective offers a novel analytical lens for understanding how dysfunctional institutions, markets, and political systems can be intentionally designed and perpetuated.

1.4
Methodological Approach and Scope of This Paper

The
rational adversary
introduced here is not an empirical model that literally depicts the psychology of a particular individual or organization. Rather, it is positioned as an analytical metaphor—or ideal type—designed to extract and purify a specific
logic
underlying complex phenomena.

The assumption of an
external adversary
in the model serves as an analytical device. It does not necessarily posit the existence of a single outside actor. Instead, it functions as a theoretical lens for analyzing dynamics oriented toward stagnation that emerge from incentive structures within the system and from the interactions among multiple actors. For example, a platform algorithm that maximizes engagement on social media may, absent any explicit hostility, come to embody the logic of the
suppressive adversary
—an instance of
adversarial logic without hostility
in a
cooperative system
. The aim of this paper is less to propose concrete defensive countermeasures than to offer a diagnosis that enables the structure of the problem to be understood from a new vantage point.

Organization of This Paper

2
Literature Review and Conceptual Framework

2.1
Introduction: Identifying the Conceptual Gap

Classical game theory has compellingly demonstrated how rational choices under fixed rules can yield collectively irrational outcomes.
[
11
,
13
]
However, the scope of that analysis typically presumes a closed system in which the rules of the game and the potential payoffs are exogenously given. This presumption creates a critical blind spot for analyzing real-world
cooperative systems
: namely, the existence of agents who profit not from winning the game, but from keeping the game itself inefficient—deriving gains precisely from the failure of cooperation.

This section demonstrates how the
rational adversary
and
rational stagnation
proposed in this paper occupy a distinct place within existing theoretical families so as to bridge this conceptual gap. First, we provide a systematic taxonomy of existing models of
adversarial rationality
and show that our framework establishes a new category, the
suppressive adversary
. Second, we clarify the formal and conceptual distinctions from other nonstandard utility specifications such as spite and regret.
[
16
,
17
]
Finally, we show that
rational stagnation
constitutes a novel dynamic equilibrium fundamentally different from established mechanisms of stagnation such as vested interests or failures of cooperation.

2.2
A New Taxonomy of Adversarial Rationality

Prior work modeling adversaries can be broadly classified into two categories based on the nature of their objective functions. This paper proposes a third, distinct category.

2.2.1
Value-Extractive Adversaries

Adversaries in this category derive utility from directly acquiring or destroying specific assets or states of the system. A representative example is the family of
security games
developed in the contexts of infrastructure defense and cybersecurity.
[
21
]
In these models, the attacker’s utility typically arises from
direct gains
such as asset destruction or capture, and is defined independently of the system’s
latent capacity
. For instance, in
Stackelberg Security Games (SSG)
, the adversary’s (attacker’s) utility is tied to the
value
of the target asset (e.g., success rewards and capture penalties).
[
20
,
14
]
Likewise, in the
Byzantine Agreement (BA)
problem, classical results establish the limits of safety and resilience that consensus protocols can satisfy, rather than specifying an explicit utility function for the adversary.
[
15
]
In essence, these adversaries seek to
extract
concrete “value” from the system as destroyers.

2.2.2
Evasive Adversaries

Adversaries in this category seek to achieve a separate primary goal while
evading
detection or defense mechanisms. A canonical example is
adversarial machine learning
, particularly
evasion attacks
in spam filtering and related applications.
[
5
]
Here, the adversary’s primary objective (e.g., delivering spam) motivates minimal modifications to inputs that circumvent the classifier, albeit at an explicit
cost
.
[
9
,
18
]
Their utility can thus be formalized as a
trade-off
between achievement of the primary objective (e.g., misclassification or delivery success) and the costs of evasion (e.g., the magnitude of feature changes or the number of manipulable steps).
[
6
]
Moreover, an
evasive
subtype that tempers attack intensity—because the disutility of being detected is comparatively large—can be positioned as a
cautious (risk-averse)
attacker within this class.
[
5
]

2.2.3
Suppressive Adversaries

By contrast, this paper introduces a third category: the
suppressive adversary
. The utility of this adversary is defined by what this paper terms the
principle of potential loss
,
u_{D}=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
. Such an adversary need not steal or destroy anything from the system. Rather, utility arises from the
gap between the ideal and the actual state
—that is, the shortfall from what the system would have achieved under optimal functioning. This captures a fundamental distinction between adversaries who aim to win the game and adversaries who aim to preserve a state in which no one can win. In this sense, the adversary is a
governor
who holds the system’s potential hostage. Hereafter, for convenience, this paper refers to the suppressive adversary simply as the “adversary” or the “
rational adversary
.”

2.3
Conceptual Distinctions from Other Nonstandard Utility Functions

The utility specification in this paper is clearly distinguishable from existing models of nonstandard preferences, both in its formal structure and in the locus of the agent who experiences utility.

2.3.1
Difference from Regret Theory: Locus of Utility

In decision theory, regret theory models the emotion that arises from comparing realized outcomes with the counterfactual outcomes that would have obtained under unchosen alternatives.
[
17
]
Its utility function is sometimes written as
U(x,y)=v(x)-R!\left(v(x)-v(y)\right)
, incorporating the difference between the utility of the chosen act
x
and that of the forgone act
y
. This structure appears, at first glance, to resemble the form
U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
used in this paper.

However, there is a
decisive difference
. In regret theory, regret (and rejoicing) constitutes an
intrapersonal
utility experienced by the
decision maker herself
, who is a player within the game. By contrast, in this paper the potential loss
u_{D}=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
is experienced as
positive utility
by an
external third party
, namely the
rational adversary
. That is, rather than the player introspecting about her own foregone gains, an
external agent monetizes the “system-wide foregone welfare.”
Accordingly, both the
locus of utility
and the
level of the reference object
(individual counterfactual versus a system-level ideal–actual gap) fundamentally differ.

2.3.2
Difference from Models of Spite and Envy: Object of Utility

Prior research on spite and envy has consistently formalized preference structures in which an agent’s utility depends on
others’ payoffs
.
[
16
,
12
]
These models are grounded in fundamentally
relative
concerns that differ from the adversary introduced in this paper.

In
spite
models, an agent’s utility is expressed, for example, as a weighted difference between her own payoff and others’ payoffs.
[
16
]

U_{i}=(1-\alpha_{i})u_{i}-\alpha_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}u_{j}

Here,
U_{i}
is the utility of agent
i
,
u_{i}
is her own payoff, and
\alpha_{i}
parameterizes the degree of spite toward others.

By contrast,
envy
(inequity aversion) models provide a more refined formalization of aversion to
relative allocations
.
[
12
]

U_{i}=u_{i}-\alpha_{i}\max\{u_{j}-u_{i},0\}-\beta_{i}\max\{u_{i}-u_{j},0\}

In this specification, agent
i
’s utility decreases with both disadvantageous inequality (
u_{j}-u_{i}>0
, corresponding to envy) and advantageous inequality (
u_{i}-u_{j}>0
, corresponding to guilt). The ERC model likewise incorporates an agent’s
relative rank
(share) in total payoffs into utility.
[
7
]
Relatedly, work on the “Joy of Destruction” shows that the act of destroying others’ resources can itself yield positive utility.
[
1
]

The
suppressive adversary
, by contrast, is wholly
indifferent
to
relative payoffs among players
. Her utility depends only on the
absolute loss of social surplus
relative to what the system ought to produce under ideal functioning—namely, the following
potential differential
in a two-player (dyadic) system:

{\text{potential differential}}=2R-(u_{A}+u_{B})=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}

Here,
R
is the benchmark payoff each player would obtain under mutual cooperation. Whether
u_{A}>u_{B}
or
u_{B}>u_{A}
is immaterial to the adversary; The adversary prefers that the sum
(u_{A}+u_{B})
be minimized.

It follows that the focus of analysis in this paper shifts away from
interpersonal competition and relative standing
toward
strategies that intentionally maintain or amplify system-wide dysfunction
.

2.4
Comparison Between Rational Stagnation and Existing Mechanisms of Stagnation

The
rational stagnation
derived in this paper occupies a distinct position among existing theoretical accounts that explain the persistence of suboptimal equilibria.

2.4.1
Difference from Vested-Interests Models: Motivation and Temporal Horizon

Public choice theory explains the persistence of inefficient institutions by the presence of
vested interests
.
[
2
]
Vested actors resist reforms that threaten the flow of benefits they derive from the status quo.

The decisive difference between those models and the present framework lies in the source of benefits and the temporal outlook of strategic behavior. The motivation of vested interests is essentially conservative and backward-looking: their rents are drawn from the current stream of realized benefits,
U_{\text{actual}}
, and their objective is to preserve that flow.

By contrast, the
rational adversary
in this paper is speculative and forward-looking. Her gains are given by the very gap between the ideal and the actual—
U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
. The adversary maintains fragility in order to cultivate the possibility of future losses; that is, to maximize
E[U_{ideal}(t+1)-U_{actual}(t+1)]
. This strategy may even welcome the system’s growth potential, because a higher
U_{\text{ideal}}
enlarges the harvestable
potential loss
.

2.4.2
Difference from Poverty-Trap Models: Nature of the Mechanism

A poverty trap denotes a structural mechanism by which poverty as an initial condition is self-reinforcing and reproduced over time.
[
4
,
10
]
In game-theoretic terms, it can be described as a failure of cooperation whereby each agent, taking others’ investment levels as given, lacks the incentive to unilaterally move to the high equilibrium, so that the aggregate settles at a Pareto-inferior low equilibrium.
[
8
,
3
,
19
]
This form of stagnation is an
autonomously emergent equilibrium
arising from decentralized interactions among actors; it does not posit intentional control by a specific external actor.

By contrast,
rational stagnation
models a situation in which such an endogenous stagnation structure is
superimposed by exogenous intervention
. Specifically, a
suppressive adversary
actively suppresses the system’s latent growth capacity, thereby
maintaining and amplifying
the self-reinforcing structure characteristic of poverty traps. Stagnation is no longer a merely incidental outcome; it is stabilized as a
strategically preserved low-level equilibrium
.

2.5
Conclusion: The Redefined Conceptual Gap

On the basis of the foregoing analysis, the conceptual lacuna addressed by this paper can be redefined as follows. Prior research has theorized primarily: (1) value-extractive adversaries targeting specific assets (security games); (2) internal players focused on relative payoffs or affective rewards (models of spite, envy, and regret); (3) conservative strategies aimed at preserving realized benefits (vested-interests models); and (4) emergent stagnation driven by bounded rationality or informational constraints (poverty traps).

While each of these strands has made important contributions to explaining real-world inefficiencies, none has explicitly modeled a rational agent who
intentionally suppresses the system’s potential efficiency
—that is, its
latent capacity
—and
derives utility from the unrealized portion
of social surplus over time; in short, the
suppressive adversary
that
intertemporally monetizes
the “ideal–actual gap.”

This paper addresses that theoretical void by introducing the
principle of potential loss
and a
dynamic optimization
framework, thereby offering the first formalization of the rational maintenance of stagnation.

Analytical Results

3.1
Model Formulation

As the analytical foundation, consider the classical Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD) with players
A
and
B
, parameterized by
T>R>P>S
. The socially optimal outcome is the cooperative equilibrium
(C,C)
, which yields the total payoff
U_{\text{ideal}}=2R
, whereas the static Nash equilibrium is the defection profile
(D,D)
, which yields
U_{\text{actual}}=2P
.

Introduce an external
rational adversary

D
. The adversary influences the game by manipulating the players’
subjective utility
functions. For each player
i\in\{A,B\}
, the transformed utility is given by

u^{\prime}_{i}=au_{i}+bu_{j},\quad(i\neq j,a>0,b\geq 0)

(1)

where
u_{i}
denotes the objective payoff in the original PD matrix. The
ratio of mutual recognition (w)
, defined by
w=b/a
, serves as a proxy for the degree of interpersonal identification. The equilibrium of the transformed game depends on
w
. As shown in Appendix
A
, there exist critical thresholds
w_{\min}
and
w_{\max}
such that equilibrium states fall into three phases:

•

w<w_{\min}
: the unique equilibrium is
(D,D)
(distrust phase).

•

w_{\min}\leq w\leq w_{\max}
: both
(C,C)
and
(D,D)
are Nash equilibria (the
fragile cooperation band
).

•

w>w_{\max}
: the unique equilibrium is
(C,C)
(cooperation phase).

The adversary’s utility is defined by the
principle of potential loss
and is tied to the players’ objective outcomes:

u_{D}=U_{\text{ideal}}-U_{\text{actual}}=2R-(u_{A}+u_{B})

(2)

The adversary’s objective is to manipulate the psychological parameters
(a,b)
so as to induce an equilibrium that minimizes
U_{\text{actual}}
.

Remark (Emergence of Asymmetric Equilibria)

As shown in Appendix
A.2
,
w_{\min}=\frac{T-R}{R-S}
and
w_{\max}=\frac{P-S}{T-P}
. Hence, when
w_{\min}\leq w_{\max}
, both
(C,C)
and
(D,D)
are Nash equilibria, and the so-called
dual equilibrium band
obtains. By contrast, when
w_{\min}>w_{\max}
, this band vanishes and the equilibrium converges exclusively to the asymmetric profiles
(C,D)
or
(D,C)
. In this case, the game is equivalent to the canonical Hawk–Dove type, implying that the model’s dual equilibrium band constitutes a critical structure that transitions to asymmetric equilibria once the
ratio of mutual recognition

w
becomes sufficiently unbalanced.

3.2
General Theory of Adversarial Strategy

To determine the optimal strategy of the adversary in repeated games, we construct a general model that incorporates more realistic elements (for the rigorous formulation, see Appendix
A.4
). This model endogenizes two features: (1) the cooperative payoff
R_{t}
may fluctuate stochastically (both upward and downward), and (2) the adversary’s intervention entails a cost
C
.

From this general model, we derive the following Theorem that governs the adversary’s optimal strategy.

Theorem 3.2.1

(General Theorem on the Adversary’s Optimal Strategy)
.

In a repeated game, the optimal strategy of a
rational adversary
is determined by the trade-off among the system’s expected future growth, its intrinsic risk of collapse, and the adversary’s intervention cost. This trade-off gives rise to three
strategic regimes
:

1.

Immediate destruction
: When the expected growth rate of the cooperative surplus is low or the intervention cost is sufficiently small, the adversary’s optimal strategy is to collapse the system immediately so as to maximize short-term gains.

2.

Rational stagnation
: When the expected growth rate of the cooperative surplus is sufficiently high and the cost of maintaining fragility is reasonable, the adversary’s optimal strategy is to delay immediate destruction in order to secure larger future gains, keeping the system within the
fragile cooperation band
.

3.

Intervention abandonment
: When intervention costs exceed any anticipated gains, the adversary refrains from intervening.

Proof.

A sketch of the proof is based on the analysis of the generalized
Bellman equation
presented in Appendix
A.4
.
∎

This Theorem shows that the paper’s central contribution—
rational stagnation
—is not a phenomenon confined to special conditions, but a robust equilibrium that can emerge over a broad parameter space. In this formulation, the stopping value assumes that the post-collapse outcome is the absorbing state
(D,D)
. Under this assumption we have the expected future value
V_{t+1}=0
, and the stopping term
(2R_{t}-2P)-C_{c}(t)
is defined as the period-
t
realized loss (see Appendix
6
).

3.3
Analysis of Rational Stagnation under Ideal Conditions

To clarify the implications of Theorem
3.2.1
, consider an ideal environment in which intervention costs are zero and cooperation invariably engenders learning.

Postulate 3.3.1

(Postulate of Cooperative Learning)
.

Assume a socio-economic environment in which repeated cooperation promotes learning and efficiency, yielding a nondecreasing cooperative payoff
R_{t+1}\geq R_{t}
.

Under these ideal conditions, Theorem
3.2.1
yields the following sharper conclusion.

Corollary 3.3.2

(Sufficient Condition for Rational Stagnation)
.

In an environment that satisfies the Postulate of Cooperative Learning (Postulate
3.3.1
) with zero intervention costs, the optimal strategy for a sufficiently patient adversary (with a high discount factor
\delta
) is always
rational stagnation
.

Specifically, the adversary delays collapse as long as the growth rate
g_{t}
of the
cooperative surplus

\Phi_{t}=2R_{t}-2P
exceeds her discount rate, i.e., as long as

\delta>\frac{1}{1+g_{t}}.

(3)

Proof.

This corollary follows directly from the generalized
Bellman equation
underlying Theorem
3.2.1
by setting
C_{c}=C_{m}=0
and imposing
E_{t}[R_{t+1}]\geq R_{t}
(see Appendix
A.4
).
∎

This corollary distills the central phenomenon analyzed in this paper: the mechanism by which the adversary transforms from a pure destroyer into a
governor of unrealized potential
.

4
Discussion: Applications and Interpretations

4.1
Introduction

The previous section established a formal theory of
adversarial rationality
and derived a general Theorem (Theorem
3.2.1
) characterizing the rational adversary’s optimal strategy. The Theorem showed that the adversary’s behavior is determined by a trade-off among the expected future growth of the cooperative surplus, the system’s intrinsic instability, and the cost of
adversarial intervention
. As a result, three
strategic regimes
emerge:
immediate destruction
,
rational stagnation
, and
abandonment of intervention
.

This section applies the general theory to social phenomena and assesses its explanatory power and observational implications. The focus here is the
empirical interpretation
of the adversary’s utility function

u_{D}=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}

defined in the previous section. The ideal (
U_{\text{ideal}}
) is defined as the best attainable state of stability, trust, and cooperation in the system. Empirically, it may be instantiated by the maximum values on satisfaction or trust indices, or by institutionally specified optimal benchmarks. By contrast, the actual (
U_{\text{actual}}
) is the realized measurement, a dynamic variable that reflects social noise and institutional frictions. The difference
U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
simultaneously quantifies the health of a
cooperative system
and serves as an empirical variable capturing the remaining scope for
adversarial intervention
.

Moreover, the theoretical thresholds
w_{\min}
and
w_{\max}
for the
ratio of mutual recognition
are, in real societies, observed not as single tipping points but as an
empirical tipping band
. That is, there exists a range over which the gradients of trust, cooperation, and exit (churn) rates change markedly, revealing a probabilistic zone that separates stability from instability. Within this band, the actions of players and the adversary are mutually adjusted, and
rational stagnation
is sustained as an equilibrium.

Building on this observational framework, this section presents two case studies.

Case 1: Adversarial rationality in social media.
Here, a healthy and trustworthy deliberative space constitutes the ideal (
U_{\text{ideal}}
), whereas polarization, misinformation, and conflict define the actual (
U_{\text{actual}}
). Platform-internal algorithms, oriented toward engagement maximization, manipulate the balance between cooperation and conflict, maintaining instability short of full collapse. This structure demonstrates that
rational stagnation
can arise not as a by-product of irrational behavior, but as the outcome of a
strategic rationality
embedded in a rational objective function.

Case 2: Meta-game intervention in political and institutional systems.
In this context, societal trust in the state or institutions constitutes the ideal (
U_{\text{ideal}}
), while realized trust scores and political-stability indicators constitute the actual (
U_{\text{actual}}
). External players (states, media, or other strategic actors) intervene in the trust architecture of rival polities to maximize their own payoffs. The aim is not necessarily
immediate destruction
, but rather to keep the rival’s growth potential and trust level
within a manageable range
so as to secure competitive advantage. In this way, the
meta-game
appears as a hierarchical structure in which multiple layers of rational players influence one another.

Through these two cases, the section situates
rational stagnation
as an
observable equilibrium structure
in social and institutional domains. Phenomena such as stagnation, polarization, and impasse are thus understood not as side effects of irrationality, but as the outcomes of
rational intervention by a suppressive adversary
.

4.2
Definitions of the Ideal and the Actual, and an Observational Framework for Intervention Variables

The utility function at the core of this paper’s theory,
u_{D}=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
, is not a mere abstraction. This difference is defined as a gap between
observable
social states, thereby endowing the theory with empirical testability. This section clarifies the observational framework and lays the groundwork for the analyses in the subsequent case studies.

Ideal (
U_{\text{ideal}}
)
is defined as the structurally optimal state of each system—namely, the
expected value when human and institutional stability and trust function fully
. In practice, it is often fixed as a normative upper bound or as the maximum on particular survey indices.

Actual (
U_{\text{actual}}
)
is the currently observed measurement. Whereas the ideal is a static benchmark, the actual is a dynamically fluctuating variable.

The adversary’s strategic objective is not to alter the ideal itself (which is often institutionally fixed and thus not manipulable), but to
intentionally widen the gap
between the ideal and the actual. Accordingly,
adversarial intervention
consists in
controlling the set of “actual-side” variables that generate the ideal–actual gap
. These variables function as empirically identifiable
intervention levers
, to be operationalized through social surveys and data analysis.

4.3
Ambiguous Tipping Points and the Probabilistic Structure of Rational Stagnation

Real social systems seldom transition at sharp thresholds. Rather, tipping points are probabilistically ambiguous and appear as
tipping bands
in which multiple local equilibria overlap. As observed in institutional or community satisfaction surveys, trust indices, and exit (churn) rates, the empirical distribution of
exit, switching, and stability
is continuous, and individual decisions are subject to noise.

Consequently, the deterministic thresholds
w_{\min}
and
w_{\max}
in the theoretical model should be interpreted, for practical purposes, as
empirical tipping bands
. Concretely, one identifies parameter ranges over which the gradients of exit rates or trust indices change markedly, and sets those intervals empirically as

[w_{\min}^{-},w_{\min}^{+}]\quad\text{and}\quad[w_{\max}^{-},w_{\max}^{+}].

Such fuzzy boundary settings allow us to treat tipping as a smooth probabilistic process, thereby enhancing the real-world plausibility of the theory in analyses of social stability.

Within this framework,
rational stagnation
can be understood as a probabilistic equilibrium sustained
inside
the tipping band. The
rational adversary
seeks to keep the actual state
U_{\text{actual}}
separated from the ideal
U_{\text{ideal}}
while maintaining the probability of collapse at sufficiently low levels. Institutions and communities, for their part, act in a self-correcting manner to avoid collapse. The result is a
persistent yet unstable
state. Empirically, this equilibrium manifests as slow fluctuations within the tipping band, consistent with the equilibrium logic of adversarial intervention.

4.4
Case Study 1: Platform Algorithms as an Endogenous Adversary

4.4.1
Modeling Assumptions

We model a social-media platform as a
meta-player
in a repeated game played by its users.

1.

Players and cooperative payoff:
The players are platform users. When they share truthful, high-quality information and engage in constructive deliberation, a collective
cooperative payoff

R_{t}
(shared understanding, networks of trust, and social capital) is generated.

2.

Adversary and utility function:
The
adversary
is the platform’s algorithmic architecture itself. Its utility (i.e., platform revenue) is not a direct function of
R_{t}
, but is maximized by user
engagement
(time-on-platform, interactions).

3.

Engagement maximization:
Engagement is assumed to be maximized in environments where disagreement persistently arises.

•

Cooperative/stable state (
w>w_{\max}
):
A state dominated by stable agreement and trust exhibits high predictability and weak incentives for users to post rebuttals, corrections, or identity-affirming content. This implies lower engagement.

•

Fragile cooperation band (
w_{\min}\leq w\leq w_{\max}
):
An unstable coexistence of cooperation and conflict most strongly amplifies user interaction and maximizes engagement.

4.

Intervention costs and risk:
The adversary’s
intervention cost

C_{m}(t)
is understood as a composite
risk-management
cost required to maintain fragility. Excessive polarization (
w<w_{\min}
) triggers firestorms, the spread of hate speech, and public controversies, risking mass user exit and regulatory sanctions. Expenditures on content moderation, public relations, and lobbying are included in these costs.

4.4.2
Ideal and Actual in the SNS Model

In social media, the
ideal
(
U_{\text{ideal}}
) is the
maintenance of a healthy and trustworthy deliberative space
. Operationally, this can be defined as attaining the maximum values on user-satisfaction or interpersonal-trust indices. The ideal represents the best social state the platform could achieve—stable cooperative networks, low misinformation, and a healthy dialogic structure.

By contrast, the
actual
(
U_{\text{actual}}
) is defined by the
observed prevalence
of polarization, fake news, and conflict relative to these ideal indicators. Concrete data include average survey scores, trust indices, counts of large-scale “firestorms,” and misinformation diffusion rates. These variables permit a quantitative measurement of deviation from the ideal, and the adversary (the algorithm) maximizes engagement by
controlling this deviation
.

Accordingly, the “actual-side” variables function as
intervention levers
for the adversary. Their principal dimensions are pairs such as cooperation vs. division, trust vs. distrust, and truth vs. misinformation. By shifting the equilibrium among these paired dimensions, the algorithm induces coexistence of conflict and harmony—i.e., the
fragile cooperation band
(
w_{\min}\leq w\leq w_{\max}
). A
rational
algorithm manipulates these variables while avoiding collapse (
w<w_{\min}
), thereby sustaining
rational stagnation
.

4.4.3
Equilibrium of Rational Stagnation

Under this parameterization, Theorem
3.2.1
yields a clear prediction for the algorithm’s (adversary’s) optimal strategy.

•

Rejection of
immediate destruction
:
Driving the platform into collapse (moving the state to
w<w_{\min}
) destroys the user base and revenue sources and invites legal intervention; it is therefore not optimal.

•

Rejection of
intervention abandonment
:
Neutralizing the algorithm risks allowing the community to drift toward a stable cooperative state (
w>w_{\max}
), naturally reducing engagement; this is unattractive.

•

Selection of
rational stagnation
:
The optimal strategy is to pay ongoing risk-management costs
C_{m}(t)
sufficient to avoid catastrophic collapse, while maintaining the conflict structure that maximizes engagement within the
fragile cooperation band
(
w_{\min}\leq w\leq w_{\max}
).

This model explains why engagement-maximizing algorithms neither fully eliminate misinformation nor precipitate wholesale network collapse. The observed polarization and epistemic degradation are not unintended chaos; rather, they are a
stable equilibrium
rationally maintained as the calculated outcome of a trade-off between profitability and risk management.

4.5
Case Study 2: Meta-game Intervention by an Exogenous Adversary

4.5.1
Modeling Assumptions: A Hierarchical Game

We now treat the situation in Case Study 1 as a subgame and introduce external players who play a higher-level
meta-game
.

1.

Structure of the meta-game:
At the upper layer, players such as states and political organizations compete in a meta-game where payoffs consist of geopolitical influence and advantages in domestic politics.

2.

Intervention into the subgame:
Players in the meta-game (the
exogenous adversaries
) intervene in lower-layer games (specific online communities) as a tactic to achieve their own objectives.

3.

Cooperative surplus and the adversary’s utility:
The cooperative surplus
R_{t}
of the target community (trust, cohesion, and collective problem-solving capacity) constitutes part of the rival’s state capacity and support base in the meta-game. Accordingly, the exogenous adversary’s utility is defined by the community’s
potential loss
,
U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
. Community dysfunction (a decrease in
U_{\text{actual}}
) weakens the rival and thus yields direct gains to the adversary in the meta-game.

4.

Low-cost attacks:
By exploiting the Internet’s
hyper-public
character and the fact that target communities are
open yet clustered
due to language and platform segmentation, adversaries can operate at very low intervention cost
C_{m}(t)
. Signal amplification by bots and the injection of misinformation are cheaper and less risky than physical covert operations.

4.5.2
Ideal and Actual in the Meta-game Model

The definitional structure of the
ideal
and the
actual
extends isomorphically to the meta-game with an exogenous adversary. Here, the
ideal
(
U_{\text{ideal}}
) is the state in which societal trust in the targeted polity or institution is fully maintained—operationally, “
100\%
on trust surveys.” This corresponds to the maximal point of institutional stability and public confidence and can be specified clearly as a numeric upper bound grounded in observed survey data, rather than as a purely abstract notion of perfect cooperation.

The
actual
(
U_{\text{actual}}
) is the realized level of societal trust measured by surveys, observed as the shortfall from the ideal. The adversary’s aim is not to lower the ideal benchmark but to
micro-manage the actual
so as to keep society within the region where
full trust recovery is thwarted yet collapse is avoided
, i.e., within the
fragile cooperation band

w_{\min}\leq w\leq w_{\max}
.

Intervention operates through observables that co-vary with trust. Examples include the volume of coverage that heightens perceptions of political distrust, narratives that amplify economic insecurity, and emotionally charged information flows that intensify social division. These variables are statistically correlated with trust scores; by means of information operations and the staging of economic/diplomatic events, the adversary can subtly perturb these correlates and thereby steer societal trust.

In this setting, the gap
U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
becomes the key indicator linking a system’s
latent health
to the
intensity of adversarial intervention
. The ideal is an institutionally fixed benchmark; the actual is a fluctuating observable; and the intervention variables are empirically measurable correlates. This structure renders the dynamics of social and political intervention amenable to formal, testable analysis.

4.5.3
Equilibrium of Rational Stagnation

Within this hierarchical game, Theorem
3.2.1
again predicts the exogenous adversary’s optimal strategy.

•

Rejection of
immediate destruction
:
Driving the target community into complete collapse (moving to
w<w_{\min}
) inflicts, at best, a one-off blow on the rival. Moreover, unintended regime shocks may destabilize the meta-game itself, creating uncontrollable risks.

•

Rejection of
intervention abandonment
:
Allowing the community to recover a stable cooperative state (
w>w_{\max}
) advantages the rival in the meta-game and is therefore not an option.

•

Selection of
rational stagnation
:
The optimal strategy is to avoid outright destruction while keeping persistent conflict and distrust smoldering within the
fragile cooperation band
(
w_{\min}\leq w\leq w_{\max}
). In this way, the adversary can, at low cost and low risk, continuously erode the rival’s state capacity and social foundations,
maximizing long-run strategic returns denominated in “potential loss.”

4.5.4
Cultivating Fragility: The Rational Adversary’s Paradoxical Support

The theory yields a more refined strategic implication. The adversary’s goal is not merely to produce stagnation. To maximize
u_{D}=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
, it can be
desirable
that the system’s
cooperative surplus

R_{t}

grows
. The higher the expected growth rate of
R_{t}
, the larger the future harvestable
potential loss
in expectation.

This logic predicts seemingly paradoxical behavior: the most
meta-rational
player may
stoke division
so as to keep the community in the
fragile cooperation band
(
w_{\min}\leq w_{\max}
) while
quietly supporting the community’s potential growth
(e.g., promoting user-base expansion or stimulating economic activity). For instance, the adversary might anonymously support infrastructure or seed information that attracts new members.

This strategy—best described as advanced
vulnerability farming
—is distinct from mere sabotage. The adversary preserves instruments of intervention to prevent the community from achieving autonomous stability (
w>w_{\max}
), while
raising its latent value
so as to maximize extractable strategic gains over time. This is fully consistent with Theorem
3.2.1
, which implies that
rational stagnation
is chosen precisely when the system’s expected growth is high. The adversary is thus both a destroyer and, ironically, the system’s most attentive watcher of its potential.

5
Conclusion and Future Directions

5.1
Summary of Theoretical Contributions

This study extends the classical game-theoretic paradigm by introducing and formalizing the concept of the
rational adversary
. By defining the adversary’s utility through the
principle of potential loss
(
u_{D}=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
), the paper shows that inefficiency in
cooperative systems
can exist not merely as malfunction, but as an equilibrium state
actively maintained
by a rational agent—namely,
rational stagnation
.

The central result is the general theory, stated in Theorem
3.2.1
, that characterizes the adversary’s optimal strategy. The theorem formally derives a tripartite regime—
immediate destruction
,
rational stagnation
, and
abandonment of intervention
—as a function of the system’s expected growth, intrinsic risk, and intervention costs. Among these,
rational stagnation
is a forward-looking, speculative strategy that recognizes the system’s latent growth while suppressing its realization; it is thus clearly distinct from the conservative and static behavior posited in vested-interests models.

5.2
Implications for AI and Institutional Design

This theory carries the following implications for AI alignment and institutional design.

Implications for AI systems:

AI systems designed to optimize proxy metrics in complex social environments (e.g., user engagement) can
endogenously
develop the strategic structure analyzed here for the
rational adversary
. That is, even without explicit hostility, the
maintenance of fragility
within a controllable range may become optimal relative to the objective function. This perspective reframes phenomena such as “specification gaming” and “reward hacking” in AI alignment not merely as design flaws but as the
rational
consequences of optimization.

Implications for institutional design:

If the adversary’s strategy suppresses the
cooperative surplus
(
R_{t}
), then the planner’s optimal response is to design institutional rules that
maximize the growth rate
of cooperation. Robust institutional design can thus be formalized as a
counter-optimization problem
that minimizes the payoffs to adversarial stagnation strategies. From this standpoint, a “resilient institution” is not merely stable; it is one whose design maximizes the expected gains from cooperative learning while structurally reducing the profitability of
maintenance of fragility
by a
rational adversary
.

5.3
Theoretical Extensions: Structural Adversaries and Adversarial Mechanism Design

This paper analyzed the rationality of a
psychological adversary
who operates within an existing rule system by manipulating beliefs. A key direction for future theory is to treat the rationality of a
structural adversary
—an agent capable of manipulating the rules themselves.

The structural adversary’s objective is to modify the system-wide payoff structure
(T,R,P,S)
in ways that
institutionally extend
the effective domain of psychological manipulation. The present framework offers a foundation for understanding why such structural interventions can be rational. By quantifying the tactical value of belief manipulation, it opens a path to analyze institutional design itself as an object of rational intervention.

This agenda constitutes a research program that may aptly be called
adversarial mechanism design
: a theoretical framework that describes how a rational agent—whose objective is not the maximization of social welfare but the maximization of future
manipulability and fragility
—can design or revise institutions. This paper provides the foundational theoretical structure for that program.

5.4
Limitations and Assumptions of the Theory

The model of
rational stagnation
presented in this paper offers a unified representation of intervention dynamics in adversarial games through the differential utility between the ideal and the actual. While the framework is extendable to diverse settings, its scope is bounded by the following key limitations and assumptions.

(1) Definition of the Ideal and Normative Premises

The ideal utility
U_{\text{ideal}}
is a normatively determined upper bound that depends on institutions and culture, and its specification may involve an element of arbitrariness. Although this paper mitigates arbitrariness by using observed upper bounds, the assumption that the ideal reflects ethical or social “good” cannot be entirely eliminated. See also the reference-dependent generalization in Appendix
A
, §
A.6
, where shift-stability is established (Proposition
A.6.1
).

(2) Directionality of the Differential and Identification

The structure
u_{D}=U_{ideal}-U_{actual}
treats the
reduction
of the gap as rational behavior, yet when the ideal itself moves upward (e.g., rising expectations), improvements in the actual may not reduce the gap. It is therefore necessary to observe the ideal and the actual independently and to define explicitly the meaning of their difference.
In Appendix
A.6
, Proposition
A.6.1
shows a quantitative
shift-stability
: regime decisions are preserved whenever the margin of the boundary inequalities exceeds
\frac{L_{\gamma,i}L|\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}|}{1-\delta}
.

(3) Time Lags and Dynamic Stability

Changes in
U_{actual}(t)
exhibit inertia and delay, so instantaneous differentials alone may be insufficient to explain stability. To address this, the analysis introduces a generalized
Bellman equation
that endogenizes the discount factor
\delta
and the maintenance-cost structure
C_{m}(t)
. Nonetheless, empirical identification of these parameters remains limited. For mass simultaneous interventions, Appendix
A.7
gives a one-line macro dynamics with a local stability threshold based on
J_{t}:=1+G_{t}-\rho
(Proposition
A.7.1
).

(4) Bounded Rationality and Nonlinear Preferences

Nonlinear sensitivities among players are captured endogenously by a function
F(w)
. Under the assumption
F^{\prime}(w)\geq 0
, the model can reproduce diminishing empathy and tipping effects without explicitly imposing bounded rationality. However, the functional form of
F(w)
is context-dependent, and identifying a general form empirically is nontrivial. Complementarily, the difference-based payoff in Appendix
A.6
accommodates asymmetric nonlinear sensitivities via
(\beta_{i}^{\pm},\gamma_{i}^{\pm})
without changing the regime logic.

(5) Testability and Domain of Application

For empirical validation, each domain requires clear definitions and calibration of:

•

the observational indicators of the ideal utility
U_{\text{ideal}}

•

the realized measurements of the actual utility
U_{\text{actual}}

•

proxy variables for the
ratio of mutual recognition

w=b/a

•

(for mass interventions) calibration of
(\beta^{\pm},\gamma^{\pm})
, sensitivity
\eta
, diffusion gain
\kappa
, and damping
\rho
(Appendix
A.7
)

These steps ensure that the theory avoids tautology and remains
falsifiable
.

In sum, while grounded in the simple structure of an
ideal–actual
differential, the framework is general enough to incorporate dynamics, nonlinearity, multiple agents, and bounded rationality. Applications should, however, proceed with awareness of the normative definition of the ideal and the limits of empirical estimation. Accordingly, this paper proposes not a universal theory but a
general model as a minimal, empirically testable structure
.

5.5
Conclusion

The theory of the
rational adversary
offers a new analytical framework for explaining why
cooperative systems
fail to realize their full potential. As shown in this paper, persistent stagnation can be a
strategically stable state
maintained rationally rather than a mere dysfunction. In environments where
maintenance of fragility
serves as a lever of control, stagnation can become a rational choice.

By formalizing this theory, phenomena previously labeled as “irrational failures” can be analyzed as
rational strategies
. In any social or epistemic system, the ultimate task is not merely to sustain cooperation, but to establish
institutional designs that protect the processes of learning and cooperation from a rational adversary
.

A.1
Formal Definition of the Adversarial Game

Consider a three-player game
G=(\{A,B\},D,\{u_{A},u_{B}\},u_{D})
with standard players
A
and
B
and an
adversary

D
. The objective payoff functions of players
A
and
B
follow the classical Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD) matrix parameterized by
T>R>P>S
.

Each player’s
subjective utility

u^{\prime}_{i}
is altered by the adversary via psychological coefficients
(a,b)
:

u^{\prime}_{i}=au_{i}+bu_{j},\quad(i\neq j,a>0,b\geq 0).

(4)

The adversary’s utility
u_{D}
is defined by the
principle of potential loss
and depends on the players’ objective outcomes:

u_{D}=U_{\text{ideal}}-U_{\text{actual}}=2R-(u_{A}+u_{B}).

(5)

A.2
Derivation of Equilibrium Conditions and the Fragile Cooperation Band

Under the transformed utilities in (
4
), Nash equilibrium conditions follow from best-response analysis:

•

(C,C)
is a Nash equilibrium
\iff a(R-T)+b(R-S)\geq 0\iff w\geq\frac{T-R}{R-S}=:w_{\min}
,

•

(D,D)
is a Nash equilibrium
\iff a(P-S)-b(T-P)\geq 0\iff w\leq\frac{P-S}{T-P}=:w_{\max}
,

where
w=b/a
denotes the
ratio of mutual recognition
. A necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of the
dual equilibrium band
(i.e., the interval on which both
(C,C)
and
(D,D)
are equilibria, coinciding with the
fragile cooperation band
) is
w_{\min}\leq w_{\max}
, which is equivalent to
(T-R)(T-P)\leq(P-S)(R-S)
.

A.3
Static Analysis (One-Shot Game)

In a one-shot interaction, the adversary’s utility is maximized when the sum
(u_{A}+u_{B})
is minimized.
Under the PD payoff ordering
T>R>P>S
, the minimum attainable total payoff is

\min\{\,2P,\;T+S\,\}.

Hence, when
2P\leq T+S
, the profile
(D,D)
attains the minimum total payoff; when
T+S<2P
, an asymmetric outcome
(C,D)
or
(D,C)
does so.
In the baseline specification of this paper, we maintain symmetry by assuming
T+S\geq 2P
.

A.4
General Dynamic Model: Intervention Costs and Stochastic Payoffs

We construct a more general dynamic model that relaxes the
Postulate of Cooperative Learning
and endogenizes intervention costs. Let the discount factor satisfy
0<\delta<1
.

Constituent elements of the model

•

Stochastic cooperative payoff:
The cooperative payoff
R_{t}
at time
t
is assumed to fluctuate probabilistically in the next period. Let
E_{t}[R_{t+1}]
denote the expectation of
R_{t+1}
at time
t
.

•

Intervention costs:
The adversary incurs costs when taking actions at time
t

–

C_{c}(t)
: cost of
collapse induction
(e.g., expenditures on a disinformation campaign),

–

C_{m}(t)
: cost of
maintenance of fragility
(e.g., ongoing monitoring and interventions required to sustain social division).

Supplement to the derivation of Corollary
3.3.2

Define the
cooperative surplus

\Phi_{t}:=2R_{t}-2P\geq 0
, and let its one-period growth be

\Phi_{t+1}=(1+g_{t})\Phi_{t}
(with
g_{t}>-1

C_{c}=C_{m}=0
and comparing the

\text{Continuation is preferred}\;\Longleftarrow\;\delta\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Phi_{t+1}]>\Phi_{t}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\delta\,(1+g_{t})\,\Phi_{t}>\Phi_{t}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\delta>\frac{1}{1+g_{t}}.

This is a sufficient condition; even under dynamic optimization (with
V_{t+1}\geq\Phi_{t+1}
), the same inequality supports a preference for continuation.

Generalized Bellman Equation

Let
V_{t}
denote the
value function
for the adversary at time
t

stopping (collapse induction)
and
continuing (maintenance of fragility)

V_{t}=\max\left\{\underbrace{(2R_{t}-2P)-C_{c}(t)}_{\text{stop}},\;\underbrace{\delta E_{t}[V_{t+1}]-C_{m}(t)}_{\text{continue}}\right\}.

(6)

This equation shows that the
rational adversary
weighs the immediate net payoff against the discounted
expected future value
of net payoffs.

Reference-dependent extension.

A unified, difference-based payoff (including positive/negative sensitivity and mass simultaneous interventions)
is developed in Appendix

§
A.6
–§
A.7

Assumption of an Absorbing Post-Collapse State

If the adversary stops (induces collapse) at time

\mathsf{Collapse}
in which
A
and
B
subsequently repeat
(D,D)
, fixing the objective total payoff permanently at

. The adversary’s realized payoff at time

u_{D}^{\text{stop}}(t)\;=\;\underbrace{U_{\text{ideal}}(t)}_{=\,2R_{t}}\;-\;\underbrace{U_{\text{actual}}^{\mathsf{Collapse}}}_{=\,2P}\;-\;C_{c}(t)\;=\;(2R_{t}-2P)-C_{c}(t).

Because absorption eliminates future choices, we set

Remark
: In extensions that allow

to vary exogenously even after collapse, the immediate value of stopping remains

, and the disappearance of any option value is unchanged.

Continuation and Manipulation of the Fragile Band

Let the
state variables
be
s_{t}:=(w_{t},R_{t})
, and let the adversary’s control
u_{t}\in\mathcal{U}
induce the stochastic dynamics

w_{t+1}=G(w_{t},u_{t},\xi_{t}),\qquad R_{t+1}=H(R_{t},\eta_{t}),

with noise terms
\xi_{t},\eta_{t}

\mathcal{B}:=\{w:w_{\min}\leq w\leq w_{\max}\}

\Pi
that selects

\Phi_{t}=2R_{t}-2P
, the value function becomes

V(s_{t})=\max\Big\{\underbrace{\Phi_{t}-C_{c}(t)}_{\text{stop}},\;\underbrace{\sup_{u_{t}\in\mathcal{U}}\big(\delta\,\mathbb{E}[V(s_{t+1})\mid s_{t},u_{t}]-C_{m}(t,u_{t})\big)}_{\text{continue}}\Big\},

which makes explicit that the continuation term in Equation (

.

A.4.1
Conditions Determining Strategic Regimes

Assumptions.

0<\delta<1,\quad C_{c}(t)\geq 0,\quad C_{m}(t)\geq 0,\quad T>R>P>S.

As solutions to Equation (
6
), we obtain the three
strategic regimes
corresponding to Theorem
3.2.1
. For brevity, define

\Delta_{t}\coloneqq\delta E_{t}[V_{t+1}]-(2R_{t}-2P),\qquad\Delta C_{t}\coloneqq C_{m}(t)-C_{c}(t),

(7)

where
\Delta C_{t}
is the
cost differential
(
\Delta C_{t}
).

(i) Immediate destruction

(2R_{t}-2P)-C_{c}(t)\geq\delta E_{t}[V_{t+1}]-C_{m}(t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Delta_{t}\leq-\,\Delta C_{t}.

(8)

(ii) Rational stagnation

\delta E_{t}[V_{t+1}]-(2R_{t}-2P)>C_{m}(t)-C_{c}(t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Delta_{t}>\Delta C_{t}.

(9)

(iii) Intervention abandonment

|\Delta_{t}|\leq|\Delta C_{t}|.

(10)

This band can be interpreted as the

, reflecting the cost structure and forecast errors.

Critical thresholds (boundary conditions)

\displaystyle\delta E_{t}[V_{t+1}]

\displaystyle=(2R_{t}-2P)+(C_{m}(t)-C_{c}(t))\quad

\displaystyle\text{(tipping into stagnation)},

(11)

\displaystyle\delta E_{t}[V_{t+1}]

\displaystyle=(2R_{t}-2P)-(C_{m}(t)-C_{c}(t))\quad

(12)

is allowed as a third alternative, the condition

A.5
Robustness to Nonlinear Generalizations

The linear
subjective utility
in Equation (
4
) is chosen for parsimony and analytical tractability. However, the core finding—the existence of a
dual equilibrium band
coincident with the
fragile cooperation band
—is robust to nonlinear formulations of social preference.

Consider the more general specification:

u^{\prime}_{i}=au_{i}+aF(w)u_{j},\quad\text{where }w=b/a.

(13)

Here,
F(w)
captures the effective influence of the
ratio of mutual recognition

w
and is assumed to satisfy:

\displaystyle F(0)=0,

\displaystyle F(w)\ \text{is continuous and nondecreasing},

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(w)\geq 0,

\displaystyle F(w)\in[0,1].

These are minimal conditions ensuring that stronger other-regarding recognition does not reduce own utility.

Under these assumptions, the equilibrium conditions generalize as follows:

•

(C,C)
is a Nash equilibrium
\iff F(w)\geq w_{\min}
,

•

(D,D)
is a Nash equilibrium
\iff F(w)\leq w_{\max}
.

Hence, the
dual equilibrium band
exists for any
w
such that
w_{\min}\leq F(w)\leq w_{\max}
.

It follows that the existence of the fragile band is not an artifact of the linear model but a property
endogenous to the payoff structure of the game
. The shape of
F(w)
—concave (diminishing empathy) or convex (tipping-point effects)—determines the width and location of the band; yet as long as monotonicity holds, the theoretical implication of
equilibrium multiplicity
remains robust.

Proposition A.6.1

(Reference-shift stability)
.

Assume
g_{3}
is
L
-Lipschitz on the relevant domain in
|\xi|
, and the reference is shifted
by a constant
\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\mathbb{R}
at all dates (forecasts
\hat{x}_{t}
and dynamics do not depend on
x^{\ast}
).
Let
L_{\gamma,i}:=\max(\gamma_{i}^{+},\gamma_{i}^{-})
.
Then, for any policy and state
s
, the value function satisfies

\bigl|V^{(\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}})}(s)-V^{(0)}(s)\bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{L_{\gamma,i}\,L\,|\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}|}{1-\delta}.

Consequently, the regime inequalities in §
A.4
remain unchanged whenever their
margin exceeds
\frac{L_{\gamma,i}\,L\,|\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}|}{1-\delta}
.

Sketch.

A shift
x^{\ast}\mapsto x^{\ast}+\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}
perturbs each stage payoff by at most
L_{\gamma,i}L|\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}|
(by Lipschitz continuity of
g_{3}
).
Discounting and summing over time yields
\sum_{t\geq 0}\delta^{t}L_{\gamma,i}L|\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}|=\frac{L_{\gamma,i}L|\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}|}{1-\delta}
.
Hence, for any state
s
,
|V^{(\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}})}(s)-V^{(0)}(s)|\leq\frac{L_{\gamma,i}L|\kappa_{\mathrm{ref}}|}{1-\delta}
.
Thus regime-sign tests change only if their original gaps are smaller than this bound. ∎

A.7
Mass Simultaneous Intervention (Buzz / Backlash)

We model many small agents acting simultaneously (e.g., like/share/praise vs. attack/criticize). Using
U_{i}
from §
A.6
, the positive/negative sensitivity to surprise and norm deviation is separated by
(\beta_{i}^{+},\beta_{i}^{-})
and
(\gamma_{i}^{+},\gamma_{i}^{-})
.

Aggregate response.

With a heterogeneous cost distribution and a logistic response,

\displaystyle P_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\left(\eta[\beta^{+}g_{2}((\epsilon_{t})_{+})+\gamma^{+}g_{3}((\xi_{t})_{+})]-\bar{c}\right),

\displaystyle N_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma\!\left(\eta[\beta^{-}g_{2}((\epsilon_{t})_{-})+\gamma^{-}g_{3}((\xi_{t})_{-})]-\bar{c}\right),

where
P_{t}
is the praise rate (buzz),
N_{t}
the attack rate (backlash),
\eta>0
sensitivity,
\bar{c}
a representative cost, and
\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})
is the logistic function.
In the aggregate equations we use population averages and write
(\beta^{\pm},\gamma^{\pm})
for representative values.

State update.

We consider the one-line macro dynamics

x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\kappa\,(P_{t}-N_{t})-\rho\,(x_{t}-\bar{x}),

where
\kappa>0
is the diffusion gain,
\rho>0
is damping, and
\bar{x}
is a baseline.

Local resonance (buzz/backlash) condition.

Let
\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})
, and define

\displaystyle s_{t}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\eta[\beta^{+}g_{2}((\epsilon_{t})_{+})+\gamma^{+}g_{3}((\xi_{t})_{+})]-\bar{c},

\displaystyle s_{t}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\eta[\beta^{-}g_{2}((\epsilon_{t})_{-})+\gamma^{-}g_{3}((\xi_{t})_{-})]-\bar{c}.

Linearizing around a steady state (treating
\hat{x}_{t}
and
x^{\ast}
as locally exogenous so that

\partial\epsilon_{t}/\partial x_{t}=\partial\xi_{t}/\partial x_{t}=1
) yields

\displaystyle G_{t}:=\kappa\Big(

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(s_{t}^{+})\,\eta\,[\beta^{+}g_{2}^{\prime}((\epsilon_{t})_{+})\mathbf{1}_{\{\epsilon_{t}>0\}}+\gamma^{+}g_{3}^{\prime}((\xi_{t})_{+})\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{t}>0\}}]

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle-\sigma^{\prime}(s_{t}^{-})\,\eta\,[\beta^{-}g_{2}^{\prime}((\epsilon_{t})_{-})\mathbf{1}_{\{\epsilon_{t}<0\}}+\gamma^{-}g_{3}^{\prime}((\xi_{t})_{-})\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi_{t}<0\}}]\Big),

where
\sigma^{\prime}(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))
.
At kinks (
\epsilon_{t}=0
or
\xi_{t}=0
),
g_{k}^{\prime}
denotes a one-sided derivative (or subgradient).

Proposition A.7.1

(Local stability threshold)
.

Let
J_{t}:=1+G_{t}-\rho
. Then:

1.

(
Stability
) If
|J_{t}|<1
(equivalently,
\rho-2<G_{t}<\rho
), perturbations decay locally.

2.

(
Buzz / monotone escape
) If
J_{t}>1
(equivalently,
G_{t}>\rho
), the fixed point is locally unstable with monotone divergence (“buzz”).

3.

(
Backlash / alternating escape
) If
J_{t}<-1
(equivalently,
G_{t}<\rho-2
), the fixed point is locally unstable with alternating divergence (“backlash”).

Remark (design).

Strengthening
(\beta^{+},\gamma^{+})
and/or reducing
(\beta^{-},\gamma^{-})
while increasing
\rho
pushes the system out of the fragile band towards sustained cooperation (defensive design intent).

Appendix B
Extensibility (Research Outlook)

This core framework can be extended to more complex scenarios.

B.1
Dynamics with Multiple Adversaries

Consider a set of competing adversaries
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\dots,D_{n}\}
. Model the evolution of system fragility
w_{t}
as a function of the aggregate of their interventions. The problem then becomes a game among adversaries. Coordination among adversaries may, paradoxically, stabilize fragility, whereas rivalry can precipitate
premature and suboptimal collapse
.

B.2
Networked Fragility

Let a network
G=(V,E)
represent subsystems (nodes) each with a local fragility
w_{i}(t)
. Adversarial influence can propagate along network links. In this setting, overall system stability depends on the spectral properties of the network’s adjacency matrix, providing a bridge to models of
systemic risk
and
information cascades
.
