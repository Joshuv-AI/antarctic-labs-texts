Salvianolic acid A modulates α-Ketoisovaleric acid metabolism to inhibit human cartilage endplate cells apoptosis under mechanical tensile stress
Authors: Yuxuan Du, Ke Zhao, Chong Liu, Gewen Wang, Yupeng Liang, Chuhao Cai, Weiye Zhang, Wu Sun, Zhefeng Jin, Jia Ma, Tao Han, Hongyu Yang, Liguo Zhu, Jiawen Zhan, Minshan Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) represents a key contributor to chronic back pain and cartilage endplate cell (CEPCs) are mechanically sensitive. Salvianolic acid A (SAA), a polyphenolic compound of traditional Chinese medicine Salvia miltiorrhiza, have protective potential on IDD in our previous study. This study was purposed to clarify the effect of SAA on the metabolic profiles of CEPCs under mechanical tension and its underlying mechanisms. CEPCs were isolated from human lumbar discs and subjected to mechanical tensile loading (10% strain, 1 Hz, 24 h), with or without 10µM SAA pretreatment. The CCK-8 (for viability) and flow cytometry assays (for apoptosis) of CEPCs were detected. Non-targeted metabolomics was performed using UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS, followed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Virtual molecular docking was used to screen the core targets of SAA and IDD intersected proteins. Realtime PCR and immunofluorescence were conducted. SAA enhanced cell viability and reduced apoptosis of CEPCs under mechanical tensile loading. The differentially expressed metabolite α-ketoisovaleric acid was screened. SAA upregulated collagen II, GSK-3β and downregulated BMP2, MMP3, Wnt3α, collage I expression. In addition, SAA also downregulated miR-940 exosome expression. Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD), one of the independent risk factors of low back pain, has been studied in-depth in middle-aged and older adult patients1,2. The structure of intervertebral disc is complex and which is made of the nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, and cartilage endplates2. Mechanical loading plays a crucial role to maintain the functional integrity of the cartilage endplate, and cartilage endplate cells (CEPCs) exhibit marked mechano-sensitive to mechanical stimuli. Physiological levels of mechanical stress are essential for maintaining CEPCs homeostasis. However, it was reported that CEPCs equilibrium could be broken by excessive tensile forces3,4. Furthermore, degeneration of the cartilage endplate, featured by number reduction of viable cell populations and increase of apoptosis of functionally important cell types, was one of key pathological processes of IDD2. Therefore, strategies focus on preserving cartilage endplate homeostasis and integrity could be crucial for the prevention and control of IDD3.

Metabolic syndrome (obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia) and its components (including central obesity and hypertriglyceridemia) have been implicated in the development of IDD5,6. Cartilage degeneration is exacerbated by mechanical overload, and this process is further influenced by various metabolic disturbances7. Lipid metabolism disorders exacerbate IDD through triggering inflammatory signaling pathways, inducing stress in the endoplasmic reticulum, elevating oxidative damage, and impairing autophagic processes8. Glucose metabolism is likewise participated in the development of IDD, primarily by promoting the aging of CEPCs, increasing cell apoptosis and extracellular matrix degradation9. Furthermore, during the development of IDD, the anabolic processes responsible for extracellular matrix formation are downregulated, whereas the expression and activity of catabolic enzymes become elevated, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are closely related with cartilage degradation.

Conventional symptom-targeted therapies for IDD—such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs—was reported to demonstrate only modest clinical effectiveness. In contrast, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) offers distinct therapeutic benefits, often exerting multi-targeted and synergistic actions that could enhance its potential for treating IDD, accompanied by recent progress in uncovering its pharmacological basis through histological analysis, biochemical assays, and molecular biology approaches10. Salvianolic acid A (SAA), chemical structure was shown in (Fig. 1) is a natural small-molecule drug derived from Salvia miltiorrhiza, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb11. SAA ameliorates sustained stress-induced damage to lumbar facet joint cartilage in the context of IDD, suppressing both chondrocyte apoptosis and synovial inflammation.

Chemical structure of salvianolic acid A.

Recent reports suggest that SAA may prevent osteoarthritis by modulating NF-κB and p38/MAPK pathways12. Our previous work indicated that SAA alleviated CEPCs degeneration by targeting downregulates miR-940, however, the underlying mechanisms remained unclear13,14. In this article, CEPCs was subjected to mechanical tensile loading. We detected the effect of SAA on stress loaded CEPCs and elucidated the underlying mechanism based on untargeted metabolomics and in vitro experimental validations.

Materials and methods

Ethics declaration and collection of human cartilaginous endplate tissue

To detect the effect of SAA on human CEPCs under stress loading and obtain human CEPCs for subsequent experiment, we acquired enrolled patients’ samples. Human cartilaginous endplate tissues were obtained from lumbar or sacral intervertebral disc surgical specimens of patients undergoing discectomy, following informed consent and approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Wangjing Hospital China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Approval No. WJEC-KT-2021-064-P001), according to guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients who underwent lumbar fusion surgery based on definitive clinical indications, such as lumbar fractures or degenerative conditions affecting the lumbar spine, without comorbidities like spinal deformities or neoplastic lesions; and (b) lumbar fusion was the sole surgical procedure performed. Endplate cartilage samples were obtained from 27 patients and the clinical data (Table 1) of all those were shown.

To isolate human cartilaginous endplate tissue, the nucleus pulposus and adjacent soft tissues were meticulously removed under microscopic visualization. The exposed cartilage endplate was carefully scraped to harvest chondrocytes. The obtained tissue was cut into fragments approximately 1–2 mm³ in size, rinsed with PBS. After enzymatically digesting (using 0.25% type II collagenase and keeping samples at 37 °C for 4 h), primary cells were released. After filtering the cell suspension through a 70 μm mesh, it was centrifuged (2000 rpm and 5 min) and then re-suspended in culture medium (F12, Hyclone, USA, added 15% fetal bovine serum, and with 1% penicillin-streptomycin). CEPCs were grown in an incubator (with 5% CO2 and 37 °C). The cells were sub-cultured when they reached about 70%–80% confluence. Second passage human endplate chondrocytes were used for experiments.

Experimental grouping and mechanical stress loading application

In this study, a mechanical loading model of CEPCs was established by simulate stress tensile conditions. Second-passage CEPCs were cultured on BioFlex six-well culture dishes (2 × 10⁵ cells/mL, Flexcell International Corporation), which were pre-coated with type I collagen. The cell suspension was evenly distributed, and the plates were incubated. Once the cells reached approximately 70%−80% confluence, the plates were transferred to the Flexcell FX-5000 tension system. This system utilizes a vacuum-driven mechanism to cyclically deform a flexible silicone membrane, thereby exerting mechanical strain on the adherent cells. In the tension group, CEPCs were subjected to mechanical stretch at 1.0 Hz frequency with 10% elongation for a duration of 24 hours15. The cells were assigned into four experimental groups: (a) Control group – CEPCs without mechanical stretch; (b) SAA group – CEPCs treated with 10 µM salvianolic acid A (SAA) for 24 h without stretch; (c) Stress Loading group – CEPCs exposed to 10% mechanical tensile strain at 1 Hz for 24 h; and (d) Stress Loading + SAA group – CEPCs subjected to identical mechanical loading with simultaneous treatment of 10 µM SAA for 24 h.

Cell viability was measured by CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed with CCK-8 (Beyotime, China) kit. CEPCs from four experimental group were seeded into 96 well plates (1 × 10⁴ cells per well). Incubation for 24 h, CCK-8 reagent (10 µL) was added into each well. After another 2 h incubation, the plates were gently agitated for 2 min to ensure even distribution of the reagent. The optical density was then read at 450 nm with a multimode microplate reader (BioTek, USA; Bio-Rad, USA). Cell viability was quantified using the following formula: viability (%) = optical density of experimental group divided by optical density of control group.

Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis

Cellular apoptotic levels were evaluated using Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, China). The 6-well plate was rinsed with PBS, and then trypsin was added. The digestion was stopped using DMEM/F12. The cells were centrifuged and the sediment was collected and resuspended. Annexin V binding buffer of 300 µL, FITC and PI reagent (each of 3 µL) were added. The sample was incubated in dark for 15 to 20 min. Analysis of the apoptosis rate was conducted using a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer.

Cell metabolomics

Sample preparation

CEPCs were first washed with pre-chilled PBS at 4 °C. The cells were fixed by adding 80% of methanol (pre-cooled to below − 20 °C). Following this, cells were scraped and lysed, using a mechanical cell disruptor and being kept on an ice bath. A cold solvent mixture of methanol, acetonitrile, and water (2:2:1, v/v/v) was used to extract the lysates. After centrifugation (4 °C) at 7500 × g for 20 min, the supernatant was gathered and evaporated under a nitrogen stream until dry. The residue was dissolved in 80% pre-cooled methanol and vortexed for 1 min. After 30 s of ultrasonic treatment, the sample was centrifuged again under the same conditions. A quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling 10 µL of processed sample. And 5 µL of the final supernatant was utilized for acquiring metabolomic data.

Measurement conditions

Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Acquity UPLC, Waters, USA), linked to a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 5600, AB SCIEX, USA), was used for non-targeted metabolomic analysis. Metabolite separation was carried out on a BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters, maintained at 25 °C). The autosampler tray was maintained at 4 °C to ensure the integrity of the samples. The mobile phase was made up of solvent A (which included 25 mM ammonium hydroxide and 25 mM ammonium acetate) and solvent B (which was acetonitrile). The flow rate was kept at 0.3 mL/min. The gradient elution schedule was as follows: 0–1 min, 95% B; 1–14 min, 65% B; 14–16 min, 40% B; 16–18 min, 40% B; 18–18.1 min, 95% B; 18.1–23 min, 95% B. Each sample injection volume was of 2 µL. While gathering data, the sample injection order was randomly shuffled.

For mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, the aerosol-assisted heating gas was set to 60, auxiliary heating gas to 60, curtain gas to 30 psi, ion source temperature to 600 °C, and spray voltage (ISVF) to ± 5500 V, for both positive and negative modes. For the initial MS stage, the detection range of mass-to-charge ratio was established at 60–1000 Da. For the second-stage fragment ions, the detection range was set to 25–1000 Da. The MS scans of first-stage and second-stage had accumulation times of 0.20 and 0.05 s per spectrum, respectively. Data from secondary MS were collected using information-dependent acquisition mode. The declustering potential was set to ± 60 V for both positive and negative ionization modes. The collision energy was adjusted to 35 ± 15 eV for optimal fragmentation.

This analysis for metabolomics used both a self-built local database and public databases [Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) (http://www.hmdb.ca), Metlin (http://metlin.scripps.edu), MassBank (http://www.massbank.jp/), and mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org)]. Metabolites were structurally annotated by aligning key parameters [such as retention time, accurate mass (with a mass deviation less than 10 ppm), MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and collision energy] with entries from established databases. All preliminary identifications were rigorously verified through manual curation. The confidence level for metabolite identification met or exceeded Level 2 standards16,17.

Differential metabolite screening analysis

Multivariate statistical methods, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), and Orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA), were used to identify differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs). Metabolites were identified as differentially expressed if they had a VIP score exceeding 1 and a p-value below 0.05. Furthermore, log2 fold change (log2FC) values were calculated to support the identification of candidate DEMs7.

Virtual molecular docking

The three-dimensional structural data of SAA were obtained from the PubChem database. Using Venn.R package, key molecular targets were identified by overlapping the principal targets of SAA and IDD-related targets. The sdf format of SAA was loaded into Chem3D software, where the MM2 module was used for geometry optimization and energy minimization. The results were visualized with PyMOL and Discovery Studio software was also used to visualize the docking results.

Immunofluorescence staining

CEPCs from four experimental groups were seeded on glass coverslips (4 × 10⁵ cells per well). The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 minutes. The coverslip was treated with 0.3% Triton X-100 to permeabilize the cell membranes. Non-specific binding was blocked using QuickBlock™ buffer (Beyotime, China). Then cells were left to incubate overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (targeting collagen I, Wnt3α, MMP3, BMP2, collagen II, and GSK-3β). After that, the slides were incubated with Cy3-conjugated or FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abnova, China) in a dark environment. Subsequently, nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Beyotime, China) under light-protected conditions. Between each step, slides were rinsed three times with PBS. Finally, stained samples were visualized using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Zeiss LSM 880). DAPI is stimulated in the range of 330–380 nm and emits at 420 nm. FITC absorbs light between 465 and 495 nm and emits at 515–555 nm. CY3 is excited within the 510–560 nm and emits at 590 nm. The fluorescence intensity was analyzed and measured using the Image J software.

Extract and identify of micro-940

After 24 h mechanically loading, the conditioned medium of each CEPCs group was collected. CEPCs-exosomes (Exos) were obtained through ultracentrifugation following the protocol outlined in previous studies18. In short, when CEPCs at P3 achieved 70%−80% confluence, the DMEM medium was substituted with 15% Exos-free fetal bovine serum. Following culture, the cells produced a supernatant with high Exos content. To remove dead cells, the supernatant was centrifuged at 4 °C (2000 g and 15 min). The supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm syringe filter and then moved to a clean ultracentrifuge tube. Next, it was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min. The isolated exosomes derived from cartilage endplate cells (CEPCs-Exos) were gently resuspended in ice-cold PBS and stored at − 80 °C until further use. The concentration of CEPCs-Exos was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). The morphology, particle size, and markers of isolated CEPCs-Exos were assessed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and western blotting, respectively. For TEM observation, CEPCs-Exos precipitate was resuspended by PBS (pH 7.4). The suspension was spotted on a copper mesh carrier and equilibrated at room temperature for 5 min. Negative staining was performed for 5 min (avoiding light). Grids were thoroughly dried before viewing.

Quantitative real-time PCR

TRIzol was used to isolate total RNA from CEPCs. To evaluate mRNA and miRNA expression levels, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized. CEPCs were cultured in 6-well plates (4 × 10⁵) prior to RNA extraction. The method of reverse transcription was as follows: 25 °C for 5 min, 50 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 min, and 4 °C for 10 min. The synthesized cDNA was diluted before amplification. Quantitative real-time PCR (Q-PCR) was performed. GAPDH was used as the internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Experiments results are presented as the mean ± standard. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted for multiple groups comparisons, followed by a Bonferroni’s test for comparisons between stress loading and stress loading + SAA groups. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Graphs and statistical analyses were created using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, USA).

Results

SAA enhances CEPCs viability and suppresses apoptosis under mechanical tension

Abnormal mechanical tension serves as a risk factor for IDD2. We used mechanical tension (10% strain, 1 Hz, 24 h) onto CEPCs, and observed cell viability and apoptosis. CCK-8 assay revealed that compared to the control group, mechanical stress loading reduced CEPCs viability (P < 0.05). After treatment with 10 µM of SAA, cell viability was restored (P < 0.05). Moreover, flow cytometry analysis showed an increased apoptosis rate in stress loaded CEPCs. Nevertheless, SAA treatment attenuated the stress-induced apoptosis of CEPCs (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The intervertebral disc includes structure of nucleus pulposus, annulus fibrosus, and cartilage endplates. Endplate chondrocytes bear more tension than other cells in human body on most occasions. Mechanical tension plays a critical role for preserving the physiological function of cartilage. Moderate tensile loading supports the viability of endplate chondrocytes and keeps the endplate cartilage microenvironment homeostasis. In contrast, excessive mechanical strain disrupts cellular metabolic equilibrium, both intracellularly and extracellularly. At the microscopic level, this imbalance leads to elevated rates of apoptosis, impaired secretory function, and degradation of the extracellular matrix. From a macroscopic perspective, it compromises the mechanical resilience and nutrient transport capacity of the endplate, thereby accelerating cartilage degeneration. Although the precise molecular mechanisms underlying intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) remain incompletely defined, accumulating evidence suggests that cell loss driven by apoptosis—particularly under non-physiological conditions—might be a primary factor contributing to the progressive decline of intervertebral disc cells19–23.

We employed the effect and mechanism of SAA on cartilage endplate cell under mechanical tension. CCK 8 analysis revealed obvious reduced of cell viability in cartilage endplate cell under mechanical tension of 10% strain and 1 Hz for 24 h. This result showed that mechanical tension led to decreased cell viability and which was consistent with our previous research, indicating the reproducibility of model used in our study15. Furthermore, Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry demonstrated increased total apoptosis and decreased early apoptosis under stress loading. These results showed that mechanical tension led to decreased cell viability and increased apoptosis, which confirmed the successful establishment of the model for subsequent pharmacological intervention research. Additionally, in vitro experiments showed that SAA reduced total apoptosis, but promoted early apoptosis of stress loading CEPCs. These results confirm the effectiveness of SAA in restoring viability of stress loading CEPCs.

To elucidate the mechanisms by which SAA inhibited apoptosis and restored viability of stress loading CEPCs, we conducted non-targeted metabolomic analysis. The total ion chromatograms were shown in the Figure.3A. The chromatograms of each group were basically similar. From the PCA 2D data results, it was seen that the quality control (QC) samples was clustered together, indicating good quality control and reliable data quality. Each group was generally located in different quadrants, showing good clustering characteristics. The results of Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) from control vs. control + SAA, control vs. stress loading, and stress loading vs. stress loading + SAA showed that, except for a few specimens that was distributed more dispersedly, the rest of the samples were distributed relatively concentrated. Our metabolomics analysis provided an insight into the metabolic reprogramming induced by SAA. Stress loading altered key metabolites associated with amino acid metabolism and SAA treatment normalized several of these alterations, particularly within branched-chain amino acid metabolism. Our results demonstrated that SAA markedly regulated the expression of two metabolites, which was argininosuccinic acid and alpha-ketoisovaleric acid. We found that SAA did not significantly reverse the expression of argininosuccinic acid, but significantly down-regulated the expression of alpha-ketoisovaleric acid. Alpha-ketoisovaleric acid, as a branched chain ketoacid belonging to the keto acid family, is a precursor of leucine and valine synthesis and a degradation product of valine24,25. Although, does not directly involved in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, α-ketoisovaleric acid undergoes oxidative decarboxylation through the branched-chain keto acid dehydrogenase complex, generating acetoacetic acid and acetyl-CoA26. These products can then enter the tricarboxylic acid cycle or other metabolic pathways. However, research exploring the relationship between α-ketoisovaleric acid and IDD remains limited at present. Drawing on existing literature and metabolomics findings, we proposed that SAA might modulate the expression of α-ketoisovaleric acid, attenuate apoptosis signaling, and enhance the viability of CEPCs under stress loading conditions.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which SAA inhibited apoptosis and restored viability of CEPCs under stress loading, we conducted virtual molecular docking. Upon completion of the docking analysis, Biovia Discovery Studio Visualizer was employed to examine and illustrate the three-dimensional interactions between SAA and the active site residues of the top seven candidate compounds (binding energy <−4.6 kcal/mol). The results demonstrated that SAA showed strong binding affinity to matrix metalloproteinase 3 (MMP3) and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9). Mechanical stress is known to be a critical driver of disc degeneration, particularly by enhancing chondrocytes apoptosis and disrupting matrix composition. We further detected the expression of CEPCs regulating genes (Wnt3α, β-catenin, SOX9, MMP3, RUNX2, GSK-3β, CollagenⅠ, Collagen Ⅱ) and protein (CollagenⅠ, Wnt 3α, MMP3, BMP2, Collagen Ⅱ, GSK-3β). Our data showed that mechanical tensile strain led to significant downregulation of anabolic markers (such as Collagen II) and upregulation of catabolic enzymes (such as MMP3 and BMP2). Importantly, SAA treatment reversed these effects, supporting its chondroprotective function.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), constituted of a class of endogenous and small non-coding RNAs, have recently been recognized as crucial regulators of intercellular communication. They have garnered significant attention in current research due to their functional versatility. A multitude of studies have shown that miRNAs are crucial regulators in the processes of cartilage degeneration and chondrocyte differentiation27. MiRNAs participate in the processes of intervertebral disc cell proliferation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix synthesis through directly modulating target genes, potentially playing a significant role in the onset and progression of intervertebral disc degeneration28,29. miR-940 has been implicated in inflammatory and degenerative conditions in IDD and CEPCs regulation30. It was also reported that miR-940 regulates the expression of Wnt3α/β-catenin and related signal pathways, such as GSK-3β and collagen Ⅱ31,32. Our results show that SAA downregulated miR-940 expression, which coincided with reduced expression of Wnt3α, β-catenin, and Runx2 (central factors to Wnt pathway activation). The upregulation of GSK-3β and collagen Ⅱ by SAA also could imply the activation of chondrogenic and hedgehog-related pathways. Nonetheless, there were several limitations in our study. First, only in vitro experiments were performed and the in vitro model might not fully replicate the complexity of disc microenvironment in vivo, including vascular gradients and immune responses. Second, although our data implicate miR-940 and related Wnt3α, β-catenin, GSK-3β, collagen Ⅱ was regulated, the regulatory relationship between these factors remains unclear. In addition, our future studies should be focused on recue detection and further exploration in animal models. The research idea of our article was shown in Fig. 8.

Our experiments revealed that SAA provided protective effects and mitigated mechanical stress-induced CEPCs damage. SAA enhanced the survival rate of CEPCs subjected to tensile stress and suppressed their apoptosis, offering potential for maintaining CEPC population size and activity under pathological conditions. Non-targeted metabolomics studies further revealed that SAA reversed stress-induced metabolic change in CEPCs. This metabolic regulation might underpin its enhancement of cellular vitality and anti-apoptotic effects. SAA counteracted stress-induced damage of CEPCs through anti-apoptotic and metabolic regulatory mechanisms, positioning it as a potential research candidate for mitigating the progression of IDD.

Conclusion

SAA effectively mitigated mechanical stress-induced damage to CEPCs by promoting cell survival and inhibiting apoptosis. SAA downregulated the expression of catabolic and osteogenic mediators (MMP3, BMP2, Wnt3a, and type I collagen) while upregulating anabolic factors (type II collagen and GSK-3β) by regulating alpha-ketoisovaleric acid, which could be based on regulating of miR-940 related signal pathways.
