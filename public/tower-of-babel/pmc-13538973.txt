Developmental Fates of Progenitor/Undifferentiated Cells With Histone Trimethylation Tags in the Stony Coral, Acropora tenuis
Authors: Kaz Kawamura, Satoko Sekida, Noriyuki Satoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe stony coral, Acropora tenuis , possesses progenitor/undifferentiated cells similar to other anthozoans; however, their developmental potential and fate remain obscure. The present study initially showed that nerve progenitors and undifferentiated‐like cells were both tagged with histone H3 trimethylation at Lys 4 and Lys 27, suggesting that Acropora progenitor/undifferentiated cells might be multipotent. A rabbit anti‐reverse transcriptase (anti‐AtRTase) antibody recognized undifferentiated‐like cells in the middle region of the ectodermal layer in embryos 60–85 h post‐fertilization. At the larval stages, AtRTase signals were concentrated around the oral pole where the larval ectoderm invaginated to form the pharyngeal stomodeum. Undifferentiated‐like cells, but not the apical‐most glandular epithelium in the ectoderm, appeared to enter the pharynx. Consistent with this hypothesis, AtRTase signals were continuous from undifferentiated‐like cells to the stomodeum. Nerve progenitors (neuroblasts) expressed nerve‐specific tubulin beta‐III (TUBB3). They differentiated into nerve cells and expressed a neuroblast differentiation‐associated protein around the aboral pole of larvae. On the other hand, non‐aboral neuroblasts expressed the autophagy‐related protein 10 in addition to TUBB3 and developed into vacuolated hyaline cells (VHCs) that penetrated through the ectoderm apicobasally. The present study suggests strongly that ectodermal undifferentiated cells in A. tenuis have the potential to form the stomodeum. In contrast, nerve progenitors possess the dual potential to differentiate into nerve cells and VHCs, which appears to depend on aboral and non‐aboral positions.Keywords: ATG10, neuroblast, RTase, stomodeum, TUBB3

Introduction

Stem cells self‐renew and produce tissue‐restricted progenitors by unequal cell division (Hall and Watt 1989). Progenitor cells downstream of pluripotent stem cells amplify rapidly and give rise to terminally differentiated cells (Lajtha 1979). Various technologies have been developed to identify progenitor/undifferentiated cells, and histone modification tags are one of the most likely methods. Lysine 4 and lysine 27 of histone H3 (H3K4 and H3K27, respectively) undergo methylation, which exerts epigenetic effects on the chromatin state (Strahl and Allis 2000). H3K4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) and H3K27me3 regulate the fate of embryonic stem cells (Rajasekhar and Begemann 2007) and hematopoietic stem cells in mammals (Iwama et al. 2005).

In Cnidaria, hydrozoan interstitial cells (I‐cells) are undifferentiated pluripotent stem cells that renew themselves and differentiate into nerve cells, nematocytes, gland cells, and germ cells, except epithelial tissues (Hwang et al. 2007; David 2012; Leclère et al. 2016; Holstein 2023). In Hydra polyps, I‐cells are located in the interstitial space of both ectoderm and endoderm (Hobmayer et al. 2012). They contain “nuages” around the nucleus (Hobmayer et al. 2012) and express Piwi, Nanos, and Vasa (Leclère et al. 2016). Nerve/gland progenitor cells derived from I‐cells express Myc and Myb (Siebert et al. 2019).

Non‐hydrozoan cnidarians are also hypothesized to contain pluripotent stem cells (Denner et al. 2024; Miramón‐Puértolas et al. 2024); however, their entities have not yet been captured in the anthozoan Nematostella (Marlow et al. 2009) or in the scyphozoan Aurelia (Yuan et al. 2008). In Aurelia, the epitheliomuscular ectoderm behaves like stem lineage cells, called amoebocytes (Gold and Jacobs 2013). Even in hydrozoan Podocoryne, striated muscle dedifferentiates into epitheliomuscular stem cells (amoebocytes), and transdifferentiates into new cell types (Schmid and Alder 1984; Seipel and Schmid 2006). In contrast with stem cells, progenitor cells in Nematostella have been reported to inhabit the planula ectoderm (Sebé‐Pedrós et al. 2018) and the polyp mesentery (Denner et al. 2024; Miramón‐Puértolas et al. 2024). Neuroglandular progenitor cells provide the source for neurons, gland cells, and cnidocytes (Steger et al. 2022). They express Piwi and Vasa (Miramón‐Puértolas et al. 2024). SoxC is expressed by nerve line cells (Shinzato et al. 2008) and serves as a key upstream regulator of all neuroglandular lineages (Steger et al. 2022). Germline progenitor cells express Piwi, Nanos, and Vasa, of which Nanos is indispensable for germline formation (Denner et al. 2024).

Fewer cellular and molecular studies have been conducted on progenitor/undifferentiated cells in the anthozoan stony coral, Acropora tenuis than on those in Nematostella. Embryogenesis in A. tenuis begins with a flattened ball without blastocoel, termed as a “prawnchip” embryo (Figure 1A,B). A recent study showed that unequal cell division occurs in sequence in the embryonic ectoderm, giving rise to new types of cells; one is a nerve progenitor at the “donut” stage (Figure 1C,D), the other is an undifferentiated‐like cell at the “pear” stage (Figure 1E) (Kawamura et al., forthcoming). Nerve progenitors have been characterized cytologically and immunohistochemically (Kawamura et al. 2025, forthcoming). They are enriched at the aboral portion of larvae and are also located at the lateral portion along the oral‐aboral axis (Kawamura et al. 2025). The developmental fate of Acropora lateral neuroblasts currently remains unclear.

In the scyphozoan Aurelia, “densely packed nuclei” have been found in the ectoderm (Yuan et al. 2008). Acropora undifferentiated‐like cells resemble Aurelia “densely packed nuclei.” The developmental destinations of “packed nuclei” and undifferentiated‐like cells currently remain unclear. In late “pear” embryos, the oral concave appears (Figure 1F), and the stomodeum, a pharyngeal tube connecting the oral concave to the gastric cavity, appears in early larvae (Figure 1G).

We have recently established sustainable cell lines from larvae of A. tenuis (Kawamura et al. 2021). Some of these cell lines have been suggested to have characteristics of progenitor/undifferentiated cells (Kawamura et al. 2025). However, studies on embryogenesis and larval development are still poor in A. tenuis , interfering with the integrated understanding of progenitor/undifferentiated cells of this organism. In the present study, we aimed to unveil in vivo development of these cells. Firstly, we examined epigenetic histone modifications in Acropora progenitor/undifferentiated‐like cells. Secondly, ultrastructural studies were performed to investigate the pharyngeal ectoderm. Thirdly, novel antibodies were prepared against reverse transcriptase (RTase) and autophagy‐related enzymes to trace undifferentiated‐like cells and neuroblasts, respectively. The last, ultrastructural and immunohistochemical results were both considered in the context of the developmental pathway of progenitor/undifferentiated cells. We compared Acropora progenitor/undifferentiated cells with a Hydra I‐cell system and a Nematostella neuroglandular progenitor cell system.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Embryos and planula larvae of A. tenuis (class Anthozoa; subclass Hexacorallia, order Scleractinia, family Acroporidae) were kindly provided by Mr. Shuichi Mekaru at Onna Fisheries Cooperative, Okinawa, Japan. They were allowed to develop at room temperature (~25°C) in the Marine Genomics Unit, Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology Graduate University. Embryos were fixed at adequate developmental stages. Fixed embryos and living larvae at 6–8 days post‐fertilization (dpf) were transferred to Kochi University for subsequent experimental procedures. A batch of 14 dpf larvae began spontaneous metamorphosis. They were also used for immunohistochemistry and transcriptome.

Histology

Embryos at 40, 60, and 85 h post‐fertilization (hpf) and larvae at 6, 8, 11, and 18 dpf were fixed in Zamboni's fixative at room temperature for 30 min. They were serially dehydrated and embedded in plastic resin, Technovit 8100 (Heraeus Kulzer, Wertheim, Germany). Samples were then sectioned into 2‐μm‐thick slices with glass knives.

Electron Microscopy

Larvae at 14 dpf were fixed, embedded, sectioned, and observed, as previously described (Kawamura et al. 2025). In brief, larvae were pre‐fixed in Karnovsky's fixative and post‐fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide. Samples were embedded in Spurr's resin and sectioned with a diamond knife on an Ultracut UCT ultramicrotome (Leica, Vienna, Austria). They were observed under a Jeol JEM‐1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) and photographed using a charge‐coupled device camera.

Primary Antibodies

Rabbit antibodies against RTase (AtRTase, aten_s0416.g1.t1) and ubiquitin‐like‐conjugating enzyme autophagy‐related gene 10 (AtATG10, aten_s0329.g15.t1) were prepared as follows: GIPPELRELRKAPFKRKLT to aa 447–465 of AtRTase and HTVTEDDSERQSIEDKNWG to aa 54–72 of AtATG10. Before immunization, these oligopeptides were each conjugated to the carrier protein, keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH). Rabbit antibodies were raised by Eurofins Genomics Tokyo, Japan. They were diluted 400‐fold with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) immediately before use. A mouse anti‐tubulin beta‐III (TUBB3) monoclonal antibody and rabbit anti‐H3K4me3 and anti‐H3K27me3 antibodies were prepared and used, as previously described (Kimura‐Nagano et al. 2024; Kawamura and Satoh 2024).

Immunohistochemistry

The blocking of sections was performed in a mixture of 0.25% blocking reagent (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) and 2.5% skim milk in PBS for 30 min. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted 400‐fold containing 20 μg/mL KLH at room temperature for 1 h. The staining procedure of secondary antibodies was previously described (Kawamura et al. 2025). Samples were finally counterstained with 4′6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, 5 μg/mL) for 10 min. Some samples were also stained with 0.1 mM rhodamine‐phalloidin (Cytoskeleton Inc., Denver, CO, USA). In the negative control, primary antibodies were absorbed in advance by the corresponding oligopeptides at a concentration of 25 μg/mL. After antibody staining and washing with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, sections were observed under a confocal microscope (ECLIPSE C1si system; Nikon).

SDS‐PAGE and Western Blotting

SDS‐containing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) was performed, as previously described (Kawamura et al. 2025). In brief, crude extracts from the mixture of 12, 17, 28, 40, 60, 85 hpf embryos were prepared in RIPA lysis buffer. They were briefly centrifuged and separated in a 12.5% gel. After separation, proteins were blotted onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes for 90 min at 300 mA. The NC membranes were treated with the blocking solution and stained with antibodies.

Transcriptome

Total RNA was extracted from 0.5 mL suspension of 3.5, 10.5, 15.5, 20, 25, 36, 60, and 84 hpf embryos, of 5.5 and 8.5 dpf larvae, and of 14 dpf metamorphosing polyps with TRIzol solution. cDNA libraries were produced using a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) with IDT for Illumina‐TruSeq DNA UD Indexes v2. The quality control of RNA‐seq libraries was performed by capillary electrophoresis on a Bioanalyzer. Library sequencing was conducted on a MiSeq using MiSeq V2 reagent 300 cycles (Illumina). Transcriptomic analyses of raw data were performed as previously described (Kawamura et al. 2021). The abundance of annotated genes was estimated as transcripts per million (TPM).

Results

Histone Methylation Tags in Progenitor/Undifferentiated‐Like Cells

Histone H3 of A. tenuis matched 100% with the amino acid sequences of human, bovine, and mouse histone 3.2. Therefore, using commercially available antibodies, nuclear histone modifications were examined immunohistochemically. The trimethylation of H3K4 (H3K4me3) in 8 dpf larvae was reported in part elsewhere (Kawamura et al., forthcoming).

Nuclear histone H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 signals were rarely observed in 60 hpf embryos or in other embryonic stages (Figure 2A,B). They weakly appeared in 6 dpf larvae (not shown) and became prominent in 8 dpf larvae (Figure 2C–H). In the oral region, undifferentiated‐like cells and the stomodeum both expressed H3K4me3 signals (Figure 2C1,C2) and H3K27me3 signals (Figure 2D1,D2). In the lateral body wall of 8 dpf larvae, the nuclear signals of H3K4me3 were prominent (Figure 2E1,E2), whereas those of H3K27me3 became ambiguous (Figure 2F1,F2). At the aboral end, nerve progenitors (neuroblasts) emitted strong signals for both H3K4me3 and H3K27me3 (Figure 2G,H), which contrasted with the loss of nuclear signals in undifferentiated‐like cells. In polyps, their expression stably reappeared in undifferentiated‐like cells (Figure 2I,J). Nuclear signals were also observable in gastrodermis cells (Figure 2I,J, red arrowheads).

RTase Expressed by Undifferentiated‐Like Cells and the Stomodeum

The RTase gene encodes RNA‐directed DNA polymerase. Transcriptome data showed that RTase in A. tenuis (AtRTase) was stably expressed from embryos to planula, and its expression was very high in polyps (Table 1). The anti‐AtRTase antibody recognized an approximately 90 kDa band (Figure S1, Lane 1). This signal disappeared by pretreating the antibody with the RTase peptide used as a hapten (Figure S1, Lane 2), indicating that the anti‐AtRTase antibody recognizes the AtRTase polypeptide.

Differentiation of TUBB3‐Expressing Nerve Lineage Cells

Nerve‐specific TUBB3 expression began in 6.5–7 dpf larvae and was most abundant in the aboral pole of 8 dpf larvae (Figure 6A,B1). In the positive control, TUBB3‐expressing cells at the aboral pole developed into nerve cells with tapering axons that expressed AtAHNAK (Figure 6C1–C3), as previously reported (Kawamura et al. 2025). TUBB signals in the lateral body wall overlapped with DAPI signals (Figure 6D1,D2, arrowheads), and positive cells developed into thick and stout cells that were stained with the anti‐AtATG10 antibody (Figure 6D2). This antibody recognized a single band of 25 kDa after western blotting (Figure S1, Lane 3) and in vivo cytoplasmic vacuoles, which had a markedly different appearance from nerve cells (cf., Figure 6C2,D2). In the negative control using an AtATG10 peptide, signals diminished on the NC membrane (Figure S1, Lane 4) and disappeared from the ectoderm of 8 dpf larvae (Figure S2C1,C2). AtATG10‐positive cells grew and reached the apical surface of the ectoderm, leaving the nucleus at the basal end of the ectodermal layer (Figure 6E). Transcriptomic results showed that AtATG10 was expressed ubiquitously from embryos to larvae, and its expression rapidly declined in polyps immediately after metamorphosis (Table 1).

Identification of Atg10‐Expressing Cells

Based on the results showing the basal localization of the nucleus and a large cell size penetrating the apico‐basal axis of the ectoderm, we investigated the ultrastructure of ATG10‐expressing cells. The candidate cell had its nucleus at the basal end of the cell (Figure 7A). The apical end of the cell reached the outer ectodermal surface (Figure 7B). It possessed many electron‐lucent vacuoles (Figure 7A) that were different from the solid vacuoles of ectodermal glandular cells (cf., Figure 5C). The glandular epithelium did not respond to the anti‐AtATG10 antibody (Figure 6E) and did not reach the basal end of the ectoderm (Kawamura et al. 2025). Accordingly, the AtATG10‐expressing cell was identified as a vacuolated hyaline cell (VHC) (Kawamura et al., forthcoming). VHC precursors contained a few translucent vacuoles in the cytoplasm (Figure 7A, right). They increased not only in size but also in the number of translucent vacuoles (Figure 7C).

Discussion

Undifferentiated‐Like Cells and Nerve Progenitors May Be Multipotent

KDM5 targets the methylated histone H3K4 and affects chromatin activity (Grimaud et al. 2006). The knockout of KDM5 in preadipocytes impairs cell proliferation and consequently interferes with adipocyte differentiation (Brier et al. 2017). A recent study suggested that in A. tenuis , the KDM5 expression was inversely related to trimethylation of H3K4 (Kawamura et al., forthcoming). Transcriptomic data supported the results of immunohistochemistry and indicated that the expression level of AtKDM5 increased during embryonic stages and decreased in larval stages (Table 1 reproduced from Kawamura et al., forthcoming). KDM1 removes mono‐ or di‐methylation on lysine 4 of histone H3 (Wang et al. 2009). In Nematostella, when KDM1 was knocked out by CRISPR/Cas9, nerves and cnidocytes were suppressed to differentiate (Gahan et al. 2022).

Histone trimethylation at H3K4 and H3K27 is essential for sustaining the pluripotency of mammalian embryonic and adult stem cells (Iwama et al. 2005; Rajasekhar and Begemann 2007; Grandy et al. 2015). In A. tenuis , undifferentiated‐like cells and neuroblasts were both modified with H3K4me3 and H3K27me3, which suggested that both cells might be multipotent. In vitro studies on gene expression profile and cell differentiation repertoire would afford further evidence for multipotency.

I‐cells in Hydrozoa are undifferentiated pluripotent stem cells. They may renew themselves and differentiate into nerve cells, nematocytes, gland cells, and germ cells, except epithelial tissues (Hwang et al. 2007; David 2012; Leclère et al. 2016; Holstein 2023). I‐cells appear soon after embryogenesis (Martin et al. 1997), and express SoxB, SoxC, SoxE, Piwi, Nanos, and Vasa (Jager et al. 2011; Leclère et al. 2016). I‐cells have “nuages” in the cytoplasm that are enriched with mitochondria (David 2012; Hobmayer et al. 2012). It is also noteworthy that in the hydrozoan species Podocoryne carnea , pluripotent cells do not arise from I‐cells but from striated muscle cells by transdifferentiation (Schmid and Alder 1984). Non‐hydrozoan cnidarias have been proposed to possess putative stem cells destined to be germline progenitors and neuroglandular progenitors (Denner et al. 2024). Some researchers insist that planula larvae of Anthozoa, Schyphozoa, and Cubozoa contain I‐cells similar to hydrozoan species (Martin and Chia 1982; Nordström et al. 2003; Reyes‐Bermudez and Miller 2009; cited by Gold and Jacobs 2013). The others are skeptical of or negative about this argument (Yuan et al. 2008; Marlow et al. 2009; Nakanishi et al. 2012; Richards and Rentzsch 2015; Gahan et al. 2022; Holstein 2023). Recent literature seems to be in favor of the latter arguments.

In addition to ultrastructural studies on the Acropora ectoderm (Kawamura et al. 2025), epigenetic evidence in the present study was in favor of the hypothesis that larval and polyp undifferentiated‐like cells are “undifferentiated.” However, Acropora undifferentiated‐like cells did not express Sox and did not have “nuages” and a prominent nucleolus in late embryos and larvae (Kawamura et al., forthcoming). The cytoplasm was not enriched with mitochondria. Accordingly, it is premature to conclude whether Acropora undifferentiated‐like cells are “undifferentiated.”

Undifferentiated‐Like Cells Are Likely to Develop Into the Stomodeum

The larval ectoderm in A. tenuis consists of four types of cells stratified apicobasally: the glandular epithelium in the apical‐most layer, undifferentiated‐like cells in the second “middle” layer, nerve progenitors in the third “bottom” layer, and yolk cells in the fourth layer (Kawamura et al., forthcoming). Of these four layers, three layers except undifferentiated‐like cells were scarcely observable in the oral ectoderm and the invaginating stomodeum of 8 dpf larvae. Previous immunohistochemical studies showed that the antibody recognizing the glandular epithelium did not stain the stomodeum (Kawamura et al. 2025). The present study showed that RTase signals were continuous from undifferentiated‐like cells to the stomodeum. Therefore, ectodermal undifferentiated‐like cells are likely to form mainly the stomodeum, although cell lineage analyses remain unsolved (Figure 8).

Summary of the developmental fate of progenitor/undifferentiated cells in A. tenuis . Outer blastomeres and the blastomere‐derived glandular epithelium give rise to yolk cells, “bottom” cells, and “middle” cells by unequal cell division (Kawamura et al., forthcoming). “Bottom” cells become TUBB3‐expressing nerve progenitors that are morphologically characterized by a notched nucleus and poor endoplasmic reticulum associated with the plasma membrane. Nerve progenitors differentiate into nerve cells or VHCs, depending on the oral‐aboral position. “Middle” cells become undifferentiated‐like cells that differentiate into the pharyngeal stomodeum at an early larval stage.

Nerve Progenitors Possess Dual Potential to Nerve and Vacuolated Cells

Neuroglandular progenitors in Nematostella express SoxB2 (Sebé‐Pedrós et al. 2018) and SoxC (Denner et al. 2024). In A. millepora , SoxC is expressed in putative sensory neurons throughout the larval stage (Shinzato et al. 2008). In A. tenuis , TUBB3 signals overlapped with SoxB2/SoxC signals in both aboral and non‐aboral positions (Kawamura et al., forthcoming), confirming that aboral and non‐aboral TUBB3‐positive cells both belonged to the nerve lineage.

We investigated the developmental fate of nerve progenitors located laterally in Acropora larvae using TUBB3 signal as a probe. A novel type of double‐positive cell for TUBB3 and ATG10 was identified and termed as VHC. In N. vectensis , some progenitor cells, such as neuroglandular progenitors, neurocnidocyte progenitors, and germline progenitors serve as a multipotent source of differentiated cells (Richards and Rentzsch 2015; Steger et al. 2022; Denner et al. 2024; Miramón‐Puértolas et al. 2024). Similar to Nematostella, Acropora nerve progenitors were multipotent; however, in contrast to Nematostella, they did not give rise to gland cells, but formed VHCs (Figure 8). Gland cells have not been discovered ultrastructurally from Acropora ectoderm (Kawamura et al. 2025). The translucent vacuoles of VHCs have already appeared in primitive neuroblasts, which suggests that VHCs appear at the earliest stage of cell commitment to nerve progenitors, depending on the position along the oral‐aboral axis of animals.

In A. tenuis , nerve cells were mainly located around the aboral pole of 8 dpf larvae onward (Kawamura et al. 2025), similar to nerve cells in Hydrozoa (Gajewski et al. 1996; Leitz and Lay 1995; Katsukura et al. 2004; Piraino et al. 2011), Scyphozoa so‐called jellyfish (Nakanishi et al. 2008), and Anthozoa (Sinigaglia et al. 2013; Attenborough et al. 2019; Zang and Nakanishi 2020; Gilbert et al. 2022). The present study showed that in A. tenuis , TUBB3‐positive nerve progenitors declined in number from the aboral pole toward the oral pole, and were scarcely observable in the oral end of the larva. The nerve progenitor originated from “bottom” cell that was segregated from the glandular epithelium by unequal cell division (Figure 8) (Kawamura et al., forthcoming). The cell segregation might rarely occur at the oral area, which would explain why fewer number of nerve progenitors were located in the oral pole. Alternatively, the cell segregation may occur similarly in both oral and aboral domains, but the TUBB3 expression may be suppressed in the oral region. β‐Catenin in sea anemone appears to establish the oral‐aboral patterning by promoting the expression of NvSix3/6 (Leclère et al. 2016). Six3/6 is a homeodomain transcription factor that acts as a key regulator for the development of an aboral territory in Nematostella (Sinigaglia et al. 2013). SoxB2 is one of the positive regulators of neurogenesis (Zang and Nakanishi 2020). In bilaterians, Sox proteins bind β‐catenin and modulate Wnt signaling, although the precise mechanism of modulation remains unclear (Kormish et al. 2010). If similar molecular interactions operate in Acropora embryos, AtSoxB2 (Kawamura et al., forthcoming) and the downstream β‐catenin would regulate the developmental fate of nerve progenitors.

VHCs in A. tenuis expressed AtATG10. The roles of the autophagy‐related gene product in coral embryogenesis and larval morphogenesis have yet to be elucidated. In mammals, Sox2‐mediated ATG10 gene expression activates lysosomes and intracellular vacuole formation (Cho et al. 2013).

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated for the first time that in the coral, A. tenuis , the pharyngeal stomodeum is one of the developmental fates of ectodermal undifferentiated‐like cells. The results obtained herein also showed that nerve progenitors in A. tenuis are multipotent similarly to other cnidarians; however, the developmental fates of nerve progenitors are different from those of them, one for nerve cells and the other for VHCs.
