Background

Despite the widespread use of retrograde cardioplegia in most institutions, some surgeons continue to encounter technical difficulties during insertion of the retrograde cardioplegia cannula (RCC). Techniques for RCC insertion using manual palpation have been described by Gundry and colleagues [1] and Drinkwater et al. [2]. Both approaches rely on placement of the index or long finger along the left atrioventricular (AV) groove to guide the catheter into the coronary sinus (CS) prior to establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). However, as a posterior low-pressure structure, the CS can be difficult to reliably identify, which may result in prolonged manipulation that is occasionally associated with hemodynamic instability or arrhythmias. In addition, coronary sinus rupture is a recognized complication, particularly when forceful advancement of the cannula is attempted against the thin and poorly palpable wall. We introduce a 4-finger technique to facilitate identification, localization, and palpation of the CS at the left AV groove during RCC insertion on the beating heart, after venous cannulation but before initiation of CPB. Using this approach, the right atrial opening of the CS can be more readily localized, facilitating controlled cannula advancement. The four fingers are positioned to identify key anatomical landmarks, including the inferior vena cava (IVC), left atrial (LA) wall, left ventricular (LV) contour, and the advancing tip of the RCC. The aim of this study was to describe the 4-finger technique, evaluate its efficacy, and assess the learning curve among individual surgeons utilizing this approach.

Methods

Description of the 4-finger technique

From the surgeon’s side, through a stab incision in the centre of a purse-string suture, the flexible retrograde cardioplegia cannula is introduced into the right atrium (RA) and held with the surgeon’s left hand. Using gentle and controlled movements, the little finger wraps around the inferior surface of the IVC, while the ring finger contacts the soft posterior LA wall and the index finger rests against the contracting LV myocardium. In this configuration, the middle finger slides along the left AV groove and meets the ring finger at the junction of the atrial wall and the IVC (Fig. 2). From our experience, it is quite important to have moderate flexion of the little finger to gently compress the IVC against the venous cannula by applying upward and rightward traction. This maneuver prevents inadvertent advancement of the RCC into the IVC and assists in straightening the CS ostium to facilitate cannulation. Mild flexion of the ring finger, combined with maintaining the index finger in an extended position, allows the middle finger a broad range of motion along the left AV groove. This facilitates localization of the cannula tip through the atrial wall at the atriocaval junction. Once the cannula tip is palpated, controlled advancement of the RCC toward the tip of the middle finger allows smooth and atraumatic entry into the CS. In this technique, the middle finger serves as the primary tactile guide for safe and efficient cannula passage through the CS ostium. We also recommend positioning the purse-string suture relatively higher on the right atrial wall, while avoiding proximity to the right AV groove and right coronary artery. This positioning allows the distal portion of the RCC to be accommodated within the right atrial cavity, thereby facilitating unrestricted movement of the cannula tip during guidance into the coronary sinus.

Confirmation of cannulation

Following successful cannulation, CS cannula position was routinely and frequently confirmed using the standard intraoperative criteria, including darker venous blood return, an appropriate coronary sinus pressure waveform, and effective delivery of retrograde cardioplegia evidenced by pressure build-up and, where applicable, inspection of the backflow from the coronary ostia. In addition, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was available intraoperatively and used selectively based on the indication for the indexed cardiac procedure and underlying cardiac pathology. TEE confirmation was performed at the discretion of the anaesthetic team, particularly in cases with anatomical distortion or uncertainty regarding cannula position. The purpose of the present study was not to replace echocardiographic confirmation, but rather to describe a tactile guidance technique for initial cannula placement prior to, or in conjunction with, standard confirmation methods. We emphasize that TEE remains an important adjunct for verification of correct coronary sinus cannula positioning where available.

Study design

We conducted a prospective observational study involving 200 consecutive adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery requiring retrograde cardioplegia cannulation. The 4-finger technique was the primary method of cannulation. The study aimed to evaluate the learning curve associated with the technique by assessing procedural performance across participating surgeons and the confidence gained through structured understanding of 4-finger positioning and posterior cardiac anatomy to achieve reliable and time-efficient cannulation. The primary outcome was successful first-attempt cannulation of the coronary sinus. Secondary outcomes included time to successful cannulation, intraoperative hemodynamic disturbances, and the need for conversion to an alternative cannulation method. Cases were evenly distributed among four attending surgeons (designated A, B, C, and D), each performed 50 consecutive procedures. Learning-curve analysis was performed by dividing each surgeon’s experience into two sequential cohorts: the first 25 cases (Phase I) and the subsequent 25 cases (Phase II). In addition, an aggregate learning-curve analysis of all 200 cases was performed to provide a single-centre reference. Surgeon feedback and subjective experience with the technique were independently assessed using a standardized five-point Likert scale questionnaire (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). Cannulation time was measured in real time using a stopwatch by an independent assistant positioned at the anaesthetic side of the operating table (nurse, resident physician, or surgical assistant). After performing the stab incision within the purse-string suture and dilating the opening to facilitate passage, the operating surgeon introduced the retrograde cardioplegia cannula into the right atrium and held it with the left hand. Timing commenced at the moment the retrograde cardioplegia cannula was introduced through the right atrial purse-string suture and ended when the cannula was securely positioned and stabilized by tightening the purse-string suture around it. Stable coronary sinus cannula placement was confirmed by the operating surgeon based on tactile feedback and an appropriate coronary sinus pressure waveform, with transesophageal echocardiographic confirmation when available. The same timing protocol and measurement criteria were applied uniformly across all surgeons and all cases. The study intentionally included surgeons with varying levels of experience to evaluate the reproducibility and adaptability of the technique, rather than to assess novice performance alone. Both self-inflating balloon cannulas and syringe-inflated balloon cannulas were used according to surgeon preference and case requirements.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables, including cannulation time, were summarised as median and range, and compared between phases using the Mann–Whitney U test due to non-normal distribution. Categorical variables, such as first-attempt success rates and complication frequencies, were expressed as counts and percentages, and analysed using Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The primary outcome was improvement in cannulation time and first-attempt success rate between phases. Secondary outcomes included complication rates and need for conversion to alternative cannulation techniques. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version XX, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Between July 2022 and July 2025, the 4-finger technique was applied in 200 consecutive adult patients undergoing cardiac surgery requiring retrograde cardioplegia delivery. The study population was heterogeneous and included a broad range of cardiac pathologies and procedures, such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), aortic valve replacement (AVR), mitral valve repair or replacement, and complex aortic surgery, including ascending aorta and hemiarch procedures. The mean patient age was 65 ± 9 years, with a male predominance (68%). The mean left ventricular ejection fraction was 54 ± 10%, reflecting preserved or mildly reduced systolic function in the majority of patients. Inter-surgeon variability

A clear learning curve was observed in the individual surgeon analyses (Fig. 3). During the first 25 cases (Phase I), the mean cannulation time was 54 ± 20 s, with an 88% first-attempt success rate. In the subsequent 25 cases (Phase II), mean cannulation time improved to 33 ± 12 s, and first-attempt success increased to 100%. The overall mean cannulation time across the single-centre cohort was 42 ± 18 s.

Each line shows how cannulation time decreased significantly after the first 25 cases, reflecting a rapid acquisition of skill. All four surgeons demonstrated consistent improvement in cannulation time, particularly during Phase I. Performance converged during Phase II, with all surgeons achieving reliable first-attempt success and cannulation times consistently below 50 s by case 25. Importantly, no coronary sinus injuries, air embolism, or hemodynamic compromise occurred in either phase, and no patient required intraoperative escalation or additional venous drainage related to retrograde cardioplegia cannulation (Table 2). Despite variation in baseline experience among the participating surgeons, all demonstrated improvement in cannulation efficiency and success rates, with convergence of performance by the end of the study. Initial variability observed during Phase I was attributed to differing levels of prior experience with blind retrograde coronary sinus cannulation. The learning curve plateaued rapidly for all surgeons, with most achieving greater than 90% first-attempt success within 30 cases. Phase II success rates remained consistently above 90%, with low variability in cannulation time. Notably, Surgeon A demonstrated the most rapid adaptation, achieving cannulation times below 75 s by case 15, whereas Surgeon B exhibited greater early variability but demonstrated the steepest improvement trajectory over time.

Single-centre corrected analysis

Overall, successful initial cannulation using the four-finger technique was achieved in 188 of 200 cases (94%). No cases required direct visual confirmation, conversion to an alternative cannulation technique, or initiation of partial cardiopulmonary bypass. The mean cannulation time across all cases was 42 ± 18 s.

Discussion

The facile, yet blind, placement of RCC was first described by Gundry and associates [1] using manual palpation of the AV groove to guide the catheter into the coronary sinus before establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass. Prolonged manipulation may occur due to difficulty in accurately identifying the position of the coronary sinus on the posterior surface of the heart, particularly in the presence of cardiomegaly. Such prolonged manipulation may be associated with transient hemodynamic instability and arrhythmias. The 4-finger technique, although still partially blind, provides guidance for quick and accurate detection of the CS posteriorly while also helping to straighten the CS opening and support its thin wall. Drinkwater and associates [2] mainly relied on low position of the purse-string into the right atrium to avoid redundancy and directing the cannula at an approximate 45-degree angle toward the left shoulder. In contrast, the four-finger technique directs the cannula tip toward the guiding middle finger, providing continuous tactile support along its course toward the coronary sinus lumen. Placement of the purse-string suture relatively higher on the right atrial wall, while avoiding proximity to the right AV groove and the right coronary artery, facilitates unrestricted movement of the cannula tip during guidance into the CS. Drinkwater and associates abandoned proceeding if two or three attempts were unsuccessful. In such scenario, they recommended an alternative approach involving canulation after initiation of CPB with partial cardiac filling and exposure of the posterior heart. This could minimize hemodynamic changes during placement. Similarly, Arom and Emery [3] described coronary sinus cannulation using manual palpation exclusively after initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), a strategy that may reduce the incidence of hemodynamic instability during placement. However, air entrainment into the venous line during cannula manipulation is not uncommon with this approach. In selected clinical scenarios, secure placement of the retrograde cardioplegia cannula prior to initiation of CPB may therefore be preferable.

Geha and Lee [4] described placement of the retrograde catheter after initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) under direct visual inspection of the coronary sinus following cephalad retraction of the decompressed heart. Although this approach has been reported as safe, air entrainment into the venous line may complicate the procedure. In addition, attempts to partially fill the heart to minimize air entry can obscure visualization of posterior cardiac structures, thereby increasing technical difficulty. In this context, the 4-finger technique provides a tactile approach that facilitates CS cannulation prior to CPB with minimal hemodynamic disturbance and a favourable learning curve.

In anatomically challenging cases, the traditional palpation or single-finger techniques may fail to provide reliable anatomical orientation. In contrast to single-point palpation methods, the four-finger technique allowed stable control of the IVC vena cava via the little finger, tactile triangulation of the LA wall and LV contour, and localization of the CS junction through coordinated movement of the middle and ring fingers. Cannulation was successfully achieved using the 4-finger technique without the need for cardiac retraction or initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass.

Since introduction, retrograde cardioplegia has become widely used in contemporary cardiac surgery, particularly in cases of aortic regurgitation and complex coronary pathology. However, blind cannulation of the coronary sinus remains technically demanding and is associated with reported challenges including misplacement, arrhythmias, and, rarely, coronary sinus injury [5]. Published data suggests that variability in operator experience contributes to inconsistent success rates using conventional techniques. These limitations highlight the need for structured, reproducible approaches that improve anatomical orientation and facilitate safe and efficient cannulation. The findings of our study highlight the potential value of the 4-finger technique especially as teaching tool. We note that a structured tactile framework may facilitate earlier skill acquisition and potentially result in an even steeper learning curve in supervised training settings. This represents an important area for future study.

Conclusion

The four-finger technique was associated with a high first-attempt success rate, reduced cannulation time, and a short learning curve across operators. This technique provides a reproducible approach for retrograde coronary sinus cannulation. We also wish to state that cannulation time and first-attempt success serve as surrogate markers of procedural efficiency and feasibility, but do not capture elements such as tissue handling, spatial judgment, or decision-making.

Limitations

Despite its benefits, several limitations to the current study should be acknowledged:

Partially Blind Technique: While more structured than single-finger methods, the technique remains palpation-based and blind to direct visualization. In anatomically distorted hearts or re-do cases, this may still pose challenges.

Operator Dependence: Successful execution depends on the surgeon’s ability to recognize subtle tactile cues. Variability in finger length, hand size, or positioning may influence precision, particularly in smaller patients or paediatric populations.

Lack of Comparative Randomized Data: While favourable outcomes were observed, this was a single-arm experience without direct comparison to traditional cannulation methods or other tactile guidance techniques. Controlled studies are needed to confirm its superiority or equivalence.
