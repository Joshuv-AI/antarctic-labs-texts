Statistical solutions to the Euler system of gas dynamics
Eduard Feireisl

Abstract
We consider the (complete) Euler system describing the motion of a compressible perfect fluid. We propose a platform suitable for constructing the statistical solutions. The main ingredients of our approach include: • The concept of dissipative (measure–valued) solution to the Euler system. • A single step selection procedure based on minimizing the Bregman divergence of a given solution to the maximal entropy equilibrium. • A construction of a Markov semigroup via push forward measures.

1
Introduction

The Euler system of gas dynamics belongs to the iconic examples of non–linear
hyperbolic systems of conservation laws. The motion of a compressible perfect fluid (gas) confined to a (bounded) container

\Omega\subset R^{d}
,
d=1,2,3
is described by means of three field equations
expressing the basic underlying physical principles of

the mass conservation

\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}{\bf m}=0;

(1.1)

the momentum balance

\partial_{t}{\bf m}+{\rm div}_{x}\left(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{\varrho}+p\mathbb{I}\right)=0;

(1.2)

the energy balance

\partial_{t}E+{\rm div}_{x}\left((E+p)\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}\right)=0.

(1.3)

We retain the mass density
\varrho=\varrho(t,x)
and the momentum

{\bf m}={\bf m}(t,x)
as the state variables, while the pressure

p
as well as the energy
E
will be given constitutively. Specifically, we write the energy in the form

E=\frac{1}{2}\frac{|{\bf m}|^{2}}{\varrho}+\varrho e,

(1.4)

where the internal energy
e
is related to the pressure by the polytropic equation of state

p=(\gamma-1)\varrho e,\ \gamma>1.

(1.5)

Finally, we introduce the entropy
s
interrelated to the internal energy and the pressure through Gibbs’ equation

\vartheta Ds=De+pD\left(\frac{1}{\varrho}\right),

(1.6)

where the new variable
\vartheta
is the absolute temperature. For the sake of simplicity, and actually without loss of generality, we may suppose

p=\varrho\vartheta,\ e=c_{v}\vartheta,\ c_{v}=\frac{1}{\gamma-1},\ s=c_{v}\log(\vartheta)-\log(\varrho),

(1.7)

where
c_{v}
is the specific heat at constant volume. Observe that the polytropic equation of state (
1.5
) is incomplete (cf. the monograph
of Benzoni–Gavage and Serre
[
7
]
), and its specific form (
1.7
) is determined by a proper choice of the absolute temperature
\vartheta
.

It is convenient to complete the family phase variables
\varrho
,
{\bf m}
, by adding the total entropy
S(t,x)=\varrho(t,x)s(\varrho(t,x),\vartheta(t,x))
.
The problem is formally closed by imposing

the impermeability boundary conditions

{\bf m}\cdot{\bf n}|_{\partial\Omega};

(1.8)

and

the initial conditions

\varrho(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0},\ {\bf m}(0,\cdot)={\bf m}_{0},\ S=\varrho s(0,\cdot)=S_{0}.

(1.9)

As is well know, the Euler system is locally well posed in a class of sufficiently regular solutions, however, singularities, typically shock waves, may appear in a finite time no matter how smooth and small the initial data are cf. e.g. the monograph by Dafermos
[
15
]
. The weak (distributional) solutions are able to accommodate singularities but are, in general, not unique cf. Smoller
[
36
]
.

To
recover uniqueness, the entropy admissibility criterion has been proposed, namely

the entropy inequality

\partial_{t}(\varrho s)+{\rm div}_{x}(s{\bf m})\geq 0.

(1.10)

The inequality (
1.10
) can be seen as a mathematical formulation of the Second law of thermodynamics, see Dafermos
[
16
]
.

There has been a common belief that the admissibility condition (
1.10
) may recover uniqueness in the class of weak solutions. This is partially
true in the one space dimension
d=1
. Besides the classical results that can be found in
[
15
]
, there is a number of
quite recent results by Vasseur et al.
[
12
]
,
[
32
]
.
However, the recent adaptation of the method of convex integration initiated in the seminal work of De Lellis and Székelyhidi

[
17
]
,
[
18
]
revealed a number rather disturbing facts concerning the well posedness of the Euler system in the class of admissible weak solutions in the space dimension
d=2,3
:

•

There is a large class of initial data for which the
Euler system admits infinitely many admissible weak solutions,
see
[
21
]
. In particular, a multidimensional extension
of the classical Riemann problem is ill posed, see
[
1
]
,
[
33
]
, at least for certain initial data.

•

The set of the “wild” initial data that give rise to infinitely many entropy admissible weak solutions is dense in the
L^{p}
-topology of the phase space
[
13
]
.

•

The ill–posedness of the problem persists under stochastic perturbations, see
[
14
]
.

We consider the Euler system in the framework of
statistical solutions
. These were introduced by
Foias
[
27
]
, and Vishik and
Fursikov
[
37
]
in the context of the incompressible Navier–Stokes system. The original theory has been
later developed by
Roger Temam
and his collaborators
in a series of works
[
28
]
,

[
29
]
,
[
30
]
. Very roughly indeed, a statistical solution is a stochastic process supported by the solution set of a given problem. The initial data are understood as random variables ranging in the associated phase space.

In
[
19
]
, a direct construction of a statistical solution was proposed being simply a pushforward measure of the distribution of the initial data. In such a way, a Markov semigroup on the space of probability measures is obtained characterizing the distribution of the solution at any positive time
t>0
. Obviously, such an approach requires the problem
to admit a Borel measurable semigroup of solutions.

The method of Krylov
[
35
]
and its adaptation by Cardona and Kapitanski
[
11
]
was used in
[
19
]

to select a solution semigroup for the compressible Navier-Stokes system (cf. also Basarić
[
6
]
). Motivated
by
[
20
]
,
[
24
]
, we extend the class of
admissible (weak) solutions of the Euler system to the dissipative
measure valued solutions introduced in
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
. In contrast with
[
19
]
, where an infinite family of cost functionals is needed to select a unique solution, we propose a single step selection criterion
enforced by the Second law of thermodynamics. Specifically,
we elaborate on the classical statement of Clausius (Rudolf Clausius, Poggendorff’s Annals of Physics 1865 (125), 400) considered as the most flagrant formulation of the Second law of thermodynamics:

The energy of the world (closed system) is constant; its entropy tends to a maximum.

Accordingly, we propose a selection criterion based on minimizing the distance to the equilibrium state that maximizes the entropy. Once a proper selection of the solution mapping is made, the construction of the associated Markov semigroup is a routine matter.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2

we recall the definition of dissipative (measure–valued) solutions of the Euler system and their basic properties.
In Section
3
, we propose a single step selection criterion based on minimization of the distance to the maximal entropy equilibrium. In particular, we show that the energy defect of the selected solution vanishes for
t\to\infty
, which is a result of independent interest. The main result on the existence of a suitable statistical solution is stated in Section
4
.
The proof of of the basic properties of statistical solutions is given in Section
5
. The paper is concluded by a short discussion in Section
6
.

2
Dissipative solutions

Despite the vast set of the (wild) initial data, for which the Euler system admits infinitely many admissible
weak
solutions, the
existence
of weak solutions for general initial data remains
an outstanding open problem. Following
[
9
]
we extend the class
of solutions incorporating all possible limits of their consistent
approximations, cf.
[
23
, Part II, Chapter 5]
. We point out that, in contrast with the “pure” measure–valued approach
proposed by e.g. by Fjordholm et al.
[
25
]
,
[
26
]
, Gwiazda et al.
[
31
]
, Klingenberg et al.
[
34
]
, the dissipative solutions are represented by

functions
rather than parametrized (Young) measures.

First, we introduce the kinetic energy

\frac{1}{2}\frac{|{\bf m}|^{2}}{\varrho}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{1}{2}\frac{|{\bf m}|^{2}}{\varrho}\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho>0,\\
0\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho=0,{\bf m}=0,\\
\infty\ \mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

Similarly, the internal energy and the pressure are defined as

p(\varrho,S)=(\gamma-1)\varrho e(\varrho,S)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varrho^{\gamma}\exp\left(\frac{S}{c_{v}\varrho}\right)\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho>0,\\
0\ \mbox{if}\ \varrho=0,\ S\leq 0,\\
\infty\ \mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

Finally, the total energy reads

E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{|{\bf m}|^{2}}{\varrho}+\varrho e(\varrho,S),

(2.3)

where we have adopted (
2.1
), (
2.2
).
In accordance with (
2.1
), (
2.2
), the total energy
E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)
is a convex l.s.c. function of
(\varrho,{\bf m},S)\in R^{d+2}
, strictly convex on its domain.

The dissipative solutions introduced below will belong to the phase space

\mathcal{P}=\left\{(\varrho,{\bf m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})\ \Big|\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\mathcal{E}_{0},\ S\geq\underline{s}\varrho,\ \int_{\Omega}\varrho\ \,{\rm d}{x}\geq m_{0}>0\right\},

where
\underline{s}\in R
,
m_{0}>0
are a given constants. It is easy to check that the set
\mathcal{P}
is convex.

Definition 2.1

(
Dissipative solution).

We say that
(\varrho,{\bf m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
is
dissipative
solution
of the Euler system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
),
(
1.10
), with the boundary condition (
1.8
), and the initial data

(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\in\mathcal{P}

in the space time cylinder
(0,\infty)\times\Omega

if there exists a parametrized measure

\mathcal{V}_{t,x},\ t\in(0,\infty),\ x\in\Omega,\ (t,x)\mapsto\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm weak-*}((0,\infty)\times\Omega;\mathfrak{P}(R^{d+2}))\ \mbox{for a.a}\ t\in(0,\infty),\ x\in\Omega,

(2.4)

and a matrix–valued measure

\mathfrak{C}\in L^{\infty}_{\rm weak-*}(0,\infty;\mathcal{M}^{+}(\overline{\Omega};R^{d+2}))

(2.5)

such that the following holds:

1.

The trio
(\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t,x)
coincides with the expected
value of the parametrized measure
\mathcal{V}_{t,x}
,

(\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t,x)=\int_{R^{d+2}}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S})\ {\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ t\in(0,\infty),\ x\in\Omega;

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t\pm,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{0},\ S(t\pm,\cdot)\geq\underline{s}\varrho(t,\cdot),\ \int_{\Omega}\varrho(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\geq m_{0}\ \mbox{for any}\ t>0,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(0+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}_{0},\ S(0+,\cdot)\geq\underline{s}\varrho_{0}.

2.

The equation of continuity (
1.1
) is satisfied in the sense of distributions,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\Big[\varrho\partial_{t}\varphi+{\bf m}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t=\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}\varphi(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.6)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega})
.

3.

The momentum equation
1.2
is replaced by the integral identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[{\bf m}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\int_{R^{d+2}}\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{\varrho}>0}\left(\frac{\widetilde{{\bf m}}\otimes\widetilde{{\bf m}}}{\widetilde{\varrho}}+p(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{S})\right){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\cdot\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}\mathfrak{C}:\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right)\,{\rm d}t-\int_{\Omega}{\bf m}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.7)

for any
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in C^{1}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega};R^{d})
,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot{\bf n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

4.

The entropy balance (
1.10
) holds as an integral inequality

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left[S\partial_{t}\varphi+\int_{R^{d+2}}\left(\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{\varrho}>0}\widetilde{S}\frac{\widetilde{{\bf m}}}{\widetilde{\varrho}}\right){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\leq-\int_{\Omega}S_{0}\varphi(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.8)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega})
,
\varphi\geq 0
.

5.

The energy balance (
1.3
) is replaced by the total energy balance

\int_{\Omega}\int_{R^{d+2}}E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S}){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{\tau,x}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+c(d,\gamma)\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}{\rm trace}[\mathfrak{C}(\tau,\cdot)]\leq\mathcal{E}_{0},\ c(d,\gamma)=\min

(2.9)

for a.a.
\tau\geq 0
.

We refer the reader to
[
23
, Chapter 5]
for
a detailed discussion of the concept of dissipative solutions to various problems in fluid mechanics and their use in numerical analysis.
They have been identified as limits of the so-called consistent approximation. The parametric family
(\mathcal{V}_{t,x})_{t\in(0,\infty),x\in\Omega}
coincides
with the Young measure characterizing the oscillations of the approximate sequence, while
\mathfrak{C}
represents the oscillation defect measure.

We record the following properties of dissipative solutions:

•

Global existence.
The Euler system admits global–in–time dissipative solutions for any choice of the initial data
(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\in\mathcal{P}
, see
[
8
, Proposition 3.8]
.

•

Weak continuity.

The functions
\varrho
,
{\bf m}
belong to the class

\varrho,\ {\bf m}\in BC_{\rm weak}([0,\infty);L^{\gamma}(\Omega));

(2.10)

the total entropy admits one-sided limits

\lim_{t\to\tau\pm}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\phi\ \,{\rm d}{x},\ \lim_{t\to 0+}\int_{\Omega}S(t,\cdot)\phi\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \mbox{for any}\ \phi\in L^{\gamma^{\prime}}(\Omega).

(2.11)

In particular
S(t\pm)
can be identified with a function in
L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
, where we set
S(0-)=S_{0}
. There holds

S(t-)\leq S(t+)\ \mbox{for any}\ t\geq 0,\ \|S(t\pm)\|_{L^{\gamma}(\Omega)}\leq\overline{S}\ \mbox{for all}\ t\geq 0,

(2.12)

see
[
23
, Chapter 5, Section 5.2]
.

•

Compatibility.
If

(\varrho,{\bf m},S)\in C^{1}([T_{1},T_{2}]\times\overline{\Omega};R^{d})
,

\inf_{[T_{1},T_{2}]\times\overline{\Omega}}\varrho>0
, and

\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ t\in(T_{1},T_{2}),

then
(\varrho,{\bf m},S)
is a classical solution of the Euler system
in
[T_{1},T_{2}]
, see
[
23
, Chapter 5, Theorem 5.7]
.

•

Weak–strong uniqueness.

The dissipative solution coincides with the strong
W^{1,\infty}
solution of the Euler system as long as the latter solution exists,
[
23
, Chapter 6, Theorem 6.2]
.

3
Selection criterion

Similarly to their weak counterparts, the dissipative solutions are not unique for a vast class of physically admissible initial data. In
[
8
]
, a selection procedure in the spirit of Krylov
[
35
]
and Cardona, Kapitanski
[
11
]
was proposed to identify a unique physically relevant solution. This process consists of a successive minimization of a countable family of “cost functionals” separating points in the phase space
\mathcal{P}
. A proper choice of these as well as the order of selection is, however, arbitrary and any “physical” justification is unclear. A significant progress has been made in
[
22
]
, where the selection process was reduced to merely two steps, and even a single step selection was proposed. Developing the ideas of
[
22
]
, we consider a single step selection process minimizing the Bregman distance
of solutions to the constant equilibrium maximizing the entropy.

3.1
Stable equilibrium state

It follows directly from the equation of continuity (
2.6
) that the total mass

\int_{\Omega}\varrho(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}\ \,{\rm d}{x}=M_{0}>0

(3.1)

is conserved. Given the total energy
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, the stable
equilibrium density-temperature is given by the constant state

(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})
,

\overline{\varrho}=\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}\,{\rm d}{x},\ \mathcal{E}_{0}=c_{v}\int_{\Omega}\overline{\varrho}\overline{\vartheta}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \Rightarrow\ \overline{\vartheta}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{c_{v}M_{0}}.

Next, we introduce the Bregman distance (divergence) associated to the convex energy
E
,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\left(\varrho,{\bf m},S\Big|\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S}\right)

\displaystyle=E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)-\frac{\partial E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S})}{\partial\varrho}(\varrho-\widetilde{\varrho})-\frac{\partial E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S})}{\partial{\bf m}}\cdot({\bf m}-\widetilde{{\bf m}})-\frac{\partial E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S})}{\partial S}(S-\widetilde{S})

\displaystyle\quad-E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S}).

We refer to
[
23
, Chapter 5]
for a detailed discussion of the concept of Bregman divergence associated to the total energy
E
.

Evaluating the Bregman distance of a dissipative solution
(\varrho,{\bf m},S)
to the equilibrium solution

(\overline{\varrho},0,\overline{\varrho}s(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}))
, and integrating over
\Omega
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}E\left(\varrho,{\bf m},S\Big|\overline{\varrho},0,\overline{\varrho}s(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S]\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\overline{\vartheta}M_{0}s(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})-\mathcal{E}_{0},

(3.2)

cf.
[
23
, Chapter 5]
.
This motivates the choice of the cost functional

\mathcal{F}(\varrho,{\bf m},S)=\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x},\overline{\vartheta}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{c_{v}M_{0}}

(3.3)

considered already in
[
22
]
.

3.2
A single step selection

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]=

\displaystyle\left\{(\varrho,{\bf m},S)\ \Big|\ (\varrho,{\bf m},S)\ \mbox{is a dissipative solution in}\ (0,\infty)\times\Omega\right.

\displaystyle\mbox{emanating from the data}\ (\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0})\Big\}

be the set of all dissipative solutions associated to the initial data
(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0};\mathcal{E}_{0})
.
We introduce the weighted time-measure

\omega\,{\rm d}t=\exp(-t)\,{\rm d}t,

along with the associated weighted spaces

L^{q}_{\omega}(0,\infty;L^{r}(\Omega;R^{d+2})),\ L^{q}_{\omega}(0,\infty;W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;R^{d+2})),\ \ell>d.

We refer to
[
23
, Chapter 5, Theorem 5.2]
for the proof of the following properties of the solution set

\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
:

1.

The set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is non–empty and convex whenever
(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\in\mathcal{P}
.

2.

The set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is closed and bounded in the Banach space

L^{q}_{\omega}(0,\infty;L^{r}(\Omega;R^{d+2})),\ 1\leq q<\infty,\ 1\leq r\leq\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}.

3.

The set
\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
is compact in the Banach space

L^{q}_{\omega}(0,\infty;W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;R^{d+2})),\ \ell>d.

We are ready to introduce the concept of
admissible dissipative solution
to the Euler system selected in a single step.

Definition 3.1

(
Admissible dissipative solution).

We say that
(\varrho,{\bf m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
is
admissible dissipative solution
to the Euler system with the initial data

(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\in\mathcal{P}

if

(\varrho,{\bf m},S)={\rm argmin}_{\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-t)\left(\mathcal{F}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S})(t,\cdot)\right)\,{\rm d}t,

(3.4)

meaning
(\varrho,{\bf m},S)\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)-\overline{\vartheta}S\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\Big[E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S})-\overline{\vartheta}\widetilde{S}\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t

(3.5)

for all
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S})\in\mathcal{U}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}]
.

As shown in
[
22
]
, admissible dissipative solution enjoy the following properties:

1.

Existence and uniqueness.

For any initial data
(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})
, the Euler system admits a unique admissible dissipative solution.

2.

Semigroup property.
The solution mapping

\mathcal{S}:(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\mapsto(\varrho(t,\cdot),{\bf m}(t,\cdot),S(t-,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0}))

enjoys the semigroup property:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0}](t+s,\cdot)=\mathcal{S}\Big[\mathcal{S}[\varrho(t,\cdot,{\bf m}(t,\cdot),S(t-,\cdot),\mathcal{E}\Big](s,\cdot),\ t,s\geq 0.

(3.6)

3.

Borel measurability.
The mapping

(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\in\mathcal{P}\mapsto(\varrho(t,\cdot),{\bf m}(t,\cdot),S(t-,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0})\in\mathcal{P}

is Borel measurable with respect to the
W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;R^{d+2})\times R
Hilbert topology for any
t\geq 0
.

3.3
Vanishing energy defect for
t\to\infty

In addition to the properties of admissible dissipative solutions proved in
[
22
]
, we show that
any such solution is “almost weak” for
t\to\infty
. To this end, we first introduce the
energy defect

d_{E}(t)=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}.

(3.7)

Note that, in accordance with (
2.9
),

\displaystyle d_{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\int_{R^{d+2}}E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S}){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\int_{\Omega}E\left(\int_{R^{d+2}}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S}){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle+c(d,\gamma)\int_{\overline{\Omega}}{\rm d}{\rm trace}[\mathfrak{C}(t,\cdot)]

(3.8)

for a.a.
t>0
. By virtue of Jensen’s inequality, the first term

\int_{\Omega}\int_{R^{d+2}}E(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S}){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\int_{\Omega}E\left(\int_{R^{d+2}}(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S}){\rm d}\mathcal{V}_{t,x}\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

is always non-negative and vanishes only if
\mathcal{V}_{t,x}
is the Dirac mass centred at
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S})(t,x)
for a.a.
x\in\Omega
.
Consequently, if
d_{E}=0
, the admissible dissipative solution is a weak solution satisfying the Euler system in the following sense:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}{\bf m}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf m}+{\rm div}_{x}\left(\frac{\widetilde{{\bf m}}\otimes\widetilde{{\bf m}}}{\widetilde{\varrho}}+p(\varrho,S)\mathbb{I}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S+{\rm div}_{x}\left(S\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ t>0,

(3.9)

where the derivatives are understood in the sense of distributions. Note that the energy defect
d_{E}
is related
to the magnitude of the so-called Reynolds stress in the theory of deterministic turbulence, cf. Bardos et al.
[
2
]
.

Our objective is to show the following result that is of independent interest.

Proposition 3.2

(
Vanishing energy defect).

Let
(\varrho,{\bf m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
be an admissible
dissipative solution of the Euler system in
(0,T)\times\Omega
.

Then

d_{E}(t)\equiv\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ t\to\infty.

The conclusion of Proposition
3.2
can be interpreted in the way that any admissible dissipative solutions approaches a weak solution of the Euler system in the long run. The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
3.2
. We proceed in several steps.

3.3.1
Preliminaries

We recall that dissipative solutions
always satisfy

\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t\pm,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\mathcal{E}_{0},\ (\varrho,{\bf m},S)(0-,\cdot)\equiv(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0}),

(3.10)

S(t\pm,\cdot)\geq\underline{s}\varrho.

(3.11)

Moreover,
it follows from (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) that

\displaystyle\varrho(t,x)

\displaystyle=0\ \Rightarrow\ {\bf m}(t,x)=0\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\varrho(t,x)

\displaystyle=0\ \Rightarrow\ S(t\pm,x)=0\ \mbox{for a.a.}\ x\in\Omega

(3.12)

3.3.2
Positive energy defect and jumps of the cost functional
\mathcal{F}

Suppose that

d_{E}(\tau)>0\ \mbox{for some}\ \tau>0.

(3.13)

Our aim is to show that the solution
(\varrho,{\bf m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
can be extended beyond
the time
\tau
in such a way that the cost functional
\mathcal{F}
evaluated at
\tau
experiences a negative jump proportional
to
d_{E}(\tau)
.

First, using (
3.12
), we evaluate the temperature

\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot)
from the relation

S(\tau+,\cdot)=\mathds{1}_{\varrho(\tau,\cdot)>0}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\Big(c_{v}\log(\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot))-\log(\varrho(\tau,\cdot))\Big).

Next, we introduce

\widetilde{\vartheta}(\tau,\cdot)=(1+\varepsilon)\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot)\ \mbox{whenever}\ \varrho(\tau,\cdot)>0

for
\varepsilon>0
to be determined below. Denoting the internal energy

E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)=c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot),

we get

\widetilde{E}_{\rm int}(\tau,\cdot)\equiv c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\widetilde{\vartheta}(\tau,\cdot)=E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)+\varepsilon c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot),

(3.14)

and

\widetilde{S}(\tau,\cdot)\equiv c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(\widetilde{\vartheta}(\tau,\cdot))-\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(\varrho(\tau,\cdot))=S(\tau+,\cdot)+c_{v}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(1+\varepsilon)

(3.15)

Now, we adjust
\varepsilon>0
so that

\widetilde{d}_{E}(\tau)=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},\widetilde{S})(\tau,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0,

(3.16)

meaning

\varepsilon c_{v}\int_{\Omega}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\vartheta(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=d_{E}(\tau)\ \Rightarrow\ \varepsilon=\frac{d_{E}(\tau)}{\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}}.

(3.17)

The relations (
3.16
), (
3.17
) imply that we may continue the original solution
(\varrho,{\bf m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
by concatenating it at the time
\tau
with
a solution
(\widetilde{\varrho},\widetilde{{\bf m}},\widetilde{S},\mathcal{E})
starting from the data

(\varrho(\tau,\cdot),{\bf m}(\tau,\cdot),\widetilde{S}(\tau,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0})\ \mbox{for}\ t\geq\tau,

with the energy defect
\widetilde{d}_{E}(\tau)=0
.

Next, we evaluate the cost functional at the time
\tau
of the concatenated solution:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},\widetilde{S})(\tau,\cdot)\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},{S})(\tau+,\cdot)\Big)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\overline{\vartheta}c_{v}\int_{\Omega}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(1+\varepsilon)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(3.18)

Moreover, using (
3.3
), (
3.17
) we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle-\overline{\vartheta}c_{v}\int_{\Omega}\varrho(\tau,\cdot)\log(1+\varepsilon)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle=d_{E}(\tau)-\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(1+\frac{d_{E}(\tau)}{\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}}\right).

(3.19)

Finally, we observe

\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\int_{\Omega}E\Big(\varrho,{\bf m},S)(\tau+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\mathcal{E}_{0}-d_{E}(\tau);

whence

d_{E}(\tau)-\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(1+\frac{d(\tau)}{\int_{\Omega}E_{\rm int}(\tau+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}}\right)\leq d_{E}(\tau)-\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(1+\frac{d(\tau)}{\mathcal{E}_{0}-d(\tau)}\right).

(3.20)

Introducing the function

G:y\mapsto\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(1+\frac{y}{\mathcal{E}_{0}-y}\right)-y=\mathcal{E}_{0}\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{\mathcal{E}_{0}-y}\right)-y=\mathcal{E}_{0}\log(\mathcal{E}_{0})-\mathcal{E}_{0}\log(\mathcal{E}_{0}-y)-y

(3.21)

satisfies

G(0)=0,\ G^{\prime}(y)=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{0}}{\mathcal{E}_{0}-y}-1>0\ \mbox{as long as}\ y>0.

(3.22)

Thus we may infer

\widetilde{d}_{E}(\tau)=0,\ \mathcal{F}\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},\widetilde{S})(\tau,\cdot)\Big)\leq\mathcal{F}\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},{S})(\tau+,\cdot)\Big)-G(d_{e}(\tau))

(3.23)

for the concatenated solution,
where
G
is defined in (
3.21
).

3.3.3
Vanishing defect for the selected solution as
t\to\infty

Having prepared the preliminary material, we are ready to prove Proposition
3.2
.

Assume that
(\varrho,{\bf m},S,\mathcal{E}_{0})
is an admissible dissipative solution, meaning the global minimizer of the cost functional

\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-t)\left(\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}\Big(\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right)\,{\rm d}t

(3.24)

on the set
\mathcal{U}(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})
.
Our aim is to show that the associated defect vanishes in the long run,

d_{E}(t)\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ t\to\infty.

(3.25)

We show (
3.25
) arguing by contradiction. If (
3.25
) is not true, than there exists
\overline{d}>0
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}d_{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\overline{d}>0,

(3.26)

\displaystyle d_{E}(\tau_{n})

\displaystyle\to\overline{d}\ \mbox{for a certain sequence}\ \tau_{n}\to\infty.

(3.27)

1.

Since

d_{E}(\tau_{n})=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(\tau_{n}+)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x},

we may rephrase (
3.26
), (
3.27
) as

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{E}_{0}-\overline{d},

(3.28)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{E}_{0}-\overline{d}\ \mbox{for a certain sequence}\ \tau_{n}\to\infty.

(3.29)

It follows from (
3.28
), (
3.29
) that
for any
\omega>0
, there exists
n=n(\omega)
such that

\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\geq\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\omega\ \mbox{for any}\ t\geq\tau_{n},\ n\geq n(\omega).

(3.30)

2.

As the total entropy admits a limit for
t\to\infty
,

\int_{\Omega}S(t\pm)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\nearrow\mathcal{S}_{\infty}<\infty\ \mbox{as}\ t\to\infty,

we conclude, similarly to (
3.30
),

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\overline{\vartheta}\int_{\Omega}S(t+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\quad\geq\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\overline{\vartheta}\int_{\Omega}S(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\omega\ \mbox{for all}\ t\geq\tau_{n},\ n\geq n(\omega).

(3.31)

3.

Now, we use the construction of Section

, namely increasing the value of entropy at
\tau_{n}+
and concatenating
the solution as in (
3.23

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},\widetilde{S})(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\overline{\vartheta}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{S}(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\quad=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\overline{\vartheta}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{S}(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},{S})(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-\overline{\vartheta}\int_{\Omega}{S}(\tau_{n}+,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}-G(d(\tau_{n})),

(3.32)

cf. (
3.23

In addition,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},\widetilde{S})(t\pm,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle-\overline{\vartheta}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{S}(t\pm,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\mathcal{E}_{0}-\overline{\vartheta}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{S}(t\pm,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(3.33)

G(d_{E}(\tau_{n}))>2\omega\ \mbox{whenever}\ n\geq n(\omega).

4
Statistical solutions - the main result

In the previous part, we have constructed a Borel measurable semigroup

\mathcal{S}:\Big[t,(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\Big]\in[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{P}\mapsto\Big(\varrho(t,\cdot),{\bf m}(t,\cdot),S(t-,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0}\Big)\in\mathcal{P}

(4.1)

consisting of admissible dissipative solutions of the Euler system.

Suppose we are given a complete Borel probability measure
\sigma\in\mathfrak{P}\Big[W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;R^{d})\times R\Big]
,

\sigma[\mathcal{P}]=1.

(4.2)

Next, we associate to
\sigma
its push forward measure
\sigma_{t}
with respect to the semigroup
\mathcal{S}
,

\int_{W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;R^{d+2})\times R}\mathcal{G}(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\ {\rm d}\sigma_{t}=\int_{\mathcal{P}}\mathcal{G}\left(\mathcal{S}\Big[t,(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\Big]\right){\rm d}\sigma\ \mbox{for any}\ t\geq 0,

(4.3)

and any
\mathcal{G}\in BC(W^{-\ell,2}(\Omega;R^{d})\times R)
.

Definition 4.1

(
Statistical solution).

The family of Markov operators

\mathcal{M}_{t}:\sigma\in\mathfrak{P}[\mathcal{P}]\to\sigma_{t}\in\mathfrak{P}[\mathcal{P}],\ t\geq 0,

(4.4)

defined through (
4.3
) is called
statistical solution
of the Euler system.

Let us summarize the basic properties of the statistical solutions:

1.

Markov semigroup.

The mapping

\mathcal{M}_{t}:\mathfrak{P}[\mathcal{P}]\to\mathfrak{P}[\mathcal{P}]

is a semigroup, meaning

\mathcal{M}_{0}=\mathbb{I},\ \mathcal{M}_{t+s}=\mathcal{M}_{t}\circ\mathcal{M}_{s},\ s,t\geq 0.

(4.5)

Moreover,

\mathcal{M}_{t}\Big[\delta_{(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})}\Big]=\delta_{\mathcal{S}[t,(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})]},\ t\geq 0;

(4.6)

\mathcal{M}_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{M}_{t}[\sigma_{i}]\ \mbox{whenever}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1,\ \lambda_{i}\geq 0;

(4.7)

t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{t}[\sigma]\ \mbox{is c\`{a}gl\`{a}d in the narrow topology on}\ \mathfrak{\mathcal{P}}.

(4.8)

2.

Regularity.
The semigroup
(\mathcal{M}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is regular, with the corresponding dual operators

\mathcal{M}^{*}_{t}[\mathcal{G}]=\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{S}[t,\cdot]

(4.9)

for any
\mathcal{G}\in BC(\mathcal{P})
, where
\mathcal{G}\circ\mathcal{S}[t,\cdot]
is a bounded Borel function on
\mathcal{P}
.

3.

Vanishing energy defect.

We define the energy defect as a concave u.s.c function on
\mathcal{P}
,

d_{E}=\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E(\varrho,{\bf m},S)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\geq 0.

There holds

\int_{\mathcal{P}}d_{E}\ {\rm d}\mathcal{M}_{t}[\sigma]\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ t\to\infty

(4.10)

for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{P}[\mathcal{P}]
.

5
Proof of the properties of the statistical solution

Our ultimate task is to verify the basic properties of statistical solutions listed in the previous section.

1.

The semigroup property (
4.5
) as well as (
4.6
), (
4.7
) follow immediately from the
the semigroup property of the admissible dissipative solutions.

The càglàd continuity stated in (
4.8
) follows from the analogous property of the
solution semigroup, more precisely its entropy component,

\mathcal{S}\left[t,(\varrho_{0},{\bf m}_{0},S_{0},\mathcal{E}_{0})\right]=(\varrho(t,\cdot),{\bf m}(t,\cdot),S(t-,\cdot),\mathcal{E}_{0}),\ t\geq 0.

2.

The existence of the adjoint semigroup (
4.9
) follows directly from formula (
4.3
).

3.

As for the vanishing energy defect claimed in (
4.10
), we first rewrite

\int_{\mathcal{P}}d_{E}\ {\rm d}\mathcal{M}_{t}(\sigma)=\int_{\mathcal{P}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]{\rm d}\sigma.

In accordance with Proposition
3.2
,

\left[\mathcal{E}_{0}-\int_{\Omega}E\Big((\varrho,{\bf m},S)(t+,\cdot)\Big)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ t\to\infty\ \sigma-\mbox{a.s.},

and the desired conclusion follows from the Lebesgue Dominance Convergence theorem.

6
Conclusion

We have proposed a construction of statistical solutions to the Euler system of gas dynamics applying a single step selection criterion
in the class of dissipative solutions. The selection criterion is based on minimizing the Bregman divergence to the maximal entropy
equilibrium solution in an an exponentially weighted time integral norm. We have shown that this criterion is physically relevant as its
energy defect decays to zero with growing time. This observation seems consistent with the decay of turbulent phenomena initiated in the flow by the
data. Indeed the amplitude of the energy defect is directly related to that of Reynolds stress arising in models of deterministic turbulence,
cf. Bardos et al.
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]

its variant defined in (
3.9

[

combined with those of

Suppose that

\lambda

\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}

We leave the proof of this statement to the interested reader. The resulting solution should be compared to the
