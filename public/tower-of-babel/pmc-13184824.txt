Neurological Complications After Pediatric Liver and Kidney Transplantation: A Comprehensive Review of Acute Symptomatic Seizures
Authors: Semash Konstantin, Dzhanbekov Timur, Nasirov Mansur, Umarov Bakhtiyorjon, Qambarov Navruz, Khamdamov Bakhtiyor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute symptomatic seizures (ASS) represent one of the most frequent and clinically significant neurological complications following pediatric liver and kidney transplantation. Despite their clinical relevance, available evidence remains fragmented; pediatric‐specific data are limited, and no standardized diagnostic or management framework has been established. To summarize current evidence regarding the epidemiology, etiological mechanisms, diagnostic evaluation, risk factors, and management strategies of acute symptomatic seizures after pediatric liver and kidney transplantation, and to propose a practical clinical diagnostic and management algorithm. A structured narrative review with elements of a semi‐systematic approach was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. Studies published between 2000 and 2025 addressing neurological complications after pediatric liver and kidney transplantation were reviewed. Particular emphasis was placed on metabolic disturbances, calcineurin inhibitor neurotoxicity, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), structural central nervous system lesions, infectious complications, hypertensive encephalopathy, dialysis disequilibrium syndrome, and uremic encephalopathy. Pediatric‐specific evidence was prioritized whenever available, while adult‐derived data were included for contextual interpretation when pediatric evidence was limited. The reported incidence of acute symptomatic seizures ranges from 2.8% to 42% after liver transplantation and up to 30% after kidney transplantation. In liver transplant recipients, the most common etiologies include calcineurin inhibitor neurotoxicity, metabolic disturbances, PRES, sepsis‐associated encephalopathy, central nervous system infections, and cerebrovascular events. In kidney transplant recipients, seizures are more frequently associated with electrolyte imbalance, hypertensive encephalopathy, dialysis disequilibrium syndrome, uremic encephalopathy, and PRES. Early postoperative hyponatremia, renal dysfunction, severe metabolic abnormalities, elevated tacrolimus concentrations, malnutrition, and severe systemic infection represent major risk factors for seizure development. Diagnostic evaluation relies on biochemical assessment, immunosuppressant level monitoring, neuroimaging, and electroencephalography, particularly in patients with persistent encephalopathy or suspected non‐convulsive status epilepticus. Based on the synthesized evidence, a practical diagnostic and management algorithm was proposed integrating early stabilization, etiological stratification, neurodiagnostic assessment, and targeted therapy. Acute symptomatic seizures after pediatric liver and kidney transplantation arise from heterogeneous and frequently overlapping mechanisms. Early identification of reversible triggers and individualized management are essential to reduce neurological morbidity and improve outcomes. The proposed diagnostic and management framework may support earlier recognition, structured evaluation, and targeted treatment of acute symptomatic seizures in pediatric transplant recipients.Keywords: acute symptomatic seizures, calcineurin inhibitor neurotoxicity, dialysis disequilibrium, kidney transplantation, liver transplantation, metabolic disturbances, pediatric transplantation, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, post‐transplant neurological complications

Introduction

Organ transplantation remains the definitive therapeutic option for patients with end‐stage hepatic and renal failure. Advances in operative techniques, immunosuppressive regimens, and perioperative management have substantially improved early post‐transplant outcomes, with 1‐year survival rates now approaching 90% [1, 2]. Despite these achievements, postoperative complications remain frequent. Neurological complications following orthotopic solid organ transplantation occur in 9%–42% of recipients [3]. Among these, acute symptomatic seizures (ASS) constitute the second most prevalent neurological event after post‐transplant encephalopathy in both liver and kidney transplant recipients, with reported incidence rates ranging from 9% to 36% [4, 5]. Importantly, seizure syndromes are associated with adverse neurological outcomes and continue to serve as predictors of increased mortality among solid organ transplant recipients [6, 7]. Consequently, early recognition and effective management of ASS in the postoperative period are of critical clinical relevance.

According to the International League Against Epilepsy, ASS are defined as seizures provoked by identifiable acute insults, including toxic, metabolic, infectious, inflammatory, traumatic, or cerebrovascular events [8]. Their distinguishing feature is a typically favorable long‐term prognosis: these seizures are situational and exhibit a low likelihood of recurrence once the underlying cause is corrected, thus generally not necessitating prolonged antiepileptic therapy [8, 9].

The clinical presentation of ASS is heterogeneous. In adults, generalized bilateral tonic–clonic seizures are most commonly observed, whereas pediatric recipients more frequently exhibit focal seizure patterns [10, 11]. ASS may recur within a short time frame, presenting as seizure clusters, or evolve into continuous seizure activity without restoration of consciousness, meeting criteria for status epilepticus. In patients with altered mental status, non‐convulsive status epilepticus must be considered and can only be reliably identified through electroencephalography [7].

Etiological factors contributing to postoperative seizures include the pro‐convulsant effects of anesthetic agents [12], cerebral edema, refeeding syndrome [13], disturbances in metabolic and intravascular volume homeostasis, drug‐induced neurotoxicity [14], anoxic brain injury, and structural central nervous system abnormalities. Additionally, liver and kidney transplant recipients often possess organ‐specific vulnerabilities that may potentiate seizure risk.

Compared with adult transplant recipients, acute symptomatic seizures and other neurological complications following pediatric liver and kidney transplantation remain considerably less studied, resulting in limited pediatric‐specific evidence regarding their epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnosis, and management. The aim of this review is to summarize the current evidence on the epidemiology, etiological mechanisms, diagnostic approaches, and management strategies for acute symptomatic seizures in pediatric patients following orthotopic liver and kidney transplantation.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted as a structured narrative review with elements of a semi‐systematic approach aimed at synthesizing current evidence on acute symptomatic seizures following pediatric liver and kidney transplantation. The primary objective was to provide a clinically oriented analysis of etiological mechanisms, diagnostic strategies, and management approaches, with particular emphasis on applicability in pediatric transplant practice.

A comprehensive literature search was performed using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search included publications from January 2000 to March 2025. A combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms was used, including “acute symptomatic seizures,” “seizures,” “epilepsy,” “pediatric transplantation,” “liver transplantation,” “kidney transplantation,” “solid organ transplantation,” “posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome,” “PRES,” “calcineurin inhibitor neurotoxicity,” “tacrolimus,” “cyclosporine,” “electrolyte disturbances,” “hyponatremia,” “uremia,” “dialysis disequilibrium syndrome,” and “neurological complications.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to refine the search and ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant studies. In addition, reference lists of selected articles were manually screened to identify further pertinent publications. Study selection and data extraction were independently performed by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus‐based discussion and final agreement.

Studies were considered eligible if they reported on neurological complications following liver or kidney transplantation and included data on acute symptomatic seizures or seizure‐like events. Priority was given to pediatric studies involving patients younger than 18 years. However, due to the limited availability of pediatric‐specific data, adult studies were also included when they provided relevant mechanistic or clinical insights, and such evidence was explicitly interpreted in the context of pediatric extrapolation. Eligible study designs included original research articles, cohort studies, case series, and relevant narrative or systematic reviews published in English. Studies focusing exclusively on chronic epilepsy without identifiable acute triggers, non‐peer‐reviewed publications, and conference abstracts lacking sufficient methodological detail were excluded.

Data from the selected studies were extracted and organized according to the type of transplantation, specifically liver versus kidney transplantation, as well as underlying etiological mechanisms, including metabolic, toxic, structural, infectious, hypertensive, and dialysis‐related factors. Additional variables included clinical presentation, diagnostic approaches, and management strategies. Given the heterogeneity of study designs, patient populations, and reported outcomes, quantitative synthesis and meta‐analysis were not feasible. Therefore, a qualitative synthesis approach was employed to integrate findings across studies.

Particular emphasis was placed on pediatric‐specific aspects of acute symptomatic seizures, including age‐related differences in pathophysiology, pharmacokinetics, and clinical presentation. When pediatric data were limited, adult‐derived evidence was incorporated and clearly identified as extrapolated, with careful consideration of its applicability to pediatric populations.

Based on the synthesized evidence and clinical interpretation, a practical diagnostic and management algorithm for acute symptomatic seizures in pediatric liver and kidney transplant recipients was developed. This framework integrates early stabilization, etiological differentiation, and targeted therapeutic strategies, with the aim of providing a clinically applicable tool to support decision‐making in the early post‐transplant period.

Acute Symptomatic Seizures Following Orthotopic Liver Transplantation

The reported incidence of ASS following orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT) varies considerably across studies, ranging from 2.8% to 42% [4, 7, 10]. The etiological spectrum in the early postoperative period is broad and encompasses metabolic disturbances, calcineurin inhibitor–induced neurotoxicity, structural central nervous system lesions, and infectious processes.

A substantial proportion of early post‐OLT ASS is attributed to immunosuppressive therapy. According to E. Derle et al., calcineurin inhibitors account for approximately 34% of seizure episodes in this population [4]. Notably, seizure occurrence does not correlate consistently with supratherapeutic drug levels, as events may arise even within therapeutic concentration ranges [4]. Most ASS develop within the first postoperative week [11].

In 1%–10% of liver transplant recipients, seizures result from vasogenic edema in the posterior cerebral regions [11, 15, 16], corresponding radiologically to posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), also termed reversible posterior leukoencephalopathy syndrome (RPLS). In pediatric cohorts, PRES appears less frequently than in adults [6, 10]. This syndrome is strongly associated with the neurotoxic effects of calcineurin inhibitors [15, 17]. Although the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood, experimental evidence indicates that cyclosporine disrupts the blood–brain barrier by altering endothelial and astrocytic function [18]. Clinically, PRES may manifest with cortical blindness, seizures, and impaired consciousness progressing to coma, often in the absence of arterial hypertension.

Beyond calcineurin inhibitors, several other postoperative pharmacological agents may precipitate neurotoxicity and provoke ASS, including isoniazid, methylprednisolone (particularly in combination with cyclosporine), imipenem, penicillamine, ciprofloxacin, acyclovir, and others [14, 19].

Metabolic derangements such as hypoglycemia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hyponatremia, and hypophosphatemia account for approximately 17.4% of acute symptomatic seizures in liver transplant recipients [3, 4]. Preoperative hyponatremia is common in end‐stage liver disease, and serum sodium levels below 115–120 mmol/L are strongly associated with seizure risk [8, 20]. Rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia may lead to central pontine and extrapontine myelinolysis (CPM and EPM) [21], clinically presenting as impaired consciousness, locked‐in syndrome, ophthalmoparesis, tetraparesis, and bulbar dysfunction [22]. Refeeding syndrome represents another critical metabolic trigger, arising after initiation of enteral or parenteral nutrition following prolonged fasting and characterized by hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia, hypokalemia, and thiamine deficiency. Neurological manifestations may include focal deficits, seizures, cerebral edema, and respiratory failure.

Structural central nervous system abnormalities contribute to approximately 13% of ASS cases [4]. These include hemorrhagic and ischemic cerebrovascular events (CVE), brain abscesses, and meningoencephalitis. Hemorrhagic or ischemic CVEs occur in 2%–4% of liver transplant recipients [23], with a higher risk noted in adults with pre‐transplant diabetes mellitus [24].

Among infectious causes, herpesviruses are of particular significance. Human herpesvirus 6 may cause limbic encephalitis with seizures [25]. Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a rare but fatal demyelinating disorder caused by reactivation of the John Cunningham virus (JCV), occurs in profoundly immunosuppressed patients [26, 27, 28]. Although MRI features of PML may mimic PRES, PML does not respond to reduction or adjustment of immunosuppressive therapy and typically follows a relentlessly progressive course [29].

Sepsis constitutes an additional major cause of early post‐OLT ASS, responsible for 8.7% of cases [4]. Sepsis‐associated encephalopathy manifests as acute disturbances in mentation, cognition, sleep–wake regulation, attention, and orientation, often without direct CNS infection [30]. Clinical features may include motor agitation, hallucinations, or fluctuating states of agitation and somnolence; less frequently, asterixis, tremor, and multifocal myoclonus may occur [31].

In a considerable proportion of patients, ASS arise from multifactorial etiologies rather than a single precipitating factor. Indeed, 26.1% of affected individuals exhibit two or more concurrent contributing mechanisms [4].

Diagnosis

At present, no universally accepted diagnostic algorithm exists for determining the etiology of acute symptomatic seizures in liver transplant recipients. Nevertheless, P.W. Shepard et al. propose a comprehensive diagnostic approach that includes evaluation of acid–base balance, detailed serum electrolyte analysis with particular attention to phosphate and magnesium levels, neuroimaging to exclude cerebrovascular events, electroencephalography, and cerebrospinal fluid examination via lumbar puncture [32]. This multimodal assessment is essential to differentiate metabolic, structural, infectious, and drug‐induced mechanisms of ASS in the early post‐transplant period.

Treatment and Prevention

Management of ASS after orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT) is fundamentally dependent on the underlying cause. In cases attributed to immunosuppressant‐induced neurotoxicity or posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), therapeutic strategies include dose reduction or conversion of the offending agent [7, 17]. Ismail et al. describe successful reintroduction of tacrolimus following complete neurological recovery in patients previously affected by tacrolimus‐associated PRES, underscoring the reversibility of this condition when addressed promptly [33].

Correction of metabolic abnormalities frequently leads to rapid cessation of seizure activity. Particular caution is required when treating hyponatremia, as overly rapid correction increases the risk of central pontine and extrapontine myelinolysis (CPM and EPM). Recommended limits for serum sodium correction are ≤ 15 mmol/L within 24 h and ≤ 18 mmol/L over 48 h [34].

With regard to antiepileptic therapy, levetiracetam is preferred due to its minimal hepatic enzyme interaction profile, which reduces the likelihood of drug–drug interactions and enables lower immunosuppressant dosing [35]. Levetiracetam, gabapentin, pregabalin, and lacosamide are considered appropriate first‐line agents for focal seizures in transplant recipients, having demonstrated favorable efficacy and tolerability [32]. For the acute cessation of seizure activity, benzodiazepines, fosphenytoin, intravenous levetiracetam, valproic acid, or lacosamide may be utilized [32]. Limitations include the absence of regulatory approval for fosphenytoin and injectable lorazepam in certain countries [36], and contraindication of lacosamide in children under 16 years of age [37].

Duration of antiepileptic therapy remains variable. D.R. Chabolla et al. recommend a treatment course of 1–3 months when no structural brain lesion is present [38]. In contrast, P.W. Shepard et al. advise prolonged therapy when structural abnormalities are identified [32].

Preventive strategies emphasize rigorous monitoring of metabolic parameters and immunosuppressant concentrations [4, 39]. In patients with seizures occurring before transplantation, antiepileptic treatment is indicated only in the presence of a confirmed neurological disorder such as previous traumatic brain injury or spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage. According to the Epilepsy Foundation of America, seizures occurring in the context of grade III–IV hepatic encephalopathy prior to OLT should be classified as acute symptomatic rather than epileptic events [40].

Risk Factors for ASS in Liver Transplant Recipients

Several studies have identified specific clinical and perioperative factors that increase the risk of developing ASS after OLT. Balderramo et al. demonstrated an association between calcineurin inhibitor–induced neurotoxicity and pre‐transplant liver failure, early postoperative hyponatremia, and prolonged surgical duration exceeding 7 h [41].

In 2016, Wu et al. expanded the spectrum of risk determinants, identifying post‐transplant hyponatremia, high MELD scores reflecting advanced hepatic dysfunction, bacterial infection within 1 week prior to transplantation, malnutrition (BMI < 21 kg/m2), overweight status (BMI > 27.6 kg/m2), renal insufficiency, cirrhosis, Child–Pugh class C, and recipient age younger than 29 years or older than 60 years as significant predictors of neurological complications following OLT [42].

Further evidence reported by S.S. Kumar, G.A. Mashour, and P. Picton in 2018 indicates that tacrolimus concentrations ≥ 8.9 ng/mL during the first postoperative week constitute an independent risk factor for ASS [3].

Recurrent seizures are most frequently observed in the presence of cerebrovascular events, graft rejection, or sepsis, and these clinical contexts warrant the introduction or continuation of antiepileptic therapy [32].

Acute Symptomatic Seizures Following Orthotopic Kidney Transplantation

According to Sawhney et al., approximately 30% of kidney transplant recipients experience acute symptomatic seizures during the early postoperative period [43]. Similar to liver transplant recipients, the etiological profile includes metabolic disturbances, drug‐related neurotoxicity, and structural or infectious central nervous system lesions. However, kidney transplant recipients also encounter pathophysiological triggers unique to renal dysfunction, such as hypertensive encephalopathy, dialysis disequilibrium syndrome, and uremia [43].

Metabolic abnormalities constitute one of the most frequent causes of postoperative seizure activity in this population. Pochineni and Rondon‐Berrios highlight the roles of hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia, and hypocalcemia as key biochemical precipitants of ASS [44]. Complementing these findings, Meena et al. reported that hyponatremia occurring within the first 24 h after kidney transplantation may similarly provoke acute symptomatic seizures [45]. Malignant hypertension in patients with end‐stage renal disease, including those with hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) in the pre‐ and post‐transplant periods, is strongly associated with the development of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) [46, 47, 48]. The clinical presentation closely resembles tacrolimus‐associated PRES but is distinguished by markedly elevated arterial pressure. During hypertensive crises, abrupt increases in systemic blood pressure disrupt cerebrovascular autoregulation, producing vasogenic edema predominantly in the parietal and occipital lobes. On MRI, these changes appear as symmetrical hyperintensities in FLAIR and T2‐weighted sequences [49], whereas CT typically demonstrates corresponding hypodense lesions [50]. R.K. Garg emphasized that the presence of seizures may assist in differentiating PRES from bilateral occipital infarction [50].

Disequilibrium syndrome represents another important etiology of ASS following kidney transplantation. It results from a rapid decline in plasma urea levels during hemodialysis, leading to osmotic fluid shifts, cerebral edema, impaired consciousness, and seizures [51]. Predisposing factors described by B. Bhandari and S. Komanduri include blood urea nitrogen > 60 mmol/L, pre‐existing neurological disease, hyponatremia, HUS, and sepsis [51].

Uremic encephalopathy is characterized by depressed mental status, motor dysfunction, ataxia, and seizures. Motor phenomena such as tremor, asterixis, and multifocal myoclonus may mimic ASS and complicate clinical assessment [52]. According to the Epilepsy Foundation, approximately one‐third of patients with uremic encephalopathy develop acute symptomatic seizures [53]. Differential diagnosis is facilitated by video EEG monitoring, which demonstrates an absence of epileptiform discharges in cases of non‐epileptic motor disturbances [43].

Diagnosis

Although a standardized diagnostic algorithm has not been established, routine evaluation of ASS in kidney transplant recipients includes arterial blood gas analysis, comprehensive serum electrolyte assessment, neuroimaging, and electroencephalography [32]. These investigations help distinguish metabolic, structural, hypertensive, and uremic causes of seizure activity.

Treatment and Prevention

Management strategies for ASS following kidney transplantation are tailored to the underlying etiology. Correction of electrolyte abnormalities, stabilization of blood pressure, adjustment of dialysis parameters, and administration of benzodiazepines typically result in rapid resolution of seizures. Effective blood pressure control is critical, as reversal of hypertensive PRES can fully restore neurological function. D. Haughey and S.S. Narsipur advocate the use of magnesium sulfate for hypertensive PRES [49], whereas S.S. Medeni et al. report successful treatment with calcium channel blockers in a case of atypical HUS‐associated hypertensive PRES.

For disequilibrium syndrome, C.J. Doorenbos proposed the administration of 5 mL of 23% saline or 12.5 mL of intravenous mannitol to increase plasma osmolality and attenuate osmotic fluid shifts; however, supporting evidence remains limited [54]. K. Mistry recommends prolonging dialysis sessions and restricting the reduction of serum urea to no more than 40% during the first 2 h of treatment to prevent syndrome onset [55].

Antiepileptic Therapy

Valproic acid is widely regarded as the antiepileptic agent of choice for kidney transplant recipients. Although it functions as a hepatic enzyme inhibitor and may influence immunosuppressant drug levels [43], it possesses several clinically important advantages. Valproic acid is not renally metabolized, does not require dose adjustments based on glomerular filtration rate, necessitates only a supplemental dose following high‐flux hemodialysis, demonstrates efficacy against nearly all seizure types, is available in intravenous formulation, and is suitable for pediatric patients.

Levetiracetam is likewise appropriate for the treatment of acute symptomatic seizures (ASS) in kidney transplant recipients, particularly because of its rapid anticonvulsant effect when administered intravenously. However, dosing must be carefully adjusted in accordance with the patient's glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and modified further when hemodialysis is ongoing. Risk Factors for Acute Symptomatic Seizures in Kidney Transplant Recipients

Given that acute symptomatic seizures represent one of the most significant neurological complications in the early postoperative period after kidney transplantation, identifying predictors of seizure development is of substantial clinical importance. Preoperative electroencephalography (EEG) has been suggested as a potential tool for risk stratification. In patients with uremia, EEG abnormalities are detected in up to 70% of cases, typically manifesting as bifrontal slow‐wave activity or bilateral 3–6 Hz spike–wave discharges [56], patterns consistent with underlying encephalopathy. These changes correlate with the stage of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and may aid in detecting subclinical uremic encephalopathy [57]. They are believed to be linked to elevated serum concentrations of urea and chloride, as well as hypocalcemia [58]. However, despite their frequency, EEG abnormalities do not reliably predict post‐transplant seizure recurrence; instead, they have been associated more closely with developmental delays [58].

In contrast, postoperative EEG has clear diagnostic utility, especially in evaluating comatose patients to distinguish non‐convulsive status epilepticus from metabolic encephalopathy, and in differentiating epileptic from non‐epileptic motor manifestations [32]. Findings from a single‐center study by D.H. Lorie indicate that seizures prior to kidney transplantation do not predict post‐transplant seizure recurrence [58].

Because acute symptomatic seizures may be precipitated by rapid fluctuations in serum sodium, calcium, magnesium, or glucose irrespective of the underlying disease, these biochemical parameters serve as physiologically plausible predictive markers. Consequently, routine monitoring of serum electrolytes is essential in patients with suspected metabolic seizure triggers. R. Nardone et al. emphasize that prompt correction of such abnormalities is critical for preventing irreversible neurological injury [59].

Pediatric‐Specific Considerations

Compared with adult transplant recipients, pediatric patients demonstrate several clinically important differences in the presentation and pathophysiology of acute symptomatic seizures after solid organ transplantation [60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73]. Children more frequently exhibit focal or mixed seizure semiology, whereas generalized tonic–clonic seizures predominate in adults [74]. Pediatric recipients are also characterized by greater metabolic instability, more pronounced pharmacokinetic variability of calcineurin inhibitors, and increased susceptibility to rapid electrolyte fluctuations because of limited physiological reserves [68, 72]. In addition, seizure activity and encephalopathy in children may present with subtle or non‐specific manifestations, making electroencephalographic evaluation particularly important for the detection of non‐convulsive status epilepticus [69]. Unlike adults, pediatric patients are additionally vulnerable to potential long‐term neurodevelopmental consequences of neurological complications occurring during critical stages of brain maturation [74]. However, despite these clinically relevant distinctions, current evidence remains largely extrapolated from adult studies because pediatric‐specific data are limited. Proposed Diagnostic and Management Algorithm for Acute Symptomatic Seizures After Pediatric Liver and Kidney Transplantation

In children who develop seizures after liver or kidney transplantation, management should begin with immediate stabilization [60]. Airway, breathing, and circulation should be assessed first, with oxygen support, intravenous access, bedside glucose testing, and administration of a benzodiazepine when clinically indicated. If seizure activity persists or recurs without recovery of consciousness, the patient should be managed according to a status epilepticus protocol.

After initial stabilization, the diagnostic work‐up should proceed in parallel with treatment. The first‐line evaluation should include serum glucose, sodium, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, arterial blood gas analysis, renal function tests, liver function tests, tacrolimus or cyclosporine trough levels, blood pressure assessment, temperature, inflammatory markers, and infection screening. These investigations allow early identification of potentially reversible causes of acute symptomatic seizures.

The next step is etiological stratification. If hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, or hypophosphatemia is detected, targeted correction should be initiated promptly, with particular caution to avoid rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia. If severe hypertension is present, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome should be suspected, and urgent blood pressure control with brain MRI should be considered [61]. If calcineurin inhibitor toxicity is suspected because of elevated trough levels, compatible neurological findings, or PRES‐like imaging changes, immunosuppressive therapy should be reduced, temporarily withheld, or converted according to transplant team assessment [62].

Organ‐specific mechanisms should then be considered. In liver transplant recipients, priority should be given to calcineurin inhibitor neurotoxicity, hyponatremia, graft dysfunction, sepsis, PRES, and cerebrovascular events [6, 62]. In kidney transplant recipients, particular attention should be paid to hypertensive encephalopathy, electrolyte disturbances, uremic encephalopathy, dialysis disequilibrium syndrome, and calcineurin inhibitor toxicity [5, 6, 63].

Neuroimaging should be performed when seizures are focal, prolonged, recurrent, associated with persistent altered consciousness, or accompanied by focal neurological deficits. MRI is preferred for suspected PRES, ischemia, hemorrhage, abscess, or demyelinating lesions [64, 65]. CT may be used initially when MRI is not immediately available. EEG should be obtained in patients with persistent encephalopathy, suspected non‐convulsive status epilepticus, recurrent unexplained events, or unclear differentiation between epileptic and non‐epileptic motor phenomena [63, 66, 67, 68].

Antiseizure medication should be individualized. A single seizure with an identified and corrected reversible trigger may not require prolonged therapy. Recurrent seizures, status epilepticus, structural brain lesions, epileptiform EEG abnormalities, or persistent neurological impairment justify initiation or continuation of antiseizure therapy [66]. Levetiracetam is generally preferred because of its favorable interaction profile [5, 6, 67]. Valproic acid may be considered in kidney transplant recipients when clinically appropriate, while enzyme‐inducing antiseizure drugs should generally be avoided because of interactions with immunosuppressive agents.

Follow‐up should include reassessment of neurological status, correction of modifiable risk factors, repeat monitoring of electrolytes and immunosuppressant levels, and review of neuroimaging or EEG findings when indicated [68]. In patients without structural brain injury or recurrent seizures, short‐term antiseizure therapy for approximately 1–3 months may be sufficient. Longer treatment should be considered in patients with structural lesions, recurrent seizures, or persistent EEG abnormalities [69, 70, 71, 72, 73]. Conclusion

Acute symptomatic seizures remain among the most significant neurological complications following pediatric liver and kidney transplantation. Their development is associated with a broad spectrum of overlapping mechanisms, including metabolic disturbances, calcineurin inhibitor neurotoxicity, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, infectious complications, cerebrovascular events, hypertensive encephalopathy, and dialysis‐related factors. Despite this heterogeneity, many episodes are potentially preventable through early recognition, careful monitoring of metabolic parameters and immunosuppressant levels, timely neurodiagnostic evaluation, and prompt correction of reversible triggers.

Current evidence remains limited by the predominance of adult‐derived data and the absence of standardized pediatric‐specific diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. In this review, we summarize the principal etiological mechanisms and clinically relevant risk factors associated with acute symptomatic seizures after pediatric liver and kidney transplantation and propose a practical diagnostic and management algorithm integrating early stabilization, etiological stratification, neurodiagnostic assessment, and targeted therapy.

The proposed framework may facilitate earlier recognition of neurological complications, improve clinical decision‐making, and support individualized management in pediatric transplant recipients.

Further prospective pediatric studies are needed to validate risk stratification strategies, optimize antiseizure management, and establish standardized evidence‐based protocols for neurological complications after solid organ transplantation.
