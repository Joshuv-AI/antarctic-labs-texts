History of Presentation

A 69-year-old man presented with several months of intermittent vertigo and recurrent syncope, the most recent episode occurring during a Valsalva maneuver. He also reported progressive exertional dyspnea.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the severity of the conduction disorder, infiltrative cardiomyopathies and systemic inflammatory diseases with cardiac involvement were considered. However, these possibilities were considered unlikely because of the preserved ventricular function, normal inflammatory markers, and the absence of structural abnormalities on cardiac imaging.1 Furthermore, no complex ventricular arrhythmias were documented that would suggest an active pathologic substrate of systemic origin.

Investigations

Physical examination revealed no signs of heart failure. The results of laboratory tests, including electrolytes, renal function, and thyroid function tests, were within normal limits, ruling out reversible metabolic or endocrine etiologies.

The baseline electrocardiogram demonstrated a Rosenbaum pattern (right bundle branch block and left anterior hemiblock) associated with first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block.2 Given the exertional symptoms, coronary computed tomography angiography was performed, which showed no obstructive coronary artery disease (Figure 3). Subsequently, an exercise stress test was conducted to evaluate the functional reserve of the infra-Hisian conduction system.3 During maximal exertion, the patient exhibited a characteristic electrical sequence: conduction evolved from the baseline pattern into fascicular alternans, characterized by a beat-to-beat shift between left anterior and posterior hemiblocks (Figure 1). This was followed by a symptomatic, transient high-grade AV block. Upon immediate recovery, the withdrawal of adrenergic tone triggered a catastrophic failure manifested by progressive pauses and unstable ventricular escape, culminating in a critical 7-second ventricular asystole (Figure 2).4

Coronary Computed Tomography Angiography

Coronary computed tomography angiography demonstrates the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease or significant angiographic lesions. These findings exclude an ischemic etiology, supporting a nonischemic substrate and the diagnosis of Lenègre-Lev disease (primary degenerative fibrosis of the cardiac conduction system).

Exercise-Induced Infra-Hisian Conduction Failure and Ventricular Asystole

The recovery-phase tracing demonstrates ventricular electrical silence due to complete infra-Hisian conduction failure, resulting in a 7-second episode of asystole. Motion-related artifact limits the visualization of low-amplitude P waves without excluding persistent atrial activity. The prolonged pause, associated with an isolated ventricular escape beat, confirms true ventricular asystole due to exhaustion of the His-Purkinje system, consistent with advanced Lenègre-Lev disease.

Management

Based on these diagnostic findings, a permanent dual-chamber pacemaker was implanted in accordance with current American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association clinical practice guidelines.5,6

Outcome and Follow-Up

After pacemaker implantation, the patient experienced complete resolution of symptoms and has remained asymptomatic during follow-up, with stable pacing parameters.

Discussion

This case illustrates the physiopathologic transition to Lenègre-Lev disease. The electrocardiographic presentation was characterized by a baseline Rosenbaum pattern that progressed to fascicular alternation and, ultimately, paroxysmal high-grade block. In the absence of myocardial ischemia or other reversible causes, these findings confirm the presence of a substrate of diffuse idiopathic fibrosis of the His-Purkinje system.

Exercise stress testing was instrumental in unmasking an infra-Hisian conduction failure that was not evident at rest. During the study, alternating left anterior and posterior fascicular block was recorded, reflecting advanced conduction system disease with marked frequency-dependent refractoriness. Electrophysiologically, the increase in heart rate and incomplete repolarization reduce sodium channel availability, precipitating a functional phase 3 block.7 This dynamic explains the variable and alternating QRS widening due to fluctuating conduction through the remaining fascicles in the setting of incomplete recovery of excitability.

Detailed analysis of the electrocardiographic recording confirms the persistence of sinus rhythm and allows for the exclusion of differential diagnoses, such as 2:1 AV block associated with ventricular ectopy. The morphology of the wide QRS complexes is not due to an ectopic origin but rather to aberrant conduction, as each ventricular depolarization is preceded by a conducted P-wave with a constant, albeit prolonged, PR interval. This stability in the PR-QRS relationship, coupled with the absence of compensatory pauses and QRS widening strictly linked to the increase in heart rate, confirms that the underlying pathophysiological mechanism is advanced infranodal conduction system disease.3

Conclusions

The presence of a Rosenbaum pattern associated with syncope should alert clinicians to the possibility of Lenègre-Lev disease. This case highlights that normal laboratory findings, the absence of structural cardiac abnormalities, and a nonischemic coronary tree do not exclude lethal electrical risk. Exercise stress testing remains a vital diagnostic tool to unmask pan-conduction system failure, allowing for timely life-saving intervention through permanent cardiac pacing.
