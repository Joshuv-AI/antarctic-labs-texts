Explainable deep learning ensemble framework for accurate classification of wild poisonous mushroom species
Authors: Aras Fahrettin Korkmaz, Fatih Ekinci, Eda Kumru, Abdullah Aydoğan, Hasna Sena Kaymak, Mustafa Sevindik, Mehmet Serdar Güzel, Ilgaz Akata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study introduces an explainable deep learning framework for the accurate classification of wild poisonous mushroom species, contributing to food safety. A balanced dataset of 3600 high-resolution images representing 18 species was employed, split into training, validation, and test subsets. To enhance variability and reduce overfitting, the images were expanded using advanced augmentation techniques (rotation, flipping, brightness/contrast adjustments, noise injection, etc.), increasing the training set to 7200 samples. Four pretrained CNN architectures DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B3, MobileNet-V3, and ShuffleNet-V2 were fine-tuned via transfer learning and evaluated with multiple performance metrics. Among the individual models, EfficientNet-B3 achieved the highest accuracy of 93.0%. However, ensemble strategies based on soft voting consistently outperformed single models. The four-model ensemble (DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B3, MobileNet-V3, ShuffleNet-V2) achieved the best results with 95.67% accuracy, 95.42% MCC, and a log loss of 0.175. Explainable AI methods (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++) revealed that classification decisions corresponded to biologically meaningful regions, thereby improving interpretability and reliability. This study holds direct life-saving potential by reducing poisoning incidents caused by misidentifications. In addition, it contributes to food safety by supporting reliable identification of toxic species within the agricultural and food supply chain. Furthermore, it pioneers the integration of AI methodologies in fungal taxonomy, providing a robust foundation for future ecological, agricultural, and biotechnological research.

Introduction

Globally, nearly 150,000 mushroom species have been identified, but only about 2000 are edible, with roughly 700 recognized for medicinal uses. Many species are also toxic, leading to fatal accidents each year, often caused by misidentification [1]. Poisonous mushrooms contain bioactive compounds such as small organic molecules, peptides, and proteins, which can cause effects ranging from mild gastrointestinal discomfort to severe liver and kidney damage, sometimes resulting in death [2]. Most mushroom poisonings are linked to a few primary toxins: amatoxins, phallotoxins, orellanine, muscarine, psilocybin, illudin, and coprine [3].

Amanita phalloides, A. virosa, and Galerina marginata contain amatoxins, a group of deadly toxins [4, 5]. Amanita muscaria and A. pantherina produce isoxazole compounds, distinctive metabolites of these mushrooms [6]. Gyromitra esculenta contains Gyromitrin, a toxic compound known for its unique structure and effects on health [7]. Coprinopsis atramentaria contains Coprine, an alcohol dehydrogenase inhibitor that causes the alcohol flush reaction [8]. Inocybe geophylla, Mycena pura, and M. rosea contain muscarine, an alkaloid traditionally linked to mushroom poisoning [6]. Gymnopilus junonius is a toxic, hallucinogenic mushroom associated with unusual behavior and uncontrollable laughter [9]. Paxillus involutus is deadly, causing rare but fatal immune-mediated hemolysis with rapid gastrointestinal and cardiovascular symptoms [10]. Agaricus xanthodermus, Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca, Hypholoma fasciculare, Rubroboletus satanas, and Tricholoma sulphureum can cause gastrointestinal poisoning, but the specific toxins responsible are still unknown [3].

Accurately identifying poisonous mushrooms is difficult because many toxic species resemble edible ones and can only be distinguished by subtle traits. This often leads to misidentification with serious consequences [11]. Traditional taxonomy is slow, requires expertise, and visual similarities can obscure correct identification [12]. AI offers a solution, as machine learning and computer vision can effectively recognize and differentiate fungal species [13–18]. Incorporating AI could greatly improve the accuracy of poisonous mushroom identification.

In deep learning-based image classification tasks, the combined use of diverse architectures and auxiliary techniques plays a vital role in improving performance and producing generalizable results [19]. In this framework, convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures such as DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B3 [20], MobileNet-V3 [21], and ShuffleNet-V2 [22], pretrained on ImageNet, were fine-tuned through transfer learning [23]. To boost data diversity and reduce overfitting, advanced augmentation techniques (e.g., rotations, flips, brightness/contrast adjustments, Gaussian noise, and scaling) were applied using the Albumentations library [24]. To further enhance classification accuracy, ensemble strategies based on soft voting were employed, with model weights optimized via an exhaustive grid-search procedure [25]. Additionally, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods such as Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++ were integrated to ensure that classification decisions aligned with biologically meaningful regions [26, 27]. The models were thoroughly evaluated using multidimensional metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, Log Loss, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Area Under the Curve (AUC) [28, 29]. In recent years, machine learning and deep learning methods have been increasingly used for mushroom classification, especially for toxic species.

The primary objective of this study is to develop a reliable and interpretable AI-based framework for the classification of poisonous mushroom species. Beyond improving classification accuracy, this work makes a distinct contribution to the literature by jointly addressing performance, robustness, and explainability—aspects that are often treated separately in previous studies. Unlike earlier approaches that rely on limited datasets or single-model evaluations, the proposed methodology employs a fully balanced multi-class dataset and integrates multiple state-of-the-art CNN architectures through a weighted ensemble strategy. Moreover, the systematic incorporation of explainable AI techniques enables biologically meaningful interpretation of model decisions, directly linking learned features to key morphological traits. By unifying high accuracy with transparent decision-making, this study fills an important methodological gap and establishes a transferable reference framework for future research in fungal taxonomy, food safety, agricultural monitoring, and AI-driven biological classification.

Materials and methods

The methodological workflow followed in this study is as follows: (i) First, a balanced dataset was constructed comprising 18 poisonous mushroom species, with 200 images per class; the majority of the images were obtained from verified open-access sources such as GBIF, and species identification was visually revalidated by an expert mycologist. (ii) The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets in a 50:25:25 ratio while preserving class balance. (iii) Data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training set to reduce overfitting and increase variability, using the Albumentations library with transformations including rotation, horizontal and vertical flipping, brightness and contrast adjustment, Gaussian noise, blurring, and shift–scale–rotate operations, thereby expanding the number of training samples. (iv) Subsequently, four ImageNet-pretrained CNN architectures—DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B3, MobileNet-V3, and ShuffleNet-V2—were fine-tuned via transfer learning; the final classification layer of each architecture was adapted to 18 classes, and inputs were resized to architecture-specific resolutions and normalized using ImageNet statistics. (v) The models were trained for a fixed number of epochs using the Adam optimizer and categorical cross-entropy loss; the model weights achieving the best validation performance were saved and evaluated on the test set. (vi) Following single-model evaluations, ensemble structures consisting of pairwise, triple, and four-model combinations were constructed; for each ensemble, a weighted soft-voting strategy was employed, with weights selected via a 0.1-step exhaustive grid search on the validation set. (vii) Performance was assessed for both individual models and ensembles using multiple metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, AUC, MCC, and Log Loss. (viii) Finally, to examine whether the decision-making process relied on biologically meaningful regions, explainability analyses were conducted using Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++; ensemble explanations were obtained by combining the heatmaps of the constituent models according to their selected weights.

The images used in this study were primarily obtained from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, [30]), which provides publicly available and taxonomically verified records curated by professional mycologists. Each image in the dataset corresponds to an officially identified specimen within the GBIF repository. Although no new physical specimens were collected or deposited in a herbarium, the taxonomic validity of all mushroom species used in this study was visually re-verified by Prof. Dr. Ilgaz Akata (Department of Biology, Faculty of Science, Ankara University, Türkiye), an expert mycologist specializing in fungal taxonomy. As the study relied exclusively on digital image data from verified public databases, no physical voucher specimens were created or deposited, but GBIF accession references are available upon request.

The dataset, consisting of 3600 original images from 18 balanced classes, was partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in a 50:25:25 ratio, corresponding to 1800, 900, and 900 images, respectively, with class balance preserved across all splits [31]. Data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training set to increase variability and reduce overfitting, whereby each original image was transformed into three additional variants using the Albumentations library, yielding 5400 augmented samples and expanding the training set to 7200 photos. The augmentation techniques included horizontal and vertical flips, 90° rotations, brightness/contrast adjustments, Gaussian noise, blurring, and random shift–scale–rotate operations. At the same time, all images were normalized using the ImageNet mean and standard deviation before model training [31].

The classification framework in this study leverages four pretrained convolutional neural network architectures: DenseNet121 [32], EfficientNet-B3 [33], MobileNet-V3 [34], and ShuffleNet-V2 [35], each initialized with ImageNet weights and employed at their recommended input resolutions (224 × 224 for DenseNet121, MobileNet-V3, and ShuffleNet-V2; 300 × 300 for EfficientNet-B3). DenseNet121 adopts dense connectivity to promote feature reuse and mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, enabling efficient information flow across layers [32]. EfficientNet-B3 utilizes compound scaling to balance network depth, width, and resolution, achieving a substantial trade-off between accuracy and computational cost [20]. MobileNet-V3, designed through neural architecture search and optimized for speed, is well-suited for resource-constrained environments while maintaining competitive performance [36]. ShuffleNet-V2 incorporates a channel shuffle mechanism to enhance efficiency by reducing computational complexity without significantly sacrificing accuracy [22]. The diversity in architectural design, input resolution, and computational requirements among these models provides a robust foundation for comparative performance evaluation in the multi-class mushroom classification task. All models were initialized with pretrained ImageNet weights to leverage transfer learning benefits. The final classification layer of each architecture was replaced with a fully connected layer matching the number of target classes, enabling task-specific adaptation. Fine-tuning was applied to the entire network instead of restricting training to only the classifier layer, allowing all parameters to be updated during optimization. Model inputs were resized to each architecture’s specific resolution and normalized using the ImageNet channel-wise mean and standard deviation. Training was conducted for 15 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001 and default momentum parameters (β_1 = 0.9, β_2 = 0.999) [37]. The categorical cross-entropy loss function was used to optimize multi-class classification performance. A batch size of 32 was used for all experiments, and the model state with the highest validation accuracy was saved for later evaluation. No learning rate scheduling or extra regularization techniques were applied. Training and validation loss and accuracy were systematically monitored after each epoch to track convergence and detect potential overfitting [38].

To comprehensively assess model performance in the multi-class fungal classification task, a set of well-established evaluation metrics was employed. Accuracy quantifies the proportion of correctly classified samples relative to the total number of samples, providing an overall measure of predictive performance. Precision represents the proportion of accurate positive predictions among all positive predictions, reflecting the model’s ability to avoid false positives. Recall (or sensitivity) measures the proportion of true positives correctly identified among all actual positive cases, indicating the model’s ability to capture relevant instances. F1-Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, offering a balanced metric that accounts for both false positives and false negatives. Log Loss evaluates the accuracy of predicted probability distributions rather than hard labels, penalizing confident but incorrect predictions more heavily. Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) is a correlation-based measure that accounts for true and false positives and negatives, making it particularly robust to class imbalance. These auxiliary metrics were computed exclusively for base models, as their values are inherently tied to the characteristics of individual architectures and their hardware-level execution. In the case of ensemble configurations, such quantities cannot be meaningfully measured, since the predictions are generated by aggregating the outputs of multiple independently trained models rather than executing a single unified network. As a result, hardware-dependent measures such as inference time, memory usage, and energy efficiency would not represent a direct property of the ensemble itself but rather a combined overhead of heterogeneous components, which varies according to the implementation strategy and execution environment. Therefore, to ensure interpretability and comparability, these metrics were omitted for ensemble models.

To enhance interpretability and visualize the decision-making process of the models, both Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) [39] and its improved variant Grad-CAM++ were employed [40]. Grad-CAM generates a coarse localization map by computing the weighted sum of the activation maps from the final convolutional layer, where the weights are derived from the gradients of the target class score with respect to the feature maps. Grad-CAM++ extends this method by introducing a more refined weighting scheme that accounts for pixel-level contributions, enabling higher-resolution and more precise localization of class-discriminative regions [40].

For all base CNN architectures (DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B3, MobileNet-V3, and ShuffleNet-V2), the techniques were applied to the final convolutional block, enabling a direct mapping between model activations and the corresponding input image regions. For ensemble configurations, heatmaps were first generated individually for each constituent model and then combined using a weighted summation strategy, where the weights corresponded to the optimal values obtained from the ensemble’s validation performance. This approach preserved each model’s relative contribution to the final decision, producing a unified visual explanation for the ensemble prediction.

The deep learning and ensemble-based analyses presented in this study were designed to be carried out without requiring advanced high-performance computing infrastructure. Model training and evaluation were conducted on a standard desktop workstation, and, on average, a multi-core CPU (e.g., Intel i7 or AMD Ryzen 7 level), at least 16 GB of system memory, and a mid-range GPU (e.g., NVIDIA RTX 3060 or an equivalent with 8–12 GB VRAM) are sufficient to reproduce this work. In addition, Python-based open-source libraries (PyTorch, Albumentations, NumPy, and scikit-learn) were used, and no proprietary or specialized software infrastructure was required. In this respect, the proposed approach offers an accessible and practical solution for both academic and applied research.

Discussion

Among the four CNN architectures compared in this study, EfficientNet-B3 demonstrated the highest performance. In terms of test accuracy, EfficientNet-B3 ranked first with 93.0% [41], followed by MobileNet-V3 (91.0%) [40], DenseNet121 (90.0%) [42], and ShuffleNet-V2 (86.78%) [43]. This indicates that EfficientNet-B3 achieved 3.0% higher accuracy than DenseNet121, 2.0% higher than MobileNet-V3, and 6.22% higher than ShuffleNet-V2. In terms of the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), EfficientNet-B3 reached the highest value at 92.60% [43], which is 3.15% higher than DenseNet121, 2.11% higher than MobileNet-V3, and 6.57% higher than ShuffleNet-V2. Furthermore, regarding log loss, EfficientNet-B3 (0.2549) produced approximately 39% lower error compared to DenseNet121 (0.4192), highlighting its ability to provide more reliable outputs. These differences clearly demonstrate that the compound scaling strategy of EfficientNet-B3 enables more successful representation of complex morphological variations.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research in different disciplines. In agricultural image classification, EfficientNet-based models have been reported to achieve high success rates in the accurate detection of plant leaf diseases [20, 44]. Similarly, MobileNet-V3 has shown promising results in histopathological image analysis [45], offering a favorable balance between classification accuracy and rapid processing speed. When considered together, these results suggest that deeper and more scalable architectures (DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B3) provide stronger outcomes in biologically complex classification tasks [20], while lighter architectures (MobileNet-V3, ShuffleNet-V2) offer advantages in applications requiring high-speed performance [46]. Thus, the characteristics observed in this study are consistent with those reported across other fields, pointing to the cross-disciplinary generalizability of these models.

The mushroom dataset used in this study, with its balanced class distribution across 18 species and extensive data augmentation, offered strong generalization capacity. Having an equal number of samples per class provided a clear advantage over many health-related datasets, where class imbalance is frequently encountered. In ecological datasets, variability in background, lighting, and environmental conditions often presents challenges [47], yet the application of augmentations such as rotation, brightness/contrast adjustment, and noise injection in this study contributed to the model’s adaptability to such diversity [48]. Moreover, the use of ensemble strategies was consistent with similar approaches reported in the literature (e.g., CNN–Transformer hybrid classification of puffball species, explainable AI-based classification of Discomycetes) [15]. In this context, the four-model ensemble not only achieved the highest accuracy (95.67%) [49] and MCC (95.42%) [50] but also demonstrated reliability in line with ensemble-based approaches reported across different disciplines.

In conventional mycological practice, mushroom species are typically identified based on a combination of macroscopic characteristics (cap morphology, color, gill structure, presence of volva or ring), microscopic features (spore size and ornamentation, basidia and cystidia structures), chemical reactions (KOH, Melzer’s reagent), and ecological attributes (habitat, substrate, symbiotic plant species) [51, 52]. These methods, although effective, often require expert mycologists, are time-consuming, and may lead to misidentification when morphologically similar species are involved. The integration of deep learning methods in the current study therefore provides a complementary and efficient alternative to overcome these limitations [51–57].

Given the high overall accuracy levels (93–95.67%), the analysis of misclassified samples indicates that the remaining errors primarily arise from natural visual similarities between certain species and from environmental variability in the images. Most misclassifications occurred between morphologically similar species pairs, particularly Mycena pura – Mycena rosea and Hypholoma fasciculare – Galerina marginata, which share overlapping macroscopic features under certain conditions. In addition, factors such as camera angle, lighting variation, and background diversity contributed to minor inconsistencies in classification. Therefore, incorporating more diverse imaging conditions and segmentation-based preprocessing steps may help further reduce these residual errors in future studies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that deep learning architectures can achieve highly accurate classification of wild poisonous mushroom species. Among the individual models, EfficientNet-B3 yielded the highest accuracy (93.0%), MCC (92.60), and the lowest log loss (0.2549). However, the most remarkable results were obtained with the four-model ensemble (DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B3, MobileNet-V3, ShuffleNet-V2), which achieved 95.67% accuracy, 95.42% MCC, and a log loss of 0.1751. Explainable AI analyses using Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++ confirmed that the models consistently focused on biologically meaningful morphological features such as pileus, gills, and stipe. These results not only advance fungal taxonomy but also provide a reliable methodological framework with potential to reduce poisoning incidents caused by misidentification.

Beyond mushroom taxonomy, the proposed deep learning and ensemble approach can serve as a reference methodology for other domains where accurate visual classification is critical. In the medical field, it could be adapted for cancer detection and sub-type classification in histopathological images. In agriculture, the same framework can support the identification of crop diseases, monitoring of soil fertility, and assessment of plant growth conditions. In environmental sciences, it could be applied to evaluate water quality parameters and detect ecological anomalies. Importantly, in the context of food safety and consumer protection, this framework provides a practical foundation for digital quality control systems capable of detecting hazardous or substandard products before they reach the market. Such an integration into agricultural production pipelines would align with the broader vision of smart and sustainable agriculture. Therefore, this study not only contributes directly to the accurate classification of mushroom species but also illustrates the broader applicability of explainable ensemble-based deep learning methods across diverse health, agricultural, and environmental contexts.
