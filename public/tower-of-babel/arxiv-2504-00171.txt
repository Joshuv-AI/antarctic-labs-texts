Shadowing maps
Alfonso Artigue

Abstract
. This article is about the shadowing property of homeomorphisms on compact metric spaces and the map associating a point of the space to each pseudo-orbit, called ’shadowing map’. Based on some particular dynamical properties, as expansivity, we develop a brief theory and a hierarchy of such maps. We consider examples as odometers, shifts on infinite spaces, topologically hyperbolic homeomorphisms and north-shouth dynamics. We revisit a well-known technique for proving shadowing of expansive homeomorphisms with canonical coordinates due to R. Bowen, to obtain a shadowing map with the property we call ’self-tuning’ from a hyperbolic bracket. This notion is introduced as part of the hierarchy of shadowing maps studied in this paper.

Introduction

Let
f\colon M\to M
be a homeomorphism of the compact metric space
(M,\dist)
.
For
\delta>0
given we say that a sequence
x_{i}\in M
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, is a
\delta
-
pseudo orbit
(or simply a
\delta
-
orbit
) if

\dist(x_{i+1},f(x_{i}))\leq\delta
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The homeomorphism is said to have the
shadowing property
(also known as
pseudo-orbit tracing property
) if for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that for each
\delta
-orbit
x
there is
p\in M
such that

\dist(f^{i}(p),x_{i})\leq\varepsilon
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We say that such
p
is an
\varepsilon
-
shadow
of the
\delta
-orbit
x
.

The definition of the shadowing property is about a function associating to each
\delta
-orbit a point of the space.
In this article we study some properties of such function, their relations and the dynamical consequencies.
We assume two basic axioms:

•

continuity and

•

orbits are shadowed by themselves.

For the continuity we consider the
\delta
-orbits as elements of
M^{\mathbb{Z}}
endowed with the product topology.
For the second axiom notice that for
\delta=0
we have a
real orbit
,
i.e.
,
x_{i}=f^{i}(x_{0})
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. For such an orbit
x
we demand the
shadowing point
to be
x_{0}
. These are quite natural conditions but, alone, they do not imply the shadowing property, see Example
2.6
. A map satisfying these two axioms will be called
pseudo-orbit map
, see §
2
.
A pseudo-orbit map inducing the shadowing property will be called
shadowing map
, see §
3
.
In this article we develope a hierarchy of shadowing maps by adding some properties to these elementary axioms.

There are some families of examples with a canonical shadowing map which guided our research.
The strongest family in our set of examples is that of
expansive homeomorphisms with shadowing.
We say that
f
is
expansive
if there is
\xi>0
such that if
p\neq q
,
p,q\in M
, then there is
i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\dist(f^{i}(p),f^{i}(q))>\xi
.
Expansive homeomorphisms with shadowing are called
topologically hyperbolic
.
This notion is at the top of the hierarchy shown in Table

From the definitions we already see an important property: if
\varepsilon\leq\xi/2
and
\delta
is taken from the definition of shadowing then for a
\delta
-orbit there is a unique shadowing point.
The uniqueness implies two properties:

•

dynamical-invariance and

•

shift-invariance.

To fix notation denote as
\shm_{f}(x)
the point that shadows the pseudo-orbit
x
.
On the one hand, we can transform
x
by applying
f
to obtain the pseudo-orbit
y
with
y_{i}=f(x_{i})
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is shadowed by
\shm_{f}(y)
. But notice that
f(\shm_{f}(x))
also shadows
y
. The uniqueness of the shadowing point implies the dynamical-invariance:
\shm_{f}(f(x))=f(\shm_{f}(x))
.
On the other hand,
x
can be transformed by a shift to obtain
z=\shift(x)
where
z_{i}=x_{i+1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Again,
f(\shm_{f}(x))
is a shadow of
\shift(x)
and the uniqueness implies the shift-invariance:

\shm_{f}(\shift(x))=f(\shm_{f}(x))
.

If we forget expansivity and dynamical-invariance we have shift-invariant shadowing maps. In §
6
we prove several properties for systems with such maps. Also, we show that shift homeomorphisms of compact spaces with infinitely many points have a shift-invariant shadowing map which is not dynamically-invariant.

In
[
9
]
R. Bowen proves the shadowing property for expansive homeomorphisms with canonical coordinates.
This is done by considering the hyperbolic contraction of local stable and unstable sets.
In
[
22
]
D. Ruelle defines Smale spaces in terms of a bracket with hyperbolic behaviour.
The canonical coordinates induces a bracket as
[p,q]=r
where
r
is the point in the local stable set of
q
and in the local unstable set of
p
, provided that
p
and
q
are close enough.
In §
5
we revisit Bowen’s technique for hyperbolic brackets. We forget the expansivity condition and look for minimal conditions on the bracket in order to induce a shadowing map. In §
5
we prove that Bowen’s technique gives more than a shadowing map,
see Theorem
5.14
.
This extra technical condition will be called
self-tuning
.
It has the following dynamical meaning: if
p\in M
shadows the pseudo-orbit
x
and for some
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\geq 1
large the distances
\dist(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})
are small for all
|i-j|\leq n
then the shadowing is more precise,
i.e.

\dist(f^{j}(p),x_{j})
is small.
In other words, the shadowing is better where the pseudo-orbit has smaller jumps.
In §
5.2
we show that Bowen’s technique for a north-south homeomorphism gives rise to a self-tuning shadowing map which is not shift-invariant.
Between shadowing maps and self-tuning shadowing maps we place the
L-shadowing maps
; which are shadowing maps inducing the L-shadowing property

2.1.
Preliminaries on pseudo-orbits

Let
(M,\dist)
be a compact metric space.
Let
\tilde{M}=M^{\mathbb{Z}}
be the set of sequences
x\colon\mathbb{Z}\to M
.
We denote
x(i)
as
x_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The set
\tilde{M}
is endowed with the product topology and, consequently, it is compact.
For
\mu\in(0,1)
we consider the distances

\displaystyle\tdist_{s}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu^{|i|}\dist(x_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle\tdist_{m}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu^{|i|}\dist(x_{i},y_{i})

for all
x,y\in\tilde{M}
.
Notice that as
M
is compact, the set
\{\mu^{|i|}\dist(x_{i},y_{i}):i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
has indeed a maximum value.
Both distances induce the product topology on
\tilde{M}
.
On
\tilde{M}
we consider the
shift homeomorphism

\shift\colon\tilde{M}\to\tilde{M}

defined as

(1)

(\shift(x))_{i}=x_{i+1}\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}.

Define
\homeo(M)
as the set of homeomorphisms of
M
.
For
f\in\homeo(M)
and
\delta\geq 0
define:

\tilde{M}(f,\delta)=\{x\in\tilde{M}:\dist(x_{i+1},f(x_{i}))\leq\delta\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Any element of
\tilde{M}(f,\delta)
is called
\delta
-
orbit
of
f
.

Remark 2.1
.

The set
\tilde{M}(f,\delta)
is closed and shift-invariant,
i.e.
,
\shift(\tilde{M}(f,\delta))=\tilde{M}(f,\delta)
.

Define the
orbit map

\orb\colon\homeo(M)\times M\to\tilde{M}
as

x=\orb(f,p)=\orb_{f}(p)\in\tilde{M}
where
x_{i}=f^{i}(p)
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
From the definitions we have that the next diagram commutes

\begin{CD}\tilde{M}@>{\shift}>{}>\tilde{M}\\
@A{\orb_{f}}A{}A@A{}A{\orb_{f}}A\\
M@>{}>{f}>M\\
\end{CD}

and
\orb_{f}
is injective.

Remark 2.2
.

This means that there is a homeomorphic copy of
M
in
\tilde{M}
which is the image of
\orb_{f}
,
i.e.
, the subset of orbits of
f
. Also,
\orb_{f}
is
a dynamical conjugacy between
f
and
\shift\colon\orb_{f}(M)\to\orb_{f}(M)
.

2.2.
Pseudo-orbit maps

For
\delta>0
given we consider a function

\pom_{f}\colon\tilde{M}(f,\delta)\to M
.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that
\pom_{f}
is a
pseudo-orbit map

if

(1)

\pom_{f}
is continuous,

(2)

\pom_{f}(\orb_{f}(p))=p
for all
p\in M
.

The problem is to find a connection between this elementary definition and the shadowing property.
The next definition is natural in this direction.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that
\pom_{f}

induces shadowing
if for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
\gamma\in(0,\delta]
such that
if
x\in\tilde{M}(f,\gamma)
then
\pom_{f}(x)
is an
\varepsilon
-shadow of
x
.

In §
3
we study when a pseudo-orbit map induces shadowing. We finish this section with some properties and examples.

Proposition 2.5
.

If
\pom_{f}
induces shadowing
1
1

1

We do not assume the continuity of
\pom_{f}
.
then

\pom_{f}(\orb_{f}(p))=p
for all
p\in M
.

Proof.

Given
p\in M
we have that
x=\orb_{f}(p)
is a
\delta
-orbit for all
\delta>0
.
Then,
\pom_{f}(x)

\varepsilon
-shadows
x
for all
\varepsilon>0
.
This means
\dist(f^{i}(\pom_{f}(x)),x_{i})<\varepsilon
for all
\varepsilon>0
and
f^{i}(\pom_{f}(x))=x_{i}
,
i.e.

f^{i}(\pom_{f}(x))=f^{i}(p)
and for
i=0
we have
\pom_{f}(\orb_{f}(p))=p
.
∎

Example 2.6
.

Perhaps, the simplest example of a pseudo-orbit map is the projection
\pom_{f}(x)=x_{0}
defined for all
x\in\tilde{M}
.
It is continuous because the product topology in
\tilde{M}
makes continuous its projections.
It is easy to see that
\pom_{f}(\orb_{f}(p))=p
, thus, the projection on the 0-coordinate is a pseudo-orbit map.
Thus, any
f
has a pseudo-orbit map and its existence has no dynamical consequence.
However it is well understood when the projection induces shadowing, see §
3.3
.

In Example
3.4
we will show that a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism of the two-dimensional sphere has the shadowing property but has no pseudo-orbit map inducing shadowing.

2.3.
Discrepancy functions

This section is to introduce some techniques that will be used in
§
2.4
and in the proof of Proposition

5.2
.
Following
[
16
]
,
for
x\in\tilde{M}
define the
f
-
discrepancy
functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{1}_{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\dist(x_{i+1},f(x_{i})),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{2}_{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i})).

Remark 2.7
.

It is clear that
x\in\tilde{M}
is a
\delta
-orbit if and only if

\mathcal{D}^{1}_{f}(x)\leq\delta
.

The next result proves that both functions are equivalent ways to measure how far is
x\in\tilde{M}
from being an orbit of
f
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The discrepancy functions satisfy:

(1)

for all
x\in\tilde{M}
,
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{f}(x)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
x
is an orbit of
f
,
*=1,2
,

(2)

\mathcal{D}^{*}_{f}(x)=\mathcal{D}^{*}_{f}(\shift(x))
for all
x\in\tilde{M}
,
*=1,2
,

(3)

\mathcal{D}^{1}_{f}(x)\leq 2\mathcal{D}^{2}_{f}(x)
for all
x\in\tilde{M}
,

(4)

for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that
if
\mathcal{D}^{1}_{f}(x)\leq\delta
then

\mathcal{D}^{2}_{f}(x)\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

It is clear that they are non-negative and that
\mathcal{D}^{1}_{f}
vanishes only at orbits. The same conclusion for
\mathcal{D}^{2}_{f}
follows from
(3)
and
(4)
that we prove below.
The proof of
(2)
for
*=1
is direct from the definition. For
*=2
we define
y=\shift(x)
and

\mathcal{D}^{2}_{f}(y)=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(y),\orb_{f}(y_{i}))=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i+1}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i+1}))=\mathcal{D}^{2}_{f}(x).

To prove
(3)
notice that for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\displaystyle\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\dist([\shift^{i}(x)]_{j},[\orb_{f}(x_{i})]_{j})}{2^{|j|}}

\displaystyle{\geq}\frac{\dist([\shift^{i}(x)]_{1},[\orb_{f}(x_{i})]_{1})}{2}=\frac{\dist(x_{i+1},f(x_{i}))}{2}

the inquality follows by putting
j=1
in the sum.
Taking
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
on both sides we obtain
(3)
.

To prove
(4)
consider
\varepsilon>0
given.
Take
n\geq 1
such that

\sum_{|i|>n}\frac{\diam(M)}{2^{|i|}}<\varepsilon/2
.
Let

\gamma=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\sum_{|i|\leq n}\frac{1}{2^{|i|}}\right)^{-1}.

Take
\delta>0
such that if
x
is a
\delta
-orbit then
\dist(f^{i}(x_{0}),x_{i})<\gamma
for
|i|\leq n
.
Then

\displaystyle\tdist_{s}(x,\orb_{f}(x_{0}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\dist(x_{i},f^{i}(x_{0}))}{2^{|i|}}=\sum_{|i|\leq n}\frac{\dist(x_{i},f^{i}(x_{0}))}{2^{|i|}}+\sum_{|i|>n}\frac{\dist(x_{i},f^{i}(x_{0}))}{2^{|i|}}

\displaystyle<\sum_{|i|\leq n}\frac{\gamma}{2^{|i|}}+\sum_{|i|>n}\frac{\diam(M)}{2^{|i|}}<\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon.

Since
\shift^{i}(x)
is also a
\delta
-orbit, we have

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}((\shift^{i}(x))_{0}))=\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i}))<\varepsilon

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. Taking the supremum again the proof ends.
∎

2.4.
A characterization

The next characterization of pseudo-orbit maps will be used in the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Proposition 2.9
.

For a continuous map
\pom_{f}\colon\tilde{M}(f,\delta)\to M

the following are equivalent:

(1)

\pom_{f}
is a pseudo-orbit map,

(2)

for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
\rho>0
such that if

x\in\tilde{M}(f,\rho)
then

\dist(x_{i},\pom_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)))<\varepsilon
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(3)

for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
\rho>0
such that if

x\in\tilde{M}(f,\rho)
then

\dist(x_{0},\pom_{f}(x))<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

First notice that
(2)
and
(3)
are equivalent because
(\shift^{i}(x))_{0}=x_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and

x\in\tilde{M}(f,\rho)
if and only if
\shift^{i}(x)\in\tilde{M}(f,\rho)
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
(Remark
2.1
).

To prove that
(1)
implies
(3)
consider
\varepsilon>0
given and take
\nu>0
such that
if
\tdist_{s}(x,y)<\nu
then
\dist(\pom_{f}(x),\pom_{f}(y))<\varepsilon
(uniform continuity of
\pom_{f}
).
By Proposition
2.8
there is
\rho>0
such that if
x
is a
\rho
-orbit then

\tdist_{s}(x,\orb_{f}(x_{0}))<\nu.

Therefore, given any
x\in\tilde{M}(f,\rho)
we have

\dist(x_{0},\pom_{f}(x))=\dist(\pom_{f}(\orb_{f}(x_{0})),\pom_{f}(x))<\varepsilon
.

Let us show that
(3)
implies
(1)
. Given
p\in M
let
x=\orb_{f}(p)
.
Then,
x_{0}=p
and
x\in\tilde{M}(f,\rho)
for all
\rho>0
.
Thus,

\dist(x_{0},\pom_{f}(x))<\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon>0
.
This implies
x_{0}=\pom_{f}(x)
and

p=x_{0}=\pom_{f}(x)=\pom_{f}(\orb_{f}(p)).

Therefore
\pom_{f}
is a pseudo-orbit map.
∎

2.5.
Connecting map

Define the
connecting map

\con\colon\homeo(M)\times M^{2}\to\tilde{M}

as

\con(f,p,q)=\con_{f}(p,q)=x
where

x_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f^{i}(p)&\text{ for }i<0,\\
f^{i}(q)&\text{ for }i\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

On
\homeo(M)
we consider the
C^{0}
distance

\distcc(f,g)=\sup\{\dist(f(p),g(p)),\dist(f^{-1}(p),g^{-1}(p)):p\in M\}.

Proposition 2.10
.

The connecting map is continuous and uniformly continuous with respect to the variables in
M^{2}
:
for all
f\in\homeo(M)
and
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that
if
\distcc(f,g)<\delta
,
\dist(p_{1},p_{2})<\delta
and
\dist(q_{1},q_{2})<\delta

then

\dist(\con_{f}(p_{1},q_{1}),\con_{g}(p_{2},q_{2}))<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Given
\varepsilon>0
take
j\geq 0
and
\gamma>0
such that

\sum_{|i|>j}\frac{\diam(M)}{2^{|i|}}<\varepsilon/2\text{ and }\sum_{|i|\leq j}\frac{\gamma}{2^{|i|}}<\varepsilon/2

Given
f\in\homeo(M)
consider
\delta>0
such that
if
\dist(r_{1},r_{2})<\delta
and
\distcc(g,f)<\delta

then
\dist(f^{i}(r_{1}),g^{i}(r_{2}))<\gamma
for all

|i|\leq j
.

In this way, if
\distcc(f,g)<\delta
,

\dist(p_{1},p_{2})<\delta
,
\dist(q_{1},q_{2})<\delta
,

x=\con_{f}(p_{1},q_{1})
and
y=\con_{g}(p_{2},q_{2})

3.
Shadowing maps

In this section we introduce the shadowing maps.
In §
3.1
we prove a useful equivalence.
In §
3.2
we show that cw-expansivity implies expansivity assuming the existence of a shadowing map and the local connection of the space.
In §
3.3
we present known results characterizing when the projection is a shadowing map.
In §
3.4
we define shadowing brackets. We show
that cw-expansivity with a shadowing bracket implies cw
1
-expansivity; which in turn implies expansivity on compact surfaces.

3.1.
Shadowing maps

The definition of shadowing map is given by considering two things. On the one hand, at least it should be a pseudo-orbit map inducing the shadowing property.
On the other hand, as explained in §
1
, topologically hyperbolic homeomorphisms have shift-invariant shadowing maps.
The shift-invariance means that
\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x))=0
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
In Proposition
3.2
we prove that both viewpoints coincide.
Definition
3.1

is given as a weak form of shift-invariance.

Definition 3.1
.

A pseudo-orbit map

\shm_{f}\colon\tilde{M}(f,\delta)\to M

is a
shadowing map
if
for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
\gamma\in(0,\delta]
such that if
x\in\tilde{M}(f,\gamma)
then

(2)

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x))\leq\varepsilon\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}.

This condition is exactly what a pseudo-orbit map needs to induce the shadowing property.

Proposition 3.2
.

A pseudo-orbit map induces shadowing if and only if it is a shadowing map.
In particular, every homeomorphism with a shadowing map has the shadowing property.

Proof.

(Direct). Given
\varepsilon>0
take
\gamma>0
such that
every
\gamma
-orbit
x
is
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
-shadowed by
\shm_{f}(x)
.
Notice that
\shift^{j}(x)
also is a
\gamma
-orbit for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Thus

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(\shift^{j}(x))),(\shift^{j}(x))_{i})\leq\varepsilon/2\text{ for all }i,j\in\mathbb{Z}.

For
j=0
we have

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),x_{i})\leq\varepsilon/2\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}

and for
i=0
we conclude

\dist(\shm_{f}(\shift^{j}(x)),(\shift^{j}(x))_{0})=\dist(\shm_{f}(\shift^{j}(x)),x_{j})\leq\varepsilon/2\text{ for all }j\in\mathbb{Z}.

The triangle inequality gives

\begin{array}[]{rl}\dist(f^{k}(\shm_{f}(x)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{k}(x)))&\leq\dist(f^{k}(\shm_{f}(x)),x_{k})+\dist(x_{k},\shm_{f}(\shift^{k}(x)))\\
&\leq\varepsilon/2+\varepsilon/2=\varepsilon\end{array}

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. This proves (
2
).

(Converse). Given
\varepsilon>0
take
\gamma>0
from Proposition
2.9

such that if
x\in\tilde{M}(f,\gamma)
then

\dist(x_{i},\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)))\leq\varepsilon/2,\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}.

By hypothesis, considering a smaller
\gamma
if needed, we can also assume

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x))\leq\varepsilon/2\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}.

The triangle inequality gives us

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),x_{i})\leq\varepsilon\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}.

This proves that
\shm_{f}
induces shadowing.
∎

3.2.
Cw-expansivity and shadowing maps

We will show that there are homeomorphisms with shadowing do not admitting a shadowing map.
For this purpose we recall the notion of continuum-wise expansivity.

A subset
C\subset M
is a
continuum
if it is closed and connected.
Following H. Kato we say that
f
is
cw-expansive
if there is
\xi>0

such that if
C\subset M
is a continuum and
\diam(f^{i}(C))\leq\xi
for all

i\in\mathbb{Z}
then
C
is a singleton.
If we define the
dynamical ball

\Gamma_{\xi}(p)=\{q\in M:\dist(f^{i}(p),f^{i}(q))\leq\xi\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}\}

then
f
is cw-expansive if and only if there is
\xi>0
such that

\Gamma_{\xi}(p)
is totally disconected for all
p\in M
.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
M
is locally connected,
f
is cw-expansive and has a shadowing map then
f
is expansive.

Proof.

Let
2\xi
be a cw-expansivity constant and suppose that
\shm_{f}(x)
is an
\xi/2
-shadow of each
\delta
-orbit
x
.
Take
\gamma>0
,
\gamma<\delta/2,\xi/2
such that

(3)

if
\dist(a,b)<\gamma
then
\dist(f(a),f(b))<\delta/2
.

As
M
is locally connected there is
c>0
such that if
\dist(a,b)\leq c
then there is a continuum of diameter smaller than
\gamma
containing
a
and
b
.
We will show that
c
is an expansivity constant.

Suppose that
\dist(f^{i}(p),f^{i}(q))\leq c
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
take a continuum
C_{i}\subset M
containing
f^{i}(p)
and
f^{i}(q)
with
\diam(C_{i})<\gamma
.
Define the set

A=\{x\in\tilde{M}:x_{i}\in C_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Note that
\orb_{f}(p),\orb_{f}(q)\in A
.

In this paragraph we will show that
A\subset\tilde{M}(f,\delta)
.
If
x\in A
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}
then

\dist(f^{i}(p),x_{i})<\gamma
(because both points belong to
C_{i}
) and
\dist(f^{i+1}(p),f(x_{i+1}))<\delta/2
(by (
3
)).
Since
x_{i+1},f^{i+1}(p)\in C_{i+1}
we have
\dist(x_{i+1},f^{i+1}(p))<\gamma<\delta/2
and the triangle inequality implies
\dist(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})\leq\delta/2
.
Thus, each
x\in A
is a
\delta
-orbit and
A\subset\tilde{M}(f,\delta)
.

Since
\shm_{f}
is a pseudo-orbit map we conclude
\shm_{f}(A)
is a continuum (
A
is a continuum and
\shm_{f}
is continuous) and
p,q\in\shm_{f}(A)
.

For
r\in\shm_{f}(A)
there is
x\in A
such that
r=\shm_{f}(x)
.
Since
\shm_{f}(x)
is an
\xi/2
-shadow of
x
we have

\dist(f^{i}(r),x_{i})\leq\xi/2
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Since
\dist(x_{i},f^{i}(p))\leq\gamma\leq\xi/2
,
by the triangle inequality we have
\dist(f^{i}(r),f^{i}(p))\leq\xi
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This means
\shm_{f}(A)\subset\Gamma_{\xi}(p)
. Since
2\xi
is a cw-expansivity constant the set
\Gamma_{\xi}(p)
is totally disconnected. Thus,
\shm_{f}(A)
is connected (proved above), totally disconnected and contains
p,q
.
This implies that
\shm_{f}(A)
is a singleton and
p=q
. This proves that

is expansive with expansivity constant
c
.
∎

Definition 3.9

A

Proof.

\varepsilon>0

,

3.5.

In what follows we show that the pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism of the two-sphere does not admit a shadowing bracket.

A continuum is
\varepsilon
-
stable
if
\diam(f^{n}(C))\leq\varepsilon
for all

\varepsilon
-

if
\diam(f^{-n}(C))\leq\varepsilon
for all

For

we say that

is

N
-expansive

[

]
if there is
\xi>0
such that if

is an
\xi
-stable continuum and

is

-unstable then

contains at most

It is stronger than cw-expansivity.

The next proof is similar to that of Theorem
3.3
, and in both cases the topological connection is a key property. We learnt this kind of argument from J. Lewowicz, see for instance
[
19
]
*Lemma 2.3.

Theorem 3.11
.

If
f
is cw-expansive and has a shadowing bracket then
f
is cw
1
-expansive.

Proof.

Suppose that
2\xi>0
is a cw-expansivity constant.
Suppose that the bracket is defined in
\Delta_{\delta}
.
Taking a smaller
\delta
if needed we can assume that

[p,q]\in W^{s}_{\xi/3}(q)\cap W^{u}_{\xi/3}(p)
whenever
\dist(p,q)\leq\delta
.

Suppose that
C_{s}
and
C_{u}
are
\frac{\xi}{3}
-stable/unstable continua respectively.
Assume that
\diam(C_{s}\cup C_{u})\leq\delta
.
In this way we can define

A=[C_{u},C_{s}]=\{[p,q]:p\in C_{u},q\in C_{s}\}.

Note that
A
is connected as it is the image of a connected set by a continuous function.
If

we can write

r_{j}=[p_{j},q_{j}]
,
p_{j}\in C_{u}
and
q_{j}\in C_{s}
for
j=1,2
.
In this way we have for
i\geq 0

\begin{array}[]{rl}\dist(f^{i}(r_{1}),f^{i}(r_{2}))&\leq\dist(f^{i}(r_{1}),f^{i}(q_{1}))+\dist(f^{i}(q_{1}),f^{i}(q_{2}))+\dist(f^{i}(q_{2}),f^{i}(r_{2}))\\
&\leq\xi/3+\xi/3+\xi/3=\xi.\end{array}

Analogously, for

we have
\dist(f^{i}(r_{1}),f^{i}(r_{2}))\leq\xi
.
Thus, if we fix some

we conclude

.
Since
2\xi
is a cw-expansivity constant we have that
\Gamma_{2\xi}(r)
and

are totally disconnected. As

is connected, it is a singleton.

Finally notice that for each

we have

,
i.e.
,
C_{u}\cap C_{s}
is contained in the singleton

and it is a singleton or empty.
Let us show that
\gamma=\min\{\delta/2,\xi/3\}
is a cw
1
-expansivity constant.
Suppose that
C_{s}
and
C_{u}
are
\gamma
-stable/unstable continua respectively.
If
C_{s}
intersects
C_{u}
then the diameter of their union is at most
\delta
, so we can apply our previous results to conclude that their intersection is a singleton.
∎

such that if

x
is a
\rho
-orbit then
\mathcal{D}^{2}_{f}(x)<\gamma
and
consequently

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i}))<\gamma
for all

.
Thus, every
\rho
-orbit satisfies (
4
) for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

is a shadowing map (Definition

) and it induces shadowing.

Suppose that

is a
\rho
-orbit that is also a two-sided limit pseudo-orbit. That is,
\dist(f(x_{i}),x_{i+1})\to 0
as
|i|\to\infty
.
This implies that
\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i}))\to 0
as

|i|\to\infty
.
Thus,

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)))\to 0
as
|i|\to\infty
.
Also, from the uniform continuity of

.
Finally,

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),x_{i})\leq\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)))+\dist(\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)),x_{i})\to 0.

We have neither a counterexample nor a proof for the converse of Proposition
5.2
. The next characterization is used to prove that hyperbolic brackets induce the self-tuning property.

Proposition 5.3
.

A pseudo-orbit map
\shm_{f}\colon\tilde{M}(f,\delta)\to M
has self-tuning if and only if for all
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
there is
\gamma^{\prime}>0
such that if

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i}))<\gamma^{\prime}
for some
x\in\tilde{M}(f,\delta)
and some
i\in\mathbb{Z}
then

(5)

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),x_{i})\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}.

Proof.

We start with an argument for both directions.
Given
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
take
\gamma^{\prime\prime}>0
such that
if
\tdist_{s}(y,z)<\gamma^{\prime\prime}
then

\dist(\shm_{f}(y),\shm_{f}(z))<\min\{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\}/2
.

To prove the direct take
\gamma\in(0,\gamma^{\prime\prime})
such that if

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i}))<\gamma
then

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)))\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}/2.

Since

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i}))<\gamma^{\prime\prime}
we have

\dist(\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)),x_{i})=\dist(\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)),\shm_{f}(\orb_{f}(x_{i})))<\varepsilon^{\prime}/2.

Thus,
\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),x_{i})\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
.

The converse is quite similar.
Take
\gamma^{\prime}\in(0,\gamma^{\prime\prime})
such that if

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{i}(x),\orb_{f}(x_{i}))<\gamma^{\prime}
then

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),x_{i})\leq\varepsilon/2.

Again we conclude
\dist(\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)),x_{i})<\varepsilon/2
and

\dist(f^{i}(\shm_{f}(x)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{i}(x)))\leq\varepsilon
.
∎

5.1.
Hyperbolic brackets

In this section we show that hyperbolic brackets induce shadowing maps with self-tuning.
We will assume that
f
and
f^{-1}
are Lipschitz.

Definition 5.4
.

Suppose that
c\geq 1
and
\mu\in(0,1)
.
A bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]\colon\Delta_{\gamma}\to M
has
(c,\mu)
-
hyperbolic contraction
if
\dist(p,q)\leq\gamma
implies

\displaystyle\dist(f^{n}([p,q]),f^{n}(q))

\displaystyle\leq c\mu^{n}\dist(p,q),

\displaystyle\dist(f^{-n}([p,q]),f^{-n}(p))

\displaystyle\leq c\mu^{n}\dist(p,q)

for all
n\geq 0
.

5.1.1.
Lipschitz homeomorphisms

In this section we give some elementary estimates for Lipschitz maps.
For
L>0
and
n\geq 1
define
L_{n}=1+L+L^{2}+\dots+L^{n-1}
. Note that

L_{1}=1
and
LL_{n}+1=L_{n+1}
.
Define

\delta_{i}(x)=\dist(f(x_{i-1}),x_{i})

for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and

x\in\tilde{M}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
f
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
L>0

then

(6)

\dist(f^{n}(x_{0}),x_{n})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}(x)L^{n-j}\leq L^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}(x)\text{ for all }n\geq 1

for all
x\in\tilde{M}
.
If
x
is a
\delta
-orbit then

(7)

\dist(f^{n}(x_{0}),x_{n})\leq\delta L_{n}\text{ for all }n\geq 1.

Proof.

We prove the first inequality by induction.
For
n=1
we have

\dist(f(x_{0}),x_{1})\leq\delta_{1}(x)
(in fact an equality by definition).
If the inequality is true for
n\geq 1
then

\displaystyle\dist(f^{n+1}(x_{0}),x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq\dist(f^{n+1}(x_{0}),f(x_{n}))+\dist(f(x_{n}),x_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq L\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}(x)L^{n-j}+\delta_{n+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\delta_{j}(x)L^{n+1-j}

Since
M
is compact we have
L\geq 1
. Thus
L^{n}\geq L^{n-j}
for all
1\leq j\leq n
and

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}(x)L^{n-j}\leq L^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}(x).

If
x
is a
\delta
-orbit then
\delta_{j}(x)\leq\delta
for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{j}(x)L^{n-j}\leq\delta\sum_{j=1}^{n}L^{n-j}=\delta L_{n}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

5.1.2.
A pseudo-orbit map

In this section we define a pseudo-orbit map from a hyperbolic bracket.
In §
5.1.3
we show that in fact it has self-tuning.
Suppose that
f
is Lipschitz and has a hyperbolic bracket

[\cdot,\cdot]\colon\Delta_{\gamma}\to M

with parameters
c,\mu
as explained before.
Fix
m
large enough that

(8)

c\mu^{m}\leq 1/2c.

For a
\delta
-orbit
x
we try to
define the sequences

, for
n\geq 0
, as

(9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}q_{0}=x_{0}\\
q_{n}=[f^{m}(q_{n-1}),x_{nm}]\text{ if }n\geq 1,\\
p_{n}=f^{-nm}(q_{n}).\end{array}\right.

This makes sense if the bracket can be applied, that is, if

\dist(f^{m}(q_{n-1}),x_{nm})\leq\gamma
for all
n\geq 1
.

Lemma 5.6
.

If
0<2\delta L_{m}\leq\gamma
and
x
is a
\delta
-orbit then

\dist(f^{m}(q_{n-1}),x_{nm})\leq 2\delta L_{m}
for all
n\geq 1
.
In particular, the sequence
q_{n}
is well-defined for
\delta
-orbits with

0<2\delta L_{m}\leq\gamma
.

Proof.

Arguing by induction, notice that for
n=1
we can apply (
7
) and

\dist(f^{m}(q_{0}),x_{m})=\dist(f^{m}(x_{0}),x_{m})\leq\delta L_{m}.

Now suppose that
\dist(f^{m}(q_{n-1}),x_{nm})\leq 2\delta L_{m}
for some
n\geq 1
.
Since
q_{n}=[f^{m}(q_{n-1}),x_{nm}]
we can apply the hyperbolic contraction condition, the fact
c\geq 1
and (
8
) to obtain

\dist(f^{m}(q_{n}),f^{m}(x_{nm}))=\dist(f^{m}([f^{m}(q_{n-1}),x_{nm}]),f^{m}(x_{nm}))\leq c\mu^{m}\dist(f^{m}(q_{n-1}),x_{nm})\leq\frac{1}{2c}2\delta L_{m}\leq\delta L_{m}.

Also, by (
7
) we have

\dist(f^{m}(x_{nm}),x_{(n+1)m})=\dist(f^{m}(x_{nm}),x_{nm+m})\leq\delta L_{m}.

The triangle inequality gives

\dist(f^{m}(q_{n}),x_{(n+1)m})\leq\dist(f^{m}(q_{n}),f^{m}(x_{nm}))+\dist(f^{m}(x_{nm}),x_{(n+1)m})\leq\delta L_{m}+\delta L_{m}=2\delta L_{m}

and the proof ends.
∎

In what follows we assume that
x
is a
\delta
-orbit with

0<2\delta L_{m}\leq\gamma
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For all
k\geq 0
,
t\geq 1

(10)

\dist(f^{-k}(q_{t}),f^{-k}(f^{m}(q_{t-1})))\leq c\mu^{k}\dist(f^{m}(q_{t-1}),x_{tm})\leq c\mu^{k}(2\delta L_{m}).

Proof.

Since

q_{t}=[f^{m}(q_{t-1}),x_{tm}]

we can apply the hyperbolic contraction to obtain the first inequality.
The last inequality follows from Lemma
5.6
.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

For all
n\geq 0
we have
\dist(p_{n+1},p_{n})\leq c\mu^{(n+1)m}(2\delta L_{m})
.

Proof.

By (
10
) with
k=(n+1)m
and
t=n+1
gives

\displaystyle\dist(p_{n+1},p_{n})

\displaystyle=\dist(f^{-(n+1)m}(q_{n+1}),f^{-nm}(q_{n}))

\displaystyle=\dist(f^{-(n+1)m}(q_{n+1}),f^{-(n+1)m}(f^{m}(q_{n}))\leq c\mu^{(n+1)m}(2\delta L_{m})

and the proof ends.
∎

For
n\geq 1
we can define

\shm_{f,n}^{+}\colon\tilde{M}(f,\delta)\to M
as

\shm_{f,n}^{+}(x)=p_{n}
.
From the definitions it is clear that
\shm_{f,n}^{+}
is continuous.
Lemma

means the uniform convergence of this sequence of maps.
Therefore, we can define the continuous map

\shm_{f}^{+}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\shm_{f,n}
.
Analogously, we can define

\shm_{f}^{-}\colon\tilde{M}(f,\delta)\to M
as

\shm_{f}^{-}=\shm_{f^{-1}}^{+}
,
where
\shm_{f^{-1}}^{+}
is the map obtained from the procedure just explained but with

in place of

.
Finally consider

(11)

\shm_{f}=[\shm_{f}^{-},\shm_{f}^{+}].

Proposition 5.9
.

The function
\shm_{f}
is a pseudo-orbit map.

Proof.

It is clear that
\shm_{f}
is continuous.
In order to conclude that it is a pseudo-orbit map we need to check

\shm_{f}(\orb_{f}(p))=p
for any
p\in M
.
Let
x=\orb_{f}(p)
. Then
x_{i}=f^{i}(p)
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Thus,
p_{0}=q_{0}=x_{0}=p
.
For
n\geq 0
suppose that
p_{n}=p
and
q_{n}=f^{nm}(p)
.
Then
q_{n+1}=[f^{m}(q_{n}),x_{(n+1)m}]=[f^{m}(f^{nm}(p)),f^{(n+1)m}(p)]=f^{(n+1)m}(p)
. Then

. By induction we proved that

for all

.
Thus,

and
\shm_{f}^{+}(x)=p
.
Analogously,
\shm_{f}^{-}(x)=p
and

\shm_{f}(\orb_{f}(p))=\shm_{f}(\con_{f}(p,p))=[\shm_{f}^{-}(\con_{f}(p,p)),\shm_{f}^{+}(\con_{f}(p,p))]=[p,p]=p
.
This proves that

is a pseudo-orbit map.
∎

5.1.3.
Proving the self-tuning property

In this section we will show that the pseudo-orbit map of Equation (

\delta
for

jumping at each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
as we need to

the shadowing where the jumps from

to

are small.

As in Lemma
5.6
suppose that

0<2\delta L_{m}\leq\gamma
and
x
is a
\delta
-orbit.
Define

\delta_{l}^{m}(x)=L^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{(l-1)m+j}(x).

Lemma 5.10
.

For all
u\geq 1

(12)

\dist(f^{m}(q_{u-1}),x_{um})\leq\sum_{l=1}^{u}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{u-l}}.

Proof.

Arguing by induction, notice that
u=1
implies
l=1
and
we have to prove

\dist(f^{m}(q_{0}),x_{m})\leq\delta_{1}^{m}(x)

and this follows from (
6
) with
u=m
. Now assume that
(
12
) holds for some
u\geq 1
. For

we have

\displaystyle\dist(f^{m}(q_{u}),x_{(u+1)m})

\displaystyle\leq\dist(f^{m}(q_{u}),f^{m}(x_{um}))+\dist(f^{m}(x_{um}),x_{(u+1)m})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\dist(f^{m}(q_{u-1}),x_{um})+\delta_{u+1}^{m}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{u}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{u-l}}+\delta_{u+1}^{m}(x)=\sum_{l=1}^{u}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{u+1-l}}+\frac{\delta_{u+1}^{m}(x)}{2^{0}}=\sum_{l=1}^{u+1}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{u+1-l}}

and the proof ends.
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

If
n\geq 1
,
i\in[0,nm]
and

sm\leq i<(s+1)m
then

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\dist(f^{i}(p_{n}),f^{i-sm}(q_{s}))&\leq\frac{4c}{3}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{|l-s-1|}},\\
\dist(f^{i-sm}(q_{s}),f^{i-sm}(x_{sm}))&\leq 2c\sum_{l=1}^{s}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{s+1-l}},\\
\dist(f^{i-sm}(x_{sm}),x_{i})&\leq\delta_{s+1}^{m}(x)\end{array}\right.

and

(13)

\dist(f^{i}(p_{n}),x_{i})\leq\kappa\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{|l-s-1|}}

where
\kappa=\frac{10}{3}c+1
.

Proof.

(First inequality). The hyperbolic contraction and Lemma
5.10
with
u=t
imply

(14)

\begin{array}[]{rl}\dist(f^{-k}(q_{t}),f^{-k}(f^{m}(q_{t-1})))&\displaystyle\leq c\mu^{k}\dist(f^{m}(q_{t-1}),x_{tm})\leq c\mu^{k}\sum_{l=1}^{t}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{t-l}}.\end{array}

whenever
1\leq t\leq k
.
The triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\dist(f^{i}(p_{n}),f^{i-sm}(q_{s}))=\dist(f^{i-nm}(q_{n}),f^{i-sm}(q_{s}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\dist(f^{i-tm}(q_{t}),f^{i-(t-1)m}(q_{t-1}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\dist(f^{i-tm}(q_{t}),f^{i-tm}(f^{m}(q_{t-1})).

From (
14
) for
k=tm-i\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\dist(f^{i-tm}(q_{t}),f^{i-tm}(f^{m}(q_{t-1}))\leq c\mu^{tm-i}\sum_{l=1}^{t}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{t-l}}\leq c\mu^{(t-s-1)m}\sum_{l=1}^{t}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{t-l}}

because

.
From (
8
)
\mu^{m}\leq 1/2
and

\mu^{(t-s-1)m}\leq\frac{1}{2^{t-s-1}}
.
Then

\dist(f^{i-tm}(q_{t}),f^{i-tm}(f^{m}(q_{t-1}))\leq c\frac{1}{2^{t-s-1}}\sum_{l=1}^{t}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{t-l}}.

Consequently

\dist(f^{i}(p_{n}),f^{i-sm}(q_{s}))\leq\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{t}c\frac{1}{2^{t-s-1}}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{t-l}}.

We transform de double sum as follows

\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{t}=\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{s}+\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\sum_{l=s+1}^{t}=\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{s}+\sum_{l=s+1}^{n}\sum_{t=l}^{n}.

For the first sum

\displaystyle\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{s}\frac{1}{2^{t-s-1}}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{t-l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{-s-1}}\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{2t}}\sum_{l=1}^{s}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{-l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{-s-1}}\frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{1}{4^{s+1}}-\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}\right)\sum_{l=1}^{s}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{-l}}<\frac{4}{3}\sum_{l=1}^{s}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{s+1-l}}.

For the second sum

\displaystyle\sum_{t=s+1}^{n}\sum_{l=s+1}^{t}\frac{1}{2^{t-s-1}}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{t-l}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=s+1}^{n}\sum_{t=l}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{t-s-1}}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{t-l}}=\sum_{l=s+1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{-l-s-1}}\sum_{t=l}^{n}\frac{1}{2^{2t}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=s+1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{-l-s-1}}\frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{1}{4^{l}}-\frac{1}{4^{n+1}}\right)<\frac{4}{3}\sum_{l=s+1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{-l-s-1}}\left(\frac{1}{4^{l}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\sum_{l=s+1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{l-s-1}}.

Then

\dist(f^{i}(p_{n}),f^{i-sm}(q_{s}))\leq\frac{4c}{3}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{|l-s-1|}}.

(Second inequality). Notice that

and again the hyperbolic contraction and Lemma
5.10
give

\displaystyle\dist(f^{i-sm}(q_{s}),f^{i-sm}(x_{sm}))

\displaystyle\leq c\mu^{i-sm}\dist(f^{m}(q_{s-1}),x_{sm})\leq c\sum_{l=1}^{s}\frac{\delta_{l}^{m}(x)}{2^{s-l}}

(Third inequality). We will apply (

\dist(f^{i-sm}(x_{sm}),x_{i})=\dist(f^{i-sm}(x_{sm}),x_{i-sm+sm})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i-sm}\delta_{sm+j}(x)L^{i-sm-j}\leq L^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{i-sm}\delta_{sm+j}(x)\leq\delta_{s+1}^{m}(x)

(Last inequality). The inequality (

∎

we have

[x,y]=\shm_{\sigma}(\con_{\sigma}(x,y))

and we see how a shadowing map induces a bracket.

Conversely, we now start with the bracket.
Take
\alpha\in\tilde{M}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
As in §
5.1.2
define sequences

, for
n\geq 0
as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x_{0}=\alpha_{0},\\
x_{n}=[\sigma(x_{n-1}),\alpha_{n}],n\geq 1,\\
y_{n}=\sigma^{-n}(x_{n}).\end{array}\right.

The next result formally explains the beautiful work of these equations.

Proposition 6.9
.

For all
n\geq 0
and
0\leq i\leq n
we have

(y_{n})_{i}=(\alpha_{i})_{0}
.

Proof.

For
n=0
,
i=0
we have
(y_{0})_{0}=(x_{0})_{0}=(\alpha_{0})_{0}
.
Take
n\geq 1
and assume that

(y_{n})_{i}=(\alpha_{i})_{0}
for
0\leq i\leq n
.
If
0\leq i\leq n
then

(y_{n+1})_{i}=(\shift^{-n-1}(x_{n+1}))_{i}=(x_{n+1})_{i-n-1}=[\sigma(x_{n}),\alpha_{n+1}]_{i-n-1}=(\sigma(x_{n}))_{i-n-1}=(x_{n})_{i-n}\text{ and }

(\alpha_{i})_{0}=(y_{n})_{i}=(\sigma^{-n}(x_{n}))_{i}=(x_{n})_{i-n}.

Thus
(y_{n+1})_{i}=(\alpha_{i})_{0}
for

.
For

(y_{n+1})_{n+1}=(\shift^{-n-1}(x_{n+1}))_{n+1}=(x_{n+1})_{0}=[\sigma(x_{n}),\alpha_{n+1}]_{0}=(\alpha_{n+1})_{0}

and the proof ends.
∎

From this we see that

is a convergent sequence and its limit

satisfies
z^{+}_{n}=(\alpha_{n})_{0}
for all

.
Analogously, with
\sigma^{-1}
in place of

with

for all

.
Finally, the shadowing map is recovered by

This way of inducing a shadowing map from a bracket is analogous to Bowen’s technique shown in §

.

.

In

[

]
proved that topologically stable homeomorphisms of closed manifold have the shadowing property.

.

If

Proof.

Let

\varepsilon>0
.
By the continuity of
\shm_{f}

6.4.
Bracket with uniform contraction

In §
5.1
we considered brackets with hyperbolic contraction. We can prove that the bracket induced by a shift-invariant shadowing map satisfies a weaker condition.

Definition 6.13
.

An L-bracket has
uniform contraction
if
for all
\varepsilon>0
there is
m\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\dist(f^{n}([p,q]),f^{n}(q))<\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\dist(f^{-n}([p,q]),f^{-n}(p))<\varepsilon

for all
n\geq m
.

Remark 6.14
.

The brackets with hyperbolic contraction defined in §
5.1
have uniform contraction.

Proposition 6.15
.

The bracket induced by a shift-invariant shadowing map has uniform contraction.

Proof.

As explained in §
6.1.2

the shift homeomorphism has a hyperbolic bracket,
i.e.

for
x,y\in\tilde{M}
we have

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{n}([x,y]),\shift^{n}(y))=\mu^{n}\tdist_{s}(x,y)
for all
n\geq 0

and

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{-n}([x,y]),\shift^{-n}(x))=\mu^{n}\tdist_{s}(x,y)
for all
n\geq 0
.
Recall that this bracket is defined from
\tilde{M}\times\tilde{M}
to
\tilde{M}
.
On
M
we define
[p,q]_{M}=\shm_{f}(\con_{f}(p,q))
.
As
\shm_{f}
is uniformly continuous there is
\delta>0
such that if
\tdist_{s}(x,y)<\delta
then
\dist(\shm_{f}(x),\shm_{f}(y))<\varepsilon
.
Take
m\geq 1
such that

\mu^{n}\diam(\tilde{M})<\delta
whenever
n\geq m
.
For
n\geq m
we have

\tdist_{s}(\shift^{n}(\orb_{f}(q)),\shift^{n}(\con_{f}(p,q)))<\mu^{n}\diam(\tilde{M})<\delta

and applying

we conclude

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle>\dist(\shm_{f}(\shift^{n}(\orb_{f}(q)),\shm_{f}(\shift^{n}(\con_{f}(p,q))))

\displaystyle=\dist(f^{n}(\shm_{f}(\orb_{f}(q))),f^{n}(\shm_{f}(\con_{f}(p,q))))

∎

We follow
