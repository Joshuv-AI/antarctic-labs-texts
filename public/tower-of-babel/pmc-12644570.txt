A layered Prussian blue analogue as fast-charging negative electrode material for lithium-ion batteries
Authors: Chongwei Gao, Ming Chen, Jiantao Li, Sungsik Lee, Tian Sun, Xunan Wang, Shuhua Zhang, Guang Feng, Dengyun Zhai, Feiyu Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe simultaneous achievement of fast-charging and high specific capacity remains a critical challenge for lithium-ion battery negative electrodes. Here we report a layered manganese-based Prussian blue analogue, synthesized through vacancy control and subsequent thermal transformation. As a conversion-type negative electrode, this material exhibits high-rate performance, delivering a specific capacity of 510 mAh g−1 at a specific current of 8 A g−1, and operates at a moderate average voltage of approximately 1.2 V vs. Li/Li+, which mitigates lithium plating risks. This high-rate capability stems from the analogue’s specific linkage configurations, which facilitate a high content of active transition metal and strong Li+ adsorption at nitrogen sites. The high transition metal content enables a high reversible capacity, while strong Li+ adsorption promotes an efficient initial crystalline-to-amorphous transformation. This process induces dynamically reversible component migration during subsequent cycling, thereby enhancing conversion reaction kinetics. Our findings provide insights into the application of Prussian blue analogues as fast-charging negative electrode materials.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

Prussian blue analogues (PBAs) represent a class of low-cost electrode materials with open frameworks capable of accommodating various ions1,2. Their energy storage capability stems from redox-active transition metal atoms (TMs) within the framework. Typically, these TMs cycle between +2 and +3 oxidation states at high potentials, making PBAs suitable as positive electrode materials. For instance, the Mn2+/3+ and Fe2+/3+ redox couples in the K2MnFe(CN)6 exhibit an average voltage of 3.9 V (vs. K/K+), yielding a theoretical specific capacity of 155 mAh g−13.

Recent work has shown that TMs in PBAs can also access lower oxidation states (+1 and 0) at reduced potentials, opening a pathway for their use as negative electrodes4. At these potentials, PBAs operate via two distinct electrochemical mechanisms depending on their composition. The first mechanism involves maintaining the crystal structure during cycling. A key example, manganese hexacyanomanganate, displays a specific capacity of 67 mAh g−1 from the Mn(I/II) redox couple at –0.70 V vs. SHE, but this limited specific capacity restricts its application5. A second, more complex mechanism involves a crystal-to-amorphous (c-to-a) transformation, particularly in hexacyanoferrates6. This process, which reduces TMs to their zero-valent state, enables multi-electron transfers and unlocks a specific capacity exceeding 300 mAh g−1. However, this high-capacity conversion mechanism is often plagued by poor cycling stability and sluggish kinetics, and the nature of the resulting amorphous phase is not well understood7.

Enhancing the performance of conversion-based PBAs requires careful optimization of their initial structure. A primary goal is to maximize the proportion of redox-active TMs to increase specific capacity. Moreover, engineering the host structure to withstand the demanding structural changes during high-rate cycling offers a route to enhanced kinetics and stability, a principle effectively demonstrated in advanced oxide materials8,9. However, the rigid three-dimensional framework of conventional PBAs, with its fixed alternating arrangement of TMs and cyanide ligands, constrains such structural modification.

Vacancy engineering offers a promising route for targeted structural adjustments in PBAs. The introduction of vacancies to alter local coordination environments has proven effective for optimizing PBA-based positive electrodes. For instance, creating Mn vacancies in manganese hexacyanoferrate (MnHCF) improved Na+ storage capability10, while anion vacancies accelerated K+ kinetics11. However, inducing a global structural transformation, rather than only local adjustments, necessitates a substantially higher vacancy concentration. While fundamental studies have revealed that such high concentrations of vacancies can form diverse, non-random networks rather than being randomly distributed12, the deliberate synthesis of these highly defective frameworks and their subsequent application remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we developed a layered manganese hexacyanoferrate (AL-MnHCF), founded on the structural adjustment of a precursor via vacancy engineering. Initially, an indirect reduction strategy was proposed to create anion vacancies up to a stoichiometric fraction of 1/2 in MnHCF, yielding a precursor denoted as Mn[Fe]1/2. This adjustment enabled a longer-range micropore correlation, which was demonstrated to improve the kinetics of coordinated H2O removal without causing structural collapse. Subsequently, a thermally-driven phase transition converted this precursor into AL-MnHCF, a material suitable for application as a conversion-based lithium-ion battery (LIB) negative electrode. The layered structure of AL-MnHCF was verified by comprehensive characterization techniques, including spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy (Cs-corrected TEM) and synchrotron X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), in conjunction with theoretical calculations. The high proportion of TM elements in AL-MnHCF contributes to a high specific capacity of 809 mAh g−1. The electrochemical reaction mechanism was investigated using a combination of in-situ and ex-situ techniques, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), revealing an initial c-to-a transformation followed by subsequent conversion processes. Furthermore, AL-MnHCF exhibits a dynamically reversible component migration, which facilitates charge transfer and enhances reaction kinetics. As a result, the material delivers high rate performance (510 mAhg−1 at 8 A g−1) and shows high cyclic stability, with operation exceeding 8000 hours under a deep charge-discharge test at 50 mA g−1.

Results

Achieving a high vacancy fraction in MnHCF

In PBAs, adherence to the oxidation-state-sum rule is crucial12. For instance, in MnIII[FeIII(CN)6], the oxidation states of the transition metals sum to six. Consequently, when the framework contains transition metals in lower oxidation states, such as +2, an additional charge compensation mechanism is required. This is typically achieved by incorporating interstitial cations, most commonly K+, for which the PBA lattice has a strong affinity3. As a result, the direct reaction between Mn2+ and [Fe(CN)6]4- typically yields K2MnII[FeII(CN)6] (hereafter Mn[Fe]). Alternatively, charge balance can be achieved by forming [Fe(CN)6] vacancies (denoted as □), leading to a theoretical product of MnII[FeII(CN)6]1/2□1/2 (hereafter Mn[Fe]1/2). However, the synthesis of this vacancy-rich phase is challenging because conventional reactants, such as potassium ferrocyanide, introduce alkali metal ions that are readily incorporated into the framework.

To overcome this limitation, we developed an indirect reduction strategy (Fig. 1a). First, we replaced potassium ferrocyanide (containing Fe2+) with potassium ferricyanide (containing Fe3+), as the lower anionic charge of the [Fe(CN)6]3− complex reduces the tendency for K+ insertion. The experimental results indicated that this initial step favors a vacancy-based charge compensation, yielding MnII[FeIII(CN)6]2/3□1/3 (hereafter Mn[Fe]2/3). Subsequently, the obtained Mn[Fe]2/3 was dispersed in an alkali-ion-free aqueous solution, and a reducing agent (ascorbic acid) was introduced. This process reduced Fe3+ to Fe2+, which necessitated the formation of additional vacancies to satisfy the oxidation-state-sum rule, ultimately forming the target Mn[Fe]1/2. This strategy effectively circumvents the undesired insertion of interstitial cations, enabling the synthesis of a PBA framework compensated solely by vacancies, even when both transition metals are in the +2 oxidation state.

Experimental validation supported the viability of the proposed strategy. XPS was employed to monitor the change in TM oxidation states (Fig. 1b, left). The fitting results of Mn 2p3/2 and Fe 2p3/2 spectra of Mn[Fe] were consistent with an oxidation state of +2 for both Mn and Fe13. As for Mn[Fe]2/3, the Mn 2p3/2 spectra remained unchanged, while the Fe 2p3/2 for Mn[Fe]2/3 was located at a higher binding energy, corresponding to Fe3+. After the reduction process, the oxidation state of Fe was reduced to +2 in Mn[Fe]1/2. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) results, depicted in Fig. 1b, right and Supplementary Table S1, revealed a low potassium content in Mn[Fe]2/3 and Mn[Fe]1/2, indicating that charge compensation in both materials occurs primarily through vacancy formation. The corresponding vacancy fractions were determined to be 0.32 and 0.48 for Mn[Fe]2/3 and Mn[Fe]1/2, respectively, closely aligning with the theoretical values of 0.33 and 0.50. The XRD patterns (Fig. 1c) show a phase change from monoclinic in Mn[Fe] to cubic in Mn[Fe]2/3. This is due to the lattice expansion resulting from the removal of K+ and the introduction of vacancies. For Mn[Fe]1/2, a new low-angle peak appears at around 14.1°, indicating a larger interplanar spacing caused by its higher vacancy content.

The Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) and Raman spectra suggested diverse geometries around the Mn site in Mn[Fe]1/2 (Supplementary Fig. 1–3), with details provided in the Supporting Information. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of as-prepared Mn[Fe]1/2 displays irregular, micrometre-sized particles (Supplementary Fig. 4). When Mn[Fe]2/3 of a different particle size was used as the precursor, the final product exhibited a similar morphology (Supplementary Fig. 5), suggesting a structural reconstruction occurs during the reduction process. This is likely caused by the partial dissolution and redeposition of [Fe(CN)6]4-, which can induce the fusion of smaller particles to reduce surface energy.

As the vacancy fraction increased, significant differences in the H2O types and dehydration kinetics were observed, which were analyzed using thermogravimetric analysis-differential scanning calorimetry (TGA-DSC) and variable temperature diffuse reflection infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (VT-DRIFTS) (Fig. 1d–f). For Mn[Fe], only a small amount of surface-adsorbed H2O was detected (Supplementary Fig. 1), corresponding to the minor mass loss and weak endothermic peak in the TGA-DSC curves. For Mn[Fe]2/3 (Fig. 1e), two distinct H-O-H bending modes were observed, with absorption peaks at 1675 cm−1 and 1616 cm−1. The O-H stretching mode also exhibited two types, with broad stretching bands in the range of 3590–3000 cm−1 and a narrow band at ~3650 cm−1. These results indicated the simultaneous presence of zeolitic and coordinated H2O in Mn[Fe]2/37. For Mn[Fe]1/2 (Fig. 1f), only coordinated H2O was observed, characterized by two narrow O-H stretching bands at ~3620 cm−1 and ~3540 cm−1, along with a single H-O-H bending vibration peak at 1606 cm−1. The presence of two types of coordinated H2O can be attributed to the diverse chemical environments of Mn[Fe]1/2, as discussed in Supplementary Fig. 1–3. As the temperature increased, Mn[Fe]2/3 underwent the removal of zeolitic H2O below ~110 °C, accompanied by a rapid diminution of the corresponding absorption peaks. In the same temperature range, Mn[Fe]1/2 exhibited minor removal of coordinated H2O. When the temperature exceeded ~110 °C, the coordinated H2O in Mn[Fe]2/3 was gradually removed, with its corresponding absorption peaks decreasing slowly and persisting across a wide temperature range (Fig. 1e). In contrast, above ~110 °C, coordinated H2O in Mn[Fe]1/2 underwent rapid removal, with a sharp decrease in the corresponding absorption peaks, demonstrating more favorable dehydration kinetics (Fig. 1f). The coordinated H2O content was further calculated from the TGA-DSC curves (Fig. 1d), with details provided in Supplementary Note 1. Combining ICP and organic element analyzer (OEA) results (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2), the chemical composition of the three samples was confirmed, as shown in Supplementary Table S3.

The difference in H2O types and dehydration kinetics is strongly correlated with the distinct micropore network in these materials (Fig. 1d, right). For Mn[Fe], the micropores are smallest and occupied by K+, resulting in minimal lattice H2O. With the generation of vacancies, the cavity spaces expand, providing sites for lattice H2O, including coordinated and zeolitic H2O. However, as the vacancy fraction further increases, the expanded cavity spaces and enhanced micropore connectivity weaken the framework’s constraint on lattice H2O. This weakened constraint leads to the disappearance of zeolitic H2O and improved dehydration kinetics for coordinated H2O, as observed in Mn[Fe]1/2.

Additionally, Mn[Fe]1/2 exhibited greater structural stability during the dehydration process. As shown in Fig. 1e, the removal of coordinated H2O from Mn[Fe]2/3 induced significant changes in the -CN- stretching region. Notably, new peaks emerged and broadened (highlighted by yellow rectangles), indicating a disruption of the -CN- coordination environment. This observation was corroborated by XRD analysis, which revealed a loss of crystallinity for Mn[Fe]2/3 after heating to 190 °C (Supplementary Fig. 6). In contrast, the -CN- stretching region for Mn[Fe]1/2 remained largely unchanged, with no new peaks appearing (Fig. 1f). Instead, only a slight redshift of the original peaks was observed, accompanied by an increase in the intensity of certain peaks. This result suggests that the -CN- coordination environment remains stable. Therefore, Mn[Fe]1/2 can undergo further structural modification through thermal activation.

Synthesis and characterization of AL-MnHCF

A distinct phase transition was observed during high-temperature vacuum thermal treatment of Mn[Fe]1/2. The resulting material is termed AL-MnHCF based on its structural characteristics, which will be discussed in detail below. Figure 2a shows the crystal structure evolution of Mn[Fe]1/2 as a function of temperature. The crystal structure was stable below 130 °C, suggesting that the decomposition of coordinated water did not result in structural collapse. In the temperature range of 130–180 °C, Mn[Fe]1/2 underwent a phase transition, accompanied by significant changes in the diffraction peaks (Fig. 2a). When the temperature exceeded 180 °C, the XRD patterns remained constant, indicating the completion of the new phase formation. Therefore, 190 °C was identified as a suitable temperature for obtaining this new phase. Such phenomena were absent in both Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]2/3. Mn[Fe] retained its initial structure at this temperature (Supplementary Fig. 7), while Mn[Fe]2/3 became amorphous due to structural collapse during dehydration (Supplementary Fig. 6). In the DSC curve (Fig. 1d), an exothermic peak observed at a high temperature of around 270 °C for Mn[Fe]2/3 is likely associated with the re-bonding of the collapsed structure, indicating the difficulty of its structural reconstruction. These results highlight the specific role of the high-vacancy Mn[Fe]1/2 precursor in enabling thermally-driven phase transitions.

The as-prepared AL-MnHCF exhibited a layered morphology with good crystallinity (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 8). XPS analysis confirmed that both Mn and Fe maintained a + 2 valence state in AL-MnHCF (Supplementary Fig. 9). After the phase transition, the chemical environment of -CN- in AL-MnHCF became more homogeneous compared to Mn[Fe]1/2 (Supplementary Fig. 10), and the coordinated H2O was almost completely removed (Supplementary Fig. 11). The ICP analysis revealed an Mn: Fe molar ratio of 1:0.48 for AL-MnHCF (Supplementary Table S1). Combining TGA and OEA results, the chemical formula of AL-MnHCF was estimated as Mn[Fe(CN)6]0.48.

The Rietveld refinement based on the XRD pattern was used to determine the possible crystal structure. Initially, a triclinic model with space group P1¯ was employed, where Fe atoms occupy the 1a sites and Mn atoms occupy the 2i sites, maintaining an Mn: Fe atomic ratio of 2: 1. The fitting result agrees well with the observed XRD patterns (Fig. 2c). The refined lattice and structure parameters are listed in Supplementary Table S4. The simulated crystal structure exhibits a layered stacking mode at the atomic scale, consisting of alternating layers of FeC6 and MnN6 octahedra. (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 12). Layers of FeC6 octahedra are connected with distorted, edge-sharing MnN6 octahedra by cyanide bridges. To further validate the fitting results, Cs-corrected TEM was used to probe the atomic arrangement. The positions of Mn and Fe were observed as bright dots in the high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images, as shown in Fig. 2f−h, which are consistent with the atomic arrangement of the simulated crystal structure in various orientations. In addition, integrated differential phase contrast (iDPC)-STEM, capable of imaging light and heavy elements together, was used. The iDPC-STEM image (the inset in Fig. 2f) in the [201] orientation shows that Fe dots are surrounded by less intense signals from C and N atoms. However, the area between the adjacent Mn atoms is dark, consistent with the edge-sharing characteristic of the MnN6 octahedra. Furthermore, first-principles calculations were conducted. The simulated XRD pattern, based on the geometrically optimized model, is in good agreement with the experimental data (Supplementary Fig. 13a). The calculated formation energy of AL-MnHCF is −6.86 eV/formula, even lower than −6.61 eV/formula of Mn[Fe], indicating its thermodynamic stability (Fig. 2d). The density of states (DOS) results show that AL-MnHCF has a smaller band gap (3.72 eV versus 4.20 eV), which would be favorable for electronic conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 13b)1.

XAS was utilized to further investigate the local coordination environment of TMs in AL-MnHCF. Fe atoms exhibited similar local coordination environments in the three samples (Mn[Fe], Mn[Fe]1/2, and AL-MnHCF), all maintaining a valance state of +2 (Supplementary Fig. 14). For Mn atoms, the Mn K-edge white lines in all samples are at around 6553.5 eV (Supplementary Fig. 15), indicating a valance state of +214. The Mn Fourier-transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT EXAFS) spectra varied significantly (Fig. 2i), indicating changes in the local coordination environments of Mn atoms among the samples. The highest intensity peak at around 1.6 Å can be related to Mn-N14. To assign the other peaks, wavelet transform (WT) EXAFS analysis was conducted (Fig. 2j). In WT EXAFS, heavy atoms typically localize at higher frequencies in k-space than lighter atoms due to their larger backscattering factors F(k)15. Mn[Fe] is composed of alternating FeC6 and MnN6 octahedra with K+ ions in interstitial spaces (Fig. 2k, upper)12. The peaks at around 2.5 Å, 3.0 Å, and 4.5 Å in Mn[Fe] were thus attributed to Mn-K, Mn-C, and Mn-Fe respectively (Fig. 2j, left). Mn[Fe]1/2 exhibits similar radial distances for Mn-C and Mn-Fe as Mn[Fe]. Considering the absence of K atoms in Mn[Fe]1/2, the peaks at around 2.5 Å was attributed to Mn-O (Fig. 2j, center). Different from Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]1/2, the Mn-Fe resolved lobe is located at (7 Å−1; 4 Å) in AL-MnHCF (Fig. 2j, right), indicating a closer radial distance. In addition, a new resolved lobe located at (8 Å−1; 3 Å) was observed, which is absent in Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]1/2. The higher k-value of 8 Å−1 can be attributed to scattering from neighboring Mn atoms. Therefore, for AL-MnHCF, the MnN6 units connect not only with FeC6 units via cyanide bridges but also with adjacent MnN6 units by edge sharing, as depicted in Fig. 2k.

High-rate performance and cycling stability

When PBAs are used as conversion-type negative electrodes for LIBs, the content of TMs, serving as redox-active centers, directly affects their specific capacity. We found that the mass concentration of TMs (mTM) is theoretically positively correlated with the vacancy fraction in MnHCF (Supplementary Fig. 16), with the detailed calculation provided in Supplementary Note 2. When Fe is in the +3 and +2 valance states, the saturated vacancy fractions can reach 1/3 and 1/2, respectively, corresponding to the maximum theoretical mTM values of 8.49 and 9.32 mmol/g in the absence of coordinated water. However, for conventional PBAs with a retained framework structure, an increased vacancy fraction inevitably leads to a lower crystal density (ρ), which is detrimental to the overall energy storage capability. AL-MnHCF overcomes this trade-off through a high-temperature structural transformation. Its crystal density was determined to be 2.54 g/cm3 from Rietveld refinement of the XRD data. This value is even higher than that of Mn[Fe] (2.23 g/cm3), which contains the non-active K component (Fig. 3a)16.

The electrochemical performance of Mn[Fe], Mn[Fe]2/3, and AL-MnHCF was investigated. The charge-discharge curves are shown in Fig. 3b. The reversible specific capacity increased from 519 to 646 to 809 mAh g−1 in sequence. AL-MnHCF exhibited an average charge voltage of ~1.2 V vs. Li/Li+, alongside reduced voltage polarization compared to Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]2/3. Figure 3c and Supplementary Fig. 17a-c compare the rate performance of three samples. At a specific current of 1600 mA g−1, Mn[Fe] delivered a specific capacity of 140 mAh g−1, corresponding to a capacity retention of 27.0% relative to its capacity at 50 mA g−1. Under the same conditions, Mn[Fe]2/3 exhibited an improved capacity retention of 35.9%. In contrast, the reversible specific capacity of AL-MnHCF was maintained at 699 mAh g−1 at 1600 mA g−1. At a higher specific current of 8 A g−1, it delivered a specific capacity of 510 mAh g−1. This rate performance is competitive with that of previously reported PBA-based negative electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Table S6). Given the relatively high content of Super P (SP) utilized in this study to ensure an efficient conductive network for high-rate capability, we quantified its potential capacity contribution. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, the SP additive introduces a specific capacity discrepancy that reaches a maximum of ~60 mAh g−1 at low specific currents, but this value diminishes to less than 10 mAh g−1 at 4 A g−1.

To understand the high-rate capability of AL-MnHCF, the kinetics properties of AL-MnHCF and Mn[Fe]2/3 were analyzed through the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves at different scan rates. A couple of redox peaks can be observed in the CV curves at 0.1 mV/s (Fig. 3d), corresponding to the lithiation/delithiation process. The voltage polarization (△E) for AL-MnHCF is only 116 mV, much smaller than that of Mn[Fe]2/3 (474 mV). For fast-charging negative electrode materials such as some titanium- and niobium-based oxides, intercalation pseudocapacitive behavior is a primary contributor to the high-rate characteristics17. The high capacitive contribution calculated for AL-MnHCF is consistent with the material’s high-rate capability for fast Li+ storage (Supplementary Fig. 20 and 21). The Li-ion diffusion coefficient (DLi) was further evaluated by the relationship between scan rate and peak current from the Randles-Sevcik equation (Supplementary Fig. 22). The calculated slope values for AL-MnHCF are much higher than those for Mn[Fe]2/3, which favors a faster Li+ transport.

The cyclic stability of AL-MnHCF was further evaluated. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 17d, AL-MnHCF underwent a fast capacity increase at 500 mA g−1, reaching a reversible specific capacity of around 840 mAh g−1 and maintaining stability after 200 cycles. In contrast, the specific capacity of Mn[Fe]2/3 was only 291 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 500 mA g−1. A deep charging and discharging test of AL-MnHCF at 50 mA g−1 was also conducted (Fig. 3e). To demonstrate the fast reaction kinetics of Al-MnHCF, the cell was initially cycled at a high specific current of 1 A g−1. The initial specific capacity reached 707 mAh g−1 immediately. When cycling at 50 mA g−1, AL-MnHCF operated stably for around 8000 hours. We observed that when the specific current was switched from 1 A g−1 to 50 mA g−1, the coulombic efficiency (CE) in that cycle exceeded 100%. This phenomenon is primarily due to the more rapid discharge kinetics compared to the charge kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 23). Furthermore, AL-MnHCF also exhibited long-term cyclic stability at 2 A g−1, with a reversible specific capacity of 686 mAh g−1 after 2000 cycles (Fig. 3f). This combination of specific capacity and rate performance is comparable with a range of reported negative electrode materials for LIBs (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table S7). Compared to the traditional carbon-based negative electrodes, AL-MnHCF shows a higher rate capability. In comparison to fast-charging negative electrodes like oxide materials, AL-MnHCF has a greater advantage in specific capacity. These performance metrics, combined with its low cost and simple synthesis, highlight a promising direction for the development of PBA-based negative electrodes.

Full cell performance evaluation

The electrochemical performance of AL-MnHCF was further evaluated in full cells. We assembled full cells with both LiNi0.5Mn0.3Co0.2O2 (NCM532) and LiCoO2 (LCO) as the positive electrodes to verify the compatibility of AL-MnHCF. In these assemblies, AL-MnHCF was employed as both the sole active material and a component in the AL-MnHCF/graphite hybrid negative electrode (Fig. 4a).

The galvanostatic charge-discharge curves of AL-MnHCF | |NCM532 and Graphite | |NCM532 at different specific currents are shown in Fig. 4b. Owning to the high-rate capability of AL-MnHCF, the reversible specific capacity of AL-MnHCF | |NCM532 remained 120 mAh g−1 at 800 mA g−1, with a capacity retention of 78.5% relative to the specific capacity at 100 mA g−1 (Fig. 4c). Limited by the slow Li+ intercalation kinetics of graphite, the voltage polarization increased rapidly with the increase of specific current for the Graphite | |NCM532 (Fig. 4d). The reversible specific capacity was only 56 mAh g−1 at 800 mA g−1 (39.1% relative to the specific capacity at 100 mA g−1). When the specific current was returned to 100 mA g−1, the reversible specific capacity of Graphite | |NCM532 did not fully recover, reaching only 129 mAh g−1, and the cell exhibited capacity degradation with a capacity retention of 46.6% after 120 cycles (Fig. 4b). In contrast, the reversible specific capacity of AL-MnHCF | |NCM532 immediately recovered to its initial level. The capacity retention was 99.7% after 120 cycles, indicating high cyclic stability. As shown in Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 24, the specific energy of the two full cells at different specific currents was evaluated. The results underscore the advantages of AL-MnHCF as a high-rate negative electrode material.

To utilize the fast charging characteristics of AL-MnHCF and to expand its potential applications, we developed an AL-MnHCF/graphite hybrid electrode, with the preparation processes described in the “Methods” section. Supplementary Fig. 25 displays the XRD and SEM images of the electrode material. The AL-MnHCF/graphite hybrid negative electrode demonstrated an improved rate capability compared to the graphite-only electrode (Supplementary Fig. 26) and exhibited consistent voltage behavior even at a high mass loading of 7 mg cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 27). The corresponding full cell was assembled using LCO as the positive electrode, and a constant current-constant voltage (CC-CV) charging protocol was employed (Supplementary Fig. 28). The full cell demonstrated an areal capacity of around 1.4 mAh cm−2. The charge capacity in the CC charging stage was 0.4 mAh cm−2 at a current density of 8.5 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4f). As shown in Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 29, the full cell required only 8.2 and 17 min to reach 60 and 80% state of charge (SOC), respectively, at 8.5 mA cm−2. The cyclic performance is shown in Fig. 4h. The capacity retention was 95.7% after 60 cycles at 8.5 mA cm−2 (CC charging stage), with an average CE exceeding 99%, demonstrating high stability under fast-charging conditions.

Reaction mechanism

So far, the reaction mechanism of PBAs as conversion-type negative electrode materials for LIBs remains unclear. Here, we employed in-situ XRD and in-situ Raman spectroscopy to probe the structural changes of the material during the initial discharge process. As shown in Fig. 5a, AL-MnHCF underwent a structural transformation from crystalline to amorphous. The major peaks at around 22.1° gradually disappeared at the end of the voltage plateau. In subsequent cycles, AL-MnHCF remained an amorphous structure (Fig. 5b), consistent with previous reports on PBAs negative electrode18. In-situ Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 5c) revealed the disappearance of the -C ≡ N- signal, indicating that -C ≡ N- is involved in the initial reaction. Raman mapping images demonstrated the uniformity of this reactions (Supplementary Fig. 30). After the initial discharge, the TEM analysis showed that the particles evolved into a hollow morphology, suggesting component migration during the discharge process (Fig. 5d, upper). The corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern displayed a diffuse profile, with only two weak diffraction rings observed. This indicates that the crystalline regions within the discharged products are extremely small. These weak diffraction rings likely correspond to Li2O and Fe/Mn metals. The energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) maps are shown in Supplementary Fig. 31. The O element came from the electrolyte solvent, which participated in the initial c-to-a transformation.

In each region, the five plots shown (from bottom to top) correspond to the pristine, first discharge, first charge, second discharge, and second charge states of the AL-MnHCF electrodes, respectively.

The discharge products were further analyzed through XPS depth profiling (Supplementary Fig. 32). The elemental signals of Fe and Mn were detected after a sputtering time of 20 min (Supplementary Fig. 32a and b). Both Fe 2p3/2 and Mn 2p3/2 peak shapes exhibited asymmetry due to the multiple splitting. The Fe 2p3/2 was located at around 706 eV, with a splitting energy (ΔE) of 13.1 eV between Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2, indicating the generation of Fe0. Mn 2p3/2 peak was located at around 638 eV, with a ΔE of 11.2 eV, corresponding to Mn0. In the O 1 s region (Supplementary Fig. 32c), a low-intensity peak at around 530.5 eV can be related to O-C bonding. Another peak at a lower binding energy (around 528 eV) indicates the generation of metal oxide. According to the previous works19, this peak can be attributed to O-Li in Li2O, which is consistent with the SAED result. In the C 1 s region (Supplementary Fig. 32d), the intensity of the C-C/C-N peak decreased with sputtering time, while another peak at around 282.3 eV showed the opposite tendency. The peak at such low binding energy in C 1 s spectra is usually related to metal carbide, suggesting the strong interaction between the C atom and Li+ in the discharge products. Similarly, the low binding energy peak was also observed in the N 1 s region when the sputtering time reached 10 min (Supplementary Fig. 32e). Li 1 s XPS spectra were in good agreement with the above results (Supplementary Fig. 32f). Multiple chemical states of Li element were determined, including oxides and ‘Li+-Organic’, where C and N atoms show a strong interaction with Li+. The specific formula of ‘Li+-Organic’ remains unclear, requiring further characterization in the future.

The XPS depth profiling of the full-charge electrode was conducted (Supplementary Fig. 33), revealing increased valence states of Fe and Mn, corresponding to transition metal oxides. Compared to the full-discharge state, TM signals in the full-charge state displayed a wider distribution as the sputtering goes on. Additionally, the Li signal was nearly absent, with only a small amount present on the surface, attributed to the solid electrolyte interphase components. Figure 5e and f compare the species distribution differences between the fully discharged and fully charged states, confirming the dynamic migration of components throughout the entire electrochemical process. TEM analysis revealed that after charging, the particles reverted to a solid morphology with an amorphous structure (Fig. 5d, bottom). The corresponding elemental maps are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 34. Only some local crystal areas were observed in the HRTEM image (Supplementary Fig. 35). The measured lattice spacings of 0.22 and 0.26 nm correspond to the (200) and (111) planes of MnO, and the lattice spacing of 0.30 nm corresponds to the (220) planes of Fe3O4, which is consistent with the XPS results.

Finally, ex-situ XPS analysis demonstrates the reversibility of the aforementioned reactions (Fig. 5g). Combining the observations above, the reaction mechanism can be summarized as follows: (1) The pristine structure undergoes the c-to-a transformation during the initial discharge. The generated nanocrystalline domains serve as the active material for further reversible reactions. (2) TMs are the primary redox active sites, undergoing a conversion reaction from their metallic state to an oxide during charging, and the reverse process upon discharging. This redox activity significantly contributes to the observed high specific capacity (Supplementary Fig. 36). (3) During discharging, the inserted Li+ exists in two forms: Li2O and ‘Li+-organic’, where Li+ interacts with C and N atoms. (4) AL-MnHCF exhibits significant component migration throughout the entire electrochemical process.

Origin of enhanced reaction kinetics

In-situ XRD results show that Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]2/3 also underwent c-to-a transformations during the initial discharge (Supplementary Fig. 37). However, galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) measurements reveal significant differences in the thermodynamic voltages (electromotive force, EMF) of the c-to-a transformations among the three materials (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 38), suggesting that they followed distinct transformation pathways due to their different initial structures.

The corresponding 2D radial profiles are shown in the upper panels. f, g Structural evolution of Mn[Fe] (f) and AL-MnHCF (g) during cycling, including HAADF images at different SOCs and the corresponding schematic of reaction process.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to investigate the potential influence of initial structure on the c-to-a transformation process. The charge density was calculated for Mn[Fe] and AL-MnHCF (Supplementary Fig. 39), alongside Bader charge analysis for each atom, as shown in Supplementary Table S8. The N atoms, possessing the most negative charge values in both materials, ensure a preferential interaction with Li+ at the surface. Compared to the conventional Mn[Fe] materials, AL-MnHCF exhibits a higher electron enrichment at the N sites, leading to a stronger interaction ability with Li+, consistent with the calculated surface adsorption energy of Li+ (Fig. 6b). A stable binding of Li+ to the N atoms on the surface of AL-MnHCF is observed, characterized by a favorable adsorption energy of -0.445 eV. Conversely, a positive adsorption energy of Li+ (0.291 eV) is evident for Mn[Fe], indicating a considerable challenge for Li+ to persist on the material surface, consequently hindering the c-to-a transformation. Nevertheless, upon vacancy formation, the Li+ adsorption energy on Mn[Fe] decreases to −2.678 eV. Moreover, the adsorption energy can reach further to −4.254 eV when Li+ interacts with the vacancy. This result indicates that, for Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]2/3, surface vacancies may serve as the initiating sites for the c-to-a transformation. Therefore, two distinct c-to-a transformation mechanisms were proposed. AL-MnHCF follows an interface-dominated transformation process, while the other two materials follow a vacancy-assisted transformation process (Fig. 6c).

In-situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were employed to verify the differences in interface reaction activity during the c-to-a transformation for these samples. The discharge curves obtained during in-situ EIS testing, along with the corresponding Nyquist plots, are presented in Supplementary Fig. 40. Additionally, the distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis was applied to decouple the electrochemical processes at the interface governing the EIS spectra in the mid-to-high frequency range, as shown in Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 41. The peak at around 10−5 s can be attributed to the contact resistance (Rc), whereas the peaks at around 10−4-10−2 can be assigned to the Li+ transport across the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer (RSEI)20,21. The peaks at around 10−2-101 are associated with charge transfer processes (Rct)22.The evolution of the Rct peak in Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]2/3 is similar, but significantly different from that in AL-MnHCF, indicating two distinct transformation processes. Compared to Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]2/3, AL-MnHCF exhibits a larger Rct during the initial discharging, suggesting a higher interface reaction activity, which corresponds to an interface-dominated transformation. By integrating the DRT peaks, we obtained the values of Rct, RSEI, and Rc at different states of discharge (SODs), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 42a and b. Although Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]2/3 exhibit similar interface reaction processes, there are significant differences in their RSEI and Rc values. This difference is likely attributed to the reductive decomposition of coordinated water within the Mn[Fe]2/3, which could form passivating species and disrupts electrode integrity, thereby accounting for the observed increases in both RSEI and Rc (as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 42c).

Furthermore, the subsequent conversion reaction kinetics of AL-MnHCF and Mn[Fe] during the charging process were analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 43 and Fig. 6e). The Rct and RSEI peaks of Mn[Fe] remain at relatively high values, with only a slight left shift in Rct peaks occurring in the later stages. In contrast, for AL-MnHCF, both RSEI and Rct decrease rapidly as charging progresses, accompanied by a significant left shift in the Rct peak. This behavior indicates a gradual enhancement in interfacial Li+ conductivity and conversion reaction kinetics. The Rct, RSEI, and Rc values at different SOCs are presented in Supplementary Fig. 44a. Additionally, the distribution of capacitive times (DCT) method was used to analyze the diffusion behaviors of the three materials (Supplementary Fig. 44b). AL-MnHCF shows a continuous increase in DCT peaks, indicating improved diffusion.

These differences can be attributed to the variations in the evolution of the particle structure during cycling. As discussed in the previous section, Al-MnHCF exhibited dynamic component migration, with the particles evolving between solid and hollow morphologies during the electrochemical process. However, For Mn[Fe] and Mn[Fe]2/3, which follow vacancy-assisted transformation, the evolutions of the particle structure are quite different. Although Mn[Fe]2/3 also forms a hollow structure after the initial discharge, the shell is non-uniform compared to AL-MnHCF, and the particles become chaotic after the subsequent charging (Supplementary Fig. 45). For Mn[Fe], the component migration is not only non-uniform but also limited, leading to the formation of numerous local cracks within the particles. During the subsequent charging process, the cracks further result in the particle degradation (Fig. 6f). In contrast, AL-MnHCF demonstrates sustainable component migration (Fig. 6g), enabling dynamic self-regulation during cycling. This enhances the formation of an efficient charge transfer network and promotes mass transport (Supplementary Fig. 44), thereby improving reaction kinetics over extended cycles.

In summary, we report a synthetic route to a layered AL-MnHCF material, formed through a high-temperature-induced phase transition of a vacancy-engineered precursor, Mn[Fe]1/2. This process highlights the critical role of vacancy control in adjusting the linkage configurations of PBAs. The as-prepared AL-MnHCF exhibits a high rate capability, delivering a specific capacity of 510 mAh g−1 at 8 A g−1, a performance level that is competitive with various reported negative electrode materials. Its reversible charge-discharge process was confirmed to proceed via a conversion reaction mechanism. The improved electrochemical performance of AL-MnHCF can be attributed to its distinct linkage configurations. The higher content of TMs within this structure, which serve as electrochemical active centers for the reversible conversion reaction, contributes to the high reversible capacity. Moreover, this layered structure is shown to facilitate the initial c-to-a transformation and promotes component migration, which enhances the kinetics of the subsequent conversion reaction. This work provides some support in advancing the structural design of PBAs, suggesting their considerable potential as high-performance negative electrode materials.

Methods

Materials

Manganese(II) sulfate monohydrate (MnSO4·H2O, Aladdin, purity: ≥99.0%), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid dipotassium salt dihydrate (EDTA-2K, Macklin, purity: ≥99.0%), potassium hexacyanoferrate(II) trihydrate (K4Fe(CN)6·3H2O, Macklin, purity: ≥99.0%), iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O, Macklin, purity: ≥99.0%), manganese(II) nitrate solution (Mn(NO3)2, 50 wt. % in H2O, Aladdin), potassium hexacyanoferrate(III) (K3Fe(CN)6, Macklin, purity: ≥99.5%), L-Ascorbic acid (VC, Aladdin, purity: ≥99.0%), and graphite (Shenzhen XFH Technology Co., Ltd.) were used as received without further purification. Deionized (DI) water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm was used in all experiments.

Synthesis of electrode materials

The synthesis of Mn[Fe] was based on a co-precipitation method where Solution A was prepared by dissolving 2 mmol of MnSO4·H2O and 5 mmol of EDTA-2K in 100 mL of DI water, and Solution B was prepared by dissolving 2 mmol of K4Fe(CN)6·3H2O in 100 mL of DI water. Subsequently, Solution A was added dropwise into Solution B under continuous magnetic stirring (800 rpm) at room temperature (25 °C) for 12 h. After aging for 2 h without stirring, the resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed with DI water three times, and finally dried in a vacuum oven at 100 °C for 24 h (~1.3 kPa). The Fe[Fe] was synthesized following the same procedure, with MnSO4·H2O being replaced by an equimolar amount of FeSO4·7H2O.

For the synthesis of Mn[Fe]2/3, two different manganese precursors were used to obtain different particle sizes. Solution A was prepared using either 3 mmol of MnSO4·H2O for smaller particles (~200 nm) or the equivalent molar amount of Mn(NO3)2 solution for larger particles (1–2 µm), while Solution B contained 2 mmol of K3Fe(CN)6. The solutions were mixed and the product was collected following the same procedure as for Mn[Fe].

To obtain Mn[Fe]1/2 via the indirect reduction method, the as-synthesized Mn[Fe]2/3 powder (obtained after washing but before the final drying step) was redispersed in 100 mL of DI water. A reducing solution, prepared by dissolving 2 mmol of VC in 100 mL of DI water, was then added dropwise into the dispersion with continuous magnetic stirring (800 rpm). The reaction was allowed to proceed for 12 h at room temperature in a sealed vessel. After further aging for 2 h, the product was collected by centrifugation and washed three times with DI water. The final Mn[Fe]1/2 product was then dried under vacuum at 100 °C for 12 h.

To prepare AL-MnHCF, the resulting Mn[Fe]1/2 powder was placed in an alumina crucible and annealed in a vacuum oven (~1.3 kPa) at 190 °C for 12 h. The AL-MnHCF/graphite hybrid was prepared by first dry-mixing the as-synthesized Mn[Fe]1/2 powder and graphite in a mass ratio of 3:2, and then annealing the mixture following the same thermal treatment procedure.

Structural characterizations

XRD patterns were collected with a Rigaku D/max 2500/PC diffractometer with Cu Kα irradiation at λ = 1.5406 Å. A custom-designed in-situ cell with a Beryllium (Be) window was used for the in-situ XRD measurements. The cell was assembled using a PBAs negative electrode with a mass loading of ~3 mg cm−2, coated on copper foam which served as the current collector. A lithium metal foil was used as the counter electrode. A glass fiber separator (ADVANTEC, GB-100R; thickness: 380 µm) was placed between the electrodes. To ensure complete wetting within the in-situ device, an excess volume of electrolyte (400 µL of 1.0 M LiPF6 in EC/DEC) was used. During assembly, the PBAs electrode was positioned to face the Be window to maximize signal intensity. Prior to the measurement, the assembled cell was allowed to rest at open-circuit voltage for 4 h. XRD patterns were collected continuously during galvanostatic cycling over a 2θ range of 10-45°. The scan speed was chosen as a compromise between achieving adequate XRD signal intensity and the time required for the initial discharge. The sample preparation process for ex-situ XRD is as follows: First, the cell at a specific electrochemical state was disassembled inside an argon-filled glovebox. The harvested electrode was rinsed with dimethyl carbonate (DMC) to remove residual electrolyte. After being thoroughly dried within the glovebox, the electrode was mounted onto a sample holder and sealed with Kapton tape to ensure complete isolation from air during the measurement. The chemical formulas were confirmed with the help of ICP (Arcos Ⅱ MV) and elemental analysis (CHNS/O). TGA-DSC curves were collected using a TGA/DSC 3+ Thermal Analysis System under N2 atmosphere with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. VT-DRIFTS measurements were performed using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer equipped with a Harrick Praying Mantis DRIFTS accessory and a High-Temperature Reaction Chamber (HVC-DRM-5) under an N2 atmosphere, with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. The chemical environments of the materials were determined by XAS at beamline 12-BM at Argonne National Laboratory. FT-IR spectra were collected using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer. Raman spectra were collected by a Raman spectrometer (Horiba LabRAM HR800). A custom-designed, airtight test cell (Gaossunion Technology Co., Ltd., material: high-purity titanium) was employed for the in-situ Raman measurements. This device was designed to house a modified CR2320 coin cell. A 6 mm diameter hole was drilled through the positive case of the coin cell, and this test cell was sealed with a transparent quartz window to provide optical access to the electrode surface. The working electrode had a mass loading of ~1.5 mg cm−2 and was coated on copper foam. A lithium metal foil served as the counter electrode, and a glass fiber separator (ADVANTEC, GB-100R; thickness: 380 µm) was used. The cell was filled with 300 µL of 1.0 M LiPF6 in EC/DEC electrolyte. The assembled cell was discharged in galvanostatic mode, and Raman spectra were collected at specific potential points during cycling. XPS spectra were collected using PHI Versaprobe 4. For ex-situ XPS analysis, the cell was disassembled in an argon-filled glovebox. The harvested electrode was rinsed with DMC, dried, and subsequently moved to the XPS system using an inert gas transfer holder to prevent air exposure. The depth analysis of the samples was conducted by XPS spectra with different sputtering times of 0, 5, 10, and 20 min, using an argon ion beam. The TEM images were obtained using a cold-field-emission spherical aberration corrected transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Spectra 300), which was operated at 80 kV. The morphologies of different samples were obtained by scanning electron microscope (SU8010, 5 kV). VESTA software was employed for visualizing the crystal structures23.

Electrochemical characterizations

The negative electrode slurry consisted of the active material, conductive carbon (KLD Co., Ltd.), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder (5130, KLD Co., Ltd.) in a weight ratio of 7:2:1. The positive electrode slurries, using LCO (KLD Co., Ltd.) or NCM532 (KLD Co., Ltd.) as the active material, consisted of the active material, conductive carbon, and PVDF in a weight ratio of 8:1:1. The respective components were first thoroughly mixed manually in a mortar with pestle to ensure homogeneity. This powder was then dispersed in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP, Aladdin, purity: ≥99.0%) and mixed using a magnetic stirrer for 4 hours to form a homogeneous slurry. The positive and negative electrode slurries were coated onto Al foil (Guangdong Jiayuan Technology Co., Ltd., purity: 99.7%, thickness: 20 µm) and Cu foil (Guangdong Jiayuan Technology Co., Ltd., purity: 99.8%, thickness: 12 µm), respectively, using a doctor blade. The coated sheets were dried in a vacuum oven at 100 °C overnight (~1.3 kPa), and cut into circular disks (diameter: 12 mm) using a precision punching tool. Finally, the cut electrodes were dried again under vacuum at 100 °C for 12 h before being transferred into an argon-filled glovebox.

All 2032 coin-type cells (case and spring components: 304 stainless steel) were assembled in an argon-filled glove box where H2O and O2 levels were maintained below 0.1 ppm. The electrolyte was 1.0 M LiPF6 (KLD Co., Ltd., purity: 99.9%, dried under vacuum at 90 °C for 12 h) dissolved in a mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC) and diethyl carbonate (DEC) (1:1 by volume) (KLD Co., Ltd., purity: 99.9%). 120 µL of electrolyte was added to each cell using a calibrated pipette with 200 μL tips. A glass fiber membrane (Whatman, GF/A; thickness: 260 μm, porosity: 40%, avg. pore size: 1.6 μm) and a trilayer polypropylene/polyethylene/polypropylene (PP/PE/PP) membrane (Celgard 2320; thickness: 20 μm, porosity: 39%, avg. pore size: 0.027 μm) were cut into 19 mm-diameter discs using an MSK-T10 manual slicer (Shenzhen Kejing) and used as the separators.

For half-cell assembly, pure Li metal foil (China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd., purity: ≥99.9%, thickness: 450 μm) was used as the counter and reference electrode. Prior to assembly, the Li foil surface was mechanically scraped to remove the native oxide layer and was used immediately. The mass loading of negative electrodes was around 1 mg cm−2 for half-cells. For full cells, the mass loading of the active material was controlled to achieve a negative-to-positive electrode capacity ratio (N/P ratio) of ~1.1. The mass loading for the AL-MnHCF/graphite hybrid was around 3 mg cm−2 or, for high-loading tests, around 7 mg cm−2.

All electrochemical measurements were performed on multiple parallel cells at 25 ± 1 °C in a temperature-controlled chamber (Wuhan Land). The galvanostatic charge/discharge tests were performed using a Land 2001A battery testing system. The half-cells were cycled within the voltage range of 0.01 to 3.0 V (vs. Li/Li+). The AL-MnHCF | |NCM532 and graphite | |NCM532 full cells were cycled between 0.7–4.3 V and 2.0–4.3 V, respectively. The AL-MnHCF/graphite | |LCO full cell was charged using a CC-CV protocol to 4.4 V, where the CV step was terminated when the current decayed to 0.02 mA. Subsequently, the cell was discharged at a CC mode to a cut-off voltage of 0.5 V. CV curves were obtained using a VMP-3 multichannel workstation within the potential window of 0.01 V to 3.0 V (vs. Li/Li⁺) at scan rates of 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mV s−1. GITT measurements were performed to determine thermodynamic voltages. The protocol consisted of a constant current pulse of 50 mA g−1 applied for 10 min, followed by a 1 h rest period to allow for voltage relaxation. Data points were acquired at 20-second intervals throughout the process. In-situ EIS was performed at various states of charge using an IVIUMnSTAT electrochemical analyser. To reach each target SOC, the cell was charged or discharged at a specific current of 50 mA g−1. Subsequently, the cell was allowed to equilibrate at open-circuit potential for 5 min before the EIS measurement was performed. A potentiostatic perturbation with an amplitude of 10 mV was applied over a frequency range from 100 kHz to 10 mHz, with 50 data points overall. DRT deconvolution was carried out using the DRTtools online algorithm developed by Professor Francesco Ciucci’s team24. DCT deconvolution was conducted following the method provided by the same team25.

The specific current (Ispecific) and specific capacity (Qspecific) were calculated by normalizing the applied current (Iapplied) and measured capacity (Qmeasured), respectively, to the mass of the active material (mactive). For half-cells, mactive refers to the mass of the active material in the working electrode, while for full-cells, calculations were normalized to the mass of the active material in the positive electrode. The specific energy of the full cell was calculated from the total discharged energy (Ecell). Two distinct values were determined based on different mass normalizations. First, the specific energy based on active materials (Especific, active) was calculated by dividing Ecell by the sum of the active material masses in the positive (mpositive, active) and negative (mnegative, active) electrodes. Second, a more practical specific energy (Especific, total) was calculated by normalizing Ecell to the total mass of all electrode components (active materials, Super P, and PVDF) in both the positive (mpositive, total) and negative (mnegative, total) electrodes. For area-normalized metrics, the current density (J, in mA cm−2) and areal capacity (Qareal, in mAh cm−2) were calculated by dividing the applied current and measured capacity by the geometric area of the electrode (Aelectrode = 1.13 cm2).

DFT calculations

The computational calculations were handled using the density functional theory (DFT) in Vienna ab-initio simulation package (VASP)26 with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functions of generalized gradient approximation (GGA)27. The projector augmented wave (PAW) method27 with a cutoff energy of 520 eV was used to describe the interaction between nuclei and electrons. The dipole correction and spin polarization were added for the calculations. The effective single parameters of U–J for Mn and Fe are 5.0 eV and 4.3 eV, respectively, according to the preview literature1. The convergence criteria for energy and force were set as 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV/Å, respectively. The Γ-centered k-point meshes of 3 × 3 × 1 were adopted for AL-MnHCF of (101¯) facet; while The Γ-centered k-point meshes of 2 × 3 × 1 were adopted for Mn[Fe] of (111) facet. The atom charge was evaluated with the Bader analysis28,29. The final atomic coordinates for all optimized structures reported in this work are provided in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Data 2.

The initial crystal structure of Mn[Fe] was adopted from the work of Leigang Xue and modeled in its standard crystallographic setting, P21/c (space group No. 14). Following geometry optimization, the lattice parameters for our calculated structure are a = 7.02397 Å, b = 7.42824 Å, c = 12.33901 Å, and β = 124.5816°. The simulated XRD pattern for this structure indicates that the most intense reflection corresponds to the (111) plane, and the second most intense to the (113¯) plane. We note that many studies on this material utilize the non-standard P21/n setting and consequently index the strongest diffraction peak as (211). This difference in indexing is a direct result of the transformation between the P21/c and P21/n settings.
