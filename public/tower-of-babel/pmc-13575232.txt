SpaDC enables sequence-based integrative analysis and regulatory inference of spatial chromatin accessibility data
Authors: Chuanlong Ma, Chenghui Yang, Caiwei Zhen, Zhentao He, Yong Luo, Lihua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spatial ATAC-seq enables simultaneous profiling of cellular locations and chromatin accessibility in intact tissues but faces challenges from high dimensionality, noise, and sparsity. Moreover, existing methods often overlook DNA sequence information, which contains critical regulatory motifs. To address these limitations, we introduce SpaDC, a graph-regularized convolutional neural network that integrates spatial location, chromatin accessibility, and DNA sequence. SpaDC employs a triplet loss function to integrate multiple spatial ATAC-seq datasets and remove batch effects. Benchmark analyses on real datasets demonstrate state-of-the-art performance in spatial domain identification, data denoising, and gene regulatory network (GRN) inference. Applied to mouse embryonic brain spatial ATAC-seq data, SpaDC accurately identified known brain structures and recovered chromatin accessibility signals. On P22 mouse brain spatial multi-omics data, SpaDC revealed spatial domain-specific cis-regulatory elements and GRNs. Collectively, SpaDC provides a powerful, sequence-based solution for spatial ATAC-seq analysis, enabling more accurate and robust investigation of tissue architecture and chromatin organization.

Introduction

Recent advances in spatial epigenomics1–4 have revolutionized our understanding of gene regulation in intact tissue architectures. Spatial epigenomic technologies such as spatial ATAC-seq integrate chromatin accessibility profiling with spatially resolved information. This integration enables the mapping of open chromatin regions across complex tissue landscapes, thereby bridging a critical gap between traditional single-cell epigenomic methods5–9, which lack spatial context, and the need to resolve regulatory elements within their native tissue microenvironment. Moreover, spatial multi-omics sequencing captures the relationship between chromatin accessibility and spatially resolved gene expression. This technique sheds light on the temporal and spatial regulatory dynamics within complex tissues10–12.

Despite its potential, spatial ATAC-seq data analysis remains challenging due to its high dimensionality, sparsity, and noise13,14. Many computational methods have been developed for analyzing single-cell ATAC-seq (scATAC-seq) data. A common task in scATAC-seq data analysis is dimensionality reduction, which transforms the data into a lower-dimensional space while preserving its essential information. Existing methods employ a variety of techniques to accomplish this task. For example, methods such as Signac15 and ArchR16 utilize Latent Semantic Indexing, a technique borrowed from natural language processing to reduce dimensionality. cisTopic17 is based on Latent Dirichlet Allocation and jointly optimizes the clustering of spots and regulatory regions. SnapATAC218 uses a nonlinear, matrix-free spectral embedding algorithm to obtain low-dimensional cell embeddings. SCALE19 and PeakVI20 employ variational autoencoders to model the peak-by-cell matrices. scBasset21 models data with sequence-based convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to generate low-dimensional embeddings. Although these methods effectively learn cell representations, they often overlook spatial location information, which is critical for analyzing ATAC data and capturing the spatial organization of biological tissues. This limitation primarily arises from the high sparsity and noise inherent in spatial ATAC-seq data. Moreover, these methods cannot integrate other omics features, such as histone modification signals, or fully leverage DNA sequence and gene expression data to dissect transcriptional regulatory relationships.

To address these limitations, we propose SpaDC, a model that integrates spatial location, DNA sequence, and chromatin accessibility to represent and denoise spatial epigenomic data. In SpaDC, DNA sequence data are encoded and input into a deep CNN to predict chromatin accessibility, while spatial coordinate information constrains the parameters of the final layer (i.e., the cell embeddings). Moreover, SpaDC adopts a triplet loss function to mitigate batch effects across different tissue slices. Additionally, SpaDC incorporates motifs from JASPAR along with corresponding spatial transcriptomics data to infer gene regulatory networks (GRNs). We applied SpaDC to multiple spatial epigenomic and spatial multi-omic datasets. SpaDC shows improved performance in identifying spatial domains compared with other methods. Moreover, SpaDC demonstrates outstanding performance in spatial epigenomic data denoising and spatially specific GRN inference. Overall, SpaDC is an efficient method for analyzing spatial epigenomics data.

Results and discussion

Overview of SpaDC

SpaDC is a comprehensive tool to analyze spatial genomics data. It applied to spatial ATAC-seq or spatial histone data or spatial transcriptomics and chromatin accessibility data (Fig. 1a). Specifically, it takes DNA fragments corresponding to peaks as inputs. For each peak, a 1344-base pair DNA fragment centered on the peak summit is defined, from which the corresponding DNA sequence is extracted and one-hot encoded. CNNs have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance for predicting epigenetic profiles in both bulk and single-cell data21. We adopt deep CNNs to extract peak embeddings. The embeddings are passed through a fully connected layer to predict chromatin accessibility or epigenomic signal with binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss (Fig. 1b). Moreover, SpaDC constructs a spatial neighbor graph based on spatial location to capture spatially aware spot embedding. Then the spot embedding is used for spatial domain identification (Fig. 1c). Meanwhile, the predicted values provide a denoised representation of the spatial epigenomics data. When multiple spatial epigenomic datasets are available, we apply the mutual nearest neighbor (MNN) method on the spot embeddings to detect anchors and adopt a triplet loss to align them. When spatial transcriptomics and epigenomics data are available, we use an efficient non-linear regression model XGBoost on the denoised chromatin accessibility data to identify functional cis-regulatory elements (CREs) for each gene. Specifically, an XGBoost model is trained to predict the expression of each gene using denoised accessibility signals of peaks located within ±250 kb of the target gene. Then, we use Shapley values to identify functional CREs of each spatial domain. Next, we focus on the DNA sequence of functional CREs to further characterize functional transcription factors (TFs). Specifically, we focus on known motifs of functional CREs from the JASPAR database. These motifs are shuffled within functional CRE regions, and the modified sequences are fed into the trained model. We estimate the activity strength of each TF at individual peak sites by comparing the changes in the predicted chromatin accessibility with background sequences. SpaDC accurately captures spatial structures of the mouse embryonic brain

We first applied SpaDC to spatial ATAC-seq data of the E15.5 mouse embryonic brain sequenced using MISAR-seq technology. A previous study identified 12 anatomical structures10 (Fig. 2a). We identified domain clusters on SpaDC spot embeddings using the Leiden algorithm22 and compared the results with Signac, ArchR, pycisTopic23, SnapATAC2, SCALEX24, PeakVI, and scBasset. We used Moran’s I—a quantitative metric for spatial domain identification that requires no labeled data—to assess each model’s performance (Fig. 2b). We further evaluated spatial domain quality using the spatial coherence score, another label-free metric that quantifies the spatial continuity of identified domains (Fig. 2c). For both metrics, higher scores indicate better performance in detecting spatial domains. SpaDC achieved the highest Moran’s I and spatial coherence scores among all methods. In addition, we evaluated clustering performance using the annotated labels with several external metrics, including ARI (adjusted Rand index), NMI (normalized mutual information), AMI (adjusted mutual information), and Homogeneity (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 1). SpaDC achieved the highest scores across all metrics, demonstrating superior clustering performance compared to other competing methods. Moreover, we converted the spatial ATAC-seq data into a gene activity matrix and applied SpaGCN25 SpaceFlow26, CCST27, GraphST28, STAGATE29, and conST30 on the gene activity matrix. Next, we visualized the spatial domains for each model (Fig. 2e). We annotated the SpaDC results with anatomical references. SpaDC successfully captured major structures, including 8-Muscle, 6-DPallm, 12-DPallv, 11-Thalamus, 4-Diencephalon, 1-Hindbrain, 2-Skull, 7-Midbrain, and 10-Cerebellar Vermis. In comparison, while all other methods could identify the prominent DPallm structure, they missed smaller structures—such as DPallv, Skull, and Cerebellar Vermis. scBasset failed to extract biological structures without spatial information constraint. We further identified domain-specific peaks and compared the denoised accessibility values with the original data (Fig. 2f). SpaDC accurately denoised and captured the spatial distribution of the domain-specific peaks of “Cerebellar vermis” (chr1-120602010-120602510), “DPallm” (chr12-6638859-6639359), “DPallv” (chr13-59971685-59972185) and “Thalamus” (chr5-131596826-131597326). In contrast, the original spatial pattern of chr1-120602010-120602510 is noisy.

To evaluate whether denoising leads to over-smoothing, we compared SpaDC with five representative methods, including PeakVI, scBasset, SCALE, and pycisTopic using four complementary metrics: variance shrinkage, diffusion proxy, structural smoothness, and correlation preservation. SpaDC achieved the best structural smoothness and correlation preservation while showing the lowest diffusion proxy, suggesting that the denoised signal is not excessively spread across large spatial regions (Supplementary Fig. 3a). SpaDC also exhibited the strongest agreement between raw and denoised data, indicating high consistency with original accessibility patterns (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Overall, SpaDC ranked among the top methods, confirming that it reduces noise while preserving local heterogeneity and spatial boundaries, rather than artificially homogenizing accessibility across entire domains.

To evaluate the performance of SpaDC under varying spatial resolutions, we constructed aggregated datasets by merging k = 3, 5, and 10 adjacent spots into a single pseudo-spot using the MISAR E15.5 spatial ATAC-seq dataset (Supplementary Figs. 4–6). Benchmarking results indicate that SpaDC maintains competitive performance across different resolutions and achieves superior results on most evaluation metrics. Notably, scBasset performs particularly well on the aggregated datasets, achieving a higher ARI score than SpaDC at the intermediate resolution (k = 5) and slightly outperforming SpaDC on the Homogeneity metric at the lowest resolution (k = 10). Overall, these results demonstrate that SpaDC is robust in identifying spatial domains under varying spot sizes.

We then applied SpaDC to integrate spatial ATAC-seq data of the mouse embryonic brain at different developmental stages (E15.5 and E18.5). We treated the anatomical structures as ground truth (Fig. 3a). There was a large batch effect between the different time points (Supplementary Fig. 7a). SnapATAC2, SCALE, and PeakVI achieved higher metric scores for batch effect removal than other methods (Supplementary Fig. 7b). However, these methods could not detect the corresponding Subpallium regions. SnapATAC2 failed to capture corresponding DPallv regions across the datasets from different time points (Fig. 3b). In contrast, SpaDC demonstrated strong performance in aligning major cell regions, such as Mesenchyme, DPallm, DPallv, and Subpallium. Moreover, we further introduced the nearest cell and cell-type consistency metric, following the evaluation framework proposed in the benchmark study31. Specifically, for each cell in one modality, we identify its nearest neighbor in the other modality within the integrated embedding space and assess whether they share the same cell type. This metric quantifies the proportion of nearest neighbors that preserve cell-type consistency across modalities, thereby reflecting the biological alignment quality after integration. SpaDC achieved the highest score among all compared methods (Supplementary Fig. 7c). We then quantitatively compared the clustering performance of Leiden on the embeddings from SpaDC and other methods using ARI, AMI, NMI, and Homogeneity metrics. SpaDC achieved the highest values across all four metrics compared with other methods (Fig. 3d).

To evaluate the role of DNA sequence embeddings, we designed a sequence-free ablation setting, termed SpaDC_no_seq, in which the batch-correction stage no longer relies on sequence-derived spot embeddings. Instead, chromatin accessibility is directly mapped to spot embeddings, and batch correction is performed using the same MNN pairing strategy as in SpaDC. Importantly, this ablation removes only the sequence component; both accessibility and spatial information are retained, and the spatial prior continues to constrain the spot embeddings in the same manner as in the original model. We evaluated SpaDC and SpaDC_no_seq on the MISAR E15.5 and E18.5 spatial ATAC-seq datasets by comparing their recovered spatial domains against ground-truth spatial annotations (Supplementary Fig. 8). The results show that SpaDC accurately captures the underlying spatial structures, whereas SpaDC_no_seq produces largely mixed and less coherent domain assignments. In addition, we assessed unsupervised clustering quality using Moran’s I, supervised clustering performance on the E15.5 labels using ARI, NMI, AMI, and Homogeneity, as well as the nearest-neighbor cell-type consistency metric, which quantifies the proportion of nearest neighbors that preserve cell-type consistency across modalities, thereby reflecting the biological alignment quality after integration. SpaDC consistently outperformed SpaDC_no_seq across all metrics, indicating that incorporating sequence information improves both spatial domain identification and cross-modal biological alignment.

SpaDC accurately detects brain structures and reveals spatial domain specific regulatory networks

We identified domain clusters on SpaDC spot embeddings using the Leiden algorithm and compared the results with Signac, ArchR, pycisTopic, SnapATAC2, SCALE, PeakVI, and scBasset. Using two complementary label-free metrics, SpaDC achieved the best performance, attaining the highest Moran’s I (Fig. 4b) and spatial coherence score (Fig. 4c). To further evaluate clustering performance, we incorporated the layer annotation labels from COSMOS32 to compute the clustering metrics (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 9). Consistently, SpaDC achieved the highest scores across all metrics, clearly outperforming other competing methods. We annotated the spatial domains of SpaDC according to the Allen Brain Atlas reference. Specifically, SpaDC successfully captured four distinct layers of ctx, whereas other methods identified fewer and noisier ctx layers. Although most methods detected the ccg layer, only SpaDC simultaneously captured smaller structures like lpo and aco, as well as the more difficult-to-detect acb and ndb layers. Other methods, such as SnapATAC2—which achieved the second-best Moran’s I score—captured structures like ccg, vl, ls, and cp, but, like the remaining methods, failed to capture the acb structure. Moreover, we converted the spatial ATAC-seq data into a gene activity matrix and applied SpaGCN, SpaceFlow, CCST, GraphST, STAGATE, and conST on the gene activity matrix. SpaDC had highest spatial coherence score, ARI, NMI, AMI and Homogeneity scores on the data (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Next, we identified domain-specific peaks in this dataset and visualized the spatial patterns of their accessibility values (Fig. 4f). We could faintly discern four structures: “cp” (chr18-83126754-83127607), “ctx” (chr9-70998671-70999544), “lpo” (chr8-23687043-23687913), and “vl” (chr14-25317719-25318626). Their raw accessibility values displayed relatively blurred spatial patterns, particularly for the marker peaks of lpo (chr8-23687043-23687913) and vl (chr14-25317719-25318626). In contrast, the denoised accessibility data obtained by SpaDC clearly captured the spatial patterns of each peak.

We focused on the spots annotated as the ctx region (Supplementary Fig. 11a) and implemented spatial deconvolution method Cell2location31 to obtain the spot–cell-type association matrix. We used Spearman correlation coefficients to quantify the concordance between the spatial accessibility patterns of marker peaks and the distribution of corresponding cell types. Specifically, for each cell type, we selected its top 50 marker peaks and computed the Spearman correlation between the predicted chromatin accessibility across spatial spots and the cell-type abundance profiles estimated by Cell2location. SpaDC achieved higher Spearman correlation coefficients than baseline methods for most cell types (Supplementary Fig. 11b).

We also applied SpaDC to integrate spatial multi-omics data (integrating ATAC and H3K27ac modalities) of P22 mouse brain. SpaDC could capture corresponding structures compared to anatomical annotation on the Allen Brain Atlas (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Moreover, SpaDC had highest Moran’s I score, ARI, NMI, AMI and Homogeneity scores on both modalities than other methods (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d).

We then identified markers of major spatial domains and detected their functional CREs using SpaDC (Fig. 5a). We compared SpaDC with a recently proposed cell-state specific CRE inference method SCARlink33. Specifically, we performed enrichment analysis of cell type-specific candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) from the SCREEN database (http://screen.encodeproject.org) using a hypergeometric test. We found that the spatial domain-specific functional CREs detected by SpaDC were more highly enriched in cCREs than those detected by SCARlink, with lower variance (Fig. 5b). Next, we focused on subclusters of ctx layers and identified marker genes and marker peaks using Wilcoxon test on spatial RNA-seq data and denoised spatial ATAC-seq data. The top marker genes did not show obvious differential expression patterns, whereas the top marker peaks exhibited significant chromatin accessibility patterns (Fig. 5c, d). We then computed the signals of the enhancer-like mark H3K4me1 in the marker peaks of ctx subclusters using independent paired-tag data of cerebral cortex (ctx) layers from a previous study34. We found that the 2-ctx subcluster was enriched with stronger signals from Layer 6 (L6), the 4-ctx subcluster with stronger signals from Layer 2/3 (L2/3), and the 9-ctx subcluster with signals from L4_Rorb (Fig. 5e). These results indicated that SpaDC could accurately capture heterogeneous cerebral cortex layers. Moreover, some functional CREs showed differentially accessible patterns across different clusters (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b). We then took the marker gene Cux2 as an example, whose cortical expression is restricted to neurons in the upper layers35. Finally, we applied SpaDC to identify TFs bound to the functional CREs and constructed a GRN by linking TFs to target genes (TG) via TF-to-CRE and CRE-to-TG connections. To investigate whether SpaDC captured the key TF-TG links, we validated them using the KnockTF 2.0 database, which contains causal evidence from TF perturbation experiments. We downloaded TF perturbation data from mouse brain tissue and identified two overlapping TFs (Foxo1 and Sox11) present in JASPAR 2024. We then evaluated the inferred TF-TG links of the main spatial domains by comparing the inferred scores with fold-changes obtained from perturbation experiments. We found that TF-TG links for Foxo1 had higher AUC values in the anterior cingulate cortical area (aca) and caudoputamen (cp), whereas Sox11 exhibited higher AUC values in the lateral ventricle (vl) (Fig. 6a). Although the differently expressed TFs of main domains did not show obvious differential patterns, Foxo1 was highly expressed in the cp domain, and Sox11 was highly enriched in the vl domain (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 13c). We then extracted the regulatory network of the vl domain, selecting TF-TG links with inferred scores greater than 5 (Fig. 6c). Sox11 is essential for both embryonic and adult neurogenesis36. Other SOX TFs, such as Sox6, also exhibited high activity scores in the vl domain and were important regulators of neuronal development37. Biological process enrichment analysis of these highly activated TFs demonstrated enrichment in neurogenesis (Fig. 6d). Conclusions

In this study, we present SpaDC, a computational method for analyzing spatial ATAC-seq data by integrating spatial location, DNA sequence, and chromatin accessibility. It learns spatially-aware spot embeddings from DNA sequence by a graph-regularized deep CNN. SpaDC demonstrates outstanding performance in spatial domain identification, data denoising, and batch effect removal. SpaDC also enables robust GRN inference, highlighting its potential in analyzing spatial genomics data.

The current SpaDC focuses on the DNA sequence and does not leverage the genetic variant information, which may provide effective information to uncover phonotype associated GRN. In the future, the model can be extended to leverage large amounts of available scATAC-seq data to improve the quality of spatial ATAC-seq prediction. With the increase of data scale, the computational approach should meet the basic requirement of efficiency and scalability. When handling long sequences and large datasets, model computational efficiency remains a challenge. Future work is expected to develop a foundational model that integrates long genomic sequences and other relevant information, and then transfer it to new datasets via pre-training and fine-tuning to improve computational efficiency.

Methods

Data preprocessing

The count matrices of spatial ATAC-seq or spatial histone modification data were binarized in this study. Moreover, we used the pybedtools package38,39 to detect overlapping peaks across multiple spatial epigenomics datasets and adopted the union of these regions as the new peaks. We used the Scanpy40 package to process the spatial transcriptome data. Genes expressed in fewer than 10 spots were filtered out, and the data were then normalized and log-transformed.

One-hot encoding of DNA sequences

We first extracted a fixed-length DNA sequence (default: 1344 base pair) from each peak by extending equally in both directions from its central position. Any positions extending beyond genome boundaries were filled with “N”. Next, the nucleotides in the extracted sequence were one-hot encoded. In this encoding scheme, “N” is represented as [0, 0, 0, 0]. Finally, the resulting sequence was transformed into a tensor of size 1344 × 4, which was then fed into a CNN to extract features.

Graph regularized convolutional neural network

Motivated by previous studies41–44, we used a CNN to predict chromatin accessibility from DNA sequences. The process begins with a 1D convolutional layer containing 288 filters, each of size 17 × 4. This is followed by batch normalization, Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation, and max-pooling layer, yielding an output matrix of size 448 × 288. The resulting matrix is then processed by a convolutional tower consisting of six layers. Each layer sequentially applies 1D convolution, batch normalization, ReLU activation, and max-pooling, with the number of convolution filters changing as follows: [288, 323, 363, 407, 456, 512], while the kernel width remains fixed at 5. The resulting 7 × 512 matrix is further processed by a 1D convolutional layer with 256 filters of width 1, producing a 7 × 256 matrix. This matrix is then flattened into a 1 × 1792 vector, which is subsequently fed into a bottleneck layer with 32 units to obtain a low-dimensional peak embedding of size 32. The peak embeddings are processed through the final linear layer (fully connected layer) followed by a sigmoid function to predict peak accessibility across NN spots. The learnable parameters of this linear layer are iteratively optimized by minimizing the BCE loss, which quantifies the difference between the predicted and observed peak accessibility. Benchmarking metrics

We evaluated clustering performance using both Moran’s I, which does not require labeled data, and other clustering evaluation metrics based on ground-truth labels. To assess batch correction performance after data integration, we used iLISI and the batch entropy mixing score.

Graph iLISI

The iLISI metric was used to assess batch mixing53. We calculated the integration LISI (iLISI) on the embedding features using the ilisi_graph function from the scib.metrics package54. This function returns a value scaled between 0 and 1, instead of the original LISI range that extends from 0 to the number of batches.

Clustering metrics

The embeddings obtained from all methods were employed to identify spatial domains. For fairness, we used the Leiden algorithm22 implemented in the SCANPY package40 to standardize the number of clusters by selecting a specific resolution. We used the sklearn.metrics module to calculate commonly used clustering evaluation metrics, including ARI, NMI, AMI, and Homogeneity.

Differential chromatin analysis

After identifying spatial domains, we applied the epiScanpy56 workflow to detect domain-specific peaks and visualize their processed accessibility values in spatial plots. By examining whether the peaks in the spatial plots displayed domain-specific patterns, we evaluated the quality of the identified spatial domains. Furthermore, the identified domain-specific peaks were leveraged for further analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data were presented as boxplots, showing the median and interquartile range, based on at least three independent analyses. For the comparisons between any two groups, a Mann- Whitney U test was applied. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05. All hypothesis tests were performed using the SciPy Python library.
