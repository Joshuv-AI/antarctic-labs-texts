An autonomous multimodal AI agent for evidence-grounded ophthalmic diagnosis
Authors: Kaikai Zhao, Qixuan Sun, Daohuan Kang, Tao Yu, Wenzheng Han, Rui Yao, Rupesh Agrawal, Gui-shuang Ying, Andrzej Grzybowski, Kai Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMultimodal ophthalmic diagnosis requires integrating fundus photography, B-scan ultrasonography, and medical evidence, yet most artificial intelligence (AI) systems remain single-task or weakly grounded. AgentEYE is an auditable multimodal agent that routes ocular images to specialized fundus and B-scan tools, retrieves guideline/web evidence, and synthesizes evidence-grounded reports. In a 302-case internal benchmark, AgentEYE shows higher diagnostic correctness and completeness than large language model (LLM)-only baselines and an ablation without specialized imaging tools; performance remains similar to the no-retrieval ablation, indicating that retrieval mainly supports evidence grounding and citation auditability. Blinded evaluation of 200 cases by three ophthalmologists confirms improved diagnostic correctness, completeness, safety, and citation grounding versus an LLM-only self-citation baseline. External analyses show distribution-dependent performance. These findings support AgentEYE as a traceable decision-support prototype requiring prospective multicenter validation.Keywords: autonomous AI agents, multimodal ophthalmic imaging, evidence-grounded diagnosis, large language models, fundus photography, ophthalmic ultrasonography, clinical decision support, agentic AI, guideline-based retrieval, explainable medical AI

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has made transformative progress in ophthalmology, with deep learning models achieving expert-level or superior performance in tasks such as diabetic retinopathy screening from fundus photographs,1,2 optical coherence tomography (OCT)-based retinal layer segmentation,3,4 and automated detection of glaucomatous optic neuropathy.5,6 However, these systems remain largely confined to unimodal, task-specific applications—isolated classifiers operating on a single image type or predefined lesion detection. They often fail to reflect the integrative reasoning of ophthalmologists, who routinely synthesize multimodal imaging (fundus photography, B-scan ultrasonography, and OCT), clinical history, and guideline-driven differentials to manage diagnostic uncertainty and formulate evidence-based management plans.7,8

The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift toward generalist AI capable of multimodal reasoning and medical knowledge synthesis.9,10,11,12 Models such as GPT-413 have demonstrated near-human proficiency in interpreting complex clinical vignettes14 and achieving passing scores on standardized examinations such as the United States Medical Licensing Examination.15 LLMs augmented with vision-language capabilities have begun to bridge text and imaging, enabling preliminary report generation from fundus images or preliminary triaging of urgent referrals based on textual histories.16,17 Despite these strides, general-purpose multimodal LLMs remain limited in clinical ophthalmology,18,19 where subtle morphologic features (e.g., early epiretinal membranes or shallow detachments) and cross-modality correlations critically influence diagnosis and management. LLMs in those works remain passive predictors rather than active diagnostic assistants: they cannot coordinate heterogeneous imaging modalities, perform iterative verification, or synthesize structured ophthalmic knowledge needed for safe clinical decision-making.20

Recent efforts to address these challenges have incorporated ophthalmology-specific fine-tuning or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG21) from textbooks and clinical guidelines, thus improving factual recall and domain alignment.22,23,24 However, these approaches largely remain static, lacking autonomous or adaptive reasoning.

Building upon such foundations, emerging agentic AI systems,25,26 which are capable of planning, tool orchestration, and evidence retrieval, have shown promise in biomedical domains including oncology27 and initial applications in ophthalmology. For instance, ChatMyopia,28 an LLM-based AI agent for myopia-related consultation in primary eye care settings, integrates text- and image-based inquiries and has been clinically evaluated in a randomized controlled trial, demonstrating improved patient satisfaction. More recently, Ma et al.29 developed a multimodal AI chatbot that combines textual histories with anterior segment images to diagnose ophthalmic diseases and generate high-quality medical responses. Zhuang et al.30 introduced a multimodal reasoning agent incorporating the visual analysis, knowledge retrieval, and diagnostic reasoning modules, supported by preliminary real-world clinical validation. Chen et al.31 presented EyeAgent, an LLM-based AI agent that orchestrates multiple specialized tools for multimodal multitask ophthalmic analyses, achieving strong performance in expert-rated accuracy, completeness, safety, and interpretability. These studies illustrate the rapid progress toward more dynamic, agentic approaches in ophthalmology.

Nevertheless, even these agentic systems have typically focused on specific conditions (e.g., myopia), anterior-segment imaging, or selected multitask analyses. Comprehensive end-to-end autonomous orchestration of multimodal perception involving heterogeneous imaging modalities such as fundus photography and B-scan ultrasonography, together with dynamic evidence retrieval from guidelines and web sources and uncertainty-aware reasoning under realistic diagnostic conditions with external validation and independent ophthalmologist assessment, remain limited.

These limitations are reinforced by current evaluation paradigms, which emphasize isolated prediction tasks, such as medical visual question answering on pathology images,32 rather than the chained reasoning required in real-world workflows, including hypothesis generation from ambiguous imaging, adaptive tool selection under incomplete data, and literature-grounded differential refinement. At the same time, regulatory frameworks, including the European Union’s AI Act33 and FDA guidelines for software as a medical device, increasingly emphasize transparency, traceability, and auditable reasoning, further exposing the shortcomings of opaque, end-to-end AI models in clinical settings.34 These requirements increasingly favor modular, agentic systems whose reasoning steps, tool invocations, and evidence sources can be inspected and audited, capabilities that conventional end-to-end deep learning models lack.

To address this gap, we developed and evaluated AgentEYE, an autonomous multimodal clinical AI agent tailored for evidence-grounded ophthalmic diagnosis and decision support. AgentEYE integrates an LLM-based reasoning core with domain-specific imaging tools, guideline-based retrieval, and web-based evidence search to process fundus photographs and B-scan ultrasonography. By dynamically sequencing tools and explicitly reasoning under uncertainty, rather than operating as a single predictive model, AgentEYE aims to support auditable, clinician-supervised diagnostic decision support. We validated AgentEYE on internal and external multimodal ophthalmic cases, using an LLM-as-a-judge35,36 framework with patient-level reference diagnoses and structured B-scan finding/impression references, complemented by independent ophthalmologist evaluation, to assess diagnostic correctness, completeness, and safety.

Results

AgentEYE improves diagnostic report quality in the internal benchmark

We first evaluated AgentEYE on a 302-case same-day multimodal benchmark from the held-out SAHZU-Internal test set. Each case included fundus photographs and B-scan ultrasonography acquired on the same day, with a patient-level reference diagnosis and structured B-scan finding/impression serving as the evaluation reference. Five methods were compared under a fixed GPT-4o reasoning-core setting: LLM-only, LLM-only with self-generated citations, AgentEYE without specialized imaging tools, AgentEYE without retrieval tools, and full AgentEYE. Automated report-level evaluation used three independent LLM judges: GLM-4.5-Air, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and Grok 4.3. Each judge scored diagnostic correctness and completeness on a 0–100 scale, using the same frozen rubric (full rubric and judge prompt in Data S1, section S1.7), and case-level means across judges were used for the primary analysis.

The main benchmark (Figure 3) showed a clear pattern: systems using specialized imaging tools scored much higher than direct LLM baselines and the ablation without specialized imaging tools. Across 302 cases, full AgentEYE achieved a diagnostic correctness score of 71.93 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 68.72–75.10) and a completeness score of 75.59 (95% CI: 73.82–77.27). In contrast, LLM-only achieved 36.52 (95% CI: 32.64–40.56) for diagnostic correctness and 48.57 (95% CI: 45.84–51.33) for completeness, while the self-citation baseline achieved 36.95 (95% CI: 32.84–41.23) and 48.36 (95% CI: 45.43–51.40), respectively. Detailed method-level, per-judge, and paired-comparison results for the 302-case benchmark are provided in Table S2. Inter-LLM judge reliability was high across the 1,510 case-method outputs, with intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) (A,k) values of 0.958 for diagnostic correctness and 0.944 for completeness (Table S3). Disease-stratified 302-case benchmark results are shown in Figure S1; the five high-frequency categories contained 60 cases each, whereas choroidal melanoma and retinoblastoma were descriptive single-case strata.

AgentEYE substantially outperformed LLM-only baselines and the ablation without specialized imaging tools, whereas performance was similar to the no-retrieval ablation.

Ablation results indicated that specialized imaging tools were the dominant contributor to diagnostic performance. Removing these tools reduced diagnostic correctness to 39.40 (95% CI: 35.92–42.97) and completeness to 53.23 (95% CI: 50.86–55.58). By contrast, removing retrieval had little effect on report-level diagnostic correctness and completeness, with scores of 71.37 (95% CI: 68.29–74.33) and 74.93 (95% CI: 73.26–76.50), respectively. These findings suggest that retrieval in this setting primarily supports evidence grounding and citation auditability, rather than producing a large incremental gain in diagnostic-score metrics.

We next used auxiliary analyses to characterize how these report-level gains related to disease strata, discrete diagnosis extraction, and specialized tool behavior. As a secondary analysis, we mapped each final free-text report to one of the seven benchmark diagnoses to estimate diagnosis-level clinical metrics, without replacing the primary report-level evaluation. As shown in Figure S2A, methods retaining specialized imaging tool outputs showed better diagnosis-label extraction metrics than direct LLM baselines and the ablation without specialized imaging tools. Figure S2C compares direct fundus/B-scan single-label outputs with diagnosis labels extracted from final AgentEYE reports; because these outputs have different roles, this panel is interpreted as tool-contribution context, rather than as a direct classifier comparison.

A B-scan tool-version ablation further showed that the final AgentEYE report quality improved when B-scan diagnosis and structured ultrasound evidence improved together, but not when only the single diagnosis label was strengthened. Compared with a one-pass B-scan tool, the two-task evidence B-scan tool improved the final diagnostic correctness and completeness. A diagnosis-only hybrid achieved higher standalone B-scan classification accuracy but did not significantly improve final report quality over the two-task evidence tool (Figure S3), suggesting that stable downstream gains require diagnostically useful finding/impression evidence as well as single-label classification.

In a separate 100-case multi-disease comorbidity subset, AgentEYE also outperformed LLM-only in term of diagnostic correctness (70.95 versus 50.43; mean difference: +20.52; 95% CI: +14.65 to +26.58; paired t test p < 0.001) and completeness (73.58 versus 56.64; mean difference: +16.94; 95% CI: +12.42 to +21.64; p < 0.001). This analysis was used as a supplementary robustness assessment rather than as a multilabel classification benchmark (Table S4).

AgentEYE supports diagnostic uncertainty through traceable multimodal evidence integration

To assess AgentEYE’s ability to support diagnostic workflows, we evaluated a traceable, imaging-centered workflow under diagnostic uncertainty using structured workflow prompting informed by prior work on stepwise prompting.37 At runtime, the system decomposed each case image list into explicit workflow states, including modality routing, specialized fundus and B-scan interpretation, retrieval sufficiency assessment, optional second-round retrieval, and final evidence-grounded synthesis. This design handles uncertainty through auditable intermediate states rather than a single end-to-end prediction or an unobserved hidden reasoning transcript.

A representative held-out case illustrates how this workflow handled diagnostic uncertainty (Figures S4 and S5). The reference diagnosis was vitreous hemorrhage, and the reference B-scan finding/impression documented vitreous opacity and posterior vitreous detachment without an obvious retinal-detachment band. The LLM-only baseline over-called retinal detachment and recommended urgent detachment-directed surgery. AgentEYE routed the ultrawide-field fundus and B-scan images to the specialized tools, reconciled a fundus-tool diagnosis of vitreous hemorrhage with a B-scan hard-label tendency toward retinal detachment by using the B-scan finding/impression text, retrieved evidence focused on distinguishing vitreous hemorrhage from retinal detachment, and generated a final report centered on vitreous hemorrhage while retaining retinal detachment as a differential and follow-up risk. This example was selected to illustrate a multimodal conflict-integration mechanism rather than the largest score gap; broader failure mechanisms are summarized in Table S5.

Trace inspection and audit further showed that the representative workflow was not selectively demonstrated. Across the 302-case benchmark, AgentEYE completed the expected fundus and B-scan routing/tool sequence for all applicable cases, with no missed or unnecessary fundus/B-scan tool calls. Retrieval was invoked in 86.4% of cases, with a mean of 1.89 retrieval queries per case; core-specific workflow/tool-call audit statistics are provided in Table S4. Figure 2 illustrates the same workflow states for the representative trace, including routed image branches, fundus-only diagnosis, B-scan finding/impression/diagnosis, retrieval sufficiency assessment, optional second-round retrieval, and final report generation. These examples are workflow-state summaries intended to illustrate traceability, not hidden model reasoning transcripts.

As a secondary robustness analysis, we compared GPT-4o,38 DeepSeek-V4-Flash,39 and Qwen3-235B-A22B40 as reasoning cores while holding specialized imaging tool outputs and retrieval context fixed. This analysis evaluated evidence integration and final report synthesis rather than visual perception. Performance was broadly comparable across cores, supporting the stability of the modular workflow while retaining GPT-4o as the prespecified primary configuration (Table S4).

Ophthalmologist evaluation supports report quality, safety, citation grounding, and judge concordance

To complement automated evaluation, we conducted a blinded human expert evaluation on a 200-case stratified subset of the 302-case held-out internal benchmark. Three ophthalmologists independently reviewed outputs from full AgentEYE and an LLM-only baseline with self-generated citations. Both candidate report sets were generated under the same locked GPT-4o formal configuration used for the main benchmark. The paired design allowed clinicians to assess not only diagnostic correctness and completeness but also safety and citation grounding under the same cases.

AgentEYE substantially outperformed the self-citation baseline in human ratings (Figure 4). Majority-vote diagnostic correctness was 83.0% for AgentEYE and 42.0% for the baseline, while completeness was 82.5% versus 41.5%. Harmful-output rates were lower for AgentEYE (3.5%) than for the self-citation baseline (29.0%). The mean 0–5 diagnostic correctness score was 4.03 for AgentEYE versus 2.42 for the self-citation baseline, and the mean completeness score was 3.98 versus 2.71. Citation grounding showed the largest separation. By majority vote, 86.5% of AgentEYE citations were rated correct, compared with 46.5% for the self-citation baseline. Incorrect citations were less frequent for AgentEYE (3.5%) than for the baseline (29.5%), and irrelevant citations were also lower (10.0% versus 24.0%).

These results support the role of retrieval and source-grounded reporting as an auditability mechanism, even though retrieval produced limited incremental gains in the automated diagnostic correctness and completeness scores. Secondary physician adjudication of reports initially rated as wrong or irrelevant citations confirmed fewer citation-grounding problems for AgentEYE than for the self-citation baseline (24/200 [12.0%] versus 103/200 [51.5%]); unverifiable or likely hallucinated sources occurred only in the self-citation baseline (14/200 [7.0%]) and not in AgentEYE (Figure 4E; Table S6).

Inter-ophthalmologist agreement was high for the 0–5 diagnostic correctness scores (ICC[2,1] = 0.829; ICC[2,k] = 0.936) and moderate-to-high for completeness scores (ICC[2,1] = 0.744; ICC[2,k] = 0.897). Across 400 case-method rows, the three-judge LLM panel was concordant with the ophthalmologist consensus scores for diagnostic correctness (Pearson’s r = 0.813; Spearman’s ρ = 0.775) and completeness (Pearson’s r = 0.775; Spearman’s ρ = 0.763). When LLM-judge scores were thresholded at 70, the classification agreement with human majority labels reached 85.5% for diagnostic correctness (AUC = 0.953) and 86.0% for completeness (AUC = 0.935). These analyses (Table 2) support the use of the LLM-judge panel for scalable comparative report-level assessment while retaining blinded ophthalmologist evaluation as the clinical reference for key claims.

External validation reveals distribution-dependent generalization and boundary conditions

External validation showed heterogeneous generalization across cohorts (Figure 5). In the Wannan-China-External benchmark-category subset (50 cases), AgentEYE improved diagnostic correctness by 18.53 points (95% CI: 5.07–31.98; paired t test p = 0.008) and completeness by 11.17 points (95% CI: 2.83–19.51; p = 0.010) over LLM-only. In the IRO-Poland-External subset (25 cases), a small external cohort concentrated in retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, high myopia, and choroidal/uveal melanoma, the performance was similar between AgentEYE and LLM-only in terms of diagnostic correctness (difference: 0.51; 95% CI: −16.51 to 17.53; p = 0.952) and completeness (difference: 1.29; 95% CI: −9.20 to 11.79; p = 0.801). This neutral result should be interpreted cautiously because the cohort was small, geographically external, concentrated in a limited disease mix, and subject to cross-site differences in image acquisition, documentation style, and reference construction.

In the Wannan-China-External unseen-disease subset (35 cases), AgentEYE did not significantly improve diagnostic correctness (difference: 2.62; 95% CI: −5.85 to 11.08; p = 0.534) but improved completeness by 7.69 points (95% CI: 0.99–14.38; p = 0.026). These results support AgentEYE’s benefit under cross-site differences in case mix, image acquisition, and reporting for benchmark-category diseases while highlighting reduced diagnostic gains for diseases outside the specialized tool label space. Because the unseen-disease analysis intentionally included categories outside the seven-label internal tool scope, it is interpreted as a boundary-condition test rather than evidence of open-world diagnostic coverage.

Discussion

In this study, we developed AgentEYE, a multimodal AI agent that integrates LLMs with specialized ophthalmic tools and retrieval mechanisms for evidence-grounded ophthalmic diagnostic support. This agentic framework functions as a clinician-supervised ophthalmic assistant that synthesizes imaging inputs, invokes modality-specific tools, and generates clinically coherent reports grounded in retrieved evidence. In contrast to conventional image classifiers or static multimodal models, AgentEYE exposes a modular workflow for modality routing, tool invocation, retrieval, and report synthesis, providing a practical route toward auditable multimodal decision support. Our evaluations show that AgentEYE substantially outperformed standalone LLMs in the internal multimodal benchmark and the Wannan-China benchmark-category external subset, while the neutral IRO-Poland-External result and unseen-disease analyses highlighted important boundary conditions related to the external cohort size, cross-site imaging/documentation differences, and diseases outside the specialized tool label space.

AgentEYE was evaluated using both automated and human-centered frameworks. In the 302-case internal same-day multimodal benchmark, AgentEYE markedly outperformed LLM-only baselines and an ablation without specialized imaging tools in terms of report-level diagnostic correctness and completeness. The small difference between full AgentEYE and the no-retrieval ablation indicates that retrieval should not be interpreted as the primary driver of diagnostic score gains in this dataset. Instead, retrieval is better understood as supporting evidence-grounded reporting, citation auditability, and traceability, as confirmed by the ophthalmologist citation-grounding evaluation.

The high routing and tool-execution success rate, together with the absence of unused required tools, indicates robust decision-layer orchestration, a property fundamentally distinct from linear input-output architectures. Rather than operating as a single input-output model, AgentEYE records explicit workflow states for image-type routing, modality-specific tool execution, retrieval planning, and evidence-grounded synthesis. This capacity is particularly relevant in ophthalmology where multimodal imaging is common, clinical uncertainty is frequent, and diagnostic confidence often depends on synthesizing complementary imaging evidence.

These results align with and extend findings from similar agentic systems in other medical domains, such as oncology, where autonomous agents integrating LLMs with domain-specific tools have demonstrated strong diagnostic performance.27 AgentEYE’s workflow orchestration was reflected in the 302-case trace audit, in which appropriate tool routing, fundus routing, B-scan routing, and expected imaging tool execution each reached 100%, while retrieval was invoked in 86.4% of cases with a mean of 1.89 retrieval queries per case. This capability is especially valuable in high-uncertainty imaging scenarios, such as dense vitreous hemorrhage, where accurate interpretation requires careful integration of available fundus and ultrasonographic evidence with guideline knowledge. In representative cases, AgentEYE automatically classified imaging modalities, generated modality-specific reports, and retrieved relevant guidelines to refine diagnostic hypotheses, thereby reducing errors commonly observed with unguided LLM outputs.

Importantly, AgentEYE should be interpreted as a clinical decision-support system rather than a diagnostic authority. Its primary contribution lies in structuring multimodal information, surfacing plausible differentials, and grounding recommendations in established guidelines, thereby augmenting, rather than supplanting, clinical judgment. This distinction is critical in ophthalmology, where diagnostic certainty often evolves longitudinally with follow-up imaging and clinical response. The representative workflow examples illustrate how AgentEYE can expose conflicting modality evidence and provide evidence-linked recommendations for clinician review, but the present retrospective study does not establish autonomous uncertainty resolution.

A prospective validation study should compare clinician performance with and without AgentEYE in routine multimodal ophthalmic workflows, using randomized or crossover case review when feasible. Prespecified endpoints should include diagnostic accuracy, time to decision, referral appropriateness, change in management plan, safety events or potentially harmful recommendations, citation/evidence usability, and clinician usability ratings. Such a study should also record workflow interruptions, cases in which clinicians override AgentEYE output, and performance across sites, devices, disease distributions, and clinician experience levels. Until these prospective endpoints are tested, the present results should be interpreted as evidence of retrospective report-quality and citation-grounding improvement rather than proof of real-world clinical benefit.

Beyond ophthalmology, this work contributes to the emerging science of agentic medical AI by demonstrating how modular tool orchestration can be operationalized and evaluated in a high-stakes clinical domain. Rather than relying on monolithic multimodal models, AgentEYE illustrates the advantages of decomposing perception, reasoning, and evidence retrieval into interoperable components that can be independently improved, audited, or replaced. This architectural flexibility is particularly advantageous in medicine, where guidelines evolve, imaging modalities diversify, and domain-specific constraints demand continual adaptation.

The updated citation analysis changes the interpretation of evidence grounding. Rather than relying on self-generated references from a general LLM, AgentEYE used retrieved guideline and web evidence to support its final report. In blinded ophthalmologist evaluation, this yielded substantially higher citation correctness than the self-citation baseline. However, retrieval can still introduce irrelevant or weakly supporting evidence, and failure-case analysis identified examples in which retrieval did not improve, or even degraded, final synthesis. Future work should, therefore, focus on citation-aware retrieval, evidence reranking, and explicit source-support verification.

AgentEYE may also be relevant to settings where access to subspecialty expertise is limited, because B-scan ultrasonography is often used to evaluate posterior segment pathology when media opacity, equipment availability, or workforce constraints limit direct retinal assessment. However, this study did not test prospective deployment in low-resource or frontline clinical environments. Its potential role in triaging urgent pathology, prioritizing referrals, or reducing diagnostic delay should, therefore, be interpreted as a future clinical application that requires site-specific validation, workflow integration studies, and assessment of local infrastructure.

From a governance perspective, the agentic design of AgentEYE offers advantages over opaque end-to-end models. As regulatory frameworks evolve, most notably the European Union AI Act emphasizing transparency, traceability, and accountability, systems that expose intermediate decisions, tool invocations, and evidence sources may be better positioned for clinician-supervised evaluation and eventual implementation. By making final outputs, workflow states, tool invocations, and evidence-integration pathways inspectable, agentic systems can facilitate clinical oversight, medicolegal review, and iterative improvement, thereby addressing key challenges associated with high-risk medical AI.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered. This was a retrospective study, and although the internal benchmark used held-out patients with additional external analyses, prospective multicenter validation across devices, disease distributions, and clinical workflows is required before clinical deployment. AgentEYE was evaluated as an imaging-centered decision-support prototype using fundus photographs and B-scan ultrasonography; symptoms, visual acuity, intraocular pressure, prior ocular history, structured EHR variables, and additional modalities such as OCT were not part of the primary runtime evaluation, so the results do not establish performance for complete clinical decision-making. The principal benchmark was constrained to seven diagnostic categories, with choroidal melanoma and retinoblastoma represented by only one case each, and unseen-disease analyses should therefore be interpreted as boundary-condition tests rather than evidence of open-world diagnostic coverage. Post hoc sensitivity/specificity estimates relied on diagnosis extraction from free-text reports and are auxiliary to report-level correctness and completeness. The gender-stratified analysis was exploratory, used recorded clinical gender metadata rather than self-identified gender data, and was not powered for definitive subgroup inference. Formal generation and LLM-judge scoring were single-run evaluations, leaving run-to-run variability unresolved. Finally, retrieval improved citation grounding but did not eliminate irrelevant, weakly supporting, or erroneous citations; future work should evaluate claim-level evidence verification, degraded-image and adversarial robustness, and prospective clinician-AI interaction.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics oversight and consent

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and followed guidelines for transparent reporting of medical AI research. Ethical approvals were obtained from the institutional review boards of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. Y2023-1073), the Institute for Research in Ophthalmology, Foundation for Ophthalmology Development, Poznan, Poland (No. Y2023-1073), and the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College (No. Y2024-1015). The use of the existing internal/external dataset was approved without requiring informed consent due to its retrospective nature. All data were fully de-identified prior to analysis.

Study datasets

Three datasets were curated to develop and validate AgentEYE: one internal dataset for tool training, preliminary evaluations, and full-agent evaluation, and two external datasets for independent validation and robustness analyses. These datasets encompassed retinal and vitreoretinal pathologies including vitreous hemorrhage, high myopia, uveal melanoma, refractive error, retinal detachment, and retinoblastoma. Each case consisted of one structured clinical report paired with corresponding color fundus photographs and/or B-scan ultrasonography images.

Participant metadata

Participant age and recorded gender metadata were extracted from available de-identified clinical records and are summarized in Table 2.

Study design and blinding

Sample sizes for formal analyses were determined by prespecified data-curation criteria and the number of eligible held-out cases rather than by formal power calculations. Formal internal evaluations were performed on patient-level held-out test-set cases; the 302-case main benchmark contained 302 individual patients. For blinded ophthalmologist evaluation, paired candidate reports were presented as Report A/Report B in randomized order within each case, and method identity was masked. LLM judges received only the system output, reference diagnosis, structured B-scan finding/impression, and scoring rubric; they did not receive method identity, tool traces, retrieval traces, or other systems’ outputs.

Method details

SAHZU-internal cohort and data curation

The internal dataset, termed SAHZU-Internal, was curated into a seven-category single-disease cohort from routine clinical workflows, with the ultrasound dataset collected as described previously.41 After excluding multi-disease and diagnosis-conflict, the cohort included 9,049 eligible patients. The modality-specific subsets included 7,895 fundus patients (31,393 fundus images) and 8,517 B-scan patients (43,266 B-scan images from 15,244 reports/PDFs). The patient-level splits were 5,429 for training, 1,811 for validation, and 1,809 for testing; disease-level split details are provided in Table S7.

The same-day fundus + B-scan subset contained 10,628 candidate same-day cases and was used to construct the 302-case held-out multimodal benchmark. Throughout this study, a case denotes one same-day multimodal evaluation unit; the 302-case formal benchmark was constrained to one case per patient, yielding 302 cases from 302 individual held-out test patients. Detailed statistics are provided in Table 2. The training subset was used exclusively for developing domain-specific tools; the validation subset was used for hyperparameter tuning, model selection, and monitoring overfitting. The held-out test subset was used for assessing specialized-tool performance and B-scan report-generation metrics (Table S1) and for all formal internal agent-evaluation cohorts, including the 302-case main benchmark and internal supplementary analyses shown in Figure 3. Thus, formal internal agent evaluation was performed only on held-out test-set patients and had no patient-level overlap with tool-training or validation data.

A separate 100-case multi-disease comorbidity subset, excluded from the primary single-disease benchmark, was used only for supplementary robustness analysis comparing AgentEYE with the LLM-only baseline on report-level diagnostic correctness and completeness.

Data curation involved manual cleaning by experts to ensure fidelity. Images and reports were treated as multimodal pairs and reviewed by two attending ophthalmologists with more than five years of experience, with discrepancies adjudicated by a senior vitreoretinal specialist with more than 10 years of experience. Screening criteria included image quality, diagnostic validity, and clinical completeness. Cases failing any criterion were excluded to maintain high-quality standards.

External cohorts

External datasets were used for supplementary validation and robustness analyses rather than as the primary benchmark. External cohorts were curated using the same case-level reference-construction principle as the internal benchmark, retaining cases with an evaluable patient-level diagnosis and available imaging/report reference fields. These included IRO-Poland-External and the Wannan-China-External benchmark-category and unseen-disease subsets, curated to assess performance under cross-site differences in case mix, image acquisition, reporting, unseen or non-benchmark categories, and modality differences. The final IRO-Poland-External analysis included 25 cases after dataset curation, comprising 14 retinal detachment, 5 vitreous hemorrhage, 4 high myopia, and 2 uveal melanoma cases. Because these external cohorts were smaller and heterogeneous, their results (Figure 5) are interpreted as exploratory generalization evidence.

AgentEYE system and specialized imaging tools

AgentEYE integrates an LLM reasoning core with image-type routing, local specialized ophthalmic imaging tools, guideline retrieval, and web search. During report generation, the evaluated systems received ophthalmic imaging inputs rather than free-text patient queries or structured clinical variables; patient-level diagnoses and B-scan finding/impression fields were reserved for reference construction and evaluation, not provided to the agent. A ResNet50 image-modality classifier routes input images as fundus photographs or B-scan ultrasonography. Fundus and B-scan observations are produced by local Qwen2.5-VL-7B LoRA42 tools trained on SAHZU-Internal image-report pairs.

Runtime handling of unavailable modalities and retrieval evidence was explicitly encoded. If no images were routed to a modality, the corresponding observation was represented as modality_available = false, and the final synthesis prompt instructed the model to report that modality as unavailable rather than infer findings. If retrieval was disabled, not requested, failed to parse, or returned no usable evidence, the system provided an explicit no-retrieval context and removed external references from the structured output. In contrast, runtime tool-execution or final-output parsing failures were recorded as error outputs rather than silently converted into successful reports.

All local vision-language imaging tools used in the formal analyses and B-scan ablation experiments were LoRA42 fine-tuned for one epoch on their corresponding SAHZU-Internal training examples; for the two-task B-scan configuration, the epoch count was defined over task-specific supervised fine-tuning examples rather than individual patients, because evidence generation and B-scan-only diagnosis were trained as separate task examples. The default B-scan tool used a two-task configuration: one task generated structured ultrasound finding/impression evidence, and the other generated a B-scan-only diagnostic opinion. This default configuration was fixed before formal agent evaluations.

Fundus-tool training used fundus images from the SAHZU-Internal training split to predict the seven-category fundus-only diagnosis label. B-scan-tool training used B-scan images from the training split in task-specific supervised fine-tuning examples for B-scan-only diagnosis and ultrasound finding/impression evidence generation; validation data were used for model selection, and the selected checkpoints were fixed before formal agent evaluation. For reproducibility, LoRA training used rank 64, alpha 128, dropout 0.05, all-linear target modules, learning rate 1 × 10–4, per-device batch size 1 with 32-step gradient accumulation, AdamW fused optimization with weight decay 0.01, cosine learning-rate decay with 0.05 warmup ratio, maximum sequence length 8,192, and random/data seed 42; checkpoints were selected by validation loss.

For the B-scan tool-version ablation, three saved B-scan observation configurations were compared while holding the fundus tool, retrieval context, reasoning core, and final synthesis prompt fixed: a one-pass B-scan tool that generated finding, impression, and diagnosis in a single response; the default two-task evidence B-scan tool; and a diagnosis-only hybrid that paired the default finding/impression evidence with a separately trained diagnosis-only B-scan classifier. This ablation separated improvements in structured ultrasound evidence from improvements in B-scan single-label diagnosis alone. Tool outputs were saved with model and configuration metadata and used as fixed observations for downstream synthesis and ablation analyses.

Retrieval resources

AgentEYE uses a curated ophthalmic guideline corpus and authority-filtered web retrieval to support evidence-grounded reporting. The retrieval corpus was locked before formal evaluation and comprised 42 selected documents from a larger candidate set of Chinese Ophthalmological Society (COS43) clinical practice guidelines and related Chinese ophthalmology expert consensus documents. Selection retained documents relevant to diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and safety checking, and excluded drug-specific, device-specific, administrative workflow, public-health management, and unrelated ophthalmology documents; the locked corpus included four core diagnosis guidelines and 38 differential-diagnosis or safety-checking guidelines. Only public or study-authorized guideline and consensus materials were used; future corpus updates would require re-indexing and should be treated as a new retrieval setting for formal evaluation. Source documents were converted to markdown, split into 586 overlapping chunks (1,400 characters with 180-character overlap), and searched with a keyword-based BM25 retriever over document titles and text segments.

During report generation, a retrieval-planning prompt decided whether external evidence was needed for diagnostic explanation, differential diagnosis, recommendations, citation grounding, tool-output conflict, or ambiguous/insufficient imaging evidence. If retrieval was triggered, the system generated 1–2 case-specific queries; each retrieval round returned up to three local guideline chunks and up to three web results. A second retrieval decision could request one additional focused query when the first round failed to provide directly relevant evidence or left a key diagnostic uncertainty unresolved. In the formal experiments, web retrieval used authority-filtered Google web search with ophthalmology-oriented query expansion and quality filtering. The web filter retained authority or high-quality sources, including AAO/EyeWiki, NEI/NIH/NCBI/PubMed, and recognized ophthalmology or medical-journal domains, while removing low-quality general-content sites.

Retrieved evidence was used to support diagnostic explanation, differential diagnosis, management recommendations, and citation grounding. The final synthesis prompt instructed the reasoning core to treat image-based observations as the primary evidence and not to introduce or prioritize a diagnosis solely because it appeared in retrieved text (Data S1, section S1.3). Citation grounding was evaluated separately from the primary LLM-judge diagnostic scores.

Formal benchmark methods

For the formal main benchmark, GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-11-20) was used as the fixed reasoning core for final report synthesis. The reasoning-core sensitivity analysis compared GPT-4o with DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen3-235B-A22B while holding specialized-tool outputs and retrieval context fixed; the corresponding runtime identifiers for the two alternative cores were deepseek-v4-flash and qwen3-235b-a22b.

Five fixed methods were compared on the same 302 cases. LLM-only was the direct multimodal baseline: GPT-4o received the same fundus and B-scan images and generated a report without AgentEYE image-type routing, specialized imaging tools, retrieval, or intermediate tool outputs. LLM-only with self-generated citations used the same direct image-to-report setting but additionally required the model to provide citations from its own internal knowledge, enabling comparison with AgentEYE’s retrieval-grounded citation quality. AgentEYE without specialized imaging tools retained image-type routing, retrieval, and final synthesis but replaced the local fine-tuned fundus and B-scan tools with GPT-4o, applied separately to the routed fundus and B-scan image sets; this ablation isolated the contribution of specialized ophthalmic imaging tools. AgentEYE without retrieval tools retained image-type routing and specialized imaging-tool outputs but disabled guideline/web retrieval, isolating retrieval’s contribution to report quality and citation grounding. Full AgentEYE used both specialized imaging tools and retrieval before final synthesis.

All formal method comparisons used the locked prompts shown in Data S1, prespecified models, fixed tool checkpoints, the same case-inclusion definitions, and the same reference standards. Previously generated outputs were reused only when these prespecified settings matched the locked formal configuration. Each formal report-generation run and each LLM-judge scoring pass was performed once; variation across repeated model runs was not systematically estimated.

Blinded ophthalmologist evaluation and citation taxonomy

For clinical validation, three ophthalmologists independently evaluated a blinded 200-case subset comparing full AgentEYE with the LLM-only self-citation baseline. Candidate reports were the locked GPT-4o-based outputs from the formal configuration rather than outputs from a separate backbone setting. The evaluation workbook linked each anonymized case to the corresponding images, reference standard, candidate report, and citation/evidence display without revealing the method identity.

Physicians rated diagnostic conclusion labels as correct, wrong, or harmful; rated diagnostic correctness on a 0–5 scale; rated completeness as complete or incomplete with a separate 0–5 score; assessed citation grounding as correct, wrong, irrelevant, or not applicable/no citation; and marked an independent harmful-output flag.

A correct conclusion required agreement of the main diagnosis and key diagnostic judgment with the reference standard and case evidence without major misleading statements; a wrong conclusion indicated an incorrect main diagnosis or key diagnostic statement without clear immediate clinical risk; and a harmful conclusion indicated potential risk of delayed referral or treatment, unsafe reassurance, or dangerous management.

Completeness required coverage of clinically relevant available modality evidence, key B-scan finding/impression information, main diagnosis and necessary differentials, uncertainty when appropriate, and reasonable follow-up, referral, or management recommendations, without rewarding length or formatting. Citation grounding was judged separately from diagnostic quality. The complete translated human-evaluation rubric is provided in Data S1. Reports with majority-vote wrong or irrelevant citation labels underwent secondary physician adjudication to classify report-level citation-grounding error subtypes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The primary automated report-level metrics were Diagnostic Correctness and Completeness. Diagnostic Correctness measured alignment of the final diagnostic interpretation with the patient-level reference diagnosis and clinically necessary differentials. Completeness assessed whether the report covered available modality evidence, key B-scan finding/impression information, the main diagnosis and relevant differentials, and reasonable management recommendations. The evaluation reference consisted of the patient-level diagnosis, structured B-scan finding/impression fields, and modality-availability information; system outputs, intermediate tool outputs, retrieval records, and generated citations were not included in the reference standard. Both metrics were scored on a 0–100 scale by LLM judges using a frozen rubric; the complete rubric and judge prompt are provided in Data S1, section S1.7. Citation grounding was not included in the primary numeric LLM-judge score; it was evaluated separately in the blinded ophthalmologist study.

For scalable automated evaluation, we used an LLM-as-a-judge35,36 framework with three independent non-GPT-4o judges: GLM-4.5-Air,44 Gemini 2.0 Flash,45 and Grok 4.3.46 For reproducibility, the corresponding model identifiers were glm-4.5-air, gemini-2.0-flash, and grok-4.3, accessed through OpenAI-compatible endpoints. Judges received the system output, patient-level reference diagnosis, structured B-scan finding/impression, and the frozen scoring rubric, but not intermediate tool outputs, retrieval records, or other systems’ outputs. Each judge independently assigned 0–100 scores for diagnostic correctness and completeness. The primary analysis averaged the three judge scores within each case and method before computing method-level means, 95% confidence intervals, and paired comparisons. Inter-LLM judge reliability was quantified using absolute-agreement ICC(A,1) and ICC(A,k), and pairwise Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall correlations were computed across complete case-method scoring units.

For each method, 95% confidence intervals were computed over case-level mean scores using t-based intervals. Paired comparisons were performed at the case level because all methods were evaluated on the same cases. We report paired mean differences with 95% confidence intervals and paired t test p values, along with sign-flip/binomial tests as non-parametric directional checks. Disease-stratified analyses were performed for the five high-frequency categories with 60 cases each; categories represented by a single case were reported descriptively only. Exploratory gender-stratified analyses used recorded gender from B-scan demographic records; within-gender paired deltas and diagnosis-adjusted heterogeneity tests were summarized in Table S8.

For auxiliary clinical classification metrics, a secondary diagnosis-label extractor used only the final free-text report and did not access images, tool outputs, retrieval records, ground truth, or judge scores. The extractor was forced to map each report to one of seven predefined diagnosis labels, enabling top-1 accuracy, macro-averaged sensitivity, specificity, and F1 calculation; because extraction errors could affect sensitivity and specificity estimates, these metrics were interpreted only as auxiliary clinical-classification analyses and not as replacements for report-level evaluation.

Tool-invocation appropriateness was audited from recorded AgentEYE traces by comparing modality routing and expected fundus/B-scan tool calls with the configured workflow; the audit was treated as a workflow-quality check rather than a separate clinical-performance endpoint.

For the blinded ophthalmologist evaluation, inter-rater agreement was quantified using ICC for numeric scores and kappa-based statistics for categorical labels. Agreement between the LLM-judge panel and human evaluation was assessed using correlations, weighted kappa,47 and method-preference direction consistency. Citation-grounding pairwise comparisons used paired case-level comparisons and exact McNemar/binomial tests. For the secondary citation-error taxonomy, bootstrap confidence intervals were computed for paired rate differences over cases, and exact McNemar/binomial tests were applied to discordant pairs.
