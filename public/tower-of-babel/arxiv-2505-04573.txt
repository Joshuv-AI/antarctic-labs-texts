Local uniqueness of the Black String with small circle size
Emma Albertini, Daniel Platt

Abstract
In this article we study uniqueness of the Black String, i.e. the product of 4-dimensional Schwarzschild space with a circle of length L L . In [ 1 ] , this was reduced to a non-linear elliptic PDE, and we use this setup to show that for small L L the Black String is infinitesimally rigid as a Ricci-flat metric. Using a fixed point theorem, we prove that this implies local uniqueness, i.e. there exist no other Ricci-flat metrics near the Black String, and we give bounds for the size of the neighborhood in which the Black String is unique. We compare this with the toy problem of a scalar field satisfying an elliptic equation that was already solved in [ 1 ] using different methods. In this case we can use the fixed point theorem method to prove not just a local but a global statement.

Introduction

In both astrophysics and quantum gravity, the uniqueness, often referred to as the no-hair property, of stationary black hole solutions plays a crucial role. In four-dimensional spacetime, all regular, stationary, and asymptotically flat solutions of the Einstein–Maxwell equations are completely characterized by just three parameters: mass, angular momentum, and electric charge. These solutions are uniquely described by the Kerr–Newman family of black holes. The collaborative effort of the General Relativity community led to various versions of the proof of the uniqueness for vacuum gravity
[

,
3
,
4

]
and Einstein–Maxwell theory
[

Results on
4
-dimensional black holes can serve as inspiration in higher dimensions, for instance in cases which exploit the symmetry properties of the “static or axisymmetric” argument in four-dimensions. However, when considering product manifolds, simple connectedness of the event horizon, which is extensively used in the uniqueness proof for Kerr–Newman
[

In this context, a noteworthy example exhibiting non-uniqueness are certain static black hole solutions in Kaluza-Klein Theory, i.e. black holes on a product space

The method exploits the elliptic character of Einstein equations and uses elliptic analysis techniques, which lead to an application of Banach’s Fixed Point Theorem.

We briefly explain the proof idea of this theorem.
It has become a standard technique in geometry to construct solutions to elliptic PDE in three steps:
(1) constructing an approximate solution and bounding its error,
(2) bounding the operator norm of the inverse of the linearisation of the equation,
(3) applying a fixed-point theorem to deduce that there exists a genuine solution near the approximate solution.
This method was pioneered in
[
12
]
and has since been applied to countless other problems in geometry.
Depending on the fixed-point theorem applied, one usually also obtains a
local uniqueness
statement for solutions constructed in this way.
I.e., the method constructs a genuine solution to the elliptic PDE and guarantees that there are no other nearby solutions.

We apply the same method, but instead of beginning with an approximate solution, we begin with the exact known solution to the elliptic equation.
In our case, that is the Black String metric solving the Ricci-flat equation.
The rest of the analysis is then unchanged, and we obtain a
local uniqueness
statement stating that there exist no other nearby Ricci-flat metrics, where we can quantify what
nearby
means.

One difference to examples in the literature is that we do not work with the equations
Eq.

1.2
directly, but
with their
z
-derivative
.
While the linearisation of this new system of equations is still a differential operator, it has the interesting consequence that the non-linear terms of the system become integro-differential operators, which requires some adaptation.

Unfortunately, this does not lead to a global uniqueness statement, because we cannot rule out solutions that are far away from the Black String metric.
There is, however, hope that this can be overcome in the future, and we present one toy example in which we can use the method above to prove global uniqueness of solutions to a non-linear elliptic PDE.
The example is a lower-dimensional version of
[

Let

\displaystyle\leq cL^{1/2},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left|\left|I(\psi)\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}

\displaystyle\leq cL,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left|\left|\bar{\phi}\right|\right|_{C^{0}(B^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq c.

(3.10)

Here,
c
is independent of
L
and of the solution
\phi
.

Proof.

Multiplying
Eq.

3.2
by
\phi
and integrating by
parts gives

\int_{X_{L}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}=-\int_{X_{L}}\phi V^{\prime}(\phi).

The boundary term vanishes because
\operatorname{tr}\phi=0
. Since

tV^{\prime}(t)\geq-c^{2}
, it follows that
\left|\left|\nabla\phi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}^{2}\leq c\operatorname{vol}(X_{L})=cL
.
The Dirichlet Poincaré inequality applied on every slice
B^{2}\times\{z\}
and then integrated over
S^{1}_{L}
, gives

\left|\left|\phi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\nabla_{x}\phi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\nabla\phi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}.

This proves
Eq.

3.6
.

We next prove
Eq.

3.7
. Choose
c_{V}>0
such that

tV^{\prime}(t)\geq-c_{V}^{2}
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and set
u:=\phi^{2}
.
Using
Eq.

3.2
, we obtain
\Delta u=2\phi\Delta\phi+2|\nabla\phi|^{2}=2\phi V^{\prime}(\phi)+2|\nabla\phi|^{2}\geq-2c_{V}^{2}
.
Define

b(x,z):=\frac{c_{V}^{2}}{2}(1-r^{2}),\quad r=|x|.

Since
\Delta r^{2}=4
on
B^{2}
, we have
\Delta b=-2c_{V}^{2}
and consequently,
\Delta(u-b)\geq 0
.
Moreover,
\partial X_{L}=\partial B^{2}\times S^{1}_{L}
, so
r=1
on

\partial X_{L}
. Since
\operatorname{tr}\phi=0
, both
u
and
b
vanish there.
Thus
\operatorname{tr}(u-b)=0
, and the weak maximum principle
[
14
, Theorem 8.1]
gives

u\leq b\leq\frac{c_{V}^{2}}{2}.

Hence
\left|\left|\phi\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}\leq\frac{c_{V}}{\sqrt{2}}
,
which proves
Eq.

3.7
.

We now prove
Eq.

3.8
. Since

\psi=\partial_{z}\phi
, we have
\Delta\psi=V^{\prime\prime}(\phi)\psi
.
Furthermore,

\psi\in L^{2}_{2,D,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})
. Applying the uniform
Dirichlet estimate
Corollary

2.9

to
\Delta
, with
s=0
, gives

\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\Delta\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}

for all sufficiently small
L
. By

Eq.

3.7
, the image of
\phi
is contained in a
fixed compact interval.
Because
V^{\prime\prime}
is continuous, we have
\left|\left|V^{\prime\prime}(\phi)\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}\leq c
.
Taking both together:

\displaystyle\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}

\displaystyle\leq c\left|\left|V^{\prime\prime}(\phi)\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\phi\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{1}(X_{L})}\leq cL^{1/2},

where the last inequality follows from

Eq.

3.6
.

By the estimate for the integration operator

Proposition

2.4
and the Sobolev embedding

Eq.

2.6
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|I(\psi)\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}

\displaystyle\leq L\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}\leq cL^{1/2}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq cL.

This proves
Eq.

3.9
.

Finally, since
\bar{\phi}=\phi-I(\psi)
,

\left|\left|\bar{\phi}\right|\right|_{C^{0}(B^{2})}\leq\left|\left|\phi\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}+\left|\left|I(\psi)\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}\leq c.

This proves
Eq.

3.10
.
∎

3.2
The linear operator

In this section we prove an estimate for the inverse of the linear operator
\mathscr{L}
from
3.4
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Fix
K>0
. There exist constants
L_{0}>0
and
c>0
such that the
following holds. For every
0<L<L_{0}
, every
z
-independent function

\bar{\phi}
satisfying
\left|\left|\bar{\phi}\right|\right|_{C^{0}(B^{2})}\leq K
,
and every

\psi\in L^{2}_{2,D,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\mathscr{L}\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}.

(3.12)

Here,
c
may depend on
K
and
V
, but it is independent of
L
,

\bar{\phi}
, and
\psi
.

Proof.

Choose
c_{V}>0
such that
V^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq-c_{V}^{2}
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\psi
has zero trace, integration by parts for Sobolev functions gives

\displaystyle-\int_{X_{L}}\psi\mathscr{L}\psi

\displaystyle=\int_{X_{L}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+\int_{X_{L}}V^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\phi})\psi^{2}\geq\int_{X_{L}}(\partial_{z}\psi)^{2}-c_{V}^{2}\int_{X_{L}}\psi^{2}.

Since
\psi
has mean zero on every circle
\{x\}\times S^{1}_{L}
, the circle-direction Poincaré estimate from the proof of

Proposition

2.2
gives

\int_{X_{L}}\psi^{2}\leq cL^{2}\int_{X_{L}}(\partial_{z}\psi)^{2}.

Consequently,

-\int_{X_{L}}\psi\mathscr{L}\psi\geq\left(1-cc_{V}^{2}L^{2}\right)\int_{X_{L}}(\partial_{z}\psi)^{2}.

After decreasing
L_{0}
if necessary, we therefore have

-\int_{X_{L}}\psi\mathscr{L}\psi\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{X_{L}}(\partial_{z}\psi)^{2}\geq c^{-1}L^{-2}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}^{2},

where the final inequality is another application of the circle-direction Poincaré estimate.
On the other hand, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\left|\int_{X_{L}}\psi\mathscr{L}\psi\right|\leq\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\left|\left|\mathscr{L}\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}.

Combining the preceding two estimates gives

c^{-1}L^{-2}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}^{2}\leq\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\left|\left|\mathscr{L}\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}.

If
\psi=0
, the desired estimate is immediate. Otherwise, dividing by

\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}
gives

\displaystyle\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq cL^{2}\left|\left|\mathscr{L}\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}.

(3.13)

We now apply the uniform Dirichlet estimate

Corollary

2.9
to the operator

\Delta
, with
s=0
. Since
\psi
has zero trace and mean zero, it
gives
\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\Delta\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}
.
By the definition of
\mathscr{L}
,

\Delta\psi=\mathscr{L}\psi+V^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\phi})\psi
.
Moreover, since
\left|\left|\bar{\phi}\right|\right|_{C^{0}(B^{2})}\leq K
and
V^{\prime\prime}
is
continuous,

\left|\left|V^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\phi})\right|\right|_{C^{0}(B^{2})}\leq\sup_{\lvert t\rvert\leq K}|V^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq c.

Using
Eq.

3.13
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}

\displaystyle\leq c\left(\left|\left|\mathscr{L}\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}+\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\right)\leq c\left(1+L^{2}\right)\left|\left|\mathscr{L}\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\mathscr{L}\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})},

which proves the claim.
∎

Although
\mathscr{L}
preserves the mean-zero subspace, it will be
convenient to regard it as an operator
\mathscr{L}:L^{2}_{2,D,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})\rightarrow L^{2}(X_{L})
.
Later, this will allow us to apply its left inverse to nonlinear terms which need not have mean zero.
The following is a direct consequence of
Proposition

2.10
:

Corollary 3.14
.

For all
M>0
, there exist constants
L_{0}>0
and
c>0
such that the
following holds. For every
0<L<L_{0}
and every
z
-independent function

\bar{\phi}
satisfying
\left|\left|\bar{\phi}\right|\right|_{C^{0}(B^{2})}\leq M
,
there exists a bounded left inverse

S=S_{\bar{\phi},L}:L^{2}(X_{L})\rightarrow L^{2}_{2,D,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})

of
\mathscr{L}
satisfying

and

.
Here,

may depend on
M
and
V
, but it is independent of

and

\bar{\phi}
.

3.3
The nonlinear terms

We now prove an estimate for the non-linear terms of
3.4
:

Proposition 3.15
.

Fix
M,K>0
. There exist constants
L_{0}>0
and
c>0
such that the
following holds. Let
0<L<L_{0}
, let
\bar{\phi}
be independent of
z

and satisfy

\left|\left|\bar{\phi}\right|\right|_{C^{0}(B^{2})}\leq M
, and let

\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in L^{2}_{2,D,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})
satisfy

\left|\left|\psi_{i}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq KL^{1/2}
for
i=1,2
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\left|\mathscr{N}(\psi_{1})-\mathscr{N}(\psi_{2})\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq cL\left|\left|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}.

(3.16)

Here,
c
may depend on
M
,
K
, and
V
, but it is independent of

L
,
\bar{\phi}
,
\psi_{1}
, and
\psi_{2}
.

Proof.

By
Propositions

2.4
and
2.6
, we have

\left|\left|I(\psi_{i})\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}\leq cL^{1/2}\left|\left|\psi_{i}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq cKL

for
i=1,2
.
The same estimates give

\left|\left|I(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}\leq cL^{1/2}\left|\left|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}
.

After decreasing
L_{0}
if necessary, we may assume that

cKL\leq 1
. The functions
\bar{\phi}+I(\psi_{i})
therefore take values
in the fixed compact interval
[-M-1,M+1]
.
By the definition of
\mathscr{N}
we have:

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}(\psi_{1})-\mathscr{N}(\psi_{2})={}

\displaystyle(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})\left(V^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\phi})-V^{\prime\prime}\bigl(\bar{\phi}+I(\psi_{1})\bigr)\right)+\psi_{2}\left(V^{\prime\prime}\bigl(\bar{\phi}+I(\psi_{2})\bigr)-V^{\prime\prime}\bigl(\bar{\phi}+I(\psi_{1})\bigr)\right).

By the mean value theorem and the fact that
V^{\prime\prime\prime}
is bounded on
[-M-1,M+1]
, the
L^{2}
-norm of the first term is bounded
by

c\left|\left|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\left|\left|I(\psi_{1})\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}
.
The preceding estimates therefore bound it by

cL\left|\left|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}
.

Similarly, the
L^{2}
-norm of the second term is bounded by

c\left|\left|\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\left|\left|I(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}
.
Since

\left|\left|\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq\left|\left|\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq KL^{1/2}
,
this term is also bounded by

cL\left|\left|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}
, which proves the claim.
∎

3.4
An application of the contraction mapping theorem

In this section, we conclude the proof of
Theorem

3.3
.
So far, we have shown two things:
(1) we showed in
Section

3.1
that any solution
\psi
to the non-linear equation
3.4
must satisfy a certain smallness estimate;
(2) taking together the results from
Sections

3.2
and
3.3
, we have that the map
S\mathscr{N}
is a contraction on a small ball around
0
.
Solutions of
3.4
give rise to fixed points.
We already know that
\psi=0
solves the equation, so taking everything together we will find that
\psi=0
is the
unique
solution, proving the claim.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

Assume that
\phi\in L^{2}_{3,D}(X_{L})
satisfies

Eq.

3.2
, and set
\psi:=\partial_{z}\phi
.
Then
\psi\in L^{2}_{2,D,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})
and satisfies

3.4
.
By
Proposition

3.5
, there are constants

M,K>0
, independent of
L
and
\phi
, such that

\left|\left|\bar{\phi}\right|\right|_{C^{0}(X_{L})}\leq M,\qquad\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq KL^{1/2}.

For
L
sufficiently small, let
S=S_{\bar{\phi},L}
be the left-inverse
from
Corollary

3.14
. Define

B_{K,L}:=\left\{f\in L^{2}_{2,D,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L}):\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq KL^{1/2}\right\},\qquad F(f):=-S\mathscr{N}(f).

Then:

1.

The map
F
maps
B_{K,L}
into itself for
L
small enough.

Indeed, for
f\in B_{K,L}
,

\left|\left|F(f)\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\mathscr{N}(f)\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq cL\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq cKL^{3/2},

where we used

Corollaries

3.14
and
3.15

and the fact that
\mathscr{N}(0)=0
.
This is at most
KL^{1/2}
for
L
small enough.

2.

The map
F:B_{K,L}\rightarrow B_{K,L}
is a contraction for
L

small enough.

For
f_{1},f_{2}\in B_{K,L}
,

\left|\left|F(f_{1})-F(f_{2})\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq c\left|\left|\mathscr{N}(f_{1})-\mathscr{N}(f_{2})\right|\right|_{L^{2}(X_{L})}\leq cL\left|\left|f_{1}-f_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}.

After making
L
smaller if necessary, the factor
cL
is less
than
1
.

3.

The ball
B_{K,L}
is a closed subset of the Hilbert space

L^{2}_{2,D,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})
and is therefore complete.
By the Banach fixed point theorem, it contains a unique fixed point
of
F
.
Since
\mathscr{N}(0)=0
, the function
0
is a fixed point, and
hence it is the unique fixed point in
B_{K,L}
.

4.

By assumption,
0=\mathscr{L}(\psi)+\mathscr{N}(\psi)
.
Applying
S
and using
S\mathscr{L}=\operatorname{Id}
gives
\psi=-S\mathscr{N}(\psi)=F(\psi)
.
Thus
\psi
is a fixed point of
F
.
By the choice of
K
,
\psi\in B_{K,L}
, so the preceding point
gives
\psi=0
.
Therefore
\partial_{z}\phi=0
, and
\phi
is independent of the

z
-direction.
∎

The linear terms

\displaystyle\quad\left|\left|B_{\mathscr{N}}(\psi_{1},u_{1})-B_{\mathscr{N}}(\psi_{2},u_{2})\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{5/2}(\partial X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{3/2}(\partial X_{L})}

\displaystyle\leq cL^{-1/2}\left[L\left|\left|\psi_{1}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})}\left|\left|u_{1}-u_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}+L\left|\left|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})}\left|\left|u_{2}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq cLd,

where we used

Eq.

4.23
,
Eq.

4.24
,
Eq.

4.25
and the assumptions of the
proposition.

Combining the interior and boundary estimates proves the claim.
∎

4.3
An application of the contraction mapping theorem

In this section we will conclude the proof of

Theorem

1.5
, and this will be done in analogy
with
Section

3.4
.
We will show that the map

-T(\mathscr{N},B_{\mathscr{N}})
is a contraction on a small ball,
showing that
\psi=0
and
u=0
is the only solution to the set of
equations
Eq.

4.1
and
Eq.

4.2

near zero
.
In other words, a solution near
\phi=\phi_{0}
and
A=0
must be
independent of the
z
-direction.

We take one last step to show that a solution that is independent of the

z
-direction must be the homogeneous black string.
Taking both together, this implies that the unique solution to

Eq.

1.2
near
\phi=\phi_{0}
and

A=0
is the homogeneous black string.

We begin by proving this last step, namely that a solution that is
independent of the
z
-direction must be the homogeneous black string:

Proposition 4.26
.

Let
\phi\in L^{2}_{4}(X_{L}),A\in L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})
be

\operatorname{SO}(3)
-invariant solutions to

Eq.

1.2
that are independent of
the
z
-direction and satisfying the boundary conditions

Eq.

1.4
.
Then
\phi=\phi_{0}
and
A=0
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
1
, Section III.D]
.
∎

We are now ready to complete the proof of

Theorem

1.5
.
By virtue of the previous proposition, it suffices to show that

\phi_{z}=0
and
A_{z}=0
for a solution
\phi,A
near the homogeneous
black string.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Let
\phi,A
be a solution satisfying the hypotheses of

Theorem

1.5
, and write

\psi:=\phi_{z}
and
u:=A_{z}
so that
(\psi,u)\in L^{2}_{3,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{2,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})
.
As in the proof of
Proposition

4.4
we find

\left|\left|\bar{\phi}-\phi_{0}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{4}(X_{L})}\leq\left|\left|\phi-\phi_{0}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{4}(X_{L})},\qquad\left|\left|\bar{A}\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})}\leq\left|\left|A\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})}.

Thus, by choosing the constant
c
in the statement of the theorem
sufficiently small, independently of the solution, the zero modes
satisfy the assumptions of
Proposition

4.4
and

Proposition

4.9
.
After making
c
smaller if necessary, the smallness
assumptions in the theorem also give

\left|\left|(\psi,u)\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq L^{1/2}.

Let
T=T_{\bar{\phi},\bar{A},L}
be the left-inverse of

(\mathscr{L},B_{\mathscr{L}})
from

Corollary

4.8
. We consider the closed ball

B:=\left\{(\eta,\xi)\in L^{2}_{3,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{2,\mathrm{mean}-0}(X_{L}):\left|\left|(\eta,\xi)\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq L^{1/2}\right\}

and define

\mathcal{F}(\eta,\xi):=-T\left(\mathscr{N}(\eta,\xi),B_{\mathscr{N}}(\eta,\xi)\right).

We first check that
\mathcal{F}
maps
B
to itself. Since

\mathscr{N}(0,0)=0
and
B_{\mathscr{N}}(0,0)=0
, for

(\eta,\xi)\in B
we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|\mathcal{F}(\eta,\xi)\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\left|(\mathscr{N},B_{\mathscr{N}})(\eta,\xi)-(\mathscr{N},B_{\mathscr{N}})(0,0)\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{1}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{5/2}(\partial X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{3/2}(\partial X_{L})}

\displaystyle\leq CL\left|\left|(\eta,\xi)\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}\leq CL^{3/2},

where we used
Corollary

4.8
and

Proposition

4.9
.
For sufficiently small
L
, this is bounded by
L^{1/2}
.

Similarly, if
(\eta_{i},\xi_{i})\in B
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\left|\mathcal{F}(\eta_{1},\xi_{1})-\mathcal{F}(\eta_{2},\xi_{2})\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}

\displaystyle\leq CL\left|\left|(\eta_{1},\xi_{1})-(\eta_{2},\xi_{2})\right|\right|_{L^{2}_{3}(X_{L})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(X_{L})}.

Thus, after making
L_{0}
smaller if necessary,
\mathcal{F}
is a
contraction on
B
. By the Banach fixed point theorem,
(0,0)
is
its unique fixed point in
B
.
Differentiating the equations and the boundary condition satisfied
by
\phi,A
gives

(\mathscr{L},B_{\mathscr{L}})(\psi,u)+(\mathscr{N},B_{\mathscr{N}})(\psi,u)=0.

Applying the left-inverse
T
and using

T(\mathscr{L},B_{\mathscr{L}})=\operatorname{Id}
shows that

(\psi,u)=-T\left(\mathscr{N}(\psi,u),B_{\mathscr{N}}(\psi,u)\right)=\mathcal{F}(\psi,u).

Hence
(\psi,u)
is a fixed point of
\mathcal{F}
in
B
.
By uniqueness of the fixed point,
(\psi,u)=(0,0)
, so

\phi=\bar{\phi}
and
A=\bar{A}
.

Finally,
Proposition

4.26
gives

\bar{\phi}=\phi_{0}
and
\bar{A}=0
, which completes the proof.
∎
