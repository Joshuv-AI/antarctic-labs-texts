Statistical optimization of crumb rubber modified bitumen performance through material blending analysis
Authors: Naeem Aziz Memon, Ahmed Suliman B Ali, Nur Izzi Md Yusoff, Allam Musbah Al Allam, Haytham F Isleem, Mohammad Khishe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a comprehensive investigation into the optimization of crumb rubber modified bitumen (CRMB) performance by systematically analysing the effects of blending parameters and material characteristics. The effects of blending temperature, mixing speed, blending time, and mixer type on the viscosity and stability of CRMB were systematically evaluated using a fixed 40/60 penetration grade binder and 15% CR-A (ambient ground, #40 mesh) in the first phase of this study. However, the influence of three bitumen grade (40/60, 70/100, 100/150), two crumb rubber types (CR-A and CR-B), three mesh sizes (#30, #40, #50), and four CR contents (10%, 15%, 17.5%, and 20%) was evaluated with blending protocol devised initially. Viscosity, penetration, and softening point were measured, yielding significant improvements; 15% CR addition in the 40/60 binder reduced penetration from 48 to 38 dmm and increased the softening point from 52.5 °C to 58.4 °C. Chemical interactions were assessed through SARA fraction analysis, revealing a strong inverse correlation between aromatic content and final viscosity with r = -0.78. The blending process was further optimized using factorial design and statistical analysis, identifying high shear mixing at 180 °C for 90 min (2000–3000 rpm) as the ideal condition. This setting produced homogenous, stable blends, with equilibrium viscosity values for the tested combinations ranging from 2.7 to 3.6 Pa·s. Multi-factor ANOVA confirmed significant effects (p < 0.001) of blending temperature, CR type, and bitumen grade. Regression models developed for the materials tested achieved strong predictive power (R2 = 0.85), highlighting that higher aromatic content reduced viscosity, while resins and asphaltenes contributed to increased stiffness. The study demonstrates that performance optimization requires not only controlled blending conditions but also informed material selection. This dual approach provides a reliable foundation for scalable, efficient, and sustainable CRMB production.Keywords: Crumb rubber, CR; CRMB; Bitumen; Asphalt rubber; Blending, SARA, storage stabilitySubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The performance of asphalt is based on several parameters and properties of the materials’ elements1. The viscoelastic and thermoplastic properties of bituminous materials make them especially sensitive to changes in temperature, as well as to extreme and repetitive loading and exposure to UV radiation2. This study shows how the incorporation of crumb rubber (CR) with polyurethane (PU) affects the rheological and aging properties of modified bitumen3. In the same way, Asphalt production facilities execute CR modification using various techniques, which may involve one or multiple passes with mixing equipment and mills4. Among the various bitumen additives, crumb rubber (CR) sourced from discarded tires offers notable economic and environmental advantages. The effectiveness of CR modification is influenced by several factors, including temperature, mixing speed, duration, and particle size3. Therefore, the accumulation of end-of-life tires (ELTs) poses a significant environmental challenge due to their non-biodegradability, high flammability, and large landfill volume. Globally, millions of tires reach the end of their service life each year, intensifying the problem of solid waste management5. One sustainable solution gaining attention is the incorporation of crumb rubber (CR), derived from waste tires, into asphalt binders to produce crumb rubber modified bitumen (CRMB). This approach not only recycles tire waste but also enhances the mechanical and environmental performance of asphalt pavements6. CRMB has emerged as a viable alternative to conventional polymer-modified binders due to its proven ability to improve resistance to rutting, fatigue cracking, and thermal cracking7. In addition to superior mechanical properties, CRMB increases elasticity, stiffness, and resistance to oxidation and aging, contributing to longer pavement life and reduced maintenance needs8. Furthermore, CRMB has been shown to reduce tire-pavement noise and rolling resistance, offering environmental benefits such as lower fuel consumption and decreased greenhouse gas emissions9. These multifaceted advantages make CRMB an attractive option for sustainable pavement construction.

The use of CRMB is a prime example of waste valorisation, but it is important to situate it within the broader research landscape of circular materials in pavement engineering. CRMB is part of a larger family of research aimed at diverting diverse waste streams from landfills to enhance pavement performance. For instance, recent studies have demonstrated significant progress in incorporating other major waste streams, such as waste plastics (PET, LDPE), recycled glass, and construction/demolition waste, into asphalt mixtures10,11. This broad field of ‘circular materials’ seeks to create more sustainable, durable, and cost-effective infrastructure solutions, moving the industry towards a true circular economy12,13. While all these materials show promise, CRMB remains one of the most widely studied options, yet it still suffers from the persistent technical challenges, such as storage stability and blend optimization, that our paper directly addresses14,15.

Despite its advantages, the widespread adoption of CRMB faces several technical challenges. One of the primary obstacles is the inherent incompatibility between rubber and bitumen due to their differing molecular structures, which often leads to phase separation during blending and storage16. Without precise control over blending conditions, such as temperature and shear energy, rubber particles may settle or degrade, compromising the homogeneity and stability of the blend17.This incompatibility stems from differences in density and chemical polarity between the components. The non-polar, low-density rubber particles have a strong tendency to separate from the denser, complex polar-non-polar bitumen matrix, leading to phase separation and sedimentation during high-temperature storage18. To combat this, much of the prior research has focused on chemical modification, such as adding sulfur to promote vulcanization and create a stable chemical network, or by adding compatibilizers like Styrene-Butadiene-Styrene (SBS) to bridge the polarity gap19. While effective, these methods add significant cost and chemical complexity, driving the need for optimized physical blending protocols that can create a stable, homogeneous dispersion through mechanical energy and thermal control alone.

Another significant barrier is the lack of standardized blending protocols across the industry. Variations in CR source, particle size, base bitumen grade, and mixing equipment have led to inconsistent results and limited reproducibility20.Much of the existing literature has focused on comparing a few pre-defined recipes or modification methods (e.g., comparing ambient vs. cryogenic rubber, or one blending time vs. another)21. While valuable, this comparative approach often fails to capture the complex, synergistic interactions among the blending parameters themselves (the effect of time depends on temperature and shear energy). This gap has hindered the development of a truly ‘optimized’ and universally applicable blending protocol. While foundational studies have provided critical insights by comparing the final properties of CRMB produced under different blending protocols, the statistical significance and interaction effects of individual process variables often remain underexplored. For example, discerning the precise impact of temperature versus blending duration can be challenging without a statistical framework. Our study rectifies this by employing a multi-factor ANOVA and regression analysis. This approach allows us to move beyond comparative observations by rigorously quantifying the main effects (p < 0.001) and synergistic interactions of each blending parameter, providing a more robust, data-driven basis for process optimization22.However, optimizing the physical blending process alone is insufficient, as it ignores the fundamental chemical interactions that govern performance. The modification of bitumen with CR is primarily a thermo-chemical swelling process: the rubber particles absorb the light components of the bitumen (the aromatic fraction), causing them to swell. This, in turn, concentrates the remaining structural components (the Resins and Asphaltenes), stiffening the binder matrix23. Therefore, a blending protocol that is ‘optimal’ for a high-Aromatic (swelling-rich) binder may completely fail for a high-Asphaltene (structure-rich) binder. This chemical dependency is the critical, often-overlooked variable. Moreover, the influence of bitumen chemistry, particularly the role of SARA (Saturates, Aromatics, Resins, and Asphaltenes) fractions, remains underexplored in relation to CR interaction mechanisms24.A more integrated understanding of physical parameters (CR morphology), chemical composition (SARA fractions), and blending kinetics is essential for achieving reproducible, stable, and high-performance CRMB. Addressing these gaps is crucial for developing reliable guidelines that can support the large-scale implementation of CRMB technologies25.

The primary objective of this study is to optimize the production of CRMB by evaluating the effects of key blending parameters and material variables. Specifically, it aims to determine the influence of blending temperature, mixing time, rotational speed, and mixer type on CRMB performance, using a 40/60 penetration-grade bitumen and ambient-ground CR-A as a reference formulation. An optimized blending protocol is established based on viscosity and physical performance. The second objective is to assess the adaptability of the optimized protocol by applying it to different bitumen grades (40/60, 70/100, 100/150), CR types (CR-A and Aquablast-treated CR-B), mesh sizes, and dosage levels. Conventional binder tests (penetration, softening point, viscosity) and SARA fraction analysis are employed to understand the interactions between materials and their impact on CRMB behaviour, while prior research has indeed identified the influence of bitumen chemistry on CRMB performance, the translation of this understanding into quantitative, predictive models for mix design remains a key research gap. Our study addresses this by moving beyond correlational analysis to develop a multiple linear regression model (R2 = 0.85) that utilizes S.A.R.A. fractions as a direct input for predicting CRMB properties. The novelty, therefore, lies in transforming S.A.R.A. analysis from a descriptive characterization technique into a validated, predictive tool for material selection and formulation.Statistical methods, including multi-factor ANOVA and multiple linear regression, are used to quantify the influence of various parameters and to develop predictive models for CRMB performance. This combined experimental-statistical approach enables the systematic optimization of both blending procedures and material selection, thereby advancing the development of reproducible and scalable CRMB formulations for practical use in sustainable road construction26.

Blending protocol

Two types of mixers were employed to simulate different shear energy inputs: a High Shear Mixer (Silverson L4RT) and a Mechanical Stirrer (low shear, simulating field-scale conditions). The inclusion of both mixer types was a key aspect of the experimental design, intended to bridge the gap between idealized laboratory conditions and the practical realities of industrial-scale blending, thereby addressing scalability challenges from the outset. Blending temperatures were precisely maintained at either 180–200 °C (± 2 °C), and rotational speeds of 1000, 2000, and 3000 rpm were used to represent variable shear energy input28,29.

Laboratory testing

For all experiments, a minimum of three replicates were prepared for each unique blend. The average values are reported in all tables and figures. Standard deviations were consistently low, and the primary sources of variance were effectively captured and quantified by the multi-factor ANOVA.

Conventional characterization

Viscosity measurements were conducted at 177 °C using a Brookfield DV-II viscometer. This temperature is explicitly specified by ASTM D6114 (‘Standard Specification for Asphalt-Rubber Binder’) for measuring the rotational viscosity of CRMB, in contrast to the 135 °C Superpave standard for neat binders. Readings were taken at 30-minute intervals until viscosity equilibrium was achieved. Equilibrium was defined as the point where the viscosity values from two consecutive 30-minute readings stabilized within ± 0.05 Pa·s of each other. Selected CRMB samples from the material variation study were also subjected to conventional binder tests to evaluate their physical behaviour. Penetration was measured according to EN 1426, and softening point was determined using the ring-and-ball method as per EN 1427, providing insights into the temperature susceptibility of the CRMBs. These conventional tests complemented the viscosity trends, offering comparative insights into binder stiffness and thermal performance31,32. SARA analysis

SARA (Saturates, Aromatics, Resins, Asphaltenes) fractions represent the four most common chemical components of asphalt binders, and their proportions fundamentally influence the macroscopic properties of the binder and its interaction with modifiers33. This chemical fingerprint of the bitumen is crucial for understanding how different bitumen grades interact differently with crumb rubber, providing a mechanistic basis for the observed performance variations. For this study, SARA fraction quantification was conducted using an IATROSCAN MK-6s analyser, which employs thin-layer chromatography with flame ionization detection (TLC-FID). The analysis rigorously followed the procedural framework outlined in ASTM D4124-09. This method was selected for its high reproducibility and efficiency in separating the four component fractions34,35.

SARA analysis revealed significant compositional differences among the base bitumen grades, which are critical for understanding their interaction with crumb rubber and the resulting CRMB properties36. The SARA fractions of bitumen (Saturates, Aromatics, Resins, Asphaltenes) are fundamental chemical components that influence its macroscopic properties and interaction with modifiers37,38. This chemical fingerprint of the bitumen provides a mechanistic basis for explaining why different bitumen grades interact differently with crumb rubber and exhibit varying performance characteristics. Statistical analysis framework

To move beyond descriptive results and quantitatively assess the influence of various parameters, a robust statistical analysis framework was adopted. This approach provides a rigorous basis for testing hypotheses about the means of different groups (e.g., different temperatures, different mixer types) and determining whether observed differences are statistically significant, rather than merely visually apparent. Multi-factor Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was employed to determine the main effects and interaction effects of blending temperature, mixing speed, blending time, mixer type, bitumen grade, CR type, mesh size, and CR content on key CRMB properties, including viscosity, penetration, and softening point. This method is particularly well-suited for the study’s factorial experimental design, allowing for the quantification of the degree of influence of each factor and the uncovering of synergistic interactions between them (e.g., whether the effect of CR type is dependent on bitumen grade)39. Furthermore, Multiple Linear Regression was utilized to develop predictive models. These models correlate CRMB properties (e.g., equilibrium viscosity, penetration, softening point) with continuous variables such as blending parameters and, crucially, the percentages of SARA fractions in the base bitumen. This allows for the development of predictive tools where SARA composition can be an input to determine optimal CR dosage and type for a desired performance outcome, moving towards a more scientifically driven mix design.

Results and discussion

The Results and Discussion are presented in line with the study’s 2-phase approach. First, we address the optimization of the blending parameters (Phase I), focusing on the mixing conditions as suggested by the reviewer. This establishes a statistically validated, optimal protocol. Second, we apply this protocol as a consistent baseline to conduct the study’s main statistical analysis (Phase II). This statistical framework is the ‘main purpose,’ as it allows us to quantify the significant interactions between material chemistry (SARA) and material properties (CR type, bitumen grade) under these optimized conditions.

Impact of blending parameters on CRMB viscosity

The viscosity development in CRMB was strongly influenced by blending temperature, mixing duration, and device type. A detailed comparison of the Brookfield viscosities of CRMB samples produced with high and low shear mixers at specified blending times and temperatures are presented in Figs. 2 and 3.

CRMB blend viscosities produced with high and low shear mixers at 180 °C.

CRMB blend viscosities produced with high and low shear mixers at 200 °C.

The blending temperature and mixer type were found to be critical factors in determining CRMB viscosity and stability, as detailed in Figs. 2 and 3. At 180 °C, a steady and gradual increase in viscosity was observed. Using a high shear mixer, equilibrium was consistently reached within 90 min, yielding final viscosity values in the 3.5–3.7 Pa·s range. This condition ensures thermal stability, offering an ideal balance between sufficient rubber swelling and the prevention of thermal degradation40.

In contrast, blending at a higher temperature of 200 °C accelerated initial viscosity development, achieving equilibrium faster (within 60–90 min)41. However, a critical degradation threshold was identified: extended mixing beyond 120 min at this higher temperature caused a significant drop in viscosity. This finding establishes a definitive degradation threshold, indicating that 200 °C poses a significant risk to CRMB integrity if mixing is prolonged42. This aligns with previous work suggesting the thermal breaking down of CR at high temperatures31.

High shear mixing consistently yielded more uniform and thermally stable blends than mechanical stirring. Mechanical stirrers, representing low-shear environments, required significantly longer durations (up to 180 min) to achieve comparable results. This highlights the limitations of low-shear environments for effective rubber-bitumen interaction43. While less efficient, mechanical stirring was found to be an acceptable alternative if operated at high speeds (3000 rpm) and for extended durations (≥ 120 min).

Based on these findings, an optimal blending protocol of high shear mixing at 180 °C for 90 min (2000–3000 rpm) is proposed44. This protocol consistently produced homogeneous, stable blends with equilibrium viscosities above 3.5 Pa·s, validating that sufficient shear energy and thermal control are essential for effective rubber dispersion45. It is important to note, however, that a key limitation of this study is the direct applicability of its findings to industrial production. Significant differences in energy transfer and thermal profiles are anticipated between laboratory- and industrial-scale mixers. As such, the optimized parameters identified herein should be considered a foundational starting point, requiring further process validation and modification during scale-up46.Table 6 summarizes the optimal and acceptable blending conditions identified.

All optimized viscosity values, presented in Figs. 4, 5 and 6, fell within the acceptable 1.5 to 5.0 Pa·s range per ASTM D6114. As shown in the results, applying the optimized protocol revealed that viscosity outcomes were highly dependent on the intrinsic properties of the CR and the base binder47.

A clear performance difference was observed between the two CR types. CR-A, characterized by its rough, porous texture and higher ash content, consistently facilitated mechanical anchoring and more aggressive swelling48. This led to higher peak viscosity values across all bitumen grades (3.6 Pa·s for 40/60). In contrast, the smoother, lower-ash CR-B promoted more uniform particle dispersion and demonstrated superior storage homogeneity, resulting in slightly lower viscosities (3.3 Pa·s for 40/60)49.

Significantly, a key interaction was observed: the performance difference between CR-A and CR-B became less pronounced as the bitumen binder grade softened (moving from 40/60 to 100/150). This phenomenon is directly linked to the base bitumen’s chemical composition, specifically its SARA fractions50.

The softer binders (100/150) contain a much higher aromatic content (50%) compared to the stiffer 40/60 grade (38%). As aromatic content increases, the bitumen acts as a stronger solvent, leading to greater swelling of the rubber particles51. This heightened chemical swelling appears to dominate the modification mechanism. Consequently, the physical interlocking provided by CR-A’s rougher surface, which is critical in stiffer, low-aromatic binders, becomes less significant.

This intricate interaction, where increased aromatics enhance swelling but may simultaneously weaken the binder matrix by diluting structural components, highlights the need for a “smart material selection” strategy. Statistical analysis of key performance indicators

The application of ANOVA provided measurable proof of the statistical influence of various blending settings and material characteristics on CRMB performance. This technique enabled precise quantification of the significance of each aspect (p < 0.001) and allowed for a comparison of their relative importance, transitioning from qualitative findings to robust, data-driven conclusions. ANOVA results for blending parameters indicated that temperature, mixing speed, blending duration, and mixer type exerted statistically significant main effects on CRMB viscosity, penetration, and softening point53. Temperature consistently exhibited the most significant impact on viscosity, quantitatively validating that the observed variations between 180 °C and 200 °C were not attributable to random chance. The investigation found substantial interaction effects, including the dependency of blending time’s impact on temperature, and the effect of mixing speed varies by mixer type. These interactions underscore the intricate dynamics of processing circumstances23. It is given in Fig. 7.

Multiple linear regression models were developed to explore the predictive power of SARA fractions on CRMB properties. These models demonstrated a statistically significant relationship between the chemical composition of the base bitumen and the final CRMB performance. For instance, a regression model for viscosity as a function of SARA fractions yielded a high R-squared value, indicating that the SARA composition could explain a substantial proportion of the variability in viscosity54,55. The coefficients for each SARA fraction quantified their specific influence: an increase in aromatic content, for example, was consistently correlated with a reduction in final CRMB viscosity, even as it promoted rubber swelling. This quantitative relationship underscores that by understanding and potentially tailoring the SARA composition of the base bitumen, one can anticipate and design for specific CRMB properties. This represents a significant step towards a more scientific, rather than empirical, approach to CRMB design. The critical findings are highlighted earlier in Fig. 7. To ensure the integrity and stability of the regression model, a multicollinearity diagnostic was performed on the SARA fraction predictors. Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) were calculated for each predictor, with all resulting values falling well below the conservative threshold of 5. This provides strong evidence for the absence of problematic multicollinearity. Consequently, the model’s coefficients are considered robust, and the individual effects of each SARA fraction on CRMB properties can be interpreted with confidence.

The SARA analysis revealed increasing aromatic content and decreasing asphaltenes from 40/60 to 100/150 grades. Higher aromatic content facilitated greater CR swelling but resulted in softer final blends. A clear trend was observed: increased aromatics correlated with lower final viscosities (r = -0.78), while resins and asphaltenes had a stiffening effect. The significant interaction effect observed in Fig. 9, where the performance difference between CR-A and CR-B diminishes in softer binders, can be explained by the interplay of physical and chemical modification mechanisms. In stiff binders like 40/60, which have lower aromatic content, the rough surface texture of CR-A provides a significant advantage through enhanced mechanical interlocking. However, in softer binders like 100/150, the SARA composition is fundamentally different, containing a much higher percentage of aromatics (50% vs. 38%). This high aromatic content acts as a powerful solvent, promoting significant swelling of the rubber particles for both CR types. In this scenario, the chemical swelling mechanism starts to dominate over the physical interlocking effect. As a result, the initial physical differences between the rough CR-A and smooth CR-B become less critical to the final viscosity, causing their performance to converge.

The three-way ANOVA for softening point, visualized in Fig. 10, revealed a significant interaction effect between CR content, mesh size, and CR type (p = 0.041). The plot illustrates that the stiffening effect of increasing CR content is most pronounced for the rough-textured CR-A, particularly when using finer mesh sizes (40 and #50). For these combinations, the softening point increases substantially as the CR content moves from 10% to 20%. In contrast, for the smoother CR-B, the softening point shows a more modest increase with higher CR content, and this effect is far less dependent on the mesh size. This significant three-way interaction implies that optimizing for high-temperature performance (a higher softening point) is not merely a matter of increasing the rubber dosage. Instead, it requires a synergistic selection of rubber type and particle size to maximize the stiffening effect, with finer, rough-textured particles showing the most significant impact at higher concentrations.

A closer analysis of the regression plot in Fig. 11 indicates that while the model’s predictive power is strong across the board, its predictions are most accurate for the mid-range viscosity blends (i.e., those using the 70/100 bitumen). It is noted that SARA analysis provides a robust, simplified chemical fingerprint but is not a complete representation of bitumen’s complex molecular weight and structural architecture. The model shows a slight tendency to under-predict the viscosity for the stiffest 40/60 blends and slightly over-predict for the softest 100/150 blends. [248] This may be because the linear model, while robust, does not fully capture the complex, non-linear effects at the extremes: the powerful structural stiffening from high asphaltene content in the 40/60 grade, and the dual effect of swelling versus weakening from the high aromatic content in the 100/150 grade.

The statistical analysis culminating in this model provides a clear chemical rationale for the performance trends observed. The SARA fractions of the base bitumen are confirmed as strong predictors of the final CRMB properties. The model quantifies the intricate, inverse relationship between Aromatics and viscosity (r = -0.78). Aromatics act as solvents for rubber components56. and while this promotes greater CR swelling, it also softens the binder matrix, leading to decreased viscosity, particularly in the softer bitumen grades. Conversely, Resins and Asphaltenes showed strong positive correlations with stiffness, enhancing the structural integrity of the binder matrix57. and leading to lower penetration values. This strong predictive power supports the integration of SARA analysis into routine binder selection and quality control, a move advocated by recent studies to improve CRMB consistency and performance58.

A central finding of this study is that optimizing CRMB performance requires understanding the profound interplay between processing and material chemistry. The SARA analysis (Table 9) directly explains why the ideal material choice is context dependent. The superior stiffness from the rough-textured CR-A in the 40/60 binder is a direct result of mechanical interlocking dominating in a low-aromatic system. Conversely, in the softer, high-aromatic 100/150 binder, this physical advantage diminishes. The high aromatic content acts as a powerful solvent, making chemical swelling the primary driver of modification for both CR types59.

This finding shifts the engineering question from “What is the best crumb rubber?” to “What is the best crumb rubber for this specific bitumen chemistry?“. The developed regression model (R2 = 0.85) serves as the critical tool to answer this. It provides a quantitative framework to move beyond trial-and-error, allowing formulators to use SARA fractions as a proactive design input60. This integrated approach represents a more advanced and reliable framework for CRMB formulation.

The practical implications of this integrated framework are twofold. First, the study establishes a reliable benchmark protocol for industrial production: high shear mixing at 180 °C for 90 min. Second, it provides a “stepped approach” for flexible, high-quality production: engineers can use this optimized protocol as a consistent baseline, while tailoring material selection (CR-A for stiff binders, CR-B for softer conditions61. based on the specific SARA profile of the available bitumen to ensure quality.

The recommendation to integrate routine SARA analysis into quality control is central to this, transforming it from a descriptive test into a predictive tool for performance modelling32. By providing this robust, chemistry-driven framework, this study supports the wider adoption of CRMB, offering engineers and stakeholders a reliable method to balance performance and sustainability in road construction62.

Conclusion and future recommendations

This study successfully developed an integrated framework for optimizing CRMB by merging process mechanics with a predictive, chemistry-driven approach to material selection.

The primary contributions of this research are threefold:

Optimized blending protocol A statistically validated, optimal protocol of high shear mixing at 180 °C for 90 min was established. This benchmark condition was proven to produce homogeneous, stable blends by balancing sufficient rubber swelling with the prevention of thermal degradation.

Taken together, these findings move CRMB design beyond empirical, trial-and-error recipes. By leveraging SARA analysis as a proactive design input, this work provides a reliable, sustainable, and scalable model for producing high-performance CRMB. Future research should focus on validating these findings in long-term field performance and aging resistance, further integrating SARA analysis into complex industrial applications.

A key limitation of this study is the direct applicability of its findings to industrial production. Significant differences in energy transfer and thermal profiles are anticipated between laboratory- and industrial-scale mixers. As such, the optimized parameters identified herein should be considered a foundational starting point, requiring further process validation and modification during scale-up to ensure that the target dispersion quality and performance metrics are met. Furthermore, this framework should be validated for specific regional pavement conditions, such as evaluating the high-temperature rutting resistance of optimized blends for tropical climates.

Furthermore, the most critical next step is to subject these SARA-optimized blends to a full rheological and performance characterization, as expected by modern acceptance criteria. This advanced testing is essential to validate the practical accuracy of our predictive SARA model (R2 = 0.85)2. This would include DSR, MSCR, and BBR testing to determine their final Performance Grade (PG).In parallel, these blends must be subjected to long-term aging protocols (RTFOT and PAV) and stability tests, including ASTM D7173 for phase separation (storage stability) and thermal decomposition (TGA), to validate their performance against rutting, fatigue, aging, and practical storage standards.

This study’s scope was limited to material science and optimization. A full Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) to quantify the environmental impacts was not performed and should be considered in future research.
