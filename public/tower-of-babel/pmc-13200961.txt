Fibromyalgia, quality of life, and thiol/disulfide homeostasis in patients undergoing hemodialysis: A multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Hatice Ağir, Aydin Çifçi, Turgut Kültür, Hakki Öztürk, Duygu Felek, Aykut Haciömeroğlu, Artuner Varlibaş, Salim Neşelioğlu, Özcan Erel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) is a common but frequently overlooked cause of chronic pain in patients with chronic kidney disease, particularly those undergoing hemodialysis (HD). In addition to its impact on quality of life (QoL), FMS may be associated with oxidative stress pathways involved in chronic kidney disease pathophysiology. However, the relationship between FMS and thiol/disulfide homeostasis in HD patients remains unclear. This study examined the association between FMS, QoL, and thiol/disulfide parameters in a multicenter HD population. In this multicenter cross-sectional study, 126 HD patients (66 with FMS and 60 without FMS) from 2 dialysis centers in Turkey were evaluated between July and December 2024. FMS was diagnosed using the 2016 American College of Rheumatology criteria. Demographic data, laboratory parameters, QoL measures (short form-36 [SF-36], Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire [FIQ], Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire), and oxidative stress markers (native thiol, total thiol, disulfide, and related ratios) measured by automated spectrophotometry were analyzed. Group comparisons were performed using appropriate statistical tests. Correlations were assessed using Spearman analysis. Multivariable binary logistic regression was conducted to identify variables independently associated with FMS. Post hoc power analysis demonstrated adequate statistical power (87.3%, α = 0.05). Native thiol (242.35 ± 40.45 vs 225.36 ± 40.01 μmol/L, P = .019), total thiol (284.39 ± 46.60 vs 264.76 ± 46.29 μmol/L, P = .019), and disulfide (21.02 ± 3.74 vs 19.70 ± 3.62 μmol/L, P = .047) levels were higher in patients with FMS, whereas thiol/disulfide ratios did not differ between groups. FMS patients had significantly higher FIQ scores and lower SF-36 domain scores, indicating poorer QoL. Weak correlations were observed between native thiol and FIQ (r = 0.202) and between total thiol and SF-36 mental health (ρ = −0.242). In multivariable analysis, total thiol remained independently associated with FMS (OR = 1.009, P = .023), although explained variance was modest (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.058). FMS in HD patients is associated with impaired QoL and modest alterations in thiol-related oxidative parameters without a shift in thiol/disulfide ratios. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design. Prospective studies are needed to clarify underlying mechanisms.

1. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global public health challenge that significantly compromises patients’ quality of life. Individuals with CKD frequently present with various musculoskeletal disorders. Among these, fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) is a complex chronic pain condition characterized by widespread musculoskeletal pain, persistent fatigue, sleep disturbances, reduced functional capacity, and mood disorders.[1,2] The prevalence of FMS is estimated to range from 2% to 4% in the general population and from 7% to 14% among individuals with CKD.[3]

Although the precise pathophysiology of FMS remains unclear, central and peripheral sensitization mechanisms, along with inflammatory and immune processes, are thought to contribute to its development. In recent years, increasing evidence has suggested an association between FMS and elevated oxidative stress alongside impaired antioxidant defense mechanisms.[4,5] Oxidative stress is defined as an imbalance between reactive oxygen species and antioxidant defense systems. Thiol/disulfide homeostasis, which reflects the balance between antioxidant thiol groups and oxidized disulfide bonds, is considered a dynamic marker of oxidative stress.[6] Alterations in thiol/disulfide homeostasis have been reported in various conditions, including diabetes mellitus (DM), cardiovascular diseases, cancer, rheumatic disorders, CKD, neurological diseases, and liver diseases.[7]

Previous studies in FMS patients have reported increased lipid peroxidation and protein carbonylation, along with decreased antioxidant capacity. Recent findings suggest an association between oxidative stress and FMS.[4,8] Hemodialysis (HD), one of the most commonly used renal replacement therapies for end-stage CKD, prolongs survival but is also associated with various musculoskeletal complications.[9,10] Both CKD and HD treatment are associated with increased oxidative stress through factors such as dialysis membrane biocompatibility issues, leukocyte activation, and accumulation of uremic toxins.[11,12]

The present study aimed to investigate the association between FMS and demographic characteristics, quality-of-life indicators, comorbid conditions, treatment-related factors, laboratory parameters, and oxidative stress markers (thiol/disulfide balance) in patients undergoing HD. To our knowledge, no previous study has specifically examined the relationship between thiol/disulfide homeostasis and FMS in this patient population. Understanding this association may contribute to a better characterization of FMS in HD patients and provide a basis for future hypothesis-driven and prospective studies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This prospective, multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted among patients receiving chronic hemodialysis (HD) treatment at the Ankara Private Balgat Dialysis Center and a dialysis center in Kirikkale, Turkey.

2.2. Participants

2.2.1. Inclusion criteria

A total of 126 patients aged 18 to 75 years who had been undergoing routine HD treatment 3 times/wk for at least 3 months and agreed to participate in the study were included.

2.2.2. Exclusion criteria

Patients with comorbid conditions that could mimic fibromyalgia, such as diabetic neuropathy, rheumatic diseases, or active infections, were excluded.

2.3. Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kirikkale University Faculty of Medicine (Date: June 5, 2024; Decision No. 2024.06.14). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures adhered to ethical guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.3.1. Fibromyalgia diagnosis and group allocation

Fibromyalgia was diagnosed using the 2016 American College of Rheumatology criteria. According to these criteria, a diagnosis requires a Widespread Pain Index (WPI) score ≥ 7 and a Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) score ≥ 5, or a WPI between 3 and 6 with an SSS score ≥ 9. Symptoms must be present at a similar level for at least 3 months, and no other disorder should explain the pain.[13] Based on this standardized diagnostic protocol, applied uniformly to all participants, patients were classified into an FMS group (n = 66) and a non-FMS group (n = 60). Group allocation was determined solely by diagnostic scoring criteria (WPI and SSS), minimizing the risk of investigator bias.

2.4. Instrument

Demographic and clinical data: Age, height, weight, CKD duration, sex, comorbidities (DM, hypertension [HT], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure), history of blood transfusion, source of drinking water, vascular access type (catheter/fistula), Charlson Comorbidity Index, antibiotic use in the past 6 months, history of peritoneal dialysis, familial Mediterranean fever, malignancy, and renal biopsy history were recorded. Medication use, including phosphate binders, intravenous iron, calcitriol, paricalcitol, cinacalcet, and etelcalcetide, was also noted.

Medication use, including phosphate binders, intravenous iron, calcitriol, paricalcitol, cinacalcet, and etelcalcetide, was also noted.

Short form-36 (SF-36) health survey: The SF-36 was administered to assess participants’ overall health status and quality of life. It evaluates 8 domains: physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health. The Turkish validity and reliability of the SF-36 have been established.[14] Scores are coded for each item and transformed into a 0 to 100 scale, with higher scores indicating better health status.

Oxidative stress markers: During routine blood sampling for laboratory tests, an additional 5 mL of venous blood was drawn from the cubital vein into a separate tube for thiol/disulfide measurements. Samples were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes, and sera were separated. Thiol/disulfide homeostasis was assessed using the fully automated spectrophotometric method described by Erel and Neselioglu, measuring native thiol, total thiol, and disulfide levels. This method is a simple, cost-effective, and fully automated (or optionally manual) spectrophotometric procedure for evaluating thiol/disulfide homeostasis. Serum samples were stored at −80°C until analysis, and all samples were processed simultaneously. Disulfide/native thiol, disulfide/total thiol, and native thiol/total thiol ratios were calculated. Disulfide elevation reflects oxidative load, whereas increased native thiol levels indicate enhanced antioxidant capacity.[18]

2.5. Statistical methods and power analysis

Post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*power 3.1.9.4 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Power analysis based on an independent-samples t test comparing total thiol levels between patients with and without FMS indicated that the study’s actual power was 87.3, with an effect size of 0.5 and an α error of 0.05 (Cohen’s d = 0.50).

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk). Descriptive data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (interquartile range, 25th to 75th percentiles) for continuous variables, and as frequency (n) and percentage (%) for categorical variables. Group comparisons between patients with and without fibromyalgia were performed using the independent-samples t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, depending on the distribution of the data. Cohen’s d and 95% Confidence intervals (CI) for the independent samples t test were calculated. And Pearson’s chi-square (χ2) or Fisher’s exact test was compared for categorical variables, as appropriate. Correlations between QoL scores and biochemical parameters were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ), given the non-normal distribution of several variables. A multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify variables associated with FMS in hemodialysis patients. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

This multicenter cross-sectional study evaluated data from 126 patients undergoing HD between July 2024 and December 2024 at 2 dialysis centers in Ankara and Kirikkale, Turkey. Of these, 66 patients (52.4%) were diagnosed with fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS), while 60 patients (47.6%) comprised the non-FMS control group.

4. Discussion

Our findings indicate that HD patients with FMS have significantly higher native thiol, total thiol, and disulfide levels than those without FMS. Furthermore, thiol concentrations showed weak associations with FMS severity and certain quality-of-life measures. These results suggest that thiol/disulfide homeostasis may play a role in the biochemical background of FMS; however, the relationship appears to be complex and likely influenced by secondary mechanisms. The findings related to oxidative stress markers should be interpreted cautiously, as the cross-sectional design of the study does not allow causal inference.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusting for demographic and laboratory variables, total thiol levels remained independently associated with FMS. Nevertheless, the observed effect size was small (OR = 1.009), and the overall explained variance was limited (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.058). Although statistically significant, the observed effect sizes were small, reflecting weak associations and a limited magnitude of effect. Therefore, these findings have restricted clinical applicability.

In our study, no significant differences were observed between patients with and without FMS in terms of demographic characteristics, clinical variables, or basic laboratory parameters. This finding is consistent with previous reports indicating that, although FMS is prevalent among HD patients, it cannot be reliably distinguished based on specific demographic or biochemical indicators.[19,20] Nonetheless, the presence of comorbidities such as DM and HT, while not statistically significant, was more frequent in the FMS group. The literature similarly suggests that metabolic–vascular comorbidities such as DM and HT may predispose individuals to FMS through mechanisms involving chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and neuroendocrine dysfunction.[1,21,22] The absence of significant demographic or clinical differences between hemodialysis patients with and without FMS suggests that routine clinical parameters cannot predict this syndrome. Therefore, systematic screening using standardized assessment tools such as the American College of Rheumatology 2016 classification criteria is crucial for identifying underlying cases of FMS.

In our study, patients with FMS had significantly higher FIQ scores and markedly lower scores across all SF-36 domains. These findings indicate a generalized and substantial deterioration in quality of life associated with FMS, particularly characterized by increased pain severity and reduced physical and mental functioning. Similar results have been reported in the literature: HD patients with FMS have been shown to score significantly lower across all SF-36 subscales.[3] Türk et al[2] reported significantly higher FIQ scores in HD patients with FMS, while Doğan et al[3] demonstrated lower scores in both SF-36 and KDQOL-36 compared to non-FMS patients. Likewise, Abdelsalam et al[1] reported that approximately 60% of HD patients with FMS had reduced quality-of-life scores and that FIQ scores were positively correlated with Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) scores. Taken together, these findings align with our results, demonstrating that FMS substantially reduces quality of life in HD patients, both in disease-specific (FIQ) and general (SF-36) measures.

When the relationship between quality-of-life measures and oxidative stress indicators was examined in HD patients with FMS, we found a weak but significant positive correlation between FIQ scores and native thiol levels, and a weak negative correlation between the SF-36 Mental health subscale and total thiol levels. These results indicate a weak association between fibromyalgia symptom severity, mental health status, and thiol levels; however, the direction and underlying mechanisms of this relationship remain unclear.

The literature reports variable findings regarding this association. Tuzcu et al[23] found a positive correlation between disulfide levels and FIQ scores, and a negative association between the native thiol/total thiol ratio and FIQ scores. Similarly, Bakirci et al[5] reported that disulfide levels in FMS patients were positively correlated with the SF-36 “bodily pain” subscale and negatively correlated with Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) scores. Aktekin et al[24] reported no correlations between thiol/disulfide parameters and FIQ, SF-36 subscales, Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), BAI, or Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Similarly, Karataş et al[25] observed no significant associations between thiol/disulfide ratios and clinical measures such as pain, symptom duration, and quality of life.

Although our study found significantly higher thiol and disulfide levels in HD patients with FMS, there was no significant difference in thiol/disulfide ratios between groups. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Previous studies have reported heterogeneous findings: some[5,23,26] have described decreased thiol levels and increased disulfides, indicating elevated oxidative stress in FMS, whereas others[25,27] have reported a reductive shift with increased thiols and enhanced antioxidant defenses. Aktekin et al[24] found no significant differences between FMS patients and healthy controls. Importantly, all of these studies compared FMS patients with healthy controls, whereas our cohort consisted entirely of individuals with CKD undergoing HD, a population already exposed to sustained oxidative stress.

Several factors may explain our observation of elevated thiol and disulfide levels without a change in thiol/disulfide ratio. Importantly, given the cross-sectional design of the study, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistic. The absence of ratio differences suggests that the increase in absolute thiol levels may reflect expansion of the overall thiol pool size rather than a proportional shift in redox balance. First, our study population consisted of HD patients, who already have exposed to sustained oxidative stress, rather than healthy individuals. Second, the heterogeneous nature of FMS and the influence of coexisting conditions may lead to variable redox responses. Conducting the study in a chronic oxidative stress environment, such as HD, may account for the stability of the thiol/disulfide ratio. Additionally, although statistically significant, the correlations between thiol parameters and quality-of-life measures were weak (r ≈ 0.20), indicating limited clinical magnitude. Therefore, the observed oxidative alterations should not be interpreted as evidence of a definitive adaptive antioxidant response, but rather as modest and nondirectional biochemical associations. Similar patterns have been reported in chronic inflammatory diseases[28] and in individuals engaged in regular exercise.[29]

A key strength of our study is its focus on fibromyalgia, a frequently overlooked comorbidity in patients undergoing chronic hemodialysis despite its substantial impact on quality of life. In addition, the combined assessment of oxidative stress markers, such as thiol/disulfide parameters, alongside multidimensional clinical scales provides a comprehensive evaluation of both biochemical and clinical aspects of the condition.

The findings related to oxidative stress markers should be interpreted cautiously, as the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference. The observed associations were weak (r ≈ 0.20), and the regression model demonstrated limited explanatory power (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.058), indicating that oxidative parameters account for only a small proportion of the variability in FMS among HD patients.

While our results clearly support an association between fibromyalgia and impaired quality of life in this population, the findings related to oxidative stress pathways should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than indicative of a definitive mechanistic relationship. Therefore, these results have limited clinical applicability and require confirmation in well-designed prospective and mechanistic studies.

4.1. Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be inferred. Second, key inflammatory biomarkers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which could provide further insight into the inflammatory dimension of oxidative stress, were not assessed. Third, although multivariable regression analysis identified total thiol as independently associated with FMS, the observed effect size was modest and the explained variance was low (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.058), indicating limited explanatory power.

Furthermore, although strict exclusion criteria were applied, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded. Variables such as nutritional status, dialysis adequacy, inflammatory burden, medication use, and lifestyle factors were not fully evaluated and may have influenced the observed associations. In addition, while a post hoc power analysis was performed, this approach does not substitute for an a priori sample size calculation and should be interpreted with caution.

Overall, these limitations suggest that the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future longitudinal studies integrating both inflammatory and oxidative stress markers are needed to better clarify the relationships among fibromyalgia, CKD, and redox imbalance.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that FMS in patients undergoing CHD is associated with substantial impairment in quality of life and modest alterations in thiol-related oxidative stress markers. Although total thiol levels were independently associated with FMS in multivariable analysis, the small effect size and low explained variance suggest that oxidative imbalance represents only one component of a complex and multifactorial pathophysiological process.

No significant shift in thiol/disulfide ratios was observed, indicating that the observed changes may reflect expansion of the overall thiol pool rather than a disproportionate alteration in redox balance. Given the cross-sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established.

Fibromyalgia is a frequently overlooked syndrome in HD patients that can severely impair quality of life through its association with depression, pain, and sleep disturbances. Therefore, incorporating routine assessment and early detection strategies for FMS into nephrology practice is of great importance. Early diagnosis and appropriate management may not only reduce symptom burden but also positively influence oxidative stress parameters. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to elucidate the clinical burden of FMS better and clarify the oxidative/inflammatory mechanisms underlying its pathophysiology.
