A positional 𝚷 3 0 \mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3} -complete objective
Antonio Casares, Pierre Ohlmann, Pierre Vandenhove

Abstract
We study zero-sum turn-based games on graphs. In this note, we show the existence of a game objective that is 𝚷 3 0 \mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3} -complete for the Borel hierarchy and that is positional , i.e., for which positional strategies suffice for the first player to win over arenas of arbitrary cardinality. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first known such objective; all previously known positional objectives are in 𝚺 3 0 \mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{3} . The objective in question is a qualitative variant of the well-studied total-payoff objective , where the goal is to maximise the sum of weights.

We consider infinite-duration zero-sum two-player games played on (potentially infinite) graphs

In this context, two players, Eve and Adam, take turns in moving a token along the edges of an edge-labelled directed game graph.

An

A strategy is called

An objective is called

\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
-completeness of
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}

We refer to
[
8
]
for definitions on the Borel hierarchy.

To show that
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
is in
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
, observe that

\mathsf{SumToInfinity}=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcap_{k\geq j}\{w_{0}w_{1}\ldots\in\mathbb{Z}^{\omega}\mid\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{i}\geq n\},

where the inner sets
\{w_{0}w_{1}\ldots\in\mathbb{Z}^{\omega}\mid\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{i}\geq n\}
are clopen.

To show that
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
-hard, we reduce the following
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
-hard objective
[
8
, Ex. 23.2]
to it:

\mathsf{FinOcc}=\{x\in\mathbb{N}^{\omega}\mid\forall c\in\mathbb{N},|x|_{c}\text{ is finite}\}.

This objective is the complement of objective
\mathsf{InfOcc}
discussed above.
We recall that for a reduction, we need to show a continuous mapping
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{\omega}\to\mathbb{Z}^{\omega}
such that
f^{-1}(\mathsf{SumToInfinity})=\mathsf{FinOcc}
. Such a mapping is defined by:

f(c_{0}c_{1}\dots)=w_{0}w_{1}\dots,\;\;\text{ with }w_{i}=c_{i+1}-c_{i}.

The function
f
is continuous, as if
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\omega}
are two words with a common prefix of size
k
, then
f(x)
and
f(x^{\prime})
have a common prefix of size
k-1
.

Let us show that
f^{-1}(\mathsf{SumToInfinity})=\mathsf{FinOcc}
. Note that

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(c_{i+1}-c_{i})=c_{k}-c_{0}
.
If
c_{0}c_{1}\ldots\notin\mathsf{FinOcc}
, then
c_{k}-c_{0}
takes infinitely often a constant value
c\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore,
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(c_{i+1}-c_{i})\nrightarrow+\infty
.
Conversely, if
c_{0}c_{1}\ldots\in\mathsf{FinOcc}
, then, for all
c\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{k}-c_{0}>c
for all sufficiently large
k
, so
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(c_{i+1}-c_{i})\to+\infty
.

Positionality of
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}

To keep this note short, we include only the crucial formal definitions; we refer the reader to
[
9
]
for additional context.

In what follows, the word
graph
stands for a directed graph with edges labelled by elements of
C
. The set of vertices of a graph
G
is written
V(G)
, and its edges
E(G)\subseteq V(G)\times C\times V(G)
.

Almost-universality.

A
tree
is a graph with a root
t_{0}
such that all vertices admit a unique path from
t_{0}
.
A graph morphism from
G
to
H
is a map
f\colon V(G)\to V(H)
such that for each edge
v\xrightarrow{c}v^{\prime}
in
G
,
f(v)\xrightarrow{c}f(v^{\prime})
is an edge in
H
.
We write
G\to H
if there exists such a morphism.
A
well-ordered graph
is a graph whose vertices are well-ordered by an ordering
\leq
.
An ordered graph is
monotone
if
u\geq v\xrightarrow{c}v^{\prime}\geq u^{\prime}
implies
u\xrightarrow{c}u^{\prime}
.
A graph
satisfies
an objective
W
if the labelling of any infinite path on it belongs to
W
.
For a prefix-independent objective
W
and a cardinal
\kappa
, a graph
U
is said to be
(\kappa,W)
-almost-universal
if

•

U
satisfies
W
, and

•

for all trees
T
of size
<\kappa
satisfying
W
, there is a vertex
v_{0}
such that
T[v_{0}]\to U
,

where
T[v_{0}]
is the restriction of
T
to vertices reachable from
v_{0}
.
We will rely on the following result:

Lemma 2

(
[
9
, Theorem 3.2 and Lemma 4.5]
)
.

Let
W
be a prefix-independent objective.
If, for all cardinals
\kappa
, there exists a well-ordered monotone
(\kappa,W)
-almost-universal graph, then
W
is positional.

Objective
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
is prefix-independent.
To prove that it is positional, it therefore suffices, for every cardinal
\kappa
, to build a
(\kappa,\mathsf{SumToInfinity})
-almost-universal graph
U
.
In what follows, let
C=\mathbb{Z}
and let
\kappa
be a cardinal.

Definition of
U
.

We will manipulate finite tuples of ordinals.
For such a tuple
u
, we write
u_{<i}
for the restriction of
u
to its first
i
coordinates:

(u_{0},\dots,u_{n})_{<i}=(u_{0},\dots,u_{i-1}).

We let
|u|
denote the length of
u
; for instance,
|(0,1)|=2
.
Recall the lexicographic ordering:

u>_{\text{lex}}u^{\prime}\iff u^{\prime}\text{ is a prefix of $u$ or }\exists i,[u_{<i}=u^{\prime}_{<i}\text{ and }u_{i}>u^{\prime}_{i}].

Consider the graph
U
defined over
V(U)=\bigcup_{n<\omega}\kappa^{n}
by

E(U)=\{u\xrightarrow{w}u^{\prime}\mid|u|+w\geq|u^{\prime}|\text{ and }[|u|+w=|u^{\prime}|\implies u>_{\text{lex}}u^{\prime}]\}.

Intuitively, the length of the tuples in
U
encodes an underapproximation of the sum of weights in a given path: an edge either tracks precisely the sum of weights (when
|u|+w=|u^{\prime}|
), or it underestimates it (when
|u|+w>|u^{\prime}|
).
In the former case, there is an additional requirement on
u^{\prime}
, which is that it decreases for
<_{\text{lex}}
.
In the latter case, the tuple can have any value.
These rules prevent in particular the existence of cycles with sum of weight
0
in
U
; to go back to the same vertex, some underestimating of the sum of weights is necessary.

The order over
U
is then defined by

u>u^{\prime}\iff|u|>|u^{\prime}|\text{ or }[|u|=|u^{\prime}|\text{ and }u>_{\text{lex}}u^{\prime}].

We raise the reader’s attention on the fact that the order over
U
does not coincide with the lexicographic order: for instance,
(0,0)>(1)
in
U
.

Lemma 3
.

The graph
(U,\leq)
is a well-ordered monotone graph.

Proof.

It is immediate that the order over
U
is well-founded and total. Let us check that
U
is monotone. Let
u\geq v\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}\geq u^{\prime}
in
U
.
Then,
|u|+w\geq|v|+w\geq|v^{\prime}|\geq|u^{\prime}|
.
If one of these inequalities is strict, then,
|u|+w>|u^{\prime}|
.
Otherwise,
|u|+w=|u^{\prime}|
and
u\geq_{\text{lex}}v>_{\text{lex}}v^{\prime}\geq_{\text{lex}}u^{\prime}
.
We conclude that
u\xrightarrow{w}u^{\prime}
.
∎

Almost-universality of

.

We now prove the following.

Theorem 5
.

The graph
U
is
(\kappa,\mathsf{SumToInfinity})
-almost-universal.

We prove the two conditions for almost-universality in two separate lemmas.

Lemma 6
.

The graph
U
satisfies
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
.

Proof.

Take an infinite path
u^{0}\xrightarrow{w_{0}}u^{1}\xrightarrow{w_{1}}\dots
in
U
.
For all
i
, let

b_{i}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }|u^{i}|+w_{i}=|u^{i+1}|,\\
1&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

For each
i
, we have
|u^{i}|+w_{i}\geq|u^{i+1}|+b_{i}
.
Therefore, for all
k
,

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{i}\geq|u^{k}|-|u^{0}|+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}b_{i}\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}b_{i}-|u^{0}|.

If
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}b_{i}
goes to
+\infty
, then by the above it also holds that
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{i}\to+\infty
, as wanted.
So we assume otherwise: there is
i_{0}
such that for
i\geq i_{0}
,
b_{i}=0
.
Then, for all
i\geq i_{0}
, we have
|u^{i}|+w_{i}=|u^{i+1}|
and thus
u^{i}>_{\text{lex}}u^{i+1}
; this contradicts the well-foundedness of
<_{\text{lex}}
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

For all trees
T<\kappa
satisfying
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
, there exists a vertex
v_{0}
of
T
such that
T[v_{0}]\to U
.

Proof.

Let
T<\kappa
be a tree satisfying
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
.
Given a finite path
\pi
, we let
w(\pi)
denote the sum of the weights appearing on
\pi
.
For all
v\in T
, define

n(v)=-\inf\{w(\pi)\mid\pi\text{ is a non-empty finite path from }v\}\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{+\infty\}.

We note that for all edges
v\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}
, it holds that
n(v)+w\geq n(v^{\prime})
.

Claim 8
.

There exists a vertex
v_{0}
such that
n(v_{0})<0
.

{claimproof}

Assume towards a contradiction that for all vertices
v
,
n(v)\geq 0
.
In other words, from all vertices, there is a non-empty path of weight
\leq 0
.
By concatenating such paths, we get a path whose weight does not converge to
+\infty
, which contradicts the fact that
T
satisfies
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
.

Using the above claim, let
v_{0}
be a vertex such that
n(v_{0})<0
.
We will construct a mapping
\phi\colon T[v_{0}]\to U
.
The following claim will be useful for the definition of this morphism.
We say that an edge
v\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}
of
T
is
tight
if
n(v)+w=n(v^{\prime})
, and that a (finite or infinite) path is
tight
if it is comprised only of tight edges.

Claim 9
.

Let
\pi
be an infinite tight path from
v_{0}
.
For each
k\geq 0
, there are finitely many vertices
v
on
\pi
satisfying
n(v)\leq k
.

{claimproof}

Denote
\pi=v_{0}\xrightarrow{w_{0}}v_{1}\xrightarrow{w_{1}}\dots
;
since all edges are tight, we have

n(v_{i})=n(v_{0})+\sum_{j<i}w_{j}.

Since
\pi
satisfies
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
,
\sum_{j<i}w_{j}
converges to
+\infty
; therefore, so does
n(v_{i})
.
The result follows.

We now define a morphism
\phi\colon T[v_{0}]\to U
.
First, notice that all vertices
v
in
T[v_{0}]
are such that
n(v)<+\infty
; otherwise, we would also have
n(v_{0})=+\infty
.
For
v
in
T[v_{0}]
, we define the length of the tuple
\phi(v)
to be
\max\{n(v)+1,0\}\in\mathbb{N}
(in particular, if
n(v)<0
, then
\phi(v)
is the empty tuple).
For a vertex
v
in
T[v_{0}]
with
n(v)\geq 0
and
0\leq k\leq n(v)
, the
k
-th coordinate of
\phi(v)
is defined as follows.
Informally, we count the number of vertices
v^{\prime}
with
n(v^{\prime})\leq k
on a tight path starting in
v
.
Formally, define
T_{v,k}
to be the graph with vertices

V(T_{v,k})=\{v^{\prime}\in V(T)\mid\text{there is a tight path from $v$ to $v^{\prime}$ and $n(v^{\prime})\leq k$}\},

and edges

\displaystyle E(T_{v,k})=\{v^{\prime}_{1}\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}_{2}\mid w\text{ is the weight of a tight path from $v^{\prime}_{1}$ to $v^{\prime}_{2}$}

\displaystyle\text{whose inner vertices $v^{\prime}$ satisfy $n(v^{\prime})>k$}\}.

The graph
T_{v,k}
is actually a tree with root
v
when
k=n(v)
, but is in general a disjoint union of trees.

Claim
9
implies that
T_{v,k}
is well-founded (it does not admit any infinite path).
The rank of a vertex in a well-founded disjoint union of trees is an ordinal number defined to be
0
for leaves, and one plus the supremum rank of its successors for non-leaves.
The rank of
T_{v,k}
, written
\mathrm{rk}(T_{v,k})
, is the supremum rank of its vertices (note that it is
<\kappa
).

We set the
k
-th coordinate of
\phi(v)
to be the rank of
T_{v,k}
, thus

\phi(v)=(\mathrm{rk}(T_{v,0}),\mathrm{rk}(T_{v,1}),\dots,\mathrm{rk}(T_{v,n(v)})).

Claim 10
.

The map
\phi\colon V(T[v_{0}])\to V(U)
defines a morphism from
T[v_{0}]
to
U
.

{claimproof}

Consider an edge
v\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}
; we show that
\phi(v)\xrightarrow{w}\phi(v^{\prime})
is an edge in
U
.

First, notice that we have in general that
n(v)+w\geq 0
: indeed,
\pi=v\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}
is a non-empty path from
v
so, by definition of
n(v)
,
-n(v)\leq w
.

If
n(v^{\prime})<0
, then

|\phi(v)|+w=\max(n(v)+1,0)+w\geq n(v)+1+w\geq 1>0=|\phi(v^{\prime})|,

thus
\phi(v)\xrightarrow{w}\phi(v^{\prime})
is an edge in
U
.

We now assume in the rest of the proof that
n(v^{\prime})\geq 0
.
We reason according to whether the edge
v\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}
is tight.

•

First, assume that edge
v\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}
is not tight, i.e., that
n(v)+w>n(v^{\prime})
.
Then the argument is similar to the previous one:

|\phi(v)|+w\geq n(v)+1+w>n(v^{\prime})+1=|\phi(v^{\prime})|,

where the last equality uses that
n(v^{\prime})\geq 0
.

•

Second, assume
v\xrightarrow{w}v^{\prime}
is tight, i.e.,
n(v)+w=n(v^{\prime})
.
Therefore,

|\phi(v)|+w\geq n(v)+1+w=n(v^{\prime})+1=|\phi(v^{\prime})|,

so it suffices to show that
\phi(v)>_{\text{lex}}\phi(v^{\prime})
.

Let
k\leq\min\{n(v),n(v^{\prime})\}
.
As
v^{\prime}
is reachable from
v
,
T_{v^{\prime},k}
is a subgraph of
T_{v,k}
.
Therefore,
\mathrm{rk}(T_{v,k})\geq\mathrm{rk}(T_{v^{\prime},k})
.
If
n(v)>n(v^{\prime})
, we deduce that
\phi(v)>_{\text{lex}}\phi(v^{\prime})
(
\phi(v)
is a longer tuple and starts with values at least as large).
If
n(v)\leq n(v^{\prime})
, with
k=n(v)
, since
v
is a vertex (in fact, the root) of
T_{v,n(v)}
but not of
T_{v^{\prime},n(v)}
, we get
\mathrm{rk}(T_{v,n(v)})>\mathrm{rk}(T_{v^{\prime},n(v)})
.
We also conclude that
\phi(v)>_{\text{lex}}\phi(v^{\prime})
, as required.

\claimqedhere

This ends the proof of positionality of
\mathsf{SumToInfinity}
.
