Impact of lactation stage, maternal age, and parity on mammary epithelial cell populations in fresh milk
Authors: Marion Salmon-Legagneur, Julie Perrin, Nina Tabardel, Coralie Barrier, Morgane Mellare, Léonie Ducrocq, Adrian Kotevski, Lucie Poggi, Héloïse Coutelier, Eugénie Pezé-Heidsieck, Alexandra Benachi, Thibault Wartelle, Antoine Perrin, Jean-Marc Ayoubi, Virginie Rigourd, Gaëlle Neveu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Human milk contains viable mammary epithelial cells (MECs), providing a unique, non-invasive source of primary breast cells. As these cells originate from the lactating breast, they enable direct insights into mammary epithelial biology in vivo, and on the maternal influence on epithelial composition and function potential. Yet, such influences remain poorly characterized. We analyzed 127 milk samples to investigate how lactation stage, maternal age, and parity affect milk-derived MECs abundance, phenotype, and functional properties. Cells were characterized by flow cytometry, gene expression profiling, and protein quantification at isolation and after 21 days of in vitro culture. The lactation stage significantly influenced milk’s cellular composition: total cell counts declined over time, while epithelial cell proportion and expression of lactation-related genes increased. Advanced maternal age was associated with lower prolactin receptor expression, fewer epithelial cells, and reduced proliferative potential in vitro. MECs from multiparous women exhibited a higher luminal-to-basal ratio, supporting the existence of parity-associated epithelial imprinting. Such shifts in epithelial subtypes may contribute to long-term breast remodeling and disease risk. This study provides a comprehensive characterization of milk-derived MECs. It highlights human milk as a valuable model to study lactation, epithelial plasticity, and the cellular contexts relevant to breast cancer risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33232-y.

Introduction

The mammary gland is composed of a diverse array of epithelial, endothelial, immune, and stromal cells, including fibroblasts and adipocytes, supporting its development and function. The epithelial compartment is organized into a bilayered structure consisting of luminal epithelial cells, which form the ducts and alveoli, and are primarily responsible for milk production and secretion, and basal cells, which form the outer layer and aid in milk ejection through contractile activity. Populations of unipotent progenitors, at the adult stage, are responsible for the mammary gland remodeling during hormonal cycles, pregnancy and lactation1. It is the aberrant proliferation of epithelial cells in the mammary gland that gives rise to most breast cancers2.

Understanding the biology of the human mammary gland across different physiological stages is essential to unravel the mechanisms underlying breast development, function, and disease. However, access to primary human MECs for research purposes remains limited. Most available tissue samples are obtained from breast reduction surgeries, which are typically performed when the gland is in a quiescent, non-lactating state3,4. As a result, these human samples provide limited insight into the dynamic changes that occur during active stages such as pregnancy and lactation.

Human breast milk offers a unique, non-invasive source of viable mammary epithelial cells. Several studies have shown that epithelial cells found in milk, whether shed by passive exfoliation or active extrusion, can be isolated and cultured, providing a model for studying lactational mammary biology5–8. These studies highlighted the fact that the different MEC populations, including basal, luminal and progenitor cells can be found in maternal milk. Consequently, extracting cells from breast milk enables researchers to examine epithelial cell function in vitro without requiring surgical intervention.

While the phenotypic and molecular characteristics of human breast milk-derived cells have been increasingly elucidated, particularly through recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies8–12, there remains a paucity of data in regards to how these cell populations evolve over the course of lactation or in response to maternal factors. It is plausible that the composition and functional states of MEC differ between early and late lactation stages, or according to maternal age and parity (number of children). Despite their potential relevance to both lactational biology and maternal-infant health, these variables have not been systematically investigated in humans, highlighting a critical gap in our current understanding of the nature of cellular populations in cells isolated from milk.

Although the total cellular concentration in human milk tends to decline over the course of lactation11,13,14, cellular and transcriptomic analyses have shown that the relative proportion of epithelial cells increases with time11,14,15. Moreover, the expression of the prolactin receptor (PRLR) progressively rises throughout lactation in milk-derived cells, suggesting a sustained or even enhanced hormonal responsiveness of the mammary epithelium as lactation progresses11. Additionally, it has been shown, only at the transcriptomic level, that late lactation cells exhibit increased expression of key milk protein genes, including caseins (CSN), alpha-lactalbumin (LALBA), and lactoferrin (LTF)15. To date, few studies have examined the impact of maternal age and parity on the cellular composition of human milk. Available data suggest that these maternal factors do not substantially influence milk cell populations11. Only the concentration and structural diversity of human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) have been shown to vary with parity, but not with maternal age16,17.

Furthermore, the capacity of cells isolated from human milk from diverse donors to be cultured in vitro and retain their phenotypic and transcriptional characteristics over time has not yet been investigated. Developing an in vitro culture model of milk-derived human MECs would be of great interest to assess these differences and the impact of maternal factors on their abilities to grow.

The quantity and composition of cells in the milk of dairy-producing animals are more extensively documented, largely due to their relevance in the food industry. In dairy cows, milk cell concentration tends to increase over the course of lactation, in contrast to what is observed in humans. In both bovine and ovine species, higher parity is associated with an elevated somatic cell count, while maternal age does not appear to significantly influence cell levels18–24. As mammary gland structure and composition is significantly different in human and dairy-producing animals it is highly relevant to study the composition of human milk cells for research and clinical purposes.

To systematically investigate cellular variations in human breast milk, we established a biobank comprising 127 milk samples from 119 distinct donors. Both the cellular and protein content of the milk were analyzed at isolation and after 21 days of culture in vitro to assess potential variations in the number and type of cells, capacity to grow and form colonies in vitro, and secretion of key constituents across the course of lactation, as well as associations with maternal parity, and age.

We robustly confirmed that the concentration of cells in human breast milk declines over the course of lactation, while remaining unaffected by maternal age or parity. We were able to successfully cultivate cells isolated from milk of over 70% donors and demonstrate that the phenotype of shed epithelial cells shifts with lactational stage. Finally, we observed that PRLR expression decreased with advancing maternal age, and that increasing parity was associated with an enrichment of luminal cells relative to basal cells.

Results

Cohort of breast-milk donors and maternal factors

To ensure robust and consistent data, we analyzed 127 human milk donations collected from 119 different lactating mothers recruited in the Paris area, France. Each donor provided information regarding her age, parity, and the birth date of the infant she was currently breastfeeding. Based on this information, samples were categorized according to lactation stage, maternal age, and parity, with each variable divided into three groups. For the lactation stage, group 1 included donations collected before 1 month postpartum, group 2 between 1 and 4 months, and group 3 after 4 months postpartum. Regarding maternal age, group 1 comprised donors under 31 years old, group 2 included those aged 31–35, and group 3 donors over 35 years of age. For parity, group 1 consisted of primiparous donors, group 2 of those with two children, and group 3 of donors with three or more children (Table 1).

In our cohort, donor age and parity did not differ significantly across lactation stage groups, and vice versa (Supplementary Fig. S1a). As expected, parity increases as age increases, and age increases as parity increases (Supplementary Fig. S1b and c). Although the volume of donated milk was not measured using previously established strict protocols25–27, we can nevertheless observe that donors that have been lactating for less than 4 months donated significantly more milk per donation than after 4 months (Supplementary Fig. S1a). This aligns with previously reported declines in milk volume over the course of lactation, likely due to the introduction of solid food in the child diet28. Thus, the results presented below are not confounded by unequal distribution of maternal characteristics across groups.

Analysis of the epithelial cell populations after isolation from fresh milk

To investigate the cellular content of human milk in relation to maternal characteristics, we isolated milk cells from donor samples following established protocols6–8,29. The isolation protocol allows recovery and counting of eukaryotic cells only. Cell counting was performed following staining of cells with acridine orange and propidium iodide in order to only count nucleated cells among non-nucleated milk vesicles (Supplementary Fig. S2a). While cell concentration did not vary significantly with maternal age or parity, it declined significantly over the course of lactation (Fig. 1a), decreasing from an average of 0.157 × 106 cells/mL during the first month postpartum to 0.046 × 106 cells/mL in late lactation. Similar conclusions can be drawn when analysing the results by correlation and performing Spearman test (Supplementary Fig. S2b), supporting the relevance of the arbitrarily defined groups.

Following isolation from milk, cell content was analyzed by flow cytometry to determine cell identity (gating strategy shown in Supplementary Fig. S2c). Endothelial, hematopoietic, and erythroid cells were characterised using lineage-specific markers (CD31, CD45, and glycophorin A, respectively). No differences related to maternal factors were observed in the proportion of lineage positive cells in the fresh milk (Supplementary Fig. S2d). Among lineage-negative cells, cell populations were characterized by dual staining with CD49f (integrin α6, a basal cell marker) and EpCAM (a luminal cell marker). CD49fneg/EpCAMneg particles contain non-nucleated vesicles (as milk-fat globules or extracellular vesicles) and stromal cells, including fibroblasts, and adipocytes, hereafter called non-epithelial cells. After isolation, only a few proportions of the particles recovered from human milk were epithelial cells (< 10%). The epithelial cells proportion did not significantly vary with lactation stage or parity. However, donor age was negatively correlated with epithelial cell enrichment (Fig. 1b).

Gene expression profiling of total cells isolated from milk provides further insight into the epithelial subtypes present, and their modulation according to lactation stage and maternal characteristics. Increased maternal age correlated with decreased expression of epithelial markers (CD24, KRT18, KRT14, and ITGA6) (Supplementary Fig. S2e), consistent with flow cytometry data (Fig. 1b). Over the course of lactation, we observed increased expression of certain epithelial genes, particularly a 2.8 fold increase of CD24 and a 1.8 fold increase of KRT14 expression (Fig. 1c). Notably, expression of the PRLR, a key regulator of lactational function and a marker of luminal epithelial cells, was markedly upregulated (3-fold) as lactation progressed (Fig. 1c).

We also observed a 3-fold decrease in PRLR expression with increased maternal age that could be linked to a decrease of the epithelial cell proportion in the milk and suggest decreased hormone responsiveness with aging (Supplementary Fig. S2e). However, further studies are needed to determine whether it reflects a true age-dependent modulation of prolactin responsiveness in the lactating mammary epithelium. No differences related to parity were observed (Supplementary Fig. S2f).

Analysis of the epithelial cell population after 21 days of culture in vitro

Following initial characterization, total cells isolated from milk were cultured in vitro to assess their growth potential and cellular profile. An equal number of viable cells (0.5 × 10⁶ per sample) was seeded under identical culture conditions. After 21 days, colony-forming ability was evaluated as a measure of in vitro growth capacity. Indeed, not all samples were able to lead to sustained cell culture in vitro and overall, successful cell growth was observed for 71% of the samples (representative image of cell colonies in Supplementary Fig. S3a). Due to the limited number of donors with more than two children, groups 2 and 3 were combined to analyze the effect of parity on cell phenotype after 21 days of culture. We therefore compared donors with one child to those with two or more children. Although no significant differences were observed between groups stratified by lactation stage, maternal age, or parity there is a tendency for more colonies forming in milk samples from early lactating mothers (≤ 1 months) and younger mothers (≤ 30 years old) (Fig. 2a).

Once seeded in vitro, cells derived from donors over 35 years of age exhibited a significantly reduced proliferation rate compared to those from mothers under 31 years, around 3.9 times less cells were obtained after 21 days of culture (Fig. 2b). Proliferative capacity appeared unaffected by lactation stage and parity (Fig. 2b).

Flow cytometry analysis of the cells after 21 days of culture using EpCAM and CD49f markers (gating strategy in Supplementary Fig. S3b) revealed significant differences in epithelial cell composition according to lactation stage (Fig. 2c) and parity (Fig. 2d), but not in relation to maternal age (Supplementary Fig. S3d). Specifically, as lactation progressed, the proportion of basal epithelial cells capable of proliferating in vitro decreased, forming 42.80% of the population in early lactation groups to less than 1% in late lactation groups, while the proportion of luminal cells increased accordingly (Fig. 2c). This conclusion is also in line with correlation analysis (Supplementary Fig.S3c), with Spearman coefficient R > 0 for luminal and R < 0 for the basal proportion, and significant p-value for both. These findings were further corroborated by immunofluorescence staining: the proportion of CK14-positive (mature basal/basal progenitors30–32 cells declined with advancing lactation, whereas CK8-positive (luminal30–32 cells became more prominent (Fig. 2e). Transcriptomic profiling further supported these trends, with a 2 to 3 fold increase of expression of luminal lineage markers (PROM1, CD24, KRT18) and decreased expression of basal markers (ITGA6, KRT14) in later lactation stages (Fig. 2f). Expression of PRLR, after 21 days in culture, also significantly increased (3.5 fold) over lactation groups, mirroring that observed in freshly isolated milk cells. Importantly, these phenotypical changes appear specific to the evolution of lactation because no differences were observed according to maternal age (Supplementary Fig. S3e).

Very interestingly, an increase in the luminal to basal ratio (Fig. 2d) correlating with a 2-fold increase of the expression of PROM1 and CD24 (luminal markers) and decreased expression of KRT14 (7-fold decrease) and ITGA6 (basal markers) (Fig. 2g) was observed as parity increased.

Analysis of the main milk components in the fresh milk

To investigate the secretory potential of exfoliated cells in human breast milk, we assessed the expression of genes encoding major milk proteins in freshly isolated cells. For all genes analyzed (LTF, LALBA, CSN1S1, CSN2, and CSN3) we observed a significant 3-fold increase in expression over the course of lactation (Fig. 3a). These findings suggest that although fewer cells are present in late lactation stages, they remain transcriptionally active and continue to express high levels of genes associated with milk production. Expression of these genes was not significantly influenced by maternal age or parity (Supplementary Fig. S4a, b).

To determine whether gene expression levels were reflected in actual milk protein content, we performed biochemical analyses on cell-depleted milk supernatants collected during cell isolation. As reported by many studies and widely accepted within the scientific community, total protein concentration decreased significantly with advancing lactation (Fig. 3b), going from 12 g/L during the first month to 18 g/L after 4 months of lactation, while remaining unaffected by maternal age or parity (Supplementary Fig. S4c). While global protein content decreased, quantification of lactoferrin, alpha-lactalbumin, and beta-casein by ELISA assays showed no significant variation across lactation stages, maternal age groups, or parity (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. S4d, S4e and S4f).

Discussion

In this study, we provide a detailed analysis of MECs exfoliated in human milk, revealing how lactation stage, maternal age, and parity shape their abundance, phenotype, and functional characteristics. While most transcriptomic studies have focused on freshly isolated milk cells, we further characterized the proliferation potential and the epithelial phenotype after 21 days in culture.

We show that the stage of lactation is a primary determinant of MEC characteristics: while overall cell counts decline as lactation progresses21,33, the proportion of epithelial cells increases, and their transcriptional profile shifts toward a luminal, milk-producing phenotype. This aligns with earlier transcriptomic studies indicating increasing epithelial specialization during late lactation and suggests that exfoliated MECs reflect dynamic changes in the mammary gland over time11,14,15. The low number of CD49f+/Epcam+ cells shedded in fresh milk have already been observed in several studies8,9 and could be explained by a potential downregulation of the expression of these adhesion molecules. Once again, this study confirms and highlights the marked differences in cellular composition between human milk and cattle milk: in human milk, cell concentration decreases as lactation progresses, whereas in bovine milk, the number of cells increases over the course of lactation. We also observed an increase in PRLR expression with lactation progression. This increase could be linked to the increased proportion of luminal cells while lactation progresses, however it is also interesting to notice that several studies have reported that systemic prolactin levels decline after early lactation34,35. Hence, the observed upregulation of PRLR may represent a compensatory mechanism to maintain cellular responsiveness and sustained milk synthesis despite lowered amount of circulating hormone.

Maternal age was associated with a lower proportion of epithelial cells and reduced proliferative capacity in vitro. This suggests that epithelial cell exfoliation may decrease with advancing maternal age, potentially reflecting reduced shedding of milk-producing cells. This shift may be age-related mammary involution (which may be strongly linked to parity increasing with age) or altered tissue dynamics with age, including increased immune or stromal infiltration. Results on proliferation capacities suggest that even modest age differences and intrinsic cellular aging phenotypes, potentially reflecting telomere shortening or epigenetic alterations that impair proliferative potential, can be uncovered by our in vitro culture conditions.

Parity was associated with luminal dominance in cultured cells, suggesting a lasting influence of reproductive history on mammary epithelial identity. Recent papers have set out to investigate the effect of pregnancy and lactation on the epigenetic memory of mouse mammary epithelial cells, demonstrating that mammary epithelial cells have long lasting DNA-methylation modifications priming them for a faster response to hormonal cues36–38. In particular, dos Santos et al. showed that a full-term pregnancy establishes an epigenetic memory in luminal cells through stable hypomethylation at regulatory regions, priming them for enhanced responsiveness in subsequent reproductive cycles36. Similarly, Ciccone et al. found that post-pregnancy mammary epithelial cells in organoid cultures respond more robustly to pregnancy hormones, particularly through upregulation of milk-related genes in the luminal lineage38. Together, these studies suggest that parity may endow luminal cells with increased plasticity or proliferative capacity. It is a possibility that the differences observed in our cultures reflect such long-lasting epigenetic remodeling due to past lactation, favoring luminal secreting cells over basal cells. Future studies could directly test this hypothesis by comparing the epigenetic and transcriptomic landscapes of mammary epithelial cells from donors with different parities, for example using DNA-methylation profiling and single-cell RNA sequencing. Longitudinal sampling of the same women across successive lactations, combined with functional assays of proliferative and secretory capacity in culture, would also help determine whether parity-associated luminal dominance reflects a durable epigenetic imprint or more transient adaptations.

Finally, although we show that the expression of several signature genes encoding major milk components increases over the course of lactation, these results are not corroborated at the protein level. This discrepancy can be explained by differences in cellular concentration across milk samples during lactation. As lactation progresses and the total number of cells declines, individual cells may increase their transcriptional activity to maintain a stable overall level of milk protein production, which would account for the absence of detectable changes in protein concentrations across lactation stages. Additionally, the LALBA and CSN2 concentrations obtained in our milk samples slightly differ from those described in the literature (LALBA is usually 2,5–4 g/L in the literature39,40 while measured at 4,7 g/L in average in our samples, CSN2 is usually 1,5–5 g/L in the literature40–42 while measured at 9,9 g/L in our samples). Although substantial differences in concentrations have already been reported in the literature, the discrepancy between our results and previously published data may be explained by the different technologies used to measure milk protein concentrations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our analysis is based on exfoliated MECs, which may not fully capture the complexity of the mammary epithelium in situ. These cells represent only those naturally shed into milk and may exclude more deeply embedded or quiescent epithelial subpopulations. In this study, we chose to assess the impact of several maternal factors (age, parity, and stage of lactation), but it is important to note that other maternal factors (BMI, chronic diseases, mode of delivery, smoking status, etc.) could also affect the cellular and protein composition of human breast milk43–50.

Moreover, the culture conditions used in this study may not capture the full differentiation potential of MECs in vitro. We employed a 2D monolayer culture system, which enabled us to compare the influence of maternal factors across samples. However, it would also be relevant to explore in further studies 3D culture models, such as mammospheres or organoids, which more closely recapitulate the physiological architecture of the mammary gland51. In addition, we chose to assess maternal factors using a commercial culture medium optimized for the 2D growth of MECs, though its exact composition is not disclosed. The presence of growth factors and hormones in this medium could influence the epithelial phenotype observed in vitro. Although more complex models could be envisaged, the system used here nonetheless provides a robust framework to compare the effects of lactation stage, maternal age, and parity on the in vitro phenotype of milk-derived MECs. Finally, future studies integrating comprehensive transcriptomic and proteomic analyses of cultured cells would provide additional insights into the phenotype of human milk–derived mammary epithelial cells.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that lactation stage, maternal age, and parity significantly shape the cellular composition, and functional characteristics of mammary epithelial cells in human milk.

Notably, we show that while total cell concentration decreases with lactation, luminal epithelial markers, and PRLR expression increase, suggesting a refined adaptation of the epithelium to maintain lactational capacity over time. PRLR expression was also found to decline with maternal age, highlighting a potential age-associated modulation of prolactin signaling.

A unique strength of this work lies in the in vitro assessment of MECs after 21 days of culture, which preserved and amplified phenotypic trends observed at isolation. Culturing the cells revealed differences associated with maternal age, and parity, particularly a reduced proliferative capacity in cells from older donors and a luminal enrichment in multiparous individuals, supporting the concept of epigenetic imprinting by reproductive history.

These findings establish human milk as a valuable, non-invasive source of epithelial cells for research, and underscore the importance of accounting for maternal characteristics when analyzing MECs, as they significantly influence the cellular composition and characteristics. By improving our understanding of human mammary gland dynamics across lactation and maternal history, this work provides a foundation for future investigations into lactation biology, breast development, and disease, including breast cancer.

Methods

Human milk donation

Milk donors were recruited through maternity wards, independent midwives, or regional human milk banks.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: being ≥ 18 years of age; being affiliated with a social security/health insurance scheme; having access to a refrigerator and a breast pump; having received full information about the study; and having co-signed, with the investigator, a non-opposition form.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: known infectious disease transmissible via breast milk (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV-1 or HIV-2, or syphilis); and current or past personal history of breast cancer.

A minimum donation volume of 30 mL was required for inclusion in the study. Donors were instructed to express their milk under hygienic conditions, which included cleansing the breast with a disinfectant wipe and sterilizing all pumping equipment prior to collection.

Milk samples were stored and transported at 4 °C, and processed within the next 4 h after reception in the laboratory.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Human milk donors were recruited from maternity wards, and midwife practices under certification of a RIPH3 protocol (Research Involving the Human Person, Category 3) (2023-A02270-45 approved by relevant French ethic committee CPP - Comité de Protection des Personnes- Sud-Est III), in compliance with ethical, and regulatory standards for non-interventional research. A declaration was also submitted to the CODECOH (Comité d’Examen du Caractère Observatoire des Données Humaines, number DC-2025-7320), in order to benefit from samples collected via the Lactarium of île de France, in accordance with French data protection regulations. All experiment protocols were approved by CPP and CODECOH. All participants provided written informed consent, and completed a detailed questionnaire to document relevant clinical and demographic information.

Cell isolation

Human milk cells were isolated by diluting milk samples in an equal volume of 1X sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at 805 × g for 15 min. The fat layer was discarded and supernatant was frozen at −80°C for further protein analysis (ELISA and protein quantification) and the cell pellet was washed by resuspension in 1X PBS. This washing step was repeated once.

Samples were then filtered on a wet 40 µm cell strainer, to eliminate possible fibers. Cells were resuspended in Epicult Plus Epithelial Stem Cell Culture Medium (EpiPlus) (Stem Cell), complemented with Hydrocortisone (1 µg/mL) (Sigma), Penicillin-Streptomycin (100 µg/mL) (Gibco Scientific), Amphotericin B (2.5 μg/ml) (Dutscher) and Gentamicine (50 µg/mL) (Gibco Scientific). Cell counting was performed using Acridine Orange/Propidium Iodide staining on a LUNA-FL cell counter (Logos Biosystems).

Cell culture

All culture plates were coated with rLaminin-521 (Human) (Corning), diluted at 10 µg/mL in 1X PBS containing 0.5 mM magnesium and 0.9 mM calcium (Cytiva) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours before usage.

For each milk sample, 0.5x106 of freshly isolated cells were seeded in one well of a 24-well plate in EpiPlus, complemented with hydrocortisone and the antibiotics cited above. Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

After letting epithelial cells adhere to the plate for a week, the medium was changed.

Colony-forming capacity represents the ability of a milk sample to grow cell-colonies after 21 days in culture. Confluence of the cells was then assessed regularly. When reaching a 60–80% confluency, cells were detached using TrypLE (Gibco Scientific) and replated according to the number of cells until 21 days of culture. In samples that did not reach 70% confluency, cells were detached and counted after 21 days of culture. The medium was changed every 2 or 3 days.

Flow cytometry

Cells were stained with antibodies α-CD49f-PE (130-119-767) and α-CD326-VioBright 515 (EpCam, 130-111-007) to assess their epithelial phenotype. α-CD45-APC Vio770 (130-110-635), α-CD235a-APC Vio770 (130-120-471), α-CD31-APC Vio770 (130-110-672) were used to mark non-epithelial cells (Lineage gate). All antibodies are from Miltenyi and were diluted to 1:50 in PBS-2mM EDTA. After a 10 min incubation at 4 °C, stained cells were diluted in PBS-2mM EDTA and filtered through 35 μm cell strainer caps of round-bottom tubes. Flow cytometry was performed using a MACSQuant Analyzer 10 cytometer (Miltenyi). Obtained data were analysed using the FlowJo_V10 Software (FlowJo LLC).

RNA extraction, RT-qPCR analyses

Total RNA was extracted from dry cell pellets, using the NucleoSpin RNA Plus kit (Macherey-Nagel), following the manufacturer instructions. RNA concentration and quality was determined using a TECAN Infinite 200 PRO Microplate Reader (Tecan Group). A260/280 ratio, as a measure of RNA quality, are displayed for each sample in Supplementary Table S2. 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed using QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen). A control of the reverse transcription (RT-), incubated without reverse transcriptase, was performed for each qPCR plate.

Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR reactions on each sample were performed in triplicate. Water control and RT- control were included for each plate. Primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Table S1. The geometric mean of two reference genes GAPDH, and ACTB was utilized to normalize target gene data in the same sample. The ∆CT values were calculated individually for each target gene as ∆CT =CT (target gene) -CT (geometric mean of the reference genes).

Immunofluorescence assay

Cells were plated on glass coverslips coated with rLaminin-521 (Human) (Corning) for two days before being fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. They were then permeabilized 10 min with PBS 0.5% Triton X-100, blocked 30 min in PBS with 5% BSA and 0.25% Triton X-100, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with CK14 (MA511599) and CK8 (MA5-32118) primary antibodies, diluted 1/1000. The following day, secondary antibodies Mouse IgG-488 (A32766) and Rabbit IgG-555 (A32794) were added for an hour at room temperature, diluted 1/2000. All antibodies are from Invitrogen. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1/10000) (461059, Biotium). All antibody incubations were made in PBS with 1% BSA and 0.25% Triton X-100 (Sigma) and all washes in PBS with 0.25% Triton X-100. Finally, coverslips were mounted using Fluoromount-G (Invitrogen) and imaged with a Revolve Microscope (ECHO). The resulting images were mounted with Fidji (ImageJ 2.16.0).

Protein quantification and detection

Total protein quantification and ELISA were performed on frozen milk supernatant, stored at−80°C, without the fat layer. Overall protein concentration in milk samples was determined by Pierce BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Scientific), read on the TECAN Infinite 200 PRO Microplate Reader (Tecan Group). ELISA kits were used to assess the amount of lactoferrin (Abcam, ab200015), alpha-lactalbumin (Antibodies.com, A77524) and beta-casein (Antibodies.com, A77920). Samples were diluted in PBS and kit buffer to fit within the standard curve. ELISA were performed according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. For BCA and ELISA, reactions were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using GraphPad Prism v10.4. After removing outliers with the ROUT method (Q=1%), multiple comparisons one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey multiple-comparisons test was performed to determine statistical significance, unless otherwise stated. P-val<0.05 were considered statistically significant.
