Short history of signed exceptional sequences Thanks: First author supported by Simons Foundation Grant #686616
Kiyoshi Igusa, Gordana Todorov

Abstract
. Whereas exceptional sequences have a long history with many well-known connections to combinatorics, signed exceptional sequences are relatively recent. The authors introduced this concept in 2017 [ 20 ] , although it was retroactively realized that the category of noncrossing partitions [ 25 ] is a special case of this construction. Buan and Marsh [ 5 ] have introduced the concept of τ \tau -exceptional sequences to generalize the definitions and theorems to all finite dimensional algebras. This short paper is the story of the original concept of signed exceptional sequences for hereditary algebras and how it developed out of the two authors’ study of algebraic K-theory, link invariants, and cluster combinatorics.

Introduction

Exceptional sequences originated in the theory of sequences of line bundles over weighted projective space
[
34
]
and the action of the braid group on such sequences. This was almost immediately converted into analogous constructions and theorems about representations of quivers. Combinatorial versions of these constructions seem to have been known much longer
[
14
]
,
[
32
]
. We will stick to the quiver representation formulation of these objects for now.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite dimensional hereditary algebra over a field
K
. Often we assume
\Lambda=KQ
the path algebra of a quiver
Q
without oriented cycles. We call a
\Lambda
-module
E

exceptional
if it is rigid, meaning
\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(E,E)=0
, or equivalently
\Hom_{\Lambda}(E,\tau E)=0
and a
brick
which means the endomorphism ring of
E
is a division algebra, i.e., any nonzero endomorphism of
E
is an automorphism of
E
. If
Q
is a Dynkin quiver, all indecomposable
KQ
-modules are rigid bricks.

An
exceptional sequence
of length
k
is a sequence of exceptional modules
(E_{1},\cdots,E_{k})
with no backward Hom or Ext, i.e.,

\Hom_{\Lambda}(E_{j},E_{i})=0=\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(E_{j},E_{i})

for
i<j
. The exceptional sequence is called
complete
if it is maximal, i.e.,
k=n
the rank of
\Lambda
which is the number of nonisomorphic simple
\Lambda
-module. This is also the number of vertices of the quiver
Q
.

Every exceptional sequence
(E_{1},\cdots,E_{k})
produces a “perpendicular category” or, more precisely, a “Hom-Ext perpendicular category”
(E_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus E_{k})^{\perp_{01}}
which it the full subcategory of all modules
M
so that
\Hom_{\Lambda}(E_{i},M)=0=\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(E_{i},M)
for all
i\leq k
. Call this category
{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
. Then
{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
is a finitely generated “wide subcategory” of
mod\text{-}\Lambda
which is equivalent to the module category of a hereditary algebra of rank
n-k
. The exceptional sequence
E_{1},\cdots,E_{k}
can be completed on the left by adjoining a complete exceptional sequence of
{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
. We get a complete exceptional sequence

(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n-k},E_{1},\cdots,E_{k}).

In this sequence
X_{n-k}
is called
relatively projective
if it is a projective object of the perpendicular category
{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
. More generally, an object
E_{i}
in an exceptional sequence is relatively projective if it is a projective object of the perpendicular category of the objects to its right. In particular, the last term
E_{k}
is relatively projective if and only if it is projective. A
signed exceptional sequence
is an exceptional sequence together with signs (+/-) on it relatively projective terms.

In this paper, we will tell the story of how this concept evolved. However, the narative is in heuristic rather than in chronological order.

Signed exceptional sequences arose from the study of “pictures”, “picture groups” and “picture spaces”. The broad outline is as follows. In the 1970’s, for his PhD thesis work, the first author developed the concept of a “picture” and used it to define algebraic K-theory invariants for diffeomorphisms of smooth manifolds. Later, working with Kent Orr, pictures were used to settle a conjecture about link invariants. Around the turn of the century, Fomin and Zelevinsky developed the theory of cluster algebras and the second author, together with Buan, Marsh, Reineke and Reiten related this to tilting theory. They “categorified” cluster theory by creating the cluster category. Fomin-Zelevinsky clusters were shown to correspond to “cluster-tilting objects” in the cluster category. These were extensions of “tilting objects” which were sets of indecomposable modules which formed the vertices of a triangulation of the
n
-simplex. By adding shifted projective objects, this triangulation was extended to a triangulation of the
n
-sphere. The first author saw the resemblance with his “pictures” for the Steinberg group. Jerzy Weyman recognized these pictures as variants of the semi-invariant pictures from his work with Harm Derksen. The four authors: Igusa, Orr, Todorov, Weyman combined their ideas in
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
.

Next, came the study of picture groups and their homology. The first author had developed “pictures” to study the third homology of groups, the Steinberg group for his thesis and torsion-free nilpotent groups for studying link invariants with Kent Orr. The question was: what was the underlying group for the semi-invariant pictures of
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
? The two authors of this paper, together with Jerzy Weyman, invented “picture groups” and computed their homology and cohomology in the case of quivers of type
A_{n}

[
19
]
. To do that, they computed the homology of the “picture space” which is a simplicial complex with simplices corresponding to cluster-tilting objects. They needed to know that the picture space is a
K(\pi,1)
with fundamental group the picture group and therefore has the same integral homology as the picture group.

There are several proofs that the picture space is a
K(\pi,1)
. The most satisfying proof shows that the space is a cubical complex which is locally CAT(0) and, therefore, a
K(\pi,1)
. To covert a simplicial complex into a cubical complex, we take the category of simplices with inclusion maps. This is the cluster morphism category! Sequences of minimal simplex inclusions are signed exceptional sequences! Every simplex (corresponding to a cluster tilting object) produced a cube of morphisms. There are
n!
edge paths for each cube and therefore we obtain
n!
signed exceptional sequences for every cluster-tilting object!

Semi-invariant pictures

A semi-invariant comes from the action of a group
G

X

K

Our motivation for computing the cohomology of

[

The advantage of the picture space for

The rational cohomology of the group

6.
Cluster morphism category

The cube category
{\mathcal{I}}^{n}
is a graded category. Every object and morphism has a rank. Objects are sequences of
n
zeros and ones, say
a=(a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with rank equal to the sum:
rk\,a=\sum a_{i}
. The rank of a morphism

is
rk\,b-rk\,a\geq 0
and only identity morphisms have rank 0. We are, in general, interested in factoring morphisms as compositions of morphisms of rank 1.

For the cube category
{\mathcal{I}}^{n}
, there are
n!
ways to factor the longest morphism
(0,0,\cdots,0)\to(1,1,\cdots,1)
as a composition of rank 1 morphisms since the entries must be increased one by one in any order.

Recall that we have a triangulation
\Sigma(Q)
of the sphere

\rho\subset D(M_{\alpha})

The cluster morphism category

with morphisms

in

so that

and

We give the original intrinsic definition of cluster morphism. We need the following well-known formula relating vertices and walls in the wall-and-chamber structure for a finite dimensional algebra. Recall that
T

Lemma 6.1

(a) The

\Hom_{\Lambda}(T,M)=0=\Hom_{\Lambda}(M,\tau T)

(b) The

Proof.

(b) is easy. So, we assume

\tau

P\xrightarrow{p}Q\to T\to 0
be a minimal projective presentation of

(6.1)

0\to\Hom_{\Lambda}(T,M)\to\Hom_{\Lambda}(Q,M)\xrightarrow{p^{\ast}}\Hom_{\Lambda}(P,M)\to D\Hom_{\Lambda}(M,\tau T)\to 0.

If
\Hom_{\Lambda}(T,M)=0=\Hom_{\Lambda}(M,\tau T)
then

is an isomorphism and

g(T)\cdot\undim M=\dim\Hom_{\Lambda}(Q,M)-\dim\Hom_{\Lambda}(P,M)=0.

For

. So,

. So,

.

Conversely, suppose that

. Then
\dim\Hom_{\Lambda}(T,M)=\dim\Hom_{\Lambda}(M,\tau T)

7.
Signed exceptional sequences

As a corollary of Theorem
6.6
, we see that every cluster morphism
mod\text{-}\Lambda\to 0
has exactly
n!
factorizations into rank 1 morphisms. In fact, we showed:

Lemma 7.1

(I-Todorov)
.

Every cluster morphism
[A]
of rank
k
has exactly
k!
factorizations into a composition of
k
morphisms of rank
1
, rank 1 morphisms being those given by a single object. These factorizations are in bijection with total orderings of the components of
A
.

Proof.

The cluster morphism
[A]
determines an embedding
{\mathcal{I}}^{k}\to{\mathcal{C}}_{\Lambda}
and the
k!
factorizations of the unique rank
k
morphism in
{\mathcal{I}}^{k}
into rank 1 morphisms map to the factorizations of
[A]
into rank 1 morphisms.
∎

Definition 7.2
.

A
signed exceptional sequence
in
{\mathcal{W}}
is a factorization of any cluster morphism

[X]:{\mathcal{W}}\to{\mathcal{W}}^{\prime}

into rank 1 cluster morphisms. The signed exceptional sequence is called
complete
if
{\mathcal{W}}=mod\text{-}\Lambda
and
{\mathcal{W}}^{\prime}=0
.

Remark 7.3
.

A rank 1 morphism
[X]:{\mathcal{W}}\to{\mathcal{W}}^{\prime}
consists of one object
X
in the cluster category of
{\mathcal{W}}
. This object must be either an exceptional object (module) in
{\mathcal{W}}
or a shifted projective
P[1]
where
P
is an indecomposable projective object of
{\mathcal{W}}
. We call this a
relatively projective
object. When we factor a rank
k
cluster morphism
{\mathcal{W}}_{0}\to{\mathcal{W}}_{k}
into rank 1 morphisms we get:

{\mathcal{W}}_{0}\xrightarrow{[X_{1}]}{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\xrightarrow{[X_{2}]}{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\cdots\xrightarrow{[X_{k}]}{\mathcal{W}}_{k}

Since
{\mathcal{W}}_{i}=(X_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus X_{i})^{\perp_{01}}\cap{\mathcal{W}}_{0}
, the sequence

X_{k},X_{k-1},\cdots,X_{1}

forms an exceptional sequence (
\Hom_{\Lambda}(X_{i},X_{j})=0=\Ext^{1}_{\Lambda}(X_{i},X_{j})
for
i<j
) with the embellishment that some terms could be shifted if they are relatively projective. Thus, a signed exceptional sequence is the same as an exceptional sequence in which the relatively projective terms are allowed to be negative (shifted).

Theorem 7.4

(I-Todorov)
.

There is a bijection between (complete) signed exceptional sequences and ordered clusters.

We give some examples to illustrate this. We use semi-invariant pictures with chambers labeled with torsion classes. Recall that a torsion class

This formula defines a torsion class for any point
