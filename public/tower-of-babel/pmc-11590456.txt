Hierarchical individual variation and socioeconomic impact on personalized functional network topography in children
Authors: Shaoling Zhao, Haowen Su, Jing Cong, Xue Wen, Hang Yang, Peiyu Chen, Guowei Wu, Qingchen Fan, Yiyao Ma, Xiaoyu Xu, Chuanpeng Hu, Hongming Li, Arielle Keller, Adam Pines, Runsen Chen, Zaixu Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe spatial layout of large-scale functional brain networks exhibits considerable inter-individual variability, especially in the association cortex. Research has demonstrated a link between early socioeconomic status (SES) and variations in both brain structure and function, which are further associated with cognitive and mental health outcomes. However, the extent to which SES is associated with individual differences in personalized functional network topography during childhood remains largely unexplored.MethodsWe used a machine learning approach—spatially regularized non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)—to delineate 17 personalized functional networks in children aged 9–10 years, utilizing high-quality functional MRI data from 6001 participants in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study. Partial least square regression approach with repeated random twofold cross-validation was used to evaluate the association between the multivariate pattern of functional network topography and three SES factors, including family income-to-needs ratio, parental education, and neighborhood disadvantage.ResultsWe found that individual variations in personalized functional network topography aligned with the hierarchical sensorimotor-association axis across the cortex. Furthermore, we observed that functional network topography significantly predicted the three SES factors from unseen individuals. The associations between functional topography and SES factors were also hierarchically organized along the sensorimotor-association cortical axis, exhibiting stronger positive associations in the higher-order association cortex. Additionally, we have made the personalized functional networks publicly accessible.ConclusionsThese results offer insights into how SES influences neurodevelopment through personalized functional neuroanatomy in childhood, highlighting the cortex-wide, hierarchically organized plasticity of the functional networks in response to diverse SES backgrounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03784-3.

Background

Childhood socioeconomic status (SES), encompassing factors such as family income, parental education, and neighborhood conditions, plays a pivotal role in shaping cognitive, emotional, and behavioral development that extends into adolescence and adulthood [1–4]. SES is associated with cognitive development, academic achievement, and the risk for mental health disorders [5, 6]. Evidence suggests that these associations are mediated by changes in brain development [7, 8]. Thus, understanding the relationship between SES and brain development is critical, especially during early childhood. Particularly, understanding how SES differentially affects distinct cortical regions can reveal their varying susceptibility to neural plasticity and external influences, such as environmental insults and interventions, which is crucial for promoting mental health and well-being throughout childhood and adolescence.

A growing body of research using non-invasive MRI has revealed associations between SES with both brain structure and function [2, 9–22]. Socioeconomic backgrounds—such as family income, parental education, and neighborhood conditions—are related to hippocampal volume [9–11], as well as cortical volume, thickness, and surface area across broad regions of the frontal, parietal, and temporal cortices [2, 9, 11–14]. Studies utilizing resting-state fMRI to assess the temporal synchronization of signals, termed functional connectivity (FC), across these distributed brain regions have revealed a robust association between various SES factors and the organization of large-scale functional networks during childhood [2, 15–17, 19, 22]. For example, high neighborhood advantage, parental education, and family income are linked to lower connectivity within and between sensorimotor networks, as well as enhanced connectivity between sensorimotor and frontal functional networks [15]. The SES advantages are also associated with stronger functional connectivity in the default mode network and between default mode network and other higher-order functional networks [17, 22]. These studies typically employed a single group atlas of functional networks for all participants, mapping this standard atlas to individual brain space by aligning it with brain structure, thereby assuming a correspondence between functional and structural anatomy.

However, evidence from multiple independent studies underscores the pronounced inter-individual variability in the spatial layout of functional networks, commonly referred to as functional topography, in both youths and adults [23–32]. This personalized functional topography has been shown to be reproducible across scanning sessions and correlates closely with individuals’ activation patterns across a range of tasks [23–25, 28]. Ignoring such individual differences can obscure the measurement of inter-regional functional connectivity, potentially introducing biases into both inference and interpretation [31, 32]. Notably, research reveals that individual variation of functional topography is minimal within the primary sensorimotor cortex but markedly higher in higher-order association cortices [33, 34]. Regions exhibiting greater individual variation also subserve higher-order executive and psychosocial functions, undergo prolonged developmental trajectories, are disproportionately sensitive to environmental influences, and confer a higher risk for psychopathology [4, 34]. Prior studies also demonstrated that this individual variation in personalized functional network topography has been linked to differences in cognitive development [35, 36] and an increased susceptibility to psychopathology [37, 38].

Despite the growing evidence highlighting the significance of individual differences in functional neuroanatomy, relatively few studies have explored how SES factors, including family income, parental education, and neighborhood disadvantage, are associated with individual variation in functional network topography and how these associations organized across the cortex during childhood. To address this gap, we investigated three inter-related hypotheses. First, recent research has proposed a canonical sensorimotor-association cortical axis that characterizes a dominant pattern across the human cortex, along which diverse neurobiological properties progressively change in a continuous order [34]. We hypothesized that individual variations in childhood functional network topography are hierarchically distributed across the cortex along the sensorimotor-association cortical axis, as in adults and youths. Second, drawing from the existing literature on functional associations with SES [15–17, 19], we expected that individual variation of functional topography would be significantly linked to individual differences of all three SES factors. Third, we hypothesized that the spatial distribution of SES impacts on functional topography would also align with sensorimotor-association cortical axis, with the most pronounced associations found in higher-order association cortices. Leveraging a large, multi-site dataset of children aged 9–10 years from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study [39], we employed spatially regularized non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) [35–37, 40, 41] to delineate personalized functional networks. We then used partial least squares analysis to evaluate their associations with SES factors, aiming to provide insights into how socioeconomic factors associate the development of functional neuroanatomy.

Methods

Participants

We utilized baseline data from the ABCD study (Release 4.0.1), which recruited 11,876 children aged 9–10 years across 21 sites in the USA [39, 42]. The ABCD study received approval from the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, San Diego, with each participating site obtaining local Institutional Review Board approval. We obtained preprocessed resting-state fMRI data for 9480 participants from the ABCD-BIDS Community Collection (NDA Collection 3165, https://nda.nih.gov/edit_collection.html?id=3165) [43, 44]. We excluded participants with missing data or information, low-quality functional or structural MRI data, excessive head motion during acquisition, and incomplete scan sessions. Specifically, since the ABCD study primarily acquired four sessions of fMRI data for participants [39], each lasting approximately 5 min, we excluded participants who did not complete all four sessions. These criteria resulted in a sample of 6001 children obtained (see Additional file 1: Fig. S1 For details) [12, 15, 17, 24, 25, 28, 34, 35, 37, 39, 40, 42–76]. We constructed personalized functional networks for each of these participants.

This study examined the association between personalized functional network topography and individual differences in three SES factors: family income, parental education, and neighborhood disadvantage. To mitigate the potential impact of heritability in our analyses, we randomly selected one participant from families with multiple participants, which resulted in a sample of 5314 children. Missing data for each of the three SES factors led to a final sample of 4835 participants for family income analysis, 5308 participants for parental education analysis, and 4965 participants for neighborhood disadvantage analysis. Demographic information and details regarding the inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in Additional file 1: Table S1 and Fig. S1.

ABCD image acquisition and processing

MRI data were acquired using Siemens, Philips, or GE scanners equipped with a 32-channel head coil [39]. We utilized preprocessed resting-state fMRI data from the ABCD-BIDS Community Collection (https://collection3165.readthedocs.io/en/stable/) [43]. The resting-state fMRI data underwent initial processing through the modified Human Connectome Project’s minimal preprocessing pipeline [45], followed by denoising with the Developmental Cognition and Neuroimaging (DCAN) BOLD Processing software [50] to eliminate nuisance signals. Further details can be found in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods.

Socioeconomic status measurements

We examined the associations between functional network topography and three SES factors: household SES, encompassing family income which is measured by income-to-needs ratio (INR) and parental education, and neighborhood SES, as measured by the area deprivation index (ADI). The INR was calculated by dividing the median income of family income bins by the federal poverty line, adjusted for the year of the interview and the household size [51–54, 76]. An INR greater than one indicates that family income exceeds the poverty threshold. Parental education was assessed as the mean years of educational attainment of both parents or caregivers; in instances where data from both parents were unavailable, information from one parent was utilized [12, 15]. The parental education was re-coded from categorical variables to years (Additional file 1: Table S2). The ADI is a composite measure of neighborhood disadvantage, derived from participants’ primary residential addresses at the census tract level [55]. Higher ADI values indicate greater levels of neighborhood disadvantage, encompassing various indicators like income, education, employment, and housing quality. The ABCD study provided composite ADI metrics, including the weighted ADI score and its national percentile rank; this study utilized the national percentiles as recommended. A description of factors contributing to ADI was provided in Additional file 1: Table S3. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Defining personalized functional networks with NMF

As in previous studies [35–37], we employed spatially regularized NMF [58] to map individual-specific large-scale functional networks across the cerebral cortex [40]. The NMF method decomposes the data by positively weighting cortical elements that covary, yielding a highly reproducible parts-based representation [58]. Our approach first randomly selected 100 participants and constructed a group-level atlas comprising 17 functional networks, based on prior research [28, 35–37, 61]. This process was repeated 50 times to generate 50 different group network atlases, which were then combined into a single consensus group atlas using spectral clustering. Using this group consensus atlas as initialization, we applied NMF to each participant’s functional time series to delineate personalized functional networks [40]. Finally, we created a probabilistic network atlas in which each vertex had 17 loadings, quantifying the extent to which this vertex belongs to each network. See Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods for details.

Inter-individual variability of functional network topography

Previous studies have shown greater variability in the association networks and lower variability in the primary sensorimotor networks in both adults and youths [24, 25, 28, 31, 33]. Here, we extended this analysis to children. For each of the 17 networks, we calculated the median absolute deviation of loading values across all participants for each vertex [35]. These 17 maps were then averaged to generate a final variability map, representing inter-individual variability in functional network topography at each vertex.

Prediction of SES factors from functional network topography using PLS-R

Prior studies have shown that machine learning-based multivariate pattern analyses can effectively utilize high-dimensional data and capture spatially distributed patterns [35, 37, 77]. Here, we employed machine learning regression to evaluate the relationship between multivariate patterns of functional network topography and individual differences in three SES factors, family income, parental education, and neighborhood disadvantage, separately.

We initially integrated the loading maps of 17 networks into a feature vector to capture the multivariate pattern of individual’s functional topography for the probabilistic network. We analyzed the data in fsaverage5 space, resulting in 18,715 vertices across the cortex after removing the medial wall (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Each participant’s feature vector consisted of 17 × 18,715 features, yielding 121,086 network loading features after excluding those with zero loadings across all participants. A partial least squares regression (PLS-R) was used to train a model predicting individuals’ SES scores using the network loading features [37]. A nested 2-fold cross-validation (2F-CV) was employed to assess the generalizability of the prediction accuracy to unseen individuals. The unseen individuals refer to the samples in the test set that were not used during model training. The outer 2F-CV evaluated the model’s generalizability, while the inner 2F-CV identified the optimal parameter (component number L), consistent with previous studies [35, 37, 67]. Specifically, we divided the dataset into two equal subsets, one as training data to train the model and the other as testing data to evaluate the generalizability of the model. Next, we exchanged the training and testing datasets and repeated this process. Within each cross-validation fold, we regressed out age, sex, motion, and site from each feature in the training data using a linear model. The coefficients derived from the training data were then applied to regress these covariates from each feature in the testing data, thereby preventing information leakage that could result in overfitting.

To measure prediction accuracy, we utilized Pearson’s correlation and the mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and actual SES scores, averaging these metrics across the two subsets [35, 37, 68, 70]. Given the random nature of the two-subset splitting, we repeated the 2F-CV process 101 times, calculating the median prediction accuracy (correlation r and MAE) across all resamples to ensure reliability [37]. We selected 101 resamples instead of 100 to streamline median selection. The significance of the prediction performance (correlation r) was evaluated using a permutation test with 1000 iterations [35, 37]. In each iteration, SES scores were randomly shuffled across the training samples without replacement, and the entire prediction procedure was repeated.

We then assessed the contribution of each feature to SES prediction. Using the 101 repetitions of random 2F-CV, we obtained 202 sets of regression coefficients (or weight maps) for each network. The absolute values of these weights reflect each feature’s contribution to the prediction, while the sign indicates whether the feature’s loading increases (positive) or decreases (negative) with higher SES [35, 37, 68, 70]. To summarize these contributions, we calculated the median weight for each feature (defined as the loading of a vertex in one network) across the 202 sets. To identify which networks contributed the most to SES prediction, we summed the contribution weights across all vertices within each network. A positive network-level weight indicates that individuals with higher SES scores tend to have a greater cortical representation in that network. Additionally, to map the spatial contribution of individual vertices, we summed the absolute weights across all 17 networks, thereby determining the prediction weight of each vertex. Finally, to evaluate the specific contribution of each network to SES prediction, we conducted separate predictions using only the multivariate functional network topography of each individual network and ranked their prediction accuracies. Further details on the prediction analyses can be found in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods.

Alignment between cortical maps and sensorimotor-association cortical axis

Prior studies have established the sensorimotor-association cortical axis as a unifying organizing principle of the human cortex, reflecting a continuous gradient of diverse neurobiological properties [34]. We hypothesized that this axis is linked to the cortical patterns of individual variability in functional topography and SES contribution weights. Using Spearman’s rank correlation, we assessed the relationship between functional topography variability and the sensorimotor-association cortical axis rank across vertices. We also evaluated the correlation between the cortical axis and the vertex-wise sum of SES prediction weights. Next, we calculated the average cortical axis rank for each of the 17 networks and analyzed its relationship with network-level contribution weights and prediction accuracies. A spatial permutation (1000 times) procedure called spin test (https://github.com/spin-test/spin-test) was used to evaluate the significance of the spatial alignment between cortical maps and sensorimotor-association cortical axis [73, 74].

Sensitivity analysis

We performed three additional analyses to confirm the robustness of our findings against methodological variations. First, we assessed whether discrete NMF network labels, rather than probabilistic loadings, could predict SES factors. Second, we tested whether the atlas from an independent network parcellation (multi-session hierarchical Bayesian model, MS-HBM) could also predict SES. Finally, we replaced PLS-R with ridge regression [35, 67, 70] to determine if SES could still be predicted. For more details on the MS-HBM parcellation and ridge regression method, see Additional File 1: Supplementary Methods.

Results

In this study, we evaluated the association between functional network topography and individual differences in three SES factors, including family income measured by income-to-needs ratio (INR), parental education, and neighborhood disadvantage measured by area deprivation index (ADI). We first delineated personalized functional networks for each child using resting-state fMRI data. As described in the “Methods” section, we included 6001 participants for personalized functional network construction, 4835 participants for INR analysis, 5308 participants for parental education analysis, and 4965 participants for ADI analysis.

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that the cerebral cortex can be parcellated into 17 large-scale functional networks in both adults and youths [28, 35, 61]. To facilitate comparison with these prior studies, we also parcellated the cortex into 17 functional networks for each child using resting-state fMRI time series and a spatially regularized NMF [40]. Specifically, we first generated a group atlas of probabilistic functional networks (Fig. 1, left), in which each cortical vertex had 17 loadings to quantify the extent to which the vertex belonged to each functional network. A group-level discrete functional atlas (Fig. 1, right) was acquired by categorizing each vertex into the network with the highest loading. By comparing the overlap between this discrete atlas with the priori canonical functional atlases [28, 61], our 17 networks were named as two visual (Network 3, 9), three somatomotor (10, 15, 16), one auditory (2), three dorsal attention (5, 7, 17), one ventral attention (13), two fronto-parietal (1, 14), three default mode (4, 6, 8), one temporo-parietal (11), and one limbic (12) networks. Consistent with previous studies, we observed that association networks were more widely distributed across the cortex, whereas sensorimotor networks were more localized. For example, the default mode network includes the medial prefrontal cortex, precuneus, inferior parietal lobe, lateral temporal, and prefrontal cortices, while the visual network includes the lateral and medial occipital cortices. To evaluate the robustness of this NMF-based group atlas, we compared our discrete atlas (Fig. 1, right) to the canonical 17 functional networks from the Yeo atlas [61] and the customized group atlas using another widely used approach, MS-HBM [28]. Our group atlas derived from the NMF significantly (adjusted Rand index = 0.4, spin test Pspin < 0.001) aligned with the canonical 17 networks using a spin-based spatial permutation test. Moreover, the similarity between our NMF atlas and the atlas from the MS-HBM was also significantly (adjusted Rand index = 0.43, Pspin < 0.001) higher than expected by chance. We made our personalized functional networks publicly available at the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) data archive.

Spatial cortical topography of functional networks varies across children

Previous studies have consistently reported marked individual variations in functional network topography across participants, especially in the higher-order association cortex, in both adults [24, 25, 28] and youths [35]. Here, we extended these findings to evaluate the inter-individual variability in the spatial topography of functional networks across children aged 9–10 years.

Using the NMF group atlas (Fig. 1) for initialization, we defined 17 personalized functional networks for each child with individual-specific resting-state fMRI data and the NMF approach. Visual comparisons of both probabilistic and discrete functional networks across participants indicated distinct individual-specific features in the spatial layout of functional networks on the cortex (Fig. 2A). As an illustration, we displayed three association networks, including the fronto-parietal (Network 1), default mode (Network 6), and dorsal attention (Network 17) networks, and two sensorimotor networks, including the visual (Network 9) and somatomotor (Network 10) networks. Based on this observation, we hypothesized that individual variation in functional network topography would be highest in the association cortex in children. To evaluate this hypothesis quantitatively, we calculated the median absolute deviation of the network loadings across all participants for all vertices in each network and acquired 17 individual variation maps. We then summed the 17 maps, resulting in an overall inter-individual variation map of the functional network topography (Fig. 2B). We observed that the inter-individual variability in functional network topography was highest in the association cortex, including the prefrontal and temporo-parietal areas, and lowest in the primary sensorimotor cortex. Finally, we ordered the median value of the inter-individual variability for all 17 networks (Fig. 2C). These results confirm that individual variability was highest in association networks, including the limbic, default mode, fronto-parietal, and attention networks, and lowest in sensorimotor networks, including the visual, somatomotor, and auditory networks. This pattern is similar to that observed in adults [24, 25, 28] and youths [35].

Previous studies have reported that individual variability in functional topography increases along the cortical axis from the primary sensorimotor networks to higher-order association networks in both adults and youths [25, 28, 34, 35]. We hypothesized that this phenomenon would also be observed in children. To test this hypothesis, we acquired a priori canonical map of the sensorimotor-association cortical axis (Fig. 2D), which was derived by averaging diverse cortical neurobiological properties [34]. Cortical vertices were continuously ranked along this axis, with the sensorimotor cortices representing the lowest rank and the association cortices representing the highest rank. Using Spearman’s rank correlation and conserved spin-based permutation test, we observed that individual variability was significantly associated with the ranks of the sensorimotor-association axis across all cortical vertices (rho = 0.55, Pspin < 0.001; Fig. 2E). Overall, our results reveal that the inter-individual variability in functional network topography in children was patterned along the sensorimotor-association cortical axis, similar to the findings in adults and youths [25, 28, 34, 35].

The association between functional topography and income-to-needs ratio organizes along sensorimotor-association cortical axis

Having demonstrated marked inter-individual variability in the functional network topography across participants, especially in the association cortex, we next examined how childhood SES factors were related to individual variations in functional topography. We initially focused on the income-to-needs ratio (INR) [54, 78] before assessing two additional SES factors in the following sections.

We combined the loading maps of all 17 networks into a high-dimensional feature vector to represent the multivariate pattern of the functional topography for each participant. Using PLS-R and nested 2F-CV, we found that functional network topography significantly predicted unseen individuals’ INR. The median correlation between the actual and predicted INR was r = 0.27 across 101 repetitions (Fig. 3A). A permutation test (1000 iterations) indicated that this prediction accuracy was significantly higher than that expected by chance (Pperm < 0.001). The median MAE between the actual and predicted INR was 1.97. To visualize the correlation between the actual and predicted INR intuitively, we displayed a scatter plot of the 2F-CV with the median correlation r across the 101 repetitions (Fig. 3B). We next evaluated how functional network topographic features contributed to INR prediction. For each network, the PLS-R model returned a contribution weight for the loading feature of each vertex (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). A positive weight suggests that the network loading of a vertex increases with higher INR, whereas a negative weight suggests a decrease in network loading. For example, the default mode network (Network 6) exhibited the highest positive contribution weight in the lateral temporal lobe (Fig. 3C). To derive a network-level measure of the total contribution weight, we summed the weights of all loading features within each network. The results indicate that the association between the total representation of the functional network topography and INR showed the highest positive values in the default mode networks and the lowest negative values in the somatomotor networks (Fig. 3D). This result suggests that children with higher INR exhibited a larger functional network representation in the default mode networks and a relatively smaller representation in the somatomotor networks. Using Spearman’s rank correlation, total network contribution weights were positively (r = 0.32, Pspin = 0.06) associated with the network-level average ranks of the sensorimotor-association axis across the 17 networks (Fig. 3E). This result indicates a positive INR-topography relationship in the sensorimotor-association axis association pole and a negative INR-topography relationship in the sensorimotor pole.

Next, we evaluated how each cortical vertex contributed to INR prediction by summing the absolute values of the contribution weights of all 17 network loadings for the vertex. The occipito-temporal, parietal, and prefrontal areas contributed most to the prediction (Fig. 3F). Using a spatial permutation test, we observed that the absolute contribution weights were positively correlated with the ranks of the sensorimotor-association axis (r = 0.29, Pspin < 0.001) across all cortical vertices (Fig. 3G). This result further confirms that the association vertices located at the top end of the sensorimotor-association axis contributed most to the prediction.

To further verify the relative association between functional topography and INR at the network level, we predicted the INR using multivariate functional topography for each network separately and ordered the prediction accuracies. The results indicate that the prediction accuracies were highest in the attention and default mode networks and lowest in the primary sensorimotor networks (Fig. 3H). Moreover, the network-level prediction accuracies were correlated with the network-level average ranks of the sensorimotor-association axis across the 17 networks (r = 0.29, Pspin = 0.08, Fig. 3I), further confirming that the topography-INR association was explained by the sensorimotor-association cortical axis at the network level. Overall, our results robustly demonstrate that the association between functional topography and INR is hierarchically patterned in the cortex along the fundamental sensorimotor-association axis.

The associations between functional topography and both parental education and neighborhood disadvantage unfold along the sensorimotor-association cortical axis

After establishing the hierarchical cortical organization of the relationship between functional topography and INR, we further examined two additional SES factors: parental education and neighborhood disadvantage, measured by the area deprivation index (ADI).

Using our prediction framework with PLS-R and repeated nested 2F-CV, we found that the functional topography significantly predicted both parental education and the ADI in unseen individuals. Specifically, the median correlation between actual and predicted scores was r = 0.295 (Pperm < 0.001, Fig. 4A) for education and r = 0.25 (Pperm < 0.001, Fig. 4G) for ADI across 101 repetitions. Scatter plots illustrating the correlation between actual and predicted scores for education (Fig. 4B) and ADI (Fig. 4H) from the 2F-CV with median correlation further supported these findings. For each network, the PLS-R model returned a contribution weight for the loading feature of each vertex for both parental education (Additional file 1: Fig. S4) and ADI (Additional file 1: Fig. S5) predictions. By summing the prediction weights of all vertices for each functional network, we found that primary somatomotor networks exhibited negative weights, while higher-order association networks—including the default mode, fronto-parietal, and dorsal attention networks—showed positive weights for parental education (Fig. 4C). In contrast, an opposite trend was observed for the contribution weights in predicting ADI (Fig. 4I). These findings suggest that, similar to INR, the advantages associated with parental education and neighborhood context correspond to greater representation in default mode network and reduced representation in somatomotor network.

Furthermore, summing the absolute weights of all 17 network loadings for each vertex revealed that, as with INR, the occipito-temporal, parietal, and prefrontal regions contributed most significantly to the prediction of both parental education (Fig. 4D) and ADI (Fig. 4J). When examining the multivariate functional topography of each network individually, the ventral attention and default mode networks demonstrated the highest prediction accuracy, while the primary sensorimotor networks showed the lowest for both parental education (Fig. 4E) and ADI (Fig. 4K).

Finally, we evaluated the spatial alignment between the contribution patterns and the sensorimotor-association cortical axis across the cortex. Specifically, the network-level summed weights correlated with the average ranks of the sensorimotor-association cortical axis across the 17 networks for both parental education (r = 0.39, pspin = 0.02, Fig. 4F left) and ADI (r = − 0.30, pspin = 0.061, Fig. 4L left). These correlations suggest that the relationship between SES advantage and functional network representations is positive in the association poles of the cortical axis and negative in the sensorimotor pole for both factors. Additionally, the vertex-wise absolute summed weights showed significant correlations with sensorimotor-association axis ranks across all vertices for both parental education (r = 0.35, pspin < 0.001, Fig. 4F middle) and ADI (r = 0.34, pspin < 0.001, Fig. 4L middle), suggesting association cortices contributed the most to the prediction. Furthermore, network-specific prediction accuracies displayed a significant positive relationship with sensorimotor-association axis ranks across the 17 networks for both parental education (r = 0.59, pspin < 0.001, Fig. 4F right) and ADI (r = 0.51, pspin = 0.004, Fig. 4L right), highlighting a stronger association between SES and functional topography of the higher-order association networks.

Overall, these results indicate that, similar to INR, the association between functional network topography and both parental education and ADI is also patterned along the sensorimotor-association cortical axis.

Association between functional topography and SES factors is robust to methodological variation

Four sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the association between functional topography and SES factors against methodological variance. First, we assessed whether discrete network labels from NMF significantly predicted SES factors, instead of using the probabilistic network loadings. The median correlation between the actual and predicted SES across 101 repetitions was r = 0.25 for INR (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S6A and S6B), r = 0.23 (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S7A and S7B) for parental education, and r = 0.27 (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S8A and S8B) for ADI. The cortical contribution map showed the highest values in the higher-order association cortex for all three predictions (Additional file 1: Fig. S6C, S7C, and S8C). Second, we evaluated whether a discrete network atlas derived from another independent parcellation approach (MS-HBM) could also predict SES. The median correlation between actual and predicted SES across 101 repetitions was r = 0.22 for INR (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S6D and S6E), r = 0.20 for parental education (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S7D and S7E), and r = 0.22 for ADI (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S8D and S8E). The contribution weights were consistent with our primary results (Additional file 1: Fig. S6F, S7F, S8F). Third, we replaced PLS-R with ridge regression to predict SES. The median correlation between actual and predicted SES across 101 repetitions was r = 0.18 for INR (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S6G and S6H), r = 0.20 for parental education (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S7G and S7H), and r = 0.15 for ADI (Pperm < 0.001, Additional file 1: Fig. S8G and S8H). The contribution weights remained similar to our primary findings (Additional file 1: Fig. S6I, S7I, S8I). Finally, to ensure the reproducibility of the association between functional topography and SES, we performed bootstrap resampling with 500 iterations, each involving 80% of the participants. The median correlation between actual and predicted SES across these 500 bootstraps was r = 0.27 for INR, r = 0.29 for parental education, and r = 0.25 for ADI (Additional file 1: Fig. S9). Collectively, these analyses indicate that our primary results are robust to methodological variations.

Discussion

Leveraging a large cohort of children from the ABCD study, we demonstrated that individual variations in personalized functional network topography and their associations with SES factors unfold along the sensorimotor-association cortical axis. Specifically, individual variability in functional topography exhibited a continuous decline along this axis, spanning from the higher-order association cortex to the primary sensorimotor cortex. Moreover, personalized functional topography significantly predicted unseen individuals’ SES factors, including family income measured by INR, parental education, and neighborhood disadvantage measured by ADI. A convergent cortical spatial organization was observed in the relationship between functional topography and three SES factors. Notably, higher-order association networks, such as attention and default mode networks, contributed the most to the prediction of SES scores, indicating a stronger relationship with functional topography compared to sensorimotor networks. In terms of directionality, we found that higher SES scores were associated with an increased total representation of default mode networks and a decreased representation of somatomotor networks across all three factors. Finally, we have made the personalized functional networks of ABCD children publicly available as an open-access resource. Together, our findings highlight a unified socioeconomic impact on the individual variation of personalized functional neuroanatomy.

Previous studies have consistently reported that the spatial topography of functional networks in the cortex differs substantially across adults and youths [23–32, 35, 79]. This individual variability of functional topography is observed to be highest in the higher-order association cortex and lowest in the primary sensorimotor cortex, aligning with the axis of the sensorimotor-association cortical hierarchy [25, 28, 34, 35]. Building on these results from adults and youths, we provided evidence that the functional network topography also exhibits the highest inter-individual variability in the association cortex and the lowest in the sensorimotor cortex for children aged 9 to 10 years. Moreover, the spatial pattern of this individual variability was explained by the fundamental organizing principle of the sensorimotor-association cortical axis, as observed in previous studies on youths [34, 35]. This finding contributes to our understanding of the evolution and emergence of the cortical individual variability of personalized functional network topography by demonstrating that this pattern is already evident in early childhood.

Our findings indicate that functional network topography significantly predicts individual differences in SES. The prediction accuracies for family income, parental education, and neighborhood disadvantage were remarkably similar, underscoring the robustness of the association between functional topography and SES. This consistency aligns with previous research linking functional connectivity and these SES factors, which exhibit overlapping effects [2, 15, 16]. While prior research focused on connectivity between regions or networks using group atlases, our work reveals that the spatial arrangement of personalized functional networks is related to individual SES differences. Studies have emphasized that cortical topography and connectivity each contribute uniquely to individual variations, and neglecting topographic information can obscure the signals in connectivity [31, 32]. By incorporating functional topography, our results provide understanding of how SES factors influence neurodevelopmental trajectories through functional neuroanatomy. These findings also align with recent research showing that functional network topography is associated with a general exposome factor, capturing broader environmental influences [80].

We found that the relationships between functional topography and SES were heterogeneously distributed across the cortex and consistent among the three SES factors: family income-to-needs ratio, parental education, and neighborhood advantage. Notably, this distribution aligned with the sensorimotor-association cortical axis [34], which spans from primary sensorimotor regions to higher-order association areas. Our analysis, using summarized prediction weights at both network and vertex levels, revealed that network-level summed weights shifted from strong negative to positive contributions along the sensorimotor-association cortical axis, while vertex-level absolute contribution weights increased continuously. Higher-order association networks also demonstrated stronger prediction accuracy than sensorimotor networks. Previous studies have identified the sensorimotor-association axis as a unifying organizing principle across diverse cortical properties, such as structure, function, metabolism, and evolution [34, 81–90]. Our findings extend this by linking the sensorimotor-association axis to the spatial distribution of the association between SES and functional topography in childhood. Prior work showed that association networks undergo prolonged developmental changes and are more sensitive to environmental influences during childhood and adolescence [4, 34], partially explaining our results. Consistent with our findings, cortical distribution of the relationship between intrinsic functional amplitude and environmental factors has been shown to align with the sensorimotor-association axis [34].

We found that higher family income, greater parental education, and neighborhood advantages were consistently associated with enhanced cortical representation in the default mode network and reduced representation in the somatomotor network. This finding aligns with recent studies showing a positive correlation between SES advantages and functional connectivity in the default mode network, alongside a negative correlation in the somatomotor network [15, 22]. Notably, we observed subtle differences among the three SES factors: the dorsal attention network showed a stronger relationship with income and neighborhood advantage compared to the fronto-parietal network, which was more closely linked to parental education. These findings are consistent with prior work highlighting both similarities and differences in SES associations with functional connectivity [15]. Additionally, while the ventral attention network (Network 13) did not show strong positive or negative relationships in terms of contribution weights, it demonstrated the highest prediction accuracy for the three SES factors when analyzed individually. This suggests that the functional topography of the ventral attention network has a complex and heterogeneous relationship with SES, warranting further investigation.

The ABCD study encompasses a large-scale cohort of children, integrating multimodal neuroimaging, genetic data, and a comprehensive array of behavioral, cognitive, environmental, and mental health measurements [39, 91–93]. We present personalized functional network atlases for ABCD children who had high-quality resting-state fMRI data. This resource would help elucidate how genetic and environmental factors shape neurodevelopmental trajectories to influence human cognition and mental health outcomes [39]. Given the variability in the functional network topography among children, our resource offers valuable insights into normal neurodevelopmental trajectories and their deviations associated with mental disorders from a personalized functional neuroanatomy perspective [23–25, 35].

This study has several limitations. First, all data presented were cross-sectional; future studies utilizing longitudinal acquisitions from the ABCD study [39] are needed to characterize within-individual effects of SES on functional network topography. Second, the parcellation of personalized functional networks was based on a data-driven approach, and no standard has been established to ascertain the neurobiological ground truth in human participants. However, our functional networks are similar to previous canonical network parcellations [61, 94], and our results are robust to various methodological variations, including an independent approach for functional network parcellation [28]. This reproducibility demonstrates the robustness of our study. Third, our resource of personalized functional networks is limited to one specific approach (i.e., NMF). The functional networks from alternative approaches would be useful for comparison [95]. Fourth, our study utilized the federal poverty line to calculate the family income-to-needs ratio. However, given that the 21 ABCD sites are distributed across states with varying costs of living, future research should consider using state-specific poverty thresholds to provide a more accurate assessment of the income-to-needs ratio. Fifth, although our model effectively captures individual differences in SES factors, the mean absolute error remains high. Future research could enhance predictive accuracy by employing advanced machine learning techniques or incorporating multi-modal neuroimaging data. Finally, although the effect size of SES predictions was relatively small, our large sample size (> 4,800 children for all three SES factors) mitigates the risk of systematic inflation of effect sizes, providing a more accurate estimate of the relationships observed [96, 97].

Conclusions

In summary, our findings offer insights into how early SES backgrounds impact the spatial cortical topography of personalized functional networks during childhood. We found that both individual variability in functional topography and its relationship with SES exhibit distinct patterns across the cortex, aligned with the sensorimotor-association cortical axis. These findings enhance our understanding of the spatial variation of cortical plasticity in functional neuroanatomy, highlighting region-specific adaptations in response to diverse SES backgrounds.
