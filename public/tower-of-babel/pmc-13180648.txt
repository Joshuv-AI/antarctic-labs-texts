Chemical composition of Deverra scoparia essential oil under different drying conditions and its insecticidal properties against Parlatoria blanchardi (Hemiptera: Diaspididae)
Authors: Roukia Hammoudi, Farid Chebrouk, Hayet Benameur-Saggou, Hakim Belkhalfa, Mahfoud Hadj Mahammed, Mohamed Abou-Mustapha, Ahmed K Rashwan, Amged El-Harairy, Ahmed A A Aioub, Amr Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Parlatoria blanchardi Targ. (Hemiptera: Diaspididae) is a destructive scale insect that causes serious damage to date palm plantations. The essential oil (EO) of Deverra scoparia Coss. & Durieu was investigated for its insecticidal activity against P. blanchardi under different drying methods. This is the first time to our knowledge that the insecticidal activity against P. blanchardi has been reported for the plant species D. scoparia. This study evaluates, for the first time, the bioactivity of D. scoparia EO, as well as its insecticidal activity against P. blanchardi under different drying techniques (air drying, sun drying, and microwave drying at 264, 400, and 800 W). The chemical composition of D. scoparia EO was identified and characterized using gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC–MS). In addition, the responses of the target enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and 2 key detoxification enzymes, carboxylesterase (CarE) and glutathione S-transferase (GST), in P. blanchardi were investigated to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of toxicity. Our data showed that a total of 32 compounds were identified, with myristicin (43.44% to 69.73%), D-limonene (6.61% to 12.57%), dillapiole (0.74% to 10.87%), α-phellandrene (0.47% to 8.16%), and α-pinene (4.36% to 8.10%) as the dominant constituents. Insecticidal activity varied markedly with drying technique; microwave drying at 264 W produced the most toxic EO against adult stages (LC50 = 0.12 to 0.13 µl/ml), while sun-dried EO showed the highest efficacy against immature stages. Enzyme assays revealed that microwave-derived oils significantly increased AChE activity while strongly suppressing CarE and GST activities. Our findings suggest that this plant might be efficient as a potential alternative control agent for P. blanchardi.Keywords: Deverra scoparia, essential oil, drying method, enzyme assay, Parlatoria blanchardi

Introduction

The white cochineal Parlatoria blanchardi (Hemiptera: Diaspididae) is considered one of the most serious date palm pests (Mlik et al. 2025). Adults and nymphs of this insect feed on leaf sap, sucking a great amount of sap, which contains macro- and microelements. At a high level of infestation with this scale insect, remarkable damage occurred, resulting in early fresh leaves drooping and a reduction in fruit yield (Moussa et al. 2012). P. blanchardi can cause heavy damage by sucking the plant sap, which gives low rates of photosynthesis and respiration, leading to curling, yellowing, and drooping of leaves (Kumar and Naqvi 2020). A characteristic symptom of infestation by P. blanchardi is the appearance and accumulation of its scales on attacked palm parts (Salman and Moussa). Infestation of this pest in the early stage of the plantation may cause saplings to die. P. blanchardii influences photosynthetic pigments like chlorophyll a and b and carotenoids; it also affects the area of leaflet, moisture, dry weight, and wax proportion, ultimately reducing yield production by 30 to 50 kg per palm (Idder-Ighili et al. 2015). It may result in losses of up to 85% to 90%, depending on the resistance of varieties, the intensity of infestation, and orchard management (Ahmed et al. 2015). Therefore, this insect causes significant economic losses and poses a serious threat to global date production.

Although chemical control methods against P. blanchardi are generally effective, the extensive and repeated use of insecticides has led to serious resistance problems and has undesirable effects on nontarget organisms (Wan et al. 2025). Furthermore, European legislation (Regulations EC No. 1107/2009) has significantly restricted and revised the use of pesticides on crops. Therefore, the development of environmentally friendly and economically viable alternative control strategies should be encouraged. Consequently, there is an urgent need for safer and more sustainable approaches to ­manage P. blanchardi infestations.

Essential oils (EOs) are complex and heterogeneous mixtures of terpenoids and phenylpropanoids extracted by hydrodistillation, steam distillation, dry distillation, or mechanical processes (de Sousa et al. 2023). EOs have gained popularity because they are readily available in various plants, and they exhibit low toxicity to mammals and high degradation patterns (Garrido-Miranda et al. 2022). Deverra scoparia Coss. & Dur. is an endemic species growing in the Algerian Sahara. It plays an important role in traditional medicine, where it is used as a remedy for several diseases (Harchaoui et al. 2018). Numerous phytochemical and pharmacological investigations have been done on this plant, harvested from different Saharan regions. These studies revealed a significant number of secondary metabolites with proven biological properties, phenolic compounds, and EOs (Attia et al. 2011, Benarfa et al. 2019, Boukhalfa et al. 2023). Previous studies investigated the acaricidal effect of D. scoparia on Tetranychus urticae (Attia et al. 2011) and Dermanyssus gallinae (Ghrabi-Gammar et al. 2009).

Drying is one of the most common methods of preserving plants, whose purpose is to inhibit enzymatic degradation and prevent the growth of microorganisms by decreasing water activity (Kaveh et al. 2024). The quality of volatile components in aromatic plants mainly depends on drying methods and the biological characteristics of the plant species (Sowndhararajan et al. 2016). This process causes changes in the chemical composition of EOs and therefore affects their organoleptic characteristics (Caputo et al. 2022). It has also been proven that the drying operation can cause important changes in the biological activities of aromatic oils (Farahmandfar et al. 2020). To date, no study has been published on the toxicity of D. scoparia EO against P. blanchardi.

Our study hypothesized that the EO of D. scoparia exhibits insecticidal activity against P. blanchardi, and that this activity may vary depending on the drying method applied before extraction. Since drying conditions are known to influence the qualitative and quantitative composition of volatile constituents, we further hypothesized that variations in drying techniques would alter the chemical profile and, consequently, the biological efficacy of the EO. Therefore, the objectives of this study were: (i) to evaluate the effect of different drying methods on the yield and chemical composition of D. scoparia EO using GC–MS analysis; (ii) to assess the insecticidal efficacy of the obtained oils against P. blanchardi; and (iii) to determine the activities of detoxification and neurotoxicity-related enzymes, including carboxylesterase (CarE), glutathione S-transferase (GST), and acetylcholinesterase (AChE), and correlate these biochemical responses with the observed toxicity.

Materials and Methods

Insect Rearing

Leaflets of date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.) infested with the white cochineal Parlatoria blanchardi were collected in April 2023 from a palm grove located in Ouargla, southeastern Algeria (31°57′N, 5°20′E). The insects were collected from the cultivar Deglet Nour, which is of high commercial importance worldwide. Parlatoria blanchardi was reared under controlled laboratory conditions at the Biogeochemistry of Desertic Environments Laboratory, Faculty of Natural and Life Sciences, University of Ouargla, Algeria. The insects were maintained on potted date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L., cv. Deglet Nour) seedlings. The seedlings used for rearing were 8 to 12 mo old, approximately 40 to 60 cm in height, and cultivated in 3-L plastic pots containing a sterilized substrate mixture of peat, sand, and local loamy soil (1:1:1, v/v/v). Before infestation, seedlings were carefully inspected to ensure that they were free from pesticide residues and insect infestation. Infested leaflets containing different developmental stages were collected from the field and attached to healthy palm leaves to establish a laboratory colony. The insects were maintained in a growth chamber at 27 ± 2 °C, 60% to 70% relative humidity, and a 16:8 h (light:dark) photoperiod. Fresh, uninfected seedlings were periodically introduced to maintain the colony, while old and senescent leaves were removed to prevent fungal contamination. Under these controlled conditions, P. blanchardi completed its life cycle within approximately 6 to 8 wk, ensuring continuous culture throughout the experimental period.

Plant Material

Aerial parts (leaves, young stems, and inflorescences) of Deverra scoparia Coss. & Dur. were harvested from Ghardaïa (32°16′22″ N, 3°37′39″ E) during the flowering stage in March 2021. Botanical identification and taxonomic authentication were carried out by botanists from the Faculty of Natural and Life Sciences (Ouargla University). A voucher specimen (PM/02-C) was deposited at the Herbarium of the Laboratory of Biogeochemistry of Desert Environments at the same institution. Immediately after collection, plant materials were manually cleaned to remove soil particles, dust, and foreign plant debris. The samples were gently washed with distilled water when necessary and air-dried with paper towels to remove surface moisture. The aerial parts were then cut into small uniform segments (approximately 2 to 3 cm in length) using sterile stainless-steel scissors to ensure homogeneity and facilitate uniform drying. For each drying treatment, approximately 500 g (fresh weight) of plant material was used.

Drying Procedures

The aerial parts of D. scoparia were subjected to different drying methods before EO extraction. For open-air drying, plant materials were spread on an open surface at ambient temperature (mean = 30 °C) for 14 d and turned over several times to ensure uniform drying. Sun drying was performed by placing the samples on a wire mesh tray positioned on a sand platform under direct sunlight (mean = 40 °C) for 2 d. Microwave (MW) drying was conducted in a domestic digital MW oven (model MW73B, Samsung, Australia; 230 V, 50 Hz, maximum power output = 1,000 W) at different power levels (264, 400, and 800 W).

GC–MS Analysis

Dried samples of D. scoparia were subjected to hydrodistillation for 4 h using a Clevenger-type apparatus. The obtained EOs were dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and stored at +4 °C until use. GC–MS analysis was performed using a Hewlett–Packard computerized system comprising a 6890-gas chromatograph coupled to a 5973A mass selective detector, equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 0.5 ml min−1. The injection volume was 0.2 μl, operated in split mode (1:20), with the injector temperature set at 250 °C. The oven temperature program was maintained at 60 °C for 8 min, then programmed to 250 °C at a rate of 2 °C min−1, and held isothermally for 10 min. Mass spectrometric detection was carried out in electron ionization mode at 70 eV, with a mass scan range of 55 to 550 m/z. The total GC run time was approximately 283 min. Each EO sample was analyzed in triplicate using independent injections to ensure analytical reproducibility. The identification of EO constituents was based on a comparison of their retention indices, calculated by using a homologous series of C8 to C28 n-alkanes with those in the literature (Babushok et al. 2011, Adams 2017). Compound identification was further confirmed by matching the obtained mass spectra with those stored in the NIST 2020 and Wiley 07 mass spectral libraries and by comparison with published data (Adams 2017). The relative concentrations of individual components (%) were calculated from the mean peak areas of the GC chromatograms without the use of correction factors, using ChemStation software.

Concentration–Mortality Bioassay

Three 2-cm leaflet pieces of the Deglet-Nour cultivar infested with P. blanchardi were excised and the number of scales was counted under a binocular stereomicroscope. The bioassay was conducted using a leaf-spray method. Each leaflet was sprayed with 1 ml of D. scoparia EO at concentrations of 0.0075, 0.15, 3.75, 7.5, and 15 µl/ml (v/v) using a hand-held atomizer to ensure uniform coverage. After air-drying for 5 min, the treated leaflets were immediately placed into 0.25-L glass jars, which served as the exposure units. Each jar contained 1 treated leaflet and 20 insects (adult males, adult females, and first- and second-instar nymphs). Jars were maintained at 30 ± 0.1 °C for 24 h. Each concentration was tested in triplicate (60 insects per concentration). Distilled water and chlorpyrifos (97%) were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. Insects were exposed to the treated date palm leaflets for 24 h inside the glass jars, after which mortality was recorded. Surviving insects were transferred to clean containers supplied with fresh leaves.

Enzyme Assay

Based on the bioassay results, surviving adult males and females of P. blanchardi were sampled after the treatment with the LC50 of D. scoparia EO under a binocular magnifying glass for AChE, GST, and CarE extraction. For each replicate, 20 insects were pooled, and 3 independent biological replicates were prepared for each treatment. Individual P. blanchardi male and female under different drying methods were homogenized in ice-cold 0.1 mol L−1 sodium phosphate buffer solutions to measure the activities of 3 enzymes as follows:

AChE Assay

The surviving adults were homogenized in a pre-chilled mortar, and the homogenate was suspended in 1 ml of ice-cold distilled water and 2 ml of phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.5) containing 0.1% Tween 20. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm (≈11,000 ×g) for 20 min at 4 °C (Yi et al. 2006). The resulting supernatant was used as the enzyme source. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method (Bradford 1976), with bovine serum albumin (BSA F-V) as the standard. The inhibition of AChE activity by D. scoparia EO and the determination of its LC50 value were performed using a modified Ellman’s colorimetric method (Ellman et al. 1961). For each assay, 150 µl of the enzyme extract and 50 µl of acetylthiocholine iodide (15 mM) were mixed with 50 µl of EO solution prepared in acetone containing 0.01% Tween 20. The mixture was incubated at 30 °C for 15 min, and the reaction was terminated by adding 1,800 µl of 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (0.1 M). Negative control tubes contained 50 µl of acetone without EO, while positive control tubes contained 50 µl of acetone with chlorpyrifos. Absorbance was measured at 412 nm against a reagent blank.

CarE Assay

The activity of alpha (α)- and beta (ß)-esterase was examined according to Van Asperen (1962). The adults were homogenized in a phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min. A mixture of the supernatant (50 μl) with 30 mM (α) or (ß)-naphthyl acetate (NA) (50 μl) was incubated at 30 °C for 15 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 μl of the stop solution, which consisted of Fast Blue B (1%) and sodium dodecyl sulphate (5%) (2:1). The hydrolysis of α-NA and ß-NA was measured at 600 and 550 nm, respectively, using a UV/Vis Spectrophotometer (Jenway 7205UV/Vis). The mean levels of the CarE activity were derived from the standard curves of the protein content and α- and ß-naphthol.

GST Assay

The GST activity was measured as indicated by Habig et al. (1974) using 1-chloro-2, 4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB) as a substrate. The adults were homogenized in a phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The reaction solution consisted of 10 μl supernatant, 25 μl of 30 mM CDNB, and 25 μl of 50 mM glutathione. The GST activity was determined with a UV/Vis Spectrophotometer (Jenway 7205UV/Vis) at 340 nm using a kinetic mode for 5 min.

Statistical Analysis

The corrected mortality percentages according to Abbott (1925) were statistically analyzed according to the method of Finney (1972) using Probit-MSChart computer program (Chi 2009) to determine LC50 and LC90 values. All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of 3 replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using XLSTAT software version 2.5. The dataset consisted of 32 compounds. Before analysis, data normality and homogeneity of variances were verified. Enzyme activities were statistically analyzed and graphically represented using the one-way analysis of variance was used to assess differences among treatments within GraphPad Prism 10. When significant differences were detected, means were separated using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Identification of D. scoparia EO by GC–MS

The GC-MS chromatogram of D. scoparia EO recorded 32 compounds under various drying methods, representing 98.18% to 99.85% of the total EO composition (Supplementary Fig. S1). Myristicin was the predominant component in all samples, reaching 69.73 ± 0.43% under MW400, followed by 64.33 ± 0.36% in MW800, 60.37 ± 2.22% in sun-dried, and 46.42 ± 1.04% in air-dried oils, with the lowest content in MW264 (43.44 ± 2.67%). α-Pinene and D-limonene were present at moderate levels. α-Pinene was highest in air-dried oil (8.10 ± 0.45%) and decreased under MW400 (4.36 ± 1.45%), while D-limonene reached 12.57 ± 1.56% in air-dried and 11.92 ± 0.19% in MW800 oils. α-Phellandrene content was highest in MW264 (8.16 ± 1.48%) and air-dried oils (7.41 ± 0.51%), but markedly lower in sun-dried (2.25 ± 0.16%), MW800 (1.01 ± 0.07%), and MW400 (0.47 ± 0.04%) samples. Dill apiole exhibited the greatest variation, with sun-dried (10.87 ± 1.15%) and MW264 (9.31 ± 1.55%) oils containing the highest levels, while MW800 oil had the lowest (0.74 ± 0.14%). Air-dried and MW400 oils contained 5.30 ± 0.73% and 4.68 ± 0.66%, respectively. The EO yield of D. scoparia varied markedly depending on the drying method applied before extraction. The highest oil yield was obtained from sun-dried samples (6.51 ± 0.71 ml/100 g dry weight), followed by MW400 (3.97 ± 0.41%), MW264 (3.69 ± 0.24%), and air-dried materials (3.14 ± 0.31%). The lowest yield was recorded for MW800-treated samples (2.52 ± 0.73%) (Table 1).

Insecticidal Activity

The insecticidal activity of D. scoparia EO against P. blanchardi varied considerably depending on the drying method used before oil extraction. Under air-drying conditions, the D. scoparia EO exhibited moderate toxicity, with LC50 and LC90 values of 2.04 and 3.66 µl/ml for males, 2.61 and 4.69 µl/ml for females, and 6.05 and 10.87 µl/ml for second-instar nymphs, while no detectable toxicity was observed against first-instar nymphs. In contrast, MW at MW264 showed the highest toxicity toward both adult stages, with LC50 values of 0.12 and 0.13 µl/ml and LC90 values of 0.22 and 0.24 µl/ml for males and females, respectively. The sun-dried D. scoparia EO displayed superior toxicity against the immature stages, with LC50 values of 0.33 and 0.36 µl/ml and LC90 values of 0.59 and 0.64 µl/ml for the first- and second-instar nymphs, respectively. A distinct trend was evident with decreasing MW power from 800 to 264 W, where LC50 and LC90 values sharply declined, indicating enhanced potency. For instance, LC50 and LC90 values decreased from 4.39 and 8.23 µl/ml to 0.12 and 0.22 µl/ml in males, from 7.26 and 10.89 µl/ml to 0.13 and 0.24 µl/ml in females, and from 2.29 and 4.12 µl/ml to 0.38 and 0.66 µl/ml and 6.05 and 11.89 µl/ml to 0.38 and 0.66 µl/ml in the first- and second-instar nymphs, respectively, when compared with chlorpyrifos (Table 2).

Effect of D. scoparia EO on AChE of P. blanchardi

The effect of D. scoparia EO on the enzyme activities of AchE, CarE, and GST in P. blanchardi male and female under different drying methods was illustrated in Figs 1–3. AChE activity in P. blanchardi was relatively low in air-dried insects, measuring 5.14 and 5.62 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in males and females, respectively, and increased moderately following sun drying (8.22 and 8.33 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein, respectively). In contrast, all MW treatments caused a substantial elevation in AChE activity in both sexes, with the highest values observed under MW800 (13.00 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in males and 12.98 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in females), followed by MW400 (12.22 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in males and 12.76 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in females) and MW264 (10.02 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in males and 11.07 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in females). By comparison, chlorpyrifos treatment resulted in the lowest AChE activity (0.80 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in males and 0.92 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein in females). Conversely, CarE and GST activities exhibited an opposite trend. The chlorpyrifos-treated group showed the highest activities of CarE (8.28 and 8.87 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein) and GST (10.39 and 12.76 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein) in males and females, respectively. Moderate activities of CarE and GST were recorded in air-dried insects (4.15 and 4.96 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein; 3.14 and 3.63 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein) and sun-dried insects (3.21 and 3.54 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein; 3.11 and 3.47 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein) for males and females, respectively. In contrast, all MW treatments markedly suppressed CarE and GST activities, with the highest reductions observed under MW800 (0.69 and 0.63 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein for CarE, and 1.30 and 1.19 μmol min−1 mg−1 protein for GST) in males and females, respectively.

Discussion

GC-MS is an excellent tool for studying the VOCs from spices and aromatic plants, even at trace levels (Farag et al. 2022). Our findings revealed the identification of 32 compounds, which accounted for more than 98% of the total oil content across all treatments, indicating the reliability of the extraction and analytical procedures. Myristicin, D-Limonene, α-Pinene, and Dill apiole were the most abundant constituents of D. scoparia EO across the examined drying treatments. Our data are consistent with research by Jiang and Zhang (2012) reporting that the high percentages of bioactive components of phenylpropanoids and phathalids were recorded in the EOs of this plant (especially myristicin “up to 58.19%” and dill apiole “up to 25.52%”). Another study demonstrated that the major components of D. scoparia EO showed the greatest variability, with α-pinene (53.25%) and limonene (59.68%) (Benarfa et al. 2020). Such variability highlights the strong influence of post-harvest processing and environmental conditions on EO composition. The difference between the obtained yields for sun drying and open-air drying could be attributed to the time, which is longer in the case of open-air drying (Rahimmalek and Goli 2013). An optimal balance between drying time and temperature is therefore likely to minimize thermal degradation and volatilization losses of EO constituents. Moreover, the adequate ambient Saharan air temperature and its low relative humidity contribute to the good drying of the plant. Our results are in accordance with other study on various aromatic species (Sefidkon et al. 2006). Nevertheless, they are not congruent with those reported by Omidbaigi et al. (2004), who reported higher losses during sun drying of Roman chamomile. These contrasting outcomes further support the notion that drying effects are species- and compound-dependent (Mashkani et al. 2018).

Importantly, MW drying treatments resulted in significant differences in D. scoparia EO activity, as reflected by both compositional shifts and biological efficacy. The largest reduction in EO yield was observed at 800 W (2.52% ± 0.73), suggesting that excessive MW power may intensify volatilization and thermal degradation processes. High MW energy accelerates internal water evaporation through dipole rotation and ionic conduction mechanisms, generating rapid heating within plant tissues (Franco-Vega et al. 2016). While this shortens drying time, it may also cause cell rupture, oxidation, molecular rearrangement, and the formation of secondary artefacts, thereby altering the chemical profile of the oil. Selective loss of highly volatile monoterpenes under high-power MW conditions may modify both GC–MS composition and insecticidal performance. These observations are consistent with previous reports describing adverse effects of excessive MW power on EO yield and quality (Rahimmalek and Goli 2013, Mashkani et al. 2018).

The largest loss in EO contents is found for MW drying at 800 W, which may be associated with enhanced volatilization and thermal degradation of oil constituents rather than with a single chemical instability mechanism. High MW power accelerates moisture removal but may also promote cell rupture, oxidation, molecular rearrangement, and the formation of drying-induced artefacts, all of which can alter the chemical profile of the oil. Selective loss of highly volatile constituents during MW drying may further bias both GC–MS composition and effective insect exposure during bioassays, complicating direct comparisons among treatments. These observations are consistent with previous studies reporting adverse effects of excessive MW power on EO yield and quality. This result aligns with other studies (Rahimmalek and Goli 2013, Mashkani et al. 2018). The slightly higher yield obtained at 400 W compared with 264 W suggests that moderate MW power may improve drying efficiency without excessive thermal damage, as moisture reduction is known to enhance EO extraction efficiency (Franco-Vega et al. 2016). Therefore, controlled MW drying at moderate power may represent a technological compromise that preserves EO integrity while ensuring efficient dehydration. These findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the significant differences observed in insecticidal activity among MW treatments.

Several studies have been carried out on the chemical composition of the EOs, obtained from aerial parts of D. scoparia (stems, flowers, and seeds) harvested in different regions (East, West, South, Central, and North Sahara) in Algeria. It has been found that these EOs were characterized by the presence of high amounts of phenylpropanoids or/and/or monoterpenes (Ksouri et al. 2017). Moreover, it has been established that the chemical profile of D. scoparia EOs also depends on its growth stage, as well as the nature of the soil, growing conditions, bioclimatic and edaphic factors (Malti et al. 2018). In the present study, the relative abundance of major chemical groups varied with the drying method, indicating that post-harvest processing is an additional determinant of oil composition. They are consistent with those of several studies on other medicinal and aromatic plants (Pirbalouti et al. 2013, Ebadi et al. 2015). These variations result from the loss or increase of oil constituents due to the formation of new compounds by oxidation, rearrangement, glycoside hydrolysis, isomerization, esterification, and/or other processes (Díaz‐Maroto et al. 2004).

Some investigations have found that the plant cell can be damaged during drying (Lewicki and Pawlak 2003, García and González 2010). Other studies also reported slight expansion and collapse of the cell structure during drying, which may result in the release of volatile compounds into the atmosphere (Orav et al. 2004, Xie et al. 2023). Monoterpenes decreased faster than sesquiterpenes due to their lower molecular weight (Khangholil and Rezaeinodehi 2008, Khademi Doozakhdarreh et al. 2022). Nevertheless, there were also results other than substances with relatively low volatility, such as sesquiterpenes, which are released (Mashkani et al. 2018, Qin et al. 2022). In contrast, previous studies have indicated that the drying method did not significantly affect the chemical composition of the EOs of many plants, such as Eucalyptus camaldulensis (Zrira and Benjilali 1991), Origanum ehrenbergii Boiss (Zgheib et al. 2019), and Satureja hortensis (Sefidkon et al. 2006). These discrepancies likely reflect interspecific differences in secretory structures, oil localization, and inherent chemical composition (Dhifi et al. 2016).

Our findings clearly demonstrate that the insecticidal efficacy of D. scoparia EO against P. blanchardi is strongly influenced by the pre-extraction drying method. The observed differences in toxicity are associated with drying-induced changes in EO composition, rather than being attributable to a single compound. Several studies have been reported on the strong synergistic nature of D. scoparia EO to various insects and mites including Spilarctia obliqua (Lepidoptera: Erebidae) (Srivastava et al. 2001), Trichoplusia ni (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) (Afshar et al. 2017), Pseudaletia unipuncta (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae) (Passreiter et al. 2005), Amyelois transitella (Lepidoptera: Pyralidae) (Niu et al. 2012), Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, Dermatophagoides farinae, and Tyrophagus putrescentiae (Acariformes: Pyroglyphidae) (Song et al. 2011). The insecticidal activity of D. scoparia EO is primarily attributed to its high content of biologically active terpenoid compounds, particularly monoterpenes and oxygenated monoterpenes. These compounds are well known for their strong neurotoxic, respiratory, and metabolic effects on insects. Major constituents commonly reported in Dysphania species, including myristicin, α-phellandrene, and limonene, play a key role in the observed toxicity. Previous studies have shown that Dysphania scoparia EO exhibits strong acaricidal activity against adult female spider mites, with an LD50 of 1.79 mg L−1 and complete mortality achieved at 3.42 mg L−1 after 72 h (Attia et al. 2011).

Our findings demonstrate that D. scoparia EO markedly altered the activity of key detoxification and target-site enzymes in P. blanchardi, and these effects were strongly influenced by the drying method applied before oil extraction. Changes in AChE, CarE, and GST activities should be interpreted as physiological responses to complex EO mixtures rather than as evidence of direct inhibition by individual compounds. AChE activity was substantially elevated following exposure to EO obtained from MW-dried plant material, particularly at higher MW powers, whereas chlorpyrifos caused pronounced AChE inhibition. This effect may be due to the EOs being volatile and lipophilic, which can quickly penetrate insects as well as the elevated AChE activity observed following EO exposure may reflect a compensatory neurophysiological response to stress or neural overstimulation rather than direct enzymatic inhibition. Unlike chlorpyrifos, which acts through irreversible AChE inhibition, EOs are known to exert toxicity via multitarget mechanisms including modulation of GABA receptors, membrane disruption, and oxidative imbalance (Almadiy 2021). Certain compounds bind to GABA receptors associated with the chloride channels of the membrane of post-synaptic neurons and disrupt the functioning of inhibitory synapses (Mssillou et al. 2022). It has been reported that the hydrophobic active site of AChE is sensitive to hydrophobic interactions, which promotes interaction with diverse cyclic monoterpenes (1,8-cineole and alpha-pinene) and acyclic compounds (geraniol and linalool) composed of a hydrophobic hydrocarbon skeleton. This affinity can contribute to their anticholinesterase activity (Mukherjee et al. 2007).

The stronger suppression of CarE and GST activities observed under high-power MW treatments may reflect qualitative and quantitative shifts in oil composition that increase the relative abundance of enzyme-modulating compounds, rather than a direct inhibitory action of a single molecule (Yingngam 2023). Inhibition of detoxification enzymes compromises the insect’s metabolic defense system, thereby enhancing EO toxicity and contributing to the lower lethal concentration values observed in MW-treated oils (Al-Nazawi et al. 2026). Such multitarget and mixture-driven effects are widely recognized as a hallmark of plant EOs and underpin their insecticidal potential (Hashem et al. 2020).

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that different drying methods markedly influence the yield, chemical composition, and biological activity of D. scoparia EO. MW drying, particularly at 264 and 400 W, enhanced insecticidal selectivity against male and female P. blanchardi, respectively, and significantly increased oil bioefficacy. Correlation analysis revealed a strong relationship between insecticidal activity and enzymatic responses, indicating that toxicity is associated not only with AChE related neurophysiological disruption but also with the suppression of key detoxification enzymes, including CarE and GST. Overall, the results highlight the potential of D. scoparia EO, particularly when optimized through appropriate drying techniques, as a promising eco-friendly alternative for sustainable pest management in crop protection.
