“Now you’re talking my language” - Improving health literacy and patient-directed knowledge of scientific abstracts through provision of plain language summaries created by artificial intelligence: A cross sectional infodemiology study
Authors: John E. Moore, Beverley C. Millar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SCIENTIFIC ABSTRACT (Author Written)BackgroundDue to free and digital availability of scientific abstracts in medical journals, as well as search engines including PubMed, many patients are increasingly looking to these as reliable and trusted sources of information, amidst an information ecosystem of potential mis- and disinformation. However, such scientific abstracts are difficult-to-read by the lay community, as they are not written purposefully for a lay audience. The Plain Language Summary now offers such readers a new medium to engage with, thereby helping with their health literacy and understanding of the research findings being described. The aims and objectives of the present study were to: calculate the readability of all scientific abstracts published in the Ulster Medical Journal over the five year period 2020 – 2024 (n=48), (ii) using artificial intelligence, prepare a plain language summary of each scientific abstract (n=48) with (a) minimal prompts and (b) with extensive prompts and (iii) calculate the readability of AI-generated plain language summaries.MethodsReadability was calculated using Readable software, defined by the (i) Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), (ii) Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), (iii) Gunning Fog Index and (iv) SMOG Index and four text metrics [word count, sentence count, words/sentence, syllables/word] on abstracts from all original clinical papers (n=48) published in the Ulster Medical Journal in the last five years (2020-2024). Plain language summaries were created from the existing scientific abstract using artificial intelligence with (a) minimal prompts and (b) extensive prompts. The readability of all AI-created plain language summaries was further determined.ResultsScientific abstracts had a mean FRE and FKGL score of 24.2±14.1 (standard deviation) and 14.4±2.8, respectively (Reference target values of ≥60 and ≤8, respectively). AI created plain language summaries with improved readability scores of 59.8±7.4 and 8.9±1.6, respectively for summaries with minimal prompts, thereby almost meeting reference readability targets. AI-created summaries with extensive prompts had mean readability scores of 71.3±6.1 and 6.3±0.9, respectively, with 46/48 (96%) of scientific abstracts now reaching reference readability target values. Scientific abstracts and Plain Language Summaries were statistically different (p<0.0001) in terms of both FRE and FKGL scores. Inputting the necessary and appropriate prompts to the AI-tool is critical to attaining the desired readability values.ConclusionsMedical journals may reach out to lay readers, including service users, patients, family and friends, through new innovation with the inclusion of a Plain Language Summary. Scientific abstracts are written at a level which is beyond the average reading age of 11 years old in the UK. Computational creativity through the employment of AI platforms can successfully generate narrative text for specific reading ages, with optimal readability. Effective communication of medical research findings from medical and scientific papers is vital for service users to enhance their health literacy, thereby helping promote better clinical outcomes, as well as promoting inclusivity for lay readers. With thorough checks and controls by the authors of clinical papers, AI-created plain language summaries may provide a new medium for medical journals to communicate with patients and service users, the results of clinical and original studies. The ability to create fit-for-purpose and easy-to-read Plain Language Summaries allows the lay public and service users to now become included in the family of readers of the journal and further supports the health literacy of patients and service users.

Scientific papers often use very hard words. Many patients try to read these papers online, but the language is too complex. This makes it hard for them to understand important health information. Introduction

Individuals need adequate literacy skills in order to understand and navigate healthcare systems. They also need health literacy skills which will allow them to make informed decisions about their healthcare.1 The World Health Organization (WHO) has defined health literacy as “ …the personal characteristics and social resources needed for individuals and communities to access, understand, appraise and use information and services to make decisions about health.”1 Health literacy is about how people understand information relating to health and healthcare, and how they apply that information to their lives, use it to make decisions and act on it.1 According to the WHO, the impact for individuals with low levels of health literacy may include (i) significantly more negative outcomes across a range of health indicators, (ii) more likely to have unhealthy lifestyles, (iii) less likely to make informed healthy lifestyle choices and engage with preventative programmes, (iv) higher levels of illness and preventable premature mortality, (v) less able to manage long term conditions and (vi) less likely to adhere to medication regimes effectively.1,2

A 2015 Public Health England commissioned report entitled “Improving health literacy to reduce health inequalities”3 highlights the extent of the health literacy challenge. It identified that up to 61% of the English working age population do not routinely understand health and wellbeing information they are given.

In Northern Ireland, health literacy was one of the key themes in Making Life Better, the Department of Health’s 10-year public health framework.4 Recent data from the 2023/2024 Health Survey Northern Ireland showed that just over one fifth of people interviewed found it difficult to make decisions to improve their health and well-being.5 Of these, most notably were respondents from the most deprived background, as well as those from an urban environment.5 In 2021, the Northern Ireland HSC Patient and Client Council published a report entitled “Towards an action plan for health literacy in Northern Ireland A Patient and Client Council scoping paper”6 whose objective was to (i) establish the current context of health literacy in Northern Ireland and in the wider UK, (ii) collate evidence on the importance of health literacy, (iii) provide evidence on the scale of the issue for different sections of the population and (iv) review specific interventions for improving health literacy. Recommendations from this report included promoting effective communication through improving how health information is communicated.6

It is hoped that data from the current study will help identify gaps and promote innovative ways of communicating research findings to the lay audience, to support and enhance health literacy with Northern Ireland service users.

Many authors of scientific, clinical and research papers find scientific communication with the lay audience challenging.7-10 There are several potential reasons why this is so, including academic training, where authors are trained to be succinct and concise, employing specific scientific and medical terminologies.7-10 Additionally, many are fearful of oversimplifying their written work, especially for lay audiences, as this may be perceived to dilute out the science or distort or misrepresent what the authors are trying to say and convey to the reader.7-10 Furthermore, there is the so-called “curse of knowledge”, which is a cognitive bias, where authors assume the readers have the same background information and understanding as they do, even when most often, they do not.7-10 One solution to this is the Plain Language Summary (PLS). While not replacing traditional abstracts, plain language summaries are where authors are expected to communicate their research in less-technical ways that would appeal to new audiences.11 Artificial intelligence (AI) tools are now widely available which can help authors write for a lay audience. Recently (April 2025), McMinn and colleagues performed extensive studies between large language AI generators and the medical writer, for the generation of plain language summaries (PLSs). In Study 1, bespoke AI PLSs were easier to read than medical writer PLSs across all readability metrics (p<0.01).12 In Study 2, bespoke AI output saved medical writers >40% in time for PLS creation and required less effort than unassisted writing. Subject matter expert-assessed quality was higher for AI-assisted PLSs, and physicians preferred bespoke AI-generated outputs for patient use.12 In Study 3, bespoke AI PLSs were more readable and rated of higher quality than medical writer PLSs. Overall, this study concluded that the bespoke AI process may enhance access to health information by helping medical writers produce PLSs of scientific content that are fit for purpose.

Furthermore, several healthcare regulators, including the General Medical Council (GMC) and the Health and Care Professions Council (HCPC), acknowledge the benefits that AI can offer to patient care and have offered up specific guidance on how best to use AI tools within the professional practice of their registrants. With the GMC guidelines, AI tools are not regulated, more so, employment of such tools by doctors in terms of professional responsibility, accountability, training and competence, places the onus on the clinician to use their professional judgement, as well as verifying the accuracy of AI-generated outputs. With the HCPC, the regulator urges registrants and education providers to remain aligned with standards and to critically appraise emerging technologies in the context of safety, ethics, and professional integrity.

Readability is a measure of how easy a piece of text is to read and has become an important measurable parameter within healthcare, particularly when trying to assess how well patient-facing written material has been prepared. Readability is assessed using established evidence-based formulae quantifying words per sentence, syllables per word, as well as other syntax parameters. Readability formulae commonly quoted in healthcare analysis include the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) and the Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) scores.13 To date, there has not been any analysis conducted that has compared the readability of scientific abstracts published in the Ulster Medical Journal and Plain Language Summaries of the same abstract. If scientific abstracts have poor readability, then patients may be less likely to understand the research being undertaken and discussed. Examples of this may include that a good understanding of medication information is therefore vital for an individual to adequately comprehend and be compliant with post-procedure requirements, in a way to maximise health outcomes and clinical success of the medical procedure/treatment, as well as helping improve clinical trial involvement.

Methods

Retrieval of Scientific Abstracts

Scientific abstracts (n=48) were obtained from the Ulster Medical Journal, from all Clinical Papers published in the last five years (2020, n=5; 2021, n=12; 2022, n=9; 2023, n=14; 2024, n=8). Full Clinical Papers, including each of their abstracts were obtained from the journal’s website (https://www.ums.ac.uk/journal.html). All papers and their abstracts were freely available to the public and in the public domain.

Preparation of AI-generated Plain Language Summaries (PLS)

Each abstract was copied from the UMJ website into Microsoft Word and data-cleansed, including the deletion of extra spaces and correcting any formatting errors, due to the copying process. AI-generated Plain Language Summaries were created in this study using ChatGPT 5.0, employing a standard PC computer with access to the internet. No specialised training was necessary to use this AI platform, apart from following given prompts and instructions from the AI-platform. ChatGPT 5.0 (https://chatgpt.com/) was opened and each cleansed scientific abstract was imported in, along with two separate commands, namely (i) (minimal prompts) - “rewrite this scientific abstract as a plain language summary for an 8th grade student in the US” Allow sequential simplification until a Flesch Reading Score of ≥60 has been obtained” and (ii) (extensive prompts) “Rewrite the following scientific abstract as a plain language summary for a young person aged 11 years old in the UK and include the following subheadings in the plain language summary: What was the purpose of the study? What did the researchers do? What were the findings of the study? What does this mean? Ensure that the plain language summary has a Flesch Reading Ease score of 60 or greater and make sentences short. Also please give the plain language summary a title. Try to make the plain language summary the same length as the original abstract.”

Determination of readability scores and text metrics

The readability of each source of information, namely the scientific abstract and the paired two AI-generated plain language summaries, was individually copied into the online subscription-based software, Readable (www.readable. com), which was used as guided by the manufacturer. All readability analyses were performed on text written in the English language. Readability values, including the Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, Gunning Fog Index and SMOG Index were calculated, as described previously.13 Two text metrics were also calculated, including words/sentence and syllables/word, as is generally the case with such studies. Readable.com was the software of choice as it has been used in several readability and recent studies within healthcare.14 McGrath and colleagues concluded that Readable was the best analytical tool to employ in readability studies, due to its user experience, capacity and accuracy.14

Statistical analyses

The readability data obtained underwent statistical analyses using GraphPad PRISM version 10.5.0 (774) (Boston, USA). To determine if the data followed a normal distribution, a normality test was performed on each set of data using the Shapiro-Wilk Test. Dependent on the normality of data distribution, for data that were normally distributed, one-way ANOVA (parametric) was performed to compare the means of normally distributed parameters. Data sets that were not normally distributed, the Friedman test (non-parametric) with multiple comparisons was performed. A p value of <0.05 (5%) was considered as statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we calculated the readability of all scientific abstracts published from original clinical papers over the last five years (2020-2024) in the Ulster Medical Journal. Having quantitatively established that these were outside the UK average reading age of 11 years, we employed AI-platforms to generate a lay/service user-friendly Plain Language Summary from the original Scientific Abstract. Through careful choice of input prompts, we were able to instruct the AI-platform to generate easy-to-read narratives, aligning to readability reference targets. The ability to create fit-for-purpose Plain Language Summaries allows the lay public and service users to be become included in the family of readers of the journal.

On an average working day, the search engine, PubMed from the US National Library of Medicine estimates about 2.5 million users globally, generating around 3 million searches and 9 million page views.16 This is a free and reliable service cataloguing the scientific abstracts of scholarly works published by peer-reviewed journals globally. It is estimated that approximately one third of these users are not healthcare professionals, but rather lay members of the public attempting to access healthcare information from a respected and trusted source.17 These figures suggest that non-specialist users make up a significant minority of PubMed’s daily audience.

Whilst scientific abstracts are not intended to be read by the general public, there is therefore no formal requirement of authors of such works to moderate their language to attempt to include the lay audience. Therefore, most lay readers of scientific abstracts would find such abstracts to be difficult to read. Our study showed that the readability of scientific abstracts published in the last five years from original clinical papers published in the Ulster Medical Journal is poor, where the readability of scientific abstracts published ranged from -36.6 to 44.4, as determined by the Flesch Reading Ease score, thereby exceeding the average 11 year old reading age of someone in the UK. In the UK, Health Education England (HEE) recommends that patient resources be written at a level suitable for comprehension by an average 11-year-old.18,19

None of the scientific abstracts meet the readability reference targets. When we used minimal instructions for the AI platform, approximately only half of the plain language summaries now met the reference target. However, previous evidence-based guidance from Zarshenas and colleagues,20 as well as by Stoll and colleagues21 have indicated important prompts to include to produce a quality plain language summary. Adopting this guidance, we developed an extensive prompt, to be fed into the AI platform along with the authors’ scientific abstract, which when applied, drove readability to within reference targets for 46/48 (96%) scientific abstracts.

AI-generated Plain Language Summaries were created in this study using ChatGPT 5.0, employing a standard PC computer with access to the internet. No specialised training was necessary to use this AI platform, apart from following given prompts and instructions from the AI-platform.

Study limitations

In this study, we examined papers from a single journal source, in order to ensure that all papers were written using a standardised set of “Instructions to Authors” guidelines, prior to the application of AI tools and thus attempting to avoid bias due to variations in style. Future studies may wish to compare AI narratives from several journals with differing “Instructions to Authors” guidelines, to establish how robust AI-generated narratives are from sources with differing styles and formats.

Readability describes how easily a piece of text can be read without difficulty by the target audience. Readability considers numerous factors including the complexity of the literature, familiarity with the topic, typography and legibility. Therefore, readability is a crucial component when service users read medical information relating to their diagnosis, management and treatment. However, readability does not calculate or assure any level of understanding. For example, if a piece of text narrative has a good readability score, the reader could still find it challenging to understand the topic being discussed.

Additionally, this study recognises that it did not attempt to check the accuracy or completeness of the AI-generated narratives, due to the fact that the authors were not specialists in the topics described in the 48 abstracts, so could not authoritatively comment on these aspects of AI-created materials. However, to control this aspect, we strongly advocate that all Plain Language Summaries generated by AI are thoroughly checked by the original authors, particularly for accuracy and completeness, during the proofing stage, prior to publication and subsequent release to the service user community. Moving forward, we believe that further studies could now be undertaken along with service users in a co-design and co-production manner, to help build upon the objective nature of the current study, and help inform patient and service user acceptability of AI.

Proposed adoption of AI-generated Plain Language Summaries

Plain language summaries are becoming more frequently employed by editors of medical journals, although they remain in a minority. A recent study by Gainey and colleagues found that of 534 journals across 10 non-communicable disease areas and 11 journal categories, only 27 (5.1%) contained text-based instructions for Plain Language Summaries.22 These authors showed that there was much heterogeneity and inconsistency in guidance given for the writing of plain language summaries. Further related work by Pal and colleagues showed that minimising variability of the Plain Language Summary may help greater adoption and impact of these resources.23 Most recently (June 2025), in a seminal paper by Edwards published in Medical Writing, the findings of a collaboration between experts from over 15 US and EU organisations, including industry, academia, and a patient-focused nonprofit, were discussed, which detailed how AI can be responsibly applied to Plain Language Summaries.24 These included (i) prompt engineering considerations, (ii) considerations for AI governance, (iii) considerations for AI disclosure, (iv) examples of advanced AI architecture for Lay Summary creation and (v) helpful tools and resources.24 These findings by Edwards will help to enhance the current practice of adopting and employing Plain Language Summaries to support patient health literacy.

When the editor and editorial board of a medical journal commit to the inclusion of Plain Language Summaries, the delivery of PLS may follow two routes, namely (a) that the PLS is prepared and submitted by the authors of the original work or (b) the journal prepares a PLS from the authors’ submitted scientific abstract using AI-platforms, using extensive prompts. This draft AI-generated PLS is sent to the authors as part of the peer-review process, for checking and proofing, particularly relating to accuracy and completeness. Both the Scientific Abstract and the Plain Language Summary are subsequently published side-by-side with the original work in the journal.

Conclusions

Medical journals may reach out to lay readers, including service users, patients, family and friends, through new innovation with the inclusion of a Plain Language Summary. Scientific abstracts are written at a level which is beyond the average reading age of 11 years old in the UK. Employment of AI platforms may generate narrative text which can be successfully tailored for specific reading ages, with optimal readability. Effective communication of medical research findings from medical and scientific papers is vital for service users to enhance their health literacy, thereby helping promote better clinical outcomes, as well as promoting inclusivity for lay readers.
