FedDriftGuard adaptive federated learning with differential privacy for concept drift in edge environments

ABSTRACT
AbstractFederated learning (FL) has become a highly promising paradigm for privacy-preserving distributed model training by enabling edge devices to train without sharing raw data. But in practice, edge environments are both non-stationary and asymmetric, with varying data distributions due to shifts in user behaviour, sensing conditions, and overall environmental dynamics. This causes concept drift (sudden, gradual, and recurrent), leading to poor model performance, slower convergence, and predictive bias. Current approaches to FL are not combined to tackle problems of drift adaptation, differential privacy (DP) and resource efficiency (FedAvg, DP-FedAvg). To address these constraints, we present FedDriftGuard. This Federated learning layer unifies client-level drift detection, drift-adaptive aggregation, and adaptable differential privacy into a single, FLE architecture-compatible system. The proposed DP-DriftNet model implements attention-based time encoding to capture changing data patterns and drift-directed feature weighting to allow greater flexibility in the presence of distributional changes. A drift-optimal privacy scheduler allocates noise probabilistically, subject to a limited privacy budget, thereby enforcing an appropriate privacy-utility trade-off without cancelling formal DP guarantees. Also, update sparsification, compression and periodic transmission techniques are used to reduce communication overhead. Decades of experimentation on real-world and synthetic drift datasets have shown that FedDriftGuard outperforms baseline FL techniques, achieving accuracy and F1-score gains of 9–14% and 11–17%, respectively, with adaptation latency 28% shorter and communication cost 20–35% lower. Such findings are statistically significant and confirm the soundness of the suggested method. Introduction

The recent paradigm of federated learning (FL) has been proposed to facilitate privacy-aware joint training of models across edge devices. In contrast to centralised learning, the raw data in FL are not aggregated on a central server. Still, they are kept locally on client devices, and only model updates are exchanged, which is why it is especially well-suited to privacy-constrained applications such as smart homes, intelligent transportation, industrial IoT, and healthcare analytics. Not only does this decentralised methodology achieve greater data privacy, but it also enables scalable learning in heterogeneous settings.

But as a matter of fact, edge environments in the real world are dynamic and mobile. The data generated across devices varies with user behaviour, environmental factors, and device heterogeneity. These changes cause various types of concept drift, such as sudden, gradual, and recurring drift, which dramatically deteriorate model performance, slow convergence, and pose a risk of biased or obsolete predictions. Current FL schemes, such as FedAvg and DP-FedAvg, largely assume that data distributions remain unchanged over time; therefore, they do not efficiently accommodate temporal changes in real-world deployments.

Concept drift is a term used to describe how the underlying data distribution changes over time, thereby altering the associations between input features and target variables. This drift is increased by decentralised data generation, clients’ varying behaviour, and changing real-world conditions in federated learning environments. Drift may take various forms, such as sudden drift (when there is a dramatic change in the environment), gradual drift (when a long-term behaviour changes through evolution), and periodic drift (when there is a repetitive pattern). These differences lead to obsolete model representations, poor predictive performance, and inconsistent global model convergence. Managing concept drift in FL is thus an urgent issue, especially when coupled with privacy controls and resource constraints in the edge environment.

To overcome them, several investigations have recommended drift-conscious optimisation methods, temporal characteristic modelling, fairness-centred customer modification, and a differential privacy (DP) framework. The drift-conscious FL algorithms use distribution monitoring and adaptive learning control to make them more resistant to non-stationarity, and DP-based algorithms ensure data confidentiality by adding scheduled noise to model updates. These innovations, however, are usually created in solitude. For example, drift-conscious algorithms do not account for privacy limitations, whereas DP-based algorithms do not account for dynamic data distributions. In addition, current systems lack mechanisms to dynamically match privacy budgets to the level of drift or to the efficient (or jointly efficient) use of communication resources in constrained-resource environments.

The result is a series of systems that work seen in one dimension of the three: drift adaptation, privacy preservation and communication efficiency, but do not prosper when all three have to be accommodated. Hence, a coherent federated learning model that can tackle concept drift, ensure differential privacy, and optimise edge efficiency in a concerted effort is urgently required.

This work suggests FedDriftGuard, a federated learning system that is adaptive and flexible for dynamic edge conditions. The proposed framework combines client-side drift detection, drift-sensitive aggregation, and dynamic differential privacy into a single architecture. At the model level, we propose DP-DriftNet. This drift-aware deep learning model uses temporal feature encoding and attention mechanisms to predict changes in data dynamics without degrading predictive performance. At the system level, an update-priority strategy that accounts for drift ensures that a client undergoing distributional shifts receives priority updates, enabling it to adapt more quickly globally. Also, the DP noise scheduler uses a drift-sensitive policy to dynamically adjust privacy noise based on the intensity of drift, thereby providing a more reasonable trade-off between privacy and utility. To further achieve scalability, communication-efficient techniques such as update sparsification, compression, and periodic transmission are included to reduce bandwidth usage in the edge environment.

Related work

This section discusses progress in federated learning across several areas, including concept drift adaptation, privacy, and edge efficiency. It evaluates the constraints in the field and why an integrated, adaptive framework is essential.

Concept drift adaptation in federated learning

The environment in which federated learning (FL) is implemented is highly dynamic, with data distributions across clients that are non-IID and change over time. This is a concept drift phenomenon that seriously affects model convergence and generalisation. Initial analyses reveal that traditional FL algorithms, such as FedAvg, do not perform well in non-stationary settings because they assume fixed distributions1,2. Drift-aware extensions provide principles for estimating distribution divergence, memory-based rehearsal, and temporal adaptation policies to mitigate the onset of performance degradation1,3. Federated averaging algorithms that are concept-drift-aware combine an additional distribution-based approach to detect drift and memory-driven adaptation to reduce performance loss in non-stationary data1. Nevertheless, these do not account for privacy preservation or communication effectiveness in edge environments.

Recent literature extends the concept of adaptive learning in the presence of drift by incorporating domain adaptation and client-specific optimisation methods4,5. Normalisation strategies and architecture-level adaptations are also among the approaches that achieve better performance in heterogeneous drift environments6. Additionally, federated continual learning systems strive to strike an equilibrium between storing knowledge and adapting to changing data streams3.

More sophisticated solutions combine fairness-conscious adaptation with drift-sensitive aggregation schemes to combat bias arising from time-varying data distributions7,8. In addition, drift-sensitive anomaly detectors and clustering techniques are also more resilient to changing patterns and adversarial examples9,10. Additionally, real-time, flexible, streaming-based FL systems and explainable, rule-based architectures are offered11,12.

Though these developments are underway, most available solutions focus on either detecting drift or adapting individually. They are likely to disregard privacy limitations and system-level performance limits, and so are constrained in practical edge door implementations. In addition, the vast majority of methods employ simple drift metrics and do not adequately capture the complexity of temporal and spatial heterogeneity in distributed systems13,14.

Privacy and security in federated learning

Privacy maintenance, particularly in vulnerable fields such as healthcare, IoT, and finance, is one of the most important factors in FL. To guarantee data confidentiality, differential privacy (DP), secure multiparty computation (SMPC), homomorphic encryption, and blockchain-related methods are popular15–18. DP-based methods introduce noise into gradients or model updates, thereby providing formal privacy guarantees but generally reducing model accuracy19,20. In application-specific systems, including power systems, privacy-sensitive FL has also been applied, with privacy-sensitive collaborative learning aiding the provision of distributed grid services to meet operational capacity constraints21.

This indicates that recent studies propose using adaptive privacy mechanisms to address the privacy-utility trade-off. The dynamic DP models learn to adjust the noise across training settings, the gradient sensitivity, or the client contribution, thereby improving performance on non-IID data19,22. In hybrid algorithms, to increase inference and poisoning resistance, DP is combined with encryption and secure aggregation23,24. Dynamic differential privacy models adopt noise borrowing between two modes and a conditional privacy budget that depends on the gradient’s sensitivity and the client’s contribution22. However, these methods do not explicitly relate drift detection signals to individual-aggregation and edge-optimising algorithms.

FL frameworks that are security-heavy-handed also include anomaly detectors, trust-based client selection, and zero-trust to increase resistance to adversarial attacks9,25. Also, fairness-conscious and privacy-conscious aggregation schemes provide fair contributions while guaranteeing privacy8. The significance of privacy metrics, attack models, and assessment frameworks in FL systems has also been emphasised in survey works14–16.

Privacy-preserving FL is effective in the medical domain, the Internet of Things, and smart industries. Yet, it also faces challenges related to scalability, computational costs, and non-persistent data flows23,26,27. Although there are improvements, the current methods tend to treat privacy and drift adaptation as separate issues, rather than integrating dynamic privacy planning with evolving data properties.

Edge-efficient federated learning

The implementation of FL in edge and IoT systems imposes limitations on communication overhead, computing resources, latency, and energy. Some of them deal with minimising communication costs using gradient compression, sparsification, quantisation, and selective update transmission28,29. Scalability and reduction of redundant communication are further boosted by event-driven and adaptive participation mechanisms29,30.

The lightweight model architecture, pruning methods, and resource-aware optimality are incorporated into edge-aware FL frameworks to enhance computational efficiency31–33. Decentralised learning paradigms and collaborative edge-cloud architectures enable scalable, low-latency deployments34–36. Moreover, it has been investigated in computer vision pipelines using FL and resource-limited IoT, where, in practice, it is necessary to trade off model performance and hardware support against communication overhead37–39.

They suggest adaptive aggregation and optimisation schemes to address heterogeneity among clients and enhance convergence under resource constraints40–42. Other emerging studies also address integrating FL with edge intelligence, 6G networks, and IoT environments to enable real-time distributed learning43–45. Associated system-level visions place more emphasis on the operation of green IoT, split federated learning to support 6G-enabled networks, and scaled federated edge AI to detect intrusion examples, expressing the increasing requirement to collaboratively optimise the three metrics, namely privacy, scalability, and resource efficiency in a real deployment46–48.

Application and survey research demonstrate the potential of FL in smart cities, health care, manufacturing systems, and financial systems49–51. Nonetheless, the related approaches adopt an efficiency- and scalability-driven strategy without addressing concept drift or adaptive privacy processes. Consequently, cohesive frameworks that account for drift adaptation, privacy preservation, and edge efficiency are lacking.

Analysis of research gaps

The literature demonstrates that important progress has been made in three crucial aspects of federated learning: concept-drift adaptation, privacy preservation, and edge efficiency. But these dimensions are given in great detail in isolation. Vegetation-sensitive FL algorithms aim to enhance adaptability to non-stationary data but do not always account for privacy limitations and data communication efficiency1,4,12. Privacy-sensitive methods, including the elimination of privacy via the differential privacy approach, prioritise data protection yet do not account for changing data distributions, leading to suboptimal results under data drift19,22.

Likewise, edge-efficient FL systems optimise communication and computation overhead while avoiding drift and dynamically scheduling privacy controls28,30. State-of-the-art adaptive DP techniques are based on sparse indicators, such as gradient sensitivity or client contribution, and place little focus on drift detection in privacy control22. Similarly, adaptive aggregation strategies can handle heterogeneity but do not address drift-aware or privacy-aware weighting40,41.

Additionally, extensive surveys and system-level evaluations of scalability, heterogeneity, safety, and real-time placement in edge environments have been reported14,26,34,52,53. The new paradigms of blockchain-integrated FL mechanisms, edge intelligence, and IoT-driven systems introduce complexities related to trust, energy, and decentralised coordination43,44,35,54. Federated adaptation consists of fair and drift-sensitive learning and incorporates fairness-conscious weighting to handle bias and non-procession of streaming conditions7. Nevertheless, they lack differentiation between privacy and communication, and effective deployment at scale. Moreover, the incorporation of reinforcement learning, enhanced edge knowledge, blockchain-based protection, and a novel 6G-based distributed framework has been further examined in several recent studies, thereby expanding the line of federated learning frameworks for shifting and large-scale environments33,45,55–57.

The latter includes empirical non-IID mitigation and edge data rebalancing, demonstrating that entropy-based selection and oversampling algorithms can enhance convergence under heterogeneous client distributions. Nevertheless, they do not combine drift adaptation with adaptive privacy control58,59. Finally, large-scale surveys and practical reviews clearly indicate unresolved problems in FL design, including scalability, deployment complexity, domain heterogeneity, and system integration in dynamic settings60.

Federated learning foundations

Federated Learning (FL) is a decentralised system allowing hundreds of clients, including edge devices, to create a global model and privacy-turn in their data together. This is done by local training on every client which does not transmit model parameters or gradients to a central server but maintains secrecy and incurs less communication expenses.

This architecture performs best in edge environments because it supports a wide range of data sources while running on devices with limited resources. Formally, consider a set of K clients each with a local dataset . The global optimization objective in FL can be expressed as in Eq. (1).

The local training process employs stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or its variants, and the server collects updates through Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithms. The server creates a weighted average from the updates it receives to produce a new global model. It sends this back to clients for additional training.

The main benefit of FL lies in its ability to protect privacy through local data storage, but multiple obstacles continue to exist when using this method at the edge. The system needs to handle data that isn’t identically distributed across clients and also deal with clients who don’t join in fully, and handle limited communication bandwidth, and manage changing data patterns (concept drift). The solution to these problems needs adaptive systems which keep their learning capabilities while protecting user privacy.

System model and problem formulation

Federative Learning FedDriftGuard is designed as a dynamic and privacy-aware federated learning system for non-stationary dynamical edge environments and dynamical data distributions. Figure 1 illustrates the framework with three key building blocks: client-side drift detector, local training on the frontend, and federated training and aggregation on the backend based on the different privacy and federated aggregation. In contrast to the very sharp frequency of conventional federated learning models, which assume fixed data distributions, FedDriftGuard considers the time-varying characteristics of data generated by heterogeneous network components, i.e., sensors, mobile computing systems, and IoT networks.

System architecture of FedDriftGuard illustrating client-side streaming data processing, local concept drift detection, DP-DriftNet-based privacy-preserving training, communication optimization, and server-side secure aggregation with drift-aware global model updating in edge environments.

At each edge client, streaming data is continuously monitored using drift-detection mechanisms to detect changes in the underlying data distribution, including sudden, gradual, and recurring concept drift. When drift is detected, the local model adjusts its learning process with the proposed DP-DriftNet architecture. In the case of local training, a method to ensure differential privacy is to add calibrated noise to model updates before transmission, so that sensitive data cannot be revealed. This guarantees that client data remains confidential while still enabling a universally applicable learning process.

Federated communication between clients and the central server occurs through an iterative learning process that incorporates drift detection and preserves privacy. Every communication round, the server is initialised and sends the global model to the chosen clients. It is implemented by having every client conduct local training and track drift conditions, and by applying differential privacy to its updates. These privatised updates, together with drift-related signals, are sent to the server, where a drift-sensitive aggregation approach is used to revise the global model. The new model will be retransmitted to the clients, and further learning will take place amid changing data conditions. This is a closed loop mechanism that will help in maintaining robustness and dynamic global model without infringing on privacy limitations.

Overview of FedDriftGuard framework

FedDriftGuard framework comprises an adaptive and privacy-sensitive framework of federated learning that is suitable to dynamic edge environments. It is tailor-made to suit the dual problem of the changing data distributions and restrictive privacy needs in decentralized systems. The framework, as shown in Fig. 1, is comprised of three major components that are closely interconnected: local drift detection, differential privacy protection and drift-aware federated aggregation. All of these elements cooperate to support the strong and safe model learning in non-stationary scenarios.

On the client-side, the edges are constantly fed with data streams provided by sensors, mobile platforms, and IoT sources. This type of data has diverse types of concept drift such as sudden, gradual, and recurrent changes due to changes in user behavior, environmental factors, and device peculiarities. To combat this, every client has its own local drift detection mechanism, which tracks changes in the distributional real-time changes. When the drift is detected, the system activates adaptive learning by using the suggested DP-DriftNet model, which aims to learn the corresponding variations of time and modify model representations.

When set up on top of this adaptive learning, the differential privacy mechanisms will be used at the client level to provide the privacy of data. In particular, gradients or updates are simulated by adding calibrated Gaussian or Laplace noise before transmission, which inhibits the reconstruction of delicate information. Such a step is important to make sure that the preservation of privacy is not affected even by the adversarial attack, and that collaborative learning among the distributed clients remains effective.

Privatized updates translated to client supporters are securely sent at the server side and combined to form an internationally distributed model. The proposed framework, unlike traditional aggregation schemes, has drift-aware weighting where higher importance is attributed to the updates made by the clients with drastic distributional changes. This allows being more adapt to changing data patterns and stable across the global model. One round of communication is then complete with the updated global model being sent back to the clients.

This cycle of learning is an ongoing process whose results enable the system to continually change to adapt to concept drift and keep privacy bounds. FedDriftGuard has a high level of robustness and scalability through detection of drift, adaptive model training, privacy assurance and communication efficient aggregation in real world edge based implementation all in one architecture.

Overall, Fig. 1 demonstrates that the proposed scheme develops a reliable pipeline based on the balance of flexibility, privacy, and efficiency. The FedDriftGuard framework, offering a comprehensive integration of edge smartness applications of the next generation, subsumes the shortfalls of open federated learning systems, namely, its inertia to non-hypothesis data, and dynamically contaminated privacy requirements.

Drift detection and adaptation strategy

The success of federated learning models operating at network edges depends on their ability to handle concept drift because fixed models do not perform well when data distributions change. The first stage of FedDriftGuard detects drift at client side while the second stage performs adaptive learning through DP-DriftNet after drift detection. The system maintains global federated model accuracy and stability during changing conditions.

Theoretical detection of drift occurs when the joint probability distribution of features and labels transforms across time according to Eq. (2). Privacy preservation with differential privacy

The main requirement of federated edge learning is user data protection, as sensitive information must remain confidential during model updates exchanged with the central server. The federated learning system protects data privacy by avoiding direct data sharing, but gradient and parameter exchanges still pose potential risks of individual record exposure through inference and reconstruction methods. The system FedDriftGuard applies Differential Privacy (DP) as a protective measure to safeguard privacy while maintaining operational performance.

Differential Privacy establishes mathematical methods for measuring the exact amount of information an algorithm leaks during its operation. Federated aggregation and communication

The main principle of the FedDriftGuard framework is based on federated aggregation, that is, local training models on edge customers are combined into a global one. Once local training is completed, every client uses differential privacy when perturbing their gradients and sending updates to the central server. These privatized updates with drift-related signals are then combined to create the updated global model.

In typical federated learning, aggregation is carried out by Federated Averaging (FedAvg) in which client updates are weighted according to the size of the local data. Along with the adaptive aggregation, the efficiency of the communication is an important factor in the edge environments. FedDriftGuard also makes use of update sparsification and compression to deal with the bandwidth and energy limitations, enabling the clients to send the most informative components of the gradient. Moreover, there are periodic communication strategies, which allow the clients to conduct several local updates, and then communicate with the server. Scalable, efficient, and robust in practice, FedDriftGuard is achieved by the combination of drift-friendly aggregation, communication optimization, and the safety of transmitting the updates.

Comparison with existing methods

To contextualise the contribution of FedDriftGuard, we qualitatively compare the approach of FedDriftGuard with five representative federated learning systems, which solve concept drift, privacy, or edge-centric problems, first. The CDA-FedAvg aims to solve federated continual learning under drift by building FedAvg with the distribution-based drift detection and rehearsal buffers, but does not incorporate any differential privacy or edge-oriented communication control. Conversely, FAC-fed, with a more intricate configuration that integrates drift management and fairness limitations53, concentrates on the notions of fairness and concept drift conscious stream classification and overlooks privacy payoffs and DP noises schedules as part of this design.

DSDP (Dual-Mode Switching Differential Privacy) by direct connection between data drift and differential privacy with the adaptive dose of privacy budgets and switch between Gaussian and Laplace noise depending on the sensitivity of memory of gradient and contribution of clients. However, DSDP considers a general FL scenario and does not provide explicit observability of concept drift or edge-resource–aware aggregation. FedACM: adaptive client momentum for robust state-of-charge estimation in electric vehicles using heterogeneous, non-IID clients and varying participation; no coupling of aggregation rule with DP guarantees, or weight-aware to DP drift9. Privacy-preserving adaptive aggregation for collaborative healthcare analytics shows that adaptive aggregation rules can improve convergence. Still, privacy-aware frameworks in multi-centre FL generally address privacy, heterogeneity, and non-stationarity in an orthogonal manner.

Instead of these methods, FedDriftGuard jointly combines four perspectives: explicitness drift detection at the client side, drift-aware aggregation on the server, differential privacy with adaptive noise scheduling, and edge-oriented communication optimisation. There has been a great deal of research on adapting to concept drift, calibrating DP noise using drift signals, or controlling the communication rounds and update sizes for resource-limited edge devices at the same time, e.g., CDA-FedAvg and FAC-fed focus on drift robustness, DSDP focuses on improving the DP–utility trade-off under drifted gradients, etc. Still, none of them forms a unified framework. Discussion

As the trend of more widespread use of edge-intelligent systems emerges, we are increasingly using federated learning (FL) to train models in a digital setting, with privacy preservation among distributed clients. Incidentally, edge environments are the most dynamic: data distributions shift, connections are inconsistent, and client dynamics vary. Traditional FL has an assumption that all the clients are active at the same time in each learning round, which is not fulfilled by many environments with rapid, sudden or gradual concept drifts that are breaking those assumptions of heterogeneity and stationary distributions that are taken into account by FedAvg and its privacy–booster alternative45,56. In response, recent drift-aware FL techniques exploit this flaw by offering only partial remedies: drift-sensitive optimisation, fairness-driven adaptation, or differential privacy scheduling; however, these proposed solutions follow an independent design paradigm in which drift robustness, privacy preservation, and edge efficiency are modelled as non-interdependent optimisation constraints. This fragmentation leads us to models that perform very well in one dimension but plummet in the others.

We fill in the areas of the state of the art: (i) integrated drift detecting modules on the client-side, (ii) dynamic aggregation schemes that get scale weights corrupted by drifts11, (iii) drift-sensitive differential privacy budgeting25, and (iv) communication efficiency of bandwidth-bounded edge networks41. These limitations, combined, are a great impediment to the scalability, stability, and deployability of existing federated systems in real-life dynamic environments.

Considering the issues above, we introduce FedDriftGuard. The framework is an unambiguous, holistic, and deep-learning-based platform that combines drift identification, drift-resilient aggregation, and differential privacy as a co-unit federated pipeline, meant to optimise the deployment of edges. A DP-DriftNet model described in the current paper can adapt to changing trends without compromising state-of-the-art predictive accuracy by employing attention-based temporal encoding and drift-adaptive feature processing. Drift-sensitive weighting enables updating more clients (value added during distributional changes), and adaptive differential privacy tailors the additional noise to the drift intensity, resulting in an efficient trade-off between user privacy and utility. It also provides communication schemes for slashing, compression, and period that are effective at minimising communication and whose effect on the models’ convergence rate is minimal.

The experimental results repeatedly show that FedDriftGuard dramatically outperforms the baseline methods in terms of accuracy, F1-score, RMSE, adaptation latency, robustness, and utility across heterogeneous clients and different privacy budgets. The statistics support the improvements, showing that the combined approach fills the gaps in current systems. Also, the qualitative comparisons return to the fact that FedDriftGuard is not locked to drift to gain privacy and resource efficiency, hence it is realistic to deploy at the edges.

The results of the work have broader implications for integrating concept drift modelling, privacy mechanisms, and communication efficiency in federated learning. With this type of integration, even more robust, adaptive, and resilient FL frameworks capable of reliably operating in dynamically changing edge environments can be achieved. Limitations of the study

While the proposed FedDriftGuard framework shows favourable adaptability, privacy protection, and communication efficiency, there are still some limitations that can be addressed in future work. One, the evaluation largely depends on controlled public and synthetic datasets that have artificially introduced or well-identified drift patterns. Edge deployments in real-world applications tend to exhibit irregular, multi-source drift due to factors such as changes in user behaviour, device failures, and environmental noise. The lack of large-scale, real-time deployment experiments limits the external validity of performance assertions in very dynamic operational environments.

Secondly, differential privacy is applied using adaptive noise scheduling. Still, they do not consider adversarial threat models, including gradient inversion, inference attacks when drift is extreme, or adaptive attackers seeking to undermine the drift-detection pipeline. Security guarantees for deployment in sensitive domains will benefit from a more extensive analysis of vulnerability under stronger adversarial capabilities.

Third, testing of communication efficiency is conducted under man-made extreme conditions rather than on real heterogeneous hardware platforms. Large-scale federated executions can involve factors such as compute speed, unreliable network access, and device energy, which can be studied through experimental results. It would be pleasant to extend this piece of work with the addition of cross-device resource profiling and on-device benchmarking, which would allow the practically proven attained communication savings to be cemented. These inefficiencies can be used as paths to improvement work in the future to become more realistic, secure, and deployable at the system and security levels.

Though the framework suggested by FedDriftGuard works quite well when the conditions at the edge of a simulated model are assumed to be met, the current research does not involve the use of physical, resource-limited equipment. Additional constraints can also be introduced by physical edge devices, such as constraints on memory, energy consumption, and thermal considerations, which are not well modelled in a simulation model. The additional effort is scheduled as a traditional edge (Raspberry Pi and NVIDIA Jetson) optimisation of the framework and its work after verifying that it has the prospective validity and stability when applied under actual conditions. The existing implementation uses a Bi-LSTM-based temporal model; developmental efforts in the future will go into less power-intensive and more scalable models like TCNs and edge-optimising Transformers.

Conclusion and future work

To overcome these issues in a dynamic edge environment, we propose FedDriftGuard, a new adaptive federated learning framework that incorporates a concept drift management mechanism, a data privacy guarantee, and a communication-efficient component. The proposed DP-DriftNet model integrates client-side drift detection, drift-aware aggregation, and a dynamic differential privacy scheduler into its framework, enabling it to detect and capture evolving temporal patterns while offering strong privacy guarantees and an acceptable level of communication overhead. For everyday FL scenarios involving abrupt, gradual, and recurrent drift, we demonstrated significant gains in accuracy, F1-score, robustness, and adaptation latency over existing FL baselines. The statistical analysis reinforced the notion that these improvements are not coincidental but generalizable gains in performance that can be expected in the real world, even under a non-stationary environment. By doing so, the study fills crucial gaps identified in the literature, notably the lack of a joint strategy that balances time drift adaptation, privacy protection, and efficient bandwidth use. FedDriftGuard, with its coupled architecture, provides a practical yet operational systems-level solution to adapt federated learning for dynamic-edge applications such as competent healthcare, intelligent transportation, industrial IoT, and large-scale sensor networks. Future work can extend this framework to online and multi-device deployments where diversity in hardware, network conditions, and multi-source drift might exist. Its security-sensitive applicability could be improved by assuming longer adversarial threat models, modelling device resources, and making it adaptively secure against general privacy attacks. The scope could be expanded to the system, such as self-supervised drift forecasting, multimodal federated drift learning, and cross-silo extensions.
