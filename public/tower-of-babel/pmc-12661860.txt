Factors associated with suicidal ideation and behaviour: analysis from the national suicide prevention helpline in Lebanon
Authors: Dina Farran, Mahatab El Haj, Myriam Zarzour, Yara Chamoun, Bedros Kazazian, Perrine Posbic, Pia Zeinoun, Maya Hazimeh, Ziad Nahas, Mia Atoui, Rabih El Chammay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide is a global public health concern, influenced by complex psychological, social, and economic factors. This study examines risk and protective factors associated with suicidal ideation and behaviour among callers to Lebanon’s National Lifeline for Emotional Support and Suicide Prevention.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using routinely collected data from the Lifeline between June 2021 and February 2023. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with passive and active suicidal ideation, suicidal intent, suicide attempts, and self-harm. Predictor variables included age, sex, relationship status, employment status, history of mental illness, and psychosocial risk and protective factors. Analyses were conducted using R, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsIndividuals aged 25–35 were more likely to report suicidal ideation and self-harm compared to those under 25, while those aged 55 and above were less likely to report any form of suicidality. Mental illness, psychological stress, social isolation, and relationship problems were consistently associated with higher odds of suicidal ideation and behaviour. Financial difficulties and chronic illness were linked to increased risk of ideation and intent but not self-harm. Mental health awareness was associated with decreased odds across all outcomes.ConclusionThese findings underscore the need for age-responsive and contextually grounded suicide prevention strategies. Interventions targeting social isolation, financial stress, and relationship problems, alongside efforts to enhance mental health awareness, are critical. Future research should explore these associations longitudinally to inform tailored, evidence-based prevention efforts in Lebanon and similar contexts.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07602-9.Keywords: Suicidal ideation, Suicidal intent, Suicide attempt, Self-harm, Risk factors, Protective factors, Suicide prevention, Humanitarian emergencies

Introduction

Suicide is a global public health issue, with nearly 720,000 people dying by suicide each year, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. It is the third leading cause of death among individuals aged 15–29 [1]. Approximately 73% of global suicides occur in low- and middle-income countries, where access to mental health support is often limited. Suicide is a complex phenomenon influenced by social, cultural, biological, psychological, and environmental factors across the life course [1].

Beyond deaths by suicide, a far greater number of people experience suicidal ideation or make non-fatal attempts, contributing to a significant and often overlooked burden [2]. Suicidal behaviour typically follows a continuum, beginning with passive ideation, where individuals think about death or express a desire not to live without forming specific plans [3, 4]. This may escalate into active suicidal ideation, where thoughts become more concrete and plan-oriented, and in some cases progress to suicide attempts or self-harm. These acts, although not always intended to be fatal, are closely tied to suicidal thinking [3, 4].

In Lebanon, although reported suicide rates have historically been lower than the global average, underreporting and societal stigma are believed to obscure the true scale of the problem [5]. The greatest barrier to seeking help remains a low perceived need for treatment, further hindering prevention efforts [5]. On average, one suicide is reported every two days in Lebanon [6]. In 2023, reported suicides rose to 168, an increase of 21.7% from 2022 to 46% from 2021, marking one of the highest figures recorded in the past 15 years [6]. However, due to the absence of a robust suicide surveillance system, it is unclear whether this rise reflects a true increase or improved reporting practices. Regardless, Lebanon’s compounding crises have undeniably intensified mental distress across the population [7].

Since 2019, Lebanon has experienced one of the most severe financial collapses in modern history, marked by hyperinflation, rising unemployment, and the breakdown of public services [7]. These economic hardships have placed immense psychological strain on individuals, fuelling anxiety, hopelessness, and emotional distress, all strongly linked to suicidal ideation and behaviour [7–9]. While there is a well-established connection between suicide and mental health disorders such as depression, anxiety, and substance use [10, 11], many suicides occur impulsively during acute crises, such as those involving financial strain, relationship breakdowns, or chronic illness [1]. Economic hardship, in particular, is a frequent stressor, with studies linking financial instability to increased suicide risk and higher levels of hopelessness and suicidal thoughts [12, 13]. Other risk factors include violence, abuse, grief, and social isolation [14, 15]. Conversely, protective factors such as strong social support, religious or spiritual beliefs, and access to mental health services can mitigate risk, though their effectiveness varies across contexts and populations [16, 17].

In response to growing concerns, suicide prevention efforts in Lebanon have evolved over the past decade. In 2017, Lebanon launched the first and only national suicide prevention hotline in the MENA region. Co-managed by the National Mental Health Programme (NMHP) at the Ministry of Public Health and Embrace, a local NGO, the National Lifeline provides 24/7 emotional support to individuals in crisis [18]. Trained operators assess suicide risk, employ active listening and collaborative crisis de-escalation techniques, and refer callers to mental health services using national MHPSS mapping tools such as the 4Ws platform. The Lifeline is also integrated into Lebanon’s National Mental Health Emergency Response Mechanism, enabling coordinated responses to severe mental health crises, which may involve medical teams and Internal Security Forces (ISF) when needed [18].

The Lifeline adopts a non-interventionist model inspired by crisis lines in the US and UK [19, 20], while incorporating culturally appropriate adaptations to the Lebanese context. The Lifeline protocol emphasizes building a therapeutic connection through empathic, active listening and the use of open-ended questions to foster trust and safety. Its key steps include: (1) establishing rapport with the caller (2), identifying potential suicide risk (3), assessing the level of risk (4), providing emotional support and instilling hope through validation and reflection of feelings, and (5) orienting callers to appropriate community resources or dispatching emergency mobile crisis teams when necessary. A 2021 evaluation showed that the Lifeline significantly reduced callers’ distress during the calls [21], and a follow-up evaluation conducted in 2025 is expected to be published soon.

Despite this progress, limited data exist on the specific factors associated with suicidal ideation and behaviour in Lebanon. This study aims to address this gap by analysing data from the National Lifeline between June 2021 and February 2023. By examining the characteristics and contextual stressors of callers experiencing varying degrees of suicidal ideation and behaviour, from passive thoughts to suicide attempts, this study seeks to inform more targeted, culturally grounded interventions and enhance suicide prevention strategies in the region.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The National Lifeline for Emotional Support and Suicide Prevention, co-managed by the National Mental Health Programme (NMHP) and Embrace, provides non-toll-free, telephone-based emotional support to individuals experiencing suicidal ideation or psychological distress [18]. Callers receive support from trained operators and are often guided toward appropriate mental health services based on need. All operators receive standardized training prior to taking calls, which includes general mental health knowledge such as signs and symptoms, and risk and protective factors for both suicide and common mental health problems that are associated with suicide, de-escalation techniques, assessment of suicide risk and active listening skills. Training also involves role-plays and instruction on emergency dispatch procedures. Competence is formally evaluated using the Suicide Intervention Response Inventory and a suicide knowledge assessment, both pre- and post-training, performance during role plays, as well as an assessment of the ability of operators to follow through the Lifeline call taking protocol. Only operators who demonstrate sufficient competence in following through the protocol are selected to answer calls.

For quality assurance and research purposes, calls are routinely recorded, and trained operators collect data during each call, documenting caller characteristics, presenting concerns, and risk/protective factors. This does not take the form of a questionnaire but rather a standardized call monitoring form. This form captures socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, relationship status, education level, and employment status), self-reported mental health diagnoses, suicidal ideation and behaviour (ideation, intent, plan, means, and past attempts), psychosocial risk factors (such as stress, social isolation, relationship problems, financial difficulties, and chronic illness), protective factors (such as optimism, religious or spiritual beliefs, help-seeking behaviour, future outlook, and mental health awareness), access to mental health services, call duration, and reasons for contacting the Lifeline [22].

Measures

Prior to analysis, all variables were harmonized and recoded to ensure consistency. Age was categorized into five groups: 18–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, and 55 years and above. Relationship status was coded as single, currently in a relationship, or previously in a relationship. Education level was grouped into three categories: school level or below, vocational/technical degree, and university level. Self-reported mental health diagnoses were classified into four diagnostic groups: serious mental illness (including bipolar disorder and psychotic disorders), depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, and other mental illnesses.

Suicidal ideation and behaviour were categorized based on definitions from the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) [22]. Individuals expressing a wish to be dead without active thoughts of suicide (currently thinking of killing oneself), were categorized as experiencing passive ideation. Those reporting experiencing suicidal thoughts were classified as having active ideation. When a caller expressed an intent to act on suicidal thoughts (including having thought of a means of suicide, a plan, and/or a timeframe), this was categorized as suicidal intent. In addition, any reported history of suicide attempts or engagement in self-harm behaviour was coded accordingly.

Psychosocial risk and protective factors were identified by the operators during interactions and included psychological stress, social isolation, financial and relationship problems, living alone, and chronic illness, as well as protective elements such as religious or spiritual faith, optimism, a positive outlook, engagement with mental health services, and general mental health awareness.

To convert the data from call-level to caller-level, demographic information reported during the first call was carried forward to subsequent calls. For example, if a caller stated that they were married during their initial call, their relationship status was treated as married across all records. For continuous variables such as age, call duration, and distress level, averages across all calls by the same individual were computed. If a mental health diagnosis, suicidality indicator, or psychosocial factor was reported at any point across multiple calls, it was coded as present in the aggregated caller-level dataset.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to examine the characteristics of individuals reporting passive or active suicidal ideation, suicidal intent, suicide attempts, or self-harm. Multivariate logistic regression models were then applied to explore factors associated with each category of suicidality. Predictor variables initially considered included age, sex, relationship status, employment status, mental health history, and the presence of psychosocial risk or protective factors. Variables not showing significant associations in univariate analyses were excluded from the multivariate models. To minimize confounding, the final multivariate models adjusted for socio-demographic, clinical, and psychosocial factors, specifically: age, sex, relationship status, employment status, history of mental illness, stress, social isolation, relationship problems, financial problems, living alone, chronic illness, religious/spiritual faith, future outlook, optimism, awareness/insight, and access to mental health services. Subgroup analyses were conducted by modelling factors associated separately with passive suicidal ideation, active suicidal ideation, suicidal intent, suicide attempts, and self-harm. No interaction terms were tested, as the study was designed to identify main correlates of suicidality, and limited sample sizes in certain strata reduced statistical power for reliable interaction testing.

To address potential bias from missing data, multiple imputation was applied to variables with missing values, while no imputation was conducted for variables that were fully observed. A total of 50 imputed datasets with 100 iterations each were generated, and results were pooled across imputations. All statistical analyses were performed in R, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

At the start of each call, a pre-recorded message informs callers that calls are recorded for quality assurance and research purposes. Callers who do not wish their information to be used can explicitly opt out, in which case their data are excluded. For this study, inclusion was therefore based on implied consent unless an opt-out was registered. Strict protocols were followed to ensure confidentiality and the secure storage of all data. All identifying information was removed to ensure caller anonymity, and a unique identifier was assigned to each call or caller-level record. The study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Institut National de Santé Publique, d’Épidémiologie Clinique et de Toxicologie - Liban (INSPECT-LB) at the Lebanese University, as well as ethical clearance from the National Mental Health Program (NMHP) at the Ministry of Public Health.

Results

Call characteristics

Between June 2021 and February 2023, the Lifeline received a total of 17,849 calls from 8,095 unique callers. Passive suicidal ideation was reported in 54.6% of calls, active ideation in 40.4%, suicide attempts in 43.8%, and self-harm in 28% (Fig. 1).

In terms of reported risk factors, psychological stress was the most noted (83.5%), followed by interpersonal relationship problems (30.0%), financial difficulties (21.7%), and social isolation (12.8%). Among protective factors, 34.5% of calls mentioned access to mental health services. A positive future outlook and religious or spiritual faith were reported in 21.4% and 18.5% of calls, respectively. Fewer calls referenced mental health awareness (16.5%), engagement in hobbies (10.2%), or optimism (7.0%) (Fig. 2).

Callers’ characteristics

Among all callers, 39% were aged between 18 and 25. Within this younger age group, 42% reported a history of suicidal ideation, and 51% reported self-harm, proportions that declined steadily with increasing age, reaching their lowest levels among those aged 55 and above. The majority of callers were female (61%) and held a university degree (59%).

Among individuals reporting suicidal intent, suicide attempts, or self-harm, most were single and had a history of mental illness. Stress was prevalent across all groups, with more than 90% of callers experiencing suicidal ideation or behaviour also reporting stress. Social isolation, relationship problems, financial difficulties, and access to means were more commonly observed among callers who reported suicidal intent, attempts, or self-harm (Table 1).

Factors associated with suicidal ideation and behaviour

Multivariate logistic regression identified several significant predictors of suicidal ideation and behaviour:

Passive suicidal ideation

Individuals aged 25–35 were more likely to report passive suicidal ideation than those under 25 (OR = 1.4; 95% CI: 1.1–1.9). Other significant predictors included mental illness (OR = 1.6; 95% CI: 1.2-2.0), stress (OR = 2.8; 95% CI: 2.0-3.8), social isolation (OR = 2.3; 95% CI: 1.6–3.4), relationship problems (OR = 1.6; 95% CI: 1.2–2.1), financial difficulties (OR = 1.7; 95% CI: 1.3–2.2), and chronic illness (OR = 2.3; 95% CI: 1.3–4.2). In contrast, optimism (OR = 0.6; 95% CI: 0.4–0.9) and mental health awareness (OR = 0.7; 95% CI: 0.5–0.9) were associated with reduced odds of passive ideation (Table 2).

Active suicidal ideation

Compared to those under 25, individuals aged 25–35 had higher odds of active ideation (OR = 1.4; 95% CI: 1.1–1.8). Both unemployment (OR = 1.3; 95% CI: 1.1–1.7) and mental illness (OR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.6–2.5) were significant predictors. Other associated risk factors included stress (OR = 2.5; 95% CI: 1.8–3.5), social isolation (OR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.4–2.5), relationship issues (OR = 1.5; 95% CI: 1.2–1.9), financial problems (OR = 1.5; 95% CI: 1.2–1.9), and chronic illness (OR = 2.3; 95% CI: 1.5–3.7). Mental health awareness remained a protective factor (OR = 0.7; 95% CI: 0.5–0.9) (Table 2).

Suicidal intent

Callers aged 55 and older were less likely to report suicidal intent compared to those under 25. Significant predictors of suicidal intent included mental illness (OR = 1.8; 95% CI: 1.4–2.3), stress (OR = 2.9; 95% CI: 1.8–4.6), social isolation (OR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.5–2.6), relationship problems (OR = 1.5; 95% CI: 1.2-2.0), financial hardship (OR = 1.6; 95% CI: 1.3–2.1), living alone (OR = 1.6; 95% CI: 1.1–2.3), and chronic illness (OR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.3–2.9). Awareness of mental health was again associated with reduced odds (OR = 0.6; 95% CI: 0.4–0.8) (Table 2).

Suicide attempts

Older age (45 and above) was associated with lower odds of suicide attempts. In contrast, unemployment (OR = 1.5; 95% CI: 1.2-2.0) and mental illness (OR = 2.8; 95% CI: 2.2–3.6) were significant risk factors. Stress (OR = 2.7; 95% CI: 1.8–4.1), social isolation (OR = 1.7; 95% CI: 1.2–2.3), relationship issues (OR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.7–2.7), and chronic illness (OR = 1.7; 95% CI: 1.1–2.8) were also associated with greater odds of suicide attempts. Notably, access to mental health services was linked to higher odds (OR = 1.4; 95% CI: 1.1–1.9), while mental health awareness remained protective (OR = 0.7; 95% CI: 0.5–0.9) (Table 2).

Self-harm

Self-harm was more likely among individuals aged 25–35 (OR = 1.7; 95% CI: 1.2–2.4) and significantly less likely among those aged 55 and above. A history of mental illness was strongly associated with self-harm (OR = 2.6; 95% CI: 2.0-3.5). Stress (OR = 2.6; 95% CI: 1.7–4.2), social isolation (OR = 1.5; 95% CI: 1.1–2.2), and relationship problems (OR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.4–2.5) were also significant. As with suicide attempts, access to mental health services was associated with increased odds of self-harm (OR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.5–2.7) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study identified several key factors associated with suicidal ideation and behaviour, offering a comprehensive understanding of risk and protective correlates within a large sample of individuals who sought support from the National Lifeline in Lebanon. Individuals aged 25 to 35 were more likely to report suicidal ideation and self-harm compared to those under 25, while those aged 55 and above were less likely to report these behaviours. Suicidality was significantly associated with mental illness, stress, social isolation, financial and relationship problems, and chronic illness. Conversely, mental health awareness emerged as a protective factor. Interestingly, access to mental health services was associated with higher odds of suicide attempts and self-harm, indicating a complex relationship warranting further exploration.

Compared to Zakhour et al.‘s findings of a 28.9% prevalence of suicidal ideation in the general Lebanese population, our study reported a similar prevalence of suicidal intent (25.5%) among Lifeline callers [23]. However, passive suicidal ideation was markedly higher than the 19.8% seen in the general population, likely reflecting the distress levels of individuals actively seeking crisis support. This underscores the vital role of hotlines in servicing those at risk, particularly individuals experiencing passive ideation who may not yet be engaged in formal care.

Age-related trends in this study are consistent with global evidence indicating heightened suicidality among young adults transitioning into adulthood [24]. In Lebanon, national suicide statistics show the highest number of suicide deaths in 2023 occurred among those aged 23–32 [6], aligning with our findings. This life stage is often marked by unique stressors, including career uncertainty, financial instability, and complex relationship dynamics, that may amplify emotional distress [12, 25]. The perceived gap between societal expectations and lived realities may contribute to feelings of inadequacy and hopelessness. Emerging research among slightly younger cohorts also underscores the association between frequent social media use, bullying and persistent feelings of sadness and hopelessness as early predictors of suicide risk [26]. The long-term and or cumulative impact of these experiences remains unclear but may compound vulnerability to suicidal ideation by early adulthood. These findings highlight the urgent need for age-tailored targeted prevention strategies, that address both psychosocial and structural determinants of distress among youth and their transitional vulnerabilities. Priority interventions should include emotional skills and resilience training integrated within school curriculum, peer support and mentorship networks, and the expansion of psychosocial services within schools, universities, and workplaces. Beyond individual coping interventions, policies must also address the economic insecurity, underemployment and social fragmentation that heighten distress in this age group and advocate for the strengthening of social and economic safety nets. Aligned with WHO’s LIVE LIFE framework, Lebanon’s mental health strategy is highlighting the need to move towards an integrated model that couples early life skills with accessible community-based care [27]. Strengthening social and economic safety nets should be a joint collaborative interministerial work, essential to mitigate risk, promote well-being and prevent the progression of youth suicidal ideation into crisis.

Our findings also emphasize the role of financial strain as a major contributor to suicidal ideation. Prior research has identified financial hardship, unemployment, and cumulative economic stress as significant predictors of suicidality [12, 28, 29]. In Lebanon, while no studies have definitively quantified the relationship between the economic crisis and suicide rates, several scholars argue the post-2019 financial collapse has severely impacted population mental health [30]. Rising unemployment, inflation, and public mistrust, combined with limited social protections, likely contribute to increasing suicide risk. Recent national data show alarming rates of depression (47.8%), anxiety (45.3%), and PTSD (43.5%), all strongly correlated with financial insecurity [9, 31]. These findings underscore the urgency of economic support measures, including social protection policies, financial counselling, and employment support for young adults. Importantly, some researchers caution against the media’s role in amplifying suicide contagion during crises [32], especially economic and political. Lebanon’s NMHP has proactively addressed this through the development of national guidelines on responsible suicide reporting and training for media professionals, in line with WHO recommendations [33].

Relationship difficulties also emerged as a key risk factor, echoing prior research that links both romantic and non-romantic interpersonal conflict with increased suicide risk [25, 34]. The strong role of relationship difficulties calls for relationship-focused interventions and family-based programs that address interpersonal conflict. Additionally, our findings confirm the strong association between mental health disorders and suicidality. Studies in Lebanon have consistently demonstrated links between depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation [23, 35, 36]. Chronic physical illnesses, such as diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease, were also associated with increased risk, reflecting the psychological burden of managing long-term health conditions [37–39].

Mental health awareness was found to reduce the likelihood of suicidality, reinforcing the protective role of mental health literacy and stigma reduction. Since 2017, the NMHP and its partners have implemented nationwide awareness campaigns promoting mental health as a human right, encouraging help-seeking, and addressing specific topics such as depression, workplace mental health, and psychosocial support during emergencies. Interventions such as the Step-by-Step e-mental health program and the large-scale dissemination of psychoeducational materials have extended the reach of these efforts. Targeted initiatives within schools, workplaces, and primary healthcare centers have further advanced early identification and helped normalize conversations around mental health across diverse populations. The new National Mental Health Strategy 2024–2030 integrates suicide prevention as a central pillar, aligned with the WHO’s LIVE LIFE framework. Its key objectives include strengthening multi-sectoral collaboration, scaling up service coverage, raising public awareness, training frontline professionals, addressing social determinants, and ensuring financial sustainability of core suicide prevention infrastructure, including the National Lifeline and National Mental Health Emergency Mechanism mobile crisis teams. Future crowdsourcing research should explore how and through which channels mental health awareness effectively reaches different population groups, particularly youth and marginalized communities. Studies could examine the comparative impact of traditional media, digital and social media platforms, community outreach, and peer-led initiatives in shaping mental health knowledge, attitudes, and help-seeking behaviors. Understanding these communication pathways would enable more targeted, culturally attuned, and evidence-based awareness strategies that maximize reach and sustainability.

In addition to awareness, optimism and psychological insight emerged as key protective factors. Optimism is a well-established resilience trait that buffers the effects of stress and hopelessness on suicidal behaviour [40]. Evidence from international studies also suggests that a future-oriented mindset is associated with lower risk of suicidal ideation, partly by enhancing coping mechanisms and reducing feelings of entrapment [40, 41]. In Lebanon, resilience, measured with the validated Arabic Resilience Scale for Adolescents (READ), shows strong inverse associations with depression, anxiety, stress and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in school-based samples, underscoring its protective relevance in this context [42]. Among Lebanese adults, emotion-regulation patterns, particularly higher cognitive reappraisal and lower expressive suppression, are linked to greater resilience and moderate the effects of coping on resilience, highlighting specific, malleable targets for suicide prevention [43]. These findings underscore the importance of prevention strategies that not only mitigate risk factors but also actively cultivate positive psychological resources. Programs in schools, universities and community settings should integrate evidence-based modules on emotion regulation, problem-solving and meaning-making, skills that foster optimism and self-understanding. In Lebanon, such programs could explicitly train cognitive reappraisal and reduce maladaptive expressive suppression, and their impact on suicidal ideation/behaviour should be evaluated using locally validated measures. Future research should examine how these protective factors develop across life stages and socio-economic contexts, with priority given to trials of school- and university-based emotion-regulation/life-skills curricula that test effects on suicidal thoughts/behaviours and mediating gains in resilience.

Interestingly, the association between access to mental health services and increased odds of suicide attempts and self-harm should be interpreted with caution. This finding likely reflects reverse causality, whereby individuals experiencing more severe suicidal distress are more likely to seek care. In other words, the association is more plausibly explained by help-seeking behaviour among high-risk individuals rather than access to services constituting a risk factor in itself. This underscores the need for further research into the types, quality, and accessibility of services. Despite ongoing expansion efforts, Lebanon still faces a shortage of trained mental health professionals, inequitable service distribution, and limited affordability. To address this, the NMHP has implemented several capacity-building initiatives using task-shifting and scalable interventions. Thousands of health and social workers have been trained in evidence-based approaches, including Group Interpersonal Therapy (IPT-G), Self-Help Plus (SH+), and Problem Management Plus (PM+). Additionally, the WHO’s mhGAP program has been adapted for primary healthcare settings, with defined referral pathways and stepped care models. Outreach has also extended through innovative formats, such as SH + podcasts available on Anghami application, to improve access to remote, self-guided support. The planned expansion of mental health services to 100 primary healthcare centers will further increase service availability and strengthen human resources. Continued investment in integrated community-based care, especially in underserved regions, remains essential.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected by hotline operators based on callers’ self-reports, which may be subject to bias or omission. Despite rigorous training, some variability in how operators interpreted or coded caller responses may have occurred. The presence of missing data may have also influenced the results. Second, the study relied on cross-sectional data, which limits the ability to infer causality and prevents assessment of temporality in the observed associations. The associations observed between risk/protective factors and suicidality may be influenced by unmeasured confounders. Additionally, some callers may have chosen not to disclose their mental health history or psychosocial circumstances, further limiting data completeness. Furthermore, individuals without phone access or those unwilling to seek support from a hotline may be systematically excluded, introducing potential selection bias. Finally, as the sample consists of individuals who reached out to a crisis hotline, the findings may not be generalizable to the wider Lebanese population. Help-seeking individuals may differ systematically from those who do not access such services.

Conclusion

This study highlights key psychosocial and clinical factors associated with suicidal ideation and behaviour among Lifeline callers in Lebanon. Young adults aged 25–35 emerged as a particularly vulnerable group, with suicidality strongly linked to mental illness, stress, financial strain, and relationship difficulties, while mental health awareness acted as an important protective factor.

Moving forward, practical priorities include strengthening social protection policies to reduce financial stressors, expanding access to evidence-based mental health interventions, and sustaining national awareness campaigns to challenge stigma and promote resilience. In addition, policymakers should prioritize targeted prevention programs for young adults, expand relationship- and family-based support services, and integrate optimism- and resilience-building initiatives into national education and community strategies.

Future research should focus on longitudinal monitoring of suicide risk, evaluation of scalable community-based interventions, and strategies to integrate suicide prevention into primary and public health systems.
