Switching Diffusion Systems with Past-Dependent Switching and Countable State Space: Successful Couplings and Strong Ergodicity
Fubao Xi, Yafei Zhai, Chao Zhu

Abstract
This work studies a class of switching diffusion systems where the switching component takes values in a countable state space and its transition rates depend on the history of the continuous component. Under suitable conditions, we construct a successful coupling that establishes stability of the underlying process in the total variation norm. The coupling approach also enables us to derive strong ergodicity for the underlying process. Finally, we illustrate the main results with an N N -body mean-field model featuring past-dependent switching and a countable state space. Key Words and Phrases. Switching diffusion system, past-dependent switching, Feller property, successful coupling, stability, total variation norm, strong ergodicity. 2000 MR Subject Classification. 60J25, 60J27, 60J60, 60J75.

Introduction

Regime-switching stochastic differential equations have become a powerful framework for modeling and analyzing systems subject to random environmental changes, with applications in areas such as biological systems (
Bao and Shao, 2016
,
Greenhalgh et al., 2016
,
Cai et al., 2021
,
Li et al., 2017
), control systems (
Fleming and Soner, 2006
,
Wen et al., 2023
,
Savku, 2024
,
Song et al., 2011
), neural networks (
Whitt, 2002
,
Huang et al., 2016
), mathematical finance and risk management (
Zhang, 2001
,
Zhou and Yin, 2003
,
Elliott and Siu, 2010
), and population dynamics (
Kuang, 1993
,
Luo and Mao, 2009
,
Zhu and Yin, 2009
,
Settati and Lahrouz, 2014
).
These systems are characterized by abrupt transitions between different regimes, each governed by distinct dynamical laws. Due to their theoretical and practical importance,
such systems have attracted growing attention from researchers and practitioners in recent decades.
We refer to
Mao and Yuan, 2006
and
Yin and Zhu, 2010
for comprehensive investigations of such processes as well as their applications in areas such as mathematical biology, finance, and risk management; with the former deals with regime-switching diffusions with Markovian switching and the latter focuses on state-dependent regime-switching diffusions.

The recent decades have witnessed extensive developments of the theoretical framework of regime-switching diffusions. To name just a few,

Xi and Yin, 2010
established asymptotic properties for nonlinear autoregressive Markov processes with state-dependent switching, while
Xi and Yin, 2011
subsequently examined a class of jump-diffusions with state-dependent switching,

Zhu et al., 2015
derived Feynman-Kac formulas for regime-switching jump diffusions, and
Xi and Yin, 2015
obtained the exponential ergodicity for stochastic Liénard equations with state-dependent switching. Further developments include the work of
Shao, 2018
, who provided sufficient conditions for the existence and uniqueness of invariant measures for state-dependent regime-switching diffusion processes.
Nguyen and Yin, 2020
investigated the almost sure and
L^{p}
stability of stochastic functional differential equations with regime switching, while
Nguyen et al., 2021
focused on their ergodicity and stability properties.
Tran et al., 2022
analyzed stability in distribution of Markovian switching jump diffusions, and
Cao et al., 2024
studied the weak convergence of stochastic functional diffusion systems with singularly perturbed regime switching.

Several works have also addressed control methodologies. For instance,
Li et al., 2020
proposed delay feedback control strategies based on discrete-time observations of both the system and Markovian states, and
Li et al., 2022
achieved stabilization in distribution for hybrid stochastic differential equations using feedback control from discrete-time state observations. Applications to biological systems have produced valuable insights:
Chen et al., 2023
examined extinction and permanence in a stochastic tumor-immune system with Markovian switching, while
Hieu et al., 2024
analyzed a stochastic SIS epidemic model with vaccination in a randomly switching environment. Methodological advancements include
Li et al., 2023
on numerical solutions for hybrid systems with infinite delay, and
Hu et al., 2019
on stability in distribution for numerical solutions of neutral stochastic functional differential equations with Markovian switching.

Recent efforts have also focused on regime-switching (jump) diffusions where the switching component takes values in a countable state space.
Shao, 2015
studied the existence of strong solutions and the Feller property for such systems.
Xi and Zhu, 2017
established Feller and strong Feller properties and proved exponential ergodicity for regime-switching jump diffusions with countable regimes.
Xi et al., 2021
explored stochastic damped Hamiltonian systems with state-dependent switching in a countably infinite regime space. In addition,
Shao, 2017
investigated stabilization of regime-switching diffusion processes using feedback control based on discrete-time observations of both the state and switching processes, and
Shao and Xi, 2019
extended these results to state-dependent regime-switching processes.

An important extension of regime-switching diffusions is to consider past-dependent switching, where the transition rates depend on the past trajectory of the process. This line of research was initiated in
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
, which established existence and uniqueness results for a class of stochastic differential equations with past-dependent switching, and investigated their Markov and Feller properties. More recently,
Nguyen et al., 2024
examined the stability of stochastic functional differential equations with past-dependent random switching in a countably infinite state space. The past-dependent switching allows for more flexible modeling of systems where the transition rates are influenced by historical states, making it suitable for applications in ecology, biology, and finance. For instance, in ecological systems, the transition rates of species populations may depend on their historical states, such as past population densities or environmental conditions. In financial markets, the transition rates of the general market trend (bull or bear) may depend on the historical prices and/or the volatility patterns of the stocks. The past-dependent switching framework provides a more realistic representation of such systems, capturing the influence of historical states on current dynamics.

Building upon the work of
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
, we consider the asymptotic properties for regime-switching diffusion process
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
with past-dependent switching, in which the switching component
\Lambda(t)
has a countable state space. Since the switching rates of
\Lambda(t)
depends on the past states of
X
, the process
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
itself is not Markovian. Consequently, much of our analyses are focused on the Markovian segment process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
. In particular, we wish to establish strong ergodicity for
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
. Due to the complexity of the past-dependent switching, the analysis of such systems is significantly more challenging than that of standard regime-switching diffusions. The main difficulty arises from the fact that the past-dependent switching introduces inherently nonlinear memory effects, which invalidate the conventional approaches for (jump) diffusions with or without switching. Furthermore, the interaction between regime-switching and past-dependent dynamics complicates stability analysis, making it a non-trivial task to establish strong ergodicity.

In this paper, we aim to address these challenges by developing a novel coupling methodology tailored for past-dependent switching diffusion processes. Our approach is inspired by the coupling methods developed in the study of (jump) diffusion processes such as
Chen, 2004
,
Chen and Li, 1989
,
Lindvall and Rogers, 1986
,
Priola and Wang, 2006
, and
Wang, 2010
. We construct a successful coupling for the past-dependent regime-switching diffusion processes, which allows us to derive strong ergodicity. This extends previous work by
Xi, 2013
on Markovian switching jump-diffusion processes and
Xi and Shao, 2013
on multidimensional diffusions with state-dependent switching. Specifically, we first introduce a coupling operator
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}
in (
4.9
), which is a combination of reflection and marching couplings when the discrete components are in the same regime and an independent coupling when the discrete components are in different regimes; the operator
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}
also incorporates the basic coupling for the discrete components. This gives rise to the coupling process
(X(t),\Lambda(t),Y(t),\Lambda^{\prime}(t))
, which, in turn, leads to the segment process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t),Y_{t},\Lambda^{\prime}(t))
. To handel the past-dependent switching, we utilize the temporal intervals on which the discrete components
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
are the same, and show that the processes
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
and
(Y_{t},\Lambda^{\prime}(t))
will coelesce in one of these intervals a.s. This establishes that the coupling
(X_{t},\Lambda(t),Y_{t},\Lambda^{\prime}(t))
is successful in Theorem
4.4
.

The successful coupling is then used in Corollary
4.5
to show that the process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
possesses an unique invariant measure
\pi
and that the transition semigroup
P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)
converges to
\pi
in the total variation norm as
t\to\infty
. Furthermore, the coupling method allows us to show that the process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
is strongly ergodic in the total variation norm. In other words, the exponential convergence of the transition semigroup
P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)
to the invariant measure
\pi
is uniform with respect to the initial condition
(\phi,k)
; see Theorem
5.2
for details. This result extends previous work on Markovian switching jump-diffusion processes and multidimensional diffusions with state-dependent switching.

The paper also establishes Feller property for the segment process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
in Theorem
3.2
. The Feller property is a fundamental property that ensures continuity of the transition semigroup and the strong Markov property of the underlying process. It also plays a key role in proving the ergodicity of the process. We show that the transition semigroup
P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)
associated with the past-dependent switching diffusion processes is continuous with respect to the initial condition
(\phi,k)
, thereby implying the Feller property. While the Feller property was established in
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
, the proof was rather involved. In this paper, we present a more streamlined approach by employing coupling methods to derive the Feller property.

As an application of the established results, we consider an
N
-body mean field model with past-state-dependent switching. This model is an extension of the mean field model considered in
Xi and Yin, 2009
where the switching rates depend only on the current state of the system, not on the past. Additionally, instead of a finite state space for the switching component, we allow for a countable state space. Under some additional assumptions on the diffusion coefficients and switching rates,
Xi and Yin, 2009
established strong ergodicity for the underlying model. In this paper, we derive strong ergodicity for the more general model with less restrictions; the details are spelled out in Theorem
6.2
.

The rest of the paper is arranged as follows. Section
2
presents the precise formulation for past-dependent switching diffusion processes. The standing assumptions are also collected in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we establish the Feller property for the corresponding regime-switching process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
. Sections
4
develops a successful coupling for the past-dependent switching diffusion processes. Section
5
is devoted to the analysis of strong ergodicity in
the sense of convergence in the total variation norm. Finally, as an application of the previous results, we discuss an
N
-body mean field model with past-state-dependent switching in Section
6
.

2
Formulation

To facilitate the presentation, we introduce some notation and definitions.
For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\sigma=(\sigma_{ij})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, define

|x|=\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2},\qquad|\sigma|=\bigl(\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}|\sigma_{ij}|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2},

where
d
is positive integer. Let
{\mathbb{S}}:=\{1,2,\cdots\}
and denote by
d(\cdot,\cdot)
the discrete metric on
{\mathbb{S}}
. We next define a metric
\lambda(\cdot,\cdot)
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}
by

\lambda\bigl((x,m),(y,n)\bigr)=|x-y|+d(m,n),\quad\forall\,(x,m),(y,n)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}.

Let
{\cal B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}})
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}
. Then
({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}},\lambda(\cdot,\cdot),{\cal B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}))
is a locally compact and separable metric
space. Next, denote by
{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathcal{C}}([-r,0],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the set of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued continuous functions, where
r
is a fixed positive number. Equip
{\mathcal{C}}
with the super norm, i.e.,
\|\phi\|:=\sup\{|\phi(t)|:t\in[-r,0]\}
for any
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}
. For any
y\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
t\geq 0
, denote by
y_{t}
the so-called segment function (or memory segment function)
y_{t}:=\{y(t+s):-r\leq s\leq 0\}
. Let
E:=\mathcal{C}\times\mathbb{S}
and denote by
\mathcal{P}(E)
the collection of all probability measures on
(E,\mathcal{B}(E))
. The space of bounded and continuous functions on
E
is denoted by
C_{b}(E)
. Throughout the paper,
\mathbf{1}_{A}
denotes the indicator function of a set
A
while
I
is the identity matrix of appropriate dimension. Finally,
(\Omega,{\cal F},\{{\cal F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
is a complete probability space with a
filtration
\{{\cal F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the usual
conditions (i.e., it is increasing and right continuous and
{\cal F}_{0}
contains all
{\mathbb{P}}
-null sets).

To formulate our model, let
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
be a switching diffusion process with past-dependent switching on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}
. The
first component
X(t)
satisfies the following stochastic
differential equation (SDE)

\mathrm{d}X(t)=\displaystyle b(X(t),\Lambda(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X(t),\Lambda(t))\mathrm{d}B(t),

(2.1)

where

b(x,k):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma(x,k):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are Borel measurable functions, and

B(\cdot)
is a
{d}
-valued Brownian motion.
The second component
\Lambda(t)
is a discrete random
process with
state space
{\mathbb{S}}
such that

\mathbb{P}\{\Lambda(t+\Delta)=l|\Lambda(t)=k,X_{t}=\phi\}=\begin{cases}q_{kl}(\phi)\Delta+o(\Delta),&\!\!\!\displaystyle\,\,\hbox{if}\,\,l\neq k,\\
1+q_{kk}(\phi)\Delta+o(\Delta),&\!\!\!\displaystyle\,\,\hbox{if}\,\,l=k,\end{cases}

(2.2)

provided
\Delta\downarrow 0
, where the switching tensity matrix

Q(\phi)=\bigl(q_{kl}(\phi)\bigr)
depends on the past trajectory of
X(t)
in the interval
[t-r,t]
, that is, the
segment process
X_{t}
. As
usual, we assume that
\sum_{l\in{\mathbb{S}}}q_{kl}(\phi)=0
for all
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
.

For the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution

(X(t),\Lambda(t))
to the system (
2.1
)–(
2.2
), we make the following assumption:

Assumption 2.1
.

(i)

For each
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
,
b(x,k)
and
\sigma(x,k)
satisfy the local Lipschitz condition. That is, for every
R>0
, there exists a positive constant
L_{R}
such that

|b(x,k)-b(y,k)|+|\sigma(x,k)-\sigma(y,k)|\leq L_{R}|x-y|,

(2.3)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|x|\vee|y|\leq R
and
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
.

(ii)

For each
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
, there exist a positive constant
\gamma_{k}
and a nonnegative function
V_{k}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\inf\{V_{k}(x):x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},|x|\geq R\}=\infty,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{k}V_{k}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\hbox{tr}\bigl(\sigma(x,k)\sigma^{\top}(x,k)D^{2}V_{k}(x)\bigr)+\langle b(x,k),DV_{k}(x)\rangle\leq\gamma_{k}(1+V_{k}(x)),

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
DV_{k}(x)
and
D^{2}V_{k}(x)
denote the gradient and Hessian matrix of
V_{k}
with respect to
x
, respectively.

(iii)

There exists a constant
H>0
such that

\sup\{q_{k}(\phi):\phi\in{\mathcal{C}},k\in{\mathbb{S}}\}\leq H<\infty,

(2.4)

Under Assumption
2.1
, for each
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
, the SDE

\mathrm{d}X^{(k)}(t)=b(X^{(k)}(t),k)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X^{(k)}(t),k)\mathrm{d}B(t),\quad X^{(k)}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(2.5)

admits a unique non-explosive strong solution
X^{(k)}(\cdot)
. Furthermore, using the interlacing procedure together with exponential killing method as in
Xi and Zhu, 2017
,
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
, or
Applebaum, 2009
, we can prove that for given initial data
(\phi,k)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}
, there exists a unique non-explosive solution such that
X(t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in[-r,0]
,
\Lambda(0)=k
, and
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
satisfies system (
2.1
)–(
2.2
). For the simplicity of presentation, we will assume that Assumption
2.1
holds in the rest of the paper.

Our model can be applied in ecological systems and biological control. Consider the evolution of two interacting species. One is micro,
which is described by a logistic differential equation perturbed by a white noise as follows:

\mathrm{d}X(t)=\displaystyle X(t)\bigl(a(\Lambda(t))-b(\Lambda(t))X(t)\bigr)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(\Lambda(t))X(t)\mathrm{d}B(t),

where
a(k)
,
b(k)
and
\sigma(k)
are positive constants for each
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
.
The other is macro, and we assume that its number of individuals follows a birth-death
process. Let
X(t)
be the density of the micro species and
\Lambda(t)
the population of the macro species. The life cycle of a micro species is usually very short, while the reproduction process of
\Lambda(t)
is assumed to be non-instantaneous
and the reproduction depends on the period of time from egg formation to hatching, say,
r
. Therefore, it is natural to assume that the transition rates of
\Lambda(t)
is history dependent. This gives rise to the model (
2.1
)–(
2.2
). We refer the reader to Example 2.2 of
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
for more details.

In general, the solution
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
to system (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) is not a Markov process, but
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
is a strong Markov process under Assumption
2.1
; see Theorem 4.1 of
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
.

3
Feller Property

This section is devoted to establishing the Feller property for the process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
. To this end, we impose the following condition:

Assumption 3.1
.

There exists a concave function
\gamma:[0,\infty)\mapsto[0,\infty)
with
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\gamma(x)=\gamma(0)=0

such that for every
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
and
R>0
,

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k\}}|q_{kj}(\phi)-q_{kj}(\psi)|\leq{\kappa_{R}\gamma(\left\|\phi-\psi\right\|)},\quad\forall\phi,\psi\in{\mathcal{C}}\text{ with }\|\phi\|\vee\|\psi\|\leq R,

(3.1)

where
\kappa_{R}
is a positive constant.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
3.1
hold. Then for any
f\in C_{b}({\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}};\mathbb{R})
and
t>0
, the function

P_{t}f(\phi,k):={\mathbb{E}}[f(X^{\phi,k}_{t},\Lambda^{\phi,k}(t))],\quad(\phi,k)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}},

(3.2)

is continuous. Moreover, we have
\lim_{t\downarrow 0}P_{t}f(\phi,k)=f(\phi,k)
.

We will prove Theorem
3.2
by the coupling method. To this end, instead of the infinitesimal equation (
2.2
), we need to describe the evolution of
\Lambda(t)
using a stochastic differential equation. We first construct a family
\{\Delta_{ij}(\phi),j\not=i\in{\mathbb{S}},\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}\}
of left-closed, right-open intervals on the positive half real line. In addition, for each
j\neq i
, the interval
\Delta_{ij}(\phi)
has length
q_{ij}(\phi)
. We set

\Delta_{ij}(\phi)=\emptyset
if
q_{ij}(\phi)=0
,
i\not=j
. We refer to
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
for more details on the construction of these intervals. Next we define a function

h:{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by

h(\phi,i,z)=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{i\}}(j-i)\mathbf{1}_{\{z\in\Delta_{ij}(\phi)\}}.

(3.3)

That is,

h(\phi,i,z)=j-i
if
z\in\Delta_{ij}(\phi)
; otherwise
h(\phi,i,z)=0
.
We can now consider the following stochastic differential equation for
\Lambda(t)
:

d\Lambda(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}h(X_{t-},\Lambda(t-),z){\mathfrak{p}}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z),

(3.4)

where
{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z)
is a Poisson random measure on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
with intensity
\mathrm{d}t\times\mathfrak{m}(\mathrm{d}z)
, and
\mathfrak{m}(\cdot)
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The Poisson random
measure
{\mathfrak{p}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is independent of the Brownian motion

B(\cdot)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

(
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
, Lemma A.1)

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
3.1
hold, then the system (
2.1
)–(
2.2
) is equivalent to the system (
2.1
)–(
3.4
).

In view of Lemma
3.3
and Theorem 3.5 of
Nguyen and Yin, 2016
, the system (
2.1
)–(
3.4
) is well-posed in the strong sense. Consequently, we can assume that the filtration
\{{\cal F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is generated by the initial condition
(X_{0},\Lambda(0))
, the Brownian motion
B(\cdot)
, and the Poisson random measure
{\mathfrak{p}}(\cdot,\cdot)
, augmented by the
\mathbb{P}
-null sets. In other words,
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}
is generated as follows. First, we let

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}:=\sigma(X_{0},\Lambda({0}),B(s),\mathfrak{p}((0,s]\times U):0\leq s\leq t,U\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}_{+})),\ 0\leq t<\infty,

and
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\infty}:=\sigma\big(\bigcup_{t\geq 0}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\big)
, as well as the collection of null sets:

\mathcal{N}:=\{N\subset\Omega:\text{there exists a }G\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\infty}\text{ with }N\subset G\text{ and }\mathbb{P}(G)=0\}.

We then create the augmented filtration

{\mathcal{F}}_{t}:=\sigma(\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\cup\mathcal{N}),\ 0\leq t<\infty;\ \ {\mathcal{F}}_{\infty}:=\sigma\bigg(\bigcup_{t\geq 0}{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\bigg).

In particular, the process
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
(and hence the segment process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
as well) is adapted to the filtration
\{{\cal F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
3.1
hold, then
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
is a Markov process. In other words, for any
t\geq s\geq 0
and any bounded Borel measurable function
\bar{h}:E\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{h}(X_{t},\Lambda(t))|\mathcal{F}_{s}]={\mathbb{E}}[\bar{h}(X_{t},\Lambda(t))|(X_{s},\Lambda(s))].

Proof.

For any initial time
s\geq 0
, we consider the following system for
t\geq s
,

X^{s,\phi,k}(t)=\phi(0)+\int_{s}^{t}b(X^{s,\phi,k}(u),\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(u))\mathrm{d}u+\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(X^{s,\phi,k}(u),\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(u))\mathrm{d}B(u),

and

\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(t)=k+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}h(X^{s,\phi,k}_{u-},\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(u-),z){\mathfrak{p}}(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}z),

with the initial condition

X^{s,\phi,k}(m)=\phi(m-s),\text{for $m\in[s-r,s]$ and $\phi\in\mathcal{C}$}.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
3.1
, the system admits a unique non-explosive strong solution
(X^{s,\phi,k}(t),\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(t))
and we denote the corresponding segment process by
(X^{s,\phi,k}_{t},\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(t))
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{s}=\sigma\{B(u)-B(s),{\mathfrak{p}}(u,U)-{\mathfrak{p}}(s,U):u\geq s,U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\}
. Clearly,
\mathcal{G}_{s}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}
. Moreover, the process
(X^{s,\phi,k}_{t},\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(t))
depends completely on the increments
B(u)-B(s),{\mathfrak{p}}(u,\mathrm{d}z)-{\mathfrak{p}}(s,\mathrm{d}z)
for
u\geq s
and so is
\mathcal{G}_{s}
-measurable. Hence
(X^{s,\phi,k}_{t},\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(t))
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{s}
for all
t\geq s
. On the other hand, the strong uniqueness of the solution implies that

X(u)=X^{s,X_{s},\Lambda(s)}(u)\ \text{for any $u\geq s-r$},

and

\Lambda(u)=\Lambda^{s,X_{s},\Lambda(s)}(u)\ \text{for any $u\geq s$}.

Thus, with probability one, we have
X_{t}=X^{s,X_{s},\Lambda(s)}_{t}
for any
t\geq s
.

For any bounded Borel measurable function
\bar{h}:E\to\mathbb{R}
, by Proposition 1.12 of
Da Prato and Zabczyk, 2014
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{h}(X_{t},\Lambda(t))|\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\bar{h}(X^{s,X_{s},\Lambda(s)}_{t},\Lambda^{s,X_{s},\Lambda(s)}(t))|\mathcal{F}_{s}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\bar{h}(X^{s,\phi,k}_{t},\Lambda^{s,\phi,k}(t))|(\phi,k)=(X_{s},\Lambda(s))]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\bar{h}(X_{t},\Lambda(t))|(X_{s},\Lambda(s))].

This proves the Markov property.

\qquad\Box

In view of Lemma
3.3
, we now consider the basic coupling for the process
(X,\Lambda)
:

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X(t)=b(X(t),\Lambda(t))dt+\sigma(X(t),\Lambda(t))\mathrm{d}B(t),\\
\mathrm{d}\Lambda(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}h(X_{t-},\Lambda(t-),z)\mathfrak{p}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z),\\
\mathrm{d}\widetilde{X}(t)=b(\widetilde{X}(t),\widetilde{\Lambda}(t))dt+\sigma(\widetilde{X}(t),\widetilde{\Lambda}(t))\mathrm{d}B(t),\\
\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\Lambda}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}h(\widetilde{X}_{t-},\widetilde{\Lambda}(t-),z)\mathfrak{p}(\mathrm{d}t,\mathrm{d}z),\end{cases}

(3.5)

with initial condition
(\phi,k,\psi,k)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}
. For
f\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S};\mathbb{R})
,
we define the operator associated with (
3.5
) as

\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}f(\phi,i,\psi,j):=\widetilde{\varOmega}_{\text{d}}f(\phi(0),i,\psi(0),j)+\widetilde{\varOmega}_{\text{s}}f(\phi,i,\psi,j),\quad\forall(\phi,i,\psi,j)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}},

(3.6)

where
\widetilde{\varOmega}_{\text{d}}
and
\widetilde{\varOmega}_{\text{s}}
are defined as follows.
For
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
i,j\in\mathbb{S}
, we set

\displaystyle a(x,i,z,j)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma(x,i)\\
\sigma(z,j)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{\top}(x,i)&\sigma^{\top}(z,j)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a(x,i)&\sigma(x,i)\sigma^{\top}(z,j)\\
\sigma(z,j)\sigma^{\top}(x,i)&a(z,j)\end{pmatrix},

(3.7)

and

\displaystyle b(x,i,z,j)=\begin{pmatrix}b(x,i)\\
b(z,j)\end{pmatrix},

(3.8)

where
a(x,i)=\sigma(x,i)\sigma^{\top}(x,i)
. Then we define for any
(x,i,z,j)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varOmega}_{\text{d}}f(x,i,z,j):=\frac{1}{2}\hbox{tr}\bigl(a(x,i,z,j)D^{2}f(x,i,z,j)\bigr)+\langle b(x,i,z,j),Df(x,i,z,j)\rangle,

(3.9)

where
Df(x,i,z,j)=(D_{x}f(x,i,z,j),D_{z}f(x,i,z,j))
and
D^{2}f(x,i,z,j)
denote the gradient and the Hessian matrix of
f
with respect to the variables
x
and
z
, respectively. Finally, the operator
\widetilde{\varOmega}_{\text{s}}f
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\varOmega}_{\text{s}}f(\phi,i,\psi,j)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{S}}[q_{il}(\phi)-q_{jl}(\psi)]^{+}(f(\phi(0),l,\psi(0),j)-f(\phi(0),i,\psi(0),j))

\displaystyle\quad\ +\sum_{l\in\mathbb{S}}[q_{jl}(\psi)-q_{il}(\phi)]^{+}(f(\phi(0),i,\psi(0),l)-f(\phi(0),i,\psi(0),j))

(3.10)

\displaystyle\quad\ +\sum_{l\in\mathbb{S}}[q_{il}(\phi)\wedge q_{jl}(\psi)](f(\phi(0),l,\psi(0),l)-f(\phi(0),i,\psi(0),j)).

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The assertion that
\lim_{t\downarrow 0}P_{t}f(\phi,k)=f(\phi,k)
is obvious because the process
(X,\Lambda)
is càdlàg and
f
is bounded and continuous. To establish the continuity of the function
P_{t}f
of (
3.2
), we consider the coupling process
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{\Lambda},X,\Lambda)
given by (
3.5
) with initial condition
(\phi,k,\psi,k)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}
; here we take
\widetilde{\Lambda}(0)=\Lambda(0)
because
{\mathbb{S}}
has a discrete topology.
For each
R>0
, let

\tau_{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\|X_{t}\|\vee\|\widetilde{X}_{t}\|\geq R\}.

Also denote by

\zeta:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\Lambda(t)\neq\widetilde{\Lambda}(t)\}

the first time when the discrete components
\Lambda
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}
differ. We have
\mathbb{P}(\zeta>0)=1
. For any
s\in[0,\zeta)
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}(s)-X(s)

\displaystyle=\psi(0)-\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{s}[b(\widetilde{X}(r),\Lambda(r))-b(X(r),\Lambda(r))]\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{s}[\sigma(\widetilde{X}(r),\Lambda(r))-\sigma(X(r),\Lambda(r))]\mathrm{d}B(r).

Then it follows from the local Lipschitz condition for
b(\cdot,k)
and
\sigma(\cdot,k),k\in{\mathbb{S}}
that for any
t\in[0,T]
with
T>0
being temporarily fixed,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}

\displaystyle\bigg[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}|\widetilde{X}(s)-X(s)|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 3|\psi(0)-\phi(0)|^{2}+3t{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}|b(\widetilde{X}(r),\Lambda(r))-b(X(r),\Lambda(r))|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad+12{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}|\sigma(\widetilde{X}(r),\Lambda(r))-\sigma(X(r),\Lambda(r))|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 3|\psi(0)-\phi(0)|^{2}+3(t+4)L_{R}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}|\widetilde{X}(r)-X(r)|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 3|\psi(0)-\phi(0)|^{2}+3(T+4)L_{R}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sup_{0\leq u\leq r\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}|\widetilde{X}(u)-X(u)|^{2}\bigg]\mathrm{d}r.

The Gronwall inequality then implies that

{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}|\widetilde{X}(s)-X(s)|^{2}\bigg]\leq 3|\psi(0)-\phi(0)|^{2}e^{3(T+4)L_{R}^{2}t}.

Then it follows that for any
s\in[0,t]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|\widetilde{X}_{s\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}-X_{s\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}\|]

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi-\psi\|\vee{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}|\widetilde{X}(s)-X(s)|\bigg]\leq C_{T,R}\|\phi-\psi\|,

(3.11)

where
C_{T,R}>0
is a constant depending on
T
and
R
.

Consider the function
\Xi(\phi(0),k,\psi(0),l):=\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq l\}}
for
(\phi,k,\psi,l)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}
. It follows directly from the definition of
\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}
that

\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}\Xi(\phi,k,\psi,l)=\widetilde{\varOmega}_{\text{s}}\Xi(\phi,k,\psi,l)\leq 0,\text{ if }k\neq l.

When
k=l
, we have from (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}\Xi(\phi,k,\psi,l)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Omega}_{\text{s}}\Xi(\phi,k,\psi,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}}[q_{ki}(\phi)-q_{ki}(\psi)]^{+}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i\neq k\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq k\}})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}}[q_{ki}(\psi)-q_{ki}(\phi)]^{+}(\mathbf{1}_{\{i\neq k\}}-\mathbf{1}_{\{k\neq k\}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k\}}\left|q_{ki}(\phi)-q_{ki}(\psi)\right|\leq\kappa_{R}\gamma(\left\|\phi-\psi\right\|).

Hence

\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}\Xi(\phi,k,\psi,l)\leq{\kappa_{R}\gamma(\left\|\phi-\psi\right\|)}

(3.12)

for all
(\phi,k,\psi,l)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}
with
\|\phi\|\vee\|\psi\|\leq R
.

Note that
\zeta\leq t\wedge\tau_{R}
if and only if
\widetilde{\Lambda}(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta)\neq\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta)
. In addition,
\Lambda(s)=\widetilde{\Lambda}(s)
for all
s\in[0,t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta)
. Thus we can use (
3.12
) to compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\{\zeta\leq t\wedge\tau_{R}\}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[\Xi(\widetilde{X}(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta),\widetilde{\Lambda}(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta),X(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta),\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta))]

\displaystyle=\Xi(\widetilde{x},k,x,k)+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}\widetilde{\mathscr{A}}\Xi(\widetilde{X}_{s},\widetilde{\Lambda}(s),X_{s},\Lambda(s))\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{R}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}{\gamma(\|\widetilde{X}_{s}-X_{s}\|)}\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{R}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\big[\gamma(\|\widetilde{X}_{s\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}-X_{s\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}\|)\big]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{R}\int_{0}^{t}{\gamma\big(\mathbb{E}[\|\widetilde{X}_{s\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}-X_{s\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}\|]\big)}\mathrm{d}s,

where we used the concavity of
\gamma
to derive the last inequality.
Note that
\|\widetilde{X}_{s\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}-X_{s\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta}\|\leq 2R
for all
s\in[0,t]
. Then it follows from
(
3.11
) and the bounded convergence theorem that

\lim_{\|\phi-\psi\|\to 0}\mathbb{P}\{\zeta\leq t\wedge\tau_{R}\}=0.

(3.13)

Now for any
f\in C_{b}({\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}};\mathbb{R})
and
t\geq 0
, we compute

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle P_{t}f(\psi,k)-P_{t}f(\phi,k)|

\displaystyle=|{\mathbb{E}}[f(\widetilde{X}_{t},\widetilde{\Lambda}(t))-f(X_{t},\Lambda(t))]|

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}[|f(\widetilde{X}_{t},\widetilde{\Lambda}(t))-f(X_{t},\Lambda(t))|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{R}\leq t\}}]+{\mathbb{E}}[|f(\widetilde{X}_{t},\widetilde{\Lambda}(t))-f(X_{t},\Lambda(t))|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{R}>t\}}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{R}\leq t\}+{\mathbb{E}}[|f(\widetilde{X}_{t\wedge\tau_{R}},\widetilde{\Lambda}(t\wedge\tau_{R}))-f(X_{t\wedge\tau_{R}},\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}))|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{R}>t\}}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{R}\leq t\}+{\mathbb{E}}[|f(\widetilde{X}_{t\wedge\tau_{R}},\widetilde{\Lambda}(t\wedge\tau_{R}))-f(X_{t\wedge\tau_{R}},\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}))|\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta\leq\tau_{R}\wedge t\}}]

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{E}}[|f(\widetilde{X}_{t\wedge\tau_{R}},\widetilde{\Lambda}(t\wedge\tau_{R}))-f(X_{t\wedge\tau_{R}},\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}))|\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta>\tau_{R}\wedge t\}}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{R}\leq t\}+2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\zeta\leq t\wedge\tau_{R}\}

(3.14)

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{E}}[|f(\widetilde{X}_{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta},\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta))-f(X_{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta},\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta))|\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta>\tau_{R}\wedge t\}}].

For any
\varepsilon>0
, since
\tau_{R}\to\infty
a.s. as
R\to\infty
, we can choose an
R
sufficiently large so that
2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{R}\leq t\}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
. Likewise, (
3.13
) allows us to choose
\|\psi-\phi\|
sufficiently small so that
2\|f\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\{\zeta\leq t\wedge\tau_{R}\}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
. In a similar manner, we can use (
3.11
), the continuity of
f
, and the bounded convergence theorem to conclude that for all
\|\psi-\phi\|
sufficiently small, we have

{\mathbb{E}}[|f(\widetilde{X}_{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta},\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta))-f(X_{t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta},\Lambda(t\wedge\tau_{R}\wedge\zeta))|\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta>\tau_{R}\wedge t\}}]<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Plugging these observations into (
3
), we derive

|P_{t}f(\psi,k)-P_{t}f(\phi,k)|<\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we obtain the desired continuity of the function
P_{t}f
defined in (
3.2
). This completes the proof.

\qquad\Box

Remark 3.5
.

While Feller property was established in

4
Successful Coupling

In order to construct a coupling process
(X(t),\Lambda(t),Y(t),\Lambda^{\prime}(t))
for two copies
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
and
(Y(t),\Lambda^{\prime}(t))
of solutions to the system (
2.1
)–(
2.2
). For
(x,k,y,l)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{S}}
, set a
2d\times 2d
matrix as follows:

\displaystyle\tau(x,k,y,l)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{1}}_{\triangle}(k,l){\mathbf{1}}_{\{x\neq y\}}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma(x,k)&0\\
\sigma(y,k)H(x,y,k)&0\end{pmatrix}\,+{\mathbf{1}}_{\triangle}(k,l){\mathbf{1}}_{\{x=y\}}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma(x,k)&0\\
\sigma(x,k)&0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\displaystyle\ \ \quad+{\mathbf{1}}_{\triangle^{c}}(k,l)\begin{pmatrix}\sigma(x,k)&0\\
0&\sigma(y,l)\end{pmatrix},

(4.1)

where
\triangle=\{(k,k):k\in{\mathbb{S}}\}
and
H(x,y,k)
is an appropriate
d\times d

orthogonal matrix. Let us explain the coupling given in (
4
).
When
k=l
and
x\neq y
, we can take

H(x,y,k)=I-2\frac{(x-y)(x-y)^{\top}}{|x-y|^{2}};

(4.2)

this is the so-called coupling by reflection. When
k=l
and
x=y
, the coupling in (
4
) is the so-called march coupling.
When
k\neq l
, we use the independent coupling in (
4
).
We refer to
Chen and Li, 1989
and
Lindvall and Rogers, 1986
for details about different couplings.

We now construct a coupling process
(X(t),\Lambda(t),Y(t),\Lambda^{\prime}(t))
as follows. Let
(X(t),Y(t))
satisfy the following SDE in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
,

\mathrm{d}\begin{pmatrix}X(t)\\
Y(t)\end{pmatrix}=\tau\bigl(X(t),\Lambda(t),Y(t),\Lambda^{\prime}(t)\bigr)\mathrm{d}W(t)+\begin{pmatrix}b\bigl(X(t),\Lambda(t)\bigr)\\
b\bigl(Y(t),\Lambda^{\prime}(t)\bigr)\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}t,

(4.3)

where

\tau(x,k,y,l)
is the defined in (
4
) and
W(t)
is an

{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
-valued standard Brownian motion. Meanwhile, let
(\Lambda(t),\Lambda^{\prime}(t))

be a discrete random process with space
{\mathbb{S}}\times{\mathbb{S}}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\{(\Lambda(t\!+\!\delta),\Lambda^{\prime}(\!t+\!\delta))\!=\!(m,n)|(\Lambda(t),\Lambda^{\prime}(t))\!=\!(k,l),(X_{t},Y_{t})\!=\!(\phi,\psi)\}

\displaystyle\quad=\begin{cases}q_{(k,l)(m,n)}(\phi,\psi)\delta+o(\delta),&\hbox{if}\ (m,n)\neq(k,l),\\
1+q_{(k,l)(k,l)}(\phi,\psi)\delta+o(\delta),&\hbox{if}\ (m,n)=(k,l)\end{cases}

(4.4)

provided
\delta\downarrow 0
, where

\bigl(q_{(k,l)(m,n)}(\phi,\psi)\bigr)
is the basic coupling of

\bigl(q_{kl}(\phi)\bigr)
and
\bigl(q_{kl}(\psi)\bigr)
derived from the basic coupling of (
3
).
Note that for any
f:{\mathbb{S}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
(k,l)\notin\triangle
, we can rewrite (
3
) as

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle(\phi,\psi)f(k,l)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\}}(q_{km}(\phi)-q_{lm}(\psi))^{+}\bigl(f(m,l)-f(k,l)\bigr)+(q_{kl}(\phi)-q_{ll}(\psi))^{+}\bigl(f(l,l)-f(k,l)\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\}}(q_{lm}(\psi)-q_{km}(\phi))^{+}\bigl(f(k,m)-f(k,l)\bigr)+(q_{lk}(\psi)-q_{kk}(\phi))^{+}\bigl(f(k,k)-f(k,l)\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\}}q_{km}(\phi)\wedge q_{lm}(\psi)\bigl(f(m,m)-f(k,l)\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+q_{kk}(\phi)\wedge q_{lk}(\psi)\bigl(f(k,k)-f(k,l)\bigr)+q_{kl}(\phi)\wedge q_{ll}(\psi)\bigl(f(l,l)-f(k,l)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\}}(q_{km}(\phi)-q_{lm}(\psi))^{+}\bigl(f(m,l)-f(k,l)\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\}}(q_{lm}(\psi)-q_{km}(\phi))^{+}\bigl(f(k,m)-f(k,l)\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\}}q_{km}(\phi)\wedge q_{lm}(\psi)\bigl(f(m,m)-f(k,l)\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+q_{kl}(\phi)\bigl(f(l,l)-f(k,l)\bigr)+q_{lk}(\psi)\bigl(f(k,k)-f(k,l)\bigr).

This says that when the coupling process
(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})
leaves state
(k,l)\notin\triangle
, it jumps to one state in
\{(k,m),m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{l\}\}\cup\{(m,l),m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k\}\}\cup\{(m,m),m\in{\mathbb{S}}\}
with respective rates

\displaystyle q_{(k,l)(m,l)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=(q_{km}(\phi)-q_{lm}(\psi))^{+},\ \quad m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\},

\displaystyle q_{(k,l)(k,m)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=(q_{lm}(\psi)-q_{km}(\phi))^{+},\ \quad m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\},

\displaystyle q_{(k,l)(m,m)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=q_{km}(\phi)\wedge q_{lm}(\psi),\ \quad m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\},

(4.5)

\displaystyle q_{(k,l)(l,l)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=q_{kl}(\phi),

\displaystyle q_{(k,l)(k,k)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=q_{lk}(\psi).

The sum of these rates is

\displaystyle-q_{(k,l)(k,l)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\}}[(q_{km}(\phi)-q_{lm}(\psi))^{+}+(q_{lm}(\psi)-q_{km}(\phi))^{+}+q_{km}(\phi)\wedge q_{lm}(\psi)]

\displaystyle\quad+q_{kl}(\phi)+q_{lk}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k,l\}}q_{km}(\phi)\vee q_{lm}(\psi)+q_{kl}(\phi)+q_{lk}(\psi).

Using a similar argument as that for (
4

leaves state

\displaystyle q_{(k,k)(m,k)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=(q_{km}(\phi)-q_{km}(\psi))^{+},\ \quad m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k\},

\displaystyle q_{(k,k)(k,m)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=(q_{km}(\psi)-q_{km}(\phi))^{+},\ \quad m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k\},

\displaystyle q_{(k,k)(m,m)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=q_{km}(\phi)\wedge q_{km}(\psi),\ \quad m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k\}.

The sum of these rates is

\displaystyle-q_{(k,k)(k,k)}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k\}}[(q_{km}(\phi)-q_{km}(\psi))^{+}+(q_{km}(\psi)-q_{km}(\phi))^{+}+q_{km}(\phi)\wedge q_{km}(\psi)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{S}}\setminus\{k\}}q_{km}(\phi)\vee q_{km}(\psi).

(4.7)

Using the interlacing procedure together with exponential killing method as used before,

(4.9)

where

Definition 4.1

(4.14)

Remark 4.3

1) Assumption

Let us present a sufficient condition for the uniform boundedness of

Thus (

Corollary 4.5

Suppose Assumptions

Proof.

By virtue of the coupling inequality, for any

\displaystyle\|P(t+u,(\phi,k),\cdot)-P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{Var}}

\displaystyle\quad=\|P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)-\displaystyle\int_{E}P(u,(\phi,k),\mathrm{d}\psi\times\mathrm{d}\{l\})P(t,(\psi,l),\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{Var}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\displaystyle\int_{E}P(u,(\phi,k),\mathrm{d}\psi\times\mathrm{d}\{l\})\|P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)-P(t,(\psi,l),\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{Var}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\displaystyle\int_{E}P(u,(\phi,k),\mathrm{d}\psi\times\mathrm{d}\{l\}){\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}(T>t)\to 0,\quad\hbox{as}\quad t\to\infty.

thanks to Lemma

Similarly, applying the coupling inequality again yields

\displaystyle\|P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{Var}}

\displaystyle\quad=\|P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)-\displaystyle\int_{E}\mathrm{d}\pi(\psi,l)P(t,(\psi,l),\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{Var}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\displaystyle\int_{E}\mathrm{d}\pi(\psi,l)\|P(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)-P(t,(\psi,l),\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{Var}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\displaystyle\int_{E}\mathrm{d}\pi(\psi,l){\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}(T>t)\to 0,\quad\hbox{as}\quad t\to\infty.

Namely,

is ergodic.

Lemma 4.6

Let

be a sequence of probability measures on

that is Cauchy in the total variation distance. Then

is tight.

Proof.
Suppose on the contrary that

Fix

(4.23)

Choose

5
Strong Ergodicity

For Markov processes, the strong ergodicity (or uniform ergodicity) in
the sense of convergence in variation norm is the strongest one; see

Chen, 2004
,
Meyn and Tweedie, 2009
. Strong ergodicity for certain Markov processes has been studied using coupling methods. This includes work on one-dimensional diffusion processes and birth-death processes
Mao, 2002
,
Mao, 2006
. Additionally, strong ergodicity was investigated for a mean-field model with a continuous-state-dependent switching process in
Xi and Yin, 2009
.

In this section we study strong ergodicity for the Markov process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
. To proceed, we first recall the definition of strong ergodicity.

Definition 5.1
.

(
Mao, 2006
, Definition 1.1)

The Markov process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
is said to be strongly ergodic in variational norm (in short, strongly ergodic) if there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that

\sup\{\|{\mathbb{P}}(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{Var}}:(\phi,k)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\}=O({\rm e}^{-\varepsilon t})\quad\text{ as }\ t\rightarrow\infty,

(5.1)

where
\pi
is the invariant distribution of the process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
. For
\gamma\geq 2
, define

\beta(\gamma)=\sup\{\varepsilon\geq 0:\sup\{\|{\mathbb{P}}(t,(\phi,k),\cdot)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{Var}}:(\phi,k)\in{\mathcal{C}}\times{\mathbb{S}}\}\leq\gamma{\rm e}^{-\varepsilon t}\text{ for all $t\geq 0$}\}.

(5.2)

Furthermore, let
\beta:=\beta(\infty)=\lim\limits_{\gamma\rightarrow\infty}\beta(\gamma)
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
,
3.1
, and
4.2
hold. Then the Markov process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
is strongly ergodic with

\beta\geq\frac{\delta_{2}}{2(M+r+N)}>0,

(5.3)

where
r
denotes the delay duration, while
\beta
,
\delta_{2}
,
M
and

are the constants defined in (

), (
4
), (

) and (

), respectively.

Proof.

Adopting the notation from the preceding discussion, for any
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
and

that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(T<M+r)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(T<M+r,\eta_{1}\geq M+r)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(\eta_{1}\geq M+r){\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(T<M+r|\eta_{1}\geq M+r)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(\eta_{1}\geq M+r){\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(\widehat{T}<M|\eta_{1}\geq M+r)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(\eta_{1}\geq M+r){\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi(0),\psi(0),k)}\bigl(T^{(k)}<M\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\{-H(M+r)\}=\delta_{2}>0.

holds for any

such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}(T<M+r+N)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}(T<M+r+N,\zeta_{1}<N)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl[\mathbb{E}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T<M+r+N\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta_{1}<N\}}|\mathcal{F}_{\zeta_{1}}]\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta_{1}<N\}}\mathbb{E}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T<M+r+N\}}|\mathcal{F}_{\zeta_{1}}]\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta_{1}<N\}}\mathbb{E}^{(X_{\zeta_{1}},\Lambda(\zeta_{1}),Y_{\zeta_{1}},\Lambda^{\prime}(\zeta_{1}))}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T<M+r+N-\zeta_{1}\}}]\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{\zeta_{1}<N\}}\mathbb{E}^{(X_{\zeta_{1}},\Lambda(\zeta_{1}),Y_{\zeta_{1}},\Lambda^{\prime}(\zeta_{1}))}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T<M+r\}}]\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\delta_{2}\mathbb{P}^{{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}}[\zeta_{1}<N]\geq\frac{\delta_{2}}{2}.

(5.6)

On the other hand, for any initial condition
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
and
k\in\mathbb{S}
, we have from (
5
) that

{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(T<M+r+N)\geq{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,k)}(T<M+r)\geq\delta_{2}>\frac{\delta_{2}}{2}.

(5.7)

By combining (
5
) and (
5.7
), we conclude that for any
(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}
and
(k,l)\in\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}
, the following uniform estimate holds:

{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}(T<M+r+N)\geq\frac{\delta_{2}}{2}>0.

(5.8)

In other words, for any
(\phi,k,\psi,l)\in\mathcal{C}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times\mathcal{C}\times{\mathbb{S}}
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl(T\geq R\bigr)\leq\rho,

(5.9)

where
R:={M+r+N}
and
\rho:=1-\frac{\delta_{2}}{2}<1
.
Using the Markov property and an inductive argument, we establish that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}(T\geq nR)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq nR\}}\bigr]={\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq R\}}{\mathbb{E}}^{(X_{R},\Lambda(R),Y_{R},\Lambda^{\prime}(R))}[\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq(n-1)R\}}]\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq R\}}\rho^{n-1}\big]\leq\rho^{n}.

(5.10)

Obviously, (
5
) also holds for
n=0
. Hence, denoting
\Xi:=\frac{T}{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl(\Xi\geq t\bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}\bigl(\Xi\geq\lfloor t\rfloor\bigr)\leq\rho^{\lfloor t\rfloor},\quad\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

(5.11)

Then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have from (
5.11
) that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}[\Xi^{n}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}nt^{n-1}\mathbb{P}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}(\Xi\geq t)\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}nt^{n-1}\rho^{\lfloor t\rfloor}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}nt^{n-1}\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{k}^{k+1}nt^{n-1}\rho^{k}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{k}[(k+1)^{n}-k^{n}]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{k}n(k+1)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{n}{\rho}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{k}k^{n-1}-\rho\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{\rho}\mathrm{Li}_{-(n-1)}(\rho),

(5.12)

where the second last inequality follows from the mean value theorem, and
\mathrm{Li}_{-(n-1)}(\rho)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho^{k}k^{n-1}
is the polylogarithms function of negative integer order (see
https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Polylogarithm.html
or
https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.12
). We now claim that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Li}_{-(n-1)}(\rho)\leq\frac{\rho(n-1)!}{(1-\rho)^{n}},\quad\forall\rho\in[0,1).

(5.13)

To see this, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{(n-1)!}{(1-\rho)^{n}}

\displaystyle=(n-1)!+(n-1)!\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{n(n+1)\dots(n+k-1)}{k!}\rho^{k}

\displaystyle=(n-1)!+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(n+k-1)(n+k-2)\dots(k+1)k!}{k!}\rho^{k}

\displaystyle\geq(n-1)!+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k+1)^{n-1}\rho^{k}

\displaystyle=(n-1)!+\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{n-1}\rho^{k}-1

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{n-1}\rho^{k}=\frac{1}{\rho}\mathrm{Li}_{-(n-1)}(\rho).

This of course implies (
5.13
).

Now, plugging (
5.13
) into (
5
) yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}[\Xi^{n}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n!}{(1-\rho)^{n}}.

Recall that
\Xi=\frac{T}{R}
. Thus it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}[T^{n}]

\displaystyle=R^{n}{\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}[\Xi^{n}]\leq R^{n}\frac{n!}{(1-\rho)^{n}}=n!\widehat{R}^{n},

(5.14)

where
\widehat{R}=\frac{R}{1-\rho}=\frac{2(M+r+N)}{\delta_{2}}
.

Finally, for all
\lambda<\widehat{R}^{-1}
, we have from (
5.14
) that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}[{\rm e}^{\lambda T}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}{\mathbb{E}}^{(\phi,k,\psi,l)}[T^{n}]\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\big(\lambda\widehat{R}\big)^{n}=\frac{1}{1-\lambda\widehat{R}}<\infty.

(5.15)

Note that the inequality (
5.15
) holds uniformly for all
(\phi,k,\psi,l)\in\mathcal{C}\times{\mathbb{S}}\times\mathcal{C}\times{\mathbb{S}}
.
By applying Theorem 2.1 of
Mao, 2006
in conjunction with the inequality (
5.15
), we prove that the Markov process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
is strongly ergodic and that
{\beta}(\gamma)\geq\widehat{R}^{-1}(1-2/\gamma)
for
\gamma>2
. This gives (
5.3
) and hence completes the proof.
\qquad\Box

Combining this result with
(
Mao, 2006
, Theorem 2.2)
, we obtain the following upper bounds for the convergence rates
\beta(\gamma)
in strong ergodicity of the Markov process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
.

Corollary 5.3
.

In the setting of Theorem
5.2
, we have

\displaystyle\beta(\gamma)\leq\inf\Bigg\{\left[\frac{2}{\pi(A)}\log\frac{\gamma}{\pi(A)}\right]\left(\sup_{(\phi,k)\in E}\mathbb{E}^{(\phi,k)}[\tau_{A}]\right)^{-1}:\ A\in\mathcal{B}(E)\text{ is closed with }\pi(A)>0\Bigg\},

where
\tau_{A}
denotes the hitting time of
A
, i.e.,
\tau_{A}=\inf\{t\geq 0:(X_{t},\Lambda(t))\in A\}
.

6
Application:
N
-Body Mean Field Model with Past-State-Dependent Switching

In this section, we consider an
N
-body mean field model with a past-state-dependent switching process; it is a generalization of the model considered in
Xi and Yin, 2009
. The model is described by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{i}(t)=\big[\alpha(\Lambda(t))X_{i}(t)-X_{i}^{3}(t)-\beta(\Lambda(t))(X_{i}(t)-\overline{X}(t))\big]\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{i}(X(t),\Lambda(t))\mathrm{d}W_{i}(t),

(6.1)

for
i=1,\dots,N
,
where
X(t)=(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),\ldots,X_{N}(t))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the state vector of the system,
\overline{X}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}(t)
is the arithmetic mean of the ensemble,
\Lambda(t)
is a past-state-dependent switching process taking values in
\mathbb{S}
and satisfies (
2.2
),
and
W_{i}(t),i=1,\dots,N
are independent standard one-dimensional Brownian motions. In (
6.1
),
\alpha(k)
and
\beta(k)
are positive constants for each
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
, and the coefficient
\sigma_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a Borel-measurable function for each
i=1,\dots,N
.

As introduced earlier, the model (
6.1
) generalizes the framework studied in
Xi and Yin, 2009
. In their work, the switching component
\Lambda(\cdot)
has a finite state space, and the transition rates
q_{kl}(\cdot)
depend solely on the current state of
X
. In contrast, our model permits
\Lambda(\cdot)
to take values in a countable state space, with transition rates depending on the past trajectory of
X
. Furthermore, we relax several restrictive technical assumptions on
q_{kl}(\cdot)
and
\sigma_{i}(\cdot)
imposed in
Xi and Yin, 2009
.

Notably,
Xi and Yin, 2009
established strong ergodicity for the process
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
under additional constraints—namely, that
q_{kl}(x)\equiv q_{kl}>0
for all
k\neq l
and that
\sigma_{1}(x,k)=\sigma_{2}(x,k)=\dots=\sigma_{N}(x,k)
. In Theorem
6.2
below, we prove strong ergodicity for
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
without these restrictions. These extensions and relaxations result in a more flexible and comprehensive framework for analyzing mean-field models with path-dependent switching.

For the convenience of presentation, for
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}
and
i=1,\dots,N
, we set

\displaystyle b_{i}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\alpha(k)x_{i}-x_{i}^{3}-\beta(k)(x_{i}-\overline{x}),

where
\overline{x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}
, and

\displaystyle b(x,k)

\displaystyle=\big(b_{1}(x,k),b_{2}(x,k),\ldots,b_{N}(x,k)\big)^{\top},

\displaystyle\sigma(x,k)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\big(\sigma_{1}(x,k),\sigma_{2}(x,k),\ldots,\sigma_{N}(x,k)\big).

Then (
6.1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X(t)=b(X(t),\Lambda(t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X(t),\Lambda(t))\mathrm{d}W(t),

(6.2)

where
W(t)=(W_{1}(t),W_{2}(t),\ldots,W_{N}(t))^{\top}
is the
N
-dimensional standard Brownian motion.

Lemma 6.1
.

Assume (
2.4
) holds and

\displaystyle\sup\{\alpha(k):k\in{\mathbb{S}}\}

\displaystyle<\infty,

(6.3)

\displaystyle|\sigma(x,k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|^{2}),\quad\forall(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}},

(6.4)

\displaystyle|\sigma(x,k)-\sigma(y,k)|

\displaystyle\leq C|x-y|,\quad\forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N},\ k\in{\mathbb{S}},

(6.5)

where
C
is a positive constant. Then there exists a unique strong solution
(X(t),\Lambda(t))
to the system (
6.2
) and (
2.2
).

Proof.
We need to verify that Assumption
2.1
holds. Apparently, the coefficients
b(x,k)
and
\sigma(x,k)
satisfy the local Lipschitz condition in (
2.3
). It remains to show that Assumption (ii) is satisfied. To this end, we consider the function
V(x,k):=|x|^{2}+1,(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}
and note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}-\overline{x}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\Bigg)^{2}\geq 0.

(6.6)

Use this observation in the first inequality below, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}V(x,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\sigma(x,k)\sigma(x,k)^{\top}2I)+\langle 2x,b(x,k)\rangle

\displaystyle=|\sigma(x,k)|^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\big(\alpha(k)x_{i}-x_{i}^{3}-\beta(k)(x_{i}-\overline{x})\big)

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma(x,k)|^{2}+2\alpha(k)|x|^{2}-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq(C+2\alpha(k))(1+|x|^{2})-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{4}

\displaystyle=V(x,k)\Bigg[C+2\alpha(k)-\frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{4}}{1+|x|^{2}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq-V(x,k)+K,\quad\forall(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}},

where
K
is a positive constant, and the second and third inequalities above follows from (
6.4
) and (
6.3
), respectively. Thus, Assumption
2.1
is satisfied. The existence and uniqueness of the strong solution to the system (
6.2
) and (
2.2
) then follows.
\qquad\Box

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume that the conditions of Lemma
6.1
hold. In addition, suppose that Assumption
3.1
and (
4.16
) are satisfied and that there exists a positive constant
\lambda_{0}\in(0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}|y|^{2}\leq\langle\sigma(x,k)\sigma^{\top}(x,k)y,y\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}|y|^{2},

(6.7)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
.
Then the Markov process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
is strongly ergodic.

Proof.
In view of Theorem
5.2
, we just need to verify that Assumption
4.2
(i) holds. In other words, we need to show that there exists a positive constant
M
such that (
4.15
) holds for all
(x,y,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0})
be a fixed constant and set

\sigma_{\lambda}(x,k):=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{\lambda,1}(x,k),\dots,\sigma_{\lambda,N}(x,k)),

where
\sigma_{\lambda,i}(x,k)=\sqrt{\sigma_{i}(x,k)^{2}-\lambda}
for
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}
and
i=1,\dots,N
. Thanks to (
6.7
), we have
\sigma_{\lambda,i}(x,k)\geq\sqrt{\lambda_{0}-\lambda}
for all
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}
and
i=1,\dots,N
. Moreover, using (
6.7
) and (
6.5
), we can derive

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\lambda,i}(x,k)-\sigma_{\lambda,i}(y,k)|

\displaystyle=\frac{|\sigma_{i}^{2}(x,k)-\sigma_{i}^{2}(y,k)|}{(\sigma_{i}(x,k)^{2}-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}+(\sigma_{i}(y,k)^{2}-\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(|\sigma_{i}(x,k)|+|\sigma_{i}(y,k)|)|\sigma_{i}(x,k)-\sigma_{i}(y,k)|}{2\sqrt{\lambda_{0}-\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|x-y|}{\sqrt{\lambda_{0}(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)}},\quad\forall(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N},\ k\in{\mathbb{S}},\ i=1,\dots,N.

This, of course, implies that

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\lambda}(x,k)-\sigma_{\lambda}(z,k)|\leq K|x-z|,\qquad\forall(x,z,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}},

(6.8)

where
K
is a positive constant.

We now fix an arbitrary
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
and consider the coupling process
(X^{(k)}(t),Y^{(k)}(t))
starting from
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
and satisfying the following SDEs:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X^{(k)}(t)=b(X^{(k)}(t),k)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{\lambda}(X^{(k)}(t),k)\mathrm{d}W(t)+\sqrt{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}B(t),\\
\mathrm{d}Y^{(k)}(t)=b(Y^{(k)}(t),k)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{\lambda}(Y^{(k)}(t),k)\mathrm{d}W(t)+\sqrt{\lambda}H(X^{(k)}(t),Y^{(k)}(t))\mathrm{d}B(t),\end{cases}

(6.9)

where
W(\cdot)
and
B(\cdot)
are independent standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motions, and

H(x,y)=I-2\frac{(x-y)(x-y)^{\top}}{|x-y|^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x\neq y\}}.

Denote the coupling time by
T^{(k)}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{(k)}(t)=Y^{(k)}(t)\}
. As explained in
Wang, 2023
, the coupling process
(X^{(k)}(t),Y^{(k)}(t))
given in (
6.9
) is well-defined. In addition, we have
X^{(k)}(t)=Y^{(k)}(t)
for all
t\geq T^{(k)}
. Using Lévy’s characterization of Brownian motion and the fact that
\lambda I+\sigma^{2}_{\lambda}(x,k)=\sigma(x,k)\sigma^{\top}(x,k)
, we can verify that the marginal distribution of (
6.9
) agrees with that of the solution of
\mathrm{d}X^{(k)}(t)=b(X^{(k)}(t),k)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(X^{(k)}(t),k)\mathrm{d}W(t)
.
Thus, the process
(X^{(k)}(t),Y^{(k)}(t))
is indeed a coupling of the process
X^{(k)}(t)
.

The operator
\widehat{\Omega}_{k}
associated with the coupling process
(X^{(k)}(t),Y^{(k)}(t))
is given by the following. First, we denote

\displaystyle a(x,y,k):=\begin{pmatrix}a(x,k)&c(x,y,k)\\
c(x,y,k)^{\top}&a(y,k)\end{pmatrix},\text{ and }b(x,y,k):=\begin{pmatrix}b(x,k)\\
b(y,k)\end{pmatrix},

where
c(x,y,k):=\lambda H(x,y)+\sigma_{\lambda}(x,k)\sigma^{\top}_{\lambda}(y,k)
.
One can verify that
a(x,y,k)
is symmetric and positive definite. For any
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we define the operator
\widehat{\Omega}_{k}
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{k}f(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\big(a(x,y,k)D^{2}\phi(x,y)\big)+\langle b(x,y,k),D\phi(x,y)\rangle.

(6.10)

In particular, for any
\phi\in C^{2}([0,\infty))
and all
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
x\neq z
, we have from straightforward calculations that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{k}\phi(|x-z|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\phi^{\prime\prime}(|x-z|)\overline{A}(x,z,k)+\frac{\phi^{\prime}(|x-z|)}{2|x-z|}[\mathrm{tr}A(x,z,k)-\overline{A}(x,z,k)+2B(x,z,k)],

(6.11)

where

\displaystyle A(x,z,k)

\displaystyle:=a(x,k)+a(z,k)-2c(x,z,k),

\displaystyle\overline{A}(x,z,k)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{|x-z|^{2}}\langle x-z,A(x,z,k)(x-z)\rangle,

\displaystyle B(x,z,k)

\displaystyle:=\langle x-z,b(x,k)-b(z,k)\rangle.

Direct calculations show that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}A(x,z,k)=|\sigma_{\lambda}(x,k)-\sigma_{\lambda}(z,k)|^{2}+4\lambda,

(6.12)

and

\displaystyle\overline{A}(x,z,k)=4\lambda+\frac{1}{|x-z|^{2}}|(\sigma_{\lambda}(x,k)-\sigma_{\lambda}(z,k))(x-z)|^{2}

for all
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
x\neq z
and
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
.
Since
\sigma(x,k)\sigma^{\top}(x,k)=\lambda I+\sigma^{2}_{\lambda}(x,k)
, it follows from (
6.7
) that
|\sigma_{\lambda}(x,k)y|^{2}\leq(\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}-\lambda)|y|^{2}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
. This, in turn, implies that
|\sigma_{\lambda}(x,k)|^{2}\leq\theta
for all
(x,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}
, where
\theta
is a positive constant. Then, it follows that for all
x\neq z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
k\in{\mathbb{S}}
, we have

\displaystyle 4\lambda\leq\overline{A}(x,z,k)\leq 4(\lambda+\theta),\quad\forall(x,z,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}\text{ with }x\neq z.

(6.13)

Next we compute

\displaystyle B(x,z,k)

\displaystyle=\langle x-z,b(x,k)-b(z,k)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-z_{i})\big[\alpha(k)(x_{i}-z_{i})-(x_{i}^{3}-z_{i}^{3})-\beta(k)\big((x_{i}-\overline{x})-(z_{i}-\overline{z})\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha(k)(x_{i}-z_{i})^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-z_{i})^{2}(x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}z_{i}+z_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle\quad-\beta(k)\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-z_{i})^{2}+\beta(k)\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-z_{i})(\overline{x}-\overline{z})

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(k)|x-z|^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-z_{i})^{4},

where we used (
6.6
) and the elementary inequality that
x_{i}^{2}+x_{i}z_{i}+z_{i}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}(x_{i}-z_{i})^{2}
to derive the last inequality. Another application of (
6.6
) gives us

\displaystyle B(x,z,k)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(k)|x-z|^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-z_{i})^{4}\leq\alpha(k)|x-z|^{2}-\frac{1}{4N}|x-z|^{4}.

(6.14)

A combination of (
6.8
) and (
6.14
) then leads to

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\lambda}(x,k)-\sigma_{\lambda}(z,k)|^{2}+2B(x,z,k)

\displaystyle\leq\big(K{{}^{2}}+2\alpha(k)\big)|x-z|^{2}-\frac{1}{4N}|x-z|^{4}

(6.15)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa|x-z|^{2}-\frac{1}{4N}|x-z|^{4},

for all
(x,z,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}
,
where
\kappa=K^{2}+2\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{S}}}\alpha(k)
, which is finite thanks to (
6.3
).
From (
6.12
), (
6.13
), and (
6.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{tr}A(x,z,k)-\overline{A}(x,z,k)+2B(x,z,k)}{\overline{A}(x,z,k)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa|x-z|^{2}-\frac{1}{4N}|x-z|^{4}}{\overline{A}(x,z,k)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\kappa}{4\lambda}|x-z|^{2}-\frac{1}{16N(\lambda+\theta)}|x-z|^{4}

\displaystyle=|x-z|g(|x-z|),

(6.16)

where

g(r)=\frac{\kappa}{4\lambda}r-\frac{1}{16N(\lambda+\theta)}r^{3},\quad\text{ for }r\geq 0.

Motivated by

Priola and Wang, 2006
, we now consider the function
G
defined by

\displaystyle G(\rho):=\int_{0}^{\rho}f(s)\mathrm{d}s,\ \ \rho\geq 0,

where

f(s):=\exp\bigg\{-\int_{0}^{s}g(w)\mathrm{d}w\bigg\}\int_{s}^{\infty}\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{v}g(u)\mathrm{d}u\bigg\}\mathrm{d}v,\ \ s\geq 0.

Note that
G(\rho)
is well-defined for each
\rho\geq 0
because

\int_{s}^{\infty}\exp\bigg\{\int_{0}^{v}g(u)du\bigg\}\mathrm{d}v=\int_{s}^{\infty}\exp\bigg\{\frac{\kappa}{8\lambda}v^{2}-\frac{1}{64N(\lambda+\theta)}v^{4}\bigg\}\mathrm{d}v<\infty,

for each
s>0
. Obviously, we have
G(0)=\lim_{\rho\rightarrow 0}G(\rho)=0
. In addition, direct calculations show that the function
G
is twice continuously differentiable with

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(\rho)=f(\rho)\geq 0,\text{ and}\ \ G^{\prime\prime}(\rho)=-1-g(\rho)G^{\prime}(\rho).

(6.17)

Hence
G(\rho)\geq 0
for all
\rho\geq 0
.

We next verify that
G(\infty):=\lim_{\rho\to\infty}G(\rho)<\infty
.
To this end, we consider the auxiliary function

\displaystyle h(s):=\frac{16N(\lambda+\theta)}{s^{3}},\quad s>0.

Obviously, we have

\int_{1}^{\infty}h(s)\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

(6.18)

Thanks to l’Hôpital’s rule and straightforward calculations, we can show that
\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{f(s)}{h(s)}=1
.
Combining this result with (
6.18
) yields
\int_{1}^{\infty}f(s)\mathrm{d}s<\infty
.
Note also that
\int_{0}^{1}f(s)\mathrm{d}s<\infty
. Therefore it follows that

G(\infty)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(s)\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

Consequently,
G
is nonnegative and uniformly bounded.

We now use (
6.11
), (
6.12
), (
6
), and (
6.17
) to compute

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}_{k}G(|x-z|)

\displaystyle=\frac{G^{\prime\prime}(|x-z|)}{2}\overline{A}(x,z,k)+\frac{G^{\prime}(|x-z|)}{2|x-z|}\big[\mathrm{tr}A(x,z,k)-\overline{A}(x,z,k)+2B(x,z,k)\big]

\displaystyle=\Bigg[-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}g(|x-z|)G^{\prime}(|x-z|)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{G^{\prime}(|x-z|)}{2|x-z|}\frac{\mathrm{tr}A(x,z,k)-\bar{A}(x,z,k)+2B(x,z,k)}{\overline{A}(x,z,k)}\Bigg]\overline{A}(x,z,k)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg[-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}g(|x-z|)G^{\prime}(|x-z|)+\frac{G^{\prime}(|x-z|)}{2|x-z|}|x-z|g(|x-z|)\Bigg]\overline{A}(x,z,k)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\overline{A}(x,z,k)}{2}\leq-2\lambda,

(6.19)

for all
(x,z,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times{\mathbb{S}}
with
|x-z|>0
.

Finally, for the coupling process
(X^{(k)}(t),Y^{(k)}(t))
given in (
6.9
) with initial condition
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
x\neq y
, let
\beta_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{(k)}(t)\vee Y^{(k)}(t)\geq n\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For any
t\geq 0
, it follows from Itô’s formula, (
6
), and the optional stopping theorem that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

{}^{(x,y,k)}\big[G(|X^{(k)}(T^{(k)}\wedge\beta_{n}\wedge t)-Y^{(k)}(T^{(k)}\wedge\beta_{n}\wedge t)|)\big]

\displaystyle=G(|x-y|)+\mathbb{E}^{(x,y,k)}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T^{(k)}\wedge\beta_{n}\wedge t}\widehat{\Omega}_{k}G(|X^{(k)}(s)-Y^{(k)}(s)|)\mathrm{d}s\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq G(|x-y|)-2\lambda\mathbb{E}^{(x,y,k)}[T^{(k)}\wedge\beta_{n}\wedge t].

Since
G
is nonnegative and uniformly bounded, we can take the limit as
n\to\infty
and then as
t\to\infty
to obtain

\mathbb{E}^{(x,y,k)}[T^{(k)}]\leq\frac{G(|x-y|)}{2\lambda}\leq K,

where
K
is a positive constant independent of
x,y
, and
k
. This of course implies that Assumption
4.2
(i) and hence the process
(X_{t},\Lambda(t))
is strongly ergodic thanks to Theorem
5.2
. The proof is complete.
\qquad\Box
