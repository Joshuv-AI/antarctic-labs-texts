A bimetallic CuBi catalyst for enhanced electrochemical CO reduction to acetate
Authors: Ruoou Yang, Chengyi Zhang, Jiguang Zhang, Meng Wang, Shuo Chen, Sibo Wang, Bo Wu, Yingxia Zhao, Sung-Fu Hung, Ziyu Mi, Ziyun Wang, Yanwei Lum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Catalyst design principles for generating multicarbon (C2+) products from electrochemical carbon dioxide (CO2) reduction (CO2R) and carbon monoxide (CO) reduction (COR) are often similar and used interchangeably. However, here we demonstrate how a copper bismuth (CuBi) catalyst generates a drastically different product output for CO2R versus COR, offering a valuable case study. During CO2R, we observe predominant formate production, due to selective adsorption of the CO2 reactant onto Bi sites rather than Cu sites. On the other hand, acetate is observed as the primary product for COR because formate production on Bi sites is shut down. Specifically, with the optimal CuBi catalyst, we achieve a high acetate Faradaic efficiency of 68.2% at 100 milliamperes per square centimeter under COR conditions. Experiments involving the coreduction of 13CO2/12CO feeds of varying mixing ratios show that acetate has the highest carbon-12 content among the products. This suggests that CO-selective sites (CuCO) could play a role in facilitating selective acetate production. Notably, analysis of the acetate isotopologues also reveals a preference for the formation of 12CH313COO− over 13CH312COO−. We propose this to be due to an acetate-selective pathway involving asymmetric hydrogenation of *12CO13CO to *12CH213CO (ketene). This pathway is promoted at high pH and is postulated to occur at the interfaces between CuCO and CO2-selective sites (CuCO2). Together, our results provide mechanistic insights into acetate formation under COR conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Electrochemical CO2 reduction (CO2R) is a promising strategy for the sustainable production of value-added chemicals and fuels (1–4). This can help reduce our overreliance on nonrenewable fossil fuels and offers a route for mitigating climate change using renewable energy (5, 6). At present, Cu remains the most promising catalyst for generating value-added products at reasonable current densities and Faradaic efficiencies (FEs) (7, 8). Except for CO2-to-formate, where hydride transfer is a possible key step (9, 10), it is widely accepted that CO2 is first reduced to *CO, which can undergo coupling with another *CO (11). This is then followed by a series of hydrogenation steps that result in the formation of various multicarbon (C2+) products such as ethylene and acetate.

Because *CO serves as the common starting intermediate, the scope of C2+ products that can be generated from electrochemical CO reduction (COR) is known to be very similar to that of CO2R. As a result, many fundamental studies often use COR as a proxy to investigate the mechanisms of CO2R (12). This has the benefit of circumventing issues such as bi(carbonate) salt deposition, which can result in flooding and rapid system failure. In addition, COR can also eliminate the competitive CO2-to-formate and CO2-to-CO pathway, thus allowing studies to focus more on C2+ product formation. There is, however, growing evidence that such an assumption may be too overly simplistic, with certain caveats that should be better understood (13).

For instance, an important work by Gao et al. (14) used isotopic labeling experiments to reveal the presence of distinct active sites on their dendritic Cu catalyst. Specifically, they found that there are at least two types of active sites: one which is selective for CO2-to-CO conversion (CuCO2) and the other favoring CO-to-C2+ product conversion (CuCO). Furthermore, a prior work by Lum et al. (15) proposed that active sites on oxide-derived Cu can be product specific, with different sites using CO2 versus CO at varying rates. Hence, these reports demonstrate that catalytic outcomes for CO2R versus COR can be highly dependent on the active site.

Another notable finding is that CO2R to acetate on Cu-based catalysts is typically challenging, with reported FEs typically below 20% (16, 17). On the other hand, it has been empirically observed that the formation of acetate tends to be favored under COR conditions, especially when highly alkaline electrolytes are used (18). For instance, Dorakhan et al. (19) reported COR to acetate with a high FE of 70% at 200 mA cm−2 in a 1 M KOH electrolyte using a silver–copper oxide catalyst. Jin et al. (20) found that a catalyst consisting of dilute Cu embedded in Ag allowed for a CO-to-acetate FE of 91% at 10 atm in a 5 M KOH electrolyte. Therefore, a cascade strategy involving the initial conversion of CO2-to-CO in one reactor, followed by CO-to-acetate in a second reactor, can be a promising alternative that can circumvent the low acetate selectivity under CO2R conditions (21, 22).

In sum, these studies indicate that there are notable differences between CO2R and COR that should be studied more closely. This is especially so for acetate formation, which appears to be the product most sensitive to this.

The generation of acetate has been proposed to follow a substantially different pathway compared to the other C2+ products such as ethylene. After the initial common *CO-*CO coupling step, successive hydrogenation steps can then lead to the formation of *CCO→*CHCO→*CH2CO (ketene) (23). If this ketene intermediate desorbs from the catalyst surface, chemical reaction with OH− in the electrolyte can occur to form acetate (24). As such, this mechanistic picture provides a plausible explanation on why a more alkaline electrolyte favors acetate formation in COR. On the other hand, a lower alkalinity is expected under CO2R conditions due to neutralization of OH− with CO2, and this has been speculated to be the reason for its lowered acetate selectivity.

Although much progress was achieved in these prior studies, several open questions remain. First, the prior literature attributes the higher acetate selectivity in COR to be entirely due to a higher pH microenvironment (24, 25). However, contradicting this are prior reports where the selectivity toward acetate can be low, even when COR is performed in highly alkaline electrolyte (26–28). On the other hand, it has not yet been considered whether specific active sites could also potentially play a role in promoting COR to acetate. Second, it is not known whether the ketene intermediate exclusively leads to acetate formation or if it can also be further converted to other C2+ products such as ethanol. Third, although Cu bimetallic catalysts have shown improved COR-to-acetate selectivity (29–31), the mechanism for this is not always entirely clear. Last, most prior studies often do not study and compare the CO2R versus COR performance of their catalysts, which can potentially provide valuable insights.

Here, we synthesized a bimetallic CuBi catalyst, which we found to demonstrate high acetate selectivity under COR conditions. In stark contrast, acetate selectivity was poor under CO2R conditions, with formate being the dominant product due to preferential adsorption of CO2 on Bi sites rather than Cu sites. To provide further mechanistic insight, we performed coreduction of 13CO2/12CO with different mixing ratios using both CuBi and a Cu catalyst for comparison. In both cases, we found that acetate primarily originates from 12CO in the reactant feed, although both reactant species should experience similar pH microenvironments. Hence, we postulate that CuCO active sites could also be responsible for the higher acetate selectivity under COR conditions. We also found an increased preference for the formation of 12CH313COO− over 13CH312COO− as the 12CO composition in the feed is increased, which was not observed with ethanol. We propose that this is due to asymmetric hydrogenation of *12CO13CO to *12CH213CO (ketene) at the interfaces between CuCO2 and CuCO active sites. This pathway is promoted at high pH and leads to selective acetate formation. Furthermore, we found this to be occurring to a larger extent on the CuBi bimetallic catalyst, which could explain its higher acetate FE compared to Cu under COR conditions.

RESULTS

We began with density functional theory (DFT) calculations to aid in the identification of a suitable Cu-based bimetallic catalyst that would be selective toward acetate. For this purpose, we screened the CuBi system with varying ratios of each element, considering the acetate pathway: *CCO→*HCCO→*CH2CO (31, 32). As shown in Fig. 1A, increasing the Bi content leads to a higher Bi coordination number around the surface C atom in the *CCO intermediate. This results in a progressive weakening of the interaction between *CCO and the alloy surface. To gain deeper insight into this trend, we calculated the d-band centers of the various CuBi alloys (Fig. 1, B and C). We observed a clear shift of the d-band center away from the Fermi level as the Bi concentration increases (Fig. 1B), indicating a reduced binding strength between the surface and the *CCO intermediate. This weakened interaction is further supported by Bader charge analysis (Fig. 1C and fig. S1), which showed a consistent increase in electron accumulation (more negative charge) on the C atom with increasing Bi content. This suggests that the catalyst surface becomes increasingly less effective at withdrawing electrons from *CCO, further weakening the metal-adsorbate interaction.

We then calculated the activation energy barriers for hydrogenation of *CCO through a two-step proton-electron transfer process on the different CuBi alloys (Fig. 1D and fig. S2). The activation energy barriers first decrease and then increase with rising Bi concentration, achieving a minimum on the Cu3Bi surface. This indicates that an optimal binding strength between *CCO and the surface is essential for efficient hydrogenation. Excessively strong interactions hinder hydrogenation due to the rigid adsorption of *CCO, whereas overly weak interactions result in poor activation of the intermediate. These results highlight the importance of achieving a balanced *CCO adsorption strength to maximize acetate selectivity and activity.

Furthermore, we evaluated the activation energy barriers for hydrogenation of the key intermediates (*CCO and *HCCO) on other p-block element Cu alloys (Cu3Sn and Cu3In). As for the hydrogenation potential barriers of these key reaction steps, a clear trend emerges: Cu > Cu3Sn > Cu3In > Cu3Bi (fig. S3). These results suggest that Bi incorporation provides a more favorable electronic and adsorption environment for stabilizing the relevant intermediates and lowering the hydrogenation barriers, thereby promoting acetate formation more effectively than the other Cu–p-block bimetallic systems examined.

Our DFT analysis predicts that a CuBi catalyst of optimized composition is potentially effective at producing acetate. Hence, a series of CuBi catalysts of varying composition (5, 10, 15, 20, 22, 25, and 30 atomic % Bi) were then synthesized using a simple coprecipitation procedure (see the Materials and Methods section for more details), including single-component Cu and Bi for comparison. The composition of each catalyst was analyzed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (table S1). In this work, we will refer to the catalyst samples as CuBiX, where “X” refers to the Bi composition.

We first performed CO2R bulk electrolysis experiments using the CuBi and Cu catalysts in a 5 M KOH electrolyte, at current densities of 100 to 250 mA cm−2 with product analysis and quantification (see the Materials and Methods section for more details). Although our DFT calculations (Fig. 1D) predicted CuBi to be a promising catalyst for acetate production, the maximum FE was only 7.2% at a current density of 250 mA cm−2 with CuBi22 (Fig. 2A and figs. S4 to S6). Although the acetate FE at 250 mA cm−2 did gradually increase with a higher Bi composition, formate was found to be the dominant product (Fig. 2B and figs. S7 to S9). To better understand this, we performed further DFT calculations (fig. S10), where we found a more favorable CO2 adsorption energy of −0.34 eV on Bi as compared to −0.08 eV on Cu. Hence, this indicates that the CO2 reactant preferentially adsorbs onto Bi sites rather than Cu sites, which explains why CuBi predominantly generates formate.

In situ Raman spectroscopy experiments were then performed on CuBi22 to study the catalyst under CO2R conditions. As shown in fig. S11, we first observed a CO32− peak at 1070 cm−1 under open-circuit potential (OCP) conditions, with CO32− species formed due to the spontaneous reaction between CO2 and the highly alkaline electrolyte (33). As a cathodic potential is applied, we observed peaks related to *CO2− appearing at 1160 and 1540 cm−1 peak that can be assigned to νsCO2− and νasCO2−, respectively (fig. S12) (34–39). On the basis of the prior literature (40), both peaks were proposed to be related to formate production and are consistent with our observed high selectivity of CuBi22 to this product.

On the basis of this result, we took the view that COR conditions could better favor the generation of acetate by turning off the CO2-to-formate pathway. We thus proceeded to perform COR at current densities of 100 to 250 mA cm−2 in a 5 M KOH electrolyte. In alignment with our expectations, a much higher FE to acetate is obtained with a maximum value of 68.2% at 100 mA cm−2 with CuBi22 (Fig. 2C and figs. S13 to S16). Notably, we also find that increasing the Bi composition enhanced the acetate FE, primarily at the expense of the ethylene FE (Fig. 2D and figs. S14 and S15). However, beyond the optimum CuBi22 case, further increasing the Bi composition led to a substantial increase in the FE to H2 and decrease in the FE to acetate (fig. S17). This is likely due to a substantial increase in Bi sites, which are inactive toward the CO reactant (fig. S15F) and hence only perform H2 evolution. Furthermore, we found that the acetate FE for the Cu catalyst is also substantially higher for COR as compared to CO2R (figs. S8A and S14), which is consistent with the prior literature (13).

We also synthesized CuIn and CuSn catalysts to understand if alloying with other p-block metals can enhance the CO-to-acetate FE of Cu. Under COR conditions (fig. S18), we observed that the acetate FE has values of 28.5% for CuSn and 34.2% for CuIn. This is similar to the Cu sample at 27.7% and noticeably lower than CuBi with an acetate FE of 68.2%. These results are consistent with their higher calculated *CCO and *HCCO hydrogenation activation energy barriers (fig. S3). Hence, we find that only alloying with Bi can substantially enhance the acetate FE of Cu.

We then performed COR with CuBi22 at the optimal current density of 100 mA cm−2 over a 15-hour duration. Over this period, we found a minimal decline in performance, with the acetate FE maintaining at >60% (Fig. 2E). During this period, we find minimal leaching (table S2), with the catalyst displaying a consistent composition (table S3) and morphology (figs. S19 to S26) maintained throughout. Furthermore, in comparison with prior literature reports (Fig. 2F), we find that the CuBi22 catalyst exhibits good performance, with a high acetate FE at a less cathodic potential (20, 30, 41–44).

Next, we performed a series of characterizations to better understand the CuBi catalysts. SEM images show that Cu and CuBi22 have a nanosheet-like structure (figs. S27 and S28). From x-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements, the as-synthesized CuBi22 catalyst appears to exist as a mixed oxide (fig. S29), with separate Cu(OH)2 and CuBi2O4 peaks and is consistent with high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) (fig. S28C). As for the different composition CuBi catalysts, we found that they did not exhibit appreciable morphological differences in their as-synthesized state (fig. S30).

We also characterized the structure of CuBi22 and Cu catalysts after COR electrolysis. TEM and HRTEM images show that both catalysts lost their initial nanosheet-like morphology and evolved into agglomerated nanoparticles (Fig. 3A and figs. S31 and S32). We note that Cu2O is observed in HRTEM, which is likely due to oxidation in ambient air during sample preparation. The energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping of the CuBi22 catalyst exhibits that Cu and Bi elements are evenly distributed, consistent with alloy formation after cathodic reduction (Fig. 3B). Atomic-scale imaging of the CuBi22 catalyst after COR was also performed using aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). As shown in fig. S33A, the alternating arrangement of Cu and Bi atoms indicates the formation of Cu-Bi bimetallic sites. Furthermore, the high-resolution EDX mapping images show a uniform distribution of Cu and Bi elements, consistent with alloy formation (fig. S33B).

From x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of CuBi22 before and after reaction, we also confirmed that the Cu(OH)2 or CuBi2O4 phases in the sample are reduced to metallic Cu and Bi under an applied cathodic potential (fig. S34). In addition, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show that the CuBi catalysts with different compositions have similar postreaction morphologies (fig. S35). From in situ angle-dispersive x-ray diffraction (ADXRD) experiments (figs. S36 and S37), we found that all catalysts are reduced to the metallic state under operating conditions. Furthermore, the Cu(111) crystal plane at q = 3.11 Å−1 gradually shifted toward lower q values with increasing Bi content (Fig. 3C). This indicates an increase in the interplanar spacing of Cu(111), which supports the existence of alloying between Cu and Bi.

In situ x-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was also carried out to study changes in the catalyst oxidation state under reaction conditions. At OCP, the CuBi22 catalyst is found to be in an oxidized state as would be expected for Cu(OH)2 (fig. S38). Upon application of a cathodic potential at −0.8 VRHE, the red shift of the Cu K-edge (Fig. 3D) and disappearance of the Cu─O bonding peak in the extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis (Fig. 3E and fig. S39) indicates the reduction of Cu to the metallic state. At −0.8 VRHE, we observe the metal-metal bonding peak is shifted to 2.33 Å as compared to 2.22 Å (without phase shift) with Cu foil, which indicates the presence of CuBi alloying (Fig. 3E). Hence, we assign this peak to Cu─Cu(Bi) bonding, which is consistent with the high wave number shift of the metal coordination in wavelet transform (WT) analysis in Fig. 3F. On the basis of EXAFS fitting (fig. S40 and table S4), the coordination number of metallic Cu─Cu is 9.6 with a bond length of 2.55 Å and Cu─Bi is 2.0 with a bond length of 2.68 Å. As a comparison, the Cu catalyst which also reduced to the metallic state, was found to have a higher coordination number of 11.2 and a bond length of 2.56 Å (see figs. S41 and S42 and table S4).

As for the Bi L3-edge, in situ XAS showed that Bi in CuBi22 was also in an oxidized state under OCP conditions as expected of Bi2O3 (Fig. 3G and fig. S43). At a potential of −0.8 VRHE, the red shift of the Bi L3-edge also indicates reduction to metallic Bi (Fig. 3G). As for EXAFS and WT analysis (Fig. 3, H and I, and fig. S44), we found that the peak at 1.63 Å representing the Bi─O bond disappeared, along with the appearance of peaks at 2.55 and 3.08 Å representing the Bi─Bi(Cu) metal bond. Hence, our characterization efforts show that our catalysts exist in an oxidized state, which reduce to their metallic state under operating conditions and hence can be considered to be “oxide derived” (45, 46).

Next, we sought to assess the reasons for the improved acetate selectivity over the CuBi22 catalyst. To provide a qualitative assessment of the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA), cyclic voltammetry was first carried out in a region where Faradaic reactions do not take place. The results (figs. S45 to S47 and table S5) show that there is no appreciable difference in the double-layer capacitance values of CuBi22 and other Bi ratio catalysts. This indicates that ECSA differences is not the reason for the observed acetate selectivity differences.

We also compared the postelectrolysis structure of the CuBi22 catalyst after COR and CO2R or under an inert Ar environment. SEM and EDX mapping images showed that the catalyst structure is similar after both COR and CO2R conditions, with even distribution of the Cu and Bi elements (figs. S48 and S49). However, the structure of CuBi22 reduced in an inert Ar environment was noticeably different from that under COR or CO2R conditions (fig. S50). On the basis of the prior literature, this is likely because adsorbed *CO and *CO2 species alter surface energies and induce reconstruction (47, 48). Furthermore, according to in situ ADXRD (fig. S51) results, there is no appreciable alloying between Cu and Bi under an inert Ar environment.

To further study the role of CuBi sites in facilitating selective acetate production, we synthesized and tested catalysts with substantially lowered Cu loadings (CuBi91, CuBi93, CuBi95, CuBi97, and CuBi99). With such catalysts, it is likely that any Cu atoms are present as part of a CuBi site rather than clustered together as a Cu site. As shown in fig. S52, the main C2+ product observed in all cases was acetate at current densities of 100 to 250 mA cm−2. Notably, for CuBi99, acetate is the sole COR product across all tested current densities. This strongly indicates that CuBi sites are selective toward CO conversion to acetate.

The common consensus in the literature on why acetate production is favored under COR conditions is due to a higher pH microenvironment in the absence of acidic CO2 molecules (49). However, we note that this explanation may be incomplete because the impact of CO-selective sites (CuCO) was not considered. Hence, we reasoned that the coreduction of 12CO/13CO2 with varying mixing ratios could be used to investigate this hypothesis. Here, the presence of 13CO2 would also reduce the local microenvironment pH, similar to conditions during CO2R. As a result, both 12CO and 13CO2 should, in principle, experience similar local pH microenvironments. However, if acetate contains a higher 12C composition compared to the other C2+ products, this means that 12CO contributes most toward its formation. This would indicate that CuCO sites could also play a role in facilitating acetate formation.

For these isotopic labeling experiments, we used 12CO/13CO2 gas feeds of ratios 3:7, 1:1, 7:3, and 9:1 at a current density of 100 mA cm−2 with both CuBi22 and Cu for comparison, using 5 M KOH as the electrolyte. Analysis of the isotopic composition was then carried out using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (fig. S53). To exclude the influence of 13CO2 versus 12CO2 on our results, we performed 13CO2 reduction with the CuBi22 catalyst at 100 mA cm−2 and compared the results obtained with 12CO2. As shown in fig. S54, we found very similar product FE distributions in both cases, which indicates that there is no appreciable difference between 13CO2 reduction and 12CO2 reduction. We also modeled that the local pH on the catalyst surface changes under conditions of different CO2/CO ratios at 100 mA cm−2 in a 5 M KOH electrolyte (fig. S55). In alignment with expectations, we find that a higher CO2 proportion does lead to a lower local pH at the catalyst surface (fig. S56).

A previous work by Gao et al. (14) analyzed the ethylene and ethanol product isotopologues and examined whether these fit a binomial distribution. Cases that deviate substantially from a binomial distribution were proposed to be evidence for the presence of distinct CuCO and CuCO2 active sites. Similarly, here we define the isotopic fractions p1 = P(12C) and p2 = P(13C), with p1 + p2 = 1. Under the assumption of statistically independent carbon incorporation, the probability of forming a product containing k 13C atoms of a total of n carbons follows a binomial distributionP(k)=(nk)p2kp1k−k

This binomial model represents the hypothesis of random isotope incorporation without positional or mechanistic bias. Therefore, such deviations provide quantitative evidence for mechanistic pathways beyond simple statistical mixing, enabling discrimination between random coupling and pathway-dependent carbon incorporation, which may arise from correlated C─C coupling events, active site–specific pathways, or intermediate-specific pathways. Similarly, here we find that the isotopologue distribution of our ethylene and ethanol products (Fig. 4, A and B; figs. S57 and S58; and tables S6 to S23) also do not adhere well to a binomial distribution, especially for the case with 12CO/13CO2 (3:7) on both Cu and CuBi22. This suggests that CuCO and CuCO2 sites are also present in our catalysts.

Further analyzing the data, we found that the 12C composition of acetate was consistently higher than that of ethylene and ethanol for the different 12CO/13CO2 mixing ratios (Fig. 4, C and D, and figs. S59 to S61). For instance, the 12C composition of acetate was 79.2% as compared to 55.2% for ethylene and 53.0% for ethanol with Cu when 12CO/13CO2 (3:7) was used. Hence, this indicates that acetate predominantly originates from 12CO, even for cases where 13CO2 constitutes the major component of the feed. This result is consistent with our hypothesis, whereby CuCO sites primarily interact with 12CO in the feed and facilitate their conversion to acetate, especially under high pH conditions. This is evident when comparing the acetate production rate (fig. S55), which we observed to drop substantially with a higher 13CO2 composition in the feed. As for the Bi sites in CuBi22, their effect is that they result in an increase in the formate production rate (fig. S55) with a higher proportion of 13CO2 in the feed. On the basis of 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis (fig. S62), the formate generated is composed entirely of the carbon-13 isotopologue, which is consistent with expectations.

In addition, we find that the 12C composition of products generated by the CuBi22 catalyst appears to be higher than that with Cu (Fig. 4, C and D). For instance, using the 12CO/13CO2 (3:7) feed, the 12C composition of CuBi catalyst is 73.1, 70.2, and 86.8% for ethylene, ethanol, and acetate, respectively. This is observed to be higher than that of Cu with values of 55.2, 53.0, and 79.2% for ethylene, ethanol, and acetate, respectively. This suggests that the introduction of Bi results in CuCO sites with higher activity and/or the formation of more CuCO sites as compared to Cu.

We also performed 1H NMR analysis of the acetate and ethanol products (figs. S63 to S69), which allows for better differentiation between isotopologues. For ethanol, we first observed that the ratio between 12CH313CH2OH and 13CH312CH2OH is close to a value of 1 with both Cu and CuBi22 catalysts for all 12CO/13CO2 compositions (Fig. 5A and figs. S63 to S65). This indicates that there is no preference for 13C or 12C to be either in the α carbon or β carbon position. On the other hand, for acetate we unexpectedly found that the ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− increases with a higher proportion of 12CO in the feed (Fig. 5B and figs. S66 to S69). Specifically, on Cu, this ratio was 1.03 with 12CO/13CO2 (3:7) and increases to 1.77 with 12CO/13CO2 (9:1). The prior literature proposed that ketene can lead to the formation of both ethanol and acetate (23). However, our results here indicate that these two products should diverge earlier in the C2 pathway, meaning that ketene is not an intermediate to ethanol.

We then hypothesized two possible reasons for the preferential formation of 12CH313COO−. First, we considered an acetate-selective pathway involving direct C─C coupling of a *12C1 intermediate with *13CO2. However, we ruled this out as a possible explanation because increasing the 13CO2 composition clearly decreases the acetate production rate (fig. S55). Hence, the only possible explanation involves asymmetric hydrogenation, whereby the 12C atom undergoes preferential H addition, leading to *12CH213CO (ketene) and then 12CH313COO− formation.

We next considered whether asymmetric hydrogenation occurs before or after the C─C coupling step and the potential role of CuCO2/CuCO active sites. In the first Scenario A (Fig. 5C), 13CO2 is selectively adsorbed onto CuCO2 sites and converted to *13CO. At the same time, 12CO is preferentially adsorbed onto CuCO sites and converted to *12CH2 or *12CH intermediates. These can then couple with *13CO at the interfaces between CuCO and CuCO2 sites to generate *12CH213CO, followed by 12CH313COO−. For the second Scenario B (Fig. 5D), *12CO first couples with *13CO at the interfaces between CuCO2 and CuCO sites to form the *12CO13CO intermediate. In this case, hydrogenation occurs more readily on the 12C atom, presumably due to it being bound on the CuCO site (strong adsorption) as opposed to the 13C atom on the CuCO2 site (weak adsorption).

A previous work by Li et al. (50) performed electrochemical reduction of CH2I2 and CH3I (alkyl halides) on Cu, which break down on the electrode surface to form *CH2 and *CH3 species, respectively. Electrochemical reduction of these species then resulted in the formation of methane as the dominant major product. Relating this to our results, Scenario A would require *CH2 to be generated at a substantial rate on our catalyst surface, which, in principle, should also lead to considerable methane production. However, the methane FE on our catalysts tends to be low, with a maximum methane FE of only 0.5% at 250 mA cm−2 with Cu under COR conditions (fig. S14A). On this basis, we reasoned that Scenario B, which involves formation of the *12CO13CO dimer, followed by asymmetric hydrogenation to *12CH213CO, is the more likely pathway that promotes selective acetate formation.

Another interesting observation was the continuous increase in the ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− as the 13CO2 composition is lowered (Fig. 5B). We note that this appears to correlate well with an expected higher local pH with a lower 13CO2 composition and a higher acetate production rate (fig. S55). This suggests that the acetate-selective pathway (Scenario B) becomes more pronounced as the pH is increased. When 13CO2 is the major feed component, CuCO2 primarily converts 13CO2 to 13CO, which then migrates to CuCO sites. On these sites, *13CO becomes scrambled with *12CO before further reduction to C2+ products. Under such conditions, the ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− is therefore close to a value of 1.

On the other hand, when 12CO is the major feed component, the local pH can rise to higher values, which promotes the acetate-selective pathway. Furthermore, the CuCO sites should also become increasingly covered with *12CO under these conditions. Hence, 13CO generated from CuCO2 sites become less likely to transfer over to CuCO sites for further conversion. Instead, we propose that *12CO and *13CO can couple at the interfaces between CuCO and CuCO2 sites, with the resulting *12CO13CO undergoing asymmetric hydrogenation to form *12CH213CO and lastly 12CH313COO−.

As for the case with CO as the sole feed component, this is expected to be when the highest local pH and acetate production rate (fig. S55) is attained. Hence, the acetate-selective pathway should be the most active and the coverage of *CO on CuCO sites at its highest. Because of poor interaction of CuCO2 sites with CO and saturation of CuCO sites, we speculate that excess CO from the feed then primarily adsorbs onto the interfaces between CuCO and CuCO2. This is where asymmetric hydrogenation occurs, thus leading to a higher acetate selectivity. We note that our proposed mechanism is consistent with prior studies, which have demonstrated the beneficial effects of a higher CO pressure on acetate production (20, 31).

Next, we also discuss the higher ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− with CuBi22 as compared to Cu. For instance, with 12CO/13CO2 (9:1), a maximum value of 2.32 was attained with CuBi as compared to 1.77 with Cu (Fig. 5B). We note that this could be possibly correlated to the higher acetate FE exhibited by CuBi22. Hence, we propose that CuBi sites could function similarly to CuCO2/CuCO interface sites where the asymmetric hydrogenation pathway is promoted, thereby improving the acetate selectivity. Although the specific mechanism for this is not entirely clear to us, our DFT calculations suggest that Bi alloying can alter the adsorption energies of Cu for *CO2 and *CO (figs. S70 and S71), which could play a role.

A recent work by Wang et al. (51) proposed that the acetate formation pathway on Cu(111) nanoparticles is through the coupling of *CH2 with *CO to form acetate (Scenario A). This was based on the selectivity switching from acetate at high CO partial pressure to methane at low CO partial pressure. Using commercial Cu nanoparticles (Cu NPs), which are Cu(111) dominant as compared to Cu and CuBi22 (fig. S72), we also observed similar results to their work (fig. S73A). This was not the case for Cu and CuBi22, where the methane FE remains low at <1.5% for all CO partial pressures (fig. S73, B and C). Hence, this suggests that the *CH2 coverage on Cu and CuBi22 is inherently low and that the acetate formation mechanism is different for these catalysts. Specifically, the Cu(111)-dominant Cu NPs generate acetate through Scenario A whereas Cu and CuBi22 operate through Scenario B.

To further investigate this, we proceeded to perform coreduction of 13CO2/12CO (9:1 ratio) with the Cu NPs in 5 M KOH at 100 mA cm−2 (fig. S74). Here, we found that the obtained ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− is a value of 1.16, which is close to 1. Hence, these results support that Scenario B for acetate formation does not occur on these Cu NPs. This is likely because Cu(111) surfaces have previously been shown to function as CuCO2 sites (14); hence, the lack of CuCO sites on the Cu NPs means that acetate formation from Scenario B is unable to occur. Furthermore, we found the Cu NPs to primarily produce CO under CO2R reduction, with an FE consistently over 40% (fig. S75). This supports the notion that Cu(111) sites primarily serve to convert CO2 to CO and are less inclined to generate multicarbon products.

We also performed coreduction of 12CO:13CO2 (9:1) with the CuBi22 catalyst at varying electrolyte pH. As shown in fig. S76, we found that the ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− clearly decreases at the lower pH values. For instance, the ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− with the pH 5 electrolyte was only 1.05, which is close to a value of 1. This indicates that acetate is no longer formed from Scenario B and is consistent with the acetate formation rate decreasing with a lower pH (fig. S77). We note that, in a previous work by Gao et al. (14), a preference for 12CH313COO− formation was not observed. On the basis of our results, this is likely because they used a neutral electrolyte, whereas highly alkaline conditions are required to observe this phenomenon. Hence, in sum, we can conclude that both a high electrolyte pH and CuCO2/CuCO mixed active sites are needed to facilitate selective acetate production through Scenario B.

Last, anion-exchange polymer coatings are known to affect the transport of OH− and thus have an impact on the local pH. To investigate their impact on the acetate production of CuBi22, we replaced Nafion with Sustainion and PiperION, which are both anion-exchange polymers. However, neither case yielded a higher acetate rate as compared to Nafion (fig. S78). On the contrary, these alternative coatings appeared to inhibit catalytic activity and result in an increased selectivity toward H2 formation (fig. S79).

DISCUSSION

In this work, we developed a CuBi bimetallic catalyst that accentuates and highlights the important differences between CO2R versus COR. Specifically, this catalyst generates predominantly formate under CO2R conditions, due to preferential adsorption of the CO2 reactant on Bi sites rather than Cu sites. On the other hand, COR conditions turn off the formate pathway and CuBi bimetallic sites selectively converting CO to acetate, which then becomes the main product. Using the optimal CuBi22 catalyst, we achieved a high acetate FE of 68.2% at 100 mA cm−2, with stable operation over a 15-hour period and exhibiting minimal performance decline.

To provide further mechanistic insight, we then performed coreduction of 12CO/13CO2 feeds with varying mixing ratios on both CuBi22 and Cu. Analyzing the products, we first found that acetate has the highest 12C composition and originates mostly from 12CO even for cases where most of the feed is 13CO2. We also observed a preference for 12C to be in the β carbon position, manifesting in a ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− that is greater than 1. This was proposed to be due to an acetate-selective pathway involving asymmetric hydrogenation of the 12CO13CO intermediate to 12CH213CO, which occurs at the interfaces between CuCO2 and CuCO sites. On the other hand, for ethanol, the ratio between 12CH313CH2OH and 13CH312CH2OH was close to a value of 1. This indicates that ethanol does not go through an asymmetric hydrogenation pathway and its formation does not involve ketene as an intermediate.

Furthermore, the ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− was also found to increase with a lower 13CO2 composition in the feed. This was correlated with increasing local pH conditions, which promotes this asymmetric hydrogenation pathway and leads to higher selectivity toward acetate. Last, the higher ratio between 12CH313COO− and 13CH312COO− with CuBi22 was proposed to be due to a higher propensity for asymmetric hydrogenation at CuBi sites, which enhances its acetate FE. Overall, our work provides mechanistic insights into acetate formation under COR conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

Copper nitrate hemi(pentahydrate) (≥99.99%), bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (≥99.0%), ammonium hydroxide solution (∼25% NH3 basis), sodium hydroxide (≥98%), and potassium hydroxide (≥85%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Carbon dioxide (99.9%), carbon monoxide (99.9%), nitrogen (99.99%), argon (99.9995%), and hydrogen gas (99.9995%) were obtained from Air Liquide Singapore Pte. Ltd. Carbon dioxide gas (13C, 99%) (<2% 18O) was purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories. FAA-3-PK-130 anion-exchange membrane and Sigracet 28 BC carbon paper were purchased from Fuel Cell Store. Deionized water (18.2 MΩ) was used for all the experiments.

Synthesis of CuBi catalysts

First, a total of 2 mmol of copper nitrate hemi(pentahydrate) and bismuth nitrate pentahydrate were dissolved in 40 ml of deionized water. The amounts of bismuth nitrate pentahydrate were controlled to be 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.44, 0.5, or 0.6 mmol, depending on the Bi composition desired. After magnetic stirring for 30 min, 40 ml of 0.15 M ammonium hydroxide solution was gradually added dropwise into the above solution. Then, 20 ml of 1.0 M sodium hydroxide was quickly added, which results in the formation of a solid precipitate. The CuBi catalysts were obtained by washing this precipitate three times with water and ethanol, followed by drying at 60°C for 24 hours. These catalysts are termed as CuBiX, where X denotes the Bi composition atomic %. In this work, X can be 5, 10, 15, 20, 22, 25, or 30 and are all synthesized according to this procedure.

Synthesis of the Cu catalyst

The synthesis method is similar to that of CuBi, except that bismuth nitrate pentahydrate was not used.

Synthesis of the Bi catalyst

The synthesis method is similar to that of CuBi, except that copper nitrate hemi(pentahydrate) was not used.

Materials characterization

SEM images and EDX measurements were collected using a JSM-7610FPlus. XRD analysis was carried out using a Bruker D8 Discover 3 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation under 40 kV and 40 mA. The TEM images were obtained using an FEI Tecnai G2 F30. XPS measurements were performed using a Kratos AXIS Supra+.

In situ angle-dispersive x-ray diffraction

These experiments were conducted in a custom-built cell at BL17UM in the Shanghai Synchrotron Facility (SSRF). The electrolyte was 5 M KOH. Before the experiment, calibration was performed by standard sample LaB6 (fig. S36). Catalyst ink (200 μl) was sprayed into the 1-cm2 gas diffusion layer (GDL; Sigracet 28 BC) as the working electrode. Carbon rod and Ag/AgCl were used as the counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. In situ electrochemistry was performed using a CHI 1140C workstation (CH Instruments, Shanghai, China).

In situ Raman spectroscopy

These experiments were collected in a custom-made in situ flow cell system with continuous CO2 flow. A Horiba LabRam Odyssey Nano Raman Spectrometer system equipped with a 633-nm laser source was used for these measurements. The Olympus N2667700 water-immersion objective lens was immersed in a 5 M KOH electrolyte to collect Raman spectra. Before the experiment, calibration was performed using the Raman peak of a Si wafer standard at 520 cm−1. The Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) reference electrode was used for all the experiments.

In situ x-ray absorption spectroscopy

These measurements at the Cu K-edge and Bi L3-edge were collected in a custom-built XAFS flow cell at the BL-01C1 beamline in the fluorescence mode at National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC; Taiwan). The measurements were collected by using a custom-designed flow cell, featuring a gas chamber with an opening sealed by Kapton tape.

Electrochemical measurements

All CO2R and COR bulk electrolysis experiments were conducted in a flow cell with a standard three-electrode system and controlled using an Autolab PGSTAT204 potentiostat system. Twenty milligrams of the catalyst was mixed with 100 μl of Nafion solution (5%) and dispersed into 1900 μl of isopropanol solution, followed by 30 min of ultrasonic treatment to form a homogeneous ink. The catalyst ink was spray coated onto a GDL (Sigracet 28 BC) as the working electrode, Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) electrode as the reference electrode, and Pt mesh as the counter electrode. The electrochemical reduction reaction was performed using 5 M KOH as the electrolyte with a CO2/CO flow rate of 20 ml min−1.

COR/CO2R product analysis

The gas products were quantified using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 8600 GC and Agilent 8860 GC), equipped with a thermal conductivity detector and a flame ionization detector. The quantification of liquid products was carried out using high-performance liquid chromatography (Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC) equipped with a refractive index detector and an ultraviolet detector. The eluent was 1 mmol of H2SO4, and the Aminex HPX-87H column from Bio-Rad was used.

Isotopic labeling studies

The gas-phase products were collected using a gas bag for analysis. Both gas and liquid products were analyzed using a gas chromatography (Agilent 8890A)–mass spectrometry (5977C inert XL) system equipped with a 50-m-long Agilent PoraBOND Q capillary column. Detailed information on our analysis can be found in our previous works (15, 52–55). The isotopic compositions of the liquid-phase products were determined using GC-MS (5977C GC/MSD) and 1H NMR spectroscopy (JEOL JNM-ECA500II 5 FT NMR), respectively. For 1H NMR spectroscopy, samples were prepared by mixing 500 μl of the catholyte containing the liquid products with 5 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and 100 μl D2O. Note that the fraction used for the isotope binomial calculations is based on what was measured from the products using GC-MS and not the gas feed.

Computational details

All DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) (56, 57). The plane-wave basis set was used with a kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV, ensuring convergence of total energy. The exchange-correlation interactions were described using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) (58). All symmetry operations were explicitly turned off to allow full structural relaxation without constraints. For the electronic smearing, Methfessel-Paxton first-order smearing was adopted with a width of 0.2 eV, suitable for metallic systems. The electronic self-consistency loop was considered converged when the total energy change between iterations fell below 10−7 eV, with at least 5 and at most 60 SCF iterations performed per ionic step. The electronic minimization was handled using the fast Davidson iteration scheme, and real-space projection operators were automatically selected to improve computational efficiency. Medium precision was used for the fast Fourier transform (FFT) grid, and Grimme’s DFT-D3 dispersion corrections were included to account for van der Waals interactions (59). The Brillouin zone was sampled using a Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack grid of 3 × 3 × 1, which was found sufficient to converge the total energy for the chosen surface model (60). We modeled the (111) surface of different Cu-Bi alloy using a periodic slab consisting of four atomic layers in a (3×3) surface supercell. The CuxBi models were constructed from the CuBi parent structure (mp-569792) by supercell expansion and controlled Cu/Bi substitution, followed by slab construction along the (101¯1) facet. This procedure ensures that the calculated trends mainly reflect compositional modulation rather than changes in crystal phase or surface orientation. To mimic realistic surface conditions while minimizing computational cost, the bottom two atomic layers were fixed in their bulk positions, and only the top two layers were allowed to relax. This partial relaxation approach is commonly used in surface calculations to reduce spurious slab polarization and simulate the semi-infinite bulk beneath the surface. The vacuum layer was set sufficiently large (typically >15 Å) to prevent spurious interactions between periodic images along the surface. According to our previous research (54), we adopted the LH mechanism for evaluating the activation barrier of the hydrogenation of *CCO and *HCCO.
