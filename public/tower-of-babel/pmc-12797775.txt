Factors influencing career identity in relation to the clinical practice learning environment, satisfaction, and stress among nursing students in the Republic of Korea: a comparative survey
Authors: Se Young Im, Min Ha Park, Hyeon Seok Kang, Sung Min Cho, Hye Won Lim, Myung Kyung Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo identify regional differences in the relationship of the clinical practice-learning environment, satisfaction with clinical practice, and clinical practice stress with career identity among nursing students in the Seoul and Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan regions.MethodsThis comparative study was conducted using an online survey administered to nursing students from the Daegu/Kyungbuk and Seoul metropolitan regions between August 14, 2024, and October 2024.ResultsClinical practice regions showed no significant association with the career identity of nursing students. However, nursing students in the Seoul region reported higher scores in clinical practice learning environment, satisfaction with clinical practice, and career identity, while those in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region reported higher scores in clinical practice stress. Satisfaction with clinical practice hours was identified as a key factor influencing career identity among students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region, while satisfaction with supervision and the clinical practice environment were significant factors for students in the Seoul metropolitan region.ConclusionEfforts are necessary to enhance the clinical practice-learning environment and satisfaction with clinical practice while reducing clinical practice stress in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region by drawing insights from the Seoul region. A comprehensive analysis of supervision and clinical learning systems in the Seoul metropolitan region is necessary to identify and implement improvements in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region. Additionally, a support plan for clinical practice institutions and the development of regionally balanced infrastructure for healthcare education are essential.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Between 2012 and 2016, the overall nurse turnover rate in acute care hospitals in South Korea was 49.7%, significantly higher than that observed in the United States [1]. The study revealed that new nurses with less than one year of experience faced a turnover risk twice as high as those with over five years of experience. Among newly employed nurses, the turnover rates during the first six months and the subsequent six months were 20.1% and 6.3%, respectively [2]. The primary factors contributing to resignation among clinical nurses include “excessive workloads and job maladaptation.” This suggests that newly graduated nurses encounter significant challenges in adapting to clinical environments, leading to their departure from the nursing workforce. These trends highlight the critical need for strategies aimed at retaining skilled nursing professionals.

Professionalism in nursing is a multidimensional concept manifested by the combined knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors that underlie successful clinical practice [3]. This professionalism is dynamically developed through the process of socialization within formal nursing education. The core values of nursing professionalism encompass professional attributes, caring, and altruism. This signifies possessing a systematic body of nursing knowledge, creating a healing and caring environment, and demonstrating kindness, concern, empathy, and dedication to others.

A major factor influencing nursing professionalism is career identity. Career identity represents the degree of self-assurance an individual has about their career path and the alignment of personal goals, interests, personality traits, and abilities [4]. Furthermore, it relates to an individual’s perception of the job’s importance and the social impact of their work, forming the basis for skillful performance and the realization of organizational goals [5]. For nursing students, career identity involves rational decision-making in career selection, seeking career-related information, engaging in self-development, and demonstrating proficiency in acquiring professional nursing knowledge and skills [6].

Developing a strong career identity during university significantly enhances self-confidence and a sense of belonging, ultimately improving job satisfaction and serving as a key motivator for job retention in the nursing profession [7]. Indeed, a positive perception of professionalism directly correlates with enhanced job satisfaction, nursing performance, and organizational commitment, which consequently leads to more efficient patient care [8].

According to The Essentials: Core Compositions for Professional Nursing Education (2021) presented by the AACN, clinical practice experience is crucial for forming a professional identity, enhancing the sense of belonging as a nurse, and integrating previous learning to enable competence in more complex situations [9]. Given that career identity is a critical determinant in the formation of professionalism, strengthening nursing students’ career identity through curriculum design is essential. Therefore, there is a clear necessity to systematically explore the relationship between clinical practice education and career identity and to discuss the policies for clinical practice education that can effectively promote students’ career identity.

Many nursing students indicate that, early in their programs, their intended path is clinical nursing, and their initial choice is often shaped by external influences (e.g., family/role models) and altruistic motives rather than extensive deliberation about specific career trajectories (e.g., Lommi 2024) [10]. In preparing for employment, they typically prioritize recruitment information from medical institutions over actively exploring career options that align with their individual skills and interests [11]. Deficits in self-reflection are associated with lower career certainty (confidence in decision-making) in nursing students [12], resulting in lower job satisfaction and challenges in adapting to nursing duties, ultimately contributing to high turnover rates [13]. Therefore, identifying factors influencing the career identity of nursing students and implementing targeted educational interventions is crucial.

The clinical practice learning environment encompasses factors such as instructor attitudes, guidance quality, and interactions with hospital staff and faculty, all of which significantly influence learning outcomes and student adaptation (e.g. ‘clinical placement experiences can affect learning outcomes, satisfaction, as well as influence their choice of future career.’) [14]. Nursing students often demonstrate a stronger sense of career identity when they express satisfaction with their clinical practice environment [15]. However, when standards in the clinical learning environment are lacking or when instructor guidance is inconsistent, students may experience confusion and frustration, undermining their confidence and increasing stress [16]. Moreover, regional differences in clinical practice environments, such as differences in hospital size and available medical resources, significantly affect the satisfaction levels of nursing students. A comparative study involving 78 nursing students in Seoul, South Korea, and 65 students in Colorado, USA [17] indicates significant differences in their motivation for pursuing nursing, satisfaction with their academic major, and overall clinical practice experience. Korean students are more likely to choose nursing as a career primarily for employment opportunities, while their American counterparts often base their choice on alignment with personal traits. Additionally, American students reported greater satisfaction with their academic major and clinical practice, suggesting that regional factors are significant determinants of clinical practice satisfaction. However, studies exploring regional differences in the career identity of nursing students are limited, highlighting the need for further research to understand the relationship between clinical practice learning environments and career identity.

Clinical practice satisfaction represents the positive emotional experiences nursing students derive from various aspects of their clinical training [18]. Higher levels of clinical practice satisfaction have been associated with a stronger sense of career identity [19]. Considering that current nursing students have a low career identity and make career choices that lack confidence, this can lead to future turnover [20], enhancing satisfaction with clinical practice and fostering career identity may serve as an effective strategy to prevent turnover among newly graduated nurses. Therefore, further research is necessary to explore the relationship between clinical practice satisfaction and career identity, with particular attention to regional differences.

Clinical practice stress refers to the anxiety and apprehension nursing students experience in unfamiliar clinical environments, which can hinder their learning and adaptation [21]. Higher stress levels directly and negatively influence professional identity among nursing students [22]. This stress is often attributed to uncertainty and the challenges of adapting to new environments, particularly as they are not yet fully integrated members of the healthcare team [16]. Therefore, identifying the factors contributing to stress during clinical practice and implementing strategies to mitigate them is essential.

Clinical practice is a critical component in facilitating the learning of nursing students and shaping their career identity [17]. Regional differences in clinical environments and experiences can influence how students perceive their careers and build confidence. For instance, the clinical practice environments in the Seoul metropolitan region and Daegu/Kyungbuk differ based on hospital size, medical infrastructure, and access to educational resources. The Seoul metropolitan region tends to have larger hospitals and more medical resources, offering nursing students opportunities to experience diverse clinical cases and advanced medical technologies. In contrast, nursing students in Daegu/Kyungbuk may have limited access to these resources. Understanding how regional differences in the clinical practice environment affect satisfaction, stress, and career identity is crucial. Meanwhile, the Korea Accreditation Board of Nursing provides a four-year curriculum, and clinical practice begins in the third year. To obtain a nurse’s license in Korea, students are required to complete more than 1,000 hours of practice at a clinical institution such as a hospital, regardless of region. The number of clinical practice hours is consistent across regions, and regional differences related to practice time are considered absent.

Previous studies on factors influencing career identity have primarily focused on nursing students from a single region, limiting the generalizability of the findings necessitating research that expands the sample size and geographic scope [15, 22]. Therefore, this study aims to broaden its focus to include nursing students from the Seoul metropolitan region and Daegu/Kyungbuk, analyzing differences in clinical practice learning environments, satisfaction, and stress while identifying the factors influencing career identity. This study targeted third- and fourth-year nursing students who had already begun clinical practice, and the primary comparison was geographical—between students in the Seoul Capital Area and those in Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan areas.

This study aims to identify the factors influencing career identity among nursing students in the non-Seoul metropolitan (Daegu/Kyungbuk region) and Seoul metropolitan regions. The specific objectives are as follows: 1) to examine the general characteristics of participants based on their clinical practice region. 2) to assess the clinical practice-learning environment, satisfaction with clinical practice, clinical practice stress, and career identity levels of the participants in relation to their clinical practice region. 3) to analyze the influence of the clinical practice-learning environment, satisfaction, and stress on career identity across the two clinical practice regions. The research framework is presented in Fig. 1. Clinical practice learning environment, satisfaction, and stress were modeled as independent variables predicting career identity. Analyses were stratified by region (Seoul vs. Daegu/Kyungbuk) to examine potential contextual differences, and only direct effects were assessed. The findings could contribute to improving the quality of nursing education by addressing regional differences, potentially helping nursing students to develop a positive career identity and enhance their adaptation to nursing roles, which may reduce burnout and turnover among new nurses.Fig. 1Research framework of the study

Methods

Study design

In this study, a comparative survey design was used to identify factors affecting career identity among 3rd-year and 4th-year nursing students in the Seoul metropolitan and Daegu/Kyungbuk regions. The study focuses on general characteristics, the clinical practice learning environments, satisfaction, and stress of the participants.

Setting

Data were collected from 3rd-year and 4th-year nursing students, both current and on leave, at four universities in the Seoul metropolitan region and four universities in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region. Students on leave included those who had completed up to the second semester of the 3rd year or the first semester of the 4th year before taking a leave of absence.

Participants

Third- and fourth-year nursing students with at least one semester of clinical practice experience were recruited from four universities in the Seoul metropolitan region and four in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region. Students from non-nursing majors or non-four-year programs were excluded. Those on leave were included if they had accumulated more than one year of clinical practice experience, which was considered sufficient to provide meaningful responses regarding the clinical practice learning environment, satisfaction, and stress. These cohorts were selected because they are actively engaged in intensive clinical training and are in the process of shaping career identity through direct experiences with practice.

Data collection

Data were collected from August 14 to October 5, 2024, following approval from the Kyungpook National University IRB (IRB No. 2024–0333). Participants included 3rd-year and 4th-year nursing students, both current and on leave, from four universities in the Seoul metropolitan region and four universities in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region, all of whom had completed ≥ 1 semester of clinical practice. Eligible participants were invited through university online communities and social media platforms. A survey invitation with a URL was posted on student online communities and group social media channels. The objectives of the study, methods, duration, and incentives were clearly outlined for potential participants. Only those who voluntarily consented by selecting “Agree” on the consent form could proceed with the survey. Informed consent was obtained electronically, ensuring participant anonymity and data security. All data were stored in password-protected files for 3 years post-study and securely disposed of after that. Anonymity was maintained for all responses, and the survey link was automatically closed once the target sample size was reached. The survey took approximately 10–15 min to complete. The first question screened participants to confirm they were 3rd-year or 4th-year nursing students from the Seoul or Daegu/Kyungbuk regions, with ≥ 1 semester of clinical practice completed.

Measurement

Structured self-reported questionnaires comprising 113 items were used, with all instruments approved by their developers via email. By using these verified tools, it is believed that reliable data can be provided to the international field of nursing education.

Clinical practice learning environment

The clinical practice learning environment, encompassing factors such as the overall atmosphere, attitudes of nursing managers, quality of guidance, and relationships with hospital staff, was evaluated using the Korean validated version of the CLES+T [23]. This instrument was adapted from the original CLES+T scale developed and validated by Saarikoski et al. (2008) [24], and the Korean version has also demonstrated established validity and reliability. The scale comprises seven sub-domains with 33 items, which were grouped into three categories for this study: learning environment, supervision, and clinical practice faculty. The sub-factors of the clinical practice learning environment tool are learning environment, supervision, and clinical profiler, and all of the above factors are independent factors of the actually applied tool. Each item on the scale is rated using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5), with higher scores indicating a more positive perception of the learning environment. The reliability of the tool in this study was Cronbach’s α = 0.95, and the Cronbach’s α reported in the original study was 0.94.

Clinical practice satisfaction

Clinical practice satisfaction, defined as positive emotional responses to various aspects of clinical practice [18], was measured using a scale adapted by Lee et al. (2004) [25] from the original tools developed by Cho and Kang (1984) [26] and Lee (2001) [27]. This 31-item scale includes six sub-domains and uses a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher satisfaction. The sub-factors of the clinical practice satisfaction tool are satisfaction with the training environment, satisfaction with the training time, satisfaction with the training evaluation, satisfaction with the training subject, satisfaction with the training content, and the above factors are all independent factors of the actually applied tool. The reliability of the tool in this study was Cronbach’s α = 0.94, and the Cronbach’s α reported in the original study was 0.86.

Clinical practice stress

Clinical practice stress refers to the stress nursing students experience due to negative factors related to clinical practice, which can disrupt their clinical training experience [21]. Clinical practice stress, referring to the negative aspects encountered during clinical practice, was measured using the Korean version of the Beck and Srivastava (1991) scale [28]. Beck and Srivastava (1991) developed this instrument by combining items from the previously validated General Health Questionnaire (GHQ) and the Stress Inventory. In the present study, the scale was translated into Korean and back-translated to ensure accuracy before use. The scale includes five sub-domains with 24 items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater stress. The sub-factors of the clinical practice stress tool are the stress about practice environment, stress about undesirable role models, stress about practical work burden, stress about interpersonal conflict, and stress about a parent, all of which are independent factors of the actually applied tool. The reliability of the stress in this study was Cronbach’s α = 0.79, and the Cronbach’s α reported in the original study was 0.91.

Career identity

Career identity, defined as confidence in career decisions, was measured using the version of the validated My Vocational Situation scale (Holland et al., 1980) [29] that had been previously adapted for nursing students in another study [5]. This scale comprises 14 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating a stronger career identity. The reliability of the tool in this study was Cronbach’s α = 0.87, and the Cronbach’s α reported in the original study was also 0.87.

Study size

The minimum required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7, with a moderate effect size of 0.15, a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80. This calculation yielded a target sample size of 139 participants for multiple regression analysis based on 15 predictor variables. To compensate for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, the target sample size was adjusted to 155 participants. This resulted in 140 valid responses, meeting the minimum sample size requirement.

The online survey required participants to select one of four to five options for each item and could not be submitted if any responses were missing or invalid. This process eliminated missing data and errors, resulting in a complete dataset.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2024–0333). Participants were informed about the confidentiality of their data, its secure storage, and the destruction of identifiable information post-reward distribution. They were assured that participation was voluntary, and they could withdraw at any time without penalty. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the participants in the study. Additionally, participants experiencing distress during the study were offered access to counseling services and other necessary support at no cost.

Statistical methods

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level of 0.05. Independent t-tests and chi-square tests were used to compare general characteristics and clinical practice factors (learning environment, satisfaction, stress, and career identity) by region. Multiple regression analysis was conducted to identify the factors influencing career identity by region, adjusting for variables such as age, grade, religion, learning environment, supervision, and satisfaction in areas including the practice environment, clinical practice hours, practice evaluation, practice subject, practice content, and practice teaching, along with stress related to practical work burden and the method of supervision.

Results

General characteristics of participants by clinical practice region

This study included 140 participants, with 72 from the Daegu/Kyungbuk region and 68 from the Seoul metropolitan area. The questionnaire comprised 113 items, all derived from validated instruments assessing the clinical learning environment, satisfaction, stress, and career identity. Despite its length, the survey was designed to take 10–15 minutes, and participants were informed of this in advance, which helped maintain engagement. No incomplete responses were recorded, and all cases were included in the final analysis. While the questionnaire length is unlikely to have biased the results, future studies may consider more concise instruments to further reduce participant burden.

Significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, academic year, and religious affiliation. The average age of nursing students was 22.2 years in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region and 23.4 years in the Seoul metropolitan region, showing a statistically significant difference (p = 0.001). Sex distribution was comparable between the groups, with females representing 93.1% in Daegu/Kyungbuk and 83.8% in Seoul (p = 0.086). For the academic year, third-year students constituted the majority in Daegu/Kyungbuk (58.3%), while fourth-year students were more prevalent in Seoul (61.8%; p = 0.017). Differences in the clinical practice learning environment, satisfaction, stress, and career identity by region

Students from the Seoul metropolitan region reported significantly higher mean scores for the clinical practice learning environment (M = 3.4) than those from the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region (M = 3.0, p < 0.001), with significant differences observed in the subcategories of the learning environment (p < 0.001) and supervision (p < 0.001). Similarly, clinical practice satisfaction was significantly higher among students in the Seoul metropolitan region (M = 3.7) than those in the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region (M = 3.2, p < 0.001), with significant differences observed across all subdomains, including practice environment, time, evaluation, curriculum, content, and guidance. While clinical practice stress was marginally higher in students from the Seoul metropolitan region (M = 3.5) than those from the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region (M = 3.4), this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.227). However, stress related to workload was significantly higher in students from the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region (p = 0.012). Significant differences were observed in the subscale measuring the presence of supervision between the two groups (p < 0.001). In the Daegu/Kyungbuk group, 15 students (20.8%) reported “No supervision at all,” compared to only 1 student (1.5%) in the Seoul metropolitan group. Factors influencing career identity by clinical practice region

The multiple regression analyses identified satisfaction with clinical practice hours as a significant predictor of career identity in the Daegu/Kyungbuk group (β = 0.27, p < 0.001). Discussion

This study aims to identify differences in the effects of clinical practice learning environment, clinical practice satisfaction, and clinical practice stress on career identity between nursing students in the Seoul metropolitan region and those in the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region. The findings showed that regional differences in the clinical practice learning environment, particularly in the presence of supervision, with Daegu/Kyungbuk students more often reporting no supervision compared to their Seoul counterparts. Furthermore, factors influencing career identity varied by region: satisfaction with clinical practice hours was significant in the Daegu/Kyungbuk group, whereas supervision and satisfaction with the practice environment were key predictors in the Seoul group.

The mean score for the clinical practice learning environment was significantly lower in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region than in the Seoul metropolitan region, particularly in the subdomains of the learning environment and supervision. This finding suggests that nursing students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region may perceive hospital wards as less supportive learning environments and report a lack of mentorship to aid their learning. In contrast, students in the Seoul metropolitan region experienced more diverse clinical cases and more exposure to advanced medical technology and perceived the ward as a more conducive learning environment, with supervisors providing more individualized instruction. In particular, for nursing students, field leaders serve as important role models who demonstrate professional practice and provide opportunities for active participation in nursing care [30]. When guidance is limited or varies in consistency, students may feel that their learning opportunities are somewhat reduced, which can influence their sense of confidence and satisfaction with the clinical experience. These findings suggest that regional differences may reflect variations in supervisory support. Strengthening support systems for field leaders can help them provide effective and consistent guidance, thereby enhancing students’ learning experiences and fostering a greater sense of engagement and satisfaction during clinical practice.

The clinical learning environment is essential for nursing students to acquire skills, gain knowledge, and build confidence in performing clinical tasks [15]. Therefore, ensuring quality nursing education across regions requires analyzing the supervision and clinical learning systems in the Seoul metropolitan region and identifying areas for improvements in the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region to foster positive perceptions of clinical learning environments.

Clinical practice satisfaction was lower in the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region than in the Seoul metropolitan region. Significant differences favoring the Seoul metropolitan region were observed across all subscales: practice environment, clinical practice hours, practice evaluation, practice curriculum, practice content, and practice guidance. The lower clinical practice satisfaction reported in the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region than that of the Seoul metropolitan area may be due to disparities in resources, facility quality, and educational support. Seoul, as a major metropolitan area, typically offers better-equipped institutions, more comprehensive practice environments, and a higher concentration of experienced clinical instructors, all of which can enhance student learning and satisfaction. Addressing these discrepancies requires increased investment in clinical facilities, improvements in the quality of practice environments, and consistent adherence to practice standards by the institutions in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region. A previous study indicates that higher levels of clinical practice satisfaction are positively correlated with stronger career identity [19]. The finding that nursing students in the Seoul metropolitan region reported higher mean career identity scores than those in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region further supports the relationship between clinical practice satisfaction and career identity.

In this study, significantly higher clinical practice stress was identified among nursing students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region than those of students in the Seoul metropolitan region, particularly regarding practical work burden. Although total clinical practice stress did not differ significantly between regions, students from the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan area reported higher workload-related stress. This finding is plausible because overall scores combine multiple subdomains, and differences in one area may be masked when averaged with others. Established instruments recognize workload as an independent subdomain, supporting the view that domain-specific differences can emerge despite nonsignificant total scores [31]. Prior studies also highlight workload and time constraints as distinct stressors for nursing students, underscoring the importance of examining subdomain-level effects rather than relying solely on composite indices [32]. Given that clinical stress influences competence and professional development, identifying which subdomains drive between-group variation is essential [33]. Moreover, stress profiles may vary by year of study, suggesting that recency and intensity of clinical exposure can differentially shape subdomain scores [34].

This finding suggests that nursing students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region experience greater challenges with practice-related tasks than those in the Seoul metropolitan region. Therefore, instructors should consider regional differences in clinical practice education environments when developing strategies to reduce the workload burden on nursing students.

In the univariate analysis, career identity scores were significantly higher among nursing students in the Seoul metropolitan region than those in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region. However, the multivariate analysis revealed no significant relationship between region and career identity. The absence of a significant regional difference in career identity in the multivariate analysis suggests that factors other than regional background may have a more direct influence on the career identity of nursing students. This finding suggests that the quality of clinical practice education may be more significant in shaping career identity than regional differences alone. Positive, well-supported clinical environments facilitate the development of a stronger professional identity by promoting hands-on learning, professional values, and socialization. Studies indicate that when students benefit from high-quality mentorship, constructive feedback, and a supportive clinical environment, their career identity and professional commitment are significantly enhanced, irrespective of geographic location [35].

The differences in factors correlated with career identity between nursing students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk and Seoul metropolitan regions were due to “satisfaction with the practice environment” and “supervision.” Supervision and a supportive practice environment significantly contribute to professional socialization and self-efficacy, both of which are essential for developing a stable career identity among nursing students [36]. Studies indicate that satisfaction with the practice environment—including resources, support, and a structured learning environment—directly affects the confidence and career commitment of the students. Positive practice settings are associated with higher satisfaction, enhancing the preparedness and alignment of the students with their career goals [14]. Effective supervision offers students guidance and feedback, enabling them to navigate clinical complexities more effectively and develop a clearer sense of their professional identity. Regular and supportive interactions with mentors or supervisors facilitate the integration of students into the healthcare environment, reinforcing their commitment to the profession [37]. This finding highlights the necessity for structured support and guidance within clinical practice institutions, enabling nursing students to perceive the environment as an enriching learning space.

In this study, satisfaction with clinical practice hours was identified as a significant factor influencing career identity for students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region, while satisfaction with supervision and the clinical practice environment remained key factors for those in the Seoul metropolitan region. The differences in factors influencing career identity between nursing students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk and Seoul metropolitan regions may be due to variations in clinical training environments, available resources, and structural support across these regions. In regions such as Seoul, clinical settings are generally larger and more specialized, offering students a structured environment where regular supervision is more feasible. This makes supervision and the overall practice environment critical for career identity formation, as students often receive consistent support and feedback, facilitating the development of their professional competencies and a sense of belonging in the field [14]. In urban centers such as Seoul, supervision plays a more prominent role due to a potentially higher staff-to-student ratio, facilitating more personalized and continuous mentoring. Supervision helps students build confidence and clarity regarding their professional identity by modeling professional behaviors and offering structured guidance, which is essential in high-pressure clinical environments. Consequently, supervised experiences have a more significant influence on the career perceptions of students.

In interpreting these findings, “region” itself is unlikely to be a causal factor, but rather reflects differences in clinical training conditions, particularly supervision systems. In our data, students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region more often reported receiving no or inconsistent supervision, whereas students in Seoul experienced more structured and designated supervision. These conditions may explain why satisfaction and supervision were more strongly linked to career identity in Seoul, while in Daegu/Kyungbuk, the quantity of practice hours appeared to play a more central role. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence that both supervisory support and the extent of clinical exposure contribute to professional identity formation among nursing students [14, 38].

In contrast, in the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region, resources such as the time allocated for hands-on practice may vary, with fewer facilities or supervisors available per student. In this context, satisfaction with clinical practice hours directly influences the perception of students regarding their learning experience. Limited practice time may restrict learning opportunities, leading to students placing greater value on having sufficient practice hours to feel adequately prepared for their careers [37]. In regions with limited hospital placements, students may prioritize the total hours allocated for clinical practice to strengthen their skills and competencies. For students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk metropolitan region, satisfaction with clinical hours may serve as a primary factor influencing career identity, as limited opportunities could lead them to perceive every hour of practical experience as crucial for skill development and career preparedness [39].

Domain 9 of the AACN Essentials emphasizes the formation of professional nursing identity, underscoring clinical practice experience and the quality of supervision as key drivers in shaping students’ professional roles [7]. The present findings align with this framework, as students in the Seoul metropolitan region—who reported higher satisfaction with the clinical practice environment and more consistent supervision—demonstrated stronger career identity compared to their peers in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region. Similar patterns have been reported internationally; for example, a study in China found that a supportive clinical practice environment enhances professional identity, while role stress undermines it [39].

Importantly, these regional differences within a single country should not be understood merely as local variations but as reflections of broader issues of equity in nursing education. Disparities in supervision and practice environments highlight the need for policies that ensure consistent, high-quality clinical learning experiences regardless of geographic location. This resonates with global discussions on equity and quality in nursing education, as competency-based curricula increasingly emphasize fairness and consistency across diverse settings.

Professional identity has long been recognized as a cornerstone of nursing practice worldwide, rooted in traditions dating back to Florence Nightingale, and remains central across all health systems. Thus, situating the Korean findings in this global context underscores their broader significance: closing qualitative gaps in clinical education—whether across regions within a country or between nations—represents a critical step toward cultivating a globally competent nursing workforce with a strong and unified professional identity.

While our regression models controlled for demographic variables including grade level, gender, socioeconomic background, and prior work experience, recent research suggests these factors may also operate via more proximal psychological mechanisms. For instance, Tu et al. (2024) found that nursing interns’ perceived stress negatively correlated with professional identity, and that coping styles mediated this relationship [40]. Moreover, Zhao et al. (2024) showed that career adaptability and commitment are longitudinal predictors of career identity among nursing students [41]. These findings underscore that even after statistical control, demographic and personal variables may interact with psychological and contextual processes in shaping satisfaction, stress, and professional identity. We therefore suggest that future research should examine mechanistic pathways (e.g., mediation, moderation) rather than treat these variables merely as covariates.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, only the direct effects of each variable on career identity were analyzed, without considering potential mediating or indirect pathways (e.g., clinical learning stress influencing career identity through satisfaction). Future studies should employ more comprehensive models, such as structural equation modeling, to examine indirect effects. Second, although regional differences were of interest, the analysis did not employ a multilevel modeling approach that accounts for the hierarchical structure of students nested within regions or institutions. Future research should consider multilevel analysis to better capture the influence of higher-level contextual factors. Third, the study design limits the generalizability of the findings. Data were collected via an online survey from a convenience sample of 140 third- and fourth-year students at eight universities in Seoul and the Daegu/Kyungbuk regions. Although participants included both enrolled and on-leave students, the sample does not represent all nursing students in South Korea. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future research should include larger, more diverse samples across multiple regions and consider potential differences by academic year and the elapsed time since the last clinical practice. In addition, as third-year and fourth-year students were analyzed together, future studies should examine the effects of the academic year on the independent variables. Consequently, expanding the scope of study participants in future studies is recommended. Finally, the cross-sectional and self-report design restricts causal inference and may be subject to response bias. Despite these limitations, the use of validated and reliable instruments in this study strengthens the credibility of the findings. By employing scales that have been previously tested for validity and reliability, the results can be more confidently interpreted and compared with international research in nursing education. Emphasizing this point highlights that the present study not only contributes to understanding career identity in Korean nursing students but also provides evidence that is relevant and comparable in a broader, global context.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that satisfaction with clinical practice hours is a significant predictor of career identity among nursing students in the Daegu/Kyungbuk region. In contrast, for nursing students in the Seoul metropolitan region, both supervision and satisfaction with the practice environment are significant factors influencing career identity. A structured support plan for clinical practice institutions should be implemented to address disparities in supervision and educational quality between clinical practice instructors in metropolitan and regional areas, thereby enhancing the development of career identity development of the students. Additionally, healthcare education infrastructure evolves in a regionally equitable manner.

In Korea, where healthcare infrastructure is concentrated in metropolitan areas, students in these regions have greater access to high-quality medical services, severely ill patients, advanced equipment, and skilled staff compared to those in provincial areas [42]. Such disparities contribute to unequal clinical practice environments and, consequently, unequal learning opportunities among nursing students. These findings parallel inequities in clinical education resources reported in other countries, highlighting the need for policies that ensure equitable supervision and clinical training. Strengthening the quality of clinical education across regions is essential not only for fostering consistent career identity among nursing students in Korea but also for addressing global challenges in producing a well-prepared nursing workforce.
