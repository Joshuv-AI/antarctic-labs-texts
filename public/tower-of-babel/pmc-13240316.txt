Targeting ParPMC Susceptibility Hubs in Prunus: eEF1A as a Conserved Mediator of Plum Pox Virus Infection in Nicotiana benthamiana
Authors: Ángela Polo‐Oltra, Alba Lloret, Jesús A Sánchez‐Navarro, Gabino Ríos, Carlos Romero, Elena Zuriaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSharka, caused by Potyvirus plumpoxi (plum pox virus, PPV), is the most destructive viral disease affecting Prunus species worldwide. Two MATH domain‐containing genes, ParPMC1 and ParPMC2, have been identified in apricot as host susceptibility factors required for PPV infection, but their molecular function remains unclear. Here, we investigated ParPMC functions by identifying their interacting partners and assessing their contribution to viral replication and movement. Yeast two‐hybrid screening of PPV‐infected apricot tissues revealed 13 ParPMC interactors involved in defence and stress responses, primary metabolism, protein folding and gene expression. Among them, two eukaryotic elongation factor 1‐α proteins (eEF1A; Pruar1 and Pruar9) were selected for further analysis. In planta assays showed that both ParPMC proteins colocalize with eEF1A at endoplasmic reticulum‐derived perinuclear structures associated with viral replication complexes; however, a specific physical interaction was detected only for ParPMC1, suggesting paralog specialisation. Phylogenetic analyses revealed independent diversification of eEF1A gene families in Rosaceae and Solanaceae. Despite the lack of strict orthology, silencing clade B eEF1A isoforms in Nicotiana benthamiana using a cucumber mosaic virus‐based virus‐induced gene silencing system strongly reduced PPV accumulation and systemic spread, highlighting a conserved proviral role. These results support a model in which ParPMC1 facilitates PPV infection by recruiting or stabilising eEF1A at viral replication sites, whereas ParPMC2 may contribute through alternative interactors. This study advances our understanding of ParPMC‐mediated PPV‐susceptibility in apricot and highlights host–virus interactions with potential relevance for resistance strategies in Prunus and other potyvirus‐susceptible crops.Keywords: apricot, CMV, MATHd, PPV, PPVres locus, resistance, VIGS

Introduction

Potyvirus plumpoxi (plum pox virus, PPV), which causes sharka disease, represents the most destructive viral threat to Prunus cultivation worldwide, with severe economic impact (García et al. 2014; Cambra et al. 2024). Sharka was first reported in Bulgaria in 1917–1918 on European plums ( Prunus domestica ) (Atanassov 1932) and later in 1933 on apricot ( Prunus armeniaca ) (Atanassov 1935). Its emergence and unstoppable spread across the world prompted major international efforts to identify both resistance sources and, ultimately, the underlying genes, with the aim of supporting stone fruit breeding programmes (García et al. 2025; Polo‐Oltra et al. 2020). In apricot, genetic studies of PPV resistance identified the paralogous genes ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 as host susceptibility factors required for PPV infection, both located within the major dominant PPVres locus (Zuriaga et al. 2013, 2018). In PPV‐resistant apricot cultivars, both genes are downregulated and carry allelic sequence variants in coupling with the PPV resistance haplotype (Zuriaga et al. 2018). Recently, Polo‐Oltra et al. (2025) demonstrated that silencing the Nicotiana benthamiana ParPMC ortholog (NbPMC) through tobacco rattle virus (TRV)‐based virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) significantly reduced PPV infection. Moreover, these genes were found to be primarily expressed in vascular bundle‐rich tissues, suggesting a potential role in the long‐distance movement of the virus.

Functional and interaction‐based approaches have been instrumental in identifying host factors involved in potyvirus infection, as reviewed by Pollari et al. (2024). In Prunus, ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 are a key interface between host–virus interaction studies and resistance genetics and offer a genetically grounded framework to investigate how proviral host factors contribute to PPV infection. ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 belong to the MATHd‐only protein family, each containing two MATH domains. This domain, also known as TRAF (TNF‐Receptor Associated Factor), consists of a fold of seven antiparallel β‐helices that mediates protein–protein interactions (Zapata et al. 2007). MATHd‐only proteins are generally described as adaptors involved in signal transduction across diverse physiological processes (Zapata et al. 2007; Qi et al. 2022), although the functions of many family members remain unknown. In this context, characterising the protein–protein interactions of ParPMC proteins could provide key insights into their biological functions.

Despite extensive advances in understanding the potyviral life cycle, the view of the infection process remains fragmented, with individual steps described in detail but the overall picture still unclear (Rodamilans et al. 2020). Recently, the limited set of virus and host factors characterised for PPV (García et al. 2025), as well as those reported more broadly for potyviruses (Pollari et al. 2024), have been comprehensively reviewed. Viral proteins have been studied extensively; however, their multiple functions often complicate their characterisation, whereas host factors remain comparatively underexplored. Among host susceptibility factors, several components of the translation initiation and elongation apparatus—including eIF4E, eIF(iso)4E, eIF4G, eIF(iso)4G and multiple eIF3 subunits—have been repeatedly identified as essential for potyvirus infection across both model and crop species including Prunus spp. (Wang et al. 2013; Zlobin and Taranov 2023). In addition to these canonical initiation factors, the eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1‐α (eEF1A) has emerged as a moonlighting host protein frequently co‐opted by positive‐strand RNA viruses (Sasikumar et al. 2012; Li et al. 2013). Beyond its central role in ribosomal elongation, eEF1A participates in the assembly and stabilisation of viral replication complexes, interacts with viral RNA‐dependent RNA polymerases (RdRp) and viral protein genome‐linked (VPg) proteins, and associates with endoplasmic reticulum‐derived membranous structures where potyvirus replication takes place. Despite this growing body of evidence, the contribution of eEF1A to PPV infection remains unexplored.

Given that ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 represent the first host susceptibility genes genetically mapped for PPV resistance in apricot, we hypothesised that ParPMC proteins function as susceptibility hubs exploited by viral or host proviral factors. Characterising the ParPMC interactome therefore offers a rational approach to link genetic resistance at the PPVres locus with the molecular mechanisms underlying PPV infection. In this study, we employed yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screening to identify ParPMC interactors and gain mechanistic insights into their roles during PPV infection. Among the interactors identified, we focused on two eEF1A isoforms, central hubs coordinating a broad spectrum of cellular and viral processes. We further validated these interactions using bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) analysis, assessed their subcellular co‐localization with ParPMC proteins and PPV, and employed VIGS in N. benthamiana to evaluate their contribution to viral infection. Taken together, our findings reveal novel potential determinants of PPV susceptibility in apricot and highlight promising targets for resistance breeding and biotechnological interventions in Prunus species and other potyvirus‐susceptible crops.

Results

Identification of ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 Interactors by Y2H

This study was specifically designed to identify host proteins that functionally complement or interact with ParPMC1 and ParPMC2, the first genetically mapped susceptibility genes for PPV resistance in apricot, which likely act as central hubs exploited by viral or host proviral factors. To explore this, the coding sequences of ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 were used to screen for potential interacting partners using a cDNA library generated from PPV‐infected apricot leaves. Autoactivation assays confirmed that neither pGBKT7‐ParPMC1 nor pGBKT7‐ParPMC2 exhibited autoactivation (Figure 1). A total of 313 positive colonies were initially selected, and their restriction digestion patterns were analysed. From these, 192 unique clones were identified, amplified and sequenced, revealing 67 distinct genes including a single clone whose sequence was identical to two peach genes (Prupe.7G051200 and Prupe.7G051500) (Table S1). According to the functional annotation available in the Phytozome v12.1.6 database (Berkeley, CA, USA), the identified genes were associated with a wide range of cellular processes, including gene expression, protein metabolism, stress and defence responses, signalling and transport, and plastid‐related functions, as well as several proteins of unknown or hypothetical function (Table S1). To select genes for further analysis, we integrated functional annotation with literature evidence indicating potential roles in PPV susceptibility or defence, leading to a set of 28 prioritised candidates. To ensure clarity, Table S1 presents the full dataset including both the prioritised candidates and the genes that were not selected for further study. Then, Y2HGold yeast cells were transformed with pGBKT7‐ParPMC1, pGBKT7‐ParPMC2, or the empty pGBKT7 vector, each combined individually with one of the 27 plasmids representing the 28 candidate genes. Following the screening, 12 positive clones were able to grow on QDO/X/A medium: eight interacting with both ParPMC1 and ParPMC2, and four interacting exclusively with ParPMC2 (Figure 1). Finally, the 13 identified genes were named Pruar1–Pruar13 (Table 1). According to Phytozome annotation, these genes are associated with diverse cellular functions including defence and stress responses (i.e., thaumatin‐like protein, glutathione S‐transferase and 18.0 kDa class II heat shock protein‐related), protein folding and degradation (i.e., chaperonin 60 subunit β1, peptidyl‐prolyl cis‐trans isomerase cyp19‐4 and ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase activase), gene expression and translation (i.e., elongation factor 1‐α, transcription initiation factor TFIID subunit 4 and ATP‐binding cassette sub‐family E member 1), and metabolic processes (i.e., carbonic anhydrase 1 and aldehyde dehydrogenase family 7 member A1).

BiFC Results Confirm Interaction Between eEF1A and ParPMC1

Two eEF1A isoforms (Pruar1 and Pruar9) identified in the Y2H screen (Table 1) were selected for in planta BiFC validation, given their known role as multifunctional regulators in both cellular and viral processes (Sasikumar et al. 2012; Li et al. 2013). Although distinct eEF1A isoforms have been reported to interact with viral proteins from tomato bushy stunt virus (TBSV), tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), turnip yellow mosaic virus (TYMV) and cucumber mosaic virus (CMV) (Li et al. 2013), their potential involvement in PPV infection has not previously been investigated. These properties make eEF1A particularly informative for assessing functional interactions with ParPMC1, potentially shedding light on PMC‐mediated susceptibility and complementing activity. Other candidates with comparable Y2H profiles were not tested in this initial round, as we prioritised a manageable subset; validation of additional interactors is currently ongoing.

Pruar1 and Pruar9 are the apricot orthologs of the peach genes Prupe.1G010400 and Prupe.6G095700, respectively. All eight possible N‐ and C‐terminal YFP fusion combinations between ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 (bait) and Pruar1 and Pruar9 (prey) were tested for fluorescence complementation using BiFC and laser scanning confocal microscopy (Figure S1A) including appropriate negative controls (Figure S1B). Two days post‐infiltration, reconstitution of the YFP fluorescent signal was observed in the ParPMC1 combinations (Figure 2), confirming that ParPMC1 interacts with Pruar1 and Pruar9. The YFP fluorescent signal was observed when ParPMC1 was fused to either NYFP or CYFP at the N‐terminus, independently of the NYFP or CYFP position in the Pruar constructs. In contrast, no fluorescence was detected when ParPMC1 was fused to NYFP or CYFP at the C‐terminus, suggesting that the fusion orientation affects interaction detection. Interestingly, interactions involving CYFP‐ParPMC1 localised to cytoplasmic vesicles (Figure 2a,b,e,f), whereas NYFP‐ParPMC1 localised to the plasma membrane, likely at plasmodesmata (Figure 2c,d,g,h).

Bimolecular flourescence complementation visualisation of ParPMC1 interactions with Pruar1 (left) and Pruar9 (right). Protein fusions to NYFP or CYFP at N‐ or C‐termini are indicated. Representative confocal images are shown; scale bar = 20 μm.

Notably, ParPMC2 showed no detectable interactions in planta for any of the tested combinations despite positive Y2H signals (Figure S1). Western blot analysis (Figure S1C) confirmed accumulation of ParPMC2 fused to NYFP, whereas ParPMC2 fused to CYFP was not detected, indicating that the lack of fluorescence in NYFP combinations is not due to insufficient protein expression. Thus, the discrepancy between Y2H and BiFC results most likely reflects context‐dependent differences between yeast and plant cells rather than false‐positive Y2H interactions.

Pruar1 and Pruar9 eEF1A Proteins Localise in the Cytoplasm and Endoplasmic Reticulum

Subcellular localization plays a key role in determining protein function by regulating accessibility to interacting partners. In this study, we examined the subcellular localization of the proteins Pruar1 and Pruar9 fused to the mCherry protein in epidermal cells of healthy N. benthamiana leaves, as well as their colocalization with ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 proteins fused to GFP. Both Pruar1 (Figure 3a) and Pruar9 (Figure 3b) displayed a reticulate pattern characteristic of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) localization, extensively overlapping with the ER marker (EYFP‐ER) (Figure 3a,b, first row). They also colocalized with ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 (Figure 3a,b, second and third rows, respectively), suggesting shared subcellular compartments. As previously observed (Polo‐Oltra et al. 2025), ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 exhibited both nuclear and cytoplasmic localization, with ParPMC1 distributed throughout the nucleus and ParPMC2 specifically associated with the nuclear envelope. In this study, we observed that both Pruar1 and Pruar9 colocalize in the cytoplasm and at the nuclear envelope, but not inside the nucleus.

Subcellular localization of Pruar1 (a) and Pruar9 (b) in Nicotiana benthamiana leaf epidermal cells. mCherry‐tagged proteins co‐expressed with EYFP‐ER, ParPMC1/2:GFP or PPV:GFP. Magenta, green, and merged channels are shown; arrows indicate perinuclear structures. Z‐stack projections captured at 48 h post‐infiltration. Scale bars = 10 μm.

Moreover, to evaluate changes in their distribution upon PPV infection, the analysis was repeated in N. benthamiana plants infected with GFP‐labelled PPV (Pasin et al. 2014). In both cases, strong overlap was observed with PPV at the ER, particularly in perinuclear regions (arrowheads in Figure 3a,b, fourth row), indicating potential recruitment of these host proteins to viral replication sites. Overall, these results indicate that Pruar1 and Pruar9 are ER‐localised proteins that colocalize with ParPMC1, ParPMC2 and PPV.

Diversification of eEF1A Occurred After the Rosaceae–Solanaceae Split

Following the strategy established in our previous study (Polo‐Oltra et al. 2025), in which N. benthamiana was used to functionally validate the role of ParPMC through VIGS of the ortholog NbPMC, we first sought to identify potential orthologs of the apricot eEF1A genes Pruar1 and Pruar9 in N. benthamiana. Reciprocal BLASTP searches were conducted with Pruar1 and Pruar9 peach orthologs (Prupe.1G010400 and Prupe.6G095700, respectively) as queries (Table S2). No best‐reciprocal hits (BRHs) were detected but eight homologous genes (e‐value = 0) were identified in N. benthamiana and four each in peach, apricot and Arabidopsis thaliana. Given the low variability observed at the amino acid sequence level, nucleotide sequences were instead employed to construct a phylogenetic tree using a maximum‐likelihood approach (Figure 4). N. benthamiana genes segregated into two distinct clades, with six genes in Clade A and the remaining two in Clade B, consistent with previous reports (Helderman et al. 2021). In contrast, Prunus genes formed two clades (X and Y), each containing clear orthologs between peach and apricot (Pruar1 in Y and Pruar9 in X). Arabidopsis thaliana sequences clustered in a more divergent, basal clade. The resulting topology, reflecting a many‐to‐many relationship among genes from the different species, indicates that lineage‐specific diversification occurred in both N. benthamiana and Prunus after the Rosaceae–Solanaceae split, precluding clear one‐to‐one ortholog assignments between them. In contrast, clear orthologous relationships are maintained between peach and apricot within Prunus.

The multiple sequence alignment was performed in MEGA12 using MUSCLE. The TN93 + G was used as the best‐fitting evolutionary model. eEF1A Silencing Reduces Susceptibility to PPV in N. benthamiana

Although N. benthamiana genes in clades A and B lack clear one‐to‐one orthologs with the apricot clades X and Y, this species provides a convenient system to investigate the functional role of this eEF1A subfamily in PPV infection, as stable transformation in Prunus is technically challenging (Aranzana et al. 2019). Importantly, both apricot genes, Pruar1 and Pruar9, although located in distinct clades (Y and X, respectively), have been shown to interact with ParPMC1, highlighting that their functional role may extend beyond clade boundaries. Previous work has shown that broad silencing of N. benthamiana Clade A leads to plant collapse, whereas Clade B silencing reduces susceptibility to tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV) (Helderman et al. 2021). Other studies have also implicated eEF1A in susceptibility to TMV (Yamaji et al. 2010), but its role in PPV infection remained unexplored. Guided by these findings, and to avoid deleterious effects of Clade A knockdown, we designed three CMV‐based VIGS constructs (Figure S2) allowing graded silencing (Villar‐Álvarez et al. 2024) of N. benthamiana eEF1A genes: CMV3[eEF1A.b1] for strong silencing of the two Clade B paralogs, CMV3[eEF1A.b2] for partial silencing of the same paralogs, and CMV3[eEF1A.a] for partial silencing of all six Clade A members.

In two independent assays, each of the modified CMV RNA3 constructs (CMV3) described above was agroinfiltrated individually into two leaves of six plants, together with wild‐type CMV RNAs 1 and 2 (CMV1 and CMV2). Wild‐type CMV RNA 3 (CMV3[WT]) was used as a control. Ten days later, all four plant types (control, eEF1A.b1‐silenced, eEF1A.b2‐silenced and eEF1A.a‐silenced) developed normally and displayed only mild CMV‐related symptoms, with no apparent phenotypic differences among treatments. At this point, PPV expressing the RFP reporter (pLX‐PPVr; Rodamilans et al. 2021) was mechanically inoculated. Viral infection was monitored over the following days, and at 7 days post‐infection (dpi), silenced plants exhibited slightly reduced growth compared with controls, although this effect was not pronounced (Figure 5a, Figures S3 and S4).

Gene expression of eEF1A Clade A and Clade B members, as well as PPV accumulation, was quantified by reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) in non‐inoculated systemic leaves from the two independent assays, revealing distinct effects depending on the silenced target genes (Figure 5b,c; Table S3). Compared with control plants, the CMV3[eEF1A.a] construct resulted in a 1.7‐ and 2.6‐fold reduction in eEF1A Clade A transcript levels, confirming efficient silencing, whereas Clade B transcript levels remained largely unaffected. This treatment caused a moderate decrease in PPV accumulation (1.5‐ and 1.2‐fold reduction), suggesting that Clade A genes contribute only minimally to viral infection. In contrast, targeting eEF1A Clade B with either CMV3[eEF1A.b1] or CMV3[eEF1A.b2] caused a significant reduction in the transcript levels, which was accompanied by a marked decrease in PPV accumulation. The strongest effects were observed with the CMV3[eEF1A.b1] construct, where eEF1A Clade B transcript levels were drastically reduced (7.3‐ and 10.7‐fold), corresponding to the most pronounced decrease in PPV accumulation (21.9‐ and 6.4‐fold reduction). As expected, CMV3[eEF1A.b2], which carries a shorter insert (30 bp vs. 54 bp), induced a weaker reduction in Clade B transcript levels (3.7‐ and 3.2‐fold in assays 1 and 2, respectively), with an approximately 2.2‐fold decrease in PPV compared with control levels in both assays. This provides the first evidence supporting that insert size influences silencing efficiency in this CMV‐based VIGS system.

Systemic spread of PPV‐RFP also depended on the construct, as observed at 7 dpi in systemic leaves (Figure 6, Figures S5 and S6). In control plants inoculated with CMV3[WT], PPV‐RFP fluorescence was abundant and widely distributed throughout the leaf vasculature and mesophyll in both assays, indicating efficient systemic infection. Although with a slight reduction in signal intensity, particularly in the second assay, a similar fluorescence pattern was observed in CMV3[eEF1A.a]‐silenced plants. In contrast, CMV3[eEF1A.b1]‐silenced plants displayed a strong reduction in PPV accumulation, with only sparse and faint fluorescent foci detected across systemic leaves. Plants inoculated with CMV3[eEF1A.b2] showed an intermediate phenotype, with reduced fluorescence compared with controls but higher signal intensity and wider dispersion than CMV3[eEF1A.b1]‐silenced plants. These results demonstrate that silencing eEF1A Clade B members reduces PPV accumulation and systemic spread in N. benthamiana. Nevertheless, the absence of clear one‐to‐one orthologs between Prunus and N. benthamiana precludes assignment of this phenotype to a specific apricot isoform (Pruar1 and/or Pruar9).

Discussion

ParPMC Interactions Suggest a Proviral Role in Coordinating Cellular Processes

ParPMC1 and ParPMC2, members of a cluster of meprin and TRAF‐C homology domain (MATHd)‐containing genes, are down‐regulated in PPV‐resistant apricot cultivars (Zuriaga et al. 2018). They belong to the MATHd‐only family, implicated as adaptors in signal transduction across diverse physiological processes (Qi et al. 2022; Zapata et al. 2007), though the roles of many members remain unclear. We recently demonstrated that silencing their ortholog in N. benthamiana (NbPMC) reduced viral load and delayed PPV spread through leaf veins (Polo‐Oltra et al. 2025), although the underlying molecular mechanism remains unknown. Considering the role of other MATHd‐only proteins such as MUSE13 and MUSE14 in ubiquitination‐mediated modulation of plant immunity (Huang et al. 2016), we proposed then that ParPMC proteins may facilitate post‐translational modifications of viral and/or host proteins required for viral movement. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that ParPMC proteins also impact additional stages of the viral infection cycle. Furthermore, observed differences in subcellular localization between the two paralogs (Polo‐Oltra et al. 2025) suggest that they may perform distinct, non‐redundant functions.

To gain insight into ParPMC functions, we aimed to identify their potential interactors, as characterisation of protein–protein interactions is important for placing proteins within cellular pathways. Y2H screening identified 13 ParPMC interactors involved in defence and stress responses, protein folding and degradation, primary metabolism and gene expression and translation, suggesting that ParPMC proteins may act as hubs linking multiple cellular processes during viral infection. Given that evidence suggests PPV resistance in apricot is more closely linked to restricting viral long‐distance movement than to specific host defences (Dicenta et al. 2003; Ion‐Nagy et al. 2006), we examined the literature for clues about potential roles of the identified interactors. These results open new research avenues, particularly considering the highly interconnected nature of potyviral infection processes (Pollari et al. 2024).

Among the 13 identified interactors, two proteins involved in primary metabolism (carbonic anhydrase 1 [Pruar4] and aldehyde dehydrogenase 7 [Pruar13]) were found to interact with ParPMC2. This suggests potential roles in pH regulation, aldehyde detoxification and stress adaptation, processes that may indirectly affect viral accumulation and spread (Floryszak‐Wieczorek and Arasimowicz‐Jelonek 2017; Yang et al. 2024). A second group of interactors was associated with protein folding and degradation including chaperonin 60 subunit β1 (CPN60β1, Pruar6), peptidyl‐prolyl cis‐trans isomerase (PPIase) Cyp19‐4 (Pruar7) and ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase (RuBisCO) activase (Rca, Pruar8). Proteins like Pruar6 and Pruar8 are primarily involved in chloroplast protein homeostasis and stress responses; for example, CPN60β has been reported to associate with Rca to protect photosynthesis during heat stress (Salvucci 2008). Although their potential involvement in the response to PPV in apricot cannot be ruled out, the interactions of Pruar6 and Pruar8 with ParPMC may also reflect heat stress responses, as the cDNA library samples were collected during the summer in Valencia, Spain. On the other hand, PPIases such as Cyp19‐4 have been shown to stabilise viral proteins, and NbFKPPIase facilitates begomovirus replication in N. benthamiana by interacting with the tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV) movement protein, with their modulation directly affecting viral accumulation and spread (Chang et al. 2022). Thus, ParPMC interactors involved in protein folding and metabolism could function as susceptibility factors exploited by PPV to promote infection.

Moreover, four interactors were associated with defence and stress responses including one 18.0 kDa Class II heat shock protein (HSP) (Pruar2), one glutathione S‐transferase (GST) U19 (Pruar5) and two thaumatin‐like proteins (Pruar11 and Pruar12). Pruar2 is an 18.0 kDa Class II HSP, also annotated as HSP20‐like. HSPs, particularly HSP20‐like proteins, are central to the cellular stress response, enhancing stress tolerance and viability, but can be exploited by viruses for replication (Zhang and Yu 2022). Interestingly, some transmembrane HSP20‐like proteins, such as RTM2 (Whitham et al. 2000) and SLI1 (Kloth et al. 2017), have been implicated in resistance to potyviruses including PPV, and their vectors, restricting long‐distance viral movement or vector‐mediated transmission. Notably, Pruar2 is homologous to AT5G12020 and AT5G12030, which are both different from RTM2 and SLI1. Nevertheless, if the involvement of Pruar2 in PPV resistance in apricot is confirmed, it would be interesting to explore the similarities and differences with ParPMC‐ and RTM‐mediated resistance systems, as they share MATH domain‐containing proteins, such as ParPMC and RTM3, in addition to the HSP20‐like proteins Pruar2 and RTM2. In relation to the putative role of Pruar5, annotated as GSTU19, it is noteworthy that certain GST isoenzymes function not only as antioxidants but also contribute to the establishment and signalling of virus resistance (Gullner et al. 2018). Interestingly, GSTU19 has been reported as a key factor in plant resistance to turnip mosaic virus (TuMV) in A. thaliana (Otulak‐Kozieł et al. 2022). In the context of PPV, Clemente‐Moreno et al. (2010, 2013) demonstrated that treatment with a GSH activator in pea and peach induced a tolerant or partially resistant response, with reduced symptom occurrence. In addition, Pruar11 and Pruar12 are annotated as thaumatin‐like proteins (TLPs), also known as pathogenesis‐related‐5 (PR‐5) proteins. These proteins have long been recognised as defence markers; however, recent findings indicate that TuMV can selectively subvert a plasmodesmal PR‐5 protein to facilitate infection, highlighting their potential dual role as both antiviral effectors and susceptibility factors (He et al. 2025). The study showed that AtOSM34 acts as a proviral factor by reducing plasmodesmal callose deposition to promote viral intercellular movement, enhancing viral replication and suppressing reactive oxygen species (ROS)‐mediated antiviral defences. Although AtOSM34 is not homologous to Pruar11 or Pruar12 (located on LG7), it shares high similarity with other TLPs on LG5 (74% identity with Prupe.5G094200 and Prupe.5G093500). The involvement of Pruar11 and Pruar12 in PPV infection remains to be confirmed, but they could represent novel susceptibility genes required for viral infection.

Finally, we also identified interactors involved in gene expression and translation including one elongation factor 1‐α (eEF1A, Pruar9), one eEF1A 1‐related (Pruar1), one transcription initiation factor TFIID subunit 4 (TAF4, Pruar10) and one ATP‐binding cassette sub‐family E member 1 (ABCE1, Pruar3). Beyond its canonical role in protein synthesis, eEF1A has been repeatedly identified as a proviral factor in various plant viruses, particularly positive‐strand RNA viruses, where it contributes to viral replication and the assembly of replication complexes (Li et al. 2013). For instance, in potyviruses, eEF1A interacts with TuMV RdRp and VPg‐Pro within virus‐induced vesicles (Thivierge et al. 2008) and with the P3 protein from soybean mosaic virus (Luan et al. 2016). In contrast, TAF4 (Pruar10), a key component of the transcription factor TFIID, has no direct evidence of interaction with plant viruses; however, studies in mammals suggest it may play a regulatory role in antiviral gene expression (Roeder 2019). Finally, ABCE1 (also known as RNase L inhibitor, RLI) is a highly conserved protein essential for translation initiation and ribosome biogenesis. Although its antiviral and proviral roles are well established in animals, evidence in plants is limited, although the Arabidopsis homologue AtRLI2 has been reported to suppress RNA silencing (Sarmiento et al. 2006).

Taken together, the set of 13 identified interactors highlights the potential involvement of ParPMC proteins in multiple cellular layers including primary metabolism, stress adaptation, gene expression and translation. This diversity of functions reinforces the idea that ParPMC‐mediated resistance does not rely on a single mechanism but instead reflects the integration of several cellular processes that may converge to limit viral infection. These findings open the way for experimental validation of the identified protein–protein interactions and the assessment of their roles during PPV infection. Furthermore, these results align with the idea that plant viruses interact efficiently with a relatively small set of host proteins, often moonlighting or multifunctional factors, to simultaneously manipulate diverse cellular pathways required for efficient viral replication and spread (Martínez‐Pérez et al. 2021; Pallás and García 2011; Kumar and Dasgupta 2021; Li et al. 2021).

Pruar1 and Pruar9 Are ER‐Localised eEF1A Proteins That Colocalize With ParPMC1, ParPMC2 and PPV

Among the identified interactors, we focused on Pruar1 and Pruar9, which encode eEF1A proteins, central components of the host translational machinery that are frequently involved in virus–host interactions (Li et al. 2013; Yamaji et al. 2006). Its recurrent role as a proviral factor in positive‐strand RNA viruses, including potyviruses, suggested that association with ParPMC proteins could contribute to PPV infection. To our knowledge, this study provides the first indication of a potential link between eEF1A and PPV, which justified selecting these proteins for initial functional analyses, whereas the remaining interactors will be explored in future work.

BiFC assays provided in planta evidence of a physical interaction between both eEF1A isoforms and ParPMC1 within the native cellular context. In contrast, no BiFC signal was detected between eEF1A and ParPMC2 under the same experimental conditions, despite the interaction observed in Y2H assays. This discrepancy likely reflects differences between heterologous and in planta interaction systems, as Y2H can detect interactions that may not occur in vivo due to subcellular compartmentalization, protein conformation, or the requirement for additional cofactors. Importantly, these results indicate that ParPMC1, but not ParPMC2, forms a detectable complex with eEF1A in planta, supporting functional divergence between the two ParPMC proteins.

Subcellular localization analyses showed that Pruar1 and Pruar9 are predominantly localised in the cytoplasm and at the ER. Consistent with previous observations, ParPMC1 localises to both the nucleus and cytoplasm, whereas ParPMC2 is restricted to the cytoplasm and the nuclear envelope (Polo‐Oltra et al. 2025). Despite these differences, both ParPMC proteins were found to colocalize with eEF1A at the ER. This spatial convergence supports the possibility of functional interactions within this compartment, particularly for ParPMC1, which directly interacts with eEF1A in planta.

Upon PPV infection, eEF1A, together with both ParPMC proteins, accumulated in perinuclear aggregates consistent with viral replication complexes (VRCs), a hallmark of potyvirus infection. As described for many positive‐strand RNA viruses, potyviruses replicate in association with ER‐derived membranous vesicles that reorganise host endomembranes and engage chloroplasts to form functional VRCs (Pollari et al. 2024). The colocalization of ParPMC1 and eEF1A at these ER‐associated structures, combined with their direct interaction in planta, suggests that ParPMC1 is well positioned to facilitate the recruitment or stabilisation of host translation factors at viral replication sites.

Taken together, these observations support a model in which ParPMC1 and ParPMC2, although closely related, may play partially distinct roles during PPV infection. ParPMC1 appears to be more directly involved in eEF1A‐associated processes at ER‐derived replication complexes, whereas ParPMC2 may participate in PPV infection through alternative mechanisms or interactions that remain to be elucidated. This functional divergence is consistent with their different interaction profiles and subcellular localizations and provides a framework for understanding the specific contribution of ParPMC1–eEF1A interactions to PPV susceptibility.

Silencing of NbeEF1A Isoforms by CMV‐VIGS Reduces PPV Accumulation in N. benthamiana

Following a similar strategy to our previous ParPMC functional assay (Polo‐Oltra et al. 2025), we searched the putative orthologs of Pruar1 and Pruar9 in N. benthamiana. In this case, no clear one‐to‐one orthology appears to exist between this species and apricot, suggesting that these genes diversified after the split between Rosaceae and Solanaceae. Interestingly, Helderman et al. (2021) identified two distinct clades of eEF1A genes across various Solanaceae species and showed that silencing Clade B genes significantly impaired systemic spread of TSWV and reduced disease symptom development. In contrast, the knockdown of the clade A genes led to plant collapse, highlighting their essential role in host viability. A similar approach was previously applied by Yamaji et al. (2010) using N. benthamiana and TMV infection.

To our knowledge, no evidence had previously linked eEF1A genes to PPV infection. Here, we used CMV‐based VIGS in N. benthamiana to investigate their potential involvement for the first time. This system allows modulation of silencing levels (Villar‐Álvarez et al. 2024), helping to avoid host viability issues. We observed that silencing efficiency is directly dependent on insert size, with longer fragments yielding stronger knockdown, representing the first report of insert‐size dependence in CMV‐based VIGS, previously described only in alfalfa mosaic virus (AMV)‐based systems (Villar‐Álvarez et al. 2024).

Using this approach, efficient silencing was achieved within approximately 10 days, and RT‐qPCR confirmed transcript reductions that correlated with decreased PPV accumulation. Taken together, these results highlight that both isoform specificity and insert length are critical determinants of CMV‐based VIGS outcomes. Silencing of Clade B isoforms caused a pronounced reduction in viral accumulation and systemic spread, whereas partial silencing of Clade A isoforms had only minor effects. These findings identify Clade B members as proviral factors for PPV and are consistent with the subfunctionalisation observed among eEF1A paralogs during TSWV infection by Helderman et al. (2021). Importantly, previous studies indicate that eEF1A silencing does not impair global transcription or translation in N. benthamiana (Yamaji et al. 2010), ruling out nonspecific effects.

A Working Model for ParPMC1–eEF1A Complex During PPV Infection

The specific interaction between ParPMC1 and eEF1A in apricot, together with the proviral role of Clade B eEF1A isoforms in N. benthamiana, supports a model in which ParPMC1 functions as a host factor facilitating the exploitation of the translational machinery during PPV infection. Both ParPMC proteins colocalize with eEF1A at ER‐derived perinuclear replication structures; however, in planta interaction assays detected a specific association only between ParPMC1 and eEF1A, consistent with functional divergence between the two ParPMC paralogs.

Although eEF1A's canonical role is in translation elongation, many positive‐strand RNA viruses co‐opt eEF1A for replication‐specific tasks including binding viral RNA elements and participating in the assembly of replication complexes (Li et al. 2013). These moonlighting functions can stabilise viral RNA, recruit additional host factors, and organise membranous replication sites independently of ribosomal activity. Given the role of MATH domain‐containing proteins as adaptors or scaffolds, ParPMC1 may promote the recruitment, stabilisation, or spatial organisation of eEF1A at ER‐derived viral replication sites. By positioning eEF1A and potentially other host factors at these sites, ParPMC1 may enhance replication efficiency and support the generation of viral particles competent for cell‐to‐cell and long‐distance movement. This scaffolding function aligns with the strong reduction in PPV accumulation and systemic spread observed upon Clade B eEF1A silencing, and with the impaired viral movement previously reported upon NbPMC silencing (Polo‐Oltra et al. 2025).

Although the precise molecular mechanism remains to be fully elucidated, this model positions the ParPMC1–eEF1A interaction as a critical host–virus interface, linking viral replication to propagation within the plant and highlighting the functional significance of Clade B eEF1A isoforms in PPV infection.

Conclusion

This study identifies ParPMC proteins as key host factors modulating PPV infection in apricot and reveals a functional divergence between the two paralogs. ParPMC1 directly associates with eEF1A at ER‐derived viral replication complexes, whereas ParPMC2 appears to act through alternative host interactions. Other ParPMC interactors identified here, including thaumatin‐like proteins, may further modulate viral movement, suggesting that ParPMC proteins operate within a broader susceptibility network coordinating multiple host pathways that influence PPV infection outcomes. These findings uncover specific molecular components underlying PPV susceptibility and resistance, providing concrete targets for breeding strategies aimed at improving apricot resistance. More broadly, the conservation of these host pathways suggests that similar approaches could be extended to other Prunus species and potyvirus‐susceptible crops.

Experimental Procedures

Plant Material and Experimental Design

This study combined in silico analyses with molecular, cellular and functional assays to investigate the role of ParPMC proteins and their interaction with host factors during PPV infection. The experimental workflow consisted of four main steps: (i) identification of ParPMC interactors by Y2H screening; (ii) validation of selected interactions by BiFC and subcellular localization in planta; (iii) in silico identification and phylogenetic analysis of eEF1A orthologs and (iv) functional assessment of eEF1A isoforms during PPV infection using CMV‐based VIGS.

Different plant systems and materials were employed according to the requirements of each assay. Apricot material was obtained from mature trees growing in the fields of the IVIA germplasm collection (Moncada, Valencia, Spain). Leaves from PPV‐susceptible cultivars were used for RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and Y2H‐library construction. N. benthamiana plants were used as a heterologous system for transient expression assays, including BiFC, subcellular localization and VIGS, as well as for PPV infection assays. N. benthamiana plants were maintained under controlled conditions of 23°C with 16 h of light and 18°C with 8 h of darkness, at 70% relative humidity.

RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 60 mg of powdered plant tissue using the Plant/Fungi Total RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek Corp.), with the addition of polyvinylpyrrolidone (2% w/v PVP‐40) and β‐mercaptoethanol (2% v/v) to the extraction buffer. RNA samples were treated with the RNase‐Free DNase I Kit (Norgen Biotek Corp.) to remove genomic DNA contamination. RNA quality and concentration were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, NanoDrop ND‐1000 spectrophotometry (Nanodrop Technologies), and the Qubit RNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesised from 500 ng of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit with an oligo(dT) primer (Takara Bio) in a total reaction volume of 10 μL. RNA extraction and subsequent cDNA synthesis were performed using tissues specified for each experiment from mature apricot trees maintained in the germplasm collection at IVIA (Moncada, Valencia, Spain).

Y2H Assay

The Y2H assay was conducted using the Matchmaker Gold Yeast Two‐Hybrid System (Clontech‐Takara Bio). The full‐length coding sequences of ParPMC1 and ParPMC2 were PCR‐amplified from cDNA derived from leaves of the PPV‐susceptible apricot cultivar Katy (primers listed in Table S4) and cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate fusions with the Gal4 DNA‐binding domain (BD) under the following PCR conditions: 5 min at 95°C; 3 cycles of 30 s at 95°C, 30 s at 57°C and 1 min at 68°C; 30 cycles of 30 s at 95°C, 30 s at 69°C and 1 min at 68°C; and a final extension of 10 min at 68°C.

The Y2H library was constructed using the pGADT7‐Rec vector, which expresses a fusion with the Gal4 activation domain (AD), following the Make Your Own ‘Mate & Plate’ Library System protocol (Clontech‐Takara Bio). For this, 1 μg of pooled RNA extracted from leaves of the PPV‐susceptible cultivar Mitger and from the hybrid G×Ca‐17 infected with PPV was reverse transcribed. The resulting cDNA was then recombined in vivo into the pGADT7‐Rec vector in yeast strain Y187, following the manufacturer's instructions. To assess the autoactivation of the bait proteins, yeast cells transformed with the bait vectors were grown in the absence of activation domain (AD)‐fused proteins and assayed on minimal medium supplemented with aureobasidin A (AbA) and the chromogenic substrate X‐α‐Gal at the recommended concentrations (Clontech‐Takara Bio).

Protein–protein interactions were screened on selective medium lacking histidine and adenine and supplemented with 40 μg/mL X‐α‐Gal and 125 ng/mL AbA (QDO/X/A). Inserts from positive colonies were PCR‐amplified with the Matchmaker Insert Check PCR Mix 2 (Clontech‐Takara Bio) and digested with AluI and RsaI to detect redundant clones.

Independent clones were rescued from yeast using the Easy Yeast Plasmid Isolation Kit (Clontech‐Takara Bio), transformed into Escherichia coli , and sequenced. Positive interactions were confirmed by subsequent transformation of Y2HGold cells with pGBKT7‐ParPMC1 or pGBKT7‐ParPMC2 with the candidate AD clones; growth assays were performed in triplicate for each bait–prey combination. Prey inserts from positive colonies were sequenced using T7 promoter primers (Table S4) and analysed by BLAST against Phytozome v12.1.6 database (Berkeley, CA, USA). Candidates were prioritised for further analysis based on functional annotation and supporting literature indicating potential roles in PPV susceptibility or defence.

BiFC

The coding sequences of ParPMC1, ParPMC2, Pruar1 and Pruar9 were amplified from cDNA derived from Katy apricot leaves using Phusion High‐Fidelity DNA Polymerase (ThermoFisher Scientific) and specific primers incorporating PscI/NheI restriction sites (Table S4). The resulting amplicons were cloned into the pSK plasmids described by Leastro et al. (2015) (pSK + 35S‐N‐YFP:eGFP‐PoPit, pSK + 35S‐C‐YFP:eGFP‐PoPit, pSK + 35S‐eGFP:N‐YFP‐PoPit and pSK + 35S‐eGFP:C‐YFP‐PoPit), which permitted fusion of the N‐YFP or the C‐YFP at the N‐ or the C‐terminus of the foreign gene by replacing the eGFP gene using the NcoI/NheI restriction sites. The resulting expression cassettes were subsequently transferred into the HindIII‐digested binary vector pMOG800 (Knoester et al. 1998). All DNA manipulations were confirmed by plasmid DNA sequencing.

Binary vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens C58C1 by electroporation. Transformed cells were cultured overnight in liquid Luria Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and rifampicin (100 μg/mL). The bacterial cultures were subsequently pelleted and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, pH 5.6, and 150 μM acetosyringone) to a final optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.5. For co‐expression of two proteins, Agrobacterium suspensions were mixed in equal volumes prior to infiltration into the abaxial surface of two leaves of 2‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants. Fluorescence was visualised as described in Section 4.6. Subsequently, leaf samples were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for western blot analysis, as described in Section 4.7.

Subcellular Localization Assay in N. benthamiana

The Pruar1 and Pruar9 amplicons previously generated for the BiFC assay were digested and cloned in‐frame at the 3′ end of the mCherry fluorescent protein (ChFP) coding sequence in a pSK + 35S‐ChF:GFP‐Popit vector (Leastro et al. 2020), to obtain pSK + 35S:ChFP:Pruar1‐Popit and pSK + 35S:ChFP:Pruar9‐Popit, respectively. The resulting expression cassettes were excised with HindIII and subcloned into the binary vector pMOG800 (Knoester et al. 1998). Agrobacterium cultures were grown and prepared under the same conditions as described for the BiFC experiments.

To investigate protein localization, two leaves of 3‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants were agroinfiltrated with A. tumefaciens C58C1 cultures carrying the ChFP:Pruar1 or ChFP:Pruar9 and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker (EYFP‐ER; Clontech Laboratories Inc.) mixed in equal ratio (OD600 = 0.4). Subsequently, to assess whether the localization was altered during PPV infection, two leaves of 3‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants were co‐agroinfiltrated with A. tumefaciens carrying the pSN‐PPV‐GFP binary vector (Pasin et al. 2014) mixed in equal ratio with the corresponding pMOG800 construct described earlier. Additionally, co‐localization with ParPMC proteins was evaluated using the ParPMC1:eGFP and ParPMC2:eGFP vectors described in Polo‐Oltra et al. (2025). Fluorescence was visualised as described below.

Laser Scanning Confocal Microscopy and Image Analysis

Fluorescence from both BiFC and subcellular localization assays was monitored 2 days post‐infiltration with a confocal laser scanning microscope LSM780 (Zeiss) equipped with a 40× water‐immersion objective. GFP fusion proteins and chlorophyll autofluorescence were excited at 488 nm, and their emissions were recorded at 495–520 and 660–720 nm, respectively. RFP and mCherry were excited at 552 and 561 nm, respectively, and their emissions were detected between 585 and 630 nm. Images, including overlays and z‐stack projections, were acquired with a 12‐bit depth and a line average of 4 and processed using Fiji ImageJ software (v. 1.52) (Schindelin et al. 2012). All images shown are representative of at least three independent experiments. Colour palettes were chosen to ensure accessibility for colour‐blind users.

Total Protein Extraction and Western Blot

For testing expression in infiltrated plants, total protein extracts were obtained from 50 μg of ground infiltrated leaf tissue boiled in Berger buffer (750 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.8; 4% w/v SDS; 4% w/v β‐mercaptoethanol; 40% w/v saccharose) for 5 min at 95°C (Berger et al. 1989). After centrifugation at 11,000 g for 5 min, 20 μL of the supernatant was resolved by discontinuous SDS‐PAGE (stacking gel 3.5% acrylamide; resolving gel 12% acrylamide) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Membranes were blocked overnight at 4°C in 1% blocking solution and incubated with primary antibodies: an anti‐GFP N‐terminal antibody (Sigma; cat. no. G1544) for N‐terminal YFP (NtYFP) fusions, and an anti‐GFP antibody (Roche; cat. no. 11814460001) for C‐terminal YFP (CtYFP) fusions. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:10,000; Sigma‐Aldrich), and proteins were detected using chemiluminescence (Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent). Signals were visualised by exposure to Kodak X‐Omat AR film for 30–45 min and digitally imaged using the LAS 3000 Imaging System (Fuji).

Identification and Phylogenetic Relationships of Putative Orthologs of eEF1A Genes

The putative orthologs of Pruar1 and Pruar9 apricot genes were analysed in peach, A. thaliana and N. benthamiana. Because the peach genome is considered as the reference for Prunus genome analysis, peach protein sequences (Prupe.1G010400 and Prupe.6G095700) were used as BlastP queries against the rest of the databases: A. thaliana TAIR10 and the peach genome v2.0.a1 using the Phytozome v.14 portal (https://phytozome‐next.jgi.doe.gov/), the N. benthamiana Genome v1.0.1 (https://solgenomics.net/) and the Prunus armeniaca cv. Stella v1.0 (https://www.rosaceae.org/Analysis/11326140). Hits with E‐value = 0 were selected and subsequently subjected to reciprocal BLAST searches against the peach protein database. The corresponding CDS sequences were aligned with MUSCLE v. 11.0.13 (Tamura et al. 2021) using default parameters and manually edited with Bioedit (Hall 1999). Poorly aligned positions and divergent regions of the alignment were eliminated using Gblocks v. 0.91b (Castresana 2000). The best‐fitting evolutionary model (TN93+G), based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), was subsequently applied in the maximum‐likelihood (ML) phylogenetic analysis (Felsenstein 1985; Jones et al. 1992) with 100 bootstrap replicates.

Silencing NbeEF1A Genes Using CMV‐Derived VIGS and Its Effects on PPV Infection

VIGS constructs were generated using the CMV‐derived vectors (pCMV1, pCMV2 and pCMV3) recently developed by Villar‐Álvarez et al. (2024), to selectively silence all N. benthamiana eEF1A genes from either Clade A or Clade B. For this purpose, the movement protein (MP) gene in pCMV3 was disrupted using a 30 bp fragment for Clade A (pCMV3[eEF1A.a]) and two fragments of 30 bp (pCMV3[eEF1A.b1]) and 54 bp (pCMV3[eEF1A.b2]) for Clade B (Figure S2). Site‐directed mutagenesis was performed using the QuikChange reaction (Stratagene) with reverse complementary mutational primers containing the corresponding NbeEF1A insert sequence (Table S4) to replicate the parental pLX‐CMV54 plasmid (Villar‐Álvarez et al. 2024). All constructs were verified by sequencing. Moreover, the wild‐type CMV virus version, referred to as pCMV3[WT], was used as a control.

Agrobacterium tumefaciens C58C1 was transformed by electroporation with pCMV1, pCMV2 and all pCMV3‐derived constructs. Transformed bacteria were grown overnight at 28°C and 200 rpm in LB medium supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) and rifampicin (100 μg/mL). Cells were then harvested and resuspended in an infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES pH 5.6) to a final OD600 of 0.2. Two leaves per plant were infiltrated on the abaxial side with a 1:1:1 (v/v/v) mixture of Agrobacterium cultures harbouring pCMV1, pCMV2 and the corresponding pCMV3 construct. Ten days after CMV agroinoculation, six plants per construct were mechanically inoculated with RFP‐tagged PPV (pLX‐PPVr; Rodamilans et al. 2021) on two upper, non‐agroinfiltrated leaves. The RFP tag allowed direct visualisation of systemic infection, which was also monitored between 4 and 7 dpi using a Leica MZ 16F stereomicroscope.

Gene silencing and PPV accumulation were monitored by RT‐qPCR over 7 dpi using primers listed in Table S4. Systemic (non‐inoculated) leaves were collected, ground in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until subsequent analysis. Total RNA and cDNA were obtained as previously explained in Section 4.2. qPCR was performed on a StepOnePlus Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseHplus) (Takara Bio Inc.). Primer pairs are listed in Table S4. Cycling conditions were 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. PCR specificity was confirmed by a single peak in the dissociation curve. Protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A, TC21930) and F‐box (Niben.v0.3.Ctg24993647) were used as reference genes (Liu et al. 2012). Relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative C t (ΔΔC t) method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Six independent biological replicates, each with three technical replicates, were analysed. Primer efficiencies were determined from serial dilutions of cDNA. Statistical significance was assessed by Student's t‐test (p < 0.05) and differences were indicated by distinct letters.
