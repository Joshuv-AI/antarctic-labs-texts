Splenic Sequestration and Suspected Transient Aplastic Crisis in Sickle-Cell SC/Hemoglobin-G Disease in an Adult: A Case Report
Authors: Evani Patel, Simon Gunter, Lee Hartner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute Splenic Sequestration Crisis (ASSC) and Transient Aplastic Crisis (TAC) are severe, but distinct, hematologic complications of sickle cell disease (SCD). ASSC typically presents with acute splenomegaly and anemia due to red blood cell (RBC) pooling, while TAC, often secondary to Parvovirus B19 infection, results in a sudden halt in erythropoiesis and reticulocytopenia. The concurrence of these two conditions is rare, particularly in adults and non–Hb-SS genotypes. We describe a 36-year-old woman with Hb-SC and Hb-G Philadelphia variant SCD who presented with non-specific symptoms who was found to have marked splenomegaly and laboratory findings of severe anemia (hemoglobin 3.8 g/dL), thrombocytopenia, and reticulocytopenia. These combined findings raised concern for ASSC with suspected TAC, especially in light of her positive Parvovirus B19 IgM antibodies. We describe the importance of having a structured and precise diagnostic work-up to elucidate differences between the two conditions in the absence of a bone marrow biopsy, and the rarity of concurrent ASSC with presumed TAC in a patient with Hb-SC SCD. Our case highlights that early recognition of overlapping sickle cell complications despite a bone marrow biopsy is critical as it led to timely diagnosis, optimized management strategies, and reduced morbidity.Keywords: sickle cell disease, parvovirus b19, splenic sequestration, hemoglobin SC

Background

Acute Splenic Sequestration Crisis (ASSC) is a known complication of sickle cell disease (SCD), predominantly in the Hemoglobin (Hb)-SS subtype. Transient Aplastic Crisis (TAC) is often a complication of Parvovirus B19, a high-risk infection among patients with SCD. 1 The presence of both conditions, ASSC and TAC, is rare and requires tailored diagnostic work-up and management to help reduce morbidity and mortality. We present a unique case of ASSC an TAC in an adult patient with Hb-SC-Philadelphia G variant SCD.

Case Presentation

A 36-year-old female with a past medical history of varicose veins, Hb-SC and Hb-G-Philadelphia variant SCD, presents with 5 days of worsening left upper quadrant pain. She initially presented to the Emergency Department (ED) with fevers measuring as high as 104°F at home. She had concurrent chills and back pain. She was hemodynamically stable with labs most notable for chronic anemia with a hemoglobin of 8.9g/dL, and urinalysis with signs of urinary tract infection. She was discharged home from the ED with a course of oral antibiotics. After 6 days, she re-presented to the ED with persistent back pain, nausea, and progressive body aches. She denied any recurrent fevers since her first ED visit and had no new sick contacts. Review of systems was unremarkable. Her last menstrual period started a week prior to presentation, and she experienced no new bleeding episodes following admission. Upon further history, she mentions working an indoor-warehouse job. She has two school-aged children. She was not vaccinated against influenza or Covid-19. She had no recent travel history outside of the United States and has been within the same place of residence for over 12-months. Her birth history was not available.

Her SCD had previously been genotyped as Hb-SC with Hb-G-Philadelphia variant. She had minimal symptomatic disease at baseline, requiring only one hospitalization 5 years prior for a pain crisis. She had not previously been on disease-modifying sickle cell therapies, and her only medication was folic acid. Her baseline Hb per chart history was around 8-10g/dL.

In the ED, she remained tachycardic, and physical exam was most notable for diffuse abdominal tenderness to light palpation. Labs were most remarkable for a hepatocellular transaminitis (AST 169u/L, ALT 311u/L), and a creatinine of 1.3mg/dL from an unknown baseline. Her white blood cell (WBC) count was 7,800uL, hemoglobin was 6.9g/dL, and platelets were 133,000K/uL. A CT Pulmonary Embolism (PE) was negative, however, marked splenomegaly was seen (Figure 1).

Vitals throughout her admission were most remarkable for daily febrile episodes, ranging from 103-104°F. Over her first 24 hours, blood counts showed a down-trending hemoglobin to 3.8g/dL, leukocytosis to 20,900uL, and thrombocytopenia with platelets as low as 83,000K/uL. She had a ferritin level at 1,493ng/mL, which later increased to 24,986ng/mL following simple red blood cell (RBC) transfusions. Lactate Dehydrogenase was elevated to 1,422U/L, and haptoglobin was <30mg/dL. Total, unconjugated, and conjugated bilirubin levels remained normal. Her reticulocyte count remained 0.5%. Peripheral blood smear was remarkable for target cells, sickle cells, and microcytic anemia (Figure 2). A bone marrow biopsy was declined by the patient. A CT Angiogram Abdomen Pelvis Gastrointestinal Bleed Protocol was pursued out of concern for active hemorrhage as a cause of her anemia, showing no active bleeding but marked splenomegaly measuring 16.5cm x 14.5cm x 11.4cm, and bibasilar consolidations concerning for an infectious process. Infectious workup yielded negative blood and urine cultures at 24-hours post-collection, non-reactive HIV 1-p24 assay, and significantly positive Parvovirus B-19 immunoglobulin (Ig) M (24.86 IV) and IgG (5.17 IV) antibodies.

Differential Diagnosis

Differentials for complications of SCD were multifactorial throughout her admission and included infectious etiologies, such as pneumonia driving acute chest syndrome, or acute parvovirus infection leading to aplastic crisis. Given the marked increase in ferritin combined with her worsening clinical status, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) was also considered but was less likely following resolution of ferritin levels. Lastly, ASCC, given rapidly worsening anemia in the presence of splenomegaly, was also considered.

Treatment

Throughout her hospital course, she developed hypoxia requiring 4 litres of oxygen. Given an unexplained concurrent drop in her hemoglobin, she was transferred to the critical care unit. She was transfused with 4 units of blood gradually over the course of days, and her hemoglobin improved to 8g/dL. She was treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics until bacterial cultures returned negative. Narcotics were administered through a patient-controlled-analgesia (PCA) pump for her sickle cell pain. Following positive Parvovirus B19 IgM titres, she was given 10g Intravenous gamma globulin (IVIG) for two days. Unfortunately, following IVIG administration, she developed a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test resulting in positive IgG and C3 antibodies. However, this was considered a side effect of IVIG treatment and not indicative of autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged home following her second transfusion of IVIG. Her labs on discharge improved to a normal creatinine, and resolving ALT and AST elevations. Her hemoglobin was 7.0 g/dL, and there were normal WBC and platelet counts. On her outpatient follow-up appointment, she primarily complained of continued fatigue. Her back and abdominal pain resolved. Her counts were stable, and her reticulocyte count improved to 8.8%. Her hemoglobin electrophoresis showed 64.3% HbA1, 3.1% HbA2, 13.6% HbS, and 19% HbC, confirming Hb-SC disease. She was advised to discontinue iron supplementation because of her elevated ferritin. Her folic acid was increased to a high-dose supplementation. She received no surgical interventions. A year after her presentation, an abdominal ultrasound revealed splenic regression measuring 7.6cm x 7.4cm x 16.9cm.

Discussion

Splenic Sequestration is a rare, yet life-threatening complication which involves the sudden entrapment of sickled RBCs within the splenic sinusoids. 2 This mechanical obstruction propagates, leading to rapid spleen enlargement, significant drop in hemoglobin, and reticulocytosis. 3 In these patients, markers of hemolysis can remain normal as the entrapment of RBCs reduces the immediate substrate required for bilirubin production in systemic circulation. 4 While often observed in children with Hb-SS disease due to autoinfarction, ASSC can also occur at older ages with variant hemoglobinopathies because of persisting splenic function and splenomegaly, as observed in our patient with Hb-SC disease. Hb-SC results in a milder sickling tendency due to a lower concentration of HbS, hence, with lower polymerization. Thus, up to 30-50% of patients with Hb-SC disease retain splenic function into adulthood due to intermittent sickling. 5 Our patient was found to have active Parvovirus B19, an infection that is naturally predisposed to patients with SCD. TACs secondary to Parvovirus B19 can also present an acute drop in hemoglobin due to an arrest in erythropoiesis of RBC precursors in the bone marrow, causing significant reticulocytopenia. 6 Therefore, the diagnosis of TAC and ASSC may seem mutually exclusive with classical definitions, as TAC requires reticulocytopenia while ASSC requires reticulocytosis. In such cases, a bone marrow biopsy may prove useful in confirming a diagnosis of TAC.

Our patient raised concern for concurrent TAC in the setting of ASSC in an adult with Hb-SC disease. While she clinically had serology positive for Parvovirus B19 and reticulocytopenia closely resembling TAC, she also presented signs of ASSC such as enlarging splenomegaly, thrombocytopenia and severe anemia. Follow-up imaging demonstrating splenic regression furthermore supports ASSC, although, serial measurements were not obtained and remains a limitation. Prior case studies have similarly noted the association of both conditions, however, mostly in children. 7 These cases were often fatal or with increased morbidity. Immediate recognition of the distinct pathophysiologies and their manifestations can improve outcomes. For example, recognizing the need for gradual instead of rapid RBC transfusions can avoid hyperviscosity when spleen-sequestered blood re-enters systemic circulation in ASSC. Similarly, monitoring reticulocyte counts can differentiate a hypoplastic process from an over-compensatory bone-marrow response when distinguishing suspected TAC from ASSC. If reticulocytopenia is suspected, Parvovirus serology can be helpful in finding an infectious cause for hypoplastic anemia, and eventually, a bone marrow biopsy is warranted to confirm the diagnosis of TAC. In our patient, her morbidity risk of ASSC was high. Thus, the decision to treat with transfusion therapy, supplemental oxygen, and IVIG was made. While there exist no clear guidelines for the management of Parvovirus infections with IVIG, retrospective reviews have supported its use to promote viral clearance and hemoglobin levels.8,9 However, none of these studies were conducted in patients with SCD. Further evidence accommodating for adult patients with Hb-SC SCD is required to outline management strategies.

Conclusions

We present a unique case of a patient with sickle cell disease with Hb-SC complicated by acute splenic sequestration and suspected transient aplastic crisis. The simultaneous presence of splenomegaly, severe anemia, thrombocytopenia, and reticulocytopenia in the setting of acute Parvovirus B19 infection underscores the importance of diagnostic complexity and the need for high clinical suspicion. Distinguishing the pathophysiologies of both conditions can tailor management strategies, including the use of transfusion support, and prompt providers to pursue specific laboratory testing that can improve patients’ morbidity.
