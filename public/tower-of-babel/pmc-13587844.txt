Microwave-induced efficient degradation of tetracycline using magnetic ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles anchored on C/SiO2
Authors: Minghuan Liu, Shubo Ai, Wenxin Wu, Bingqing Wang, Yisong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In response to the ecological and health risks posed by Tetracycline Hydrochloride (TCH) residues in the environment, this study develops a microwave-induced catalytic system using ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposites for efficient TCH degradation. The composites were prepared by anchoring ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles onto a rice husk-derived C/SiO2 support, leveraging both the thermal and non-thermal effects of microwave irradiation to synergistically enhance catalytic performance. Our results showed that the porous structure of C/SiO2 effectively dispersed ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles, reduced agglomeration, and significantly improved microwave absorption by balancing dielectric loss and magnetic loss, with a reflection loss reaching −41.97 dB. Under optimized conditions (microwave power: 1000 W, catalyst dosage: 100 mg, reaction time: 21 min), the degradation rate of 60 ppm TCH exceeded 99%. Free radical trapping and EPR analyses confirmed that holes (h+) are the dominant active species responsible for degradation, and oxygen vacancies promote electron–hole separation. Recycling experiments showed that the catalyst maintained a degradation efficiency of over 75% after four cycles, indicating good stability. This study offers an efficient and sustainable microwave catalytic technology for organic wastewater treatment, with the added environmental benefit of utilizing agricultural waste.

Introduction

Tetracycline Hydrochloride (TCH) antibiotics, widely used in human medicine and livestock farming, pose significant environmental and health risks due to their persistence and incomplete metabolism in organisms.1 Residual TCH enters aquatic ecosystems via wastewater discharge, leading to bioaccumulation and the proliferation of antibiotic resistance genes.2,3 These resistance genes threaten microbial ecological balance and reduce the efficacy of clinical antibiotics, thereby posing long-term risks to human health. Furthermore, conventional wastewater treatment methods are largely ineffective at degrading TCH, allowing it to persist in water bodies and subsequently enter the food chain.4,5 Therefore, addressing TCH contamination is critical to safeguarding environmental sustainability and public health.

Microwave (MW) technology offers a promising solution for the degradation of organic pollutants, leveraging its unique thermal and non-thermal effects.6 Unlike conventional methods such as photocatalysis or chemical oxidation, MW irradiation enables rapid and uniform heating, which enhances reaction kinetics and energy efficiency.7,8 The thermal effect generates localized hotspots that accelerate molecular vibrations and decomposition, while the non-thermal effect promotes catalyst activation and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS).9,10 These synergistic mechanisms allow MW to achieve higher degradation rates within shorter reaction time. Moreover, MW processes are generally energy-efficient and easy to control, making them an attractive option for environmental remediation.11

Herein, ZnFe2O4 is selected as the key component due to its excellent magnetic loss and high-temperature adaptability, which enable efficient microwave absorption through mechanisms such as hysteresis and eddy current loss.12 It effectively converts microwave energy into thermal energy, and the resulting hotspots contribute to the rapid decomposition of TCH.13,14 Furthermore, under microwave irradiation, ZnFe2O4 generates electron–hole pairs, which further enhance the production of ROS, including hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anion radicals—both crucial for the degradation process. However, ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles tend to aggregate easily, reducing the number of available reaction sites and decreasing catalytic efficiency.15,16 To address this agglomeration issue, various inert inorganic materials, including alumina, mesoporous silica, as well as platinum, have been employed as effective carriers.17,18

Rice husk, a common agricultural byproduct with an annual global production of approximately 260 million tonnes, is rich in inert silica (15–28%) and organic compounds that can be converted into biochar.19 Despite growing interest in sustainable applications, its utilization remains limited.20,21 Conventional disposal methods for rice husk, such as incineration, open dumping, as well as landfilling, often lead to resource wastage and significant environmental pollution, including air contamination and greenhouse gas emissions.22 The strategic utilization of rice husk (an abundant and often undervalued agricultural byproduct) offers a dual advantage in the synthesis of ZnFe2O4-based catalysts. On one hand, it effectively mitigates the agglomeration of ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles; on the other hand, rice husk possesses good chemical stability and corrosion resistance. This enables rice husk-derived biochar to maintain stable performance in complex chemical environments, thereby extending the service life of catalysts and enhancing their catalytic performance. Simultaneously, this approach contributes to sustainable waste management and resource conservation, aligning with circular economy principles. In addition, the high conductivity and polarization effects of carbon materials enable efficient absorption of microwave energy through dielectric loss.23–25 However, carbon materials lack magnetic loss and, when used alone, struggle to cover a wide frequency range, resulting in weak low-frequency absorption.26 The combination of carbon materials with ZnFe2O4, which exhibits strong magnetic loss, achieves an optimal balance between dielectric loss and magnetic loss, thereby greatly improving the material's overall microwave absorption performance.

The integration of ZnFe2O4 with rice husk-derived C/SiO2 composite materials offers several synergistic advantages. First, the porous structure of the C/SiO2 matrix provides a high surface area, which enhances the adsorption capacity for TCH. This brings pollutant molecules closer to the catalytic sites, thereby improving overall degradation efficiency. Second, the amorphous carbon within the C/SiO2 composite acts as an effective support that uniformly disperses ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles and prevents their aggregation, maximizing the catalytically active surface area. Furthermore, the high electrical conductivity and polarization effect of the carbon component enhance microwave absorption through dielectric loss. When combined with ZnFe2O4, an optimal balance between dielectric loss and magnetic loss is achieved, significantly improving the material's microwave absorption performance. The ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 hybrid not only leverages the microwave-absorbing and catalytic properties of ZnFe2O4 but also benefits from the synergistic effects of the composite structure. Unlike previously reported single-component carbon supports, the present rice husk-derived C/SiO2 serves as a dual-functional support that concurrently enhances nanoparticle dispersion and dielectric–magnetic loss balance, as corroborated by the reflection loss of −41.97 dB. This innovative catalyst design aims to achieve rapid and efficient degradation of TCH under microwave irradiation, offering a sustainable and effective solution for treating antibiotic-contaminated water.

This study aims to synthesize ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposites from rice husk-derived C/SiO2 as a dual-functional support, to evaluate their microwave-induced catalytic degradation performance toward tetracycline hydrochloride under varied operational conditions, and to elucidate the underlying degradation mechanism with emphasis on the roles of reactive species and oxygen vacancies.

Experimental section

Materials

All chemicals used in this experiment were of analytical grade and were used as received without further purification. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), iron(iii) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), zinc chloride (ZnCl2), sodium acetate (CH3COONa), and ethylene glycol were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Rice husks were collected from local farms and cleaned via a three-step procedure consisting of initial washing with tap water to remove surface impurities, soaking at room temperature (20–25 °C) for 24 h, and final rinsing twice with deionized water, followed by oven-drying at 60 °C to constant weight.

Synthesis of C/SiO2

Initially, 10 g of dried rice husks were placed in a porcelain boat. The boat was then transferred into a tube furnace and heated to 600 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere. The carbonization process was carried out at this temperature for 2 h. The resulting carbonized product was subsequently subjected to acid treatment by mixing with 2 M HCl and stirring for 12 h to remove metallic impurities. After acid treatment, the material was washed with deionized water until the pH of the rinse reached 7.0 ± 0.1. The washed product was then dried in an oven at 60 °C until a constant weight was achieved. Finally, the dried sample was thoroughly ground using an agate mortar and pestle to obtain a fine, homogeneous powder.

Synthesis of ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposites

The preparation process for ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanoparticles is illustrated in Scheme 1. In the first step, two separate solutions were prepared: 8 mM of FeCl3·6H2O and 4 mM of ZnCl2 were each dissolved in 30 mL of ethylene glycol under continuous magnetic stirring. In the second step, varying quantities of C/SiO2 (0.25 g, 0.5 g, and 1 g) were separately dispersed in the previously prepared FeCl3·6H2O solutions. All solutions were then subjected to ultrasonication at 600 W for 1 h to ensure thorough dispersion. In the third step, the Fe3+ solution was gradually added to the Zn2+ solution under continuous magnetic stirring until complete homogenization was achieved. Subsequently, 3.2 g of sodium acetate, serving as a precipitation agent to facilitate the formation of the final product, was introduced into the homogeneous mixture under vigorous stirring. The obtained homogeneous reaction solution was sealed in a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and maintained at 200 °C for 15 h. The resulting precipitate was sequentially washed three times with ethanol and then three times with deionized water. After each washing step, the suspension was centrifuged to separate the solid product. Finally, the washed precipitate was collected and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h to obtain the final products, which were labeled as ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2-1, ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2-2, and ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2-3, respectively. For comparison, pure ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles were synthesized following the same procedure, but without the addition of the C/SiO2 composite carrier.

Characterizations

The material's composition and its crystalline phase structure were examined through X-ray diffraction (XRD) employing a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker, Germany). The XRD measurements were performed using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) under operating conditions of 40 kV accelerating voltage and 40 mA tube current. The Raman spectroscopy measurements for the samples were conducted using a Renishaw Raman spectrometer, with the laser set at a wavelength of 470 nm. The chemical bonds within the samples were identified using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR, model iS50). Elemental analysis of the material was conducted using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA), with C 1s (284.8 eV) serving as the reference calibration peak. The surface morphology of the material was examined with a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, ZEISS MERLIN Compact, Germany), which was also fitted with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS). The internal microstructure of the materials was probed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Tecnai G20, FEI, USA). The electrical potential of the samples was measured by ζ-potentiometry (Zeta, ZS90, Malvern, UK). A light source (300 W Xe lamp, CEL-HXF300-T3, China) was employed to assess the photocatalytic and photothermal characteristics of the materials. The UV-vis absorption spectra were obtained using a UV2600 spectrophotometer. The MW absorption properties of catalyst was characterized by a vector network analyzer (Agilent N5230C) in the frequency range of 2 to 18 GHz. The electron spin resonance (EPR) spectrometer captured the signals of radicals such as SO4˙−, ˙OH, 1O2, and ˙O2−, which were trapped by 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) under visible light irradiation (λ ≥ 420 nm), using a Bruker JES FA200. A liquid chromatography-mass spectrometer (LC-MS; Agilent 1200HPLC/MicrOTOF II) equipped with an electrospray ionization source was employed to characterize the intermediate products resulting from photodegradation.

Microwave experiment

For microwave experiments, 100 mg of C/SiO2 composite material, pure ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles, and ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite material were each dispersed in 150 mL of TCH aqueous solution with an initial concentration of 60 ppm. The mixtures were reacted under 500 W microwave irradiation at 2.45 GHz for 21 min. Every 3 min, 3 mL of the solution was withdrawn and filtered through a membrane filter. To verify that microwave irradiation is a key factor affecting TCH degradation, a parallel experiment was conducted under nonmicrowave conditions. Finally, the residual TCH concentration in the filtrate was determined by measuring the absorbance at 350 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer.

The influencing factors of ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2-catalyzed TCH degradation under microwave conditions were investigated by examining variables such as catalyst dosage, initial TCH concentration, and MW power. To study the effect of catalyst dosage, 50 mg, 75 mg, 100 mg, and 150 mg of ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 catalyst were added to four beakers, each containing 60 ppm TCH solution, under a microwave power of 500 W. To investigate the effect of initial TCH concentration on degradation, 100 mg of catalyst was added to beakers containing TCH solutions at concentrations of 60, 100, 200, and 400 ppm, respectively, while maintaining the MW power at 500 W. The effect of microwave power was examined under conditions of 100 W, 200 W, 500 W, 800 W and 1000 W, using 60 ppm TCH solution and 100 mg of catalyst in each beaker.

In addition, quenching experiments were conducted to investigate the role of active species generated by the catalyst in the degradation of TCH. In these experiments, isopropanol (IPA), p-benzoquinone (BQ), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt were added in equal amounts as free radical scavengers to assess the contribution of different radicals in the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 system.

Furthermore, four consecutive cycles of degradation experiments were performed to evaluate the recyclability of the synthesized ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 composite. After each cycle, the composite material was recovered using magnetic separation, followed by three washes with a mixture of distilled water and ethanol.

Results and discussion

Morphology and characterization of catalysts

The crystal structures of the ZnFe2O4 nanoparticle, C/SiO2 composite, and ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite were characterized by XRD analysis, as shown in Fig. 1a. The XRD pattern of the ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 18.24°, 30.01°, 35.34°, 36.97°, 42.95°, 53.28°, 56.79°, 62.36°, 70.74°, 73.77°, and 74.76°, corresponding to the (111), (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), (440), (620), (533), and (622) crystallographic planes, respectively. This pattern matches well with the standard ICDD card no. 77-0011 for ZnFe2O4, confirming the formation of a pure spinel ferrite phase. The XRD pattern of the carbonized rice husk-derived C/SiO2 composite showed a broad, diffuse peak without any sharp diffraction features, which is characteristic of an amorphous material, indicating a lack of long-range crystalline order. This broad peak is attributed to the overlapping contributions from amorphous carbon and SiO2.27 The XRD pattern of the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite displayed the characteristic diffraction peaks of both ZnFe2O4 and C/SiO2. Distinct ZnFe2O4 peaks corresponding to the spinel ferrite structure were observed, along with a weak and broad peak indicative of amorphous C/SiO2. This confirms the successful integration of ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles within the C/SiO2 matrix.

The FT-IR spectrum of the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite (Fig. 1b) exhibited a strong absorption band centered at 1040 cm−1, attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–Si bonds in the silica network.28 This band may also include contributions from Si–O–Si bending modes. Two additional distinct absorption bands were observed at 467 cm−1 and 799 cm−1. The band at 467 cm−1 is assigned to the Si–O–Si bending (rocking) vibration, while the band at 799 cm−1 corresponds to the symmetric stretching vibration of Si–O bonds in the silica network.

The spectrum of the synthesized ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite displayed characteristic absorption bands from both the ZnFe2O4 and C/SiO2 components. Specifically, the band at approximately 572 cm−1 is attributed to metal–oxygen (M–O) stretching vibrations in ZnFe2O4, whereas the bands at around 1040 cm−1 (Si–O–Si asymmetric stretching), 799 cm−1 (Si–O symmetric stretching), and 467 cm−1 (Si–O–Si bending) are characteristic of the SiO2 present in the C/SiO2 composite.29,30 The presence of these distinct spectral features provides strong evidence for the successful synthesis of the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite.

To further characterize the structural properties of the synthesized material, Raman spectroscopy was performed on the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite (Fig. 1c). The Raman spectrum exhibits two prominent bands: the D and G bands are located at approximately 1350 and 1590 cm−1, respectively, which are characteristic of carbonaceous materials. The calculated intensity ratio of the D band to the G band (ID/IG) is 0.92. This relatively high ID/IG ratio suggests a significant degree of structural disorder in the carbon component of the C/SiO2 composite, indicating a predominantly amorphous carbon structure. This observation is consistent with the broad, diffuse peak observed in the XRD pattern at 2θ ≈ 20–30°, which is characteristic of amorphous carbon. The agreement between the Raman and XRD results provides complementary evidence for the amorphous nature of the carbon in the C/SiO2 composite material.

This observation confirms the successful preliminary fabrication of the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite. The FE-SEM images of the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposites (Fig. 2a–c) reveal that the nanostructured material consists of spherical ZnFe2O4 particles uniformly distributed and firmly anchored on the carbon matrix. These nanoparticles exhibit a relatively narrow size distribution, with diameters of approximately 200 nm. The C/SiO2 carrier possesses a well-developed porous structure and a robust skeleton, with clearly visible pores, which likely contribute to the high surface area of the composite and provide numerous anchoring sites for the ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles. The uniform dispersion of ZnFe2O4 on the biochar support enhances the catalytic activity of the material and prevents agglomeration of the active phase. In the absence of a supporting matrix, ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles tend to aggregate strongly due to their high surface energy and magnetic interactions (Fig. 2b), a common issue for nanoscale magnetic materials that can significantly affect their physicochemical properties and potential applications.

TEM analysis offers detailed insights into the internal structure and composition of the ZnFe2O4 and ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposites (Fig. 2d and e). The micrograph reveals a multi-component system, where each component exhibits distinct characteristics. A translucent carbon layer, clearly visible and serving as a stable support, is decorated with well-defined ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles. These nanoparticles appear as black spots due to their high electron density, attributable to the high atomic number of Zn and Fe. EDS analysis (Fig. 2f–k) demonstrates the uniform distribution of Zn, Fe, O, Si, and C elements within the nanocomposite. The close contact between the ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles, carbon layer, and SiO2 suggests strong interfacial interactions, which may benefit the material's stability and functional properties.

As shown in the high-resolution TEM images, the carbon layer lacks distinguishable ordered structures, consistent with the Raman spectroscopy results and indicating its amorphous nature. Similarly, as previously reported,37,38 the SiO2 component shows no visible lattice fringes in HRTEM images, confirming its amorphous state-typical of SiO2 derived from rice husk carbonization. In contrast, the ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles exhibit clear lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.251 nm, corresponding to the (311) crystal plane of the spinel structure. This crystallographic information is in excellent agreement with the XRD pattern analysis, confirming the phase purity and crystallinity of the ZnFe2O4 component in the nanocomposite.39

The microwave absorption performance of materials can be evaluated by the reflection loss (RL). Generally, a greater microwave loss (i.e., a smaller RL) indicates better microwave absorption and higher microwave-induced catalytic activity. An RL below −10 dB corresponds to a microwave absorption rate of over 90%, while an RL below −20 dB corresponds to over 99% absorption. As shown in Fig. 3a, the RL of ZnFe2O4 at 16.96 GHz is −25.16 dB, which is below −20 dB, indicating a microwave absorption rate exceeding 99%. For C/SiO2 (Fig. 3b), the RL at 6.45 GHz is −25.63 dB, also below −20 dB, corresponding to over 99% absorption. In contrast, the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite achieves an RL of −41.97 dB at 14.31 GHz (Fig. 3c), which is below −30 dB, corresponding to a microwave absorption rate exceeding 99.9%. These results demonstrate that ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 is a high-performance microwave-absorbing material. Collectively, the XRD, Raman, XPS, SEM/TEM, EDS, and microwave absorption analyses provide quantitative evidence supporting the successful construction of the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite and its superior microwave absorption performance, which underpins the observed catalytic activity.

Degradation of TCH by ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 under MW

The mechanism of microwave-induced catalytic degradation of organic pollutants typically involves both thermal and non-thermal effects. To investigate the thermal effect, the degradation efficiency of TCH was tested under different conditions (Fig. S1). In the absence of microwave irradiation but with heating alone, the highest TCH degradation rate was 44.2%, confirming that thermal effects contribute to the degradation process. However, in the absence of MW, the catalytic degradation ability of the material was relatively limited. These results clearly separate the thermal and non-thermal contributions: conventional heating alone achieved only 44.2% degradation, whereas the microwave-induced catalytic process achieved 95.8% within the same reaction time, indicating that non-thermal effects (e.g., generation of electron–hole pairs and reactive oxygen species) play a dominant role in the degradation.

Conversely, the MW-induced degradation of TCH was evaluated under different catalyst conditions (Fig. 4a). In the absence of any catalyst and with MW only, only 21.6% of TCH was degraded within the reaction time, indicating that MW alone contributes to degradation but at a very slow rate. When a catalyst was introduced under MW irradiation, both the degradation efficiency and rate improved significantly. Specifically, C/SiO2 achieved a TCH removal rate of 57.5% within 21 min, while pure ZnFe2O4 exhibited higher efficiency, removing 67.2% of TCH under the same conditions. This suggests that C/SiO2 possesses certain microwave absorption capability beyond its adsorption properties, and ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles can more effectively absorb and utilize microwave energy for TCH degradation. These results highlight the potential of combining ZnFe2O4 and C/SiO2 to leverage their complementary properties for enhanced TCH degradation under MW.

Remarkably, under MW irradiation, the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposite achieved a TCH removal rate of 95.8% within 21 min. This significant improvement can be attributed to the C/SiO2 support, which enhances the dispersion of ZnFe2O4 nanoparticles and increases the number of accessible catalytic sites. The porous C/SiO2 matrix provides a high surface area for TCH adsorption, enriching pollutants near the catalytic sites. However, excessive carbon content may lead to pore blockage or reduced accessibility of ZnFe2O4 active sites, thereby lowering catalytic efficiency. When the mass ratio of ZnFe2O4 to C/SiO2 is 1 : 2, an optimal balance is achieved between sufficient support for ZnFe2O4 dispersion and adequate porosity for pollutant adsorption. Overall, the TCH degradation process can be summarized as follows: TCH first adsorbs onto the catalyst surface; a portion is degraded by the thermal effect of MW, while the majority is catalytically degraded by the non-thermal effect of MW.

To further quantify the performance differences among ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposites with varying C/SiO2 ratios, kinetic analysis was performed (Fig. 4b). The results showed that ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2-2 exhibited the highest rate constant (k = 0.136 min−1), indicating superior catalytic performance. This optimal composition demonstrated a 147% increase in degradation rate compared to ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2-1 (k = 0.055 min−1).

The adsorption kinetics of TCH on ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposites were systematically investigated (Fig. 4c and d). All nanocomposite samples reached adsorption–desorption equilibrium within 15 min, indicating rapid TCH uptake. The kinetic modeling results for pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order adsorption dynamics are summarized in Table 1. The pseudo-first-order model showed strong agreement between the calculated (qe,CAL) and experimental (qe,EXPT) equilibrium adsorption capacities, with high R2 values across different compositions. Thus, the pseudo-first-order kinetic model provides the most appropriate description of the adsorption process.

To achieve rapid TCH degradation, the effects of several influencing factors, including catalyst dosage, initial TCH concentration, and microwave power, were investigated. As shown in Fig. 4e, catalyst dosage is positively correlated with degradation efficiency. At a dosage of 50 mg, the TCH removal rate within 21 min was 90.5%. When the dosage was increased to 150 mg, 88.6% of TCH was removed within 6 min, and nearly complete degradation was achieved at 18 min, greatly accelerating the degradation rate. Higher catalyst dosage provides more active sites, thereby accelerating TCH degradation.

The effect of initial TCH concentration on MW-induced degradation is shown in Fig. 4f. As the initial concentration increased, the degradation efficiency decreased. At initial concentrations of 60, 100, 200, and 400 mg L−1, the degradation rates were 99.2%, 95.8%, 88.9%, and 82.2%, respectively. When the initial concentration was below 200 mg L−1, the removal rate exceeded 95%. However, at or above 200 mg L−1, trace amounts of TCH remained in the solution.

Microwave power is a key factor influencing TCH degradation efficiency (Fig. 4g). When the MW power increased from 100 W to 1000 W, the degradation rate within the first 3 min rose from 20.8% to 72.9%, demonstrating significant improvements in both efficiency and rate. After 21 min, the degradation rates at 100, 200, 500, and 1000 W were 71.6%, 85.5%, 95.8%, and 99.2%, respectively. Higher MW power not only enhanced degradation efficiency but also shortened the reaction time, indicating that increased MW power improves the catalytic activity of ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2. From the perspective of energy consumption, the rated energy input for the degradation of 150 mL of 60 ppm TCH solution under the optimized conditions (1000 W, 21 min) was approximately 0.35 kWh (≈2.3 kWh L−1), and the energy input could be markedly reduced at lower microwave power (e.g., ≈0.07 kWh at 200 W) while still achieving 85.5% degradation, indicating that the energy efficiency can be flexibly optimized according to the treatment requirements.

Mineralization of organic pollutants is a key aspect of microwave-assisted catalytic degradation. UV-visible spectrophotometry was used to monitor the degree of mineralization. As shown in Fig. 4h, under MW irradiation with or without a catalyst, the characteristic peak of TCH at 350 nm decreased over time. However, in the absence of the catalyst, peak intensities at other wavelengths increased, indicating that TCH was only thermally decomposed into intermediates. The disappearance of TCH-related UV-vis absorption indicates the destruction of its molecular structure, suggesting the potential for deep mineralization over ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2. The disappearance of the characteristic UV-vis absorption band of TCH suggests the destruction of its aromatic chromophore. Full mineralization would theoretically yield CO2, H2O and inorganic nitrogen-containing ions (e.g., NH4+ and NO3−). Nevertheless, direct measurement of these inorganic end-products was not carried out in this study.

Catalyst cycle life is an important indicator of reusability and stability.40 As shown in Fig. 4i, after four consecutive cycles, the TCH degradation rate gradually decreased from an initial 99.11% to 88.14%, 81.70%, and 75.82%. The decrease in degradation efficiency was mainly due to catalyst loss during centrifugal separation and interval sampling. Nevertheless, after four cycles, the degradation rate remained above 75%, demonstrating that ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 possesses good stability and a long cycle life, making it suitable for the removal of antibiotic pollutants.

Possible mechanism of catalytic degradation

To elucidate the possible degradation mechanism of tetracycline (TCH) over the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 catalyst, the roles of active species in the degradation reaction were investigated through radical quenching experiments. As shown in Fig. 5a, four parallel experiments were conducted with the addition of equal amounts of scavengers for ˙OH, ˙O2−, e−, and h+, respectively. In the presence of isopropanol (IPA, a ˙OH scavenger), p-benzoquinone (BQ, a ˙O2− scavenger), and AgNO3 (an e− scavenger), the TCH degradation rates decreased to 92.0%, 90.3%, and 87.5%, respectively, indicating that the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 catalyst promotes electron transfer and generates ˙O2− and ˙OH. Among these, the addition of EDTA-2Na (an h+ scavenger) led to the most significant decrease in degradation efficiency, with a rate of only 80%. The inhibitory effect of EDTA-2Na was slightly stronger than that of IPA, BQ, and AgNO3, suggesting that h+ is the dominant active species responsible for TCH degradation. The contribution of active species follows the order: h+ > e− > ˙O2− > ˙OH. Notably, during the first 9 min of reaction, the removal of h+ resulted in a marked decrease in the degradation rate. The quenching experiments confirmed that all four species play important roles in the degradation process, with h+ contributing most significantly to the rapid degradation of TCH.

Kinetic parameters fitted after the addition of scavengers (Fig. 5b) further support this conclusion. The lowest rate constant (k = 0.014 min−1) was observed upon the addition of the h+ scavenger, consistent with the above analysis. EPR results (Fig. 5c and d) indicate that the quantities of active species generated by ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 under different microwave powers follow the same order (h+ > e− > ˙O2− > ˙OH) and increase significantly with rising MW power, which aligns with the positive correlation between TCH degradation efficiency and MW power.

Furthermore, room-temperature EPR measurements were conducted to evaluate the relative concentration of oxygen vacancies in ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 before and after microwave irradiation (Fig. 5e). The broadening of the EPR linewidth in ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 is attributed to iron oxide (Fe–O) vacancies, appearing around g = 2.00–2.01. As shown in Fig. 5f, after MW irradiation, a broader and more intense signal was observed at g = 2.0015–2.0049, indicating a high concentration of oxygen vacancies. In contrast, the oxygen vacancy signal without microwave irradiation was relatively weak. Additionally, XPS analysis of the catalyst after the degradation reaction (Fig. S2) revealed a significant change in relative oxygen content. Typically, OC (adsorbed oxygen species) and OV (oxygen vacancies) are associated with oxygen vacancies;41 thus, the ratio (OC + OV)/OL can reflect the relative content of oxygen vacancies.42 According to Table S1, the ratio of adsorbed oxygen to lattice oxygen increased from 1.48 to 4.34 after the reaction. The oxygen vacancy concentration was evaluated semi-quantitatively by the EPR signal intensity at g ≈ 2.00 and the (OC + OV)/OL ratio derived from O 1s XPS deconvolution. The enhanced oxygen vacancies under microwave irradiation promote electron–hole separation and thus increase the availability of h+, consistent with the power-dependent increase in both the EPR signal intensity and the TCH degradation efficiency. These results confirm the generation of oxygen vacancies in the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 catalyst upon microwave irradiation, which is consistent with the EPR analysis. Overall, microwave catalysis exhibits a strong capability for the efficient degradation of organic pollutants.

When microwaves encounter a medium in air, part of the radiation is reflected at the air–medium interface, while the remainder penetrates the medium. The absorbed microwave energy is then converted into other forms of energy (e.g., thermal, mechanical, or electrical energy). Materials with good microwave absorption performance can efficiently convert MW energy. Upon absorbing microwaves, ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 converts them into thermal energy, generating localized hotspots on the catalyst surface. The temperature of these hotspots is extremely high, enabling the rapid decomposition of surrounding water molecules into ˙OH and ˙H, and further promoting the production of ˙O2− and ˙OH.43,44 These reactive species can attack the active centers of TCH, leading to its decomposition. Additionally, TCH can be directly decomposed at hotspots exceeding 1000 °C, explaining why the degradation efficiency of TCH under microwave irradiation is higher than that under conventional heating.

Furthermore, although the degradation rate of TCH decreased during the first 9 min after h+ was scavenged, most of the TCH was still ultimately degraded within 21 min, owing to the oxidation by ˙O2− and ˙OH generated from thermal cracking and hotspots. The XPS results also showed a change in the (OC + OV)/OL ratio of ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 after the degradation reaction, indicating that lattice vibrations occur on the catalyst surface under MW irradiation. Such lattice vibrations generate a large number of electron–hole pairs on the catalyst surface.45 Electrons (e−) are captured by oxygen vacancies in the catalyst, which may explain the abundant generation of h+ observed in the EPR tests under MW (eqn (5)). The photogenerated h+ rapidly oxidizes and decomposes the non-pyrolyzed TCH. Meanwhile, both h+ and e− can react with water and oxygen to generate ˙O2− and ˙OH (eqn (6)–(8)).56h+ + TCH → degradationproducts7h+ + OH− → ˙OH8e− + O2 → ˙O2−

To further elucidate the degradation pathway of TCH under microwave radiation, LC-MS analysis was performed on the original TCH solution and on solutions treated with the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 catalyst under MW irradiation for 6, 12, and 21 min, respectively. The molecular weight of pristine TCH is approximately 445.067 g mol−1 (Fig. 6a), which is in good agreement with the molecular formula C22H24N2O8 (M = 445.442 g mol−1). At reaction times of 6, 12, and 21 min, several intermediates appeared (Fig. 6b–d), with molecular weights of 427.08, 401.433, 323.09, 273.18, 261.1, and 199.56 g mol−1, corresponding to the molecular formulas C22H22N2O7 (M = 427.08 g mol−1), C20H20N2O7 (M = 401.433 g mol−1), C18H22O5 (M = 323.09 g mol−1), C10H13O8 (M = 273.18 g mol−1), C10H13O8 (M = 261.1 g mol−1), and C13H12O2 (M = 199.56 g mol−1). It is evident that the abundance of these intermediates gradually increases with longer reaction time. These results indicate that TCH is progressively degraded into smaller molecules during the microwave-catalytic process.

Nevertheless, several limitations of the present study should be noted. The experiments were performed at the laboratory scale using model TCH solutions, and the influence of coexisting ions and natural organic matter in real wastewater on the degradation performance remains to be investigated. The slight decrease in activity during cycling was mainly caused by catalyst loss during separation, and the possible leaching of Zn and Fe ions has not yet been quantitatively evaluated. In future work, we will extend the cycling tests to a larger number of cycles, quantitatively evaluate stability leaching, evaluate the degradation performance in real wastewater matrices, and assess the ecotoxicity of the degradation intermediates, so as to advance the practical application of the ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 catalyst for antibiotic-contaminated wastewater treatment.

Conclusions

In this study, ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 nanocomposites were successfully synthesized from rice husk-derived C/SiO2 as a dual-functional support, and their microwave-induced catalytic degradation performance toward tetracycline hydrochloride was systematically evaluated under varied operational conditions. The underlying degradation mechanism was further elucidated with emphasis on the roles of reactive species and oxygen vacancies. Experimental results demonstrated that under optimized conditions (MW power = 1000 W, reaction time = 21 min, catalyst dosage = 100 mg, TCH concentration = 60 mg L−1 in 150 mL solution), the degradation efficiency exceeded 95%. Owing to the combined thermal and non-thermal effects of MW, TCH was almost completely mineralized within 21 min. The ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 catalyst enables rapid and nearly complete degradation of TCH and can be reused multiple times. Importantly, under MW irradiation, the catalyst not only generates electron–hole pairs but also produces oxygen vacancies, which enhance the separation efficiency of electrons and holes. Degradation mechanism analysis revealed that h+ is the dominant active species responsible for TCH degradation. The ZnFe2O4/C/SiO2 catalyst prepared in this study is an excellent microwave-catalytic oxidation material, capable of achieving rapid and efficient degradation of TCH under microwave irradiation, and holds great promise for application in organic wastewater purification.
