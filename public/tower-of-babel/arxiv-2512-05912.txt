Interpretation of a Discrete de Rham method as a Finite Element System
Snorre H. Christiansen, Francesca Rapetti

Abstract
We show that the DDR method can be interpreted as defining a computable consistent discrete L 2 \mathrm{L}^{2} product on a conforming FES defined by PDEs. Without modifying the numerical method itself, this point of view provides an alternative approach to the analysis. The conformity and consistency properties we obtain are stronger than those previously shown, even in low dimensions. We can also recover some of the other results that have been proved about DDR, from those that have already been proved, in principle, in the general context of FES. We also bring VEM, the Virtual Element Method, into the discussion.

65N30, 58A12.

Introduction

We give a new presentation of the DDR method of
[
36
,
34
,
13
]
. We interpret it as a numerical method based on discrete spaces defined piecewise by solving certain new Partial Differential Equations (PDEs), on each cell of a mesh. The method is conforming in the sense of producing subcomplexes of natural complexes of Sobolev spaces. Even though the solutions to these new local PDEs are not computable, the numerical method itself is ”fully discrete” because it allows the explicit computation of enough moments against polynomials, from the degrees of freedom. The method is then consistent in the sense of Strang’s lemmas. This type of consistency and conformity are stronger than those previously proved for DDR. The interpretation also embeds the method in the Finite Element System framework, and allows to leverage the results already obtained in that setting.

Previous work on FES

The framework of Finite Element Systems (FES) was introduced in
[
16
]
and expanded upon in
[

, §5]
(this independent section is available on arXiv

Finite element systems of differential forms,

) and subsequent papers. It is an environment in which to define and analyze sequences of spaces of differential forms, that are finite elements in a broad sense. The underlying mesh can consist of cells (in particular polytopes) and the basis functions can be general (not necessarily based on polynomials). Thus one may call them polytopal discrete de Rham sequences, though this expression is given an a priori different meaning below. These spaces were conforming in the sense that the fields were
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-integrable with exterior derivative also
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-integrable, which enforces that traces, in the sence of pullbacks to faces, coincide (they are ”single-valued”). This encodes a type of partial continuity that is common for the mixed finite elements of electromagnetics and fluid flow
[
12
]
, such as those of Raviart-Thomas-Nédélec. The latter were extended to differential forms in
[
43
]
. A synthesis with extensions to elasticity is provided in Finite Element Exterior Calculus (FEEC)
[
4
]
.

Since then the framework of FES has been generalized to accomodate higher regularity of the fields
[
25
]
(as in so-called ”Stokes complexes” relevant to fluid flow) and other types of fields
[
26
]
(e.g. symmetric tensors and their associated curvature tensors, as in Riemannian geometry). The definition has a natural expression in the language of categories: a FES is a just a contravariant functor from the (poset) category determined by the mesh, to the category of vector spaces (interpreted as spaces of fields on cells). A short summary is provided in
[
21
]
. This definition covers all finite elements described according to Ciarlet’s definition, including non-conforming ones (such as Morley and Crouzeix-Raviart elements).

This point of view connects finite elements with sheaf theory
[
46
]
. Indeed the combinatorial structure of the mesh can be seen as defining a ”site” with a Grothendieck topology. A contravariant functor from such a site into an algebraic category is a presheaf, which for us represents function spaces than can be both localized (from global to local) and glued back (from local to global).

For the purposes of this paper, however, the definition given in
[
16
]
is sufficient. The goal back then was to define a discrete de Rham finite element sequence on polytopal meshes that mimicked the lowest order mixed finite elements that correspond to Whitney forms. Indeed these were known only for simplices (and products of simplices handled by tensor product constructions). For this presentation we will also refer to the more comprehensive presentation in
[
27
, §5]
. Short presentations can also be found in later publications, in particular
[
28
, §2]
.

A high order version of the construction of
[
16
]
yielding minimal spaces (sometimes referred to as ”serendipity spaces”) was given in
[
18
,
22
]
. The method of
[
16
,
22
]
was conceptually based on harmonic extensions of differential forms, which is not in general computable. But with the help of, for instance, mesh refinements and known discrete spaces on the refinement, adequate discrete harmonic extensions were defined, yielding ”fully discrete” numerical methods with algebraic and analytical properties identical with those of standard mixed finite elements.

By modifying the harmonic extension PDE that underlied the above constructions, one can obtain upwinded complexes, to treat convection dominated diffusion problems
[
19
,
24
]
. The framework has also been used to give compact descriptions of several known finite element methods
[
22
,
28
]
. The generalized Whitney forms introduced in
[
16
]
have also been used to analyze Discrete Exterior Calculus (DEC) in
[
40
]
.

Other polytopal methods

The Virtual Element Method (VEM), reviewed in
[
9
]
, has been developed with similar goals as FES and some similar basic principles, such as definition by recursive extension of the fields, from low dimensional cells. But, crucially, no submesh is used in VEM to define the discretization spaces or the numerical method. The challenge is to reconstruct enough information on the fields from the degrees of freedom, to obtain stable and consistent approximations of the bilinear forms defining the partial differential equation to solve. A connection between the mixed VEM (defined in dimension 3) of
[
8
]
and FES (in arbitrary dimension) was made in
[
22
, §2.1]
and will be used later.

The Discrete de Rham (DDR) method was introduced in dimension 2 and 3 in
[
36
]
and has since been further developed in several papers. We refer to
[
13
]
for an overview and a description of the method valid for differential forms of arbitrary degree in arbitrary dimension.

The DDR construction was inspired at least in part by Hybrid High Order (HHO) methods. Accordingly, it has been presented as a non-conforming method involving spaces of non-matching fields on cells of all dimension. Non-conformity of the spaces poses major challenges for the analysis of the method, many of which have been addressed in the literature
[
34
,
35
]
. In particular an original notion of ”adjoint consistency” has been developed.

The close relationship between DDR and VEM has already been pointed out in
[
10
]
. In some versions these methods can be seen to have the same degrees of freedom, and they coincide with those of FES provided in
[
22
, §2.1]
.

Lowest order numerical methods have been designed on various polytopes for a long time and can be traced back to work of Yee and Arakawa in the 1960ies and 1970ies. Many seem to share a common algebraic approach to the differential operators whereas they have different approaches to the construction of the discrete
\mathrm{L}^{2}
inner product.

Contribution

In this paper, we reinterpret DDR as a conforming FES equipped with computable consistent approximations of the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
inner products. The degrees of freedom of
[
22
, §2.1]
are used. This shows that these examples of DDR, VEM and FES are very similar, if not identical, in practice. They seem to differ mainly in terms of interpretation and analysis. The main novelty here is just that a new harmonic extension PDE, modifying the one used in
[
22
, §2.1]
, is introduced to define the discrete spaces.

This point of view allows us to recover some of the results that have been proved for VEM or DDR as special cases of results already proved, in their principle, in the general context of FES. We summarize:

•

Consistency of the discrete
\mathrm{L}^{2}
scalar product now follows from the fact that the new local PDE allows the explicit recursive computation of
\mathrm{L}^{2}
products with enough polynomials, from the degrees of freedom.

•

Cohomological properties developed for FES, especially
[
27
, Proposition 5.15]
, now carry over to DDR. This passage from local exactness (on each cell of the mesh), to globally correct cohomology (on the computational domain) illustrates that the existence of unisolvent degrees of freedom on a FES can be interpreted as a softness condition on sheaves (see
[
46
, §III.2]
for definitions).

•

Uniformly bounded commuting projections, building on
[
49
,
15
]
, have been described for FEEC
[
4
,
30
]
and FES
[
27
, §5.3]
. They enable one to recover Poincaré-Friedrich estimates, which are basic to the stability analysis of mixed methods.

In the setting of FES one furthermore obtains refinements in the analysis:

•

Translation estimates are given in
[
27
, Proposition 5.71]
, and have applications to the equations of fluid flow.

•

Discrete Rellich compactness, as introduced by Kikuchi for Maxwell
[
11
, §IV.19]
, holds generally, as proved first in
[
15
, Corollary 5]
with applications to the Hodge-Laplace eigenvalue problem on manifolds. See also
[
31
]
on the relationship of bounded commuting projections to such properties, at an abstract level.

•

Discrete Sobolev injections hold
[
27
, Propositions 5.69, 5.70]
and have applications to, for instance, non-linear Maxwell wave equations as in
[
29
]
. See also
[
42
]
on this topic.

•

Fractional order Sobolev estimates are provided in
[
20
]
, with applications to the Dirac equation.

We insist that in our point of view the method is conforming in the sense that we obtain subspaces of standard Sobolev spaces and that the discrete differential operators (instances of the exterior derivative) are simply the restriction of the ordinary ones. These operators are computable in terms of the chosen degrees of freedom. Taking into account the variational crime arizing from the approximation the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
inner product is viewed as a separate ingredient in the analysis, addressed with the help of Strang’s first lemma.

An example of a FES determined by solving local PDEs and equipped with a computable and consistent bilinear form, has been analyzed in
[
23
]
. It concerned the Schrödinger equation with a magnetic field (representing an
\mathbb{U}(1)
-connection). The local PDEs used on the cells are often related, but not identical, to the global PDE one wants to solve. This aspect can be compared with Trefftz methods which often glue exact local solutions in non-conforming ways
[
45
]
.

Notice that many arguments involve a straightforward scaling combined with an appeal to compactness inspired by
[
4
, Remark p. 64 and proof p. 69]
. An optimal formulation of such compactness arguments remains to be delineated in the context of FES.

Remark
.

More recent constructions of
local
bounded commuting projections
[
39
,
7
]
carry over to FES in a very natural way. The approach of
[
39
]
extends to FES of differential forms and the approach of
[

]
extends to general FES as in
[

]
(the latter approach does not appeal to a wedge product). In fact the FES framework seems to identify exactly the algebraic properties of discretization spaces needed for these constructions. A general analysis of stability and compactness properties of these methods remains, in the context of FES, to be fleshed out. See
[
38
]
for the state of the art.

In a sense we have little to add to the design of the DDR and VEM methods themselves. Many of the defining integrations (by parts or not) against cleverly chosen polynomial forms have of course already been exemplified in the literature, though perhaps not in this order and generality. We hope the FES perspective can contribute usefully to the analysis, leading to various convergence proofs. Besides, by providing a clarified rationale for neglecting certain terms and identifying key underlying algebraic facts (such as short exact sequences of complexes and their long exact sequences of cohomology groups), this presentation can ease the extension of the method in various directions. This could be for instance the above mentioned upwinding problem, or the existing variants of DDR developed for other differential operators
[
37
]
and regularities
[
41
]
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a polytopal mesh of some Euclidean polyhedral domain. When considering a polytope
T

by the inclusion of
T^{\prime}

For each

and

we let

be the space of differential

which are in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(T)
with exterior derivative in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(T)
. The
\mathrm{L}^{2}
norm is denoted
|\cdot|
and the natural graph norm on

is denoted
\|\cdot\|
.

The subspace of

consisting of forms with

is denoted

:

X^{k}_{0}(T)=\{u\in X^{k}(T)\ :\ u|_{\partial T}=0\}.

(1.1)

The subscript

have well defined traces, in negative index Sobolev spaces, only on codimension one faces

. However, if these traces in turn are imposed to be in

[

The space of Whitney
k
-forms (which could also be attributed to Weil and de Rham) was originally presented in terms of partitions of unity, such as barycentric coordinates, but it can also be presented in terms of the Koszul operator
[
4
, §3.2]
. For each cell

, we let

be its barycentre (we suppose it is ”computable”). On
T
, the Koszul operator
\kappa_{T}
is the contraction by the vector field on
T
given as

(for

). We adopt notations from
[

]

\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{1}\Lambda^{k}(T)

\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{k}(T)=\{u\in\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{k}(T)\ :\ \kappa_{T}u\in\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{k-1}(T)\}.

(1.2)

These spaces were defined in
[
43
]
in terms of the standard Poincaré null-homotopy operators rather than the Koszul operators and were described in FES terms in
[
28
]
under the name ”trimmed” polynomial differential forms.

The Hodge star operator is denoted
\scriptstyle\bigstar
. It maps from
k
-forms on
T
to
(d-k)
-forms on
T
, when
d=\dim T
. It is characterized by the property that, when

and

are both
k
-forms:

u\wedge\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}v=(u\cdot v)\,\omega,

(1.3)

where the pointwise scalar product is denoted
(\cdot)
and the volume form on

associated with the scalar product and the orientation is denoted

. For instance
\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}1=\omega

The formal adjoint of

with respect to the

\mathrm{d}^{\star}=\pm\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}\mathrm{d}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}

A third point of view on Whitney forms, developed in

, is that they are the solutions to the PDE system:

\mathrm{d}^{\star}\mathrm{d}u=0,\quad\mathrm{d}^{\star}u=0,

supposed to hold on simplices of all dimensions (equipped with translation invariant metrics). For

Even though we adopt the FES point of view, we first present our results without relying on those definitions. On the other hand

Lowest order case

Let
T
be a cell. The case
\dim T=0

\dim T=d>0

In this section we define
W^{k}(T)
as a slight modification of
\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{1}\Lambda^{k}(T)
, namely, that for,
k=0
we impose that the elements of
W^{0}(T)

.

Then we define:

\displaystyle Z^{k}_{0}(T)

\displaystyle=\{u\in X^{k}_{0}(T)\ :\ \forall w\in W^{d-k}(T)\quad\int_{T}u\wedge w=0\}.

(2.1)

Proposition 2.1
.

The spaces
Z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}_{0}(T)
form a sequence which is exact except at index
k=\dim T
, where the integral determines an isomorphism from the cohomology group to
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We have a short exact sequence of complexes:

0\to Z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}_{0}(T)\to X^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}_{0}(T)\to W^{d-{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}}(T)^{\star}\to 0.

(2.2)

The sequence
W^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}(T)
is exact, including at indices
0
and
d
. Hence
Z^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}_{0}(T)
inherits the cohomology of
X^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}_{0}(T)
, by the snake lemma, in its full version. See
[
1
, Theorem 3.5, p. 600]
or
[
17

.
∎

We define:

Y^{k}(T)=\{u\in Z^{k}_{0}(T)\ :\ u\perp\mathrm{d}Z^{k-1}_{0}(T)\}.

(2.3)

Orthogonality is taken with respect to the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
scalar product (also elsewhere in this paper).
We remark that Proposition
2.1
entails that the following is an isomorphism:

\mathrm{d}:Y^{k-1}(T)\to\ker\mathrm{d}|_{Z^{k}_{0}(T)},

(2.4)

for

\mathrm{d}:Y^{d-1}(T)\to\ker\textstyle\int_{T}(\cdot)|_{Z^{d}(T)}.

(2.5)

We define

to be the subspace of

consisting of fields

\displaystyle\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\cdot\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}+\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}\wedge\kappa_{T}a+\int_{T}u^{\prime}\wedge\kappa_{T}b+\int_{T}u^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=0,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\wedge\kappa_{T}a^{\prime}=0,\ \int_{T}u\wedge\kappa_{T}b^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\int_{T}u\cdot\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0,

(2.8)

for all

, for some

, some

Remark 2.1

Removing the finite dimensional space of constraints represented by

]

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\star}\mathrm{d}u+\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=0,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\star}u

\displaystyle=0.

(2.10)

The Lagrange multiplier

In both cases the boundary condition is that

]

\displaystyle\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\cdot\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}+\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}\wedge\kappa_{T}a=0,

\displaystyle\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\wedge\kappa_{T}a^{\prime}=0.

(2.13)

For

\displaystyle\int_{T}u^{\prime}\wedge\kappa_{T}b+\int_{T}u^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{d}v=0,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\int_{T}u\wedge\kappa_{T}b^{\prime}=0,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\int_{T}u\cdot\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}=0,

(2.16)

Notice that
\kappa_{T}b

. It has integral

. Let

Then we define
A^{k}(T)
to be the subspace of

We make the following claims, which we will justify subsequently:

•

A field in
B^{k}(T)
, for

is uniquely determined by its boundary traces. Moreover the extension problem defined by the above PDE is wellposed.

•

The family of spaces

is closed under traces and exterior derivative, making them a FES.

•

The spaces

are finite dimensional and have one degree of freedom per

•

For each
T\in\mathcal{T}
, the spaces

form an exact sequence resolving

. Together with softness this ensures
compatibility
of the FES.

•

The space

contains the Whitney forms
\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{1}\Lambda^{k}(T)
. This ensures approximation properties.

•

For any
u\in A^{k}(T)
and any

, the integral

On a cell

Lemma 2.4

Fix

If not, construct sequences

Extracting subsequences we suppose futhermore that

. Also

But this is false, since we can use

(Coercivity on the kernel)

Proposition 2.6

(FES)
.

The family of spaces
A^{k}(T)
is closed under traces and the exterior derivative.

Proof.

Closedness under traces follows from the definition of
A^{k}(T)
, that already took into account all the traces.

Closedness under the exterior derivative will follow from the fact that if
u\in B^{k}(T)
then
\mathrm{d}u\in B^{k+1}(T)
. Suppose then that
u\in B^{k}(T)
. We have for all

:

\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\cdot\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}+\int_{T}u^{\prime}\wedge w+\int_{T}u^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{d}v=0.

(2.32)

If now

, the second and third terms are 0. This gives the last constraint on
\mathrm{d}u
.

The two finite dimensional constraints are trivially satisfied.
The top equation is also trivially satisfied, with zero Lagrange multipliers.
∎

Proposition 2.7

(Degrees of Freedom)
.

On
A^{k}(T)
the integrals on
k
-faces of
T
constitute unisolvent degrees of freedom.

Proof.

Any given
u\in A^{k}(T)
is determined by its codimension one traces, that can be followed all the way down to faces of dimension
k
, where
u
must be constant, hence determined by its integral. Conversely from any values of these integrals, a
u\in A^{k}(T)
can be reconstructed by recursive extension of the corresponding constant forms on
k
-cells.
∎

Proposition 2.8

(Local sequence exactness)
.

The sequence
A^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}(T)
is exact (more precisely it resolves
\mathbb{R}
).

Proof.

The degrees of freedom provide a cochain isomorphism with the cellular cochain complex, which is known to be exact on any given cell.
∎

Proposition 2.9

(Approximation)
.

The spaces

contain
\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{1}\Lambda^{k}(T)
.

Proof.

Whitney forms satisfy

and

It was proved in

∎

Proposition 2.10

(Computation of moments)
.

For
u\in A^{k}(T)
, the integrals
\int_{T}u\wedge a^{\prime}
against constant
a^{\prime}\in\mathrm{P}^{0}\Lambda^{d-k}(T)
can be computed recursively from the degrees of freedom.

Proof.

Let
T
be a cell of dimension
d
. The case
k=d
is immediate, so we suppose
k<d
.

We integrate by parts the first constraint (as before, but now with a boundary term):

\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\wedge\kappa_{T}a^{\prime}=\int_{\partial T}u\wedge\kappa_{T}a^{\prime}+\int_{T}u\wedge\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{d-k}a^{\prime}.

(2.33)

The left side is zero by definition. On the right side, the second term is of the form that we would like to compute.

We are left with the problem of evaluating the first term on the right side. It will be decomposed into integrals on the
(d-1)
dimensional faces

of
T
. That is, we require terms of the form:

\int_{T^{\prime}}u\wedge\kappa_{T}a^{\prime}.

(2.34)

We use that

is constant on
T^{\prime}
and that by definition:

\int_{T^{\prime}}u\wedge\kappa_{T^{\prime}}a^{\prime}=0.

(2.35)

The boundary term has been reduced to integrals of
u
wedged against constants.

This method can be pursued, decreasing the dimension by one at each step, all the way down to the case where the integrals against constants are actually degrees of freedom.
∎

on
\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{d}(T)
. Due to the second equation (

\int_{T}(u-\overline{u})\wedge b^{\prime}=0,

(3.30)

for all

. We can choose

\mathrm{d}t=u-\overline{u}
and for all

(3.31)

In particular

.

Since

, the last equation (

) is actually true for any

. Using it for

\int_{T}|\mathrm{d}t|^{2}=\int_{T}g\cdot t=\pm\int_{T}t\wedge\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}g=0.

(3.32)

We get
u=\overline{u}
, so

.

This is also sufficient for the existence of a corresponding

, since one can choose

and

, and remark that

solves the system with

and

.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

For
k=d
the system (
3.19
) can be solved iff

. In that case

is determined up to a constant.

Proof.

The first equation can be rewritten:

\pm\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}b+\mathrm{d}v=f.

(3.33)

Integrating this we get
\int_{T}f=0
. Since
f
is constant (by the condition
\mathrm{d}^{\star}f=0
), we get
f=0
.

On the other hand, if

, we can find
u\in\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{d}(T)
such that
\mathrm{d}^{\star}u=g
. Then
u
solves the system with
b=0
and

.

We notice that the constants solve the homogeneous problem. Suppose now that
u
solves the homogeneous problem and
\int_{T}u=0
. Since

from the second equation, we have

such that

.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

For
k<d
the above PDE system is wellposed and has a unique solution
u
, given boundary data for

and any right sides
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\Lambda^{k}(T)
and
g\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\Lambda^{k-1}(T)
with
\mathrm{d}^{\star}f=0
and
\mathrm{d}^{\star}g=0
.

Proof.

As before (by abstract arguments involving compactness) the inf-sup condition and the coercivity on the kernel hold, for this saddlepoint problem.
∎

We define
A^{k}(T)
as before from
B^{k}(T)
viz.:

A^{k}(T)=\{u\in X^{k}(T)\ :\ \forall T^{\prime}\subseteq T\quad u|_{T^{\prime}}\in B^{k}(T^{\prime})\}.

(3.34)

It is understood that

means that
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
is a face of

.

We proceed to show that the spaces

constitute a FES with some very good properties.

Proposition 3.6

(FES)
.

The family of spaces
A^{k}(T)
is closed under traces and the exterior derivative.

Proof.

As before, given
u\in B^{k}(T)
we prove that
\mathrm{d}u\in B^{k+1}(T)
. Given
u^{\prime}\in Y^{k}(T)
we have, since the Lagrange multipliers cancel on
Y^{k}(T)
:

\displaystyle\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\cdot\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}=\int_{T}f\cdot u^{\prime}.

(3.35)

The two finite dimensional constraints required on
\mathrm{d}u
are also satisfied.
∎

Proposition 3.7

(Approximation)
.

The space
A^{k}(T)
contains
\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{k}(T)
.

Proof.

Given
u\in\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{k}(T)
we define
f=\mathrm{d}^{\star}\mathrm{d}u
and
g=\mathrm{d}^{\star}u
.
Then the PDE system is solved with zero Lagrange multipliers.

This holds also on the faces of
T
.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

For
k<d
the map
G^{k}(T)\to A^{k}_{0}(T)
obtained from solving (

) with homogeneous boundary conditions for

is injective.

Proof.

Suppose that the solution to (
3.19
) with

is
u=0
. We then get:

\displaystyle\int_{T}u^{\prime}\wedge w+\int_{T}u^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{T}f\cdot u^{\prime},

(3.36)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{T}g\cdot v^{\prime},

(3.37)

for all
u^{\prime}\in X^{k}_{0}(T)
, for some
w\in W^{d-k}(T)
, all
w^{\prime}\in W^{d-k}(T)
, some
v\in Y^{k-1}(T)
and all
v^{\prime}\in Y^{k-1}(T)
.

With
u^{\prime}\in Y^{k}(T)
we get:

\int_{T}f\cdot u^{\prime}=0.

(3.38)

This also holds for
u^{\prime}=\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}
for
v^{\prime}\in Y^{k-1}(T)
. We deduce that for all
u^{\prime}\in Z^{k}_{0}(T)
:

\int_{T}f\cdot u^{\prime}=0.

(3.39)

By a density argument with respect to
\mathrm{L}^{2}
norm, this can be extended to any

such that:

\forall w^{\prime}\in W^{d-k}(T)\quad\int_{T}u^{\prime}\wedge w^{\prime}=0,\textrm{ i.e. }\ u^{\prime}\perp\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}W^{d-k}(T).

(3.40)

This implies that

. But in view of (
3.8
) we have
\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}W^{d-k}(T)\cap\mathcal{P}^{r-1}\Lambda^{k}(T)=0
, so we get

.

The proof that

is identical.
∎

Proposition 3.9

(Degrees of freedom)
.

Any element
u\in A^{k}(T)
is uniquely determined by the following degrees of freedom, where
T^{\prime}
are faces of
T
:

\int_{T^{\prime}}u\wedge e,\quad e\in\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{d^{\prime}-k}(T^{\prime}),\ d^{\prime}=\dim T^{\prime}.

(3.41)

Proof.

The case
k=d
is immediate. We proceed with the case
k<d
. Recall the notation that
A^{k}_{0}(T)=A^{k}(T)\cap X^{k}_{0}(T)
. We prove that the following bilinear form on
A^{k}_{0}(T)\times\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(T)
is invertible, in several steps:

(u,e)\mapsto\int_{T}u\wedge e.

(3.42)

(i)
For
u\in A^{k}_{0}(T)
we obtain
u\in Z^{k}_{0}(T)
from the finite dimensional constraints, and, for all
u^{\prime}\in Z^{k}_{0}(T)
, and all
v^{\prime}\in Y^{k-1}(T)
:

\displaystyle\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\cdot\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}+\int_{T}u^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{T}f\cdot u^{\prime},

(3.43)

\displaystyle\int_{T}u\cdot\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{T}g\cdot v^{\prime}.

(3.44)

We use
u^{\prime}=\mathrm{d}v
and use
\mathrm{d}^{\star}f=0
to deduce
\mathrm{d}v=0
so
v=0
.

(ii)
Suppose now that for all
e\in\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(T)
:

\int_{T}u\wedge e=0.

(3.45)

Using (
3.8
) we then get, actually, that for all
e\in\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r+1}\Lambda^{d-k}(T)
:

\int_{T}u\wedge e=0.

(3.46)

In particular:

\int_{T}f\cdot u=0.

(3.47)

From the choice
u^{\prime}=u
in the first equation, this gives
\mathrm{d}u=0
. For the case
k=0
we then deduce
u=0
.

(iii)
For
0<k<d
we proceed as follows.

If, in addition
k>1
, we remark that for any
v^{\prime}\in Z^{k-1}_{0}(T)
such that
\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}=0
there is
v^{\prime\prime}\in Z^{k-2}_{0}(T)
such that
v^{\prime}=\mathrm{d}v^{\prime\prime}
. For such
v^{\prime}
we have:

\displaystyle\int_{T}u\cdot\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.48)

\displaystyle\int_{T}g\cdot v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{T}g\cdot\mathrm{d}v^{\prime\prime}=0.

(3.49)

Combining this with (
3.44
) we conclude that for all
v^{\prime}\in Z^{k-1}_{0}(T)
:

\int_{T}u\cdot\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}=\int_{T}g\cdot v^{\prime}.

(3.50)

If
k=1
there is no
v^{\prime}\in Z^{k-1}_{0}(T)
such that
\mathrm{d}v^{\prime}=0
, so the above conclusion also holds.

We introduce
\tilde{Z}^{k}_{0}(T)
defined by:

\tilde{Z}^{k}_{0}(T)=\{u\in X^{k}_{0}(T)\ :\ \forall e\in\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r+1}\Lambda^{d-k}(T)\quad\int_{T}u\wedge e=0\}.

(3.51)

We have
\tilde{Z}^{k}_{0}(T)\subseteq Z^{k}_{0}(T)
. The complex
\tilde{Z}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}_{0}(T)
is exact, by the snake lemma and the exactness properties of
\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r+1}\Lambda^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}}(T)
. It is a variant of Proposition
2.1
but now exactness of

holds also at index

.

Since
\mathrm{d}u=0
and (

) holds we deduce that there is

such that
\mathrm{d}t=u
. This entails that for all

:

\int_{T}t\wedge e=0.

(3.52)

Then we have, setting

and

in (

\int_{T}\mathrm{d}t\cdot\mathrm{d}t=\int_{T}g\cdot t=\int_{T}t\wedge\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigstar$}g=0.

(3.53)

This gives

.

We have proved that the bilinear form (

A^{k}_{0}(T)\to\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(T)^{\star}.

(3.54)

(iv)
We proceed to prove that the two spaces above have the same dimension.

For
k<d
, we have from

\dim G^{k}(T)=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(T).

(3.55)

Recall also the injectivity of the map

\dim G^{k}(T)=\dim A^{k}_{0}(T)=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(T).

(3.56)

For

\dim A^{d}(T)=\dim\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{d}(T)=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{0}(T).

(3.57)

It follows that the bilinear form (

) is invertible.

(v)
Now it follows from Proposition
3.5
that the FES
admits extensions
in the sense of
[

(or
[

). The unisolvence of the degrees of freedom follows then from a general induction argument given for FES in
[

, Proposition 2.5]
(or
[

, Proposition 5.35]
).
∎

Proposition 3.10

(Computation of moments)
.

For elements
u\in A^{k}(T)
its moments against
\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(T)
can be computed inductively from the degrees of freedom.

Proof.

(i)
Let
u\in A^{k}(T)
. Suppose that
w\in\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{d-k}(T)
and that we want to compute:

\int_{T}u\wedge w.

(3.58)

We write as before:

(d-k+r)w=\mathrm{d}\kappa_{T}w+\kappa_{T}\mathrm{d}w-Q_{r-1}w.

(3.59)

On the right side the two last terms are degrees of freedom, so we concentrate on the first. We have:

\int_{T}u\wedge\mathrm{d}\kappa_{T}w=\pm\int_{\partial T}u\wedge\kappa_{T}w\pm\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\wedge\kappa_{T}w.

(3.60)

(ii)
As an intermediate step, we now treat the case where
\mathrm{d}u=0
. Then there is only the first term on the right side. It reduces to terms of the following form, where
T^{\prime}
is a codimension one face of
T
:

\int_{T^{\prime}}u\wedge(\kappa_{T}-\kappa_{T^{\prime}})w+\int_{T^{\prime}}u\wedge(\kappa_{T^{\prime}}w-P_{r}\kappa_{T^{\prime}}w)+\int_{T^{\prime}}u\wedge P_{r}\kappa_{T^{\prime}}w.

(3.61)

Here
P_{r}
is
L^{2}
projection onto polynomial differential forms of polynomial degree
r
. Since
P_{r}
is applied to polynomial forms (or degree at most
r+1
) on a polytope, it is
computable
.

In (
3.61
) the first and last terms are moments against
\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{d-1-k}(T^{\prime})
, so amenable to an induction step. The middle term is zero, since it is a moment against
U^{d-1}(T^{\prime})
representing a constraint.

This settles the intermediate step: we can compute the moments of
u
against polynomials of degree
r
in the case
\mathrm{d}u=0
, from the dofs of
u
, by induction on dimension.

(iii)
In the general case we remark that
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}u=0
and that the degrees of freedom of
\mathrm{d}u
are computable from those of
u
. Applying the intermediate step to
\mathrm{d}u
, its moments against polynomials of degree
r
can be computed. Going back to (
3.60
) and looking at the last term, we write:

\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\wedge\kappa_{T}w=\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\wedge(\kappa_{T}w-P_{r}\kappa_{T}w)+\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\wedge P_{r}\kappa_{T}w.

(3.62)

On the right side the first term is zero by construction, since
\kappa_{T}w-P_{r}\kappa_{T}w\in U^{d-k-1}
. The last term is computed by the above intermediate step.

In (
3.60
) we are left with the first term on the right side, for which (
3.61
) still applies. Again, this provides the beginnings of an induction procedure.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

We now relate some of the above results to the vocabulary of the FES framework.

That the family of spaces
A^{k}(T)
is closed under traces and the exterior derivative, as proved in Proposition
3.6
, is the definition of a FES. However, not all FES produce global spaces that are suitable for Galerkin methods. Two additional conditions are important.

The first condition, referred to as
softness
, has several equivalent formulations, see especially
[
28
, Propositions 2.5 and 2.6]
. One requires that discrete fields on the boundary of a cell, that satisfy a compatibility condition with respect to faces inside the boundary, can be extended to a discrete field on the cell. Here, this follows from Proposition
3.5
. This condition turns out to be equivalent to the existence of unisolvent degrees of freedom which we have also proved, in Proposition
3.9
.

The second condition, referred to as local exactness, is that for each cell
T
, the sequence
A^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}(T)
resolves
\mathbb{R}
. Here also, many stategies are possible. Given that softness holds, one can prove instead that the sequences
A^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}_{0}(T)
are exact, except at index
\dim T
where the cohomology is determined by the integral, see
[
28
, Proposition 2.3]
. This seems easier in this particular case. But even easier is to deduce the sequence exactness from a property of the degrees of freedom as in
[
28
, Proposition 2.7 and 2.8]
. From that perspective, if
u\in A^{k}(T)
and we let
v
be a degree of freedom acting on
\mathrm{d}u\in A^{k+1}(T)
, we write:

\int_{T}\mathrm{d}u\wedge v=\int_{\partial T}u\wedge v-(-1)^{k}\int_{T}u\wedge\mathrm{d}v.

(3.63)

The right side only involves degrees of freedom acting on
u
. This identity is used to assert that the discrete exterior derivative is computable in the chosen degrees of freedom and that the interpolator associated with the degrees of freedom commutes with the exterior derivative. As already indicated, a third consequence is that local exactness holds.

The two algebraic conditions, softness and local exactness, ensure that we have a
compatible
FES. When a compatible FES contains polynomials of given degree, is stable with respect to small perturbations of the mesh, and is invariant under scalings, it behaves like standard finite element spaces, such as the spaces
\mathcal{P}_{\!\!-}^{r}\Lambda^{k}(T)
on simplices.

4
Some schemes and their analysis

In this section we sketch some numerical methods and elements of their analysis, relative to a choice of polynomial degree
r
. The choice
r=0
is the lowest order case described in §
2
, whereas the case
r\geq 1
was described in §
3
.

Given
u\in A^{k}(T)
and based on the computation of moments against forms which are polynomials of degree
r
obtained in Propositions
2.10
and
3.10
, one is able to inductively compute
\gamma_{T}u
, which is the
\mathrm{L}^{2}(T)
projection of
u
on
\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{k}(T)
.

Following VEM practice
[
9
]
one also introduces a symmetric bilinear form
s_{h}
on
A^{k}(T)
, defined from the degrees of freedom, which is bounded and coercive in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
, uniformly with respect to
T
. It should scale like the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
inner product (under scaling maps
x\to h_{T}x
, where
h_{T}
is the diameter of
T
) but no consistency is required. It can be block diagonal in the degrees of freedom.

One then defines the discrete
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-product on
A^{k}(T)
by:

\langle u,u^{\prime}\rangle_{h}=\int_{T}\gamma_{T}u\cdot\gamma_{T}u^{\prime}+s_{h}(u-\gamma_{T}u,u^{\prime}-\gamma_{T}u^{\prime}).

(4.1)

The second term on the right side is referred to as the stabilizing term.

The crucial points are that this discrete inner product is computable, coercive and consistent. It is consistent in the sense that if at least one of
u,u^{\prime}
is in
\mathcal{P}^{r}\Lambda^{k}(T)
, it gives the exact value:

\langle u,u^{\prime}\rangle_{h}=\int_{T}u\cdot u^{\prime}.

(4.2)

Remark also that the exterior derivative
\mathrm{d}:A^{k}(T)\to A^{k+1}(T)
is computable from the degrees of freedom on
A^{k}(T)
to those on
A^{k+1}(T)
. These two properties, which are here stated in their local forms, pass to a global form. From there, numerical methods for various PDEs can be defined. Property (
4.2
) make these methods amenable to an analysis based on Strang’s first lemma
[
32
, Theorem 26.1 p. 192]
. Such studies have been carried out in the context of VEM, as illustrated with a complete model problem in
[
9
, §3]
. See also
[
23
]
.

As an example of PDE on differential forms that features many generic difficulties, we consider the Hodge-Laplace problem
[
6
]
. Given a
k
-form
f
one seeks a
k
-form
u
such that:

-\Delta u=f,\ \textrm{with }-\Delta=\mathrm{d}^{\star}\mathrm{d}+\mathrm{d}\,\mathrm{d}^{\star},

(4.3)

with, say, natural boundary conditions. We suppose the domain is contractible for simplicity. One introduces the auxiliary variable
p=\mathrm{d}^{\star}u
which is a
(k-1)
-form. We let
X^{k}
and
X^{k-1}
be the global spaces of
k
- and
(k-1)
-forms, which are
\mathrm{L}^{2}
with exterior derivative in
\mathrm{L}^{2}
. The mixed formulation in terms of
(u,p)\in X^{k}\times X^{k-1}
is:

\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\forall u^{\prime}\in X^{k}&\int\mathrm{d}u\cdot\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}+\int u^{\prime}\cdot\mathrm{d}p&=\int f\cdot u^{\prime},\\
\forall p^{\prime}\in X^{k-1}&\int u\cdot\mathrm{d}p^{\prime}-\int p\cdot p^{\prime}&=0.\end{array}\right.

(4.4)

For essential rather than natural boundary conditions, one just replaces
X^{k}\times X^{k-1}
by
X^{k}_{0}\times X^{k-1}_{0}
.
We discretize the above problem as:

\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\forall u^{\prime}\in X^{k}_{h}&\langle\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}\rangle_{h}+\langle u^{\prime},\mathrm{d}p\rangle_{h}&=l_{h}(u^{\prime})\sim\langle f,u^{\prime}\rangle,\\
\forall p^{\prime}\in X^{k-1}_{h}&\langle u,\mathrm{d}p^{\prime}\rangle_{h}-\langle p,p^{\prime}\rangle_{h}&=0.\end{array}\right.

(4.5)

For the convergence of such methods one establishes some stability estimates. We define:

\displaystyle W_{h}

\displaystyle=\{u\in X^{k}_{h}\ :\ \mathrm{d}u=0\},

(4.6)

\displaystyle V_{h}

\displaystyle=\{u\in X^{k}_{h}\ :\ \forall w\in W_{h}\quad\langle u,w\rangle=0\},

(4.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{h}

\displaystyle=\{u\in X^{k}_{h}\ :\ \forall w\in W_{h}\quad\langle u,w\rangle_{h}=0\}.

(4.8)

Proposition 4.1

(Poincaré-Friedrichs)
.

There exists
C>0
such that for all
u\in\widetilde{V}_{h}
we have:

|u|_{h}\leq C|\mathrm{d}u|_{h}.

(4.9)

Proof.

(i)
The difficulty is not so much that discrete
\mathrm{L}^{2}
norms
|\cdot|_{h}
appear here, than that it concerns elements of
\widetilde{V}_{h}
rather than
V_{h}
. We take for granted that the result has been established for
V_{h}
by FES methods involving bounded commuting projections.

(ii)
Given
u\in\widetilde{V}_{h}
, we find
v\in V_{h}
such that
u
is the projection of
v
onto
\widetilde{V}_{h}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{h}
. This can also be expressed by
u-v\in W_{h}
, i.e.
\mathrm{d}u=\mathrm{d}v
.
We then have:

\displaystyle|u|_{h}\leq|v|_{h}\preccurlyeq|v|\preccurlyeq|\mathrm{d}v|\preccurlyeq|\mathrm{d}v|_{h}=|\mathrm{d}u|_{h}.

(4.10)

Here
a\preccurlyeq b
means
a\leq Cb
for some constant
C
independent of
h
.
∎

This, and the corresponding result in
X^{k-1}_{h}
instead of
X^{k}_{h}
, is enough to establish the stability of the above discrete Hodge-Laplace problem.

Remark 4.1
.

The situation in the above proof is that we have
u\in\widetilde{V}_{h}
and
v\in V_{h}
, such that
u-v\in W_{h}
. The space
W_{h}
is orthogonal to
V_{h}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and to
\widetilde{V}_{h}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{h}
. Thus
v
is the projection of
u
onto
V_{h}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
u
is the projection of
v
onto
\widetilde{V}_{h}
with espect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{h}
. It follows that we have a uniform equivalence of norms:

|u|\approx|v|.

(4.11)

For corresponding eigenvalue problems such as those of Maxwell
[
11
,
31
]
one needs a stronger result called discrete compactness. Some further notations:

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\{u\in X^{k}\ :\ \mathrm{d}u=0\},

(4.12)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\{u\in X^{k}\ :\ \forall w\in W\quad\langle u,w\rangle=0\}.

(4.13)

Let
P_{V}
be the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
projection onto
V
. We suppose that we have
\mathrm{L}^{2}
bounded commuting projections
\Pi_{h}
onto
X^{k}_{h}
. We start with a
v\in V_{h}
. We notice that
v
,
P_{V}v
and
\Pi_{h}P_{V}v
have the same exterior derivative. So
v-\Pi_{h}P_{V}v\in W_{h}
is
\mathrm{L}^{2}
orthogonal to both
v
and
P_{V}v
. We deduce:

|v-P_{V}v|^{2}+|v-\Pi_{h}P_{V}v|^{2}=|P_{V}v-\Pi_{h}P_{V}v|^{2}.

(4.14)

Hence

|v-P_{V}v|\leq|P_{V}v-\Pi_{h}P_{V}v|.

(4.15)

The space
V
, which is equipped with the norm of
X^{k}
, encodes also vanishing coderivative. An alternative norm on
V
is
|\mathrm{d}\cdot|
. The injection of
V
into
\mathrm{L}^{2}
is compact. One deduces that for some sequence
\epsilon_{h}\to 0
:

|v-P_{V}v|\leq\epsilon_{h}|\mathrm{d}v|.

(4.16)

This gives the vanishing gap condition (concerning the one-sided gap from
V_{h}
to
V
in
X^{k}
norm), which is equivalent to discrete compactness
[
31
, Proposition 3.9]
. If
(v_{h})
is a sequence in
\widetilde{V}_{h}
with
\mathrm{L}^{2}
bounded
(\mathrm{d}v_{h})
, one can get a subsequence converging in the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
norm, simply by considering
(P_{V}v_{h})
.

We now prove the vanishing gap condition also for
\widetilde{V}_{h}
.

Proposition 4.2

(Vanishing gap)
.

There is a sequence
\epsilon_{h}\to 0
such that when
u\in\widetilde{V}_{h}
and
v\in V_{h}
is defined (as before) by
\mathrm{d}v=\mathrm{d}u
we have:

\|u-v\|\leq\epsilon_{h}\|u\|.

(4.17)

Proof.

Let
Q_{h}
be the map
X^{k}\to X^{k}_{h}
defined so that, for
t^{\prime}\in X^{k}_{h}
:

\langle Q_{h}t,t^{\prime}\rangle_{h}=\langle t,t^{\prime}\rangle.

(4.18)

It is not a projection, but it maps
V
to
\widetilde{V}_{h}
and satisfies approximation properties (by stability and consistence).

We have:

\displaystyle|u-v|^{2}_{h}

\displaystyle=\langle u-v,Q_{h}P_{V}u-v\rangle_{h}\leq|u-v|_{h}|Q_{h}P_{V}u-v|_{h}.

(4.19)

We get, remarking that
P_{V}u=P_{V}v
(they have the same exterior derivative):

\displaystyle|u-v|

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq|Q_{h}P_{V}u-v|\leq|Q_{h}P_{V}u-P_{V}u|+|P_{V}v-v|,

(4.20)

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\epsilon_{h}|\mathrm{d}u|,

(4.21)

using the already established results.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

This proposition can be interpreted as saying that the symmetrized gap
\delta(\widetilde{V}_{h},V_{h})
, computed in the norm
\|\cdot\|
of
X^{k}
, converges to
0
as
h\to 0
. On the other hand Remark
4.1
can be interpreted as saying that the symmetrized gap
\delta(\widetilde{V}_{h},V_{h})
computed in the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
norm
|\cdot|
stays away from 1.
