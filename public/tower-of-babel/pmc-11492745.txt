Father-child attachment on children’s screen time: mediating role of ego resilience
Authors: Hwa-Mi Yang, Hye-Ryoung Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren spend a significant amount of time engaging in sedentary behaviors, defined as activities that do not increase energy expenditure significantly above resting levels, such as watching TV and playing PC/video games. Increased screen time is a significant public health concern because children are in a developmental stage where lifestyle behaviors predict various health outcomes in adulthood and beyond. This study explores the links between parent-child attachment, ego resilience, and children’s screen time.MethodsThis cross-sectional correlation study uses 1,163 parents and their children data from the 12th Panel Survey of Korean Children. The study instruments were a modified Armsden and Greenberg scale of parent-child attachment and modified the children’s ego resilience scale of the Block and Kremen. Children’s screen time was classified as more than 2 h per day spent watching television, playing on computers, or playing video games in their leisure time.ResultsStudy results show a negative association between father-child attachment and children’s screen time (ß = -0.43, p < .001) and a positive association between father-child attachment and ego resilience (ß = 0.24, p < .001). Ego resilience showed a negative link to children’s screen time after adjusting for parental attachment (ß =- 0.03, p = .009). Father-child attachment link to children’s screen time (ß = -0.36, p < .001) continued even though the association was slightly reduced in magnitude after adjusting for ego resilience.ConclusionsOur findings show the mediating role of ego resilience on a negative path of father-child attachment to children’s screen time. This finding emphasizes the importance of fostering solid father-child relationships and enhancing children’s psychological resilience as critical strategies for reducing excessive screen time. By addressing emotional support and resilience-building, interventions can more effectively promote healthier behavioral outcomes in children.Keywords: Attachment, Resilience, Screen time, Children, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Over the past few decades, dramatic social changes and technological advances, such as the proliferation of smartphones and the popularity of social media streaming, may have led to a sedentary lifestyle with fewer opportunities for physical activity [1]. As a result, children and adolescents spend a significant amount of time engaging in sedentary behaviors, such as watching TV and using computers or smartphones, which use approximately 1.0 to 1.5 metabolic equivalents of work [1, 2]. This phenomenon is a significant public health concern because children and adolescents are in a developmental stage where lifestyle behaviors such as physical activity and diet predict various health outcomes in adulthood and beyond [3]. Sedentary behaviors associated with overweight and obesity are a risk factor for health deterioration and adverse health outcomes in children [4]. Additionally, screen-based sedentary behaviors are related to children’s poor psychosocial well-being [5].

Children’s sedentary behavior differs from that of adults because children are more likely to be physically active through school activities or recreation, unlike adults who may have to sit for long periods due to work [6]. Sedentary behavior in children is related chiefly to screen time, while sedentary behavior in adults is associated with the work environment, including working conditions and hours [6, 7]. Parents or caregivers may influence children’s screen time and sedentary behaviors [7].

According to Bronfenbrenner’s (1977) ecological theory, parents are the closest and most influential members of a child’s growing-up environment [8]. The microsystem, the first level of Bronfenbrenner’s theory, refers to the immediate environment in which an individual interacts directly. It includes family, peers, school, and neighborhood [9]. In the context of sedentary behaviors, the microsystem plays a crucial role. For example, parental behaviors, household rules, and social support from the family can impact a child’s screen time-based sedentary behavior.

Parents play a significant role in shaping children’s behaviors, including sedentary behaviors [10, 11]. Parental modeling, rules, and restrictions can influence children’s sedentary behaviors. Parental attitudes and behaviors toward physical activity, screen time, and leisure activities can impact children’s sedentary behaviors [12]. Parental factors such as parenting style, family environment, and socioeconomic status can also influence a child’s psychological and emotional relationship with sedentary behaviors.

The influence of parental factors such as parenting style, family environment, and socioeconomic status on children’s behaviors is consistent with family systems theory, which assumes that the behaviors of family members are interdependent and mutually influence each other and that the behavior of one family member affects the entire family system [13]. Parent-child attachment is an affectionate, mutually satisfying relationship between parents and children [14]. Lower levels of parent-child attachment may be associated with higher levels of screen time among children because of lower parental monitoring of their screen time. In addition, attachment, a solid emotional bond a child feels to a close caregiver, can significantly impact a child’s development and psychological health [15]. A stable parent-child attachment relationship may contribute to ego resilience formation through positive emotions toward self and others.

Ego resilience refers to the individualized, integrated coping resources and abilities that lead to an individual’s ability to adapt to various adversities and challenges, including physical, psychological, and social well-being [16]. Block and Block defined ego resilience as an individual’s dynamic ability to modify his/her level of ego control in either direction according to the demanding characteristics of the environmental context [17]. According to Block and Block, high ego resilience involves great adaptation to changing situations and flexible use of problem-solving strategies, whereas low ego resilience involves little adaptive flexibility and a tendency to be unable to respond to changing circumstances [17]. Ego resilience is a personality trait that reflects how an individual responds and adapts to stress in various situations. A study reports that ego resilience is negatively associated with smartphone dependence in school students [18]. Ego resilience may reflect children’s ability to control time spent on television, playing computers, or playing video games, which may be related to decreased sedentary behavior. Previous research has shown that ego resilience can play a protective role in children’s development, helping them manage the demands of their environment and resist negative influences [19]. However, the mechanisms through which parental attachment influences the development of ego resilience and how this, in turn, affects children’s screen time behaviors remain underexplored.

This study aims to address these gaps by investigating the mediating role of ego resilience in the relationship between parent-child attachment and children’s screen time. While much of the existing literature has focused on maternal attachment and its effects on children’s well-being, our study aims to investigate both mother-child and father-child attachment, which has received less attention. By integrating insights from ecological systems theory and family systems theory with empirical research, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of how parental attachment contributes to children’s ego resilience and influences their screen time behaviors. Furthermore, this research would have practical implications for developing targeted interventions that promote healthier behaviors in children by enhancing parent-child relationships and building emotional resilience.

The specific hypotheses are as follows. Parent-child attachment would be negatively associated with children’s screen time (Hypothesis 1). Parent-child attachment would be positively associated with children’s ego-resilience (Hypothesis 2). Ego resilience would mediate the association between parent-child attachment and children’s screen time (Hypothesis 3).

Methods

Data and study participants

This cross-sectional correlation study analyzed data from 1,163 parents and their children from the 2019 12th Panel Survey of Korean Children (PSKC). The PSKC covers a nationally representative sample of parents and children born between April and July 2008. The survey population consisted of newborns delivered between April and July 2008 at selected hospitals across the country, with the selection criteria requiring hospitals to have recorded over 500 births in 2006. A stratified multistage sampling method was employed, resulting in a total sample size of 2,150 households. During the panel selection process, cases were excluded if the mother was under 18 years old, if either the mother or newborn had a severe illness, if the child was part of a multiple births, if the mother had communication difficulties, or if the child was intended for adoption. This study’s sample included 11-year-old children (5th grade in elementary school) who participated in the 12th (2019) PSKC survey. The PSKC data are open to the public and can be used free of charge by all researchers for academic purposes. The survey investigates the impact of children’s growth and developmental characteristics, parenting, parenting support, and policy functions. Among the 2,150 family data of the 12th PSKC, 738 children’s screen time was not recorded, 240 cases of parent-child attachment were not recorded, and nine children with disabilities were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 1,163 parents and their children were selected for this study.

Measurement

Participant’s general characteristics

This study measured perceived socioeconomic status on a single item on a 10-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 10 = living very well). “Where do you think your economic level is on the ladder?” The average monthly household income was classified as less than four million Korean won versus more than four million Korean won (approximately $3,076) [20]. Self-rated health was measured using a single item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor to 5 = very good). “How would you rate your overall health now?” Higher scores indicate good self-rated health in children [21]. A child’s alone time was measured using a single question: How much time does a child spend alone a day? Higher scores indicate more time spent alone without adults. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the body weight by the square of height (kg/m2).

Children’s screen time

We categorized children’s screen time as spending more than two hours on television, playing computers, or playing video games in their leisure time, referring to children’s sedentary behavior. This categorization was informed by guidelines from the American Academy of Pediatrics and prior research on sedentary behaviors in children [21, 22]. Children’s screen time was asked by a single item: “How many hours do you usually spend watching videos on TV, computer, or mobile phone during the week?”

Parent-child attachment

A parent-child attachment was measured with modified Armsden and Greenberg’s (1987) scale, 12 items on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always), one of the most widely used and best psychometrically researched attachment self-report instruments, consists of communication, trust, and alienation [23]. The higher the score, the higher the attachment to the mother or father. The father responded to the father-child attachment, and the mother responded to the mother-child attachment. In a previous study, father-child attachment had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80, and mother-child attachment had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81 [24]. In this study, the reliability of father-child attachment was Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80, and mother-child attachment was Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81.

Ego resilience

The children’s ego resilience scale consisted of 14 items on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 4 = strongly agree), modified by Korean Children’s Panel researchers based on the original instrument of Block and Kremen [25]. A higher score indicates a higher ego resilience. In a previous study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.79 [26]. A higher score indicates a higher ego resilience. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85.

Ethical consideration

This study conducted a secondary analysis using public data from the PSKC. All information was anonymized prior to analysis. This study was approved by the institutional review committee of Daejin University (Approval No. IRB 1040656-202204-SB-01-03).

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS/WIN 23.0 program. Participants’ general characteristics were expressed as numbers (percentages) or means (standard deviations). Logistic regression analysis was used to test the crude association between each variable and children’s sedentary behavior. Multiple logistic regression analysis was performed on the association of time spent alone, parent-child attachment, and ego resilience in children’s screen time.

We adopt logistic regression to test hypothesis 1; the first equation regressed children’s screen time on parental attachment. We adopt multiple linear regression to verify hypothesis 2; ego resilience was regressed on parental attachment in the second equation. We adopt logistic regression to test hypothesis 3; in the third equation, children’s screen time was regressed on both ego resilience and parental attachment. We compared the first and third equations to evaluate the mediatory role of ego resilience in the association of parental attachment with children’s screen time. We assessed whether the association between parental attachment and children’s screen time decreases or becomes non-significant when ego resilience acts as a mediator [27].

Results

General characteristics of participants

The average age of the participants was 11.4 years (standard deviation; SD = 0.13). The gender distribution of the children in the sample was 51.2% boys (596 children) and 48.8% girls (567 children)​. The percentage of children’s screen time more than two hours by sex was 58.8% for boys, higher than 41.2% for girls. (χ²= 24.4, p < .001) (Table 1). A higher percentage of individuals with more than two hours of screen time (23.9%) had an annual household income of less than $3,076 than those with under two hours of screen time (14.0%) (χ²=18.7, p < .001). The BMI level was 19.3 kg/m2 in the group under two hours of screen time, lower than in the group with more than two hours (19.9 mg/m2) (t= -2.9, p = .003). The average spending alone time was 1.8 h for children with more than two hours of screen time, higher than 1.4 h for children under two hours (t= -6.3, p = .001). The mean father-child or mother-child attachment score was lower in the group with more than two hours of screen time than in the group under two hours (all p-values < 0.05). The under two hours of screen time group’s average score of ego resilience was 42.9, higher than 41.5 for the group with more than two hours (t = 4.3, p < .001).

Mediator effect of ego resilience in the association of father-child attachment with children’s screen time

A father-child attachment was negatively associated with children’s screen time (ß = -0.43, p < .001) in the first equation (Hypothesis 1). In the second equation, a father-child attachment was positively associated with ego resilience (ß = 0.24, p < .001) (Hypothesis 2). Ego resilience was negatively linked with children’s screen time after adjusting for parental attachment (Hypothesis 3) (ß =- 0.03, p = .009). Furthermore, father-child attachment continued to exert a direct effect on children’s screen time (ß = -0.36, p < .001), even though the association was slightly reduced in magnitude after adjusting for ego resilience in the third equation, rather than the first (ß =- 0.43, p < .001) (Table 4; Fig. 1). Sobel’s test showed a significant mediating effect of ego resilience (z = -3.43, p < .001).

Discussion

We found that father-child attachment was significantly associated with reduced screen time in children, with ego resilience mediating. These findings highlight the importance of parental involvement, particularly by fathers, in mitigating the adverse effects of sedentary behaviors in children. Our results suggest that fostering solid father-child relationships and enhancing children’s emotional resilience could be critical strategies for reducing excessive screen time.

Environments unsupervised by parents or other adults may affect sedentary behaviors among children [28]. This evidence suggests that the more a child spends time alone, the higher the risk of screen-related sedentary behavior exposure [28, 29]. Our study found that when a child spends more time alone, it will likely increase screen time. This result was similar to that of a survey, which reports that the more child spends time alone, the higher media addiction among children in Korea [30]. In addition, Rusby, Westling, Crowley, and Light found that adolescents who spend much time alone are likely to behave sedentarily [31]. Even though children naturally prefer physically active play, they tend to sit when they have nothing to do or nobody to play with [32]. Being alone is a crucially potential determinant of a child’s screen time.

Our first hypothesis assumed a negative association between parent-child attachment and child screen time. Parent-child attachment, which refers to the emotional bond between parents and their children, has been linked to various health behaviors in children. Attachment-based positive parenting could be beneficial in decreasing sedentary behaviors in children. Children hurt by psychological rejection, indifference, and low parental support are more prone to immerse in the Internet [33]. Lan and Wang [34] found that low father- or mother-child attachment is associated with problematic internet use among Chinese adolescents.

Moreover, adolescents with high attachment to their parents did not exhibit problematic internet use as a virtual hideout to protect themselves from loneliness [34]. However, our finding shows that only father-child attachment, not mother-child attachment, is negatively associated with children’s screen time. Fathers are often more likely than mothers to initiate children’s physical activity within families [35]. Fathers are more likely to use play and physical activity as a bonding strategy from a very early age and interact or play with children in a lively, compelling, and competitive manner [36, 37]. Fathers also tend to provide a better model of sports skills [35]. This fact suggests that the father’s role is essential in reducing and preventing screen time in children and that it is necessary to encourage fathers’ participation in physical activity promotion programs [37, 38].

Our second hypothesis was that parental attachment would be positively associated with children’s ego resilience. We can confirm a positive correlation between the father-child attachment and the child’s ego resilience from the results of the second equation of mediation analysis. This result aligns with a systematic review that explored the relationship between attachment and resilience [15]. Stable attachment to parents may enable children to self-regulate their emotions and cope well in stressful or challenging situations, resulting in high ego resilience [39, 40]. According to Engels and Finkenauer’s study, parental attachment predicts adolescents’ emotional adaptation [41]. A meta-analysis study implicated that parent-child attachments affect children’s emotional development, positive emotions, control of their feelings, and more socially supported coping strategies [42].

Our third hypothesis was on the mediation effect of ego resilience on parent-child attachment and sedentary behaviors. Our findings show the mediating role of ego resilience on a negative path of father-child attachment to screen time. These findings may indicate that children with secure attachments to their fathers were more likely to develop higher levels of ego resilience, reducing the likelihood of engaging in excessive screen time. Ego resilience is associated with strong self-regulation skills, such as impulse control, emotional regulation, and attentional focus [43–45]. Ego resilience can be understood as an individual’s ability to adapt and bounce back from stress or adversity. Parent-child attachment, the emotional bond between a child and their primary caregiver, can impact child development, including their behaviors and coping strategies [46, 47]. Sedentary behaviors, such as excessive screen time or lack of physical activity, have been associated with adverse health outcomes, including increased risk of obesity and other health problems. Um et al. found that low ego resilience was associated with smartphone dependence among middle school students [18].

Our study findings suggest that enhancing ego resilience could serve as a protective factor, buffering the influence of insecure or disrupted parent-child attachment on children’s sedentary behaviors. Individuals with high ego resilience were likely to cope well in stressful situations [46, 48] with health-promoting behaviors [49]. Higher ego resilience may be related to improving sedentary behavior by controlling TV and gaming time among children. Our study implies that strategies to strengthen parent-child and especially father-child attachment and increase self-resilience should be considered in planning interventions to reduce screen time in children.

This study may offer several contributions to the existing literature. First, it highlights the significant role of father-child attachment in reducing children’s screen time, which has received less attention than mother-child dynamics. This finding suggests that interventions to increase paternal involvement could be particularly effective in promoting healthier screen time habits among children.

Moreover, our study underscores the importance of ego resilience as a mediating factor. This finding suggests that fostering emotional resilience in children can be a critical component of strategies to reduce sedentary behavior. By strengthening children’s ability to cope with stress and adapt to challenges, we can indirectly influence their screen time behaviors.

For policymakers and decision-makers, these findings indicate that policies and programs should focus on limiting screen time, enhancing the quality of parent-child interactions, and building children’s emotional resilience. Specifically, we recommend developing programs encouraging active father involvement in children’s lives and integrating resilience-building activities into school curricula and community programs. Such interventions could help mitigate the adverse effects of excessive screen time, promoting better health and well-being among children.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of the study restricts our ability to draw causal inferences, as we can only observe associations between variables at a single point in time. Future longitudinal studies are needed to establish causality and further explore the temporal dynamics of the relationships between father-child attachment, ego resilience, and children’s screen time. Secondly, the study relied on self-reported questionnaires to measure study variables. And single-item measure for children’s screen time. While single-item measure is practical and commonly employed in large-scale surveys, it may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of screen time behaviors, such as differences in content type or context of use. More comprehensive approaches, such as detailed time-use diaries or multi-item scales, could provide a richer and more accurate assessment of children’s screen time in future research.

Additionally, potential residual confounding remains a concern. Although we controlled for several confounding variables, unmeasured factors such as parental mental health, parenting styles, family dynamics, or the home environment may have influenced the observed relationships. These unaccounted-for variables could provide alternative explanations for the associations we observed, warranting cautious interpretation of the findings. Moreover, this study was conducted with elementary school students in Korea, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations. Cultural factors and contextual differences in parenting practices and screen time behaviors might affect the applicability of these findings to different settings.

Conclusion

Our study underscores the critical role of father-child attachment in influencing children’s screen time behaviors, with ego resilience as a critical mediating factor. The findings suggest that children who experience solid emotional bonds with their fathers are more likely to develop resilience in managing stress and avoiding excessive screen time. This fact highlights the importance of fostering secure attachments within the family, particularly with fathers and implementing resilience-building strategies in the home and school environments.

Moreover, the study contributes to the broader literature by demonstrating the interconnectedness of family dynamics and children’s behavioral outcomes. It suggests that interventions to reduce screen time should focus on setting limits, enhancing the quality of parent-child relationships, and supporting children’s emotional development. Policymakers and practitioners should consider these factors when designing programs to promote healthy behaviors in children. Future research should continue to explore these relationships in diverse populations and settings to validate and extend these findings.
