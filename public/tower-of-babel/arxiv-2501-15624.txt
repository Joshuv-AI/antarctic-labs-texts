Improving Estonian Text Simplification through Pretrained Language Models and Custom Datasets
Eduard Barbu, Meeri-Ly Muru, Sten Marcus Malva

Abstract
This study introduces an approach to Estonian text simplification using two model architectures: a neural machine translation model and a fine-tuned large language model (LLaMA). Given the limited resources for Estonian, we developed a new dataset, the Estonian Simplification Dataset, combining translated data and GPT-4.0-generated simplifications. We benchmarked OpenNMT, a neural machine translation model that frames text simplification as a translation task, and fine-tuned the LLaMA model on our dataset to tailor it specifically for Estonian simplification. Manual evaluations on the test set show that the LLaMA model consistently outperforms OpenNMT in readability, grammaticality, and meaning preservation. These findings underscore the potential of large language models for low-resource languages and provide a basis for further research in Estonian text simplification.

Introduction and Related work

Text simplification involves converting complex text into simpler forms while preserving the original meaning. This is typically done by splitting the text into sentences, shortening sentence length, simplifying vocabulary, and simplifying grammatical structures. It is essential for improving accessibility for readers with lower language proficiency, cognitive impairments, or for those learning English as a second language. Additionally, it has applications in educational tools, assistive technologies, and content tailored for broader audiences.

Automatic text simplification (ATS) leverages natural language processing (NLP) algorithms to enhance readability while maintaining the core information. Most research in text simplification focuses on the English language. Comprehensive reviews of work in ATS focusing on the types of simplification, tools and datasets can be found in
Saggion 2017
;
Espinosa-Zaragoza et al. 2023
;
Shardlow 2014
.

Over the years, various projects have applied ATS to different target groups, leading to the development of various tools. Notable examples include the HAPPI project
Devlin and Unthank 2006
, aimed at individuals with aphasia, the PorSimples project
Aluisio et al. 2010
for people with low literacy, and the FIRST project
Barbu et al. 2015
, which focused on individuals with autism and cognitive challenges.

Most research in ATS has not specified a target group, instead focusing on different simplification phenomena.
As in other areas of NLP the best architectures for text simplification work a based on deep neural networks
(
Zhao et al. 2018
;
Anjum and Lieberum 2023
)

Pretrained LLMs, such as GPT
OpenAI 2023
, LLaMA
Touvron et al. 2023
, and T5
Raffel et al. 2020a
, have become powerful tools for various natural language processing tasks, including text simplification. These models are trained on massive amounts of text data, enabling them to understand and generate human-like language. When fine-tuned for text simplification, LLMs can simplify complex sentences by rephrasing them, reducing sentence length, and substituting difficult words with simpler alternatives while maintaining the original meaning.

Estonian ATS has only been explored at the bachelor thesis level, with two attempts: one focused on lexical simplification using the Estonian WordNet and cosine similarity, and the other on syntactic simplification, guided by English structures.

In this study, we benchmark two models for Estonian text simplification. The first model is based on neural machine translation, treating ATS as a translation task. The second model involves fine-tuning the LLaMA language model on the Estonian Simplification Dataset. While standard metrics such as BLEU and SARI are often used to evaluate text simplification models, they do not capture essential aspects like readability and meaning preservation. This study combines these metrics with manual evaluations to provide a comprehensive assessment

Building the Estonian Simplification Dataset

In this section, we outline the process of constructing the Estonian Simplification Dataset.
The primary goal is to create a sufficiently large dataset to effectively train deep neural network models.
Given the required scale and the lack of any existing simplification dataset for Estonian,
it is clear that manually compiling this dataset would be impractical.

Where possible, the dataset is aligned with simplified English sentences, and we also aim to establish guidelines
to capture linguistic phenomena specific to Estonian, suitable for simplification.

The first source for our dataset is the Turk corpus
Xu et al. 2016
, which contains 2,359 original Wikipedia sentences,
each paired with eight simplified versions created via Amazon Mechanical Turk.
This corpus provides a variety of simplification styles, including lexical substitution and sentence rephrasing.
Since it is widely used in text simplification research, we manually translated relevant portions into Estonian,
focusing on sentences that preserved a simplification relationship.

The second source is Wikipedia Data Set 2.0
Kauchak 2013
,
which contains 167,689 aligned Wikipedia sentences.
Small samples from this dataset were translated using machine translation and then corrected by Estonian annotators.
However, the dataset ultimately proved unreliable, as many sentences lack coherence.

To ensure consistency and reproducibility of simplification techniques, we developed the Estonian Simplification Guidelines, inspired in part by the Newsela Corpus
Xu et al. 2015
.
The Newsela Corpus includes 1,130 news articles, each rewritten at four different levels to cater to readers of varying abilities,
with professional editors ensuring quality and readability.
Although the corpus is not freely available, it can be accessed for academic or research purposes.

Our Estonian Simplification Guidelines consist of a detailed 10-page document outlining specific simplification techniques tailored to Estonian.
These guidelines focus on three main areas:

•

Grammatical complexity:
Simplifying cases, tenses, and grammatical moods.

•

Syntactic complexity:
Reducing sentence length, splitting long sentences, and rearranging word order for clarity.

•

Lexical simplification:
Replacing complex words with simpler synonyms or broader terms to improve comprehension.

Following the approach used in English-language research, the primary source for Estonian simplification data is Estonian Wikipedia. Articles are downloaded from the latest dumps, split into individual sentences, and only sentences with more than 15 words are selected for simplification.
To efficiently generate many simplified sentences, we employed GPT-4.0
OpenAI 2023
, a state-of-the-art language model with a high ranking on the Chatbot Arena LLM Leaderboard.
Although generating data with GPT-4.0 incurs costs, they are significantly lower than those associated with human simplification.

The model, pretrained on extensive datasets, can be fine-tuned to adopt specific personas, enabling it to emulate professional tones and styles tailored to various roles, such as technical support or educational tasks. Studies have shown that persona-based prompting techniques outperform other prompting methods and are widely adopted in practice
Pataranutaporn et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
.

In our research, we developed a variety of prompting templates incorporating different simplification strategies. These templates were tested across various instances of GPT-4.0.
The initial templates did not incorporate any persona style, while later versions introduced persona agents focused on syntactic and lexical simplification.
After experimentation, we found that the best results were achieved by first applying lexical simplification, followed by syntactic simplification. Although recent research has raised questions about the effectiveness of persona-based prompting
Zheng et al. 2024
, we found that, for this specific task, agent prompts are indeed more effective.

Benchmarking Simplification Models for English Language

To select an appropriate baseline model for Estonian text simplification, we benchmarked established English simplification models with accessible and usable code. For this benchmarking, we followed the approach outlined by
Zhang and Lapata 2017
.

The first model to benchmark leverages machine translation (MT) techniques for text simplification. While MT systems are traditionally used to translate text between languages, the underlying technology—specifically sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) models and transformer architectures—can be adapted to translate complex sentences into simpler forms within the same language.

One foundational architecture for text simplification
Nisioi et al. 2017
, capable of lexical simplification and content reduction, is based on the OpenNMT framework. OpenNMT
Klein et al. 2018
uses a Seq2Seq architecture with an Encoder-Decoder structure, typically utilizing Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) such as LSTMs or GRUs. The Attention Mechanism in OpenNMT allows the model to focus on relevant parts of the input, improving the quality of the simplified output.

The tested MT simplification uses OpenNMT-py, built on PyTorch, for flexibility and integration with modern deep learning tools.

The model architecture includes:

•

2 layers with 500 hidden units per layer,

•

A 0.3 dropout rate to prevent overfitting,

•

Training over 15 epochs using the Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer with a 0.1 learning rate.

The DRESS model
Zhang and Lapata 2017
is a sentence simplification algorithm based on reinforcement learning. Unlike traditional supervised learning models, DRESS optimizes simplification by continuously updating itself through feedback, aiming to maximize the quality of the simplified output.

The model utilizes a pretrained encoder-decoder architecture, where the encoder processes the complex input sentence and generates a latent representation, while the decoder generates a simpler version of the sentence. Reinforcement learning is applied to fine-tune this process, using a reward function that encourages the model to balance grammatical correctness, meaning preservation, and simplification. By optimizing the rewards, DRESS can iteratively improve the quality of the generated simplified sentences.

This reinforcement learning approach makes DRESS more adaptive, allowing it to refine its simplifications over time by leveraging feedback from the reward system.

The last model for English text simplification involves fine-tuning the T5 (Text-To-Text Transfer Transformer) model
Raffel et al. 2020c
.
The T5 model uses a unified text-to-text framework, treating all NLP tasks as text generation problems. It leverages transfer learning by pre-training on the C4 dataset
Raffel et al. 2020b
and fine-tuning on specific tasks, enhancing its generalization capabilities. T5 follows an encoder-decoder architecture, based on the original transformer model.

The T5 model is fine-tuned over 5 epochs using the Adam optimizer. The model uses its own tokenizer and preprocesses the data with control tokens that guide the simplification process. These tokens, such as character length ratio and Levenshtein distance, help improve output quality.

The data is fed to the model with the "simplify" instruction, enabling T5 to generate simplified text based on its pre-trained knowledge and the task-specific fine-tuning.

The above models were trained on WikiSmall
Zhu et al. 2010
, a benchmark corpus containing aligned complex and simple sentences from both standard and Simple English Wikipedias. The training set consists of 89,042 pairs of complex and simplified sentences, while the test set includes 100 sentence pairs.

The evaluation of the three models was conducted using the following metrics:

•

BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy Score)
Papineni et al. 2002
: Measures the accuracy of machine-generated text by comparing it to reference translations based on the overlap of n-grams. It does not award deletion, but it is a good metric to measure how similar the machine-generated text is to the reference sentences.

•

SARI (System Output Against References and Input)
Xu et al. 2016
: It is a metric meant purely to measure text simplicity. Evaluates text simplification by comparing system-generated text with both the original input and simplified references, rewarding good edits and penalizing unnecessary changes.

•

FKGL (Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level)
Flesch 1948
: Assesses the readability of a text by estimating the U.S. school grade level required to understand it. It is calculated based on word count in a sentence and syllable count in words. It can give valuable info about occurrence of sentence splitting and word substitution.

4
Simplification with Fine-Tuned LLaMA

LLaMA
Touvron et al. 2023
is a family of advanced LLMs developed by Meta with a focus on efficiency and scalability. Despite being smaller than many competing models, LLaMA delivers high performance, making it particularly suitable for use in resource-constrained environments. Trained on publicly available multilingual data, LLaMA is highly versatile and can be applied to a variety of tasks, such as text generation, summarization, translation, and text simplification. Additionally, LLaMA allows users to fine-tune the model for specific applications.
LLaMA 3.1, the latest iteration in the series, that in principle should provide enhanced performance and efficiency over previous versions. Available in configurations with up to 65 billion parameters, the model is optimized for high computational efficiency, making it an ideal choice for AI tasks in environments with limited computational resources. The models are trained on a diverse dataset of publicly available text in multiple languages, focusing on high-quality content from sources such as books, scientific papers, and web data. The Meta Models have been trained on Estonian, although Meta has not disclosed the exact sources used for the training data.

The process of fine-tuning the LLaMA models (Llama 3.0 and Llama 3.1 with 8 billion parameters) for Estonian sentence simplification was carried out using the Estonian Simplification Dataset
2
. The steps involved are outlined below:

1.

Loading the Pre-Trained Model
: The pre-trained LLaMA models and tokenizer were first loaded. These components, already trained on multilingual data, provide a robust foundation for further fine-tuning to adapt to the specific requirements of Estonian sentence structures.

2.

Utilizing the Unsloth Library
: The Unsloth library

https://github.com/unslothai/unsloth
accelerates and optimizes fine-tuning for large language models like LLaMA by efficiently managing GPU memory and leveraging mixed-precision training through Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and QLoRA frameworks. These frameworks help reduce computational requirements while maintaining model performance, aligning with the library’s purpose to optimize and accelerate training processes.

3.

Training Process
: The fine-tuning process involved adjusting key hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and the number of training epochs.

The models were fine-tuned on Estonian sentence pairs, learning to transform complex sentences into simpler versions. Both the fine-tuned model and the tokenizer were then serialized for future use.

5
Evaluation

A subset of 100 sentences was randomly selected from the Estonian Simplification Dataset, and these sentences were manually simplified by two native Estonian speakers, creating the gold standard for evaluating the simplification models. This gold standard serves as the reference point against which the OpenNMT and LLaMA-based simplification algorithms are evaluated.

The evaluation consisted of two complementary approaches. In the automatic evaluation, BLEU and SARI scores are calculated to measure the quality of the simplified sentences compared to the gold standard. As stated before BLEU focuses on comparing n-gram overlap between the machine-generated simplifications and the reference, while SARI evaluates how well the simplified versions retain meaning and simplify sentence structure.

In the manual evaluation, two independent annotators rated a subset of the simplified sentences, focusing on aspects such as grammaticality, meaning preservation, and readability.

5.1
Automatic Evaluation

Neural machine translation algorithms like OpenNMT, used for text simplification, require more training data than is currently available in the Estonian Simplification Dataset.

To enhance the performance of OpenNMT for the Estonian language, the English Wiki-Small Corpus has been automatically translated into Estonian using the Tartu NLP MT service
Korotkova and Fishel 2024
.

Llama 3.0 and Llama 3.1 were qualitatively evaluated by fine-tuning them on the Estonian Simplification Dataset, excluding the gold standard, and applying them to simplify the original sentences from the gold standard. Llama 3.1 demonstrated slightly better performance and was therefore selected for the final evaluation.

Figure 2:
Comparison of Model Performance on BLEU, SARI, and FKGL Metrics for Llama 3.1 and OpenNMT

As in the English case, training was conducted with the OpenNMT-py library, using the same parameters except for an increased vocabulary size of 60,000 with vocabulary sharing enabled. Vocabulary sharing allowed better handling of tokens unique to the input set. During training, 12 checkpoints were saved, all later evaluated to select the best-performing model and prevent overfitting.
The fine tuning parameters for fine-tuning LLaMA 3.1 were configured as follows: a batch size of 8 per device with gradient accumulation over 2 steps, and a total of 500 training steps with a warmup of 100 steps. The learning rate was set to 0.00005, using the AdamW optimizer in 8-bit mode and cosine learning rate scheduling. Weight decay was applied at 0.01 to regularize the model, with mixed precision enabled via FP16. Logging occurred every 50 steps, and a fixed seed of 42 ensured reproducibility.
The pretrained model and tokenizer have been serialized for use in simplifying the sentences in the test set.

The figure
2
compares Llama 3.1 and OpenNMT models based on BLEU, SARI, and FKGL scores. OpenNMT achieves a slightly higher BLEU score (30.05 vs. 27.04), indicating closer alignment with reference texts, but Llama 3.1 performs better on SARI (49.72 vs. 47.43), suggesting it produces more effective simplifications by adding, deleting, or modifying content to improve readability. Llama 3.1 also has a marginally lower FKGL score (8.71 vs. 9.02), implying its outputs may be slightly easier to read.
The automatic comparison reveals no clear winner, as both algorithms show similar performance based on standard metrics. Therefore, we should turn to a manual comparison for a definitive result.

This criterion evaluates how well the simplified text retains the original meaning and important information.

•

0

•

1
: The meaning is poorly preserved, with significant loss of information.

•

2

Simplification (S) 0-4

This criterion evaluates how effectively the text has been simplified. It considers whether the text is shorter, uses simpler verbs, and removes unnecessary information.

6
Conclusions

This study explored two approaches to Estonian text simplification: a neural machine translation model using OpenNMT and a fine-tuned large language model, LLaMA. Given the limited resources for Estonian ATS, we created the Estonian Simplification Dataset by combining translated data and GPT-4.0-generated simplifications. The experimental results show that the LLaMA model, fine-tuned on this dataset, consistently outperforms OpenNMT across key criteria, including readability, grammaticality, and meaning preservation.

The evaluation showed that standard metrics, such as BLEU and SARI, were insufficient to determine a clear winner between OpenNMT and LLaMA. While BLEU scores marginally favored OpenNMT, reflecting closer alignment with reference texts, SARI scores suggested that LLaMA might better capture the simplification process by adding, deleting, or altering content for readability. However, neither metric alone fully encapsulated critical aspects of simplification quality, such as meaning preservation and readability. These findings underscore the limitations of automated metrics and the necessity of manual evaluation.

The manual evaluations highlighted LLaMA’s superior performance, particularly in maintaining the original meaning and applying effective simplification techniques, such as sentence splitting and lexical substitution. These findings underscore the potential of large language models for handling low-resource languages, with fine-tuning proving effective in adapting pre-trained models to the specific linguistic and structural features of Estonian.

This research contributes to the underexplored area of Estonian ATS, demonstrating that LLMs, supported by targeted persona prompting and data resources, can achieve meaningful simplifications in a low resource language.
Future work will focus on expanding the dataset and incorporating additional human corrections of the simplified sentences. Furthermore, this research lays the groundwork for exploring document-level simplification for Estonian.
