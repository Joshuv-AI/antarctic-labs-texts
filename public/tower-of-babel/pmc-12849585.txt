How do clinical networks operationalize safety and quality stewardship? A qualitative study of Australian clinical networks
Authors: Jade Hart, Lucio Naccarella, Helen Dickinson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth system stewardship involves steering and oversight through authoritative vision-setting, effectively intervening and influencing within the health system, and placing high-value on evidence. Governments apply a multitude of strategies to assure the safety and quality of clinical care. Governments have predominately relied upon hierarchy and markets to influence health systems, but both have demonstrated limitations resulting in increased interest in networks. Clinical networks are groups that traverse institutional and professional boundaries to define and support the adoption of expectations for evidence-based care. Well-established across Australia, the unique contribution of clinical networks to stewardship and safety and quality aims has yet to be examined.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted to examine how Australian clinical networks operationalize stewardship. Methods involved interviews at the national level and comparative case study of three clinical networks within the state of Victoria. Semi-structured interviews were conducted involving government public service representatives, network managers, leads and members (n = 47). A blended deductive and inductive thematic analytical approach was applied.ResultsFindings show that the execution of safety and quality stewardship must rely on a binding structure, such as networks, to coordinate diverse actors whose behaviours and decisions ultimately affect clinical care. Networks collaborate to develop a shared vision for services, placing reliance on socially mediated influence to motivate actors to act in accordance with the vision. Yet safety and quality problems can be wicked and opaque, necessitating the use of diverse evidence types to understand problems and develop solutions that can be implemented. Networks balance evidence quality and applicability to context, elevating the importance of capability in evidence use.ConclusionsEmpirical contributions show the necessity of networks to execute safety and quality stewardship as a shared responsibility of actors. Stewardship is not a function of governments alone. Findings show the necessity of motivation at the level of the individual to shape understanding, behaviours and quality of actor decision-making. Evidence of all types retain an instrumental role in building knowledge as a form of influence. This research argues that health system stewardship, networks and evidence use need to be considered in an interdependent way to realize safety and quality aims.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-025-01433-6.Keywords: Health system stewardship, Safety and quality, Clinical networks, Evidence use

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) considers that at the most strategic level, health system goals are founded on improvements to population health, health system responsiveness and fairness of financial contribution [1]. Understandings of health system performance expand on this notion of responsiveness through attention to safety, effectiveness, efficiency, timeliness, consumer experience and equity [2]. There is a need to steward the system and coordinate actors with these strategic intentions in mind.

Networked health system stewardship models are being increasingly relied upon to engage actors as a collective to deliver system oversight to address complex and wicked problems. Problems can be viewed as being critical, tame or wicked in nature and each necessitates a different type of response [3]. Specifically, critical problems, such as clinical treatment of a heart attack, require a rapid command response. Tame problems are more complex than critical problems but are suited to planning that necessitates a management response. Wicked problems, such as reducing rates of obesity, depend on distributed leadership across a system [3, 4]. Problems of these types have been shown to be not as amenable to hierarchical command and market-based strategies predominately relied upon by governments [4]. Networks are suited to wicked problems [4].

Networks provide a structure to solve problems of a shared interest. Literature explains that the unique contribution of networks is that they organize actors on the basis of the principle of self-determination and this places importance on relationships [5–7]. In Australia, clinical networks are a model of intersectoral collaboration that are tasked to deliver quality functions, mobilizing diverse actors to examine complex safety and quality problems, and shape the design and implementation of improvements in clinical care across the health system [8]. Despite long-standing Australian clinical network practice and research programmes, continued “puzzlement” as to what clinical networks have been established to achieve remains [9–11, p. 1]. Understanding of the rationale and intended contribution of networks to safety and quality aims remain a source of the confusion. Literature cautions that cultures of cynicism and blame can emerge in the absence of adequate safety and quality stewardship capability [12]. Hence, it remains necessary to understand the unique features of networks to deliver health system stewardship functions in support of safety and quality aims.

This qualitative study sought to elucidate how understandings of health system stewardship are operationalized by clinical networks in Australia. In this paper, insights derived from semi-structured interviews conducted at the national level and within the state of Victoria explore the factors that influence the execution of safety and quality stewardship. This paper observes the prominence of unique forms of influence given the inherent self-determination of system actors. It is within this context that evidence and evidence use must be understood.

Introduction

The WHO defines stewardship as “the careful and responsible management of the well-being of the population” [13, p. 3]. Stewardship remains the responsibility of government, grounded in ethical conduct, effective use of resources and a focus on outcomes [14]. Government stewardship seeks to assure trust in and legitimacy of the execution of responsibilities to citizens with regard to public services, elevating attention to the credibility of decision-making processes [14].

The WHO conceptualizes health system functions as being inclusive of service provision, financing, resource generation and stewardship [13, 14]. From this, there are six stewardship functions that encompass vision-setting, system alignment and the use of policy instruments. The WHO framework has been considered a universal guide that has been designed for application to health systems around the world, a leading framework that has been researched and most often utilized across diverse health system contexts [15–19]. Understandings of quality stewardship establish greater connection to safety and quality tasks through the identification of implementation strategies and approaches for each stewardship function, whereby efforts are understood as a threefold focus on setting priorities and standards, organization and monitoring action, and assuring improvement in outcomes [20]. Effective implementation of these tasks provides a means to mitigate the risk of the potential pitfalls of quality stewardship that threaten the ability to adequately enact health system oversight [12].

The application of stewardship to health care emphasizes strong oversight and accountability, and that fundamentally, the health system can be influenced by rules and normative values [21]. However, a “steward cannot influence such factors by acting alone, and must involve other actors if positive change is to occur” [13, p. 8]. Partnerships, collaborations and coalitions among actors are required to advance a steward’s vision for health that places trust in health system participants [14]. As complex wicked problems are a product of long-standing conflicts and tensions over time, there exists a case for networked stewardship models in offering a means to resolve these conflicts [22].

Literature explains that the means to address problems affecting health professionals, institutions and systems have generally relied upon either hierarchy or markets, but more recently through networks [5, 6, 23, 24]. The three means of organizing are well-established in health care. For example, medical professional dominance relies on hierarchical command within a clinical team in treatment decision-making, where critical problems, such as clinical treatment of a heart attack, require a rapid command response. In contrast, an understanding of health care as a market applies principles and assumptions to influence consumer decision-making, such as through changes in the price of health services. Hierarchy and markets are suited to critical and tame problems, whereby established relationships are based in dominance and subordination of unequal actors, necessitating a management response [5]. However, other responses are necessary to more complex problems.

Networks provide a structure to solve problems of shared interest to actors. The WHO describes networks as “a grouping of individuals, organizations and agencies organized on a non-hierarchical basis around common issues or concerns, which are pursued proactively and systematically, based on commitment and trust” [25, p. 361]. Networks are suited to the examination of problems that are of a more wicked nature, problems for which hierarchy and market-based responses are known to be less effective at solving [4]. Wicked problems, such as reducing rates of harm to patients by assuring the safety and quality of care, are known to depend on a distributed leadership response across a system [3, 4, 26]. Hence, networks seek to empower actors in accordance with the principle of self-determination [5–7]. Social features such as trust, merit and egalitarianism gain prominence within networks [6]. Therefore, networks are positioned as an alternative means to address safety and quality stewardship aims.

Clinical networks are a mechanism for improving safety and quality of health services in Australia. The Australian health care system continues to grapple with the challenge of assuring safety and quality of health services within the context of split roles and responsibilities for health, devolution of powers through the decentralization of health services and parallel public and private health systems, including not-for-profit service providers practice [27]. Health services such as hospitals are decentralized and governed by independent management boards and executives [28]. To illustrate, the state of Victoria contains an estimated resident population of approximately 6.5 million people, with the population largely residing in the capital city area of Greater Melbourne [29]. Victoria has over 300 hospitals and health services [30]. Primary care services, including general practice services, are predominately delivered through the private sector, which places reliance on partnerships between this sector and Australian state government funded hospitals and health services [31]. Victoria’s devolved governance arrangements preceded national health reforms, with 85 Local Hospital Networks in place at the time of study.

In Australia, clinical networks have been a prevalent and long-standing feature that have been established in some Australian states for over two decades. Clinical networks serve as “voluntary clinician groupings that aim to improve clinical care and service delivery using a collegial approach to identify and implement a range of strategies” [8, p. 16]. In exploring the wicked issues that influence the safety and quality of care, clinical networks sought to provide a mechanism for clinicians at the “coal face” to self-determine strategies for “which they would be otherwise subjected” through hierarchical and institutional approaches [24, p. 399, 32–34]. Clinical networks focus on safety and quality goals that are specific to clinical specialities (e.g. paediatrics, cardiology and emergency medicine). A clinical network engages individuals relevant to these disciplines, with individuals recruited on a flexible and voluntary basis to undertake roles as illustrated in Table 1 [23].

Whilst the peer-reviewed literature shows that health system stewardship and the WHO framework have been applied to improve services (e.g. cancer screening, public health, mental health services and bio-banking services), literature is scant with regard to the unique contribution of networks as models of intersectoral collaboration in operationalizing stewardship in healthcare [16, 18, 40, 41]. To illustrate, the Australian health system has been credited as one of the most fragmented health systems in the world [42]. Despite this, the study of health system stewardship in Australia using the WHO framework is limited [17]. As health systems become increasingly fragmented through devolved governance and service decentralization, there is increasing need to understand the unique position of networks to influence in contexts where greater decision-making occurs at arm’s length from governments.

Methods

Research approach

There are several factors that contribute to the research approach applied. Literature shows that the study of stewardship using the WHO framework has been mostly conceptual in nature, placing reliance on survey methods to examine health system stewardship and evidence use by stewards as tacitly understood concepts [14, 16, 43]. There remain conceptual and practical considerations associated with stewardship measurement and evaluation using the WHO framework, with literature highlighting limits on the study of concepts as mutually exclusive and via certain research methods such as close-ended questionnaires [14]. Research must continue at a scale and pace to enhance understandings of stewardship in diverse health system contexts.

Literature acknowledges that actor understanding of study concepts is contextually specific and constructed, which necessitates comprehensive examination of how these concepts apply in practice. Scholars highlight that “seldom do we explore whether a group of actors working in a particular context have the same situation in mind” [44, p. 834]. This quotation prompts attention to actor understandings of study phenomena, whereby the multiplicity of constructions of phenomena contribute to challenges in scholarly research and practical application.

Existing clinical networks research is largely descriptive in nature, with emphasis on evaluation-based study. Yet literature cites the existence of fundamental conceptual questions related to clinical networks objectives for both scholars and clinical network members alike [9]. Summative evaluation efforts have yet to examine in depth the underlying causal mechanisms that explain the findings of research into the determinants of network effectiveness and the predictors of network success [8, 11, 37, 45]. In fact, the task of evaluating the effectiveness and sustainability of networks has been described as a wicked problem in and of itself [46]. Literature points to limitations in conceptual understandings of the phenomena of clinical networks.

Accordingly, this research is aligned with constructionism in its epistemic position, recognizing the importance of interactions between subjects and objects in the construction of reality. Constructionism allows for the examination of the interplay between objects and subjects [47]. There is provision for multiple realities in a given context. Accordingly, we apply an interpretivist approach to advance understanding of safety and quality stewardship and clinical networks. This research is interested in ascertaining what and how different features influence evidence use. It seeks to understand why network members act as they do and how they interact with evidence when tasked to steward safety and quality.

Data collection and analysis

The research design applies a sequential approach through the conduct of two studies. Research involved semi-structured interviews at the national level and within the state of Victoria, including the comparative case study of three clinical networks. The use of a sequenced research design enabled the examination of study phenomena with increasing specificity of inquiry.

Interpretivist qualitative research methods include documentary analysis, focus groups, observation and semi-structured interview [48, 49]. Individual interviews were advantageous over other group methods, such as focus groups, given the breadth and complexity of study phenomena. The benefit of individual interviews over focus groups was that it allowed for personalized reflection and exploration of phenomena without the elevated risk of social desirability bias in the professional group setting.

Firstly, a national qualitative study allowed for familiarization with study phenomena. National qualitative study provided initial orientation with the diversity of contextually based understandings of safety and quality stewardship, clinical networks and evidence use via the perspectives of government agency or equivalent staff across four diverse Australian state health systems. Six semi-structured interviews were conducted involving eight participants with representation from all four Australian states with an active clinical network programme at the time of data collection, namely New South Wales, Queensland, Western Australia and Victoria. Selection involved non-random sampling of representatives from Victoria, Western Australia, New South Wales and Queensland. Potential participants were identified through a review of publicly available information and telephone contact with government departments. Invitations were sent by the research team. From this, individuals self-selected to participate in the semi-structured interviews. National semi-structured interview participant roles were a blend of director/senior manager and network manager (with equal representation from the two role groups). Departmental representatives with general or network-specific oversight for clinical networks participated in this research. Participants had been involved with clinical networks for an average of 5.8 years (6 months to 13 years). The educational background of participants spanned nursing, allied health and health administration.

Secondly, a qualitative study was conducted within the state of Victoria. The state of Victoria was selected given the features of devolved governance and service decentralization that are conducive to stewardship models. The study permitted detailed and context-specific exploration of study phenomena through the experience of clinical network members and government agency staff. The Victorian qualitative study involved semi-structured interviews with departmental representatives with oversight for the Victorian clinical networks programme, case study clinical network leads and managers, and other departmental representatives as clinical network partners (generally or case study clinical network specific). Potential participants were identified through engagement with departmental representatives and network managers. Invitations were sent by the research team and invitees self-selected to participate. The educational background of participants spanned medical, nursing, allied health and health administration.

To enhance depth of understanding, comparative case study analysis was undertaken with three Victorian clinical networks that differed in scope and maturity. A purposeful case selection strategy was applied. In defining the selection strategy and consistent with literature, it was desirable to establish diversity of cases across clinical network categories (i.e. network type and maturity) for the purposes of enhancing contrast [50, 51]. At the time of study there were nine Victorian clinical networks that were potential cases, of which three clinical networks were invited and agreed to participate [52]. Semi-structured interviews were conducted involving 40 participants. Data collection occurred between May 2016 and May 2018. Additional file 1 contains the interview guides.

The state of Victoria was selected given the features of the devolved governance model. The Victorian health system approach to the decentralization of health services is distinct from other Australian states. There are distinctive contextual features associated with the Victorian health system that necessitate a reliance on models of stewardship.

Interviews were conducted in-person or via telephone. Plain language statements and consent forms were circulated in advance of the interview. Transcripts were prepared with the assistance of the web-based software Transcribe. Data were de-identified during the analysis and write-up stage of the study. Following this, transcribed data were imported into and systematically analysed using NVivo 12.

A codebook was used, refined over the course of the two studies, in accordance with a blended deductive and inductive analytical approach. Thematic analysis relied on deductive methods and the use of a codebook, which was grounded in literature and guiding conceptual understandings, followed by inductive methods, which supported the refinement of the codebook. A combined deductive and inductive coding approach was also suited to the exploration of study phenomena and research questions. Analytics methods were consistent with the practice guidance that specify expectations for rigour in the application of a blended deductive and inductive approach [53]. Deductive coding techniques were guided, but not constrained, by the literature review and conceptual framework, and were combined with inductive analysis that allowed for emerging concepts from the data consistent with an exploratory approach.

Thematic analysis was suited to theoretical orientation and research design, providing a means to ensure flexibility and facilitate the derivation of socially constructed meaning within and across cases. Case study designs enable the appreciation of “contemporary phenomena within the real-life context” [54, p. 18]. The inclusion of three cases in this study allowed for cross-case examination of similarities and differences to establish a means to corroborate findings across cases to establish explanatory theory consistent with replication logic [55–57]. Accordingly, in the conduct of analysis, consideration was made for features across and within case study clinical networks. Coding practices achieved saturation in codes [58]. These activities established the basis for the generation of theme development, with themes understood as expressions of shared meaning, to form the basis of empirical findings [59]. Theoretical saturation was achieved across case study clinical networks [60]. The details of results across case study clinical networks are published [52].

This study was a component of a broader research approach that supported integrated theory building. The broader study included diversity of research methods (e.g. documentary analysis) and engagement of participants through different methods (i.e. semi-structured interview and Q-methodology study). Further details on research methods and findings, including Q-methodology study, are published [52, 61, 62].

Results

This research elucidates understandings of how safety and quality stewardship is operationalized by clinical networks in Australia. We find that network management and participatory arrangements are a means to achieve reach both into government agencies and among disparate actors to achieve a shared focus on safety and quality. We identify the unique approach used by networks to deliver health system stewardship functions and maximize strategic forms of influence. We determine that evidence is an important means for shaping actor understanding, behaviours and decision-making over time, necessitating that networks assure credible stewardship of the evidence base.

Clinical networks connecting among otherwise disparate system actors to advance safety and quality aims

Australian clinical networks are found to be a formal means through which governments foster engagement with actors to stimulate attention to and collective improvement of safety and quality problems. Government agencies are responsible for sponsoring clinical networks, including the design of network management arrangements, including the provision of funding and other resources. Interviews reveal that the shift towards devolved governance and service decentralization across Australia strengthened the delineation of accountabilities between governments and health services as independent entities, necessitating that government actors interact with clinical practice and service provision in new ways. Semi-structured interviews reveal the necessity of clinical networks to governments in bridging professional and institutional divides within the health system, particularly those created by devolved governance. Clinical networks stewardship functions with regard to intersectoral collaboration are found reflect these aims. As explained by Participant 11, “What I understand is that they’re now given the authority to say, ‘we need something. We need guidelines here. We need new legislation. This is unsafe.’ Whereas I don’t know that we had that opening for them to make those demands. I think they are able to make demands now that anything that is not safe, that they need to take a stand on, and we as the department, need to actually make an allowance for it” (Participant 11 – department/clinical). This quotation highlights the importance of convening other disparate health system actors to influence understanding and strengthen the focus on safety and quality.

Government agencies partner with clinical networks to elicit information, advice and recommendations from the sector. The partnered approach is found to be necessary to establish a shared understanding of problems and their root causes in ways not afforded by an individual professional, institutional or government perspective. Participants from medical, nursing and allied health backgrounds, as well as those from government agencies valued access to other actors through the formality of network structures. One interviewee explains the benefits of early and sustained clinical engagement, specifically, “I’m not suggesting that the clinicians have all the answers, but I do think that having a clinician involved in those policy and funding decisions actually helps the people with the financial instruments direct the money to where it’s going to have the most benefit in terms of improving quality, and safety, and equity” (Participant 13 – network/clinical). Findings reveal concerns with the outcomes of historical government policy and funding determinations that were seen to constrain clinical practice improvement and model of care innovation, which in some instances resulted in unintended consequences for patients. Participants contrasted the clinical network approach with prior ministerial taskforce arrangements that were limited in that they required to follow a tightly prescribed terms of reference that limited the flexibility of actors to examine a system-wide view and risking a top-down approach from government. Participant 20 acknowledges the resistance to top-down approaches and explains the importance of establishing a link to the role of evidence in supporting the shared understanding of the issues and collective decision-making, “top-down always implies to me to make someone do something that they may not have naturally done. I think the ambition really is to arrive at a point where we’re all actually seeing the same information and being able to participate in the decision” (Participant 20 – department/clinical).

We observe that network leads are mainly of a clinical background spanning senior medical, nursing and allied health and are appointed to formal network positions with government agencies. Network leads are figureheads who represent the clinical network and its associated medical specialities, retaining a whole-of-system purview rather than that of a single institution. Network leads are creative, entrepreneurial and amenable to emerging care practice developments. We observe an emphasis on being active, whereby, “The network lead needs to be out there, collaborative, assisting to be able to create the best influence that it can. You are part of the department, so you need to be quite savvy. You need to be a good figurehead. A collaborative figurehead.” (Participant 11 – government agency/clinical). Participant 20 (government agency/clinical) describes network leads as “chameleons”, emphasizing the need for leads to represent both the sector and the government, tailoring approaches to engage multiple actors. These facilitative leadership skills applied are found to be unique compared with those relied upon within clinical practice and government, which are predominately hierarchical in nature.

Participants emphasize the importance of clinical networks being inclusive and problem-solving oriented. Networks convene experts and champions in their respective field, such as senior medical practitioners with a comprehensive understanding of better practice developments locally, nationally and internationally. Clinical network arrangements are found to be responsive to the clinical practice, professional and institutional context. For example, leading practitioners and institutions are considered essential to involve in the network, whilst representatives from other interdependent areas such as rural health, palliative and end-of-life care, and emergency services can broaden the network’s perspective. Hence, clinical networks membership features are found to be inherently heterogeneous. Clinical networks rely on diversity of representation, spanning different sectors, disciplines and experience. Consumer knowledge and experience is also considered a skill. Participation in a clinical network offers a unique opportunity to contribute to safety and quality stewardship, whereas in substantive roles, such as for those in clinical practice, there was a perception of limited capacity to influence, given institutional-based hierarchies and decision-making structures.

Network manager knowledge and expertise are found to be central to the credibility of the network. Participants highlight the importance of network managers being skilled in the compilation and generation of evidence through stakeholder engagement, research and evaluation. Network managers need to be able to apply evidence in the development of models of care, clinical practice guidelines and position statements. Participant 13 explains the importance of the network manager being multi-skilled in their approach, “I mean the network manager is a key role, I think. Not only have they got to understand the sector, but they’ve also got to be the key driver…” (Participant 13 – network/clinical.) Effective network managers have skills in maximizing individual contributions, developing a vision and exerting influence to support implementation. One interviewee explains the necessity of managers having productive working relationships and knowing who the “movers and shakers are who can actually drive the changes” (Participant 13 – network/clinical). This quotation reveals an understanding that the capacity to realize change occurs beyond the network. Clinical networks are found to be strategic in understanding how to exert novel forms of influence across the health system to advance safety and quality aims.

Clinical network stewardship functions focus on vision-setting, influence, and evidence use

This research reveals that clinical networks undertake several functions and activities that align with the WHO framework. Clinical networks are found to serve as a means for intersectoral collaboration in support of vision-setting, influence and advocacy, and the compilation, dissemination and use of evidence to advance safety and quality. Clinical networks engage with actors as partners to operationalize health system stewardship through activities including clinical service planning (i.e. vision setting), quality improvement audit and assurance activities (i.e. good governance), and provision of advice to inform measures of system performance and other government policy guidance (i.e. influence and optimisation of instruments). Australian clinical network activities and output

The development of recommendations, in its various forms, are found to align with all six health system stewardship functions, and particularly vision-setting, influence and evidence use. We observe the importance of clinical leadership to the definition of the vision for health services by networks as stewards. The involvement of clinicians is found to be central to realizing legitimacy of the vision-setting process and attaining endorsement of network outputs by other actors. Semi-structured interviews highlight the need for effective network leads to have a strong focus on a commitment to safety and quality, the implementation of evidence-based clinical practice and overall systems improvement, including improvement of individual practice. Network leads draw from clinical and service experience but apply a whole-of-system view. Participant 8 describes expectations as, “Clinical champions with credibility. Whether they are medical, nursing, or allied health it doesn’t matter… That’s what leads a network.” (Participant 8 – network/clinical). As clinical networks are multidisciplinary and multisectoral in nature, we identify that network processes were contrary to the hierarchical nature within the clinical professions and clinical services, shifting the influence of the medical profession by allowing other health professions, consumers and others to actively contribute to decision-making. To illustrate, one clinician describes the importance of network activities to “keep us all honest and keep us all practising consistently” (Participant 29 – network/clinical). This quotation highlights the reliance on an appeal to professional values in order to motivate clinical actors.

Clinical networks rely on influence to increase motivation in the execution of safety and quality stewardship. Clinical network activities and outputs are found to be largely reliant on non-binding and soft powers and forms of influence, such as inspirational appeal and rational persuasion. As listed in Table 3, the implementation of models of care, clinical practice guidelines and position statements produced by clinical networks remain dependent on changes in behaviours and decision-making by professions, institutions and governments. Clinical networks are found to operate within established power structures, with variable means to authoritatively influence these. Clinical networks can be seen to risk lacking the necessary “muscle” to direct other actors (Participant 19 – network/managerial). Participant 1 describes the government department perspective, whereby active network membership offers a means for government departments to facilitate the likelihood of successful implementation of changes and improvements, “So the theory is, basically, if you bring them, people from the shop floor, right across that patient’s continuum, they will then start to influence policy, that policy will then get signed off, and when that policy then comes down for implementation, then the likelihood of them being implemented is higher because those same people actually put into it” (Participant 1 – departmental/clinical). This quotation highlights the importance of intersectoral collaboration to all health system actors to achieve influence and change.

We observe that governments retain health system stewardship responsibility for the use of policy, regulatory and funding instruments as forms of hard powers and influence, whereby network efforts may provide advice to inform their application. Hence, clinical networks are found to place greater emphasis on operationalizing health system stewardship functions related to influence and advocacy, good governance and realizing system alignment. One interviewee describes strengths of a partnered approach between a clinical network and government agencies, where despite a lack of formal powers to direct there can be “a flip between who leads and who supports” in advancing a collaborative programme of work (Participant 12 – network/clinical). Clinical networks operationalize health system stewardship in ways that may be necessarily opportunistic, informal and relationally driven. However, the risks of inadequate attention to policy, regulatory and funding instruments could be significant. The introduction of new methods to improve the safety and quality of health services can occur in the absence of authoritative policy to direct their use. Participant 19 explains the consequences where policy, regulatory and funding instruments are not optimized for safety and quality and a reactive approach where, “The only way in which we can hope to have real influence then is if a child dies. That’s at the end of the day what it would take to be able to have a conversation with that health service to say, ‘You can’t do this any more. You must now use these tools. If you’d used these tools this wouldn’t have happened’”. (Participant 19 – network/managerial).

Interviewees emphasize the integrity with which clinical network functions must be delivered to assure credibility. Here, the dissemination, compilation and use of evidence was critical to assuring the legitimacy with which clinical networks operationalize health system stewardship. Interviewees reveal the necessity of generating and synthesizing evidence with population-level safety and quality aims in mind (i.e. consideration for population-based clinical registries and health services datasets). As shown in Table 3, clinical networks use evidence to persuade actors of the necessity of change through state-wide benchmarking, facilitating the identification of trends and outliers, and the provision of a state-wide perspective not otherwise available to hospital clinicians, hospital heads of unit and governments. This research demonstrates that it is the sector-wide and whole-of-population purview that challenges actors to make decisions in ways that might differ from those made in the operational context. Networks engage clinicians of global significance, “experts at the top of the game” to conduct activities to a high standard for the benefit of the population (Participant 16 – network/clinical). Networks are considered neutral collective structures that differ in their adherence to professional, institutional and other forms of self-interest. Participant 1 describes the importance of the unique experience of consumers and carers to retain that population view, “When the consumer actually says ‘are you talking about me here? You want to hear what I have to say?’ it actually brings people back to reality. It is about the patient that we are doing this for, not about their own needs and wants, or their own institutions” (Participant 1 – department/clinical). For instance, clinical networks emphasize the importance of identifying and responding to trends in unwarranted clinical variation by developing evidence-based clinical practice guidelines and other quality improvement resources. The multidisciplinary and intersectoral nature of networks providing a unique working environment that fostered rationality in different ways. Hence, evidence served as a means to shape understanding over time, elevating the importance with which it enacts credible stewardship of the evidence base.

Clinical networks retain responsibility for credible stewardship of the evidence base

In operationalizing health system stewardship functions, clinical networks are found to be focused on safety and quality problems for which there lacked contextually relevant and high-quality research evidence that provided definitive advice on approach. The nature of these complex problems necessitates innovative approaches to the sourcing of different types of evidence to establish clarity and develop options. The health system was described as “data rich and information reasonable” (Participant 12 – network/clinical). Clinical networks are found to collate, commission or procure, and advocate for the requisite evidence. To do this, clinical networks foster localized research partnerships, commission quantitative data analysis and literature reviews, undertake professional survey and patient engagement strategies and partner with government-led analytical projects. To illustrate, in one network, clinical practice guidelines were maintained through commissioned arrangements from two partners: one paediatric teaching hospital and one primary care institution. From this, the two network partners undertook a programme of work with dedicated staff sourcing activities with a view to informing the development of network advice, which was subject to network governance committee endorsement.

Access to evidence, particularly quantitative evidence, experiential evidence and other grey literature reports (e.g. service reviews, government agency briefings and formal reports) is facilitated by the maturation of relationships with government agencies that are the custodians of evidence, such as health datasets, with government actors placed to support the interpretation of evidence to derive meaningful conclusions. Efforts are led by individual members on behalf of the collective, members being clinical champions interested in achieving population-level improvements and translating new discoveries into practice. Yet individual efforts are supported by collective input facilitated by network governance arrangements. A participant highlights, “health data is never really served to you on a platter and say, ‘Here’s a beautiful set of data we can analyse.’ It generally doesn’t work that way” (Participant 25 – department/non-clinical). For example, the development of state-wide clinical recommendations occurs in accordance with governance standards pertaining to areas such as working group selection and appointment, the sourcing and evaluation of higher levels of research evidence and the endorsement of outputs in accordance with majority and consensus-based decision-making by network members. These arrangements are essential to executing credible stewardship of the evidence base.

We find that a broad range of evidence is necessary to deliver health system stewardship functions with safety and quality aims in mind. Research evidence was found to be an important feature of the clinical network methodology. Commensurate with the maturation of a clinical network, it is commonplace for research evidence to expand from being medically focused to be inclusive of a broader multidisciplinary-based understanding of research evidence. Interviews also reveal the unique contribution of quantitative evidence to understandings of and accountability for patient needs and clinical practice outcomes across the health system. Clinical networks are found to use quantitative evidence in different ways as networks mature. For instance, newly established networks tend to focus on understanding of population health needs and service activity issues to seek collective agreement on priorities. Whilst established and maturing networks focus on using quantitative evidence to build a longitudinal and comparative perspective of performance in accordance with the defined model of care. The task is to establish a contemporary, comprehensive and cohesive perspective of care and mitigate the issues that arise with health system participants only seeing “a disjointed picture” of service (Participant 12 – network/clinical). Examples of datasets used by clinical networks include that of national and international clinical registries, hospital data collections and consumer surveys.

Yet findings identify the challenges of establishing a clear understanding of safety and quality problems solely through numeric terms given the risk of error and misinterpretation. Rather, insights garnered from quantitative evidence was often considered alongside other types of evidence, including the experiential evidence and grey literature. Experiential evidence is found to encompass individual perspectives, including that of consumers and professionals. Clinical networks have in place mechanisms to proactively engage to elicit experiential evidence to raise awareness, prompt attention and persuade, whilst prompting rational decision-making in support of safety and quality aims. One interviewee highlights the unique contribution of experiential evidence in its emotional appeal and power to motivate over logic-based approaches as “vignettes or the personal stories… they shift hearts as opposed to minds” (Participant 5 – government agency/managerial). The process of deliberating different types of sourced evidence involves being mindful of both the risks of data errors and the potential for misinterpretation, and the necessity to prompt an appeal to action.

Clinical networks use deliberative strategies to consider sourced evidence. Networks evaluate evidence against criteria, with methods of formality spanning the hierarchies of research evidence (e.g. Cochrane and GRADE methods), through to informal methods to examine the relevance of evidence to the local context. We observe the necessity of pragmatism in the approach. Clinical networks recognize the importance of trust, clear and respectful communication, recognize the contributions of diverse professional groups and patients, and aims to be non-hierarchical in interactions to ensure open discussion. Yet, deliberative processes that are ostensibly intended to be rational in their focus are also found to be prone to interpersonal issues and communication constraints. Yet clinical networks can encounter challenges with facilitating deliberative processes, such as in relation to KPI (key performance indicator) data, amongst a network of peers. As one participant explains, “to a certain extent we haven’t really solved that issue of what we do with outliers. All we can do at the moment is flag that and hope that the professional pride of the groups will drive their performance up” (Participant 13 – network/clinical). The dominance of senior medical practitioners and hospitals heads of unit is found to be prevalent. Participants reflect on the necessity of individual capabilities to operate in the stewardship context and examine issues beyond the realm of principal roles in clinical practice. One interviewee shares a concern of looking like a “twit” among peers, where as a doctor, “you go through university. You get trained in medical issues. You don’t get trained in politics. You get a little stuff in research and you don’t get any training in health systems or anything like that. Or business, we don’t get any training in funding, business, health systems” (Participant 22 – network/clinical). Individuals are recruited on the basis of their prior clinical expertise but must transmute this expertise into the requisite network capabilities to contribute to effective stewardship of the evidence base.

This research finds that clinical networks operationalize safety and quality stewardship in ways that are uniquely responsive to the structures of power and influence in a given health system context. Clinical networks work within the complexities of professional, institutional and government arrangements to draw actor attention to fundamental safety and quality aims. Governments are ultimately reliant on networks as means to deliver health system stewardship functions at the population level given the limitations and sensitivity of hard powers and influence to be effective, such as policy, regulatory and funding instruments. Yet, to do this, we find that network functions are delivered at a higher degree of subtlety and nuance than the simplicity of activities as described in Tables 1 and 2 might imply. Clinical networks apply a strategic approach to understanding safety and quality problems, developing a unified vision for services and identifying the breadth of opportunities to implement the vision. Clinical networks must assure the requisite capabilities and work within existing health system arrangements that may or may not be conducive to hard and soft powers and forms influence. We find that effective stewardship of the evidence base as essential to the network approach in strengthening clinical practice, management and policy decision-making.

Discussion

This research advances scholarly understanding of networked safety and quality stewardship models. The WHO draws a distinction between health system stewardship and service provision, with the role of stewards being to steer health system developments but not be directly responsible for service delivery [1]. The ultimate outcome is to achieve reach in and assure the quality of private interactions between a health professional and a consumer within an institutional setting [12]. Therefore, an organized policy and strategy is required to coordinate contributions from diverse actors that directly and indirectly influence these private interactions [63]. We identify three empirical contributions to the study of how clinical networks operationalize safety and quality stewardship. Contributions relate to the necessity of clinical networks to motivate autonomous actors to act in ways that accord with safety and quality aims, the reliance placed on strategic application of soft powers and forms of influence, and the importance of evidence stewardship to shape understanding, behaviours and quality of actor decision-making.

Firstly, this research contributes new understandings of the role of networks as a necessary means of health system stewardship within the context of limits to hierarchy and markets. The WHO health system stewardship functions place emphasis on intersectoral collaboration to achieve goals [13, 14]. Yet, the stewardship literature often conceptualizes there being one steward for a country, the National Ministry of Health [13, 14]. Findings challenge the understanding of there being a single steward. Indeed, Australia’s federated health system, devolved governance and service decentralization approach means that there can be no single national or state-wide mechanism responsible for health system oversight [27]. The international phenomenon of devolution and decentralization has been attributed to increases in the number of autonomous health system participants [64]. Networks, as structures that transcend traditional professional and institutional boundaries, are designed to support the execution of health system stewardship functions within fragmented systems and in contexts where significant decision-making occurs by autonomous entities at arm’s reach from governments. We argue the necessity of a shared recognition of the limits to the authority of government on the breadth of autonomous actors that contribute to the safety and quality of clinical care.

We propose that the practice of health system stewardship in support of safety and quality aims is not solely the function of governments. Rather, it is for governments to foster diverse network stewardship models that mobilize autonomous actors to actively contribute to the design and implementation of the vision for health services. Findings align with the peer-reviewed literature that emphasizes the importance of clinical leadership and input into government agency decision-making to assure legitimacy [24, 34, 65]. To illustrate, prior to the establishment of clinical networks in the Australian state of New South Wales, the extent of clinician mistrust and frustrations in government decision-making had reached “boiling point” [34, p. 597]. We show that clinical networks offer governments a means to strengthen its overall reach and rigour of processes in ways that are necessary to meeting its responsibilities to the population. We argue the necessity of networked stewardship models to motivate actors to make changes in ways that are not possible through government agency-driven hierarchical command and market intervention alone.

Secondly, an empirical contribution of this research is the unique capability of networked stewardship models to influence at the individual level. Literature explains that the effectiveness of networks assumes that they are provided with authority to enact intended functions [6]. However, clinical networks hold “no mandate to command services and practitioners to collaborate or implement” [32, p. 232]. Given health systems have traditionally relied on hierarchy and market-based responses, the authorizing environment for networks can be less transparent. Health system actors are able to make decisions that can directly or indirectly influence the safety and quality of clinical care. Yet, on the basis that a proportion of these decisions can be undeterred and unaffected by hard forms of influence (i.e. use of coercive powers), we argue the importance of soft powers and forms of influence on the individual. This research builds on this understanding to show how clinical network functions, which are found to align with the responsibilities of stewards, utilize diverse forms of influence.

We show how clinical networks exert influence across a health system through the delivery of workforce training and professional development, engagement with clinical and hospital leadership on issues and working relationships with government agency actors to input and shape public policy. The participatory and relational features of these methods of influence place attention on an individual as the unit of focus. Literature explains that stewardship assumes an intrinsic motivation, whereby individuals gain a shared sense of priorities, goals and incentives in accordance with altruistic intentions and in order to serve the collective [66]. In contrast, directive approaches that rely on hard forms of influence such as authority or coercion are grounded in an understanding that the inherent motivation of individuals must be extrinsic [66]. Findings add to literature regarding the merits of stewardship as a means to harmonize diverse interests and achieve a shared value on safety and quality [67]. Socially mediated forms of influence enables actors within complex systems to self-organize and self-determine in accordance with an intrinsic motivation for the safety and quality of clinical care.

The third empirical contribution of this research is the link between the role of evidence as a necessary means for stewards to influence. Stewards of population health have responsibilities to ensure optimal use of evidence [68]. Evidence is crucial to the capacity of stewards to oversee a system at risk of retaining “a void at the centre of the stewardship enterprise” [40, p. 400]. The risk of prevailing cultures of confusion, cynicism and blame remains in the absence of clarity with regard to the safety and quality stewardship model [12]. Networked stewards must have a comprehensive picture of safety and quality to deliver system oversight and steering. The health system stewardship literature acknowledges the relevance of functions relating to the compilation, dissemination and use of evidence, but findings strengthen the connection between evidence as the basis for exerting soft powers and forms of influence.

This research finds that what can influence the hearts and minds of an individual can vary, necessitating a broader definition of evidence that is inclusive of all types. The peer-reviewed literature explains that it is the processes of examination, deliberation and co-construction of the evidence by diverse actors that supports the establishment of shared understandings [69, 70]. Within these collective processes, contextually relevant evidence is assessed to show the applicability and acceptability of interventions within a given setting [71]. Findings align with theories of conceptual use of evidence that focus on using evidence to raise awareness, facilitate shared sense-making and establish institutional memory [72–74]. Research contributions strengthen the links between the networked stewardship models and the deployment of evidence as a strategic resource that seeks to coordinate influence over time. We argue the inter-connectedness of health system stewardship, networks and evidence use are a linked phenomena.

Finally, this research draws attention to the importance of research into networked stewardship models within fragmented health systems such as Australia. The WHO health system stewardship framework is a suitable framework to examine safety and quality on the basis that there is a recognition that it is a shared responsibility of health system actors. This research advances the understanding of the normative features that contribute to forms of influence. Trust has been shown to be relevant to markets as are diverse forms of organization [75]. Assessments of trustworthiness stem from threats to self-interest that bear relevance across hierarchy, markets and networks [6]. We find that the underlying model of safety and quality coordination and governance shapes the way in which use of evidence occurs to exert trust-based and control-based strategies to influence practitioners and institutions. Stewardship provides a means for health system participants to collaborate and influence in ways that place trust in professionals and institutions.

Therefore, this research represents a valid contribution to knowledge in ways that may assist governments in Australia and internationally address enduring questions of how to best organize health systems to advance safety and quality aims. This research provides new understandings of clinical network causal mechanisms in support of clinical practice improvement outcomes. Further research examining the impact of clinical network on safety and quality are warranted. The lack of consensus in standard evaluation theory and the limits of summative evaluation have been identified as constraining understanding as to why or how clinical network outcomes are achieved [9, 76]. Our contribution to understandings of networked models of safety and quality stewardship may contribute to new approaches to the study of impact.

Research limitations

We acknowledge the potential limitations of this research. This research is focused on Australian clinical networks that may differ to other networks in other health system settings or other forms of collaboratives or communities of practice (i.e. managed clinical networks in other settings that retain direct service provision and funding roles) [23]. The conduct of thematic analysis was consistent with better practice approaches to ensure legitimacy [59]. Finally, this research was nested in a broader study that applied a mixed methods design to examine research questions in accordance with an interpretivist orientation, which provided a means to strengthen overall research quality.

Literature explains that the complexity associated with the mixed methods design is attributed to the cumulative effect of needing to assure the quality of individual research methods, the implementation of methods and the overall integration and interpretation of results [77]. Quality considerations for the planning, undertaking, interpretation and dissemination of mixed methods have been defined, focusing attention to the credibility and trustworthiness of mixed research to establish legitimacy rather than validity [78–80]. This research ensured robustness in method planning, undertaking and interpretation across all stages, with attention placed to the sequential study and triangulation in the derivation of empirical contributions. In addition, an examination of the robustness of research methods applied was conducted using an established framework [81]. This research met or exceeded relevant standards relating to appropriateness, triangulation, contribution and transparency [81].

Conclusions

Safety and quality problems are wicked in nature. Whilst responsibility for population health is retained by governments, there remain limits to its controls. The task of assuring the safety and quality of care is complex and it remains the fundamental responsibility of all health system actors. As such, governments must elicit advice that can strengthen the effectiveness of its ostensibly authoritative instruments, but also coordinate and influence autonomous actors that are beyond the reach of these instruments. Yet, these contextual features that form the rationale for the establishment and reliance on networked stewardship models risk being omitted from the discourse. Empirical contributions advance understandings of the unique contribution of networked stewardship models in supporting self-organization and self-determination by actors in accordance with safety and quality aims. We argue the importance of developing stewardship capability within the health system, with attention to the role evidence play in exerting influence.
