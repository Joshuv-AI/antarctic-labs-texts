Operational mechanisms and application advances in artificial olfactory systems
Authors: Chongyang Wang, Zhikai Wang, Shengqi Gan, Bin He, Yejingwen Tong, Yaojie Zhu, Dong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial olfactory systems represent biomimetic platforms that emulate biological olfaction for volatile compound detection and discrimination. Biological olfaction achieves efficient perception through the specific binding of volatile molecules to olfactory receptors (OR), odorant-binding proteins (OBPs), and associated chemosensory proteins, followed by neural encoding, providing a theoretical foundation for artificial olfactory system design. This review synthesizes the current literature sourced from Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus databases, with selection criteria emphasizing sensing mechanisms, device architectures, and translational applications. Bioelectronic nose platforms utilizing ORs, OBPs, and synthetic peptides are critically evaluated alongside nonbioreceptor-based sensing approaches, specifically colorimetric arrays employing chemo-responsive dyes and metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) sensors, which rely on synthetic rather than biological recognition elements. Four principal application domains constitute the thematic framework: medical diagnosis through breath volatile biomarker detection, food safety assessment via freshness monitoring, environmental surveillance of air and water quality, and public safety applications in hazardous substance detection. Signal processing methodologies encompassing feature extraction and machine learning-based pattern recognition are examined. Critical translational challenges including limited long-term stability of biological recognition elements, sensor drift, and environmental interference are addressed. The comparative analysis indicates that bioreceptor-based platforms achieve superior sensitivity suitable for trace biomarker detection, whereas nonbioreceptor-based sensors offer enhanced operational stability for continuous monitoring, and hybrid architectures integrating biological selectivity with robust synthetic transduction mechanisms represent a promising direction for next-generation devices.

Introduction

Organisms have developed specialized structures, functions, and mechanisms through prolonged evolutionary adaptation to their environments. The complexity and adaptability exhibited in biological systems have inspired human innovation, leading to the emergence of the interdisciplinary field of bionics. By integrating life sciences with engineering, materials science, and information technology, bionics offers novel approaches to solving engineering challenges through the emulation of biological principles.

The human body perceives its environment through sophisticated sensory systems that construct multidimensional cognition. The visual system employs photoreceptor cells in the retina to capture light information, converting object morphology, color, and motion into electrical signals [1]. The auditory system utilizes hair cells within the cochlea to decode sound vibrations into pitch, loudness, and spatial orientation [2]. The somatosensory system relies on mechanosensitive neurons beneath the skin to transduce tactile stimuli into electrochemical signals [3]. Olfaction and gustation detect airborne molecules and oral substances through chemoreceptors, enabling odor and flavor discrimination. These signals propagate through neural networks to specialized brain regions, where integration and analysis generate coherent environmental perception, allowing for real-time interpretation of external changes and adaptive responses to facilitate dynamic interaction with complex surroundings [4].

Advancements in sensor technology have ushered in a networked and intelligent era in the twenty-first century. Modern sensors incorporate processors and wireless modules, forming Internet of Things (IoT) nodes with edge computing capabilities for real-time data processing. This sensing-computing integration exhibits functional parallels with biological olfaction. Consequently, electronic nose (e-nose) technology has emerged from the convergence of biomedical bionics and sensor engineering. Pioneered by Persaud et al., this approach employs sensor arrays to detect distinct gases within complex mixtures, identifying specific odors through differential signal patterns [5]. In this architecture, sensors emulate olfactory receptor (OR) cells in molecular recognition, while computational systems replicate the brain's analytical functions for signal interpretation and decision-making.

Artificial olfactory systems have made tremendous progress in the last five years because of the emergence of nanomaterials, biotechnology, and computational intelligence. An editorial overview was provided by Wilson that summarized research and review articles published within the Sensors journal’s special issue on the topic of portable electronic nose devices for noninvasive early disease detection, with topic areas covered that range from the realm of veterinary–wildlife pathology to human clinical pathology and the detection of dietary effects on VOC emissions, highlighting the increasing scope of e-nose diagnostics in biological systems. [6]. Park et al. have designed bioelectronic nose systems using trace amine-associated receptor nanodiscs for the purpose of food quality testing with femtomolar sensitivity [7]. Gebicki et al. have worked on the development of electrochemical sensor arrays and signal processing algorithms required for the development of odor-sensing systems for field applications [8]. Gardner et al. have worked on wearable electronic nose systems based on Internet of Things for healthcare applications [9]. Mitsubayashi has developed wearable cavitas biosensor systems in the form of mouthguard sensors and contact lenses for the purpose of continuous monitoring of body fluids [10]. These developments have led to the application of artificial olfaction in the clinical and industrial fields.

E-nose systems have been implemented in diverse domains, including clinical diagnostics, chemical analysis, food safety, and environmental monitoring [11]. Within this technology, sensors fulfill dual critical roles as molecular receptors and as discriminative signal transducers. Although recent reviews have focused on particular issues in artificial olfaction, for example, biomaterial design for bioelectronic noses [12], an integrated synthesis of biologically informed olfactory principles with various bioelectronic/chemical sensing platforms, in addition to an assessment of related challenges for practical applications, has not yet been achieved. The literature search underpinning this review was conducted across Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus databases with no restriction on publication date. The search strategy employed Boolean combinations of the terms “electronic nose,” “artificial olfaction,” “bioelectronic nose,” “olfactory receptor sensor,” “odorant-binding protein sensor,” “gas sensor array,” “colorimetric gas sensor,” and “volatile organic compound detection.” The inclusion criteria for studies subjected to comparative analysis varied by thematic section. Sect. “Artificial olfactory systems and classification” comprises studies that feature original architectures of the sensors or original designs of the recognition elements with experimentally verified performance. Sect. ”Data processing for artificial olfactory systems” selected studies reporting quantitative classification outcomes applied to artificial olfactory sensor data, as well as investigations describing neuromorphic device architectures that demonstrate biomimetic synaptic functions relevant to olfactory signal encoding and memory. Sect. ”Applications of electronic noses” comprises studies that feature original demonstrations of the application of electronic nose systems in medical diagnosis, food safety, environmental monitoring, or public safety. These studies include quantitatively benchmarked studies as well as proof-of-concept studies. In addition to the studies considered in the comparative analysis, other references on biological olfactory physiology, chemical sensing principles, algorithm development, and previous reviews are cited to provide the necessary background to establish the theory and context. Some of the references cited throughout this review have illustrative or contextual purposes, rather than a direct contribution to the quantitative cross-platform comparisons. This is because they provide methodological background information, describe enabling technologies, or establish the biological and chemical bases on which artificial olfactory sensor design is founded. This particular review seeks to fill this void in existing knowledge by examining vertebrate olfactory transduction biology for informing biomimetic design, comparisons of artificial olfactory architectures including olfactory receptor-based, odorant-binding protein-based, synthetic peptide-based, and chemical sensor systems, analyses of data processing strategies ranging from statistical to machine learning schemes, in addition to an assessment of applications in multiple domains, together with an analysis of technological limitations to practical implementation. The primary takeaways for this particular review therefore lie in an integrated comparison for platform assessment in relation to performance criteria, in addition to an assessment for platform-applications matching to enable informed platform choice for particular applications.

Olfactory transduction mechanisms in humans

Olfactory perception in vertebrates arises from a cascade of molecular and cellular reactions initiated by volatile molecules interacting with the olfactory system [13]. Upon inhalation, volatile compounds bind specifically to odorant receptors located on the immotile cilia of olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) within the olfactory epithelium at the apex of the nasal cavity. As bipolar cells, OSNs project unbranched dendrites to the epithelial surface, where olfactory vesicles extend laterally from dendritic terminals.

Receptor activation triggers G-protein coupled receptors (GPCR) signaling, subsequently activating type III adenylyl cyclase. This enzymatic reaction elevates intracellular levels of the second messenger cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), leading to the opening of cyclic nucleotide-gated (CNG) cation channels in the plasma membrane. The resulting ion flux, characterized by calcium influx and chloride efflux, induces membrane depolarization and action potential generation. The pathway of olfactory formation

Axons of olfactory neurons form fila olfactoria, which traverse the cribriform plate to reach the glomerular layer. Within this layer, synaptic connections between olfactory nerve terminals and dendrites of mitral and tufted cells organize into spherical neuropil structures termed olfactory glomeruli [14]. Each glomerulus receives input exclusively from olfactory neurons expressing identical odorant receptor types. Conversely, neurons expressing the same OR project their axons to specific medial and lateral glomeruli within the bulb. This convergence pattern establishes precise topographic mapping, where odorant molecules activate spatially distinct glomerular ensembles. Resultant neural impulses propagate via the olfactory tract to higher brain regions [15–17]. Critically, the combinatorial activation patterns across glomeruli constitute the fundamental neural code for odor discrimination.

Artificial olfactory systems and classification

Artificial olfactory systems are electronic devices that use gas sensor arrays to identify odor profiles. These systems enable continuous, real-time monitoring of volatile compounds at designated locations over extended durations. Comprising a sensor array paired with an automated pattern recognition system, they detect and discriminate complex odors and most volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Within this architecture, sensors function as bio-inspired transducers that capture environmental signals and transmit data to computational units for analysis. Distinct odor signatures elicit unique response patterns across the sensor array, generating digital odor codes. A variety of sensor technologies have been utilized [18–20], including metal oxide semiconductors that rely upon changes in conductivity induced by gas adsorption, colorimetric sensor arrays that produce optical patterns based upon dye–analyte reactions, and quartz crystal microbalance and surface acoustic wave (SAW) sensors that measure changes in frequency induced by mass accumulation. Other technologies include conducting polymers that rely upon changes in conductivity induced by analyte absorption and field effect transistors with nanomaterials like graphene and carbon nanotubes as sensing pathways. Bioelectronic nose

Olfactory receptor-based bioelectronic nose

ORs are transmembrane proteins belonging to the GPCR superfamily of metabotropic receptors in vertebrates [22]. ORs exhibit affinity for classes of odorant molecules rather than binding exclusively to single ligands [23]. Receptors extracted directly from living cells or tissues serve as biological recognition elements when developing OR-based biosensors. Although this approach yields structurally intact and functional ORs, it typically lacks binding specificity [24]. Advances in genetic engineering have enabled the use of whole-cell systems expressing ORs as biosensor components. The research demonstrates that ORs can be functionally expressed beyond the olfactory epithelium through heterologous expression systems [25].

Specific OR genes can thus be inserted into recombinant vectors to construct heterologous platforms for large-scale production and purification of target-sensitive receptors. Common expression systems include yeast (e.g., Saccharomyces cerevisiae), bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli), and mammalian cell lines. E. coli offers advantages in genetic manipulation, where plasmid vector construction enables precise regulation of protein expression levels [26]. Yeast systems achieve high-expression yields while facilitating post-translational modifications [27]. Mammalian cells provide optimal environments for eukaryotic protein glycosylation and proper folding, enhancing heterologous expression of mammalian ORs [24].

To achieve sensor miniaturization and enhance surface utilization efficiency, purified ORs are immobilized on transducer surfaces using specialized techniques. Given the pronounced hydrophobicity of ORs, lipid bilayers are typically employed to ensure stable fixation while preserving protein functionality. This approach maintains the receptor's native lipid membrane microenvironment in situ, effectively circumventing conformational distortion or functional inactivation associated with conventional covalent immobilization methods [28].

ORs exploit the biophysical coupling between receptors and lipid bilayers to undergo noncovalent anchoring through membrane interface interactions. This strategy, which avoids direct chemical bonding to surface-exposed residues, preserves the receptor's intrinsic topology and dynamic conformational features. Sung et al. established a methodology where quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) sensors were functionalized with crude membrane extracts containing the Caenorhabditis elegans olfactory receptor ODR-10. These extracts, produced through heterologous expression in E. coli membranes, demonstrated dose-dependent responsiveness to the native ligand diacetyl [29].

Jin et al. pioneered the design of a bioelectronic nose utilizing engineered nanovesicles, establishing a platform with high selectivity and sensitivity for advanced odor discrimination [30]. Their experimental methodology involved: transient transfection of HEK-293 cells with plasmids encoding the hOR2AG1 gene; isolation of hOR2AG1-enriched nanovesicles from transfected cells; and functionalization of single-walled carbon nanotube field-effect transistors (swCNT-FETs) by incubation in nanovesicle solution, enabling electrostatic attraction to the poly-D-lysine coating and uniform surface distribution.

Critically, these nanovesicles preserve endogenous membrane proteins and cytosolic signaling molecules, maintaining the integrity of signal transduction cascades. This structural fidelity allows nanovesicles to recapitulate key functions of native olfactory sensory neurons. Advantages of nanovesicle technology include scalable production capacity and enhanced storage stability [31], alongside facile surface functionalization through conjugation with nanomaterials, enabled by their nanoscale dimensions [32].

Odorant-binding protein-based bioelectronic nose

Vogt et al. first identified an insect odorant-binding protein (OBP) through isotopic labeling in the antennae of male Antheraea polyphemus moths, characterizing it as a pheromone-binding protein (PBP) [33]. OBPs are soluble transport proteins that facilitate olfactory perception by shuttling odorant molecules from the external environment to olfactory sensory neurons [34].

Molecular mechanism When hydrophobic odorants enter nasal mucus or sensillar lymph, they bind to specific amino acid residues within OBPs, forming structurally stable complexes. These OBP-bound ligands undergo transport through the aqueous milieu before undergoing ligand transfer to membrane-bound ORs.

Structural divergence Notably, vertebrate and insect OBPs exhibit significant three-dimensional structural differences. Vertebrate OBPs belong to the lipocalin superfamily, featuring a characteristic β-barrel architecture with a calyx-shaped cavity, composed of eight antiparallel β-strands, a short C-terminal α-helix, and a final β-sheet segment. The binding pocket resides within the central β-barrel [35, 36]. In contrast, insect OBPs generally fold into compact globular structures stabilized by six α-helical domains, with three interlocked disulfide bridges formed by six conserved cysteine residues conferring exceptional conformational stability [37].

Sensor applications The intrinsic thermostability, structural diversity, and efficient binding kinetics of OBPs render them increasingly valuable for constructing high-performance biosensors. These properties enable precise molecular recognition in artificial olfactory systems while maintaining functionality under operational stresses.

Current OBP research predominantly focuses on insect-derived proteins. For instance, Lu et al. immobilized Bactrocera dorsalis (oriental fruit fly) OBPs onto interdigitated electrodes via covalent attachment to specially designed SH-PEG-COOH polymers following heterologous expression and purification [38]. This platform enabled semiochemical analysis through combined electrochemical sensing and molecular docking simulations, facilitating the detection of host plant volatiles (e.g., isoamyl acetate, β-ionone, benzaldehyde) via specific OBP-ligand interactions. Mammalian OBP-based biosensors demonstrate complementary capabilities, with a SAW resonator array functionalized with wild-type bovine OBP, its double mutant, and wild-type porcine OBP successfully discriminating structural analogs such as octenol and carvone [39].

Comparative advantages Relative to ORs, OBPs exhibit superior characteristics, including operational stability under thermal/pH fluctuations, proteolytic resistance to enzymatic degradation, and structural robustness facilitating sensor integration [40].

The recent developments have seen the extension of bioelectronic nose design from in vitro systems to in vivo systems, which utilize entire biological olfactory systems in their entirety as sensing units. Zhu et al. developed an in vivo bioelectronic nose using bioengineered rats by detecting electrophysiological signals from the olfactory bulb using multichannel microelectrodes, detecting and differentiating multiple odorants with high accuracy [41]. In vivo systems retain all components of olfactory signal transduction, with advantages including biologically relevant receptor-ligand binding rates and natural signal amplification pathways, which provide increased sensitivity compared to systems using proteins alone, although issues concerning long-term reliability, standardization of biologically generated responses, and compatibility with readout systems remain to be overcome.

Synthetic peptide-based bioelectronic nose

The requirement of a lipid bilayer membrane for ORs to function normally—coupled with the significant difficulty in thoroughly immobilizing lipid bilayers onto chips—renders the fabrication process of OR-based sensors complex and labor-intensive. This limitation hinders guaranteed reusability and reproducibility [42]. Peptides form specific conformations driven by the individual R groups within their amino acid sequences. The secondary structure of peptides is stabilized by noncovalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, π-stacking, as well as hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions [43]. Proteins often interact with ligands through subdomains featuring secondary structural backbones formed by their folding. α-Helical synthetic peptides, as a primary class of protein secondary structure, play a critical role in mediating protein–protein interactions [44, 45]. The interaction kinetics and structural stability reflected in peptide responses to ligands are essential parameters for the development of biosensors and biochips [46].

The design of peptides with ligand specificity, based on the binding regions of ORs and odorant-binding proteins (OBPs), has been employed in the development and application of sensors for detecting a range of VOCs. The first OR-derived peptides (ORPs) designed for detection were based on the binding site between an OR and trimethylamine (TMA). Among these, the peptide sequences orp61 and orp188 exhibited high sensitivity toward TMA and ammonia [47].

Regarding sensor immobilization, ORP attachment can be achieved via physical immobilization methods (such as direct coating or adsorption) or through chemical conjugation using coupling reagents to covalently link the ORPs to the carrier material. Compared with physical immobilization, the chemical conjugation method offers superior connection efficiency for ORPs, resulting in sensors with significant advantages in detection sensitivity, selectivity, and stability [48, 49].

Gas-sensing peptide sequences developed based on OBPs and ORs exhibit inherent limitations, being somewhat constrained by the restricted diversity of known VOC binders for these proteins. To enhance the physicochemical properties of peptides and improve their affinity and selectivity toward target VOCs or specific VOC categories, methodologies such as phage display, virtual screening, and combinatorial peptide libraries are commonly employed [50–52].

Phage display involves translating a phage library onto a solid surface or an immobilized target similar to the structure of VOCs. The target can be the VOC itself or an analog processed in solution or the crystalline phase. The selection of the target for screening in phage display is crucial, as it determines the affinity and selectivity of the selected peptide sequences. One study attempted to apply phage display to gas-phase binding sensing. A phage display library was screened using Trinitrotoluene (TNT) and 2,4-dinitrotoluene (DNT) crystals for peptide selection. Two peptides, TNT-BP and DNT-BP, selectively bound to TNT and DNT in the liquid phase after transitioning from the liquid to the gas phase [53]. Another micro/nanomechanical device, the cantilever sensor, can detect target molecules at very low concentrations due to its high sensitivity. After immobilizing a DNT-affinity peptide (DNT-BP1C), it showed high sensitivity to DNT [54, 55].

Computational virtual screening facilitates the prediction of binding scores between small peptide receptors and VOCs. To account for both ligand and peptide flexibility, each peptide conformational isomer (conformer) is treated as a potential receptor within a dedicated binding pocket. Docking is subsequently performed against all possible conformers of the target VOCs [51]. The cost-effectiveness, speed, and reproducibility of in silico testing have facilitated its application in gas-sensing peptide discovery [56].

In one study, virtual screening of five peptides (Cysteinylglycine, Glutathione, CIHNP, CIQPV, CRQVF) against 14 distinct VOCs was conducted, followed by experimental validation to determine ligand affinity profiles [51]. The results demonstrated a 100% positive correspondence between virtual and experimental results for ethyl acetate, ethyl butanoate, and hexane.

Another study employed a semi-virtual combinatorial screening protocol to evaluate the relative binding affinities of tripeptides from an elemental library against molecular representatives of five chemical classes. Based on maximizing inter-class discriminative power, 120 tripeptides were selected as scaffolds to generate a ‘core’ tetrapeptide library. The relative binding affinities of these tetrapeptides against the five chemical classes were assessed, leading to the identification of five tetrapeptides exhibiting differential affinity towards alcohols, aldehydes, esters, hydrocarbons, and ketones. Terminal cysteine was appended to the carboxyl-terminus for gas-sensing applications [52]. Experimental validation confirmed the virtual screening-predicted selectivity for four out of these five peptides.

Careful assessment of these bio-electronic nose platforms shows that each one has a differentiated suitability profile depending on the deployment scenario. OR-based platforms offer detection limits in the femtomolar range, but because of the fragility associated with lipid bilayer membrane integrity, stability in practical operation is compromised. OBP-based platforms offer improved stability because of the natural stability of these soluble proteins, making them suitable for deployment in portable, field-operable devices. Synthetic peptide receptors provide favorable stability profiles, together with the ability to rationally design an optimized sequence by computational design. For implantable biomedical applications, existing platforms are challenged by serious issues such as biocompatibility, stability in physiological conditions, and long-term functional integrity. Chemical sensors based on conducting polymers and MOS provide superior stability in terms of operation duration, but these sensors are nonselective in distinguishing between structurally similar biomarkers, an indication that hybrid platforms that merge biological selectivity with robust transduction methods might provide a promising approach in developing implantable artificial olfactory systems.

Chemical sensor-based electronic nose systems

Artificial olfactory colorimetric systems

Chemical e-nose platforms employ synthetic materials as recognition elements, offering advantages in stability, reproducibility, and cost-effectiveness compared to bioelectronic approaches. Colorimetric sensors identify and quantify gaseous analytes through observable color changes. These color transitions result from distinct physical or chemical interactions between the analyte gas and the sensing material, including Lewis acid–base interactions, π-π stacking, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and donor–acceptor interactions [57, 58]. A colorimetric sensor comprises two key components: chemo-responsive dyes and a solid support matrix. The chemo-responsive dyes, which undergo color changes in response to environmental stimuli, serve as the primary sensing unit. Hydrogels are frequently employed as responsive elements in colorimetric sensors due to their microfluidic environment, which facilitates analyte dissolution and diffusion. When airborne target molecules encounter hydrogel, they are physically adsorbed onto its surface, become trapped, and subsequently react with it, inducing a measurable color change [59, 60].

The dyes must be immobilized onto the solid support through methods such as adsorption, encapsulation, ion exchange, or covalent bonding. One study demonstrated a nanofibrillated cellulose hydrogel colorimetric film (fabricated using SHNC, PVA, SA, and anthocyanin (Anth)) with excellent tensile strength, fatigue resistance, and colorimetric stability [61]. The film's surface developed a dense, fish-scale-like microstructure, enhancing hydrophobicity and effectively mitigating humidity-induced interference with anthocyanin’s color transitions.

Under experimental ammonia exposure, the hydrogel’s color progression shifted from pale pink to brown, then dark green, and finally yellow with increased exposure time. This chromogenic response results from structural changes in anthocyanin under acidic/basic conditions, causing corresponding alterations in the hydrogel film’s color [62].

In another investigation, dye/metal–organic frameworks (MOFs)@metal–organic frameworks (COFs) core–shell composites were developed by coating dye-loaded MOFs with a hydrophobic COF layer. The uniform distribution of dye molecules within the porous MOF structure endowed these composites with superior sensitivity (sub-ppm level) compared to conventional VOC dye sensors. They successfully detected and discriminated eight trace plant-emitted VOCs, demonstrating their utility for the accurate and early detection of Fusarium graminearum in wheat, the causal agent of Fusarium head blight.

Metal oxide semiconductor-based sensors

Metal oxide semiconductor (MOS) sensors remain the predominant choice in e-nose systems, owing to their robustness, fast response time, and relatively low cost [63]. MOS sensors operates based on redox reactions occurring at the surface, wherein oxygen is adsorbed onto the oxide surface under normal atmospheric conditions. This adsorbed oxygen then reacts with the conduction band electrons and forms oxygen ions (O2⁻, O⁻, O2−), which generate a charged-deficient layer at the surface, resulting in higher resistance. In the case of exposure to reducing analytes such as CO, ethanol, acetone, and H₂S, the oxygen ions interact with the analyte molecules, releasing electrons back into the conduction band, hence lowering resistance levels. On the other hand, in the case of oxidizing analytes, for instance, NO₂, there is electron depletion in the surface layer, thus increasing the resistance level [64]. The above-described resistive switching process provides the main sensing strategy, where sensitivity is regulated by factors such as metal oxide composition, number of grain boundaries, and doping level. Popular metal oxides, such as SnO2, ZnO, In2O3, WO3, and TiO2, have different sensitivities to various types of VOCs, while selectivity can be adjusted using noble metal coatings and nanosizing techniques. The high operational temperature required for chemical reactions at the device surface, typically in the range of 200–400 °C, is controlled using microheaters integrated into microscale chip systems, supporting deployment in portable and low-power configurations. The inherent capability of the MOS platform for broad-spectrum gas detection is exploited by designing multicomponent sensor arrays along with multidimensional pattern recognition techniques that enable the separation of gases in a mixture.

In practice, MOS sensors are used together with electrochemical sensing techniques in artificial olfaction systems to expand the range of detectable substances and to improve their discriminatory capabilities. Electrochemical sensors operate based on the oxidation or reduction reaction occurring on the surface of a working electrode with an electrolyte in either its liquid or solid states. The resulting Faradaic current is directly proportional to the concentration of the target analyte, enabling sensitive quantification at sub-ppm levels for reactive gases including CO, NO2, H2S, SO2, and O3. The selectivity of the sensor is influenced by the electrode material, the potential applied, and the electrolyte formulation. The integration of MOS into an electrochemical transducer-based hybrid sensor array takes advantage of the fast kinetics and low fabrication cost of the MOS device, as well as the higher sensitivity and selectivity of electrochemical transduction, to expand the range of detectable molecules and improve classification efficiency through information processing in multivariate pattern recognition schemes. Comparative performance characteristics of artificial olfactory sensing platforms

The descriptors employed in Table 2 for response time and stability require operational clarification. “Response time” is the time from the introduction of the sample to the time when a signal change is detectable by the transducer, where “real time” means the platforms have response times short enough to provide the ability for continuous dynamic monitoring without apparent latency, “seconds” indicating intermediate equilibration periods; where quantitative response data were available from the original references, specific values are provided (e.g., ≈0.2 s for colorimetric systems, 11 s for MOS sensors). Thus, “stability” in this comparative sense is the operating lifespan and reproducibility of the sensing element itself, as opposed to the temporal robustness of pattern recognition models used subsequently, while the qualitative terms used to define the range from “limited” to “moderate,” “enhanced,” and finally “long-term” represent a continuum of increasing operating lifespan as a function of the physicochemical robustness of the recognition element itself, with reusability data (e.g., number of operational cycles) as reported in the literature.

As illustrated in Table 2, the comparative analysis reveals a fundamental trade-off between detection sensitivity and operational stability across artificial olfactory platforms. Bioreceptor-based systems utilizing ORs or synthetic peptides possess detection limits at the femtomolar range, along with high molecular specificity, but their operational stability is limited due to the intrinsic fragility of biological material, where the stability of the OR-nanovesicle system is low due to susceptibility to lipid bilayer degradation, and the stability of the OR-nanodisc and synthetic peptide receptor systems is moderate. In contrast, OBP-based platforms possess a unique intermediate position where the intrinsic thermostability and proteolytic resistance of their globular protein domains provide enhanced operational stability, along with nanomolar-to-micromolar sensitivity and class-selective recognition properties. MOS sensor systems possess detection limits in the ppb-to-ppm range, along with broad-spectrum selectivity and high operational stability, which is their most significant advantage for field-deployed systems, whereas colorimetric arrays possess analyte detection limits at the ppm level, along with array-dependent selectivity and stability depending upon the dye-substrate composition and exposure conditions.

The choice of a suitable platform is application-dependent. Bioelectronic systems are preferred for applications requiring very high sensitivity and molecular selectivity, such as detecting markers for early-stage diseases, while chemical sensors are preferred for applications requiring system ruggedness, cost-effectiveness, and scalability, such as environmental and food quality monitoring.

Data processing for artificial olfactory systems

A complete olfactory system necessitates a sensor array capable of generating patterned VOC signals, along with sophisticated data processing techniques. Multichannel sensor arrays function analogously to ORs by performing recognition, reception, and signal transduction. Increasing the number of signal receptors enables advanced signal classification through diverse pattern recognition approaches.

Data acquired from artificial olfactory sensors undergoes two primary processing methodologies: statistical model analysis and intelligent model analysis. Key statistical methods include Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and other linear analyses. In contrast, intelligent model analysis primarily encompasses Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithms [67].

Regardless of whether statistical or intelligent approaches are employed, the processing stream within artificial olfactory systems is generally divided into two sequentially distinct functional stages, which must necessarily be clearly defined: signal preprocessing and pattern classification. Signal preprocessing involves correction of the baseline, reduction of noise, and correction of sensor drift, after which discriminative features are extracted from the signal. Features could be based on steady-state response amplitudes, response-recovery kinetic parameters, area-under-the-curve calculations, or the frequency domain parameters obtained from time-dependent signals. The goal of such a process is to extract the fingerprint information relevant to the analysis of the chemical composition and concentration levels of the analytes, and to filter out any variation not related to chemical composition. The subsequent step of classification involves applying statistical or machine learning methods to the feature vector data in order to classify analytes, calculate concentrations, or detect abnormal behavior. This two-step analysis process, in which signal processing and feature extraction occur before pattern recognition, mimics the biological system of olfaction, and is the typical architecture that underpins the signal processing approaches discussed in this section.

Multivariate analysis of the sensor array output is essential for identifying specific chemical gases within complex mixtures [68]. Zeng et al. used PCA on data from an electronic nose system based on a multiparameter Virtual Sensor Array (VSA) and achieved over 97% accuracy in distinguishing and classifying six distinct VOCs. PCA reduces redundancy in the sensitivity profiles across sensor classes and orthogonally projects the sensitivity data onto fewer uncorrelated dimensions, identifying the principal components that capture maximum variance for odor group classification [69]. Similarly, Vaughan et al. achieved 98.6% accuracy in classifying ten VOCs using PCA on data from a four-channel QCM array. This study employed PCA to assess the dimensional characteristics of the sensor data and facilitate visual separation of four compound classes. It also directly constructed a Discriminant Analysis (DA) classification model using the original four sensor parameters. Although representing sensor array information using a few selected principal components, which incorporate the major variance pattern, is widely used when the number of samples is smaller than the number of analytes to be predicted, in the present case, the number of samples met the criteria for performing a DA model on the original four-dimensional sensor information [70].

The choice of the most appropriate data processing algorithm for the specific artificial olfactory task depends on the trade-off between computational complexity, accuracy, and robustness of the algorithm. PCA is particularly suitable for data visualization and variance decomposition when signals from sensor arrays exhibit significant cross-correlation. PCA identifies the orthogonal directions of maximum variance in the original data space and maps the measured values from sensor arrays onto uncorrelated principal components. Thus, variance redundancy is eliminated while keeping the original signal information unchanged. Nevertheless, since the linear projection assumption is at the core of the PCA algorithm, it is less appropriate for use when sensor signals exhibit significant nonlinearity across different analyte concentration values, a property typical of the MOS platform. On the other hand, LDA incorporates knowledge about class labels in order to obtain discriminating axes that maximize inter-class separation relative to within-class variability. Thus, this method becomes more suitable for the task of classifying sensor data in quality control processes. For problems that involve generalization over a multidimensional space of features, SVM is known to perform better, as found in the categorization of Parkinson's disease subtypes through nanoparticle-based sensors [88] and in signal classification from artificial chemosensory neuronal synapses [19]. On the other hand, the complexity of KNN increases directly with the number of elements in the training set, which may hinder its applicability in real time or online settings.

Numerous machine learning methods have been developed to date [71–75], including Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression, SVM, ANNs, Decision Trees (DT), and KNN. Learning via ANNs or deep learning has become the predominant framework in contemporary machine learning [76]. ANNs iteratively adjust connection weights to minimize the discrepancy between observed and predicted outputs. As biomimetic technologies, ANNs classify outcomes and make predictions based on the similarity and proximity within complex, nonlinear data. An ANN comprises interconnected artificial neurons exchanging signals, typically structured into sequential layers: an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer [77].

Input data enters the system through the input layer. Within the hidden layers, the signals are weighted (using weights and biases) and transformed nonlinearly via activation functions (e.g., ReLU), progressively extracting abstract features. The output layer then generates predictions based on these processed signals. Loss function calculates the error between these predictions and the true values, which is then propagated backward through the network via the backpropagation algorithm. Optimization techniques, such as gradient descent, adjust the network parameters (weights and biases) at each layer to iteratively minimize prediction error. Through repeated iterations, the network autonomously learns complex patterns within the data, acquiring generalization capabilities for tasks such as image recognition and language comprehension. Its core strengths lie in hierarchical feature extraction, nonlinear modeling, and end-to-end autonomous learning.

Olfactory memory refers to the specific sensory information evoked by odors. Stored olfactory information in the brain undergoes further processing to form specific perceptions, which may subsequently guide actions. Artificial Olfactory Memory (AOM) mechanisms are biomimetic systems that employ cross-reactive sensor arrays for chemo-electrical signal transduction. These systems combine dynamic baseline correction with joint time–frequency analysis to extract high-dimensional response features. An AOM system integrates gas sensors, memory devices, and computational units, thereby enabling advanced sensing functionalities such as intelligent monitoring for multiple gases, information storage, and action execution, thus mimicking the perception and memory capabilities of biological organisms [78].

Organic synaptic transistors exemplify this approach, capable of mimicking fundamental synaptic functions such as Short-Term Plasticity (STP), Long-Term Potentiation (LTP), and Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity (STDP) [79–81]. Gerasimov et al. modulated channel conductance in such transistors via the in-situ polymerization of monomers within the channel [82]. Changes in the conductance state of neuromorphic synaptic devices emulate neural plasticity. In the brain, synapses exhibit long-term plasticity through activity pattern-induced LTP and Long-Term Depression (LTD). This was simulated on a PETE-SOECT device, where sending conditioning spikes induced persistent changes in the amplitude of the device’s characteristic response spikes, effectively mimicking LTP. Conversely, transient enhancements or suppressions of the characteristic spike amplitude (analogous to STP) were achieved by modulating ion concentrations within the OECT channel using spike durations shorter than those required for full anion doping.

Within artificial olfaction (electronic nose) technology, data processing serves as a core function. The workflow encompasses preprocessing raw sensor responses to eliminate drift and environmental interference, extracting key features while reducing data dimensionality and preserving chemical fingerprint information, and finally applying pattern recognition algorithms for classification or regression analysis to achieve gas/odor identification and concentration quantification. This comprehensive process transforms raw signals into reliable and interpretable chemical information. Applications of electronic noses

The unique electrical and biological properties of biosensors incorporating biological recognition elements have led to the deployment of various electronic noses in olfactory analysis, enhancing detection sensitivity and specificity. Applications of electronic noses

The appropriate selection of sensor platforms for given tasks is based on the harmonization of the sensor's performance features and the task’s requirements. In the area of medical diagnostics, which requires the trace analysis of biomarkers, OR-based and peptide-based bioelectronic noses are the most suitable due to their high sensitivity of femtomolar concentrations, while food safety monitoring is better done using OBP-based sensors due to their better adaptability to the surrounding environment. In industrial process control and long-term monitoring of the environment, the use of MOS sensors is most appropriate due to their long-term reliability and ready availability on the market.

Applications in medical diagnosis

Skin, sputum, urine, and exhaled breath serve as sources of disease-associated odors. Exhaled breath contains numerous VOCs released from the body, providing insights into health status, such as infections, intoxications, or metabolic disorders, which inform about a patient's physiological condition [90]. Electronic noses (e-noses) that use biological recognition elements based on living cells, proteins, or peptides exhibit high sensitivity and selectivity in detecting physiological or biochemical processes.

Lin et al. pioneered the development of a synthetic peptide-based e-nose, using virtual simulations and molecular docking techniques to target 3D structures for detecting biomarkers of uremia odor (dimethylamine, DMA, and TMA) [91, 92]. The premise for developing diagnostic breath tests lies in identifying disease-specific VOC markers. Lung cancer, a primary target for respiratory diagnostics, has yielded various VOC biomarkers for cancer diagnosis [93]. Screening of lung cancer patients is achieved by analyzing specific VOC changes associated with cancer cell culture [94]. Janfaza et al. developed a molecularly imprinted polymer-based bioelectronic nose device for detecting the lung cancer biomarker hexanal. The sensor consists of a composite formed from molecularly imprinted nanoparticles and multiwalled carbon nanotubes, enabling selective detection of gaseous hexanal at room temperature. The e-nose exhibited a linear response in the 10 to 200 ppm range with a limit of detection of 10 ppm.

High concentrations of octenol can irritate respiratory mucosa, resulting in cough, sore throat, or transient dyspnea. Octenol exhibits neurodegenerative and cytotoxic effects and is a human metabolite associated with cancer and Crohn's disease. Chronic inhalation in industrial or laboratory settings may exacerbate symptoms in patients with asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Yamada et al. developed a VOC sensor composed of insect ORs reconstituted in lipid bilayers, which employ microfluidic channels and hydrophobic micro-slits to facilitate gas introduction into droplets, thereby enabling highly sensitive detection [95].

These technological advancements in VOC sensing have led to the development of e-nose applications beyond the realm of respiration and metabolism-related diagnostics. In neurodiagnosis, breath VOCs with cross-reactive nanoparticle sensor arrays have been utilized for the differentiation of idiopathic Parkinson’s disease patients from those with other forms of parkinsonian syndromes. More recently, applications have been explored in the realm of metabolic disease monitoring. In these applications, bioelectronic noses with artificial neural processing capabilities have been utilized for the precise diagnosis of diabetes mellitus and categorization of diabetic complications by exhaled breath volatile organic compounds [96]. Gastrointestinal applications have also been explored with e-nose platforms showing promise for gastric cancer screening and colorectal cancer screening by specific disease-related volatile organic compound patterns [97]. Future developments with optimized AI algorithms incorporating multiomics data are expected to bring e-nose applications closer to the realm of precision medicine for disease-specific volatile organic compound profiling for personalized therapeutic strategies and home patient monitoring with IoT-enabled devices, especially in resource-challenged environments during epidemic outbreaks. Applications in food safety

Food safety remains a critical concern in the modern food industry. Contaminants, bacteria, and toxins can enter food products during production and storage or form through chemical reactions within the food. High-protein foods rank among the most perishable commodities due to the degradation of amino acids by endogenous and microbial enzymes. This degradation triggers decarboxylation and deamination reactions, resulting in the formation of toxic biogenic amines and volatile amines. Although volatile amines produce off-odors leading to sensory quality degradation, biogenic amines are often toxic and harmful to humans when consumed. Consequently, monitoring the concentrations of both volatile and biogenic amines in food is essential [98].

E-noses serve as effective technologies for rapidly and reliably screening food spoilage, particularly microbial contamination. Panigrahi et al. developed a biomimetic olfactory sensor by depositing synthetic polypeptides onto a QCM electrode. This sensor can detect low concentrations (10–100 ppm) of specific indicator VOCs, such as acetic acid, associated with Salmonella contamination in beef [99].

In fruit production, quality is determined by freshness, flavor, firmness, and color changes. Recent research has demonstrated that odorants emitted by fruits correlate with their ripeness and quality, suggesting their potential use as odor markers. Studies have confirmed that bio-electronic nose technology can monitor fruit ripeness based on three key indicators: penetration force measurements, soluble solids content, and starch content [100].

Lee et al. developed a genetically engineered phage membrane-based optoelectronic nose, using phage membranes engineered for tryptophan and histidine to differentiate volatile organic compounds (γ-hexalactone, 2-isopropyl-4-methylthiazole, ethanol, acetone, ethyl acetate, and acetaldehyde). This system demonstrates capability in assessing various stages of peach freshness conditions, including ripeness, flavor profile, and decay state [83].

Applications in environmental monitoring

E-noses effectively address limitations inherent in traditional environmental monitoring techniques, such as insufficient real-time capability, high costs, and limited coverage. Conventional methods rely on laboratory instruments for offline analysis, involving cumbersome procedures that hinder dynamic pollution tracking. In contrast, e-noses enable real-time monitoring of volatile pollutants present in the atmosphere, water bodies, and soil—rapidly identifying industrial leaks or odor sources at parts-per-billion concentrations. This capability stems from simultaneous multicomponent detection, highly sensitive sensor arrays, and integration with the IoT.

MOS gas sensors are widely employed across industries for measuring and monitoring environmentally critical trace gases (e.g., CO and CO₂) due to their cost-effectiveness, high material sensitivity, and rapid response times [101]. Specifically for e-nose systems, the use of gas sensor arrays and tailored sensing materials facilitates multiplexed analysis of gas mixtures for diverse detection targets [102]. Lee et al. developed an e-nose based on neural pattern separation to detect various polycyclic aromatic compounds, employing genetically engineered M13 bacteriophage to mimic the mammalian olfactory system [103]. This system achieved exceptional atomic-level precision and a high selectivity detection rate of 97.5%.

Matatagui et al. proposed a SAW sensor-based e-nose for identifying and monitoring airborne benzene, toluene, and xylene. Their study pioneered the use of colored (Au, Pt, Pd) and uncolored ferrite nanoparticles as sensitive coatings in these sensors. Classification results based on principal components 1, 2, and 3 were used for probabilistic neural network learning, achieving 100% correct classification after repeated training [85]. Son et al. constructed a bioelectronic nose incorporating human ORs and swCNT-FETs for real-time water quality assessment. The results indicated that this bioelectronic nose could detect geosmin and 2-methylisoborneol in water at concentrations as low as 10 ng/L [104].

Applications in public safety

E-noses are paramount in public safety, facilitating rapid, noncontact detection and identification of hazardous gases and enhancing traditional detection methods through superior sensitivity, response speed, and coverage. Key applications include airport security screening for explosives, intercepting hazardous materials in logistics centers, real-time monitoring of chemical leaks with early warning systems in industrial plants, safeguarding personnel at large-scale events, pinpointing fire hazards in their initial stages, and conducting post-incident forensic analysis. This technology is indispensable in counter-terrorism efforts, crime prevention, accident mitigation, and safeguarding public lives and property.

Inspired by the complementarity-determining regions of antibodies, Komikawa et al. developed a disulfide bond-crosslinked three-dimensional peptide matrix for surface plasmon resonance detection of TNT. This structure forms a rigid network via the sequential linkage of cysteine-modified linear peptides, significantly enhancing capture capacity for small-molecule TNT. Experiments confirmed selective TNT binding via π–π stacking and electrostatic interactions, providing a novel strategy for highly sensitive, label-free detection of explosives, disease biomarkers, and other small molecules [105].

Phage-based genetic engineering also holds significant promise for biosensor development. Park et al. developed a reduced graphene oxide-based gas sensor array functionalized with DNT-specific peptide receptors, achieving highly selective detection of DNT, a TNT byproduct, at room temperature. Employing differential signal processing to effectively eliminate environmental noise, their sensor demonstrated efficient and specific detection of gaseous explosives including TNT, DNT, and cyclotrimethylenetrinitramine [106].

Jaworski et al. leveraged phage display technology to screen billions of peptides to identify specific receptor peptides for explosives, such as TNT and DNT, achieving high selectivity through multivalent interactions (with a dissociation constant of 71 nM for TNT binding). Innovatively, they embedded the DNT receptor peptides within a polyethylene glycol hydrogel coating, facilitating the first successful transition from liquid-phase screening to gas-phase detection[[53]].

Collectively, these e-nose technologies enable noncontact, real-time detection of hazardous substances, such as explosives and toxic chemicals at room temperature with parts-per-billion level sensitivity. They combine high selectivity, rapid response times (in seconds), and reusability, offering effective, low-power active defense solutions for security screening, counter-terrorism, and environmental monitoring.

Aside from explosives detection, electronic nose technology has shown promising emerging capabilities in narcotics identification for law enforcement and border control applications. The volatile signature characteristics of illicit substances like cannabis, methamphetamine, and cocaine by-products have unique chemical fingerprints that can be identified by sensor arrays. A 56-sensor platform consisting of MOS, EC, NDIR, and PID sensors was developed by Noh et al., which achieved 93.03% classification accuracy for cannabis and methamphetamine in six different laboratory environments[107], while Hernández et al. have successfully used zeolite-modified SnO2/Cr2O3 MOS sensor arrays to detect methyl benzoate, a volatile cocaine marker identified by detection dogs, with 94.1% SVM classification accuracy in five seconds of vapor exposure to cocaine[108], while Leite et al. have successfully used a portable electronic nose consisting of commercial gas sensors to detect seized marijuana samples in collaboration with law enforcement agencies with 100% classification accuracy using SPA-LDA[109]. Although the application domain for this technology is still in an early stage of development when compared with the explosives detection domain, the combination of improved selectivity in sensors and miniaturized device technology points to narcotics detection as another promising emerging area for artificial olfactory technology in the field of public safety.

Summary and outlook

Based on the comparative analysis of sensing platforms, data processing methodologies, and translation applications discussed in Sect."Artificial olfactory systems and classification" through "Applications of electronic noses", the following synthesis and outlook are based on performance metrics and technological limitations outlined in the accompanying tables and the primary literature cited. The biological olfactory transduction mechanism from odorant-receptor complex formation on the olfactory epithelium to GPCR-mediated signal transduction to combinatorial encoding in the olfactory bulb represents the fundamental design principles that guide both bioreceptor-based and synthetic material-based artificial olfactory systems. Bioreceptor-based platforms such as those using ORs, OBPs, and synthetic peptides, as well as nonbiological sensors such as MOS sensors and colorimetric arrays, have been found to offer detection capabilities from femtomolar concentrations for trace biomarker identification to parts per billion concentrations for continuous environmental monitoring. The addition of multisensor arrays with data processing pipelines from statistical decomposition techniques to machine learning classifiers has greatly improved the signal interpretation and pattern recognition capabilities of these platforms in the medical diagnostic, food safety, environmental monitoring, and public safety domains of interest.

Despite such accomplishments, there exist some important challenges for the implementation of artificial olfactory systems. The stability of biological recognition components is one of the most important issues because ORs and OBPs have shown a degradation of functionality with time because of denaturation and the absence of the native environment. The variability of production and the stability problem because of the changes of the electrical properties of the sensors also make it difficult to have a high degree of autonomy. The presence of interference caused by humidity and temperature changes, as well as the presence of complex matrices in real samples, makes it essential to develop calibration systems and processing algorithms. The scalability problem is also a factor that makes it difficult to have commercialized artificial olfactory systems because the production processes of bioelectronic sensors have been carried out through laborious immobilization processes that cannot be scaled up.

These persistent challenges are empirically substantiated by the cross-platform performance comparison presented in this review, which reveals that no single platform simultaneously optimizes sensitivity, selectivity, response time, and long-term stability, and by the data processing analysis, which demonstrates that algorithmic performance remains contingent upon sensor-specific signal characteristics and application-dependent classification demands. However, to address these shortcomings, the future developments are expected to propel the e-nose systems towards increased biomimetic realism, intelligence, and multifunctionality by the convergence of various technological frontiers. The developments in the area of genome editing can provide opportunities for the improvement of the stability of olfactory receptors by optimizing the heterologous expression efficiency using enhanced genome editing tools. The integration of neuromorphic computing frameworks with sensor arrays can provide real-time odor classification at low power consumption for wearable devices. The integration of the e-nose technology platform with the Internet of Things framework can provide a network of sensors for perpetual environmental and remote health surveillance. The challenges identified in the biological component stability, sensor reproducibility, and environmental robustness must be addressed for the application of artificial olfactory systems from laboratory instruments to verified diagnostic and monitoring devices in practical applications. The critical knowledge gaps in the area that need to be addressed in depth include the incomplete knowledge of molecular interactions at the odorant–receptor level hindering the receptor design process, the lack of standardization in the performance benchmarking procedures for cross-comparisons in different laboratories, and the requirement for the development of the verified VOC biomarker panels defining the particular volatile patterns for the various disease states or quality attributes.
