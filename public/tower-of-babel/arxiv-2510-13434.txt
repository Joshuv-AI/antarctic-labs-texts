Beyond Single-Reward: Multi-Pair, Multi-Perspective Preference Optimization for Machine Translation
Hao Wang, Linlong Xu, Heng Liu, Yangyang Liu, Xiaohu Zhao Bo Zeng, Liangying Shao, Longyue Wang, Weihua Luo, Kaifu Zhang Alibaba International Digital Commerce

Abstract
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a powerful paradigm for aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) to human preferences in Machine Translation (MT), but current methods are hindered by two fundamental challenges: (1) flawed reward signals from Quality Estimation (QE) models that overlook critical errors like translation hallucination, and (2) inefficient data utilization that discards valuable learning signals by selecting only a single win-loss pair. To address these limitations, we introduce M 2 PO: Multi-Pair, Multi-Perspective Preference Optimization . Our framework integrates a multi-perspective reward engine that creates a more robust signal by combining two key viewpoints: a new hallucination penalty for factuality, and an innovative dynamic quality score that adaptively fuses external evaluations with the model’s own evolving judgment. This is synergistically paired with a multi-pair construction strategy that systematically creates a comprehensive set of preference pairs from the entire pool of translation candidates. This synergistic approach ensures the model learns from a richer spectrum of quality trade-offs, leading to more robust and faithful translations. On challenging WMT21-22 benchmarks, M 2 PO substantially outperforms existing preference optimization methods and demonstrates highly competitive performance against leading proprietary LLMs.

Introduction

The field of Machine Translation (MT) has been revitalized in the era of large language models (LLMs)
(
Jiao et al. 2023
;
Hendy et al. 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023
;
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Kocmi et al. 2024
)
. The dominant paradigm—large-scale pre-training followed by Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on task-specific data—has yielded unprecedented performance. This methodology enables models to generate fluent translations by minimizing cross-entropy loss against a single reference sentence
(
Alves et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Lu et al. 2024
)
.

However, the SFT paradigm is inherently limited. The standard supervised fine-tuning approach, which trains a model to maximize the likelihood of a single demonstrated output, restricts its capabilities to surface-level mimicry. This objective is often insufficient for capturing the deeper principles of quality, helpfulness, or safety that are reflective of true human preference
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
. To produce translations that are not just adequate but preferred by humans, the field has shifted towards preference-based learning. Methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
have emerged as a stable and effective alternative, directly training models on "chosen" (better) and "rejected" (worse) translations.

Despite its promise, applying preference optimization to MT has revealed significant bottlenecks. The first is the narrow perspective of reward models. Current practices often rely on an auxiliary Quality Estimation (QE) model to score translations and automatically generate preference data
(
Xu et al. 2024b
;
Cui et al. 2025
)
. However, our in-depth analysis, detailed in Section
3
, reveals a critical flaw in this approach: **QE models are unreliable judges for nuanced yet critical errors like partial translation hallucinations**. They tend to assign erroneously high scores to fluent but factually inaccurate translations, creating a flawed reward signal that can misguide the optimization process. Compounding this is a second major issue: inefficient data utilization, where methods typically select only a single win-loss pair from a pool of candidates, squandering valuable learning signals.

To address these challenges, we introduce
M
2
PO: Multi-Pair, Multi-Perspective Preference Optimization
. Our framework is built on two deeply integrated innovations. The first is a multi-perspective reward engine that tackles flawed signals from two angles: it introduces a dedicated hallucination penalty to ensure factual accuracy, and it makes the traditional quality perspective itself dynamic. This is achieved by an adaptive curriculum that fuses static, external scores with the model’s own online judgments, ensuring the reward signal evolves with the model and remains reliable.

This multi-faceted and dynamic reward is the cornerstone of our second innovation: a multi-pair optimization strategy. Instead of selecting a single best-worst pair, we use our nuanced scores to partition the
entire
candidate pool into multiple, rank-ordered pairs and construct a rich set of preference pairs for optimization. This comprehensive data exploitation leads to a more robust and well-rounded translation model.

By integrating these strategies, M
2
PO pushes the performance ceiling of preference-optimized MT. Our experiments on WMT21-22 benchmarks confirm that our model substantially outperforms previous preference models and leading general-purpose LLMs. The main contributions of this work are threefold:

•

We identify and analyze two critical limitations in current preference learning for MT: a narrow, static reward perspective that overlooks hallucinations, and inefficient data utilization that discards valuable learning signals.

•

We propose M
2
PO, a novel framework that synergistically integrates: (1) a multi-perspective reward engine featuring both a dedicated hallucination penalty and an innovative dynamic scoring curriculum; and (2) a multi-pair optimization strategy to maximize data exploitation.

•

We demonstrate that M
2
PO establishes a new state-of-the-art for preference optimization in MT, outperforming existing methods and top-tier LLMs on WMT benchmarks.

Related Work

2.1
Large Language Models for Machine Translation

The advent of LLMs
(
Jiao et al. 2023
;
Achiam et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Comanici et al. 2025
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
has fundamentally shifted the MT paradigm. Unlike previous NMT architectures
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Koishekenov et al. 2022
)
, decoder-only models like GPT-4o
(
Hurst et al. 2024
)
and Llama
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
have demonstrated remarkable translation capabilities, even in zero-shot settings. The dominant approach to adapt these models is SFT on high-quality parallel corpora
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Rei et al. 2025
)
. Our work builds upon these powerful LLMs as the backbone for our preference optimization framework.

2.2
Preference Optimization for Machine Translation

Supervised Fine-Tuning, while effective for adapting LLMs, is limited by its Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) objective. This approach struggles to capture nuanced human preferences and can penalize diverse yet correct outputs. To address this, researchers have shifted towards learning from comparative feedback, first through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
. More recently, direct preference optimization methods have emerged, offering simpler and more stable alternatives that align policies directly on preference data without the complexities of RL
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
;
Ethayarajh et al. 2024
;
Meng et al. 2024
)
.

This paradigm shift has garnered significant attention from the machine translation community, leading to a surge of research into applying preference optimization to improve translation quality
(
Xu et al. 2024a
;
Sun et al. 2025
;
Zeng et al. 2024
)
. A prevailing strategy in MT is to employ Quality Estimation (QE) models as a proxy for human judgment to generate preference datasets
(
Xu et al. 2024b
;
He et al. 2024
;
Agrawal et al. 2024
)
. However, this approach suffers from two fundamental limitations that M
2
PO is designed to address. First, it relies on a narrow and static reward perspective; as demonstrated in Section
3
, QE scores struggle to penalize translation hallucinations, and their static nature allows these flawed signals to be propagated throughout training. Second, the method is data-inefficient, as selecting only a single
chosen
and
rejected
sample discards valuable learning signals from the remaining candidates.

2.3
Detecting and Mitigating Hallucination in MT

Translation hallucination is a critical failure mode for modern MT systems
(
Yang et al. 2019
;
Guerreiro et al. 2022
;
Guerreiro et al. 2023
)
. Detection methods aim to identify hallucinatory content via specialized classifiers
(
Dale et al. 2022
;
Vamvas and Sennrich 2022
;
Guerreiro et al. 2022
;
Gogoulou et al. 2025
)
. Mitigation strategies include modifying the training objective or augmenting data with negative examples
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
. While effective, these approaches often treat hallucination as an isolated problem. In contrast, M
2
PO integrates hallucination mitigation directly into the preference learning framework. It uses a dual-factor reward signal (QE + alignment penalty) to systematically penalize unfaithful translations during preference pair construction, treating factuality as a core dimension of translation quality.

3
Motivation: The Unreliability of QE-based Rewards

The efficacy of preference optimization hinges on the quality of its underlying reward signal. In machine translation, QE models
(
Guerreiro et al. 2024
)
are commonly used as a proxy for human judgment to automatically label preference data at scale. While these models show a reasonable correlation with general translation quality, we posit that their reliability breaks down when confronted with nuanced yet critical errors, particularly translation hallucinations. To validate this hypothesis, we conducted an in-depth analysis that motivates the design of M
2
PO.

Implication.

Taken together, these findings reveal a critical flaw in the prevailing preference optimization pipeline for MT: relying on a standard QE model as the sole reward source introduces a systematic bias. The model is prone to assigning abnormally high scores to fluent but partially hallucinated translations, leading to flawed preference pairs where a factually incorrect translation might be chosen over a slightly less fluent but accurate one. This is the central challenge our M
2
PO framework is designed to address.

4
The M
2
PO Framework

Our proposed framework,
M
2
PO
, systematically enhances standard preference tuning to address unreliable reward signals and inefficient data utilization. The workflow, depicted in Figure
2
, consists of three main stages: candidate set construction, dynamic preference modeling, and a multi-component optimization objective.

4.1
Preliminaries: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Our method builds upon DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
, which optimizes a policy model
\pi_{\theta}
against a reference model
\pi_{ref}
using pairs of winning (
y_{w}
) and losing (
y_{l}
) responses. The standard DPO loss is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\log\sigma\left(\beta\left(\Delta_{\pi_{\theta}}-\Delta_{\pi_{ref}}\right)\right)

(1)

where
\Delta_{\pi}=\log\pi(y_{w}|x)-\log\pi(y_{l}|x)
. DPO’s performance depends heavily on the quality and diversity of its preference pairs, which M
2
PO is designed to improve.

4.2
Stage 1: Multi-Source Candidate Set Construction

The foundation of robust preference learning is a diverse dataset. We construct a heterogeneous candidate pool of
K
translations per source by sourcing candidates from leading proprietary models (GPT-4o-mini) and our own SFT model. This multi-source strategy is critical for building model-agnostic quality discernment. Crucially, we enrich this pool with difficult negatives—translations containing subtle but critical flaws (e.g., factual hallucinations) while maintaining high surface-level fluency—to explicitly train the model on challenging cases.

4.3
Stage 2: Dynamic Preference Modeling

A key innovation of M
2
PO is its dynamic approach to create a reliable preference signal. A purely
static
, offline reward model (
r_{s}
) may become unreliable as the policy model improves. Conversely, a purely
dynamic
, self-reward signal based on the model’s own probabilities (
\log\pi_{\theta}
) is prone to "reward hacking."

M
2
PO resolves this by fusing these two signals. First, we compute a robust static score (
r_{s}
) for each candidate using an offline
multi-perspective evaluator
. This process is designed to create a faithfulness-aware reward signal that corrects the known blind spots of standard QE models.

Specifically, our static score is a composite of a general quality score,
r_{qe}
, and a factuality bonus,
S_{align}
, both of which are on a 0-100 scale. The quality score is obtained from a QE model (e.g., KIWI-XXL), while the factuality bonus comes from a word-level alignment tool,
WSPAlign

(
Wu et al. 2023
)
. Since both scores share the same scale, they can be combined directly. The final static reward
r_{s}
is formally defined as:

r_{s}=r_{qe}+\lambda_{f}S_{align}

(2)

where
\lambda_{f}
is a hyperparameter that balances the relative importance of the factuality bonus against the base quality score. This formulation ensures that candidates with strong source-target alignment are systematically up-ranked, yielding a holistic reward signal that considers both translation quality and factual accuracy.

During online training, for each source sentence, we z-score normalize both the static scores (
r_{s}
) and the model’s log-probabilities (
\log\pi_{\theta}
) across the
K
candidates to get
\hat{r}_{s}
and
\hat{r}_{d}
, respectively. These are fused into a dynamic fused score (
r_{\text{fused}}
):

r_{\text{fused}}=(1-\alpha_{t})\hat{r}_{s}+\alpha_{t}\hat{r}_{d}

(3)

The scheduled weight
\alpha_{t}
acts as a curriculum. Early in training, the model relies on the normalized signal from the trusted external evaluator (
\hat{r}_{s}
). As training progresses, the signal from its own refined judgment (
\hat{r}_{d}
) is given more weight. This dynamic score
r_{\text{fused}}
is then used for ranking candidates in the final optimization stage.

4.4
Stage 3: Multi-Component Optimization

The final stage of M
2
PO is the optimization itself. Rather than relying on a single loss, we introduce a composite objective function where each component serves a distinct but complementary purpose. This multi-component approach ensures that the model not only learns nuanced preferences but also maintains global stability and generative quality.

Multi-pair Construction.

The foundation of our optimization is the multi-pair strategy, which maximizes data utilization. For each source text with
K
candidates, we first rank them from best to worst using the dynamic fused score
r_{\text{fused}}
(from Stage 2). Instead of only using the top and bottom candidates, we construct a comprehensive set of
K/2
preference pairs via a head-to-tail pairing scheme: the best candidate is paired with the worst, the second-best with the second-worst, and so on. This ensures that all candidates contribute to the learning process, exposing the model to a rich spectrum of quality trade-offs.

After constructing these pairs, the model is trained with the total loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{M\textsuperscript{2}PO}}=\lambda_{\text{pref}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DM-DPO}}+\lambda_{\text{rank}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Rank}}+\lambda_{\text{bc}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}

(4)

The Preference Learning Engine (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DM-DPO}}
).

The Dynamic Multi-Pair DPO Loss is the core engine driving preference alignment. It operates on the
K/2
pairs generated by our multi-pair strategy:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DM-DPO}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{K/2}w_{i}\cdot\log\sigma\left(\beta\Delta_{\log\pi_{\theta}}^{i}\right)

(5)

where the weight
w_{i}=\text{softmax}(\Delta r_{\text{fused}}^{i}/\tau_{w})
. This dynamic weighting scheme is crucial; it assigns higher weights to pairs with larger score differences (
\Delta r_{\text{fused}}^{i}
), compelling the model to prioritize learning from the most informative and clear-cut examples.

The Global Stability Anchor (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Rank}}
).

The Listwise Ranking Loss
(
Cao et al. 2007
)
acts as a global anchor, preventing the model from drifting as it learns from relative preferences. It compels the model’s output distribution to correlate with the stable, external ranking provided by the static scores (
r_{s}
). We implement this using a ListNet-style cross-entropy loss between the static score distribution (
P_{s}
) and the model’s predicted distribution (
P_{\pi}
):

\displaystyle P_{s}(y_{k})

\displaystyle=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{r_{s}(y_{k})}{\tau_{s}}\right)

(6)

\displaystyle P_{\pi}(y_{k})

\displaystyle=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{k}|x)}{\tau_{s}}\right)

(7)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Rank}}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{s}(y_{k})\log P_{\pi}(y_{k})

(8)

The Generative Quality Safeguard (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}
).

The behavior cloning (BC) Loss
(
Hejna et al. 2023
)
is a simple yet vital safeguard for the model’s fundamental language capabilities. It prevents "reward over-optimization"—a common issue where models sacrifice fluency to maximize a reward signal. This loss is a standard Negative Log-Likelihood objective applied only to the single best candidate
y^{*}
(as determined by the fused score
r_{\text{fused}}
):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}=-\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{*}|x)

(9)

In summary, the M
2
PO objective functions as a synergistic system.
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DM-DPO}}
acts as the engine, pushing the model to learn nuanced quality distinctions from a wide array of examples. Simultaneously,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Rank}}
serves as a compass, providing a stable, global sense of direction grounded in expert scores. Finally,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}
provides the safety rails, ensuring the model does not "derail" by sacrificing its core generative fluency. This balanced approach is key to producing a robust and well-aligned translation model.

5.1
Experimental Setup

Preference Data.

Our preference dataset, M
2
PO-Preference, originates from 20,090 unique source sentences from the FLORES-200 dev/test sets
(
Costa-Jussà et al. 2022
)
, spanning 10 translation directions (cs, de, is, zh, ru
\leftrightarrow
en). For each source sentence, we generate a diverse candidate pool of 16 translations (
K=16
) by sampling responses from our initial ALMA-7B-LoRA model
4
4

4

https://huggingface.co/haoranxu/ALMA-7B-Pretrain-LoRA
and querying GPT-4o-mini (see Appendix
A
for translation prompts). Crucially, this pool is augmented with difficult negatives: fluent translations specifically mined for their high KIWI-XXL scores despite low factuality bonuses from our
WSPAlign
-based tool. Every candidate in this enriched pool is then scored offline by our Multi-Perspective Evaluator, which produces the final static score (
r_{s}
) by combining the KIWI-XXL quality score and the factuality bonus, as defined in our methodology.

Implementation Details.

Our experiments are based on the open-source ALMA-7B-LoRA model
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
, which we adapt using a parameter-efficient LoRA strategy. The LoRA adapters are configured with a rank of 32 and a LoRA alpha of 64. The models are trained for 2 epochs using the AdamW optimizer
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017
)
with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-5}
and a batch size of 32. Key M
2
PO hyperparameters include a factuality bonus weight of
\lambda_{f}=1.0
, a DPO temperature of
\beta=0.1
, multi-component loss weights (
\lambda_{\text{pref}}=1.0,\lambda_{\text{rank}}=0.5,\lambda_{\text{bc}}=1.0
), a linear scheduler for the dynamic score weight
\alpha_{t}
(increasing from 0.1 to 0.9), and temperatures for the ranking and weighting losses (
\tau_{s}=0.4,\tau_{w}=0.3
).

5.2
Evaluation Protocol

Our evaluation protocol is designed for a rigorous and comprehensive assessment, centered on a multi-faceted metric suite and a strict separation of training and testing signals. All evaluations are conducted in a zero-shot setting.

Test Corpora and Decoding.

We evaluate on a suite of official test sets from WMT’21, WMT’22. Our primary benchmark covers all 10 target translation directions, combining pairs from WMT’21 (en
\leftrightarrow
is) and WMT’22 (the remaining 8 directions). Unless stated otherwise, translations from all open-source models are generated via beam search with a beam size of 5, following the ALMA setting.

Evaluation Metrics.

We employ a three-pronged approach to measure translation quality, adhering to a strict no-leakage principle where the training reward metric (KIWI-XXL) is never used for evaluation. Our metric suite includes the following:

•

Reference-Based Metric
: We use the highly-correlated wmt22-comet-da
5
5

5

https://huggingface.co/Unbabel/wmt22-comet-da
(COMET-22) for a robust assessment of translation quality against ground-truth references.

•

Reference-Free Metric
: To evaluate quality beyond a single reference, which is crucial for diverse LLM-generated translations, we leverage a state-of-the-art reference-free metric, specifically XCOMET.

•

Faithfulness Metric
: To evaluate faithfulness, we introduce a
Coverage Score
. This metric is generated by a Gemini-2.0-Flash-based evaluator, inspired by recent LLM-as-judge methodologies
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
(see Appendix
A
for the prompt). It specifically quantifies the semantic overlap between the source and translation, penalizing both hallucinations (added content) and omissions (lost content). This directly addresses the content integrity blind spot of traditional QE metrics, which we identified as a key issue in our motivation (Section
3
).

6.2
Impact of Preference Pairing Strategies

The multi-pair component of M
2
PO is flexible, allowing for different strategies in constructing preference pairs
(y_{w},y_{l})
from the ranked candidate pool. We investigate three primary strategies to understand their impact:

•

Many-vs-Many:
Our default head-to-tail approach, which utilizes all
K/2
pairs by matching the best candidate with the worst, the second-best with the second-worst, and so on.

•

Many-vs-One:
All
K/2
winning candidates are paired against the single candidate with the lowest reward score.

•

One-vs-Many:
The single candidate with the highest reward score is paired against all
K/2
losing candidates.

As shown in Table
5
, the performance differences across these strategies are minimal. In the en
\rightarrow
xx direction, the top score (93.53) is only 0.11 points higher than the next-best strategy, while in the xx
\rightarrow
en direction the total performance spread is less than 0.2 points. This indicates that the M
2
PO framework is highly robust to the specific pairing method. Nevertheless, the
Many-vs-Many
strategy, which leverages the most diverse set of quality trade-offs, consistently yields the best results in both directions and is thus used as our default configuration.

6.3
Ablation Studies

To dissect the M
2
PO framework and quantify the contribution of its key components, we conduct a series of ablation studies. The experiments are performed on the WMT21-22 test sets by systematically removing individual components from the full framework. The results are summarized in Table
6
.

The analysis reveals that the composite loss function is the primary driver of performance, with two components being particularly critical. The most significant degradation occurs when removing the regularization loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}
), causing a catastrophic drop of over 5 XCOMET points in both directions. This confirms its vital role as a safeguard against reward over-optimization, preventing the model from sacrificing generative quality. The core preference learning engine (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DM-DPO}}
) is also essential; its removal results in a substantial performance decline of -1.12 (en
\rightarrow
xx) and -1.53 (xx
\rightarrow
en) points, affirming its central role in aligning the model with nuanced preferences.

Our core architectural innovations also prove to be crucial. Disabling the Multi-Pair strategy leads to a significant performance drop, especially in the en
\rightarrow
xx direction (-1.02 points), which validates that maximizing data utilization is key to robust learning. Similarly, removing the Dynamic Score by relying solely on the static reward (
\alpha_{t}=0
) consistently harms performance, demonstrating the value of fusing an expert signal with the model’s own evolving judgment. The ranking loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Rank}}
) also serves as an important stabilizer, as its absence leads to a notable decline in scores.

These studies confirm that all components of the M
2
PO framework are integral to its success. They work in synergy to strike a crucial balance between aggressive preference alignment and the preservation of fundamental generative quality.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced M
2
PO, a data-centric framework designed to address the challenges of narrow reward perspectives and inefficient data utilization in preference optimization for MT. Our framework’s core innovations are a multi-perspective reward engine—which uniquely combines a hallucination penalty for factuality with a dynamic scoring mechanism for adaptive quality assessment—and a multi-pair optimization strategy that exploits the entire pool of candidate translations. Extensive experiments on WMT21-22 benchmarks show that M
2
PO establishes a new performance standard for its model class, outperforming strong open-source baselines. Crucially, it elevates our 7B model to a level that is highly competitive with powerful proprietary systems like GPT-4o and even surpasses its "teacher" model, GPT-4o-mini, validating its ability to learn a superior preference function.
