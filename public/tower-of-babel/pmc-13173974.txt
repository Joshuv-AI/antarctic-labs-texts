Correlates of marital dissolution among ever-married women in Cameroon: a cross-sectional demographic and health survey
Authors: Miranda Ngumbusi Tumanjong, Stephen Ojiambo Wandera, Betty Kwagala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to assess the factors associated with marital dissolution among women in Cameroon.MethodsThe 2018 Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey data was used. A sample of 4,562 women was selected from the domestic violence module. Frequency distributions, Chi-square tests, and Complementary Loglog regressions were used to establish the factors that were associated with marital dissolution among married women in Cameroon.ResultsOver one in ten women (11%) of the ever-married women experienced marital dissolution. Marital dissolution was more among women from older age groups (35–39 and 40–49 years); certain regions e.g. Adamawa (aOR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.05–3.47), Centre region (aOR = 2.43; 95% CI: 1.38–4.27), Far-north (aOR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.10-3.00) and North-west (aOR = 2.65; 95% CI: 1.36–5.17); and in urban residence (aOR = 1.57; 95% CI: 1.12–2.19). In addition, MD was higher among women from the poorer wealth quintile (aOR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.05–2.67); working status (aOR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.03–1.84); those whose partners had control behavior (aOR 1.78; 95% CI: 1.29–2.47) and those who experienced any form of IPV (aOR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.42–2.56). Conversely, MD was less among women who had six to nine children (aOR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.21–0.82), and ten or more children (aOR = 0.24; 95% CI: 0.07–0.81); and those economically empowered (aOR 0.48; 95% CI: 0.33–0.70).ConclusionsMarital dissolution in Cameroon was higher among older women, from some regions (Adamawa, Centre, Far North, and North West), urban residents, the poorest wealth quintile, being employed, those with partners’ controlling behaviors, and intimate partner violence. Conversely, MD was lower among women who had a higher number of children ever born and were economically empowered (owned property alone or jointly with their spouses).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04686-0.

Background

Marriage or marital dissolution (MD), also known as divorce or separation, occurs when the bond of matrimony between a married couple is terminated. In addition, the loss of one’s partner in death also dissolves a marriage [1–3]. However, for this study, marital dissolution means divorce and separation other studies have defined it [4, 5].

Globally, the divorce rate more than doubled between 1970 and 2008 from 2.6 divorces to 5.5 for every 1,000 married persons [6]. In the developed world, 20% to 30% of European marriages dissolve within 15 years compared to 42% in the United States [7].

Estimates for 33 countries in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) showed that 25% of marriages ended in a divorce between 15 and 19 years after the commencement of the first union [8]. In some African countries like Liberia, Congo, and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), at least half of unions ended within the first 20 years [8, 9]. In Ethiopia, marital dissolution was estimated at 26% [10]. However, these studies focused on individual determinants and gave no recognition to the broader cultural, social, and marital context in which marital dissolution unfolded. Studies on the determinants of marital dissolution in SSA, where marriage dissolution has traditionally been rare, but has increased considerably in recent years [11].

In Cameroon, details on marital life courses are usually lacking, as most countries in SSA do not have established civil registration systems that permit a continuous and systematic collection of nuptiality data [9]. They depend on data sources such as the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), which lack such critical information as the date of union dissolution, and whether the union dissolution was due to divorce or separation [9]. The 2018 Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey - Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (CDHS-MICS) established that 6% of Cameroonian women were divorced or separated [12].

Marital dissolution has serious consequences for partners and family members, including the children. It has psychological, social, and economic implications for men and women as marital partners [1, 13, 14]. Dissolution results in economic disadvantage among couples, with women experiencing a steeper decline than men [13]. The standard of living of women decreases by 27%, while men may experience a 10% increase [15]). Mental health problems occur after marital dissolution. The effect of divorce on children is associated with academic difficulties and disruptive behaviors such as theft, disrespect, violent behavior, drug and alcohol abuse. Such children have higher rates of depressed mood, lower self-esteem, and emotional distress [16].

Theoretical framework

This study is guided by Becker’s theories on marriage (1973 and 1974) and Becker and collaborators’ 1977 economic analysis of marital instability [17–20]. These theories include factors that are associated with the rise in divorce. Marriages dissolve when the utility expected from staying married falls below the utility expected from divorce [19–21]. The theory assumes that individuals maximize utilities from commodities they expect to consume in a lifetime, ranking marital strategies by their full wealth and choosing the highest. Since uncertainty prevails, unfavorable outcomes may occur [19–21].

In their study, Becker and collaborators predict that dissolution probabilities are lowered by an increase in the expected value of positively sorted variables (e.g., men’s earnings); an increase in the time spent seeking a spouse (age at marriage); and an increase in marital-specific capital (e.g., children), which increases with duration and decreases with order of marriage. Dissolution probabilities are raised by unexpected changes in values of positively sorted variables (e.g., husband’s earnings), by an increase in the expected value of negatively sorted variables (e.g., wife’s earnings relative to husband’s), and by a larger discrepancy in mate traits (e.g., IQ, religion, race) than would occur in optimal sorting [19] .

Becker’s predictions are generally consistent with empirical findings. Husband’s income relates negatively to marital dissolution. An increase in wife’s income and the ratio of her earnings to family income increases dissolution [22], and duration of marriage relate negatively to dissolution. Becker’s theory explains a variety of findings. However, it has several problems from a sociological perspective. First, it fails to predict explicitly the utility of situations for the individuals experiencing them [17, 18].

Second, every divorce involves two people whose experience and perceptions of benefits and costs may differ [23]. In principle, Becker’s theory could separate such effects, but in practice Becker does not attempt to do so. Yet some variables could affect a husband’s or wife’s desire for divorce oppositely [17, 23]. A wife’s high earnings may motivate her to end a bad marriage; this effect could be reinforced if the husband were threatened by his wife’s earnings or if he felt that her earnings relieved him of financial responsibilities. In contrast, the wife might be a more attractive partner if she had higher earnings; her motivation to end the marriage might be offset by his desire to continue it. In sum, Becker’s theory predicts that a particular event will increase the probability of dissolution, but it does not tell us why. Third, Becker’s theory does not distinguish the ways men and women may perceive cost-benefit. Thus, the theory doesn’t consider the household division of labour which my bring contention between a married couple [17, 23].

This theory is based on two basic assumptions. The first is that, since marriage is practically always voluntary, either by the persons marrying or their parents, the theory of preferences can be readily applied, and persons marrying (or their parents) can be assumed to expect to raise their utility level above what it would be where they remain single. The second is that, since many men and women compete as they seek mates, a market in marriages can be presumed to exist. Each person tries to find the best mate, subject to the restrictions imposed by market conditions [20].

Risk factors for marital dissolution

Marital dissolution is explained by several factors. Some studies show that marital dissolution is associated with age, age differences, age at first marriage, level of education, place of residence, number of children, wealth quintile, religion, region, and intimate partner violence – either physical, sexual or emotional [1, 24–26]. Other studies have shown that an increase in women’s autonomy, weakening ties, polygamy, fertility, and HIV are associated with marital dissolution [27, 28].

In Cameroon, cultural norms have greatly influenced marriages. For instance, some cultural norms such as traditional gender roles, often place a heavy burden on women, expecting them to fulfill specific duties such as child-rearing and household [29]. When women seek to break away from these roles, it can lead to marital conflicts and eventual dissolution [30]. Economic dependency on husbands can limit women’s autonomy and decision-making power in the marriage. Also, divorce was often stigmatizing in many Cameroonian cultures, which discouraged women from leaving unhappy marriages. However, as societal attitudes slowly changed, more women were willing to pursue divorce despite the stigma. In addition, inheritance laws and property rights also influence marital stability, as women who were denied their rightful inheritance or property rights felt compelled to leave the marriage [31]. These cultural factors interact in complex ways, influencing the dynamics of marital relationships and the likelihood of dissolution.

Age at first marriage affects marital dissolution. Women who marry while still in their teens were twice as likely to divorce compared to women in their thirties [24]. However, another study done in 19 countries in SSA indicated that most women who had experienced marital dissolution were those who married late [25]. These findings are controversial and therefore warrant further investigation in Cameroon.

Women’s place of residence has been associated with marital dissolution. For instance, women living in urban areas are more likely to experience marital dissolution than those in rural settings [25, 26].

The socioeconomic position of women, including wealth status, education, and employment are associated with marital dissolution [32, 33]. Education is a fundamental step towards promoting equal participation of women in society [1]. Moreover, education empowers women to acquire knowledge, skills, and self-confidence. It enables them to take advantage of health and economic opportunities, raises their standard of living, and thus enables them to participate fully in development processes [34]. The risk of marital dissolution is lower among women with a college degree in developed countries [13]. However, among women in developing countries, the higher their education attainment, the higher their chances of divorce [10, 35].

Religion and culture influence marital dissolution. India’s low rate of divorce (1%) is attributed to its culture and religion, which promotes monogamy [1]. The lowest divorce rates are often found in countries with large Catholic populations, such as Chile, Colombia, and Ireland, as well as Muslim countries like Libya, Uzbekistan, and Bahrain [36]. It is believed that cultural influence is still strong in sub-Saharan Africa [11].

Children ever born (CEB) have been associated with marital dissolution. Women who had children were more likely to stay in unions compared to those who did not [24, 32]. Voluntary and involuntary childlessness has been associated with divorce [24, 26–33, 37, 38].

Heavy drinking and intimate partner violence (IPV) are associated with marital dissolution. For women experiencing IPV, the end of the union may represent a positive development for the victim [39, 40]. However, women who end abusive unions often experience distressing, conflict-ridden interactions with their ex-partners, especially if involved in shared parenting [41]. Even in the absence of children, the initial period post-dissolution is both physically and psychologically dangerous for the victim. More than a third of women who became separated from their abusers experienced continued physical abuse [39, 42, 43].

Despite available evidence on marital dissolution in Cameroon, there is a dearth of information on factors associated with marital dissolution among women [12]. The study done in Cameroon was based on systematic reviews on marital (union) dissolution in SSA [3]. However, a country-level analysis of factors associated with marital dissolution has not been done. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the factors associated with marital dissolution among women in Cameroon.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study used secondary data obtained from the 2018 Cameroon Demographic and Health Survey (CDHS), which was accessed with permission from Measure DHS. The CDHS used a cross-sectional study design. It is a national population-based household survey that uses a random, stratified, 2-stage sample technique. The Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) are designed as nationally representative surveys [12].

Participants: sampling and sample size

The DHS employs a probability sampling methodology that balances representativeness with feasibility, ensuring that selected households and individuals reflect the broader population while maintaining efficiency in terms of cost, time, and logistics. The DHS adopted a two-stage stratified sampling design. In the first stage, clusters, typically census enumeration areas, are selected. In the second stage, households within these clusters are randomly chosen, and only eligible women residing in those households are interviewed. Eligibility is generally restricted to women aged 15–49. Women outside this age range are excluded, as they fall beyond the scope of the core indicators measured by DHS [12].

By carefully selecting clusters and households, DHS ensures that the resulting sample mirrors the population’s distribution across regions, urban and rural settings, and socioeconomic groups. This methodological approach allows DHS to generate reliable, generalizable data on key health and demographic indicators without the impracticality of interviewing all women in each country [12].

A sample of 14,677 women (unweighted) aged 15–64 years was surveyed in the 2018 CDHS. The selection of observations was at three levels. First, we dropped women who had never been married (n = 4,915) and kept those who had ever been in a union (n = 9,762). Second, we kept women who were selected and interviewed for the domestic violence module (variable v044). Thus, a sample of 4,690 women (unweighted) was considered for further analysis. The number of excluded women who were not interviewed for the domestic violence module was 5,072.

The rationale for this choice was that some of the key predictors, like partners’ control behavior, decision-making, and empowerment of women, had over 5,072 missing values because these questions were not asked of women who had not been selected for the domestic violence module. Previous studies investigating domestic violence and other outcomes have used the same approach [44, 45]. Finally, we dropped those who were widowed (n = 194) and ended with a final unweighted sample of 4,496 women.

Variables and measures

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was marital dissolution through divorce or separation from husband/primary sexual partner. Women who were married and living together with a partner were recoded as “0”, while those who were divorced/separated were recoded as “1” as reported in one study [25].

Explanatory variables

Explanatory variables included women’s and their partners’ demographic and socioeconomic variables, decision-making, ownership of household assets, economic empowerment, sexual empowerment, and intimate partner violence.

Women’s socio-demographic variables included age, region, residence, and children ever born. Age was recoded into 5 categories: 15–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39 and 40–49. The residence was recoded into urban and rural areas. The region was recoded into 10 categories: Adamawa, Centre, East, Far-north, Littoral, North, North-west, West South, and South-west. Religion was recoded into Catholic, Protestant, Muslim, and Others. Children ever born were recoded into four categories: None, 1–5 children, 6–9 children, and 10 + children.

Socio-economic factors included educational level (no education, primary, secondary, or higher), religion, wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest), respondent currently working (no and yes) and payment for work in cash or kind. The ‘yes” responses were coded ‘1’ and the “no” responses were coded ‘0’.

Partners’ characteristics included age group, controlling behavior, having cowives, and drinking alcohol (Kwagala et al., 2016b). Age group was a categorical variable. Partners’ controlling behavior was based on five questions for the most recent partner: husband/partner jealous if the respondent talks with other men; husband/partner accuses respondent of unfaithfulness; husband/partner does not permit respondent to meet female friends; husband/partner tries to limit respondent’s contact with family; and husband/partner insists on knowing where respondent is. An aggregate index for controlling behavior was created using the egen command specifying the value of 1 for any control issues and otherwise. The number of cowives was categorized. Partners’ alcohol drinking was a binary variable (0 = No and 1 = Yes).

A decision-making index was created as an index from four questions about women’s earnings, their own health, household purchases, and family visits. A woman deciding alone or jointly with a partner was recoded as 1 and 0 otherwise. Economic empowerment was measured by women’s responses to ownership of property (house, land, land tenure rights). We defined economic empowerment to mean a woman owning property, whether either alone or jointly with a partner (recoded as 1 = Yes and 0 = No).

Sexual empowerment was a woman’s ability to refuse sex or ask a partner to use a condom (recoded as 1 = yes and 0 = No). Finally, three indices were used to measure intimate partner violence: physical violence, emotional violence, and sexual violence [45, 46]. These were derived from three questions: “experienced any physical violence?” “experienced any emotional violence?” and “experienced any sexual violence?”.

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is measured as a lifetime experience rather than a point-in-time occurrence. This means the indicators capture whether a woman has experienced IPV at any time in her life, not just in the past year or during the current relationship. The responses of these variables were categorized into “yes” and “no”. An aggregate measure for experiencing any of the three forms of intimate partner violence was generated by combining the three indices. The “yes” responses were coded ‘1 for experiencing any of the three forms of violence, and the “no " responses for not reporting any of the forms of violence were coded ‘0’.

The challenge with the dataset is the fact that empowerment of women was not captured among ever-married women. This question was asked only to currently married women. Second, most of the background characteristics of partners, such as age, are not captured. This makes it difficult to ascertain the relationship between age differences and marital dissolution. Also, although they are nationally representative, DHS data are cross-sectional and therefore cannot determine causal relationships. For example, employment, wealth quintile, and marital dissolution.

Statistical analysis

Frequency distributions were used to describe the descriptive characteristics of women and their partners. Means were presented for age as a continuous variable. Cross-tabulations with Pearson’s Chi-Square test (χ2) tests were used to investigate associations between marital dissolution and explanatory variables (women’s background factors and IPV). The level of statistical significance using the p-value was set at p < 0.05. For all the models, the variables that were insignificant or showed perfect failure (p-value not computed) at the bivariate level of analysis were excluded.

Finally, two complementary log-log (clog-log) regression models were used to identify factors associated with MD. Model 1 adjusted for women’s socio-demographics. Model 2 adjusted for partners’ factors, controlling behavior, economic empowerment, and intimate partner violence. Clog-log regression technique was used because the outcome variable was binary and had an asymmetrical distribution - as a rare outcome [47]. The complementary log-log regression model is recommended for rare outcomes or when the data are asymmetrical and the outcome is binary [48]. In this case, marital dissolution had an uneven distribution of 11%. The strengths of the relationships were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Multicollinearity was assessed by running the “collin” command and the pairwise correlation technique. The variance inflation factor (VIF) of economic empowerment and IPV were 7.5 and 5.0, respectively. The rest of the variables had VIF below 2.0 and the mean VIF was 2.35. Pairwise correlations were conducted to check for any strong relationships among explanatory variables. The results indicated the use of indices for scales such as economic empowerment and intimate partner violence (Results not presented).

The data were weighted using the domestic violence module weights (variable d005) to adjust the sample to reflect the key population, to cater for non-response, and to limit potential bias. All analyses were performed using STATA version 18.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

Table 1 shows the descriptive characteristics of the sample. The mean age of all the women was 31 years. One in ten (11%) of Cameroonian women reported marital dissolution through separation or divorce. Nearly half (47%) of the women had a cohabitation duration between 0 and 19 years. Nearly a quarter (24%) of the women were aged 15–24 years. The Centre region had the highest representation (20%). More than half (52%) of the women lived in rural areas.

Descriptive statistics

Four in ten (41%) had attained a secondary or higher educational level. 35% of the respondents were Catholics. About three-quarters (73%) of women had between one and five children. 21% belonged to the poorer and middle class respectively. More than half (71%) of women were employed, and nearly six in ten (59%) received payment for their work either in cash or in kind.

The majority (36%) of the women had partners who were in the age group 30–39 years. Most (63%) women had husbands or partners who were controlling. Over half (66%) of the women had no co-wives. Almost half (46%) of the women had partners who drank alcohol.

The majority of the women decided either alone or jointly with their partners on their earnings (47%), health (48%), household purchases (51%), and family visits (55%). Personal or joint ownership of property among women was low: house (19%), land (15%), and land tenure rights (6%). Economic empowerment was low (23%). Sexual empowerment was high (64%): refusing sex (71%) and asking a partner to use a condom (54%). Experiencing any form of IPV was 45%: physical IPV (35%), emotional IPV (29%), and sexual IPV (10%).

Association between marital dissolution and independent factors

Table 1 shows the factors that are (not) associated with marital dissolution. Factors which were associated with marital dissolution were women’s age group (p = 0.015), region (p < 0.001), residence (p = 0.003), educational level (p = 0.002), religion (p < 0.001), children ever born (p = 0.035), wealth quintile (p < 0.001), respondent currently working (p < 0.001), and being paid in cash (p < 0.001). Other statistically significant factors were husband/partner’s control issues (p < 0.001), co-wives (p < 0.001), husband/partner drinking alcohol (p = 0.016), decision-making indicators, and Intimate partner violence (physical, emotional, and sexual) (p < 0.001), respectively.

On the other hand, the factors that were not associated with MD were land ownership, land tenure rights, and duration of cohabitation. Those that showed perfect failure were the partner’s age, refusing sex, and asking a partner to use condoms. There were no variations because some women lacked partners due to marital dissolution (Table 1).

The women’s age group was correlated with MD. Women in older age groups had increased odds of MD: age 35–39 years (aOR = 2.33; 95% CI: 1.54–3.55) and age 40–49 years (aOR = 2.76; 95% CI: 1.82–4.18), compared to those age 15–24 years.

The likelihood of MD was higher among women in Adamawa (aOR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.05–3.47), Centre region (aOR = 2.43; 95% CI: 1.38–4.27), Far-north (aOR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.10-3.00) and North-west (aOR = 2.65; 95% CI: 1.36–5.17), compared to those in the North.

Place of residence was correlated with MD. Urban women had higher odds of dissolved marriages compared to rural ones (aOR = 1.57; 95% CI: 1.12–2.19). The number of children ever born was associated with MD. Women who had six to nine children (aOR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.21–0.82) and ten or more children (aOR = 0.24; 95% CI: 0.07–0.81) had decreased odds of dissolved marriages compared to those with no children.

Women from the poorer wealth quintile had higher odds of MD compared to the poorest (aOR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.05–2.67). Marital dissolution was identified to be more common among women who were currently working compared to those who were not (aOR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.03–1.84).

Women whose partners had control behavior had higher odds (aOR 1.78; 95% CI: 1.29–2.47) of MD compared to those who did not. Economically empowered women (owned property either alone or jointly with their partners) had a lower prevalence of MD (aOR 0.48; 95% CI: 0.33–0.70). Finally, women who experienced any form of IPV (either physical, or sexual or emotional) were at higher odds of marital dissolution (aOR = 1.91; 95% CI: 1.42–2.56) compared to those who did not.

Discussion

This paper aimed to investigate the correlates of marital dissolution among ever-married women in Cameroon. The prevalence of MD among these women is relatively low (11%) compared to an average of 25% for SSA [26]. This prevalence was higher than the national prevalence (6%) for all women 15–49 years old [12]. The apparent discrepancy in prevalence is largely attributable to our focus on ever-married women, who are at risk of dissolution, whereas the DHS denominator includes all women aged 15–49, many of whom have never married. In addition, our definition of dissolution encompasses both divorce and separation, while DHS statistics emphasize formal divorce. Thus, our findings reflect a broader conceptualization of marital instability among women in Cameroon.

The key correlates of MD were women’s demographic factors (age group, region, and place of residence, children ever born), socioeconomic factors (wealth quintile, respondent currently working, and economic empowerment), and partners’ factors (controlling behaviors and IPV).

Older women (age groups 35–39 and 40–49 years) had a higher prevalence of marital dissolution. “The fondness males have for women while they are younger may begin to wane as the women grow older” [33]. This may be due to the broad cultural shifts in the meanings of marriage, divorce, and separation that influence all generations, including older adults. There is a shift in cultural norms among women, with women being more exposed to modern values through education, employment, media, and legal reforms. These reforms have led to growing awareness and empowerment. As a result, many women tend resist some patriarchal practices like polygamy, infidelity, or domestic violence. Women are more empowered to challenge these patriarchal expectations of obedience and silence. These findings are like those of other studies carried out in SSA [24, 25, 49]. Specifically, the weakening appreciation of marriage as a lifelong institution may contribute to an increase in divorce among middle-aged and older adults. Also, marriages change and evolve over the course of life and thus may no longer meet one’s needs at later life stages.

MD was more in Adamawa, Centre, Far-North, and North-West regions. The higher odds of MD in the Centre region could be attributed to the fact that the region, being mostly urban, is the capital and is more populated and has diverse people and cultures. Some women may be influenced by the lifestyle in this area, which may cause them to divorce or separate from their partners. However, other community-related factors were not considered. The focus was on regional differentials in the risk of divorce [50, 51].

Women in urban areas of Cameroon had dissolved unions more than those in rural areas. Various findings showed that women in urban areas were more likely to divorce or separate from their partners. Urbanization brings weakening kinship ties and strength in unions, giving way to marital dissolution [25, 26]. This could imply that women in urban areas are less tolerant compared to those in rural areas. In urban settings, women are more exposed to modern norms, such as globalized ideas on gender equality and individual rights. They have more access to civil laws, which offer women legal recourse in cases of abuse or neglect [52], thus increasing the odds of dissolving unions. An increase in rights awareness and the possibility of independent sustenance could contribute to this. Also, there is less stigma attached to marital dissolution in urban areas where people tend to be more anonymous [33, 51].

The number of children ever born (CEB) was significantly associated with MD. Women who had 6 or more children had a lower prevalence of marital dissolution. Large numbers of children call for commitment to maintain stability in the home [32]. Often, such women may have no way out, given the large numbers of children [24, 53]. This finding matches those of other studies in SSA, which indicated that women who had many children were less likely to dissolve their unions. Some women also think that the more children they have, the less likely their partners will have extramarital affairs [54]. The effect sizes of CEB did not change much when we adjusted for all variables (Model 2).

Findings about wealth index were that women in the poorer class had dissolved unions more than those from the poorest category. This implies that having some resources increases the ability and autonomy of a woman to leave the union, while extreme poverty constrains exit and traps women in unions. Couples separate to seek better standards of living and wellbeing [55]. Marital dissolution significantly reduces with increasing wealth status. Higher wealth status among women may connote a higher sense of economic empowerment and autonomy [33]. These findings are like those of other studies across SSA [24, 25, 56]. However, the strengths of the association were not that strong. They gained magnitude when we adjusted for women’s employment status.

Women who were currently working reported MD more than those who were not working. Being employed as a woman reduces the dependence on one’s partner, thereby empowering them to dissolve marital unions that violate their rights [33]. Some studies have indicated that an increase in employment rates was associated with an increase in MD [57]. This is arguable, however, since the exact time of the dissolution and the employment status of the women were not clearly captured in the survey, given that it was a cross-sectional study. If men hold a conservative gender ideology, they will exhibit dissatisfaction with their wives’ employment. This breeds conflict, and when not resolved, leads to MD [58].

Partners’ controlling behaviors were associated with MD. The findings show a clear and statistically significant association between partner’s controlling behaviour and the likelihood of union dissolution. More women whose marriages had dissolved reported experiencing controlling behaviour with the former parters (most recent). Using power and control is the most destructive force any human can bring to a marital relationship [59]. Partner’s controlling behavior is linked to IPV and limited marital satisfaction [40, 44, 46].

Economic empowerment was associated with a reduced prevalence of MD. Women who owned property (house, land, and land tenure rights) either alone or jointly with their spouses reported fewer dissolved marriages. This shows the protective nature of solo or joint property ownership in this case. “Women of higher wealth status are less likely to be dependent on their partners. Consequently, they are more likely to easily end their marital unions when they are abused, or when they feel threatened or intimidated” [33]. However, we know that ownership of property can be a source of conflict in marriage and motivate MD.

Intimate partner violence was associated with MD in Cameroon. Low levels of forbearance and differences between the partners result in emotional strain for couples, which leads to MD in many cases. Perhaps, women walk away from such abusive relationships to regain their self-confidence and heal from the traumatic past [33]. IPV also indicates poor marital relationships between couples. This is usually accompanied by extramarital affairs, which may lead to divorce or separation [46].

Strengths and limitations of the data

The strengths of this paper are that it uses a nationally representative sample of women from the Cameroon Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS). Second, it shows the association between economic empowerment, intimate partner violence, and marital dissolution. Third, the stepwise cloglog regression models show the net effect sizes of the correlates of MD.

Despite the strengths, several limitations are worth noting. First, the cross-sectional survey data is collected at a single point in time. Therefore, it is not possible to establish whether the observed factors, such as exposure to domestic violence, economic status, or education, preceded marital dissolution or emerged as consequences of it. For example, women who are divorced may subsequently pursue higher education or employment, making it unclear whether these variables are causes or outcomes [49, 60].

Second, the association between the wealth index and MD requires further interrogation. Perhaps longitudinal surveys would be better placed to address these relationships [50, 51]. Finally, the absence of longitudinal data limits the ability to establish causation and understand the dynamic nature of marital relationships over time. Longitudinal studies would provide a more robust foundation for concluding the causal relationships between the identified factors and marital dissolution.

To address these limitations, future research should employ longitudinal designs that follow individuals over time to capture the sequence of events leading to dissolution. Panel studies or cohort surveys would allow researchers to disentangle cause and effect by observing changes in marital status alongside evolving social, economic, and cultural conditions. Additionally, mixed methods approach, that is, combining quantitative data with qualitative interviews, could provide a richer context on how cultural norms and gender roles interact with individual trajectories. Finally, integrating survival models into longitudinal datasets would not only help to identify correlates but also the timing and risk factors that increase the likelihood of marital dissolution.

The exclusion of certain groups, such as women who had never been married, those not interviewed in the domestic violence module, and widowed women, may introduce selection bias. The final sample affects the representativeness of the findings as it omits other factors that may lead to union dissolution, such as widowhood. Also, restricting to the DV module may also overemphasize associations between partner behaviors and dissolution.

Conclusions

Marital dissolution among ever-married women in Cameroon reflects a convergence of regional, socioeconomic, and behavioral determinants. Evidence from the Demographic and Health Survey indicates that dissolution is disproportionately higher among women in Adamawa, Centre, Far North, and North-West regions, as well as among urban residents, those in the poorest wealth quintile, employed women, and those exposed to partners’ controlling behaviors and intimate partner violence (IPV).

In response to these findings, several policy directions are warranted. First, regional targeting through culturally sensitive interventions is necessary to address localized drivers of marital instability, particularly in high-prevalence regions. Second, economic empowerment initiatives, including poverty reduction and employment support programs, are critical to mitigating the economic stressors that contribute to marital breakdown. Third, the expansion of urban support systems such as counseling, mediation, and legal aid services would provide essential assistance in urban centers where dissolution rates are elevated.

Fourth, violence prevention must be prioritized by strengthening IPV prevention and response mechanisms, enforcing protective laws more rigorously, and promoting community education. Finally, behavioral change campaigns are needed to challenge controlling partner behaviors and foster healthier relationship dynamics, thereby reducing one of the key relational drivers of dissolution. Collectively, these measures underscore the importance of integrated, multi-level interventions that combine economic, social, and behavioral strategies to reduce marital instability and enhance family well-being in Cameroon.
