Case Series of Sustained Fluid‐Associated Weight Gain Following Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator (CFTR) Modulator Therapy
Authors: Emma M. Tillman, Arina Lazutina, Ross C. Robinson, Abi Colwell, Cynthia D. Brown
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

During screening of prospective participants for a pharmacokinetic study of individuals with cystic fibrosis (CF) experiencing adverse events associated with cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulators, we identified four patients with CF or CF‐related disorder who developed significant fluid‐associated weight gain or edema following initiation of CFTR modulator therapy. IRB review was waived per institutional policy (Indiana University IRB). This report does not require approval by the ethics committee or written consent from the patient.

For each case, we collected demographic information, CFTR genotype, baseline and post‐treatment body mass index (BMI), timing and pattern of weight change, and clinical evidence of edema. Laboratory investigations and, when available, cardiac assessments and pharmacokinetic data were reviewed to evaluate potential alternative causes of fluid retention. CFTR modulator dosing, dose adjustments, treatment interruptions, and clinical responses to these changes were documented. Each case was assessed using the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale to evaluate the likelihood of a drug‐related effect [1].

Cases

Case 1

An 18‐year‐old White non‐Hispanic female with genotype F508del/G551D initiated elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI). Due to a prior history of swelling with ivacaftor, she began treatment on a modified regimen consisting of elexacaftor 200 mg/tezacaftor 100 mg/ivacaftor 150 mg once daily without the evening ivacaftor dose. Baseline BMI was 24 kg/m2. Shortly after treatment initiation, she developed marked swelling and rapid weight gain, with BMI increasing to 37.8 kg/m2. The regimen was subsequently reduced to elexacaftor 100 mg/tezacaftor 50 mg/ivacaftor 75 mg daily, which she currently tolerates. Her Naranjo score was 9, indicating a definite association with ETI.

Case 2

A 44‐year‐old White non‐Hispanic female with genotype F508del/F508del initiated tezacaftor/ivacaftor therapy with a baseline BMI of 28 kg/m2. She developed significant lower extremity edema and weight gain, with BMI increasing to 31.6 kg/m2. She subsequently trialed ETI at reduced doses but experienced persistent swelling. She is currently self‐titrating vanzacaftor 10 mg/tezacaftor 50 mg/deutivacaftor 125 mg 3−4 days per week in order to balance CF symptoms and swelling. Her Naranjo score was 9, suggesting a definite relationship with CFTR modulator therapy.

Case 3

A 46‐year‐old White non‐Hispanic male with genotype F508del/R352Q was newly diagnosed with CF and initiated ETI therapy with a baseline BMI of 23.8 kg/m2 and no prior exposure to CFTR modulators. Shortly after starting standard‐dose ETI, he developed significant lower extremity edema and rapid weight gain, with BMI increasing to 33 kg/m2. The regimen was subsequently reduced to elexacaftor 100 mg/tezacaftor 50 mg/ivacaftor 75 mg administered 5 days per week, which he currently tolerates. His Naranjo score was 8, indicating a probable association with ETI.

Case 4

A 54‐year‐old White non‐Hispanic female with a single F508del variant and a diagnosis of CF versus CF‐related disorder initiated standard‐dose ETI with a baseline BMI of 29.4 kg/m2. She developed progressive swelling and weight gain following treatment initiation, with BMI increasing to 31.4 kg/m2. ETI therapy was discontinued due to persistent symptoms. She is not currently receiving CFTR modulator therapy, and BMI has normalized to baseline. Her Naranjo score was 8, suggesting a probable association.

CFTR modulators have transformed the treatment of CF, with clinical trials demonstrating substantial improvements in lung function, nutritional status, and BMI [2, 3]. Post‐marketing experience has confirmed weight gain as a common effect of therapy, typically attributed to improved appetite, reduced energy expenditure, and improved gastrointestinal function [4]. However, sustained hypervolemic edema or fluid‐associated weight gain has not been reported in clinical trials and may therefore be under‐recognized in clinical practice. Published adverse event reports primarily describe hypersensitivity‐related swelling, such as rash or angioedema, rather than persistent non‐allergic fluid retention.

We report four cases of clinically significant edema and fluid‐associated weight gain temporally associated with CFTR modulator therapy (Table 1). In all cases, symptoms led to dose reduction or discontinuation of therapy to manage the fluid‐associated weight gain. Across patients, mean BMI increased from 26.3 ± 2.83 kg/m2 before treatment to 33.45 ± 2.99 kg/m2 following CFTR modulator exposure. Post‐modulator blood pressure was not significantly different than baseline blood pressure. The mean Naranjo score was 8.5, indicating a probable to definite association between therapy and the observed adverse effect.

Although weight gain is commonly observed following initiation of CFTR modulators, it is generally attributed to improvements in nutritional status and metabolic efficiency. In contrast, the cases described here were characterized by clinically apparent edema and rapid weight gain suggestive of fluid accumulation rather than increased adiposity.

The mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain unclear. However, CFTR modulators are known to influence sodium and fluid homeostasis. Berg et al. reported changes in systemic fluid balance following initiation of ETI, including increases in blood pressure and plasma sodium concentrations, altered diuretic responses to oral sodium bicarbonate and volume loading, and reductions in aldosterone concentrations and heart rate [5]. These effects were most pronounced 4−6 weeks after treatment initiation, yet despite these changes, patients continued therapy [5].

Our observations suggest that sustained fluid retention may occur in a small subset of individuals treated with CFTR modulators. Recognition of this potential adverse effect is important, as it may lead to substantial weight gain and necessitate dose adjustment, discontinuation of CFTR modulator therapy, or significant lifestyle modifications.

Sustained fluid‐associated weight gain and edema may occur in a minority of patients receiving CFTR modulators. Clinicians should consider this possibility when evaluating rapid or disproportionate weight gain and unexplained edema following initiation of CFTR modulator therapy.
