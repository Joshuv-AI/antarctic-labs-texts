Risk of excessive macular thinning in diabetic macular edema with ellipsoid zone disruption treated with anti-VEGF: 2-year real-world evidence
Authors: Yu-Kuei Lee, Sheng-Min Hsu, Chun-Hsiang Chang, Jia-Horung Hung, Yi-Sheng Chang, Chia-Hua Lee, Jung-Der Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study investigates whether different morphological features affect the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and central macular thickness (CMT) in patients with diabetic macular edema (DME) undergoing anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy.MethodsWe retrospectively collected data from 727 eyes of 464 patients with DME who received either ranibizumab or aflibercept. We constructed mixed-effects regression models to determine if different morphological features shown on optical coherence tomography would affect post-treatment BCVA and CMT after controlling for clinical variables. In eyes with ellipsoid zone disruption (EZD), the relationship between baseline BCVA and achieving a final CMT < 200 μm was further examined.ResultsAfter 24 months of anti-VEGF treatment, patients demonstrated improvements in BCVA and reductions in CMT. Poorer post-treatment BCVA was associated with older age, longer treatment duration, EZD (p = 0.017) and disorganization of the retinal inner layers (DRIL) (p = 0.001). Excessive macular thinning (final CMT < 200 μm) was more frequently observed in eyes with EZD, particularly among those with a baseline BCVA < 15 ETDRS letters, in exploratory subgroup analyses. The association appeared more pronounced among individuals with increased hyperreflective foci (HRF) in the outer retina.ConclusionIn DME eyes with EZD, a poorer baseline BCVA and increased HRF appear to be statistically associated with excessive macular thinning following anti-VEGF treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00417-026-07221-4.Keywords: Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor, Diabetic macular edema, Ellipsoid zone disruption, Hyperreflective foci, Mixed effects model, Macular thinning, Real-world study

Introduction

In 2025, diabetes affects an estimated 589 million adults worldwide, corresponding to a global prevalence of approximately 11%. Among individuals with diabetes, approximately 30% develop diabetic retinopathy, while fewer than 10% develop sight-threatening disease, including diabetic macular edema (DME), a major cause of vision impairment [1]. Therefore, early diagnosis and treatment of DME are essential.

Currently, the primary treatment for DME involves the intravitreal injection of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents. A systematic review demonstrated that anti-VEGF therapy significantly improved best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) over a 24-month treatment period [2]. Real-world studies have shown that anti-VEGF agents enhance BCVA and reduce central macular thickness (CMT) [3]. Several observational studies comparing different anti-VEGF agents, such as ranibizumab and aflibercept, have found no significant differences in patient outcomes [3, 4].

Despite anti-VEGF therapy, some cases of DME remain refractory to treatment. The limited anatomical response to anti-VEGF agents may be influenced by various clinical factors, including age [5], sex, duration of diabetes [6], glycemic control [7], and specific morphological features shown on optical coherence tomography (OCT) [8, 9]. Clinically, DME can be categorized into distinct morphologies based on OCT findings, including diffuse retinal thickening (DRT), hyperreflective foci (HRF), cystoid macular edema (CME), epiretinal membranes (ERM), ellipsoid zone disruption (EZD), serous retinal detachment (SRD), posterior hyaloidal traction (PHT), and disorganization of the retinal inner layers (DRIL) [10, 11]. Although numerous studies have investigated the impact of the above features on DME outcomes, discrepancies remain, particularly regarding visual outcome and the role of EZD [5, 12–14].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of clinical factors and morphological features, with a focus on EZD, on treatment outcomes in patients with DME undergoing anti-VEGF therapy.

Methods

Study setting and design

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the National Cheng Kung University Hospital (NCKUH) (approval number: B-ER-107-246). We conducted all study procedures in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. After retrieving the relevant medical records of patients with DME who received anti-VEGF agents at a tertiary referral center, we deleted all personal identification information before further analysis.

Data source

All clinical information at the institution, including OCT images stored in the Picture Archiving and Communication Systems, was captured for analysis using an electronic medical record system. Repeated measurements of the BCVA and CMT were used to compare the effects of the two anti-VEGF agents in the treatment of patients with DME.

Participants of different treatment regimens

Among patients newly diagnosed with DME (patients having diabetes with diabetic retinopathy and CMT > 300 μm) in NCKUH between January 2015 and December 2019, we included those who received anti-VEGF therapy of either ranibizumab (Lucentis®; Genentech, California, United States) or aflibercept (EYLEA®; Regeneron, New York, United States) in this study.

Exclusion criteria were as follows; patients who (i) received intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF agents before inclusion, (ii) received more than 1 type of anti-VEGF agent during the follow-up period, (iii) received a dexamethasone intravitreal implant, (iv) had macular edema secondary to conditions besides diabetes, such as age-related macular degeneration or retinal vein occlusion, (v) had other eye diseases that could affect visual acuity, such as glaucoma, vitreous hemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, or uveitis, or (vi) underwent cataract surgery or pars plana vitrectomy during the follow-up period that could affect BCVA.

Study variables

Clinical data, including age, sex, hypertension, duration of diabetes, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels throughout the enrollment period, pan-retinal photocoagulation, diabetes duration, comorbid hypertension, and doses and dates of anti-VEGF injections, were retrieved. The BCVA was converted from a Snellen chart to an Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) letter using the formula ETDRS = 85 + 50 × log10(Snellen) [15]. The BCVA and CMT were recorded at baseline and each clinical visit, approximately 1 month after each intravitreal injection. Spectral Domain-OCT (Topcon, 3D OCT 2000, Tokyo, Japan) was used to assess macular thickness, measured from the internal limiting membrane to the retinal pigment epithelium. The CMT was defined as the average macular thickness within a 1-mm diameter circle centered on the fovea. In this study, excessive macular thinning was defined as a final CMT of less than 200 μm on spectral-domain OCT and was used as a structural surrogate marker rather than direct evidence of retinal atrophy. Macular morphological features, including DRT, HRF, CME, ERM, EZD, SRD, PHT, and DRIL, were assessed and classified by two board-certified ophthalmologists independently. In case of disagreement, they jointly reviewed the OCT images to reach a consensus. Then, we compare their results for all 727 eyes and calculate the intergrader agreement and Cohen’s kappa. The intergrader agreement was consistently high across all 8 OCT morphological features, with observed agreement ranging from 97.4% to 99.6% and Cohen’s kappa coefficients ranging from 0.89 to 0.98, indicating excellent reliability (see Supplementary Table 1 for details). HRF, defined as small punctate lesions with reflectivity equal to or greater than that of the retinal pigment epithelium, were manually quantified on a single fovea-centered OCT B-scan (retinal slab). HRF were counted within the macular region (6-mm scan width) and categorized by location within the inner retina (internal limiting membrane to inner nuclear layer) or outer retina (outer plexiform layer to retinal pigment epithelium).

Treatment protocol

After the patients were enrolled, they received three consecutive monthly injections of anti-VEGF (ranibizumab or aflibercept) as a loading dose. Following this initial phase, a treat-and-extend strategy was adopted. Treatment intervals were progressively extended by 2 weeks if no evidence of macular edema was observed on OCT at follow-up visits. If recurrent or persistent macular edema was detected, the treatment interval was shortened accordingly. Once the most effective interval was identified, patients continued to receive injections at a fixed interval. The VA and CMT were assessed at baseline and 1 month after each injection.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables, including age, baseline HbA1c, duration of diabetes, baseline BCVA (ETDRS letters), and baseline CMT, are presented as means and standard deviations. Categorical variables are presented as patient numbers and percentages, including sex, eye side, hypertension, pan-retinal photocoagulation, and morphological features of DME. Independent t-tests and McNemar’s Chi-square tests were used to compare continuous and categorical variables between the two anti-VEGF agents.

BCVA and CMT were analyzed as outcome variables to evaluate the effects of the two anti-VEGF agents in patients with DME. Because BCVA and CMT were measured repeatedly at multiple clinical visits for each patient, we constructed linear mixed-effects regression models with patients nested within physicians. Random intercepts were specified at both the physician and patient levels, and random slopes for time were included at the patient level. Here, the fixed effects were the different anti-VEGF agents and other major predictors in the model, including patient age, sex, HbA1c level, duration of diabetes mellitus, comorbid hypertension, pan-retinal photocoagulation, morphological features of DME, number of anti-VEGF injections, and treatment duration. Missing data were handled in the mixed-effects regression models without imputation. Linear mixed-effects models use maximum likelihood estimation and incorporate all available observations, allowing valid inference under the assumption that data are missing at random. As this was an observational study conducted under Taiwan’s National Health Insurance reimbursement regulations, patients routinely underwent visual acuity testing and OCT imaging at each visit; therefore, the proportion of missing data was negligible. Model assumptions were evaluated using graphical and quantitative diagnostics. Linearity, homoscedasticity, and residual distribution were assessed using residual-versus-fitted plots and normal Q–Q plots. Influential values were evaluated using leverage and Cook’s distance diagnostics. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and all predictors showed low collinearity (VIF < 2) except for a moderate correlation between treatment duration and number of injections, as anticipated given their conceptual linkage (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). With a limited sample size, we further applied Fisher’s exact test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test to analyze patients with EZD based on whether their final CMT was less than 200 μm or not. All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Analysis System software (version 9.4, SAS Institute, NC, US).

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Our cohort included 464 patients with DME (727 eyes) who met the inclusion criteria. As summarized in Table 1, 485 eyes were treated with intravitreal injections of ranibizumab, and 242 eyes received aflibercept. Among these, 424 eyes (58.4%) were from men, 355 (48.8%) were from the right eye, and 389 (53.6%) were from patients with a history of hypertension. The patient’s average age was 62.6 ± 10.3 years, with a mean diabetes duration of 8.0 ± 7.0 years. Baseline measurements revealed an average HbA1c of 7.6% ± 1.4%, a BCVA of 49.4 ± 20.1 ETDRS letters, and a CMT of 385.8 ± 104.7 μm.

Overall BCVA and CMT outcomes

The mean baseline BCVA was 49.4 ± 20.1 ETDRS letters, which improved to 52.8 ± 20.6 letters after 3 months and stabilized between 54 and 60 letters after 24 months (Fig. 1). The mean baseline CMT was 385.8 ± 104.7 μm, which decreased to 300.3 ± 77.4 μm at 3 months and remained stable between 286 and 293 μm for 24 months (Fig. 1).

Relationship between variables and BCVA

A mixed-effects linear regression model was used to assess the relationships among BCVA, anti-VEGF agents, and all other clinical variables (Table 2). The analysis revealed no significant difference in BCVA outcomes between the ranibizumab and aflibercept groups (p = 0.689). Poorer BCVA was significantly associated with older age (p < 0.001), longer treatment duration (p = 0.005), and the presence of EZD (p = 0.017) and DRIL (p = 0.001) on morphology. In contrast, CME (p = 0.035) and SRD (p = 0.025) were associated with better BCVA outcomes.

Relationship between variables and CMT

A mixed-effects linear regression model was employed to examine the relationships among CMT, anti-VEGF agents, and other clinical variables (Table 2). The analysis revealed no significant difference in CMT outcomes between the ranibizumab and aflibercept groups (p = 0.668). Predictors of CMT reduction included men (p = 0.019), higher HbA1c levels (p = 0.015), a higher number of anti-VEGF injections (p < 0.001), and the presence of SRD (p = 0.006) or EZD (p = 0.001). Conversely, the predictors of increased CMT included higher baseline CMT (p < 0.001), longer treatment duration (p = 0.001), and presence of an ERM (p < 0.001).

Anti-VEGF injection times and treatment duration

In years 1 and 2, the mean number of injections for ranibizumab and aflibercept were 3.92 ± 1.87, 3.72 ± 2.02 (p = 0.113), and 2.56 ± 1.07, 2.25 ± 1.24 (p = 0.547), respectively. The mixed-effects regression model revealed that a higher number of injections was significantly associated with improved BCVA (+ 0.56 letters per injection, p = 0.008; Table 2) and reduced CMT (-6.46 μm per injection, p < 0.001; Table 2). Conversely, longer treatment duration was associated with poorer BCVA (-0.28 letters/month, p = 0.005) and increased CMT (+ 1.54 μm/month, p = 0.001).

Risk of excessive macular thinning (final CMT < 200 μm) in DME with EZD

Excessive macular thinning occurred in 61 of 727 eyes (8.4%) after 24 months of treatment and was more frequently observed in eyes with baseline EZD. Among eyes with baseline EZD, partial restoration of the ellipsoid zone was observed during the 24-month follow-up period. EZD restoration occurred in 38 of 72 eyes (52.8%) treated with ranibizumab and in 25 of 41 eyes (61.0%) treated with aflibercept. The difference between treatment groups was not statistically significant (chi-square test, p = 0.19).

Moreover, among DME eyes with EZD, 18 had a final CMT < 200 μm and 95 had a final CMT > 200 μm (Supplementary Table 3). Both baseline and final BCVA were significantly lower in the group with final CMT < 200 μm (p = 0.016 and p = 0.028, respectively). When comparing the average BCVA among the three groups, eyes without EZD had the highest BCVA, whereas those with EZD and a final CMT < 200 μm had the lowest BCVA (Fig. 2). We explored several cutoff values for the baseline BCVA, namely, 15, 20, 25, and 30 ETDRS letters. However, because of small sample sizes, we could only perform exploratory analyses, of which the results of univariate logistic regression showed that eyes with baseline value BCVA < 15 ETDRS were more frequently observed among those with a final CMT < 200 μm after anti-VEGF treatment (odds ratio = 3.68, 95% confidence interval: 1.22–11.11, p = 0.021; Table 3). Eyes with a poorer baseline BCVA seemed to show a greater improvement than those with a better baseline BCVA (Supplementary Table 4). Moreover, in eyes with EZD and a baseline BCVA < 15 ETDRS letters, the presence of HRF was more frequently observed among eyes with a final CMT < 200 μm (p = 0.09; Table 4). Further analysis disclosed that HRF number in the group of final CMT < 200 μm was significantly higher than that in the group of final CMT ≥ 200 μm (total HRF: 13.6 ± 5.9 vs. 5.5 ± 7.0, p = 0.02); if counting the HRF numbers detected in the outer retina, the difference in distribution was more evident: 7.9 ± 4.4 vs. 1.6 ± 2.3, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study evaluated the outcomes of BCVA and CMT in patients with DME treated with two different anti-VEGF agents. After adjusting for potential risk factors, including age, sex, HbA1c level, duration of DM, presence of hypertension, prior pan-retinal photocoagulation, and macular morphological features (DRT, HRF, CME, ERM, EZD, SRD, PHT, and DRIL), we found no significant difference in therapeutic effects between ranibizumab and aflibercept after two years of treatment [3, 4]. We also corroborated previous findings on greater improvements in these treatments for patients with higher baseline BCVA and lower baseline CMT levels [5, 16], as well as those who received a higher number of injections [17]. However, we found higher HbA1c levels associated with a greater reduction in CMT. Moreover, although the presence of EZD in DME morphology was associated with a reduction in CMT, the patient’s BCVA did not appear to improve consistently. Baseline BCVA cutoff analysis further revealed that eyes with EZD and baseline BCVA < 15 ETDRS letters were associated with developing excessive macular thinning (final CMT < 200 μm) following anti-VEGF therapy (Table 3). We defined excessive macular thinning using a CMT-based criterion, but the observed anatomical changes likely indicate a degenerative process, especially in eyes with EZD and increased outer retinal HRF. Structural compromise of the photoreceptor layer and external limiting membrane may facilitate fluid resolution, but it may also predispose the macula to excessive thinning.

A previous cross-sectional study found a higher HbA1c associated with a higher CMT [18]. The longitudinal follow-up of our patients showed that those with initially higher HbA1c levels presented with a more severe edema or a higher baseline CMT, allowing for a larger absolute reduction after treatment. Patients with an initial poor glycemic control usually experienced more pronounced symptoms or complications, which would lead to an increased vigilance, a stricter adherence to anti-VEGF therapy, and a greater reduction in CMT.

After adjusting for other major predictive factors, we corroborated previous reports on the association between increased CMT with the presence of ERM [19, 20] and decreased CMT with the presence of SRD [21]. Generally, reduced CMT would be accompanied by a trend of improved BCVA, as also shown in Table 2. Remarkably, we also found decreased CMT in patients with EZD, but accompanied by reduced BCVA.

EZD is defined as a discontinuity of the second hyperreflective band at the fovea on OCT, implying damage to the photoreceptor inner segment/outer segment junction, and is regarded as a critical factor influencing treatment outcomes [22, 23]. A systematic review concluded that an intact ellipsoid zone is a strong predictor of favorable outcomes in DME [12]. Moreover, two retrospective studies identified EZD as a poor prognostic factor for the final BCVA [13, 14]. Conversely, another retrospective study of 91 DME eyes reported that EZD was associated with a thinner final CMT but better BCVA recovery after one year of treatment [5]. Our real-world findings of partial EZD restoration in more than half of the eyes treated are consistent with the report by Murakami et al. [24], supporting the concept that anti-VEGF therapy may facilitate EZD restoration and improve VA in a subset of DME patients.

The mixed effects model construction in our study showed that eyes with EZD generally resulted in a significant reduction in CMT (-26.14 μm, p = 0.001), accompanied by a decline in BCVA (-4.00 ETDRS letters, p = 0.017) after treatment. In our cohort, 15.9% of eyes with EZD achieved a post-treatment CMT < 200 μm, compared to only 7.0% in those without EZD. Furthermore, among eyes with EZD and a final CMT < 200 μm, both baseline and final BCVA were significantly worse than in those with a final CMT ≥ 200 μm (p = 0.016). Despite a greater anatomical response, patients with EZD did not demonstrate a consistent functional visual improvement. The presence of EZD often reflects damage to the external limiting membrane, a critical structural interface between Müller cells and photoreceptors [25]. As disruption of the ellipsoid zone and/or external limiting membrane may enhance fluid clearance following anti-VEGF treatment, it would result in a greater CMT reduction but also predispose to excessive macular thinning [26]. Further analysis detected that a baseline BCVA < 15 ETDRS letters was associated with a final CMT < 200 μm after anti-VEGF therapy (odds ratio = 3.68, 95% confidence interval: 1.22–11.11, p = 0.021; Table 3). Morphologically, the co-occurrence of a greater number of HRF (particularly within the outer retina) was associated with developing excessive macular thinning after treatment (p = 0.02). HRF, visualized as small, dot-like lesions on OCT, were thought to represent possibly an inflammatory response or photoreceptor debris [27]. A greater number of outer retinal HRF correlated with photoreceptor damage and poorer visual outcomes [28, 29]. In addition to corroborating the above reports, our study further suggests that the coexistence of EZD and outer retinal HRF in DME eyes was associated with subsequent excessive macular thinning. We thus postulated that although DME patients with EZD exhibited greater CMT reduction following anti-VEGF therapy, the excessive thinning and structure disruptions may reflect a greater loss of underlying photoreceptors, thereby limiting visual recovery despite anatomical improvement. Importantly, the poorer visual outcomes observed in these eyes may reflect more advanced or chronic disease states, including macular ischemia or longstanding diabetic retinal damage, rather than the EZD alone. Therefore, EZD observed on OCT may serve as a surrogate marker of structural change in disease severity, coexisting with ischemic and/or chronic retinal changes that independently limit visual recovery following anti-VEGF therapy.

DRIL was significantly associated with poorer BCVA, whereas CME and SRD were associated with better visual outcomes. Notably, the observed association between CME and BCVA suggests a possible pathophysiological connection between CME and DRIL rather than independent effects. Anatomically, CME predominantly involves the macular region, especially the inner retinal layers, potentially predisposing to the development of DRIL. However, DRIL was not associated with changes in CMT. This finding suggests that inner retinal disorganization primarily influences visual function rather than overall macular thickness.

This study had several limitations. First, although our sample size was larger than that of many previous studies on DME, the single-center design and relatively short follow-up period may limit the generalizability of our findings. Moreover, because the sample sizes of many subgroups were very small, our exploratory findings should be interpreted cautiously and await future corroborative studies, particularly the results of Tables 3 and 4 on baseline characteristics in patients with final excessive thinning of CMT. Second, because this study used CMT < 200 μm as a structural surrogate marker rather than direct evidence of retinal atrophy, one should avoid overinterpretation. Future studies exploring more detailed pathophysiological processes underlying DME and visual function are warranted. Third, using a treat-and-extend protocol may not fully capture the potential benefits of alternative treatment regimens. Exploring other approaches could help optimize treatment strategies based on individual patient factors and morphological features. Finally, we only included those who received ranibizumab and aflibercept therapy. The exclusion of other anti-VEGF agents or combination therapies limited the ability to compare different treatment modalities. Future multicenter longitudinal studies with standardized imaging protocols and multiple therapeutic arms are required to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

Among DME eyes receiving anti-VEGF therapy, patients with EZD and a baseline BCVA of less than 15 ETDRS letters were more frequently observed among eyes with excessive macular thinning. Such a relationship was particularly prominent among DME eyes showing HRF in the outer retina, indicating a more extensive disruption of essential structure and photoreceptors. Therefore, we recommend that more corroborative studies be conducted to identify those with a poor prognosis early.
