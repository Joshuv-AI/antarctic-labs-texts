Decision tree with randomized grid search-based hyperparameter tuning and optimal feature scaling for diabetes diagnosis
Authors: Mudatheer M Al-Slivani, Ibrahim O Alrubaye, Mayameen S Kadhim, Ahmed Dheyaa Radhi, Nor Samsiah Sani, Hussein A A Al-Khamees, Mohd Aliff Afira Sani, Mohammed Amin Almaiah, Rusul Mansoor Al-Amri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes is a chronic condition that arises when the body cannot effectively regulate blood glucose levels, either due to insufficient insulin production or insulin resistance. If left unmanaged, diabetes can lead to serious complications including heart disease, nerve damage, kidney failure, and blindness. Machine learning classifiers are widely employed to predict diabetes onset based on patient data.MethodsThis paper presents a robust data-driven machine learning framework for enhancing the classification of diabetes. Unlike previous decision tree models that employed the classic grid search method, we propose a decision tree (DT) model utilizing randomized grid search for the diagnosis of diabetes through two stages (1) applying eight feature scaling methods (StandardScaler, MaxAbsScaler, RobustScaler, PowerTransformer, QuantileTransformer, MinMaxScaler, Normalizer, and KBinsDiscretizer), and then identifying the best one according to different measurements (accuracy, F1-score, and Friedman ranking), and (2) tuning the hyperparameters via randomized grid search with 5-fold cross-validation to identify the best configuration that maximizes classification performance. These hyperparameters include criterion, max_depth, min_samples_split, and min_samples_leaf. The public diabetes datasets PIMA and IPDD are used to evaluate the proposed model. The results of this model are evaluated by accuracy, precision, recall, F1_score, Area Under the Curve (AUC), and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC).ResultsThe results are 99.5%, 99.52%, 99.5%, 99.51%, 99.8%, and 97.97% on the IPDD dataset and 77.27%, 77.19%, 77.27%, 77.23%, 77.9%, and 70.2% on the PIMA dataset for the six metrics, respectively. Then, these results are compared against different machine learning classifiers, including classic decision tree, K-nearest neighbors, logistic regression, support vector machine, AdaBoost, random forest, gradient boosting, and XGBoost. Furthermore, the p-value is computed to analyze the feature interactions for the clinical relevance.ConclusionsThis paper offers a scalable, interpretable, and ethically aligned tool towards improving diabetes diagnosis techniques using machine learning, contributing to early detection of the disease and reduction of associated health risks.Keywords: Diabetics disease, Machine learning, Decision tree, PIMA India diabetes dataset (PIMA), Iraqi patient dataset for diabetes (IPDD)

Introduction

Currently, 589 million adults (20–79 years) are living with diabetes mellitus (DM) worldwide, and by 2045, it is predicted that this number will have increased to 783 million.1

In order to stop the advancement of pre-diabetes to Type 2 diabetes, a reversible stage in which lifestyle changes can lower incidence, early detection, and risk stratification are essential [1]. DM is a major cause of numerous complications such as heart disease, kidney failure, and nerve damage, and its prevalence continues to rise worldwide [2]. A major challenge is early diagnosis, as many patients are unaware of their condition until it has worsened [3].

Artificial intelligence (AI)-driven healthcare systems now rely heavily on machine learning (ML) classifiers [4]. Practically, the application of these classifiers to medical diagnosis has enabled new methods for accurate, effective, and early diagnosis of disease, with the aim of reducing adverse health outcomes through timely therapeutic intervention [5]. Meanwhile, the ML classifiers have revolutionized DM risk assessment and early identification. Recent studies have investigated the use of various ML classifiers, including the decision trees, as well as ones to predict the likelihood of diabetes [6]. These classifiers are trained on DM datasets to analyze multiple variables that may influence the onset of the disease [7].

ML models ML models typically comprise several phases, including preprocessing. A key step in this phase is feature scaling, which aims to standardize the range of independent features or variables in a dataset, ensuring that no feature dominates the modeling process. In general, the ML models are sensitive to the magnitude of input features. In this context, the grid search is a widely used method for tuning hyperparameters [8]. It is based on the exhaustive evaluation of all predefined combinations of the discrete values (e.g., 10, 20, 30, 40) [9].

Although this progress is made through applying different ML classifiers, challenges remain in applying them to DM diagnosis [10]. The decision tree (DT) classifier represents one of the most important and widely used classifiers that is applied for classification tasks [11]. Its main idea is based on recursively splitting the dataset into subsets depending on the feature values that best separate the target variable, forming a tree-like structure of decisions. Every internal node indicates a test on a feature, each branch represents the results of the test, and every leaf node represents a class label (classification) [12]. The DT classifier is intuitive and interpretable and needs to have its parameters tuned. On the other hand, it is possible to demonstrate that even a simple ML classifier (like DT), when intelligently tuned and contextually adapted, can serve as a powerful tool for early DM detection [13].

The authors in [14] proposed a new framework by applying three ML classifiers: support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbors (K-NN), and logistic regression (LR). The framework aims to construct an active model for predicting diabetes by combining the SVM classifier with others, achieving an accuracy of 96.4% on the IPDD dataset. This model limits the application of traditional classifiers of ML, highlighting the use of hyperparameter optimization for ML classifiers. The proposed framework is tested by only the IPDD dataset. The central concept of the study in [15] was to introduce a novel method that basically is a dynamic hybrid metaheuristic optimization method. In reality, this method combined the Al-Biruni earth radius with the dipper-throated optimization algorithm (DBERDTO). Among seven ML classifiers for the IPDD dataset, the RF classifier achieved the best accuracy with 86.5%, while the DT classifier achieved 75.8%. This method, although recent, might not be suitable for many diabetes datasets (or even classification issues). This is due to the fact that the results did not involve the application of suitable preprocessing methods in different scenarios. Through five ML classifiers, the work of [16] was suggested for predicting the diabetes on the IPDD dataset. The general impact of feature selection on the model performance was discussed in this paper as well as the relative risks of diabetes. The DT classifier has the best accuracy of 95.07% among all. The paper fails to process the imbalance issue. Depending on integrating prior knowledge obtained from shapley additive description (SHAP), the authors in [17] presented a framework that enhances the transformer models. They explained the transparency and trustworthiness of artificial intelligence applications in healthcare. However, this framework proved that this method is able to improve the overall accuracy for different datasets, including the IPDD. The accuracy of this framework on the IPDD dataset was low (at 89.75%); besides, it didn’t handle the class imbalance. In study [18], several ML classifiers, including: K-NN, SVM, DT, random forest (RF), Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), and AdaBoost, are employed to evaluate the IPDD dataset. The reported accuracies of these classifiers were 97.1%, 94.8%, 96.8%, 98.8%, 92.6%, and 96.1%, respectively, where all of these classifiers were tested exclusively on the IPDD dataset. In [19], DT, RF, LR, XGBoost, and K-NN were applied without addressing class imbalance. Among these, the highest accuracy was achieved by the XGBoost classifier with 98.5%, while the DT attained an accuracy of 97.99%. The work was evaluated by only the IPDD dataset and only two metrics (accuracy and F1_score).

The framework proposed in [17] was also evaluated on the PIMA dataset, without addressing the issue of imbalanced classes, achieving an accuracy of 65.30%. Similarly, the study in [15] employed the PIMA dataset to assess a model developed using DT classifier, achieving an accuracy of 68.0%.In [20], the authors evaluated ten different ML classifiers, including the DT, to improve diabetes prediction through a mobile application. They highlighted the benefit of combining different classifiers to improve prediction accuracy. However, the DT classifier achieved an accuracy of 72.0%, while all these classifiers were evaluated by only the PIMA dataset. Without applying any class imbalance handling method, the idea behind the work of [21] was to combine two ML classifiers, DT and LR, that are trained independently in the first step. The second step was to feed the output of these classifiers to the neural network. Finally, the authors integrated the results of DT and LR. The accuracy of the DT is still low at 65.84%. Similarly, the work in [22] employed many ML classifiers to diseases prediction, including the DT. This work did not focus on the DM but rather on a wide range of diseases. However, among all classifiers, the DT attained the highest accuracy on the PIMA dataset of 71.42%. Various feature selection methods and ML classifiers are employed in the work of [23] on the PIMA dataset. A hybrid convolutional neural network (CNN) with a long short-term memory (LSTM) model enhanced by conditional generative adversarial network (cGAN)-based augmentation methods was proposed in [24]. Before that, through the MinMaxScaler method and SMOTE method, the authors applied the SVM classifier, and they achieved an accuracy of 74.0% on the PIMA dataset. In the ML-based early diagnosis of DM, the class imbalance present in clinical datasets (like DM datasets) is a significant issue [27], where data samples in one class are more than others in another class [28].

To address this issue, this paper applies the widely used data-level resampling technique known as the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) [29]. SMOTE creates synthetic minority-class samples by interpolating between existing occurrences in feature space. SMOTE improves model generalization while maintaining the underlying data distribution by producing new, realistic examples along the line segments connecting minority-class samples’ k-nearest neighbors [30].

A common algorithm for hyperparameter adjustment and then fine-tuning the model performance in the ML community is the grid search (GS) [9]. It finds the optimal performance by thoroughly experimenting with all possible configurations of the given hyperparameter values. In essence, GS trains a model for each possible combination of hyperparameters and then chooses the model that performs the best, thus performing both model selection and hyperparameter tuning at the same time [42].

The randomized search cross-validation (RandomizedSearchCV), a stochastic optimization method that effectively explores high-dimensional parameter spaces. More deeply, the RandomizedSearchCV is applied to sample hyperparameter configurations stochastically from continuous probability distributions, in contrast to exhaustive grid search, which evaluates every possible combination of predefined discrete values. This allows for a more thorough and effective exploration of the hyperparameter space with a significantly reduced number of evaluations.

To ensure robust performance estimation, this search method does 30 randomized trials, each of which is evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the SMOTE-balanced training dataset. The configuration that achieves the highest mean CV accuracy is selected.

Machine learning classifiers

In this paper, eight different ML classifiers are applied on both datasets to analyze performance and extract patterns and trends. These classifiers are carefully selected based on their superior ability to handle diverse data and provide accurate results in classification and prediction. These classifiers are as follows:

Decision tree

Dts are supervised learning models used for classification and regression tasks. It works by splitting the data into branches based on specific feature conditions, forming a tree-like structure [43]. At each decision node, the algorithm evaluates the most informative feature to split the data until it reaches a leaf node, which assigns a prediction based on the majority class (for classification) or average value (for regression) of the data points in that branch [44].

Random forest

The RF is an ensemble learning classifier that combines multiple decision trees to improve accuracy and reduce overfitting [45]. In RF, each tree is trained on a random subset of the dataset (bootstrapping) and considers a random selection of features at each split. The final prediction is based on the majority vote for classification or the average for regression among all trees. This ML method enhances generalization and makes the model more robust, especially with high-dimensional data [46].

Gradient boosting

The GB is an ensemble classifier that builds a series of weak learners (usually decision trees), where each successive tree corrects the errors of the previous one. It does this by minimizing the loss function using gradient descent. The model learns gradually, adding trees iteratively, with each one focusing on the residual errors from the preceding model. The GB is highly effective for complex data but can be computationally intensive and prone to overfitting without proper tuning [47].

K-nearest neighbors (K-NN)

K-NN is a simple, instance-based learning algorithm used for both classification and regression. For a given data sample, K-NN identifies the ‘K’ closest data samples (neighbors) in the feature space and classifies the sample based on the majority class of these neighbors. For regression, it predicts the average of the target values of the neighbors [48]. K-NN is easy to implement but can be computationally expensive on large datasets and is sensitive to feature scaling and irrelevant features [49].

Logistic regression

LR is a linear model primarily used for binary classification, although it can be extended for multi-class problems. It estimates the probability of a class label by applying a logistic function to a linear combination of input features [50]. LR is efficient and interpretable, but it assumes a linear relationship between features and the log odds of the output, which may limit its effectiveness with complex datasets [51].

AdaBoost

The AdaB is an ensemble learning classifier that combines multiple weak learners to create a strong classifier. It focuses on misclassified data samples by adjusting the weights of each sample after every iteration so that subsequent models give more attention to previously misclassified samples. AdaBoost generally uses short decision trees (stumps) as weak learners. It is robust against overfitting to some extent but can be sensitive to noisy data and outliers [52].

XGBoost

XGB is an optimized implementation of the gradient boosting algorithm, known for its speed and performance in structured/tabular data tasks. It introduces regularization parameters that help prevent overfitting, along with optimizations like parallel processing, tree pruning, and better handling of missing values. XGBoost is often used in competitions and high-stakes applications for its accuracy and efficiency, but it requires careful tuning to achieve optimal performance [53, 54].

This comprehensive evaluation ensures the proposed model’s performance is contextualized against the full spectrum of ML classifiers.

Rational to choose the DT

Due to clinical interpretability requirements and empirical evidence indicating that a properly tuned decision tree (DT) outperforms alternative ML classifiers on both datasets, the DT was selected as the base model in this study. Importantly, the proposed model–combining a decision tree with randomized search and hyperparameter tuning–preserves the intrinsic transparency of DTs, which is a prerequisite for clinical adoption, while enhancing its performance from a weak classifier to a competitive one.

Furthermore, the DT classifier is easy to interpret. It generates decision rules that are easily understood by humans, which is essential for clinicians’ confidence and applicability in classifying diabetes risk [67].

The observed performance improvement stems from enhanced SMOTE synthetic sample quality rather than DT sensitivity. This nuance is critical for the proposed methodological transparency: scaling benefits the preprocessing pipeline, not the DT itself.

Limitations

While the proposed model demonstrates strong performance on the IPDD and PIMA datasets, several limitations are identified, which are as follows:

Feature selection method: The proposed model achieves an accuracy of 99.5% on the IPDD dataset and 77.27% on the PIMA dataset. One of the main reasons for the latter accuracy includes the univariate method’s limitations on datasets with binary class separation. On the other hand, not applying any feature selection method will inevitably lead to increased accuracy [7], but it will take more time, even if the classifier attains good classification results, such as the work of [68], which achieved an accuracy of 76.07% by the DT.

Conclusion

With its steadily increasing incidence and prevalence, Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is one of the biggest global public health concerns of the twenty-first century. Indeed, DM reduces life expectancy and quality of life. On the other hand, it is possible to decrease the severity of DM and its consequences by detecting this chronic condition early. Thus, DM is considered one of the greatest challenges for health institutions and governments alike. This paper suggests a decision tree (DT) model with a randomized grid search for diabetes diagnosis by (1) using eight different feature scaling methods, and (2) tuning the hyperparameters via randomized grid search to determine the best configuration. Two public diabetes datasets, IPDD and PIMA, are used to evaluate the proposed model, while accuracy, precision, recall, F1_score, Area Under the Curve (AUC), and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) are the metrics to measure the results. The findings are 99.5%, 99.52%, 99.5%, 99.51%, 99.8%, and 97.97% on the IPDD dataset and 77.27%, 77.19%, 77.27%, 77.23%, 77.9%, and 70.2% on the PIMA dataset for the six metrics, respectively. Moreover, different machine learning classifiers, including classic decision tree, K-nearest neighbors, logistic regression, support vector machine, AdaBoost, random forest, gradient boosting, and XGBoost, are applied in this paper to compare the results. Finally, this model represents more than just a model; it is a strategic healthcare asset that can enhance worldwide efforts to fight the diabetes epidemic, lower systemic costs, and improve individual outcomes.
