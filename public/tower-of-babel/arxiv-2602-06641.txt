Introduction

Let

T_{t}:f(x)\mapsto f(x-t),\quad M_{\xi}:f(x)\mapsto e^{2\pi i\xi x}f(x),

be respectively the
translation (i.e., time shift) operator
and the
modulation (i.e., frequency shift) operator
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Given
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a
Gabor system
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
window

g
and
support

\Lambda
is defined and denoted by

\mathcal{G}(g,\Lambda)=\{M_{a}T_{b}g:(b,a)\in\Lambda\}.

A typical choice of
\Lambda
would be the lattice
\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\beta\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha,\beta>0
. And the corresponding Gabor system is

\mathcal{G}(g,\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\beta\mathbb{Z})=\{M_{\beta j}T_{\alpha k}g:j,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Gabor systems are fundamental building blocks in signal processing and time-frequency analysis since they offer a natural way to discretize a function on the time-frequency plane. A trivial example of a Gabor system is the orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
formed by taking the window to be the characteristic function on the unit interval
\chi_{[0,1)}
and
\alpha=\beta=1
.

A set of functions
\{h_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is called a
frame
in a Hilbert space
H
if there exist constants
A,B>0
so that

A\|f\|_{H}^{2}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\langle f,h_{k}\rangle_{H}|^{2}\leq B\|f\|_{H}^{2}

holds for any
f\in H
. The constants
A,B
are then called respectively the lower and the upper
frame constants
of this frame. If
\{h_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a frame, then both the analysis operator
f\mapsto\{\langle f,h_{k}\rangle_{H}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
and the synthesis operator
\{\langle f,h_{k}\rangle_{H}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mapsto f
are bounded from above and below.
Hence it allows a stable decomposition and reconstruction of
f
into and from the coefficients
\{\langle f,h_{k}\rangle_{H}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

A
Gabor frame
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a Gabor system that forms a frame on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The set of parameters
(\alpha,\beta)
that makes
\mathcal{G}(g,\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\beta\mathbb{Z})
a frame is called the
frame set
of
g
and denoted by
F(g)
. The problem of determining
F(g)
for an arbitrary function
g
is a long standing difficult open problem in this field. Even for simple window functions such as
\chi_{[0,1)}
, the corresponding frame set could be fairly complicated (see
[
14
]
). The classical density theory points out that if
\alpha\beta>1
, then
\mathcal{G}(g,\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\beta\mathbb{Z})
is incomplete and thus can not possibly be a frame (see e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 3a]
or
[
1
]
).

The critical case
\alpha\beta=1
is in general not so interesting since such windows are already completely characterized by the Zak transform. More importantly it has been excluded from frame sets of functions with good time-frequency concentration. This is due to the fact that in such case
\mathcal{G}(g,\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\beta\mathbb{Z})
is a frame if and only if the Zak transform of
g
is essentially bounded away from both
0
and
\infty
(see e.g.,
[
6
, Corollary 8.3.2]
), and it is well known that if the transformed function is continuous, then it always admits zeros (see e.g.,
[
6
, Lemma 8.4.2]
) and thus can not be lowered bounded. A class of functions that will guarantee continuity after the Zak transform is the set of continuous Wiener functions.

Wiener functions
on
\mathbb{R}
form a
Banach space equipped with the norm (see e.g.,
[
6
, Chapter 6.1]
).

\|g\|_{W(\mathbb{R})}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\esssup_{x\in[0,1)}|g(x+k)|<\infty.

It is thus a mixed norm space that is locally of
L^{\infty}
bound and globally of
\ell^{1}
decay. The subspace of continuous functions in it is denoted by
W_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. That the frame set of a
W_{0}(\mathbb{R})
function (or its Fourier transform) can not contain any pair
(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha\beta=1
is known as the Amalgam Balian-Low theorem.

We shall say that a function
g

has maximal frame set
(or
F(g)
is maximal), if
F(g)
contains all pairs
(\alpha,\beta)
with
\alpha\beta<1
and
\alpha,\beta>0
. A primal question is then to identify all
W_{0}(\mathbb{R})
functions whose frame sets are maximal. It was first conjectured in
[
4
]
that Gaussians possess such a property. This is proved in
[
20
,
24
,
25
]
. Later the hyperbolic secant
1/(e^{x}+e^{-x})

[
15
]
, the one sided exponential
e^{-x}\chi_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}(x)

[
12
]
(actually this is not a
W_{0}(\mathbb{R})
function since it is not even continuous, its Zak transform has no zero and its frame set contains also the critical case
\alpha\beta=1
, see
[
14
, Section 3.2.1]
) and the two sided exponential
e^{-|x|}

[
13
]
are also found to have this property. Then apparently automorphisms of the
L^{2}
space such as translations, modulations, dilations, scalings and Fourier transforms of these functions will inherit this property.

It has been noticed that these functions are not only positive but also totally positive. A function
g
is called
totally positive
if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any two sequences of non-decreasing numbers
x_{1}\leq\ldots\leq x_{n}
and
y_{1}\leq\ldots\leq y_{n}
, the determinant of the matrix
[g(x_{i}-y_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{n}
is non-negative. Alternative
g
is totally positive if and only if its Fourier transform factors into

\hat{g}(\xi)=ce^{-\gamma\xi^{2}}e^{2\pi i\nu\xi}\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1+2\pi i\nu_{j}\xi)e^{-2\pi i\nu_{j}\xi},

(1)

where
c>0
,
\gamma\geq 0
,
\nu,\nu_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
and
0<\gamma+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nu_{j}^{2}<\infty
. This is characterized in
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. In a series of papers
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
it is proved that if
N
is finite or if
N=\infty
but
\alpha\beta\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
\alpha\beta<1
is in the frame set of
g
(in particular, if
g
is at the same time in the Feichtinger algebra, then
F(g)
is open
[
5
]
, and thereof maximal), which covers and also greatly extends all aforementioned known cases. Mysteriously the Zak transforms of all these functions except the one-sided exponential possess a unique simple zero in the unit square (see
[
2
,
17
,
18
,
26
]
).

Is total positivity necessary for maximality of frame sets? If we modulate a totally positive function, or simply multiply it by a negative number, then it is not totally positive any more but its frame set stays invariant. Therefore to discuss necessity it perhaps makes sense to phrase it as “is total positivity necessary for real non-negative Wiener functions to have maximal frame sets?” In fact it is pointed out in
[
3
]
that certain linear combinations of Cauchy kernels do admit maximal frame sets, these functions are neither totally positive nor (compositions of) time-frequency shifts or dilations or Fourier transforms of totally positive functions, but they are not Wiener functions either.

The purpose of this paper is to produce a class of complex valued
W_{0}(\mathbb{R})
functions whose frame sets are maximal and whose Zak transforms also have unique simple zeros at center of the unit square. It is clear from their expressions that these functions are as well neither totally positive nor (compositions of) time-frequency shifts or dilations or Fourier transforms of totally positive functions. Let

\varphi(x)=e^{-\pi x^{2}},\quad h_{\lambda}(x)=e^{-\pi i\lambda x^{2}}

be respectively the Gaussian and a chirp where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
is a parameter. For
\gamma>0
, let

\varphi_{\gamma}(x)=e^{-\pi\gamma^{2}x^{2}}

be the dilated Gaussian. The main results are the following (Theorem
2
, Theorem
3
, Theorem
4
):

Theorem
.

The chirped Gaussian
h_{r}\cdot\varphi_{\sqrt{u}}
has maximal frame set for any
r\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and any
u>0
.

Theorem
.

Let
\gamma>0
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, then
\mathcal{G}(\varphi_{\gamma},Q\mathbb{Z}^{2})
is a frame if and only if
0<|\det Q|<1
.

Theorem
.

Z(h_{\lambda}\cdot\varphi_{\gamma})
has a unique simple zero at the center
(1/2,1/2)
of the unit square
S
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and any
\gamma>0
.

In the next section we align notations.
Since the techniques for maximality of the frame set and for zeros of the Zak transform are quite independent,
these results are split into two sections, with the first subsection of each section being an exposition of preliminaries.
Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
will be presented in Section
3
, and Theorem
4
in the final section. The proof of maximality of
F(h_{r}\cdot\varphi_{\sqrt{u}})
is by showing in Theorem
1
the maximality of frame sets of product-convoluted (with chirps) Gaussians
h_{\lambda}\cdot(h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*\varphi_{1/\gamma})
for certain
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime},\gamma
first, then we show that there are
r,u
so that
h_{r}\cdot\varphi_{\sqrt{u}}=h_{\lambda}\cdot(h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*\varphi_{1/\gamma})
and
r/u
can take any non-zero real value.

2
Notations and conventions

In this article the Fourier transform of a function
f
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}(f)
or
\hat{f}
, and for
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
it takes the following integral form:

\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\xi x}dx.

The inverse Fourier transform of
f
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}^{-1}f
.

It is customary and convenient to use the following notation for time-frequency shifts:

\pi(z)=M_{a}T_{b},

where
z=(a,b)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(Hereafter
T
in the superscript stands for transposition). We also remind the readers about the commutativity relation

M_{a}T_{b}M_{a^{\prime}}T_{b^{\prime}}=e^{2\pi i(ab^{\prime}-a^{\prime}b)}M_{a^{\prime}}T_{b^{\prime}}M_{a}T_{b}.

(2)

Let
Q\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
and
\mathcal{L}
a linear operator (could be the identity operator, in which case it is omitted then) on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, to reduce the overuse of the summation symbol, we will adopt the notation
\left\|\langle f,\mathcal{L}\pi(Q\mathbb{Z}^{2})g\rangle\right\|_{\ell^{2}}
for

\left\|\langle f,\mathcal{L}\pi(Q\mathbb{Z}^{2})g\rangle\right\|_{\ell^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left|\langle f,\mathcal{L}\pi(Qz)g\rangle\right|^{2}},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The left-hand side is indeed the
\ell^{2}
norm of the the sequence
\{\langle f,\mathcal{L}\pi(Qz)g\rangle\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
generated by fixed
f,g,Q,\mathcal{L}
. Taking
Q=\diag(\beta,\alpha)
and
\mathcal{L}
the identity operator we get the sequence that corresponds to the usual lattice Gabor system
\mathcal{G}(g,\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\beta\mathbb{Z})
.

We will also use the
\asymp
symbol for equivalence type inequalities, i.e., we may write

\left\|\langle f,\pi(Q\mathbb{Z}^{2})g\rangle\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\asymp\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

instead of
\sqrt{A}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\left\|\langle f,\pi(Q\mathbb{Z}^{2})g\rangle\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\sqrt{B}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
.

The dilation of a function
g
by a parameter
\gamma>0
will be denote by

g_{\gamma}(x)=g(\gamma x).

It is easy to see that

\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(x)|^{2}dx=\gamma\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(\gamma y)|^{2}dy=\gamma\left\|g_{\gamma}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

(3)

Similarly if
D_{\gamma}=\diag(1/\gamma,\gamma)
, then for any
z=(a,b)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

|\langle f,\pi(D_{\gamma}z)g\rangle|=|\langle f,M_{a/\gamma}T_{b\gamma}z)g\rangle|=\gamma|\langle f_{\gamma},M_{a}T_{b}g_{\gamma}\rangle|=\gamma|\langle f_{\gamma},\pi(z)g_{\gamma}\rangle|.

(4)

There might be confusions concerning this notation of dilation since
h_{\lambda}(x)=e^{-\pi i\lambda x^{2}}
is not a dilation of
h_{1}(x)
by
\lambda
, but we have to write
h_{\lambda}
this way because we need
\lambda<0
in certain cases, which can not be achieved by dilations. In compensation for that we will never dilate a chirp in this paper, dilations will only be applied to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
functions.

Frame sets of chirped Gaussians

3.1
The fractional Fourier transform and chirps

Let
2\pi\mathbb{T}
be the Torus obtained by taking
\mathbb{R}
modulo
2\pi
, and identified as
[0,2\pi)
. The
fraction Fourier transform

\{\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}\}_{\theta\in 2\pi\mathbb{T}}
is a family of operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mathcal{F}^{(\pi/2)}
being the usual Fourier transform, and satisfying

\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}\mathcal{F}^{(\theta^{\prime})}=\mathcal{F}^{(\theta+\theta^{\prime})}.

It is thus a representation of the additive Abelian group
2\pi\mathbb{T}
with
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}
being the identity. For
\theta\notin\pi\mathbb{Z}
and
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
it has the following explicit integral formula:

\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}f(\xi)=\sqrt{1-i\cot\theta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{i\pi\left((x^{2}+\xi^{2})\cot\theta-2x\xi\csc\theta\right)}dx.

The angle
\theta
has the meaning that the Wigner distribution of
\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}f
differs from that of
f
by a counterclockwise axis rotation of
\theta
on the time-frequency plane. This also gives an intuitive explanation to property
(ii)
below, since the Wigner distribution of
\varphi
is the two dimensional Gaussian, which is rotational invariant.

Proposition 1
.

(i)

\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}
is unitary on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

(ii)

\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}\varphi=\varphi
holds for all
\theta\in 2\pi\mathbb{T}
.

(iii)

Let
z=(a,b)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and

R_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},

(5)

then we have

\pi(z)\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}=e^{-2\pi i\left(\frac{(b^{2}-a^{2})\sin(2\theta)}{4}-ab\sin^{2}\theta\right)}\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}\pi(R_{\theta}z).

These properties are well known, see perhaps
[
19
, Section 4]
and references therein for a concise exposition of properties of
\mathcal{F}^{(\theta)}
tailored to time-frequency analysis.

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we define
h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*f
to be

h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*f=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\hat{h}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cdot\hat{f}).

Lemma 1
.

Let

U_{\lambda}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\lambda\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad L_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}&1\end{pmatrix}.

Then the following properties hold for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and any
z=(a,b)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

(i)

\left\|h_{\lambda}\cdot(h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\|h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*(h_{\lambda}\cdot f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=|\lambda^{\prime}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(ii)

\pi(z)(h_{\lambda}\cdot f)=e^{-\pi i\lambda b^{2}}h_{\lambda}\cdot\left(\pi(U_{\lambda}z)f\right).

(iii)

\pi(z)(h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*f)=e^{-\frac{\pi ia^{2}}{\lambda^{\prime}}}h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*\left(\pi(L_{\lambda^{\prime}}z)f\right).

Proof.

Recall that if
\lambda^{\prime}>0
, then

\hat{h}_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda^{\prime}}}e^{-\frac{\pi i}{4}}h_{-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}},

where the term
e^{-\pi i/4}
comes from taking the reciprocal of a square root of

, while if
\lambda^{\prime}<0
, then we have to take it on

and the formula becomes (alternatively this can also be obtained by applying
\mathcal{F}
again, see also the table in
[
16
, A-7]
)

\hat{h}_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\lambda^{\prime}|}}e^{\frac{\pi i}{4}}h_{-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}}.

Since the result of our derivations does not depend on whether it is
e^{\pi i/4}
or
e^{-\pi i/4}
in the constant
|\lambda^{\prime}|^{-1/2}e^{\pm\pi i/4}
, we simply denote this constant by
c_{\lambda^{\prime}}
so that

\hat{h}_{\lambda^{\prime}}=c_{\lambda^{\prime}}h_{-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}}.

(6)

Consequently

\left\|h_{\lambda}\cdot(h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\|h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=|c_{\lambda^{\prime}}|\cdot\left\|h_{-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}}\cdot f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=|\lambda^{\prime}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

\left\|h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*(h_{\lambda}\cdot f)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
is obtained in the same way, together these establish
(i)
. Next, straightforward computation shows

\displaystyle\pi(z)(h_{\lambda}f)

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi iax}\cdot e^{-\pi i\lambda(x-b)^{2}}\cdot f(x-b)

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi iax}\cdot e^{-\pi i\lambda x^{2}}\cdot e^{2\pi i\lambda bx}\cdot e^{-\pi i\lambda b^{2}}\cdot f(x-b)

\displaystyle=e^{-\pi i\lambda b^{2}}\cdot h_{\lambda}\cdot(M_{a+\lambda b}T_{b}f)

\displaystyle=e^{-\pi i\lambda b^{2}}h_{\lambda}\cdot\left(\pi(U_{\lambda}z)f\right),

which is
(ii)
. Finally, with similar computations we get

\displaystyle\pi(z)(h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*f)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\mathcal{F}\left(\pi(z)(h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*f)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(T_{a}M_{-b}(c_{\lambda^{\prime}}h_{-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}}\hat{f})\right)

by (
6
)

\displaystyle=c_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cdot\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(T_{a}M_{-b}h_{-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}}\hat{f}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cdot e^{2\pi iab}\cdot\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(M_{-b}T_{a}h_{-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}}\hat{f}\right)

by (
2
)

\displaystyle=c_{\lambda^{\prime}}\cdot e^{2\pi iab}\cdot e^{\frac{\pi ia^{2}}{\lambda^{\prime}}}\cdot\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(h_{-\frac{1}{\lambda^{\prime}}}\cdot(M_{-b-\frac{a}{\lambda^{\prime}}}T_{a}\hat{f})\right)

by
(ii)

\displaystyle=e^{2\pi iab}\cdot e^{\frac{\pi ia^{2}}{\lambda^{\prime}}}\cdot h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*(T_{b+\frac{a}{\lambda^{\prime}}}M_{a}f)

by (
6
)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\pi ia^{2}}{\lambda^{\prime}}}h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*(M_{a}T_{\frac{a}{\lambda^{\prime}}+b}f)

by (
2
)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\pi ia^{2}}{\lambda^{\prime}}}h_{\lambda^{\prime}}*\left(\pi(L_{\lambda^{\prime}}z)f\right).

This establishes
(iii)
.

4
Zeros of Zak transforms of chirped Gaussians

4.1
The Zak transform and the theta function

The
Zak transform
of
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a function in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined as

Zf(t,\omega)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(t-k)e^{2\pi ik\omega}.

The unit square

S=[0,1)\times[0,1)

is called the
fundamental domain
of
Z
since not only that
Z
is an isometry from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to
L^{2}(S)
(it maps the orthonormal Gabor basis element
M_{j}T_{k}\chi_{[0,1)}
to the orthonormal Fourier basis element
e^{2\pi i(jt+k\omega)}
) but also it has the quasi-periodicity that

Zf(t-j,\omega)=e^{-2\pi ij\omega}Z(t,\omega),\quad Zf(t,\omega-k)=Z(t,\omega),\quad\forall j,k\in\mathbb{Z},

which indicates that the values of
Zf
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is completely determined by its values on
S
. See
[
6
, Chapter 8]
for more of its properties.

We shall call
(t_{0},\omega_{0})
a
simple zero
of
Zf
if
Zf(t_{0},\omega_{0})=0
and there is a neighbourhood
\Omega
of
(t_{0},\omega_{0})
and a constant
C
so that

\frac{|Zf(t,\omega)|}{\sqrt{(t-t_{0})^{2}+(\omega-\omega_{0})^{2}}}\geq C,

holds for all
(t,\omega)\in\Omega
.

So far all functions that found to have maximal frame sets possess only one simple zero in their fundamental domains, with the one sided exponential (which is not a continuous Wiener function) being the only exception: its Zak transform has no zero. We would like to give an exposition in the appendix (as they are not directly related to this paper) of known facts about zeros of the Zak transform since they seem to be playing some mysterious roles in the analysis of frame sets.

Theta functions are elliptic analogs of exponential functions.
For convenience we will use the following form of the theta function, which is consist with the convention and results in
[
2
]
:

\Theta(z,q)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{k^{2}}z^{k},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},q\in\mathbb{C},|q|<1.

This form is closest to the usual definition of

\Theta_{3}(z,q)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{k^{2}}e^{2kiz},

and can be easily translated into each other. As in
[
2
]
, we also imposed the extra condition
|q|<1
.
Otherwise the propositions below will hinge on absolute convergence of
\sum_{k}|q|^{k}
and
\prod_{k}|1-q^{k}|
.

This form of the theta function is closely related to the Zak transform of Gaussians (see e.g.,
[
15
]
).
In this article the following decomposition is relevant (
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
, which is also available in
[
27
, Chapter 21.3, p.469]
):

\Theta(z,q)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{2j})\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1+q^{2k+1}z)(1+q^{2k+1}z^{-1})\right).

From this decomposition we immediately obtain that (
[
2
, Corollary 2.2]
)

Proposition 2
.

\Theta(z,q)=0
is only attained at
z=-q^{m}
where
m
can be any odd integer.

If we fix
q
, take
z=e^{2ix}
for
x\in\mathbb{C}
and set

H(x)=\Theta(e^{2ix},q),

then we have (
[
27
, Chapter 21.3, p.469]
)

Proposition 3
.

H(x)
is an entire function on
\mathbb{C}
and all zeros of
H(x)
are simple zeros.

\mathcal{G}(g,\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\beta\mathbb{Z})
is a frame if and only if
\mathcal{G}(g_{1/\beta},\alpha\beta\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z})
is.

The Zak transform method is particularly effective in analyzing frame sets of the critical case and other integer oversampling cases since in such cases it diagonalizes the frame operator and makes it a multiplication operator
[
6
, Corollary 8.3.2, Equation 8.21]
, i.e., if
\alpha=1/n
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\mathcal{G}(g_{\gamma},\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z})
is a frame if and only if

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|Zg_{\gamma}(t-\frac{j}{n},\omega)|^{2}\asymp 1

holds for almost every
t,\omega
. Therefore in these cases locations of zeros of
Zg_{\gamma}
play a prominent role, since as long as
Zg_{\gamma}
is continuous, then its essential lower boundedness on
S
(which by quasi-periodicity is equivalent to its global essential lower boundedness) is equivalent to having no zero in
S
.

The other cases are more complicated.
But in general they can be characterized as (
[
11
]
, also stated as
[
8
, Theorem 2.3]
):
\mathcal{G}(g_{\gamma},\alpha\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z})
is a frame if and only if

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}f(\omega)Zg_{\gamma}(t-k\alpha,\omega)d\omega\right|^{2}\asymp\|f\|_{L^{2}([0,1))}^{2}

(13)

holds for all
f\in L^{2}([0,1))
and all
t\in[0,1)
.

To understand how the estimates change upon dilation is often a major difficulty since it is hard to relate and control
Zg_{\gamma}
through
Zg
. Another issue is that unlike in the critical case or in other integer oversampling cases, here merely lifting the lower bound of
|Zg_{\gamma}|
does not seem to help. Indeed, for an individual
g_{\gamma}
, if we fix
t
, then for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the function
h_{k}^{(t)}(\omega)=Zg_{\gamma}(t-k\alpha,\omega)
is a univariate function of
\omega
, and the sum in (
13
) becomes
\sum_{k}|\langle f,\overline{h_{k}^{(t)}}\rangle_{L^{2}([0,1])}|^{2}
. Therefore (
13
) is actually equivalent to requiring
\{h_{k}^{(t)}\}_{k}
to be a frame of
L^{2}([0,1))
.
Hence even if each
|h_{k}^{(t)}|
is away from
0
, together they can still be collided together for some
f
to be orthogonal to all of them to make the sum in (
13
) vanish.

Despite that, as mentioned, mysteriously so far all functions that found to have maximal frame sets possess at most one simple zero in
S
. Hence we would like to provide an overview of what is known (other than Theorem
4
) about existence, simplicity and uniqueness of zeros of Zak transforms.

Proposition 4
.

Existence:

(a)

If
Zf
is continuous, then it has a zero in
S
.

(b)

If
f
is real valued, then zeros of
Zf
in
S
are symmetric with respect to the line
\omega=1/2
. If in addition
Zf
is continuous, then
Zf
must have a zero on the line
\omega=1/2
.

(c)

If
f
is an even function, then
Zf(1/2,1/2)=0
.

(d)

If
f
is an odd function, then
Zf(0,0)=Zf(1/2,0)=Zf(0,1/2)=0
.

(e)

If
f,\hat{f}\in W(\mathbb{R})
and
f
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{F}
whose eigenvalue is not
-i
, then
Zf(1/2,1/2)=0
.

(f)

If
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is non-negative, supported on and strictly decreasing on
[0,+\infty)
, then there is some constant
A
so that
|Zf|>A>0
holds everywhere.

Uniqueness and simplicity:

(1)

If
f
is even, continuous, and can be written as
g+T_{1}g
on
[0,+\infty)
for some strictly convex, non-negative
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then
Zf
has a unique zero in
S
.

(2)

If
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is even, continuous, non-negative and strictly convex on
[0,+\infty)
, then
Zf
has no zero on
S\setminus\{(t,\omega):t=1/2\}
.

(3)

If
f
is an exponential
B
-spline of finite order or a totally positive function (excluding the one sided exponential and its translations) or the convolution of these two types of functions, then
Zf
has a unique zero in
S
, the zero is simple and is on the line
\omega=1/2
.

(a)
is a classical result, and we refer to
[
6
, Lemma 8.4.2]
for a proof and references therein for its origin.
(f)
is
[
14
, Section 3.2.1]
.

(b)
to
(e)
are mentioned at many places.
But they are often mixed with stronger assumptions.
We give a simple sketch here: for
(b)
, if
f
is real valued then

\overline{Zf(t,\omega)}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{z}}\overline{f(t-k)e^{2\pi ik\omega}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{z}}f(t-k)e^{-2\pi ik\omega}=Zf(t,-\omega)=Zf(t,1-\omega),

where the last equality follows from the quasi-periodicity. This indicates that all zeros are symmetric with respect to the line
\omega=1/2
, and on this line we have that

Zf(t,\frac{1}{2})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(t-k)\cdot(-1)^{k},

is also a real valued function of
t
, then
Zf
being continuous implies it is also continuous. By the quasi-periodicity we have
Zf(0,1/2)=-Zf(1,1/2)
, thus leading to the existence of a zero for some
t\in[0,1)
by the intermediate value property.

For
(c)
, if
f
is even, then

Zf(t,\omega)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(t-k)e^{2\pi ik\omega}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k-t)e^{2\pi ik\omega}.

Replacing
k
with
-j
we obtain

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k-t)e^{2\pi ik\omega}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}f(-j-t)e^{-2\pi ij\omega}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}f(j+t)e^{-2\pi ij\omega}.

Plugging in
(t,\omega)=(1/2,1/2)
we see that both equations are signed sums of
f
at half integers.
But they have opposite signs, i.e.,
Zf(1/2,1/2)=-Zf(1/2,1/2)
, which means it is
0
.

For
(d)
, if
f
is odd, then similar as above we have

Zf(t,\omega)=-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k-t)e^{2\pi ik\omega}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}f(j+t)e^{-2\pi ij\omega}.

Plugging in
(t,\omega)=(1/2,0)
we get sums of
f
at half integers with opposite signs.
Thus
Zf(1/2,0)=0
, and it is also clear from oddity by direct computation that
Zf(0,0)=Zf(0,1/2)=f(0)=0
.

For
(e)
, suppose that
\lambda\neq-i
is the eigenvalue. Then we simply apply the formula (
[
6
, Proposition 8.2.2]
) that

Zf(t,\omega)=e^{2\pi it\omega}Z\hat{f}(\omega,-t),\quad f,\hat{f}\in W(\mathbb{R})

followed by the quasi-periodicity to get

Zf(t,\omega)=e^{2\pi it\omega}Z\hat{f}(\omega,-t)=\lambda e^{2\pi it\omega}Zf(\omega,-t)=\lambda e^{2\pi it\omega}Zf(\omega,1-t).

Plugging in
t=\omega=1/2
we obtain
Zf(1/2,1/2)=i\lambda Zf(1/2,1/2)
, and
i\lambda\neq 1
since
\lambda\neq-i
, which shows
(e)
.

(1)
and
(2)
are in
[
14
, Section 3.2.2-3.2.3]
.

(3)
is established in
[
2
]
for Gaussians, in
[
17
]
for exponential
B
-splines of finite order and for finite type totally positive functions without the Gaussian factor, in
[
18
]
for infinite type totally positive functions without the Gaussian factor, and finally in
[
26
]
for totally positive functions with the Gaussian factor and for their convolutions with exponential
B
-splines.
