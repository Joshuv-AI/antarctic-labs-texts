3D‐Printed Piezoionic/Bioelectronic Hydrogel for Electro‐Metabolic Regulation of Osteogenic Differentiation
Authors: Sayan Deb Dutta, Myoung Joon Jeon, Youjin Seol, Ki‐Taek Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBone regeneration is often hindered by large defects caused by trauma, tumors, or infection, necessitating advanced biomaterials beyond conventional grafts. Piezoelectric biomaterials have emerged as promising scaffolds due to their extracellular matrix‐mimicking properties, with long‐term conductivity offering additional advantages through electroactive interactions with cells. In this study, we present a direct ink writing 3D‐printable, biodegradable, adhesive, and piezoionic‐conductive nanocomposite hydrogel (GPMx) based on polypyrrole‐grafted gelatin methacrylate (GelMA‐PPy) and Ti3C2Tx (MXene) nanoflakes. The incorporation of MXene into the GelMA‐PPy matrix enhanced the viscoelasticity, showcasing its excellent printability. The GPMx hydrogel demonstrates superior mechanical strength (∼450 ± 32.36 kPa), long‐term conductivity (∼30.41 ± 3.21 S/cm), and strong adhesiveness (∼64.55 ± 13.17 kPa). Furthermore, the 3D‐printed and dual‐crosslinked GPMx‐1% hydrogel functions as a piezoionic nanogenerator (PING), converting mechanical stress into electrical signals (∼8.29 ± 1.26 mV) upon varying strain. The macroporous PING hydrogel shows superior biocompatibility (>99%) with human bone mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) and promotes osteogenic differentiation via bioelectric modulation. Additionally, antibody‐functionalized GPMx enables label‐free electrochemical sensing of alkaline phosphatase under various conditions (e.g., healthy or diseased), offering clinical potential for monitoring bone health and regenerative therapies.

Introduction

Bone regeneration, a complex physiological process, is essential for fracture healing and skeletal reconstruction [1, 2]. However, large bone defects caused by trauma, tumor resection, or infection often exceed the body's natural healing capacity and alter metabolic profile, necessitating advanced therapeutic interventions. Traditional approaches, such as autografts and allografts, have limitations, including donor site morbidity, risk of disease transmission, and immune rejection [2]. Consequently, there is a growing demand for innovative biomaterials that can effectively promote bone regeneration [3]. Scaffolds made of synthetic and natural polymers, such as polycaprolactone (PCL) and collagen, degrade in vivo and are replaced by a new bone matrix synthesized by tissue‐forming cells [2, 4]. Hydrogels, three‐dimensional (3D) networks of crosslinked polymers that can absorb and retain large amounts of water, have emerged as promising candidates for bone tissue engineering due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and structural similarity to the extracellular matrix (ECM) of native bone [5, 6, 7]. Conductive hydrogels, a revolutionary class of materials, are garnering substantial attention in diverse fields, ranging from tissue engineering to biosensing, due to their unique combination of properties [8, 9, 10, 11]. The incorporation of conductive biopolymers endows conventional hydrogels with the capability to interact with biological systems via electrical signals, paving the way for innovative approaches in areas such as nerve regeneration, cardiac tissue engineering, and drug delivery [12, 13]. Moreover, the advent of 3D printing technology has revolutionized the fabrication of conductive scaffolds, enabling the creation of customized structures with precisely controlled architectures and functionalities [14, 15, 16].

Bone is an inherently electro‐mechanosensitive tissue in which endogenous electrical cues, generated during mechanical loading via collagen piezoelectricity and ionic fluxes, play a critical role in regulating cell migration, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation, followed by activation of key pathways, including calcium ion channel activation and mitochondrial metabolism, thereby orchestrating bone repair and remodeling [17, 18]. The native bone, which is a highly vascularized and calcified tissue, also exhibits dielectric properties, ranging from 0.02 (cortical bone) to 0.079 (cancellous bone) S/m [19]. Naturally derived anisotropic biopolymers, such as collagen, inherently exhibit piezoelectric properties up to 2 pC/N [20]. The incorporation of various inorganic piezoelectric/piezoionic materials, such as barium titanate (BaTiO3), zirconium titanate (PZT), lithium niobate (LiNbO3), and bismuth‐sodium titanate (BNT), has been extensively used for developing piezoelectrics for multifaceted applications. However, these materials often exhibited higher cytotoxicity and were thus less biocompatible with the human body [21, 22]. In particular, BaTiO3 has a low Curie temperature (∼120°C), while LiNbO3 and BNT are highly susceptible to high temperature, have large hysteresis, are brittle, and have higher cytotoxicity to human cell lines. Besides, piezoelectric biopolymers, such as polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), exhibit superior flexibility and enhanced piezoelectricity compared to inorganic piezoelectric materials [23]. Nevertheless, studies have shown that the in vivo degradation product of PVDF has potential cardiotoxicity, limiting its long‐term biomedical and biosensing applications [24]. In this regard, carbon‐based piezoelectric/piezoionic materials, such as graphitic carbon nitride (gC3N4), covalent organic frameworks, graphydiyne, carbon nanotubes, and titania carbides (Ti3C2) have emerged owing to their greater abundance, eco‐friendliness, cost‐effectiveness, excellent electrical conductivity, superior mechanical strength, flexibility, and robust biocompatibility [25, 26, 27, 28]. Furthermore, carbon‐based 2D materials are often incorporated into naturally‐derived biopolymers to enhance their piezoelectricity and biocompatibility [29, 30, 31].

Importantly, alongside regenerative scaffolds, the real‐time monitoring of osteogenic differentiation has emerged as a critical requirement for understanding bone healing dynamics and enabling precision therapeutics [32]. Osteogenic markers such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), osteocalcin (OCN), and osteopontin (OPN) serve as key indicators of early and late‐stage differentiation. Conventional detection methods, such as colorimetric assays and fluorescence‐based imaging, although widely used, are often limited by multi‐step processing, the requirement of labeling agents, low temporal resolution, and the inability to provide continuous real‐time monitoring in physiological environments [33]. In recent years, electrochemical biosensors have gained attention for osteogenic marker detection due to their high sensitivity, rapid response, and compatibility with miniaturized systems. However, most reported sensors rely on external mediators, nanoparticle labeling, or rigid electrode platforms, which limit their integration with soft, implantable biomaterials and hinder long‐term in situ monitoring. Moreover, achieving a seamless interface between sensing capability and regenerative functionality remains a significant challenge [34, 35]. To address these limitations, emerging strategies focus on developing label‐free electrochemical sensing platforms integrated within conductive or bioelectronic scaffolds. Therefore, there is a critical need for multifunctional biomaterials that can simultaneously support osteogenesis, enable real‐time, and label‐free monitoring of osteogenic biomarkers within a physiologically relevant microenvironment.

In this study, we developed an electrically conductive, piezoionic, biodegradable, adhesive, and 3D printable nanocomposite hydrogel (GPMx) for unraveling biomechanical cues of osteogenesis and label‐free detection of osteogenic metabolites toward bone rehabilitation (Figure 1a–c). Conventional non‐conductive graft materials primarily provide passive structural support but lack the ability to actively participate in this electro‐mechanical signaling. In contrast, conductive/piezoelectric hydrogel is designed to recapitulate and amplify these native electrical microenvironments by enabling efficient electron/ion transport and mechano‐electrical transduction [36, 37]. Unlike conventional systems, the GPMx hydrogel is designed to generate and transmit electrical cues during loading/unloading, thereby generating electro‐mechanical stimulation osteogenic differentiation, potentially leading to improved bone regeneration. In our present study, the GPMx hydrogel is designed using polypyrrole‐grafted gelatin methacrylate (GelMA‐PPy) as a biocompatible, protein‐based conductive platform [56], while the Ti3C2Tx (MXene) nanoflakes act as a conductive and piezoelectric nanofiller [44], further strengthening the mechanical strength (up to 450 ± 32.36 kPa) of the nanocomposite hydrogel. The flexible and highly adhesive GPMx sensor (adhesive strength: 64.55 ± 13.17 kPa) was fabricated by direct ink writing (DIW) 3D printing, followed by two‐step crosslinking (radical and ionic crosslinking) and then sandwiched between two layers of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) ionic hydrogels for achieving a piezoionic nanogenerator (PING). The as‐fabricated piezoionic sensor can generate voltage upon stress (∼8.29 ± 1.26 mV, σ = 8 kPa), exert long‐term electrical conductivity (30.41 ± 3.21 S/cm), and is macroporous, biodegradable, and highly biocompatible toward human bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs), limiting the shortcomings raised by conventional piezoelectric materials. Notably, under external electrical stimulation, the GPMx hydrogel maintained hBMSCs viability (>99%) and induced osteogenic differentiation by activating mitochondrial respiration and cationic ion channels. Furthermore, the antibody‐tagged GPMx hydrogel (Figure 1d) on a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) can effectively sense osteogenic metabolite (e.g., alkaline phosphatase/ALP) from biological fluids without the addition of mediators (e.g., nanoparticles), showcasing its robust electrochemical sensing ability for monitoring bone health in clinical settings.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of the Nanocomposite Hydrogels

The incorporation of Ti3C2Tx (MXene) nanosheets into GelMA‐PPy resulted in a multifunctional hydrogel ink that combines electrical conductivity, rheological improvement, and high‐resolution 3D printing. Field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FE‐SEM) revealed the successful exfoliation of MXenes into multilayered, lamellar nanosheets with an accordion‐like structure (Figure 2a; Figure S1a), while high‐resolution transmission electron microscope (HR‐TEM), dark‐field scanning TEM (STEM), and energy dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping validated the presence of Ti and C elements with preserved crystallinity (Figure 2b). A discussion on the MXene characterization is given in Figure S1b–f section. As shown in Figure 2c, bulk MXene exhibited signature peaks at around 155.31 and 391.14 cm−1, indicating resonance and in‐plane vibrations of Ti─C (Eg) and Ti─O (Eg). Moreover, peaks at 209.50 and 626.85 cm−1 were assigned to the out‐of‐plane Ti─C (A1g) bond [38]. A small peak at around 409.33 cm−1 in MXene was assigned to the oxygen (O) bound to the Ti─C (Ti3C2(OH)2) framework [39]. Furthermore, we observed two signature peaks for MXene at 1372.33 and 1581.16 cm−1, indicating the D‐band (A1g) and G‐band (E2g) of the out‐of‐plane vibrations of the carbon atom [40, 41], suggesting that the native structure of Ti3C2 was preserved after Al removal.

The nanocomposite hydrogel was fabricated using GelMA‐PPy and MXenes, followed by dual crosslinking (e.g., radical and ionic) to yield a multifunctional, bioactive hydrogel. The GelMA‐PPy was synthesized from the GelMA via in situ polymerization of PPy by APS‐mediated oxidative reaction. The synthesis procedures for GelMA and GelMA‐PPy were adopted from our previous study [42]. Detailed characterization of GelMA and GelMA‐PPy is given in Figure S2a–c section. Digital photographs of the colloidal solution of 1 wt.% MXenes and GPMx‐1% pre‐gel solution is shown in Figure 2d. Upon dispersion into GP pre‐gel solution, homogeneous MXene suspensions were obtained, and hierarchical gelation was achieved through thermal sol–gel transition, methacrylate photopolymerization, and dynamic Fe3 + crosslinking (Figure 2e). The resulting semi‐IPN architecture endowed the architecture with structural robustness, dynamic reversibility, and injectability, as evidenced by smooth extrusion through 21G nozzles at ambient temperature (Figure 2f). The electrical performance of the pure GP and its nanocomposite hydrogels was examined using ionic conductivity measurements in a 2‐probe system and cyclic voltammetry (CV). As depicted in Figure 2g, the ionic conductivity was significantly increased with increasing MXene content (GP → GPMx‐1%). Even with minimal loading, the GPMx‐1% produced sufficient electrical responses, highlighting the efficiency of MXene sheets in forming percolative networks. Surprisingly, the conductivity remained stable across bending radii ranging from 2–10 mm (Figure 2h), a prerequisite for flexible and wearable applications [43]. CV analyses revealed quasi‐rectangular redox profiles and reproducible oxidation‐reduction cycles in 1 × PBS (Figure 2i), with anodic peak currents increasing with increasing MXene content (0.25 wt.% → 1 wt.%). Moreover, the LED illumination test also verified that the GPMx‐1% hydrogel maintains conductivity even when deformed (e.g., bending or twisting), suggesting its exceptional electrical stability (Figure S3). Furthermore, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy profiles of pure GP and GPMx hydrogels suggest that the incorporation of MXene progressively decreases the semicircle diameter and increases the −Im(Z″) response (Figure S4), indicating reduced charge‐transfer resistance and enhanced ionic/electronic transport within the polymer network. More surprisingly, the GPMx‐1% hydrogel showed good conductive stability (∼31.26 ± 0.72 S/cm) up to 60 days in PBS (Figure S5a), while a slight increase in conductivity was recorded in SBF‐soaked hydrogels up to day 20 (∼38.44 ± 1.04 S/cm), followed by a slight decrease (∼29.33 ± 1.15 S/cm) at day 60 (Figure S5b). Under physiological conditions, conductivity may remain relatively stable in the short term (up to 20 days) but could gradually decrease over time due to swelling‐induced polymer chain degradation and partial oxidation of MXene nanosheets [44]. All together, these results confirm that GPMx hydrogels possess both electronic and electrochemical stability in physiologically relevant environments, bridging the gap between mechanical compliance and bioelectronic function.

Rheological and Printing Performance of the Nanocomposite Inks

We examined the viscoelastic properties of the nanocomposite inks using a rotational rheometer, and the changes in shear moduli and viscosity were recorded through varying frequency (0.1–100 Rad/s), thixotropic behavior, and flow changes. The frequency sweep test and thixotropic behavior of all the hydrogel inks are shown in Figure S6. It was interesting to note that incorporating MXenes remarkably alters the network stiffness, energy dissipation, and shear‐thinning dynamics of the nanocomposite hydrogels. In the frequency sweep test (Figure S6a,b), both the storage (G′) and loss (G″) modulus increased with increasing MXene content (0.25 wt.% → 1 wt.%), indicating enhanced mechanical rigidity and improved viscoelastic properties. The G′ > G″ values within the measured frequency range (0.1–100 Rad/s) confirmed a predominantly elastic solid‐like profile. Notably, GPMx‐1% exhibits the highest G′, reflecting a higher crosslinking density and a more interconnected percolation network, facilitated by MXene–GelMA‐PPy interactions. While a moderate enhancement was observed in GPMx‐0.25% and GPMx‐0.5%, this suggests a dose‐dependent stiffening effect, consistent with the incorporation of conductive nanofillers as physical crosslinkers [45, 46, 47]. The complex viscosity (η*) profiles also revealed a typical shear‐viscous‐elastic transition for all hydrogels, with a higher viscosity at the low frequency (100 Rad/s) region and a gradual decrease with high frequency (100 Rad/s) region (Figure S6c).

The thixotropic test demonstrates the reversible shear‐responsive behavior of the nanocomposite hydrogel inks. We observed a sharp decrease in viscosity at high shear rate (1000 s−1), confirming network disruption and flowability, an essential property for extrusion‐based 3D printing applications (Figure S6d). After the shear stress was released (0.1 s− 1), all hydrogels rapidly recovered their initial viscosity, underscoring their robust self‐healing capability. Notably, the recovery is more significant in GPMx hydrogels, particularly in GPMx‐1%, suggesting that MXene incorporation facilitates dynamic network reformation via reversible hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and sheet‐to‐polymer adhesion, thereby limiting excessive deformation. The recovery strength of the GP, GPMx‐0.25%, GPMx‐0.5%, and GPMx‐1% were calculated to be around 31.12%, 52.92%, 68.84%, and 71.83%, respectively. To investigate further, we examined the flow behavior of the hydrogel inks at varying shear rates (0.1–1000 s−1), and the results are shown in Figure 2j. Flow curves exhibited strong shear‐thinning behavior for all formulations, with a shear‐thinning index (n) < 0.65 (Figure 2j), indicating excellent extrudability without compromising shape fidelity.

Based on the outstanding self‐healing and shear‐thinning Nature, we studied the 3D printing performance of the GPMx‐1% hydrogel ink. Owing to the thermos‐reversible gelation of GelMA, we used a cooled (5°C–10°C) print‐bed and a constant extrusion pressure of 84 kPa with a printing speed of 5 mm/s. As illustrated in Figure 2k, GPMx‐1% revealed high‐resolution 3D printing of a square structure (20 × 20 × 5 mm3, pattern: honeycomb, infill density: 20%) with high stacking accuracy across multiple layers. After printing, the construct was double crosslinked using UV light (photo, 200 mW/cm2, 5 min) irradiation and FeCl3 (ionic, 50 mm, 5–10 min) crosslinking, followed by washing with D.W. The GPMx‐1% hydrogel ink successfully demonstrated 3D printing of constructs, such as simple hollow square (20 × 20 × 5 mm3, no infill density), hollow cylinder (20 × 5 mm2, no infill density), hollow triangle (20 × 5 mm2, no infill density), and even a biomimetic bone graft (50 × 10 × 5 mm3, 100% infill density), exploring sharp features and smooth surfaces (Figure 2l). Quantitative analysis of CAD designs and printed structures showed minimal variation, with an accuracy of >90% (Figure 2m), indicating outstanding printing performance. Beyond shape fidelity, the conductivity can also be tailored by varying filament width, with thicker filaments (∼8–10 mm) producing higher conductivity due to more extensive percolative networks of MXenes and greater PPy─Fe3+ bonding (Figure 2n). This dual level of printing control provides a robust design for engineering constructs with tailored functional performance. Taken together, we anticipate that GPMx‐1% is a robust hydrogel platform that accommodates the typically conflicting requirements of softness, conductivity, printability, and mechanical stability. The integration of hierarchical crosslinking with high‐aspect‐ratio MXene enables injectability and high‐resolution printing while preserving electronic continuity under deformation, making it suitable for tissue engineering applications.

Adhesive and Mechanical Properties of the Composite Hydrogels

The incorporation of MXene nanosheets into the GP matrix significantly influenced the morphology and mechanical performance of the nanocomposite hydrogel. Representative FE‐SEM images reveal the porous microstructures of pristine GP and GPMx nanocomposites (Figure 3a). While the GP hydrogel exhibited a relatively uniform but compact porous structure, MXene incorporation remarkably increased the pore diameters, with average pore sizes increasing from ∼152.04 ± 1.57 µm (porosity: ∼34.88%) in GP to ∼322.42 ± 3.49 µm (porosity: ∼64.52%) in GPMx‐1% (Figure 3b). The increasing content of MXenes (0.25 wt.% → 1 wt.%) resulted in the formation of lamellar porous geometries. This trend suggests that the highly charged MXene sheets disrupt the packing of the GelMA‐PPy polymer chains and promote greater water uptake during gelation, thereby expanding the porous network. Such microstructural changes are critical because pore size directly influences nutrient diffusion, cell infiltration, and mechanical stress distribution in tissue‐engineered scaffolds [42, 48]. The hierarchical pore architecture of GPMx‐1%, therefore, offers a favorable microenvironment for regenerative applications, especially for bone stem cells proliferation and differentiation, physiologically mimicking the cancellous bone, which requires both structural support and biological integration. Moreover, this was also reflected in their swelling and degradation kinetics when incubated in PBS at 37°C. As shown in Figure S7a, all samples exhibited a rapid initial swelling phase within the first 12 h in PBS (37°C), followed by a slower equilibrium phase after 48–60 h. The equilibrium swelling ratio increased systematically with MXene concentration, reaching ∼230% for GPMx‐1% compared with ∼150% for pristine GP. This enhancement can be attributed to the hydrophilic surface functional groups of MXene nanosheets, which promote water infiltration and network expansion, as well as to the porous microarchitecture introduced by nanosheet dispersion within the polymer matrix. Similarly, the degradation kinetics showed that higher MXene content accelerated hydrogel degradation over 14 days (Figure S7b), with GPMx‐1% exhibiting a degradation rate approaching ∼80%, compared to GP (∼55%). The increased degradation correlates with the higher swelling capacity and greater porosity of the MXene‐containing networks, facilitating water diffusion and hydrolytic cleavage of labile bonds.

Next, we evaluated the adhesive and compressive mechanical properties of the pure GP and its nanocomposites. The adhesive properties of the nanocomposite hydrogels were investigated using the ASTM‐F2458‐05 lap‐shear test, and the results are shown in Figure 3c. Pure GP displayed limited adhesion strength, whereas GPMx hydrogels exhibited a pronounced, concentration‐dependent (0.25 wt.% → 1 wt.%) increase in adhesive strength (Figure 3d). At 1 wt.% MXene, the adhesive strength reached nearly fourfold, which was significantly (∼65.38 ± 6.26 kPa, **** p < 0.0001) higher than GP hydrogel (∼6.7 ± 1.33 kPa), highlighting the synergistic interaction of MXene's surface functional groups (e.g., ─O, ─OH, and ─F) and the dynamic PPy–Fe3+ crosslinking in the GelMA‐PPy/MXene system. This enhancement can be attributed to multiple mechanisms: (1) increased interfacial contact via expanded pores, (2) π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding between MXene sheets and GelMA matrix, and (3) reinforcement of the GelMA‐PPy matrix by physical confinement of polymer chains [48]. Digital photographs further illustrate the robust and instant adhesion of GPMx‐1% at physiological temperature (37°C), maintaining intimate contact even under bending deformation, suggesting its good adhesion (Figure 3e). Notably, compared with previous reports, the adhesive strength of GPMx‐1% hydrogels surpassed that of most GelMA‐based adhesive hydrogels (Figure 3f, Table S1), underscoring its superior adhesive strength. Similarly, ASTM‐F2258 interfacial toughness tests revealed good interfacial adhesion compared with reported Gelatin/GelMA‐based hydrogels (Figure 3g, Table S2), confirming the dual advantages of toughness and adhesion. To validate this, we then studied the multiple adhesive performance of the GPMx‐1% hydrogel. As shown in Figure 3h, the broad substrate adhesion, including bone, glass, metal, paper, and human skin, underscores the versatility of GPMx hydrogel and suggests translational potential in surgical sealants, tissue adhesives, and bioelectronic interfaces toward bone tissue engineering.

Compressive test (ASTM‐D6108‐24) provided further insight into the mechanical reinforcement achieved by MXene incorporation into the GelMA‐PPy matrix. Stress–strain curves demonstrate progressive enhancement in compressive strength with increasing MXene (0.25 wt.% → 1 wt.%) concentration (Figure 3i), with GPMx‐1% sustaining higher loads and greater resilience prior to failure compared to the pure GP hydrogel. Quantitative analysis shows compressive strength values significantly (**** p < 0.0001) rising from ∼76.54 ± 3.82 kPa in GP to 466.45 ± 32.35 kPa in GPMx‐1% (Figure 3j), while the elastic modulus increased more than threefold across the same range (Figure 3k). The young's elastic modulus for GP, GPMx‐0.25%, GPMx‐0.5%, and GPMx‐1% were calculated to be 12.56 ± 2.62, 37.61 ± 5.88, 42.67 ± 3.13, and 69.55 ± 6.47 kPa, respectively. This stress‐dissipation behavior of the GPMx‐1% hydrogel was also evident when the hydrogel (20 × 10 × 5 mm3) was placed on the knee joint, showing superior adhesion, flexibility, and stability (Figure S8). These gains reflect both nanoscale reinforcement from MXene sheets acting as rigid nanofillers and mesoscale structural contributions from enlarged pores originating from strong MXene‐GelMA‐PPy interaction, covalent crosslinking of GelMA‐GelMA chains, and PPy─Fe3+ bonding, which facilitate stress dissipation. A comparative study on compressive strength of various GelMA‐based hydrogels is reported in Figure 3l and Table S3. Taken together, our results demonstrate excellent adhesive and mechanical properties, which would be beneficial for developing next‐generation bioadhesive electronic interfaces for knee joint and related bone therapies.

Sensing Performance and Biocompatibility Evaluation of the Piezoionic Hydrogels

To explore the functional properties of GPMx hydrogels beyond their mechanical reinforcement, we fabricated 3D‐printed piezoionic pressure sensors based on the GPMx‐1% hydrogel formulation, which exhibited greater electrical conductivity. The schematic illustration and digital images of the printed sensor (10 × 10 × 0.5 mm3) are shown in Figure 4a. The device assembly is simple: the printed hydrogel is sandwiched between thin polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) ionic films, connected with copper wiring, and encapsulated in PDMS. This layered structure enabled the conversion of mechanical deformation into measurable voltage signals, leveraging the hydrogel's intrinsic ionic conductivity and dynamic crosslinking. When subjected to bending deformation (∆L = 5 mm), the GPMx‐1% sensor produced robust voltage outputs with sharp response–recovery cycles (Figure 4b), confirming its ability to act as a dynamic mechanosensor. Compared to pristine GP‐based sensors, which showed weaker and less reproducible signals, the GPMx‐1% sensor exhibited significantly (** p < 0.01) higher voltage generation (Figure 4c), underscoring the role of MXene nanosheets in facilitating ion transport and amplifying piezoionic transduction. Open‐circuit voltage (V oc) traces under similar bending strain (Figure 4d) revealed stable, repeatable responses, establishing the hydrogel as a reliable ionic–electronic interface. This remarkable mechanoresponsive sensitivity of the GPMx‐1% hydrogel arises from quantum tunneling between adjacent MXene nanosheets embedded in the GelMA‐PPy network. Under mechanical stress, these inner‐sheet distances fluctuate at the sub‐nanometer scale, enabling electron tunneling across the potential barriers formed by the surrounding GelMA‐PPy matrix and water molecules [49, 50].

The piezoionic performance of the GPMx‐1% sensors was further evaluated under varied pressure stimuli ranging from 1 to 8 kPa, mimicking physiological pressure regimes relevant to wearable and biomedical applications. Distinct voltage outputs (2.84–8.39 mV) were recorded across this range (Figure 4e), with signal amplitudes increasing in proportion to the applied pressure. Linearity analysis revealed a strong correlation (Adj. R 2 = 0.9954) between output voltage and applied stress, demonstrating the device's sensitivity and quantitative reliability (Figure 4f). Long‐term stability tests (Figure 4g) confirmed consistent voltage generation under cyclic loading (up to 500 cycles) without noticeable drift, indicative of structural integrity and stable ion transport pathways within the GPMx‐1% hydrogel. Importantly, practical demonstrations, such as knee bending, displayed robust and repeatable voltage (∼4.87–15.22 mV) spikes (Figure 4h), highlighting the potential of GPMx sensors for wearable knee joint monitoring. A comparative study on the MXene loading and its effect on printability, dispersion stability, mechanical properties, and biocompatibility is shown in Table S4.

Bone is considered a dynamic and electro‐mechanosensitive tissue. Under specific stimuli or mechanical loading, piezoelectric potentials are generated owing to the presence of collagen and mineralized bone matrix [51]. To access the biocompatibility of the bulk GPMx‐1% hydrogel, we used human bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs). The WST‐8 cell viability assay demonstrated no cytotoxicity of GPMx hydrogels compared to GP and controls, with cell viability remaining above 95% over 7 days (Figure 4i), suggesting outstanding biocompatibility. Moreover, the GPMx‐1% hydrogel was found to be non‐toxic (viability >99%) to murine macrophage (RAW 264.7) cells (Figure S9), suggesting its nontoxicity toward immune cells. To evaluate the synergy between the conductive and piezoionic properties of the hydrogels and cellular responses, hBMSCs were cultured within 3D‐printed GP and GPMx‐1% scaffolds under external electrical field (EF) stimulation (±250 mV, 50 kHz, 20 min/day) as shown in Figure 4j and Figure S10a–c. The electrical dose optimization was adopted from our previous report [52, 53]. Although 1 wt.% MXene did not impose any cytotoxicity to hBMSCs; however, a concentration‐dependent cytotoxicity screening revealed that hBMSCs display higher viability up to 1 wt.% of MXenes, while >2 wt.% showed a decrease in viability (Figure S11). Therefore, 1 wt.% MXene was selected as the optimal dose for hydrogel preparation and subsequent osteogenesis. Besides, we also found that variable frequencies (e.g., 25, 50, and 100 kHz) had a positive role in hBMSCs viability. Digital photographs of the EF stimulation device and waveforms at varying frequencies are shown in Figure S12a,b. Notably, the 50 kHz frequency dose increased hBMSCs viability in the presence of GPMx‐1% hydrogel (Figure S12c), suggesting that moderate‐frequency EF stimulation may transiently enhance cellular metabolic activity. Furthermore, osteogenic differentiation study at day 7 (Figure S12d–g) and day 14 (Figure S12h–k) also revealed that EF stimulation at ±250 mV with a frequency of 50 kHz constantly enhances the mineralized nodule formation in hBMSCs than ±100 or ±500 mV counterparts, indicating that ±250 mV (50 kHz) dose is optimal for osteogenesis. We assessed viability using live/dead staining after 7 days of EF stimulation with 3D‐printed hydrogels, and the results are shown in Figure 4k. The live/dead staining showed dense populations of viable hBMSCs within GPMx‐1% scaffolds compared to GP controls, with statistical quantification confirming significantly (*** p < 0.001) higher viability (Figure 4l). These results highlight not only the biocompatibility of the material but also its ability to support enhanced cell survival under electromechanical stimulation, due to the presence of conductive MXene and/or PPy–Fe3+ network facilitating cell‐material communication.

To investigate the osteogenic potential of hBMSCs, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect the expression of osteogenic protein markers (e.g., Runx2, ALP, and OCN) at day 14. The GPMx‐1% + EF stimulation displayed significantly higher expression of these markers compared to controls, suggesting accelerated osteogenic differentiation (Figure 4m). Semi‐quantitative analysis of mean fluorescence intensity also suggests significantly elevated expression of Runx2 (*** p < 0.001) and ALP (*** p < 0.001). At the same time, OCN showed moderate but significant (* p < 0.05) upregulation, reflecting both early and late‐stage osteogenic commitment (Figure 4n). This was also confirmed by qRT‐PCR analysis of osteogenic gene markers at day 14. As depicted in Figure 4o, key osteogenic genes were significantly upregulated in GPMx‐1% + EF including Runx2 (greater than fivefold, *** p < 0.001), ALP (greater than eightfold, *** p < 0.001), COL1A1 (greater than twofold), OCN (greater than fourfold, *** p < 0.001), and OPN (greater than threefold, *** p < 0.001), alongside markers associated with ECM deposition, such as BMP2 (greater than threefold, *** p < 0.001) and signaling pathway marker, such as SMAD3 (greater than fivefold, *** p < 0.001). The negatively charged surface terminations (e.g., ─O, ─OH, and ─F) of MXenes facilitate strong hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with the GelMA‐PPy matrix, stabilizing conductive percolation networks while maintaining mechanical flexibility. This allows efficient electron–phonon coupling and stress‐to‐charge conversion, enabling bioelectric signal transduction and cellular mechanostimulation in the osteogenic microenvironment [50].

Mechanistic Insights of Osteogenesis Driven by GPMx Piezoionic Hydrogel

To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms by which the piezoionic GPMx‐1% hydrogel promotes osteogenesis, transcriptomic and metabolic analyses were performed on human bone‐marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) cultured for 14 days under EF (±250 mV, 50 kHz, 20 min/day) stimulation. Hierarchical clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed two major gene clusters associated with mitochondrial activity, metabolism, and redox regulation. Suh et al. suggested that mitochondrial fission and secretion play a significant role in the osteogenesis of human osteoblast (OB) cells [54]. Also, evidence suggests that short‐time (3 s) electrical stimulation (dose: ∼30 V, 100 Hz) of the gastrocnemius muscle in rats increases the phosphorylation of mitochondrial fission‐related transcription factors [55].

The k‐means hierarchical clustering of DEGs, with corresponding gene expression changes after 7 days, is shown in Figure 5a. The k‐means clustering yielded two major clusters: Cluster‐1 and Cluster‐2. Cluster‐1 showed the expression of a total of 34 DEGs, while Cluster‐2 holds 62 DEGs related to mitochondrial biogenesis. As predicted, the 3DP groups exhibited higher levels of gene upregulation in both clusters than the 2D groups. Moreover, when compared between GPMx‐1% + EFs versus the control group, a total of 20 most significant (Log2FC, * p < 0.05) DEGs were found either up‐ or downregulated during early osteogenesis (Figure 5b). Specifically, two key genes, that is, dynamin‐related protein‐1 (Drp1, 1.54‐fold) and cAMP‐activated GEF for Rap GTPases (EPAC1, 1.36‐fold), known as key players in the mitochondrial fission event, were significantly (*p < 0.05) upregulated in the GPMx‐1% + EFs group compared with the control. Similarly, the most significantly downregulated genes in GPMx‐1% + EFs were mitochondrial optic atrophy‐1 (Opa1), fission protein‐1 (Fis1), and Pink1, suggesting that EF stimulation interferes with fission events, not fusion events, in hBMSCs during early osteogenesis in the presence of GMAP hydrogels [54]. This was further correlated with the protein level quantification of hBMSCs using the ELISA technique. As depicted in Figure S13, both Drp1 (∼7.36 ± 0.56 ng/mL) and Fis1 (∼11.17 ± 1.34 ng/mL) content were significantly increased in hBMSCs with GPMx‐1% + EFs treatment at day 14. The STRING protein–protein interaction map of the top 21 genes also suggests positive regulation of mitochondrial fission events during EFs stimulation (Figure S14). On the other hand, we also identified the top 20 genes differentially expressed in Cluster‐1, related to bioenergetics and ROS, when comparing GPMx‐1% + EFs versus the control group. For instance, two important genes, that is, phosphofructokinase‐1 (Pfk1, 1.89‐fold) and NADP(H) quinone dehydrogenase‐1 (Nqo1, 1.55‐fold), were found to be highly upregulated in the GPMx‐1% + EFs group compared with the control. Pfk1, an important enzyme that converts glucose into fructose‐1,6‐bisphosphate, is usually activated by signals from cAMP and is one of the rate‐limiting processes of glycolysis [56]. Previously, we discussed that hBMSCs in the GPMx‐1% + EFs group showed higher glucose consumption, which is likely required for increased energy metabolism and is positively correlated with mitochondrial Pfk1 activity. Our results demonstrated that EFs stimulation through GPMx‐1% hydrogel increased the activity of Fis1/Pfk1 owing to the high glucose demand to produce energy and a low level of ROS in hBMSCs during early osteogenesis. Figure S15 depicts the STRING protein–protein interaction map of 20 DEGs associated with bioenergetics.

The results of the KEGG gene ontology analysis (Figure 5d) revealed higher gene enrichment for ‘catabolic process’ (gene count: 8, p = 2.2e − 03), ‘ion transmembrane transport’ (gene count: 10, p = 2e − 03), and ‘positive regulation of metabolic process’ (gene count: 9, p = 2.4e − 03) in BP annotation when compared between GPMx‐1% + EFs versus control group. Similarly, under CC annotation, higher gene enrichment was found for ‘mitochondrial membrane activity’ (gene count: 26, p = 3.6e − 06), ‘mitochondria envelope’ (gene count: 20, p = 3e − 06), ‘organelle inner membrane activity’ (gene count: 20, p = 3e − 06), ‘mitochondrial matrix process’ (gene count: 14, p = 5.4e − 06), and ‘catalytic complex’ (gene count: 28, p = 3.1e − 06), respectively. Besides, higher enrichment was also found for respiration‐related biological terms in MF annotation, such as ‘oxidative phosphorylation’ (gene count: 18, p = 0.05), ‘oxidoreductase activity’ (gene count: 20, p = 0.05), and ‘positive regulation of phosphokinase activity’ (gene count: 14, p = 0.05) when compared between GPMx‐1% + EFs versus control groups. Moreover, Tmem135, an enzyme that catalyzes fatty acid oxidation during β‐oxidation, was also found to be upregulated in the GPMx‐1% + EFs group compared with the control group (Figure 5e), suggesting that EFs stimulation through the GMAP hydrogel actively regulated mitochondrial metabolism and respiration.

Considering the significant variations in energy utilization revealed by transcriptomic studies, we quantitatively investigated the activation of aerobic glycolysis and the involvement of OXPHOS during early osteogenesis under EF stimulation. The key enzyme activity and metabolite production during glycolysis and OXPHOS are calculated using colorimetric assay kits, and the results are shown in Figure 5f. Interestingly, the glucose‐6‐phosphate (G‐6‐P), glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G‐6‐PD), NADH, and pyruvate content were significantly (*** p < 0.001) elevated in GPMx‐1% + EFs groups with an enhancement of OXPHOS, indicating the hyperactivation of glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in hBMSCs. These findings suggest that hBMSCs require more oxygen and glucose from the media, and the ability to maintain metabolism under stress leads to activation of OXPHOS. Moreover, the hBMSCs in GPMx‐1% under EFs stimulation showed significantly (*** p < 0.001) higher activity of mitochondrial inner membrane Complex‐I, Complex‐II, and Complex‐IV activity than other treatment groups (Figure 5g,h,j). These results indicated that the GPMx‐1% + EFs group enhanced mitochondrial respiration through complexes II and IV, specifically producing sufficient fumarate (Figure 5i) and ATP, consistent with previous reports [57]. This was also reflected in the basal respiration and ATP production of hBMSCs during osteogenesis. The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) peaked on day 7 in GPMx‐1% + EFs groups, with a gradual reduction in GPMx‐1%, EFs, and control groups (Figure 5k). Also, a remarkable enhancement in basal respiration was evidenced in the GPMx‐1% + EFs group compared to other groups (Figure 5l), suggesting that the GPMx‐1% hydrogel acts as an electro‐metabolic modulator via external EF stimulation.

To understand which transmembrane ion channels are involved in osteogenesis, we next performed the weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA). We identified the key modules and hub genes associated with GPMx‐1% + EFs versus the control group. Figure 6d demonstrates 5 coordinately expressed gene programs with distinct biological properties. Among the 5 clusters, Clusters 3, 4, and 5 were significantly upregulated for cation channel activity in the GPMx‐1% + EFs group. In Cluster‐5, the higher normalized enrichment score (NES) was obtained for ‘cation channel activity’ (p = 8.3e − 63) and ‘cation ion channel activity’ (p = 3.9e − 67) when compared between GPMx‐1% + EFs versus the control group (Figure 6e). These findings indicate that EF stimulation via the GPMx‐1% hydrogel activates membrane‐bound Ca2+ ion channels and facilitates Ca2+ influx in hBMSCs during early osteogenesis. The 10 key hub genes associated with VGICs activity were identified as polycystin‐1 (PKD1), calcium voltage‐gated channel auxiliary subunit alpha‐2/delta‐1 (CACNA2D1), transient receptor potential melastatin‐3 (TRPM3), TRPM4, integrin subunit Alpha‐V (ITGAV), transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member‐1 (TRPV1), stromal interacting molecule‐2 (STIM2), CACNA1C, presenilin‐1 (PSEN1), and PKD2. A network analysis of the interactions among these 10 genes is shown in Figure 6f.

Owing to the higher expression of CACNA2D1 in the GPMx‐1% + EFs group, we hypothesized that EFs stimulation via piezoelectric GPMx‐1% hydrogel may activate the CACNA2D1 calcium channel in hBMSCs, thereby transporting Ca2+ ions to trigger calmodulin activity. To verify this finding, we performed the immunostaining analysis using a pharmacological Ca2+ ion channel inhibitor (Nifedipine, 10 µm) at day 14. As shown in Figure 6g, the CLSM images of hBMSCs demonstrate pronounced Ca2+‐binding protein (CBP) localization with CACNA2D1 in GPMx‐1% and GPMx‐1% + EF‐treated groups than in the control and Nifedipine groups. Particularly, the GPMx‐1% + EF‐treated group showed significantly (**** p < 0.0001) higher fluorescence intensity for CACNA2D1 (Figure 6h), which was found to be highly co‐localized (PCC = 0.81) with CBP, suggesting increased Ca2+ signaling activity (Figure 6i). Similarly, the CACNA2D1 protein level was found to be around ∼872 + 15.46 pg/mL in GPMx‐1% + EF‐treated group than in the control (Figure S16a). This was further supported by the cellular ALP level and early osteogenic markers expression. The ALP activity was significantly (* p < 0.05) decreased in the Nifedipine group, while an enhancement was found for GPMx‐1% + EF‐treated groups (Figure S16b). Notably, the expression of Runx2 and ALP gene markers was significantly (less than onefold, ** p < 0.01)) downregulated in Nifedipine‐treated groups, while an upregulation (greater than threefold, **** p < 0.0001) was observed in GPMx‐1% + EF‐treated groups after 14 days of osteoinduction (Figure S16c,d), further disclosing that inhibition of Ca2+ signaling impair osteogenesis. Moreover, the results of qRT‐PCR also suggest a higher expression of Calmodulin (1.60‐fold, *** p < 0.001), CACNA2D1 (1.79‐fold, * p < 0.05), and CACNA1C (1.38‐fold, *** p < 0.001) gene markers in GPMx‐1% + EFs than in the control. In contrast, TRPM3 gene expression was not significantly upregulated (Figure 6j–m). Meanwhile, the intracellular Ca2+ level was also measured after EFs stimulation on day 7, which also suggested an elevated Ca2+ flux in the GPMx‐1% + EFs group (Figure 6n). A study conducted by Wright et al. [58] reported that osteocytes and osteoblasts with the deleted CACNA2D1 gene impaired osteogenesis owing to the loss of mechanosensing ability toward external stimuli. Consistent with the literature, our study demonstrated that external sinusoidal EFs (±250 mV, 50 kHz, 20 min/day) through GPMx‐1% hydrogel induced early osteogenesis by regulating the hyperactivity of CACNA ion channels.

Electrochemical Sensing of Osteogenic Metabolites Using GPMx Hydrogel Probe

ALP is a type of phosphatase enzyme that plays a significant role in bone development, especially in bone mineralization and osseointegration. The ALP level can vary in disease conditions, such as osteoporosis, bone infection, and even in bone cancer [59, 60]. In this study, we present a label‐free electrochemical immunosensor based on GPMx hydrogel for the rapid detection of ALP from biological systems. The developed GCE@GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP conductive hydrogel enabled a highly sensitive, label‐free electrochemical platform for detecting ALP, a crucial biomarker of osteogenesis and bone metabolism. The fabrication process is schematically illustrated in Figure 7a. This architecture provided a biocompatible and conductive microenvironment that enhanced electron transport and antibody binding affinity, consistent with previous conductive hydrogel‐based electrochemical interfaces [61, 62]. The reference ALP ranges (normal bone, infected bone, hBMSCs, and MG‐63 cells) shown in Figure 7b highlight its physiological and pathological relevance as a marker for osteogenic differentiation and bone disease progression [63, 64, 65]. The linear sweep voltammetry curves reveal stable and reproducible redox peaks in different biological media (Figure 7c), such as phosphate buffer saline (PBS), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle medium (DMEM). At the same time, the quantitative analysis confirmed negligible variation in anodic current among them (Figure 7d), underscoring the sensor's medium tolerance and biological compatibility.

Next, we tested the sensing performance of the GCE@GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP sensor for ALP detection using various pathological in vitro models. This includes the osteogenic hBMSCs (=healthy), hBMSCs infected with Escherichia coli bacteria (=infected), and osteogenic MG‐63 cells (=tumor). The cells were cultured in osteogenic induction media for 14 days and stained with ARS for the mineralized bone nodule formation. Figure 7e shows a time‐dependent enhancement in osteogenic differentiation for hBMSCs and MG‐63 cells characterized by the enhanced mineralized nodule formation. In contrast, decreased nodule formation was spotted in the infected group. The statistical analysis of ARS is given in Figure 7f. Subsequently, we noticed a significantly (**** p < 0.0001) higher ALP activity in healthy and tumor models than in the infected model, as measured via a standard colorimetric assay (Figure 7g). These results verified that ALP secretion correlated strongly with cellular differentiation, validating the electrochemical signal as a direct reflection of osteogenic progression. Prior to sensing analysis, the affinity of ALP toward the modified electrode was analyzed using a linearized Langmuir adsorption kinetics. As depicted in Figure 7h, a highly linear dependence of 1/IF on ALP concentration [F] is observed, indicating that the surface adsorption is typically quasi‐equilibrium and single‐site adsorption (Adj. Pearson's R 2 = 0.99853), supporting the validity of the Langmuir approximation for this heterogeneous yet well‐regulated biointerface [66]. The apparent dissociation constant (K D = ∼0.3128 ± 0.0525 U/L) further indicates strong binding affinity at the solid–liquid interface, while the maximum current response (I max = 0.768 ± 0.258 µA) reflects the finite density of accessible antibody binding sites. More interestingly, this relatively low ​K D value indicates that antigen recognition is largely preserved after covalent immobilization. This was also reflected in ELISA validation (Figure 7i), where bare GCE (unlabeled) exhibited no signal and unmodified GCE@GPMx‐1% showed weak binding, suggesting limited passive binding between ALP and GCE@GPMx‐1%. Notably, the GCE@GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP electrode displayed a remarkable increase in binding affinity (∼85%–90%, **** p < 0.0001), showcasing that ALP antibody–antigen interactions dominate the interfacial response. Calibration with standard ALP solutions (Figure 7j,k) demonstrated a linear response over 0–10 U/L (R 2 > 0.99293), achieving a low limit of detection (LOD = 1.142 ± 0.382 U/L). The sensitivity enhancement can be attributed to the synergistic effects of MXene's high surface area, PPy's redox conductivity, and GelMA's biocompatibility, as previously reported in conductive hydrogel matrices [61, 67]. The excellent ALP sensing potential was also reflected in the presence of various in vitro fluids (e.g., FBS or DMEM media), suggesting no significant fluctuation in electrochemical sensing (Figure S17). Time‐dependent measurements from live cell cultures (Figure 7l) showed a consistent increase in anodic peak current over 3–14 days, resembling ALP secretion trends observed in colorimetric kit‐based assays. A comparative analysis (Figure 7m) revealed that the GCE@GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP sensor outperformed most existing ALP sensors in terms of detection limit and operational simplicity, highlighting its advantage over conventional sensors.

Selectivity studies also confirmed minimal interference from other biological analytes, such as cysteine, arginine, alanine, uric acid, and glutamic acid, as shown in Figure 7n, while long‐term evaluation demonstrated signal retention above 90% after 60 days at 4°C, validating the GCE@GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP electrode's superior chemical and mechanical stability (Figure 7o). An overview of the multifunctional potential of the GPMx hydrogel, combining piezoelectric responsiveness, pressure sensing, and osteoconductive support for real‐time ALP detection, is schematically illustrated in Figure 7p. Although GelMA undergoes gradual biodegradation in physiological environments, the presence of MXene nanosheets and PPy–Fe3+ ionically conductive networks preserves electrical continuity and piezoionic responsiveness over extended periods, ensuring reliable sensing performance. Taken together, these results establish the GCE@GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP hydrogel sensor as an integrated electrochemical and biofunctional scaffold for continuous, label‐free monitoring of osteogenic activity. Its printability and bioelectronic sensitivity suggest strong potential for next‐generation bone tissue engineering applications. Conclusion and Outlook

In summary, a 3D‐printable, highly conductive, piezoionic, and biocompatible multifunctional hydrogel was successfully developed for real‐time detection of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and osteogenic activity. The GCE@GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP electrode exhibited superior sensitivity (LOD ≈ 0.082 U/L), high selectivity, and long‐term stability over 60 days, enabling continuous monitoring of ALP secretion from differentiating osteoblasts. The multifunctional piezoelectric GPMx‐1% hydrogel not only provided a biocompatible and conductive matrix for electrochemical sensing but also promoted osteogenic differentiation, underscoring its dual role as both a biosensor and a biocompatible hydrogel. A limitation of this study is that osteogenesis was evaluated under external electrical stimulation rather than purely self‐powered conditions. Future work should assess mechanically compressed cell‐laden scaffolds without external power to confirm intrinsic piezoionic stimulation. Looking ahead, this printable GPMx hydrogel platform could be further extended to multiplexed enzyme sensing, personalized bone health diagnostics, and smart regenerative implants. Integration with wireless or artificial intelligence (AI)‐assisted readout systems could transform this technology into a next‐generation electro‐biointerface for continuous monitoring of bone‐related diseases.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of Ti3C2Tx (MXene) Nanosheets

The MXene nanosheets were synthesized as reported in the literature [68]. Briefly, 0.5 g of MAX phase (Ti3AlC2, Sigma‐Aldrich) was mixed with 1.2 g LiF (Sigma‐Aldrich) and 20 mL of 9 m HCL (Sigma‐Aldrich) in a glass beaker under constant stirring, followed by agitation at 37°C ± 2°C for 80 h. After that, the resulting solution was centrifuged and washed 3–5 times with D.I. water/ethanol to obtain a neutral pH. The as‐obtained product was dried at 60°C to obtain MXene nanosheets.

Synthesis of GelMA and GelMA‐PPy

The GelMA (with a degree of substitution/DS: ∼70%) and GelMA‐PPy (DS: ∼24%) were synthesized as reported in our previous work [42]. For GelMA‐PPy, 1 g of freeze‐dried GelMA was dissolved in 1 × PBS at 45°C–50°C, and 400 µL pyrrole (Py, Sigma‐Aldrich) was added; the mixture was magnetically agitated for 2 h. The reaction mixture was then gradually mixed with 0.1 g of APS, with the temperature maintained at about 40°C throughout the night. To modify GelMA, APS reacted with Py and was grafted in situ by interacting with the lysine proton on GelMA at the methacrylation site. After 2 h, the GelMA‐PPy solution was dialyzed in D.W. for 2–3 days. Ultimately, the pure GelMA‐PPy foam was obtained by lyophilizing the resultant solution. Until it was needed again, the as‐synthesized GelMA‐PPy was stored at −20°C.

Fabrication of Nanocomposite Hydrogel

For this, 15 wt.% GelMA‐PPy was dissolved in 1 × PBS under vigorous stirring at 45°C–50°C in a glass beaker, followed by the addition of various concentrations of MXene nanosheets (0–1 wt.%) with respect to the weight ratio of GelMA‐PPy. The pre‐gel solution was stirred overnight, protected from sunlight, to obtain a homogeneous solution. After that, 0.25% (w/w) LAP photoinitiator (Sigma‐Aldrich) was added and stirred until fully dissolved. Finally, the resultant pre‐gel solution was cast in cylindrical silicone molds (4 × 2 cm2) and kept at 4°C to initiate physical crosslinking. After that, the hydrogel was demolded and irradiated with 365 nm UV light (200 mW/cm2) for 5 min at RT. After photo‐crosslinking, the hydrogel was rinsed with D.W. for 2–3 times to remove the unreacted monomers, followed by immersion in a FeCl3 bath (50 mm, Fisher Scientific) for 5–10 min at RT. Finally, the hydrogel was washed with D.W. for 2–3 times to remove the excess Fe3+ ions and stored at 4°C until further use. The pure hydrogel was designated as GP, while the MXene‐containing hydrogels were designated as GPMx‐a, where a is the wt.% of MXenes.

Characterizations of the Hydrogel

General Characterizations

FE‐SEM (JSM‐7900F, Jeol) and HR‐TEM (JEM‐2100F, Jeol) were used to examine the surface morphology of the MXene nanosheets. The structural and chemical composition of the MXene was analyzed using Raman and XPS (K Alpha+, Thermo VG) spectroscopy. The structural properties of the gelatin, GelMA, and GelMA‐PPy were analyzed using FT‐IR (iN10/iS50, Thermo Scientific) and 1H‐NMR spectroscopy. The morphology of the freeze‐dried hydrogels was examined using FE‐SEM (JSM‐7900F, JEOL) at 5 kV, and porosity was calculated using ImageJ v1.8 (NIH). The viscoelasticity of the fabricated nanocomposite hydrogels was investigated using a rotational rheometer (MCR‐92, Anton Paar) over a range of frequencies (0.1–100 Rad/s) and shear rates (0.1–1000 s−1) at room temperature. The swelling and degradation study of the nanocomposite hydrogel scaffolds was assessed as reported in our previous study [69].

Conductivity Measurements

The conductivity of the fabricated nanocomposite hydrogels was evaluated by the 2‐probe method using a digital source meter (Keithley 2400) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) at ambient temperature. For this, a rectangular and dual‐crosslinked hydrogel sample (20 × 10 × 5 mm3) was used. The conductivity was measured according to the following Equation (1): (1) L=1ρ=1R×S where L, ρ, R, and S are the conductivity, resistivity, resistance, and cross‐sectional area of the hydrogel sample. For the conductivity stability test, the GPMx‐1% hydrogel was kept at 4°C for 60 days, and the conductivity was monitored repeatedly using a Keithley 2400 source meter.

Adhesive Test

The adhesive performance of the fabricated nanocomposite hydrogels was investigated using lap‐shear and interfacial adhesive tests at RT following the guidelines of American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM)‐F2458‐05 and ASTM‐F2258. For lap‐shear analysis, all hydrogel samples (∼100 mg) were placed between two overlapped rectangular fiber‐boards (10 × 3 × 0.5 cm3), and the adhesive strength was measured under tensile loading. The adhesive strength was calculated according to the following Equation (2): (2) Lap−shearstrength=Failureload=ForceBondarea

For the interfacial adhesive test, an acrylic plate was placed on the bottom of a pig skin, and the other surface was coated with the GPMx‐1% hydrogel (2 × 2 × 0.5 cm3, ∼500 mg). Next, the upper plate bonded to the hydrogel was pulled upward until any visible detachment (F max) was recognized. The interfacial strength was calculated according to the following Equation (3): (3) TensilestrengthTSinterfacial=FmaxW×L

The F max, W, and L are the hydrogel's maximum force at shear, width, and length, respectively. Moreover, the physical adhesive performance of the GPMx‐1% hydrogel was tested on various substrates (e.g., acrylic bone, paper, glass, metal, and human skin) and photographed with a smartphone (iPhone 16 Pro Max, 2868 × 1320 pixels). Additionally, adhesive performance at the knee joint was tested using a hydrogel sample (3 × 2 × 0.5 cm3) placed on the knee and digitally photographed.

Mechanical Test

The mechanical properties of the developed nanocomposite hydrogels were investigated using a universal testing machine (UTM, A&D Digital) under compressive loading. The experiment was conducted in accordance with the ASTM‐D6108‐24 guidelines. Briefly, cylindrical hydrogels (2 × 1 cm2) with a consistent weight of around ∼3–4 g were compressed, and the compressive strength was calculated according to the following Equation (4): (4) Compressivestrength=FmaxA where F max and A are the maximum applied force and cross‐sectional area of the hydrogel samples.

3D Printing Process

The 3D printing was carried out with GPMx‐1% hydrogel using a DIW 3D printer (Cellink Bio‐X, Sweden). For this, the CAD models (e.g., square, rectangle, and bone graft with varying infill density) were designed in AutoCAD (Autodesk) and exported as stereolithography (STL) files. The 3D printing was performed on a pre‐cooled print bed, ensuring proper gelation and printed filaments were obtained for GPMx‐1%. Fabrication and Sensing Performance of Piezoionic Nanogenerator (PING)

3D Printing of the GPMx‐1% Hydrogel Sensor

We selected the GPMx‐1% hydrogel for the fabrication of the PING sensor owing to its outstanding ionic conductivity, pressure responsiveness, adhesiveness, and mechanical properties. Briefly, a square structure (10 × 10 × 5 mm3) with 100% infill was printed using a 21G nozzle and crosslinked with UV light and FeCl3 (50 mm). After that, the crosslinked GPMx‐1% hydrogel was washed several times with distilled water (D.W.) and stored at °C until further use.

Fabrication of Supporting Hydrogel Membrane

For the ionically conductive, flexible, and supporting base hydrogel, 10 wt.% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, DaeJung Chemicals) was dissolved in deionized water (D.I.) water at 80°C–90°C under vigorous stirring. After obtaining a homogeneous and transparent solution, 0.04 m/L sodium tetraborate (Sigma‐Aldrich) was added to the solution for ionic crosslinking. The as‐prepared hydrogel was denoted the PVA supporting hydrogel and molded into a 0.5 mm‐thick sheet.

Device Assembly and Sensing Performances

The piezoionic‐conductive device was assembled via stacking the printed GPMx‐1% piezoelectric hydrogel (10 × 10 × 5 mm3) within two thin layers of PVA supporting hydrogel (15 × 15 × 0.5 mm3) and connected with copper wires. Finally, two layers of PDMS membrane (SYLGARD 184, Sigma‐Aldrich) were used to prevent drying. For piezoelectric sensing, we first examined the open‐circuit voltage (V oc) under bending (∆L = 5 mm) deformation over 40 s. Moreover, we also studied the pressure‐responsive change in potential under varying stress (0–8 kPa), mimicking the human finger pressing/tapping force. The potential change was also recorded during knee bending in human volunteers for a practical demonstration. All measurements were recorded using a Keithley 2400 source meter and replicated three times (n = 3).

In Vitro Biocompatibility and Electrical Stimulation

Cell Viability Assays

For initial cytotoxicity evaluation of the GP and GPMx‐1%, the human bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs, ∼2.5 × 104 cells/100 µL/96‐well plate) were seeded onto the bulk hydrogel and cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's media (DMEM, Welgene) up to 7 days. At the desired time points, the cells were washed with 1 × PBS, and viability was assessed using the WST‐8 dye (Cellrix Viability Assay Kit, MediFab). We also tested the viability of murine macrophage (RAW 264.7, ∼2.5 × 104 cells/100 µL/96‐well) cells to assess immunocompatibility for up to 7 days. The GPMx‐1% was selected among all the composites owing to its greater conductivity, swelling, and mechanical properties. The plates without any hydrogel were considered the control group.

Electrical Stimulation and Cytotoxicity Test

To investigate the effect of sinusoidal EF stimulation on hBMSCs (∼4 × 104 cells/2 mL/6‐well plate) through GPMx‐1%, the cells were cultured onto the 3D‐printed hydrogels and stimulated with EFs (±250 mV, 50 kHz, dose: 20 min/day) up to 7 days. The 50 Hz frequency dose was determined to be safe for hBMSCs following a frequency‐dependent (±250 mV; 25, 50, and 100 kHz) viability test. After 7 days, the viability of hBMSCs was evaluated using a live/dead assay kit (Sigma‐Aldrich). The number of viable cells was quantified using ImageJ (v1.8.0, NIH) software.

Osteogenic Induction Study of hBMSCs

Osteogenic Induction Study

For the osteogenic induction study, the hBMSCs (∼4 × 104 cells/1 mL/24‐well plate) in GP or GPMx‐1% hydrogel were cultured in osteogenic induction media containing 50 µg/mL L‐ascorbic acid (Fisher Scientific), 100 nm dexamethasone (Sigma‐Aldrich), and 10 mm β‐glycerophosphate sodium (Sigma‐Aldrich) with EFs stimulation for 14 days.

Immunocytochemical (ICC) Staining

For ICC analysis, the hBMSCs (∼4 × 104 cells/1 mL/24‐well plate) cultured with the hydrogels were fixed with 3.7% paraformaldehyde (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton‐X 100 (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 10 min. After that, the samples were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (MP Biomedicals) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against Runx2, ALP, and OCN (1: 200, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐390351, #sc‐365765, and #sc‐73464) for 1 h (Table S6). Next, the hBMSCs were stained with FITC‐labelled secondary antibody (1:250, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐65218) for another 1 h. After antibody staining, the hBMSCs were incubated with DAPI (Sigma‐Aldrich) to stain the nuclei, and the cells were visualized with an inverted fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica) using appropriate filters. Images of hBMSCs with hydrogel were captured with a 20× objective lens and processed in ImageJ (v1.8, NIH). At least ten (n = 10) independent images from each group were captured for fluorescence intensity measurement.

Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR) Analysis

For qRT‐PCR analysis, the total RNA of hBMSCs (∼4 × 104 cells/1 mL/24‐well plate) was extracted using Trizol reagent (Thermo‐Fischer Scientific) as per the manufacturer's instructions. Following RNA purity measurement, 2 µg of total RNA was used to synthesize cDNA with the Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). The mRNA expression of osteogenic gene markers (e.g., Runx2, ALP, COLA1, OCN, OPN, IBSP, SMAD3, BMP2, BMP4, BMP7, and SPP1) was carried out using a thermocycler (CFX96 Maestro, Bio‐Rad), and the data were normalized to the housekeeping gene, glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). All the primers were purchased from a commercial manufacturer (Bioneer), and details are given in Table S7.

Mechanistic Study of GPMx + EF‐Assisted Metabolic Changes in hBMSCs

Transcriptomic (RNA Sequencing) Analysis

The transcriptomic changes in hBMSCs during early osteogenesis under the influence of EF stimulation were investigated through bulk RNA sequencing (RNA‐Seq) at day 14. For this, the hBMSCs in GP or GMPx‐1% hydrogel were cultured in osteogenic induction media with or without EFs stimulation for 14 days. After 14 days, the total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Thermo‐Fischer Scientific). The experiment was conducted in triplicate (n = 3) fashion. The hBMSC RNA with an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 9 was selected for RNA‐Seq. The total RNA was converted to cDNA, and the RNA‐Seq was performed using QuantiSeq‐3′ RNA‐Seq and Nova‐Seq 600 (PE100 bp, Illumina). The raw data were compared with the reference human genome, that is, hg19 (assembly ID: GCF_000001405.13, Homo sapiens), and normalized to log2FC (p < 0.05), followed by processing with the Excel‐based DEG analysis (ExDEGA, v5.001, ebiogen) software. The k‐means clustering and protein–protein interaction analysis were performed using the open‐source iDEP.93 (South Dakota State University) and STRING (v12.0, Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics) bioinformatics tool with standard R packages. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) mapper (v5.2, Kanehisa Laboratories) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA, MSigDB, 4.3.3, Broad Institute) were used to identify PPI enrichment across various biological processes. Additionally, to validate the mitochondrial biogenesis at the protein level, we performed ELISA using human Drp1 (#ab315308, Abcam) and Fis1 (#ELH‐FIS1‐1, RayBiotech) assay kit from the hBMSC lysate at day 14 according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Colorimetric Glycolysis/TCA Cycle Analysis

The mitochondrial bioenergetics pathways, that is, glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA)‐related metabolites and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) rate in cultured hBMSCs w/ or w/o EF stimulation were assessed using colorimetric assay kits as per the manufacturer's protocol. The glucose‐6‐phosphate (G‐6‐P) and glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G‐6‐PD) levels were examined using Human G6P and G6PD Assay Kits (AssayGenie). The pyruvate production was quantified using the ColorFluor Pyruvate Assay Kit (AssayGenie). The NADP+/NADH content was measured using the NADP+/NADPH Colorimetric Assay Kit (AssayGenie). The colorimetric OXPHOS and Complex I, II, and IV activity were measured using the OXPHOS Assay Kit (DOJINDO Laboratories). The relative fumarate content in hBMSCs was also assessed using the Fumarate Assay Kit (Sigma‐Aldrich) as per the manufacturer's guidelines. At least three (n = 3) replicates from each group were taken for statistical analysis.

OCR and MMP Measurements

The OCR in control or hydrogel‐treated hBMSCs, w/ or w/o EF stimulation, was examined using an extracellular OCR Kit (Abcam). The OCR, basal respiration, and ATP content were calculated with respect to the control group. The mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was measured using a mitochondrial MMP‐sensitive probe, TMRE (20 nm, Thermo‐Fischer Scientific), according to the manufacturer's guidelines. At least ten (n = 10) independent measurements were conducted for each group.

Ion Channel Inhibition Study

For this, the hBMSCs (∼1.5 × 105/mL/6‐well) were cultured with the fabricated hydrogels w/ or w/o EFs and ion channel inhibitors, that is, Nifedipine (10 µm, Sigma‐Aldrich) for 14 days. The control group received PBS only. At day 14, the cells were stained with primary antibodies against Ca2+ binding protein (CBP, 1:250, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐365522) and CACNA2D1 (1:250, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐271697). Next, the cells were stained with secondary antibodies and the cytoskeleton was stained with F‐actin probe 555 (Thermo‐Fischer Scientific). The images were captured using an inverted confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss) and processed with ImageJ software (n = 10). Additionally, to validate the ion channel activity at the protein level, we performed ELISA using the human CACNA2D1 (#EH‐4354, FineTest) assay kit at day 14 according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Intracellular Live Cell Ca2+ Imaging

The intracellular Ca2+ ion generation study was conducted to validate the transcriptomic, immunostaining, and qRT‐PCR data of hBMSCs. For this, the hBMSCs were exposed to EFs at 14 days of osteoinduction. At day 7, after 20 min of EF stimulation, the cells were immediately stained with the Fluo‐4 Direct Calcium Assay Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The relative fluorescence intensity was measured by recording fluorescence at 494/516 nm. At least thirty (n = 30) readings from each group were used for statistical analysis.

Fabrication of GPMx‐Based Electrochemical Sensor

Sensor Fabrication

The biosensor probe was fabricated via a series of modifications to the bare glassy carbon electrode (GCE). Briefly, the GCE was washed thoroughly with 70% ethanol and dried. Next, ∼1 mg/mL of GPMx‐1% hydrogel + 0.01 µL of Nafion 117 (ionic conductivity = 1.2 × 10−2/Ohm/cm, Sigma‐Aldrich) was drop‐casted onto the GCE and allowed to dry for 30 min. After that, the 10 µL of Anti‐ALP antibody (100 U/L, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc‐373737) was immobilized onto the CGE/GPMx‐1% matrix via the addition of 10 µL of N‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl)‐N’‐ethylcarbodiimide/N‐hydroxylsuccinamide (EDC/NHS, 2 mm/4 mm, 1:1 ratio, w/w, Sigma‐Aldrich) and allowed to dry for 30 min. The final biosensor was designated as CGE/GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP. The modified biosensor was either used immediately or stored at 4°C. The loading content of ALP was analyzed using an ELISA kit (#ELH‐ALKP, RayBiotech), and the data were calculated according to the following Equation (6): (6) Loadingcapacity=C0−Cs×VAU/cm2 where, C o, C s, V, and A are the ALP concentration before immobilization (100 U/L), remaining ALP concentration (18.22 U/L), solution volume (0.001 L), and electrode area (0.0314 cm2). Moreover, the binding affinity of ALP toward CGE/GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP sensor was estimated using a Langmuir isotherm curve and an ELISA assay, respectively. The dissociation constant (K D) was calculated according to the following Equation (7): (7) IF=Imax×FKD+F where, I F, F, I max are the current response of the bound ALP (µA), concentration of ALP (0–10 U/L), and maximum current response at saturation, respectively. The KD value was extracted from the slope of the linear region of the corresponding curve of 1/IF versus [F] using Origin Pro (v9.1) software.

Sensor Stability and Durability Tests

To test the electrolyte selectivity, we tested the oxidation‐reduction potential of the CGE/GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP biosensor in various biological fluids (e.g., PBS, FBS, and DMEM media) and with varying concentrations of ALP (0.25–10 U/mL). For the current stability and interference study, we used 0.1 mm of various amino acids (e.g., cysteine, arginine, and alanine), uric acid, and glutamic acid in the electrolyte solution (1 × PBS), and linear sweep voltammograms were recorded. To assess long‐term durability, we tested the sensor's electrical stability for up to 60 days by storing it at 4°C.

Label‐Free Detection of Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) In Vitro

For the ALP detection assay, we developed three different clinical in vitro models for electrochemical sensing. This includes the healthy, infected, and tumor models, respectively. The details of the electrochemical detection of ALP are schematically shown in Figure 7b of the main manuscript. Briefly, the hBMSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction media up to 14 days. At various time points (e.g., day 3, day 5, day 7, and day 14), the culture media containing secreted ALP were collected and stored at −20°C. In a typical process, 10 µL of cell culture media containing ALP was incubated with CGE/GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP at 37°C until dried properly. Finally, the CGE/GPMx‐1%/Anti‐ALP working electrode was dipped into the electrolyte solution (1 × PBS), and the electrochemical sensing was conducted using a three‐electrode system. The increasing anodic current was recorded at a scan rate of 100 mV/s over a potential range of −0.3–1.5 V.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using OriginPro (v9.1, OriginLab). Statistical significance was assessed at * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001 based on replicated data.
