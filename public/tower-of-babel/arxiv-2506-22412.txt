Shuang-Hong Li, Zhuo-Ran Huang, Wei Chen, Hong-Ying Jin

Abstract
We performed a comprehensive next-to-leading order (NLO) QCD sum rule analysis of the light hybrid meson with quantum numbers J P ​ C = 1 − ⁣ − J^{PC}=1^{--} . Utilizing both Laplace sum rules (LSR) and Gaussian sum rules (GSR), we investigate the two-point correlation function of the light hybrid interpolating current, including condensates up to dimension-8. A conservative range 2 − 2.5 ​ GeV 2-2.5\text{GeV} is obtained. Notably, the GSR yield mass predictions 2.27 ​ GeV 2.27\text{GeV} for the s ​ s ¯ ​ g s\bar{s}g and 2.19 ​ GeV 2.19\text{GeV} for u ​ u ¯ ​ g u\bar{u}g . This result is notably lower than many previous sum rule estimates and significantly reduces the discrepancy with predictions from lattice QCD and flux-tube models. This result revitalizes the interpretation of the ϕ ⁡ ( 2170 ) \phi(2170) resonance as a possible candidate for the light vector hybrid meson.

Introduction

Non-
q\bar{q}
mesons play an important role in modern hadron spectroscopy. Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) permits states in which gluons act as structural constituents. These hybrid mesons (
q\bar{q}g
) provide a testing ground for studying hadron structure, confinement, and the non-perturbative aspects of QCD.

While research on hybrids often focuses on states with exotic
J^{PC}
quantum numbers (e.g.,
0^{+-}
,
1^{-+}
,
2^{+-}
) that are forbidden for conventional
q\bar{q}
mesons, hybrids with conventional quantum numbers are also intriguing. For the
J^{PC}=1^{--}
hybrid, the flux-tube model predicts masses of
1.8-1.9\text{GeV}
for
u\bar{u}g
and
2.1-2.2\text{GeV}
for
s\bar{s}g

[
1
]
. In contrast, QCD sum rule calculations have consistently yielded heavier masses, with early estimates for the
s\bar{s}g
hybrid around
2.9\text{GeV}

[
2
,
3
]
and a more recent study providing a mass of
2.5\text{GeV}

[
4
]
. Lattice QCD results fall in between, placing the isovector hybrid at
2.2-2.3\text{GeV}
and its isoscalar partner at
2.4-2.5\text{GeV}

[
5
]
. Another lattice study noted a potential candidate near
2.1-2.3\text{GeV}
, but could not confirm its nature due to possible contamination from higher energy states
[
6
]
.

Experimentally, the most prominent candidate for a strangeonium-like
1^{--}
hybrid is the
\phi(2170)
resonance, also known as
Y(2175)
. This state was first observed by the BaBar collaboration
[
7
]
in the
e^{+}e^{-}\rightarrow\phi(1020)f_{0}(980)
process and subsequently confirmed by other experiments
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
through various production mechanisms and decay modes. The Particle Data Group (PDG)
[
12
]
currently lists its world-average mass as
2164\pm 6\text{MeV}
and its width as
106_{-18}^{+24}\text{MeV}
. It has been observed decaying mainly into final states containing a
s\bar{s}
pair, such as
\phi f_{0}(980)
,
\phi\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
, and
\phi\eta
.

The substantial discrepancy between the experimental mass and QCD sum rule predictions has led to controversy regarding the internal structure of
\phi(2170)
. The
\phi(2170)
has also been explored as a tetraquark state
[
13
,
14
]
, a
\Lambda\bar{\Lambda}
molecular state
[
15
,
16
]
, a
\phi K\bar{K}
system or
\phi f_{0}(980)
molecule
[
17
,
18
]
, or a conventional excited
s\bar{s}
meson
[
19
,
20
]
. While each of these alternative scenarios is supported by theoretical arguments and experimental observations, they also face difficulties in fully explaining the particle’s mass and decay properties.

In this paper, we perform a comprehensive next-to-leading order (NLO) analysis of the
1^{--}
hybrid meson. Using both Laplace sum rules (LSR)
[
21
]
and Gaussian sum rules (GSR)
[
22
]
, the conservative mass prediction is in the range of
2-2.5\text{GeV}
, while GSR fitting gives preciser mass predictions (with standard error):
2.27\pm 0.01\text{GeV}
for the
s\bar{s}g
hybrid and
2.19\pm 0.14\text{GeV}
for
u\bar{u}g
. This result significantly narrows the gap between QCD sum rule predictions and those from other theoretical approaches, and brings the predicted mass closer to that of the
\phi(2170)
, suggesting that the
1^{--}
light hybrid is a plausible interpretation within the QCD sum rule framework.

2.1
Laplace Sum Rules

The Laplace sum rule (LSR) method involves applying Borel transformation
[
23
,
21
,
26
]
to the correlation function. This procedure eliminates subtraction terms from the dispersion relation
[
21
,
26
]
and exponentially suppresses contributions from higher states and the continuum, thereby isolating the contribution of the lowest-lying resonance. Applying the Borel transformation to the moments of spectral functions yields:

\mathcal{M}^{n}(\tau,s_{0})=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{s_{0}}ds\ s^{n}e^{-\tau s}\,\text{Im}\Pi_{v}(s)=f^{2}m^{2n}e^{-\tau m^{2}},

(11)

where
\tau
is the Borel parameter; the renormalization group improved moments are obtained by setting
\mu^{2}\rightarrow 1/\tau

[
31
]
. The mass of the lowest resonance can then be extracted from the ratio of these moments:

\mathcal{R}^{n}(\tau,s_{0})=\frac{\mathcal{M}^{n+1}(\tau,s_{0})}{\mathcal{M}^{n}(\tau,s_{0})}=m^{2}.

(12)

2.2
Gaussian Sum Rules

The Gaussian sum rule (GSR)
[
22
,
32
]
enables a more detailed examination of the hadronic spectral function. The GSR employs a Gaussian kernel,
e^{-(s-t)^{2}/4\tau}
, which focuses on a specific region of the spectrum. By varying the center of this kernel, one can, in principle, probe different regions of the spectrum and study excited states. Moreover, the GSR utilizes a fitting procedure to extract hadron properties, which reduces the bias from the choice of the continuum threshold
s_{0}
, and yields a more robust prediction.

The GSR of the
1^{--}
hybrid is defined as:

G(\tau,s,s_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\tau}}\int_{0}^{s_{0}}dt\,e^{-\frac{(s-t)^{2}}{4\tau}}\frac{1}{\pi}\text{Im}\Pi_{v}(t).

(13a)

On the hadron side, applying the Gaussian transformation to the “pole
+
continuum” ansatz yields:

G_{h}(\tau,s)=\frac{f^{2}}{\sqrt{4\pi\tau}}e^{-\frac{(s-m^{2})^{2}}{4\tau}}.

The renormalization-group improved GSR is obtained by setting
\mu^{2}=\sqrt{\tau}

[

]
. We can normalize the
G(\tau,s,s_{0})
and
G_{h}(\tau,s)

\begin{split}\widetilde{G}(\tau,s,s_{0})=&\,\,\frac{G(\tau,s,s_{0})}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,G(\tau,s,s_{0})},\\
\widetilde{G}_{h}(\tau,s)\,\,\,\,=&\,\,\frac{G_{h}(\tau,s)}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,G_{h}(\tau,s)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\tau}}e^{-\frac{(s-m^{2})^{2}}{4\tau}},\end{split}

(13c)

where the resonance strength
f
from eq.
6
has been eliminated, which makes the mass estimation more robust.

It is necessary to keep the width (resolution) of Gaussian kernel finite. A very sharp Gaussian (
\tau\rightarrow 0
) would require knowing the spectral function exactly, which is beyond the current theoretical capacity. A sufficiently wide Gaussian kernel also ensures that a resonance in the spectrum can be approximated by a
\delta
-function. Following Ref.
[
3
]
, we choose
\tau=10\,\text{GeV}^{4}
; the results are not sensitive to the specific choice of
\tau
.

The mass of the lowest-lying resonance can then be estimated by minimizing the
\chi^{2}
:

\chi^{2}(\tau,s_{0})=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\big[\widetilde{G}(\tau,s_{i},s_{0})-\widetilde{G}_{h}(\tau,s_{i})\big]^{2}.

(14)

Here,

N=\frac{s_{\text{max}}-s_{\text{min}}}{\delta s}+1,\quad s_{i}=s_{\text{min}}+i\,\delta s,

(15)

and we choose

s_{\text{min}}=-15\text{GeV}^{2},\quad s_{\text{max}}=30\text{GeV}^{2},\quad\delta s=0.2\text{GeV}^{2}.

(16)

The
s_{\text{min}}
and
s_{\text{max}}
are chosen to be far from the peak of
\widetilde{G}(\tau,s,s_{0})
, while
\delta s
is chosen to be small enough to capture the detailed structure of
\widetilde{G}(\tau,s,s_{0})
.

To perform a detailed numerical analysis, we vary
s_{0}
and, for each value, minimize the
\chi^{2}
with respect to the mass
m
. This yields a minimized
\chi^{2}
as a function of
s_{0}
, providing a visualization of the solution’s dependence on the continuum threshold
s_{0}
, as presented in figure
8

s\bar{s}g

s_{0}
yields an extremely small
\chi^{2}

u\bar{u}g
is lower than
s\bar{s}g
by
\simeq 0.1\text{GeV}
. As summarized in table

2-2.3\,\text{GeV}
, agree with the experimentally measured mass of

3
Conclusion

In a summary, by performing a comprehensive NLO QCD sum rule analysis for the
J^{PC}=1^{--}
hybrid meson, a conservative mass prediction is in the range of
2-2.5\text{GeV}
is obtained, and GSR gives preciser mass predictions, with
2.27\pm 0.01\text{GeV}
for the
s\bar{s}g
and
2.19\pm 0.14\text{GeV}
for
u\bar{u}g
. This result is significantly lower than many previous LO sum rule calculations, and reduces the inconsistency between QCD sum rule results and those from lattice QCD (
2.2-2.5\,\text{GeV}

[
5
,
6
]
) or the flux-tube model (
2.1-2.2\,\text{GeV}

[
1
]
). This demonstrates the importance of NLO corrections in the QCD sum rule analysis of hybrid mesons.

This result also places the predicted mass of the
1^{--}
hybrid roughly agree with the mass of
\phi(2170)
resonance, implying that
\phi(2170)
could be a candidate of
1^{--}
hybrid, or could contain a large hybrid component if it is a mixed state. To reach a decisive conclusion, further work is required on both the theoretical and experimental sides.

\end{split}

(18)

where the corresponding diagrams are shown in figure

Several observations simplify the determination of the counterterms. The loop integrals in the first, third, fifth, and seventh diagrams in figure
11

G_{\mu\nu}\Psi_{f_{b}}
part of the operator in eq.
17

B.2
Evaluation of Dimension-8 Four-Quark Condensates

For the dimension-8 condensate, we adopt the notations:

D_{\rho}G_{\alpha\nu}=gt^{n}D_{\rho}^{nm}G^{m}_{\alpha\nu}\qquad\text{and}\quad G_{\alpha\nu}=gt^{n}G^{n}_{\alpha\nu}.

(31)

The last diagram in figure
14
contributes non-factorizable dimension-8 condensates such as
i\langle\bar{q}D_{\rho}{G_{\alpha}}^{\mu}G_{\mu\nu}\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\alpha}.\gamma^{\nu}q\rangle
and
i\langle\bar{q}G_{\mu\nu}D_{\rho}G^{\mu\nu}\gamma^{\rho}q\rangle
, which can be rewritten using the Bianchi identity and the equation of motion
[
36
]
. For example:

\begin{split}\langle\bar{q}G^{\mu\alpha}D_{\rho}G_{\alpha\mu}\gamma^{\rho}q\rangle&=\langle\bar{q}{G_{\mu}}^{\alpha}D_{\rho}G_{\alpha\nu}\frac{1}{2}(\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\mu}.\gamma^{\nu}+\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}.\gamma^{\mu})q\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\langle\bar{q}{G_{\mu}}^{\alpha}D_{\rho}G_{\alpha\nu}(2g^{\rho\mu}\gamma^{\nu}-\gamma^{\mu}.\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}+\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}.\gamma^{\mu})q\rangle\\
&=\langle\bar{q}D^{\rho}G_{\alpha\rho}G_{\alpha\nu}\gamma^{\nu}q\rangle+\langle\bar{q}\nabla^{\rho}(G_{\alpha\rho}G_{\alpha\nu})\gamma^{\nu}q\rangle\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\langle\bar{q}{G_{\mu}}^{\alpha}D_{\rho}G_{\alpha\nu}(-\gamma^{\mu}.\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}+\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}.\gamma^{\mu})q\rangle.\end{split}

(32)

Applying the Bianchi identity, the last line becomes:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{4}\langle\bar{q}{G_{\mu}}^{\alpha}\big[(D_{\rho}G_{\alpha\nu}+D_{\nu}G_{\alpha\rho})-D_{\alpha}G_{\nu\rho}\big](-\gamma^{\mu}.\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}+\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}.\gamma^{\mu})q\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\langle\bar{q}{G_{\mu}}^{\alpha}(D_{\rho}G_{\alpha\nu}+D_{\nu}G_{\alpha\rho})(-\gamma^{\mu}g^{\rho\nu}+g^{\rho\nu}\gamma^{\mu})q\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\langle\bar{q}{G_{\mu}}^{\alpha}D_{\alpha}G_{\nu\rho}(\gamma^{\mu}.\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}-\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}.\gamma^{\mu})q\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{4}\langle\bar{q}D^{\alpha}G_{\alpha\mu}G_{\nu\rho}(\gamma^{\mu}.\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}-\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}.\gamma^{\mu})q\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\langle\bar{q}\nabla^{\alpha}(G_{\alpha\mu}G_{\nu\rho})(\gamma^{\mu}.\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}-\gamma^{\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}.\gamma^{\mu})q\rangle,\end{split}

(33)

where
\nabla_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-igt^{n}A^{n}_{\mu}
and
D^{nm}_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\delta^{nm}+gf^{nlm}A^{l}_{\mu}
. The first term yields a factorizable dimension-8 condensate, while the second can be factorized into
m\langle\bar{q}q\rangle\langle GG\rangle
, which vanishes in the
m\rightarrow 0
limit. After a tedious calculation, the last diagram in figure
14
gives:

\begin{split}&\Big(g^{\mu\nu}+\frac{q^{\mu}q^{\nu}}{2q^{2}}\Big)\Big(-\frac{4i}{9q^{2}}\langle\bar{q}D^{\alpha}G_{\alpha\mu}G_{\nu\rho}\gamma^{\mu\nu\rho}q\rangle-\frac{16i}{9q^{2}}\langle\bar{q}D^{\alpha}G_{\alpha\rho}G_{\rho\mu}\gamma^{\mu}q\rangle\Big)\\
&=\Big(g^{\mu\nu}-\frac{q^{\mu}q^{\nu}}{q^{2}}\Big)\frac{100\pi}{81q^{2}}\alpha_{s}\langle\bar{q}q\rangle\langle\bar{q}Gq\rangle+\frac{q^{\mu}q^{\nu}}{q^{4}}\frac{50\pi}{27}\alpha_{s}\langle\bar{q}q\rangle\langle\bar{q}Gq\rangle,\end{split}

(34)

where
\gamma^{\mu\nu\rho}=\gamma^{[\rho}.\gamma^{\nu}.\gamma^{\rho]}
.

The condensate in the second to last diagram in figure
14
exhibits an ambiguity under factorization. Applying the equation of motion before factorization yields:

\Big(\frac{q^{\mu}q^{\nu}}{q^{2}}-g^{\mu\nu}\Big)\frac{73\pi}{81q^{2}}\alpha_{s}\langle\bar{q}q\rangle\langle\bar{q}Gq\rangle+\frac{q^{\mu}q^{\nu}}{q^{4}}\frac{23\pi}{9}\alpha_{s}\langle\bar{q}q\rangle\langle\bar{q}Gq\rangle.

(35)

In contrast, applying the vacuum saturation hypothesis first gives:

\Big(\frac{q^{\mu}q^{\nu}}{q^{2}}-g^{\mu\nu}\Big)\frac{8\pi}{9q^{2}}\alpha_{s}\langle\bar{q}q\rangle\langle\bar{q}Gq\rangle+\frac{q^{\mu}q^{\nu}}{q^{4}}\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha_{s}\langle\bar{q}q\rangle\langle\bar{q}Gq\rangle.

(36)

Fortunately, the difference is small and can be neglected. The result in eqs.
3
and
4
correspond to choosing eq.
35
.
