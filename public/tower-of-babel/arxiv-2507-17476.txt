MultiNRC: A Challenging and Native Multilingual Reasoning Evaluation Benchmark for LLMs
Alexander R. Fabbri, Diego Mares, Jorge Flores, Meher Mankikar

Abstract
Résumé Although recent Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown rapid improvement on reasoning benchmarks in English, the evaluation of such LLMs’ multilingual reasoning capability across diverse languages and cultural contexts remains limited. Existing multilingual reasoning benchmarks are typically constructed by translating existing English reasoning benchmarks, biasing these benchmarks towards reasoning problems with context in English language/cultures. In this work, we introduce the Multilingual Native Reasoning Challenge ( MultiNRC ), a benchmark designed to assess LLMs on more than 1,000 native, linguistic and culturally grounded reasoning questions written by native speakers in French, Spanish, and Chinese. MultiNRC covers four core reasoning categories : language-specific linguistic reasoning, wordplay & riddles, cultural/tradition reasoning, and math reasoning with cultural relevance. For cultural/tradition reasoning and math reasoning with cultural relevance, we also provide English equivalent translations of the multilingual questions by manual translation from native speakers fluent in English. This set of English equivalents can provide a direct comparison of LLM reasoning capacity in other languages vs. English on the same reasoning questions. We systematically evaluate current 14 leading LLMs covering most LLM families on MultiNRC and its English equivalent set. The results show that (1) current LLMs are still not good at native multilingual reasoning, with none scoring above 50% on MultiNRC ; (2) LLMs exhibit distinct strengths and weaknesses in handling linguistic, cultural, and logical reasoning tasks ; (3) Most models perform substantially better in math reasoning in English compared to in original languages (+10%), indicating persistent challenges with culturally grounded knowledge.

Introduction

Enhancing the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) is a critical area of ongoing research and development
(
OpenAI 2024
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
.
Consequently, thorough and diverse reasoning evaluation benchmarks in English have been developed to assess LLMs’ improved reasoning capabilities.
These evaluation datasets cover a variety of reasoning tasks, including commonsense reasoning
(
Zellers et al. 2019
;
Sakaguchi et al. 2020
;
Clark et al. 2018
)
, language-understanding reasoning in English
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, STEM reasoning
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
;
Zhong et al. 2024
;
Hendrycks et al.
)
, and logical reasoning
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2020
;
Geva et al. 2021
)
.
These benchmarks play a critical role in guiding the evolution of the reasoning capacity of LLMs.

While a large number of these benchmarks have been developed in English, the landscape of multilingual reasoning benchmarks is less developed, comprehensive, and equitable.
Existing multilingual reasoning benchmarks are largely created by translating existing English reasoning benchmarks (automatically, manually, or hybrid) into target languages
(
Shi et al. 2023
;
Patel et al. 2021
)
.
Benchmarks created by directly translating English-centric content may reflect the cultural framing and linguistic characteristic of English, which are not globally representative.
As a result, reasoning tasks that require native linguistic or cultural understanding are often missing, leaving gaps in the evaluation of non-English language reasoning abilities.
Consequently, we are still largely unaware of how state-of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs perform on genuinely native multilingual reasoning tasks that demand both linguistic diversity and cultural or contextual depth.

Motivated by this gap, we develop the Multilingual Native Reasoning Challenge (MultiNRC), a new multilingual evaluation benchmark that contains native and challenging reasoning questions of the targeted language, to assess LLMs’ reasoning capability under a natural and native context.
Specifically, MultiNRC contains four categories of reasoning questions : language-specific linguistic reasoning, wordplay riddles, cultural/tradition reasoning, and finally mathematical reasoning with cultural relevance.
We first release MultiNRC in French, Spanish, and Chinese ; Table
1
shows examples of the four categories in different languages.
To build MultiNRC, we recruit native speakers of each language to create challenging reasoning questions and ground-truth final answers according to the definitions of the four categories above.
We only accept reasoning questions that cause at least
3
out of
5
SOTA LLMs to fail, and we facilitate MultiNRC with automatic evaluation with LLM-as-a-judge, for fast and accurate model assessments.
Automatic evaluation is made possible by only including reasoning questions with an objective and short ground-truth final answer ; we find that our automatic evaluation has more than 95% alignment with human judgment on MultiNRC.

We thoroughly evaluate current SOTA LLMs on MultiNRC. We find that the best performing LLM, o3-pro
(
OpenAI 2025
)
released in April 2025, achieves only 49% accuracy according to automatic evaluation with respect to our ground-truth final answers, indicating the high difficulty level of MultiNRC for SOTA models.
We find that o3 variants along with Gemini-2.5-Pro (June 2025)
(
Comanici et al. 2025
)
greatly outperform other models, and that LLMs also rank differently across MultiNRC reasoning categories, suggesting that the taxonomy of MultiNRC provides a more informative view to guide LLM performance improvement.
For example, Gemini-2.5-Pro ranks third on average accuracy among models but ranks first in math reasoning with cultural relevance.

We also augment MultiNRC with English equivalent translations of the non-English prompts by having our annotators translate the prompts into English while retaining the structure and solvability of the original question.
In this English equivalent set, we only translate cultural reasoning and math categories. This is because linguistic and wordplay categories cannot be translated into reasonable equivalents ; for example, a play on words in French translated to English would no longer make sense.
This English-translated evaluation set can provide a direct comparison of LLM reasoning capability in other languages vs. English on same reasoning questions.
We find that LLMs perform much better on the resulting set of English math reasoning, achieving 10% higher accuracy on average, compared to their performance on the original multilingual math reasoning with cultural relevance.
Our case study shows that the reason for this phenomenon is likely that LLMs are able to better retrieve cultural context/knowledge in English for math problems, compared to retrieving such cultural context/knowledge in its original language.
However, in the category of cultural reasoning, there is no large performance difference in English equivalent prompts compared to the original multilingual prompts.
Our case study shows that the cultural/tradition context in this category is much more specific and nuanced compared to the math category, and thus often absent in both the LLM knowledge base of English or original language, leading to no performance improvement on English-equivalent prompts.

Our contributions are as follows. First, we introduce MultiNRC, a native, challenging multilingual reasoning benchmark authored by native speakers to cover reasoning questions with linguistic and cultural nuances.
Second, we systematically evaluate 14 strong LLMs in French, Spanish, and Chinese, on both native questions and English translations, to understand the true multilingual reasoning capabilities of models.
Third, we perform extensive ablation studies across languages and reasoning categories, as well as the effect of model translation and reasoning in English to disentangle the impact of language, translation, and cultural context on model reasoning.
Our analyses highlight the underlying cultural and contextual knowledge required to answer MultiNRC questions.
Finally, we release the dataset to facilitate further research on multilingual and culturally informed evaluation.

Related Work

Reasoning benchmarks in English.
A wide range of benchmarks have been developed to evaluate the reasoning capabilities of LLMs across commonsense, logical, and STEM reasoning domains.
Commonsense reasoning benchmarks aim to evaluate an LLM’s ability to apply everyday knowledge and logical inference to understand and interact with the world in a human-like way. HellaSwag
(
Zellers et al. 2019
)
and WinoGrande
(
Sakaguchi et al. 2020
)
are two widely-adopted examples.
Logical and deductive reasoning benchmarks, such as ReClor
(
Yu et al. 2020
)
and StrategyQA
(
Geva et al. 2021
)
, and ARC-AGI
(
Chollet et al. 2024
)
, are designed to evaluate an LLM’s capacity to apply formal rules of logic and to perform deductive, inductive, or abductive inference given a set of premises.
To comprehensively assess both general knowledge and reasoning, multitask benchmarks like MMLU-Pro
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
have emerged, expanding on prior efforts in MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
by including more rigorous reasoning components and a broader coverage of academic subjects, rather than just factual recall.
STEM and math reasoning benchmarks, such as AIME
(
Mathematical Association of America 2024
)
and MATH
(
Hendrycks et al.
)
, are specifically designed to assess an LLM’s proficiency in solving complex, multi-step mathematical problems.
Distinct from prior benchmarks, we focus on evaluating reasoning over linguistic, wordplay, cultural/tradition, and math questions, each written by native speakers to capture local language and cultural nuance.

Multilingual reasoning benchmarks.
Compared to LLM reasoning evaluation in English, multilingual reasoning benchmarks are less developed. A majority of them are translated from existing English reasoning benchmarks
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
.
Among them, MMLU-ProX
(
Xuan et al. 2025
)
, is built by translating the existing English benchmark, MMLU-Pro
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, to evaluate advanced reasoning across multiple languages. The initial translation of the English questions into the other 28 languages was performed using multiple LLMs, followed by an expert review phase.
Similarly, MGSM
(
Shi et al. 2023
)
and BenchMAX
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
are translated from GSM8K
(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
and GPQA
(
Rein et al.
)
.
BenchMAX is built with an initial machine translation and a meticulous post-editing process in which each sample was independently reviewed by three distinct native-language annotators.
MGSM was created by manually translating 250 problems selected from the English GSM8K dataset.
An approach to avoid translating existing English benchmarks is by using exam questions in non-English languages
(
Hardalov et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
. However, such benchmarks, with M3Exam as an example, have mostly multichoice questions that assess knowledge instead of reasoning and are too easy for SOTA models, as suggested by their reported GPT-4 results in 2023
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
.
Another example of data collected from native languages is MultiLoko
Hupkes & Bogoychev 2025
, which collects SQuAD-style
Rajpurkar et al. 2016
questions from the most popular Wikipedia pages for each language, rated for their local relevance to the language. While this work also analyzes model performance on English equivalents, the focus of our work is on reasoning questions over linguistic and cultural knowledge and nuance, and not just factual knowledge.

Evaluation Benchmark Overview

Below we describe the Multilingual Native Reasoning Challenge (MultiNRC) benchmark, detailing our choice of reasoning categories, English equivalent set of multilingual prompts, as well as dataset construction methodology and statistics.

Language-specific Linguistic Reasoning
questions are based on grammatical rules, honorifics, or language-specific conventions that only exist in the target language but not English. Such questions require reasoning about language structure or usage.
Examples under this category include word formation problems, relation inference based on language conventions, etc.
We enforce that the questions should require multi-step reasoning, instead of mere linguistic feature identification without reasoning.
In the French example in Table
1
, the question requires recognizing that the noun délice is masculine in the singular and feminine in the plural, an uncommon grammatical characteristic not present in English.

Wordplay & Riddles
contain puzzles that rely on the clever and ambiguous use of the target language, often involving multiple meanings, homophones, or puns.
Since such reasoning questions usually use very language-specific homophones or puns, there are no English translations that can provide the exact meaning of such questions.
Our evaluations later show that this category is one of the most difficult for LLMs.
In the Chinese example shown in Table
1
, the question requires the respondent to use a Chengyu (Ancient Chinese idiom) involving a pun or homophone as an answer to a playful riddle, thus demanding clever reasoning.

Cultural/Tradition Reasoning
questions require reasoning through timelines, behaviors, or customs derived from local traditions, holidays, or ceremonies.
Similar to other categories, we enforce the assessment of LLM reasoning capabilities and exclude purely factual questions.
In the Spanish example in the table, the question requires identifying the date of a local holiday (Día de la Candelaria) and reasoning about travel dates to ensure two full weekends are spent at the destination, demonstrating an understanding of local traditions and applying multi-step temporal reasoning.

Math Reasoning with Cultural Relevance
questions involve calculations based on cultural-specific counting systems, calendars, currencies, or numerical phrasing. As above, it must require at least 1 reasoning step based on a culturally specific element, and we do not allow pure arithmetic or logic without cultural grounding.
In the French example in the table, the question requires the application of culturally specific knowledge about the ’viager’ real estate system and historical events (such as the date of the Cannes Film Festival) to determine the financial break-even point for a property purchase, thereby testing mathematical reasoning within a local context.

3.2
English Equivalent Prompts

In order to test the reasoning gap in English vs. non-English languages, we further ask annotators to translate the native prompts and ground-truth final answers into English.
Annotators judge the validity of machine-translated prompts from 5 SOTA models and choose the most relevant machine-translated prompt from the above pipeline as a base for translation, or translate from scratch as needed.
Our annotators focus on translating the logic, not literal words, ensuring that the English translation is also culturally grounded and requires similar reasoning.
We only produce English translations for the Cultural/Tradition Reasoning and Math Reasoning categories.
The translated English version retains the structure and solvability of the original, with the translator trying to translate cultural or linguistic characteristics into English.
Examples of English translated prompts and answers are shown in the Appendix in Table
8
.
We found that the categories of Language-specific Linguistic Reasoning and Wordplay & Riddles could not be reliably translated into English, as they often rely on linguistic phenomena that do not translate or exist in English. Thus, we exclude these categories from the English equivalent set.

3.3
Data Collection/Review and Statistics

To collect data for MultiNRC, native speakers start by writing reasoning prompts according to the definition of one of the four categories mentioned above.
For each prompt, the annotator also writes a ground-truth final answer (GTFA), consisting of either a single word or a short phrase/sentence.
We then pull model responses from five top models at the time of creation across model providers : Google’s Gemini-2.5-pro
(
Comanici et al. 2025
)
, o4-mini
(
OpenAI 2025
)
, Claude 3.7-Sonnet
(
Anthropic 2025
)
, Deepseek R1
(
DeepSeek-AI 2025
)
, and Llama-4 Maverick
(
AI 2024
)
.
The 5 model responses are presented to annotators in a random order.
We only keep the reasoning questions that 3 or more of the 5 models fail to correctly answer.

After the raw data is collected, we have two native-speaker reviewer layers to assess the quality of the data from different aspects.
The first native-speaker reviewer layer is to assess the quality of the prompt and GTFA pairs. At this layer we would assess both the GTFA’s accuracy and the prompt’s reasonableness and alignment with category definition.
The second native-speaker reviewer layer assesses whether there are truly 3 out of 5 SOTA model failures, given the verified prompt and GTFA pairs. This is to verify the difficulty level of the collected data.
We show the statistics of the final dataset after these collection/review steps in Table
2
.
We collect roughly the same amount of examples across categories and languages, more than the 250 per-language count of the popular MGSM dataset
(
Shi et al. 2023
)
.
We also annotate subcategories of the cultural and linguistic nuances present in the prompts. The results of this analysis are found in the Appendix in Table
9
.

Experimental Settings

Reasoning Settings

We perform experiments in the following settings :
1)
Original
in which the model is given the original multilingual question, and prompted to provide an explanation and a short final answer in the native language of the question.
2)
En
in which the model answers human-translated English equivalent questions (illustrated in Section 3.2) in English. By comparing
En
vs
Original
, we can see the difference in model’s abilities to understand and reason about local linguistic and cultural nuance in the local language versus English.
Hupkes & Bogoychev 2025
refer to this as the Mother Tongue Effect.
3)
EnT/R
in which the model is prompted to produce an English translation of the native prompt by itself, reason/explain in English and return the native final answer.
4)
EnR
in which the model is asked to reason in English on the native prompt and return the native language response.
The results of these last two settings are found in Appendix
A.2
.

Automatic Evaluation

As described above, we collect human judgment of the correctness of model responses for 5 SOTA models on our benchmark. In order to scale annotations, we leverage an LLM judge that takes the prompt, GTFA, and model response and produces a correct/not correct judgment as well as reasoning behind the judgment. We found that GPT-4.1
(
OpenAI 2024
)
as the judge results in an over 95% agreement with our human judgments and a Scott’s Pi
(
Scott 1955
)
of 0.88, indicating strong reliability of our metric, and thus we utilize this automatic judge for all reported results.
Note that all experimental results use pass@1.

We test a range of recent LLMs including Google’s Gemini-2.5-pro

5
Results and Analysis

The main results on MultiNRC are found in Table
3
, showing model performance in the Original setting, along with 95% confidence intervals.
We note that o3 variants along with Gemini-2.5-pro-preview-06-05 score clearly better than any other model.
However, no model surpasses 50% accuracy on the dataset, underscoring the difficulty of MultiNRC and the substantial performance gaps even among top models.
We also find trends of progress in recent models ; for example, Deepseek-R1-0528 improves considerably over the initial Deepseek-R1, and GPT-4.1 outperforms GPT-4o. Furthermore, thinking model variants (e.g., Claude-4-Opus-20250514-thinking) consistently show better performance within the same family, indicating the benefit of enhanced reasoning effort.

In Table
4
, we break down model performance on MultiNRC in the Original setting by both category and language.
Math reasoning questions are the most challenging overall, with an average accuracy of just 23.3% across all questions, and especially low scores in Spanish.
In contrast, linguistic and cultural reasoning tasks see higher scores, with average accuracies of 34.8% and 31.1%.
Wordplay remains a consistently difficult category for all languages, although the models score better in French for this category.
Spanish stands out as the most challenging language across categories, particularly for math and wordplay.
Some models exhibit distinct strengths in particular languages and categories, as seen in Deepseek-R1-05-28’s higher rank on Chinese Math and Linguistic categories.
These results illustrate that model performance is affected not only by the reasoning category itself but also by the interaction between languages and reasoning categories.
In Appendix
A.2
, we provide an additional comparison of the gap between the top and worst performing language for each model.

Within categories, we see large performance gaps between models. For example, although top-performing models generally struggle on Math overall, Gemini performs much better than others due to its strong results in Spanish Math questions. Similarly, o3-pro demonstrates a significant improvement in Wordplay, especially French Wordplay (75.9%).
As a result, models often rank differently across MultiNRC reasoning categories and languages.
These results suggest that the taxonomy of MultiNRC provides a more informative view to guide LLM performance improvements.

These results suggest that for certain languages and categories, language-specific strengths and weaknesses remain a significant factor in LLM performance.

For math reasoning, we observe a substantial improvement when models are given English equivalents, with especially pronounced gains for Spanish (+19.1) and Chinese (+12.5).
Our case studies highlight that LLMs are able to surface the correct background knowledge and relevant values more reliably for the math problems presented in English, leading to higher accuracy on these tasks.
In contrast, for cultural reasoning, the difference is negligible on average.
This suggests that while translation to English can unlock knowledge for certain tasks such as math reasoning questions, performance improvement on culturally grounded tasks remains limited, likely due to the lack of exposure of the models to specific cultural knowledge, regardless of language.

6
Discussion and Limitations

We analyze the errors of o3-2025-04-high and Gemini models and include representative examples in Table
6
.
We observe that 66% of the problems are either solved correctly or incorrectly by both models.
Among the problems that the two models perform differently on, our error analysis suggests that o3 performs better on French Wordplay examples, where Gemini is more likely to miss cultural nuance or wordplay (yielding literal or surface-level responses).
However, Gemini performs better on Chinese cultural prompts.
The Spanish example in Table
6
is an example in which both models fail.
Both models used incorrect values for the codo de ribera unit, leading to a difference in calculating the number of porrones required.
In the French example shown, Gemini fails to recognize the homophonic pun between ”marre tôt” and ”marteau,” while o3 correctly identifies and explains this wordplay. In the Chinese example, o3 undercounts the days between festivals by one.

These case studies highlight the sensitivity of large language models to linguistic and cultural nuances, especially when reasoning in languages other than English.
In our work, we do not explore models designed or finetuned specifically to be multilingual, and we leave an evaluation of these models on the benchmark for future work.
We selected French, Spanish, and Chinese as initial languages given the large number of global speakers and the authors’ familiarity with these languages.
To draw wider conclusions about multilingual performance, it will be be important to include additional languages in future work.
We expect that extending this benchmark to low-resource languages would magnify the struggle of current models in dealing with local knowledge, linguistic phenomena, and complex reasoning.
Furthermore, our analysis does not explicitly consider the effect of regional or dialectal variation, which is present in languages like Spanish and Chinese, and future work could examine how these differences impact model performance.
This underscores the need for both more diverse training data and tailored model evaluation to ensure robust, equitable progress in multilingual LLMs.

7
Conclusion

We introduce MultiNRC, a challenging benchmark designed to evaluate the multilingual abilities of LLMs through linguistically and culturally-nuanced reasoning questions.
By constructing diverse native reasoning questions in French, Spanish, and Chinese, we highlight ongoing challenges for LLMs when faced with linguistically and culturally nuanced reasoning tasks across multiple languages.
Our evaluation shows that while English translation can boost performance on some tasks, substantial gaps remain for many culturally and linguistically nuanced questions.
We hope that MultiNRC will serve as a robust testbed for future advances in multilingual LLM development, and we release our dataset to further research in this direction.
