Abstract
. The back-and-forth relations ℳ ≤ α 𝒩 \mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N} are central to computable structure theory and countable model theory. It is well-known that the relation { ( ℳ , 𝒩 ) : ℳ ≤ α 𝒩 } \{(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}):\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\} is (lightface) Π 2 ​ α 0 \Pi^{0}_{2\alpha} . We show that this is optimal as the set is 𝚷 2 ​ α 0 \mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2\alpha} -complete. We are also interested in the one-sided relations { 𝒩 : ℳ ≤ α 𝒩 } \{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\} and { 𝒩 : ℳ ≥ α 𝒩 } \{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{M}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\} for a fixed ℳ \mathcal{M} , measuring the Π α \Pi_{\alpha} and Σ α \Sigma_{\alpha} types of ℳ \mathcal{M} . We show that these sets are always 𝚷 α + 2 0 \mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+2} and 𝚷 α + 3 0 \mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+3} respectively, and that for most α \alpha there are structures ℳ \mathcal{M} for which these relations are complete at that level. In particular, there are structures ℳ \mathcal{M} such that there is no Π α \Pi_{\alpha} (or even OPEN Π α + 1 ) \Pi_{\alpha+1}) sentence φ \varphi such that 𝒩 ⊧ φ ⟺ ℳ ≤ α 𝒩 \mathcal{N}\models\varphi\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N} . This is unfortunate as not all Π α + 2 \Pi_{\alpha+2} sentences are preserved under ≤ α \leq_{\alpha} . We define a new hierarchy of syntactic complexity closely related to the back-and-forth game, which can both define the back-and-forth types as well as be preserved by them. These hierarchies of formulas have already been useful in certain Henkin constructions, one of which we give in this paper, and another previously used by Gonzalez and Harrison-Trainor to show that every Π α \Pi_{\alpha} theory of linear orders has a model with Scott rank at most α + 3 \alpha+3 .

Introduction

Originally used to prove that any two countable dense linear orders without endpoints are isomorphic, the back-and-forth method is a classical tool in mathematical logic dating back over one hundred years. A general viewpoint, beginning with Ehrenfeucht
[
8
]
and Fraïssé
[
9
]
, is that by playing back-and-forth games between two first-order structures
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
we can capture which sets of formulas they agree on. Generally two players,
Spoiler
and
Duplicator
, take turns playing elements from the two structures, with
Duplicator
trying to maintain atomic facts between the elements in
\mathcal{M}
and the elements in
\mathcal{N}
. In the Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game most commonly used in model theory, both players play single elements, and (in a finite language) the second player wins the game of length
n
if and only if the two structures
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
satisfy the same sentences with quantifier depth
n
. Other versions, such as pebble games, can be used for finite variable logics.

In this paper, we work with the back-and-forth game that corresponds to infinitary logic, and our main goal is to understand the complexity of deciding which player has a winning strategy. The key features of this particular back-and-forth game are that (a) on their turn, players play tuples of arbitrary size with the size chosen by
Spoiler
, (b)
Spoiler
must alternate between playing in
\mathcal{M}
and playing in
\mathcal{N}
, with
Duplicator
alternating between playing in
\mathcal{N}
and playing in
\mathcal{M}
, and (c) the games are played over a possibly infinite ordinal clock.

This back-and-forth game yields the back-and-forth relations. (For simplicity, we assume that all languages in this paper are relational.)

Our main motivation has to do with Scott’s isomorphism theorem. Given a countable

\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{N}.

Must

have a
\Pi_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence? A

Scott sentence?

2.
The definability of the back-and-forth relations in the
\Sigma
and
\Pi
hierarchies

We begin by demonstrating the lower bound of
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2n}
in the symmetrical case of the theorem for finite values.
In other words, we demonstrate that the set
\{(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}):\mathcal{M}\leq_{n}\mathcal{M}\}
is sometimes as difficult to define as possible.
Note that
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2n}
matches the classical upper bound, as the definition of the back-and-forth game gives a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2n}
description of the back-and-forth relations.
(We expand on this point regarding the classical upper bound in the proof below.)

2.1
.

For any language
\mathcal{L}
containing a relation symbol of arity
\geq 2
, the set

\{(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}):\mathcal{M}\leq_{n}\mathcal{N}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2n}
-complete in
\Mod(\mathcal{L})\times\Mod(\mathcal{L})
.

Proof.

Any language containing a relation symbol of arity
\geq 2
can simulate, via effective bi-interpretation, any other language. Thus, in showing completeness we can work with whichever language is most useful.

The fact that this set is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2n}
has been observed previously (see, e.g., Lemma VI.14 in
[
24
]
).
To be a bit more explicit, the
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2n}
definition follows directly from the definition of the back-and-forth relations.
In the product space,
\Mod(\mathcal{L})\times\Mod(\mathcal{L})
, a universal quantifier over the elements of the first coordinate (or second coordinate) naturally corresponds to a countable intersection of sets.
Dually, an existential quantifier naturally corresponds to a countable union.
The definition of
\leq_{n}
alternates quantifiers (some over the first structure, some over the second structure)
2n
-times and begins with a universal quantifier, so it gives a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2n}
definition.

We focus the rest of the proof on showing that the set is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2n}
-hard.
We will give an inductive construction but we need a stronger statement to carry out the induction. For
n\geq 2
, given a
\Pi^{0}_{2n-2}
set
U\subseteq 2^{\omega}\times\omega
and
x\in 2^{\omega}
we will construct structures
\mathcal{A}^{x,U}
and
\mathcal{B}^{x,U}_{i}
such that

\theenumviii

if
(x,i)\in U
then
\mathcal{A}^{x,U}\geq_{n}\mathcal{B}^{x,U}_{i}
, and

\theenumviii

if
(x,i)\notin U
then
\mathcal{B}^{x,U}_{i}\ngeq_{n-1}\mathcal{A}^{x,U}
.

For a given value of
n
, the language of these structures will consist of equivalence relations
\mathbb{E}_{n},\ldots,\mathbb{E}_{3}
and unary relations
R^{n^{\prime}}_{i,m}
for
2\leq n^{\prime}\leq n
and
i,m\in\omega
. The equivalence relation
\mathbb{E}_{i}
will refine
\mathbb{E}_{j}
if
i<j
. Inductively, in constructing these structures for
n+1
, we may assume that we know how to construct them for
n
.

Our base case is
n=2
.
We assume that we work relative to an oracle that makes a given
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2}
set
\Pi^{0}_{2}
and note that our proof relativizes; we make a similar assumption as needed throughout this proof.
Given a
\Pi^{0}_{2}
set
V\subseteq 2^{\omega}\times\omega
,
x\in 2^{\omega}
, and
k\in\omega
we must construct structures
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}
and
\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}
with the desired properties. Write

(x,i)\in V\Longleftrightarrow\forall m\exists j\;(x,i,m,j)\in U,

where
U
is computable.
We will have unary relations
R^{2}_{i,m}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}
have infinitely many elements
a^{s}_{i^{\prime},m,j}
.
The elements
a^{s}_{i^{\prime},m,j}
will behave the same for fixed values of
i^{\prime},m,j
.
In other words, each element is infinitely replicated by the index
s
.
Put
a^{s}_{i^{\prime},m,j}\in R^{2}_{i^{\prime},m}

. Let
\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}
have infinitely many elements
b^{s}_{i^{\prime},m,j}
as well as elements
b_{m}^{*}
.
Put
b^{s}_{i^{\prime},m,j}\in R^{2}_{i^{\prime},m}

.
Informally,
\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}
is just the same as
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}
except for the fact that there is an additional element added to
R^{2}_{i,m}
for each
m
.
Then:

item*

If
(x,i)\in V
, then we can check that
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}\cong\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}
and hence
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}\geq_{2}\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}
. We see that
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}
embeds into
\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}
. The elements not in the image are the

m

j_{m}

b_{m}^{*}\leftrightarrow a^{0}_{i,m,j_{m}}
and

item*

If
(x,i)\notin V
then there is
m
such that for all
j
we have
(x,i,m,j)\notin U
. Note that in
\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}
there is always an element
b_{m}^{*}
satisfying
R^{2}_{i,m}
.
In contrast, in
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}

must have

and
m^{\prime}=m

.
This cannot happen by the choice of

which is not true in

\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}\ngeq_{1}\mathcal{A}^{x,V}
.

This finishes the case of

.

Now, suppose that we have a construction for
n
, and we will give a construction for
n+1
. Let

be a
\Pi^{0}_{2n}
set, say

x\in V\Longleftrightarrow\forall m\exists j\;(x,i,m,j)\in U

where

\Pi^{0}_{2n-2}

By the inductive hypothesis, we have structures
\mathcal{A}^{x,U_{i,m}}
and
\mathcal{B}^{x,U_{i,m}}_{j}
such that

\theenumix

if
(x,j)\in U_{i,m}
then
\mathcal{A}^{x,U_{i,m}}\geq_{n}\mathcal{B}^{x,U_{i,m}}_{j}
, and

\theenumix

if
(x,j)\notin U_{i,m}
then
\mathcal{B}^{x,U_{i,m}}_{j}\ngeq_{n-1}\mathcal{A}^{x,U_{i,m}}
.

To be explicit, the above definitions and hypotheses immediately give that

(x,i)\in V\iff\forall m\exists j~(x,j)\in U_{i,m}.

Therefore,

(x,i)\in V\implies\forall m\exists j~\mathcal{A}^{x,U_{i,m}}\geq_{n}\mathcal{B}^{x,U_{i,m}}_{j}\text{ and }(x,i)\not\in V\implies\exists m\forall j~\mathcal{A}^{x,U_{i,m}}\nleq_{n-1}\mathcal{B}^{x,U_{i,m}}_{j}.

Define
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}
and
\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}
as follows:

item·

\mathcal{A}^{x,V}
is split into sorts indexed by

by the relations

.
Each sort is, in turn, split into equivalence classes with respect to the equivalence relation

with each equivalence class being the domain of one of the structures from the previous inductive case.
The
(i,m)

for each

\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}

Again, each sort is split into equivalence classes with respect to the equivalence relation

Informally,
\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}

\mathcal{A}^{x,V}

\mathcal{A}^{x,U_{i,m}}

.
We now demonstrate that the
\mathcal{A}^{x,V}
and
\mathcal{B}^{x,V}_{i}

Sublemma 2.1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}

\mathcal{B}_{i}

\mathcal{L}

:

()()

For each
k,\ell

a_{k}\mathbb{E}a_{\ell}

()()

Given (A), we may reorder

.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}

.

3.
Upper bounds for the definability of the
\alpha
-types of structures

The remaining statements of Theorem
1.3
are concerned with the case where we fix a structure
\mathcal{M}
and consider whether some other structure
\mathcal{N}
satisfies
\mathcal{N}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}
or
\mathcal{N}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}
.
In this section, we will prove the needed upper bounds on the complexities of the sets
\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\}
and
\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{M}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\}
. We begin with general bounds which hold for most
\alpha
, followed by certain exceptional cases where we can prove better bounds for small
\alpha
or
\alpha
near limit ordinals.
All of these results are optimal; we provide the corresponding proofs of the lower bounds in future sections.

3.1.
General bounds

The following two lemmas provide general bounds for the
\alpha
-types of structures that are superior to the
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2\alpha}
bounds provided by the previous analysis.

3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a fixed countable structure and let
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
. For each
\alpha<\omega_{1}
there is a
\Pi_{\alpha+2}
formula
\varphi

such that
\mathcal{N}\models\varphi(\bar{b})
if and only if
(\mathcal{N},\bar{b})\geq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
. Hence the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+2}
.

Proof.

The classical analysis of the back-and-forth relations (see, e.g., Lemma VI.14 in
[
24
]
) gives several base cases for this claim.
The formulas given there are
\Pi_{2\alpha}
, and for
\alpha=1
or
2
and
\alpha=\lambda
or
\lambda+1
for a limit level
\lambda
we have
2\alpha\leq\alpha+2
and so the claim is already shown.
(In fact, for
\alpha=1
, there is even a
\Pi_{1}
formula satisfying the lemma.)

We now complete the proof by proving the following inductive claim.

Claim
.

If for every
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
there is a formula
\varphi^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{M},\bar{a}}(\bar{x})
satisfying the claim for
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
then for every
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
there is a formula
\varphi^{\alpha+2}_{\mathcal{M},\bar{a}}(\bar{x})
satisfying the claim for
\alpha+2
.

Proof.

Suppose that for each
\mathcal{M}
and
\bar{q}\in\mathcal{M}
there is a
\Pi_{\alpha+2}
formula
\varphi^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{M},\bar{q}}(\bar{x})

such that
\mathcal{N}\models\varphi(\bar{p})
if and only if
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p})\geq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},\bar{q})
.
Given
\mathcal{M}
and
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
we must show that there is a a
\Pi_{\alpha+4}
formula
\varphi(\bar{x})

such that
\mathcal{N}\models\varphi(\bar{p})
if and only if
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p})\geq_{\alpha+2}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
.
For each
\bar{b}\in\mathcal{M}
, there is a
\Pi_{\alpha}
formula
\theta_{\bar{b}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
defining the
\Pi_{\alpha}
type of
\bar{a}\bar{b}
in
\mathcal{M}
, i.e., such that
\mathcal{M}\models\theta_{\bar{b}}(\bar{a},\bar{c})
if and only if
(\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{c})\geq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{b})
(see
[
24
]
Lemma II.62). Let

\psi(\bar{x}):=\bigdoublewedge_{\ell}\forall y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell}\bigdoublewedge_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\theta_{\bar{b}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\longrightarrow\varphi^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{b}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\right)

and let

\chi(\bar{x}):=\bigdoublewedge_{k}\forall y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\bigdoublevee_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\in\mathcal{M}}\bigdoublewedge_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}\;\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z}).

Note that
\psi
is
\Pi_{\alpha+2}
and
\chi
is
\Pi_{\alpha+4}
, so that
\psi\wedge\chi
is
\Pi_{\alpha+4}
. We claim that
\mathcal{N}\models\psi(\bar{p})\wedge\chi(\bar{p})
if and only if
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p})\geq_{\alpha+2}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
and therefore
\psi\wedge\chi
is our desired formula.

First, suppose that
\mathcal{N}\models\psi(\bar{p})\wedge\chi(\bar{p})
. Given
\bar{q}\in\mathcal{N}
, by
\mathcal{N}\models\chi(\bar{p})
there is
\bar{b}\in\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathcal{N}\models\bigdoublewedge_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}\;\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{p},\bar{q},\bar{z}).

Then for any
\bar{c}\in\mathcal{M}
, there is
\bar{r}\in\mathcal{N}
such that

\mathcal{N}\models\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{p},\bar{q},\bar{r}).

By
\mathcal{N}\models\psi(\bar{p})
, we get

\mathcal{N}\models\varphi_{\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{b}\bar{c}}^{\alpha}(\bar{p},\bar{q},\bar{r}).

This implies that
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p}\bar{q}\bar{r})\geq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{b}\bar{c})
.
In particular, to win the
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p})\geq_{\alpha+2}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
game, the Duplicator can play
\bar{b}
in response to
\bar{q}
on the first move,
\bar{r}
in response to
\bar{c}
on the second move, and then play the winning strategy for
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p}\bar{q}\bar{r})\geq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{b}\bar{c})
to win the game, as desired.

On the other hand, suppose that
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p})\geq_{\alpha+2}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
. We must argue that
\mathcal{N}\models\psi(\bar{p})
and
\mathcal{N}\models\chi(\bar{p})
. First, we argue that
\mathcal{M}\models\psi(\bar{a})
and
\mathcal{M}\models\chi(\bar{a})
. The former is due to the definitions of
\theta_{\bar{b}}
and
\varphi^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{b}}
. The latter is because
\mathcal{M}\models\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{a},\bar{b},\bar{c})
for any
\bar{b}
and
\bar{c}
as the quantifiers and conjunctions/disjunctions are essentially both over elements of
\mathcal{M}
. Now since
\psi
is
\Pi_{\alpha+2}
, and
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p})\geq_{\alpha+2}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
, we have
\mathcal{N}\models\psi(\bar{p})
. Though
\chi
is
\Pi_{\alpha+4}
, we can also argue that because
(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})\models\chi
and
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p})\geq_{\alpha+2}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
we get
\mathcal{N}\models\chi(\bar{p})
. We can argue this by hand, but one could also note that
\chi
is
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha+2}
and apply Proposition
1.6
(and indeed the complexity of the argument below is one justification for considering this complexity class of formulas as we do in Section
6
). To show that
\mathcal{N}\models\chi(\bar{p})
, for all
k
and
\bar{q}
we must show that

\mathcal{N}\models\bigdoublevee_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\in\mathcal{M}}\bigdoublewedge_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}\;\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{p},\bar{q},\bar{z}).

Choose
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
(\mathcal{N},\bar{p}\bar{q})\leq_{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{u})
. Since
\mathcal{M}\models\chi
, we have

\mathcal{M}\models\bigdoublevee_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\in\mathcal{M}}\bigdoublewedge_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}\;\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{a},\bar{u},\bar{z}).

So, for some disjunct
\bar{b}
(in fact for
\bar{b}=\bar{u}
) for every
\bar{c}
there are
\bar{v}_{\bar{c}}\in\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathcal{M}\models\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{a},\bar{u},\bar{v}_{\bar{c}}).

For each
\bar{c}
choose
\bar{r}_{\bar{c}}
such that
(\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{u}\bar{v}_{\bar{c}})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{N},\bar{p}\bar{q}\bar{r}_{\bar{c}})
. Since
\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}
is
\Pi_{\alpha}
we get

\mathcal{N}\models\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{p},\bar{q},\bar{r}_{\bar{c}}).

Thus there is
\bar{b}
such that for every
\bar{c}
there is
\bar{r}_{\bar{c}}
such that

\mathcal{N}\models\bigdoublevee_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}\in\mathcal{M}}\bigdoublewedge_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell}\;\theta_{\bar{b}\bar{c}}(\bar{p},\bar{q},\bar{z}).

Since we have shown this for all
k
and
\bar{q}
, we have that
\mathcal{N}\models\chi(\bar{p})
.
∎

This completes the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Note that for
\alpha
odd, finite, and greater than
1
we do not get a better complexity even though the base case is stronger than required by the lemma.
This is of no help in the inductive steps—the complexity comes from the formula
\chi
, which does not involve the formula given by the inductive hypothesis.

We now prove the analog of Lemma

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}

3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a fixed countable structure. For each
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
there is a
\Pi_{\alpha+3}
formula
\varphi

such
\mathcal{N}\models\varphi(\bar{b})
if and only if
(\mathcal{N},\bar{b})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
. Hence the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+3}
.

Proof.

If
\alpha

Therefore, we can assume that
\alpha=\beta+1
.
We note that

(\mathcal{N},\bar{b})\leq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})\iff\mathcal{N}\models\bigwwedge_{\beta<\alpha}\bigwwedge_{\bar{c}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists\bar{x}(\mathcal{N},\bar{b},\bar{x})\geq_{\beta}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a},\bar{c}).

By Lemma
3.1

(\mathcal{N},\bar{b},\bar{d})\geq_{\beta}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a},\bar{c})
can be expressed in a
\Pi_{\beta+2}

This means that, overall, the formula is
\Pi_{\alpha+3}
.
∎

One contrast between the formulas described in Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
to capture the back-and-forth relations and those described in the classical analysis of Lemma VI.14 in
[

lies in the computability of the formulas.
The method in Lemma VI.14 in
[

\mathcal{M}
.
On the other hand, Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
rely on

-type of a tuple
within

Extracting such a formula given a tuple and a structure is not an effective procedure.
In sum, there is a tradeoff at play here.
The formulas in Lemmas

and

may be far simpler in quantifier complexity when compared to the previously known descriptions of back-and-forth relations, but they are less computable.
We conjecture that this tradeoff is necessary.

3.2.
Exceptional bounds

We now consider the simplest cases of Theorem
1.3
with
\alpha=1
,
\alpha=2
, or
\alpha
near a limit ordinal.
These small values have improved descriptions for the
\alpha
-type when compared to the general results of the previous section.

3.5
.

For any structure
\mathcal{M}
the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\geq_{1}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{1}
.

Proof.

The conjunction of the finitary universal sentences true in
\mathcal{M}
defines the set as all
\Pi_{1}
formulas are a conjunction of these formulas.
∎

3.6
.

For any structure
\mathcal{M}
the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\leq_{1}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2}
.

Proof.

This is the same upper bound given previously, straight from the definition. Let
\theta_{\bar{a}}(\bar{x})
be the finitary quantifier-free formula which says that
\bar{x}
and
\bar{a}
satisfy the same atomic formulas (from among the first
|\bar{a}|
-many formulas). Then

\mathcal{N}\leq_{1}\mathcal{M}\iff\mathcal{N}\models\bigdoublewedge_{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}^{n}}\exists y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\;\theta_{\bar{a}}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}).\qed

3.7
.

For any structure
\mathcal{M}
the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\leq_{2}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
.

Proof.

Note that

\mathcal{N}\leq_{2}\mathcal{M}\iff\bigwwedge_{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists\bar{x}\;(\mathcal{N},\bar{x})\geq_{1}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})

which is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
by the upper bound established in Lemma
3.5
.
∎

Another place where the bounds are exceptional is near limit ordinals.
Just like the small finite values, we can improve the bounds for these ordinals.
We consider these cases below.

3.8
.

For any structure
\mathcal{M}
we have the following.

\theenumxii

If
\alpha=\lambda
where
\lambda
is a limit ordinal, the set
\{\mathcal{N}\;:\;\mathcal{N}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}=\{\mathcal{N}\;:\;\mathcal{N}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}
.

\theenumxii

If
\alpha=\lambda+1
where
\lambda
is a limit ordinal, the set
\{\mathcal{N}\;:\;\mathcal{N}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+1}
.

\theenumxii

If
\alpha=\lambda+1
where
\lambda
is a limit ordinal, the set
\{\mathcal{N}\;:\;\mathcal{N}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+1}
.

Proof.

Each of these follows immediately from the classical description of the back-and-forth relations from Lemma VI.14 in
[
24
]
.
In particular, note that
\lambda=2\lambda
and
(\lambda+1)+1=2(\lambda+1)
.
∎

3.9
.

If
\lambda
is a limit ordinal the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{M}\geq_{\lambda+2}\mathcal{N}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\lambda+3}
for all
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

For this proof, we let
p_{\bar{a}}
denote the
\Pi_{\lambda}
-type of
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
, and note that
p_{\bar{a}}(\bar{x})
can be expressed as a
\Pi_{\lambda}
formula (as the conjunction of the formulas
\varphi^{\alpha}_{\bar{a}}(\bar{x})
for
\alpha<\lambda
expressing that
(\mathcal{N},\bar{x})\geq_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})
). First, if
\mathcal{M}\geq_{\lambda+2}\mathcal{N}
, then the
\Pi_{\lambda}
-types realised in
\mathcal{N}
are the same as the
\Pi_{\lambda}
-types realised in
\mathcal{M}
. (Or, what is the same,
\mathcal{M}\equiv_{\lambda+1}\mathcal{N}
). This can be expressed by the
\Pi_{\lambda+2}
sentence

\psi=\bigdoublewedge_{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists\bar{x}\;p_{\bar{a}}(\bar{x})\;\;\wedge\;\;\bigdoublewedge_{n}\forall x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\bigdoublevee_{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}}p_{\bar{a}}(\bar{x}).

Now for each
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
, let
P_{\bar{a}}
be the set of all
\Pi_{\lambda}
-types extending that of
\bar{a}
realized in the structure
\mathcal{M}
,

P_{\bar{a}}=\{p_{\bar{a}\bar{b}}(\bar{x},\bar{y}):\bar{b}\in\mathcal{M}\}.

Let
\overline{P_{\bar{a}}}
be the set of all
\Pi_{\lambda}
types realised in
\mathcal{M}
but not in
P_{\bar{a}}
.
Consider the sentence

\theta=\bigdoublewedge_{\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}}\exists\bar{x}\bigdoublewedge_{p\in\overline{P_{\bar{a}}}}\neg\exists\bar{y}\;p(\bar{x},\bar{y}).

Note that
\theta
is true in
\mathcal{M}
by construction and that it is a
\Pi_{\lambda+3}
formula. In particular,
\theta
is a conjunction of
\Sigma_{\lambda+2}
formulas.
If
\mathcal{M}\geq_{\lambda+2}\mathcal{N}
, then since
\theta
is true in
\mathcal{M}
, each of its
\Sigma_{\lambda+2}
conjuncts is true in
\mathcal{N}
.
This means that
\theta
is true in
\mathcal{N}.

(Alternatively, one can note that
\theta
is
\overline{\mathfrak{E}}_{\alpha}
and appeal to Proposition
1.6
which we prove later.) Thus if
\mathcal{M}\geq_{\lambda+2}\mathcal{N}
then
\mathcal{N}\models\psi\wedge\theta
.

On the other hand, suppose that
\mathcal{N}\models\psi\wedge\theta
. Then
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
realise the same
\Pi_{\lambda}
types. Given
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
, choose
\bar{b}\in\mathcal{N}
such that

\mathcal{N}\models\bigdoublewedge_{p\in\overline{P_{\bar{a}}}}\neg\exists\bar{y}\;p(\bar{b},\bar{y}).

Now given
\bar{b}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
, we have that

\mathcal{N}\models\bigdoublewedge_{p\in\overline{P_{\bar{a}}}}\neg p(\bar{b},\bar{b}^{\prime}).

But the
\Pi_{\lambda}
types realised in
\mathcal{N}
are the same as the
\Pi_{\lambda}
-types realised in
\mathcal{M}
because
\mathcal{N}\models\psi
, and so since
\bar{b}\bar{b}^{\prime}
does not realise one of the types in
\overline{P_{\bar{a}}}
it must realise one of the types in
P_{\bar{a}}
, say
p_{\bar{a}\bar{a}^{\prime}}
. Then
(\mathcal{M},\bar{a}\bar{a}^{\prime})\equiv_{\lambda}(\mathcal{N},\bar{b}\bar{b}^{\prime})
. Working backwards through the proof gives that
(\mathcal{M},\bar{a})\geq_{\lambda+1}(\mathcal{N},\bar{b})
and so
\mathcal{M}\geq_{\lambda+2}\mathcal{N}
. Thus if
\mathcal{N}\models\psi\wedge\theta
then
\mathcal{M}\geq_{\lambda+2}\mathcal{N}
.
∎

4.
Lower bounds for the definability of the
\alpha
-types of structures

We now consider lower bounds for the complexity of the sets
\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\}
and
\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{M}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}\}
.
We only consider particular values of
\alpha
and will show how to use these results to prove the complete characterization in Section
5
.
We begin by considering small finite values for which the back-and-forth relations are very easy to define.
We then look at slightly larger finite values that exhibit more general behavior and are therefore suitable base cases for the jump inversion argument in Section
5
.
Lastly, we analyze exceptional cases near limit ordinals. In all cases, we have already shown that the sets are of the specified complexity, and so it is just the hardness results that remain.

4.1.
Lower bounds for small finite cases

The following three cases are too small to exhibit general behavior, so they are treated separately.

4.1
.

There is a structure
\mathcal{M}
such that the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\geq_{1}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{1}
-complete.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a structure in the language of one unary relation
U
where
U
holds of exactly one element. Given a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{1}
set
A
, we can build for each
x\in 2^{\omega}
a structure
\mathcal{N}^{x}
such that
U
holds of exactly one element in
\mathcal{N}^{x}
(and hence
\mathcal{N}^{x}\geq_{1}\mathcal{M}
) if
x\in A
and
U
holds of exactly two elements in
\mathcal{N}^{x}
(and hence
\mathcal{N}^{x}\ngeq_{1}\mathcal{M}
) if
x\notin A
.
∎

4.2
.

There is a structure
\mathcal{M}
such that the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\leq_{1}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2}
-complete.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the structure in the language of one unary relation
U
where
U
holds of an infinite-coinfinite subset of the elements of
\mathcal{M}
. The set
\Inf\subseteq 2^{\omega}
of strings with infinitely many 1’s is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{2}
-complete (see e.g., Section 23.A of
[
17
]
for this and other examples of complete sets), so we will reduce it to
\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\leq_{1}\mathcal{M}\}
. Given
x\in 2^{\omega}
we construct
\mathcal{N}^{x}
so that
x\in\Inf\iff\mathcal{N}^{x}\leq_{1}\mathcal{M}
. If
\mathcal{N}^{x}
has domain
\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{N}^{x}\models U(2i)
if and only if
x(i)=1
(
U
will never hold of any odd elements).
If
x
has infinitely many 1’s then
\mathcal{N}^{x}\cong\mathcal{M}
, so in particular
\mathcal{N}^{x}\leq_{1}\mathcal{M}
.
If
x
has finitely many 1’s, say
n
of them, then in
\mathcal{M}
at least
n+1
distinct elements satisfy the relation
U
, but in
\mathcal{N}^{x}
there are only
n
. Thus
\mathcal{N}^{x}\not\leq_{1}\mathcal{M}
as desired.
∎

4.3
.

There is a structure
\mathcal{M}
such that the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\leq_{2}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
-complete.

Proof.

Identify
(2^{\omega})^{\omega}
with all lists
(a_{i})_{i\in\omega}
of infinite binary strings. Let
A\subseteq(2^{\omega})^{\omega}
be the set of lists
(a_{i})_{i\in\omega}
such that every
a_{i}
has finitely many
1
’s. This set is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{3}
-complete.

Consider structures of the following type: they have countably many sorts, labeled by unary relations
R_{i}
, each sort consisting of a linear ordering.
Given a structure
\mathcal{C}
of this form, we write
\mathcal{C}_{i}
to mean the linear ordering defined by the
R_{i}
points.
We let
\mathcal{M}
be the unique structure of this form with
\mathcal{M}_{i}\cong\omega
for all
i
.
Given
x=(x_{i})_{i\in\omega}
we construct in a continuous way
\mathcal{N}^{x}
, also a structure of the above form, so that
x\in A\iff\mathcal{N}^{x}\leq_{2}\mathcal{M}
.
Given
i
, we explain how to construct
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}
the
i
th sort of
\mathcal{N}^{x}
.
Start with a copy of
\omega
.
Whenever we see another 1 in the string
x_{i}
, add an element below all of the previously added elements.
If
x_{i}
has finitely many 1’s, then in
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}
we add only finitely many elements below the initial copy of
\omega
, and so
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}\cong\omega
.
If
a_{i}
has infinitely many 1’s, then
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}=\omega^{*}+\omega\cong\zeta
.
If
x\in A
, all of the
x_{i}
have finitely many 1’s and
\mathcal{N}^{x}\cong\mathcal{M}
, so in particular
\mathcal{N}^{x}\leq_{2}\mathcal{M}
.
On the other hand, if
x\not\in A
, some
x_{i}
has infinitely many 1’s, so
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}\cong\zeta
.
Let

\varphi:=\exists y\;\left[R_{i}(y)\land\forall z\left(R_{i}(z)\longrightarrow z\geq y\right)\right].

Note that
\mathcal{M}\models\varphi
, yet
\mathcal{N}^{x}\models\neg\varphi
, so
\mathcal{N}^{x}\nleq_{2}\mathcal{M}
as desired.
∎

4.2.
Critical finite cases

The next two cases we consider are critical because they exhibit all of the complexity of the behavior of the general case. In Section
5
, we will use jump inversion to obtain all of the other non-exceptional cases from these two.

4.4
.

There is a structure
\mathcal{M}
such that the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\geq_{2}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{4}
-complete.

Proof.

The structures we work with will be
flower graphs
(sometimes also called bouquet graphs). Let
\mathcal{S}
be a non-empty family of subsets of
\omega
. (From now on, all of our families will be assumed to be non-empty.) There is a corresponding flower graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
. This flower graph will have, for each
S\in\mathcal{S}
, infinitely many connected components (all isomorphic to each other) consisting of a central vertex with, for each
n\in S
, a loop of length
n
.

This graph encodes what we will call
positive enumerations
of the family
\mathcal{S}
. A positive enumeration of
\mathcal{S}
should be an object from which positive information about
\mathcal{S}
can be obtained positively, though one has to express this formally. There are many equivalent definitions, but one is as follows. A positive enumeration of a family is a function
f\colon\omega\times\omega\times\omega\to\{0,1\}
such that, for each
x,y
,
f(x,y,0)=0
and if
f(x,y,s)=1
then
f(x,y,t)=1
for all
t\geq s
. We set

\hat{f}(x,y)=\lim_{s\to\infty}f(x,y,s).

Let

X_{i}=\{j:\hat{f}(i,j)=1\}.

Then
f
is a positive enumeration of the family
\{X_{i}:i\in\omega\}
. Note that the order in which the subsets of
\omega
appear does not matter, and sets can be repeated. The same set can show up multiple times in the enumeration.

The idea is that
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
has the property, easily verified, that (a) from any enumeration of
\mathcal{S}
, we can compute a copy of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
, and (b) from any copy of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
we can enumerate a copy of
\mathcal{S}
. (In particular, there is a continuous map that takes an enumeration of
\mathcal{S}
to a presentation of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
, and a continuous map taking a presentation of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
to an enumeration of
\mathcal{S}
.)

We say that a family
\mathcal{S}
is
closed under finite additions of elements
if whenever
S\in\mathcal{S}
and
F
is finite, then
S\cup F\in\mathcal{S}
.

4.4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be two families of subsets of
\omega
, and suppose that both
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
are closed under finite additions of elements. Then
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\leq_{2}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
if and only if for each
T\in\mathcal{T}
there is
S\in\mathcal{S}
such that
S\subseteq T
.

Proof.

Suppose that there is
T\in\mathcal{T}
such that for all
S\in\mathcal{S}
we have
S\nsubseteq T
. We describe a winning strategy for Spoiler in the back-and-forth game to witness
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\nleq_{2}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
. First, Spoiler plays the central vertex
u\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
of a connected component corresponding to
T
. Say that Duplicator responds with the central vertex
v\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
of a connected component corresponding to
S\in\mathcal{S}
. Since
S\nsubseteq T
, there is some
n\in S
with
n\notin T
. Then Spoiler plays an
n
-cycle in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
in the connected component of
v
, to which Duplicator cannot respond with an
n
-cycle in the connected component of
u
. If Duplicator responds with a vertex
v\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
that is not a central vertex, then Spoiler can play a path connecting
v
to its central vertex
v^{\prime}
along with an
n
-cycle connected to
v^{\prime}
. Again, Duplicator cannot respond with an
n
-cycle in the connected component of
u
. Thus
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\nleq_{2}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

Now suppose that for each
T\in\mathcal{T}
there is
S\in\mathcal{S}
such that
S\subseteq T
. We will describe a winning strategy for Duplicator in the back-and-forth game to witness
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\leq_{2}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
. On their first play, Spoiler plays elements of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
; we may assume that they play central vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
together with, for each
i
, the elements of finitely many loops in the connected component of
u_{i}
. For each
i
, let
F_{i}
be the finite set of sizes of loops that Spoiler plays connected to
u_{i}
. For each
i
, let
T_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
be the set coded by the connected component of
u_{i}
, and choose in
\mathcal{S}
a set
S_{i}
with
F_{i}\subseteq S_{i}\subseteq T_{i}
. Duplicator will respond, in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
, with vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
chosen such that the connected component of
v_{i}
codes
S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
; using the fact that
F_{i}\subseteq S_{i}
, Duplicator also responds to each loop played by Spoiler on
u_{i}
with a loop of the same size on
v_{i}
. Now in the second round of the game suppose that Spoiler plays, in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
, finitely many further loops on the
v_{i}
, together with new central vertices
v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{\ell}^{\prime}
and finitely many loops in their connected components. Since
S_{i}\subseteq T_{i}
, Duplicator can respond to each loop on
v_{i}
with a loop on
u_{i}
of the corresponding size. For each
j
let
G_{j}
be the set of sizes of loops played by Spoiler on
v_{j}^{\prime}
, and choose
T_{j}\supseteq G_{j}
. Then Duplicator can respond to each
v_{j}^{\prime}
with a central vertex
u_{j}^{\prime}
coding
T_{j}
, and to the loops played on
v_{j}^{\prime}
with loops of the same lengths on
u_{j}^{\prime}
. Thus
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\leq_{2}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{i}\cup F:i\in\mathbb{N},F\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}\}
where
S_{i}=\{\mathopen{\langle}i,j\mathclose{\rangle}\}_{j\in\omega}
.

4.4.2
.

The set of enumerations
f
of families
\mathcal{T}
such that for all
T\in\mathcal{T}
there is
S\in\mathcal{S}
with
S\subseteq T
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{4}
-complete. Moreover, given any
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{4}
set
A
, there is a Wadge reduction
x\mapsto f_{x}
such that each
f_{x}
is an enumeration of a family which is closed under finite additions of elements.

Proof.

Let
A
be the
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{4}
-complete set of all lists
f=(f_{u,v})_{u,v\in\omega}
of graphs of functions
f_{u,v}:\omega\to\omega
, with

f\in A\Longleftrightarrow\forall u\exists v\;f_{u,v}\text{ is total}.

Given
f=(f_{u,v})
, we produce an enumeration
g
of the family

\mathcal{T}=\{T_{u,F}:u\in\mathbb{N},F\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}\}
where

T_{u,F}=F\cup\{\mathopen{\langle}v,j\mathclose{\rangle}:v\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }f_{u,v}(j)\downarrow\}.

We must show that
f\in A
if and only if for all
T\in\mathcal{T}
there is
S\in\mathcal{S}
such that
S\subseteq T
.

Say that

. In this case, we must show that there is a pair

such that

. Let
F=F^{\prime}
and take
i
to be the witness showing that

is total. In particular this means that
f_{u,i}(j)\downarrow
always holds so for all
j
,
\mathopen{\langle}i,j\mathclose{\rangle}\in T_{u,F}
.
It follows at once that
T_{u,F}\supseteq S_{i}\cup F^{\prime}
as desired.

Conversely say that there is an
S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
S_{i}\subseteq T_{u,\emptyset}
.
This means that for all
j
,
f_{u,i}(j)\downarrow
, that is,
f_{u,i}
is total.
This holds for any

, so we have that

, as desired.
∎

Together, these two claims finish the proof. We take
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
. We reduce to
\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\geq_{2}\mathcal{M}\}
any
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{4}
set
B
by producing, for each
x
, an enumeration

of a family
\mathcal{T}_{x}
which is closed under finite additions of elements, and from this a presentation of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}_{x}}
. Then
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}_{x}}\geq_{2}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
if and only if

.
∎

4.5
.

There is a structure
\mathcal{M}
such that

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\leq_{3}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{6}
-complete.

Proof.

We will make use of the construction of Lemma
4.4
. Let
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{i}\cup F:i\in\mathbb{N},F\in[\mathbb{N}]^{<\omega}\}
, where
S_{i}=\{\mathopen{\langle}i,j\mathclose{\rangle}\}_{j\in\omega}
, be the family of subsets of
\omega
defined in Lemma
4.4
and let
\mathcal{S}_{\fin}
be the family of all finite subsets of
\omega
.

In addition to what we proved earlier, we will need two additional facts. First, in Claim
4.4.2
we can replace the family
\mathcal{T}
by
\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{S}
to assume that
\mathcal{T}
contains
\mathcal{S}
. This modification does not change whether for all
T\in\mathcal{T}
there is
S\in\mathcal{S}
with
S\subseteq T
. Second, under this assumption, we can improve one direction of Claim
4.4.1
as follows.

4.5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
be two families of subsets of
\omega
, and suppose that both
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
are closed under finite additions of elements. Suppose that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and that for each
T\in\mathcal{T}
there is
S\in\mathcal{S}
such that
S\subseteq T
. Then
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\geq_{3}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.

Proof.

We give a strategy for Duplicator in the back-and-forth game to witness
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\geq_{3}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
On their first play, Spoiler plays elements
\bar{p}\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
intersecting connected components coding
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
.
Because
S\subseteq T
, there is a natural embedding
\iota:\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\to\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
Duplicator plays
\iota(\bar{p})
in response.
We claim that
(\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}},\bar{p})\leq_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}},\iota(\bar{p}))
, so this is a winning move.
Duplicator will play according to
\iota
on the connected components coding
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
in each structure.
This is a winning strategy as
\iota
restricts to an isomorphism on these components.
The remaining connected components on each side only finitely differ from
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
In particular, they are isomorphic to the whole of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
.
This means that Duplicator can play a winning strategy for
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}\leq_{2}\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
on these components to win the
(\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}},\bar{p})\leq_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}},\iota(\bar{p}))
game.
Under the assumptions provided, such a winning strategy is provided in Claim
4.4.1
.
∎

The following general claim will allow us to conclude the desired result.

4.5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a structure such that for all
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\beta}
sets
C
there is a continuous map
x\mapsto\mathcal{N}_{x}
such that:

\theenumxiii

if
x\in C
then
\mathcal{N}_{x}\leq_{\alpha+1}\mathcal{M}
,

\theenumxiii

if
x\not\in C
then
\mathcal{N}_{x}\not\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}
, and

\theenumxiii

there is a structure
\mathcal{F}
such that for all
x

\mathcal{F}\leq_{\alpha}N_{x}
and
\mathcal{F}\not\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}
.

Then there is a structure
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
such that
\{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}:\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}\leq_{\alpha+1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\beta+2}
-hard.

Proof.

Let
A
be a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\beta+2}
set, and write

x\in A\Longleftrightarrow\forall m\exists n\;(x,m,n)\in B

where
B
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\beta}
.
By hypothesis for each
m,n
there is a structure
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}
such that

(x,m,n)\in B\Longrightarrow\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}\leq_{\alpha+1}\mathcal{M}

and

(x,m,n)\notin B\Longrightarrow\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}\ngeq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}.

Fix also the structure
\mathcal{F}
. We must define a structure
\widetilde{M}
and build, for each
x
,
a structure
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}
such that

x\in A\Longleftrightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}\leq_{\alpha+1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}.

We work in the language of
\mathcal{M}
together with infinitely many fresh unary relations
U_{i}
and a fresh equivalence relation
E
.
Our structures
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}
will be divided by the
U_{i}
into infinitely many distinguished sorts, each of which consists of infinitely many infinite
E
-equivalence classes, with the equivalence classes refining the sorts.
On each equivalence class, there will be a structure in the language of
\mathcal{M}
; the choice of structures that appear in each equivalence class of each sort will be the only difference between the different structures.
For
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, in each sort
U_{n}
we have one equivalence class which is a copy of
\mathcal{M}
and each other equivalence class is a copy of
\mathcal{F}
.
For
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}
, in each sort
U_{m}
, we put as the
2n
th equivalence class a copy of
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}
, and as the
2n+1
th equivalence class a copy of
\mathcal{F}
.

We claim that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}\leq_{\alpha+1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if
x\in A
.
First, suppose that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}\leq_{\alpha+1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
.
Given
m
, let
u\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
be an element of the sort
U_{m}
and within
U_{m}
of the equivalence class on which we have a copy of
\mathcal{M}
.
There must be
v\in\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}
such that
(\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x},v)\geq_{\alpha}(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},u)
; moreover,
v
must be in the
m
th sort.
If
G_{u}
is the structure on the equivalence class of
u
, and
G_{v}
is the structure on the equivalence class of
v
, then
G_{v}\geq_{\alpha}G_{u}
.
Then
v
cannot be in the
2n+1
th equivalence class of the
m
th sort of
\mathcal{N}_{x}
, since
\mathcal{F}\not\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}
.
Thus
v
must be in the
2n
th equivalence class for some
n
, and
G_{v}\cong\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}
.
By assumption, this implies that
(x,m,n)\in B
.
So for each
m
there is
n
such that
(x,m,n)\in B
, which implies that
x\in A
.

On the other hand, suppose that
x\in A
.
For each
m
there is
n
such that
(x,m,n)\in B
, and so for each
m
there is
n
such that
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}\leq_{\alpha+1}\mathcal{M}
.
We must show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}\leq_{\alpha+1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
.
Since
\mathcal{M}
is divided into sorts by the
U_{i}
, it suffices to show that this is true on each sort.
Consider the
m
th sort, and fix
n
such that
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}\leq_{\alpha+1}\mathcal{M}
.
For each equivalence class of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
(with induced structure
G
), there is an equivalence class of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}
(with induced structure
H
) such that
G\geq_{\alpha+1}H
; for the first equivalence class where
G\cong\mathcal{M}
this is the
2n
th equivalence class with
H\cong\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}
, and for any other equivalence class with
G\cong\mathcal{F}
this is any of the infinitely many equivalence classes with
H\cong\mathcal{F}
.
Also, for each equivalence class of
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}
(with induced graph
H\cong\mathcal{N}^{x}_{m,n}
or
H\cong\mathcal{F}
) there are infinitely many equivalence classes of
\mathcal{M}
(with induced structure
G\cong\mathcal{F}
) such that
H\geq_{\alpha}G
.
This is enough to check that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}\leq_{\alpha+1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
(see Sublemma
2.1.1
).
∎

We now complete the proof of the lemma by using Claim
4.5.2
with
\alpha=2
.
By Claim
4.5.1
along with the construction in Lemma
4.4
, there is a continuous map
x\mapsto\mathcal{N}_{x}
satisfying the first two conditions of Claim
4.5.2
.
Therefore, to finish the proof, we need only to construct an
\mathcal{F}
that satisfies the last condition of Claim
4.5.2
relative to this map.
Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}_{\fin}}
.
Note that
\mathcal{N}_{x}\cong\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
where
\mathcal{T}
is always closed under finite additions of elements.
This means that for all finite sets
F
, there is a
T\in\mathcal{T}
such that
F\supseteq T
.
By Claim
4.4.1
, this means that
\mathcal{F}\leq_{2}\mathcal{N}_{x}
.
Also,
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\mathcal{S}
has only infinite sets; none of these sets are contained in any finite set, so by Claim
4.4.1

\mathcal{M}\not\leq_{2}\mathcal{F}
, as desired.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.3.
Exceptional lower bounds at limit levels

The following cases are too close to a limit ordinal to exhibit general behavior, so they are considered separately.

4.6
.

If
\alpha
is a limit ordinal,
\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}
.
There is a structure
\mathcal{M}
such that the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\equiv_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}
-complete.

Proof.

The first claim is well-known; see for example the proof of Lemma II.67 in
[
24
]
.

To see the second claim, let
\delta_{i}\to\alpha
be a fundamental sequence.
By the Ash and Knight Pair of Structures theorem
[
4
]
the set of models isomorphic to
\omega^{\delta_{i}}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\delta_{i}}
-hard.
Let the language of
\mathcal{M}
be
\{<\}
along with infinitely many unary relations
R_{i}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
contain exactly one copy of each
\omega^{\delta_{i}}
, where
R_{i}
holds exactly of the elements in
\omega^{\delta_{i}}
.

Given a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha}
set
A
, we can write
A=\bigcap_{i}A_{i}
where
A_{i}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\delta_{i}}
.
For
x\in 2^{\omega}
, let
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}\cong\omega^{\delta_{i}}
if and only if
x\in A_{i}
.
Note that this can be done in a continuous fashion.

\mathcal{N}^{x}
contains exactly a copy of each
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}
and
R_{i}
holds exactly of the elements in
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}
.
As each component of
\mathcal{N}^{x}
is constructed continuously, so is all of
\mathcal{N}^{x}
.
If
x\in A
it is immediate that
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathcal{N}^{x}
and so
\mathcal{M}\equiv_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}
.
If
x\not\in A
, then for some
i

\mathcal{N}^{x}_{i}\not\cong\omega^{\delta_{i}}
.
In particular,
\mathcal{N}^{x}
fails to satisfy the
\Pi_{2\delta_{i}+1}
Scott sentence of
\omega^{\delta_{i}}
(give explicitly in
[
24
]
Lemma II.5) relativized to the predicate
R_{i}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{N}^{x}\not\equiv_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}
, completing the proof.
∎

For the next case, we need to make use of Montalbán’s tree of structures theorem as seen in
[
26
]
,
We only need a specific case of the theorem (namely, the one where the “tree” has only one path that is the length of a limit ordinal), so we will only state the weak version needed.

4.7
Montalbán
[
26
]
.

Given a limit ordinal
\lambda
and a fundamental sequence
\delta_{i}\to\lambda
, let
\{\mathcal{L}_{i}\}_{i\in\omega}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
be structures with
\mathcal{L}_{i}\equiv_{\delta_{i}+1}\mathcal{L}_{i+1}\equiv_{\delta_{i}+1}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
for all
i
.
Given a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\lambda}
set
A=\bigcap_{i}A_{i}
where each
A_{i}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\delta_{i}}
there is a continuous procedure that given
x
outputs
\mathcal{L}_{i}
if
i
is the least such that
x\not\in A_{i}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
if
i\in A
.

Note that examples of such
\mathcal{L}_{i}
with the additional property that
M_{i}\not\equiv_{\delta_{i}+2}\mathcal{L}_{i+1}
always exist; see for example,
[
24
]
Example IX.24 where the examples are linear orderings.

4.8
.

If
\alpha=\lambda+1
where
\lambda
is a limit ordinal, there is a structure
\mathcal{M}
such that the set

\{\mathcal{N}\;:\;\mathcal{N}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+1}
-complete.

Proof.

We first construct an example
\mathcal{K}
such that
\{\mathcal{N}\;:\;\mathcal{N}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{K}\}
is
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{\lambda+1}
complete; we will use such a

as a piece of our final construction.
Let
\{\mathcal{L}_{i}\}_{i\in\omega}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
be linear orderings with
\mathcal{L}_{i}\equiv_{\delta_{i}+1}\mathcal{L}_{i+1}\equiv_{\delta_{i}+1}\mathcal{L}_{\omega}
and
\mathcal{L}_{i}\not\equiv_{\delta_{i}+2}\mathcal{L}_{i+1}
for all

.

\mathcal{K}
will be a structure in the signature of two equivalence relations
E
and
F
and an ordering

.
The equivalence relation
F
will refine
E
.
Given some
\sigma\in(\omega+1)^{\omega}

\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}
be the structure in the language

with infinitely many

\mathcal{L}_{\sigma(j)}
for each

.
Then define
\mathcal{K}
as follows with infinitely many
E

will have infinitely many equivalence classes with a copy of
\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}
.

Let
B
be a
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{\lambda+1}

Let
x\in B
if and only if
\exists m~x\in C_{m}
for a family of
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\lambda}

Let
A_{j}=C_{0}\cup\cdots\cup C_{j}

For each

\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\delta_{i}}

Let

Note that
\bigsqcup_{j\in\omega}\mathcal{N}^{x}_{j}

\mathcal{K}_{\tau_{0}}
for some
\tau_{0}\in(\omega+1)^{\omega}

Let

that differ from

on only finitely many inputs.
Let
\mathcal{N}^{x}
contain infinitely many

where the equivalence class corresponding to each

contains a copy of
\mathcal{K}_{\tau_{k}}
.
Note that
\mathcal{N}^{x}
is constructed by countably many disjoint continuous modifications of
F_{j}

is continuous.
(To modify

We now show that
x\in B
if and only if
\mathcal{N}^{x}\geq_{\lambda+1}\mathcal{K}
.
If
x\in B
, eventually there is a witness
m
such that
x\in C_{m}
.
For
j\geq m
, this means that
x\in A_{j}
.
In particular,
\tau_{0}(j)
for
j\geq m
is always
\omega
.
This means that the set of elements of
(\omega+1)^{\omega}
that are finitely different from
\tau_{0}
are exactly those that have finitely many finite values.
In particular,
\mathcal{N}^{x}\cong\mathcal{K}
, so
\mathcal{N}^{x}\geq_{\lambda+1}\mathcal{K}
as required.
Now say that
x\not\in B
.
This means that for any
j
,
x\not\in A_{j}
.
Thus
\tau_{0}
has only finite values.
Consider the formula
\psi:=\exists z~\bigwwedge_{i\in\omega}\varphi^{E}_{\tau_{0}(i)}(z)
, where
\varphi^{E}_{\tau_{0}(i)}(z)
is the
\Pi_{<\lambda}
sentence that expresses that in the
E
-equivalence class of

there is an
F
-equivalence class
\delta_{\tau_{0}(i)}+2
-equivalent to
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0}(i)}
.
By construction,
\mathcal{N}^{x}\models\psi
; any element of an equivalence class corresponding to
\tau_{0}
is a witness.
However,
\mathcal{M}\models\lnot\psi
; among the
\mathcal{L}_{j}
the
\delta_{\tau_{0}(i)}+2
-type of
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0}(i)}
isolates it up to isomorphism and no
E
class of
\mathcal{M}
has all of the
\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{0}(i)}
included (in fact, each such
E
class only has finitely many
\mathcal{L}_{j}
).
As
\psi
is a
\Sigma_{\lambda+1}
formula we see that
\mathcal{N}^{x}\not\geq_{\lambda+1}\mathcal{K}
, as desired.

Finally, we construct the desired
\mathcal{M}
. Let
D
be a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\lambda+2}
set.
Let
x\in D
if and only if
\forall n~x\in B_{n}
for a family of
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{\lambda+1}
sets
B_{n}
.
For each

let
\mathcal{K}_{n}\cong\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{n}
be the structures constructed above for
B_{n}
.

\mathcal{M}
will be a structure in the signature of
<
, the linear ordering within each
\mathcal{L}_{i}
, two equivalence relations
E
and
F
along with infinitely many unary predicates
\{S_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
denoting sorts in the structure.

\mathcal{M}
contains a copy of each
\mathcal{K}_{n}\cong\mathcal{K}
, with the predicate
S_{n}
holding exactly of the elements of
\mathcal{K}_{n}
.
Construct
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{x}
with a copy of each of the structures
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{n}
for each
n
; let
S_{n}
hold exactly of the elements in
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{n}
.
It is immediate that
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{x}\geq_{\lambda+1}\mathcal{M}
exactly when
\mathcal{N}^{x}_{n}\geq_{\lambda+1}\mathcal{K}_{n}
for all
n
.
By the above analysis, this happens exactly when
\forall n~x\in B_{n}
, that is,
x\in D
, and this procedure is continuous as desired.
∎

5.
Jump inversion

We now move the results from Lemmas
2.1
,
4.2
,
4.3
,
4.4
, and
4.5
up the Borel hierarchy. To do this, we use the method of jump inversion following the methods and notation from Chapter X.3 of
[
24
]
, which itself follows Goncharov, Harizanov, Knight, McCoy, R. Miller, and Solomon
[
11
]
.
The results listed in that chapter are unsuitable for our direct use; they lack uniformity in their statement and discuss constructions of computable reductions on indices rather than continuous Wadge reductions.
Nevertheless, the proof techniques presented in Chapter X.3 of
[
24
]
do have the desired level of uniformity, even if the results are not stated in that way, and the arguments relativize in a manner that is suitable for the construction of a Wadge reduction.
We provide a discussion of these techniques and the notation associated with them.

Fix
\alpha<\omega_{1}
. In Chapter X.3 of
[
24
]
, one works effectively, but as we are working non-effectively, we generally do not need to worry about issues like ordinal presentations. However, if we wanted, we could work relative to a presentation of
\alpha
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}
be rigid and uniformly boldface
\mathbf{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha+1}
categorical (equivalently,
\Sigma_{\alpha+1}
-atomic or having a
\Pi_{\alpha+2}
Scott sentence; see
[
24
]
Theorems II.23 and VII.21) structures with one binary relation
S
which are
\equiv_{\alpha}
-equivalent but with
\mathcal{L}_{0}\nleq_{\alpha+1}\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}\nleq_{\alpha+1}\mathcal{L}_{0}
.
Such structures
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}
exist by Lemma X.14 of
[
24
]
(but such examples were also constructed in
[
4
]
).
It follows from the Ash and Knight pair of structures theorem
[
4
]
that for any
\mathbf{\Delta}^{0}_{\alpha+1}
set
U
, there is a continuous function
x\mapsto\mathcal{C}_{x}
such that

x\in U\Longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{x}\cong\mathcal{L}_{0}

and

x\notin U\Longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{x}\cong\mathcal{L}_{1}.

This exact statement is not observed in the original paper of Ash and Knight, but it is a direct corollary; the first explicit published reference known to the authors for a boldface version of the theorem is
[
14
]
Theorem 2.2. (We also note that the boldface result follows with a small amount of extra work from Louveau and Saint Raymond’s separation theorem
[
20
]
.)

We can use these structures
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}
to define the following map of structures.

5.1
.

Given a graph
\mathcal{A}=(A,E)
construct
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})=(C;A,R)
as follows in the language with a unary relation
A
and a 4-ary relation
R
.
There is one
A
-point in
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})
for each element in
\mathcal{A}
, and we identify them.
The non-
A
-points in
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})
are split into disjoint infinite sets
B_{a,b}
for each pair of
a,b\in A
.
Given a fixed
a,b\in A
,
R(a,b,\cdot,\cdot)
will be a binary relation on
B_{a,b}
. If
E(a,b)
then
(B_{a,b},R(a,b,\cdot,\cdot))
will be isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and if
\neg E(a,b)
then
(B_{a,b},R(a,b,\cdot,\cdot))
will be isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}_{1}
.

Intuitively, one replaces each edge with a copy of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and non-edge with a copy of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
.
In Chapter X.3 of
[
24
]
, it is proven that
\Phi_{\alpha}
is an infinitary
\Sigma_{1}
bi-interpretation with the
\alpha
-canonical structural jump of the image.
We collect the relevant consequences of this claim along with other observations about this construction in the theorem below.

5.2
.

For each
\alpha\in\omega_{1}
, the map
\Phi_{\alpha}
has the following properties:

\theenumxiv

For every
\Pi_{\alpha+\beta}
formula
\varphi
there is a
\Pi_{\beta}
formula
\varphi^{*}
such that
\mathcal{A}\models\varphi^{*}\iff\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\models\varphi
.

\theenumxiv

For every
\Pi_{\beta}
formula
\psi
there is a
\Pi_{\alpha+\beta}
formula
\psi_{*}
such that
\mathcal{A}\models\psi\iff\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\models\psi_{*}
.

\theenumxiv

\mathcal{A}\leq_{\beta}\mathcal{B}
if and only if
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\leq_{\alpha+\beta}\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{B})
.

Proof.

Using Karp’s theorem
[
16
]
, (3) follows directly from (1) and (2). Essentially, (1) and (2) follow directly from the results in Section 2.2 of
[
23
]
along with Chapter X.3 of
[
24
]
.
To provide more detail, it follows from the existence of the
\Sigma_{1}
bi-interpretation and the pull-back theorem (Theorem XI.7 of
[
24
]
) that

\theenumxv

For every
\Pi_{\beta}
formula
\varphi
there is a
\Pi_{\beta}
formula
\varphi^{**}
such that

\mathcal{A}\models\varphi^{**}\iff\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})^{(\alpha)}\models\varphi.

\theenumxv

For every
\Pi_{\beta}
formula
\psi
there is a
\Pi_{\beta}
formula
\psi_{**}
such that

\mathcal{A}\models\psi\iff\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})^{(\alpha)}\models\psi_{**}.

From here, it is enough to show that

\theenumxvi

For every
\Pi_{\beta}
formula
\varphi^{**}
there is a
\Pi_{\alpha+\beta}
formula
\varphi^{*}
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\models\varphi^{*}\iff\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})^{(\alpha)}\models\varphi^{**}.

\theenumxvi

For every
\Pi_{\alpha+\beta}
formula
\psi_{*}
there is a
\Pi_{\beta}
formula
\psi_{**}
such that

\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})\models\psi_{**}\iff\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})^{(\alpha)}\models\psi_{*}.

The first item follows immediately by the definition of the
\alpha
-canonical structural jump.
The second item is shown by transfinite induction, with the only interesting case being the base case.
We point out the uniformity in Montalbán’s proof in Chapter X.3 of
[
24
]
.
In particular, over every possible
\mathcal{A}
he adds the same countably many
\Pi_{\alpha}
types to the language to Morleyize
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})
and obtain
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A})^{(\alpha)}
.
As a result, the translation of
\Pi_{\alpha+1}
formulas about the
\alpha
-canonical structural jump into
\Pi_{1}
formulas about the base structure provided in
[
23
]
Proposition 11 is uniform across all input structures and directly provides our desired base case.
∎

The following lemmas are the key technical inputs needed to iterate our previous results through the ordinals.

5.3
.

Let
X
be a Polish space. Given a
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha+1}
-measurable map
F:X\to\Mod(E)
, there is a continuous map
\Phi_{\alpha}(F):X\to\Mod(A,R)
such that
\Phi_{\alpha}(F)(x)\cong\Phi_{\alpha}(F(x))
.

Proof.

Consider a basic clopen set
U_{m,n,i}
in
\Mod(E)
for
n,m\in\omega
and
i\in\{0,1\}
. If
i=0
, then this is the set of all structures with
m\not En
, and if
i=1
, then it is the set of all structures with
m\;E\;n
. Since
F
is
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha+1}
-measurable,
C_{m,n,i}=F^{-1}(U_{m,n,i})
must be
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\alpha+1}
.
Let
G_{m,n}
be a continuous map, coming from the pair of structures theorem, reducing
(C_{m,n,0},C_{m,n,1})
to
(\mathcal{L}_{0},\mathcal{L}_{1})
. That is, for each
x\in X
,

m\not E^{F(x)}n\Longleftrightarrow x\in C_{m,n,0}\Longleftrightarrow G_{m,n}(x)\cong\mathcal{L}_{0}

and

m\;E^{F(x)}\;n\Longleftrightarrow x\in C_{m,n,1}\Longleftrightarrow G_{m,n}(x)\cong\mathcal{L}_{1}.

We now define the map
\Phi_{\alpha}(F)
. Given
x
,
\Phi_{\alpha}(F)(x)
will be the structure in the language
\{A,R\}
which has an infinite set
A
which we identify with
\mathbb{N}
, and for
m,n\in A
we have disjoint infinite sets
B_{m,n}
with
R(m,n,\cdot,\cdot)
defined on
B_{m,n}
so that
B_{m,n}
is isomorphic to
G_{m,n}(x)
. This is a continuous construction since each
G_{m,n}
is continuous, and
\Phi_{\alpha}(F)(x)\cong\Phi_{\alpha}(F(x))
by choice of the
G_{m,n}
.
∎

5.4
.

If a class of graphs
\mathbb{D}
is
\mathbf{\Pi}_{n}^{0}
hard as an invariant set in
\Mod(E)
then the class of structures

\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D}):=\{\mathcal{M}:\text{$\mathcal{M}\cong\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G})$ for some $\mathcal{G}\in\mathbb{D}$}\}

is
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha+n}^{0}
hard as an invariant set in
\Mod(A,R)
.
Moreover,
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+n}
hard within the image of
\Phi_{\alpha}
, that is, the maps witnessing these reductions always output structures isomorphic to
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{G})
for some
\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a
\mathbf{\Pi}^{0}_{\alpha+n}
subset of a Polish space
X
.
We describe a continuous reduction from
S
to
\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})
, which will immediately yield the desired result.
Let
S
be
\Pi^{0}_{\alpha+n}
relative to the parameter
Y
.
Apply a change of topology to

to make all
\Delta^{0}_{\alpha+1}(Y)
sets clopen.
In particular,
S
is a
\mathbf{\Pi}_{n}^{0}
set in this new topology.
Let
G
be the continuous map reducing
S
to
\mathbb{D}
.
Returning to the viewpoint of the original topology,
G:X\to\Mod(E)
is a
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{0}_{\alpha+1}
-measurable map.
By Lemma
5.3
, there is a continuous map
\Phi_{\alpha}(G):X\to\Mod(A,R)
such that
\Phi_{\alpha}(G)(x)\cong\Phi_{\alpha}(G(x))
.
Note that

x\in S\iff G(x)\in\mathbb{D}\iff\Phi_{\alpha}(G(x))\in\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D})\iff\Phi_{\alpha}(G)(x)\in\Phi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{D}).

Therefore,
\Phi_{\alpha}(G)
is the required continuous map witnessing the reduction.
∎

6.
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
formulas

As we have seen, the
\Sigma_{\alpha}
/
\Pi_{\alpha}
hierarchy is not optimal for defining back-and-forth types. In particular, for a fixed
\mathcal{M}
, defining the set

\{\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{N}\geq_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}\}

requires a
\Pi_{\alpha+2}
formula, but it is not true that if
\varphi
is
\Pi_{\alpha+2}
and
\mathcal{M}\leq_{\alpha}\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}\models\varphi
then
\mathcal{N}\models\varphi
. In this section, we define a hierarchy of complexity classes of
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1}\omega}
formulas which more precisely capture the back-and-forth relations. We call these the
back-and-forth complexity classes
of
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1}\omega}
formulas.

6.1
.

We define
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
(and simultaneously
{\overline{\mathfrak{A}}_{\alpha}}
and
{\overline{\mathfrak{E}}_{\alpha}}
) by mutual transfinite recursion.

item

\mathfrak{A}_{1}:=\Pi_{1}

item

\mathfrak{E}_{1}:=\Sigma_{1}

item

\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}:=
closure of
\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}\overline{\mathfrak{E}}_{\beta}
under
\forall
and
\bigdoublewedge_{i\in\omega}

item

\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}:=
closure of
\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}\overline{\mathfrak{A}}_{\beta}
under
\exists
and
\bigdoublevee_{j\in\omega}

item

\overline{\mathfrak{E}}_{\alpha}:=
closure of
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
under
\bigdoublevee_{j\in\omega},\bigdoublewedge_{i\in\omega}

item

\overline{\mathfrak{A}}_{\alpha}:=
closure of
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
under
\bigdoublevee_{j\in\omega},\bigdoublewedge_{i\in\omega}

Each
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
formula can be written in the form

\bigdoublewedge_{i\in I}\forall\bar{y}_{i}\varphi_{i}(\bar{x},\bar{y}_{i})

where the formulas
\varphi_{i}
are
\overline{\mathfrak{E}}_{\beta}
for some
\beta<\alpha
, and similarly a
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formula can be written in the form

\bigdoublevee_{i\in I}\exists\bar{y}_{i}\varphi_{i}(\bar{x},\bar{y}_{i})

where each
\varphi_{i}
is
\overline{\mathfrak{A}}_{\beta}
for some
\beta<\alpha
.

One could also make these definitions for
\mathcal{L}_{\infty,\omega}
formulas by allowing the conjunctions and disjunctions to be over arbitrary sets;
1.6
still holds in this context. For simplicity we limit ourselves to
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1}\omega}
.

The following are some basic properties of these complexity classes that can all be confirmed by straightforward induction arguments. The complexity classes
\exists_{\alpha}
and
\forall_{\alpha}
are defined by counting alternations of quantifiers but not counting infinite conjunctions and disjunctions.

6.2
.

Up to equivalence in countable structures:

item

Scott’s isomorphism theorem states that infinitary sentences can always be used as isomorphism invariants for countable structures

In this section, we show that his notion of Scott rank also aligns well with our new notion of syntactic complexity.
This gives a new set of criteria equivalent to Montalbán’s notion of Scott rank, showing that it is even more robust than we previously thought.
In particular, the addition of more infinitary conjunctions and disjunctions to our formulas does not increase their expressive power too much when it comes to defining an isomorphism invariant.

\bigwwedge_{i\in\omega}\forall\bar{x}_{i}\varphi_{i}(\bar{x}_{i})
with
\varphi_{i}\in\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
.
We build a set of
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formulas
T
over the vocabulary of
\mathcal{M}
enriched with countably many constants
C
to perform a Henkin-like construction.
We insist on the following properties:

\theenumxviii

If
\bigdoublevee\psi_{i}\in T
, then for some
i
,
\psi_{i}\in T
.

\theenumxviii

If
\exists\bar{y}\psi(\bar{y})\in T
, then
\psi(\bar{c})\in T
for some constants
\bar{c}\in C
.

\theenumxviii

If
\bigdoublewedge\psi_{i}\in T
, then for all
i
,
\psi_{i}\in T
.

\theenumxviii

If
\forall\bar{y}\psi(\bar{y})\in T
, then
\psi(\bar{c})\in T
for all
\bar{c}\in C
.

\theenumxviii

For every atomic sentence
\psi
over
\tau\cup C
, either
\psi\in T
or
\neg\psi\in T
.

\theenumxviii

For every
i
and tuple
\bar{c}
of length
|\bar{x}_{i}|
,
\varphi_{i}(\bar{c})\in T
.

\theenumxviii

For every tuple
\bar{c}
of length
|\bar{z}|
, there is a
\theta\in\Phi(\bar{z})
such that
\lnot\theta(\bar{c})\in T
.

Along the way, at each stage
s
, we will make sure that
T
is satisfiable in the sense that there is some interpretation
\nu_{s}:C\to\mathcal{M}
that assigns the constants we have used so far to a set of elements that satisfy all of the formulas in
T
that we have asserted about these constants.
If we can do this, the claim is shown, as the Henkin model will satisfy
\chi
by item (6) and omit
\Phi(\bar{z})
by item (7).

We begin with empty sets
T_{0}
,
C_{0}
, and
\nu_{0}
.
At each stage we are given
C_{s}
a finite subset of
C
,
T_{s}
, a finite set of
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formulas only mentioning constants
C_{s}
, and
\nu_{s}:C_{s}\to\mathcal{M}
with the property that
\mathcal{M}\models T_{s}(\nu_{s}(C_{s}))
.
At each stage, we address one of the properties
(1)-(7)
, one instance at a time.
We describe below how
T_{s}
,
C_{s}
, and
\nu_{s}
are modified to achieve this.

\theenumxix

Property
(1)
.
Given
\bigdoublevee\psi_{i}\in T_{s}
we know that
\mathcal{M}\models\bigdoublevee\psi_{i}(\nu_{s}(C_{s})))
.
Pick a
j
with the property that
\mathcal{M}\models\psi_{j}(\nu_{s}(C_{s})))
and add
\psi_{j}
to
T_{s}
to make
T_{s+1}
.
Let
\nu_{s}=\nu_{s+1}
and
C_{s+1}=C_{s}
.
It is straightforward to confirm that the desired properties are maintained.

\theenumxix

Property
(2)
.
Given
\exists\bar{y}\psi(\bar{y})\in T_{s}
we know that
\mathcal{M}\models\exists\bar{y}\psi(\bar{y},\nu_{s}(C_{s})))
.
Let
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
have the property that
\mathcal{M}\models\psi(\bar{a},\nu_{s}(C_{s}))
.
Take new constants
\bar{d}\in C
and let
C_{s+1}=C_{s}\cup\{\bar{d}\}
and
T_{s+1}=T_{s}\cup\{\psi(\bar{d})\}
.
Extend
\nu_{s}
by defining
\nu_{s+1}(\bar{d})=\bar{a}
.
It is straightforward to confirm that the desired properties are maintained.

\theenumxix

Property
(3)
.
Given
\bigdoublewedge\psi_{i}\in T_{s}
and
i\in\omega
let
T_{s+1}=T_{s}\cup\{\psi_{i}\}
,
C_{s+1}=C_{s}
and
\nu_{s+1}=\nu_{s}
.
It is straightforward to confirm that the desired properties are maintained.

\theenumxix

Property
(4)
.
Given
\forall\bar{y}\psi(\bar{y})\in T_{s}
and
\bar{c}\in C_{s}
of the appropriate length
T_{s+1}=T_{s}\cup\{\psi(\bar{c})\}
,
C_{s+1}=C_{s}
and
\nu_{s+1}=\nu_{s}
.
It is straightforward to confirm that the desired properties are maintained.

\theenumxix

Property
(5)
.
For any atomic sentence
\psi
and
\bar{c}\in C_{s}
of the appropriate length check if
\mathcal{M}\models\psi(\nu_{s}(\bar{c}))
or if
\mathcal{M}\models\lnot\psi(\nu_{s}(\bar{c}))
.
In the former case, add
\psi(\bar{c})
to
T_{s}
to obtain
T_{s+1}
and in the later case add
\lnot\psi(\bar{c})
to
T_{s}
to obtain
T_{s+1}
.
Either way, let
C_{s+1}=C_{s}
and
\nu_{s+1}=\nu_{s}
.
It is straightforward to confirm that the desired properties are maintained.

\theenumxix

Property
(6)
.
Given
\varphi_{i}
and
\bar{c}\in C_{s}
of the appropriate length
T_{s+1}=T_{s}\cup\{\varphi_{i}(\bar{c})\}
,
C_{s+1}=C_{s}
and
\nu_{s+1}=\nu_{s}
.
It is straightforward to confirm that the desired properties are maintained.

\theenumxix

Property
(7)
.
This is the key property of the type omitting argument and the only property that necessitates shifting
\nu_{s}
from its already established action to obtain
\nu_{s+1}
.
This step follows the standard type omitting playbook in a Henkin construction.
Fix a tuple
\bar{c}\in C_{s}
of length
|\bar{z}|
and let
\bar{d}
be the elements of
C_{s}
that are not among
\bar{c}
.
We can write
\bigwedge T_{s}=\rho(\bar{c},\bar{d})
where
\rho
is a
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formula.
Note that
\exists\bar{y}\rho(\bar{c},\bar{y})
is also a
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formula that is satisfied in
\mathcal{M}
by
\nu_{s}(\bar{c})
.
In particular, because
\Phi(\bar{z})
is not
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
supported, there is some
\bar{a}\in\mathcal{M}
that satisfies
\exists\bar{y}\rho(\bar{a},\bar{y})
but does not have type
\Phi(\bar{z})
.
Let
C_{s+1}=C_{s}
and let
T_{s+1}=T_{s}\cup\{\lnot\theta(\bar{c})\}
where
\theta\in\Phi(\bar{z})
has the property that
\mathcal{M}\models\lnot\theta(\bar{a})
.
Note that
\lnot\theta\in\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
as
\theta\in\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
, so this is an allowable extension of
T
.
Finally let
\nu_{s+1}(\bar{c})=\bar{a}
and let
\nu_{s+1}(\bar{d})=\bar{b}
where
\mathcal{M}\models\rho(\bar{a},\bar{b})
.
It is straightforward to confirm that the desired properties are maintained.

With this, we can achieve a model with properties
(1)-(7)
, and therefore the proof is complete.
∎

\forall\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence is equivalent to having one that is
\Pi_{\alpha+1}
.
This adds to a long list of equivalent conditions for having
\text{SR}(\mathcal{M})\leq\alpha
(see Theorem 1.1 of
[
22
]
). Parts of the proof below, in particular
(1)\implies(2)
and
(2)\implies(3)

7.7
.

The following are equivalent when the classes
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{E}
are restricted to
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1},\omega}
:

\theenumxx

\mathcal{M}
has a
\forall\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence.

\theenumxx

The
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
type of every tuple in
\mathcal{M}
is supported by an
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formula.

\theenumxx

Every automorphism orbit of every tuple in
\mathcal{M}
is definable by a
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formula.

\theenumxx

\mathcal{M}
has a
\Pi_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence, i.e.,
\text{SR}(\mathcal{M})\leq\alpha
.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
(1)\implies(2)
.
Say that
\mathcal{M}
has a
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
type
\Phi(\bar{z})
that is not supported by any
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formula.
Note that an
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
type is determined by its
\Pi_{\alpha}
restriction by Proposition
1.6
.
Also, note that
\Phi(\bar{z})
is not supported by any
\Sigma_{\alpha}
formula.
By Theorem
7.6
any
\chi\in\forall\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
is also true of a model
\mathcal{N}
that omits
\Phi
and therefore is not isomorphic to
\mathcal{N}
.

We now move to prove that
(2)\implies(3)
.
Let
\varphi_{\bar{a}}(\bar{x})
be the
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
formula that supports the
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
type of
\bar{a}
.
We claim that
\varphi_{\bar{a}}(\bar{x})
defines the automorphism orbit of
\bar{a}
.
To demonstrate this, we claim that the set

T:=\{(\bar{a},\bar{b})|\mathcal{M}\models\varphi_{\bar{a}}(\bar{b})\}

is a back-and-forth set.
As
\varphi_{\bar{a}}
supports the whole
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
type of
\bar{a}
it is certainly the case that for
(\bar{a},\bar{b})\in T
,
\bar{a}
and
\bar{b}
have the same atomic diagram.
Now consider
c\in\mathcal{M}
; we find a
d\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{M}\models\varphi_{\bar{a},c}(\bar{b},d)
.
In other words, we must show that
\mathcal{M}\models\exists x\varphi_{\bar{a},c}(\bar{b},x)
.
If this does not hold, we have that
\mathcal{M}\models\lnot\exists x\varphi_{\bar{a},c}(\bar{b},x)
.
Note that this is a
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
formula, therefore, it is implied by
\varphi_{\bar{a}}(\bar{b})
which isolates the
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
type of
\bar{b}
.
However, this means that
\mathcal{M}\models\lnot\exists x\varphi_{\bar{a},c}(\bar{a},x)
as
\mathcal{M}\models\varphi_{\bar{a}}(\bar{a})
.
That said,
\mathcal{M}\models\exists x\varphi_{\bar{a},c}(\bar{a},x)
as witnessed by
c
, a contradiction.
Therefore, there is such a
d
as required.

To see the “forth” part of the argument, it is enough to show that
\mathcal{M}\models\varphi_{\bar{a}}(\bar{b})\iff\varphi_{\bar{b}}(\bar{a})
and appeal to a symmetrical argument as the one above.
Say that
\mathcal{M}\models\varphi_{\bar{a}}(\bar{b})\land\lnot\varphi_{\bar{b}}(\bar{a})
.
As
\lnot\varphi_{\bar{b}}
is
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
, we obtain that it is implied by
\varphi_{\bar{a}}
which supports the whole
\mathfrak{A}_{\alpha}
type in
\mathcal{M}
.
This is a contradiction as it yields
\mathcal{M}\models\lnot\varphi_{\bar{b}}(\bar{b})
.
Therefore,
T
is a back-and-forth set and any
\bar{b}
with
\mathcal{M}\models\varphi_{\bar{a}}(\bar{b})
is automorphic to
\bar{a}
.

We now show that
(3)\implies(4)
.
This is just the same as showing that there is a
\Sigma_{\alpha}
description of any automorphism orbit in
\mathcal{M}
if there is a
\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
description of any automorphism orbit in
\mathcal{M}
.
This follows immediately from Lemma
7.5
.

Lastly, we note that
(4)\implies(1)
.
If
\text{SR}(\mathcal{M})\leq\alpha
then
\mathcal{M}
has a
\Pi_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence.
As every
\Pi_{\alpha+1}
formula is
\forall\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
, it follows that
\mathcal{M}
also has a
\forall\mathfrak{E}_{\alpha}
Scott sentence.
∎
