SIRT3 regulates mitochondrial metabolism through deacetylation of SLC25A6 to impact gastric cancer progression and drug resistance
Authors: Wenjun Meng, Chunlan Lang, Jiadi Gan, Xiaoli Mu, Liansha Tang, Jialing Wang, Yihao Liu, Yueting Zhu, Yang Du, Haoling Zhang, Qinqin He, Jiyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric cancer (GC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, largely due to late diagnosis, rapid progression, and the development of chemoresistance. Mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a critical driver of tumor progression and drug resistance. Sirtuin-3 (SIRT3), a mitochondrial NAD+ -dependent deacetylase, plays a context-dependent role in cancer biology; however, its function and underlying mechanism in GC progression and cisplatin (DDP) resistance remain unclear. This study aimed to explore the biological role and molecular mechanism of SIRT3 in GC.Materials and MethodsSIRT3 expression and prognostic value were analyzed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and validated in human GC tissues by qRT-PCR, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry. Knockdown and overexpression models of SIRT3 were established in AGS and HGC27 GC cell lines. Cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis were assessed using CCK-8, colony formation, Transwell, wound-healing, and flow cytometry assays. Cisplatin-resistant GC cell lines and nude mouse xenograft models were established to evaluate chemosensitivity in vitro and in vivo. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq), co-immunoprecipitation, acetylation assays, and cycloheximide chase assays were performed to identify downstream or upstream targets and mechanisms of SIRT3. Rescue experiments were conducted to confirm the dependency of SIRT3 and differentially expressed genes.ResultsSIRT3 expression was significantly upregulated in GC tissues and was associated with poor overall survival. SIRT3 knockdown markedly inhibited GC cell proliferation, invasion, migration, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and tumorigenesis, while promoting apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo. Conversely, SIRT3 overexpression enhanced malignant phenotypes. SIRT3 was significantly overexpressed in cisplatin-resistant GC tissues and cell lines, and its upregulation conferred resistance to DDP, whereas SIRT3 silencing sensitized GC cells to DDP in vitro and in xenograft models. RNA-seq identified SLC25A6 as a key downstream target of SIRT3. Mechanistically, SIRT3 directly interacted with SLC25A6 (ANT3/AAC3) and reduced its acetylation level, thereby enhancing its protein stability. SLC25A6 silencing phenocopied the effects of SIRT3 knockdown, and rescue experiments confirmed that the oncogenic and chemoresistant functions of SIRT3 were dependent on ANT3.ConclusionSIRT3 promotes GC progression and chemoresistance by deacetylating and stabilizing the mitochondrial ADP/ATP translocator SLC25A6 (ANT3), thereby enhancing mitochondrial metabolic activity. The SIRT3–ANT3 axis represents a novel molecular mechanism driving GC malignancy and chemoresistance and may serve as a promising therapeutic target for improving treatment efficacy in GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08404-7.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health burden with high incidence and mortality [1]. Although rates have declined due to improved control of risk factors such as Helicobacter pylori and diet, factors like high-salt intake, smoking, and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection continue to drive disease burden, especially in East Asia and developing countries [2]. Due to the lack of early symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, with a poor 5-year survival rate of about 10% [3]. In addition, significant intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity limits treatment response and the effectiveness of personalized therapy, while chemoresistance remains a major barrier to improving survival [4–6].

Drug resistance is a dynamic, multi-step process in most cancers. Emerging evidence highlights metabolic reprogramming, particularly mitochondrial dysfunction, as a key driver of chemoresistance [7]. However, current therapies targeting metabolic pathways show limited clinical benefit, suggesting that critical regulatory targets remain unclear [8]. Mitochondria play central roles in energy metabolism, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and drug resistance in multiple types of cancers including GC [9–12]. Platinum-based drugs such as cisplatin (DDP) induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitochondrial apoptosis, but tumor cells can counteract this by enhancing antioxidant capacity (e.g., SOD2) or mitochondrial biogenesis (e.g., PGC-1α) [13]. Targeting mitochondrial function and oxidative stress may therefore offer new strategies to overcome GC drug resistance.

Sirtuin-3 (SIRT3) is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent mitochondrial deacetylase in mammals and is a member of the Sirtuin (SIRT) family [14]. The SIRT family is considered a key molecular hub connecting energy metabolism and disease development, and SIRT3 has attracted more attention because it exhibits the strongest mitochondrial deacetylase activity. However, to date, there is still a lack of systematic understanding of the role and mechanism of SIRT3 in various diseases in the human body. In particular, many existing studies indicated that SIRT3 has a contradictory dual role in regulating the occurrence, development, metabolic reprogramming, metastasis and treatment resistance of different tumors [15]. Its role in tumors has a “double-edged sword” effect: on the one hand, SIRT3 exerts anti-tumor effects by maintaining redox homeostasis; on the other hand, under chemotherapy or nutritional stress, SIRT3 can enhance mitochondrial metabolic flexibility and promote tumor survival and drug resistance [15]. Currently, the expression pattern of SIRT3 in GC and its exact role in chemoresistance are not yet clear, especially the mechanism by which it affects energy metabolism by regulating downstream target proteins through deacetylation still lacks systematic study.

Therefore, this study constructed SIRT3 knockdown and overexpression models as well as DDP-resistant GC cell models, and employed high-throughput transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) to identify differentially expressed genes. Through both in vivo and in vitro experiments, we investigated the role and molecular mechanisms of SIRT3 in GC development and DDP resistance, aiming to further elucidate the biological functions of SIRT3 in GC, provide a new theoretical basis for GC drug resistance mechanisms, offer potential target support for clinically reversing drug resistance and optimizing GC treatment strategies, and provide new insights for future GC therapeutic approaches.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis methods

RNA-seq and clinical data for various cancers were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database using the “TCGAbiolinks” package (version 2.30.4) in R software (version 4.1.2) for analysis. The relationship between SIRT3 expression and clinicopathological features in the TCGA database was analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the Log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to investigate the predictive value of SIRT3 expression and clinicopathological features for prognosis. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to analyze the prognostic survival of SIRT3 expression in GC from the TCGA database. The expression differences of SIRT3 across all cancer types were cyclically analyzed using the “DESeq2” package (version 1.20.0), with log2(Fold Change)>2 and p < 0.05 used as the screening threshold.

Extraction of total RNA in gastric cancer samples

GC and adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgical resection at West China Hospital, Sichuan University. This study involves human participants and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital. It was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The informed consent has been obtained directly from the patients and the families. Participants gave informed consent to participate in the study before taking part. Additionally, DDP-sensitive and DDP-resistant GC cases were included. All tissues were paraffin-embedded or stored at −80 °C for subsequent quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), Western blot (WB), and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses. IHC staining was evaluated using the H-score method, calculated as intensity×percentage of positive cells. Scoring was performed independently by two blinded pathologists.

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. Tissue samples were ground in liquid nitrogen, homogenized, and treated with chloroform. After centrifugation, the aqueous phase was collected, and RNA was precipitated with isopropanol. The pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and dissolved in diethylpyrocarbonate-treated water. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

Quantitative real-time PCR, Western blotting, and immunohistochemistry

Reverse transcription was performed using the M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase system. RNA (2 μg) was denatured at 70 °C for 5 min with Oligo(dT) primers, then reverse-transcribed at 42 °C for 60 min followed by 72 °C for 15 min. The resulting cDNA was treated with RNase H and stored at −20 °C.

Proteins were separated using 12% pre-cast gels at 200 V for 30–50 min. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to methanol-activated polyvinylidene fluoride membranes in transfer buffer (25 mM Bicine, 25 mM Bis-Tris, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 0.05 mM chlorobutanol, 20% methanol) at 260 mA for 1.5–2 h on ice. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk, incubated with primary antibody (1:1000) at 4 °C overnight, washed, and then incubated with Horseradish Peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10000) for 2 h at room temperature. After further washing, signals were detected using an imaging system and quantified with ImageJ software (version 1.8.0).

Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and baked at 60 °C for 12 h. Sections underwent deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating in citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0) using a microwave. After phosphate buffer saline (PBS) washes and treatment with 1% periodic acid, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by Horseradish Peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody incubation. Diaminobenzidine was applied for 1–5 min for chromogenic detection, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, slides were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, mounted with neutral gum, and imaged under a microscope at × 100, ×200, and × 400 magnifications for quantitative analysis of positive staining areas.

Cell culture and transfection

SIRT3 overexpressing and SIRT3 knockdown cell lines were constructed by culturing and transfecting four types of GC cell lines (AGS, HGC27, MKN45, NCI-N87), which were obtained from the Shanghai Institute for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were maintained under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2), passaged at 80% confluence using 0.5% trypsin, and cryopreserved in freezing medium. For transfection, AGS and HGC27 cells were seeded at 6 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates. Before transfection, the medium was replaced with serum-free Opti-MEM. A transfection complex was prepared by mixing siRNA (2 μg) with P3000 reagent in Opti-MEM, incubated for 10–15 min, and added to the cells. After 4–6 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium. Cells were harvested at 24 h and 72 h for subsequent analyses.

Construction of knockdown and overexpression vectors

The SIRT3 full-length sequence was amplified by PCR using GES‑1 cell cDNA as template, followed by gel extraction and purification. The pcDNA™3.1 vector and SIRT3 fragment were digested with restriction enzymes, ligated using T4 DNA ligase, and transformed into DH5α competent cells. Positive clones were selected, cultured, and sequenced to confirm correct insertion. Verified plasmids were subsequently extracted at a large scale for transfection. The primer sequences and interference for the SIRT3 sequence are listed in Supplementary Table S1. SIRT3 overexpression full‑length coding sequence is listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Lentiviral packaging, purification, titer determination and cell infection

Lentivirus was packaged in HEK293T cells cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). At 70–80% confluence, cells were transfected using the P3000 reagent with a recombinant plasmid and helper plasmids (pMD2.G, pCMV-VSV-G, pRSV-Rev). After 4–6 h, the medium was replaced, and supernatants were collected at 48 h and 72 h. The virus was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, concentrated using a 30 kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube, sterilized via 0.22 μm filtration, and stored at −80 °C. Viral titer was quantified by ELISA. For infection, target cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 30–50% density, incubated with virus suspension and Polybrene (10 μg/mL) for 12 h, then cultured in fresh medium for 48–96 h. GFP-positive cells were sorted by flow cytometry and maintained in medium with double antibiotics before returning to standard culture conditions.

Construction of drug-resistant cell lines

The GC cell lines (AGS and HGC27) were treated with gradually increasing concentrations of DDP, starting from 0.2 μg/mL. After stable growth was achieved at each concentration, the DDP dose was incrementally raised until the cells could proliferate normally in medium containing 1 μg/mL DDP. The resulting DDP-resistant sublines, designated AGS/DDP and HGC27/DDP, were capable of tolerating DDP at a concentration of 1 μg/mL.

Detection of cell proliferation capacity

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and plate colony formation assay. For the CCK-8 assay, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at 2,000 cells per well. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well at days 1–5, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 3 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

For the colony formation assay, AGS and HGC27 cells were trypsinized 48 h post-transfection and plated at 1,000 cells per well. After treatment, cells were cultured for 14 days with medium renewal every 5–7 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with Giemsa, and air-dried. The number of colonies was counted manually using a transparent grid, and the colony formation rate was calculated as (number of colonies / number of seeded cells)×100%.

Detection of cell invasion and migration capacity

Transwell assays were utilized to detect cell invasion and migration capacity with a pore size of 8.0 μm. Cells were transfected and, after 48 hours, trypsinized, resuspended in serum-free medium, and seeded into the upper chamber at 3.0 × 104 cells per well (150 μL). The lower chamber was filled with 600 μL complete medium containing 10% FBS. After 12–18 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-invading cells were removed, and cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with Giemsa, and counted under an inverted microscope.

Then, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected at 90–95% confluency. After 48 hours, they were serum-starved with 0.5% FBS for 12 h. A uniform scratch (~1 mm wide) was created using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. After washing with PBS, the wound closure was monitored in the same fields at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h using an inverted microscope. Migration ability was quantified by measuring scratch width using the ImageJ software (version 1.8.0) via “Wound Healing Coherency Tool” plugin [16].

Detection of apoptosis capacity

Cells were washed with PBS and binding buffer, resuspended at 1–5 × 106/mL, and stained with Annexin V-FITC for 10–15 min. After washing, propidium iodide was added, and samples were analyzed by flow cytometry within 4 h.

Animal models and in vivo experiments

This study involves animals and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade male and female nude mice (BALB/c, 4–6 weeks old) were purchased from Charls River Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). They were placed in the animal room of our laboratory, and acclimatized for one week in a temperature-controlled, humidity-controlled, and sterile barrier system before animal experiments were conducted.

Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, 18 g) were randomly divided into control (Ctrl) and OE-SIRT3 groups (n = 5). AGS cells (1 × 107) suspended in 100 μL PBS were subcutaneously injected into each mouse. Tumor volume was measured and calculated as length×width2×0.5. On day 22, mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation, and tumors were excised for imaging and Ki-67 detection by IHC.

Then, male BALB/c nude mice (5–6 weeks old, 20 g) were divided into four groups (n = 5): shCtrl, shSIRT3, shCtrl+DDP, and shSIRT3+DDP. Mice were subcutaneously injected with stably transfected AGS cells (2 × 106). The DDP-treated groups received tail vein injections of DDP (2 mg/kg) every 7 days, while controls received saline. Body weight and tumor volume were measured every 3 days from day 7 to day 32. Mice were euthanized, and tumors were weighed and analyzed for Ki-67 expression. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, and complied with NIH guidelines.

Transcriptome sequencing and gene set enrichment analysis

Total RNA was extracted from shCtrl and shSIRT3 transfected AGS cells. After fragmentation and reverse transcription, cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., USA). Read counts were quantified using StringTie software (version 1.3.4d), and differential expression analysis was performed with R package “DESeq2”, applying the “nbinomWaldTest” and Fold Change criteria. Genes with zero counts were excluded, and data were log2-transformed and normalized. p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Hierarchical clustering was conducted using complete linkage and Euclidean distance. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out using DAVID (https://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/), OmicStudio (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool), and Cytoscape (version 3.7.0). A list of the top 40 upregulated and downregulated expressed genes from RNA-seq has been included in Supplementary Table S4.

Statistical analysis

All our experiments were validated through three independent replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using Graphpad Prism 8 (GraphPad, USA) and SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM, USA). For comparisons between two groups, if they were normally distributed and had homogeneous variances, an unpaired Student’s t-test was used; if they were normally distributed but had unequal variances, an unpaired Welch’s t-test was used; if they were not normally distributed, a nonparametric test (Mann-Whitney test) was used. Data are presented as the mean±SD of three independent trials. A p-value < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference (* indicates p < 0.05, ** indicates p < 0.01, and *** indicates p < 0.001).

Kaplan-Meier curves showed a significant decrease in overall survival in the high expression group (Area Under Curve, AUC = 77.1%), suggesting that SIRT3 has predictive value for prognosis (Supplementary Fig. S1). In vitro and in vivo experiments investigated SIRT3 overexpression on the development and progression of gastric cancer

Next, WB analysis first confirmed robust overexpression of SIRT3 in both GC cell lines (Fig. 3A), establishing a reliable basis for subsequent functional assays. Functionally, SIRT3 overexpression led to a marked enhancement of proliferative capacity, as evidenced by an upward shift in the growth curve and a significant increase in colony formation efficiency (Fig. 3B–C), indicating a growth-promoting role of SIRT3 in GC cells. Consistently, Transwell and wound-healing assays demonstrated that SIRT3 overexpression significantly enhanced migratory and invasive abilities (Fig. 3D–F), suggesting a pro-metastatic phenotype. Mechanistically, this phenotype was accompanied by a molecular shift characteristic of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), including upregulation of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin and Vimentin) and downregulation of the epithelial marker E-cadherin (Fig. 3G). Together with the preceding findings, these data support the notion that SIRT3 promotes metastatic potential, at least in part, through activation of EMT programs. In addition to its effects on proliferation and motility, SIRT3 overexpression significantly suppressed apoptosis. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a reduced apoptotic rate, which was further corroborated by decreased Caspase-3/7 activity compared with control cells (Fig. 3H–I), indicating an anti-apoptotic role of SIRT3 in GC cells. Importantly, these in vitro findings were recapitulated in vivo. In a subcutaneous xenograft model, SIRT3-overexpressing tumors exhibited significantly accelerated growth, with increased tumor volume and weight at endpoint. Immunohistochemical analysis (Ki-67 index: 41.7%±5.2% vs. 68.3%±6.5%, p < 0.001) further confirmed elevated expression of proliferation markers in the OE-SIRT3 group (Fig. 3J–M). Next, previously constructed DDP-resistant AGS and HGC27 cell lines (denoted as AGS/DDP and HGC27/DDP, respectively) were utilized to perform qPCR and WB to compare with normal DDP-sensitive AGS and HGC27 cells. Figure 4E shows the qPCR results, which indicate that the relative expression levels of mRNA and protein in AGS/DDP and HGC27/DDP were significantly upregulated (both p < 0.001). Finally, a colony formation assay was used to further evaluate and verify the long-term effect of SIRT3 on the DDP sensitivity of GC cells. SIRT3 acetylation modification of proteins can generally affect protein stability. To investigate the effect of SIRT3 on the stability of SLC25A6, we treated GC cells with cycloheximide (CHX), a commonly used protein synthesis inhibitor, and then examined the effect of SIRT3 expression on SLC25A6 stability. The results showed that SLC25A6 stability was significantly increased after overexpressing SIRT3, as evidenced by a slower rate of SLC25A6 protein degradation (Fig. 6F). Discussion

Biologically, SIRT3 function is context-dependent: in certain metabolic contexts, it promotes mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and reduces ROS, exhibiting tumor suppressor characteristics; in other contexts, SIRT3’s support for tumor metabolism may promote tumor growth [15, 17]. To investigate its potential functions and pathways, this study systematically explored the role and regulatory mechanism of SIRT3 in the development and progression of GC and chemoresistance.

Our experiments found that shSIRT3 inhibited GC cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and migration, and promoted apoptosis; while OE-SIRT3 promoted GC cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and migration, and inhibited apoptosis. In recent years, many studies have explored the role of SIRT3 in various types of cancers and its molecular mechanisms, but remaining contradictory conclusions [18–23]. They demonstrated the double-edged sword nature of SIRT3, and the unique heterogeneity of GC may explain this phenomenon: in normal gastric mucosa, SIRT3 maintains genomic stability by clearing ROS and plays an anti-cancer role; but in GC’s tumor tissue, it promotes cell survival and metastasis by metabolically adapting and reshaping the microenvironment, such as activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and exhibits a significant pro-cancer phenotype. Studies have shown that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a key role in the development and progression of various tumors. It can promote tumor growth by regulating stem cell characteristics, EMT and apoptosis-related gene expression [24]. In our current study, we found through gene enrichment analysis that the differentially enriched genes in the SIRT3 high/low expression group were mainly enriched in the Wnt signaling pathway. Then, through Transwell assays, we found that OE-SIRT3 promoted EMT in GC cells, while shSIRT3 inhibited EMT. These findings suggested that SIRT3 may regulate GC through Wnt-related signal transduction targeting EMT. Small molecule inhibitors targeting these targets have been developed, and more trials are needed in the future to verify our hypothesis [25].

Our study demonstrated that high expression of SIRT3 enhanced the drug resistance of GC cells to DDP, while knockdown of SIRT3 significantly increased the sensitivity of GC cells to DDP. However, there are few previous studies exploring the effect of SIRT3 on chemoresistance, remaining unclear mechanism. In ovarian cancer, ABT737 (a BCL2 protein-specific inhibitor) can effectively reverse DDP resistance by activating SIRT3 [26]. In colorectal cancer, clinical cohort showed high SIRT3 was significantly associated with poor prognosis, and its molecular mechanism may involve the regulation of antioxidant pathways, and knockout of this gene can significantly improve the therapeutic benefit of oxaliplatin [27]. These pieces of evidence suggest that studying the mechanism by which SIRT3 induces or inhibits drug resistance and targeting SIRT3 activity may become a promising direction for breaking through the current situation.

SIRT3 plays an important role in regulating mitochondrial metabolism and oxidative stress. It has been reported to regulate a variety of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes (such as SOD2, IDH2, PDH, AceCS2, etc.) through deacetylation, thereby affecting ROS levels, oxidative phosphorylation and energy metabolism [28–30]. On the other hand, ADP/ATP carriers (AACs) in the SLC25 family not only undertake the basic function of ADP/ATP exchange, but also participate in the regulation of mitochondrial permeability transition (mPTP) and apoptosis in various models [31–34]. Lysine acetylation may alter ADP/ATP binding and transport efficiency by changing carrier surface charge, disrupting/stabilizing specific conformations, or affecting interactions with chaperone proteins [14]. It directly affects mitochondrial ATP production and membrane potential (MMP), and indirectly influences mPTP openability and apoptosis sensitivity [10]. Furthermore, SIRT3’s regulation of deacetylation in multiple mitochondrial antioxidant and metabolic enzymes (such as SOD2 and PDH) suggests that SIRT3-mediated deacetylation may synergistically influence cellular responses to chemotherapy through both metabolic flux and ROS levels [35, 36]. Based on the above background and our RNA-seq results, we proposed and verified a testable model in this study: SIRT3 may regulate the stability/function of SLC25A6 through deacetylation, thereby affecting the mitochondrial energy metabolism and chemotherapy sensitivity of gastric cancer cells. The regulatory relationship between SIRT3 and SLC25A6 has been firstly revealed, and we found that the expression of SLC25A6 and SIRT3 is strongly positively correlated. SLC25A6, also known as adenine nucleotide translocator 3 (ANT3) and AAC3, is an adenine nucleotide translocator located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, responsible for ADP/ATP exchange between mitochondria and cytoplasm, and plays a key role in maintaining mitochondrial function and energy metabolism [31]. As one of the mitochondrial AACs, ANT3 may play a role in maintaining ATP supply in tumor cells with high metabolic demand. Tumor cells maintain high levels of ATP synthesis by enhancing the expression of ANT3 to support rapid proliferation and survival [31]. We demonstrated in this study that shSLC25A6 and shSIRT3 have similar effects on GC cells. Then, through rescue experiments, we verified the causal relationship between SIRT3 and ANT3, indicating that SIRT3 may be an upstream gene regulating SLC25A6, regulating the expression of ANT3, and mediating its regulation of energy metabolism and survival in GC cells. From the perspective of molecular mechanism, SIRT3 may promote the nuclear translocation of the transcription factor FOXO3a (K242 site) by deacetylation, and then bind to the FoxO response element in the promoter region of ANT3 to activate its transcription [37]. Although a regulatory association between FOXO3a and SIRT3 and its potential to influence mitochondrial gene expression were previously reported, we did not perform ChIP-qPCR or reporter assays in this study to directly demonstrate the direct binding or activity regulation of FOXO3a to ANT3. Therefore, the hypothesis regarding FOXO3a-mediated ANT3 transcription is presented as a hypothesis for future validation.

In summary, our study found that targeting the SIRT3–ANT3 axis has the potential to inhibit tumor cell development and enhance chemosensitivity by interfering with tumor energy metabolism. However, it should be clear that this study currently provides evidence of the regulatory axis based on cells, animal models and histological evidence; direct proof of specific acetylation sites and in vitro catalytic deacetylation activity still requires further mass spectrometry and in vitro experiments to confirm.

Limitations

This study needs to address several limitations in the future: Firstly, the primary evidence in this study is still mainly correlational. SLC25A6 is only one of many candidates among differentially expressed genes. Our priority validation was based on a rigorous screening process, including statistical significance, reproducibility in TCGA, and its biological priority as an ADP/ATP transporter in energy metabolism, but these are insufficient to rule out other parallel mechanisms. Secondly, direct evidence regarding the mechanistic pathway is lacking, because there is a lack of immunoenrichment-LC-MS/MS site identification, Lysine-to-arginine (K→R) and lysine-to-glutamine (K→Q) site-functional mutation verification, in vitro recombinant SIRT3 deacetylation activity assay, or shSIRT3 rescue experiment to demonstrate the direct catalytic deacetylation of SLC25A6 by SIRT3 and the site-function causal chain. Furthermore, this study has not yet completed direct measurements of mitochondrial flux/energies, such as Seahorse OCR/ATP yield calculation, membrane potential/ROS measurement, and mPTP openability testing, which formed a standard method for evaluating metabolic reprogramming and changes in membrane permeability [38–40].

Conclusion

This study found that SIRT3 plays a key role in GC development and DDP resistance. Upregulation of SIRT3 enhances the proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and migration of GC cells, inhibits apoptosis, and enhances GC cell resistance to cisplatin. Furthermore, SIRT3 function depends on SLC25A6 (ANT3/AAC3), revealing that SLC25A6 may be a downstream key gene regulated by SIRT3. Therefore, SIRT3–ANT3 holds promise as a potential therapeutic target for GC treatment.
