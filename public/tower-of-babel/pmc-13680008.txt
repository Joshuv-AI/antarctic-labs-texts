Ambient fine particulate matter components and liver fat and stiffness in youth: metabolic vulnerability in PNPLA3 risk carriers
Authors: Haonan Li, Rachel B. Schenker, Katherine M. Marquess, Devendra Paudel, Cuauhtemoc B. Ramirez, Fredrick Lurmann, Mengyuan Ren, Cholsoon Jang, Hooman Allayee, Jiawen Liao, Zhanghua Chen, Michael I. Goran, Tanya L. Alderete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & aims:Exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is linked to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), yet responsible chemical constituents and the biological basis of susceptibility remain unclear, limiting source-targeted regulation and precision prevention in vulnerable populations. We examined PM2.5 components – liver outcomes associations in youth, assessed susceptibility by PNPLA3 genotype (the strongest known MASLD genetic determinant), and evaluated genotype-specific metabolic mediation in two independent cohorts.Methods:Baseline data from 113 Los Angeles Latino adolescents with obesity (discovery cohort) were analyzed, including magnetic resonance imaging-measured hepatic fat fraction (HFF), liver stiffness (LS), and serum metabolomics and lipidomics. Visit-year average exposures to 15 PM2.5 components were estimated from residential addresses. Linear regression and g-computation assessed individual component and mixture effects, with PNPLA3 genotype as modifier. Moderated mediation analyses identified metabolic mediators, followed by pathway enrichment and total mediation effect estimation. Findings were replicated in 81 young adults of mixed race/ethnicity and body mass index (replication cohort) using level 1 metabolites confirmed by authentic standards.Results:PM2.5 components and mixture were predominantly positively associated with HFF and LS; simultaneous interquartile range increases in all components were associated with 10.0% higher HFF. Elemental carbon (EC), bromine (Br), copper, potassium, and lead were key contributors. Stronger effects were observed in PNPLA3 GG carriers. GG carriers exhibited more mediating metabolite features (n = 550 vs. 196), with broader pathway enrichment in carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, and signaling/endocrine pathways. Significant total mediation effects were observed exclusively in GG carriers. Replication confirmed GG-specific associations of EC, Br with HFF, and identified increased fatty acid 16:1, and decreased guanidinosuccinate, proline as candidate mediators.Conclusion:PM2.5 components potentially indicative of combustion and traffic sources (i.e. EC, Br) increase MASLD risk in youth via metabolic pathways, with genotype-specific responses likely heightening susceptibility in PNPLA3 GG carriers.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has become the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide (Targher et al., 2025). It is associated with hepatic complications, as well as an increased risk of cardiometabolic and other extrahepatic diseases, with particularly concerning lifetime implications when occurring in youth (Doycheva et al., 2017). Lifestyle interventions remain the cornerstone of MASLD management (Vos et al., 2017; Rinella et al., 2023). Yet accumulating epidemiologic evidence suggests that reducing exposure to air pollutants, such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5; particles ≤ 2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter), may offer an additional population-level strategy (Li et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2022; Ran et al., 2025). However, critical gaps remain, which limit causal inference and the development of effective and targeted interventions. These include characterization of PM2.5 components and emission sources driving effects, elucidation of underlying mechanisms, and understanding of susceptible populations (Colombo and Barouki, 2022).

PM2.5 is a major toxic constituent of air pollution and an important risk factor for MASLD. Although PM2.5 mass concentration is commonly used to estimate exposure and health risk, PM2.5 is indeed a complex mixture comprising chemical compositions varied spatial-temporally (Kelly and Fussell, 2012; Liang et al., 2016). Individual components exhibit distinct toxicities, and reliance on PM2.5 mass alone may thus yield imprecise risk estimates. Characterizing PM2.5 composition can provide insights into emission sources and guide targeted interventions as well, as different emission sources generate distinct chemical profiles. Yet, studies examining associations between specific PM2.5 components or component mixtures and MASLD are scarce and have largely focused on high-abundance components with relatively low toxicity and limited source-specific information (Guo et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024).

Metabolic dysfunction is central to MASLD pathogenesis and downstream consequences (Targher et al., 2025). It involves systemic and intrahepatic disturbances, including excessive lipid accumulation, insulin resistance, proinflammatory lipid signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and alterations in hepatic lipid handling (Stefan et al., 2025). Evidence also indicates that air pollution contributes to widespread metabolic disturbances, implicating lipid and fatty acid metabolism, energy metabolism, and oxidative stress–related pathways relevant to MASLD development (Liang et al., 2023). In this context, advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies have positioned metabolomics as a powerful approach for systematically investigating air pollution–related MASLD pathogenesis by enabling the comprehensive identification and quantification of thousands of metabolic features, substantially extending earlier evidence derived from a limited subset of metabolites (Ran et al., 2025; Patterson et al., 2025).

Heritability is estimated to account for approximately 50% of interindividual variation in MASLD, with the PNPLA3 I148M variant (rs738409 C > G) being the strongest known genetic contributor (Moretti et al., 2024). Carriers of this variant have an increased risk of MASLD onset and accelerated disease progression (Souza et al., 2025). Our previous studies in adolescents have found that air pollutants such as PM10 (Particles ≤ 10 μm), and oxidative gaseous air pollutants were associated with increased liver fat and stiffness with stronger effects observed in PNPLA3 GG carriers compared to GC/CC carriers (Patterson et al., 2025; Schenker et al., 2024). We therefore hypothesize that PM2.5 component exposure may interact with this genetic susceptibility as well, jointly amplifying MASLD risk.

These considerations are especially relevant for Latino adolescents with obesity, who bear a disproportionate burden of MASLD (Cumpian et al., 2025). The PNPLA3 rs738409 G risk allele is more prevalent in Hispanic/Latino populations than in other ancestral groups (Romeo et al., 2008). In addition, obesity is a major metabolic driver of hepatic steatosis and has been shown to amplify the effect of the PNPLA3 rs738409 G allele on liver fat accumulation (Stender et al., 2017). Together, these metabolic and genetic susceptibilities may make this population particularly sensitive to environmental influences on liver health and an ideal setting for investigating gene–environment interactions involving PM2.5 components.

Here, we report novel associations between PM2.5 component and MASLD risk in youth, in the context of genetic susceptibility, and assessed whether specific metabolic pathways mediate PNPLA3 genotype-specific effects (Fig. 1). A broad set of PM2.5 components, including key toxic constituents and source indicators (2 carbonaceous, 3 secondary inorganic salts, and 10 trace elements) was incorporated alongside metabolomics and lipidomics data, covering a wide range of hydrophilic and lipophilic metabolites, yielding a total of 7,943 features. Hepatic fat fraction (HFF) and liver stiffness (LS), both serving as diagnostic indicators and markers of disease severity for MASLD, were measured to represent MASLD risk. Leveraging data from two Los Angeles studies in Latino adolescents with obesity (discovery cohort), we aim to (1) investigate the associations between specific PM2.5 components and the overall component mixture with HFF and LS; (2) examine how these associations are modified by PNPLA3 genotype (GC/CC vs. GG); and (3) explore the metabolic pathways whose disruption may underline these effects. In addition, we incorporated an independent replication cohort with mixed race/ethnicity and body mass index (BMI), along with level 1 metabolomics data confirmed using authentic chemical standards (Schymanski et al., 2014), to validate and extend our findings. Understanding these could help identify toxic PM2.5 components and sources, clarify biological pathways linking air pollution to MASLD, and define vulnerable populations, thereby informing targeted pollution control, precision approaches for MASLD prevention, and protection of susceptible youth.

Methods

Participants and study design of discovery cohort

Our analysis incorporated baseline data from two prior studies conducted in the greater Los Angeles area, leveraging their comparable participant characteristics and study protocols. The first, the Healthy Eating through Reduction of Excess Sugar (HEROES) trial, was a 12-week randomized controlled intervention in 105 Latino adolescents with obesity, designed to evaluate the impact of dietary sugar intake reduction on hepatic fat accumulation (Schmidt et al., 2022). The second, the Probiotic trial, was a 16-week randomized controlled trial (RCT) in 19 obese Latino adolescents, assessing the effects of probiotic supplementation on gut microbiota composition, liver fat, and obesity-related outcomes (Jones et al., 2018). HEROES participants were recruited through pediatric gastroenterology clinics at the University of Southern California (USC) and Children's Hospital Los Angeles (CHLA) as well as community outreach efforts between October 2016 and March 2020, whereas Probiotic participants were recruited from neighborhoods surrounding USC between March and November 2015. Detailed eligibility criteria for both studies have been reported before. In brief, both studies recruited Latino/Hispanic adolescents (aged 11–18 in the HEROES study and 12–18 in the Probiotic study) with obesity [defined as age- and sex-specific BMI > 95th percentile] and excluded individuals with conditions, medications, behaviors, or recent treatments (including antibiotics) that could influence metabolic, liver, immune, or gut function, or that involved pregnancy, smoking, alcohol, or recreational drug use.

Baseline visits for both studies occurred prior to any interventions. Data collected included anthropometrics, dietary intake, physical activity, HFF, LS, whole blood samples, and fasting serum samples. The Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015; total score range: 0–100) was calculated from the Nutrition Data System for Research (NDSR) daily dietary intake output (Krebs-Smith et al., 2018). For each participant, the mean HEI-2015 score across all 24-hour dietary recalls collected at the baseline study visit was used. Physical activity was quantified as the mean metabolic equivalent of task (MET) score derived from a 3-day physical activity recall (Pate et al., 2003). The whole blood samples were later used for PNPLA3 genotyping, whereas the serum samples were used for metabolomics and lipidomics analyses. HEROES participants additionally provided residential history for the year prior to visits, whereas Probiotic participants reported their current residential address; these were subsequently used to estimate air pollutant exposures.

All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. Informed consent was obtained from all participants aged 18 years or older; for those under 18, parental/guardian consent and participant assent were obtained. Both studies were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of USC (HS-11–00446) and Johns Hopkins University (IRB00035333) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov [NCT02948647 (HEROES), NCT03115385 (Probiotic)].

Anthropometrics and liver fat and liver stiffness measures

Height and weight of participants were measured using standard procedures, and BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared.

HFF was assessed using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on a 3-Tesla scanner (GE Healthcare) with the Iterative Decomposition of water and fat with Echo Asymmetry and Least-squares estimation (IDEAL) method (Hu et al., 2010). During scanning, a 6-echo T2*IDEAL sequence was used to acquire signal intensities at multiple echo times, allowing separation of water and fat signals while accounting for T2 decay and the multi-peak spectral characteristics of fat. Eight to nine contiguous axial slices spanning from the top of the liver to the L5 vertebra level were each acquired during 20 s breath-holds to minimize motion artifacts. Images were reconstructed using fat spectrum modeling, and the liver was segmented from the volumetric data using Slice-O-Matic software (Tomovision). HFF was quantified by calculating the fat signal fraction for each voxel and then averaging these voxel-level values across the segmented liver region.

LS was assessed using magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) on a 3-Tesla scanner (GE Healthcare) equipped with the Mayo Clinic–developed MRE vibration driver system and proprietary software (Sawh et al., 2020). During scanning, the driver was positioned against the lower right chest to generate 60 Hz vibrations. A synchronized motion-encoded MRE sequence was then performed, acquiring four axial slices over the widest portion of the liver. Imaging parameters were echo time (TE) = 20 ms, repetition time (TR) = 50 ms, field of view (FOV) = 30–48 cm, matrix = 256 × 64, phase offsets = 4, and band-width (BW) = 31 kHz. Each slice was obtained during a 10–20 s end-expiration breath-hold. The scanner generated wave images, which were converted into elastograms depicting the spatial distribution of liver shear stiffness (|G*|, in kilopascals). LS was calculated as the average liver shear stiffness, representing the resistance of liver tissue to shear deformation and serving as a non-invasive measure of liver fibrosis.

Ambient PM2.5 and its components exposure estimates

Participants’ residential addresses were geocoded to latitude and longitude using the Texas A&M Geocoder (Texas A&M University). Daily ambient PM2.5 concentrations at each location were estimated using an inverse distance-squared weighting (IDW2) algorithm, based on hourly measurements from up to four nearby monitors within 50 km in the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Air Quality System and the California Air Resources Board Air Quality Monitoring Information System (AQMIS) network. One-year averages prior to the baseline visit were then computed for each location.

Annual average concentrations of 15 PM2.5 chemical components were also assigned for the calendar year of the baseline visit (or for the preceding year for nine participants assessed in 2020, as component data were available only through 2019). Components included 2 carbonaceous components [elemental carbon (EC) and organic carbon (OC)], 3 secondary inorganic salts [sulfate (SO42−), nitrate (NO3–), and ammonium (NH4+)], and 10 trace elements [bromine (Br), calcium (Ca), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), potassium (K), nickel (Ni), lead (Pb), silicon (Si), vanadium (V), and zinc (Zn)]. Component concentrations were derived from machine-learning–based exposure models with spatial resolutions of 50 m in urban areas and 1 km in non-urban areas across the contiguous U.S. (Amini et al., 2022; Amini et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2022). These models were trained on daily component measurements collected between 2000 and 2019 from 987 monitoring sites nationwide, using 166 predictors including spatiotemporal covariates, satellite observations, reanalysis data, and emissions indicators. These 15 components constitute the complete set available from the machine-learning–based exposure models, reflecting the availability of sufficient monitoring data to support model training and validation. The trace elements are recognized as important contributors to PM2.5 toxicity and collectively represent diverse periodic-table groups with distinct physicochemical and source characteristics (Jin et al., 2022).

For HEROES participants who moved within the year preceding the baseline visit, exposures were time-weighted by duration spent at each location. For Probiotic participants, only baseline residential addresses were available; therefore, we assumed residence at these locations for the full prior year and used the pollutant concentrations at those locations to assign exposure levels.

IDW2 performance for California PM2.5 has been previously evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (Eckel et al., 2016). Monthly PM2.5 estimates demonstrated a mean residual of −0.4 μg/m3, a mean relative absolute residual of ±31%, and a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.53. The PM2.5 component models showed strong predictive performance overall, with testing sets R2 values ranging from 0.821 to 0.975. In California, validation against independent monitoring data showed moderate to high agreement for EC (R2 = 0.55), OC (0.63), NO3– (0.77), NH4+ (0.62), Ca (0.53), Si (0.65), and Zn (0.92) whereas correlations were weaker for SO42− (0.21), Fe (0.26), and K (0.09) (Li et al., 2026).

PNPLA3 genotyping

To determine PNPLA3 genotype, genomic DNA was extracted from 5 mL whole blood samples using DNeasy kits (Cat # 69506; Qiagen). Genotyping of the PNPLA3 rs738409 polymorphism (C444G; Ile148-Met) was performed by restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis, following previously published methods with minor modifications (Dutta, 2011). PCR amplification was conducted using forward primer 5′-TGGGCCTGAAGTCCGAGGGT-3′ and reverse primer 5′-CCGACACCAGTGCCCTGCAG-3′. Each 25 μL PCR reaction contained 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM of each deoxyribose nucleotide triphosphate (dNTP), 0.5 μM of each primer, 20 ng genomic DNA, and 1.25 U Taq DNA polymerase. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95◦C for 2 min; 10 cycles of 94◦C for 1 min and 68◦C for 2 min; followed by 25 cycles of 94◦C for 30 s, 60◦C for 30 s, and 72◦C for 45 s; with a final extension at 72◦C for 5 min. Of the resulting 333 bp PCR product, 15 μL was digested with 8 U of NlaIII restriction endonuclease in CutSmart® buffer at 37◦C for 4 h. Digestion products were separated on a 2% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide and visualized using an ultraviolet gel documentation system. The G allele introduces an NlaIII restriction site, producing 200-bp and 133-bp fragments. Thus, PNPLA3 genotypes were identified as CC (uncut 333 bp), GG (200 bp and 133 bp), or GC (all three fragments: 333 bp, 200 bp, and 133 bp). Because of the small number of CC and GC carriers, and prior evidence that GG children exhibit approximately twice the liver fat of CC or GC carriers, CC and GC genotypes were combined as a single group (GC/CC) for further analysis (Goran et al., 2010).

Serum metabolomics and lipidomics

A detailed description of serum metabolomics and lipidomics has been previously reported (Schenker et al., 2025). For metabolomics analysis, 5 μL serum samples were extracted with 150 μL of cold methanol: acetonitrile: water (40:40:20, v/v/v) by vortexing, followed by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4◦C. The supernatant (~100 μL) was collected for analysis. Metabolite profiling was performed using a quadrupole Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive Plus; Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative ion modes. Mass spectra were acquired over m/z 70–1000 at 1 Hz with a resolution of 140,000. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an XBridge BEH Amide column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.5 μm, 130 Å; Waters Corporation) using a gradient of solvent A (95:5 water: acetonitrile v/v, containing 20 mM ammonium acetate and 20 mM ammonium hydroxide, pH 9.45) and solvent B (acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 150 μL/min. The gradient program was as follows: 0–2 min, 85% B; 3–5 min, 80% B; 6–7 min, 75% B; 8–9 min, 70% B; 10–12 min, 50% B; 13–16 min, 25% B; 18–23 min, 0% B; 24–30 min, 85% B. The autosampler was maintained at 5◦C, and the injection volume was 3 μL.

For lipidomic analysis, 5 μL serum was extracted with 150 μL of ice-cold isopropanol and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4◦C. The resulting supernatant (100 μL) was transferred to a glass vial for LC-MS analysis. Lipid species were detected using a quadrupole Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive Plus; Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating in positive ion mode with ESI, scanning from m/z 350–1700 at 1 Hz with a resolution of 140,000. Lipids were separated on a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 2.7 μm; Agilent) using a gradient of solvent A (90:10 water: methanol v/v, containing 1 mM ammonium acetate and 0.2% acetic acid) and solvent B (2:98 methanol: isopropanol v/v, containing 1 mM ammonium acetate and 0.2% acetic acid) at a flow rate of 150 μL/min. The gradient program was 0–2 min, 25% B; 4–16 min, 65–100% B; 20–21 min, 100–25% B; 27 min, 25% B. The autosampler temperature was 5◦C, and the injection volume was 3 μL.

For both analyses, quality control samples, prepared by pooling aliquots from all study samples and subjected to the same pre-processing procedures, were injected periodically to monitor instrument performance and correct for potential metabolite degradation or retention time drift. Through the metabolomics and lipidomics analyses, we obtained the response values of a set of features across the different serum samples.

Statistical analyses

Participants with complete liver outcome (i.e., HFF, and LS) and air pollutant data were included in analyses assessing exposure–outcome associations. HEROES participants with available metabolomics and lipidomics data were further included in subsequent mediation and pathway enrichment analyses. Covariates included in the linear regression, g-computation, and both mediation analyses comprised sex, BMI, study group (HEROES vs. Probiotic), and visit season, with the latter defined as “warm” for April–September and “cold” for all other months, reflecting the seasonal pattern in Southern California. No missing values were observed for these covariates.

We first assessed the associations of PM2.5 and its individual components with two liver outcomes (i.e., HFF and LS) using linear regression models. For mixtures analysis, g-computation models were applied to estimate the effects of simultaneously increasing all PM2.5 component (i.e. mixture effect). To evaluate potential effect modification by PNPLA3 genotype, an interaction term between exposure and genotype was included in each linear regression and g-computation model. Results are reported as mean changes in liver outcomes per interquartile range (IQR) increase in exposures (a single component in linear regression and all components jointly in g-computation), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), expressed in standard deviation (SD) of the outcomes. Component-specific weights, reflecting each component’s proportional contribution to the mixture effect, were also reported from g-computation. G-computation analyses, without and with modification by PNPLA3 genotype, were conducted using the R packages qgcomp (Keil et al., 2020) and qgcompint (Stevens et al., 2022), respectively.

We hypothesized that metabolic features may be the mechanisms underlying the observed genotype-dependent effects and thus conducted moderated mediation analyses. “Mediation” refers to exposures influencing liver outcomes indirectly through changes in metabolic feature levels, while moderated mediation analysis tests whether PNPLA3 genotype modifies this indirect effect, allowing estimation of the average causal mediation effect (ACME) for each genotype group and comparison between groups (ΔACME). For each analysis (i.e., for a specific exposure–feature–outcome triplet), two models were specified: The mediator model regressed each metabolic feature (potential mediator, M) on the exposure X, PNPLA3 genotype G, and their interaction: M=μM+αX+α1(X×G)+β⊤C+ε

The outcome model regressed the liver outcome Y on X, M, G, and two interaction terms (X×G and M×G): Y=μY+δX+δ1(X×G)+γM+γ1(M×G)+θ⊤C+η where C denotes the vector of covariates. Accordingly, the average causal mediation effect (ACME) was estimated separately by genotype group: ACMEG=0=α⋅γ,ACMEG=1=α+α1γ+γ1

The difference in the mediation effect between genotype groups (ΔACME) was then defined as: ΔACME=α+α1γ+γ1−α⋅γ

ACMEs and ΔACMEs with 95% confidence intervals were estimated using quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo simulation with 50,000 iterations and are presented as the change in the outcome (in SD units) per IQR increase in the exposure. All moderated mediation analyses were conducted using the R package mediation (Tingley et al., 2014).

To better interpret the biological functions of the mediating features identified, we conduct pathway enrichment analysis using the R package metapone, with results from the moderated mediation analysis as input (Tian et al., 2022). Metapone is a bioinformatics platform that predicts biological functions from untargeted metabolomic data without requiring prior metabolite identification. The predefined adduct list for feature-to-metabolite mapping was defined based on the LC conditions and the polarity mode of mass spectrometry acquisition. For metabolomics, positive-mode adducts included protonated molecules ([M + H]⁺), ammonium adducts ([M + NH4]⁺), sodium adducts ([M + Na]⁺), and potassium adducts ([M + K]⁺); negative-mode adducts included deprotonated molecules ([M− H]⁻) and acetate adducts ([M + CH3COO] ⁻). For lipidomics (positive mode), adducts included [M + H]⁺, [M + Na] ⁺, [M + K]⁺, and [M + NH4]⁺. To account for the fact that a single feature may map to multiple metabolites, each feature was weighted as (1/m)p, where m is the number of matched metabolites and p (fractional count power) controls the penalty for multiple matches. A maximum cap of 10 was applied to m, and p was set to 0.5, representing a moderate penalty. Pathway significance was assessed using a weighted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)-type test approach using metabolite-based testing with 10,000 permutations. Features with ACME p-values below 0.05 (metabolomics) or 0.1 (lipidomics) were considered significant. Pathway enrichment analyses were performed separately for each exposure–outcome combination within each genotype group, and pathways were considered significant if they met a metapone false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of <0.2 and included at least one significant metabolite, to maximize the detection of relevant biological signals. The super pathway categories were based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

To estimate the total indirect effect mediated by significant metabolic features in PNPLA3 GC/CC or GG carriers, we applied a nonparametric bootstrap procedure with 1,000 iterations. For each iteration, we first resampled the study population with replacement. In the resampled dataset, we fitted the mediator and outcome models for each significant mediator to calculate the genotype-specific mediation effect. The total mediation effect for each genotype group was then obtained by summing the mediation effects across all mediators, and the difference between genotypes was calculated for that iteration. Repeating this process generated distributions of total mediation effects and differences, from which the median and 95% confidence intervals were derived and reported as the final estimates. This analysis was conducted separately for each exposure–outcome pair, and the significant metabolic features (mediators) included were those showing a significant ACME in either the GC/CC or GG genotype group from the moderated mediation analyses. All mediators were standardized to Z-scores prior to mediation analyses to ensure comparability across mediators when estimating and aggregating indirect effects.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in linear regression, g-computation, and moderated mediation analyses. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). For total mediation analyses, due to the absence of p-values, effects whose 95% credible intervals do not span zero are considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted with R (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Replication in the Meta-AIR cohort

To verify and supplement key findings, we analyzed data from the Metabolic and Asthma Incidence Research) (Meta-AIR) cohort (Kim et al., 2019), including only participants with level 1 metabolomics data confirmed using authentic chemical standards (Schymanski et al., 2014). Our sample consisted of 81 young adults (17–22 years) of mixed race/ethnicity with varied BMI, residing in Southern California. HFF, one-year average PM2.5 prior to visits, PM2.5 component concentrations from the visit year, and PNPLA3 genotype were measured following procedures analogous to those used in the HEROES and Probiotic studies (Patterson et al., 2025). Among participants, 68 were GC/CC and 13 were GG. Fasting serum samples were analyzed in triplicate using both C18 reverse-phase LC-MS (ESI negative mode) and HILIC LC-MS (ESI positive mode) (Dionex Ultimate 3000, Q-Exactive HF-X Orbitrap MS, Thermo Scientific). A total of 131 level 1 metabolites were included, comprising 39 amino acids and derivatives, 26 lipids, 24 organic acids and derivatives, 13 cholines, betaines, carnitines, and coenzyme A (CoA), 7 amines, 6 carbohydrates, 6 vitamins and cofactors, 5 nucleotides and related compounds, and 5 others (see Table S1 for details). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Johns Hopkins University and the USC.

Linear regression models were used to evaluate associations between the PM2.5 or individual PM2.5 components and HFF, without or with PNPLA3 genotype (exposure × PNPLA3). Given the limited sample size, a meet-in-the-middle approach was applied to identify potential mediating metabolites. Specifically, a metabolite M was considered a candidate mediator if it satisfied both of the following conditions: Mi=μM+α1Xi+α2Gi+α3Xi×Gi+β⊤Ci+εi(1)(exposure model) HFFi=μY+γ1Mi+γ2Gi+γ3Mi×Gi+θ⊤Ci+εi(2)(outcome model) with α1 (or α1+α3) and γ1 (or γ1+γ3) both reaching statistical significance. Covariates included sex, BMI, season of visit, and race/ethnicity. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the BH method.

Results

Descriptive statistics

A total of 113 adolescents (N = 96 from the HEROES study and N = 17 from the Probiotic study) with complete data on PM2.5, PM2.5 components, HFF and LS were included in the analysis (Table 1). Participants had a mean (± SD) age of 14.8 ± 2.1 years, a relatively balanced sex distribution (54% female), and all met criteria for obesity, defined in present study as an age- and sex-specific BMI > 95th percentile, with a mean BMI of 34.4 kg/m2. The mean HFF and LS were 12.3%, and 2.4 kPa, respectively. Based on established and commonly used thresholds (i.e., HFF ≥ 5.5% for MASLD (Azizi et al., 2025) and LS > 2.74 kPa for clinically significant fibrosis (Venkatesh and Ehman, 2014), 79 participants (70%) met criteria for MASLD, and 17 participants (15%) met criteria for fibrosis, consistent with recently reported prevalence in U.S. adolescents with obesity (Sun and Sun, 2025).

Of the 113 participants, 43 were PNPLA3 GC or CC carriers and 70 were GG carriers; the HEROES study contributed a disproportionately higher share of GG carriers. Overall demographic characteristics were comparable between GC/CC and GG carriers; however, GG carriers exhibited significantly higher levels of HFF (14.6% vs. 8.4%) and LS (2.5 kPa vs. 2.3 kPa), as well as greater proportions meeting criteria for MASLD (80% vs. 53%) and fibrosis (21% vs. 5%).

Table 2 summarizes participants’ exposure levels to PM2.5 and its components, including 2 carbonaceous components, 3 secondary inorganic salts, and 10 trace elements. The mean concentration of PM2.5 was 12.1 μg/m3 (range: 9.9–16.3 μg/m3). Average concentrations of EC, OC, SO42− , NO3–, and NH4+ were 1.0, 2.6, 1.5, 1.7, and 0.6 μg/m3, respectively. Trace element concentrations ranged from 0.2 to 126.3 ng/m3, with Fe and K showing the highest levels, and V and Ni the lowest. Variability differed across exposures, being lowest for PM2.5 mass and secondary inorganic salts, moderate for carbonaceous components, and highest for trace elements. For most exposures, levels did not differ significantly between genotype groups among HEROES participants, except for Si, whereas among Probiotic study participants, statistically significant genotype-related differences were observed for multiple exposures (Table S2). Most exposures were positively correlated (Fig. S1). Exceptions included EC, Ni, and SO42−, which showed negative correlations with certain components. Correlations between individual PM2.5 components and PM2.5 mass ranged from −0.31 (EC) to 0.63 (Fe). The strongest positive correlation was observed between Br and OC (ρ = 0.81), and the strongest negative between Cu and EC (ρ = −0.52).

Predominantly positive PM component and mixture associations with liver outcomes, stronger in PNPLA3 GG carriers

Fig. 2A shows associations of an IQR increase in individual PM2.5 and component exposures with HFF and LS, as well as associations of an IQR increase in all components simultaneously with both liver outcomes estimated via g-computation. Only significant associations (p-value < 0.05) are displayed, the vast majority of which are positive. For example, higher K levels were positively associated with both liver outcomes, with an IQR increase in K corresponding to 0.3 SD (2.7%) higher HFF and 0.5 SD (0.2 kPa) higher LS. Simultaneous IQR increases in all 15 components were associated with a 1.1 SD (10.0%) higher HFF, exceeding the effects of individual components such as K. The sole exception was a negative association between SO42− and LS. The robustness of these significant associations was evaluated using scatter and quartile-based plots (Fig. S2). The direction of most associations remained consistent, except for V with HFF and Br with LS, which changed direction in median regression.

Several associations demonstrated significant heterogeneity by PNPLA3 genotype (p-value < 0.05 for between-genotype differences), with effect estimates shifted toward stronger positive associations in GG carriers compared with GC/CC carriers (Fig. 2B). These included OC with HFF and LS, and Br, Ca, Cu, and K with LS. For example, an IQR increase in K was estimated to be associated with a 0.17 SD (0.1 kPa) decrease (95% CI: −0.32, 0.67 SD) in LS among PNPLA3 GC/CC carriers, but a 0.76 SD (0.3 kPa) increase (95% CI: 0.41, 1.10 SD) in GG carriers. Additionally, some associations were significant only in PNPLA3 GG carriers (p-value < 0.05). These included positive associations of EC with HFF, K with HFF, Pb with LS, and Si with LS, as well as a negative association between SO42− and LS. The positive association between the component mixture and HFF in GG carriers was borderline significant (p-value = 0.06). The direction of these associations remained consistent across analyses when evaluated using scatter plots and quartile-based plots (Fig. S3).

Fig. S4 summarizes the estimated weights of individual PM2.5 components in the mixture associations with HFF and LS, overall and by PNPLA3 genotype, highlighting each component’s relative contribution. For HFF, K, Br, EC, Ni, and Ca consistently showed positive weights, with K, Br, and EC being the top contributors in both the overall and genotype-stratified analyses. For LS, Pb and Cu exhibited consistently positive weights across models. In a sensitivity analysis restricted to HEROES participants with a detailed residential history for the year preceding the baseline visit (n = 96), associations of PM2.5 components, component mixtures, and their interactions with PNPLA3 with HFF and LS were largely consistent with those observed in the primary analysis (Fig. S5). Additional sensitivity analyses further adjusting for diet quality (Healthy Eating Index-2015) in the combined population (N = 113) and for both diet quality and physical activity in the HEROES subset (N = 96) yielded nearly identical results, with the associations of PM2.5 components and component mixtures, as well as the observed PNPLA3 genotype-specific patterns, remaining essentially unchanged (Fig. S5). These findings suggest that residual confounding by these lifestyle factors is unlikely to explain the observed associations.

Wider metabolic engagement in component–liver outcome associations among PNPLA3 GG carriers

We hypothesized that differential metabolic responses partly underlie the observed PNPLA3 genotype–modified effects on liver outcomes. To test this, we conducted moderated mediation analyses to assess whether specific metabolic features mediate the component–liver outcome associations and whether the PNPLA3 genotype modifies these mediation effects. Analyses were restricted to associations that showed significant heterogeneity by genotype. Eighty-nine HEROES participants with available metabolomics and lipidomics data were included in the analyses, and their characteristics were comparable to those of the overall study population (Table S3).

More metabolomic and lipidomic features showed significant mediation effects (p-value < 0.05) across all component–liver outcome associations in PNPLA3 GG carriers compared with GC/CC carriers (Fig. 3). For example, in the association between OC and LS, only 2 lipidomic features showed significant ACME in GC/CC carriers, whereas 55 metabolomic and 48 lipidomic features were significant in GG carriers. This pattern was also reflected in the totals (GG: 207 metabolomic and 343 lipidomic mediating features vs. GC/CC: 40 metabolomic and 156 lipidomic mediating features). In most cases, most significant mediating features had ACMEs consistent with the direction of the component–liver outcome association (i.e., positive), except for the Ca–LS association in PNPLA3 GC/CC carriers and the OC–HFF association in GG carriers, where more mediators offset the effect (Fig. S6). Many mediating features were shared across different component–liver outcome pairs, particularly for the same outcome (i.e., LS; Fig. S7). Additionally, significant between-genotype differences in ACME (i.e., ΔACME) were observed for several metabolic features (p-value < 0.05), including 46 metabolomic and 156 lipidomic features in total (Table S4 and 5, Fig. S8).

Distinct metabolic pathways involved in component–liver outcome associations by PNPLA3 genotype

To facilitate biological interpretation of the identified mediating features, we performed pathway enrichment analyses (Fig. 4). Based on the metabolomics data, pathway enrichment in PNPLA3 GC/CC carriers was limited and observed only for the OC–HFF association (metapone FDR < 0.2), consistent with the small number of mediating features identified in this group (Fig. 4A). Enriched pathways primarily involved arginine and proline metabolism (amino acid metabolism) and biosynthesis of antibiotics and plant secondary metabolites (biosynthesis of secondary metabolites). In contrast, PNPLA3 GG carriers exhibited substantially broader pathway enrichment across multiple component–liver outcome associations, including Ca–LS, Cu–LS, OC–LS, and K–LS. Enriched pathways spanned multiple domains, notably amino acid metabolism (including arginine, proline, aspartate, asparagine, cysteine, methionine, histidine, tryptophan, the urea cycle, glutamine/glutamate, and glutathione metabolism), as well as nucleotide metabolism (pyrimidine), cofactors and vitamins metabolism (nicotine and nicotinamide), xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism (aromatic compounds), protein digestion and absorption, and GABAergic synapse. Among these associations, K–LS showed the greatest number of enriched pathways, followed by OC–LS, with fewer pathways identified for Cu–LS and Ca–LS.

Consistent with the metabolomics findings, lipidomics data-based analyses also demonstrated more extensive pathway enrichment in PNPLA3 GG carriers compared with GC/CC carriers (Fig. 4B). In GC/CC carriers, enrichment was again restricted to the OC–HFF association and was dominated by lipid metabolic pathways, including fatty acid elongation in mitochondria, mitochondrial β-oxidation of medium-chain saturated fatty acids, and monounsaturated fatty acid β-oxidation, alongside pathways related to xenobiotic processing (xenobiotic metabolism by cytochrome P450 and chemical carcinogenesis). By contrast, in PNPLA3 GG carriers, lipidomic mediating features were enriched across several component–liver outcome associations, i.e., Br–LS, Cu–LS, Ca–LS, OC–HFF, and K–LS, with a decreasing number of enriched pathways across these associations. Enriched pathways were mainly related to carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, with additional involvement of pathways such as signal transduction and endocrine system–related pathways, particularly steroid metabolism. When considering pathways with p-values < 0.05, the overall patterns remained consistent with those described above (Table S6).

Finally, we used a nonparametric bootstrap to estimate total mediation effects summed across all significant metabolic features, and their between-genotype differences for each component–outcome association (Table 3). Significant positive total mediation effects were observed only in GG carriers for the associations of Br, Ca, and Cu with LS, with the effects for Ca and Cu also significantly greater than those in GC/CC carriers. We also observed a significantly negative total indirect effect in GG carriers for the association between OC and HFF.

Validation and supplementation in the Meta-AIR cohort with representative young adults and level 1 metabolites

Our Meta-AIR participants (N = 81; 68 PNPLA3 GC/CC, 13 PNPLA3 GG) were young adults of mixed race/ethnicity and BMI, with a mean HFF of 4.3% ± 3.7% (see Tables S7, 8, and Fig. S9 for detailed participant characteristics and exposure information). Fe was the only PM2.5 component significantly associated with HFF overall, with an IQR increase in Fe corresponding to 0.29 SD (1.1%) higher HFF (p-value = 0.011; Fig. 5A). In PNPLA3 genotype interaction analyses, no components reached significance among GC/CC carriers. In contrast, among GG carriers, an IQR increase in EC was associated with 0.72 SD (2.7%) higher HFF, OC with 0.52 SD (1.9%) higher HFF, and Br with 1.23 SD (4.6%) higher HFF (all BH-adjusted p-value < 0.05); Fe also showed a significant positive association (p-value < 0.05). Significant gene–environment interactions were observed for Br, EC, and OC (BH-adjusted p-value < 0.05).

In meet-in-the-middle analyses restricted to components with significant PNPLA3 genotype interactions (EC, OC, Br), 3 serum metabolites emerged as candidate mediators among GG carriers: fatty acid 16:1 (FA 16:1; EC), guanidinosuccinate (OC), and proline (Br; Fig. 5B, Table S9). No candidate mediators were identified among GC/CC carriers. These metabolites span pathways including fatty acid β-oxidation (FA 16:1) and amino acid metabolism (guanidinosuccinate, proline), providing independent replication of the PNPLA3-dependent metabolic perturbations linking PM2.5 components exposure to increased HFF.

As a sensitivity analysis, PNPLA3 genotype was modeled as an ordinal variable (0, 1, and 2 corresponding to CC, CG, and GG, respectively). Results from both the PM2.5 component–liver outcome association analyses and the mediation analyses were broadly consistent with the primary findings (Figs. S10 and S11). Br, EC, and OC showed significant dose-dependent increases in their associations with HFF with increasing PNPLA3 risk-allele dosage. Consistent with the primary mediation analyses, betaine and proline were identified as significant mediators of the Br–HFF association among CG and GG carriers, respectively, while no significant mediators were detected among CC carriers.

Discussion

We provide novel and systematic evidence linking ambient PM2.5 component exposure to MASLD risk in the context of genetic susceptibility in youth, based on two independent cohorts with distinct populations. Our study integrates exposure–outcome associations with metabolism-related pathways, leveraging comprehensive PM2.5 component characterization and high-dimensional metabolomics and lipidomics, with validation using level 1 metabolites confirmed by authentic standards. Our findings indicate that associations between PM2.5 exposure and liver outcomes are driven by specific carbonaceous (e.g., EC) and elemental (e.g., Br) components and are amplified in individuals carrying the PNPLA3 risk genotype (GG). These effects are mediated by widespread metabolic alterations, particularly in fatty acid and amino acid pathways, highlighting MASLD-related metabolic dysregulation as a key biological mechanism, especially in GG carriers.

In both cohorts (i.e. discovery and replication cohort), a broad set of PM2.5 components, were positively associated with at least one liver outcomes (HFF or LS) in the overall population and/or among PNPLA3 GG carriers. Notably, when considering the joint effects of the PM2.5 component mixture in the discovery cohort, simultaneous IQR increases in all 15 components were associated with a 10.0% higher HFF, representing a substantial elevation in MASLD risk. These results are consistent with accumulating epidemiological evidence from adults showing positive associations of ambient PM2.5 (Guo et al., 2022; Ran et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2022; Ji et al., 2024; Matthiessen et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2023; VoPham et al., 2022; Chen et al., n.d.), PM1 (Particles ≤ 1 μm) (Guo et al., 2022; Ran et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2024; Xing et al., 2023), PM10; (Guo et al., 2022; Ran et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2024; Xing et al., 2023; Ciaula et al., 2025) and residential proximity to major roadways (Li et al., 2017), with adverse liver outcomes, including hepatic steatosis, advanced liver fibrosis, incident MASLD, MASLD prevalence, and MASLD severity. Evidence from youth, however, remains limited. In the same cohort, our previous analysis showed a positive association between PM10 and LS (Schenker et al., 2024). Similarly, in a study of 1,102 European children, prenatal PM10 exposure was positively associated with serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in overweight or obese participants, a biomarker of liver injury and a clinical screening tool for pediatric MASLD (Garcia et al., 2021). Overall, our study contributes to the sparse evidence in youth and, together with prior studies, supports an association between PM2.5 and MASLD, which may have clinical relevance.

Previous research on PM2.5 components and MASLD is limited to 3 studies in China, all considering only 5 major components (i.e. BC, organic matter, SO42−, NO3−, and NH4+) (Guo et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2024). Our study represents the first evidence from the U.S., and extends beyond these components to include 10 additional trace elements, some of which are likely more toxic and offer improved source-specific characterization. All three previous studies reported associations between nearly all the measured PM2.5 components and MASLD risk. However, in the present study, significant associations with liver outcomes were observed only for carbonaceous components and specific trace elements, i.e., EC, OC, Br, Ca, Cu, K, Pb, Si, and V in the discovery cohort, and EC, OC, Br, and Fe in the replication cohort. Mixture analysis (g-computation) in the discovery cohort further identified K, Br, EC, Pb, and Cu as key contributors in the discovery cohort. This discrepancy may partly be attributable to the relatively smaller sample size of the present study, leading to lower statistical power and reduced sensitivity to detect significant associations, as well as population differences. But it also suggest the potentially higher hepatotoxicity of carbonaceous and elemental components, particularly metals, a notion indirectly supported by a broad body of prior studies showing greater toxicity of these components in cardiopulmonary outcomes (Kelly and Fussell, 2012; Liang et al., 2016). From a source perspective: EC primarily represents combustion-derived particles, especially from traffic emissions; (Janssen et al., 2011) Br may indicate traffic- and port/shipping-related sources or coal/biomass combustion, and may also originate from sea-salt aerosols; K is associated with biomass burning but also originates from soil minerals, plant ash, and industrial dust; Pb reflects traffic-related emissions (especially from the historical use of leaded gasoline) as well as industrial sources; and Cu is commonly associated with traffic-related sources, particularly brake wear, and industrial processes (Zhu and Liu, 2024; Oroumiyeh et al., 2022; Reff etal., 2009; Fusselletal, 2022). Collectively, these findings may support an important contribution of combustion- and traffic-related sources to MASLD risk.

Previous epidemiologic evidence examining the underlying biological mechanisms linking air pollution exposures to MASLD risk is limited to 3 studies with a small set of metabolites and/or miRNAs (Ran et al., 2025; Patterson et al., 2025; Patterson et al., 2023). For instance, a UK Biobank study assessed 251 metabolites as potential mediators of associations between air pollutants [PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and nitrogen oxides (NOx)] and MASLD risk (Ran et al., 2025). Over 80% of these metabolites were lipids and lipoprotein subfractions; the study primarily highlighted mediation by lipoprotein subfractions, with minor contributions from fatty acids and amino acids. Similarly, a previous multi-omics study from our group, profiling 131 metabolites and 142 miRNAs, identified mediating roles of fatty acids and amino acids on the association between oxidative gaseous air pollutant exposure and HFF (Patterson et al., 2025). Limited metabolite coverage and the use of dimensionality reduction in these studies constrained the ability to capture the full spectrum of potential mediators, which could result in missing key mediators, obscured mechanistic insights, and biased pathway enrichment.

In contrast, leveraging comprehensive metabolomics and lipidomics datasets in the discovery cohort, the present study captures extensive metabolic pathways mediating PM2.5 component–liver outcome associations, particularly among PNPLA3 GG carriers. Considering prior reports of broad metabolic perturbations associated with air pollution (Liang et al., 2023), and the central role of systemic and hepatic metabolic dysfunction in MASLD pathogenesis (Targher et al., 2025), these findings are biologically plausible. The enriched pathways encompass carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, as well as signaling and endocrine-related processes, many of which are integral to MASLD pathophysiology. For instance, we identified that pathway insulin resistance, inositol metabolism, inositol phosphate metabolism, and the phosphatidylinositol signaling system mediated the associations between PM2.5 components and liver outcomes. These pathways are tightly linked to insulin resistance, a central driver of MASLD (Bo et al., 2024; Bugianesi et al., 2005). In the liver, insulin resistance manifests as impaired suppression of gluconeogenesis alongside preserved or enhanced de novo lipogenesis, leading to hepatic lipid accumulation. Insulin resistance also promotes systemic metabolic dysfunction, inflammation, and altered lipid flux from peripheral tissues, further increasing hepatic free fatty acids and creating a proinflammatory milieu. We also identified multiple fatty acid–related pathways as mediators, including mitochondrial fatty acid degradation, elongation, and β-oxidation. These pathways reflect impaired mitochondrial lipid handling, a central feature of MASLD, in which imbalances between fatty acid influx and oxidation drive hepatic lipid overload and lipotoxic stress (García-Ruiz et al., 2013).

Further validation in the replication cohort identified 3 level 1 metabolites, i.e., one fatty acid FA 16:1 (increased), and two amino acids related metabolites, guanidinosuccinate and proline (both decreased), that mediated the association between PM2.5 component exposure and higher HFF, observed only in PNPLA3 GG carriers. These findings further support and complement the pathway results from the discovery cohort. Specifically, the increase in FA 16:1, together with disruptions in mitochondrial fatty acid degradation, elongation, and β-oxidation pathways observed in the discovery cohort, may reflect reduced mitochondrial oxidation associated with PM2.5 exposure, a mechanism contributing to lipid accumulation in MASLD (Carli et al., 2024). Both guanidinosuccinate and proline are involved in arginine and proline metabolism identified in the discovery cohort. Guanidinosuccinate is an intermediate in the urea cycle contributing to arginine production, while proline can be synthesized from arginine via ornithine. Although direct mechanistic evidence linking guanidinosuccinate to MASLD is limited, a study of 896 Japanese men reported that plasma guanidinosuccinate levels were negatively associated with liver enzymes in participants with high alcohol intake, suggesting liver injury (Harada et al., 2016). In 106 Finnish patients with severe obesity, liver guanidinosuccinate levels were decreased in metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) compared with steatosis and were negatively associated with histological severity, BMI, insulin, and triglycerides (Palomurto et al., 2026). These findings are consistent with our observations. In contrast, prior studies suggest that proline promote MASLD by impairing mitochondrial function (Lyu et al., 2025; Glutaminase, 2022) or increasing lipogenic gene expression in the liver (Taniguchi et al., 2025), thereby enhancing lipid accumulation. The inconsistency with our findings suggests that proline may act as a bystander rather than a causal mediator in the association between PM2.5 exposure and MASLD. Alternatively, proline may contribute to MASLD through other biological mechanisms that warrant further investigation.

Genetic susceptibility is a major determinant of MASLD risk, and air pollution may interact with such susceptibility to further amplify disease risk (Moretti et al., 2024). In the present study, we focused on the PNPLA3 I148M variant, the strongest known genetic contributor to MASLD. We found that significant effects between multiple PM2.5 components and liver outcomes were observed only in PNPLA3 GG carriers, with certain effect sizes in GG carriers exceeding those in GC/CC individuals. This consistent with previous evidence from our group and others (Patterson et al., 2025; Schenker et al., 2024; Ciaula et al., 2025). Mechanistic evidence further supports this, as broader metabolomic and lipidomic features and pathways mediated the associations, with significant total mediation effects observed exclusively in PNPLA3 GG carriers in the discovery study, and candidate mediating metabolites identified only in GG carriers in the replication study. The PNPLA3 I148M variant increases susceptibility to MASLD by promoting intrahepatic lipolysis, enhancing ketogenesis, and impairing mitochondrial function, creating a reduced metabolic state that favors lipotoxic lipid accumulation (Mancina et al., 2025). PNPLA3 GG carriers may harbor a metabolically vulnerable liver, in which pre-existing dysregulation lowers the threshold for additional insults and renders them particularly sensitive to the cumulative and additive effects of air pollution–induced metabolic perturbations and, ultimately, increased MASLD risk. However, the metabolic pathways linking PM2.5 components to MASLD risk are likely complex and may involve both injurious and adaptive responses, as suggested by mediators that offset the overall effect and the significantly negative total indirect effect observed for OC and HFF. Notably, the overall and direct associations between OC and HFF remained positive, consistent with a harmful role of OC. We also cannot exclude the possibility that the measured metabolites capture only a subset of a broader, partially counter-regulatory metabolic response to OC exposure, such that the net effect mediated through these intermediates does not fully reflect the overall association. Further investigation, including longitudinal studies and higher-resolution profiling of PM2.5 constituents and metabolic networks, is needed to clarify the temporal dynamics and biological basis of these competing responses (Floyd et al., 2020).

Our study has several strengths. First, we assessed exposure to a broad spectrum of PM2.5 components, enabling the comprehensive evaluation of multiple potentially toxic PM constituents, including heavy metals, in relation to MASLD risk in youth. By additionally applying mixture analysis, we were able to capture the joint effects of co-occurring components and provide a more realistic and integrated assessment of PM2.5-related health risk. Second, we integrated high-resolution metabolomics and lipidomics data, capturing a wide range of hydrophilic and lipophilic metabolites and lipids, which allowed for an in-depth and systems-level investigation of biological mechanisms. Third, we applied a sophisticated, multilevel analytical framework that simultaneously accounted for PM2.5 components, genetic susceptibility, and high-dimensional molecular mediators, while systematically integrated mediation analyses across molecular features, biological pathways, and total-effect levels. It provides multi-level support for public health measures, including targeted pollution control, precision strategies for disease prevention, and protection of vulnerable populations. Moreover, this framework can be readily extended to broader exposome–health research. Fourth, we introduced a distinct population with level 1 metabolite data to validate and supplement findings from a single cohort. This approach partially mitigates limitations related to sample size and population specificity, as well as concerns regarding metabolite annotation without validation using authentic standards, thereby strengthening the robustness of our conclusions.

Several limitations should also be noted. First, the study has an inherent cross-sectional design, with omics profiles and liver outcomes assessed concurrently, precluding definitive causal inference and verification of temporal ordering. Future longitudinal human studies and refined animal experiments are warranted to address this limitation, validate these findings, and prioritize the key pathways relevant to MASLD initiation and progression in the context of air pollution exposure. Second, our estimates of total indirect effects should be interpreted cautiously. The approach we used, simply summing the effects of individual mediators, clearly does not account for the possibility that multiple features may represent the same metabolite and ignores the complex interrelationships among mediators. While numerous high-dimensional mediation methods exist to handle large numbers of features, these approaches are primarily designed to identify the most influential mediators rather than to provide more accurate estimates of total mediation effect (Hoffman et al., 2026). This limitation warrants attention in future studies. Third, PM2.5 mass and its components were estimated using exposure models that differed substantially in spatial resolution and modeling framework (IDW2 based on monitors within 50 km versus high-resolution machine-learning models at 50 m–1 km). These differences may result in differing degrees and types of exposure measurement error across pollutants, limiting direct comparisons between associations for total PM2.5 mass and individual components, as well as the interpretation of correlations between them. Fourth, exposure assessment for several components showed limited validation performance in California (i.e., SO42− , Fe, and K). Because these concentrations were estimated using geospatial models independent of individual liver outcomes, any resulting measurement error is likely to be largely non-differential, potentially reducing statistical precision and limiting the ability to detect true associations. Nevertheless, differential misclassification cannot be entirely excluded (e.g., if prediction errors vary systematically by urbanicity or exposure level). Accordingly, findings for poorly validated components, particularly K, should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the limited number of PNPLA3 GG carriers in the Meta-AIR cohort, which is expected given the cohort’s diverse racial and ethnic composition, may have reduced statistical power for the interaction and mediation analyses, resulting in less precise effect estimates and potentially limiting the detection of additional mediators. Nevertheless, sensitivity analyses modeling PNPLA3 genotype additively as risk-allele dosage yielded broadly consistent findings, supporting the robustness of the observed gene–environment interaction patterns (Figs. S10 and 11). In addition, the replication results should be interpreted primarily as confirmation of the direction and overall pattern of associations rather than the exact effect sizes, particularly given the demographic and metabolic differences between the discovery and replication cohorts.

In conclusion, our study from two independent cohorts demonstrates that exposure to specific PM2.5 components, particularly those that may be indicative of combustion and traffic emissions, is linked to increased liver fat and stiffness in youth, with effects most pronounced in PNPLA3 GG carriers. Mechanistically, these associations were mediated by broad metabolic pathways, including insulin resistance, fatty acid metabolism, and mitochondrial lipid handling, which play central roles in MASLD pathophysiology, with mitochondrial lipid handling and arginine and proline metabolism further supported by level 1 metabolite findings. The more extensive and distinct metabolic engagement observed in GG carriers may underlie their heightened susceptibility. These results highlight the metabolic vulnerability conferred by genetic susceptibility and support the implementation of targeted pollution control and precision-medicine preventive strategies in high-risk populations.
