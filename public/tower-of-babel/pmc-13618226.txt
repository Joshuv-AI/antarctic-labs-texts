Exploring Middle and High School Personnel’s Definition and Knowledge of Evidence-Based Practices for Autistic Students
Authors: Aksheya Sridhar, Olivia Michael, Kelsey S. Dickson, Adora Du, Marissa Thirion, Amy Drahota, Laura J. Hall, Bonnie Kraemer, Aubyn Stahmer, David Mandell, Jill Locke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Mandates that educators use evidence-based practices (EBP) with autistic students have not led to their widespread or consistent implementation, despite their growing availability. EBP knowledge is integral to effective implementation, and prior studies have shown that educators often are limited in their ability to identify and define existing EBPs.Objective:This study aimed to: (1) examine how middle and high school staff who support autistic students define EBPs; and (2) identify which practices they endorse as being EBPs in the school setting.Method:This study explored qualitative responses from 37 middle and high school and district staff members to the following questions: (1) “What does an EBP or support mean to you?” and (2) “What EBPs or supports do you or your colleagues use in your school?” Responses were compared to the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP) definition and registry of EBPs. The EBP definition includes: (1) an instructional or intervention procedure (or set of procedures), (2) existence of an acceptable level of research evidence supporting the practice, and (3) demonstration of positive outcomes for autistic children, youth, and/or adults.Results:Three participant responses were not consistent with any of the NCAEP EBP criteria, seven included one criterion in their response, 16 included two criteria, and 11 included all three criteria. School and district personnel listed 17 autism-focused EBPs that are listed in the NCAEP report and described the use of 41 other practices. Five respondents were unsure which EBPs their schools used.Conclusion:Findings highlight variability in school and district staff understanding of what constitutes an EBP, suggesting the need for tools to support educators in selecting and implementing such practices effectively.

Introduction

School systems are the primary service providers for autistic students (Brookman-Frazee et al., 2009). Federal law mandates that schools use effective, evidence-based practices (EBPs) to support students’ wide range of needs so that students benefit from public education (Individuals with Disabilities Educational Improvement Act, 2004). Although several EBPs have been developed for autistic individuals, they are inconsistently implemented in schools, which results in missed opportunities for support and potential growth (Brock et al., 2019).

Studies have highlighted the importance of EBP knowledge in improving EBP implementation (Cardoso et al., 2021). While previous research has examined knowledge among providers working in the medical field as well as in various community-based care settings (Cardoso et al., 2021; Lerner et al., 2022; Stahmer et al., 2005), fewer studies have examined how school and district staff define and understand EBPs (Sulek et al., 2019). Exploring school and district personnel’s perspectives regarding EBPs is an important step in identifying gaps in educators’ knowledge and understanding of best practices for autistic youth and informing efforts to enhance educators’ successful EBP use in schools.

There are several different definitions that may be used to describe and identify EBPs, with many similarities across definitions, including the emphasis on empirical support for practices and strategies used as well as improvement of outcomes among those receiving these interventions. Although there are overlaps in definitions of EBP, the lack of clarity in the communication about what defines an EBP may lead to educators’ challenges in identifying and understanding what comprises an EBP. Additionally, while these definitions incorporate the importance of student outcomes in defining EBPs, they are not specific to identifying practices developed and evaluated for use with autistic individuals. The EBP definition outlined by the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP) (updated from the National Professional Development Center on Autism Spectrum Disorder’s earlier reviews) states “an evidence-based practice is an instructional/intervention procedure or set of procedures for which researchers have provided an acceptable level of research that shows the practice produces positive outcomes for autistic children, youth, and/or adults.” The NCAEP reviewed literature from 1990 to 2017 and identified 28 EBPs and 10 manualized interventions that met EBP criteria (Steinbrenner et al., 2020). Therefore, in order to understand how individuals in school systems define and identify EBPs for autistic individuals in particular, this study used the NCAEP definition of EBP given the explicit emphasis on evaluating autism-focused EBPs this study.

Study objective

The purpose of this study is to a) explore how school and district personnel define autism-focused EBPs and b) identify which practices school and district personnel commonly cite as autism-focused EBPs.

Method

Procedures

This study is a secondary data analysis from an exploratory pilot study that redesigned and tested a multifaceted implementation toolkit to facilitate EBP uptake and implementation for use in middle and high schools (Locke et al., 2024). As part of this study, the multifaceted implementation strategy was iteratively redesigned with input from community partners (Locke et al., 2024). Due to the noted history of limited consensus and community readiness around EBP use, a group of research advisors composed of individuals with expertise in implementation science, autism, and/ or school-based research who were involved in the systematic redesign of the toolkit suggested the addition of two open-ended questions assessing knowledge of EBPs among district administrators, school administrators, and educators were added to a broader school assessment in the revised toolkit. The questions included in the assessment were: 1) “What does an EBP or support mean to you?” and 2) “What EBPs or supports do you or your colleagues use in your school?”

Once the implementation toolkit was finalized, the study team recruited schools in the states of Washington and California. Using convenience sampling, schools were recruited through prior relationships with the study Principal Investigators (PIs) who have long-term partnership with school districts due to their involvement in school-based research, while others were recruited through cold e-mails to district administrators. An initial Zoom meeting was held with each school to review study requirements, introduce the research team (e.g., role, research goals) and share information on the purpose of the study. After a school confirmed participation and administrators provided approval to contact staff, informed consent was obtained. Following consent, participants were invited via e-mail to complete the knowledge assessment through a secure survey link, which could be accessed at a time and location of their choosing. Educators were given the opportunity to invite other team members. Participants received $20 to complete the survey. The current study analyzed the responses to the two open-ended questions included in the EBP knowledge assessment.

Participants

Participants included district administrators (n = 3), school administrators (n = 6), and school staff, including special education teachers (n = 18), speech-language pathologists (n = 3), psychologists (n = 2), classroom assistants/paraeducators (n = 2), an instructional assistant (n = 1), an occupational therapist (n = 1), and a social worker (n = 1). The average age of participants was 44.6 years (SD = 10.8). Most participants identified as female (n = 31; 83.8%) and White (n = 31; 83.8%). Participants reported an average of 15.1 years working in a school (SD = 10.1), 14.9 years working with autistic individuals (SD = 9.2), and having taught or served an average of 19.5 autistic students (SD = 33.6). Participant demographics reflect the broader demographics of teachers in schools in California and Washington state (California Department of Education, 2024; Professional Educator Standards Board, 2021). There were no dropouts among participants who began the survey. Complete participant demographics are presented in Table 1.

Analysis plan

The first and second authors analyzed the qualitative responses from the survey responses and considered potential positional bias throughout the coding process. The first author is a Southeast Asian immigrant, cisgender female, non-autistic post-doctoral trainee (PhD) on the project. The second author is a White, cisgender female, non-autistic graduate student (B.S, B.A.) who served as the project coordinator at the time of the study and administered the surveys to participants. Both authors were mentored on the analysis approach by the third and senior authors who were the PIs on the study and have extensive experience in community engaged autism and implementation science research.

Qualitative data were analyzed using a descriptive qualitative design with directed content analysis (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). For the first open-ended item (“What does an EBP or support mean to you?”) participant responses were compared to three criteria derived from the NCAEP definition of EBPs. Based on the NCAEP definition noted earlier, three coding categories were established and included reference to: (a) an instructional or intervention procedure, (b) research evidence supporting the practice, and (c) demonstrated positive outcomes for autistic children, youth, or adults. Two trained research staff independently coded each response using Excel for the presence or absence of each category and then used a coding-to-consensus approach, in which discrepancies were discussed until agreement was reached. Responses from administrators and school personnel were analyzed collectively to capture shared perceptions of EBPs within the broader school context rather than role-specific differences.

For the second open-ended item (“What EBPs or supports do you or your colleagues use in your school?”), coders independently compared responses to the list of EBPs identified in the NCAEP. All practices listed in the NCAEP framework were tallied, along with the frequency with which each practice was mentioned across educators. Practices not included in the NCAEP framework were also counted to capture the frequency of other practices used in schools. Final coding results were not member-checked with participants.

Results

Question 1: “What does an EBP or support mean to you?”

Of the 37 participant responses used to describe the term EBP, 24 mentioned that an EBP is an instructional or intervention procedure. Thirty-one participants noted that an EBP requires an acceptable level of supporting research evidence. Seventeen participants referenced the demonstration of positive outcomes for autistic people in their responses. Three participants did not identify any of the three NCAEP autism-focused EBP criteria, seven identified one criterion, 16 identified two criteria, and 11 identified all three criteria in their response. The total frequency counts for definitions aligned with the NCAEP criteria are displayed in Figure 1.

In addition to responses that aligned with the NCAEP criteria, respondents also identified additional topics, such as autism-focused EBPs should drive instruction decision-making, incorporate students’ preferences and values, and benefit students across academic, social, and adaptive domains. The emphasis on autism-focused EBPs as a foundation for instructional decision-making was reflected in three responses. For example, one respondent commented that, “ . . . [EBP] or support means using research-based practices and measures to drive instruction” while another noted that “[EBPs] eliminate the guessing work that educators sometimes need to do.” In addition to driving decisions, school staff noted that EBPs should consider student perspectives. One participant explained, “EBP and/or support means integrated best practices, current research, and incorporating students’ preferences/values into [school-based] therapy sessions.” Two participants identified domains (e.g., behaviorally academically, adaptively, socially) in which EBPs could be used to support students. One participant explained that EBPs, “use effective instruction that is backed by data to support students with their success both academically and adaptively/socially.”

Question 2: “What EBPs or supports are already used in your school?”

School and district personnel were asked to report which specific practices were used in their schools that they identified as autism-focused EBPs. Of the 28 EBPs identified in the NCAEP 2020 report (Steinbrenner et al., 2020), educators identified fifteen EBPs, with 29.8% of participants identifying the use of at least one EBP. Descriptively, the proportion of participants who identified at least one EBP was similar across role groups, with 1 of 3 district administrators (33.3%), 2 of 6 school administrators (33.3%), and 8 of 28 school staff (28.6%) identifying at least one evidence-based practice. All identified EBPs have evidence of being effective for middle-school aged students, and the majority of identified EBPs in this study also have been identified as effective for high-school aged students, with the exception of Ayres Sensory Integration therapy. Participants most often reported using Visual Supports (n = 5) followed by Reinforcement (n = 4) and Modeling (n = 4; Table 2). Participants also reported the use of 50 other practices or strategies, which included manualized interventions, classroom strategies, and frameworks. Notably, some of these other practices or strategies may be evidence-based but not for autistic youth specifically or did not have sufficient evidence when the NCAEP report was developed. As a result, these practices were not categorized as EBPs in these analyses unless included in the NCAEP report. Approximately, two-thirds of participants only described the use of non EBPs. Lastly, five participants (13.5%) reported that they were “unsure” when asked which autism-focused EBPs were used at their schools.

Discussion

This study explored middle and high school and district personnel’s definitions and reported use of autism-focused EBPs in schools. Findings highlighted the variation in their understanding of autism-focused EBPs. Findings also revealed that while many autism-focused EBPs are reportedly being used in schools, many other interventions that do not meet autism-focused EBP criteria are used as well.

These findings illustrated several challenges related to understanding autism-focused EBPs in school settings, which likely impact educators’ limited use of these practices. Variations in defining EBPs underscore the inconsistencies in the language and criteria used to identify EBPs in the literature, which may lead to challenges for researchers, providers, educators, and the general public aiming to understand these practices. This is consistent with past research on public understanding of evidence-based mental health care, which found that only about one in five U.S. adults could accurately define evidence-based mental health care (Mora Ringle et al., 2019). Evidence-based mental health care is defined as involving treatments or services based on research evidence (Mora Ringle et al., 2019), patient preference, and clinical judgment (Sackett et al., 1996). In efforts to support school or district staff in implementing EBPs, it is important to begin by clearly defining what these practices are, why they matter, how they meet the needs of autistic individuals, and which specific practices are included. Consistent with prior research on engaging with community partners, establishing a common language through psychoeducation and discussion can help ensure that everyone shares a mutual understanding of what is expected (Dorsey et al., 2023). Without this shared language and clarity, staff may develop differing or even conflicting interpretations of what it means to implement EBPs, which can lead to confusion about project goals, reduce overall buy-in, decrease successful use and effectiveness of the efforts.

Similarly, this study highlighted challenges in identifying autism-focused EBPs. While the NCAEP definition and criteria were used to identify autism-focused EBPs for this study, there are several different resources that may be used to classify EBPs. For example, the California Evidence-Based Clearinghouse for Child Welfare (CEBC) aims to advance the effective implementation of EBPs for children and families involved with the child welfare system by reviewing and disseminating research-supported interventions (https://www.cebc4cw.org/) (The California Evidence-Based Clearinghouse for Child Welfare, 2025). Similarly, the What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) evaluates existing research on programs, products, practices, and policies in education to help educators make informed, evidence-based decisions on EBPs (https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/); however, these practices do not specifically focus on autistic individuals (The Insititute of Education Sciences, 2025). Moreover, middle and high school educators have limited autism-focused EBPs from which to select and use and often resort to trial and error rather than using best practices. Indeed, the EBPs identified by the NCAEP were primarily studies among children between the ages of three to five years old and six to 11 years old, although the number of studies among older children and teenagers has increased since the initial NCAEP review. Overall, these findings emphasize the challenges that educators and school staff may face when working to identify potential effective practices for their students.

Importantly, there is literature describing the three-legged chair of evidence-based practice: 1) best available research evidence, 2) clinical experience, and 3) patient values (Spring, 2007). The NCAEP definition also encourages the use of a companion decision-making process to incorporate child characteristics, intervention context, and practitioner factors when considering the use of a specific EBP with an autistic individual. Although EBPs may have a strong evidence-base, these practices may not always align with educators’ own experiences with these interventions, expertise on their student’s needs as well as their time, skills, and capacity to use such practices. Moreover, the choices of autistic students and their families also should be considered when selecting practices for use in schools. It is critical that autistic voices are prioritized, and that autistic students maintain their autonomy to advocate for their specific needs. This may be particularly relevant in middle and high school settings, as autistic students further develop their skills for self-advocacy and making and voicing decisions about their education and support needs. This approach aligns with the broader field of clinical psychology’s definition of EBP, which emphasizes an integrated process of clinical decision-making that combines research evidence, clinical expertise, and client preferences and characteristics (Spring, 2007). As reflected in these results, the latter two components (professional expertise and client or student preferences) often are underemphasized when EBPs are implemented in school settings with autistic youth. Moving forward, it is imperative that in addition to clear criteria of what constitutes an EBP, educator experience and skills as well as student voice and choice are considered in decision-making around EBP use in schools.

Limitations

There were several limitations to note. First, as mentioned, the NCAEP criteria were used due to their well-documented, evidence-informed identification of EBPs for autistic children, youth, and young adults (Steinbrenner et al., 2020). However, this report was published in 2020, based on articles published between 1990 to 2017. Therefore, it is possible that the NCAEP exclude other interventions and practices that may now be considered evidence-based. Additionally, future research should consider incorporating other criteria and integrating these perspectives to provide a more comprehensive discussion of how various practices align with differing standards for EBPs. Second, the survey questions were not explicitly worded to reference autism-specific EBPs. Although the broader context of the project centered on the use of such practices with autistic students, the lack of specificity in the wording may have led respondents to interpret the questions more generally. This ambiguity could have resulted in responses that conflated autism-specific EBPs with broader evidence-based teaching strategies. Additionally, the survey questions included both “EBPs” and “supports” in the prompt. Although “supports” was included to make the questions more accessible to participants who may not have been familiar with the term “evidence-based practices,” this lack of specificity may have led participants to include supports that are not necessarily evidence-based. Future research should aim to create and validate free-response measures that assess knowledge of EBPs, clarify which practices are recognized as such, and develop clear methods for interpreting assessment results and delivering meaningful feedback. Such measures also would be beneficial to identify non-EBP or potentially harmful practices being implemented to support the de-implementation process (Herzog & McLean, 2025). Third, researchers did not conduct member checking (i.e., participants review and confirm the accuracy of identified themes). Without this step, some aspects of participants’ intended meanings may not have been fully represented in the results (Thomas, 2016). Lastly, the limited sample size and lack of diversity among participants is a limitation. The sample lacked representation across racial and ethnic backgrounds, genders, and professional roles. Additionally, participants reported relatively high average years of experience and training in autism interventions and EBPs. A larger and more diverse sample is needed to capture perspectives from educators and school staff with varying backgrounds and professional experiences. Future research should conduct knowledge assessments across a wider range of schools and participants to better understand how educators conceptualize EBPs for autistic students.

Clinical implications

This study found that public middle and high school educators have varying definitions of what constitutes an EBP for use with autistic students. Progress in identifying and implementing EBPs should incorporate practitioner and student perspectives on EBPs to reduce the disconnect between research and community definitions. Additionally, training educators in using clearinghouses (e.g. NCAEP) may be helpful for educators to remain current with the literature on EBPs. These findings highlight the need to establish and disseminate clear, shared criteria for defining and identifying evidence-based practices in order for educators to best select and use EBPs with autistic students.

This study investigated how educators define EBPs for autistic students and evaluated whether the identified practices align with established evidence standards. Findings contribute to ongoing debates regarding the definition and implementation of EBPs in autism education and highlight the need to reexamine evidence criteria as the field continues to expand.
