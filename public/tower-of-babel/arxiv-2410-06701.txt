Oppenheimer-Snyder type collapse for a collisionless gas
Håkan Andréasson

Abstract
In 1939, Oppenheimer and Snyder showed that the continued gravitational collapse of a self-gravitating matter distribution can result in the formation of a black hole, cf. [ 34 ] . In this paper, which has greatly influenced the evolution of ideas around the concept of a black hole, matter was modeled as dust, a fluid with pressure equal to zero. We prove that when the corresponding initial data are suitably approximated by data for a collisionless gas as modeled by the Vlasov equation, then a trapped surface forms before the corresponding solution to the Einstein-Vlasov system can develop a singularity and again a black hole arises. As opposed to the dust case the pressure does not vanish for such solutions. As a necessary starting point for the analysis, which is carried out in Painlevé-Gullstrand coordinates, we prove a local existence and uniqueness theorem for regular solutions together with a corresponding extension criterion. The latter result will also become useful when one perturbs dust solutions containing naked singularities in the Vlasov framework.



1
Introduction

It is well known that in the context of General Relativity the gravitational
collapse of some matter distribution can result in the formation of a spacetime
singularity where the structure of spacetime breaks down. Historically the first
example where this was shown is the seminal paper
[
34
]
by

J. R. Oppenheimer
and
H. Snyder
.
The matter model employed in their analysis was a perfect, compressible
fluid with pressure identically equal to zero, a model often referred to
as dust; the initial data consist of a homogeneous
ball of such dust. Since such matter cannot
build up any force acting against being compressed
ad infinitum by its own gravity,
it is not surprising that the dust ball collapses.
The ground-braking insight was what this entails for the metric
and causal structure of spacetime: A spacetime singularity
forms. The comforting aspect of the analysis is that this
singularity is hidden behind
an event horizon and cannot be observed from far away.
The Oppenheimer-Snyder solution
is therefore an example where the weak cosmic censorship hypothesis, which
was later formulated by
Penrose

[
35
]
, holds.

But the dust matter model can be criticized as being somewhat peculiar
and unrealistic, since usually matter will build up a force going counter
to the compression. In addition, it is known that for dust and a class of
inhomogeneous data,
gravitational collapse can also result in naked singularities
which are not hidden behind an event horizon and are forbidden by the
weak cosmic censorship hypothesis
[
18
]
.

In the present paper we
investigate a situation analogous to the Oppenheimer-Snyder
set-up, but with a collisionless gas as matter
model instead of dust, i.e., we try to prove on analogue
to the Oppenheimer-Snyder collapse for the Einstein-Vlasov system;
the system is formulated in Section
2
.
Besides the fact that a self-gravitating collisionless gas is often used in
astrophysics to model objects like galaxies or globular clusters,
cf.
[
17
]
,
there are several more specific reasons which motivate this analysis.
Firstly, dust can be viewed as a
singular special case of Vlasov and initial dust data can in
a precise sense be approximated
by Vlasov data. Secondly, Vlasov matter has, when viewed macroscopically,
non-trivial pressure which may in general prevent collapse;
for example, there exist plenty of steady states
with Vlasov matter, but there can exist none with dust;
for Vlasov matter the pressure in general is anisotropic and also
the current is non-trivial.
Thirdly, so far no naked singularities
have been shown to exist with Vlasov matter.
Finally, dust produces singularities also in the Newtonian
regime, but with Vlasov solutions exist globally in the Newtonian
case. In
[
43
]
it was analyzed what happens if Vlasov data
are pushed towards dust data in the Newtonian case. That paper
was supposed to serve as a blueprint for the present analysis,
the main result of which is the following theorem, which we state
here in a somewhat vague fashion; the precise version follows below,
cf. Theorem
6.1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For regular initial data which approximate Oppenheimer-Snyder
data in a suitable way
the corresponding solution to the Einstein-Vlasov system approximates the
Oppenheimer-Snyder dust solution arbitrarily well. In particular, it forms
a trapped surface before
it can form a spacetime singularity so that just as
in the Oppenheimer-Snyder case
the singularity is hidden behind an event horizon.

We should add that the Hawking-Penrose singularity theorem
[
35
]

implies that the spacetime obtained in Theorem
1.1
must be geodesically
incomplete, but this does not necessarily imply that a singularity
occurs in the sense that curvature-related scalar quantities blow up,
as they do for the Oppenheimer-Snyder solution. Whether this is the case
is an interesting topic for further research.

Theorem
1.1
may
at first glance look like a result on continuous dependence on initial data.
However, there exists no mathematical framework in which both the Einstein-dust
system and the Einstein-Vlasov system are well-posed. As a matter of fact,
the result we prove gives a much more specific relation between the
solutions of the two systems: In a core region the Vlasov solution
is equal to a homogeneous solution of the Einstein-Vlasov system,
and this homogeneous solution can in turn be compared to the dust solution.
This more specific relation is then accessible to a rigorous proof.
The whole analysis is restricted to a spherically symmetric, asymptotically
flat situation and employes Painlevé-Gullstrand coordinates;
the Einstein-Vlasov system in these coordinates is formulated in
Section
2.2
.
A necessary prerequisite of the analysis is a local existence
and uniqueness result for the initial value for the
Einstein-Vlasov system in Painlevé-Gullstrand coordinates,
together with a continuation criterion which tells us exactly which
components of the solution must be controlled in order to extend it.
This result will also become important in the analysis of
Vlasov-perturbed dust solutions with naked singularities, cf.
[
16
]
.
The local existence and uniqueness result is formulated and proven in
Sections
3
and
4
.
We note that comoving coordinates, which are often used in
the dust case, do not seem to be suitable for proving a local
existence result for the Einstein-Vlasov system due to problems at the
center.
In Section
5
we review homogeneous solutions in the dust
case, construct such solutions in the Vlasov case, and establish various
relations between these solutions. Section
6
is
then devoted to the proof of the precise version of Theorem
1.1
,
cf. Theorem
6.1
.

Before we proceed some further references to the literature seem in order.
In 1965,
Penrose

[
35
]
showed that once a spacetime develops
a trapped surface then it must become singular in the sense of geodesical
incompleteness. Since trapped surfaces are stable under small perturbations
of the spacetime this result extends the Oppenheimer-Snyder
singularity formation to
more general spacetimes, at the price of a rather weak definition
of “singularity”. In addition, this result brings up the question
of which data result in the formation of trapped surfaces;
for a long time, the zero pressure
Oppenheimer-Snyder solution was the only
known example.

The present investigation is by no means the first attempt to extend the
Oppenheimer-Snyder result to a matter model with non-zero pressure.
For the fluid case this was done in
[
44
]
. In the case of a collapsing
fluid ball a major difficulty, already in the Oppenheimer-Snyder
case, arises at the interface between matter and vacuum which is treated as
a shock wave in
[
44
]
and across which two different coordinate systems
need to be matched in
[
34
]
. No such difficulties arise in our approach.
We use a single coordinate system to cover the whole
spacetime—Painlevé-Gullstrand coordinates—and rely on a general
local existence
result in which the metric is as regular at the boundary of the matter as
anywhere else; the boundary takes care of itself.

In 1984,
Christodoulou

[
18
]
showed that
with dust as matter model solutions can
develop naked singularities which violate weak cosmic censorship.
Later he showed that naked singularities occur also
for a self-gravitating scalar field,
but that for this “matter” model weak cosmic censorship does hold
in a suitable sense, cf.
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

Gravitational collapse in the context of the Einstein-Vlasov system
has previously been investigated in

[
2
,
3
,
6
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
33
,
42
]
,
both numerically and analytically. But for the analytic results the data were
specifically tailored to lead to collapse and in particular were far from
Oppenheimer-Snyder type data or “real” astrophysical situations;
the initial data constructed in
[
6
]
have the
important additional property that they have a complete regular past.
The Vlasov matter model is distinguished from other models mentioned
above by several features. There are classes of initial
data—small data, data with “outgoing” particles—which lead
to global, geodesically complete solutions of the Einstein-Vlasov system,
cf.
[
9
,
26
,
32
,
40
]
. There also exists a plethora of static and stationary solutions of this system, cf.
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
12
,
36
,
38
,
41
]
. Stability or instability
of these steady states has been investigated in

[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
, and perturbations of stable
steady states can result in time-periodic oscillations,
cf.
[
13
,
28
]
. More background on the Einstein-Vlasov system
and also its Newtonian counterpart can be found in
[
1
,
5
,
37
,
39
]
.
Altogether, a self-gravitating, collisionless gas can exhibit a large
variety of dynamical behaviors, both in the Newtonian and in the relativistic
regime, and with the present investigation we intend to add a new
one to this list which has its roots in the classical paper
[
34
]

by
Oppenheimer
and
Snyder
.

2
The Einstein-Vlasov system

2.1
The Einstein-Vlasov system in general coordinates

The Einstein-Vlasov system describes a collisionless, self-gravitating
ensemble of particles in the context of general relativity.
Let
M
denote a smooth four-dimensional spacetime manifold and

g_{\mu\nu}
a Lorentz metric on
M
with signature
(-+++)
.
In local coordinates
x^{\mu}
the corresponding line element takes the form

ds^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu};

Greek indices run from
0
to
3
,
and we use the Einstein summation convention.
The particle ensemble has a density

f=f(x^{\mu},p_{\nu})\geq 0
defined on the cotangent bundle
TM^{*}
,
where
p_{\nu}=g_{\nu\gamma}p^{\gamma}
and
p^{\gamma}
denote the canonical momenta
corresponding to the local spacetime coordinates
x^{\mu}
.
All the particles are assumed to have the same rest mass,
which we normalize to
1
,
i.e.,
f
is supported on the mass shell

PM^{*}\coloneqq\left\{(x^{\mu},p_{\nu})\in TM^{*}\mid g^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}=-1,\;p^{\mu}\ \mbox{future pointing}\right\}.

We assume that on
PM^{*}
the component
p^{0}
can be expressed by the coordinates

(x^{\mu},p_{j})
where Latin indices run from
1
to
3
. In what follows we write

x^{\mu}=(t,x^{j})
and view
t
as a time-like coordinate.
The basic unknowns in the Einstein-Vlasov system (besides the spacetime
M
)
are the Lorentz metric
g_{\mu\nu}

and the particle density
f
. They obey the Einstein equations

\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}=8\pi T_{\mu\nu}

(2.1)

coupled to the Vlasov equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\frac{p^{i}}{p^{0}}\partial_{x^{i}}f-\frac{1}{2p^{0}}\frac{\partial g^{\nu\delta}}{\partial x^{i}}\,p_{\nu}p_{\delta}\,\partial_{p_{i}}f=0.

(2.2)

Here
G_{\mu\nu}
is the Einstein tensor induced by the Lorentz metric

g_{\mu\nu}
. The two equations are coupled by defining
the energy momentum tensor as

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}=|g|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int p_{\mu}p_{\nu}f\,\frac{dp_{1}dp_{2}dp_{3}}{p^{0}},

(2.3)

where
g
is the determinant of the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
;
we use units so that the speed of light
and the gravitational constant equal unity.
The geodesic equations

\frac{dx^{\mu}}{d\tau}=p^{\mu}=g^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu},\quad\frac{dp_{\mu}}{d\tau}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial g^{\nu\delta}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\,p_{\nu}p_{\delta},

which describe the worldlines of test particles on the spacetime manifold

(M,g_{\mu\nu})
, correspond to the characteristic equations of the Vlasov
equation (
2.2
) for
f
supported on the mass-shell
PM^{*}
,
the latter being invariant under the geodesic flow.

We wish to study the Einstein-Vlasov system
(
2.1
), (
2.2
), (
2.3
)
under the assumptions of spherical symmetry and asymptotic flatness.

2.2
The system in Painlevé-Gullstrand coordinates

We consider the Einstein-Vlasov system with a metric of the form

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(dr+\beta dt)^{2}+r^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2}\right)

where
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
r\geq 0
,
\theta\in[0,\pi]
,
\phi\in[0,2\pi]
,
and
a=a(t,r),\ \beta=\beta(t,r)
. The radial coordinate
r
is the
area radius, i.e., a sphere given by fixed values of
t
and
r

and parameterized by the polar angles
\theta
and
\phi
has
surface area equal to
4\pi r^{2}
. The meaning of the time coordinate

t
can be seen as follows. If we consider an observer moving
towards the future along a radial geodesic given by

\dot{r}=\frac{dr}{dt}=-\beta(t,r),

then along this curve, i.e., for such an observer,
ds^{2}=-dt^{2}
,
so that the time coordinate
t
coincides with the proper time measured
by this observer; time is measured by radially falling geodesic clocks,
cf.
[
27
]
.
Asymptotic flatness and regularity at the center require that

\displaystyle\beta(t,0)=\beta(t,\infty)=0,\ a(t,0)=a(t,\infty)=1.

(2.4)

Taking suitable combinations
of the Einstein equations and defining

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{a^{2}}-\beta^{2}

(2.5)

results in the following set of field equations:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(r(1-A)\big)

\displaystyle=8\pi r^{2}\left(\beta(\rho+p)-(a\beta^{2}+a^{-1})j\right),

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\big(r(1-A)\big)

\displaystyle=8\pi r^{2}(\rho-a\beta j),

(2.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\beta-\beta\,\partial_{r}\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2r}(1-A)+4\pi rp,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}a-\beta\,\partial_{r}a

\displaystyle=-4\pi ra^{2}j.

(2.9)

The definition of the matter terms
\rho,p,j
in terms of
f

follows shortly, and we note that

\displaystyle T_{00}

\displaystyle=\rho-2a\beta j+a^{2}\beta^{2}p,

\displaystyle T_{01}

\displaystyle=a^{2}\beta p-aj,

\displaystyle T_{11}

\displaystyle=a^{2}p,

\displaystyle T_{22}

\displaystyle=r^{2}p_{T};

the tangential pressure
p_{T}
, which does not appear in the field equations
(
2.6
)–(
2.9
), will play its role later on.
To formulate the Vlasov equation and the matter terms we
use Cartesian coordinates

(x^{1},x^{2},x^{3})=r\,(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta),

and non-canonical momentum variables defined by the relations

v_{c}=p_{c}+\left(\frac{1}{a}-1\right)\frac{x^{j}p_{j}}{r}\frac{x_{c}}{r},\quad p_{c}=v_{c}+(a-1)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x_{c}}{r}.

Here
p^{c}
are the canonical momenta corresponding to the spatial coordinates
defined above,

\cdot
denotes the Euclidean scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
and indices of vectors

x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are raised and lowered with the Kronecker
\delta
;
in addition
|v|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
Then

p^{0}=\sqrt{1+|v|^{2}}=:\langle v\rangle.

When using these coordinates the function
f
must explicitly
be required to be spherically symmetric,
i.e.,
f(t,Ax,Av)=f(t,x,v)
for
A\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
.
The Vlasov equation reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{v}{a\langle v\rangle}-\beta\frac{x}{r}\right)\cdot\partial_{x}f

\displaystyle{}+\left[\left(\beta\frac{\partial_{r}a}{a}-\frac{\partial_{t}a}{a}+\partial_{r}\beta\right)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\frac{\beta}{r}\left(v-\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}\right)\right]\cdot\partial_{v}f=0.

(2.10)

The matter terms are

\displaystyle\rho(t,r)

\displaystyle=\rho(t,x)=\int\langle v\rangle f(t,x,v)\,dv,

(2.11)

\displaystyle p(t,r)

\displaystyle=p(t,x)=\int\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}f(t,x,v)\frac{dv}{\langle v\rangle},

(2.12)

\displaystyle j(t,r)

\displaystyle=j(t,x)=\int\frac{x\cdot v}{r}f(t,x,v)\,dv,

(2.13)

\displaystyle p_{T}(t,r)

\displaystyle=p_{T}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\int\left|{\frac{x\times v}{r}}\right|^{2}f(t,x,v)\frac{dv}{\langle v\rangle}.

(2.14)

The system (
2.6
)–(
2.13
) together with the boundary conditions
(
2.4
) and the definition (
2.5
) is the Einstein-Vlasov system
in Painlevé-Gullstrand coordinates.
We need to prescribe the following initial data:

\displaystyle f(0)=\mathring{f},\ a(0)=\mathring{a},\ \beta(0)=\mathring{\beta};

(2.15)

here
f(0)
stands for
f

restricted to the initial time slice
t=0
etc.
Besides regularity assumptions which we specify below
the data
\mathring{a}
and
\mathring{\beta}
must
satisfy the boundary conditions
(
2.4
) and the constraint equation (
2.7
).

In passing we note the following fact which will become important later on.
For a characteristic curve
s\mapsto(x(s),v(s))
of the Vlasov equation
(
2.10
),
v(0)=0
implies that
v(s)=0
for all times.
A characteristic with this property corresponds to one of the radially
falling geodesic clocks, and in general,
v/a\langle v\rangle
is the velocity relative
to the latter.

3
The local existence result

3.1
A suitable reduced system

In order to prove a local existence result we
need to select a suitable subsystem, as the system stated above is
over-determined.
We replace the combination of derivatives of
a

in (
2.10
) via (
2.9
) and keep only
(
2.8
) and (
2.9
) together with the corresponding
matter terms to determine
a
and
\beta
.
We use the integrated version of (
2.7
) to define
A

and arrive at the following reduced system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{v}{a\langle v\rangle}-\beta\frac{x}{r}\right)\cdot\partial_{x}f

\displaystyle{}+\left[4\pi r\,a\,j\,\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\left(\partial_{r}\beta-\frac{\beta}{r}\right)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\frac{\beta}{r}v\right]\cdot\partial_{v}f=0.

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\beta-\beta\,\partial_{r}\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2r}(1-A)+4\pi rp,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}a-\beta\,\partial_{r}a

\displaystyle=-4\pi ra^{2}j,

(3.3)

where
\rho,p,j
are given in terms of
f
by (
2.11
)–(
2.13
),
and

\displaystyle A(t,r)=1-\frac{8\pi}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\rho-a\beta j\right)(t,s)\,s^{2}ds.

(3.4)

It is important to make sure that

remains positive.
This fact together with the form of (
3.3
) motivates an
auxiliary quantity defined by

\displaystyle a=e^{\lambda},

(3.5)

in terms of which (
3.3
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}-\beta\,\partial_{r}\right)\,e^{-\lambda}=4\pi r\,j.

(3.6)

3.2
Initial data

The initial data prescribed in (
2.15
) must satisfy the following
assumptions:

\mathring{f}\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),\ \mathring{a}\in C^{1}([0,\infty[),\ \mathring{\beta}\in C^{2}([0,\infty[),

where the subscript

denotes compact support,
\mathring{f}

is spherically symmetric,

\mathring{f}\geq 0,\ \mathring{a}(0)=1=\mathring{a}(\infty),\ \mathring{a}^{\prime}(0)=0,\ \mathring{\beta}(0)=\mathring{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0=\mathring{\beta}(\infty),

\mathring{a}(r)>0\ \mbox{for}\ r\geq 0,

and

\displaystyle\mathring{A}(r)=\frac{1}{\mathring{a}(r)^{2}}-\mathring{\beta}(r)^{2}\ \mbox{for}\ r\geq 0,

(3.7)

where
\mathring{A}
is as in (
3.4
) with

\rho=\mathring{\rho}
and
j=\mathring{\jmath}
induced by
\mathring{f}
.

Remark 3.1
.

Such data can for example be obtained by taking
\mathring{a}=1
,

\mathring{f}
even in
v
so that
\mathring{\jmath}=0
,
and then solving (
3.7
) for
\mathring{\beta}
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathring{\beta}(r)=\left(\frac{8\pi}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\mathring{\rho}(s)\,s^{2}ds\right)^{1/2}.

(3.8)

Using l’Hospital’s rule one can show that indeed

\mathring{\beta}\in C^{2}([0,\infty[),\ \mathring{\beta}(0)=\mathring{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0.

However, we must not a-priori restrict our local existence result
to such data, because when we extend the solution to some maximal
existence interval,
f_{|t=t_{0}},a_{|t=t_{0}},\beta_{|t=t_{0}}

must for any

3.3
The solution concept and the local existence result

We first specify the regularity properties which the various parts of a
solution must satisfy.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval.

(a)

f\colon I\times\mathbb{R}^{6}\rightarrow[0,\infty[
is regular, if

f\in C^{1}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{6})
,
f(t)
is spherically symmetric for

t\in I
, and
\mathrm{supp}\,f(t)
is compact, locally uniformly in
t\in I
.

(b)

\rho
(or
p
,
p_{T}
)
\colon I\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow[0,\infty[

is regular, if

\rho\in C^{1}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
,
\rho(t)

is spherically symmetric for
t\in I
,
and
\mathrm{supp}\,\rho(t)
is compact, locally uniformly in
t\in I
.

(c)

j\colon I\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is regular, if

j\in C(I\times\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap C^{1}(I\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})
,

j(t)
is spherically symmetric for
t\in I
,
\mathrm{supp}\,j(t)
is compact,
locally uniformly in
t\in I
,
j\in C^{1}(I\times[0,\infty[)

as a function in
(t,r)
, and
j(t,0)=0
.

(d)

a\colon I\times[0,\infty[\;\rightarrow\;]0,\infty[

is regular,
if
a\in C^{1}(I\times[0,\infty[)
,
and the boundary conditions
\partial_{r}a(t,0)=0,\ a(t,0)=1
hold.

(e)

\beta\colon I\times[0,\infty[\;\rightarrow\;\mathbb{R}

is regular, if

\beta\in C^{1}(I\times[0,\infty[)
,

\partial_{r}\beta\in C^{1}(I\times[0,\infty[)
,
and the boundary conditions

\beta(t,0)=\partial_{r}^{2}\beta(t,0)=0
hold.

(f)

The fields are locally bounded, if

a^{-1}
,

,
\beta
,

\partial_{r}\beta

are bounded on

for any compact interval

J\subset I
.

Note that the boundary conditions at infinity which were part of (
2.4
)
are not included in the definition above; we will take care of them after
having established a solution in the sense of the definition.
We aim to prove the following result; by

we denote the
sup norm with respect to

or
(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}

Theorem 3.3
.

Each set of initial data
(\mathring{f},\mathring{a},\mathring{\beta})

as specified in Section
3.2
launches a unique solution
(f,a,\beta)

of the reduced system (

)–(

[0,T[
, the components of

3.2
.
The boundary conditions (

) hold.
If

is chosen maximal and

\displaystyle\sup\bigl\{|v|\mid(x,v)\in\mathrm{supp}\,f(t),\ 0\leq t<T\bigr\}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sup\bigl\{\|a(t)\|+\|\partial_{r}\beta(t)\|\mid 0\leq t<T\bigr\}<\infty,

(3.9)

then

.

This theorem will be proven by setting up an iteration scheme which generates
a sequence of approximate solutions converging to the desired solution

3.4
Recovering the full system

It is important that a sufficiently regular solution of the subsystem

Proposition 3.4

Assume that
(f,a,\beta)
is a solution of the subsystem as obtained in

2.6
)
and the Vlasov equation in the form (

) hold as well.
The full set of the Einstein equations (
2.1
) is satisfied.

In order to prove this result, the following auxiliary results will be useful.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f
be a regular solution of the Vlasov equation (
3.1
)
corresponding to a given, regular set of fields

(\tilde{a},\tilde{\jmath},\tilde{\beta})
. Then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=\tilde{\beta}\partial_{r}\rho-\frac{1}{\tilde{a}}\partial_{r}j-\frac{2}{r\tilde{a}}j+\frac{2\tilde{\beta}}{r}(\rho+p_{T})+\left(\partial_{r}\tilde{\beta}+4\pi r\tilde{a}\tilde{\jmath}\right)(\rho+p),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}j

\displaystyle=\tilde{\beta}\partial_{r}j+\frac{2\tilde{\beta}}{r}j-\frac{1}{\tilde{a}}\left(\partial_{r}p+\frac{2}{r}p-\frac{2}{r}p_{T}\right)+2\left(\partial_{r}\tilde{\beta}+4\pi r\tilde{a}\tilde{\jmath}\right)j.

Proof.

First we differentiate the formulas (
2.11
) and (
2.13
) with respect
to
t
. Then we substitute
\partial_{t}f
via the Vlasov equation
(
3.1
). The terms containing
\partial_{v}f
can be integrated
by parts. The terms containing
\partial_{x}f
can be related to
(radial derivatives of) various source terms,
more precisely,

\displaystyle\int v\cdot\partial_{x}fdv

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}j+\frac{2}{r}j,

\displaystyle\int\langle v\rangle\frac{x}{r}\cdot\partial_{x}fdv

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}\rho,

\displaystyle\int\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}\cdot\partial_{x}fdv

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}j,

\displaystyle\int\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{v}{\langle v\rangle}\cdot\partial_{x}fdv

cf.
[

∎

Notice that in the proof only the Vlasov equation is used,
the field equations play no role. This will become important
in the next section, where we will apply this
result to the iterates in an iteration scheme.
An analogous comment applies to the second auxiliary result.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
T>0
and let
\tilde{\beta}
be
regular on
[0,T[\times[0,\infty[
in the sense of
Definition
3.2
(e), and bounded on

[0,T^{\prime}]\times[0,\infty[
for any

.

(a)

For any
r\geq 0
and

there exists an unique

solution

[0,T[\,\ni s\mapsto R(s,t,r)
of the equation

\dot{r}=-\tilde{\beta}(s,r)

with
R(t,t,r)=r
. Moreover,
R\in C^{1}([0,T[^{2}\times[0,\infty[)
and

\partial_{r}R\in C^{1}([0,T[^{2}\times[0,\infty[)
with

R(s,t,0)=0=\partial_{r}^{2}R(s,t,0)
.

(b)

For
\mathring{b}\in C^{1}([0,\infty[)

and

,

defines the unique solution
b\in C^{1}([0,T[\times[0,\infty[)
of
the initial value problem

\partial_{t}b-\tilde{\beta}(t,r)\,\partial_{r}b=q(t,r),\ b(0,r)=\mathring{b}(r).

Proof.

In order to prove part (a) we note that the assumptions on
\tilde{\beta}

allow us to extend this function
via
\tilde{\beta}(t,r)\coloneqq-\tilde{\beta}(t,-r)
to

\tilde{\beta}\in C^{1}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R})
with
\tilde{\beta}(t,0)=0
.
For this extended characteristic equation the existence
of
R\in C^{1}([0,T[^{2}\times\mathbb{R})
is standard; notice that by assumption,

\tilde{\beta}
is bounded on compact subintervals of
[0,T[
so that
the characteristics do exist
on
[0,T[
. Since
\tilde{\beta}(t,0)=0
it follows that
R(s,t,0)=0
,
and no characteristic
can cross the line

. Hence we can consistently restrict

to

[0,T[^{2}\times[0,\infty[
.
Since

\frac{d}{ds}\partial_{r}R(s,t,r)=-\partial_{r}\tilde{\beta}(s,R(s,t,r))\partial_{r}R(s,t,r),\ \partial_{r}R(t,t,r)=1

it follows that

\displaystyle\partial_{r}R(s,t,r)=\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\partial_{r}\tilde{\beta}(\tau,R(\tau,t,r))\,d\tau\right),

(3.10)

and since

\partial_{r}R\in C^{1}([0,T[^{2}\times\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover,
\partial_{r}^{2}R(s,t,0)=0

\partial_{r}^{2}\tilde{\beta}(t,0)=0
.
Part (b) is standard theory of first order PDEs.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition

.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.
Assume that we are given a regular solution to the subsystem
(
3.1
)–(
3.4
). Then by (
3.3
), the original
Vlasov equation (
2.10
) holds as well. The key points are to see that

, which in the subsystem is given by (

), indeed satisfies
the relation (
2.5
) and the field equation (
2.6
);
the field equation (
2.7

).

We first compute the left hand side of (
2.6
),
using the definition (
3.4
)
and Lemma

,

\partial_{r}p
, or
\partial_{r}j

If we integrate these terms by parts, we find that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(r(1-A))

\displaystyle=8\pi r^{2}\left(\beta(\rho+p)-(a\beta^{2}+1/a)j\right)

\displaystyle{}+4\pi\int_{0}^{r}s^{2}aj\,\partial_{r}\left(\frac{1}{a^{2}}-\beta^{2}-A\right)(t,s)\,ds;

2.5

Let us define

Using (

and (

\partial_{t}d-\beta\,\partial_{r}d=4\pi rajd-\frac{4\pi}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\partial_{r}(aj\sigma^{2})(t,\sigma)d(t,\sigma)\,d\sigma.

Now the assumption on the data guarantees that

d(t,r)=4\pi\int_{0}^{t}\left[(raj)-\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\partial_{r}(aj\sigma^{2})(t,\sigma)d(t,\sigma)\,d\sigma\right](s,R(s,t,r))\,ds.

In the present context the function

To conclude this proof we must recall that (

It remains to show that

The equation

\displaystyle\frac{r}{a^{3}}\Bigl[r\,a^{3}\partial_{r}\partial_{t}\beta-r\,a^{2}\partial^{2}_{t}a-r\,a^{2}\beta^{2}\partial^{2}_{r}a-r\,a^{3}\beta\partial^{2}_{r}\beta+2r\,a^{2}\beta\partial_{r}\partial_{t}a

\displaystyle+2r\,a^{2}\partial_{t}a\partial_{r}\beta+r\,a^{2}\partial_{t}\beta\partial_{r}a-r\,a^{3}\left(\partial_{r}\beta\right)^{2}-a^{2}\beta^{2}\partial_{r}a

4
Proof of the local existence result

In this section we prove Theorem
3.3
.
The proof is split into a number of steps
which we deal with in the various subsections which follow.

4.1
The Vlasov equation and the characteristic system

We will deal with the modified Vlasov equation (
3.1
) via the method of
characteristics. When constructing the iterative scheme below it is important
that we study (
3.1
) for a prescribed set of fields

\tilde{a},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\jmath}
.
For
s\in[0,T[
,
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we define

\displaystyle F_{1}(s,x,v)\coloneqq\frac{v}{\tilde{a}(s,r)\langle v\rangle}-\tilde{\beta}(s,r)\frac{x}{r},

(4.1)

\displaystyle F_{2}(s,x,v)\coloneqq 4\pi r\,(\tilde{a}\,\tilde{\jmath})(s,r)\,\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\left(\partial_{r}\tilde{\beta}-\frac{\tilde{\beta}}{r}\right)(s,r)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\frac{\tilde{\beta}(s,r)}{r}v.

(4.2)

In what follows we often abbreviate
z=(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The characteristic system of the modified Vlasov equation (
3.1
)
can now be written in the form

\displaystyle\dot{z}=F(s,z).

(4.3)

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
\tilde{a},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{\jmath}

are regular and locally bounded
on the time interval
[0,T[
as specified
in Definition
3.2
.

(a)

For any
t\in[0,T[
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
there exists a unique
solution
[0,T[\,\ni s\mapsto Z(s,t,z)=(X,V)(s,t,x,v)
of
(
4.3
) with

Z(t,t,z)=z
. Moreover,

Z\in C^{1}([0,T[\times[0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

(b)

By
f(t,z)\coloneqq\mathring{f}(Z(0,t,z))
for
t\in[0,T[
and

z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}

the unique, regular solution to the modified Vlasov equation (
3.1
)
with
f(0)=\mathring{f}
is defined.

Proof.

We claim that
F\in C^{1}([0,T[\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The crucial point here is
the regularity at the spatial origin. The behavior of
a,\ j,\ \beta

at
r=0
together with l’Hospital’s rule imply that

\lim_{r\to 0}\left(\partial_{r}\beta-\frac{\beta}{r}\right)(s,r)=0=\lim_{r\to 0}\left(\frac{r\partial_{r}\beta-\beta}{r^{2}}\right)(s,r).

Hence the limits

\lim_{r\to 0}\partial_{x_{i}}\left(\beta\frac{x_{j}}{r}\right)=\partial_{r}\beta(t,0)\delta_{ij},

\lim_{r\to 0}\partial_{x_{i}}\left(raj\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x_{j}}{r}\right)=0=\lim_{r\to 0}\partial_{x_{i}}\left(\left(\partial_{r}\beta-\frac{\beta}{r}\right)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x_{j}}{r}\right)

exist, and
F
is
C^{1}
, also at the center.
Since for any
0<T^{\prime}<T
the function
F
is bounded with respect to

s\in[0,T^{\prime}]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
grows at most linearly in
|v|
, any solution of the
characteristic system exists
for
s\in[0,T[
. The remaining assertions are standard.
∎

4.2
The iteration scheme—the set-up

4.2.1
The
0\,
th iterates

We define the
0\,
th iterate of the fields on
[0,\infty[\times[0,\infty[

as

a_{0}(t,r)\coloneqq 1,\ j_{0}(t,r)\coloneqq 0,\ \beta_{0}(t,r)\coloneqq 0.

These iterates need not fit the given initial data, but it is
important that they satisfy (

).
For technical reasons we also define

f_{0}\coloneqq 0
.

4.2.2
Induction assumption on the
(n-1)\,
st iterates

We assume that
a_{n-1},j_{n-1},\beta_{n-1}
are defined on

[0,T_{n-1}[\times[0,\infty[
for some
T_{n-1}>0
,
and that they are regular and locally bounded
in the sense of Definition

. In addition, we
assume that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}a_{n-1}-\beta_{n-1}\,\partial_{r}a_{n-1}=-4\pi ra_{n-1}^{2}j_{n-1};

(4.4)

notice that these assumptions hold for the
0\,
th iterates, i.e., for
n=1
.

4.2.3
f_{n}
and its characteristics

We replace

\tilde{a},\ \tilde{\jmath},\ \tilde{\beta}
in (
4.1
), (
4.2
)
by the above
(n-1)\,
st
iterates and denote the resulting function by
F_{n-1}
. Then we can apply
Lemma
4.1
to obtain corresponding iterates for the
characteristics
Z_{n}=(X_{n},V_{n})
as the solution of

\dot{z}=F_{n-1}(s,z)
and
f_{n}(t,z)\coloneqq\mathring{f}(Z_{n}(0,t,z))

as the
n\,
th iterate
for
f
, which is defined on
[0,T_{n-1}[\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
As desired,
f_{n}
is regular in the sense of Definition
3.2
(a).

4.2.4
The
n\,
th iterates for the source terms

We define
\rho_{n},p_{n},j_{n},p_{T,n}

by (
2.11
)–(
2.14
) with
f_{n}
instead of
f
. These functions
are regular in the sense of Definition
3.2
(b), (c);
for the details of this assertion we refer to
[
37
, Lemma 6.8]
.

4.2.5
The
n\,
th iterates for the metric quantities—
A_{n}

We define

\displaystyle A_{n}(t,r)=1-\frac{8\pi}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\rho_{n}-a_{n-1}\beta_{n-1}j_{n}\right)(t,s)\,s^{2}ds.

(4.5)

It is easily seen that the function

(1-A_{n})/(2r)
is continuously differentiable on
[0,T_{n-1}[\times[0,\infty[

and twice continuously differentiable with respect to
r
on this set
with
[(1-A_{n})/(2r)]_{|r=0}=0=\partial_{r}^{2}[(1-A_{n})/(2r)]_{|r=0}
.

4.2.6
The
n\,
th iterates for the metric quantities—
\beta_{n}

This is the crucial step.
We define
\beta_{n}
as
the solution of the initial value problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\beta-\beta_{n-1}\,\partial_{r}\beta=\frac{1}{2r}(1-A_{n})+4\pi rp_{n},\ \beta(0,r)=\mathring{\beta}(r).

(4.6)

By the induction assumption on the previous iterates
we can apply Lemma
3.6
(a) with

b=\beta_{n-1}
.
This implies that for any
t\in[0,T_{n-1}[
and
r\geq 0

there exists a unique solution

[0,T_{n-1}[\,\ni s\mapsto R_{n}(s,t,r)
of the characteristic equation

\dot{r}=-\beta_{n-1}(s,r)

with
R_{n}(t,t,r)=r
, and
R_{n}
has the regularity properties stated
in Lemma
3.6
(a).
Following part (b) of that lemma we define the next iterate for
\beta
as

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(t,r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathring{\beta}(R_{n}(0,t,r))+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2r}(1-A_{n})\right)(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad{}+4\pi\int_{0}^{t}(rp_{n})(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\,ds

\displaystyle=:\beta_{n,1}(t,r)+\beta_{n,2}(t,r)+\beta_{n,3}(t,r).

(4.7)

The difficulty is to recover for
\beta_{n}
the regularity properties
which we assumed for
\beta_{n-1}
. To do so we first observe that analogously
to (
3.10
),

\displaystyle\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)=\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(\tau,R_{n}(\tau,t,r))\,d\tau\right).

(4.8)

Clearly,

\beta_{n,1}\in C^{1}([0,T_{n-1}[\times[0,\infty[)
with

\partial_{r}\beta_{n,1}\in C^{1}([0,T_{n-1}[\times[0,\infty[)
.
The same is true for
\beta_{n,2}
by the regularity properties
of
(1-A_{n})/(2r)
stated above.
The problematic term is
\beta_{n,3}
.
Obviously,

\partial_{r}\beta_{n,3}(t,r)=\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{r}(rp_{n})(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)\,ds.

From
[
37
, p. 98]
we see that

\displaystyle\partial_{r}(rp_{n})(s,r)=2p_{T,n}(s,r)-p_{n}(s,r)+r\int\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{v}{\langle v\rangle}\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}(s,x,v)\,dv.

(4.9)

Only the last term, which we denote by
\sigma_{n}(s,x)
,

To do so we need to exploit the fact that

f_{n}
solves the “previous” Vlasov equation.
We define a
Cartesian version of the characteristic
R_{n}(s,t,r)
, namely

s\mapsto X_{n}(s,t,x)
is the solution to

\displaystyle\dot{x}=-\beta_{n-1}(s,r)\frac{x}{r}

(4.10)

with
X_{n}(t,t,x)=x
;
note that the right hand side of this differential equation
is in
C^{1}([0,T_{n-1}[\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and vanishes at the center

. In
particular,
X_{n}(s,t,0)=0
, and no other characteristic can cross the center.
It is easy to see that
|X_{n}(s,t,x)|=R_{n}(s,t,r)
where
r=|x|
.
Moreover,

\frac{X_{n}(s,t,x)}{|X_{n}(s,t,x)|}=\frac{x}{r}

for
x\neq 0
; notice that
\sigma_{n}(s,0)=0
.
It is interesting to note that
s\mapsto(X_{n}(s,t,x),0)

is actually a characteristic
of the Vlasov equation, namely
(X_{n}(s,t,x),0)=(X_{n}(s,t,x,0),V_{n}(s,t,x,0))
.
Let

D_{t}f_{n}(t,x,v)\coloneqq\partial_{t}f_{n}(t,x,v)-\beta_{n-1}(t,r)\frac{x}{r}\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}(t,x,v).

In what follows we often abbreviate

X_{n}(s)=X_{n}(s,t,x)
and
R_{n}(s)=R_{n}(s,t,r)
.
Then the chain rule and the Vlasov equation for
f_{n}
imply that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}f_{n}(s,X_{n}(s),v)

\displaystyle=(D_{t}f_{n})(s,X_{n}(s),v)=-\left(\frac{v}{a_{n-1}\langle v\rangle}\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}\right)(s,X_{n}(s),v)

\displaystyle\quad{}-\left(F_{n-1,2}\cdot\partial_{v}f_{n}\right)(s,X_{n}(s),v);

F_{n-1}=(F_{n-1,1},F_{n-1,2})
is defined analogously to
(
4.1
), (
4.2
). This implies that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{v}{\langle v\rangle}\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}\right)(s,X_{n}(s),v)

\displaystyle=-\left(a_{n-1}\frac{x\cdot v}{r}D_{t}f_{n}\right)(s,X_{n}(s),v)

\displaystyle\quad{}-\left(a_{n-1}\frac{x\cdot v}{r}F_{n-1,2}\cdot\partial_{v}f_{n}\right)(s,X_{n}(s),v).

Integration with respect to
v
and an integration by parts imply that

\displaystyle\left(\int\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{v}{\langle v\rangle}\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}dv\right)(s,R_{n}(s))

\displaystyle=-a_{n-1}(s,R_{n}(s))\frac{d}{ds}j_{n}(s,R_{n}(s))

\displaystyle{}+2a_{n-1}(s,R_{n}(s))\left(4\pi\,a_{n-1}\,j_{n-1}\,j_{n}+\left(\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}+\frac{\beta_{n-1}}{r}\right)j_{n}\right)(s,R_{n}(s)).

We substitute this into the formula for
\partial_{r}\beta_{n,3}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\partial_{r}\beta_{n,3}(t,r)=\int_{0}^{t}(2p_{T,n}-p_{n})(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\,\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)\,ds

\displaystyle{}-\int_{0}^{t}(ra_{n-1})(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)\frac{d}{ds}j_{n}(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\,ds

\displaystyle{}+2\int_{0}^{t}\left(ra_{n-1}j_{n}\left(4\pi\,a_{n-1}\,j_{n-1}+\left(\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}+\frac{\beta_{n-1}}{r}\right)\right)\right)(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)\,ds

\displaystyle=:\beta^{\prime}_{n,31}(t,r)+\beta^{\prime}_{n,32}(t,r)+\beta^{\prime}_{n,33}(t,r).

(4.11)

The term
\beta^{\prime}_{n,31}
is continuously differentiable, where we have to
observe that
\partial_{r}^{2}R_{n}
exists and is continuous.
The term
\beta^{\prime}_{n,33}
is continuously differentiable by the properties of the
previous iterates. In the term
\beta^{\prime}_{n,32}
we integrate by parts
and observe that (

) holds:

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}_{n,32}(t,r)

\displaystyle=-ra_{n-1}(t,r)j_{n}(t,r)+(ra_{n-1}j_{n})(0,R_{n}(0,t,r))\partial_{r}R_{n}(0,t,r)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}j_{n}(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\,\Bigl[\partial_{s}R_{n}(s,t,r)\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)a_{n-1}(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))

\displaystyle\qquad+R_{n}(s,t,r)\partial_{s}\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)a_{n-1}(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))

\displaystyle\qquad-R_{n}(s,t,r)\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)\,4\pi(ra^{2}_{n-1}j_{n-1})(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\Bigr]\,ds.

(4.12)

The first two terms are continuously differentiable.
The same is true for the first and last term under the integral.
The crucial term is
\partial_{s}\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)
.
First we note that

\partial_{s}\partial_{r}^{2}R_{n}(s,t,r)=-\partial_{r}\bigl(\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(s,R_{n}(s,t,r))\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)\bigr)

which exists and is continuous.
Now it holds that

\partial_{t}R_{n}(s,t,r)=\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)\,\beta_{n-1}(t,r)

which is continuously differentiable with respect to
s
and
t
,
and this implies that also
\partial_{s}\partial_{t}\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)

exists and is continuous.
Hence the term
\beta^{\prime}_{n,32}
is continuously differentiable with
respect to
t
and
r
.
It follows that
\beta_{n}\in C^{1}([0,T_{n-1}[\times[0,\infty[)
with

\partial_{r}\beta_{n}\in C^{1}([0,T_{n-1}[\times[0,\infty[)
as desired.
Also,
\beta_{n}(t,0)=0=\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}(t,0)
.
The assertion for
\beta_{n}(t,0)
follows from the definition
(
4.7
). For the assertion of its second order derivative
in
r
we check all the terms which appear in that derivative and
observe that each of these contains at least one factor of the form

R_{n},\ \partial_{s}R_{n},\ \partial_{r}^{2}R_{n},\ \partial_{r}^{2}[(1-A_{n})/(2r)],\ j_{n},\ \partial_{r}p_{n},\ \partial_{r}p_{T,n}
,
each of which vanishes at
r=0
.
Altogether, we have shown that
\beta_{n}
is regular in the sense of
Definition
3.2
(e).

4.3
Bounds on the iterates—loop 1

We fix two constants

In what follows,

or

For

and

\displaystyle P_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup\bigl\{|V_{n}(s,0,z)|\mid z\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mathring{f},\ \ 0\leq s\leq t\bigr\},

(4.16)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup\bigl\{|X_{n}(s,0,z)|\mid z\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mathring{f},\ \ 0\leq s\leq t\bigr\}.

On the interval
[0,T_{n-1}[
,

f_{n}(t,x,v)=0\ \mbox{if}\ |x|\geq Q_{n}(t)\ \mbox{or}\ |v|\geq P_{n}(t),

\|\rho_{n}(t)\|,\ \|p_{n}(t)\|,\ \|p_{T,n}(t)\|,\ \|j_{n}(t)\|\leq CP_{n}(t)^{4},

We use the characteristic equations
which define
X_{n}(s)
and
V_{n}(s)
to estimate

and
P_{n}
:

\displaystyle Q_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\leq C+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|e^{-\lambda_{n-1}(s)}\|+\|\beta_{n-1}(s)\|\right)ds,

(4.18)

\displaystyle P_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\leq C+C\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(Q_{n-1}(s)\|a_{n-1}(s)\|\,P_{n-1}(s)^{4}+\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(s)\|\bigr)P_{n}(s)\,ds.

(4.19)

Next (
4.5
) and (

) imply that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{r}(1-A_{n})(t)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq CQ_{n}(t)P_{n}(t)^{4}\left(1+\|a_{n-1}(t)\|\|\beta_{n-1}(t)\|\right),

(4.20)

\displaystyle\|\beta_{n}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathring{\beta}\|+C\int_{0}^{t}Q_{n}(s)P_{n}(s)^{4}\left(1+\|a_{n-1}(s)\|\|\beta_{n-1}(s)\|\right)ds.

(4.21)

Before we continue with our Gronwall argument we notice that the estimates
(
4.18
)–(
4.21
),
which we could have carried out before constructing

a_{n}
in Section

, show that
\beta_{n}

satisfies the boundedness condition
which was used in constructing

, cf. the discussion
at the end of Section
4.2.7
.

We continue with the Gronwall loop by estimating
\partial_{r}\beta_{n}
.
From the discussion for the radial derivatives of the right had side
of (

) it follows that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n}(t)\|\leq C\biggl[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(\tau)\|d\tau\right)+Q_{n}(t)P_{n}(t)^{4}\|a_{n-1}(t)\|

\displaystyle\quad{}+\int_{0}^{t}P_{n}(s)^{4}\biggl(1+\|a_{n-1}(s)\|\|\beta_{n-1}(s)\|

\displaystyle\qquad+Q_{n}(s)\|a_{n-1}(s)\|\left(\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(s)\|+Q_{n}(s)\|a_{n-1}(s)\|P_{n-1}(s)^{4}\right)\biggr)ds

\displaystyle\quad{}+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(\tau)\|d\tau\right)P_{n}(s)^{4}\biggl(1+\|a_{n-1}(s)\|\|\beta_{n-1}(s)\|

\displaystyle\qquad+Q_{n}(s)\|a_{n-1}(s)\|\left(Q_{n-1}(s)\|a_{n-1}(s)\|P_{n-1}(s)^{4}+\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(s)\|\right)\biggr)ds;

(4.22)

notice that the term
\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)
, which arises when differentiating
(
4.7
), is given by (
4.8
).

Finally we bound
1/a_{n}=e^{-\lambda_{n}}
and
a_{n}
. As to the former,
(

) implies that

\displaystyle\|e^{-\lambda_{n}(t)}\|\leq C\left(1+\int_{0}^{t}Q_{n}(s)P_{n}(s)^{4}ds\right).

(4.23)

On the other hand, we can apply Lemma

(b) to the equation
(

\displaystyle\|a_{n}(t)\|\leq C\left(1+\int_{0}^{t}Q_{n}(s)P_{n}(s)^{4}\|a_{n}(s)\|^{2}ds\right).

(4.24)

The estimates (
4.18
)–(
4.24
) form a closed Gronwall-type loop
which allows us to establish uniform control of the involved quantities.
We define

[0,T[\,\ni t\mapsto(z_{Q}(t),z_{P}(t),z_{\beta}(t),z_{\partial_{r}\beta}(t),z_{1/a}(t),z_{a}(t))

as the maximal solution of the set of integral equations

\displaystyle z_{Q}(t)

\displaystyle=C+\int_{0}^{t}\left(z_{1/a}(s)+z_{\beta}(s)\right)ds,

\displaystyle z_{P}(t)

\displaystyle=C+C\int_{0}^{t}\left(z_{Q}(s)z_{a}(s)z_{P}(s)^{4}+z_{\partial_{r}\beta}(s)\right)z_{P}(s)\,ds,

\displaystyle z_{\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=C+C\int_{0}^{t}z_{Q}(s)z_{P}(s)^{4}\left(1+z_{a}(s)z_{\beta}(s)\right)ds,

\displaystyle z_{\partial_{r}\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=C\biggl[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}z_{\partial_{r}\beta}(\tau)d\tau\right)+z_{Q}(t)z_{P}(t)^{4}z_{a}(t)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad{}+\int_{0}^{t}z_{P}(s)^{4}\biggl(1+z_{a}(s)z_{\beta}(s)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+z_{Q}(s)z_{a}(s)\left(z_{\partial_{r}\beta}(s)+z_{Q}(s)z_{a}(s)z_{P}(s)^{4}\right)\biggr)ds

\displaystyle\quad\qquad{}+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}z_{\partial_{r}\beta}(\tau)d\tau\right)z_{P}(s)^{4}\biggl(1+z_{a}(s)z_{\beta}(s)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+z_{Q}(s)z_{a}(s)\left(z_{Q}(s)z_{a}(s)z_{P}(s)^{4}+z_{\partial_{r}\beta}(s)\right)\biggr)ds\biggr],

\displaystyle z_{1/a}(t)

\displaystyle=C\left(1+\int_{0}^{t}z_{Q}(s)z_{P}(s)^{4}ds\right),

\displaystyle z_{a}(t)

,

, and for

4.5
Convergence—loop 1

We fix some interval

\displaystyle|\partial_{r}R_{n}-\partial_{r}R_{n-1}|(s,t,r)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(\tau)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-2}(\tau)\|+|R_{n}-R_{n-1}|(\tau,t,r)\right)d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(\tau)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-2}(\tau)\|+\|\beta_{n-1}(\tau)-\beta_{n-2}(\tau)\|\right)d\tau.

Using this it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n,1}(t)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1,1}(t)\|+\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n,2}(t)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1,2}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\|f_{n}(s)-f_{n-1}(s)\|+\|f_{n-1}(s)-f_{n-2}(s)\|+\|\beta_{n-1}(s)-\beta_{n-2}(s)\|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad{}+\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(s)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-2}(s)\|\Bigr)ds.

(4.43)

When estimating the difference

\partial_{r}\beta_{n,3}-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1,3}

the same terms will come up again
from the terms
\beta^{\prime}_{n,31}
and
\beta^{\prime}_{n,33}
in (
4.11
),
and the only qualitatively new term is

\partial_{s}\partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)-\partial_{s}\partial_{r}R_{n-1}(s,t,r),

the one arising
after the integration by parts in
\beta^{\prime}_{n,32}
,
cf. (
4.12
). But
differentiating (
3.10
) with respect to
s
we see that the latter
difference term can be estimated by

\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n}(t)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(t)\|+|\partial_{r}R_{n}-\partial_{r}R_{n-1}|(s,t,r)+|R_{n}-R_{n-1}|(s,t,r).

Altogether,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n}(t)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\Bigl(\|f_{n}(s)-f_{n-1}(s)\|+\|f_{n-1}(s)-f_{n-2}(s)\|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad{}+\|\beta_{n-1}(s)-\beta_{n-2}(s)\|+\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(s)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-2}(s)\|\Bigr)ds.

(4.44)

We define

d_{n}(t)\coloneqq\|f_{n}(t)-f_{n-1}(t)\|+\|\beta_{n}(t)-\beta_{n-1}(t)\|+\|\partial_{r}\beta_{n}(t)-\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}(t)\|

and add the estimates (
4.41
), (
4.42
), (
4.44
)
to establish the estimate

\displaystyle d_{n}(t)\leq C\int_{0}^{t}d_{n-1}(s)\,ds

(4.45)

which holds for
n\geq 2
and
t\in[0,\delta]
; note that

\|f_{n}(s)-f_{n-1}(s)\|
can be eliminated from the right hand side
using (
4.42
).

It is now a standard argument to conclude form (
4.45
) that

f_{n}\to f,\ \beta_{n}\to\beta,\ \partial_{r}\beta_{n}\to\partial_{r}\beta

uniformly in
t\in[0,\delta]
and uniformly in the other relevant variables.
This immediately implies the convergences

\rho_{n}\to\rho,\ a_{n}\to a,\ \frac{1}{a_{n}}\to\frac{1}{a},\ \frac{1-A_{n}}{2r}\to\frac{1-A}{2r},

where
A
is given by (
3.4
),
the other source terms converge as well, and

R_{n}(s,t,r)\to R(s,t,r),\ \partial_{r}R_{n}(s,t,r)\to\partial_{r}R(s,t,r)

where
s\mapsto R(s,t,r)
solves
\dot{r}=-\beta(s,r)
with
R(t,t,r)=r
.
The fact that

\partial_{t}\beta_{n}-\beta_{n-1}\partial_{r}\beta_{n}=\frac{1-A_{n}}{2r}+4\pi rp_{n}

implies that also
\partial_{t}\beta_{n}\to\partial_{t}\beta
, in particular,

\beta\in C^{1}([0,\delta]\times[0,\infty[)
satisfies
(
3.2
) and
\beta(0)=\mathring{\beta}
.

4.6
Convergence of
\partial_{r}a_{n}
—regularity of
a

The convergence of
\partial_{r}a_{n}
does not follow directly from the
Gronwall loop in the previous subsection, it requires a separate,
non-trivial argument. First we observe that it suffices to prove the
convergence of
\partial_{r}\lambda_{n}
. If we recall the definition of

\lambda_{n}
in (
4.14
) and take a radial derivative, the first
term on the right hand side converges by the results from the previous
subsection, and we are left with proving the convergence of

\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{r}\left[(rj_{n})(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r))\right]\,ds.

From
[
37
, Lemma 6.6, Lemma 6.8]
we can conclude that

\partial_{r}(rj_{n})=r\int v\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}dv-j_{n}

so that we are left with proving the convergence of

\int_{0}^{t}\left(r\int v\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}dv\right)(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r))\,\partial_{r}R_{n+1}(s,t,r)\,ds.

By the Vlasov equation for
f_{n}
,

\displaystyle\int v\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}dv=-\int a_{n-1}\langle v\rangle\biggl(\partial_{t}f_{n}-\beta_{n-1}\frac{x}{r}\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}

\displaystyle\quad{}+\left[4\pi ra_{n-1}j_{n-1}\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\left(\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}-\frac{\beta_{n-1}}{r}\right)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\frac{\beta_{n-1}}{r}v\right]\cdot\partial_{v}f_{n}\biggl)dv.

In the term with
[\dots]
we integrate by parts with respect to
v
,
and the resulting terms converge by the previous subsection.
In order to analyze the remaining term we observe that

\frac{d}{ds}f_{n}(s,X_{n+1}(s,t,x),v)=\left(\partial_{t}f_{n}-\beta_{n}\frac{x}{r}\cdot\partial_{x}f_{n}\right)(s,X_{n+1}(s,t,x),v),

where we need to recall the meaning of the characteristic curve
X_{n+1}(s,t,x)

introduced via (
4.10
); note that
|X_{n+1}(s,t,x)|=R_{n+1}(s,t,r)

if
r=|x|
.
Paying attention to the mismatch in the subscript of
\beta
we are left with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(ra_{n-1})(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r))\int\langle v\rangle\frac{d}{ds}f_{n}(s,X_{n+1}(s,t,x),v)\,dv\partial_{r}R_{n+1}(s,t,r)ds

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\int_{0}^{t}[\ldots]\left(\beta_{n}(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r)-\beta_{n-1}(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r)\right)ds,

where the
[\ldots]
contains various terms which are bounded by the results
obtained in Section
4.3

and
\partial_{x}f_{n}
which is bounded by Section
4.4
.
Since
\beta_{n}
converges uniformly by the results from
Section
4.5
, the second integral can be dropped,
and we must show the convergence of the first one. In that term we integrate by
parts with respect to
s
. The resulting boundary terms converge by the results
in Section
4.5
, and the same is true for the two integrals
containing
\partial_{s}R_{n+1}(s,t,r)
and

\partial_{s}\partial_{r}R_{n+1}(s,t,r)
, and the remaining
integral which needs to converge is

\int_{0}^{t}R_{n+1}(s,t,r)\frac{d}{ds}a_{n-1}(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r))\rho_{n}(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r))\partial_{r}R_{n+1}(s,t,r)\,ds.

We observe that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}a_{n-1}(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r))=\partial_{t}a_{n-1}(\ldots)+\partial_{s}R_{n+1}(s,t,r)\partial_{r}a_{n-1}(\ldots)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{t}a_{n-1}-\beta_{n-1}\partial_{r}a_{n-1}\right)(\ldots)+\left(\beta_{n-1}-\beta_{n}\right)(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r))\partial_{r}a_{n-1}(\ldots)

\displaystyle=-\left(4\pi ra_{n-1}^{2}j_{n-1}\right)(\ldots)+\left(\beta_{n-1}-\beta_{n}\right)(s,R_{n+1}(s,t,r))\partial_{r}a_{n-1}(\ldots),

where in the last step we used (
4.15
).
Since
\partial_{r}a_{n-1}
is uniformly bounded
by the results obtained in Section
4.4
and
\beta_{n}

converges uniformly according to
Section
4.5
the second term vanishes,
while the first now contains only terms which
converge according to Section
4.5
.
Hence
\partial_{r}a_{n}
converges, uniformly
in
t
and
r
, by (
4.15
) the same is true for
\partial_{t}a_{n}
,
and hence
a\in C^{1}([0,\delta]\times[0,\infty[)
satisfies (
3.3
)
and its initial condition.

4.7
Convergence—loop 2

The convergence results and ensuing regularity of the limit objects
is not yet sufficient for
f
to solve the Vlasov equation.
To remedy this situation we consider the derivatives
of the characteristics
Z_{n}(s,t,z)
with respect to
z
and form certain
combinations of these in such a way that the coefficients of
the differential equations satisfied by these combinations have
better convergence and regularity properties than
the original equations. This maneuver is sometimes referred to as
the “Magic Lemma”; cf.
[
37
, Lemma 2.3]
, where its differential
geometric background is explained as well.
In the present situation it takes the following form,
which is motivated by the particular combination of second order derivatives of

a
and
\beta
which is contained in (
3.12
).

As above, we denote by

Z_{n+1}(s,t,z)=(X_{n+1},V_{n+1})(s,t,x,v)=(X_{n+1},V_{n+1})(s)

the solution to the characteristic system

\displaystyle\dot{x}=\frac{v}{a_{n}(s,r)\langle v\rangle}-\beta_{n}(s,r)\frac{x}{r},

(4.46)

\displaystyle\dot{v}=4\pi r\,(a_{n}\,j_{n})(s,r)\,\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\left(\partial_{r}\beta_{n}-\frac{\beta_{n}}{r}\right)(s,r)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\frac{\beta_{n}(s,r)}{r}v

(4.47)

with
Z_{n+1}(t,t,z)=z
. For
j\in\{1,\ldots,6\}
fixed we define

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\partial_{z_{j}}X_{n+1},

(4.48)

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle\coloneqq\partial_{z_{j}}V_{n+1}

\displaystyle\quad{}-\left(4\pi r\,a_{n}^{2}\,j_{n}+a_{n}\left(\partial_{r}\beta_{n}-\frac{\beta_{n}}{r}\right)\right)(s,X_{n+1})\langle V_{n+1}\rangle\frac{X_{n+1}}{|X_{n+1}|}\cdot\xi\frac{X_{n+1}}{|X_{n+1}|}.

(4.49)

Then

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}

\displaystyle=c_{1,n}(s,z_{n+1}(s))\xi+c_{2,n}(s,z_{n+1}(s))\eta,

(4.50)

\displaystyle\dot{\eta}

\displaystyle=c_{3,n}(s,z_{n+1}(s))\xi+c_{4,n}(s,z_{n+1}(s))\eta+c_{n}^{\ast}(s,z_{n+1}(s))\xi,

(4.51)

where
c_{1,n},\ldots,c_{4,n}
are continuous and such that for any
U>0

there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle|c_{i,n}(s,z)|\leq C,\ i=1,\ldots,4,\ n\in\mathbb{N},\ (s,z)\in[0,\delta]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times B_{U},

(4.52)

\displaystyle|\partial_{z}c_{i,n}(s)|\leq C,\ i=1,\ldots,4,\ n\in\mathbb{N},\ (s,z)\in[0,\delta]\times(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})\times B_{U},

(4.53)

and

c_{i,n}\to c_{i}\ \mbox{uniformly on}\ [0,\delta]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times B_{U},\ i=1,\ldots,4;

c_{1,n},\ldots,c_{4,n}
contain only terms the convergence
of which is already established.
This is not true for the critical coefficient

\displaystyle c_{n}^{\ast}(s,x,v)\xi=\left(4\pi ra_{n}^{2}(\beta_{n}\partial_{r}j_{n}-\partial_{t}j_{n})+a_{n}(\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}-\partial_{r}\partial_{t}\beta_{n})\right)\langle v\rangle\frac{x}{r}\cdot\xi\frac{x}{r}.

(4.54)

The justification of this maneuver is a straight forward computation
for which we introduce the notation
A\sim B
to mean that
A-B

contains only terms the convergence of which
was established in Sections
4.5
and
4.6
;
the critical terms, which we collect into
c_{n}^{\ast}
,
are those containing first order derivatives of
j_{n}
or second order
ones of
\beta_{n}
. If we substitute
Z_{n+1}
into (
4.46
) and (
4.47
)
and then differentiate with respect to
z_{j}
, the
\partial_{z_{j}}X_{n+1}

component of the resulting “variational system” obeyed by

\partial_{z_{j}}Z_{n+1}
is of the form (
4.50
).
In order to obtain (
4.51
)
we differentiate (
4.49
) with respect to
s
and take into account
the variational system for
\partial_{z_{j}}Z_{n+1}
:

\displaystyle\dot{\eta}

\displaystyle\sim\left(4\pi r\,a_{n}\,\partial_{r}j_{n}+\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}\right)\,\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}\cdot\xi\frac{x}{r}-\left(4\pi r\,a_{n}^{2}\,\partial_{t}j_{n}+a_{n}\partial_{t}\partial_{r}\beta_{n}\right)\langle v\rangle\frac{x}{r}\cdot\xi\frac{x}{r}

\displaystyle\quad{}-\left(4\pi r\,a_{n}^{2}\,\partial_{r}j_{n}+a_{n}\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}\right)\left(\frac{\frac{x\cdot v}{r}}{a_{n}\langle v\rangle}-\beta_{n}\right)\langle v\rangle\frac{x}{r}\cdot\xi\frac{x}{r}

\displaystyle\sim\left(4\pi r\,a_{n}^{2}\,\partial_{t}j_{n}+a_{n}\partial_{t}\partial_{r}\beta_{n}\right)\langle v\rangle\frac{x}{r}\cdot\xi\frac{x}{r}+\left(4\pi r\,a_{n}^{2}\,\partial_{r}j_{n}+a_{n}\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}\right)\beta_{n}\frac{x}{r}\cdot\xi\frac{x}{r}

\displaystyle=c_{n}^{\ast}\xi

as desired.

It remains to investigate the critical coefficient
c_{n}^{\ast}
,
and we start with the term containing the derivatives of
\beta_{n}
.
The idea is to use the fact that the equation

\partial_{t}\beta_{n}-\beta_{n-1}\partial_{r}\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{2r}(1-A_{n})+4\pi rp_{n},

holds by construction, cf. Section
4.2.6
,
and it can be differentiated with respect
to
r
. Comparing with the definition of
c_{n}^{\ast}
there
is a mismatch in the subscripts,
but this can be remedied as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\partial_{r}\beta_{n}-\beta_{n}\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\partial_{r}\beta_{n}-\beta_{n-1}\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}+(\beta_{n-1}-\beta_{n})\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}\left(\partial_{t}\beta_{n}-\beta_{n-1}\partial_{r}\beta_{n}\right)+\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}\partial_{r}\beta_{n}+(\beta_{n-1}-\beta_{n})\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}

\displaystyle\sim 4\pi r\partial_{r}p_{n}+(\beta_{n-1}-\beta_{n})\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n},

where we used the fact that
\partial_{r}\left((1-A_{n})/(2r)\right)

does converge by
4.5
, which is obvious from the relation
(
4.5
). Since
\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}
is bounded by Section
4.4

and
\beta_{n}
converges by Section
4.5

it follows that
(\beta_{n-1}-\beta_{n})\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{n}\to 0
,
uniformly in
t
and
r
, and we are left with the expression

4\pi r\left(a_{n}\beta_{n}\partial_{r}j_{n}-a_{n}\partial_{t}j_{n}-\partial_{r}p_{n}\right)

as the critical part of
c_{n}^{\ast}
. In order to proceed we express

\partial_{t}j_{n}
via Lemma
3.5
to find that

\displaystyle r\left(a_{n}\beta_{n}\partial_{r}j_{n}-a_{n}\partial_{t}j_{n}-\partial_{r}p_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(p_{n}-p_{T,n}\right)-2ra_{n}\left(\partial_{r}\beta_{n-1}-\frac{\beta_{n-1}}{r}+4\pi ra_{n-1}j_{n-1}\right)j_{n}

\displaystyle\quad{}+ra_{n}(\beta_{n}-\beta_{n-1})\partial_{r}j_{n}+r\left(\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-1}}-1\right)\left(\partial_{r}p_{n}+\frac{2}{r}p_{n}-\frac{2}{r}p_{T,n}\right).

The terms
\partial_{r}j_{n}
and
\partial_{r}p_{n}

are uniformly bounded by Section
4.4
,
and the difference factors
in front of them converge to
0
uniformly by Section
4.5
.
The remaining terms do not
contain any “forbidden” derivatives; they converge uniformly by
Section
4.5
,
and their radial derivatives are uniformly bounded by Section
4.4
.

It is now straight
forward to conclude that
\xi=\xi_{n}
and
\eta=\eta_{n}
converge, and hence

\partial_{z_{j}}X_{n}
and
\partial_{z_{j}}V_{n}
converge, uniformly on

[0,\delta]\times[0,\delta]\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times B_{U}
, where
U>0
was arbitrary.
This implies that
Z\in C^{1}([0,\delta]\times[0,\delta]\times\mathbb{R}^{6})

which in turn implies the missing regularity for
f
,
and the limiting objects
f,a,\beta

do constitute a regular solution to the initial value problem as claimed in
Theorem
3.3
.

Uniqueness of this solution follows if we apply the Gronwall-type estimates
established in Section
4.5
for the
difference of two consecutive iterates to the difference of two
solutions with the same data. This unique solution can be extended to a maximal
existence interval
[0,T[
, and it remains to prove the continuation
criterion contained in Theorem
3.3
.

We still need to check the boundary conditions for the metric at infinity.
As above, let
[0,T[\,\ni s\mapsto R(s,t,r)
denote the unique solution
of the characteristic equation

\displaystyle\dot{r}=-\beta(s,r)

(4.55)

with
R(t,t,r)=r
; these are the characteristics of both
(
3.2
) and (
3.3
). We fix some

T^{\prime}<T
. Then
\beta
is bounded on
[0,T^{\prime}]\times[0,\infty[
,
which for the characteristics implies that

|R(s,t,r)-r|\leq C,\ r\geq 0,\ 0\leq s,t\leq T^{\prime},

and hence

\lim_{r\to\infty}R(s,t,r)=\infty

uniformly in
s,t\in[0,T^{\prime}]
. Since by Lemma
3.6
,

a(t,r)=\mathring{a}(R(0,t,r))-4\pi\int_{0}^{t}\left(ra^{2}j\right)(s,R(s,t,r))\,ds,

the uniformly-in-
t
compact support of
j
and the boundary condition

\mathring{a}(\infty)=1
imply that
a(t,\infty)=1
as required.
Next we note that
the compact support property and (
3.4
)
immediately imply that
A(t,\infty)=1
,
and by the relation (
2.5
),
\beta(t,\infty)=0
as desired.

4.8
The continuation criterion

Let us assume now that the existence interval
[0,T[
of our local solution
is maximal with
T<\infty
,
and that the continuation condition (
3.9
) holds.
Since
f
is constant along characteristics this implies that there is a
constant
C^{\ast}>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T[
,

f(t,x,v)=0\ \mbox{if}\ |v|\geq C^{\ast},\ \|f(t)\|,\|a(t)\|,\|\partial_{r}\beta(t)\|\leq C^{\ast}.

The bound of
\partial_{r}\beta
and the boundary condition

\beta(t,0)=0
imply that
|\beta(t,r)|\leq C^{\ast}r
.
Hence by the first component of the characteristic system,

|\dot{x}(s)|\leq\|1/a(s)\|+C^{\ast}|x(s)|.

For

Q(t)\coloneqq\sup\{|X(s,0,z)|\mid z\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mathring{f},\ \ 0\leq s\leq t\}

this implies that

Q(t)\leq e^{C^{\ast}T}\left(R_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\|1/a(s)\|ds\right).

Next by Lemma
3.6
,

\displaystyle(1/a)(t,r)=(1/\mathring{a})(R(0,t,r))+4\pi\int_{0}^{t}(rj)(s,R(s,t,r))\,ds,

(4.56)

where
R(s,t,r)
are the characteristics defined above, cf. (
4.55
).
Hence

\|1/a(t)\|\leq\|1/\mathring{a}\|+C\int_{0}^{t}Q(s)\,ds,

and by these estimates for
Q
and
1/a
it follows that

\|1/a(t)\|,Q(t)\leq C^{\ast},\ t\in[0,T[,

after suitably increasing
C^{\ast}
. But now (
3.2
) together
with (
3.4
) directly yield a Gronwall inequality for
\beta
:

|\partial_{t}\beta(t,r)|\leq C^{\ast}|\beta(t,r)|+C+C\|\beta(t)\|,

where
C
is given in terms of
C^{\ast}
. Hence
increasing
C^{\ast}
again, also the bound

\|\beta(t)\|\leq C^{\ast},\ t\in[0,T[

holds.

In order to extend the solution we also have to bound various derivatives.
First we notice that we can repeat the
arguments from Section
4.6
,
which lead to the convergence of
\partial_{r}a_{n}
,
on the level of our local solution. For the latter,
we do not get any of the “error”
terms containing differences of two consecutive iterates,
and the remaining terms
which arise are bounded by the continuation assumption and
the bound which we already
derived. Thus
\partial_{r}a
remains bounded on
[0,T[
.

Next we repeat the “Magic Lemma” arguments from Section
4.7
,
but again on the level
of the local solution. This means that in the equations corresponding to
(
4.50
) and (
4.51
) we drop the subscripts,
and in the analysis of the
corresponding coefficients we can drop all the “error” terms which contain
a difference of two consecutive iterates.
But these terms were indeed the only ones
which also contained derivatives which we have not yet bounded,
so that the actual
coefficients are bounded on
[0,T[
. This implies that
\|\partial_{z}f(t)\|

and hence also
\|\partial_{r}^{2}\beta(t)\|
remain bounded.

Now we pick some
t_{0}\in]0,T[
close to
T
and consider the new initial value
problem, where we prescribe
f(t_{0})
,
a(t_{0})
,
\beta(t_{0})
as data at
t=t_{0}
;
these data are admissible in the sense of our local existence result,
which gives us a solution on some interval
[t_{0},t_{0}+\delta]
.
If we now examine which parameters of the data determined the length
\delta

we find that all these parameters are uniformly bounded on the interval
[0,T[
.
Thus we may pick
\delta>0
independently of
t_{0}
, and if the latter is chosen
close enough to
T
, we have extended the solution beyond
T
,
in contradiction to
[0,T[
being the maximal existence interval.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
is complete.

4.9
Regularity of the metric

On general principles and in particular in view of the discussion
of (
3.12
)
in Proposition
3.4
and the remark after its proof it
is desirable that

a,\beta\in C^{2}([0,T[\times[0,\infty[)
. For
\beta
this follows from
Definition
3.2
(e) and the fact that by (
3.2
) also

\partial^{2}_{t}\beta
exists and is continuous. We need to check the
regularity of
a
.

Corollary 4.2
.

In addition to the general assumptions on the data

(\mathring{f},\mathring{a},\mathring{\beta})
specified
in Section
3.2
,
let
\mathring{a}\in C^{2}([0,\infty[)
.
Then
a\in C^{2}([0,T[\times[0,\infty[)
.

Proof.

The argument basically follows that of Section
4.6
,
but now on the level
of a solution. We represent
\partial_{r}a
by differentiating
(
4.56
) and must show that

\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{r}\left[(rj)(s,R(s,t,r))\right]\,ds

is
C^{1}
. Using the relation

\partial_{r}(rj)=r\int v\cdot\partial_{x}fdv-j,

the Vlasov equation and integration by parts on the term
containing
\partial_{v}f

the crucial term concerning regularity turns out to be

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(ra\int\langle v\rangle\left(\partial_{t}f-\beta\frac{x}{r}\cdot\partial_{x}f\right)\,dv\right)(s,R(s,t,r))\partial_{r}R(s,t,r)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{t}R(s,t,r)a(s,R(s,t,r))\frac{d}{ds}\rho(s,R(s,t,r))\,\partial_{r}R(s,t,r)\,ds.

We integrate by parts and have to check that the resulting terms are
C^{1}
.
First we note that

\frac{d}{ds}a(s,R(s,t,r))=\left(\partial_{t}a-\beta\partial_{r}a\right)(s,R(s,t,r))=-4\pi\left(ra^{2}j\right)(s,R(s,t,r))

is indeed
C^{1}
, and so are all the other terms which arise,
where we recall the
regularity discussion for
\beta_{n}
in Section
4.2.6
.
Thus
\partial_{r}a
is
C^{1}
, and the remaining derivative

\partial_{tt}a
exists and is continuous due to (
3.3

5
Homogeneous solutions

5.1
The dust case

In their classical paper
[
34
]
on continued gravitational collapse
and the formation of a black hole
Oppenheimer
and
Snyder

used dust as matter model. Dust can be viewed as an ideal, compressible
fluid with pressure identically zero, or as a singular special case
of collisionless Vlasov matter which is
\delta
-distributed with respect to
the momenta. The latter viewpoint is particularly suitable in the present
context, since for any characteristic of the Vlasov equation
(
2.10
),
v(t)=0
for all
t
if
v(0)=0
. Thus

f(t,x,v)=\rho(t,x)\,\delta(v)

is a consistent ansatz for a solution of the Einstein-Vlasov system
in Painlevé-Gullstrand coordinates. It implies that

p=p_{T}=j=0

and reduces the system to the equations

\partial_{t}\rho-\beta\,\frac{x}{r}\cdot\partial_{x}\rho-\left(\partial_{r}\beta+\frac{2}{r}\beta\right)\rho=0,

1-A=1-\frac{1}{a^{2}}+\beta^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{r}\int_{0}^{r}s^{2}\rho(t,s)\,ds,

\partial_{t}a-\beta\,\partial_{r}a=0.

We restrict ourselves to the so-called marginally bound
case
a=1
, which is consistent with the
boundary conditions (
2.4
) and rewrite the equation for
\rho

in radial coordinates to obtain the equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho-\beta\,\partial_{r}\rho-\left(\partial_{r}\beta+\frac{2}{r}\beta\right)\rho=0,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}(t,r)=\frac{2m(t,r)}{r}=\frac{8\pi}{r}\int_{0}^{r}s^{2}\rho(t,s)\,ds,

(5.2)

which in turn are equivalent to a single equation for the mass
function
m
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m-\sqrt{\frac{2m}{r}}\,\partial_{r}m=0,

(5.3)

where we choose the positive root in (
5.2
).
If
\mathring{m}
denotes the initial data for the mass function, i.e.,

m(0,r)=\mathring{m}(r)=4\pi\int_{0}^{r}s^{2}\mathring{\rho}(s)\,ds,

then by standard maneuvers for first order PDEs and up to regularity
issues (
5.3
) is equivalent to the implicit equation

\displaystyle m(t,r)=\mathring{m}\left(\left(r^{3/2}+\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{2m(t,r)}t\right)^{2/3}\right).

(5.4)

For the special case when
\mathring{\rho}
is constant,

\mathring{\rho}(r)=\alpha,\ \mbox{i.e.,}\ \mathring{m}(r)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha r^{3}

with some positive amplitude
\alpha>0
we find that

\displaystyle m(t,r)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha\frac{r^{3}}{(1-\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}t)^{2}},\ r\geq 0,\ 0\leq t<\frac{1}{\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}}.

(5.5)

This implies that

\displaystyle\beta(t,r)=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha}\frac{r}{1-\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}t},\ r\geq 0,\ 0\leq t<\frac{1}{\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}}.

(5.6)

We introduce the abbreviation

\displaystyle\sigma(t)\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha}\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}t}

(5.7)

so that
\beta(t,r)=\sigma(t)r
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\tau)\,d\tau\right)=(1-\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}t)^{2/3}=:\gamma(t),\ 0\leq t<\frac{1}{\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}}.

(5.8)

Hence the trajectories of the dust particles, which are
the characteristics of the equations (
5.1
) and (
5.3
),
are given as

\displaystyle[0,1/\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}[\,\ni s\mapsto\gamma(s)r,\ r\geq 0.

(5.9)

In passing we notice that the scale function
\gamma

is the unique solution of the initial value problem

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}},\ \gamma(0)=1,\ \dot{\gamma}(0)=-\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha},

(5.10)

which will become important when we relate the homogeneous dust
solution to the corresponding solution with Vlasov matter.

The solution obtained to far is not asymptotically flat and does not
represent an isolated, collapsing matter distribution, but
we can cut it along any of the particle trajectories in (
5.9
)
and extend it by vacuum. We choose to cut along the trajectory
starting at
r=1
so that

\mathring{\rho}(r)=\alpha 1_{[0,1]}(r),\ \mbox{i.e.,}\ \mathring{m}(r)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}r^{3}&,&0\leq r\leq 1,\\
1&,&r>1,\end{array}\right.

and

\displaystyle m(t,r)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\displaystyle\frac{r^{3}}{(1-\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}t)^{2}}&,&0\leq r\leq\gamma(t),\\
1&,&r>\gamma(t),\end{array}\right.\ 0\leq t<\frac{1}{\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}},

\displaystyle\beta(t,r)=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\displaystyle\frac{r}{1-\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}t}&,&0\leq r\leq\gamma(t),\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}&,&r>\gamma(t),\end{array}\right.\ 0\leq t<\frac{1}{\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}},

and in (
5.9
) only dust particles with

0\leq r\leq 1
are present. In passing we note that
this cutting procedure works because we cut along a characteristic
curve of the relevant equations which is not crossed by other
characteristics; it is straight forward to check that the
truncated solution satisfies the Einstein-dust system in a
distributional sense. Alternatively, one can check that the
mass function (
5.1
) satisfies the implicit
relation (
5.4
) to which we have reduced
the Einstein-dust system above.

From the equation for radial null geodesics we see that a surface
given by some pair
(t,r)
is trapped if
\beta(t,r)>1
. This holds
if

0<r<\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha\ \mbox{and}\ t>\frac{1}{\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}}-\frac{2}{3}r.

The earliest (marginally) trapped surface forms at

r=\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha,\ t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6\pi\alpha}}\left(1-\left(\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha\right)^{3/2}\right),

and we shall choose
\alpha<\frac{3}{8\pi}
so that this happens to the future
of the initial hypersurface.

5.2
The Vlasov case

In order to show that Oppenheimer-Snyder type collapse also happens with
Vlasov matter we first need to establish solutions of the Einstein-Vlasov
system which are spatially homogeneous and resemble the homogeneous
dust solutions obtained in the previous section.
We demand that

j=0,\ a=1,\ \beta(t,r)=\sigma(t)r.

From the characteristic system of the Vlasov equation
(
2.10
) we get that

\dot{v}=\sigma(s)v,

and hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left(\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{s}\sigma(\tau)\,d\tau\right)|v|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{s}\sigma(\tau)\,d\tau\right)\left(-2\sigma(s)|v|^{2}+2v\cdot\dot{v}\right)=0

along characteristics. As before, let

\gamma(t)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\tau)\,d\tau\right),

and define a spatially homogeneous particle distribution

h(t,x,v)=H(\gamma^{2}(t)|v|^{2}),

where
H\in C^{1}([0,\infty[)
is non-negative and
H(\eta)=0
for
\eta

large. Then the Vlasov equation holds, and we have to determine

\sigma
respectively
\gamma
such that
the field equations hold as well.
Now

\rho(t)=4\pi\gamma(t)^{-4}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\gamma^{2}(t)+u^{2}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du

and

p(t)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\gamma(t)^{-4}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{2}}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}(t)+u^{2}}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du.

Since

A(t,r)=1-r^{2}\sigma^{2}(t)

(
2.6
) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}=4\pi(\rho+p),

(5.15)

(
2.7
) becomes

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho,

(5.16)

and (
2.8
) becomes

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}=\frac{3}{2}\sigma^{2}+4\pi p;

(5.17)

(
2.9
) is satisfied identically.
If (
5.16
) holds, then (
5.15
) and (
5.17
) become equal.
Hence we proceed as follows. We define
\sigma
as the solution of
(
5.15
) with initial condition

\sigma(0)=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho(0)};

note that
\gamma(0)=1
, so
\rho(0)
is completely determined by
H
.
We need to check that now (
5.16
) holds for all times for which

\sigma
exists. Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(\sigma^{2}-

\displaystyle\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho\Bigr)=2\sigma\dot{\sigma}-\frac{8\pi}{3}\dot{\gamma}\left[-\frac{4\rho}{\gamma}+\frac{4\pi}{\gamma^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+u^{2}}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sigma\dot{\sigma}-\frac{8\pi}{3}\dot{\gamma}\frac{4\pi}{\gamma^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+u^{2}}}-4\frac{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+u^{2}}}{\gamma}\right)H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8\pi\sigma(\rho+p)

\displaystyle{}-8\pi\sigma\frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{1}{\gamma^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\left(3\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+u^{2}}+\frac{u^{2}}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+u^{2}}}\right)H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

We can therefore use (
5.15
) as our master equation which determines

\sigma
, but we turn it into an equation for
\gamma
as follows.
Since
\dot{\gamma}=-\sigma\gamma
we find that

\ddot{\gamma}=-\dot{\sigma}\gamma-\sigma\dot{\gamma}=-4\pi(\rho+p)\gamma+\frac{(\dot{\gamma})^{2}}{\gamma};

note that
\rho
and
p
actually depend on
\gamma
. We can in
addition eliminate
\dot{\gamma}
using the relation

\dot{\gamma}=-\sigma\gamma
and (
5.16
). This results in

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{\phi(\gamma)+3\psi(\gamma)}{\gamma^{3}},

(5.18)

where

\displaystyle\phi(\xi)

\displaystyle=4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\xi^{2}+u^{2}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du,

\displaystyle\psi(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{2}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+u^{2}}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du;

these functions belong to
C^{1}([0,\infty[)
.
Eqn. (
5.18
) is supplemented with the initial conditions

\gamma(0)=1,\ \dot{\gamma}(0)=-\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho(0)}.

5.3
Relating homogeneous Vlasov solutions to homogeneous dust solutions

In what follows we need to relate homogeneous Vlasov solutions
to certain homogeneous dust solutions. To this end, let
\gamma_{D}
be
the solution to

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}=-\frac{2}{9}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}},\ \gamma(0)=1,\ \dot{\gamma}(0)=-\frac{2}{3},

(5.19)

i.e., we choose
\alpha=1/(6\pi)
in (
5.10
), and

\displaystyle\gamma_{D}(t)=(1-t)^{2/3},\ 0\leq t<1.

(5.20)

Quantities related to the corresponding homogeneous dust solution
will be denoted by
\beta_{D}
,
m_{D}
etc.
In addition, we define for
\epsilon>0
small

\alpha_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\frac{1}{6\pi}(1-\epsilon^{1/5}).

Then

\displaystyle\gamma_{D,\epsilon}(t)=(1-\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{1/5}}t)^{2/3},\ 0\leq t<\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{1/5}}}

(5.21)

is the solution to (
5.10
) with
\alpha_{\epsilon}
substituted
for
\alpha
, and notations like
\beta_{D,\epsilon}
,
m_{D,\epsilon}
etc
will again refer to the corresponding homogeneous dust solution.

For the Vlasov case we redefine the homogeneous particle distribution as

\displaystyle h(t,x,v)=h_{\epsilon}(t,x,v)=\alpha_{\epsilon}\epsilon^{-3/2}H(\epsilon^{-1}\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t)^{2}|v|^{2}),

(5.22)

for some fixed function

\displaystyle H\in C^{1}([0,\infty[),\ H\geq 0,\ \mbox{non-increasing},\ \!\int\!\!H(|v|^{2})dv=1,\ H=0\ \mbox{on}\ [1,\infty[.

(5.23)

This leads to a
homogeneous solution of the Einstein-Vlasov system, provided

\gamma_{V,\epsilon}
is the unique solution to
the initial value problem

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}(\gamma)+3\psi_{\epsilon}(\gamma)}{\gamma^{3}},\ \gamma(0)=1,\ \dot{\gamma}(0)=-\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\mathring{\rho}_{\epsilon}},

(5.24)

where

\displaystyle\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)

\displaystyle=4\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\epsilon u^{2}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du,

(5.25)

\displaystyle\psi_{\epsilon}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha_{\epsilon}\epsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{2}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\epsilon u^{2}}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du,

(5.26)

and

\mathring{\rho}_{\epsilon}=4\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{1+\epsilon u^{2}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du.

For future reference we note that the mass-energy density
induced by
h
is given as

\displaystyle\rho_{h}(t)=\frac{1}{\gamma^{4}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}\phi_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t)).

(5.27)

The following comparison result will be useful.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
0<T<1
. Then for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,

\gamma_{V,\epsilon}
exists on
[0,T]
, and for
t\in]0,T]
,

(a)

\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t)>\gamma_{D}(t)
,

(b)

\sigma_{V,\epsilon}(t)<\sigma_{D}(t)
,

(c)

|\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t)-\gamma_{D,\epsilon}(t)|+|\sigma_{V,\epsilon}(t)-\sigma_{D,\epsilon}(t)|\leq C\epsilon

where
C>0
is independent of
\epsilon
or
t
.

Proof.

By definition,
\gamma_{D}(0)=1=\gamma_{\epsilon}(0)
, and

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{(1-\epsilon^{1/5})4\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{1+\epsilon u^{2}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du}

\displaystyle>-\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{(1-\epsilon^{1/5})(1+\epsilon)}>-\frac{2}{3}=\dot{\gamma}_{D}(0),

(5.28)

provided
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small.
Hence there exists
t^{\ast}\in]0,1]
such that

\gamma_{V,\epsilon}>\gamma_{D}
on
]0,t^{\ast}[
, in particular,

\gamma_{V,\epsilon}
exists on this interval which we choose maximal.
On the interval
]0,t^{\ast}[\cap[0,T]
,

\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t)>\gamma_{D}(t)\geq\gamma_{D}(T),

and for arguments
\xi\geq\gamma_{D}(T)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)+3\psi_{\epsilon}(\xi)}{\xi^{3}}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{4\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\xi^{3}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\epsilon u^{2}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du+\epsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{2}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\epsilon u^{2}}}H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{4\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\xi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sqrt{1+\epsilon u^{2}/\xi^{2}}+\epsilon\frac{u^{2}}{\xi^{2}\sqrt{1+\epsilon u^{2}/\xi^{2}}}\right)H(u^{2})\,u^{2}du

\displaystyle\quad<\frac{\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\xi^{2}}\left(1+\frac{2\epsilon}{\xi^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\xi^{2}}\left(1+\frac{2\epsilon}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(T)}\right)=\frac{1}{6\pi}\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}(1-\epsilon^{1/5})\left(1+\frac{2\epsilon}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(T)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad<\frac{1}{6\pi}\frac{1}{\xi^{2}},

provided
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small.
Hence on
]0,t^{\ast}[\cap]0,T]
,

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}(t)=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t))+3\psi_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t))}{\gamma^{3}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}\geq-\frac{2}{9}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}>-\frac{2}{9}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}_{D}(t)}=\ddot{\gamma}_{D}(t),

(5.29)

which implies that
t^{\ast}>T
and proves part (a).

As to part (b) we recall that

\sigma_{V,\epsilon}=-\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}}{\gamma_{V,\epsilon}},\quad\sigma_{D}=-\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{D}}{\gamma_{D}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\sigma_{D}-\sigma_{V,\epsilon}=\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}\gamma_{D}-\dot{\gamma}_{D}\gamma_{V,\epsilon}}{\gamma_{D}\gamma_{V,\epsilon}}.

(5.30)

This quantity, in particular its numerator, is positive
at
t=0
, since
\gamma_{D}(0)=1=\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(0)
and

\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}(0)>\dot{\gamma}_{D}(0)
by (
5.28
).
But by (
5.29
) and since we already know that

\gamma_{V,\epsilon}>\gamma_{D}
on
]0,T]
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}\gamma_{D}-\dot{\gamma}_{D}\gamma_{V,\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\ddot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}\gamma_{D}-\ddot{\gamma}_{D}\gamma_{V,\epsilon}

\displaystyle>-\frac{2}{9}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}}\gamma_{D}+\frac{2}{9}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}_{D}}\gamma_{V,\epsilon}\geq 0

which implies that the quantity in (
5.30
) remains positive
on
]0,T]
.

Part (c) is simply a result on continuous dependence on parameters
and initial data, but since we need the order in
\epsilon
we carry
out the argument. First we note that for
t\in[0,T]
,

0<\gamma_{D}(T)\leq\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t),\gamma_{D,\epsilon}(t)\leq 1.

For arguments
\xi\in[\gamma_{D}(T),1]
it is straight forward to
see that

-\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha_{\epsilon}\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}-C\epsilon\leq-\frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}(\xi)+3\psi_{\epsilon}(\xi)}{\xi^{3}}\leq-\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha_{\epsilon}\frac{1}{\xi^{2}},

where
\epsilon>0
is small and
C>0
is independent of
\epsilon
or
\xi
.
In addition,

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{D,\epsilon}(0)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha_{\epsilon}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha_{\epsilon}4\pi\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1+\epsilon u^{2}}H(u^{2})u^{2}du}=\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}(0)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\alpha_{\epsilon}}+C\epsilon.

Hence the differential equations for
\gamma_{D,\epsilon}

and
\gamma_{V,\epsilon}
imply that

\displaystyle|\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t)-\gamma_{D,\epsilon}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(C\epsilon+\frac{4\pi}{3}\alpha_{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{s}\left|\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(\tau)}-\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}_{D,\epsilon}(\tau)}\right|\,d\tau\right)\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon+C\int_{0}^{t}|\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(s)-\gamma_{D,\epsilon}(s)|\,ds,

and Gronwall’s lemma shows that

|\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t)-\gamma_{D,\epsilon}(t)|+|\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}(t)-\dot{\gamma}_{D,\epsilon}(t)|\leq C\epsilon.

Since
\sigma=-\dot{\gamma}/\gamma
in both the Vlasov and the dust case
the remaining estimate in part (c) follows.
∎

6
Oppenheimer-Snyder type collapse with Vlasov matter

In what follows we consider solutions of the Einstein-Vlasov
system which are launched by initial data of the form

\displaystyle\mathring{f}(x,v)=\epsilon^{-3/2}H(|v|^{2}/\epsilon)\rho_{0}(x),

(6.1)

where
H
is specified in (
5.23
),

\rho_{0}\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is spherically symmetric,
non-negative, non-increasing as a function of
r=|x|
,

\rho_{0}(r)=0
for
r\geq 1+\epsilon
,

\rho_{0}(r)=\alpha_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{6\pi}(1-\epsilon^{1/5})\ \mbox{for}\ 0\leq r\leq 1,

and
\epsilon\in]0,1]
is a small parameter such that
\alpha_{\epsilon}>0
.
In addition,

\mathring{a}=1
, and
\mathring{\beta}
is given by (
3.8
).
In passing we note that
\rho_{0}
is not the mass-energy density
induced by
\mathring{f}
which as before will be denoted by
\mathring{\rho}
:
By (
5.23
),

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(r)

\displaystyle\leq\mathring{\rho}(r)=\rho_{0}(r)\epsilon^{-3/2}\int\sqrt{1+|v|^{2}}H(|v|^{2}/\epsilon)\,dv

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(r)\int\sqrt{1+\epsilon|v|^{2}}H(|v|^{2})\,dv\leq(1+\epsilon)\rho_{0}(r).

(6.2)

Notice also that dependence on
\epsilon
will not always be marked
by the corresponding subscript, but only in cases where this is necessary
in order to avoid confusion.

We want to show that data as above lead to the formation of a black hole,
more specifically, we want to prove the following theorem
which is our main result and the precise version of
the “intuitive” Theorem
1.1
, stated in the introduction.

Theorem 6.1
.

For
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small the solution to the Einstein-Vlasov system
launched by the data specified above exists on the time interval

[0,\frac{8}{9}]
.
There exist times
0<t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\frac{8}{9}
such that
there is no trapped surface for
t<t_{0}
while the surfaces given by

(t,r)
with
t\geq t_{1}
and
r=\frac{1}{3}
are trapped. For
t\geq t_{2}

all the matter is contained within the
radius
r=\frac{1}{3}
. Let
M=\int\mathring{\rho}
. Then for

t\geq t_{2}
and
r>\frac{1}{3}
,
1-A(t,r)=\frac{2M}{r}
;
a black hole with Schwarzschild radius

2M=\frac{4}{9}+\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{1/5})
forms.

We recall the definition of
A
in (
2.5
) and note that
a surface with coordinates
(t,r)

is trapped iff
A(t,r)<0
.
It should be observed that
by the time
t=\frac{7}{9}
the asymptotically flat
homogeneous dust solution has formed a
trapped surface at
r=\frac{1}{3}
and for
t
close to
\frac{8}{9}

all its mass is contained within this trapped surface. This motivates
our specific choice of various parameters, but we
emphasize
that
they could also have been kept more general.

In order to speak about a black hole it is strictly speaking
not sufficient to consider the solution only up to time
t=\frac{8}{9}
.
However, the following additional assertions hold.

Proposition 6.2
.

For
\epsilon
sufficiently small
the maximal Cauchy development of the given initial data
contains the region

O=\left\{(t,x)\mid t>t_{2},\ |x|>\frac{1}{3}\right\}

where the solution is vacuum and

a=1,\ \beta(t,r)=\sqrt{\frac{2M}{r}}.

The line
\{t\geq t_{2},r=2M\}
is part of the radially outgoing
null geodesic which passes through the point
(t_{2},2M)
. This
null geodesic is future complete and generates the event horizon
of a black hole of mass
M
.

The proof of the theorem (and the proposition) is a fairly
involved bootstrap argument which aims to compare
the solution to the homogeneous ones introduced in Section
5

and which we split in a sequence of lemmas. Before we start on this,
another remark seems in order to put these results into perspective.

Remark 6.3
.

In principle, Theorem
6.1
can be viewed as a result on continuous
dependence on initial data. However, in practice there exists no
mathematical framework within which both the Einstein-Vlasov and the
Einstein-dust system are well posed; the latter system must be viewed
as a singular limit of the former system. But once a solution of
the former system with a trapped surface is obtained, then continuous
dependence on the Vlasov data does hold and further small perturbations
of these data, which could for example make them slightly inhomogeneous on
the interval
[0,1]
, would launch a solution to the Einstein-Vlasov
system which still forms a trapped surface.

6.1
Getting the bootstrap argument started

We consider the following bootstrap assumptions on the solution to the
Einstein-Vlasov system launched by the data specified above. The solution
is to exist on some time interval
[0,T^{\ast}[\subset[0,\frac{8}{9}]

and for
0\leq t<T^{\ast}
and
r\geq 0
,

\begin{array}[]{rc}\mathrm{(i)}&\displaystyle\partial_{r}\beta(t,r)<\partial_{r}\beta_{D}(t),\\
\mathrm{(ii)}&\displaystyle\beta(t,r)>-1,\\
\mathrm{(iii)}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}<a(t,r)<2,\\
\mathrm{(iv)}&\ \mbox{for all characteristics of the Vlasov equation
starting in}\ \mathrm{supp}\,\mathring{f},\\
&\displaystyle(1-t)^{2/3}|v(t)|\leq 2\,|v(0)|;\end{array}

note that
\partial_{r}\beta_{D}
depends only on
t
.
The key assumption is (i) which is quite non-trivial to recover in an
improved form. The assumptions (ii) and (iii) are needed to get the
bootstrap argument started, but they are easy to recover in an
improved form. The latter is also true for (iv) which will be used to
keep matter terms like
j
and
p
, which vanish for dust, small.
A key step in recovering (i) will be a more sophisticated estimate
for the momentum support, which uses polar coordinates and yields
a refined estimate for
\rho
, cf. Lemma
6.15
and
(
6.25
) below.

To begin with, we have to show that the bootstrap assumptions hold at least
on some small time interval.

Lemma 6.4
.

For
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small the solution
of the Einstein-Vlasov system which according to Theorem
3.3

is launched by the data specified at the beginning of this section,
cf. (
6.1
), exists on some time interval

[0,T^{\ast}[\subset[0,\frac{8}{9}]
and satisfies the bootstrap
assumptions (i)–(iv) there. We choose
T^{\ast}=T^{\ast}_{\epsilon}>0

maximal with this property.

Proof.

By assumption,
a(0)=\mathring{a}=1
which satisfies (iii).
Next we recall that by (
3.8
),

\mathring{\beta}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{2\mathring{m}(r)}{r}},

where

\mathring{m}(r)=4\pi\int_{0}^{r}\mathring{\rho}(s)\,s^{2}ds\geq 4\pi\mathring{\rho}(r)\int_{0}^{r}s^{2}ds=\frac{4\pi}{3}r^{3}\mathring{\rho}(r);

note that
\mathring{\rho}
inherits the monotonicity in
r
from

\rho_{0}
. Thus

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathring{\beta}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathring{m}(r)/r}}\left(4\pi r\mathring{\rho}(r)-\frac{\mathring{m}(r)}{r^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathring{m}(r)/r}}\frac{8\pi}{3}r\mathring{\rho}(r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{8\pi}{3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{3}\mathring{\rho}(r)\,r^{2}}}r\mathring{\rho}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}\mathring{\rho}(r)}.

By (
6.2
),

\mathring{\rho}(r)\leq\frac{1}{6\pi}(1-\epsilon^{1/5})(1+\epsilon)
, and hence
for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\mathring{\beta}(r)\leq\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{(1+\epsilon)(1-\epsilon^{1/5})}\leq\frac{2}{3}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{1/5}\right)=\partial_{r}\mathring{\beta}_{D}(r)-\frac{1}{3}\epsilon^{1/5}.

(6.3)

Since obviously
\mathring{\beta}(r)\geq 0
we see that (i)–(iii) hold
initially and hence on some time interval
[0,\delta]
, provided

\delta>0
is small enough. As to (iv) we note the identity

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}|v|^{2}=8\pi r\,a\,j\,\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}+2\left(\partial_{r}\beta-\frac{\beta}{r}\right)\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}+2\frac{\beta}{r}|v|^{2}

(6.4)

which follows from the
v
-component of the characteristic system of the
Vlasov equation (
3.1
). The identity implies that on
[0,\delta]
,

\frac{d}{ds}|v|^{2}\leq C_{\epsilon}|v|^{2},

where
C_{\epsilon}>0
depends on the solution of the Einstein-Vlasov
system and hence on
\epsilon
, but not on
s\in[0,\delta]
. By making

\delta
smaller if necessary, (iv) now holds as well.
∎

6.2
Simple consequences of the bootstrap assumptions

In what follows we always consider the solution of the Einstein-Vlasov
system obtained in Lemma

which satisfies the assumptions
(i)–(iv) on
[0,T^{\ast}[
. We derive some consequences which in particular
will show that we recover (ii)–(iv) in an improved form.
Constants denoted by

may depend on
\gamma_{D}

but not on

We start by establishing some bounds on the source terms and on

Lemma 6.5
.

For all
0\leq t<T^{\ast}
,

(a)

\|j(t)\|,\|p(t)\|,\|p_{T}(t)\|\leq C\epsilon^{1/2}
,

\|\rho(t)\|\leq C

(b)

-1<\beta(t,r)\leq\beta_{D}(t,r)\leq Cr
for

Proof.

The form of the initial data in (

implies that
|v|\leq\epsilon^{1/2}

for
(x,v)\in\mathrm{supp}\,\mathring{f}
, the bootstrap assumption (iv)
implies a corresponding estimate for
(x,v)\in\mathrm{supp}\,f(t)
, and
this implies part (a).
The boundary condition for
\beta
together with the bootstrap
assumptions (i), (ii) imply (b).
∎

Next we estimate characteristics of the Vlasov equation which
run in the support of
f
.

Lemma 6.6
.

For any characteristic
t\mapsto(x(t),v(t))
of the Vlasov equation
which starts in the support of

\mathring{f}
and
0\leq t<T^{\ast}
,

|x(t)|\leq C\ \mbox{and}\ (1-t)^{2/3}|v(t)|\leq(1+C\epsilon^{1/4})|v(0)|.

Proof.

The bootstrap assumption (iii) and Lemma
6.5
(b) imply that

|\dot{r}(s)|\leq\frac{|x\cdot v/r|(s)}{a\langle v\rangle}+|\beta(s,r(s))|\leq 3+C|r(s)|

which implies the bound on
|x(t)|
.
If we apply this bound, the bootstrap assumption (i),
and Lemma
6.5
(a)
to (

) it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}|v|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 16\pi\|rj(s)\|\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}+2\partial_{r}\beta\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}+2\frac{\beta}{r}\left[|v|^{2}-\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon^{1/2}\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}+2\partial_{r}\beta_{D}(s)\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}+2\partial_{r}\beta_{D}(s)\left[|v|^{2}-\left(\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\right)^{2}\right]

which implies that

|v(t)|\leq\frac{\exp(C\epsilon^{1/2})}{(1-t)^{2/3}}|v(0)|\leq\frac{1+C\epsilon^{1/2}}{(1-t)^{2/3}}|v(0)|,

for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma
6.6
shows that we get
the bootstrap assumption (iv) back in an improved form
if we choose

.
Next we examine the metric quantities

and
\beta
and in particular
show that we can get back the bootstrap assumption (iii) in an improved form.

Lemma 6.7
.

It holds that

\displaystyle a(t,r)

\displaystyle=1+\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{1/2}),

\displaystyle\beta^{2}(t,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2m(t,r)}{r}+\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{1/2}),

where

\displaystyle m(t,r)=4\pi\int_{0}^{r}\rho(t,s)\,s^{2}ds.

Proof.

We recall (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a(t,r)}=1+4\pi\int_{0}^{t}(rj)(s,R(s,t,r))\,ds,

(6.6)

where

is the characteristic defined via (

If we combine (

) and (

),

\displaystyle\beta^{2}(t,r)=\frac{2m(t,r)}{r}+\frac{1}{a^{2}(t,r)}-1-\frac{8\pi}{r}\int_{0}^{r}(a\beta j)(t,s)\,s^{2}ds.

(6.7)

The assertion for

now follows by combining (

) with the
estimates for

and

from the previous lemmas. Using the same

gives the assertion for

.
∎

Lemma
6.7
shows that we get the bootstrap assumption (iii)
back in an improved form.

6.3
The homogeneous core

For what follows we need to show that in a certain core region
and for
\epsilon
sufficiently small
the solution
f
of the Einstein-Vlasov system coincides with
the homogeneous solution
h=h_{\epsilon}
introduced in
Section
5.3
, cf. (
5.22
). To this end, let

r^{\ast}\colon[0,1[\to]0,\infty[
denote the maximal solution to
the initial value problem

\dot{r}=-\epsilon^{1/4}-\beta_{D}(s,r),\ r(0)=1.

This function can be computed to be

\displaystyle r^{\ast}(t)=(1-3\epsilon^{1/4})(1-t)^{2/3}+3\epsilon^{1/4}(1-t),\ t\in[0,1[;

(6.8)

we choose
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that

1-3\epsilon^{1/4}>\frac{1}{2}
.
Obviously,

\displaystyle(1-3\epsilon^{1/4})\gamma_{D}(t)\leq r^{\ast}(t)\leq\gamma_{D}(t),\ t\in[0,1[,

which is interesting in view of the fact that

defines the

\displaystyle r^{\ast}(t)\geq\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{D}(8/9)=:r_{\ast}>0\ \mbox{for}\ t\in[0,8/9].

(6.10)

Lemma 6.8
.

Let

I\coloneqq\{(t,x)\in[0,T^{\ast}[\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid 0\leq|x|\leq r^{\ast}(t)\}.

Then for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,

f=h
on
I\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
with corresponding identities
for the metric coefficients; note that both solutions exist on

[0,T^{\ast}[
.

Proof.

Let

s\mapsto(x(s),v(s))
denote a characteristic of
f

such that

|x(t)|=r^{\ast}(t)\ \mbox{and}\ |x(s)|<r^{\ast}(s)\ \mbox{for}\ s>t,

so this characteristic is running in the
interior of the region
I
and hits its outer boundary
at time
t
when followed backwards in time.
We claim that both
f(t,x(t),v(t))=0
and

h(t,x(t),v(t))=0
, provided
\epsilon>0
is sufficiently small.
To see the assertion for
f
, we note that

\frac{x(t)\cdot v(t)}{r(t)\,a(t,r(t))\,\langle v(t)\rangle}-\beta(t,r(t))=\dot{r}(t)\leq\dot{r}^{\ast}(t)=-\epsilon^{1/4}-\beta_{D}(t,r^{\ast}(t)).

Since
r(t)=r^{\ast}(t)
and
\beta\leq\beta_{D}
by
Lemma
6.5
(b), this implies that

\displaystyle\epsilon^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{1/4}+\beta_{D}(t,r^{\ast}(t))-\beta(t,r^{\ast}(t))

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{x(t)\cdot v(t)}{r^{\ast}(t)\,a(t,r^{\ast}(t))\,\langle v(t)\rangle}\leq 2|v(t)|\leq\frac{4}{(1-8/9)^{2/3}}|v(0)|,

where we used the bootstrap assumptions (iii) and (iv). Thus

|v(0)|\geq\frac{1}{20}\epsilon^{1/4}>\epsilon^{1/2}

for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small. Together with the fact that

is constant along characteristics and (

f(t,x(t),v(t))=\mathring{f}(x(0),v(0))=0
.
But by Lemma

(b),

\beta_{h}(t,r)=\sigma_{V,\epsilon}(t)r<\sigma_{D}(t)r=\beta_{D}(t,r)

for

. Hence for a characteristic of the homogeneous solution

the above estimate for

turns into

|v(t)|\geq\epsilon^{1/4}>(1-8/9)^{-2/3}\epsilon^{1/2}\geq\gamma_{D}(t)^{-1}\epsilon^{1/2}\geq\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t)^{-1}\epsilon^{1/2},

where we used Lemma

(a).
Hence

, cf. (
5.22
).

In addition, any solution to

\displaystyle\dot{r}=-\beta(s,r)\ \mbox{or}\ \dot{r}=-\beta_{h}(s,r)

which at some time

for all

) or (

The above behavior of the various characteristics with respect to

Metric quantities or macroscopic densities corresponding to the

,

, and

\partial_{x}h=0

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(f-h)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{v}{a\langle v\rangle}-\beta\frac{x}{r}\right)\cdot\partial_{x}(f-h)

\displaystyle{}+\left[4\pi r\,a\,j\,\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\left(\partial_{r}\beta-\frac{\beta}{r}\right)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\frac{\beta}{r}v\right]\cdot\partial_{v}(f-h)

\displaystyle=D\cdot\partial_{v}h,

(6.12)

where

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{r}\beta_{h}-\frac{\beta_{h}}{r}\right)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}+\frac{\beta_{h}}{r}v

\displaystyle\quad{}-4\pi r\,a\,j\,\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}-\left(\partial_{r}\beta-\frac{\beta}{r}\right)\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{x}{r}-\frac{\beta}{r}v.

The characteristic system for (

\displaystyle(f-h)(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=(f-h)(t^{\ast},X(t^{\ast},t,x,v),V(t^{\ast},t,x,v))

\displaystyle\quad{}+\int_{t^{\ast}}^{t}(D\cdot\partial_{v}h)(s,X(s,t,x,v),V(s,t,x,v))\,ds

The difference

Based on (

\displaystyle\|f(t)-h(t)\|_{t}\leq

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|\partial_{r}\beta(s)-\partial_{r}\beta_{h}(s)\|_{s}+\|a(s)-a_{h}(s)\|_{s}+\|f(s)-h(s)\|_{s}\right)\,ds;

6.4
A formula for controlling

In order to continue the argument and in particular in order to

Lemma 6.10

For
t\in[0,T^{\ast}[

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\partial_{r}\beta(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle=(\partial_{r}\beta)^{2}(t,R(t,0,r))-\frac{d}{dt}(4\pi raj)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\quad{}+\left(4\pi(\rho-p+2p_{T})+(4\pi raj)^{2}\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\quad{}-\left(\frac{8\pi}{r^{3}}\int_{0}^{r}(\rho-a\beta j)(t,s)\,s^{2}ds\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\quad{}+\left(8\pi raj\partial_{r}\beta\right)(t,R(t,0,r)).

Proof.

The field equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
)
together with (
3.4
) imply that

\partial_{t}\beta-\beta\,\partial_{r}\beta=\frac{4\pi}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\left(\rho-a\beta j\right)(t,s)\,s^{2}ds+4\pi rp,

and

\partial_{t}a-\beta\,\partial_{r}a=-4\pi ra^{2}j.

If we now observe that
\frac{d}{dt}R=-\beta(t,R)
it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\partial_{r}\beta(t,R(t,0,r))=\left(\partial_{t}\partial_{r}\beta-\beta\partial_{r}^{2}\beta\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(\partial_{r}\left(\partial_{t}\beta-\beta\partial_{r}\beta\right)+(\partial_{r}\beta)^{2}\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\qquad=(\partial_{r}\beta)^{2}(t,R(t,0,r))-\left(\frac{8\pi}{r^{3}}\int_{0}^{r}(\rho-a\beta j)(t,s)\,s^{2}ds\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad{}+4\pi\left(\rho-a\beta j+p+r\partial_{r}p\right)(t,R(t,0,r)),

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(4\pi raj\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle=\left(-4\pi\beta aj-(4\pi raj)^{2}+4\pi ra(\partial_{t}j-\beta\partial_{r}j)\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle=\left(-4\pi\beta aj-(4\pi raj)^{2}\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\ +\left(4\pi ra\left(\frac{2\beta}{r}j-\frac{1}{a}(\partial_{r}p+\frac{2p-2p_{T}}{r})+2(\partial_{r}\beta+4\pi raj)j\right)\right)(t,R(t,0,r));

the last equality is obtained by inserting the second identity from
Lemma
3.5
with
a
,
\beta
, and
j

instead of
\tilde{a}
,
\tilde{b}
,
and
\tilde{\jmath}
. When we combine the previous
two identities several terms cancel
and the assertion follows.
∎

We use this result to prove that
\partial_{r}\beta
remains bounded;
notice that this is necessary in view of the continuation criterion
in our local existence result, Theorem
3.3
,
and that the bootstrap assumption (i) only provides a bound from above.

Lemma 6.11
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,

\|\partial_{r}\beta(t)\|\leq C
for
t\in[0,T^{\ast}[
.

Proof.

Lemma
6.10
and the bounds established in
Lemma
6.5
(a)
imply that

\frac{d}{dt}\partial_{r}\beta(t,R(t,0,r))\geq-\frac{d}{dt}(4\pi raj)(t,R(t,0,r))-C-|\partial_{r}\beta(t,R(t,0,r))|;

notice that the term which is quadratic in
\partial_{r}\beta

has the right sign.
Integrating this estimate implies that

-\partial_{r}\beta(t,R(t,0,r))\leq C+\int_{0}^{t}|\partial_{r}\beta(s,R(s,0,r))|\,ds

which together with the bootstrap assumption (i) and the boundedness of

\partial_{r}\beta_{D}
on
[0,T^{\ast}[
implies that

|\partial_{r}\beta(t,R(t,0,r))|\leq C+\int_{0}^{t}|\partial_{r}\beta(s,R(s,0,r))|\,ds.

By Gronwall’s lemma,

|\partial_{r}\beta(t,r)|=|\partial_{r}\beta(t,R(t,0,R(0,t,r)))|\leq C
.
∎

We can use the lemma above to establish a sharper bound on the spatial support
of the solution. This is not strictly necessary,
but it will yield the nice feature
that all the mass will be contained in the black
hole for
t
sufficiently large.

Lemma 6.12
.

There exists some constant
C>0
such that
at time
t\in[0,T^{\ast}[
the spatial
support of the solution is contained in the interval

[0,r^{\ast}(t)+C\epsilon^{1/5}]
; cf. (
6.8
) for the definition of
r^{\ast}(t)
.

Proof.

Let
s\mapsto(x(s),v(s))
denote a characteristic of
the Vlasov equation which
runs in
\mathrm{supp}\,f
, and let
r(s)=|x(s)|
. We aim to show that

r(s)\leq r^{\ast}(s)+C\epsilon^{1/5}
for all
s
.
If
r(t)\leq r^{\ast}(t)
at some
time
t
the assertion holds trivially for
s=t
.
If
r(t)>r^{\ast}(t)
at some time
t
, then we define
t^{\ast}\in[0,t[

minimal with the property that
r(s)>r^{\ast}(s)
on
]t^{\ast},t]
.
Since

\dot{r}(s)\leq C\epsilon^{1/2}-\beta(s,r(s)),

we need an estimate for
\beta(s,r)
from below for
r\geq r^{\ast}(s)
.
By Lemma
6.11
and the mean value theorem,

\beta(s,r)\geq\beta(s,r^{\ast}(s))-C(r-r^{\ast}(s))=\beta_{h}(s,r^{\ast}(s))-C(r-r^{\ast}(s))

for
r\geq r^{\ast}(s)
. Hence on
]t^{\ast},t]
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}(r-r^{\ast})(s)

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon^{1/4}-\beta_{h}(s,r^{\ast}(s))+C(r-r^{\ast})(s)+\beta_{D}(s,r^{\ast}(s))

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon^{1/5}+C(r-r^{\ast})(s);

in the last estimate we used the fact that by Lemma
5.1
,

\displaystyle|\beta_{D}(s,r^{\ast}(s))-\beta_{h}(s,r^{\ast}(s))|

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma_{D}(s)-\sigma_{V,\epsilon}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma_{D}(s)-\sigma_{D,\epsilon}(s)|+C\epsilon\leq C\epsilon^{1/5},

where we recall the explicit form of
\sigma
in the dust case,
cf. (
5.7
),
and choose
\epsilon
sufficiently small. This implies that on
]t^{\ast},t]
,

(r-r^{\ast})(s)\leq(r-r^{\ast})(t^{\ast})+C\epsilon^{1/5}+C\int_{t^{\ast}}^{s}(r-r^{\ast})(\tau)\,d\tau.

If
t^{\ast}=0
then
(r-r^{\ast})(t^{\ast})\leq\epsilon
, cf. the assumptions
on the data introduced in (
6.1
), if

t^{\ast}>0
then
(r-r^{\ast})(t^{\ast})=0
, and in both cases Gronwall’s lemma
completes the proof.
∎

Eventually we need to recover the bootstrap assumption (i) in an improved form.
To this end we now use Lemma
6.10
to analyze the difference

\partial_{r}\beta-\partial_{r}\beta_{D}
.

Lemma 6.13
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
\epsilon
sufficiently small,

t\in[0,T^{\ast}[
, and
r\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\beta(t,R(t,0,r)))\leq\partial_{r}\beta_{D}(t,R(t,0,r)))-C\epsilon^{1/5}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}(\partial_{r}\beta+\partial_{r}\beta_{D})(\tau,R(\tau,0,r))\,d\tau\right)\,d(s,R(s,0,r))\,ds,

where

d(s,r)=\left(\frac{2m_{D}}{r^{3}}-\frac{2m}{r^{3}}-4\pi\rho_{D}+4\pi\rho\right)(s,r).

Proof.

We derive a formula analogous to Lemma
6.10

for
\partial_{r}\beta_{D}
.
The derivation is much simpler, since in the dust case,
j=p=p_{T}=0
.
It is important to note that we again use the characteristic
R(t,0,r)

belonging
to
\beta
instead of the corresponding dust characteristic. But since
in addition
\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{D}=0
this causes no problem:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\partial_{r}\beta_{D}(t,R(t,0,r))=\left(\partial_{t}\partial_{r}\beta_{D}-\beta\partial_{r}^{2}\beta_{D}\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(\partial_{r}\left(\partial_{t}\beta_{D}-\beta_{D}\partial_{r}\beta_{D}\right)+(\partial_{r}\beta_{D})^{2}\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\qquad=(\partial_{r}\beta_{D})^{2}(t,R(t,0,r))-\left(\frac{8\pi}{r^{3}}\int_{0}^{r}\rho_{D}(t,s)\,s^{2}ds-4\pi\rho_{D}\right)(t,R(t,0,r)).

If we combine this with the formula from Lemma
6.10

it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\partial_{r}\beta-\partial_{r}\beta_{D}+4\pi raj)(t,R(t,0,r))=d(t,R(t,0,r))+e(t)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+(\partial_{r}\beta+\partial_{r}\beta_{D})(\partial_{r}\beta-\partial_{r}\beta_{D}+4\pi raj)(t,R(t,0,r)),

(6.16)

where

\displaystyle e(t)

\displaystyle=\left(4\pi(-p+2p_{T})+(4\pi raj)^{2}+4\pi raj(\partial_{r}\beta-\partial_{r}\beta_{D})\right)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle\quad{}+\left(\frac{8\pi}{r^{3}}\int_{0}^{r}(a\beta j)(t,s)\,s^{2}ds\right)(t,R(t,0,r)),

in particular,
|e(t)|\leq C\epsilon^{1/2}
,
cf. Lemma
6.5
(a)
and Lemma
6.11
.
We solve (
6.16
) using variation of
constants to find that

\displaystyle(\partial_{r}\beta-\partial_{r}\beta_{D}+4\pi raj)(t,R(t,0,r))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{r}\beta+\partial_{r}\beta_{D})(s,R(s,0,r))\,ds\right)(\partial_{r}\mathring{\beta}-\partial_{r}\mathring{\beta}_{D})(r)

\displaystyle\quad{}+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}(\partial_{r}\beta+\partial_{r}\beta_{D})(\tau,R(\tau,0,r))\,d\tau\right)\left(d(s,R(s,0,r))+e(s)\right)\,ds;

note that
j
vanishes initially.
By Lemma
6.11
the argument of the
exponential function is bounded,

e(s)
and the term with
j
on the left hand side are bounded by

C\epsilon^{1/2}
, but the initial data term is smaller that

-\frac{1}{3}\epsilon^{1/5}
,
cf. (
6.3
).
Choosing
\epsilon
small enough completes the proof.
∎

Notice that by replacing
r
by
R(0,t,\tilde{r})
so that

R(t,0,r)=\tilde{r}
and

R(\tau,0,r)=R(\tau,t,\tilde{r})
we obtain, after dropping the
tildes, an analogous formula
with
r
instead of
R(t,0,r)
on the left hand side and the
corresponding substitution
on the right hand side.
We will see below that the term
d
in Lemma
6.13
is negative
so that the bootstrap assumption can be recovered in improved form, but in order
to analyze
d
we need information on the evolution of
\rho
and
m
.

6.5
The evolution of
\rho
and
m

It turns out that in order to obtain the required information on
\rho
and
m

we must start with the latter quantity.

Lemma 6.14
.

For
t\in[0,T^{\ast}[
and
r\geq 0
,

m(t,R(t,0,r))=\mathring{m}(r)+\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{1/2}).

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.5
, integration by parts, Lemma
6.5
,
and the fact that by Lemma
6.11

\partial_{r}\beta
and
\beta/r

are bounded we find that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\partial_{t}-\beta\partial_{r}\right)m=-r^{2}\beta\rho

\displaystyle\quad{}+\int_{0}^{r}\left[\beta\partial_{r}\rho-\frac{1}{a}\partial_{r}j-\frac{2}{sa}j+\frac{2\beta}{s}(\rho+p_{T})+(\partial_{r}\beta+4\pi saj)(\rho+p)\right]\,s^{2}ds

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\partial_{r}a}{a^{2}}j\,s^{2}ds+\int_{0}^{r}\left[\frac{2\beta}{s}p_{T}+\partial_{r}\beta\,p+4\pi saj(\rho+p)\right]\,s^{2}ds-\frac{r^{2}}{a}j

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\partial_{r}a}{a^{2}}j\,s^{2}ds+\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{1/2}).

(6.17)

The latter integral is
\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{1/2})
as well, provided

\partial_{r}a/a^{2}=\mathrm{O}(1)
.
Differentiating (
6.6
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{r}a}{a^{2}}=-4\pi\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{r}(rj)(s,R(s,t,r))\partial_{r}R(s,t,r)\,ds.

(6.18)

The argument which follows is similar to the one in
Section
4.2.6
,
following (
4.9
). By
[
37
, Lemma 6.6]
,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}(rj)

\displaystyle=j+r\int v\cdot\partial_{x}fdv-r\int\left[\frac{|v|^{2}}{r}\frac{x}{r}-\frac{x\cdot v}{r}\frac{v}{r}\right]\cdot\partial_{v}fdv

\displaystyle=r\int v\cdot\partial_{x}fdv-j.

(6.19)

The Cartesian version
X(s,t,x)
of the
\beta
-characteristic
R(s,t,r)

is the solution to

\displaystyle\dot{x}=-\beta(s,r)\frac{x}{r}

(6.20)

with
X(t,t,x)=x
;
the right hand side of this differential equation
is in
C^{1}([0,T^{\ast}[\times\mathbb{R}^{3})
and vanishes at the center
x=0
. In
particular, the center is a characteristic which no other characteristic can cross.
Clearly,
|X(s,t,x)|=R(s,t,r)
where
r=|x|
.
Moreover,

\frac{X(s,t,x)}{|X(s,t,x)|}=\frac{x}{r}

for
x\neq 0
;
\beta
-characteristics are strictly radial.
Let

D_{t}f(t,x,v)\coloneqq\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)-\beta(t,r)\frac{x}{r}\cdot\partial_{x}f(t,x,v).

In what follows we often abbreviate

X(s)=X(s,t,x)
and
R(s)=R(s,t,r)
.
The chain rule and the Vlasov equation (
3.1
) imply that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}f(s,X(s),v)

\displaystyle=(D_{t}f)(s,X(s),v)=-\left(\frac{v}{a\langle v\rangle}\cdot\partial_{x}f\right)(s,X(s),v)

\displaystyle\quad{}+\left(F_{2}\cdot\partial_{v}f\right)(s,X(s),v),

where we recall the definition of
F_{2}
in (
4.2
). Hence

\left(v\cdot\partial_{x}f\right)(s,X(s),v)=-\left(a\langle v\rangle D_{t}f\right)(s,X(s),v)+\left(a\langle v\rangle F_{2}\cdot\partial_{v}f\right)(s,X(s),v).

Integration with respect to
v
and integration by parts imply that

\displaystyle\left(\int v\cdot\partial_{x}fdv\right)(s,R(s))=-a(s,R(s))\frac{d}{ds}\rho(s,R(s))

\displaystyle{}-\left[4\pi\,a^{2}\,j\,(p+\rho)+a\partial_{r}\beta(p+\rho)+a\frac{\beta}{r}(2\rho-p)+a\frac{\beta}{r}\int\frac{|v|^{2}}{\langle v\rangle}fdv\right](s,R(s))

\displaystyle=-a(s,R(s))\frac{d}{ds}\rho(s,R(s))+\mathrm{O}(1).

In view of (
6.18
) and (
6.19
) we need to show that the following
integrals are
\mathrm{O}(1)
:

\int_{0}^{t}j(s,R(s))\,\partial_{r}R(s)\,ds,

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}R(s)a(s,R(s))\frac{d}{ds}\rho(s,R(s))\,\partial_{r}R(s)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=ra(t,r)\rho(t,r)-R(0,t,r)\mathring{\rho}(R(0,t,r))\,\partial_{r}R(0,t,r)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\quad{}-\int_{0}^{t}\biggl[\dot{R}(s)a(s,R(s))\rho(s,R(s))\,\partial_{r}R(s)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+R(s)\frac{d}{ds}a(s,R(s))\rho(s,R(s))\,\partial_{r}R(s)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+R(s)a(s,R(s))\rho(s,R(s))\,\partial_{r}\dot{R}(s)\biggr]\,ds.

By (
2.9
) and the analogue of (
3.10
),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}a(s,R(s))

\displaystyle=-4\pi(ra^{2}j)(s,R(s)),

\displaystyle\partial_{r}R(s,t,r)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\partial_{r}\beta(\tau,R(\tau,t,r))\,d\tau\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\dot{R}(s,t,r)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{r}\beta(s,R(s,t,r))\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\partial_{r}\beta(\tau,R(\tau,t,r))\,d\tau\right),

all these terms are
\mathrm{O}(1)
,
and hence the same is true for
\partial_{r}a/a^{2}
.
Thus (
6.17
) turns into the equation

\partial_{t}m-\beta\,\partial_{r}m=\mathrm{O}(\epsilon^{1/2}),

which upon integration along characteristics gives the assertion.
∎

In order to obtain sufficiently sharp information on
\rho
it turns out that
we need to investigate the characteristic system in coordinates which are
adapted to spherical symmetry. For
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
x\neq 0
we define

r=|x|,\ w=\frac{x\cdot v}{r},\ L=|x\times v|^{2}.

In these coordinates the characteristic system takes the form

\displaystyle\dot{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{w}{a(s,r)\sqrt{1+w^{2}+L/r^{2}}}-\beta(s,r),

(6.21)

\displaystyle\dot{w}

\displaystyle=4\pi(raj)(s,r)\,w+\partial_{r}\beta(s,r)\,w+\frac{L}{r^{3}a(s,r)\sqrt{1+w^{2}+L/r^{2}}},

(6.22)

\displaystyle\dot{L}

\displaystyle=0.

(6.23)

The variable
w
is a radial momentum variable
while
L
is the modulus of the particle angular momentum squared
and is conserved due to spherical symmetry. In addition,

|v|^{2}=w^{2}+\frac{L}{r^{2}}.

The source terms can also be rewritten in these variables, in particular,

\rho(t,r)=\frac{\pi}{r^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{1+w^{2}+\frac{L}{r^{2}}}f(t,r,w,L)\,dL\,dw.

We obtain the following refined estimate.

Lemma 6.15
.

For
\epsilon
sufficiently small,
t\in[0,T^{\ast}[
and
r\geq 0
,

\rho(t,r)\leq\frac{\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma_{D}(t)\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}+C\epsilon^{1/2}.

Proof.

On the homogeneous core established in Lemma
6.8
,

\displaystyle\rho(t,r)=\rho_{h}(t)=\frac{1}{\gamma^{4}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}\phi_{\epsilon}(\gamma_{V,\epsilon}(t))\leq\frac{\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{3}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}+C\epsilon,

(6.24)

where we used (
5.27
) and an obvious estimate for the function

\phi_{\epsilon}
defined in (
5.25
).
By Lemma
5.1
(a) the assertion follows on the homogeneous core.

Hence it remains to estimate
\rho(t,r)
for
r\geq r^{\ast}(t)\geq r_{\ast}
,
cf. (
6.10
). Consider a characteristic
s\mapsto(x(s),v(s))
of
f

in
\mathrm{supp}\,f
with
r(t)\geq r_{\ast}
. Since
\beta
is positive if follows
from (
6.21
) and the bootstrap assumption (iv) that

\dot{r}\leq C\epsilon^{1/2}
and hence
r(s)\geq\frac{1}{2}r_{\ast}
on

[0,t]
for
\epsilon
sufficiently small. We will prove that on
[0,t]
,

\displaystyle\gamma_{D}^{2}(s)w^{2}(s)+\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(s)\frac{L}{r^{2}(s)}\leq|v(0)|^{2}+\epsilon^{3/2}.

(6.25)

Let us for the moment assume that (
6.25
) is already established.
Then the estimate for
\rho(t,r)
for the remaining case
r\geq r^{\ast}(t)

works as follows.

Since
f
is constant along characteristics and

\rho_{0}\leq\alpha_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle f(t,r,w,L)

\displaystyle=\mathring{f}(R(0,t,r,w,L),W(0,t,r,w,L),L)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{-3/2}\alpha_{\epsilon}H\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(W^{2}(0,t,r,w,L)+\frac{L}{R^{2}(0,t,r,w,L)}\right)\right).

By (
6.25
),

W^{2}(0,t,r,w,L)+\frac{L}{R^{2}(0,t,r,w,L)}\geq\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)w^{2}+\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\frac{L}{r^{2}}-\epsilon^{3/2},

and since
H
is decreasing by assumption,

f(t,r,w,L)\leq\epsilon^{-3/2}\alpha_{\epsilon}H\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)w^{2}+\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\frac{L}{r^{2}}-\epsilon^{3/2}\right)_{+}\right),

where
(\cdot)_{+}
denotes the positive part of the argument. Hence

\displaystyle\rho(t,r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{r^{2}}\epsilon^{-3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{1+w^{2}+\frac{L}{r^{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad H\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)w^{2}+\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\frac{L}{r^{2}}-\epsilon^{3/2}\right)_{+}\right)\,dL\,dw

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}\epsilon^{-3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{1+w^{2}+\frac{L}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad H\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)w^{2}+L-\epsilon^{3/2}\right)_{+}\right)\,dL\,dw

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\gamma_{D}(t)}\epsilon^{-3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\eta}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)}(\eta-L)+\frac{L}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}}\frac{dL}{\sqrt{\eta-L}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad H\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\eta-\epsilon^{3/2}\right)_{+}\right)\,d\eta;

in the last step we changed variables via

(\eta,L)=(\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)w^{2}+L,L)
.
By Lemma
5.1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\eta}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)}(\eta-L)+\frac{L}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)}}\frac{dL}{\sqrt{\eta-L}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)}\eta}\int_{0}^{\eta}\frac{dL}{\sqrt{\eta-L}}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)}\eta},

and hence

\displaystyle\rho(t,r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\gamma_{D}(t)}\epsilon^{-3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)}\eta}H\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\eta-\epsilon^{3/2}\right)_{+}\right)\,d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\gamma_{D}(t)}\int_{-\epsilon^{1/2}}^{1}\sqrt{\eta+\epsilon^{1/2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)}(\epsilon\eta+\epsilon^{3/2})}H\left(\eta_{+}\right)\,d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\gamma_{D}(t)}\int_{-\epsilon^{1/2}}^{0}\sqrt{\eta+\epsilon^{1/2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)}(\epsilon\eta+\epsilon^{3/2})}H(0)\,d\eta

\displaystyle\quad{}+\frac{2\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\gamma_{D}(t)}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\eta+\epsilon^{1/2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\gamma_{D}^{2}(t)}(\epsilon\eta+\epsilon^{3/2})}H(\eta)\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\pi\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\gamma_{D}(t)}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\eta}H(\eta)\,d\eta+C\epsilon^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\epsilon}}{\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}(t)\gamma_{D}(t)}+C\epsilon^{1/2}

as claimed.

It remains to prove (
6.25
).
By the characteristic system (
6.21
)–(
6.23
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\gamma_{D}^{2}w^{2}+\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}\frac{L}{r^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\gamma_{D}\dot{\gamma}_{D}w^{2}+\gamma_{D}^{2}w\dot{w}+\gamma_{V,\epsilon}\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}\frac{L}{r^{2}}-\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}\frac{L}{r^{3}}\dot{r}

\displaystyle\quad=\gamma_{D}\dot{\gamma}_{D}w^{2}+\gamma_{D}^{2}(4\pi raj+\partial_{r}\beta)\,w^{2}+\gamma_{D}^{2}\frac{wL}{r^{3}a\sqrt{1+w^{2}+L/r^{2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\gamma_{V,\epsilon}\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}\frac{L}{r^{2}}-\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}\frac{L}{r^{3}}\left(\frac{w}{a\sqrt{1+w^{2}+L/r^{2}}}-\beta\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\gamma_{D}^{2}4\pi raj\,w^{2}+\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}\frac{L}{r^{2}}\left(\frac{\beta}{r}+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}}{\gamma_{V,\epsilon}}\right)+\frac{Lw}{r^{3}a\sqrt{1+w^{2}+L/r^{2}}}(\gamma_{D}^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}).

In the estimate we used the bootstrap assumption (i) and

and

,

Since we are looking at characteristics in

as explained above.
Thus

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left(\gamma_{D}^{2}w^{2}+\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}\frac{L}{r^{2}}\right)\leq C\epsilon^{3/2}+2\gamma^{2}_{V,\epsilon}\frac{L}{r^{2}}\left(\frac{\beta}{r}+\frac{\dot{\gamma}_{V,\epsilon}}{\gamma_{V,\epsilon}}\right).

(6.26)

Hence the assertion follows, provided we can suitably estimate the term
in parenthesis on the right hand side.

In order to do the latter we first observe that

.
In addition to the characteristic

of

\beta

where

\displaystyle|R(s)-r(s)|\leq C\epsilon^{1/2}.

(6.27)

,

\displaystyle\beta^{2}(s,r(s))

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}(s,R(s))+C\epsilon^{1/2}\leq\frac{2\mathring{m}(r)}{R(s)}+C\epsilon^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\mathring{m}_{h}(r)}{R(s)}+C\epsilon^{1/2}=\beta_{h}^{2}(s,R_{h}(s))\frac{R_{h}(s)}{R(s)}+C\epsilon^{1/2}.

(6.28)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}R^{3/2}(s)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{2m(s,R(s))}-C\epsilon^{1/2}\geq-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{2\mathring{m}(r)}-C\epsilon^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{2\mathring{m}_{h}(r)}-C\epsilon^{1/2}\geq\frac{d}{ds}R_{h}^{3/2}(s)-C\epsilon^{1/2},
