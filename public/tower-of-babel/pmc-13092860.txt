Dual targeting of NCF1 and NLRP3 by roburic acid orchestrates redox homeostasis and inhibits macrophage death in septic lung injury
Authors: Honghao Song, Lei Peng, Yutong Yang, Kaiqiang Sun, Yan Chu, Mengqiu Deng, Xiaoyan Meng, Huawei Wei, Jiao Cai, Yi Liu, Hongbin Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-associated acute lung injury (ALI) is characterized by excessive inflammation and macrophage death, yet precise therapeutic targets remain limited. Single-cell sequencing analysis indicates that, with progression of sepsis-induced lung injury, macrophages exhibit diverse cell-death programs with prominent enrichment of pyroptosis-related signatures. Here, we identify Roburic acid (RBA) as a potent inhibitor of septic ALI and elucidate its mechanism using a nanoparticle delivery system (RBA-NPs). We demonstrate that RBA-NPs significantly attenuate lung injury through a bioactive lipid compound library screening and improve survival in cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis models. Mechanistically, using chemical proteomics and cellular thermal shift assays, we identify NLRP3 and NCF1 as direct intracellular targets of RBA. Primarily, RBA interacts with the NACHT domain of NLRP3 to directly block inflammasome assembly and pyroptosis. Furthermore, RBA binds to NCF1 to inhibit NADPH oxidase 2 assembly; this restores redox homeostasis, which not only reinforces the suppression of pyroptosis but also confers additional protection by inhibiting lipid peroxidation-mediated ferroptosis. Our study reveals a comprehensive therapeutic strategy where RBA targets the NLRP3 inflammasome while coordinating redox homeostasis via NCF1 to resolve septic lung injury.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by a dysregulated host response to infection, resulting in high mortality and multi-organ dysfunction [1]. The lungs represent the most frequently affected organ [2,3], with sepsis often concurrently inducing SALI or ARDS [4]. Clinical evidence indicates that up to 50% of septic patients develop severe acute respiratory syndrome, typified by an intense and acutely dysfunctional inflammatory response leading to adverse outcomes [5]. While significant advances have been made in sepsis management through source control, fluid resuscitation, inflammation modulation, and organ support [6], morbidity and mortality rates remain unacceptably high, largely due to incomplete understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms. Recent research has established that sepsis progression critically involves mechanisms [7] such as oxidative stress, pyroptosis [8], and ferroptosis [9], which subsequently drive pulmonary injury, which highlights the promising therapeutic potential of targeting these specific pathways for treating sepsis-induced lung injury.

Macrophages, as main immune cells, contribute critically to the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced pulmonary injury [10]. During this process, neutrophils and monocytes migrate from the circulation into the lungs, where monocytes differentiate into macrophages that collaborate with tissue-resident macrophages to mediate pathogen clearance [11]. Upon recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), macrophages activate the inflammasomes, large multiprotein complexes that govern the maturation of proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18. Among these, the NLRP3 inflammasome is the most extensively studied and is known to trigger pyroptosis, a lytic form of programmed cell death mediated by gasdermin D (GSDMD) [12]. Excessive pyroptosis releases a deluge of intracellular contents, propagating a vicious cycle of inflammation. Consequently, NLRP3 has emerged as a prime therapeutic target. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) serve as a critical signaling hub in this context. While ROS are essential upstream triggers for NLRP3 activation [13], their excessive accumulation overwhelms the cellular antioxidant defense, leading to lipid peroxidation and lethal membrane damage known as ferroptosis [14]. If these pathways co-exist and synergize to drive lung injury, targeting NLRP3 alone may be insufficient, as the upstream oxidative fuel would persist, potentially shifting the cell toward ferroptosis. Therefore, a dual-targeting strategy that simultaneously blocks the inflammatory executioner and the oxidative trigger could offer superior therapeutic efficacy.

In this study, analysis of a publicly available single-cell RNA-seq dataset [15] suggested a pyroptosis-associated transcriptional trend in lung macrophage subsets during sepsis progression, together with oxidative-stress-related signatures. We then tested this hypothesis using independent in vivo and in vitro validation assays. To identify therapeutic candidates, we performed a phenotypic screen on bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) using a lipid-focused small molecule library and identified Roburic acid (RBA) as a potent agent capable of reversing the pyroptotic phenotype. Subsequent validation using both canonical and non-canonical stimuli showed that RBA consistently suppresses pyroptotic responses across multiple induction routes, supporting its broad-spectrum anti-pyroptotic activity in macrophages. Mechanistically, immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) identified NLRP3 and NCF1 as direct binding targets, suggesting a dual-targeting mode of action. Given that NCF1 governs the generation of ROS, which serves as the common upstream fuel driving both pyroptosis signaling and the lipid peroxidation required for ferroptosis, we further investigated whether RBA exerts concurrent protective effects against ferroptosis. To overcome the biopharmaceutical limitations of RBA and facilitate clinical translation, we engineered RBA-loaded nanoparticles (RBA-NPs). Our in vitro assays demonstrate that this nanomedicine potently suppresses the ROS burst and concurrently blocks mixed cell death in BMDMs. Furthermore, in vivo pulmonary transcriptomics reveals that RBA-NPs effectively reprogram the inflammatory landscape by extinguishing the pyroptosis-ferroptosis network. Ultimately, this targeted intervention significantly mitigates septic lung injury, which in turn confers systemic protection evidenced by attenuated inflammation, preserved multi-organ function, and markedly improved survival.

Results

Increased macrophage pyroptosis and oxidative stress activation during sepsis-induced lung injury

scRNA-seq represents a cutting-edge technology that has demonstrated significant value in studying sepsis-induced lung injury [16]. ScRNA-seq facilitates the examination of gene expression in individual nuclei, thereby exposing cellular heterogeneity and elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms in complex tissues [17]. Consequently, we utilized publicly available scRNA-seq data [15] from the GEO database (GEA207651), encompassing samples from three groups: normal mouse alveolar tissue (Control group), CLP-induced septic mice (Sepsis group), and CLP-induced septic mice at 48 h post-surgery (Sepsis-48h group). Following standard clustering analysis, we identified some main distinct cell clusters and these clusters were further classified into nine major cell types based on the expression of canonical lineage-specific marker genes (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1A–C, Supporting Information): T cells (eg, Cd3g, Cd3e), Stromal cells (eg. Actc1, Susd5, Sost), NK cells (eg: Klra8, Klra4), Neutrophils (eg: Retnlg, Prok2), Monocytes (eg: Cenpm, Adgre4), Macrophages (eg: Atp6vod2, Krt79, Fabp1), Fibroblasts (eg: Clec3b, Rbp4), Endothelial cells (eg: Cdh5, Arhgef15), B cells (eg: Lglc2, Fcmr, Lglc3). Comparative analysis of cellular proportions across the three groups showed apparent alterations in cellular composition in both the Sepsis and Sepsis-48h groups relative to the Control group. This finding indicates substantial shifts in cell type abundance during the development of sepsis. Macrophages, as essential components of both innate and adaptive immunity, exhibit remarkable heterogeneity and plasticity [18,19] (Fig. 1A–B). To investigate their potential role in sepsis pathophysiology, we assessed macrophage involvement across a comprehensive panel of 18 distinct cell death modalities. The scoring results revealed widespread macrophage engagement in multiple death pathways, strongly suggesting their critical contribution to septic pathogenesis (Fig. S1D, Supporting Information). Subsequent unsupervised clustering of macrophages identified eight distinct subpopulations (designated MAC1-MAC8). Subcluster-specific marker genes were defined for each population based on their uniquely enriched expression (Fig. S2A, Supporting Information), including Il1α, Ckap2l, Gm13571, Cxcl3, Ereg, C1qa, C1qc, among others.

To further investigate the modes of cell death in macrophage subsets during sepsis-induced lung injury, we scored the death phenotypes of different macrophage subsets (MAC1-MAC8) (Fig. S2B, Supporting Information) and calculated the proportional changes of these subsets across the three experimental groups (Fig. 1C). The scoring analysis suggested that pyroptosis-associated programs were prominently enriched in macrophage subsets following sepsis onset, with MAC1 and MAC2 showing a shift toward stronger pyroptosis-associated signatures within this dataset (Fig. 1C and Fig. S2B, Supporting Information). Consistently, the proportional distribution of macrophage subsets showed an increased proportion of MAC1 and MAC2 in both the Sepsis and Sepsis-48h groups compared with the Control group (Fig. 1C). These scRNA-seq patterns suggested the involvement of macrophage pyroptosis during sepsis-induced lung injury, which we subsequently evaluated by independent protein-level and functional assays. Furthermore, descriptive comparison of expression patterns between Control and Sepsis suggested that MAC1 and MAC2 showed higher expression trends of pyroptosis-related genes (e.g., Nlrp3, Caspase1, Gsdmd, Il1β) and oxidative stress-related genes (e.g., Ncf1, Txnip) (Fig. 1E–F). These observations suggest that oxidative stress accompanies macrophage pyroptosis in the context of sepsis-related lung injury.

Consistent with our single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, immunofluorescence co-localization revealed significantly enhanced co-localization signals of N-GSDMD+/F4/80+ (Fig. 1G) and NCF1+/F4/80+ (Fig. 1H) within pulmonary macrophages, with markedly intensified staining patterns observed in the sepsis group compared to controls. This observation correlated with histopathological evidence from H&E staining (Fig. 1I), lung injury scoring (Fig. 1J), pulmonary wet/dry ratios (Fig. 1K), and serum inflammatory cytokine level (Fig. S3, Supporting Information). In summary, the scRNA-seq analysis provides an exploratory, macrophage-focused hypothesis, and our animal experiments (IF co-staining and biochemical readouts of inflammasome activation) support the engagement of macrophage pyroptosis and oxidative stress during sepsis-induced lung injury.

Phenotypic screening identifies RBA as a potent pyroptosis inhibitor independent of COX1/COX2 pathways

To screen for small-molecule compounds that inhibit pyroptosis, we utilized BMDMs and screened a bioactive lipid compound library containing 385 compounds (Fig. 2A). Pyroptosis was induced in BMDMs by stimulation with 1 μg/ml LPS for 2 h, followed by treatment with 10 mM ATP for 3 h. To assess pyroptosis inhibition, compounds from the library were added at a concentration of 10 μM concurrently with ATP (Fig. 2A). The results demonstrated that the canonical pyroptosis inhibitors MCC950 [20,21] and resveratrol [22,23] effectively mitigated the loss of cell viability induced by ATP, confirming the robustness of our screening system (Fig. 2B). Notably, in addition to MCC950 and resveratrol, we identified a previously unreported molecule, RBA, which also potently inhibited ATP-induced pyroptosis (Fig. 2B–C). Furthermore, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of RBA against ATP-induced pyroptosis was approximately 6.8 μM (Fig. 2D), which is slightly lower than that of MCC950 (IC50 ≈ 7.5 μM) (Fig. 2E). To further confirm the inhibitory effect of RBA on pyroptosis, we first employed flow cytometry to assess pyroptotic cell death (Fig. 2F). During pyroptosis, increased plasma membrane permeability allows propidium iodide (PI) and the FLICA caspase-1 activity probe to enter cells and label DNA, resulting in a double-positive population (Fig. 2F). Treatment with either RBA or MCC950 significantly reduced the proportion of double-positive cells following ATP stimulation, indicating that RBA effectively rescued cells from pyroptosis. Similarly, PI staining revealed that RBA almost completely blocked ATP-induced cell death, with an efficacy comparable to that of MCC950 (Fig. 2G). Quantification of PI-positive cells showed that BMDMs treated with either RBA or MCC950 exhibited a significantly lower percentage of PI-positive cells than the LPS + ATP group (Fig. 2H). Ultrastructural analysis by electron microscopy, which typically reveals characteristic cell swelling and vacuole formation during pyroptosis, showed that both the LPS + ATP + RBA and LPS + ATP + MCC950 groups exhibited markedly attenuated cell swelling and fewer vacuolar structures, further supporting the alleviation of pyroptosis (Fig. 2I). Since RBA is a known inhibitor of COX1 and COX2, we investigated whether its anti-pyroptotic effect relied on this activity. We utilized shRNA to knockdown COX1 (Fig. S3A–B, Supporting Information) and COX2 (Fig. S3D–E, Supporting Information) in BMDMs. RBA retained its protective effect against LPS/ATP-induced cell death in both COX1-silenced (Fig. S3C, Supporting Information) and COX2-silenced cells (Fig. S3F, Supporting Information). These data demonstrate that RBA inhibits pyroptosis through a mechanism independent of the COX1/COX2 pathway.

RBA protects against pyroptosis triggered by multiple inflammasome and non-canonical stimuli

We next assessed whether RBA protects against pyroptosis induced by multiple triggers and pathways. RBA inhibited pyroptotic cell death in both THP-1 cells (Fig. 3A) and BMDMs (Fig. 3B), supporting activity across human and murine macrophage models. Pyroptosis is characterized by GSDMD pore formation and subsequent plasma membrane rupture, we used LDH release as a quantitative readout of lytic cell death, in parallel with CCK-8-based metabolic viability measurements. We then evaluated RBA under distinct inflammasome and non-canonical pyroptosis conditions. RBA significantly reduced NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptotic cell death induced by LPS/ATP (Fig. 3C). Consistent with a direct inhibitory effect on inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis, RBA reduced pyroptosis triggered by poly(dA:dT) (AIM2 inflammasome) (Fig. 3D) and decreased the generation of N-GSDMD and mature IL-1β under this condition (Fig. S4A, Supporting Information). RBA also inhibited flagellin-triggered pyroptosis (NLRC4 inflammasome) (Fig. 3E) and suppressed N-GSDMD and cleaved IL-1β (Fig. S4B, Supporting Information). Notably, RBA suppressed non-canonical pyroptosis induced by Pam3CSK4 priming plus cytosolic LPS (Fig. 3F), accompanied by reduced N-GSDMD and cleaved IL-1β (Fig. S4C, Supporting Information). Because several agonists in these assays require Lipofectamine-mediated intracellular delivery, we further tested whether RBA affects ligand uptake. Using fluorescently labeled agonists, we found that RBA did not significantly change cellular fluorescence intensity of LPS or poly(dA:dT) compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. S5A–B, Supporting Information), arguing against altered agonist uptake as an explanation for the observed inhibition. Time-course quantification of PI fluorescence at the indicated time points showed that RBA reduced cell death in both THP-1 cells (Fig. 3G) and BMDMs (Fig. 3H). Before further characterizing these interactions, we sought to rule out other key pyroptosis regulators. In Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability (DARTS) assays, RBA failed to protect AIM2 (Fig. 4E), NLRC4 (Fig. 4F), or Caspase-11 (Fig. 4G) from proteolysis. Crucially, RBA also did not bind to GSDMD, the common downstream executor of all pyroptosis pathways (Fig. 4H). This absence of binding to specific sensors and the common effector strongly suggested that RBA targets a shared upstream signal.

Consistent with the pull-down results, we confirmed that RBA directly targets NLRP3 and NCF1 (a critical subunit of NADPH oxidase) using DARTS. RBA dose-dependently protected endogenous NLRP3 (Fig. 4I), recombinant NLRP3 (Fig. 4J), endogenous NCF1 (Fig. 4K), and recombinant NCF1 (Fig. 4L) from protease digestion. This direct binding was further corroborated by cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA), where RBA enhanced the thermal stability of both NLRP3 (Fig. 4M–N) and NCF1 (Fig. 4O–P). Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) analysis revealed a specific binding dissociation constant (KD) value of 23.7 μM for RBA to NLRP3 (Figs. 4Q) and 42.2 μM to NCF1 (Fig. 4R), respectively.

Next, we investigated in detail how RBA binds to its targets. Since the structure of RBA lacks typical electrophilic groups (such as acrylamide) that usually form irreversible covalent bonds with target proteins, we explored whether RBA exerts a reversible inhibitory effect. After treating BMDMs with RBA for 1 h, we washed the cells three times to remove the free compound and then stimulated them with LPS/ATP (Fig. S7A, Supporting Information) or Poly(dA:dT) (Fig. S7B, Supporting Information). The results showed that the anti-pyroptotic effect of RBA disappeared after washing, indicating that the interaction between RBA and its targets is reversible. Molecular docking further supported this non-covalent binding mode, predicting that RBA binds to the NACHT domain of NLRP3 (Fig. 4S) and the SH3 domain of NCF1 (Fig. 4V). Mutational analysis pinpointed R578 in NLRP3 (Fig. 4T–U) and Q293 in NCF1 (Fig. 4W–X) as the critical residues for RBA binding, while other predicted mutants (NLRP3: V353A, I574A, Y623A; NCF1: Q281A, M278A, W264A) had no effect (Fig. S8A–D, Supporting Information).

RBA inhibition of NCF1-ROS axis links pyroptosis and ferroptosis suppression

Having identified NCF1 as a target, we hypothesized that RBA inhibits non-NLRP3 inflammasomes by blocking NCF1-dependent ROS production. First, we evaluated its direct radical-scavenging capacity in cell-free systems using ESR spin trapping to exclude the possibility that RBA acts as a non-specific chemical antioxidant. Ascorbic acid (AA), used as a positive control, robustly quenched the ESR signals of both •OH and O2•-. In contrast, RBA failed to measurably reduce either radical signal compared with vehicle at the tested concentrations (10 and 50 μg/mL) (Fig. S9A–B, Supporting Information), indicating that RBA does not function as a direct radical scavenger under these conditions. Next, we supplemented cells with exogenous H2O2. While H2O2 only partially affected RBA's protection against LPS/ATP, where RBA exerts dual inhibition on NLRP3 and ROS (Fig. S10A, Supporting Information), it significantly reversed RBA-mediated protection against Poly(dA:dT) (Fig. S10B, Supporting Information), Flagellin (Fig. S10C, Supporting Information), and non-canonical stimuli (Fig. S10D, Supporting Information). This confirms that for non-NLRP3 pathways, RBA acts primarily by severing the upstream ROS signal.

Since ROS is also a key initiator of ferroptosis, we explored whether RBA's targeting of NCF1 extended its protective scope to other cell death modalities. Remarkably, RBA significantly rescued cells from RSL3-induced ferroptosis, displaying potency similar to Ferrostatin-1 (Fig. S11A, Supporting Information). However, it showed no effect on necroptosis (Fig. S11B, Supporting Information) or cuproptosis (Fig. S11C, Supporting Information). This unexpected finding highlights RBA as a unique dual-inhibitor of pyroptosis and ferroptosis via the NCF1-ROS axis. To establish whether NCF1 is functionally required for the anti-ferroptotic activity of RBA, we silenced Ncf1 in BMDMs and assessed RSL3-induced lipid peroxidation in the presence or absence of RBA. Ncf1 knockdown efficiency was verified by immunoblotting (Fig. S12A, Supporting Information). Functionally, while RBA markedly reduced RSL3-induced C11-BODIPY oxidation in control cells, this protective effect was abolished upon Ncf1 knockdown (Fig. S12B, Supporting Information). These results demonstrate that NCF1 is required for RBA-mediated suppression of lipid peroxidation during ferroptotic stress.

Consequently, to address whether RBA might indirectly activate the Nrf2 pathway, we examined NRF2 nuclear accumulation. LPS/ATP stimulation increased nuclear NRF2, consistent with an oxidative stress response. RBA did not promote NRF2 nuclear translocation; instead, it reduced the elevated nuclear NRF2 signal under LPS/ATP stimulation. Importantly, Ncf1 knockdown abrogated the RBA-associated reduction of nuclear NRF2, suggesting that this effect is secondary to suppression of NCF1-driven ROS overload rather than direct pharmacological activation of Nrf2 (Fig. S13, Supporting Information).

To further test whether the binding determinants identified for RBA are functionally required, we compared WT and point-mutant constructs for NLRP3 and NCF1. The R578A mutation did not alter NLRP3 expression relative to WT (Fig. S14A, Supporting Information), and the Q293A mutation did not alter NCF1 expression (Fig. S15A, Supporting Information), indicating that the mutations themselves do not compromise protein abundance. Functionally, under LPS/ATP stimulation, RBA suppressed inflammasome execution markers (N-GSDMD and cleaved IL-1β) in cells expressing NLRP3-WT, whereas this inhibitory effect was attenuated with NLRP3-R578A (Fig. S14B, Supporting Information). In addition, lipid peroxidation was assessed by flow cytometry using C11-BODIPY; RBA reduced RSL3-induced C11-BODIPY oxidation in the NCF1-WT condition, but this protection was diminished in the NCF1-Q293A mutant condition (Fig. S15B, Supporting Information). Together, these results support that NCF1 contributes to RBA-associated suppression of lipid peroxidation during ferroptotic stress in the knockdown setting; definitive genetic specificity will require shRNA-resistant rescue or CRISPR-based loss-of-function in future work.

Subsequently, to evaluate whether the RBA-NPs could efficiently inhibit oxidative stress driven-pyroptosis in vitro, BMDMs were pretreated with either free RBA (20 μM) or RBA-NPs (20 μM) before stimulation with LPS + ATP. The results showed that the pyroptosis-rescuing effect of RBA-NPs was comparable to that of free RBA (Fig. 5G). Consistent with pyroptotic cell death characterized by plasma membrane disintegration, LDH release from damaged cells was quantified across experimental groups [25]. LDH levels in culture supernatants were significantly elevated in LPS + ATP group compared to controls, while RBA-NPs co-treatment substantially attenuated this release (Fig. 5H). Ultrastructural analysis by SEM confirmed classical pyroptotic morphology in LPS + ATP group, featuring compromised membrane integrity, cellular swelling, and organelle damage (Fig. 5K). Conversely, RBA-NPs-treated cells exhibited marked restoration of plasma membrane continuity, reduced blebbing, and preserved organellar architecture, demonstrating potent inhibition of pyroptotic progression at the cellular level. q-PCR analysis demonstrated that compared to LPS + ATP group, RBA-NPs treatment downregulated the mRNA expression levels of Nlrp3, Gsdmd, Caspase1, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 5I). Western blot results showed reduced protein expression of NLRP3, N-GSDMD, CL-CASPASE1, and CL-IL-1β following RBA-NPs treatment (Fig. 5J, L-O). IF confirmed co-localization of NLRP3 and F4/80 in BMDMs (Fig. S17, Supporting Information), with reduced fluorescence intensity after RBA-NPs treatment relative to LPS and ATP stimulation. These findings indicate that RBA-NPs inhibits the pyroptosis-related pathway in BMDMs. Additionally, studies indicate that ROS can directly target inflammasome-associated CASPASE proteins involved in cleavage, thereby mediating the pore-forming activity of GSDMD [24]. Stimuli inducing either exogenously or endogenously derived ROS can cleave GSDMD, leading to plasma membrane rupture and pyroptosis [26]. Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed a marked decrease in the number of ROS-positive cells following RBA-NPs treatment (Fig. 5P–Q). In addition, we also assessed the fold change in the mRNA levels of the relevant genes and results indicated that compared with the LPS + ATP group, RBA-NPs treatment significantly downregulated Cybb (Fig. 5S). Notably, these pathological features were substantially reversed in the RBA-NPs treatment. As a result, the ROS pathway likely represents an upstream regulator of the NLRP3/GSDMD/Caspase-1/IL-1β pathway targeted by RBA-NPs.

To assess the anti-ferroptotic efficacy of RBA-NPs in vitro, BMDMs pretreated with free RBA or RBA-NPs were challenged with RSL3 for lipid peroxidation detection. The result demonstrates that the nanoformulation achieved a rescue effect comparable to the free drug. IF co-staining revealed decreased intensity for ACSL4 and F4/80 (Fig. S18A, Supporting Information), but increased intensity GPX4 and F4/80 (Fig. S18B, Supporting Information) in the RBA-NPs treatment group compared to the LPS + ATP group. Western blot analysis demonstrated decreased ACSL4 protein levels and increased GPX4 protein levels (Fig. S18C–E, Supporting Information). Therefore, these findings indicate that RBA-NPs can inhibit oxidative stress-linked pyroptosis as well as exhibit notable anti-ferroptosis efficacy in vitro.

RBA-NPs treatment prevents CLP-induced acute lung injury (ALI)

Based on the remarkable effects of RBA against oxidative stress driven-pyroptosis, and ferroptosis observed in vitro, we further investigated its protective potential against related diseases in vivo. Sepsis-induced lung injury, often occurring in the early stages of sepsis, is pathologically defined as ALI resulting from a dysregulated host inflammatory response. Cellular damage in this context is mediated by multiple forms of cell death, with ROS generated during oxidative stress serving as a key trigger for both pyroptosis and ferroptosis, thereby driving the progression of septic lung injury [27]. To systematically explore the therapeutic basis of RBA in this condition, we performed a network pharmacology analysis focusing on the interplay among “Roburic acid,” “sepsis-induced lung injury,” and the associated signaling pathways [28,29] (Fig. S19A–B, Supporting Information). RBA-associated targets were aggregated from three databases: 249 from Comparative Toxicogenomics Database, 51 from SwissTargetPrediction, and 4 from SuperPred. Following removal of duplicates, 181 unique potential RBA-NPs targets were retained. Concurrently, 1426 sepsis-induced lung injury-associated targets were compiled from disease databases after deduplication. Intersection analysis revealed 122 overlapping targets between RBA's potential targets and disease-associated targets, establishing a prioritized candidate pool for mechanistic investigation (Fig. S19B, Supporting Information). Functional enrichment analysis of the 122 intersecting targets was performed using GO and KEGG databases. In the GO analysis, pathways such as “cellular response to oxidative stress” were highly enriched (Fig. S19C, Supporting Information). Correspondingly, the KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to “NOD-like receptor signaling” (Fig. S19D, Supporting Information). Together, these network pharmacology results suggest that RBA may alleviate sepsis-induced lung injury by targeting pathways associated with oxidative stress driven-pyroptosis, and ferroptosis.

Ex vivo organ imaging of Cy5.5-labeled RBA-NPs demonstrated preferential lung accumulation and retention compared with Cy5.5+PBS (Fig. S20A–B, Supporting Information), supporting adequate pulmonary delivery at the efficacious dose. Sepsis frequently progresses to MODS, with the lungs, heart, liver, and kidneys being particularly vulnerable [30]. To further delineate the protective effects of RBA-NPs against sepsis-induced organ damage, comprehensive histopathological and functional assessments were performed (Fig. 7L–Q). RBA-NPs treatment significantly ameliorated lung injury, as evidenced by reduced lung injury scores (Fig. 7L), lower lung wet/dry weight ratios (Fig. 7N), and histological improvements on H&E staining (Fig. 7M). Discussion

Sepsis-induced acute lung injury remains a significant clinical challenge due to the involvement of multiple regulated cell-death pathways in macrophages and myeloid cells [[31], [32], [33]]. NLRP3-driven pyroptosis is linked to cytokine release and barrier disruption, while oxidative stress and ferroptosis also contribute to septic organ injury [[34], [35], [36], [37]]. These findings have driven pathway-targeted strategies like NLRP3 inhibition, but translation has been hindered by the heterogeneity of inflammasome activation, including canonical and non-canonical routes and distinct PRRs such as AIM2 and NLRC4, as well as the role of ROS in shaping both inflammasome signaling and lipid peroxidation [38]. Our integrated analysis of scRNA-seq data and tissue validation suggests that septic lung macrophages, particularly MAC1/2 subsets, exhibit a pyroptosis-associated transcriptional trend with increased expression of oxidative stress–related genes like Ncf1 and Txnip. Consistent with this, NLRP3 and NCF1 show increased co-localization within F4/80+ macrophages, highlighting the interplay between inflammasome activation and ROS-driven pathways.

Small-molecule screening, which is characterized by its directness and reliability of results without reliance on target structures [39], is widely used in the development of new drug targets. Consequently, through small-molecule screening, we identified RBA as an inhibitor of pyroptosis. Additionally, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) is characterized by intrinsic multi-target activity. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that natural herbal agents can simultaneously engage TRPV1, TRPA1 and allied channels to accelerate postoperative ileus resolution [40]. Likewise, the Jiawei Simiao Yong-an decoction (HJ11) exerts comprehensive cardioprotection in acute coronary syndrome by modulating the DDAH/ADMA/NO axis, thereby attenuating inflammation and oxidative stress [41]. To delineate whether RBA affords additional, complementary protection against sepsis-induced lung injury, we conducted an integrated battery of orthogonal validation assays. Our results found RBA suppresses pyroptotic cell death induced by diverse stimuli, including canonical NLRP3 activation, AIM2-and NLRC4-dependent pathways, and non-canonical caspase-11 activation. Broad suppression of pyroptosis is often attributed to direct inhibition of multiple PRRs or inhibition of the shared terminal effector GSDMD [42,43]. Our target-validation experiments argue against these explanations. In DARTS assays, RBA did not stabilise AIM2, NLRC4, caspase-11 or GSDMD, making direct engagement of these proteins unlikely under our conditions and implicating a shared upstream dependence across pathways. In line with this, chemical proteomics identified NCF1 as an intracellular binding partner, and orthogonal DARTS/CETSA assays supported target engagement. Functionally, ROS restoration by exogenous H2O2 largely reversed RBA-mediated protection in AIM2-, NLRC4- and non-canonical models, consistent with attenuation of ROS-dependent signalling required for efficient inflammasome activation and downstream signal propagation. An ROS-centric mechanism does not fully explain the effects of RBA in canonical NLRP3 activation, where H2O2 restored pyroptosis only partially. This pattern is consistent with an additional pathway-proximal action. Importantly, cell-free ESR assays showed that RBA did not directly quench O2•- or •OH signals, whereas ascorbic acid markedly suppressed both, arguing against a generalized antioxidant mechanism and supporting pathway-proximal inhibition. Bio-RBA pull-down, DARTS and CETSA converged on NLRP3 as a direct target. Docking together with mutagenesis implicated residue R578 within the NACHT domain, which is required for ATPase-dependent conformational transitions and oligomerisation. These data support a working model in which RBA attenuates a permissive redox signal via NCF1 while also constraining NLRP3, producing stronger suppression of canonical pyroptosis than expected from redox modulation alone. Washout experiments suggest that these interactions are reversible rather than covalent. We additionally assessed Nrf2 activation and found that RBA did not increase NRF2 nuclear accumulation; the reduced nuclear NRF2 observed after RBA treatment likely reflects restoration of redox homeostasis following NCF1 inhibition rather than direct Nrf2 activation.

The translational relevance of these mechanisms is supported by our nanoparticle formulation and in vivo validation. Poor solubility and exposure commonly limit natural product development and can obscure the relationship between cellular mechanism and organismal efficacy [44]. Following the high-throughput screening of 66 distinct metal–phenolic networks, Xin et al. classified the resulting NPs into five architecturally conserved clusters of small-molecule building blocks that combine high drug-encapsulation capacity with efficient gastrointestinal penetration [45]. Among these, dehydrotumulosic acid (DTA) emerged as a privileged ligand capable of orchestrating oral delivery platforms that achieve therapeutically relevant systemic exposure [45]. Complementarily, NP-encapsulated aloin has been shown to enhance bioavailability, suppress mitochondrial DNA release, and consequently interrupt the downstream cGAS–STING axis, thereby re-programming microglial polarization toward a neuroprotective phenotype [46]. Encapsulation of RBA in mPEG-PCL nanoparticles enabled in vivo testing of whether the in vitro mechanism is reflected at tissue scale. In septic lungs following RBA-NPs treatment, transcriptomic profiling showed reduced signatures associated with inflammasome, pyroptosis and oxidative stress, including Nlrp3, Gsdmd, Ncf1 and Cybb, together with suppression of NOD-like receptor and NF-κB-associated programmes and attenuation of oxidative stress and ferroptosis gene sets. Reduced N-GSDMD and NCF1 immunofluorescence within F4/80+ cells supports macrophages as an in vivo target cell type. Together with lung pharmacodynamic endpoints (reduced cleaved caspase-1 and N-GSDMD), the preferential lung enrichment of Cy5.5-labeled RBA-NPs supports effective pulmonary delivery and tissue-context pathway modulation consistent with the observed efficacy. We note that fluorescence imaging does not provide absolute drug concentrations nor differentiate intact nanoparticles from released drug, and thus we interpret these as evidence of lung enrichment rather than full PK characterization. These molecular changes accompanied improved lung pathology, reduced oedema, lower systemic cytokines, improved organ injury markers and improved survival.

Although these results support RBA-NPs as a mechanistically informed strategy to mitigate sepsis-associated lung injury, translation to humans will require substantial additional work. Notably, the mouse dosing used here was selected to achieve adequate exposure in an acute CLP setting and should not be interpreted as a clinically equivalent human dose, because interspecies dose scaling is non-linear and nanoparticle pharmacokinetics can differ across species. To the best of our knowledge, roburic acid has not been evaluated in registered human clinical trial. Accordingly, future studies should establish exposure-response relationships via formal PK/PD, assess safety, and test post-onset dosing regimens in conjunction with standard supportive care.

Several limitations remain. First, the scRNA-seq analysis was based on a publicly available dataset without biological replicates (n = 1 per condition) and therefore serves as hypothesis-generating. Accordingly, module-score visualizations (e.g., AddModuleScore) are interpreted descriptively rather than as animal-level statistical inference, and future studies with replicated cohorts should include cross-method validation (e.g., AUCell/ssGSEA) to assess robustness of pathway scoring. Second, although docking and mutagenesis implicate NLRP3-R578 and NCF1-Q293, structural studies will be required to define binding poses and conformational consequences. In addition, while we quantified RBA-NLRP3/NCF1 interactions by SPR, future work will further corroborate the affinity parameters using orthogonal methods such as MST or ITC under matched solvent conditions. Third, whether there is an interactive influence between the pyroptosis and ferroptosis changes induced by RBA-NPs still needs further exploration. Fourth, while our target-engagement and mutagenesis data support involvement of NLRP3-R578 and NCF1-Q293, we did not perform endogenous genetic epistasis experiments (e.g., CRISPR knockout/conditional deletion) or shRNA-resistant rescue in primary macrophages to formally exclude off-target effects and establish genetic necessity under physiological expression. Fifth, our in vivo dosing regimen included administration of RBA-NPs prior to CLP surgery to ensure adequate tissue exposure at the time of septic insult; consistent with this design, fluorescence biodistribution showed rapid and sustained lung enrichment of Cy5.5-labeled RBA-NPs within hours after injection. Nevertheless, defining translational utility will require establishing a therapeutic window using post-onset dosing, performing formal PK/PD to relate plasma and lung exposures to efficacy, and evaluating benefit alongside standard supportive care. Finally, advancement toward human application will require validation in models capturing clinical heterogeneity and supportive care, together with safety assessment and careful dose selection based on exposure matching.

Conclusions

In summary, our study integrates single-cell transcriptomics with chemical biology to uncover a critical pathogenic axis in sepsis, where NCF1-driven oxidative stress fuels macrophage pyroptosis and ferroptosis. We identified RBA as a potent dual-targeting modulator with direct binding to specific amino acid residues on NLRP3 (R578) and NCF1 (Q293). By concurrently attenuating the upstream ROS generator and the downstream inflammatory executor, RBA is consistent with disrupting the crosstalk between oxidative stress and programmed cell death. Future work employing genetic loss-of-function (CRISPR knockout or conditional deletion of NLRP3 or NCF1) will be necessary to establish endogenous pathway necessity under physiological conditions. Furthermore, the encapsulation of RBA into nanoparticles improved its bioavailability, demonstrating remarkable efficacy in suppressing cytokine storms and preserving alveolar structural integrity. Collectively, these findings define the NCF1-NLRP3 axis as a viable therapeutic target and present RBA as a promising candidate for mitigating sepsis-induced acute lung injury and improving clinical outcomes.

Experimental section

Analysis of single-cell sequencing data

Single-cell RNA sequencing data derived from septic murine lung tissues (GEO accession: GSE207651) were generated using the 10 × Genomics platform. We implemented stringent quality control by retaining: (1) Genes detected in ≥3 cells; (2) Cells expressing ≥200 genes, while excluding those with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 30% or total gene counts outside the 200-7500 range. Additionally, to mitigate doublet contamination sensitive to high-density pooled sequencing, we performed doublet detection using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) and Scrublet (v0.2.3) with cross-validation (expected doublet rate: 10%; pANN >0.25), excluding cells identified by both algorithms (4.8% removal rate). To address ambient RNA artifacts inherent to tissue dissociation protocols, we applied SoupX (v1.6.2) to estimate and remove contamination using empty droplets as reference. Data normalization was performed with Seurat's NormalizeData function. For explicit batch-effect correction, we applied Harmony (v0.1.0) integration to the first 30 principal components (theta = 2.0, lambda = 1) using experimental groups as batch variables; integration quality was validated by kBET and cluster stability via iterative subsampling. We subsequently identified 2000 highly variable genes using FindVariableFeatures, followed by data scaling. PCA was applied to these highly variable genes, retaining the top 30 principal components for downstream analysis. To enable composition-aware statistical modeling despite the absence of biological replicates, we employed Milo (v1.0) for neighborhood-level differential abundance estimation, reported cell-type proportions with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (1000 iterations) after downsampling to equal library sizes between groups to exclude sequencing depth bias, and validated pyroptosis module scores using DESeq2 on pseudobulk aggregates per group rather than single-cell level tests to avoid pseudoreplication errors.

Cell culture and treatment

Cell culture and differentiation

Human THP-1 monocytes (RRID: CVCL_0006) were provided by Shanghai QuiCell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). To induce differentiation into macrophages, THP-1 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 16 h. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of male C57BL/6J mice. The bone marrow cells were flushed and cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 30% L929 cell-conditioned medium (as a source of M-CSF) for 6 days to promote differentiation into mature macrophages.

Inflammasome activation and drug treatment

For the initial screening and standard inflammasome activation assays, BMDMs or differentiated THP-1 cells were primed with LPS (1 μg/mL, HY-B2176) for 2 h. Following priming, the cell culture supernatant was discarded to remove excess LPS. Cells were then incubated with fresh medium containing ATP (5 mM) combined with RBA (Standard concentrations: 5, 10, 20 μM) or MCC950 for 3 h.

To assess the specificity of RBA on distinct inflammasome pathways, specific stimulation protocols were employed. For NLRP3 activation, the standard LPS priming followed by ATP stimulation described above was used. For AIM2 and NLRC4 inflammasome activation, LPS-primed cells were transfected with Poly(dA:dT) (500 ng/mL) or Flagellin (250 ng/mL), respectively, using Lipofectamine 3000 for 6 h. For non-canonical inflammasome activation, cells were primed with Pam3CSK4 (400 ng/mL) for 4 h, followed by intracellular delivery of LPS (1.5 μg/mL) using FuGENE HD transfection reagent for 16 h. In all assays, cell viability and cytotoxicity were subsequently assessed to evaluate the inhibitory effect of RBA.

Compound library and screening

The bioactive lipid compound library used for the primary screen was purchased from TargetMol Bio. The corresponding cell-viability screening results are provided in Supplementary Table 2. In addition to the compounds included in the purchased library, MCC950 was included as an extra compound during the screening experiments to serve as an additional control.

Lentiviral transfection for gene silencing

Lentiviral transduction was utilized for efficient gene silencing in primary BMDMs. Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting mouse COX1 and COX2, along with a scramble control (sh-NC), were cloned into the pLKO. 1 vector. Lentiviral particles were packaged in HEK-293T cells using psPAX2 and pMD2. G plasmids. BMDMs were infected with the viral supernatant in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL) for 24 h. Knockdown efficiency was verified by Western blotting after 72 h of infection.

Site-directed mutagenesis and target validation

To validate the specific binding interactions between RBA and its targets, mammalian expression vectors encoding Flag-tagged Wild-Type (WT) and mutant human NLRP3 (V353A, I574A, R578A, Y623A) and NCF1 (W264A, M278A, Q281A, Q293A) were constructed based on molecular docking predictions. HEK-293T cells were transiently transfected with these plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000, and cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection.

Specificity screening against other cell death modalities

To determine the specificity of RBA, its effects were evaluated on other forms of regulated cell death, including ferroptosis, necroptosis, and cuproptosis. Ferroptosis was induced using RSL3 (0.5 μM), with Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 1 μM) serving as a positive control inhibitor. Necroptosis was induced using a cocktail of TNF-α (20 ng/mL), z-VAD-FMK (20 μM), and SM-164 (100 nM) (T + Z + S), with Necrosulfonamide (NSA, 1 μM) used as the specific inhibitor. Cuproptosis was induced using CuCl2 (1 μM) combined with Elesclomol (ES, 1 μM), with Glutathione (GSH, 2 mM) serving as a rescue agent. In all models, RBA was co-incubated with the respective inducers to assess its potential protective activity compared to specific inhibitors.

ROS rescue experiment

To investigate whether the protective effect of RBA acts through ROS scavenging, a rescue experiment was performed. Exogenous H2O2 (100 μM) was added to the culture medium simultaneously with RBA during the ATP stimulation phase. Cell viability was subsequently measured to evaluate whether restoring ROS levels could reverse the anti-pyroptotic effects of RBA.

Cell viability via CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed with a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Topscience) following the supplier's protocol with additional controls to exclude optical and chemical interference. In brief, cells were plated in a 96-well plate at 5000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Following treatment with various compounds for the indicated durations, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. After incubation for 2 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Epoch).

Structure the Bio-RBA

To synthesize the biotinylated probe, Roburic Acid (RBA, 44.1 mg, 0.1 mmol) was dissolved in 2 mL of anhydrous DMSO. The solution was mixed with N-(2-Aminoethyl)biotinamide hydrobromide (Biotin-EDA, 36.7 mg, 0.1 mmol), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC, 23.0 mg, 0.12 mmol), and 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, 14.7 mg, 0.12 mmol). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight to ensure complete conjugation. The resulting solution was purified by preparative HPLC to remove excess reagents and by-products. The desired product, Bio-RBA, was obtained as a white solid after lyophilization.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Whole-transcriptome profiling of murine pulmonary tissue was conducted via bulk RNA sequencing to assess genome-wide expression dynamics. Libraries underwent rigorous quality assurance, with technical replicates demonstrating >95% concordance (inter-replicate coefficient of variation <5%). Differential expression calling employed DESeq2 under stringent thresholds (absolute log2 fold-change >0.585, Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value <0.05). Expression patterns and sample clustering were visualized through volcano plot generation and hierarchically clustered heatmaps.

Molecular docking validation

Compound structures were acquired from PubChem in SDF format, while target protein crystal structures were retrieved from the PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/) as PDB files. Protein structures underwent preprocessing in PyMOL 2.6.0 to remove water molecules and bound ligands, followed by binding pocket parameter determination using the Getbox Plugin. Processed proteins and ligands were converted to PDBQT format using AutoDock Tools 1.5.6. Molecular docking was performed with AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 employing the Lamarckian genetic algorithm. Resultant binding poses were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL 2.6.0 for structural interaction assessment.

Polymicrobial sepsis modeling and therapeutic intervention

Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old; 22 ± 2 g) were obtained from the Naval Medical University Animal Experiment Center (Shanghai, China) and Cyagen Biotechnology Co. under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Polymicrobial sepsis was induced by standardized CLP, as previously described [47]. Briefly, anesthetized subjects underwent midline laparotomy, followed by ligation of the distal cecum just below the ileocecal valve with a 4-0 silk suture. The ligated segment was then punctured once through-and-through with a 21-gauge needle to induce fecal leakage, and a small amount of fecal content was gently extruded. Subsequently, the abdominal wall and skin were closed in layers with interrupted 4-0 silk sutures. To assess therapeutic efficacy, the RBA-NPs treatment group received an intraperitoneal injection of the nanoparticles (20 mg/kg) 1 h before the CLP procedure.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were sequentially dewaxed in environmentally benign solutions I-III (10 min per solution), rehydrated through graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 80%; 5 min each) and distilled water. Following heat-induced epitope retrieval, slides were equilibrated to ambient temperature before undergoing three 5-min PBS washes (pH 7.4) under constant agitation. Specimen perimeters were delineated with a hydrophobic barrier prior to 30-min blocking with 3% BSA. Primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) were incubated overnight at 4 °C in humidity-controlled chambers. After thorough PBS rinsing, species-specific secondary antibodies were applied for 50-min incubation at room temperature under light-restricted conditions. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using DAPI (10 min, dark environment), followed by autofluorescence suppression with reagent B (5 min) and final rinsing. Coverslips were mounted with anti-fade medium and imaged under defined spectral parameters: DAPI (λex 330-380 nm/λem 420 nm), Alexa Fluor 488 (λex 465-495 nm/λem 515-555 nm) and Cy5 (λex 608-648 nm/λem 672-712 nm). All experimental conditions were validated in triplicate independent assays.

Histopathological analysis via H&E staining

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. The resulting sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin according to standard protocols, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared, and permanently coverslipped. Histological examination was subsequently performed under a bright-field microscope.

Physiological parameter monitoring

Body temperature and body weight were tracked every 24 h in a cohort of mice (n = 10). Measurements were taken in triplicate at each time point to ensure technical consistency. Survival was assessed twice daily for 7 days following the intervention, and Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted for all experimental groups (n = 10). Mortality was recorded at 12-h intervals, starting from the first day after the procedure.

Quantification of systemic inflammatory mediators and hepatorenal injury biomarkers

Serum IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β were quantified using ELISA kits (Invitrogen: 88-7064, 88-7324, and 88-7013). Samples were thawed once, centrifuged to remove particulates, and measured in duplicate within the linear range of the standard curve. Samples exceeding the upper limit of quantification were re-assayed after appropriate dilution, and final concentrations were corrected by the dilution factor. Detection ranges and assay sensitivity followed the manufacturers' datasheets. ALT, AST, and BUN were measured by standard clinical biochemistry (Leidu Life Science, China) according to the vendor's protocol.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR validation

Total RNA was isolated with RNAfast200 reagent. Genomic DNA was removed and cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa RR037A, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, which included a dedicated gDNA Eraser step. Gene-specific primers were obtained from PrimerBank and were designed following MIQE guidelines (Supplementary Table 1). Quantitative PCR was carried out on an Applied Biosystems 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR system using SYBR Premix Ex Taq chemistry (TaKaRa RR420A, Japan). The relative mRNA expression levels, normalized to GAPDH, were determined by the comparative Ct (2^(-ΔΔCt)) method, with each sample analyzed in technical triplicate.

Western blot verification

Prior to electrophoresis, protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Denatured protein samples from cellular lysates and plasma were separated on 4-12% Bis-Tris gradient gels and electrotransferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST (containing 0.1% Tween-20) and then probed overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies at the indicated dilutions: β-ACTIN (1:10,000), NLRP3 (1:1000), FL-GSDMD (1:1000), N-GSDMD (1:1000), CASPASE1 (1:1000), CL-CASPASE1 (1:1000), CL-IL-1β (1:1000), AIM2(1:1000) and NLRC4(1:1000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, and signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate. Band intensities were quantified with Image J software (NIH), normalized to β-actin, and analyzed statistically. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation was performed using a commercial nuclear extraction kit, and β-ACTIN and Histone H3 were used as cytoplasmic and nuclear markers, respectively. Detailed antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release experiment

LDH release into culture supernatants was quantified using the CytoTox 96® Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After the indicated treatments, supernatants were collected and incubated with the LDH substrate mix, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Background was subtracted using medium-only wells. Each condition was assayed in technical triplicates with ≥3 independent biological replicates.

Caspase-1 activity assay

The FAM FLICA™ caspase-1 (YVAD) assay kit (AbD Serotec, Germany) was used to detect caspase-1 activity following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, BMDMs were plated in a 6-well plate at an appropriate density and subjected to various experimental treatments for the indicated durations. After treatment, cells were harvested, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 300 μL of 1 × binding buffer containing the FITC-conjugated caspase-1 inhibitor. Following a 1-h incubation in the dark, samples were then analyzed using Flow cytometry (BD CytoFLEX, U.S.), and the resulting data were processed with FlowJo software.

ROS assay

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) in BMDMs were detected using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033, China) based on the oxidation-sensitive probe DCFH-DA. After the indicated treatments, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μM) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed 3 times with PBS to remove excess dye, and immediately subjected to fluorescence detection.

For live-cell fluorescence microscopy, images were acquired using a Nikon Ti–U with the FITC/488 filter set (Ex 465-495 nm, Em 515-555 nm). Identical exposure and gain settings were applied across all groups within each experiment. Fluorescence quantification was performed in ImageJ by background subtraction and measurement of mean fluorescence intensity with at least three independent biological replicates.

For flow cytometry, cells were collected after DCFH-DA staining and analyzed on a BD CytoFLEX (BD Biosciences) using 488 nm excitation and the FITC channel. Cells were gated by FSC to exclude debris and, where applicable, singlets were gated to exclude aggregates. ROS levels were expressed as the DCF fluorescence intensity (MFI). All experiments were performed with ≥3 independent biological replicates.

Assessment of lipid peroxidation

Following overnight culture in six-well plates at a seeding density of 5 × 104 cells per well, cells were exposed to the indicated treatments. After treatment, cells were collected and stained in the dark with 5 μM BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Invitrogen, D3861) for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, the samples were washed and subjected to flow cytometric analysis, with a minimum of 5000 events recorded per sample. To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates.

PI staining

Cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The following day, the cells were treated with the designated compounds for the indicated durations. After treatment, cells were stained with PI at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL and imaged using a Nikon Ti–U inverted microscope. For quantification, the percentage of PI-positive cells was calculated as (number of PI-positive cells)/(total number of cells) per field, where the total number of cells was determined by counting cells in the corresponding bright-field images of the same fields of view. All experiments were performed with a minimum of three independent biological replicates.

Drug affinity-responsive target stability

Following cell collection, total protein was extracted using lysis buffer. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was incubated with either DMSO (vehicle control) or RBA at 4 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the samples were digested with pronase at specified protease-to-protein ratios for 30 min. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding a protease inhibitor cocktail for 10 min, followed by the addition of 5 × SDS loading buffer and boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. Finally, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed via immunoblotting.

CETSA and TSA

For CETSA analysis, supernatants were collected from pre-treated BMDMs and lysed using modified RIPA buffer. The lysates were incubated with either dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or RBA and then subjected to a controlled thermal shift protocol with a gradual temperature increase from 45 °C to 72 °C for 6 min and a 3-min cooling period at room temperature. Subsequently, proteins were resuspended in 5 × SDS loading buffer, denatured by boiling for 10 min, and analyzed via SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting. For TSA analysis, recombinant NLRP3 and NCF1 protein was pre-incubated with DMSO or RBA prior to undergoing the same thermal treatment regimen (45-72 °C for 6 min), with identical downstream processing as described above.

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) analysis

The binding affinities of RBA to recombinant human NLRP3 and NCF1 proteins were characterized using a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). Purified target proteins were immobilized onto Series S Sensor Chips CM5 via standard amine coupling chemistry to reaching immobilization levels of approximately 5000–8000 RU. SPR assays were performed at 25 °C in running buffer (PBS–P+ containing 5% DMSO) at a flow rate of 30 μL/min. A concentration series of RBA ranging from 6.25 to 100 μM was injected over the protein and reference surfaces with an association time of 120 s and a dissociation time of 120 s. To correct for bulk refractive index variations, solvent correction was applied using DMSO standards (4.5%-5.8%). Data were double-referenced by subtracting signals from the reference flow cell and blank buffer injections. Kinetic parameters and equilibrium dissociation constants (KD) were derived by fitting the sensorgrams to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model or a steady-state affinity model using Biacore Evaluation Software.

Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

Upon attaining 70-80% confluency, cultured cells were harvested and lysed on ice for 30 min using NP-40 lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013F). The resulting lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the supernatant was incubated with Bio-RBA at 4 °C for 2 h, followed by the addition of streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 20359) for further binding. After thorough washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), proteins captured on the beads were resuspended in loading buffer, denatured at 100 °C for 10 min, and resolved via SDS-PAGE for initial validation. Gel-separated proteins were then subjected to liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) for identification, and the list of proteins enriched by Bio-RBA is provided in Supplementary Table 3. All proteomic procedures were conducted by Hangzhou PTM Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

Transmission electron microscopy

After priming with LPS, BMDMs were incubated for 3 h in the presence of ATP and either RBA or MCC950. The cells were then washed in PBS, collected, and subjected to initial fixation using 0.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 5 min. Subsequent centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min yielded a cell pellet, which was further fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde. Finally, ultrathin sections were imaged via a JEM-1400PLUS transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Scanning electron microscope

Upon LPS priming, BMDMs were exposed to ATP together with RBA-NPs for 3 h. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and harvested. Following collection, samples were fixed at 4 °C overnight using 2.5% glutaraldehyde. After thorough PBS washes, a graded ethanol series was applied for dehydration, and the medium was then exchanged twice with isoamyl acetate. Finally, the processed cells were mounted on scanning electron microscopy (SEM) stubs, sputter-coated with a thin gold layer, and imaged under a Quanta 400 SEM (FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA).

Synthesis and characterization of RBA-NPs

One milligram of mPEG-PCL (concentration: 20.0 mg/mL; Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.) was mixed with 50 μg of roburic acid (purity: 99.8%; Topscience Co., Ltd.). The mixture was then stirred at 60 °C for 3-5 min in 700 μL of an aqueous polyvinylpyrrolidone solution (concentration: 1 mg/mL; Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.). Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to dialysis, followed by centrifugation at 21,000×g for 1 h at 4 °C. After removal of the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of distilled deionized water. To observe morphology and lattice fringes, RBA-NPs were ultrasonicated, drop-casted onto copper grids, and air-dried prior to TEM/HRTEM analysis (Thermo Fisher, U.S.). Additionally, a Zetasizer Nano (UK) was employed to determine the particle size via DLS and the zeta potential in various media including PBS, water, and DMEM.

In vitro drug release studies

For the in vitro drug release study, a measured quantity of the drug-loaded nanoparticles was placed into a dialysis bag, which was then securely sealed and immersed in 20 mL of ddH2O (pH 7.4). At predetermined intervals, 1 mL aliquots of the release medium were withdrawn. The concentration of RBA in each aliquot was quantified to determine the cumulative release profile over time. RBA content in the collected samples was measured by UV–Vis spectrophotometry using an external calibration curve of RBA standards prepared in the same release medium. Release medium without RBA was used as the blank, and concentrations were calculated from the standard curve and corrected for dilution caused by medium replacement.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

Following sample processing, cells were lysed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected for analysis. MDA concentration was determined using a commercial assay kit (Beyotime, S0131S). Briefly, the MDA working reagent was prepared according to the kit instructions and mixed thoroughly with the supernatant. The mixture was incubated at 100 °C for 15 min, cooled to room temperature, and centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min. Subsequently, 200 μL of the supernatant from each sample was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader. The MDA content was calculated based on a standard curve.

Statistical analysis

Experimental results derived from ≥3 independent biological replicates are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For datasets involving multiple simultaneous hypothesis tests (e.g., multi-group/omics-style comparisons), raw p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, and FDR-adjusted q values are reported where applicable. Parametric inferences employed either one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc correction or unpaired Student's t-tests for pairwise comparisons, while nonparametric ordinal data underwent Kruskal-Wallis testing with Dunn's multiple comparisons. Fluorescence colocalization was assessed via Pearson's correlation coefficient (R-value range: −1 to +1). All analyses were executed in GraphPad Prism 10.0.3, with significance thresholded at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001 using standard asterisk demarcation.
