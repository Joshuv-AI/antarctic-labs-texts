Body mass index and physical training-related injuries in military personnel: a systematic review and meta-regression analysis
Authors: Weijuan Lan, J Shu, Y Liu, C Xie, J Sun, L Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiscrepancies exist in findings on how varying body mass index (BMI) levels impact physical training-related injuries across different studies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the relationship between BMI and physical training-related injuries both qualitatively and quantitatively.MethodsA comprehensive search of the MEDLINE, Cochrane and EMBASE databases was performed. The study selection followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. A systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted using weighted data and a random-effects model. Subgroup analyses were based on gender and military service branches. Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed through the I2 statistic, indicating the proportion of total variation in effect size estimates. Funnel plots and Egger’s test were employed to assess publication bias.Results33 studies were included in the analysis, with abnormal BMI (underweight, overweight and obesity) identified as a clear risk factor for training-related injuries (overall RR=1.21, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.27; male RR=1.16, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.23; female RR=1.05, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.09). No significant heterogeneity was observed among navy, army and air force personnel (p=0.58), although abnormal BMI remained a notable risk factor in both the army and air force. Meta-regression analysis indicated that the lowest injury risk occurred at a BMI of 23 kg/m2. Obese individuals under the age of 27 and underweight individuals over the age of 27 were at high risk. Additionally, the highest injury risk was observed during the first 5 months of military training, with a gradual decline in subsequent months, stabilising by the 12th month.ConclusionFindings suggest that abnormal BMI increases the risk of training injuries among military personnel and that this risk varies with age and the duration of training. Emphasis should be placed on risk prevention in the early stages of military training and on enhanced protection for high-risk populations.

Introduction

The global rise in overweight and obesity is a continuing concern. The World Obesity Atlas 2024,1 published by the World Obesity Federation, reported that 2.2 billion adults had a high body mass index (BMI ≥25 kg/m2) in 2020, with projections reaching nearly 3.3 billion by 2035. Excess weight is associated with an increased risk of chronic diseases and occupational disability.2 Previous research indicates that obesity contributes to higher rates of military training injuries3 and escalates healthcare costs within military settings.4

Although BMI does not directly reflect body fat percentage and central obesity may be a more critical mortality risk in individuals with a normal BMI, it remains the most prevalent method for classifying overweight and obesity due to its simplicity and low cost.5 BMI is calculated by dividing a person’s weight in kilograms by their height in metres squared (kg/m2). The WHO classifies BMI into four categories: underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2) and obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2).6

As a modifiable predictor of physical training-related injuries in military personnel, BMI has shown varying effects across different studies and injury types. For example, a meta-analysis identified an elevated BMI as a significant risk factor for medial tibial stress syndrome (p<0.001), while another study found no significant correlation between BMI and patellofemoral pain.7 Moreover, discrepancies exist in findings on how varying BMI levels impact physical training-related injuries across different studies.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to provide greater clarity on the association between BMI and training-related injuries, addressing biases found in earlier studies while examining BMI’s specific role in military personnel. The hypothesis posits that BMI categories outside the normal range significantly affect the risk of such injuries, with variations observed by age and gender. Ultimately, the findings will offer robust evidence to inform optimal BMI standards for reducing and preventing training-related injuries in military contexts.

Methods

This systematic review followed the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses and the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement8 (online supplemental appendix 1). Since the analysis is based on previous original studies, it does not raise medical ethics concerns. The review protocol was registered in the INPLASY database (Registration No: INPLASY202390040).

Search strategy and selection criteria

A comprehensive literature search was systematically conducted in the MEDLINE, Embase and Cochrane Library databases for English-language publications from 2000 onwards. The initial search, completed in April 2023 by our research team, used a combination of ‘Medical Subject Headings’ and entry terms. MeSH terms were divided into three categories according to the study’s objectives: Category 1 terms related to BMI (eg, body mass index, body mass and obesity); Category 2 terms related to military personnel (eg, military personnel, armed forces, army personnel, navy personnel and air force personnel) and Category 3 terms related to injuries (eg, wounds and injuries and trauma). Search terms within each category were connected with ‘OR’ and combined across categories using ‘AND’ (online supplemental appendix 2). Additionally, relevant references were identified through handsearching and included in the review.

The literature search was independently conducted by two reviewers, focusing on titles and/or abstracts. Studies were considered eligible if they were analytical, involved classified BMI data, reported an association between BMI and physical training-related injuries and were focused on military personnel. Additionally, eligible studies needed to present risk estimates (ORs or relative risks (RR)) with 95% CIs or provide sufficient data to calculate these estimates. Exclusions were applied to studies without full-text access, systematic reviews, conference abstracts and letters to editors. All references were managed using EndNote X9 software. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus meetings.

Data extraction and quality assessment

One reviewer extracted the data, with another reviewer verifying its accuracy and reliability. Prespecified study characteristics were collected from all included studies, covering key details such as study design, publication year, subject characteristics, study duration, number of participants and risk estimates with their 95% CIs, using the normal BMI category as the reference group.

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS).9 Two reviewers independently assessed the studies, resolving any disagreements through consensus. The NOS comprises eight items across three domains: selection, comparability and outcome. This semiquantitative tool assigns a star rating, where each item is awarded up to one star (except for the comparability domain, which can receive up to two stars). The total NOS score ranges from 0 to 9, with studies scoring fewer than four stars classified as low quality (LQ), those with 4–6 stars considered moderate quality (MQ), and those with 7–9 stars deemed high quality (HQ). Only MQ and HQ studies were included in this systematic review. Further details on the quality assessment are presented in online supplemental appendix 3.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the influence of different BMI categories on physical training-related injuries, considering the variability in baseline characteristics across studies, the normal BMI category was designated as the reference group. The most adjusted risk estimates were used, and due to the relatively low incidence of training-related injuries in each study (<5%), RRs, HRs and ORs were treated as equivalent. For each study, the RR and its 95% CI were calculated.10 These RRs and CIs were then pooled using inverse-variance weighted random-effects models. The I2 statistic was employed to assess heterogeneity among the studies, quantifying total variance in effect estimates and categorising it as low (<25%), moderate (25%–75%) or high (>75%) to indicate the degrees of heterogeneity.10

Subgroup analyses were conducted to identify potential sources of heterogeneity, with categories including gender, injury types and military services. To further explore the relationship between BMI categories and RRs, a meta-regression was performed using age and BMI category medians as independent variables, with RRs as the dependent variable. For BMI closed categories, medians were used, and for open categories, the median was calculated by adding or subtracting half the adjacent category range to the upper or lower limit, assuming BMI conformed to a normal distribution. Given slight variations in the definition of normal BMI categories across studies, the reference value was set to the median of the medians for these normal BMI categories.11

Additionally, a meta-regression incorporating the average age of participants for each study was performed to analyse how RR changed with age across different BMI states. As follow-up time may influence the occurrence rate of training-related injuries, hazard rates were calculated across all studies to assess the instantaneous risk of injuries and their association with BMI. Assuming a constant hazard rate in each study, it was calculated as the number of events within a given time interval divided by the sum of follow-up times in that interval, with the hazard rate formula h(t)=f(t)/S(t), where f(t) is the event density function and S(t) the survival function. Hazard rates in these studies were defined as the rate of training-related injuries per month.12

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the leave-one-out approach, excluding one study at a time to ensure that no single study with an extreme outcome unduly influenced the pooled results.11 Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, and Egger’s test was applied to evaluate funnel plot symmetry. All analyses were performed using Stata V.17.0, with statistical significance set at p<0.05.

Results

Search results

A total of 887 relevant articles were identified for screening based on titles and abstracts. After removing duplicate publications and excluding studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria for the systematic review, one additional full-text paper was included through a hand search. Ultimately, 33 full-text papers were included in the study, encompassing navy, army and air force categories. The flow diagram outlining the search strategy and literature selection process is presented in figure 1.

Flow diagram of search strategy and study inclusion.

Quality assessment

The study quality was evaluated using NOS, and the detailed scores of individual studies on the methodological quality assessment are provided in online supplemental appendix 3. Among the 33 studies included in this systematic review, 22 were retrospective studies, 10 were prospective studies, and 1 was a case-control study. The average methodological quality score was 6.8 points, with a range from 4 to 9 points. Of these, 11 studies were classified as MQ, while 22 were deemed HQ.

Study characteristics

The 33 eligible studies, published between 2001 and 2023, originated from various countries, including 21 from the USA, three from Finland, three from Israel, two from China and one each from Japan, Australia, the UK and Thailand. In 12 of these studies, the subjects were exclusively male military personnel, while two studies focused solely on female personnel. One study did not report the gender of participants, and the remaining studies included both male and female personnel. 18 studies examined army personnel, 3 focused on air force personnel, 2 on navy personnel, and the rest investigated mixed personnel from various military branches. The follow-up period was reported in months, and age and BMI were presented as means or medians. BMI for the occurrence of physical training-related injuries

Individuals with physical training-related injuries had significantly higher or lower BMIs compared with those without, with normal BMI used as the reference group. The meta-analysis, which included 33 studies, assessed whether BMI was a key risk factor for such injuries. The forest plot for the RR of training-related injuries across different BMI categories (normal BMI as the reference) is presented in figure 2. The pooled data indicated that both higher and lower BMIs increased the likelihood of developing training-related injuries among military personnel. The between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I2=37.68%, RR=1.21, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.27). Subgroup analyses revealed no significant heterogeneity within male and female groups (male: I2=42.51%, p=0.06; female: I2=0.00%, p=0.89), though significant heterogeneity was observed between the two groups (p<0.001). However, no significant differences were found among subgroups based on military service branches (p=0.58, figures3 4).

Association between BMI and training-related injuries

Meta-regression analyses indicated that the RR was lowest at a BMI of approximately 23 kg/m2, and the risk of training-related injuries increased as BMI rose or fell from this point (online supplemental appendix 5). After adjusting for BMI categories, the meta-regression model suggested that for individuals over the age of 27, those who were obese or overweight had a lower risk of training-related injuries than those who were younger, while underweight individuals experienced an increased risk. Notably, obese individuals had a significantly higher risk of injuries before age 27 compared with those in the overweight and underweight groups (online supplemental appendix 6).

While the baseline follow-up for each study began at enlistment, variations in follow-up time across studies may have influenced the RR outcomes. To address this, the incidence of training-related injuries was roughly converted into a hazard rate. After excluding one study that did not meet the criteria, 32 studies were included in the final calculation. The meta-regression model revealed that the hazard rate decreased progressively as follow-up time extended, with the highest hazard rate occurring within the first 5 months, followed by a plateau after 12 months (figure 5).

Scatterplot and meta-regression analysing the hazard rate of training-related injuries in relation to follow-up time.

Sensitivity analyses revealed no significant differences in the pooled RRs when excluding individual studies, with all p values remaining below 0.01 and all RR values consistently greater than 1 (online supplemental appendix 7). A certain degree of publication bias was observed, as indicated by the funnel plot and confirmed by Egger’s test (p<0.05). To determine whether this bias influenced the previous results, the analyses were repeated, excluding six studies with large SEs. The subsequent analyses produced similar summary estimates, confirming that the strength of the evidence remained unchanged (online supplemental appendix 8).

Discussion

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to examine the qualitative and quantitative relationship between BMI and physical training-related injuries. Our findings align with those of Packnett et al,13 who demonstrated that US Army personnel with a BMI below 17 kg/m2 or above 34 kg/m2 faced a higher likelihood of medical discharge within their first year of service. Similarly, our results are consistent with the work of Jones et al,14 which suggested that males at both the lower and higher extremes of BMI were at a greater risk of injury compared with those in the middle range. Specifically, our analysis indicates that individuals classified as overweight or obese were more prone to training-related injuries than those of normal weight. One explanation for the increased risk associated with higher BMI involves the role of adipose tissue in systemic metabolism. Adipokines produced by adipose tissue may contribute to cartilage degeneration,15 and the additional load imposed by excess body weight can accelerate wear on weight-bearing joints, further promoting articular cartilage damage.16 Moreover, overweight and obesity are often linked to decreased flexibility and agility, both of which have been identified as risk factors for training-related injuries.6 17 In contrast, Miller et al18 found that enhanced agility improves intramuscular coordination, thereby reducing the risk of injury or reinjury in military personnel. Underweight individuals also presented an elevated risk of training-related injuries, a risk that became more pronounced with age. This may be due to lower lean muscle mass, which provides less protection for bones and joints during physical activity. Since lean muscle mass is positively correlated with bone mineral density, muscle contractions stimulate bone remodelling, further highlighting the protective role of muscle tissue.19

Our analysis shows the lowest relative risk for training-related injuries at a BMI of approximately 23 kg/m2, suggesting that this could serve as a target for BMI interventions aimed at reducing injury risk in military personnel. However, it is important to note that BMI can be an unreliable predictor of body fat, particularly in populations such as athletes and military personnel. Previous research has suggested that abdominal circumference may be a more accurate measure than BMI for estimating musculoskeletal injury risk in these groups.20

Subgroup analyses revealed no significant heterogeneity within the male and female groups individually, yet substantial heterogeneity was observed between the two genders. Previous studies have consistently shown that female military personnel are more prone to training-related injuries than their male counterparts.21 Our meta-analysis further demonstrated that BMI levels impact the occurrence of such injuries differently in males and females. Dada et al22 noted that male soldiers generally outperform females in muscular and cardiorespiratory endurance, with lower physical fitness being a critical factor contributing to training-related injuries.23 These findings emphasise the need for gender-specific training programmes that consider physiological differences.

For the service variable, no significant heterogeneity was detected either among or within service groups. Abnormal BMI was identified as a significant risk factor for training-related injuries in the army and air force but not in the navy. This contradicts previous claims that BMI is associated with naval training-related injuries.24 The discrepancy may be due to the limited number of naval studies included in the meta-analysis (only two), potentially introducing bias. Future research focusing on a larger cohort of naval personnel will be required to address this limitation.

Our study also accounted for the effect of time on training-related injuries, showing a clear correlation between hazard rates and the duration of military training. The highest injury rates were observed during the first 5 months of service, with a gradual decline thereafter, stabilising by the 12th month. This trend aligns with prior research by Herbert and Gabriel25 and Nelson et al,26 who also identified peak injury incidence in the early months of military service, particularly in relation to conditions like heat illness, despite some observational studies reporting varying peak injury periods.27

The financial burden of training-related injuries, both in terms of direct medical costs and lost workdays, is significant for military personnel.28 Our meta-analysis contributes to this field by providing more precise effect size estimates, resulting in findings that differ both qualitatively and quantitatively from previous studies. This research, along with BCM and other anthropometric studies, offers critical insights for authorities aiming to prevent training-related injuries and reduce the associated human and economic costs within the military.

Limitations

Several limitations are present in this study. As the meta-analyses were based on previous research, obtaining additional data for a more granular analysis was not possible. For example, the lack of precise data hindered a detailed investigation of the relationship between specific types of training injuries and BMI. Additionally, the small number of navy and air force studies included in the subgroup analysis may have skewed the results or failed to capture the full scope of the situation. The statistical methods used to equate RR, HR and OR across hypothetical studies also introduced some constraints. The hazard function, which models the probability of training-related injuries over time, presented further limitations. With varying follow-up durations across studies, the assumption was made that injury incidences were evenly distributed throughout the follow-up period. However, the risk of injury is likely higher in the early stages of military training compared with later periods. As such, the hazard function curve in this analysis reflects trends in hazard rate changes over time, rather than precise hazard rate values. Furthermore, differing ‘doses of exposure’ to physical training—some military groups undergo more intensive or prolonged physical training, particularly during basic training—means assuming a uniform hazard rate may overestimate the impact of BMI in groups exposed to higher levels of physical activity compared with those with less exposure. Moreover, the actual BMI values within the three defined BMI categories were not reported, leading to reliance on median estimates for analysis. Future research with larger sample sizes and more accurate data will be necessary to address these challenges, and caution is advised when interpreting the current findings.

Conclusion

Abnormal BMI, as a risk factor for training-related injuries, varies between males and females, underscoring the need for gender-specific training strategies. A BMI of 23 kg/m2 is associated with the lowest risk of injury, making it a useful reference for BMI interventions in military personnel. Notably, after the age of 27, underweight service members face a higher risk of injury compared with their overweight and obese counterparts, emphasising the need for enhanced protection for this group. Furthermore, the risk of training-related injuries peaks during the first 5 months of service, gradually declining and stabilising after the 12th month, highlighting the importance of early intervention in military training. These insights can inform policy development and the implementation of targeted interventions to promote the health and operational effectiveness of military personnel.
