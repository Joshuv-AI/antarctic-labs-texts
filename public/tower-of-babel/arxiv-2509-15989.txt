Model-free algorithms for fast node clustering in SBM type graphs and application to social role inference in animals
Bertrand Cloez 1 , Adrien Cotil 2 , Jean-Baptiste Menassol 3 , Nicolas Verzelen 1

Abstract
We propose a novel family of model-free algorithms for node clustering and parameter inference in graphs generated from the Stochastic Block Model (SBM), a fundamental framework in community detection. Drawing inspiration from the Lloyd algorithm for the k k -means problem, our approach extends to SBMs with general edge weight distributions. We establish the consistency of our estimator under a natural identifiability condition. Through extensive numerical experiments, we benchmark our methods against state-of-the-art techniques, demonstrating significantly faster computation times with the lower order of estimation error. Finally, we validate the practical relevance of our algorithms by applying them to empirical network data from behavioral ecology.

Introduction

Graphs have become extremely useful for representing a wide variety of systems in different contexts: biological, social, information… A basic attempt to study them may consist in partitioning the vertices of a graph into clusters that are more densely connected; that is commonly called
community detection
or
graph clustering
; see for instance
[
20
,
1
]
. Community detection and clustering are central problems in machine learning and data science. In particular, the
stochastic block model
(SBM)
[
34
,
25
]
has been widely used as a canonical model for community detection and as a building block for clustering with more structural assumptions. In its most general form, the SBM corresponds to a randomly weighted graph model where each node has an unobserved label and the probability of observing a given edge between two nodes depends only on the labels of the nodes under consideration. In its simplest form, we recover the Erdös-Renyi random graph model
[
18
]
, but many more complex variants have been proposed. Among many variants, they may include covariates
[
30
]
, continuous-valued labels
[
32
]
, mixed-membership
[
4
]
, multi-layer networks
[
22
,
39
]
or time-varying parameters
[
31
]
.

In all these settings, neither the labels are observed nor the conditional probability of observing an edge given the labels is known. One main statistical question posed by this model is then to recover the labels of the nodes and the parameters of the model from an observation of this graph.
The parameter inference for this model has been studied in
[
9
]
where it is proved that the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) is consistent and in
[
7
]
where it is proved that it is asymptotically normal. However, computing the MLE includes a difficult optimization problem. To overcome this problem, variational EM (VEM) methods
[
8
]
are often used
[
10
]
. The associated estimator remains consistent
[
9
]
and asymptotically normal
[
44
]
. This powerful method is implemented in
[
14
]
. In the Bayesian context, several MCMC algorithms have been introduced
[
33
,
35
,
15
]
, some of which are based on the Variational Bayes
[
40
]
,
i.e.
the Bayesian equivalent of VEM. Variational EM algorithms have also been developed for parameter inference in more complex models, as in
[
38
]
, where a variant of SBM with unobserved data is studied, or in
[
11
]
, where the observed graph is assumed to be organized in several layers.

If one focuses on the label recovery problem (or equivalently the node clustering), there has been a long line of research (see in particular
[
1
]
) towards developing optimal and polynomial-time algorithms, which includes semi-definite programming
[
19
]
algorithm, spectral clustering methods
[
13
,
36
,
36
,
6
]
possibly combined with iterative correction procedures
[
42
,
43
,
21
]
, or other iterative approaches
[
2
]
.

Given the importance of graph clustering problems, there is still a need for faster and more efficient algorithms. Motivated by the Lloyd algorithm for the
k
-means problem
[
27
,
17
,
28
,
3
]
, we introduce in this work a new simple and iterative algorithm for the label recovery and the parameter inference for the SBM problem for directed graphs. This algorithm is model-free in the sense that the functions to be minimized do not depend on the distribution of the edges. It is much faster than SDP or VEM type algorithms and empirically performs favorably in comparison the state of the art. Last but not least, we obtain consistency results for these algorithms. Therefore, we believe that this algorithm could be used as a building block in more complicated SBM models for fast and efficient label recovery.

Outline
. In Section

k

2.3
. In Section
3
, we compare the numerical performance of this algorithm with that of other commonly used algorithms, in terms of computation time and proportion of labels recovered, on simulated data. An application to a real dataset is provided in Section
4
.

2
Lloyd-type algorithms for the SBM problem

In this section, we introduce our new algorithm for the labels recovery from a SBM. In Section
2.1
, we show that the likelihood maximization problem is closed to a
k
-means problem in the case of the Bernoulli SBM. We also propose a first algorithm directly inspired by the Lloyd algorithm. In Section
2.3
, we propose the main algorithm of this article which is adapted to a wider class of SBMs, and we give consistency results on the associated estimator. The proof of these results is given in Appendix
A
.

2.1
Bernoulli SBM likelihood rewritten as a
k-
means problem

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}:=\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
be the number of nodes and
K\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
the number of labels. For all
i\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]:=\{1,\dots,N\}
, let
z_{i}\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]
be the label of node
i
and let
z=(z_{i})_{i\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]}
be the vector of labels. Let
P=(P_{pq})_{p,q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
be the probability matrix. We assume that the SBM
X=(X_{ij})_{i,j\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]}
has
i.i.d.
coordinates conditionally distributed to the labels as follows

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{ij}=1\right)=P_{z_{i}z_{j}},\quad\mathbb{P}\left(X_{ij}=0\right)=1-P_{z_{i}z_{j}}.

It follows that the log-likelihood of this model is given by

\ell\left(X\middle|z,P\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left(X_{ij}\log(P_{z_iz_j})+(1-X_{ij})\log(1-P_{z_iz_j})\right),

(1)

where we used the convention
0\times\log(0)=0
. Note that, unlike the VEM framework, the vector of labels is considered as a parameter. Let us give an alternative form for (
1
). For all
z\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]^{N}
, let
I_{p}(z):=\{i\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]\ |\ z_{i}=p\}
be the class of nodes with label
p\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]
. Let
N_{p}(z):=\text{card}(I_{p}(z))
be the number of nodes with label
p
. Let
\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)
,
\widehat{\nu}_{pj}(z)
and
\widehat{P}_{pq}(z)
be respectively the average number of edges from node
i
to nodes of label
q
, from nodes of label
p
to node
j
and from nodes of label
p
to nodes of label
q
. These quantities are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)=\frac{1}{N_{q}(z)}\sum_{j\in I_{q}(z)}X_{ij}\quad\text{if}\ N_{q}(z)\neq 0,\qquad\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)=0\quad\text{otherwise},

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}_{pj}(z)=\frac{1}{N_{p}(z)}\sum_{i\in I_{p}(z)}X_{ij}\quad\text{if}\ N_{p}(z)\neq 0,\qquad\widehat{\nu}_{pj}(z)=0\quad\text{otherwise},

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{pq}(z)=\frac{1}{N_{p}(z)N_{q}(z)}\sum_{i\in I_{p}(z)}\sum_{j\in I_{q}(z)}X_{ij}\quad\text{if}\ N_{p}(z)N_{q}(z)\neq 0,\qquad\widehat{P}_{pq}(z)=0\quad\text{otherwise}.

Note that if
N_{p}(z)N_{q}(z)\neq 0
, we have

\widehat{P}_{pq}(z)=\frac{1}{N_{p}(z)}\sum_{i\in I_{p}(z)}\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)=\frac{1}{N_{q}(z)}\sum_{j\in I_{q}(z)}\widehat{\nu}_{pj}(z).

(2)

With this notation, the log-likelihood (
1
) can be written as

\ell\left(X\middle|z,P\right)=\sum_{p=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in I_{p}(z)}S_{ip}(z,P),

(3)

where

S_{ip}(z,P)=\overrightarrow{S}\!_{ip}(z,P):=\sum_{q=1}^{K}N_{q}(z)\left(\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)\log(P_{pq})+(1-\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z))\log(1-P_{pq})\right),

or

S_{ip}(z,P)=\overleftarrow{S}\!_{ip}(z,P):=\sum_{q=1}^{K}N_{q}(z)\left(\widehat{\nu}_{qi}(z)\log(P_{qp})+(1-\widehat{\nu}_{qi}(z))\log(1-P_{qp})\right),

or else

S_{ip}(z,P)=\overline{S}_{ip}(z,P):=\frac{\overrightarrow{S}\!_{ip}(z,P)+\overleftarrow{S}\!_{ip}(z,P)}{2}.

To maximize the likelihood
\ell\left(X\middle|z,P\right)
, we therefore want to maximize each term of the sum (
3
). However, even though the three functions
\overrightarrow{S}
,
\overleftarrow{S}
, and
\overline{S}
lead to the same expression of
\ell\left(X\middle|z,P\right)
, their use in an optimization scheme leads to different numerical performances. For instance, the choice
S=\overrightarrow{S}
is more likely to fall into local minima since they only considers out and in edges respectively. It is therefore unable to identify classes if the
P
matrix has equal rows. The problem is similar for
S=\overleftarrow{S}
. The choice
S=\overline{S}
counteracts these problems and produces great numerical results (see Section
3
). We will therefore use
\overline{S}
as
S
function.

2.2
The Lloyd-MLE-SBM algorithm

Maximizing (
3
) is closed to a
k
-means problem. Recall that, given
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]}
a family of vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
K\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
, the
k
-means problem is to find a partition
(I_{1},\dots,I_{K})
of
\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]
minimizing the quantity:

\sum_{p=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in I_{p}}\norm{x_i-\overline{x}_p}_{2},

(4)

where for all
p\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right],\ \overline{x}_{p}=\sum_{i\in I_{p}}x_{i}/N_{p}
and
N_{p}=\text{card}(I_{p})
. We note that setting
I_{p}=\{i\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]\ |\ z_{i}=p\}
and
S_{ip}(z)=-\norm{x_i-\overline{x}_p(z)}_{2}
where
\overline{x}_{p}=\sum_{i\in I_{p}(z)}x_{i}/N_{p}(z)
, Equation (
4
) can be written as (
3
). Even if this problem is an NP-hard problem
[
29
]
as soon as
K\geq 2
, the Lloyd algorithm is an efficient iterative method that computes a local minimum. Inspired by this algorithm, we introduce Algorithm
1
, called hereafter Lloyd-MLE-SBM algorithm, for the SBM problem.

Algorithm 1

The Lloyd-MLE-SBM algorithm

1:

z^{(0)}\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]^{N}

2:

while

z^{(n)}\neq z^{(n-1)}
up to a permutation
do

3:

\widehat{P}^{(n)}\leftarrow\widehat{P}(z^{(n)})

4:

for

i\in\left[\!\left[1,n\right]\!\right]

do

5:

\ z^{(n+1)}_{i}\leftarrow\argmax_{p\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]}\overline{S}_{ip}(z^{(n)},\widehat{P}^{(n)})

6:

end

for

7:

end

while

If Algorithm
1
converges to some
(\widehat{z},\widehat{P})
then it satisfies the following Nash equilibrium type property: for every
z\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]^{N}
such that there exists
i
such that
\widehat{z}_{j}=z_{j}
for all
j\neq i
, we have

\ell(X|\widehat{z},\widehat{P})\,\geq\,\ell(X|z,\widehat{P}).

Remark 2.1
.

Let us quickly draw a parallel between Algorithm
1
and game theory. The quantity
\overline{S}_{ip}(z^{(n)},\widehat{P}^{(n)})
corresponds to the payoff for an individual
i
if he chooses to belong to class
z_{i}
and the others are in class
z
. The iterations of Algorithm
1
then correspond to a repeated game in which each individual unilaterally chooses to change class according to the information it has: the classes of the other individuals at the previous instant. However, we want to optimize the sum of the payoffs, which in game theory is known as the social optimum. Unfortunately, it is well known in game theory that individual optimization without dialogue generally does not converge to the social optimum, as illustrated by the prisoner’s dilemma.

2.3
The Lloyd-SBM algorithm

Instead of
\overleftarrow{S}\!_{ip}
,
\overrightarrow{S}\!_{ip}
or,
\overline{S}_{ip}
several
choices are possible for Algorithm
1
. Furthermore, to generalize Algorithm
1
to variants of SBM where
X_{ij}
belongs to more general sets, we need to adapt the definition of
S_{ip}
. To that end, we observe that the function
\overline{S}\!_{ip}
reaches its maximum when
(\widehat{P}(z)_{pq})_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}=(\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z))_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}
and
(\widehat{P}(z)_{qp})_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}=(\widehat{\nu}_{qi}(z))_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}
. Consequently, maximizing
\overline{S}\!_{ip}(z)
is heuristically similar to both minimizing the distance between vectors
(\widehat{P}_{pq}(z))_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]})
and
(\widehat{\mu}(z)_{iq})_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}
and the one between
(\widehat{P}_{qp}(z))_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]})
and
(\widehat{\nu}(z)_{qi})_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}
. Based on this observation, we introduce the main algorithm of this article below, called herefater Lloyd-SBM.

Let
\widehat{\pi}_{i}(z)=(\widehat{\pi}_{iq}(z))_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,2K\right]\!\right]}\in\mathbb{R}^{2K}
be defined by
\widehat{\pi}_{iq}(z)=\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)
if
q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]
and
\widehat{\pi}_{iq}(z)=\widehat{\nu}_{(q-K)i}(z)
if
q\in\left[\!\left[K+1,2K\right]\!\right]
. Let
\widehat{\Pi}_{p}(z)=(\widehat{\Pi}_{pq}(z))_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,2K\right]\!\right]}\in\mathbb{R}^{2K}
be defined by
\widehat{\Pi}_{pq}(z)=\widehat{P}_{pq}(z)
if
q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]
and
\widehat{\Pi}_{pq}(z)=\widehat{P}_{(q-K)p}(z)
if
q\in\left[\!\left[K+1,2K\right]\!\right]
. Here,
\widehat{\mu},\widehat{\nu}
and
\widehat{P}
keep the same definition as in Section
2.1
. Let finally
d
be a distance on
\mathbb{R}^{2K}
. This leads to a heuristic variant of Algorithm
1
described in Algorithm
2
, which performs numerically well; see Section
3
for details.

Algorithm 2

The Lloyd-SBM algorithm

1:

z^{(0)}\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]^{N}
, a distance
d

2:

while

z^{(n)}\neq z^{(n-1)}
up to a permutation
do

3:

\widehat{\pi}^{(n)}\leftarrow\widehat{\pi}\left(z^{(n)}\right)
and
\widehat{\Pi}^{(n)}\leftarrow\widehat{\Pi}\left(z^{(n)}\right)

4:

for

i\in\left[\!\left[1,n\right]\!\right]

do

5:

\ z^{(n+1)}_{i}\leftarrow\argmin_{p\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]}

d\left(\widehat{\pi}_{i}^{(n)},\widehat{\Pi}_{p}^{(n)}\right)

6:

end

for

7:

end

while

2.4
Consistency of the related estimator

The proof of the consistency of the estimator provided by Algorithm
2
is supported by the fact that two nodes with the same label will, in expectation, develop the same number of edges with nodes of another given label. More precisely,
(\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z^{\star}))_{i\in I_{p}(z^{\star})}
forms a sequence of
i.i.d
random variables. Moreover, by the law of large numbers,
\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)
is close to its expectation for a large
N
. It follows that if
z=z^{\star}
and
z_{i}=z_{j}
then
\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)
is close to
\widehat{\mu}_{jq}(z)
and therefore to
\widehat{P}_{z_{i}q}(z)
by (
2
). We conclude that heuristically,
z^{*}
is the vector of labels that minimizes the distance between
\widehat{\mu}_{iq}(z)
and their averages. This leads to the introduction of the estimator
\widehat{z}^{N}=\argmin\widehat{L}_{N}
where

\widehat{L}_{N}:z\mapsto\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{K}\sum_{i\in I_{p}(z)}d\left(\widehat{\pi}_{i}(z),\widehat{\Pi}_{p}(z)\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d\left(\widehat{\pi}_{i}(z),\widehat{\Pi}_{z_{i}}(z)\right).

(5)

Algorithm
2
is a minimization scheme of
\widehat{L}_{N}
. In the following, we will assume that
z^{\star}_{i}
is the true label of node
i
. We will also assume that for all
i,j\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]
,
X_{ij}
is an integrable
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variable such that
(X_{ij})_{i,j\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]}
are independent given
z
and
(X_{ij})_{i\in I_{p}(z^{\star}),j\in I_{q}(z^{\star})}
are identically distributed. Let
(P^{\star}_{pq})_{p,q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}
be the expectations of these random variables,
i.e.

\forall i\in I_{p}(z^{\star}),\ j\in I_{q}(z^{\star}),\quad P^{\star}_{pq}=\mathbb{E}(X_{ij}).

More precisely, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we consider a sequence of SBM
X=(X^{N})_{N\geq 1}
with parameter
z^{\star}=(z^{\star,N})_{N\geq 1}
and a fixed matrix
(P^{\star}_{pq})_{p,q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}
. To prove consistency, we consider an identifiable framework and then consider the following assumption, coming from
[
9
, Assumption 1]
.

Assumption 1
.

For all
p_{1},p_{2}\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]
such that
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
, there exists
q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]
such that
P^{\star}_{p_{1}q}\neq P^{\star}_{p_{2}q}
or
P^{\star}_{qp_{1}}\neq P^{\star}_{qp_{2}}
, which can be rewritten as

\delta^{\star}:=\min_{p_{1}\neq p_{2}}\max_{q}\left(\absolutevalue{P^\star_{p_1q}-P^\star_{p_2q}}+\absolutevalue{P^\star_{qp_1}-P^\star_{qp_2}}\right)>0.

(6)

Assumption
1
is a minimal identifiability condition. If this condition is not fulfilled, the label vector
z^{\star}
cannot be estimated. Furthermore, as in
[
9
, Assumption 4]
, we will assume that no class is asymptotically drained.

Assumption 2
.

There is
\gamma\in(0,1/K]
such that for all
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
and
N
large enough,

z^{\star,N}\in\mathcal{Z}^{N}_{\gamma^{\prime}}:=\left\{z\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]^{N}\ \middle|\ \forall p\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right],N_{p}(z^{\star})/N\geq\gamma^{\prime}\right\}.

In the following, we introduce
\mathcal{Z}_{\gamma}
the set of all sequences
z=(z^{N})_{N\geq 1}
which satisfy Assumption
2
for some
\gamma>0
.

We are not in the classical framework of proving the consistency of estimators, since both the parameter of interest
z^{\star}
and the state space
\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]^{N}
depend on
N
. Moreover the label identification is not unique since we can do a permutation of the classes. To overcome these problems of definition, caused in part by the growing dimension of space, we will consider the function

\Delta=\Delta_{N}:(z,z^{\prime})\mapsto\frac{K}{N^{2}(K-1)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}_{z_{i}=z_{j}}\mathbb{1}_{z^{\prime}_{i}\neq z^{\prime}_{j}}

(7)

Number
\Delta(z,z^{\prime})
counts how many pair of nodes that belong to the same class for
z
that are not in the same class for
z^{\prime}
. It is not a pseudo-metric since
\Delta(z,z^{\prime})=0
does not imply
z=z^{\prime}
up to a permutation. However, if
\Delta(z,z^{\prime})=0
then there exists
\tau:\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]\to\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]
which is not necessarily a permutation, such that
z^{\prime}_{i}=\tau(z_{i})
. But, if further
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{N}_{\gamma}
then
\tau
is necessarily a permutation. Hereafter, we will say that our estimator
\widehat{z}
is strongly consistent if
\Delta(\widehat{z}^{N})=\Delta(z^{\star,N},\widehat{z}^{N})
converges almost surly to
0
when
N
goes to infinity.

Our consistency results is then provided by the following theorem proved in the particular case where
X_{ij}\in[0,1]
and
d
is the
\ell^{1}
-norm as a proof of concept. This is not a strong restriction since in finite dimension, all distances induced from norms are bounded by this norm.

Theorem 2.2

(Strong consistency of
\widehat{z}
)
.

Suppose
X_{ij}\in[0,1]
,
d
is the
\ell^{1}
-norm, and that Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
hold. Let
\widehat{z}^{N}\in\argmin\widehat{L}_{N}
and suppose further that there is
\gamma_{0}\in(0,1/K]
such that
\widehat{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\gamma_{0}}
and
\delta_{0}>0
such that for
N
large enough,

\min_{p_{1}\neq p_{2}}\max_{q}\left|\overline{P}_{p_{1}q}(\widehat{z}^{N})-\overline{P}_{p_{2}q}(\widehat{z}^{N})\right|+\left|\overline{P}_{qp_{1}}(\widehat{z}^{N})-\overline{P}_{qp_{2}}(\widehat{z}^{N})\right|\geq\delta_{0},

(8)

where
\overline{P}_{pq}(z):=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{P}_{pq}(z)\right]
. Then
\Delta(\widehat{z}^{N})
and
\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{\gamma_{0}}}\norm{\hP(z)-\bP(z)}
converge almost surely to
0
. More precisely, for any
r<1/3
and
N
large enough, there exists
C>0
such that
\Delta(\widehat{z}^{N})\leq CN^{-r}
with probability at least
1-e^{-N}
.

The proof is detailed in Appendix
A
. However, note that our algorithm may not lead to the global minimum of
\widehat{L}_{N}
in
\mathcal{Z}^{N}_{\gamma_{0}}
or a minimum verifying (
8
). However, Theorem
2.2
ensures that, if

\forall p\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right],\quad\frac{N_{p}(\widehat{z})}{N}\gg 0,\quad\text{ and }\quad\forall p_{1},p_{2}\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right],\quad\max_{q\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}|\widehat{P}_{p_{1}q}(z)-\widehat{P}_{p_{2}q}(z)|+|\widehat{P}_{qp_{1}}(z)-\widehat{P}_{qp_{1}}(z)|\gg 0,

then we may be confident in our estimation. Note also that Assumption (
8
), which states that our estimator verifies the identifiability assumption we state for the parameter to be estimated, is quite mandatory to prove consistence since
\widehat{L}_{N}
may admit others minimizer; see Remark
A.1
.

3
Numerical Study

This section summarizes numerical experiments to evaluate the algorithmic performance of algorithms presented in Section
2
. A more complete analysis is presented in Appendices
C
and
D
.

In Section
3.1
below, we present the general protocol we use to compare these algorithms. In Section
3.2.1
, we compare variants of Algorithm
2
for different choices of distance
d
. Our study reveals that the choice
d(x,y)=\norm{x-y}_{1}
leads to slightly better performance than the others, especially when initialized with spectral clustering. In Section
3.2.2
, we compare Algorithm
2
with three other commonly used inference methods: spectral clustering, gradient descent and variational EM, which are presented in Appendix
B
.

3.1
General experimental protocol

We set the number of clusters at
K=3
and we consider two forms of probability matrices:

P_{sym}(a,b)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&b&b\\
b&a&b\\
b&b&a\end{array}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad P_{asym}(a,b)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&b&b^{\prime}\\
b^{\prime}&a&b\\
b&b^{\prime}&a\end{array}\right),

where
a,b\in[0,1]
and
b^{\prime}=b+(a-b)/a
is chosen to satisfy the following symmetry-weighted equality
(a-b)/a=(b^{\prime}-a)/(1-a)
. These two matrices satisfy Assumption
1
if and only if
a\neq b
. It follows that the smaller
\absolutevalue{a-b}
is, the less identifiable the classes of nodes are. If the parameter
a
is fixed, the identifiability of the model then depends on

5
Discussion

The aim of this work was to study the performance of a new label inference method for nodes clustering in graphs. This method is based on a rewriting of the likelihood of the model, which allows us to map the problem of label inference in an SBM to that of
k
-means type problem. Based on this mapping, we propose a new Lloyd-type heuristic algorithm. This algorithm turns out to be much faster than the algorithms commonly used in the literature for the ranges of parameter values we tested. It also proves to be consistent whatever the distribution of edge weights conditional on node labels. This is a particularly important property for the study of real datasets, since in most cases it is not possible to know this distribution explicitly. It should also be noted that it is certainly possible to obtain more detailed results on the consistency of the estimator and the local convergence of the algorithm. This will be the subject of future work.

Let us also note that our method is based on how
\widehat{\mu}_{ip},\widehat{\nu}_{ip}
remains constant over all nodes
i
in the same class
p
. We choose the mean for these quantities and our algorithm will be particularly relevant when the means of
X_{ij}
are different over all the classes (see Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
). We can nevertheless choose any other functional and do the same type of optimization. We are thinking in particular of taking other
M
-estimators, such as the maximum likelihood associated with the SBM model variant.

Finally, this algorithm is studied in a simple version that does not include automatic determination of the number of classes. Merge and split techniques (based on a ICL type criterion adapted to estimator
\widehat{z}
)
[
14
]
and a variant adapted to degree-corrected SBM
[
24
]
can also be considered.

Appendix B
Description of clustering algorithms used for comparison

Spectral clustering type methods :
The general idea of the spectral clustering for the SBM is that if the adjacency matrix is closed to a block matrix then its eigenvectors are closed to block vectors. The algorithm we use is the one described in
[
16
]
with the regularization introduced in
[
5
]
. Note that spectral clustering is only possible if the adjacency matrix is symmetric (i.e. if the corresponding graph is undirected). We will therefore apply spectral clustering by replacing the notion of eigenvector by that of singular vector, as is done in
[
16
]
. In the following, this method will be reffered as the "Spectral" method. For the sake of completeness, the algorithm we implemented is described by Algorithm
3
. We define
\lambda_{reg}\in[0,1]
the regularization coefficient used in the algorithm. In the following,
\mathbb{1}_{N\times N}
will denote the
N\times N
-matrix whose coefficients are all
1
.

Algorithm 3

The regularized Spectral algorithm

1:

Y\leftarrow(X^{\prime})^{t}(X^{\prime})
where
X^{\prime}\leftarrow X+\lambda_{reg}\times\left(\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}/N^{2}\right)\times\mathbb{1}_{N\times N}
.

2:

L\leftarrow(D)^{-1/2}Y(D)^{-1/2}
where
D\leftarrow\mathrm{diag}\left(\left(\sum_{j}Y_{ij}\right)_{i\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]}\right)
.

3:

U\leftarrow\left(u_{1}\ \dots\ u_{K}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
where
u_{p}
is the eigenvector of
L
corresponding to its
p
-th largest eigenvalue.

4:

Runs k-means with
K
clusters on the rows of
U
, seen as
N
vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{K}
.

Lloyd type methods :
These methods are generally described by Algorithm
2
. Each method is defined by the
d
metric used. This metric may be the maximum likelihood metric (corresponding to Algorithm
1
), in which case it is referred to as the "LloydMLE" method. We will also consider the case where
d
correspond to the distance related to the
\ell_{1}
-norm. This method will be refer to as "LloydL1". We have also considered other metrics similar to the
\ell_{1}
-norm (
\ell_{2}
-norm and Huber loss for different parameter values). For each of these methods, we can either initialize it with a noisy version of the true labeling (the one obtained by simulation) or with the one obtained by spectral clustering. If the spectral clustering is used for the initialization, we will refer those methods as "Spectral+LloydMLE", "Spectral+LloydL1".

Gradient descent type methods :
This type of method involves maximizing the likelihood locally with respect to each individual’s class. Based on the work of Karrer and Newman
[
24
]
, it was used in
[
37
]
as an alternative to the EM algorithm in order to compare it with the performance of an exact recovery algorithm. We call this method "gradient descent" since the principle of locally optimizing an objective function to find the global optimum is at the heart of standard gradient descent algorithms. Defining
\hat{l}(z)
as

\hat{l}(z):=l\left(X\middle|z,\hat{P}(z)\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\left(X_{ij}\log(\Hat{P}_{z_iz_j}(z))+(1-X_{ij})\log(1-\Hat{P}_{z_iz_j}(z))\right),

for a given label
\hat{z}^{(n)}
and for each node
i
, the algorithm calculates the value of the class
\hat{z}_{i}^{(n+1)}
that increases
\hat{l}
the most, leaving the labels of the other nodes invariant. After updating all the labels of all the nodes in this way, we repeat the update until the new label is identical to the previous one, up to a permutation on the labels. Defining
z[i,p]_{j}=z_{j}
if
j\neq i
and
z[i,p]_{j}=p
otherwise, the algorithm can be summarized as follows:

Algorithm 4

The Gradient Descent algorithm

1:

z^{(0)}\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]^{N}

2:

while

z^{(n)}\neq z^{(n-1)}
up to a permutation
do

3:

for

i\in\left[\!\left[1,n\right]\!\right]

do

4:

\ z^{(n+1)}_{i}\leftarrow\argmax_{p\in\left[\!\left[1,N\right]\!\right]}

\hat{l}\left(z^{(n)}[i,p]\right)

5:

end

for

6:

end

while

As for Lloyd-type algorithms, we can initialize this algorithm with a noisy version of the real labeling, referred to as the "GD" method, or with the result of the spectral clustering estimation, called the "Spectral+GD" method.

Variational EM type methods :
This is probably the most widely used method in the literature. Its principle is described in
[
30
]
. A fundamental difference with the previous algorithms is that this algorithm does not aim to estimate the labels of each node. The underlying model actually assumes that the labels are unobserved data, independently drawn according to a distribution
\alpha=(\alpha_{p})_{p\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}
on
\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]
. The idea of the algorithm is therefore first and foremost to estimate
\alpha
and the probability matrix
P
. In particular, the VEM algorithm computes an estimate of the label distribution
L\left(z\middle|X,\alpha,P\right)
conditional on the adjacency matrix and other parameters, in the form of a distribution
\rho
given by

\rho(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{iz_{i}}.

Computing
\hat{z}_{i}=\argmax_{p\in\left[\!\left[1,K\right]\!\right]}\,\rho_{ip}
, one can have an estimate of the label of each node. This method will called "VEM" if the initialization is random and as "Spectral+VEM" if the initialization is given by the spectral clustering method.
