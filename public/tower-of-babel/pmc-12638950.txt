Alternative social and reproductive niches linked to intra-sexual color variation in a facultatively protogynous North American annual killifish, Millerichthys robustus
Authors: Omar Domínguez-Castanedo, Martin Reichard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntra-sexual variation in coloration is often linked to comprehensive differences in reproductive behavior and life histories. Both males and females can express intrapopulation variation in coloration, and facultative hermaphrodism adds further complexity to the social and reproductive system. In replicated experimental tanks, we studied social interactions and reproductive behavior in males, females and sex-changing (transitional) intermediate individuals of the Mexican annual killifish, Millerichthys robustus, a facultative protogynous hermaphrodite with distinct intraspecific variation in male coloration (red, orange and yellow morphs) and female ornamentation (variable number of melanin-based ocelli). Territoriality was expressed in males and females but not in intermediates. Among males, the yellow morph was competitively inferior, but males of any morph were capable of establishing territories. Red and orange males possessed territories near the substrate. The territories of yellow males were located near the water surface. Males of all morphs also engaged in sneaking behavior and attempted to mate with females outside their own territory. Females with more ocelli were more aggressive and more likely to become dominant but were not courted more often by males. Intermediates rarely engaged in any reproductive behavior. Overall, we demonstrate differences in social and reproductive behavior corresponding to male and female coloration and associate them with variation in social and reproductive niches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25145-7.Keywords: Protogyny, Intrasexual competition, Aggression, Alternative niches, Polymorphism, Color phenotypesSubject terms: Sexual selection, Evolutionary ecology

Introduction

Natural and sexual selection sometimes favors alternative pathways to reproductive success1. In species where a few males monopolize access to females, other males deploy alternative tactics to reproductive success, such as sneaking to “steal” reproductive opportunities from dominant males2. While more common in males, alternative behavioral phenotypes have also repeatedly evolved in females (e.g. conspecific brood parasitism3. Further, dynamic sex change strategies may evolve when differential returns arise for each sex arise under particular social and ecological conditions4–8. Alternative reproductive, physiological and behavioral phenotypes are often related to body color variation and/or body size9–14. Such phenotypic color variation commonly coexists within populations15,16 and ranges from discrete color polymorphism to a continuous variation15,17,18. Color phenotypes are linked to intrapopulation variation in social communication, mate choice, competition, foraging and predation risk in diverse animal systems10,19–27.

The association between alternative behaviors (especially related to aggression, dominance and territoriality) and color phenotype is common in fishes (e.g. cichlids, and killifishes19,28–31;. For example, in polymorphic American and African cichlid species, certain male morphotypes (e.g. red and gold) tend to dominate the others and establish mating territories more frequently32–35. Similarly, alternative color morph-associated breeding tactics were documented in the livebearing fish, Poecilia velifera as large, ornamented, courtship-focused males, medium-sized sneaker males, and an opportunistic cryptic morph36. In females, alternative phenotypes are well understood in damselflies37 but also in fishes, such as Neochromis omnicaeruleus (a cichlid from Lake Victoria), where the blotched females tend to be more aggressive and dominant than brown females38.

Annual killifishes, fish associated with periodically desiccating habitats, display pronounced sexual dimorphism39. Males are intensively colored while females are cryptic and smaller. Distinct color morphs, typically red and blue or red and yellow, are common across annual killifishes from genera such as Nothobranchius, Callopanchax, Fundulopanchax, and Aphyosemion, although non-discrete variation, with an intergradation of colors, can also occur in some species40. In a rare study comparing the color morphs in N. furzeri no repeatable dominance of either morph over another was detected41.

Millerichthys robustus is a Mexican annual killifish species which expresses variation in coloration in both sexes. Males have been analytically classified into five color phenotypes which are distributed between yellow and red hue, based on the RGB color system: yellow, moderate orange, dark orange, strong orange, and red42. However, only three male color phenotypes can be perceived by the human eye: orange, red, and yellow, and we therefore assigned all three shades of orange to a single category in our study. Females show variation in the number of ocelli arranged on the caudal peduncle, which can range from one to 15, and may reach up to the dorsal region of the fish (Fig. 142;. They do not appear to be linked to female age but this has never been quantified. Female ornamentation in M. robustus is related to their fecundity and egg traits43 and female coloration has been linked to hormonal status in other species44,45. M. robustus is the only species that has evolved the annual life cycle (i.e., ability of their eggs to endure habitat desiccation) in North America, independently of other annual killifish taxa from South America and Africa46. This enigmatic species has been documented to be a facultative sequential hermaphrodite, in which females can switch to functional males (protogyny) upon induction by mate competition and aggression between females47.

Using M. robustus as a species with a combination of the expression of intraspecific color variation and hermaphrodism, we hypothesized that variations in male and female coloration, as well sex change history, are associated with behavioral differences, particularly in the ability to establish dominance through aggression. Collecting behavioral data from replicated experimental tanks, we first analyzed how male coloration and sex phenotype history (i.e., secondary (post-sex change) males and intermediates (transitional, sex-changing individuals)48, differ in their reproductive behavior in terms of territoriality and ability to defend a territory. Given high levels of aggression in M. robustus, we predicted that males of different color morphs, including secondary males would form dominance hierarchies and defend small territories. Specifically, we predicted secondary and red males to be dominant through their increased aggression34,49–51. Second, we analyzed how variation in female ornamentation is related to their behavior (and aggression in particular). As female ornamentation in M. robustus affects fecundity and egg traits43 and female coloration has been linked to hormonal status in other species (e.g44,45., we predicted females with more ocelli to be more aggressive. In addition, we analyzed variation in other behaviors recorded during our focal observations and linked them to fish coloration and ornamentation.

Methods

Approval for animal experiments

The collection and use of animals were approved by the Subsecretaría de Gestión para la Protección Ambiental, Dirección General de Vida Silvestre of SEMARNAT (permit No. SGPA/DGVS/02404/14/15). All experiments were performed with accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (individual experimental license CZ01285, and experimental procedures approval no. 31/2019 from the Ministry of Agriculture). Reporting in the manuscript follows the recommendations in the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Experimental fish

We hatched ~ 300 embryos from six 24-week-old pairs, descendants of fish originally collected at “Playa Las Salinas”, Mexico, (N 18° 54’ 39.58´´, W 95°56’ 41.05´´) during 2014. Embryos were hatched in a tank (25 × 25 × 25 cm)52 and kept there until the age of 15 days. Subsequently, 25 fish were distributed among 8 tanks (50 × 50 × 30 cm), where their behavior was studied from 6 weeks post-hatching to 10 weeks post-hatching, when the fish were sexually mature and intermediate fish could be distinguished. Laboratory-bred fish reach sexual maturity around 6 weeks after hatching and their expected lifespan is around three years. Fish were categorized into six categories. (1) Intermediate individuals were intersexual fish with uniform grey body and one or more ocelli at the base of the caudal fin, but with diffuse coloration in the anal fin47. (2) Secondary males had full male coloration (i.e., chocolate brown on the body and red, yellow or orange on the anal and dorsal fins) combined with a small but clearly visible remnant of the ocellus at the base of their caudal fin. Those phenotypes were previously associated with histologically confirmed gonadal sex states47. Other four categories were yellow, orange and red males (defined by their coloration); and females. We acknowledge that male coloration was not technically confirmed by a spectrophotometer, but rather represented classification accessible to human vision.

A total of 40 female and 95 non-female individuals (75 primary males, 9 intermediates, 11 secondary males) were observed. Secondary (i.e., sex changed) males varied in their coloration (2 red, 5 orange, 4 yellow) but were summed for data analysis as “secondary males” given their small sample size. Intermediate individuals were observed for the set of male (rather than female) behaviors, because they stopped being courted by males and did not produce eggs47. Neither oviposition nor ejaculation were observed in intermediate individuals. Sex and color morph ratios of the observed and total fish for each tank are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Fish were distributed across 8 experimental tanks, each with 24 or 25 fish altogether (of which 8–12 were females, 0–2 active intermediates, 0–2 secondary males, and 2–4 males of each color morph). Each tank had a 5 cm layer of coconut fibre on the bottom and four plant-imitating structure (mops) on the surface. In addition, 5 coconut halves with holes were added to provide fish with shelters. These shelters were distributed randomly in the tank. We used a focal-animal sampling approach53, observing behavior of a single fish during each trial, which lasted 20 min. Individuals for observations were haphazardly selected, with the aim to observe balanced ratio of fish from all morphological categories and across tanks. Fish were identifiable by their unique coloration and no fish was observed more than once. Observations were completed in the morning, between 7:00 and 9:30. Before each observation (6:00, during the dark period), we fed the fish by adding 3 mL of brine shrimp nauplii to each aquarium. All observations were made by the same person, hidden from the fish’s view at a distance of one meter behind a curtain. Behavior (as an incidence of each category) was recorded on a paper sheet directly during observation, using a set of pre-defined behaviors54,55.

Male and intermediate fish behavior

During each observation, we recorded a set of behaviors according to the reproductive and agonistic ethograms of M. robustus54–56 (Table 1). Aggressive behaviors were summed into a collective variable. Male aggression towards another male included attacks on other males that entered or approached defended territory; harassment (fish swims approaching frontally slowly and keeping at a distance that avoids physical contact with the recipient, and is generally directed towards the anterior region of the receptor fish); chase (the male swim quickly and vigorously in pursuit of another individual); and combat (we consider a fight when at least two attacks and two serial responses were observed between the fish involved). Male aggression towards a female and towards an intermediate individuals included the same set of behaviors (i.e. attack, harassment, chase and combat). Escape from a male was a movement away from another male displaying aggression. Courtship is defined as the deployment of alternating lateral displays with vibration by the male, folding and twisting dorsal and anal fins, and sigmoid displays above the substrate56.

We also recorded sneaking attempts (interference with the mating of the dominant male aiming at fertilizing the eggs in his territory), breeding acts (the breeding pair penetrates the substrate simultaneously, at the invitation of the female, and remain there for a variable time), hiding (the fish penetrate the irregularities of the substrate and remain motionless) and feeding activity (fish search the substrate for brine shrimp nauplii, only recorded when the prey digestion was apparent).

The possession of territory (i.e., the area that an individual consistently defends against other fish through aggressive displays and direct aggression) was recorded for each focal individual, along with its position (near the substrate or near the water surface) and size. Territory size was measured to the nearest 1 cm along the longest axes with a tape, using habitat elements as reference during observations. Male dominance was defined when an individual consistently controlled access to the territory and mating opportunities over other individuals through aggressive interactions.

Female behavior

Females were uniquely identified by number, position and size of their ocelli (Supplementary Table 1). Female aggressive behaviors were recorded identically to male and intermediate fish trials, including frequency of aggression directed towards each phenotype. Territoriality was defined as female engagement in defence, reproduction and aggression activities in a defined area. Such females patrolled their territory, albeit with less vigor than territorial males. For each female, territory size was recorded to the nearest 1 cm, as reported for male territory. Half the females demonstrated territorial behavior and we analysed whether this was related to female phenotype. Feeding activity was also recorded (as defined for the set of male behaviors). Escape was defensive response to an attack from another fish and was summed across escapes from males, females and intermediate fish. Received courtship was counted when a male displayed courtship towards the focal female. Diving, hiding and sign to the substrate were rare behaviors which we observed and recorded but did not analyze.

Fish size

The design of the behavioral experiment did not allow body size measurements of individual fish. To avoid conflation between variation in color and the number of ocelli with body size, we compared the body size distribution across morphotypes. We used data collected at three natural sites with M. robustusoccurrence (Tlacotalpan, Jesus Carranza and Las Salinas57;. Body size was measured as Total Length (from the tip of the snout to the most distal part of the caudal fin) in the field. Fish collected in the wild were released on site. Only a single intermediate fish was collected and its body size (37.3 mm) fell within the range of male and female body size.

Data analysis

Initial data exploration used correlation matrix, variance inflation factor (VIF), and principal component analysis (PCA) to identify correlated behaviors. This prompted summarizing aggressive behavior into a single component variable (summing all its part). This approach yielded a concordant outcome to tests with the first PC axis of aggressive behavior (data not shown) and provided a clearer interpretation (real units of aggression, counts).

Aggressive behavior of males towards intermediates was positively correlated with behavior towards males (Spearman rho = 0.203, P = 0.048, n = 95), but rare (performed only by 13 fish, 34 units), and is not reported it here as it is colinear with male-male aggression. Following behaviors were not analyzed due to their rare occurrence: hiding behavior (occurred only in 9 individuals) and escape from a female (24 individuals).

All statistical analyses were performed in the R environment (version 4.4.0)58. To test how those five categories differed in their behavior, we used Generalized Linear Models in glmmTMB package59. For data collected as counts of individual behavioral units, we fitted models with a Poisson distribution, as well as two versions with a negative binomial error structure (negbin1: with linear parametrization; negbin2: with quadratic parameterization of the mean in relation to variance) and log-link. Secondary males were always set as the reference treatment. No model simplification was performed. The fit of candidate models was compared using Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) and the best fitting model was chosen. All models were checked for misspecification and zero-inflation in DHARMa package60. Statistical significance for the model is presented as Anova (chi-squared test) in the main text and treatment level significance is presented in summary tables. Post-hoc comparisons were completed in the emmeans package61 using Tukey post-hoc tests.

The relative proportion of categorical variable “territory site” among four male categories (which displayed any territoriality) was compared using a G-test with Williams correction for small sample size in the package DescTools62 to compare how territory positions differed among male categories.

The relationship between the number of ocelli on the caudal fin and female behavior was tested using the same set of models, except that the number of ocelli was modelled as a continuous variable. The small number of females with 6 ocelli (n = 3 females) led to model misspecifications (as recognized by DHARMa diagnostic tools) so we pooled those females with those with 5 ocelli (n = 8 + 3 females in combined treatment). For response variable “escape from aggression”, we further modelled the situation with female aggression rate as a covariate, to disentangle its effect on received aggression.

Body size was analyzed using General Linear Model (Gaussian distribution). Male color (yellow, orange, red) was a categorical response variable and population identity (3 levels) was modelled as a random intercept. For females, the number of ocelli was modelled as a continuous response variable and population identity (3 levels) was modelled as a random intercept.

We acknowledge that despite effective population size in wild annual killifish populations is typically small63, our study may have suffered from genetic autocorrelation arising from the founder effect in our captive population and its maintenance in captivity over multiple generations. This might lead to the situation where, for example, a pair accidentally has a genetic characteristic that makes their offspring more aggressive as well as coding for red coloration in males. Genetic drift in small captive population may then lead to overestimating the correlation between aggressiveness and coloration. While many behavioral studies use captive populations with low effective population size (e.g64–66.,, we admit that the genetic autocorrelation was not measured in our study.

The minimum sample size has not been calculated prior to the study. Instead, we collected data on behaviour on the maximum number of fish we could individually distinguish. All primary data and R scripts for the analyses reported in this paper are accessible at Figshare (doi: 10.6084/m9.figshare.27637341).

Results

Male and intermediate fish behavior

There were considerable differences in territorial (aggressive) and reproductive (epigamic) behavior among hermaphroditic and four categories of male M. robustus. Red and orange males were significantly more aggressive than yellow males and secondary (post-sex change) males also demonstrated substantial aggression (nbinom2 GLMM: χ24 = 36.7, P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 2a, pairwise contrasts: Fig. 2a). In contrast, intermediate fish were clearly the least aggressive (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 2a). Escape behavior (fleeing from a rival) was more common in yellow males compared to the other four categories (nbinom1 GLMM: χ24 = 28.2, P < 0.001; Fig. 2b, Supplementary Table 2b). There was no difference among the five categories in aggression directed towards females (nbinom2 GLMM: χ24 = 5.7, P = 0.222; Supplementary Table 2c). Courtship rates did not vary across male categories except for intermediates who rarely courted females (nbinom2 GLMM: χ24 = 15.9, P = 0.033; Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 2 d). Diving to the substrate was least common in intermediates (nbinom2 GLMM: χ24 = 11.3, P = 0.023; Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table 3b).

Secondary, red, and orange males usually held territories near the substrate, as is typical for M. robustus and other annual killifish. However, dominant yellow males (as well as a single orange male and two (yellow) secondary males) often defended territories around plant-imitating structures near the water surface. This difference in the spatial position of territories among male morphs was statistically significant (G-test with Williams correction: G = 36.1, d.f. = 3, P < 0.001; Fig. 4d). Territories of yellow males (i.e., typically near water surface) were smallest while the territories of red males were largest (Gaussian LMM: χ23 = 36.9, P < 0.001; Fig. 4c, Supplementary Table 3 d).

Breeding rate did not vary among categories (nbinom2 GLMM: χ24 = 0.8, P = 0.945; Supplementary Table 4a, Fig. 3b). Feeding (nbinom1 GLMM: χ24 = 25.9, P < 0.001) was more common in intermediate fish than in any male morph (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Table 4b).

In the wild, yellow, orange and red males did not differ in their body size (LMM: χ22 = 0.5, P = 0.773, Suppl. Fig. S1a).

Female behavior

Females with more ocelli at the base of the caudal fin were more aggressive (nbinom1 GLMM: χ21 = 82.0, P < 0.001, Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 5a) and more likely to become territorial/dominant (Bernoulli GLMM: χ21 = 13.3, P < 0.001, Fig. 5c, Supplementary Table 5b). Territory size, however, was not related to the number of ocelli (Gaussian LMM for a subset of 20 females which defended their territories: χ21 = 1.9, P = 0.169). Finally, females with more ocelli were observed to escape from a conflict more often (Poisson GLMM: χ21 = 13.7, P < 0.001, Supplementary Table 5c). This has, however, arisen solely as a function of being exposed to more conflict situations and the significant effect of the number of ocelli disappeared (Poisson GLMM: χ21 = 1.6, P = 0.204) when initiated aggression was included as a covariate in the model of escape from conflicts (covariate: χ21 = 0.8, P = 0.387).

The number of ocelli was not related to the probability that a female was courted (Poisson GLMM: χ21 = 0.1, P = 0.832, Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 5 d) nor feeding rate (nbinom2 GLMM: χ21 = 2.3, P = 0.129, Supplementary Table 5e). In the wild, the number of ocelli was not related to female body size (LMM: χ21 = 0.1, P = 0.734, Suppl. Figure 1b).

Discussion

We studied social interactions in males, intermediate individuals and females of sequential hermaphroditic annual killifish Millerichthys robustus related to their variation in coloration. Territorial behavior was expressed in males and females but not in intermediate fish. Among males (including secondary males), the yellow morph was competitively inferior, received the most aggression and was rarely aggressive to other fish. However, males of all color morphs were capable of establishing territories. Unlike red and orange males which possessed territories near the spawning substrate, the territories of yellow morph males were located in atypical positions near the water surface, and were likely inferior. Males of all morphs also engaged in sneaking behavior and attempted to mate with females outside their own territory, a behavior so far not reported in any annual killifish. Females with more ocelli at the base of the caudal fin were more aggressive and more likely to become dominant. However, they were not courted more often by males. Intermediates rarely engaged in any reproductive behavior and, instead, spent more time feeding.

Based on the information collected in the current study, the social and breeding system of M. robustus can be characterized as follows; dominant (mainly red and orange) males possess their own territories above the substrate, often adjacent to each other and defended by aggressive behavior. Yellow males defend their smaller territories near vegetation close to the water surface. Females are choosy but promiscuous; they enter a male territory, or a succession of territories of several males, to spawn. This fits the definition of “male-territory-visiting” polygamy as defined by Kuwamura67. When females are not willing to breed, they spend most time in the water column (outside immediate male territories). Any male may attempt sneaking behavior in order to steal some reproductive success from males in neighboring territories, or from dominant males in the case of non-territorial males. Similarly, some females establish and defend territories on the bottom, although without vigorous patrolling as performed by males. Intermediate fish move freely across territories, likely not representing any threat to reproductive success of territory holders.

Millerichthys robustus territoriality is dynamic and territories are transient. Territorial disputes are a daily routine during the first hours of daylight, perhaps to reestablish territories after every night. This contrasts with observations in some pupfishes (a closely related clade of egg-laying cyprinodontiforms), where males maintain their territory throughout the days and nights for long periods of time68–71. We also observed temporary non-enforced abandonment of the territory by dominant males, probably related to rest and recovery of their energetic resources spent in costly territorial defence72,73.

We also recorded some previously undescribed behavior in this species with unclear biological significance, such as “dive”. During “dive” territorial males submerged into the substrate, as occurs during reproduction, but without any interaction with a female. We speculate that this behavior could be associated with substrate preparation prior to reproduction (e.g. loosening the substrate) or a display of vigor for females; however, this needs to be studied in more detail. On the other hand, some non-territorial males hide for an extended period in the substrate. We speculate this represents either an escape from territorial males in the confined space of the experimental tanks or a strategy to increase the participation in sneaking attempts.

Red colored morphs are, across many taxa, more likely to win agonistic contests and become dominant34,74,75, although yellow (gold) morphs can also be superior32. Yet in other taxa, color morphs are not associated with competitive ability or any apparent intrasexual differences76. In M. robustus it has been previously documented that orange and red morphs win dyadic agonistic interactions, while the yellow morph was the least competitive and exerted alternative coexistence strategies55. This finding is extended here by the finding that yellow morph individuals tend to defend alternative territories such as near water surface vegetation, which is never defended by dominant males.

Male morphs were stable across the duration of our experiment and no transition between color morphs was observed. The red-yellow hue gradient can be associated with carotenoid coloration, widely accepted as an honest signal of male quality due to its limiting nature77. Yet, red-yellow hue of many fish, including killifish, is composed from pteridine pigments29 and although their role is less understood than in carotenoid-based coloration, they can also serve to signal individual status29. The source of red, orange and yellow morphs, as well as their heritability, have not been studied in M. robustus so far.

The ability to establish and defend territory with the appropriate characteristics in terms of its quality, location and depth of the substrate seems to be critical component of reproductive success in annual killifishes, including offspring survival and quality. All annual killifishes deposit their eggs in the substrate41,56 where they persist throughout the wet and desiccated periods of their development, including embryonic diapause, until hatching in the ensuing rainy season78. It has been suggested that the exact location where the eggs are deposited in a temporary water body exposes them differentially to water availability, which determines the dynamics of egg bank development and hatching57,79–81. These characteristics influence the age structure82, expression of alternative phenotypes in the life history traits83,84 and the temporal ecological dynamics in their populations40,85.

We found that yellow males defend territories near the water surface. It was considered that there is a dichotomy between annual and non-annual killifish in the choice of spawning substrates; annual killifish defend territories near the substrate while non-annual killifish defend territories with vegetation, contingent on the respective preferred egg laying substrate78. Rather than an artefact induced by our laboratory conditions, we consider defence of non-substrate territories by yellow males to be an expression of the ancestral spawning mode of rivulids, since M. robustus has likely evolved its annual reproductive strategy only recently and its sister species are non-annual46. This strategy has potential implications for embryo development. We speculate that the deposition of eggs on vegetation (outside the substrate) could induce an alternative developmental pathway and the production of alternative developmental phenotypes84 compared to the embryos buried in the substrate. Specifically, embryos developing in vegetation could develop directly (without diapause) as in non-annual Cyprinodontiformes78,86 and as observed in M. robustus in the laboratory87. It is also possible that such embryos experience a brief diapause period (e.g. pre-hatching diapause III sensu Wourms88 during short aerial exposures (e.g. lasting days or weeks) following water level fluctuations related to evaporation-precipitation dynamics throughout the rainy season78. This possibility is supported by presence of newly hatched fish more than two months after the first filling of the natural pools, recorded during our fieldwork, suggesting additional hatching periods long after the pool flooding85. We acknowledge that this hypothesis needs to be tested, as well as an intriguing possibility of association between developmental trajectory and adult male color morph.

All secondary (i.e., post-sex change) males were territorial, except for a single individual. Prevailing dominance of secondary males is also common in labroid fishes50,89 and the finding is consistent with sex-change models in which selection maximizes individual fitness under specific social and ecological contexts (e.g. mating systems), conferring differential advantages for each sex4,5,90. Given the higher variation in reproductive fitness amongst males than females91, we can hypothesize that the secondary M. robustus males gain higher fitness than that of a typical female, which is an ultimate trigger of their sex change.

Intermediate M. robustus were observed to spend more time feeding than any other fish. Two factors, not mutually exclusive, can contribute to this. First, sex change is physiologically demanding process that requires more energy92,93. Second, the absence of territorial defense, courtship and reproductive activities enables more time to be spent on maintenance activities, such as feeding73,94.

Variation in female coloration was associated with melanin-based ocelli at the base of the caudal fin. More ocelli were associated with higher aggression and higher likelihood of becoming dominant over other females. Stronger melanin-based coloration is also linked to higher aggression in other species95,96. Previous research on M. robustus documented that large-bodied females with a high number of ocelli also ovulate more eggs, and their eggs are bigger than those of large females with fewer ocelli as well as those of small females regardless their number of ocelli43. In birds, the extent of melanin-based coloration is related to performance in high or low resource environments, suggesting the links between melanin coloration and the ability to differentially allocate resources over heterogenous environments97. Further, M. robustus males show a strong preference for females with a greater number of ocelli43. Melanin-derived colors (such as black body marks) are synthesized by essential amino acids linked to hormonal cascades and may be related to reproductive physiology and behavior and, hence, to dominance98. Interestingly, aggressive behavior among females has also been linked to the induction of sex change in M. robustus47, as aggressive behaviors in sex-changing species are linked to reduced aromatase activity which ultimately increases the production of male hormones (e.g. 11-ketotestosterone) and influences brain physiology, behavior and morphology of sex-changing females99. Female ocelli clearly play a key role in the social and sexual behavior of M. robustus, probably as an honest signal of female quality related to fertility or a heritable intrinsic quality100.

Competition among females for limited resources may give rise to intrasexual territoriality101,102. Some M. robustus females displayed territorial behavior and defended patches of the substrate from access by other females, while others kept swimming near the surface or remained stationary beneath the artificial vegetation when they were not receptive to males. It is not clear what maintains female territoriality in our study species, but possibilities include access to superior males or territories. Some females exhibited aggression towards males, perhaps associated with competition for food or shelters. Alternatively, female expression of behavior more commonly associated with males may be an early expression of sex change. Behavioral changes are the first observable signs of sex change in M. robustus47.

Conclusion

We tested how intra-sexual variation in coloration is linked to social and reproductive behavior in replicated experimental populations of facultative protandrous hermaphroditic fish. Overall, we found differences in social behavior corresponding to male and female coloration and associated them with possible alternative social and reproductive niches.
