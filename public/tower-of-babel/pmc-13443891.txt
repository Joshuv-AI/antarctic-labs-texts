Self-supervised learning can distinguish myelodysplastic neoplasms from clinical mimics using bone marrow biopsies∗
Authors: Vahid Mehrtash, Hortense Le, Bita Jafarzadeh, Mohamed Sharaf, Abdallah Flaifel, Xiaoyan Huang, Nicholas Ward, Robert Hasserjian, Sanam Loghavi, Guillermo Garcia-Manero, Aristotelis Tsirigos, Christopher Y. Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•SSL-based image analysis accurately discriminates BMBx from patients with MDS and clinical mimics.•The SSL model is interpretable, requiring minimal tissue, identifying key morphologic features, and classifying MDS BMBx across institutions.

Introduction

Myelodysplastic neoplasms (MDS) are clonal hematopoietic disorders characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis, peripheral blood cytopenia(s), and dysplasia in the marrow.1,2 Diagnosis requires an integrated approach combining morphologic, genetic, and cytogenetic features.3 Identification of dysplastic morphologic changes, a key diagnostic criterion, is associated with significant interobserver and intraobserver variability.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Given that morphologic assessment for dysplasia is required to establish a diagnosis of MDS and to distinguish MDS from benign conditions with similar presentations, this remains a significant potential diagnostic pitfall. Typically, evaluation for dysplasia is performed by assessing bone marrow aspirate smears, because, in contrast to bone marrow biopsies (BMBx), a detailed cytologic examination of all 3 major hematopoietic lineages (erythroid, granulocytic, megakaryocytic) can be made.4,8, 9, 10 However, bone marrow aspirates are sometimes inadequate for evaluation due to the absence of marrow spicules, hemodilution, and suboptimal preparation.11,12 A previous study demonstrated that supervised, deep learning–based morphologic analysis of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained BMBx from patients with MDS with established diagnosis could predict genetic subtypes of MDS.13 However, these studies did not rigorously investigate the ability of artificial intelligence (AI)/machine learning (ML)–driven approaches to distinguish MDS from its clinical mimics based solely on BMBx images. In this study, we applied a self-supervised learning (SSL) image analysis approach to develop an algorithm capable of reliably discriminating MDS from its clinically relevant mimics. We hypothesized that SSL could capture morphologic features required for the diagnosis of MDS and thus may improve the reproducibility and accuracy of diagnosis, particularly as AI/ML-assisted algorithms gain broader adoption in hematopathology. Overall, SSL has the potential to uncover domain-specific features in unlabeled data, reducing dependence on expert annotations.

Methods

Patient selection and control group assignment

A total of 243 BMBx from the NYU Langone Health (NYU) Pathology Department were included, representing 89 MDS cases based on established diagnostic criteria,8 55 cytopenic cases with clinical suspicion of MDS but resulting in non-MDS diagnoses (“non-MDS cytopenic controls” [NMCC]), and 99 unremarkable lymphoma-staging bone marrow cases from patients without MDS (“negative control” [NC]; Table 1). This retrospective cohort included sequential MDS and NMCC cases that met inclusion criteria and were evaluated for suspected MDS between 2017 and 2023. Among MDS cases, 12 cases were diagnosed with increased blasts 1 (IB-1), 22 with IB-2, and 55 with other MDS subtypes.4,8 All MDS cases in the internal NYU cohort used for model development were newly diagnosed at the time of BMBx, without any previously treated or follow-up cases included. The NYU cohort included both H&E- and reticulin-stained BMBx sections. To maximize representativeness, 2 slides per patient (with 2 levels per slide) were analyzed. Lymphoma-staging marrow cases were included as NC if they had confirmed lymphoma diagnoses with the BMBx showing no evidence of involvement by lymphoma, dysplasia, or other morphologic abnormalities. Cytopenic cases undergoing BMBx with suspicion of MDS but resulting in non-MDS diagnoses were selected as more challenging controls (supplemental Table 1). These cases encompassed a range of conditions, including autoimmune disorders, solid tumors, alcohol-related conditions, and chronic liver and kidney disorders, all confirmed through comprehensive clinical, laboratory, and histopathologic evaluations. For the NMCC, cases were included if they either had negative results on the NYU Oncomine Myeloid panel, a multigene next-generation sequencing assay covering 50 key genes associated with hematologic malignancies, or did not undergo next-generation sequencing because their cytopenias resolved and an alternative clinical diagnosis was established.14,15 NC cases were included to generate phenotype clusters that serve as normal reference HPCs and to facilitate interpretation and auditing of disease-enriched clusters, whereas also enabling the model to discount variations in stain intensity, scanner drift, and other processing artifacts. Slide selection and WSI

Slides were retrieved from the NYU pathology archives. All slides were previously prepared and stained (H&E and reticulin stains) as part of routine clinical workflow. Brightfield digital whole-slide images (WSIs) of NYU slides were acquired using an Aperio ScanScope XT (Leica Biosystems, Vista, CA) at magnification ×40, with a resolution of 0.25 μm per pixel.

Cases with poor-quality BMBx or inadequate cellularity (<1 intertrabecular space) were excluded to ensure accurate interpretation. The cellularity of NYU BMBx samples based on the original pathology report had a median of 65%, an interquartile range of 43.75% to 80.0%, and an overall range of 5% to 95%. Additionally, any cases with inconclusive clinical diagnoses were excluded from the study.

To assess generalizability and robustness, 95 MDS cases from MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC), Houston, TX, that were not part of the model development cohort were included. MDACC slides were digitized using an Aperio GT450 scanner (Leica Biosystems) at magnification ×40 (0.25 μm per pixel). Among the 83 MDACC MDS cases with available blast count data, 19 were classified as IB-2, 15 as IB-1, and 49 as other MDS subtypes. In the external validation cohort from MDACC, 9 patients had previously treated MDS and 22 had a history of previous exposure to chemotherapy. Each of the NYU and MDACC cohorts contained 4 adolescent and young adult patients aged 15 to 39 years, with no pediatric patients included.16

Image processing and SSL model development

Details regarding the analysis pipeline are provided in the supplemental Methods. WSIs were partitioned into 224 × 224–pixel image tiles using DeepPATH.17 Image tiles with 100% background pixels, defined as pixels with a gray level of >230, were excluded. We applied the Reinhard method for stain normalization before analysis.18 We used the Barlow Twins model, a validated SSL framework, to create an unbiased atlas of histomorphologic phenotype clusters (HPCs) from H&E- and reticulin-stained BMBx (Figure 1).17,19,20 Briefly, we trained a self-supervised feature encoder using the Barlow Twins framework implemented within the Histomorphologic Phenotype Learning pipeline. The model consisted of a ResNet-50 backbone, initialized with ImageNet weights, followed by a multilayer projection head to map feature representations into an embedding space. For each image tile, 2 augmented views were generated using histopathology-specific transformations (random cropping, flipping, color and stain jitter, and Gaussian blur) to encourage robustness to tissue variability. The Barlow Twins objective was used to maximize the correlation between embeddings from the 2 views while reducing redundancy across feature dimensions. Training was performed on 224 × 224–pixel tiles at magnification ×20, extracted from unannotated WSIs, and the embeddings were used for histomorphologic phenotype discovery.21 HPCs were reviewed by hematopathologists to validate clustering patterns, enabling the development of an accurate model distinguishing MDS from clinical mimics.Figure 1.Pipeline overview for histomorphologic phenotype learning framework architecture. Comparison of pathologists and AI diagnostic performance

A subset of 90 cases was uploaded to a secure cloud-based platform for observer assessment, comprising 28 NMCC, 30 MDS, and 32 NC cases. The platform enabled 6 hematopathologists (A.F., C.Y.P., N.W., R.H., S.L., and X.H.) to independently review the WSIs. For each case, observers selected 1 of 3 predefined categories: MDS, NMCC, or NC. Only BMBx WSIs were provided, without access to bone marrow aspiration findings, peripheral blood data, clinical information, or ancillary studies. No time restrictions were imposed, allowing observers to complete evaluations at their discretion. All assessments were performed independently. Observer responses were subsequently collected and analyzed to determine diagnostic performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity.

This study was conducted under the scope of an approved NYU Grossman School of Medicine institutional review board protocol (number i22-00178) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

HPCs effectively captured diagnostically meaningful morphologic features

The SSL pipeline identified 31 distinct HPCs within the NYU H&E-stained image tiles. Review by 2 hematopathologists validated that most image tiles within each HPC showed similar morphologic features, demonstrating consistent and interpretable feature extraction by the model. To ensure comprehensive analysis, we incorporated all HPCs into our classifier. However, before training the prediction model, an automated selection step was implemented to identify and prioritize the most relevant HPCs (see the supplemental Methods for details); representative image tiles of HPCs were reviewed to confirm that all selected HPCs captured diagnostically relevant regions of the BMBx, with no clusters corresponding to artifacts or nonhematopoietic areas. Final HPCs used in model training and classification are depicted in Figure 2A. Enriched HPCs in MDS cases captured morphologic features relevant for MDS diagnosis, including hypercellular image tiles (HPC 4), dysplastic and loosely clustered megakaryocytes (HPC 4), increased immature hematopoietic cells (HPCs 4 and 26), increased vascularity, stromal alterations suggestive of fibrosis (eg, streaming patterns; HPC 26).4,8,22 Depleted HPCs in MDS cases were associated with low cellularity (HPCs 21 and 24) as well as hematopoietic cells with normal morphologies and maturation (HPCs 12, 21, and 24; Figure 2A; supplemental Figure 1). A potential limitation in diagnosing MDS is BMBx length, as an adequate amount of intact hematopoietic marrow is essential to accurately represent hematopoiesis and to assess dysplasia.23 To determine the minimum amount of tissue required to achieve optimal diagnostic performance, we randomly selected varying numbers of tiles from each WSI. Selecting 200 to 300 tiles per WSI achieved an AUC comparable with using all tiles (Figure 3D). Considering an image tile size of 224 × 224 pixels (in which 1 pixel = 0.505 μm), the minimum amount of tissue area required to reach optimal performance for this model is ∼2.55 to 3.83 mm2, significantly less tissue than guideline-defined adequate samples (1.5 cm in length).24,25

The trained SSL model performed consistently on an external cohort

To evaluate the generalizability of our image analysis algorithm, we assessed the performance on a data set of BMBx WSIs acquired from an external institution. For this purpose, H&E-stained WSIs from 95 cases with confirmed diagnosis of MDS from MDACC were analyzed. To evaluate the H&E HPCs, we preprocessed the H&E WSIs from the MDACC cohort, projected its tiles into the frozen trained H&E network, and assigned HPCs to the resulting tile vector representations. We defined patient HPC compositions and compared them with our internal NYU patient HPC compositions with Wilcoxon tests. HPCs derived from the NYU data set were assigned to the MDACC cohort, enabling evaluation of the model's ability to assign clusters to an independent data set. Notably, HPCs capturing features suggestive of bone marrow fibrosis (eg, cell streaming; HPC 26), hypercellular image tiles with erythroid hyperplasia (HPC 4), and dysplastic megakaryocytes and immature hematopoietic elements (HPC 4 and HPC 26) were enriched in both MDS cohorts. In contrast, image tiles capturing adipocyte-enriched areas (HPC 21), normal trilineage maturing hematopoietic cells (HPC 12), and aspiration artifacts (HPC 9) were predominantly enriched in the control groups (Figure 2C; supplemental Figure 1). Indeed, the image tiles of the MDACC MDS cases exhibited similar patterns of HPC enrichment or depletion as observed in the original NYU training data set, highlighting the model's robustness in recognizing comparable features despite potential staining differences.

Addition of reticulin-stained BMBx to the SSL model increased model performance

One of the features evaluated during routine examination of BMBx for MDS is the degree of reticulin fibrosis, which can aid in the diagnosis and has prognostic implications.4,8,26,27 We thus analyzed reticulin-stained BMBx using the same SSL pipeline.

Analysis of WSIs of reticulin-stained BMBx identified 42 HPCs with a subset enriched in MDS cases. Evaluation of images within each HPC revealed that the clusters and enrichment patterns were primarily driven by the presence and density of reticulin fibrosis. Although morphologic features identified in H&E-stained slides were still discernible in reticulin-stained slides due to the nuclear counterstain, the clustering of image tiles appeared to be primarily driven by features related to fibrosis, because HPCs in reticulin-stained slides exhibiting these cellular features did not influence the clustering as expected (Figure 2B). The model trained exclusively on reticulin-stained slides distinguished MDS from NC and NMCC, achieving AUCs of 0.77 and 0.73, respectively.

Subsequently, an ensemble model was trained to integrate the results from both H&E and reticulin-stained WSIs. The ensemble model improved classification performance, achieving AUCs of 0.82, 0.80, and 0.76 for distinguishing MDS from NC, MDS from NMCC, and MDS from a combined group of NC and NMCC controls, respectively (Figure 3C).

Model performance was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation, with diagnostic metrics derived from out-of-fold test predictions. The model achieved sensitivities of 0.84 (MDS vs NMCC), 0.89 (MDS vs NC), and 0.78 (MDS vs combined non-MDS groups), with corresponding specificities of 0.54, 0.60, and 0.52, and accuracies of 0.73, 0.75, and 0.62, respectively. These values, obtained at a balanced operating point, indicate that this model could function effectively in a screening context by identifying a high proportion of MDS cases across clinically relevant comparisons.

Spatial proximity analysis of diagnostic HPCs

Proximity enrichment analysis demonstrated spatial segregation between HPC 4 and HPC 11, as well as between HPC 11 and HPC 12. HPC 11 corresponds to bone trabeculae, whereas HPC 4 comprises hypercellular regions characterized by increased immature hematopoietic cells, dysplastic and loosely clustered megakaryocytes, and some of the image tiles showing associated stromal alterations. HPC 12 captures areas dominated by mature myeloid cells, mature erythroid elements, and morphologically normal megakaryocytes, a predominant phenotype enriched in non-MDS cases (supplemental Figure 2).

Comparison of pathologist and AI diagnostic performance

A subset of 90 cases was used to compare hematopathologists’ performance with that of the AI model.

When MDS was considered the positive class, the mean pathologist sensitivity for detecting MDS was 62.8%, with substantial interobserver variability (supplemental Table 2). Despite some readers achieving higher sensitivity, overall performance remained modest.

To enable a true head-to-head comparison with human readers, the AI model was evaluated on the identical 90-case subset used for pathologist assessment. Consequently, the model was trained exclusively in the remaining 153 cases, ensuring complete separation between training and testing cohorts. Under this fully independent validation framework, the model achieved a sensitivity of 68%, specificity of 74%, accuracy of 72%, and AUC of 0.82. Although this sensitivity was lower than the five-fold cross-validation out-of-fold sensitivity (78%), it remained numerically higher than the mean pathologist sensitivity.

To compare performance across pathologists, differences in overall correctness, sensitivity (MDS cases only), and specificity (non-MDS cases only) were assessed using the Cochran Q test to account for the nonindependence of observations resulting from multiple evaluations of the same slides by different pathologists. The Cochran Q test did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in overall diagnostic correctness among the 6 pathologists (Q = 8.25; df = 5; P = .14). When the analysis was restricted to MDS cases, there was a significant difference among pathologists (Q = 21.61; df = 5; P = .0006), indicating heterogeneity in the sensitivity of MDS detection. When the analysis was restricted to non-MDS cases to compare specificity, differences among the pathologists approached, but did not reach, statistical significance (Q = 10.84; df = 5; P = .055).

Although the model did not consistently exceed individual pathologist performance, its diagnostic metrics fell within the expert observer range. Substantial interobserver variability was noted among pathologists, whereas the AI model potentially offers consistent behavior. The model’s sensitivity, reproducibility, and fatigue-independent operation support its potential role as a screening or decision-support tool.

Stratified performance by MDS blast categories and IPSS-R risk groups

Results were further stratified according to marrow blast percentage. In cases without increased blasts, the model distinguished MDS from NC and from NMCC, achieving AUCs of 0.73 and 0.76, respectively. In cases with blast counts ≥5% and <10%, discrimination of MDS from NC and NMCC yielded AUCs of 0.79 and 0.73. For cases with blast counts ≥10% and <20%, the model achieved AUCs of 0.78 for MDS vs NC and 0.71 for MDS vs NMCC. When all cases with increased blasts were analyzed together, the model demonstrated improved discrimination, with an AUC of 0.84 for MDS vs NC and 0.78 for MDS vs NMCC. These findings indicate that the classifier achieves consistent diagnostic performance across the spectrum of MDS subtypes, including those with lower blast content in which morphologic assessment is most challenging. The receiver operating characteristic plots for each stratified analysis are provided in supplemental Figure 3.

Because disease severity may influence model performance, we evaluated the relationship between model-derived MDS probability scores and established clinical risk stratification using the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS-R). Given the limited number of cases within individual IPSS-R categories, cases in the NYU cohort were stratified into binary groups: high risk (very high plus high) and low risk (intermediate, low, and very low).28 Model-derived MDS probabilities were then compared between these groups. The analysis showed that the predicted MDS probability was significantly higher in patients with MDS belonging to the high-risk group than among those in the low-risk group (MDS vs NC, P = .0014; MDS vs NMCC, P = .0008; Wilcoxon test).

To further assess the relationship between the IPSS-R and model output, correlation analysis was performed using continuous IPSS-R scores. A statistically significant positive correlation was observed between IPSS-R scores and model-predicted MDS probability (MDS vs NC, Spearman r = 0.235 [P = .0058]; MDS vs NMCC, Spearman r = 0.177 [P = .039]).

Collectively, these findings indicate that the classifier’s output is not only able to distinguish MDS from control groups but also reflect disease severity as supported by its correlation with IPSS-R scores in the NYU cohort (supplemental Figure 4).

Discussion

The pathologic diagnosis of MDS requires integration of laboratory, molecular, cytogenetic, and morphologic data.29 However, morphologic assessment of bone marrow specimens is subject to interobserver variability and may be limited by sample quality or availability of ancillary studies, particularly in underresourced settings. These challenges underscore the need for more accessible diagnostic approaches.

The ability to make a preliminary or screening diagnosis of MDS based solely on H&E-stained BMBx images offers a solution to these issues. Although morphologic assessment of BMBx has traditionally been considered less sensitive for assessing dysplasia, because it is difficult to sensitively capture cytologic details, including subtle nuclear changes, loss of granularity, and dyssynchronous maturation of erythroid precursors,30 recent studies have demonstrated that ML algorithms trained on BMBx images can robustly predict mutational subtypes of MDS and may discriminate MDS from normal controls.13 However, these studies did not rigorously address whether ML approaches can distinguish MDS from its clinical and morphologic mimics. In this study, we evaluated whether an SSL-based model could identify features enabling differentiation of MDS from related non-MDS conditions using BMBx images alone.

Our study demonstrates the feasibility of applying WSIs of H&E-stained BMBx for the diagnosis of MDS. We evaluated whether our SSL model could distinguish MDS not only from age-matched NCs obtained from lymphoma-staging biopsies, but also from age-matched NMCC, thereby more closely reflecting the clinical context in which a BMBx is performed to evaluate for possible MDS. The SSL model demonstrated robust performance, achieving AUC values of 0.82 for distinguishing MDS from NC and 0.80 for distinguishing MDS from NMCC. Interestingly, although many of the HPCs identified by the SSL model corresponded to morphologic features traditionally assessed by pathologists, several clusters did not align with features currently recognized as diagnostic of MDS. Although this raises questions regarding the specificity and sensitivity of such novel features, the consistent reproducibility of similar clusters in an independent external cohort of MDS BMBx from MDACC supports their biological relevance. These findings suggest that the model may be capturing patterns not yet appreciated in routine histomorphologic assessment. Future studies will be needed to characterize these clusters and to develop interpretive frameworks that will allow morphologists to recognize and incorporate them into the diagnostic workflow. One potentially novel feature identified by our analysis is the relative enrichment of HPCs corresponding to normal bone trabeculae, characterized on H&E by thin lamellar structures with eosinophilic matrix, regularly spaced osteocytes, and lining osteoblasts, in NC cases (HPC 11 and 24; supplemental Figure 1). Although bone sclerosis and fibrosis have been described in MDS with bone marrow fibrosis,31 our findings suggest that this depletion of normal trabecular architecture, may represent an underrecognized morphologic hallmark of the disease. Although these HPCs were enriched in NC compared with MDS, they were not selected in the final classification model, because they did not reach consistent statistical significance across all groups and crossvalidation folds (see the supplemental Methods for details).

To assess model limitations, we varied BMBx evaluation conditions. Specifically, we tested different image tile sizes, hypothesizing that smaller tiles (50 × 50 and 12 × 12 pixels), capturing fewer cells, would emphasize cytologic features and improve detection of dysplastic cells. However, the most robust model was based on the larger 224 × 224–pixel tiles (113 μm × 113 μm), suggesting that changes in marrow cell composition and spatial relationships are features of MDS that distinguish it from other acquired causes of cytopenia. This finding is consistent with previous studies that showed that disruption of erythroid islands or other alterations in cell composition or localization may be seen in MDS.32

Unlike conventional morphologic assessment, the SSL approach did not apply the quantitative threshold of ≥10% dysplastic cells required to diagnose MDS.4,8 Instead, clustering was performed in an unsupervised approach, with the prevalence of features such as cellular atypia and dysplastic megakaryocytes in hematopoietic cells reflected in the enrichment patterns of the corresponding image tiles that captured these features. By analyzing tiles that include both target cells and their surrounding microenvironment, the model was able to capture morphologic information at both the cellular and structural levels.

Pathologist performance assessment provided additional insights into the challenges of morphologic MDS diagnosis. Notably, interobserver variability was driven primarily by differences in MDS detection rather than in excluding MDS. This suggests that subtle or borderline morphologic features were interpreted differently among the pathologists, likely reflecting variable thresholds for diagnosing MDS. Some pathologists may adopt a more conservative approach, reserving an MDS diagnosis for unequivocal cases, whereas others may classify morphologically suspicious cases as MDS. The relative consistency among pathologists in ruling out MDS indicates that clearly benign or reactive patterns are more reproducibly identified.

The spatial dissociation between hypercellular regions enriched in immature hematopoietic cells, dysplastic and loosely clustered megakaryocytes, associated stromal alterations (HPC 4), and bone trabeculae (HPC 11), is compatible with spatial reorganization of precursor compartments and disruption of normal topographic relationships within the marrow microenvironment. This pattern is similar to the phenomenon of abnormal localization of immature precursors, in which early myeloid precursors are displaced from their typical paratrabecular niches and form aberrant interstitial aggregates.33

A limitation of our study is the lack of genetic and mutation information on all cases in our NMCC cohort; therefore, we were unable to reliably annotate and distinguish cases of clonal cytopenia of uncertain significance from idiopathic cytopenia of uncertain significance or evaluate for the ability of our model to recognize such cases. We envision that studying larger, genetically annotated clonal cytopenia of uncertain significance cohorts alongside MDS could potentially reveal early, subtle MDS features, facilitating earlier diagnosis of disease. We also were not able to independently evaluate hypocellular MDS cases (defined as bone marrow cellularity of <30% in patients aged ≤70 years, and of <20% in those aged >70 years)29 because they were relatively rare in our cohort (5 cases in the NYU data set and none in the MDACC data set). Given their low frequency, a meaningful analysis would require assembling cases from multiple institutions. Although this is an important subgroup to evaluate, its exclusion does not diminish the main finding of our study that MDS can be accurately distinguished from morphologic mimics using H&E-stained BMBx images alone.

Additionally, certain morphologic features captured by the model, such as increased vascularity, are not specific to MDS and may also occur in other myeloid neoplasms. We did not perform a dedicated comparison with additional myeloid neoplasms, and future studies incorporating these groups will be important to better define the specificity of such features for MDS.

An important consideration in the development of AI tools for bone marrow pathology is the choice between training domain–specific models vs using pretrained pathology foundation models. Although large-scale pretrained pathology foundation models can provide broadly applicable feature representations, their training data sets have not, to our knowledge, included substantial numbers of BMBx WSIs or incorporated reticulin-stained sections. In contrast, our SSL model was trained directly on both H&E- and reticulin-stained BMBx from our study cohort. This allowed the model to learn features fine-tuned to the morphologic and stromal patterns of marrow pathology, including reticulin-based assessments of fibrosis, potentially enhancing specificity for MDS and its morphologic mimics compared with more general-purpose foundation models.

An important aspect of this study is that WSIs from NYU and MDACC were not subjected to centralized staining or scanning protocols but instead were analyzed as prepared and archived at each institution. This design more closely reflects the heterogeneity encountered in routine clinical practice. Despite these differences in scanner type and slide preparation, the model achieved comparable performance across both cohorts. This finding highlights the robustness and generalizability of the approach, suggesting that clinically useful models can be developed without requiring strict harmonization of preanalytic variables, thereby increasing their potential for real-world implementation.

Although the results suggest that SSL models may be deployable in the clinical diagnostic setting, ultimate adoption will require validation using a larger number of cases from additional institutions. We envision such models will not be free-standing but will be integrated with pathologist-trained models to improve accuracy, because features that pathologists routinely evaluate, such as megakaryocyte dysplasia, were not featured prominently among the HPCs enriched in MDS. We also anticipate that integrated models will benefit from the addition of immunohistochemical stains (eg, CD34, CD61, Glycophorin). Such integrated approaches can be tested for their ability to assess mutational subtypes or clinical outcomes.

In summary, our study demonstrates that an SSL model can discriminate MDS from its morphologic mimics using H&E- and reticulin-stained BMBx with encouraging performance characteristics. Importantly, the model was developed and evaluated in the absence of any molecular, cytogenetic, or clinical data, demonstrating that morphologic features alone contain important information to enable classification. Although further refinement and validation of the model will be needed in larger, multi-institutional cohorts, our findings highlight the potential of image-based models as an accessible diagnostic tool, particularly in settings in which advanced ancillary testing approaches are not readily available, thereby increasing health equity in MDS diagnosis.
