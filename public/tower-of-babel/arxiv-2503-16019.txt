Explaining Unforeseen Congruence Relationships Between PEND and POND Partitions via an Atkin–Lehner Involution
James A. Sellers, Nicolas Allen Smoot

Abstract
. For the past several years, numerous authors have studied POD and PED partitions from a variety of perspectives. These are integer partitions wherein the odd parts must be distinct (in the case of POD partitions) or the even parts must be distinct (in the case of PED partitions). More recently, Ballantine and Welch were led to consider POND and PEND partitions, which are integer partitions wherein the odd parts cannot be distinct (in the case of POND partitions) or the even parts cannot be distinct (in the case of PEND partitions). Soon after, the first author proved the following results via elementary q q -series identities and generating function manipulations, along with mathematical induction: For all α ≥ 1 \alpha\geq 1 and all n ≥ 0 , n\geq 0, pend ⁡ ( 3 2 ​ α + 1 ​ n + 17 ⋅ 3 2 ​ α − 1 8 ) \displaystyle\pend\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}\right) ≡ 0 ( mod 3 ) , and \displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3},\textrm{\ \ \ and} pond ⁡ ( 3 2 ​ α + 1 ​ n + 23 ⋅ 3 2 ​ α + 1 8 ) \displaystyle\pond\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1}{8}\right) ≡ 0 ( mod 3 ) \displaystyle\equiv 0\pmod{3} where pend ⁡ ( n ) \pend(n) counts the number of PEND partitions of weight n n and pond ⁡ ( n ) \pond(n) counts the number of POND partitions of weight n n . In this work, we revisit these families of congruences, and we show a relationship between them via an Atkin–Lehner involution. From this relationship, we can show that, once one of the above families of congruences is known, the other follows immediately.

1.
Introduction

The theory of partition congruences has a long history, stretching back to Ramanujan’s groundbreaking work more than a century ago
[
34
]
. It is well-known that congruence relationships between the coefficients of modular forms vary substantially in their difficulty: some can be proved quite easily by techniques used by Ramanujan himself, while others continue to resist proofs to this day.

One important recent development in this subject is the study of
multiplicities
between modular congruences. This is the phenomenon in which a congruence property for a given linear progression in the coefficients of one modular form can manifest in another linear progression for the coefficients of a different modular form. Recent work in this subject include
[
10
]
,
[
16
]
(also referenced in
[
17
, Section 4]
), and
[
17
]
.

Here we report on the multiplicity between two families of congruences, modulo 3, exhibited by the PEND and POND partition functions that were originally studied by Ballantine and Welch
[
7
]
. What is especially interesting about this example is that the associated progressions corresponding to the POND partition function also exhibit a parity condition which the associated progressions for the PEND function lack. We show how the mapping used to demonstrate the multiplicity between these two sets of congruences must take this difference into account.

1.1.
PEND and POND Partitions

A partition
\lambda
of a positive integer
n
is a sequence
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{r})
such that
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{r}\geq 1
and
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\dots+\lambda_{r}=n
.
Partitions wherein the parts are distinct have long played a key role in the theory of partitions, dating back to Euler’s discovery and proof that the number of partitions of weight
n
into distinct parts equals the number of partitions of weight
n
into odd parts.

A clear refinement along these lines is to require distinct parts based on parity; i.e., to require either all of the even parts to be distinct or all of the odd parts to be distinct (while allowing the frequency of the other parts to be unrestricted). This leads to two types of partitions, those known as PED partitions (wherein the even parts must be distinct and the odd parts are unrestricted) and POD partitions (wherein the odd parts must be distinct and the even parts are unrestricted). We then define two corresponding enumerating functions,
\ped(n)
which counts the number of PED partitions of weight
n
, and
\mypod(n)
which counts the number of POD partitions of weight
n.
These two functions have been studied from a variety of perspectives; see, for example,
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
26
,
33
,
38
,
40
]
.

Recently, Ballantine and Welch
[
7
]
generalized and refined these two functions in numerous ways. One of the outcomes of their work was to consider PEND partitions and POND partitions, wherein the even (respectively, odd) parts are
not allowed
to be distinct. In a vein similar to that shared above, we let
\pend(n)
denote the number of PEND partitions of weight
n
, and
\pond(n)
denote the number of POND partitions of weight
n.
The first several values of
\pend(n)
appear in the OEIS
[
28
, A265254]
, while the first several values of
\pond(n)
appear in
[
28
, A265256]
.

In light of the work of Ballantine and Welch
[
7
]
, the first author
[
35
]

proved the following Ramanujan–like congruences satisfied by
\pond(n)
and
\pend(n)
:

Theorem 1.1
.

For all
n\geq 0,

\displaystyle\pond(3n+2)\equiv\

\displaystyle 0\pmod{2},

\displaystyle\pond(27n+26)\equiv\

\displaystyle 0\pmod{3},\textrm{\ \ and}

\displaystyle\pond(3n+1)\equiv\

\displaystyle 0\pmod{4}.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
n\geq 0,

\pend(27n+19)\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

In the same work
[
35
]
, he went on to prove the following infinite families of non–nested Ramanujan–like congruences modulo 3 by induction.

Theorem 1.3
.

For all
\alpha\geq 1
and all
n\geq 0,

\pond\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{23\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

Theorem 1.4
.

For all
\alpha\geq 1
and all
n\geq 0,

\pend\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{17\cdot 3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}\right)\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

All of the proof techniques used to prove Theorems
1.1
–
1.4

[
35
]
are elementary, relying on classical
q
-series identities and generating function manipulations, along with mathematical induction.

In the aforementioned work, little–to–no connection is made between PED, POD, PEND, and POND partitions, other than their obvious relationship in terms of the definition of the objects in question. In particular, no connection is drawn between the infinite families of congruences mentioned in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. In this work, the primary goal is to shine a light on an unexpected but clear relationship between the above families of congruences for the PEND and POND partitions by considering the corresponding generating functions as modular objects. In the process, we utilize an Atkin–Lehner involution to make the connection even more explicit, thus showing that the existence of one of these families of congruences immediately implies the other.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this work, we will use the following shorthand notation for
q
-Pochhammer symbols:

\displaystyle f_{r}:=(q^{r};q^{r})_{\infty}=(1-q^{r})(1-q^{2r})(1-q^{3r})\dots

For the work below, it will be very important to have the generating functions for the various partition functions in question. We now take a moment to collect those results here. We require one result multiple times below, so we explicitly mention it here.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

(-q;-q)_{\infty}=\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{1}f_{4}}.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma involves the following elementary manipulations. We have

\displaystyle(-q;-q)_{\infty}

\displaystyle=(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}(-q;q^{2})_{\infty}

\displaystyle=f_{2}\cdot\frac{(q^{2};q^{4})_{\infty}}{(q;q^{2})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=f_{2}\cdot\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q^{4};q^{4})_{\infty}}\cdot\frac{(q^{2};q^{2})_{\infty}}{(q;q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}^{3}}{f_{1}f_{4}}.

∎

Next, as was proven in
[
35
]
, we have the following generating functions for
\pend(n)
and
\pond(n)
, respectively.

Theorem 2.2
.

We have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pend(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{12}}{f_{1}f_{4}f_{6}},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pond(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{3}f_{12}}.

Given the close relationship of
\pend(n)
and
\pond(n)
, we might well suspect a natural way of mapping between these two generating functions. One possible approach, analogous to the methods used by Garvan and Morrow
[
16
]
, as well as Chern and Tang
[
10
]
, is to connect the two generating functions via an Atkin–Lehner involution. However, the inequality between the number of factors on the right-hand sides of (
2.1
), (
2.2
) suggests that a direct involution is not possible.

Instead, we note recent work between the authors and Garvan in
[
17
]
. We first consider replacing
q
by
-q
in Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 2.3
.

We have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\pend(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{1}f_{12}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\pond(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}f_{4}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}}.

Proof.

To prove (
2.3
) via elementary generating function manipulations, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pend(n)(-q)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\pend(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}f_{12}}{f_{4}f_{6}}\cdot\frac{1}{(-q;-q)_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{12}}{f_{4}f_{6}}\cdot\frac{f_{1}f_{4}}{f_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{1}f_{12}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}}.

Similarly, to prove (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pond(n)(-q)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\pond(n)q^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{12}}\cdot\frac{1}{(-q^{3};-q^{3})_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{12}}\cdot\frac{f_{3}f_{12}}{f_{6}^{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}f_{4}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}}.

∎

Notice the close resemblance between (
2.3
) and (
2.4
). It is much more reasonable to suspect a direct mapping between these two via an Atkin–Lehner involution. We will now show that this is in fact possible.

3.
Relating the PEND and POND Congruences via an Atkin–Lehner Involution

For this section we presume a knowledge of the theory of modular forms, especially with respect to the classical
U_{\ell}
operator for
\ell
a prime (for our purposes,
\ell=3
). In particular, we let
\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}(N)\right)
denote the space of all modular functions over the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(N)
. See
[
23
]
for a treatment of these concepts, as well as
[
14
]
for a more detailed treatment.

For our purposes one especially important property of the
U_{\ell}
operator is as follows
[
23
, Chapter 8]
: for a given power series
f(q)
in
q
, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle U_{\ell}\left(f\left(q^{\ell}\right)\right)=f(q).

This operator may be applied arbitrarily many times; indeed, we will define

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}(\cdot):

\displaystyle=U_{3}^{2\alpha+1}(\cdot)=U_{3}\circ U_{3}\circ...\circ U_{3}(\cdot),

in which
U_{3}
is applied
2\alpha+1
times in the latter expression.

With this composite operator, we now consider slight extensions of the generating functions of the
\pond
and
\pend
functions. Define

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(0)}:

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\cdot\frac{f_{3^{2\alpha+2}}^{3}}{f_{4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{3}f_{12}},

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(1)}:

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{7\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1}{8}}\cdot\frac{f_{3^{2\alpha+1}}^{2}f_{3^{2\alpha+2}}^{3}f_{4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}}^{2}}{f_{2\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}}^{6}}\cdot\frac{f_{2}f_{12}}{f_{1}f_{4}f_{6}}.

We have chosen these functions very precisely, so that by applying
U_{\alpha}
to
P_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}
and taking advantage of (
3.1
), we will obtain modular functions over
\Gamma_{0}\left(12\right)
. These resultant functions will enumerate the progressions of interest in Theorems
1.3
,
1.4
.

Indeed, if we take the standard substitution
q=\exp(2\pi i\tau)
with
\tau\in\mathbb{H}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta(3^{2\alpha+2}\tau)^{3}}{\eta(4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}\tau)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\eta(4\tau)\eta(6\tau)^{2}}{\eta(2\tau)^{2}\eta(3\tau)\eta(12\tau)},

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta(3^{2\alpha+1}\tau)^{2}\eta(3^{2\alpha+2}\tau)^{3}\eta(4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}\tau)^{2}}{\eta(2\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}\tau)^{6}}\cdot\frac{\eta(2\tau)\eta(12\tau)}{\eta(\tau)\eta(4\tau)\eta(6\tau)},

in which
\eta(\tau)
is the Dedekind eta function
[
23
, Chapter 3]
. It can be proved by standard techniques (e.g.,
[
27
, Theorem 1]
) that, for
\beta=0,1
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}\left(4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}\right)\right).

We now take advantage of a useful theorem
[
4
, Lemma 7]
:

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\ell
is a prime, and
N\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\ell^{2}|N
, then for any
f\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}\left(N\right)\right)
we have

\displaystyle U_{\ell}(f)\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}\left(N/\ell\right)\right).

If we denote

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=U_{\alpha}\left(P_{\alpha}^{(0)}\right),

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=U_{\alpha}\left(P_{\alpha}^{(1)}\right),

then by Theorem
3.1
we have

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}=U_{\alpha}\left(P_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}\right)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}\left(4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}/3^{2\alpha+1}\right)\right)=\mathcal{M}\left(\Gamma_{0}\left(12\right)\right).

Let us first examine the application of
U_{\alpha}
to
P_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}
. We have

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}\left(P_{\alpha}^{(0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}^{3}}{f_{4}^{2}}\cdot U_{\alpha}\left(q^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\cdot\frac{f_{4}f_{6}^{2}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{3}f_{12}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}^{3}}{f_{4}^{2}}\cdot U_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}\pond(n)q^{n+\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}^{3}}{f_{4}^{2}}\cdot U_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{n\geq\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\pond\left(n-\frac{\left(3^{2\alpha}-1\right)}{8}\right)q^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}^{3}}{f_{4}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{3^{2\alpha+1}n\geq\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\pond\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n-\frac{\left(3^{2\alpha}-1\right)}{8}\right)q^{n}.

Of course, rearranging the inequality restricting our sum, we have

\displaystyle 3^{2\alpha+1}n

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8},

\displaystyle 3^{2\alpha}(24n-1)

\displaystyle\geq-1,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\geq 1.

If we then shift our summand so that
n\geq 0
, then we derive the sequences of interest to us from Theorem
1.3
:

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}\left(P_{\alpha}^{(0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}^{3}}{f_{4}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 1}\pond\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n-\frac{\left(3^{2\alpha}-1\right)}{8}\right)q^{n},

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}^{3}}{f_{4}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}\pond\left(3^{2\alpha+1}(n+1)-\frac{\left(3^{2\alpha}-1\right)}{8}\right)q^{n+1},

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{3}^{3}}{f_{4}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}\pond\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{\left(23\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1\right)}{8}\right)q^{n+1}.

In similar manner, it can be shown that

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}\left(P_{\alpha}^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{1}^{2}f_{3}^{3}f_{4}^{2}}{f_{2}^{6}}\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}\pend\left(3^{2\alpha+1}n+\frac{\left(17\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1\right)}{8}\right)q^{n+1}.

Therefore,
L_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}
exhibits the subsequences of interest to us. As a consequence of Theorems
1.3
,
1.4
, we have already proved that

\displaystyle L_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}\equiv 0\pmod{3}.

for all
\alpha\geq 0
and
\beta\in\{0,1\}
.

We suspected a close relationship between these two functions. To approach that, let us first return to
P_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}
.
We define operator
\nu
, which acts on any power series in
q
by sending
q
to
-q
:

\displaystyle\nu:f(q)\rightarrow f(-q).

Next, we consider certain useful operators which we call
Atkin–Lehner involutions
. These were introduced in
[
4
]
, and are concerned primarily with the theory of cusp forms, but they are useful in the general theory of modular forms. We define them as follows (e.g.,
[
29
, Definition 2.19]
):

Definition 3.2
.

Let
N
be a positive integer,
p
a prime divisor of
N
, and
q=p^{k}
the maximum power of
p
which divides
N
. The associated Atkin–Lehner involution for
\Gamma_{0}(N)
is the operator induced by the matrix

\displaystyle W_{N,q}:=\begin{pmatrix}qA&B\\
NC&qD\end{pmatrix},

in which
A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\mathrm{det}(W)=q
.

These operators are well-defined irrespective of the precise choice of
A,B,C,D

[
4
, Lemma 10]
,
[
29
, Remark 2.20]
. We will use the Atkin–Lehner involution for
\Gamma_{0}\left(4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}\right)
and
q=4
, which we define with the following matrix:

\displaystyle W:=W_{4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2},4}=\begin{pmatrix}4&-1\\
4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}&1-3^{2\alpha+2}\end{pmatrix}.

We are interested in the conjugation mapping

\displaystyle\gamma:=\nu W\nu.

First we apply
\nu
to
P_{\alpha}^{(0)}
. Certainly the leading power of
q
will induce an alternating sign depending on
\alpha
. Moreover, we know that
\nu
will not affect any factor of the form
f_{k}
when
k
is even; and we know how
f_{k}
is to be transformed for odd
k
by recalling Lemma
2.1
above. We therefore have

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(0)}|_{\nu}=(-1)^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}q^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\cdot\frac{f_{2\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}}^{9}}{f_{3^{2\alpha+2}}^{3}f_{4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}}^{3}f_{4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{3}f_{4}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}}.

Now we need to apply the involution associated with
W
. In so doing, we utilize the transformation properties of the Dedekind eta function
[
23
, Chapters 3-4]
: for any
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}\right)
, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\eta\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right)=\left(-i(c\tau+d)\right)^{1/2}\cdot\delta(a,b,c,d)\cdot\eta(\tau),

in which we take the corresponding principal branch of the square root, and
\delta
is a certain 24th root of unity depending on
a,b,c,d
.

Of course,
W
is not a member of
\mathrm{SL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}\right)
, since it has determinant 4. But we can account for this in a straightforward manner. If
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{GL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}\right)
, and
g:=\mathrm{gcd}(A,C)
, we can express

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A/g&-y\\
C/g&x\end{pmatrix}.\begin{pmatrix}g&Bx+Dy\\
0&(AD-BC)/g,\end{pmatrix}

with
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
Ax+Cy=g
. Here
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A/g&-y\\
C/g&x\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}\left(2,\mathbb{Z}\right)
. For example, if we apply
W
to
\eta(6\tau)
, we have the corresponding matrix product

(3.4)

\displaystyle 6W=\begin{pmatrix}4\cdot 6&-6\\
4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}&1-3^{2\alpha+2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
3^{2\alpha+1}&\frac{1}{2}\left(1+3^{2\alpha+1}\right)\end{pmatrix}.\begin{pmatrix}12&-4\\
0&2\end{pmatrix}.

Notice that by combining (
3.2
) with (
3.4
), we see that the eta factor associated with
\eta(6W\tau)
will be
\eta((12\tau-4)/2)=\eta(6\tau-2)
. More generally, a straightforward computation from Definition
3.2
shows that if we apply an Atkin–Lehner operator with
\Gamma_{0}(N)
to
\eta(\delta\tau)
with
\delta|N
, we will produce an eta factor of the form
\eta(\lambda\tau+\kappa)
with
\lambda|N
and
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}
. We see that these operators are in a certain sense “well-behaved” when applied to eta quotients.

Thus we have a straightforward process of applying
W
to
P_{\alpha}^{(0)}|_{\nu}
. Doing so gives us the following:

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(0)}|_{W\circ\nu}

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\epsilon\cdot\frac{\eta\left(2\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}\tau-\frac{1}{2}\left(1+3^{2\alpha+2}\right)\right)^{9}}{\eta\left(4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}\tau-3^{2\alpha+2}\right)^{3}\eta\left(3^{2\alpha+2}\tau-\frac{3}{4}\left(1+3^{2\alpha+1}\right)\right)^{3}\eta\left(3^{2\alpha+1}\tau-\frac{1}{4}\left(1+3^{2\alpha+1}\right)\right)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\eta\left(\tau\right)\eta\left(12\tau-3\right)}{\eta\left(2\tau\right)^{2}\eta\left(6\tau-2\right)}

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\epsilon^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\eta\left(2\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}\tau\right)^{9}}{\eta\left(4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}\tau\right)^{3}\eta\left(3^{2\alpha+2}\tau\right)^{3}\eta\left(3^{2\alpha+1}\tau\right)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\eta\left(\tau\right)\eta\left(12\tau\right)}{\eta\left(2\tau\right)^{2}\eta\left(6\tau\right)}

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\epsilon^{\prime}\cdot q^{\frac{7\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1}{8}}\cdot\frac{f_{2\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}}^{9}}{f_{4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+2}}^{3}f_{3^{2\alpha+2}}^{3}f_{3^{2\alpha+1}}^{2}}\cdot\frac{f_{1}f_{12}}{f_{2}^{2}f_{6}}.

The Nebentypus
\epsilon^{\prime}
reduces to

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\prime}=(-1)^{\frac{7\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1}{8}}.

Thus, we can now apply
\nu
again, and we achieve the following:

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(0)}|_{\nu\circ W\circ\nu}

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\cdot q^{\frac{7\cdot 3^{2\alpha}+1}{8}}\cdot\frac{f_{3^{2\alpha+1}}^{2}f_{3^{2\alpha+2}}^{3}f_{4\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}}^{2}}{f_{2\cdot 3^{2\alpha+1}}^{6}}\cdot\frac{f_{2}f_{12}}{f_{1}f_{4}f_{6}}

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}}\cdot P_{\alpha}^{(1)}.

Finally, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{3^{2\alpha}-1}{8}\equiv\alpha\pmod{2},

from which we thus obtain

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}^{(0)}|_{\gamma}=2(-1)^{\alpha}\cdot P_{\alpha}^{(1)}.

At first this appears only to apply to the generating functions. However, we can take advantage of an extremely powerful theorem
[
4
]
:

Theorem 3.3
.

The operators
U_{\ell}
and
W
commute, provided that
\mathrm{gcd}(\ell,\mathrm{det}(W))=1
.

Moreover, the operators
U_{\ell}
and

4.
Closing Thoughts

4.1.
Multiplicities Hold Additional Arithmetical Information

The most striking aspect of these results is the discovery that congruence multiplicities can contain precise arithmetical information—indeed, more even than the common divisibility properties uniting two different congruences.

The usefulness of this property becomes clear when we consider the possibility of proving standing conjectures. Clearly one of the appeals to congruence multiplicity is that one can often prove a given congruence relation by appealing to work that has already been done, in lieu of a long and tedious direct proof.

Suppose we are studying a conjectured congruence with respect to a given prime—say, 3, as in our case. To apply a proof by multiplicity, we would of course look at previously established congruence results modulo 3 for other partitions, in the hope of finding a connection with our conjectured result. What is interesting here is that these previous results can involve more complex relationships—say, divisibility by 6, or 15, or 21—even if the newly conjectured result lacks such additional divisibility properties.

In this manner, one has a broader array of tools for resolving a given congruence conjecture.
