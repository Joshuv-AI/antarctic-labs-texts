An extension of Liebmann’s Theorem to surfaces with boundary
Flávio França Cruz

Abstract
. Liebmann’s Theorem is a fundamental result stating that a compact connected convex surface in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} with constant mean curvature (cmc) is a totally umbilical sphere. We extend this theorem to surfaces with boundary by showing that a locally convex, embedded compact connected cmc surface, bounded by a convex curve in a plane P P , is contained in one of the halfspaces of ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} determined by P P . This extension leads to the conclusion that spherical caps are the only examples of locally convex, embedded compact connected cmc surfaces with non-zero curvature, bounded by a circle.

53C42, 35J60

1.
Introduction

Surfaces of constant mean curvature, abbreviated by
cmc surfaces
, are among the most important objects studied in differential geometry. A cmc surface is characterized by the fact that it is a critical point of the area functional with respect to local deformations that preserve the enclosed volume. Surfaces with this property serve as mathematical models for soap bubbles in physics.
The model for a soap bubble is a closed (that is, compact and without boundary) cmc surface while the model for a soap bubble which has its boundary on a round hoop is a compact cmc surface with circular boundary. Experimental observation leads to the question if round spheres are the unique closed cmc surfaces and if spherical caps and discs are the only compact cmc surfaces bounded by a circle. Answering these questions is one of the motivations that started the study of the differential geometry of cmc surfaces. Nowadays, after has been developed over the years by several outstanding mathematicians, the theory of cmc surfaces is extremely rich.

A notable result in the theory of cmc surfaces, established by Liebmann
[
18
]
in 1900, states that a closed and convex cmc surface must be a sphere.
Later, Hopf
[
12
]
proved that the sphere is the unique closed cmc surface with genus zero and, soon after, Alexandrov
[
1
,
2
]
showed that the only closed cmc surface embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the sphere. More recently, Barbosa and do Carmo
[
6
]
proved that an immersed closed stable cmc surface must be a sphere.

However, when we consider the corresponding problem for compact surfaces with non-empty boundary, it is still unknown whether the spherical caps are the only examples of compact embedded surface with non-zero constant mean curvature bounded by the circle. In
[
15
]
Kapouleas showed that there exist examples of higher genus, compact and non-spherical immersed surfaces with constant mean curvature in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
bounded by a circle. It was conjectured in
[
9
]
the so called
Spherical cap conjecture:

Conjecture 1.1
.

A compact non-zero cmc surface bounded by a circle is a spherical cap if either of the following conditions hold:

i) The surface has genus zero and is immersed;

ii) The surface is embedded.

Let
M
be a compact embedded non-zero cmc surface with boundary
\partial M=C
a circle in the plane
\Pi
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. If
M
is contained in one of the two halfspaces determined by
P
, the Alexander reflection method
[
1
,
2
]
immediately proves
M
has the planar reflectional symmetries of
C
. Hence,
M
is a surface of revolution. Since the only compact cmc surfaces of revolution are spherical caps (by Delaunay’s classification of cmc surfaces of revolution), Conjecture
1.1
holds for the subclass of surfaces that are embedded and contained in a halfspace. It is therefore of interest to obtain natural geometric conditions that force a compact embedded cmc surface to be contained in a halfspace. In this direction, Koyso
[
17
]
proved that a sufficient condition for such an embedded non-zero cmc surface
M
with boundary
\partial M=C
a planar curve in a plane
P
to be contained in a halfspace is assume that
M
does not intersect the outside of
C
in
P
. Later, Brito, Sá Earp, Meeks and Rosenberg showed in
[
9
]
that if
M
is a compact embedded cmc surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\partial M=C
is a convex curve contained in a plane
P
, which is transverse to
M
along the boundary
\partial M
, then
M
lies on one side of
P
. More recently, Alías, López and Palmer proved that the umbilical examples are the only cmc stable immersed discs bounded by a circle
[
3
]
. Other interesting partial results were obtained in
[
4
,
5
,
8
]
. We refer the reader to
[
19
]
for a broad overview on the Spherical cap conjecture.

Remarkably, to date there is not a boundary version for one of the very first rigidity results proved for closed cmc surfaces, namely, the Liebmann’s theorem.

This is the problem we deal with in this work. Before to present our main result we recall that a surface is called locally convex if its principal curvatures are all nonnegative.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
C
be a strictly convex closed curve included in a plane
P
. Let
M
be a connected compact embedded cmc surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with boundary
C
. If
M
is locally convex, then
M
lies on one side of
P
. In particular, if
C
is a circle, then
M
is a planar disc or a spherical cap.

An outline of the proof is as follows: First,
applying a constant rank theorem due Bian-Guan
[
7
]
and a Simons’ identity, we establish that a compact embedded locally convex cmc surface
M
bounded by a convex closed curve included in a plane
P
is locally strictly convex on its interior and its asymptotic directions on the boundary are necessarily tangent to the boundary.

This result allow us to prove that
M
lives in one of the two halfspace determined by
P
locally along the the boundary. Finally, using the Alexandrov reflection method and the flux formula for cmc surfaces we can show that
M
is contained in one of the two halfspace determined by
P
.

2.
Boundary behavior

Let
M
be a connected compact embedded cmc surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with boundary
C
,
where

If the mean curvature of

First we prove the following

Proposition 2.1

Let

be as in Theorem
1.2

is locally strictly convex on its interior and its asymptotic directions on the boundary are necessarily tangent to the curve

Proof.

First we notice that

has an interior elliptic point, that is, there is an interior point of

where all the principal curvatures are positive. In fact, since

is not part of a plane, then we easily find a radius

and a point

contains

and there is a point
q_{0}\in\textrm{int}(M)\cap\partial B_{R}(q)
(englobe
M
with spheres of large radius until such a sphere touches
M
on one side at an interior point). Hence, the rank of the second fundamental form
h
of

is
2
at
q_{0}
then, it follows by the constant rank theorem of Bian and Guan (Theorem 1.5 in
[
7
]
) that the rank of
h
is constant and equal to
2
in the interior of
M
.
Now let
p\in\partial M
be such that
\det(h_{ij})(p)=0
, where
h=(h_{ij})
are the components of
h
in local frame of
M
. We choose an orthornormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of

around

such that
e_{1}=\gamma^{\prime}
and
e_{2}=\nu
on
C
, where
\gamma
is an arc lengh parametrization of
C
, and
\nu
stands for the inner unit conormal vector of
M
along
C
.
We claim that the multiplicity of first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(p)
of
(h_{ij})(p)
is one.

In fact, if
h_{11}(p)=0
then
h_{12}(p)=0
and, for any
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, one has

h_{ij}(p)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}=h_{22}(p)\xi_{2}^{2}=2H\xi_{2}^{2}

is minimized (for
|\xi|=1
) when
\xi=(1,0)
. Therefore, the dimension of the eigenspace for
\lambda_{1}(p)=0
is one. On the other hand, if
h_{11}(p)>0
then

\displaystyle h_{ij}(p)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}

\displaystyle=h_{11}(p)\xi_{1}^{2}+2h_{12}(p)\xi_{1}\xi_{2}+\dfrac{h_{12}^{2}(p)}{h_{11}(p)}\xi_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{h_{11}(p)}\xi_{1}+\dfrac{h_{12}^{2}(p)}{\sqrt{h_{11}(p)}}\xi_{2}\right)^{2}.

Then
h_{ij}(p)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}
is minimized when
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
, with
|\xi|=1
, is given by

\xi_{1}=-\dfrac{h_{12}(p)}{h_{11}(p)}\xi_{2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\xi_{2}=\left(1+\left(\dfrac{h_{12}(p)}{h_{11}(p)}\right)^{2}\right)^{-1}.

Thus the claim is proven. Let
\kappa_{1}\leq\kappa_{2}
be the principal curvatures of
M
. Since
\kappa_{1}
vanishes if and only if
\lambda_{1}
vanishes, it follows that
\kappa_{1}(p)=0
and
\kappa_{2}(p)=2H>0.
Therefore, we may choose a small neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
p
in
M
such that the smallest principal curvature
\kappa_{1}
is smooth on
\mathcal{N}
. Now we need the following

Lemma 2.2
.

For a dense open set of
M
we have

(2.1)

\Delta\kappa_{1}+|h|^{2}\kappa_{1}=2H\kappa_{1}^{2}-2\dfrac{(h_{12,1})^{2}+(h_{12,2})^{2}}{\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1}}\leq 2H\kappa_{1}^{2},

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator of
M
.

Proof.

First we recall the Simons’ identity for the Laplacian of the second fundamental form
h
of a CMC surface (see
[
16
]
)

(2.2)

\Delta h+|h|^{2}h=2Hh^{2}.

For a dense open set in
M
, we can choose a smooth orthonormal frame
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of eigenvectors of
h=(h_{ij})
corresponding to the ordered principal curvatures
\kappa_{1}\leq\kappa_{2}
of
M
. See
[
21
]
for the existence of such a frame. Thus, in term of this frame, one can rewrite (
2.2
) as

(2.3)

\Delta h_{ij}+|h|^{2}h_{ij}=2Hh_{ij}^{2}\delta_{ij}.

For
i=j=1
one has

(2.4)

\sum_{k=1}^{2}h_{11,kk}+|h|^{2}\kappa_{1}=2H\kappa_{1}^{2}.

Since
\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{1}
is orthogonal to
e_{1}
, for
k=1,2
, we have

\displaystyle h_{11,k}=\left(\nabla_{e_{k}}h\right)(e_{1},e_{1})=e_{k}(h_{11})-2h\left(\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{1},e_{1}\right)=e_{k}(\kappa_{1})

and

\displaystyle h_{12,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{e_{k}}h\right)(e_{1},e_{2})=e_{k}(h_{12})-h\left(\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{1},e_{2}\right)-h\left(e_{1},\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\kappa_{2}\langle\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{1},e_{2}\rangle-\kappa_{1}\langle\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{2},e_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2})\langle\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{1},e_{2}\rangle.

Then

\displaystyle h_{11,kk}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{e_{k}}\nabla_{e_{k}}h\right)(e_{1},e_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{k}\left(h_{11,k}\right)-2\left(\nabla_{e_{k}}h\right)(\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{1},e_{1})-\left(\nabla_{\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{k}}h\right)(e_{1},e_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{k}\left(e_{k}(\kappa_{1})\right)-2\langle\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{1},e_{2}\rangle h_{12,k}-\left(\nabla_{e_{k}}e_{k}\right)(\kappa_{1}).

Therefore

\sum_{k=1}^{2}h_{11,kk}=\Delta\kappa_{1}+2\dfrac{(h_{12,1})^{2}+(h_{12,2})^{2}}{\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1}}.

Applying this into (
2.4
) we obtain (
2.1
).
∎

From (
2.1
) we have

(2.5)

\Delta\kappa_{1}+(|h|^{2}-2H\kappa_{1})\kappa_{1}\leq 0

in
\mathcal{N}
. However,

(2.6)

|h|^{2}-2H\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}\left(\kappa_{2}-\kappa_{1}\right)>0,

and so from (
2.5
) we conclude that
\kappa_{1}\geq 0
satisfies

(2.7)

\Delta\kappa_{1}\leq 0\quad\textrm{in}\quad\mathcal{N}.

Since

, the Hopf boundary point lemma implies
|\nabla\kappa_{1}(p)|\neq 0
. As
\kappa_{1}
is smooth in
\mathcal{N}
, by the Implicit Function Theorem, the set

is a smooth curve passing through

. If

is tranversal to

then we have a contradiction since

in the interior of

. Thus,

is tangente to

.

is locally contained in one halfspace of

along

.

First we need to introduce some notations. The orientation of

Proof of Theorem
1.2

Following the lines of
[
8
]
,we prove that
