Intentional subintimal loop technique for orientation recovery in superficial femoral artery chronic total occlusion with pedal-origin controlled antegrade and retrograde subintimal tracking
Authors: Tae-Hoon Kim, Ji Bak Kim, Ho-Jun Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic total occlusion (CTO) of the superficial femoral artery (SFA) with flush occlusion and complete loss of the true lumen orientation remains a major technical challenge during endovascular interventions. Antegrade crossing may be hindered by ambiguous proximal caps and extensive collateral formation; however, intravascular imaging guidance is not always feasible. The case of a patient with a flush-occluded mid-SFA CTO in whom both antegrade and retrograde guidewire escalation failed to establish true lumen orientation is reported. External vascular ultrasound guidance and bidirectional wiring also fail to reliably identify the true position of the lumen in either direction. To overcome this limitation, an intentional subintimal loop technique using an 0.014-inch guidewire was used to restore spatial orientation and facilitate controlled advancement. Subsequently, a pedal-origin controlled antegrade and retrograde subintimal tracking technique was performed using a 4.0-mm balloon inflated at the popliteal artery, enabling successful guidewire connection and lesion traversal. The occluded SFA was treated with a drug-coated balloon to achieve satisfactory angiographic results. At the 6-month follow-up, computed tomography angiography demonstrated sustained patency of the treated SFA segment and preservation of the pedal access vessel. This case highlights the utility of an intentional subintimal loop technique to regain orientation in complex SFA CTOs when conventional strategies fail and suggests that pedal-origin controlled antegrade and retrograde subintimal tracking using a 4.0-mm balloon may represent a feasible bailout option without compromising distal vessel integrity.Keywords: Superficial femoral artery, Chronic total occlusion, Intentional subintimal loop, Pedal access, Controlled antegrade and retrograde subintimal tracking

Endovascular treatment of superficial femoral artery (SFA) chronic total occlusion (CTO) has evolved with advances in guidewire technology, reentry devices, and intravascular imaging.1,2 However, flush occlusion with complete loss of true lumen orientation remains one of the most challenging anatomical scenarios, particularly when collateral vessels originate immediately distal to the site of occlusion. In such cases, antegrade guidewire advancement may be repeatedly diverted into the collateral channels; however, retrograde access does not always guarantee successful reentry.

Although external vascular ultrasound (EVUS) guidance wiring can facilitate lesion crossing, these tools are not universally available or applicable.3 In such scenarios, a bidirectional approach through pedal access may be a useful strategy when conventional approaches fail.4 However, when both antegrade and retrograde wiring fail to provide spatial orientation, operators are often left without a clear strategy.

Herein, a case of a flush-occluded mid-SFA CTO in which the conventional antegrade and retrograde approaches failed is presented. The use of an intentional subintimal loop technique to restore orientation is described, followed by a pedal-origin controlled antegrade and retrograde subintimal tracking (CART) technique using a 4.0-mm balloon, resulting in successful revascularization with durable midterm patency.

Case report

A heavy-smoking patient with hypertension and dyslipidemia transferred to our department with lifestyle-limiting claudication of the left lower extremity (Rutherford category 3). He was initially admitted after trauma and was diagnosed with fractures of the left superior and inferior pubic ramus and a probable left iliac bone fracture. Conservative treatment with approximately 8 weeks of stabilization was recommended. During rehabilitation, however, the patient developed claudication of the left lower extremity that significantly interfered with ambulation and further rehabilitation progress. Preprocedural computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated complete flush occlusion of the mid-SFA with distal reconstitution of the popliteal artery via the collateral vessels arising from the deep femoral artery (Fig 1, A). All three below-the-knee arteries were patent. Because the patient was still recovering from multiple traumatic injuries and claudication limited rehabilitation, endovascular revascularization was considered.

Vascular access was obtained under US guidance using a 6F short sheath via an antegrade approach to the left common femoral artery. Initial angiography confirmed complete mid-SFA occlusion with extensive collateral formation directly connected to the occlusion site (Figs 2, A, B). A prominent collateral vessel originating immediately distal to the occlusion repeatedly diverted the guidewire from the occluded segment, preventing direct lesion traversal (Fig 2, C). To overcome guidewire slippage into the collateral channels at the proximal hard cap, guidewire tip stiffness was increased to facilitate penetration of the lesion (0.014 Hi-Torque Command guidewire, Abbott Vascular) and then an 0.018″ Halberd guidewire (Asahi). After successful crossing of the proximal cap, the guidewire was exchanged for wires with moderate-to-low tip stiffness to advance distally; however, guidewire manipulation in the intended distal direction remained difficult.

(C) A collateral vessel originating immediately distal to the mid-SFA occlusion diverts the guidewire into the collateral channel, thereby preventing direct traversal of the occluded segment.

External vascular US examination confirmed that the proximal guidewire was positioned within the mid-SFA; however, distal vessel visualization was insufficient to guide further advancement (Figs 3, A, B). Given the persistent crossing failure, retrograde pedal access via the anterior tibial artery was obtained after US confirmation of an adequate vessel diameter and flow.

From the pedal puncture site, a sheathless retrograde approach was performed, and a guidewire (0.014 Hi-Torque Command guide wire; Abbott Vascular) was advanced through a microcatheter (0.014″ 90 cm, CXI angled support catheter; Cook Medical). At the occluded segment, guidewire escalation was attempted using an 0.014" Halberd guide wire (Asahi Intecc) and subsequently an 0.014″ Astato XS 20 guidewire (Asahi) to advance proximally. Although the antegrade and retrograde guidewires appeared parallel, the left anterior oblique projection revealed a marked spatial discrepancy between the two wires. Despite the bidirectional wiring, the gap between the antegrade and retrograde guidewires was significant, making it impossible to reliably determine whether either wire was located within the true lumen (Fig 4, A). At this stage, an intentional subintimal loop was created using a 0.014-inch guidewire (0.014″ Regalia XS 1.0; Asahi) to restore spatial orientation and provide a directional scaffold. The retrograde 0.014” soft guidewire advanced from the pedal artery maintained a subintimal loop configuration while progressing proximally along the presumed trajectory of the SFA. By following this imagined vessel course, the looped guidewire effectively served as a dynamic reference for the expected path of the artery. Once the retrograde loop approached the antegrade guidewire, it suggested that both wires were likely located within the vessel wall rather than outside the arterial structure. This process restored spatial confidence regarding vessel orientation and allowed the operators to proceed with balloon-assisted reverse CART or CART. To minimize access vessel injury, reverse CART was initially attempted; however, repeated efforts failed to achieve true lumen reentry, and snare capture of the retrograde wire was unsuccessful (Figs 4 and 5, B-E).

(C) After balloon inflation, the antegrade guidewire was successfully advanced into the true lumen of the distal target vessel. (D) A drug-coated balloon was subsequently inflated along the previously occluded segment of the superficial femoral artery (SFA). (E) Final digital subtraction angiography demonstrates restored antegrade flow with satisfactory distal runoff through the revascularized SFA.

This was interpreted as entrapment of the antegrade and retrograde guidewires within separate subintimal spaces. In such a situation, several potential strategies may be considered. One approach is to increase the balloon size to intentionally enlarge the proximal subintimal dissection and attempt reentry of the distal guidewire. Another option is to target the inflated balloon and attempt puncture from the distal side using a reentry device. However, both approaches may increase the risk of subintimal hematoma formation or vessel perforation. Therefore, rather than aggressively escalating balloon size or using a reentry device, we performed a CART technique by inflating a balloon at the point where the distal pedal-origin guidewire entered the true lumen to direct the proximally trapped guidewire into the distal channel (Figs 5 A, B). Although concerns exist regarding potential injury to the pedal artery during balloon retrieval after deflation, this risk was considered acceptable in the present case, because there was no significant arterial stenosis proximal to the dorsalis pedis artery and US evaluation demonstrated a relatively large vessel diameter, allowing minimization of vascular injury.

This maneuver allowed the successful advancement of the antegrade guidewire into the distal true lumen (Fig 5, C). The occluded SFA segment was subsequently treated with a drug-coated balloon (5 mm × 200 mm Ranger DCB balloon; Boston Scientific) (Fig 5, D), which achieved satisfactory final angiographic results with good distal runoff (Fig 5, E).

At the 6-month follow-up, CTA demonstrated sustained patency of the treated SFA segment and preservation of the pedal-access vessel (Fig 1, B). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Discussion

Endovascular repair is generally considered the standard treatment for aortoiliac occlusive disease. However, in femoropopliteal disease, its durability remains relatively limited compared with surgical bypass; therefore, it has traditionally been considered an alternative treatment option.5 Recent randomized trials have demonstrated superior long-term patency of autologous vein bypass compared with endovascular therapy in selected patients with femoropopliteal occlusive disease. Accordingly, surgical bypass using a native vein should be considered as a primary treatment option when appropriate.6 Nevertheless, in patients who require a less invasive approach or in whom suitable vein conduit is unavailable, prosthetic bypass does not consistently demonstrate superior outcomes compared with endovascular treatment. In such situations, an endovascular-first strategy may be reasonable. In addition, reconstruction using drug-coated balloons preserves future treatment options because it avoids permanent implants within the vessel. In this context, the present case highlights a technical strategy that may help to overcome complex guidewire orientation challenges during endovascular treatment of long femoropopliteal CTOs.

CTO of the SFA, accompanied by a complete loss of luminal orientation, remains one of the most challenging scenarios in endovascular intervention. Although bidirectional wiring and wire escalation strategies are commonly used to improve crossing success, these approaches may paradoxically exacerbate spatial ambiguity when both antegrade and retrograde guidewires are entrapped in separate subintimal planes. In these situations, reliable identification of the true lumen and controlled guidewire connections may be exceedingly difficult.

Subintimal angioplasty was first introduced by Bolia et al7 as an alternative strategy to reduce procedural time by intentionally creating an extraluminal channel rather than persistently pursuing intraluminal passage. Subsequent studies demonstrated that, particularly in long femoropopliteal CTOs, subintimal approaches achieve high technical success rates with acceptable midterm patency, especially in the contemporary stent era.7 However, subintimal techniques have traditionally been discussed primarily as a means to facilitate lesion crossing, rather than as a deliberate strategy to restore spatial orientation in cases of severe guidewire misalignment.

In the present case, intentional subintimal loop formation was not performed as a substitute crossing strategy after intraluminal failure, but rather as a purposeful maneuver to reestablish spatial orientation between the antegrade and retrograde guidewires trapped in different subintimal spaces. The loop functioned as a directional scaffold, allowing visualization of the vessel course and providing a reference framework for subsequent reentry. This conceptual use of intentional subintimal looping to regain orientation distinguishes the present case from conventional subintimal angioplasty and represents its primary technical contribution.

Intravascular US (IVUS) examination has been proposed as an adjunctive tool for guidewire orientation and lumen identification in femoropopliteal CTOs.8,9 However, evidence supporting its routine use for orientation recovery remains limited, and its effectiveness is highly dependent on the presence of a discernible distal lumen. In the present case, the limitation of IVUS was not conceptual, but device specific. The currently available peripheral IVUS systems in our country include platforms from Boston Scientific and Volcano. Although the Volcano system, with its shorter distance between the catheter tip and US transducer, may allow assessment in selected occlusive settings, the Boston Scientific IVUS system has a relatively long distal catheter tip. This structural characteristic limits its ability to provide meaningful spatial orientation within a completely occluded segment, particularly when IVUS examination is used with the primary intent of defining the vessel course rather than luminal assessment. Consequently, the IVUS-guided orientation was not feasible in this case, necessitating alternative treatment strategies. Similarly, EVUS examination has recently been recognized as an important adjunctive strategy to assist in guidewire navigation and vessel orientation in selected cases. However, its utility is highly dependent on the lesion location. Unlike lesions located immediately distal to the common femoral artery or within the popliteal segment, obstructions involving the mid-to-distal SFA are subject to significant limitations in US visualization owing to vessel depth.3 Consequently, in cases such as the present one, EVUS examination can provide only limited guidance and is helpful in a very restricted subset of patients.

In this respect, pedal access has been increasingly used in complex peripheral artery disease interventions, particularly as a bailout approach when conventional antegrade techniques fail. A sheathless technique was intentionally used to minimize vessel trauma in the small-caliber distal anterior tibial artery. Nevertheless, we recognized that advancement and retrieval of the balloon through the pedal artery could still pose a potential risk of vessel injury. Previous studies have consistently reported a low incidence of pedal vessel occlusion, and, when occlusion occurs, it is frequently asymptomatic.10 Furthermore, we have previously reported preserved pedal vessel patency after the use of a pedal-origin outback reentry catheter, supporting the safety of this access route in appropriately selected patients.11 Based on these considerations, the use of a 4.0-mm balloon for pedal-origin CART was considered reasonable in the present case, given the absence of proximal dorsalis pedis artery stenosis and adequate vessel caliber confirmed by initial CT angiography.

Consistent with this rationale, follow-up CTA at 6 months demonstrated well-preserved patency of the anterior tibial artery, suggesting that pedal-origin CART with appropriately sized balloons can be performed safely without compromising distal vessel integrity in selected patients.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that, in the setting of complete loss of luminal orientation during SFA CTO intervention, intentional subintimal loop formation combined with pedal-origin CART may serve as an effective bailout strategy. By restoring spatial orientation and enabling a controlled guidewire connection, this approach expands the functional role of subintimal techniques beyond lesion crossing and highlights their potential utility in highly complex endovascular scenarios.
