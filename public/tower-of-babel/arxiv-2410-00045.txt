Abstract
. The recently introduced equivariant BV formalism is extended to the case of manifolds with boundary under appropriate conditions. AKSZ theories are presented as a practical example.



1.
Introduction

The BV formalism is a general method to describe field theory with symmetries. In the presence of a boundary of space–time, it gives rise—under some regularity assumptions—to a nice coupling with the boundary BFV formalism (the cohomological description of the reduced phase space). It was studied in
[
CMR14
]
and called the BV-BFV formalism (see also
[
CM20
]
for an introduction). This is also the starting point for perturbative quantization in the presence of a boundary
[
CMR11
,
CMR17
]
.

In
[
Bon+20
]
the BV formalism was extended to the equivariant case arising when a vector field (more generally, an involutive family of vector fields) on space–time is given.

In this paper, we study the extension of the equivariant BV formalism in the presence of a boundary and obtain the conditions under which the construction leading to the equivariant BV-BFV formalism works.

To summarize the result, just recall that, in its most general formulation, what is studied in
[
Bon+20
]
is a violation of the classical master equation of the BV formalism. Equivalently, one drops the condition that the BV charge
\mathcal{Q}
(the Hamiltonian vector field of the BV action) is cohomological (i.e.,
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]=0
). We show that the BV-BFV construction works through if
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]
is a symplectic vector field (or, equivalently, a Hamiltonian vector field) also in the presence of a boundary (in the absence of a boundary this is automatically true).

The particularly important case of the equivariant extension
[
Bon+20
]

of AKSZ theories
[
Ale+97
]
then turns out to be compatible with the boundary if and only if all the given vector fields in the involutive family are tangent to the boundary.

The original results of this paper are in Section
4
. In the previous sections we recall background material: Section
2
covers the BV formalism, the BV-BFV formalism, the equivariant BV formalism, and their quantization, whereas Section
3
gives more details on the AKSZ construction.

2.2.
Quantum BV Formalism

In the quantum setting, we consider the operator
\Delta
, the canonical
BV operator
(or
BV Laplacian
) introduced by Khudaverdian in
[
Khu04
]
, acting on half-densities and
formally extended to the infinite-dimensional setting. There are several ways of characterizing this operator. One characterization is to choose a symplectomorphism
\mathcal{F}\to T^{*}[-1]N
, for some manifold
N
, invoking the global Darboux theorem for odd symplectic manifolds
[
Sch93
]
. Then we can observe that half-densities are canonically identified with differential forms on
N
. Thus, we can consider
\Delta
as the de Rham differential on
N
. Another characterization, on a finite-dimensional odd-symplectic supermanifold
(\mathcal{M},\omega)
with local coordinates
(x_{i},\theta^{i})
, where
x_{i}
denote the even coordinates and
\theta^{i}
denote the corresponding odd momenta (i.e.,
\omega=\sum_{i}{\mathrm{d}}\theta_{i}\land{\mathrm{d}}x^{i}
), is that it is given by

\Delta=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial\theta^{i}}.

Its fundamental algebraic property is
\Delta^{2}=0
.

We often need the BV operator to act on functions. For this we pick a nowhere vanishing half-density
\mu
satisfying
\Delta\mu=0
. Such a reference half-density exists (e.g., by the symplectomorphism from
\mathcal{F}\to T^{*}[-1]N
).
Using this we can define a BV operator
\Delta_{\mu}
acting on functions via

\Delta_{\mu}f\coloneqq(\Delta f\mu)/\mu.

This operator satisfies

\Delta^{2}=0
and

\Delta_{\mu}(fg)=\Delta_{\mu}fg\pm f\Delta_{\mu}g\pm(f,g).

It can also be computed by

(2.2.1)

\Delta_{\mu}f=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{div_{\mu^{2}}}(f,\kern 5.0pt),\quad f\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{F}),

where
\mathrm{div}_{\mu^{2}}
denotes the divergence operator on vector fields determined by the density
\mu^{2}
.
When the reference half-density is understood, we will simply write
\Delta
also for the BV operator acting on functions.

See also
[
Cat23
]
for a detailed exposition on the BV Laplacian and
[
Šev06
]
for another mathematical interpretation.

An important observation by Schwarz
[
Sch93
]
(in the finite-dimensional case) is that, assuming orientation, a half-density on
\mathcal{F}
determines a density on every Lagrangian submanifold of
\mathcal{F}
.

Theorem 2.2.1

(Batalin–Vilkovisky–Schwarz

]
)

Consider two half-densities

on an odd-symplectic supermanifold

. Then

7

7

We assume below that the given half-densities have fast decay at infinity.

(1)

if

(

\int_{\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{M}}f=\int_{\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{M}}\Delta g=0,

for any Lagrangian submanifold

.

(2)

if
\Delta f=0
(

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\int_{\mathcal{L}_{t}\subset\mathcal{M}}f=0,

for any differentiable family
(\mathcal{L}_{t})

\mathcal{M}
. In particular, when two Lagrangian submanifolds

For the setting of quantum gauge field theories, we are mainly interested in the case where our odd-symplectic supermanifold is given by a pair
(\mathcal{F},\omega)
and the half-density

\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}S\right)\rho\in\mathrm{Dens}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{F}),

where

. Then, when considering an integral of the form

, the choice of Lagrangian submanifold corresponds to choosing a gauge fixing

8

8

When choosing a gauge-fixing fermion

.
Note that the second point of Theorem

Note that, in the closed setting, we have required that the CME

Indeed, one can easily prove

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}=\pi^{*}\left(2\mathcal{S}^{\partial}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\alpha^{\partial}\right)

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\pi^{*}\mathcal{S}^{\partial}.

Both equations express the violation of the CME by boundary terms.

We recall their proofs because in the equivariant case we will generalize them.
We know that

L_{\mathcal{Q}}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta-\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}

and that the same holds for
\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}
. We will now apply
L_{\mathcal{Q}}
to the equation
\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\delta\mathcal{S}+\pi^{*}\alpha^{\partial}
. By the definition of
L_{\mathcal{Q}}
and since
\mathcal{Q}^{2}=0
, we get
[L_{\mathcal{Q}},\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}]=L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}=0
and we know that

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\pi^{*}\delta\alpha^{\partial}=\pi^{*}\omega^{\partial}.

Thus, we get

(2.3.3)

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\pi^{*}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\delta\alpha^{\partial}.

If we then apply the Lie derivative
L_{\mathcal{Q}}
to the CME, we get

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=L_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\mathcal{S}+L_{\mathcal{Q}}\pi^{*}\alpha^{\partial}=\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\mathcal{S}+\pi^{*}(L_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\alpha^{\partial})\\
=\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}+\pi^{*}\Big(\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\delta\alpha^{\partial}-\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\alpha^{\partial}\Big).

In the last line, we used Cartan’s magic formula for
L_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}
. Now, using (
2.3.3
) and
\delta\mathcal{S}^{\partial}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\omega^{\partial}
, we get

\pi^{*}(\delta\mathcal{S}^{\partial})=\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}+\pi^{*}(\delta\mathcal{S}^{\partial})-\pi^{*}(\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\alpha^{\partial}),

which is equivalent to

\delta\left(L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}-\pi^{*}(2\mathcal{S}^{\partial}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\alpha^{\partial})\right)=0.

This means that function in the argument of
\delta
is constant. However, since it is a function of degree
+1
is zero, it must vanish. Therefore, we get (
2.3.1
)
proving that the CME is spoiled only up to boundary data. To get (

3.
AKSZ Theories

3.1.
Short Overview

An important class of BV theories were developed by Alexandrov, Kontsevich, Schwarz and Zaboronsky in
[
Ale+97
]
and known today as AKSZ theories. They formulated a method to obtain a solution of the CME by considering a special form for the space of fields, namely, a mapping space (actually, a space of internal homs) between graded manifolds. Let us first describe the ingredients needed to formulate these type of theories. Let
M
be a closed, connected
d
-manifold and consider a differential graded symplectic manifold
(\mathcal{M},\omega)
where the symplectic form is exact, i.e.,
\omega={\mathrm{d}}\alpha
, and of degree
d-1
. Moreover, let
\Theta
be a function on
\mathcal{M}
of degree
d
such that
\{\Theta,\Theta\}_{\omega}=0
, where
\{\kern 5.0pt,\kern 5.0pt\}_{\omega}
denotes the Poisson bracket of degree
1-d
induced by
\omega
. Moreover, consider the Hamiltonian vector field
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}
for the Hamiltonian function
\Theta
which is cohomological by definition, i.e.,
(\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}})^{2}=0
. Define the space of fields as

\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{M}:=\mathrm{Map}(T[1]M,\mathcal{M}).

Using the symplectic structure on
\mathcal{M}
, we can construct a symplectic structure on
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{M}
by
transgression
. Namely, we have a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{Map}(T[1]M,\mathcal{M})\times T[1]M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{Map}(T[1]M,\mathcal{M})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{ev}}

where
\mathsf{ev}
denotes the evaluation map and
\pi
the projection onto the first factor. Thus, on the level of forms, we can define a
transgression map

\mathbb{T}\colon\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathcal{M})\to\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathrm{Map}(T[1]M,\mathcal{M}))
by

\mathbb{T}(\eta):=\pi_{*}\mathsf{ev}^{*}\eta=\int_{T[1]M}\mathsf{ev}^{*}\eta,\quad\eta\in\Omega^{\bullet}(\mathcal{M}).

Hence, we define a symplectic form on
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{M}
by

\omega_{M}:=\mathbb{T}(\omega)=\int_{T[1]M}\mathsf{ev}^{*}\omega,

where
\omega
is the symplectic form on
\mathcal{M}
. Note that since
\omega
was of degree
d-1
, we get that
\omega_{M}
is of degree
(d-1)-d=-1
which is the correct degree for a BV symplectic form.
We can define a cohomological vector field on the mapping space as the sum of the lift of the de Rham differential on
M
and the lift of the cohomological vector field on
\mathcal{M}
:

\mathcal{Q}_{M}:=\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}_{M}}+\widehat{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{M}}},

where the hats denote the lift to the mapping space.
The BV action is then constructed by using the primitive
\alpha
of
\omega
and the Hamiltonian function
\Theta
:

\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{M}=\iota_{\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}_{M}}}\mathbb{T}(\alpha)+\mathbb{T}(\Theta).

It is then not difficult to see that
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{M}
is indeed of degree
0
and satisfies the CME.

Remark 3.1.1
.

A lot of relevant theories are of AKSZ type, such as e.g., Chern–Simons theory
[
CS74
,
Wit89
,
AS91
,
AS94
]
, the Poisson sigma model
[
Ike94
,
SS94
,
CF01
]
, or Witten’s
A
- and
B
-twisted sigma models
[
Wit88
,
Ale+97
]
.

4.
Equivariant BV-BFV Formalism

4.1.
Equivariant BV Formalism

In this section we recollect the results of

[
Bon+20
]
on the equivariant BV formalism.
The easiest way to prepare the background material is to start from the quantum version. We begin with the very general observation of what happens when the QME is violated. Namely, we introduce a functional
\mathcal{T}
, with
\gh(\mathcal{T})=1
, that measures the failure of the QME:

\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\left(\frac{\hbar}{{\mathrm{i}}}\right)^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}\right)\Delta\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S})-{\mathrm{i}}\hbar\Delta\mathcal{S}.

It turns out that the properties of the BV integral are still preserved if we only allow
\mathcal{T}
-Lagrangian submanifolds, i.e., Lagrangian submanifolds on which
\mathcal{T}
vanishes.

If we in addition assume that
\Delta\mathcal{S}=0
, as is often the case, or just ignore higher orders in
\hbar
, then we simply get

(4.1.1)

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S})=2\mathcal{T},

which expresses
\mathcal{T}
as the violation of the CME. This is the equation we are going to study in the presence of boundary.

4.2.
Preliminary Computations

We want to start with some important definitions and relations in order to understand the the core of the computation for the case with boundary.

Definition 4.2.1

(Weak BV manifold)
.

A
weak BV manifold
of degree
k
is a quadruple
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{Q},\omega,\mathcal{S})
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a graded manifold,
\mathcal{Q}
a vector field on
\mathcal{M}
,
\omega
a symplectic form on
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{S}
a function on
\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathrm{gh}(\omega)=k-1
,
\mathrm{gh}(\mathcal{S})=\mathrm{gh}(\omega)+1
,
\mathrm{gh}(\mathcal{Q})=1
, and
L_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega=0
.

An example of a weak BV manifold is a BV manifold, where, in addition,
\mathcal{Q}
is required to be the Hamiltonian vector field of
\mathcal{S}
and
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]=0
. We will see that a weak BV manifold contains anyway enough structure to be worth studying. The failure of
\mathcal{Q}
’s being the Hamiltonian vector field of
\mathcal{S}
is what typically happens in a field theory with boundary and gives rise to the BV-BFV formalism. The failure of the condition
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]=0
is what occurs in the equivariant BV formalism, where the condition is replaced by the weaker one
L_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega=0
.

Lemma 4.2.2
.

If
k\not=-1
, we get that
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]
is Hamiltonian w.r.t.
\omega
.

Proof.

Let
E
be the Euler vector field and define
\mathcal{H}:=\iota_{E}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega
. Then
\delta\mathcal{H}=\delta\iota_{E}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega=(k+1)\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega
.
∎

Remark 4.2.3
.

If we consider a BF
k
V theory
[
CMR14
]
, we never consider the case of
k=-1
. Usually, we have
k\geq 0
.

To motivate the above convention, suppose that
\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\delta\mathcal{S}
and
\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S})=\mathcal{T}
, i.e., equation (
4.1.1
). Then
\mathcal{T}=\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}=\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\mathcal{S}
. Hence, we get that (
4.1.1
) is equivalent to

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\mathcal{T}.

In turn, this is equivalent to

(4.2.1)

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega=-\delta\mathcal{T},

a characterization of the violation of the CME as in (
4.1.1
) that will be more appropriate to treat the case when a boundary is present. To prove this, simply observe that we have

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega=\frac{1}{2}[L_{\mathcal{Q}},\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}]\omega=\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega-\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega}_{=0}=\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\mathcal{S}=-\frac{1}{2}\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S},

where in the first equality we have used the following

Lemma 4.2.4
.

We have
\underbrace{[L_{\mathcal{Q}},\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}]}_{=L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}}=\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}
.

Proof.

Since we are proving an equality of derivations, it is enough to check it on functions and on exact 1-forms.
Note that for any function
f
we get
[L_{\mathcal{Q}},\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}]f=0=\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}f
. Hence, we get
[L_{\mathcal{Q}},\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}]=\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}
on functions. Moreover, we have

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta f=L_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}f-\frac{1}{2}L_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}f=\frac{1}{2}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\delta f=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta f\\
=-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}f=-L_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}f+\underbrace{\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}f}_{=0}=-\frac{1}{2}L_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}f=-\frac{1}{2}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\delta f.

Thus, we get that
[L_{\mathcal{Q}},\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}]=\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}
on exact 1-forms.
∎

4.3.
Classical Equivariant Setting

In the presence of a boundary, the equation
\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\delta\mathcal{S}
is usually violated, so we define
\underline{\alpha}:=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega-\delta\mathcal{S}
and
\underline{\omega}:=\delta\underline{\alpha}
. Then we have that
\mathrm{gh}(\underline{\alpha})=k
.

In general,
\mathcal{Q}
is not cohomological. However, the fundamental assumption for what we call boundary-compatible equivariant BV formalism is that
[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]
is Hamiltonian, i.e., we assume that (
4.2.1
) still holds.

Proposition 4.3.1
.

We have that
\underline{\omega}=-L_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega
.

Proof.

Note that

\underline{\omega}=\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=(\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta)\omega=-L_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega.

∎

Lemma 4.3.2
.

We have that
L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}=0
.

Proof.

Note that

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}=-L_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=-\frac{1}{2}[L_{\mathcal{Q}},L_{\mathcal{Q}}]\omega=-\frac{1}{2}L_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega=0.

∎

Lemma 4.3.3
.

If
k\not=-1
, there exists a unique
\underline{\mathcal{S}}
such that
\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}=\delta\underline{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.

Let
E
be the Euler vector field and define
\mathcal{H}:=\iota_{E}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}
. Then, we get

\delta\mathcal{H}=L_{E}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}=(k+1)\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}.

Hence, we have
\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{k+1}\mathcal{H}
with
\mathrm{gh}(\underline{\mathcal{S}})=k+1
.
∎

Lemma 4.3.4
.

We have

\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}=\delta\left(2\underline{\mathcal{S}}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+2\mathcal{T}\right).

Proof.

Note that we have

\delta\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}=-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=-[\iota_{\mathcal{Q}},L_{\mathcal{Q}}]\omega-L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega\\
=\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega-L_{\mathcal{Q}}(\underline{\alpha}+\delta\mathcal{S})=\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\underline{\alpha}+\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}\\
=\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}+\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}=-2\delta\mathcal{T}+\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}-\delta\underline{\mathcal{S}}.

Hence, we get that
2\delta\underline{\mathcal{S}}=-2\delta\mathcal{T}+\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}
.
∎

Proposition 4.3.5
.

If
k\not=-1
, we get that

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}=2\underline{\mathcal{S}}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+2\mathcal{T}.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\mathrm{gh}(L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S})=k+1
.
∎

Lemma 4.3.6
.

We have that

\delta\left(\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega\right)=\delta\left(\underline{\mathcal{S}}+\mathcal{T}\right).

Proof.

Note that we have

\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\left(\underline{\alpha}+\delta\mathcal{S}\right)=\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}\\
=\delta\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+\delta\left(2\underline{\mathcal{S}}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\alpha}+2\mathcal{T}\right)=2\delta\underline{\mathcal{S}}+2\delta\mathcal{T}

∎

Theorem 4.3.7
.

If
k\not=-1
, we get that

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\underline{\mathcal{S}}+\mathcal{T}.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\mathrm{gh}(\underline{\mathcal{S}})=k+1
.
∎

Remark 4.3.8
.

We will use this formula as

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega-\mathcal{T}=\underline{\mathcal{S}},

which shows that the modification of the equivariant CME
\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\mathcal{T}
is the boundary term
\underline{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proposition 4.3.9
.

We have that

\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\mathcal{S}}=-L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{T}.

Proof.

Note that we have

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}=-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}[\iota_{\mathcal{Q}},L_{\mathcal{Q}}]\omega-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega\\
=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\omega-[\iota_{\mathcal{Q}},L_{\mathcal{Q}}]\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega-L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=-2\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\mathcal{T}+\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega-L_{\mathcal{Q}}\left(2\underline{\mathcal{S}}+2\mathcal{T}\right)\\
=-2\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\mathcal{T}-2\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\mathcal{T}-L_{\mathcal{Q}}\left(2\underline{\mathcal{S}}+2\mathcal{T}\right)=-2L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{T}-2L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{T}-2L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\mathcal{S}}-2L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{T}.

Hence, we get that

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\mathcal{S}}=-2L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{T}.

∎

Definition 4.3.10
.

We define
\underline{\mathcal{T}}:=-L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{T}
, which implies the equation

(4.3.1)

\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\underline{\mathcal{T}}.

We call this the
boundary BFV equivariant CME
.

Remark 4.3.11
.

Using the boundary Poisson bracket, we can also rewrite (
4.3.1
) as

13

13

\underline{\omega}
is actually degenerate, but this bracket is well-defined because

possesses a Hamiltonian vector field.

\frac{1}{2}\{\underline{\mathcal{S}},\underline{\mathcal{S}}\}=\underline{\mathcal{T}}.

Lemma 4.3.12
.

We have that

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\underline{\omega}=-\delta\underline{\mathcal{T}}.

Proof.

Note that we have

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}]}\underline{\omega}=\frac{1}{2}[L_{\mathcal{Q}},\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}]\underline{\omega}=\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}}_{=0}\\
=\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\delta\underline{\mathcal{S}}=-\frac{1}{2}\delta L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\mathcal{S}}=-\delta\underline{\mathcal{T}}.

∎

4.4.
Reduction to the Boundary

We now want to turn to the construction of the symplectic form on the boundary fields.

Definition 4.4.1
.

We define the kernel of
\underline{\omega}
as

\ker\underline{\omega}:=\{Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{F})\mid\iota_{Y}\underline{\omega}=0\}.

Lemma 4.4.2
.

The kernel of
\underline{\omega}
is involutive.

Proof.

This follows immediately since
\delta\underline{\omega}=0
.
∎

Assume now that the leaf space of the foliation, i.e., the quotient space
\mathcal{F}^{\partial}:=\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{Y}
, is smooth. Moreover, if we denote by
\pi\colon\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}^{\partial}
the projection, then, for a uniquely determined symplectic form
\omega^{\partial}
, we have
\underline{\omega}=\pi^{*}\omega^{\partial}
.

Lemma 4.4.3
.

The vector field
\mathcal{Q}
is projectable.

Proof.

Let
Y\in\ker\underline{\omega}
. Then we get

\iota_{[\mathcal{Q},Y]}\underline{\omega}=\pm[L_{\mathcal{Q}},\iota_{Y}]\underline{\omega}=\pm\underbrace{L_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{Y}\underline{\omega}}_{=0}\pm\underbrace{\iota_{Y}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}}_{=0}=0.

Hence we get that
[\mathcal{Q},Y]\in\ker\underline{\omega}
, which proves the statement.
∎

Denote by
\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}
the vector field on
\mathcal{F}^{\partial}
such that

\delta\pi\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}.

Lemma 4.4.4
.

The function
\underline{\mathcal{S}}
is basic.

Proof.

Let
Y\in\ker\underline{\omega}
. Then, using Lemma
4.3.2
, we get that

L_{Y}\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\iota_{Y}\delta\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\iota_{Y}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\omega}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{Y}\underline{\omega}=0.

∎

Then we can write
\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\pi^{*}\mathcal{S}^{\partial}
for a uniquely determined function
\mathcal{S}^{\partial}
. Thus, we get

\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\omega^{\partial}=\delta\mathcal{S}^{\partial}

and

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega-\mathcal{T}=\pi^{*}\mathcal{S}^{\partial}.

Lemma 4.4.5
.

The function
\underline{\mathcal{T}}
is basic.

Proof.

Let
Y\in\ker\underline{\omega}
. Then we get that

L_{Y}\underline{\mathcal{T}}=\frac{1}{2}L_{Y}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{2}[L_{Y},L_{\mathcal{Q}}]\underline{\mathcal{S}}=\pm\frac{1}{2}L_{[Y,\mathcal{Q}]}\underline{\mathcal{S}}=0.

∎

Let
\mathcal{T}^{\partial}
be the corresponding function on
\mathcal{F}^{\partial}
. Then we can write

\underline{\mathcal{T}}=\pi^{*}\mathcal{T}^{\partial}.

Thus, we get that

\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\mathcal{S}^{\partial}=\mathcal{T}^{\partial},

and

L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{T}=-\pi^{*}\mathcal{T}^{\partial}.

Moreover, we get

\frac{1}{2}\big\{\mathcal{S}^{\partial},\mathcal{S}^{\partial}\big\}=\mathcal{T}^{\partial},

where
\{\kern 5.0pt,\kern 5.0pt\}
again denotes the Poisson bracket on
\mathcal{F}^{\partial}
induced by
\omega^{\partial}
, and

\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\left[\mathcal{Q}^{\partial},\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}\right]}\omega^{\partial}=-\delta\mathcal{T}^{\partial}.

4.5.
Summary

Let us summarize what we have so far. On
\mathcal{F}^{\partial}
we have
\omega^{\partial}
with
\mathrm{gh}(\omega^{\partial})=0
,
\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}
with
\mathrm{gh}(\mathcal{Q}^{\partial})=1
,
\mathcal{S}^{\partial}
with
\mathrm{gh}(\mathcal{S}^{\partial})=1
and
\mathcal{T}^{\partial}
with
\mathrm{gh}(\mathcal{T}^{\partial})=1
. Moreover, the following equations hold:

(4.5.1)

\displaystyle\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\omega^{\partial}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathcal{S}^{\partial},

(4.5.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\mathcal{S}^{\partial}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}^{\partial},

(4.5.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big\{\mathcal{S}^{\partial},\mathcal{S}^{\partial}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}^{\partial},

(4.5.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\left[\mathcal{Q}^{\partial},\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}\right]}\omega^{\partial}

\displaystyle=-\delta\mathcal{T}^{\partial}.

Furthermore, if we denote by
\pi\colon\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}^{\partial}
the projection, we get the following equations:

(4.5.5)

\displaystyle\delta\pi\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}^{\partial},

(4.5.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega-\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=\pi^{*}\mathcal{S}^{\partial},

(4.5.7)

\displaystyle L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=-\pi^{*}\mathcal{T}^{\partial}.

Finally, if
\omega^{\partial}=\delta\alpha^{\partial}
with
\underline{\alpha}=\pi^{*}\alpha^{\partial}

(4.5.8)

\displaystyle\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega

\displaystyle=\delta\mathcal{S}+\pi^{*}\alpha^{\partial},

(4.5.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}

.

This construction will naturally lead to a quantization, where the boundary BFV operator
\Omega
is given (as a first approximation) by the standard-order (a.k.a. Schrödinger) quantization of
\mathcal{S}^{\partial}
, i.e.,
\Omega=\widehat{\mathcal{S}^{\partial}}
,

Here
\widehat{\kern 5.0pt}
denotes the standard-order operator quantization.
with the property

\Omega^{2}=\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\partial}}.

Moreover, we expect the state

\psi=\int_{\mathcal{L}}\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S},

with Lagrangian submanifold
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{F}
such that
\mathcal{T}|_{\mathcal{L}}=0
, to satisfy

\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\partial}}\psi=0,

where
\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\partial}}
satisfies the equation

15

15

This is an extra assumption, not a general property.

\left(\Omega-\mathrm{i}\hbar\Delta\right)\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\partial}}=0.

We will return to this below.

Now assume that
\omega^{\partial}=\delta\alpha^{\partial}
with
\underline{\alpha}=\pi^{*}\alpha^{\partial}
. Then we get that the following two equations are satisfied:

\displaystyle\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega

\displaystyle=\delta\mathcal{S}+\pi^{*}\alpha^{\partial},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}+\pi^{*}\left(\mathcal{S}^{\partial}-\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}}\alpha^{\partial}\right).

4.6.
Prequantization

Now we want to turn to the quantization in the presence of boundary. We refer to
[
CMW22
, Section 9.2]
for more details.
Assume that we can choose a polarization such that
\mathcal{F}^{\partial}=T^{*}\mathcal{B}
. Moreover, we assume a splitting

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{B}.

We can then split the cohomological vector field and the symplectic form as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{Y}}+\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}},

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{Y}}+\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{B}}+\omega_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}.

Now we use
\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega=\delta\mathcal{S}^{f}+\pi^{*}\alpha^{f}
, where
\alpha^{f}
is adapted to the foliation and we have
16
16

16

i.e.,
\alpha^{f}
is the canonical 1-form of
T^{*}\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{f}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}+\pi^{*}f,

\displaystyle\alpha^{f}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\partial}-\delta f.

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{Y}}+\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}}\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{S}^{f},

\displaystyle\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{B}}+\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}}\omega_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathcal{S}^{f}+\alpha^{f}.

We assume a
good
splitting, i.e., one with the property that
\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}}\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{B}}=0
. Then we get that

\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{Y}}=\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{S}^{f}.

Hence, we get

\left(\mathcal{S}^{f},\mathcal{S}^{f}\right)_{\mathcal{Y}}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{Y}}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}}\omega-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}}\omega_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}.

This implies that

\left(\mathcal{S}^{f},\mathcal{S}^{f}\right)_{\mathcal{Y}}=2\mathcal{T}+\underbrace{2\mathcal{S}_{\text{BFV}}-\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}}\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathcal{B}}}\omega_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}}_{=:2\mathcal{S}^{\partial}},

which means that

\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{S}^{f},\mathcal{S}^{f}\right)_{\mathcal{Y}}=\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{S}^{\partial}.

We thus get

(4.6.1)

\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}=-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\left(\mathcal{S}^{f},\mathcal{S}^{f}\right)_{\mathcal{Y}}\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}\\
=-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\mathcal{T}\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\mathcal{S}^{\partial}\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}.

If we further assume that the splitting is discontinuous, we get
\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{B}}=0
and
\omega_{\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}}=0
. The vanishing of
\omega_{\mathcal{Y}\mathcal{B}}
implies that
\mathcal{S}^{\partial}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{BFV}}
. Thus, we get

\delta_{\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{S}^{f}=-\alpha^{f}=-\sum_{i}p_{i}{\mathrm{d}}q_{i}.

Hence, we have

\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}}{\delta q_{i}}=-p_{i}.

In this case, we can define

\Omega:=\widehat{\mathcal{S}^{f}}

as the Schrödinger quantization. That is, the operator quantization of
q\in\mathcal{B}
is given by multiplication with
q
and the operator quantization of
p\in T^{*}_{q}\mathcal{B}
is given by
\mathrm{i}\hbar\frac{\delta}{\delta q}
, together with the standard ordering. Then we have

\Omega\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}=\mathcal{S}^{\partial}\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}.

Finally, this implies the
equivariant
modified quantum master equation (emQME)

\left(\Omega+\hbar^{2}\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\right)\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}=-\mathcal{T}\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}.

Thus, the assumptions for equivariant quantization in the BV-BFV formalism are given by the equations

(4.6.2)

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\partial}},

(4.6.3)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{T}}\exp\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}^{f}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.6.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}|_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{Y},\psi}}

\displaystyle=0.

4.7.
Equivariant AKSZ Theories

We want to apply the equivariant BV-BFV formalism to AKSZ theories. Consider as the space of fields the mapping space

\mathcal{F}_{M}=\mathrm{Map}(T[1]M,N)

for a target Hamiltonian manifold
(N,\omega={\mathrm{d}}\alpha,\Theta)
. The AKSZ-action
\mathcal{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}
is then such that

\left(\mathcal{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}},\mathcal{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}\right)=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\omega_{M}=\delta\mathcal{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}},

if
\partial M=\varnothing
and such that

\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\omega_{M}=\delta\mathcal{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}+\pi^{*}\alpha^{\partial}_{\partial M},

if
\partial M\not=\varnothing
. Note that
\mathcal{Q}_{M}
is the corresponding cohomological vector field of
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{M}
and
\omega_{M}
is the symplectic structure on
\mathcal{F}_{M}
constructed out of
\omega
as we have seen before.

We first recall the equivariant extension of an AKSZ theory in the case
\partial M=\varnothing
, as introduced in
[
BCZ23
]
. Assume
\alpha^{\partial}_{\partial M}=\sum_{i}x_{i}{\mathrm{d}}y_{i}
. For
v\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, we define

\mathcal{S}_{\iota}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}\coloneqq\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{v}\mathbf{Y}_{i}.

Thus, we define
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}:=\mathcal{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}+u\mathcal{S}_{\iota}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}
, where
u
is a variable of degree
+2
. When
\partial M=\varnothing
, we have

(4.7.1)

\displaystyle\iota_{\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}}\omega_{M}

\displaystyle=\delta\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}},

(4.7.2)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{Q}_{M}+\mathcal{Q}_{\iota},

(4.7.3)

\displaystyle\left(\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}},\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}\right)

\displaystyle=u\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}},

(4.7.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}L_{v}\mathbf{Y}_{i},

(4.7.5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iota_{\left[\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{M},\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}\right]}\omega_{M}

\displaystyle=u\delta\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}},

where
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{M}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\iota}
are the Hamiltonian vector fields associated to
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{M}
,
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{M}
and
\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{\iota}
, respectively.
Note that, using the general notation, we have
\mathcal{T}=-u\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}
.

Remark 4.7.1
.

For simplicity we discuss here only the case of a single vector field
v
. The general case, also discussed in
[
BCZ23
]
, of an involutive family
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{r}\}
of vector fields is treated similarly. One just has to introduce variables
u_{1},\dots,u_{r}
of degree
+2
and define

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{\iota}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}&\coloneqq\sum_{\mu=1}^{r}u_{\mu}\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{v_{\mu}}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\\
\intertext{and}\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}&\coloneqq\sum_{\mu=1}^{r}u_{\mu}\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}L_{v_{\mu}}\mathbf{Y}_{i}.\end{split}

All the formulae above immediately generalize and, in particular, we get
\mathcal{T}=-\widetilde{\mathcal{S}}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}
. For the rest of the paper, we will focus on the case of a single vector field.

When
\partial M\not=\varnothing
, we have to check whether we still have an equivariant BV theory, namely, that (
4.7.5
) still holds. We start with computing

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\delta\mathbf{X}_{i}L_{v}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\pm\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}L_{v}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\delta\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{v}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{Y}_{i}+\int_{M}\delta\mathbf{X}_{i}{\mathrm{d}}\iota_{v}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\pm\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{v}{\mathrm{d}}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\pm\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\mathrm{d}}\iota_{v}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}.

If
v
is tangent to the boundary, then we get

\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{v}{\mathrm{d}}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\pm\int_{M}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\mathrm{d}}\iota_{v}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\\
=\pm\int_{M}\iota_{v}\mathbf{X}_{i}{\mathrm{d}}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\pm\int_{M}{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\iota_{v}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\pm\int_{\partial M}\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{v}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\\
=\pm\int_{M}{\mathrm{d}}\iota_{v}\mathbf{X}_{i}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\pm\int_{M}\iota_{v}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\pm\int_{\partial M}\big(\iota_{v}\mathbf{X}_{i}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}+\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{v}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}\big).

In this case, (
4.7.5
) holds.

Note that in general,
the integration by parts gives rise to the term
\int_{\partial M}\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{\partial}^{*}(\iota_{v}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i})
, where
\iota_{\partial}
denotes the inclusion map
\partial M\hookrightarrow M
.
If
v
were not tangent to the boundary, in addition to the good term displayed above, this would also contain a term

\int_{\partial M}\mathbf{X}_{i}\iota_{\partial}^{*}(v^{n}\delta\mathbf{Y}_{i}^{n})
, where
n
denotes the transversal components. In conclusion, we have the

Theorem 4.7.2
.

An equivariant AKSZ theory defined in terms of a vector field
v
is boundary-compatible if and only if
v
is tangent to the boundary.

Note that in the general case of Remark
4.7.1
the theory is boundary-compatible if and only if all the
v_{\mu}
s are tangent to the boundary.

From now on we then assume that
v
is tangent to the boundary.
We have

\iota_{\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}}\omega_{M}=\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\omega_{M}+u\iota_{\mathcal{Q}_{\iota}}\omega_{M}=\delta\mathcal{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}+\pi_{M}^{*}\alpha^{\partial}_{\partial M}+u\mathcal{S}_{\iota}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}=\delta\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{M}+\pi_{M}^{*}\alpha^{\partial}_{\partial M},

where
\alpha^{\partial}_{\partial M}
is the Noether 1-form of AKSZ theories, and
\mathcal{Q}^{\partial}_{\partial M}
is the cohomological vector field on the boundary of AKSZ theories. Thus, we have

\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{\partial,\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{\partial M}=\mathcal{S}^{\partial,\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{\partial M}+\mathcal{S}^{\partial,\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{\iota}.

We can now compute

L_{\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}}\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}=L_{\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}+u\underbrace{L_{\mathcal{Q}_{\iota}}\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}}_{\big(\mathcal{S}_{\iota}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}},\,\mathcal{S}_{L}^{\mathrm{AKSZ}}\big)=0}=\pm\int_{\partial M}\mathbf{X}_{i}L_{v}\mathbf{Y}_{i}.

This implies that

\frac{1}{2}\left\{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{\partial,\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{\partial M},\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{\partial,\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{\partial M}\right\}=u\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{\partial,\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{L},

which yields
\mathcal{T}^{\partial}=u\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{\partial,\mathrm{AKSZ}}_{L}
.

4.8.
Equivariant Abelian
BF
Theory

BF
theory is an AKSZ theory, so we explicitly have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{M}

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\big(\langle\mathbf{B},{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{A}\rangle+u\langle\mathbf{B},\iota_{v}\mathbf{A}\rangle\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{M}

\displaystyle=-u\int_{M}\langle\mathbf{B},L_{v}\mathbf{A}\rangle,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{S}}_{\partial M}^{\partial}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial M}\big(\langle\mathbf{B},{\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{A}\rangle+u\langle\mathbf{B},\iota_{v}\mathbf{A}\rangle\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\partial M}^{\partial}

\displaystyle=-u\int_{\partial M}\langle\mathbf{B},L_{v}\mathbf{A}\rangle.

We split the boundary
\partial M
into two disjoint components
\partial_{1}M
and
\partial_{2}M
. We choose the
\mathbf{A}
-polarization on one of them and the
\mathbf{B}
-polarization on the other. By the Schrödinger quantization we get

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle={\mathrm{i}}\hbar\left(\int_{\partial_{1}M}\big({\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{A}+u\iota_{v}\mathbf{A}\big)\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbf{A}}+\int_{\partial_{2}M}\big({\mathrm{d}}\mathbf{B}+u\iota_{v}\mathbf{B}\big)\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbf{B}}\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\partial}}

\displaystyle=u{\mathrm{i}}\hbar\left(\int_{\partial_{1}M}L_{v}\mathbf{A}\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbf{A}}+\int_{\partial_{2}M}L_{v}\mathbf{B}\frac{\delta}{\delta\mathbf{B}}.\right)

We clearly have
\Omega^{2}=\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\partial}}
.

To compute the state
\psi
, we have to pick a
v
-invariant metric and then proceed as in
[
CMR17
, Section 4]
using the Hodge decomposition. In particular, we get a propagator
\eta
which satisfies

{\mathrm{d}}\eta=\chi_{i}\otimes\chi^{i},\qquad L_{v}\eta=0,

where
\{\chi_{i}\}
and
\{\chi^{i}\}
are the chosen bases of harmonic forms relative to the boundary. We have
L_{v}\chi_{i}=L_{v}\chi^{i}=0
for all
i
. Finally, we define

\mathbf{a}=z^{+}_{i}\chi^{i},\qquad\mathbf{b}=z^{i}\chi_{i},

where
\{z_{i},z^{+}_{i}\}
are the Darboux coordinates for the BV space of residual fields. With these notations, we have
\psi=\tau\exp\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{\hbar}\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}
with

\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}=\int_{\partial_{1}M}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{b}+\int_{\partial_{2}M}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{B}+\int\mathbf{A}\eta\mathbf{B}+u\int_{M}\mathbf{b}\iota_{v}\mathbf{a}\\
+\sum_{n>1}u^{n}\left(\int\mathbf{A}\eta\iota_{v}\eta\cdots\iota_{v}\eta\mathbf{B}+\int\mathbf{A}\eta\iota_{v}\eta\cdots\iota_{v}\eta\mathbf{b}+\int\mathbf{a}\eta\iota_{v}\eta\cdots\iota_{v}\eta\mathbf{B}\right)

and
\tau
the Ray–Singer torsion of
M
with boundary.
One can easily check that

\widehat{\mathcal{T}^{\partial}}\psi=0,\qquad\big(\Omega+\hbar^{2}\Delta_{\text{res}}\big)\psi=0,

where
\Delta_{\text{res}}
denotes the BV Laplacian on residual fields.
