Factors affecting time-to-disposition at a district hospital emergency centre in Cape Town, South Africa
Authors: Gordon-Forbes CJ, Hoffe ME, Van Hoving DJ, Hendrikse C
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEmergency centre crowding adversely affects the quality of care, and the wellbeing of both patients and healthcare providers. The interplay between emergency centre crowding and clinicians’ time-to-disposition remains poorly understood, with evidence of complex associations involving system-level variables. This study describes and quantifies factors influencing time-to-disposition at a district-level hospital emergency centre in Cape Town, South Africa.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of an existing dataset comprising all emergency centre consultations in a Cape Town District Hospital Emergency Centre over three randomly selected four-week periods in 2019. A retrospective chart review was performed on a random subsample (n=1,000) to assess the impact of special investigations. Multivariable binary logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of prolonged time-to-disposition.ResultsA total of 9,356 consultations were analysed. The overall median (IQR) time-to-disposition was 95 (33–241) minutes; 32.8% exceeded three hours. Triage acuity was the strongest independent predictor of time-to-disposition exceeding three hours in multivariable analysis (Emergent vs Routine: aOR 5.30, 95% CI 3.72–7.55; Very Urgent: aOR 3.61, 95% CI 2.67–4.89), paediatric age group (aOR 0.30) and trauma presentation (aOR 0.65) were independently protective. Registrar (resident) managed consultations were independently associated with prolonged time-to-disposition (aOR 1.35, 95% CI 1.18–1.55). Special investigations were the dominant predictor in the investigations subsample (aOR 6.96, 95% CI 4.43–10.94), with laboratory turnaround, median 198 (127–301) minutes from specimen collection to registration, identified as the principal bottleneck. Very urgent presentations (47.2% of patients) and admitted patients (36.5%) represented the highest-impact subgroups by combined burden of prolonged time-to-disposition and patient volume.DiscussionTriage acuity, age group, trauma category, clinician category, and special investigations were independently associated with time-to-disposition. Laboratory turnaround is a major modifiable contributor to throughput delays. Interventions targeting high-impact subgroups, particularly very urgent presentations, admitted patients, and those requiring laboratory investigations, offer the greatest potential for improving throughput.Keywords: Emergency medicine, Crowding, Patient flow, Disposition, Length of stay, Access block

Introduction

Emergency Centre (EC) crowding is a ubiquitous challenge to healthcare systems globally, and influences quality of care. The American College of Emergency Physicians defined EC overcrowding as “when the identified need for emergency services exceeds available resources for patient care in the emergency department, hospital, or both”[1]. Crowding and increased EC length of stay (ECLOS) are both associated with increased mortality and hospital length of stay [[2], [3], [4]]. EC crowding is associated with patients being classified as higher acuity during triage, higher hospital admission rates, transfer delays and inadequate pain management [[5], [6], [7]]. There is an urgent need to address this challenge, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where there is a growing mismatch between capacity and demand.

Accurately measuring EC crowding is essential to facilitate interventions. While measures of EC crowding are largely not standardised in the literature, the National Emergency Department Overcrowding Scale (NEDOCS) combines various measures but is yet to be validated locally [8]. System measures such as EC bed occupancy and number of boarders, and throughput measures such as waiting time, time-to-disposition (the time between the start of clinician consultation and the disposition decision, which is the final determination of a patient’s ongoing care or discharge), boarding time and ECLOS are most often used [9]. This approach aligns with the input-throughput-output model of EC flow [9,10]. Due to the input and output components of this model being largely driven by community and hospital factors respectively, the throughput component, involving all processes between arrival and disposition, represents the greatest opportunity for EC-driven interventions to reduce crowding [10].

The causal relationships between time-to-disposition and other crowding measures are complex and likely bidirectional. Hoot et al [11], demonstrated that while time-to-disposition increased proportionally with the number of patients awaiting disposition, paradoxically, higher NEDOCS scores were associated with reduced time-to-disposition at one study site. These divergent relationships between time-to-disposition and other crowding metrics underscore the complex interplay between emergency department throughput and hospital surge capacity.

There is a paucity of data that describes how time-to-disposition contributes to EC crowding as well as the factors that influence it, especially in LMICs. Although a prolonged time-to-disposition may contribute to crowding and its negative consequences, interventions should be balanced against the risks of an incomplete workup to patient safety and hospital length of stay. This study aims to describe overall time-to-disposition at a district-level EC, to identify factors associated with variations in time-to-disposition, and to quantify their independent contributions through multivariable analysis.

Methods

A secondary analysis of an existing dataset was conducted to describe time-to-disposition in the EC. The dataset included all consultations in the EC over three randomly selected four-week periods in 2019. A retrospective chart review was subsequently conducted to assess the impact of special investigations on time-to-disposition. Multivariable binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with prolonged time-to-disposition.

This study was conducted in the adult and paediatric ECs at Mitchells Plain Hospital (MPH) in Cape Town, South Africa. MPH is a district (entry)-level hospital 32km from Cape Town and serves a population of approximately 800,000 residents consisting of predominantly low- to middle-income families.

EC clinicians working at MPH are divided into teams, comprising a combination of the following clinician categories: community service medical officers (CSMO) (junior medical officers in their first year of unsupervised practice), medical officers, emergency medicine registrars (specialist trainees/ residents) and interns (newly qualified clinicians in supervised practice). Each team is allocated to a generic roster revolving over 4-week cycles. There are various shift categories: three shifts per day on weekdays and two shifts per day on weekends, with one team allocated to each shift, covering both the adult and paediatric EC. Additional locum clinicians are often allocated to bolster certain shifts to optimise EC flow, and patients are occasionally consulted by clinicians allocated to other departments. Patients are first triaged according to the South African Triage Scale, which categorises them into one of four acuity categories: emergent (Red), very urgent (Orange), urgent (Yellow) or routine (Green); and are then consulted by a clinician before a disposition decision is made, after which the patient exits the EC [12]. Special investigations are ordered either by the treating clinician during consultation or informally by a clinician prior to consultation, based on the presenting complaint.

An existing dataset was utilised that comprised all patient consultations over three randomly selected four-week periods in 2019, specifically 03 February – 03 March, 05 May – 02 June and 02 – 30 September [13]. Consultations were excluded if patients were redirected or left before completion of care, or if data were incomplete. To assess the impact of special investigations on time-to-disposition, a random subsample of consultations was drawn from the full dataset using a random number generator. From this subsample, binary variables for whether radiology or laboratory testing were performed were extracted, and then laboratory timestamps were collected for consultations where laboratory testing occurred. Because the proportion of consultations involving laboratory testing was unknown, we initially selected 1,000 cases to ensure a sufficient sample. Laboratory timestamps were available for a number of consultations in which laboratory testing was performed that exceeded the minimum sample size (n=370) required for a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. This subsample was additionally used to fit a multivariable logistic regression model incorporating special investigations as a predictor.

The original electronic dataset included patients’ age and sex, triage and trauma categories (trauma or non-trauma), clinician category, shift category and length, and time stamps for each patient consultation (at arrival, triage, consultation, disposition and EC exit), as well as system measures, which were measured 2-hourly and averaged for each shift. The system measures included the average number of patients in the EC, the number of patients waiting at the beginning of the shift, the number of new patients arriving during the shift, and the average number of boarders (patients waiting for an in-patient bed) in the EC during the shift. Time-to-disposition was recorded in minutes and was calculated for each patient consultation by subtracting the time when the consultation started from the time a disposition decision was made. Additional data were then collected electronically for laboratory and radiology variables for each patient consultation in the subsample, using the patient’s folder number.

Data were analysed using SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2021. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Data are described with summary statistics. Central tendency and spread are described as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Median time-to-disposition is compared between variables using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Time-to-disposition is categorised into three groups (less than 1 hour, 1-3 hours and >3 hours) based on a local benchmark at the study site. The Pearson’s Chi-Square test is used to compare variables between these categories. Statistical significance is defined as p<0.05.

To identify factors independently associated with time-to-disposition exceeding three hours, two binary logistic regression models were fitted. Model 1 was fitted on the full cohort and included age group, triage acuity, trauma category, and clinician category as predictors. Model 2 was fitted on the investigations subsample and additionally included any special investigation (binary) as a predictor. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), Nagelkerke R2, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. As this analysis was designed to identify independent associations rather than develop a clinical prediction tool, moderate discrimination was anticipated given the broad categorical nature of the predictors.

The relationship between median time-to-disposition and the proportion of the patient population in each subgroup was examined graphically to identify high-impact groups, defined as those with both above-average time-to-disposition and a patient volume exceeding 25% of the cohort.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Stellenbosch University Health Research Ethics Committee (S24/02/024_Sub study S20/01/021) and the University of Cape Town Human Research Ethics Committee (461/2024).

Results

The initial dataset included 9,677 patient consultations, of which 321 were excluded due to patients leaving before completion of care (n=108), patients being redirected to another facility (n=40), or incomplete data (n=173). A total of 9,356 consultations were analysed.

Participants' age had a bimodal distribution, peaking at 2 years and 38 years, with a median (IQR) age of 34 (20–53) years overall. The overall median (IQR) ECLOS was 614 (333–991) minutes and time-to-disposition contributed to 15.5% of the overall ECLOS. Discussion

This study aimed to examine time-to-disposition and its associated factors, with the goal of informing strategies to address crowding. Our findings indicate that time-to-disposition varies significantly across patient, clinician, and system-level measures. Key patient-related factors include age, triage acuity, and the need for special investigations—particularly laboratory testing. Multivariable analysis confirmed that triage acuity, age group, trauma category, and clinician category were independently associated with time-to-disposition, with special investigations emerging as the dominant predictor in the investigations subsample.

Special investigations, particularly laboratory testing, were the most prominent contributors to prolonged time-to-disposition. Most delays stemmed from the interval between sample collection and registration of laboratory results, highlighting a key bottleneck in the diagnostic process. Time-to-disposition was shorter when samples were collected before consultation, suggesting that pre-emptive ordering of investigations may support more efficient decision-making. The mechanism underlying this finding warrants contextualization. In the study setting, investigations are clinician-initiated following consultation, with no formalised nurse- or triage-initiated investigation protocol. The shorter time-to-disposition observed when specimens were collected before the disposition decision likely reflects pre-emptive ordering by clinicians in higher-acuity cases where the investigation result is anticipated to be decisive, consistent with parallel rather than sequential processing of the clinical workup. Whether formalising this approach through structured investigation protocols for specific presenting complaints could further reduce time-to-disposition warrants prospective evaluation. The longer delays observed when laboratory registration occurred before the disposition decision, as opposed to after, further imply that clinicians often wait for results before finalising patient management. Expanding access to point-of-care testing may offer a valuable strategy to reduce reliance on centralised laboratory processes in patient cohorts in which the results are likely to influence the disposition decision. The strategic use of point-of-care testing has been found to improve time-to-discharge, but investing herein would require further cost-benefit analyses [14].

The relationship between median time-to-disposition and patient volume across subgroups highlights an important distinction between statistical significance and intervention impact. While emergent (Red) triage presentations had the longest time-to-disposition, they comprised only 4.5% of consultations; by contrast, very urgent (Orange) presentations, represented nearly half the cohort. Similarly, admitted patients constituted 36.5% of consultations. These subgroups represent the highest-priority targets for intervention. Having a clinician as part of the triage process has been shown to reduce ECLOS, and facilitates the rapid identification of patients requiring admission and fast-tracking select admissions prior to obtaining the results of investigations [[15], [16], [17]]. The enhanced use of senior clinicians in decision-making is associated with improved patient outcomes, but does not improve throughput [18].

The difference in time-to-disposition between clinician categories, does not appear to be driven by overall EC experience, but rather by environment familiarity and unmeasured differences in workflow and administrative responsibilities. Registrars and interns (the categories with the longest time-to-disposition) generally rotate through ECs for shorter periods compared to medical officers and CSMOs. Senior clinicians at the study site (registrars and medical officers), in addition to having more non-clinical tasks, receive more patient handovers at the beginning of the shift and see higher acuity patients which has been shown to decrease output [19]. Time-to-disposition was also unexpectedly independent of shift-related factors. This finding, while in keeping with the findings of Hoffe et al (2022) [13], is in contrast to results of similar studies in the United States, where longer shift duration was associated with reduced resident productivity, clinician output varied significantly by hour within a shift, and operational scheduling factors were shown to influence patient throughput. These differences may reflect the distinct staffing models, patient volumes, and operational contexts of high-income country emergency departments compared with district-level facilities in South Africa [[20], [21], [22]]. Determining the (likely multifactorial) reasons for this discrepancy will require further investigation. Notably, the association between registrar category and prolonged time-to-disposition persisted in multivariable analysis after adjusting for triage acuity, age group, and trauma category (aOR 1.35, p<0.001). This suggests the effect is not attributable to case-mix alone. This finding supports the hypothesis that environment familiarity, workflow differences, and non-clinical administrative responsibilities play a meaningful role in throughput, independent of clinical complexity.

Paediatric patients in this cohort had substantially shorter time-to-disposition than adults, an effect that persisted after adjusting for triage acuity and other covariates. This likely reflects the lower investigation rate among paediatric presentations (32.2% vs 79.2% in the investigations subsample) and the higher proportion discharged directly from the EC (78.9% vs 51.9% among adults), both of which support more rapid and straightforward disposition decisions. Trauma presentations similarly had shorter time-to-disposition, consistent with the more protocolised nature of trauma assessment and the tendency for disposition to be determined earlier in the clinical encounter compared with undifferentiated medical presentations.

This study has several limitations. Although conducted at a single site, the laboratory and radiology services, staffing model, and operational practices at MPH are broadly representative of other ECs in Cape Town, supporting the potential generalisability of the findings. Nonetheless, unmeasured confounding factors may have influenced time-to-disposition within the analysed categories. Laboratory timestamp data were limited to sample collection and registration, without information on when results became available. As a result, we were unable to measure the time from result availability to disposition or to distinguish between time spent on active treatment versus time awaiting investigations and decision-making. The multivariable model incorporating special investigations (Model 2) was fitted on a random subsample of 1000 consultations, which limits the precision of those estimates compared with Model 1. Additionally, investigation data were available only as binary variables, precluding analysis of the number, type, or clinical indication for investigations ordered.

A multicentre approach would help assess the generalisability of these findings across settings with similar resource constraints. Additionally, studies that incorporate more granular time data, including result availability timestamps, could help disentangle the distinct phases within the disposition process, such as active treatment, waiting for investigations, and decision-making. The contribution of laboratory testing to prolonged time-to-disposition and ECLOS observed in this study raises an important question about the role of point-of-care testing in ECs. This effect is likely to be even greater in settings with less developed laboratory infrastructure. Future research should explore whether primary investment in point-of-care testing could meaningfully reduce time-to-disposition and ECLOS in ECs in LMICs.

Conclusion

This study provides a systematic description of time-to-disposition and its determinants at a district-level EC in an LMIC setting and identifies independent predictors of prolonged time-to-disposition through multivariable analysis. Triage acuity was the strongest independent predictor of time-to-disposition exceeding three hours, with Emergent (Red) and Very urgent (Orange) presentations carrying a five- to three-fold increased odds respectively. Special investigations, particularly laboratory testing, were the dominant modifiable contributor, with centralised laboratory turnaround emerging as the principal bottleneck in the diagnostic pathway. The paediatric age group and trauma presentations were independently protective.

Critically, the analysis demonstrates that statistical significance and intervention impact are not equivalent: Very urgent (Orange) presentations and admitted patients, while not having the absolute longest time-to-disposition, represent the highest-priority targets for quality improvement by virtue of their combined burden of prolonged time-to-disposition and high patient volume. Strategies targeting laboratory turnaround, including investment in point-of-care testing, and structured support for registrar-led decision-making may offer the greatest throughput gains in similarly resourced settings. These findings provide an evidence base for quality improvement planning and underscore the value of operational data in informing EC throughput strategies in resource-constrained healthcare systems.
