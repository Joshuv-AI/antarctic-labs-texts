Carbamazepine-induced pure red cell aplasia in an elderly patient with ESKD on hemodialysis: a case report
Authors: Hwajin Park, Suyeon Han, Yunkyeong Hwang, Yu Ah Hong, Eunhee Han, Yoon-Kyung Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPure red cell aplasia (PRCA) is a rare blood disorder characterized by the selective absence of erythroblasts in an otherwise normal bone marrow, leading to severe reticulocytopenic anemia. In patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) undergoing hemodialysis, PRCA is most often caused by anti-erythropoietin (EPO) antibodies induced by recombinant human EPO therapy. However, non-EPO-stimulating agent (ESA) drugs can also trigger PRCA, although this is underrecognized in this patient population. Carbamazepine, a widely used anticonvulsant, has rarely been implicated in PRCA, with only a few cases reported in the general population and none in patients undergoing dialysis. Herein we describe a case of carbamazepine-induced PRCA in an elderly male patient with ESKD undergoing hemodialysis, whose anemia improved after carbamazepine discontinuation.Case presentationA 70-year-old male patient with ESKD undergoing hemodialysis for eight years presented with sudden-onset of severe anemia, with a hemoglobin (Hb) of 6.2 g/dL. This marked a sharp decline from his usual Hb level ranged of 10.0–11.0 g/dL while being intravenously administered an ESA (epoetin-α, three times a week). Bone marrow examination confirmed a diagnosis of PRCA. Further examination identified carbamazepine-induced PRCA as the likely cause. The results of the enzyme-linked immunoassay, used to test for anti-EPO antibodies, were also negative. Carbamazepine was discontinued and replaced with levetiracetam (Keppra®) for an anticonvulsant effect. One month later, the patient’s Hb level increased to 8.7 g/dL and red blood cell (RBC) transfusions were no longer required. However, two months later, his Hb level again decreased to 7.3 g/dL. These findings suggest that the patient’s anemia was caused by EPO deficiency due to chronic renal failure. This condition was treated by gradually increasing the ESA dose, which ensured that the patient’s Hb level remained stable at 11.0 g/dL.ConclusionsThis is the first reported case of carbamazepine-induced PRCA in a patient undergoing hemodialysis that resolved with drug withdrawal alone, thereby avoiding the need for immunosuppressive therapy.

Background

Pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), is a rare erythropoiesis disorder characterized by the absence of erythroblasts in the bone marrow, results in isolated reticulocytopenic anemia [1]. It may be a primary autoimmune disorder or secondary to other conditions, such as autoimmune diseases, malignancies, infections, or drug exposure [2]. Among the secondary cause, anti-erythropoietin (EPO) antibody-mediated PRCA has been predominantly recognized in patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) receiving recombinant human EPO (rHu-EPO) therapy for anemia, where diagnostic focus frequently centers on EPO antibody formation. However, rare drug-induced forms—including those triggered by carbamazepine—may be overlooked in this setting.

To date, there have been only a few reports of carbamazepine-induced PRCA [3], and none within the context of ESKD patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis, whose anemia is routinely attributed to anti-EPO antibody mechanisms. This significant diagnostic overlap means carbamazepine-induced PRCA in dialysis patients may escape timely identification and appropriate management.

Herein, we present the first documented case of carbamazepine-induced PRCA in an elderly ESKD patient on maintenance hemodialysis, whose anemia improved after discontinuation of carbamazepine. This case expands the differential diagnosis in this population beyond antibody-mediated etiologies, highlighting the importance of recognizing non-EPO antibody-mediated mechanisms of PRCA in dialysis patients.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old male patient with ESKD due to diabetes mellitus presented to our hospital with recurrent episodes of severe anemia requiring red blood cell (RBC) transfusions. Until early June 2024, he had been undergoing hemodialysis for eight years, maintaining a mean hemoglobin (Hb) level between 10.0 and 10.7 g/dL while receiving intravenous EPO-stimulating agent (ESA) therapy (Eposis®; epoetin-α, three times a week). However, approximately one month before his first outpatient visit, his Hb level suddenly dropped to 6.2 g/dL. Despite five sessions of RBC transfusions and an increased ESA dose of 10,000 IU three times a week, his anemia did not improve. He reported dizziness and fatigue but did not experience gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, a common cause of anemia in patients with ESKD. Due to the severity of anemia, he was admitted to our hospital on October 10 for further evaluation and management.

On admission, his laboratory test results were as follows: white blood cell (WBC) count, 3700 /mm3; Hb, 5.9 g/dL; platelet, 108,000 /mm3; hematocrit, 16.2%; mean corpuscular volume, 81.6 fl.; mean corpuscular Hb, 29.9 pg; mean corpuscular Hb concentration, 36.6%; reticulocyte count, 0.1% (normal range; 0.5%-2.0%); reticulocyte index, 0.04 (normal range; 1–2). These findings indicated severe reticulocytopenic anemia, accompanied by mild leukopenia and thrombocytopenia. His iron profile was as follows: serum iron level, 160 µg/dL; total iron-binding capacity, 180 µg/dL; transferrin saturation, 89%; ferritin levels, 1375 ng/mL. The serum EPO level increased to 1015 mIU/mL (normal range; 4.3–29.0 mIU/mL). His direct and indirect Coombs test results were negative. Serum bilirubin and haptoglobin levels were within normal limits. Serum vitamin B12 and folate levels were 1530 pg/mL (normal range; 160–950 pg/mL) and > 20 ng/mL (2.7–17.0 ng/mL), respectively, indicating the patient was not deficient in either of them. Further investigations, including upper GI endoscopy and fecal occult blood tests, did not reveal any source of bleeding.

Finally, the cause of anemia was confirmed to be PRCA based on bone marrow biopsy findings, which revealed a near absence of erythroblasts and normal maturation of other myeloid and megakaryocytic lineages with an M/E ratio of 35.5:1 (Fig. 1). Although the patient had mild leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, we considered that this mild bicytopenia might have resulted from common causes such as chronic uremia, nutritional deficiencies, and HD-related complement activations, which is of low clinical significance. Given that the patients was an adult with no history of childhood anemia, we focused on differentiating the causes of secondary acquired PRCA. Viral markers, including hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and human immunodeficiency virus were all negative, and serum polymerase chain reaction testing for B19 parvovirus, cytomegalovirus, and Epstein-Barr virus was also negative. Tests for anti-nuclear antibodies and anti-DNA antibodies were also negative, and we excluded autoimmune/collagen vascular diseases as PRCA etiologies. Chest and abdominal CT scans were performed to evaluate for possible solid tumor lesions, including thymoma and lymphoma, but no evidence of such lesions was found.

Given the possibility of drug-induced PRCA in this patient, we investigated his drug history over the six months before his first visit. Two drugs were suspected as potential causes of drug-induced PRCA: rHu-EPO, used for the treatment of anemia in patients with ESKD, and carbamazepine (Epileptol-CR®; 400 mg twice a day), which had been prescribed on June 13-two months before the worsening of anemia-for a seizure episode secondary to left encephalomalacia following an old intracranial hemorrhage. First, we outsourced enzyme-linked immunoassays (ELISAs) to detect anti-EPO antibodies triggered by the administration of rHu-EPO to a specialized external laboratory. Although the results were obtained approximately two months later, they showed the absence of anti-EPO antibodies in the patient serum compared to that in the control serum. Given the clinical suspicion, rHu-EPO administration was discontinued on October 17, the day PRCA was diagnosed based on the bone marrow biopsy findings, before the confirmation of the ELISA results. Second, we considered measuring the serum carbamazepine concentration to assess drug toxicity; however, this was not possible because the patient had spontaneously stopped taking this medication one week before the first visit. Therefore, carbamazepine, the suspected drug, was discontinued, and the patient was switched to levetiracetam (Keppra®; 1000 mg daily, with an additional 500 mg on dialysis days). During the first seven days after hospital admission, the patient required three RBC transfusions following carbamazepine discontinuation (Fig. 2). Approximately, one month after discontinuing carbamazepine on October 24th, the Hb level increased to 8.7 g/dL, and the patient no longer required RBC transfusions. However, after approximately two months, on December 2, the Hb level decreased to 7.3 g/dL, and the patient required a RBC transfusion due to poor oral intake and fatigue. Further examinations, including laboratory tests, were conducted to reassess the cause of the anemia. The results were as follows: white blood cell (WBC), 4300/mm3; hemoglobin (Hb), 7.3 g/dL; platelet, 126,000/mm3; reticulocyte count, 2.3%; reticulocyte index, 1.06; serum iron, 101 µg/dL; transferrin saturation, 53%; ferritin, 1210 ng/mL; serum EPO, 11.7 mIU/mL; high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, 0.17 mg/dL. These findings indicated deficient RBC production due to relative EPO deficiency. We also confirmed the absence of anti-EPO antibodies at that time. Therefore, EPO deficiency secondary to chronic renal failure was suspected as the cause of this patient’s anemia.

The patient first received an intravenous dose of methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin β (Mircera®) 75 µg/month on December 5, 2024. However, after one month, the hemoglobin (Hb) level was only 9.0 g/dL. Considering that the patient had also received one unit of packed red cells on December 2 alongside Mircera, this degree of increase was considered insufficient. Although Miscera dose escalation could have been attempted, the attending physician opted to switch to a different ESA, darbepoetin-α, administered at a higher dose every two weeks. Nevertheless, after one month (February 6, 2025), the Hb level remained at 8.9 g/dL without improvement. At that point, the decision was made to continue darbepoetin-α at 120 µg twice weekly for another month without switching to a different ESA. Subsequently, by March 4, 2025, the Hb level increased to approximately 11 g/dL and remained stable thereafter. Over the following six months, his Hb level remained stable between 9.8 and 11.0 g/dL, which was considered appropriate given his status as an elderly patient on dialysis. Based on the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) causality assessment system, this case was classified as “probable.” The evidence supporting this classification included a clear temporal association between carbamazepine administration and PRCA onset, exclusion of alternative causes, sustained hematologic recovery following its discontinuation, and no need for drug rechallenge.

Discussion and conclusions

Herein, we report the first case of carbamazepine-induced PRCA independent of rHu-EPO administration in an elderly patient with ESKD undergoing hemodialysis. While rHu-EPO has been the typical cause of PRCA in most previously reported cases, carbamazepine was identified as the causative agent in our patient. The novelty of our case lies in its identification of carbamazepine as an under-recognized PRCA etiology in the dialysis population where EPO antibody-mediated causes have dominated existing literature. This extends the clinical spectrum of PRCA causes in ESKD patients and highlights that standard diagnostic paradigms may miss alternative drug-induced etiologies.

Carbamazepine is associated with a range of adverse hematologic effects, including leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and fatal aplastic anemia [4]. However, PRCA has rarely been reported, and it has been described in small cases [3, 5, 6]. Reported carbamazepine doses at PRCA onset vary between 150 mg and 1000 mg daily, and upon diagnosis, drug discontinuation combined with supportive transfusion therapy typically results in hematologic improvement within 2 weeks to 3 months Consistent with these observations, our patient experienced a generalized seizure during hemodialysis and began taking carbamazepine two months before the worsening of his anemia. Following treatment discontinuation, the anemia improved, and RBC transfusions were no longer required within one month. Given this temporal relationship between carbamazepine initiation and PRCA onset, causality could be classified as ‘probable’ per the WHO-UMC causality assessment system [7].

Carbamazepine-induced PRCA can be diagnosed only after excluding other secondary causes and considering temporal associations, as no direct diagnostic method exists. While measuring serum carbamazepine concentrations may aid in differentiating drug toxicity, previous reports indicate that the hematologic side effects of this drug are often not dose-dependent [5, 8]. Although current evidence remains inconclusive, we strongly suspect that carbamazepine was the etiology of PRCA in our patient.

The pathophysiology of Carbamazepine-induced Pure Red Cell Aplasia (PRCA) is not fully understood, but three primary mechanisms have been proposed: (1) direct toxic interference with the metabolism of erythroid precursors, (2) antibody-mediated immune reactions, and (3) inhibition of DNA synthesis [3]. More detail, first, nature of carbamazepine enables the drug to integrate into the lipid layer of the erythrocytes membrane, thereby altering membrane architecture, distorting cellular morphology, and impairing their original function [9]. Second, carbamazepine undergoes oxidative metabolism and forms potentially toxic arene oxide intermediates, most notably carbamazepine-10,11-epoxide. If not adequately detoxified, carbamazepine-10,11-epoxide may covalently bind to cellular macromolecules, leading to genetic damage and direct cytotoxic injury. This mechanism has been implicated in the development of severe hematological complications, including aplastic anemia [10, 11]. Although definitive evidence is lacking, it has been proposed that these arene oxide intermediates may also exert toxic effects on erythroid precursors within the bone marrow or directly induce hemolytic anemia in mature red blood cells [10]. Such mechanisms provide a plausible explanation for the spectrum of hematologic toxicities observed during carbamazepine therapy, ranging from hemolysis to bone marrow toxicity. Third, patients on hemodialysis may be more susceptible to carbamazepine toxicity. Carbamazepine and its metabolites are primarily metabolized in the liver, with minimal excretion via the kidneys. Although the EXTRIP(Extracorporeal treatment for carbamazepine poisoning: systematic review and recommendations) guidelines do not directly state that patients with ESKD on maintenance dialysis are at higher risk for carbamazepine toxicity or accumulation compared to individuals with preserved renal function, clinical reports indicate that, in cases of overdose, recovery may be delayed and rebound phenomena may occur more frequently in dialysis patients. Repeated hemodialysis sessions and hypoalbuminemia can lead to changes in protein binding and increased levels of free (unbound) drug and metabolite. Moreover, the accumulation of uremic toxins and multiorgan metabolic impairment in dialysis patients may contribute to the greater propensity for the accumulation and toxic reactions of carbamazepine and its metabolites. Carbamazepine has a small molecular weight and a relatively low volume of distribution, and is highly protein bound (approximately 75%), making it only moderately removable by dialysis; however, with high-flux dialysis, which is commonly employed in dialysis patients today, moderate removal is feasible. Furthermore, the principal metabolite carbamazepine-10,11-epoxide has even lower protein binding, and is therefore more readily cleared by dialysis. Despite the minor role of renal clearance and the relatively moderate dialyzability, because maintenance hemodialysis is typically performed three times per week for 4 h per session, carbamazepine and its metabolites can accumulate during the interdialytic period when dialysis is not being performed [12]. These non-immunogenic mechanisms may explain the complete reversibility observed after drug withdrawal. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to clarify the precise pathogenesis.

In patients with ESKD, the most common underlying cause of PRCA is the development of anti-EPO antibodies against rHu-EPO, which cross-react and neutralize endogenous EPO, preventing its binding to EPO receptors [13]. This immune-mediated disruption of erythropoiesis typically necessitates discontinuation of rHu-EPO and the initiation of immunosuppressive therapy, including cyclosporine A, corticosteroids, or cyclophosphamide [1, 14]. However, immunosuppressive therapy carries substantial risk, including acute complications, such as bone marrow suppression and vulnerability to infection, and long-term complications, such as de novo malignancies [15]. Our case demonstrates that not all PRCA in ESKD dialysis patients is antibody-mediated; prompt identification of drug-induced PRCA may allow for rapid recovery with simple drug withdrawal, avoiding unnecessary immunosuppression and associated risks.

From a clinical standpoint, this case has important implications for diagnostic strategy and patient safety. It underscores the need for comprehensive review of medication history and consideration of rare adverse drug reactions in the differential diagnosis of anemia and PRCA, especially when standard ESA-based management is ineffective. Clinicians should be aware that drug-induced PRCA, although rare, can occur even in high-risk populations like dialysis patients, necessitating heightened vigilance in monitoring and discontinuing potential causative agents. Enhancing awareness of these atypical PRCA mechanisms can lead to improved outcomes by facilitating timely intervention, reducing treatment burden, and preventing exposure to unnecessary toxic therapies.

Mild leukopenia and thrombocytopenia were observed in this patient. These findings are commonly observed in patients with ESKD undergoing hemodialysis. Various factors, such as chronic uremia, nutritional deficiencies, and hemodialysis-related complement activation, can contribute to mild bone marrow suppression in this population. We considered these hematologic abnormalities unrelated to PRCA, as they are not typically associated with the condition.

In this patient, ESA therapy was withheld for two months and then reinitiated both methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin β (Mircera®) and darbepoetin-α. Generally, switching between Mircera and darbepoetin in patients on dialysis can be guided by clinical judgment and hematologic response. In this case, the initial response to Mircera was considered suboptimal, leading to a switch to darbepoetin-α, which also failed to produce an adequate rise in the Hb level. The poor response to both ESAs during the early phase may be attributed to incomplete recovery from PRCA. Subsequently, as hematologic recovery progressed, the patient’s Hb level rose to 11 g/dL by March 2025 and remained stable thereafter, despite the continuation of the same ESA dose and schedule.

To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of carbamazepine-induced PRCA in an elderly patient with ESKD on maintenance hemodialysis who achieved hematologic recovery without immunosuppressive therapy. This case broadens the clinical understanding of drug-induced PRCA beyond ESAs in the dialysis population, underscoring the need for heightened clinical vigilance, timely identification, and prompt discontinuation of offending agents to improve outcomes and avoid unnecessary immunosuppressive treatment.
