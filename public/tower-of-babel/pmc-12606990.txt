PGC-1α mediates hypoxia-preconditioned olfactory mucosa mesenchymal stem cells improved neuroinflammatory response via inhibiting microglial ferroptosis in ischemic stroke
Authors: Zhengtao Yu, Jiameng Li, Zhengwen He, Wenshui Li, Aojie Lian, Ying Xia, Yixiao Luo, Yi Zhuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury can induce nerve cell apoptosis and ferroptosis and activation of the neuroinflammatory cascade plays a critical role in ischemic stroke progression. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can aid in repairing nerve injuries and regulating inflammatory responses, but their roles in ischemic stroke are unknown.MethodsHere, we investigate the impact of microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis on neuronal apoptosis using an oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) model. Furthermore, we examined the effects of hypoxia-preconditioned olfactory mucosa mesenchymal stem cells (hOM-MSCs) on attenuating microglial ferroptosis and improving neuroinflammatory response in models of OGD/R and middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO).ResultsFirstly, it has been confirmed that the susceptibility of microglial to ferroptosis significantly amplifies their neuroinflammatory response, thereby accelerating neuronal apoptosis in the OGD/R model. A functional assay identified that the core functional factor, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ co-activator-1α (PGC-1α), secreted from hOM-MSCs has a critical role in the hOM-MSC-modulated recovery of cellular activity in neurons. This discovery was accomplished through inhibiting microglia ferroptosis and the enhancement of immune regulation in the area affected by an infarct, all of which are closely linked to neuroinflammatory reactions. Mechanistically, exposure to hOM-MSCs partially mediates the amelioration of neuroinflammatory response and restoration of neural function in ischemic stroke by means of PGC-1α activation within the infarct region, via initiation of the PPARα-GPX4/ACSL4-NFκB signaling cascades in microglia.ConclusionsThese findings provide convincing proof that PGC-1α plays a crucial role in facilitating the advantageous impacts of hOM-MSCs on the enhancement of neural functionality following an ischemic stroke. The treatment of hOM-MSC could be a promising and effective neuroprotective candidate to ischemic stroke, and PGC-1α may be used in isolation or in combination with hOM-MSCs for ischemic stroke treatment.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07240-5.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Hypoxia-preconditioned, Olfactory mucosa mesenchymal stem cells, PGC-1α, Microglia, Ferroptosis, Neuroinflammatory response

Background

Ischemic stroke or cerebral infarction occurs due to cerebral blood supply interruption, causing ischemia and hypoxia of brain tissue, focal necrosis, brain atrophy, and manifest as corresponding neurological deficits [1]. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent programmed cell death pathway, characterized by the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which leads to the accumulation of lipid peroxides [2], and plays a pivotal role in stroke progression, a multifaceted cerebral disorder governed by multiple cellular death pathways [1, 2]. Ferroptosis is classically associated with the depletion of the antioxidant capacity of glutathione, and its key molecule, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), plays a direct role in regulating the redox cycle of glutathione [3]. Ferroptosis has been implicated in ischemic stroke pathogenesis, and inhibiting ferroptosis ameliorates the neuroinflammatory response in ischemic stroke [3–5]. For example, genetic knockout of GPX4 exacerbates ischemic stroke severity by inducing ferroptosis and activating neuroinflammatory response, suggesting a protective role for GPX4 by suppressing ferroptosis and attenuating the neuroinflammatory response [5].Microglia also play a crucial role in the inflammatory response after ischemic stroke and are rapidly activated post-ischemic stroke. [6] into M1 and M2 phenotypes with pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory processes, reespectively [6, 7]. Previous studies on the mechanisms of ferroptosis in ischemic stroke have primarily focused on neuronal responses within the infarcted area, yet the susceptibility of microglia to iron overload remains underexplored. The impact of iron accumulation on microglia physiology and its role in inducing neuroinflammatory responses that can lead to neuronal apoptosis remains poorly understood. Thus, we investigated the impact of microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis in ischemia-reperfusion injury on neuronal apoptosis and explored strategies for inhibiting ferroptosis and neuroinflammatory response in ischemic stroke.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) function through effects on paracrine signaling, immune regulation, and multidirectional differentiation [8–10]. Stem cells have been used for nerve repair and regeneration as well as in the treatment of ischemic stroke [11]. In 2010, it was serendipitously discovered that MSCs derived from the olfactory mucosa (OM) of humans manifest themselves as an unparalleled reservoir of MSCs [12]. Our group developed a human OM-MSCs cultivation system [13] that can be applied to explore the regulation of neuroinflammatory response in ischemic stroke, and offers a unique opportunity to discern innovative therapeutic targets and strategies for ameliorating or reversing neuroinflammatory alterations.

The therapeutic efficacy of MSCs primarily relies on the quantity of transplanted cells and their migration rate to target sites, as well as their immunomodulatory effects following in vivo implantation [14]. Excessive inflammation, oxidative stress, and hypoxia in the microenvironment of the injury site all limit effectiveness of MSC in treating varying immune diseases [13]. MSCs are typically cultured in vitro in serum concentrations ranging from 10% to 20%; however, the actual in vivo serum concentration in hypoxic or ischemic microenvironments is much lower [14]. Improving culture methods for hypoxia-conditioned MSCs can augment their viability, paracrine effects, and ability to migrate towards target sites, and address existing limitations of MSC-based therapy.

In this study, we began by investigating the impact of microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis on neuronal apoptosis in an oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) model. Furthermore, we investigated the effect of hypoxia-preconditioned OM-MSCs (hOM-MSCs) on the restoration of neurological function in ischemic stroke models. By combining high-throughput sequencing-based assay for chromatin accessibility by transposase (ATAC-seq), full-length transcriptome isoform-sequencing (ISO-seq), and functional assays, we further characterized microglial ferroptosis and neuroinflammatory responses, and evaluated neural functional recovery following exposure to hOM-MSCs.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experiments were completed using authorized protocols and with institutional ethical committee and animal care committee approval by Affiliated Haikou Hospital of Xiangya School of Central South University (Approval number: 2023–402. Date of approval: December 20, 2023).

Acquisition and culture of hOM-MSCs

OM-MSCs were acquired from study volunteers at the Affiliated Haikou Hospital of Xiangya School, Central South University. Nasal mucosa was removed and rinsed three times with double antibiotic solution of penicillin (200 U/ml) and streptomycin (200 U/ml) (PenStrep). Subsequently, the tissue was placed in DF-12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (including PenStrep), fully dissected using ophthalmic scissors into 0.5 mm3 tissue blocks, and then centrifugated, with removal of the supernatant. Tissue blocks were inoculated into polylysine (PLL)-coated culture bottles and cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced after cells migrated out from the tissue mass, as well as every two to three days until sufficient cell growth for passage. The hOM-MSCs were then cultured in a reduced oxygen concentration of 3% for 48 hours to mimic hypoxic conditions.

OM-MSCs-induced differentiation

OM-MSCs were cultured until passages 3 to 5, and cells exhibiting favorable growth conditions with approximately 70–80% confluency were selected for induction. These cells were subsequently treated with 0.5–1.0 μM all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) for 24 hours, followed by a medium change to maintenance medium supplemented with neurotrophic factors. Finally, the expression level of the neuronal marker MAP2 was assessed using immunofluorescence staining.

Animals

8-week-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were procured from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China). The rodents were accommodated in a controlled environment with regulated temperature and humidity, followed a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and adequate provisions of food and water were made available throughout the duration of the experiment.

The OGD/R and the middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) models

Procedures for OGD/R model: The upper chamber of a transwell was seeded with BV2 microglial cells at a density of 1 × 105, while the lower chamber was populated with SH-SY5Y neurons (Derived from SH-SY5Y cells induced by retinoic acid) at a concentration of 1 × 105. Cells were then cultured in a three-gas incubator at 37 °C at 5% CO2 and 95% N2 for 12 hours to simulate an in vivo environment resembling cerebral ischemia characterized by hypoxia and reduced glucose levels. Harvested cells were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three time and reperfusion following ischemic injury was simulated by exposure to DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS at a temperature of 37 °C, in an environment containing 5% CO2 and oxygen levels similar to the ambient air (21% O2). Tri-Culture System: The BV2 microglia cells subjected to OGD/R treatment were seeded in the upper chamber of the Transwell, while the SH-SY5Y neurons exposed to OGD/R were placed in the lower chamber of the Transwell system (Corning, USA) to assess their response in this co-culture setup. When evaluating the BV2 microglia cells, the positions of the two cell types were interchanged. In parallel experiments, OM-MSCs were introduced into the upper chamber and co-cultured with the OGD/R-treated neuronal-microglial system for 24 hours. The entire Transwell co-culture system was maintained in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C under a controlled atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and ambient oxygen levels (21% O₂).

Procedures with the MCAO rat model involved anesthetizing 48 adult SD rats (n = 12, weighing 250 to 300 g) with 2% sevoflurane in an animal anesthesia machine (1 L/minute; RWD Life Science, China), then placed on a stereotaxic frame (Model 500, China). Rats were then positioned supine and their limbs and head were secured using rubber strips. A vertical incision measuring approximately 1.5–2 cm was performed at the central region of the neck to expose the right common carotid artery (CCA), followed by a subsequent separation of the subcutaneous fascia and muscle layer to reveal both the external carotid artery (ECA) and internal carotid artery (ICA). The CCA proximal end and ECA bifurcation were secured using surgical sutures, while an arterial clip was utilized to occlude the ICA. Subsequently, an incision was made in the mid-portion of the CCA, through which a fishing line was inserted into the ICA and advanced into its intracranial segment. After blocking approximately 10 mm of plug line for 2 hours, the line was carefully removed. Upon awakening from anesthesia, behavioral scores according to Zea Longa’s 5-point assessment criteria were assigned to evaluate model success: rats scoring between 1 and 3 were considered successful models eligible for inclusion in this study; any rats scoring either 0 or 4, as well as those that expired during experimentation, were excluded.

Transplantation of OM-MSCs

Rats were placed in a tail immobilization device, which allowed the tail to remain naturally extended and externally visible. A ventilation hole was present at the end of the fixator to ensure proper breathing for the rats, while their tails were immersed in warm water for a duration of 2 minutes. Prior to the procedure, the tail was wiped with 75% alcohol to promote vein dilation and soften the epidermal cuticle layer. Rubber bands were then used to secure the veins, facilitating further filling. Finally, after disinfection with complexed iodine, a dark red area on both sides near the proximal tail tip junction (approximately one-third from its base) was selected for cell injection. Approximately 1 × 106 cells were transplanted using a 0.5 ml suspension injected slowly into the rat’s tail vein at a rate of 0.1 ml/min.

Behavioral testing

The modified neurological severity score (mNSS) assessment was utilized to grade neurological impairment in rats following MCAO, encompassing assessments of motor function, sensory perception, reflexes, epileptic activity, myoclonus, and dystonia. The scale had a maximum achievable score of 18, with lower scores indicating more significant improvements in neurological status. To assess motor abilities including coordination, balance, and learning, we employed an automated rotarod testing system (Shanghai Jiliang Software Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). Prior to testing, the mice underwent daily training sessions 5 minutes at a constant speed of 10 rpm/min for three consecutive days. During the actual experiment, mice were placed on the accelerating rotating rod apparatus where the speed gradually increased from 4 rpm/min to 30 rpm/min over three minutes and then remained constant at 30 rpm/min for five additional minutes. Throughout the testing period, both the drop distance and the corresponding time were automatically recorded by the instrument. The exercise test results were calculated by averaging the values obtained from three separate trials.

Measurement of infarct sizes

The brain tissue was rinsed thoroughly with a 0.9% saline solution to remove residual blood, followed by storage in a −20 °C freezer for 30 minutes. Coronal sections with a standard thickness of 2 mm were subsequently prepared for analysis. These slices were immersed in a pre-prepared solution containing 1% TTC (2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride) from Wellbio in Shanghai, China. The stained brain slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for exactly 10 minutes. ImagePro software was used to quantify the size of the non-stained infarct area based on digital images obtained from the experiment.

Two-dimensional laser speckle contrast imaging

A laser speckle imaging technique (SIM BFI HR Pro, SIM Opto-Technology Co, Ltd. Wuhan, China) was employed to monitor cerebral blood flow (CBF). Prior to the surgical procedure as a baseline measurement, laser speckle perfusion images were acquired. Subsequently, CBF alterations were tracked over time and expressed relative to the pre-ischemic baselines as a percentage.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis

The OM-MSCs obtained from the 4th passage were harvested, subjected to triple washing, and subsequently prepared for transplantation. After pre-testing, OM-MSCs were incubated separately with flow cytometry antibodies CD34 (eBioscience, 12–0349-42; 1:10), CD45 (eBioscience; 12–0459-42, 1:10), CD73 (eBioscience, 12–0739-42; 1:10), CD90 (eBioscience, 12–0909-42; 1:10), and CD105 (eBioscience, 12–1057-42; 1:10) for a duration of thirty minutes in the dark. Data was collected using a flow cytometer (Beckman, CA, USA and the obtained data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

ATAC-seq

Preparation of samples

The OM-MSCs from both the normoxic and hypoxic groups were centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 minutes at a speed of 500 g. The supernatant was subsequently removed, and the cell pellet was washed with pre-cooled PBS. Afterwards, another round of centrifugation at 500 g was performed to ensure complete removal of any remaining supernatant. The cell suspension was then treated with cold lysis buffer and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 minutes at 500 g to eliminate any residual supernatant. To prepare the transposase reaction system, TN5 transposase was used, and the nuclei were suspended in this reaction system. Following an incubation period of 30 minutes at 37 °C, DNA purification was completed and the purified DNA served as input for PCR amplification in a configured PCR reaction system. Following DNA purification, appropriate modifications were performed on the Illumina platform to generate the final DNA library.

Analysis

ATAC sequencing data processing began with removal of adapters and filtering out low-quality reads to generate CleanReads. These reads were then aligned to the reference genome sequence to obtain positional information for comparison. By analyzing the genomic alignment, we identified peak positions (peaks) used for gene annotation to create a list of genes affected by the peaks. Motif analysis was then conducted on identified peaks to uncover potential information about transcription factor binding sites. In scenarios involving multiple samples, differential peak comparisons were employed to identify genes associated with these peaks. Functional enrichment analysis was subsequently conducted on these genes to elucidate their biological functions and identify genes of particular interest.

ISO-seq

The cDNA library was created by employing 1 μg of total RNA and a cDNA PCR Sequencing Kit (SQK-PCS109) from Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT). Reverse transcriptase with template-switching activity was utilized to enhance the presence of complete-length cDNA and directly attach specific PCR adapters to both ends of the first-strand cDNA. Fourteen loops were amplified using LongAmp Tag (NEB) prior to using T4 DNA ligase (NEB) to connect the PCR products to ONT adapters. Following the ONT protocol, DNA purification was conducted using magnetic beads. The resulting cDNA library was loaded onto the FLO-MIN109 cell flow and sequenced on Biomarker Technologies’ PromethION platform (Beijing, China).

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

The mitochondrial electrochemical gradient was assessed in BV2 microglial cells using MitoProbe (JC1, a cationic dye known as 5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3-tetraethyl benzimidazol-carbocyanine iodide) obtained from Beyotime Biotech Inc. (Shanghai, China). In brief, SH-SY5Y neurons from each experimental group were collected and appropriately treated before being incubated with preheated JC-1 reagent at 37 °C in the dark for 30 minutes. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with preheated phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution and analyzed using a flow cytometer to measure cellular fluorescence intensity. Subsequent data analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

Cell processing and immunofluorescence

Cellular immunofluorescence staining was completed after fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 minutes. After washing with PBS, the cells were then blocked at 4 °C for 20 minutes using a blocking solution containing 1% Triton X-100 and 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the first antibody diluted in a closed solution. The following day, stained cells were treated with the corresponding secondary antibody for one hour. To visualize cell nuclei, DAPI (Sigma, D9542; diluted to a concentration of 1:1,000) was used as a counterstain. Slides were then mounted using an anti-fluorescence quenching sealing solution (Beyotime, P0126) and analyzed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Western blot

Cell or infarct samples from SD rats were subjected to western blotting using established protocols. Following gel electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA) once the bromophenol blue dye had migrated to the bottom of the gel. Blots were then blocked with 4% BSA in a TBST (0.05%) solution at room temperature for 1 hour and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibody. The secondary antibodies were applied on the following day and incubated at room temperature for at least one hour before visualization using the ChemiDoc XRS imaging system.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-4, IL-10, and iron in cell culture supernatants and tissues using an ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following incubation with the working solution, samples were exposed to a substrate solution at 37 °C for 30 minutes. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Themo Fisher, USA) after the addition of termination solution.

Transmission electron microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy analysis, the collected cells or infarct in SD rats were fixed overnight at 4 °C with a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution, followed by washing in cacodylate buffer and subsequently fixed with a 1% osmium tetroxide solution. Following an additional washing step in cacodylate buffer, the samples were immersed in a dual-staining solution composed of 5% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Subsequently, the samples were sectioned using an ultramicrotome and examined under a transmission electron microscope (HT7700, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell shRNA and siRNA transfection

The sequences encoding four distinct shRNAs directed against human PGC-1α, shPGC-1α (seq1, targeting: GCAGAGTATGACGATGGTATT), shPGC-1α (seq2, targeting: CCGTTATACCTGTGATGCTTT), shPGC-1α (seq3, targeting: TATGACAGCTACGAGGAATAT), and shPGC-1α (seq4, targeting: GCACGCAATCCTATTCATTGT) were individually integrated into the GV493 lentiviral vector (Genechem). The constructs were individually co-transfected into HEK-293 cells along with lentivirus envelope constructs (Genechem). Subsequently, the resulting lentiviral particles were introduced into hOM-MSCs. The sequences encoding three distinct siRNAs directed against human PPARα, siPPARα (seq1, targeting: GGCGAACTATTCGGCTGAA), siPPARα (seq2, targeting: GCTAAAGTACGGTGTGTAT), and siPPARα (seq3, targeting: GGAAGCCGTTCTGTGACAT) were also used.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, and results are presented as the mean ± standard error of mean (x ± SEM). Statistical significance between two groups was determined using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test, and between > 2 groups using a one-way ANOVA, incorporating the Brown-Forsythe test to evaluate homogeneity of variance and applying Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and p < 0.01 indicated highly significant statistical findings.

Results

Identification and biological characteristics of OM-MSCs

We established a culture system to expand OM-MSCs, which exhibited a spindle-shaped morphology with radial arrangement when observed under light microscopy (Fig. 1A) and were identified using by STRO-1, a stromal cell marker, and Nestin, a stem cell marker, using immunofluorescence (Fig. 1B, C). To demonstrate lipid accumulation in the cytoplasm, lipogenic-induced cells were stained using Oil Red O highlighting red lipid droplets (Fig. 1D). Additionally, after exposure to an osteogenic inducer, Alizarin Red staining identified red mineralized nodules formed within the cells (Fig. 1E). Tretinoin was used to induce differentiation of OM-MSCs into neurons and the expression of microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2), which served as a neuronal marker (Fig. 1F). Scanning electron microscopy revealed numerous microvilli presented on the surface of OM-MSCs (Fig. 1G). Finally, flow cytometry was used to show that OM-MSCs expressed CD73, CD90, and CD105 but not CD34 and CD45. In all, the purity level of OM-MSCs in our system was > 99% based on our flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 1H).

Microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis significantly enhances neuronal apoptosis in the OGD/R Model

To investigate the impact of microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis on neuronal apoptosis, we treated neurons with iron, RSL3, and liproxstatin 1 (Lip 1) with or without microglia (Fig. 2Aleft) and used lipid peroxidation fluorescence probe BODIPY C11 tracer staining. We found a significant increase in the oxidation/reduction ratio when neurons were treated with RSL3 and iron, with or without microglia, compared to the neurons not treated with these reagents. Notably, the oxidation/reduction ratio was significantly reduced in neurons treated with Iron+RSL3+Lip1. The presence of microglia also significantly increased the oxidation/reduction ratio in neurons treated with RSL3 and iron compared to their absence (Fig. 2B, D).

Immunofluorescent staining with NeuN, a neuronal marker, and BAX, an apoptosis marker, was performed on the cells and showed an increase in apoptosis in both groups treated with RSL3 and iron, irrespective of the presence or absence of microglia, compared to untreated controls. BAX expression was also significantly reduced in the neurons treated with iron, RSL3, and Lip1. BAX expression was also significantly higher in neurons treated with iron and RSL3 with microglia compared to similar controls without microglia (Fig. 2C, D).

The concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β and anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-4 in the supernatant of each group were determined using ELISA. The results revealed a significant increase in IL-1β and a significant decrease in IL-4 levels in the group of neurons treated with Iron and RSL3 group, with or without microglia, compared to the untreated controls. However, the neurons treated with Iron, RSL3, and Lip1 showed the opposite phenotype with decreased IL-1β levels and a significant increase in IL-4 levels. The presence of microglia further increased IL-1β levels and decreased IL-4 levels in the neurons treated with Iron and RSL3 group (Fig. 2E). Together, these results demonstrate that the susceptibility of microglia to ferroptosis significantly amplifies their neuroinflammatory response, thereby accelerating neuronal apoptosis.

To investigate the impact of microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis on neuronal apoptosis in the OGD/R model, we treated neurons with or without Lip1, in the presence or absence of microglia (Fig. 2Aright). hOM-MSCs improve cellular activity in neurons by modulating neuroinflammatory responses and microglia ferroptosis in the OGD/R model

We next explored the potential neuroprotective effects of OM-MSCs in an OGD/R model at varying oxygen concentrations using normoxia-treated OM-MSCs (nOM-MSCs) and hypoxia-treated OM-MSCs (hOM-MSCs). We found a significant increase in apoptosis of SH-SY5Y cells in the OGD/R group compared to the control group, that was even less when nOM-MSCs were present. However, hOM-MSCs demonstrated the most pronounced reduction in apoptosis (Fig. 3A, B). These results suggest that OM-MSCs exhibit a significant neuroprotective effect on neurons, with the neuroprotective capacity of hOM-MSCs being significantly augmented. We investigated effects of OM-MSCs on microglial polarization into pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype which produces IL-1β versus an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype which produces CD206, in BV2 cells. BV2 cell expression of CD206 was downregulated while IL-1β expression was significantly upregulated in the OGD/R group compared to the control group yet, in the nOM-MSCs group, the expression patterns were reversed, with effects were most pronounced in the hOM-MSCs group (Fig. 3A, C). p50 and p65 protein expression is also associated with immune inflammatory response. Western blot analysis of BV2 cells from the above experimental groups revealed a significant upregulation in the expression levels of p50 and p65 proteins in BV2 cells subjected to OGD/R group compared to the control group, while the nOM-MSCs group exhibited a notable reduction in p50 and p65 expression levels, that was even more pronounced in hOM-MSCs (Fig. 3D). We also found increased IL-1β levels and a significant decrease in IL-10 levels in the OGD/R group compared to the control group, with inverse effects following treatment with nOM-MSCs and hOM-MSCs (Fig. 3E). In all, these studies demonstrate that OM-MSCs have potent immunomodulatory effects and can promote microglia polarization from an M1 to an M2 phenotype, and that hypoxia pretreatment results in the most pronounced immunomodulatory effect.

To investigate the potential of OM-MSCs effects on microglial ferroptosis within an OGD/R model, we performed mitochondrial membrane potential assays using JC-1 fluorescence staining in BV2 cells, and used transmission electron microscopy to assess mitochondrial structure. The JC-1 fluorescence staining revealed a significant decrease in the OGD/R group compared to the control group. In contrast, the nOM-MSCs group exhibited an increase in JC-1 levels, while the hOM-MSCs group demonstrated the most substantial elevation of JC-1 expression (Fig. 3F) suggesting that in comparison with the control group, BV2 cells in the OGD/R group exhibited evident mitochondrial shrinkage and thickening of membrane density. Notably, treatment with nOM-MSCs dampened that effect of OGD/R on mitochondrial shrinkage and membrane density thickening in BV2 cells, and phenotype that was reversed even more effectively by hOM-MSCs treated cells that had remarkable recovery of mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 3G). We then measured iron levels in each group and found high levels in the OGD/R group compared to controls, which was significantly reduced in nOM-MSCs and hOM-MSCs treated cells (Fig. 3H). We also measured levels of ferroptosis associated proteins GPX4 and ACSL4 in BV2 cells using Western blot and found a significant down-regulation of GPX4 expression and a up-regulation of ACSL4 expression in OGD/R conditions compared to controls. This phenotype was reversed in nOM-MSCs treated BV2 cells which had a notable up-regulation in GPX4 expression and a down-regulation in ACSL4 expression that was more pronounced with hOM-MSC treatment (Fig. 3I). Thus, OM-MSCs demonstrate the potential to inhibit microglial ferroptosis, with this effect being most pronounced in the case of hOM-MSCs.

PGC-1α mediates hOM-MSCs improved neuroinflammatory response via the inhibition of microglial ferroptosis in an OGD/R model

To better understand the beneficial effects of hOM-MSCs in ischemic stroke, we employed ATAC-seq and ISO-seq technologies to compare OM-MSCs under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Through heatmap cluster analysis, we identified differences in open chromatin regions, corresponding transcripts, and associated gene expression profiles of OM-MSCs before and after hypoxic pretreatment (Fig. 4A, B). Annotation results of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were then classified according to the types of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Analysis of significantly enriched outcomes of DEGs and transcripts between the OM-MSCs prior to and following hypoxia pretreatment revealed notable enrichment in the PPAR pathway/ferroptosis pathway/NF-κB pathway (Fig. 4C). We further characterized the differential peaks in ATAC-seq and DEGs in ISO-seq of OM-MSCs before and after hypoxic preconditioning through volcanic map clustering analysis. Discussion

Stroke is a prevalent cerebrovascular disease characterized by acute onset, significant disability, and substantial societal, medical, and economic burden [5]. Ischemic stroke specifically has a high disability, recurrence, and mortality rate, posing a grave threat to human life and health [4]. The pathophysiological basis of ischemic stroke involves disrupted blood supply with resultant hypoxia of nerve cells that causes cell injury processes including apoptosis, necrosis, excessive inflammation [1, 15]. Disordered iron metabolism and ferroptosis are commonly observed in ischemic stroke and may contribute to neuronal injury [16–19]. Microglia are immune cells in the central nervous system that quickly activate and migrate to injured areas after ischemic events, promoting inflammation [7]. In the process of injury repair after ischemic stroke, there exists a reciprocal transformation between M1 and M2 microglial phenotypes [7, 20] with recent experimental studies showing that balance between M1 and M2 microglia is closely associated with promoting neuronal repair after ischemic stroke, ameliorating neurological impairment symptoms, and enhancing prognosis in animal models [20, 21]. Here, we initially examined the impact of microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis within the context of neuronal apoptosis and demonstrated that increased microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis markedly enhances their neuroinflammatory activity, thereby accelerating neuronal apoptosis in the OGD/R model. Thus, we proposed that because of the involvement of microglial ferroptosis in amplifying neuroinflammatory response, it is imperative to explore strategies for suppressing microglial ferroptosis response in the context of ischemic stroke.

MSC transplantation has shown efficacy in repairing and reconstructing brain tissue through paracrine signaling, immune modulation, and multidirectional differentiation [22, 23]. The emerging field of targeting ferroptosis inhibition to regulating neuroinflammatory response has the potential for enhancing or reversing nerve function in ischemic stroke. MSCs can serve as a cellular system for the detoxification of ROS and act as suppliers of cytokines through paracrine effects to safeguard nerve cells and enhance nerve function [22]. We have previously demonstrated the capability to isolate OM-MSCs, which display enhanced potential for the repair of early-stage nerve injuries, and have successfully established a fully functional in vitro culture system for these cells. The use of OM-MSCs is advantageous due to their extensive distribution throughout the nasal cavity, convenient accessibility, low potential for immune rejection, minimal associated ethical concerns, and the ability to maintain consistent biological activity independent of donor age [13, 24]. The biologic properties are due in part because the nasal mucosa’ embryonic properties and potential to undergo direct neuronal differentiation under appropriate conditions [14]. Notably, genetic analyses of chromosome karyotype and tumor genes have also revealed no discernible variations following extensive in vitro passages [25]. Prior work from our group has also showed that OM-MSCs can upregulation GRP78 and Bcl-2 through miR-181a, which impedes neuronal pyroptosis in ischemia/reperfusion injury models. Meanwhile, cell tracing further confirmed that OM-MSCs are capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier in the MCAO model and migrating to the injured area to exert their therapeutic effects [14]. Therefore, the advantageous characteristics of OM-MSCs render them an optimal cellular resource for the treatment of nerve injury and inflammatory diseases, such as ischemic stroke.

The selection and validation of robust experimental models represent a key strength of this study, as previous research has predominantly focused on neuronal pathology in cell-based models of cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, often overlooking the potential roles and impacts of various glial cell types on neuronal function and recovery. Here, we provide initial evidence confirming the crucial role of microglial ferroptosis in amplifying neuroinflammatory responses and accelerating the pathological progression of neuronal apoptosis. Therefore, a neurocellular inflammation model was established by co-culturing microglia cells (BV2) and neurons (SH-SY5Y) under OGD/R conditions, aiming to simulate a crucial pathological process in the development of ischemic stroke. We also chose the classic MCAO rat model for further experimental exploration in the animal model of cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury. After a 24-hour co-culture of OM-MSCs and the OGD/R model, our findings demonstrate that OM-MSCs possess neuroprotective properties, facilitating shifts in microglia polarization while concurrently inhibiting ferroptosis. Notably, these effects were significantly enhanced following hypoxic preconditioning. OM-MSCs were administered via the caudal vein in MCAO rat models, and assessment of neural function scores revealed a significant improvement in neurologic function upon hOM-MSC transplantation, which also reduced microglial ferroptosis, ameliorated neuroinflammatory response, and safeguarded neuronal cellular activity in ischemic stroke models.

The therapeutic potential of MSCs is largely attributed to their capacity to modulate host cell function through the secretion of multifunctional factors, such as cytokines. These factors exert paracrine effects that have been shown to effectively ameliorate and restore neurological impairments [26, 27]. The specific factors secreted by hOM-MSCs that are responsible for the improvement of neurological function were not well elucidated prior to this work. To address this, we employed ATAC-seq and ISO-seq methodologies to examine disparities in chromatin accessibility and gene expression between OM-MSCs prior to and after exposure to hypoxic conditions. This high-throughput bioinformatics analysis revealed a remarkable enhancement in chromatin accessibility, transcription levels, and secretion of PGC-1α in hOM-MSCs. Using KEGG pathway classification analysis, we also observed significant enrichment in the PPAR pathway/ferroptosis pathway/NF-κB pathway when comparing gene expression profiles before and after hypoxia pretreatment.

PGC-1α is an essential coactivator that coordinates the expression of genes involved in the creation of new mitochondria through interactions with multiple transcription factors such as estrogen-related receptor α (ERRα) [28]. PGC-1α also has a pivotal role in various neurological disorders through its modulation of mitochondrial function and regulation of ROS levels [29], such as multiple sclerosis, where downregulation of PGC-1α expression is accompanied by a simultaneous decrease in mitochondrial antioxidants and uncoupling proteins, ultimately resulting in neurodegeneration [30]. PGC-1α can also have protective effects on neurons and glial cells such as primary astrocytes, where PGC-1α significantly attenuates oxidative damage and the production of proinflammatory mediators [30]. Prior research has shown a transient upregulation of PGC-1α expression following ischemic stroke, in mouse and human brain samples [31] and mice overexpressing microglial PGC-1α exhibit markedly reduced neurologic deficits after ischemic injury, accompanied by suppressed NLRP3 activation and proinflammatory cytokine secretion [31].

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) are nuclear hormone receptors that regulate genes related to energy metabolism and inflammatory processes [28]. To exert their biological activity, PPARs necessitate specific lipid ligands, form heterodimers with retinoic X receptors, and require coactivation by either PGC-1α or PGC-1β, which are encoded by the respective genes Ppargc1a and Ppargc1b [28]. Multiple reports suggest that PPARs agonists exhibit neuroprotective effects through the regulation of gene transcription associated with the pathogenesis of neuroinflammation [28, 32]. The interactions between PPARs and PGC-1α have garnered significant attention in relation to neuroprotection [32]. PPARα is a nuclear transcription factor capable of regulating fatty acid metabolism [28] and modulating ferroptosis [1, 5, 33]. PPARα regulates the transcriptional activity of GPX4 by binding to a peroxisome proliferator reaction element (PPRE) located in the intron of GPX4, inducing gene expression and effectively mitigating iron overload-induced ferroptosis in mouse liver [34]. Additionally, downregulation of PPARα leads to decreased GPX4 expression levels and increased ACSL4 expression levels, resulting in ferroptosis in a human mesangial cell (HMC) model of immunoglobulin A nephropathy [35]. NF-κB has a critical function in controlling immune and inflammatory responses, showing a notable increase in striatal neurons among individuals with neuroinflammatory disorders [36]. The NF-κB protein family exists most often as a complex of homologous or heterodimeric units, with P50/P65 being the prevailing configuration [37]. Our study provides compelling evidence that hOM-MSCs secrete PGC-1α, which binds to its receptor PPARα, leading to effective modulation of the NF-κB pathway via activation of GPX4 and inhibition of ACSL4 in microglial cells. This mechanism ultimately suppresses neuroinflammatory responses associated with ferroptosis in ischemic stroke models.

In this study, we initially investigated the impact of microglial susceptibility to ferroptosis on neuronal apoptosis in an OGD/R model and investigated the impact of hOM-MSCs on attenuating microglial ferroptosis and improving neuroinflammatory response in ischemic stroke models. We show that hOM-MSCs secrete a core functional factor called PGC-1α that can enhance microglia ferroptosis and immune regulation within the infarct focus. Exposure to hOM-MSCs not only ameliorates the neuroinflammatory response but also facilitates the restoration of neural functionality in cases of ischemic stroke. This effect is partially mediated by PGC-1α activation of the PPARα-GPX4/ACSL4-NFκB signaling cascades specifically within microglia at the site of infarction.

Limitations of our study include consideration of factors like cell survival rate, immunomodulatory effects, and homing cell numbers following MSC implantation in vivo, which determine the overall effectiveness of MSC therapy [27]. The presence of excessive inflammation and inadequate oxygen supply at the injury site can greatly impair the survival and effectiveness of MSCs in neuroinflammatory diseases [13]. While the enhanced adaptability of OM-MSCs to hypoxia and other microenvironments was achieved via prior exposure to a low-oxygen environment, this study did not investigate how the in vivo neuroinflammatory responses caused by ischemic stroke impact the survival duration, rate, and homing capacity of transplanted cells. Subsequent research should primarily concentrate on exploring the reciprocal influences and mechanisms between the neuroimmune microenvironment and MSCs in cases of ischemic stroke. Additionally, this study primarily utilized the rat MCAO model along with the mouse-derived BV2 microglial cell line (rather than human cells) and the SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line. Although these models are widely recognized and commonly used, they exhibit species-specific differences in immune responses, brain anatomy, and stroke pathology between rodents and humans. To address this limitation, future studies could adopt humanized cell models by employing a co-culture system of primary neurons and microglia differentiated from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) within the OGD/R model to validate key findings, thereby more accurately recapitulating human neuronal pathological responses. Moreover, human brain organoids could be constructed and exposed to simulated ischemic core and penumbra environments, enabling the investigation of hOM-MSC repair mechanisms and intercellular communication within a three-dimensional microenvironment that closely mimics the human brain. Furthermore, the mechanistic focus of this study was exclusively on microglial ferroptosis and polarization and their effects on neurons. However, astrocytes also play a pivotal role in post-stroke neuroinflammation and are involved in the process of ferroptosis. The PGC-1α secreted by hOM-MSCs may regulate astrocytic activity, thereby indirectly influencing neuronal survival and the inflammatory milieu. The absence of astrocyte-related investigations limits the comprehensiveness of the in vivo mechanism of hOM-MSCs. To overcome this limitation, future studies could establish a triple co-culture system comprising microglia, astrocytes, and neurons, followed by OGD/R treatment. By introducing hOM-MSCs or their conditioned medium into this more complex system, the intricate network mechanisms underlying intercellular interactions can be more thoroughly elucidated.

Conclusions

We aim to establish a robust theoretical foundation for the application of MSCs in animal models of ischemic stroke, thereby supporting their potential translation into clinical practice. To do this, we demonstrated that susceptibility of microglia to ferroptosis significantly amplifies their neuroinflammatory response, thereby accelerating neuronal apoptosis. We also explored the impact of hOM-MSCs on the restoration of neuron function in ischemic stroke, showing they can play a crucial role in promoting neuronal recovery and reducing neuroinflammatory responses in the infarct area of ischemic stroke. This is achieved through the secretion of PGC-1α, which improves microglia ferroptosis and immune regulation with the activation of PPARα-GPX4/ACSL4-NFκB signaling cascades in microglia being partially responsible for the beneficial effects observed. These findings indicate that hOM-MSC treatment may serve as a promising neuroprotective strategy for ischemic stroke and other neuroinflammatory disorders. Furthermore, there is potential for utilizing PGC-1α alone or in combination with hOM-MSCs for treating ischemic stroke.
