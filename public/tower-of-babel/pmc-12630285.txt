Europa Clipper Magnetometer Boom Deployment: A First Look at the Magnetometer Observations of the Spacecraft and the Interplanetary Magnetic Field
Authors: Corey J. Cochrane, Steven P. Joy, Haje Korth, John B. Biersteker, Jordana Blacksberg, Michael Bouchard, Jacob Contreras, Olivia R. Dawson, Krishan K. Khurana, Neil Murphy, Derek Palm, Mitch O. Perley, David R. Pierce, Ingo Richter, Christopher T. Russell, Sarah Sherman, Robert J. Strangeway, Benjamin P. Weiss, Lee Wigglesworth, Margaret G. ABSTRACT
NASA’s Europa Clipper flagship mission is designed to investigate the habitability of Jupiter’s moon Europa. A key instrument aboard the spacecraft is the Europa Clipper Magnetometer (ECM), a suite of fluxgate magnetometer sensors deployed on a boom to minimize spacecraft-induced magnetic interference. The ECM investigation aims to characterize Europa’s induced magnetic field, offering constraints on the salinity, depth, and thickness of its subsurface ocean. This work presents the first in-flight ECM observations acquired during the magnetometer boom deployment and shortly thereafter. We show how these observations provide the requisite evidence needed to validate a successful deployment. We also demonstrate how these observations can be used to calibrate the sensor offsets and to develop new magnetic field models of the spacecraft of varying complexity, thus enabling the robust removal of the instrument’s zero-levels which is critical for achieving the mission’s science objectives. We finally share preliminary calibrated magnetometer observations acquired over a two-month period after deployment, revealing a very active interplanetary magnetic field characteristic of solar maximum.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11214-025-01238-7.

Introduction

NASA’s Europa Clipper spacecraft was successfully launched from Kennedy Space Center on October 14th, 2024. It will spend five and one-half years in cruise before it reaches the Jovian system, where it will perform numerous flybys of Europa to characterize its habitability (Pappalardo et al. 2024). The Europa Clipper Magnetometer (ECM, Kivelson et al. (2023)) and the Plasma Instrument for Magnetic Sounding (PIMS, Westlake et al. (2023)) investigations are critical for jointly determining the thickness of the ice shell and the thickness and conductivity of the ocean. The ECM instrument consists of three triaxial fluxgate magnetometers, FG1, FG2, and FG3, mounted on a 8.5-m long extendable boom at distances of 8.5-m, 6.8-m, and 5.2-m, respectively. The sensors are strategically placed for optimal removal of the spacecraft magnetic field using gradiometric methods (Cochrane et al. 2023). On November 5th, 2024, with all three sensors powered on and collecting data in high-rate mode, the magnetometer boom was successfully deployed.

Most space missions equipped with magnetometers utilize a boom to distance the sensors from magnetic sources on the spacecraft, leveraging the rapid \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$1/r^{3}$\end{document}1/r3 magnetic field falloff of an assumed dipole source. It is common to witness the post-launch deployment of magnetometer booms by observing the magnetic field during this activity, e.g. Galileo (Kivelson et al. 1992), ØRSTED (Thomsen and Hansen 1999), Cassini (Dougherty et al. 2004), MESSENGER (Anderson et al. 2007), Rosetta (Glassmeier et al. 2007), BepiColombo (Heyner et al. 2021), and JUpiter ICy moons Explorer (JUICE, Brown et al. (2024). Note that these spacecraft differed in size and therefore required different boom lengths to accommodate their science needs. Europa Clipper is an exceptionally large spacecraft, as its bus is roughly 3-m wide, 4.7-m tall, and 4-m deep and contains a set of solar arrays that span an end-to-end distance of 30.5-m. The ECM magnetometer boom is only 8.5-m, but it is sufficiently long to adequately suppress the spacecraft magnetic field using gradiometry techniques with the use of three sensors. Magnetic field measurements are made during deployment for multiple reasons including: (1) verifying proper deployment if cameras are not available, (2) aiding in the validation of fluxgate frame kernel orientation, (3) updating or verifying the sensor offsets, (4) validating models of the spacecraft magnetic field developed on the ground pre-launch, and (5) revising the spacecraft magnetic model using the observations that were acquired during deployment.

The ECM boom was supplied by Northrop Grumman and is based on a design with decades-long flight heritage (e.g., Galileo, Cassini, DSCOVR, GOES-N, -O, -P, Global Geospace Science Wind and Polar spacecraft). The specific boom model designed for Europa Clipper is heavily based on the design for the GOES-R, -S, -T, -U spacecraft. The magnetometer boom is a coiled spring that deploys by natural spring tension release, where the three fluxgate sensors perform multiple rotations in the frame of the spacecraft as the boom uncoils. The orientations of the sensors in their stowed and deployed states are known (see the right panel of Fig. 1). Therefore, based on the mechanical specifications of the magnetometer boom, we know that sensor FG1 makes 8.43 rotations (8 known full rotations + \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$(291^{\circ} -136^{\circ})/360^{\circ}$\end{document}(291∘−136∘)/360∘ fractional rotations), sensor FG2 makes 6.78 rotations (6 known full rotations + \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$(291^{\circ}-10^{\circ})/360^{\circ})$\end{document}(291∘−10∘)/360∘) fractional rotations), and sensor FG3 makes 5.14 rotations (5 known full rotations + \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$(291^{\circ}-242^{\circ})/360^{\circ}$\end{document}(291∘−242∘)/360∘ fractional rotations). The anticipated total deployment time was about one minute based on deployment tests performed on the ground.

Since Europa Clipper lacks onboard cameras for direct observation of the boom, the deployment verification relied on several complementary indirect methods. The primary real-time indicator was a series of microswitches mounted on the boom, which provided start and stop pulses along with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}${\sim} 540$\end{document}∼540 intermediate pulses to monitor the deployment progress. Additional supporting evidence came from the spacecraft’s guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) system. First, transients in measured rotation rates of the spacecraft bus were observed at the moment ECM was commanded to deploy, throughout deployment, and most notably at the completion of deployment. Second, frequency-domain analysis of post-deployment dynamics revealed additional flexible modes consistent with predictions from a finite-element model of the spacecraft with the boom deployed. Third, a comparison of attitude control error before and after deployment showed an expected change in external torque due to the center-of-mass and center-of-pressure shifting closer together, modifying the torque on the spacecraft associated with the solar radiation. Finally, changes in angular rate response to thruster firings indicated an increased moment of inertia about the X-axis (i.e., the solar array axis), as indicated by a smaller angular rate change for the same thruster pulse. While these observations strongly suggest a successful deployment, they do not serve as direct verification. The uncertainties in measurements and the complexity of contributing factors — such as precise knowledge of the spacecraft’s center-of-mass, center-of-pressure, and solar radiation pressure effects — limit the confidence in drawing definitive conclusions from these indirect indicators alone.

The most compelling evidence and arguably the most precise data confirming the deployment of the boom came from observations acquired by the three fluxgate sensors. The analysis of these data is the focus of this work. We also demonstrate how these data can be used to measure sensor offsets and to develop new magnetic field models of the spacecraft. Finally, we demonstrate successful calibration during quiet periods of the interplanetary magnetic field and highlight the dynamic intervals that capture intense features characteristic of an active solar cycle, including coronal mass ejections and interplanetary shocks.

Boom Deployment Observations

On November 4th, 2024, Europa Clipper initiated the boom deployment sequence by first operating the three canister heaters to warm throughout the night. On the following morning, after an eight-hour period of maintaining the ECM canister temperature between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$20^{\circ}-25\,^{\circ}\mbox{C}$\end{document}20∘−25C∘, ECM was powered on and allowed to thermally stabilize. Just prior to deployment, the spacecraft GNC system deactivated attitude control and the inertial-measurement-unit (IMU) was enabled to acquire high-rate data. The frangibolt, which holds the boom in a stowed state prior to deployment, was then activated by a series of current pulses intended to release the latch mechanism when the temperature was in the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$80\,^{\circ}\mbox{C}$\end{document}80C∘ to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$120\,^{\circ}\mbox{C}$\end{document}120C∘. During this period, the current pulses produced large spikes that dominated the magnetic field observations prior to deployment. Frangibolt actuation occurred on November 5th, 2024 at 19:23 UTC within the interplanetary magnetic field (IMF) and beyond Earth’s magnetosphere. This released the uncoiling boom, during which time ECM measured the ambient magnetic field, comprised of the space environment and the spacecraft magnetic field. The measurements were taken with a resolution of 24-bits and a 128 sample/second cadence; however, on-board box-car averaging was used to reduce the sample rate to 16 samples/second to lower the data rate of the instrument. The long period oscillations associated with the deploying boom provide a means to capture the change in sensor offsets from those derived on the ground (here, referred to as delta-offsets), very similar to how offsets are measured at most magnetic calibration facilities. This is performed by measuring the magnetometer’s response at two different sensor orientations (rotated by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$180^{\circ}$\end{document}180∘) with respect to a constant background field. In the reference frame of the sensors, the sensor offsets rotate with the sensors and therefore appear static, while the magnetic field of the environment, including the spacecraft contribution, appears to rotate. Therefore, the delta-offsets associated with each sensor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$[\Delta B_{o,x},\Delta B_{o,y}]$\end{document}[ΔBo,x,ΔBo,y], are effectively found by determining the offset of each sinusoid. Rotating the Boom Deployment Observations

The rotation of the magnetometer observations made during deployment into the spacecraft frame is essential for validating and updating ground-based magnetic field models of the spacecraft so that the spacecraft field contribution can be robustly removed from the observations. This is not a trivial task considering that the sensors’ instantaneous positions and orientations during boom deployment are unknown; only their initial and nominal final positions and orientations are known from precise ground measurements, corresponding to the stowed and fully extended states of the boom, respectively. (ECM requires sensor deployment position uncertainty of no more than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$\pm 10\mbox{ mm}$\end{document}±10 mm (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$3\sigma $\end{document}3σ) and attitude uncertainty no more than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$\pm 8.5$\end{document}±8.5 mrad (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$3\sigma $\end{document}3σ). Ground based testing revealed that maximum axial displacement of the sensors was no more than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$1.3\mbox{ mm}$\end{document}1.3 mm from their initial positions and maximum angular misalignment of each sensor was no more than 5.41 mrad from their initial orientations. Sensor alignment and orientation knowledge will be further improved to less than 0.1 degree when spacecraft rolls are performed within Jupiter’s magnetosphere, which is further described by Kivelson et al. (2023)) In addition, the boom paused multiple times during the deployment (as was also observed during ground testing) and thus did not uncoil at a constant angular rate. The is likely due to either the stick-slip behavior associated with the boom’s mechanical unspooling mechanism or the non-continuous release of stored strain energy in the spring, both of which can mimic a stall. However, because the number of rotations for each sensor is precisely known from the mechanical design and ground testing, we can create a series of interpolated rotation matrices to despin the observations in the reference frame of the boom, each associated with an interpolated position of the sensors on their path along the boom. The Spacecraft Magnetic Field

With the observations fully calibrated (i.e. offsets removed, properly scaled, and orthogonalized) and rotated into the spacecraft frame, the data can be used to assess the magnetic field of the spacecraft. Here, we used thousands of despun measurements to develop a high-order model of the spacecraft field. In the subsequent subsections, we show these modeling results and compare them both to a single offset-dipole model, which is commonly assumed by dual-magnetometer science investigations, and to the 260-dipole spacecraft model developed before launch (Cochrane et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2025).

Multipole Model Developed from Despun Deployment Observations

When the boom is fully deployed, a three-fluxgate magnetometer suite allows the observations to be modeled as the superposition of a single offset-dipole (i.e. a magnetic moment offset in distance from the center of the spacecraft) and an ambient magnetic field vector assumed to be uniform across all three sensors. However, using the observations acquired during the boom deployment, one can effectively solve for a multiple offset-dipole model (i.e. mulitpole) to recreate the high-order moments near the spacecraft, facilitated by the thousands of measurements that were acquired by each sensor as they spun around the boom during deployment.

For the non-linear minimization, we randomized the initial orientation and location of all the dipole sources. We first attempted to initialize the locations of the dipole sources based on the known locations of the strongest magnetic sources; however, the results were not as accurate compared to when the locations were allowed to vary because there are many more sources present than the number of parameters that can be solved for. Modeling with fewer sources, therefore, becomes more accurate when the locations of the dipoles are not constrained. Because the non-linear inversion will produce slightly different parameters based on different initial guesses of these parameters, we performed a Monte Carlo simulation where both the initial positions and orientations of all the dipoles were randomized, thus providing a distribution of solutions that quantifies the uncertainty from the method.

One way to further improve the estimation errors is to develop the model using only those observations made near the end of deployment. Instead, we choose to show in the next section the performance of the gradiometer in the way it will be used in flight, leveraging a single offset-dipole developed from observations from the sensors when only in their deployed state where there is a high certainty of their position and orientation. The uncertainties associated with the sensor orientations and positions (attributed to boom stalling and various rotation rates) involved in deployment actually lead to increased errors in the multi-pole modeling results, which is partly mitigated by including ten seconds of observations pre- and post-deployment to increase the weight of those measurements that are associated with very low uncertainty in sensor position and orientation. Another source of error in this analysis that ultimately limits the performance is the slow drift of the ambient field over the 73-second deployment period. The gradiometer assumes a static field for the analysis window, so any drift in the field will tend to degrade the performance of the estimation.

Single-Pole Model Developed from Deployed Observations

It was shown in the previous sections that while it is necessary to use a high-order dipole model to accurately replicate observations made near the spacecraft, it is not necessarily needed to model the far-field of the spacecraft where the sensors are positioned on the boom. Here, we demonstrate the three-sensor gradiometer application, which involves modeling the deployed-only observations using a single offset-dipole and an ambient field vector that is assumed to be spatially uniform across all three sensors.

Figure 11 illustrates a 60-minute window of calibrated observations (with deployment delta-offsets removed and ground calibration applied) made by the magnetometers, collected roughly two minutes after the full deployment of the boom, in comparison with their model estimates. For each sample in the time series, a spacecraft magnetic field model was estimated using an approximately one-second wide window of observations (i.e., 17 samples in total, with eight samples before and after the sample being assessed), resulting in 57,600 different spacecraft models that can be analyzed statistically. Comparison to a Ground Based Magnetic Field Model

In this section, we compare the magnetic models of the spacecraft derived from in-flight observations with that developed on the ground (Cochrane et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2025). Note that even though the ground-based model was mature in its development and included measured magnetic moments from 260 sources on the spacecraft, nearly half of the sources did not have their orientations defined. The orientations of moments are important to consider because the strength of a magnetic dipole is twice as large at its pole than its equator. This implies that the ground-based model may not produce the exact vector components of the observations, but should closely agree with their magnitude.

Figure 12 compares the prediction of the ground-based model with the offset dipole model including 30 sources developed in Sect. 4.1 and with the single-dipole model developed in Sect. 4.2. The model was evaluated at various positions of the outboard sensor during boom deployment. As expected, the model predictions deviate most significantly near the spacecraft, and the discrepancy gradually decreases with distance away from the spacecraft, where the field is predominantly dipolar. In particular, the magnitude of the response from the single- and multi-offset dipole models are very similar (within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$15\%$\end{document}15%) to the magnitude of the ground-based model when the sensors are fully deployed. This result demonstrates the thoroughness of the Europa Clipper magnetic cleanliness program. The primary reason for the slightly larger errors between the estimates of the 30-dipole model and the single-dipole model near the end of deployment is due to the fact that the ambient field likely drifted during the 73-second deployment time (compared to the 1-second window used for the single-dipole model estimates) and due to uncertainties in the estimation of the inferred rotation matrices. Nevertheless, the results show that the spacecraft DC magnetic field at the ECM sensors meets the implementation requirement of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}${<} 1$\end{document}<1 nT at FG1 with margin. We will ensure this is the case by continuing to track the magnetic field of the spacecraft in cruise when all other subsystems and instruments are operational. Extraction of the Zero-Levels in the IMF

After the ECM boom had successfully deployed, the magnetometer was again turned on to observe the instrument and spacecraft checkout activities to monitor interference from November 22nd 2024 to January 22nd 2025. There was fortunately a quiet period time during the interval December 11–12 when all other instruments and subsystems were powered off, which provided an ideal opportunity to fine-tune the sensor offsets and monitor any changes in the spacecraft magnetic field. However, unlike during deployment, the sensor offsets could not be distinguished from the spacecraft magnetic field due to the fixed locations of the sensors. (The partitioning of sensor offset and spacecraft field during this period is left for future study.) Instead, we leverage a commonly used technique developed by Belcher (1973) to exploit the Alfvénic nature of the IMF to infer the zero-levels of the instrument, which consist of the combination of sensor offset and spacecraft magnetic field. Specifically, the low-frequency fluctuations of the magnetic field are mostly perpendicular to the background field while the magnitude of the field is assumed to remain constant over short timescales. Using a least-squares maximum-variance approach on the ground calibrated dataset, the zero-levels (in units of nT) were estimated for all sensors and axes, see Table 2 in Appendix A.

This could account for the larger discrepancy observed in the z-axis, but also could be attributed to the inability to resolve the sensor’s z-axis offsets during the boom deployment.

As each calibrated dataset differed from each other by no more than 0.1 nT RMSE over the entire 24-hour calibration period, the three sensors were averaged to provide a single estimate of the ambient magnetic field which is shown in Fig. 14. The data are rotated in the spacecraft frame, which is conveniently oriented in a near Radial-Tangential-Normal (RTN) frame when the spacecraft points its high-gain antenna toward the Sun to use as a heat shield. These frames are pictured in the right panel of Fig. 14, where the directions of Earth and the Sun are shown with respect to the spacecraft. These intervals show the classic characteristics of Alfvénic fluctuations that were used to fine-tune the magnetometer zero-levels. However, there are several prominent solar features, namely interplanetary shocks and coronal mass ejections, that occur with a frequency that is consistent with an active solar maximum. For example, there is a large and very abrupt rise in magnetic field strength measured on December 18th 2024 and January 1st 2025. Coincidentally, the PIMS instrument was powered on December 19th 2024 for a 1-hour period to assess the instrument’s post-launch health, directly in the wake of the first event. Further analysis of these features is underway but beyond the scope of this paper. In the meantime, this dataset demonstrates that the magnetometer is operating as intended and already showing to be extremely useful for monitoring heliophysics phenomena within the inner solar system.

Conclusion

The in-flight observations from the Europa Clipper Magnetometer during and after boom deployment provide compelling evidence for the successful mechanical and functional performance of the instrument. Analysis of the three fluxgate sensors confirms deployment and enables precise determination of sensor offsets, critical for achieving the mission’s science goals. Using these data, we developed and validated spacecraft magnetic field models of varying complexity, demonstrating that even simplified models can meet mission requirements with margin. The spacecraft’s magnetic field at the outboard sensor satisfies the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$<1~\mbox{nT}$\end{document}<1nT implementation requirement, reinforcing the effectiveness of the magnetometer boom design and sensor spacing. Preliminary science-quality data collected post-deployment reveal dynamic interplanetary magnetic field activity consistent with solar maximum conditions, highlighting the instrument’s value not only for Europa science but also for heliophysics investigations. These early results confirm that ECM is operating nominally and is well-positioned to deliver high-quality data throughout the Europa Clipper mission.
