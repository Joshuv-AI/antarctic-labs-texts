Perceived organizational digital empowerment and employee performance: the mediating role of work autonomy and the moderating role of proactive personality
Authors: Xiaogang Zhou, Qingguo Xiong, Liang Ding, Bingguo Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs digital transformation increasingly reshapes the workplace, understanding whether and how perceived organizational digital empowerment (PODE) enhances employee performance (EP) has become an important issue in organizational research. Drawing on self-determination theory (SDT), this study examines the relationship between PODE and EP, with work autonomy (WA) as a mediator and proactive personality (PP) as a moderator. Using a two-wave survey design, data were collected from 302 employees working in organizations in the technology, manufacturing, and financial sectors in China. Structural equation modeling and bootstrap analyses showed that PODE was positively associated with both EP and WA. WA, in turn, was positively associated with EP and mediated the relationship between PODE and EP. In addition, PP strengthened the positive relationship between PODE and EP. These findings underscore the importance of organizational digital empowerment in improving EP and suggest that organizations can enhance performance by strengthening employees’ WA and by using staffing and development practices that leverage employees’ PP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04639-7.Keywords: Perceived organizational digital empowerment, Employee performance, Work autonomy, Proactive personality, Self-determination theory, Two-wave longitudinal design

Introduction

Digital transformation is reshaping how work is organized, coordinated, and evaluated across contemporary organizations. Yet the benefits of digital technologies do not arise automatically from adoption alone. Prior research suggests that digital workplace arrangements are more likely to foster stronger organizational outcomes when employees perceive them as enabling rather than merely technical changes [1]. Relatedly, digitalization can function not only as a source of work demands but also as a job resource when it helps employees perform more effectively in changing work environments [2]. Against this background, the present study conceptualizes perceived organizational digital empowerment (PODE) as employees’ perceptions that their organization provides digital tools, technology-related support, and enabling digital work arrangements that help them accomplish tasks effectively and respond to digital work demands.

To explain why such perceptions matter, this study draws primarily on self-determination theory (SDT). SDT suggests that work environments are more likely to foster adaptive outcomes when they support employees’ basic psychological needs, especially the need for autonomy. Recent SDT-based workplace scholarship has likewise emphasized that autonomy-supportive conditions are central to employee motivation, functioning, and performance-related outcomes [3]. From this perspective, PODE should matter because organization-provided digital support may strengthen employees’ sense of discretion and self-direction in their work, thereby creating conditions that are more conducive to effective performance.

As a complementary perspective, the job demands-resources (JD-R) model suggests that digital work should be understood as a multifaceted context that may simultaneously generate pressures and provide enabling resources. Recent research has shown that the bright and dark sides of digitalization can be better understood by distinguishing digital demands from digital resources [4]. This complementary lens is relevant here because PODE may operate as a contextual resource that helps employees work effectively rather than merely imposing additional demands. Related research also suggests that organization-related digital conditions can shape employee adaptation and work-related outcomes. For example, research on organizational AI adoption and related digital workplace arrangements has shown that organization-level digital conditions can shape how employees respond to changing work environments [5]. Likewise, broader perceived organizational support has been linked to positive employee adaptation, including job crafting and work engagement [6]. These studies are informative, but they do not directly capture employees’ perceptions of digitally empowering organizational conditions. This gap points to the need to examine PODE as a distinct employee-perceived organizational construct.

Despite these advances, existing research has focused largely on digital leadership, digital transformation, and employee job crafting, while paying much less attention to PODE as a distinct employee-perceived organizational antecedent of employee performance (EP) [7]. Sharpening the conceptual boundaries of PODE is theoretically important because this construct is related to, but distinct from, several neighboring concepts. First, PODE differs from digital job crafting in its level of explanation. PODE captures employees’ perceptions of organization-provided digital support, whereas digital job crafting refers to employees’ self-initiated adaptation or redesign of work. The two constructs are therefore related but conceptually different: one reflects an organizationally provided condition as perceived by employees, whereas the other reflects employee-driven behavioral adjustment. Second, PODE is narrower and more digitally specific than perceived organizational support. Perceived organizational support reflects employees’ broader belief that the organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being, whereas PODE focuses specifically on whether employees perceive organizational digital arrangements as enabling their work [8]. Third, PODE differs from digital leadership. Digital leadership is leader-centered and emphasizes leaders’ behaviors or capabilities in guiding digital change, whereas PODE captures employees’ perceptions of digitally empowering organizational conditions that may extend beyond any single leader’s behavior [9]. Taken together, PODE is conceptualized here as an employee-perceived organizational condition that captures whether organization-provided digital tools, support, and work arrangements are experienced as enabling effective work, rather than as a broader socioemotional support perception, a leader-centered capability, or an employee-initiated adaptive behavior. Although prior studies suggest that organization-provided digital support can activate positive employee adaptation, they do not yet explain clearly whether and how PODE is associated with stronger EP.

A second gap concerns the mechanism linking PODE to EP. Existing research suggests that supportive organizational systems, leadership, and high-involvement work practices can stimulate proactive and innovative work behavior [10, 11]. However, the specific psychological pathway through which PODE is associated with stronger EP remains insufficiently specified. In the present study, work autonomy (WA) is proposed as the key mechanism. This focus is theoretically appropriate because the most immediate psychological implication of organization-provided digital support is likely to be employees’ perceived discretion over how, when, and with what tools they perform their work. Recent evidence also indicates that digitalization can substantially reshape employees’ subjective experience of work autonomy [12]. From an SDT perspective, when employees perceive higher levels of PODE, they are more likely to experience greater self-direction and discretion in their work, which should in turn support stronger EP.

A third gap concerns boundary conditions. Employees may differ in the extent to which they can convert PODE into improved performance outcomes. This issue points to proactive personality (PP) as a relevant boundary condition, because proactive individuals are more likely to identify opportunities, take initiative, and act on available resources [13]. Related research further suggests that the positive role of proactivity becomes more salient when employees experience greater work autonomy [14]. Accordingly, even under similar levels of PODE, employees high in PP may be better able to translate PODE into stronger EP. They may be better able to recognize, internalize, and utilize digitally empowering organizational conditions in ways that improve their performance.

Against this background, the present study examines whether and how PODE is associated with EP, focusing on the mediating role of WA and the moderating role of PP in a two-wave survey of employees in China. This study contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it sharpens construct clarity by positioning PODE as a distinct employee-perceived organizational condition in digital work research. Second, it extends SDT-based research on digital work by identifying WA as the most proximate psychological mechanism linking PODE to EP. Third, by incorporating PP as a boundary condition and drawing on JD-R as a complementary perspective, it provides a more nuanced explanation of when organization-provided digital support is more strongly associated with employee performance.

Research hypothesis and theoretical model

Perceived organizational digital empowerment and employee performance

In digital transformation contexts, PODE should be positively associated with EP. The benefits of digital technologies depend not only on their availability, but also on whether employees perceive organization-provided digital support as enabling and work-facilitating. Recent research suggests that digital transformation can reshape employees’ work experiences by influencing their autonomy, engagement, resilience, and job attitudes [15]. Employees also tend to respond more positively when digital transformation is experienced as augmentation and support rather than merely as automation or replacement [16].

From an SDT perspective, PODE should be positively related to EP because organization-provided digital support can function as an autonomy-supportive resource that fosters more self-endorsed motivation and more adaptive work regulation [17]. When employees perceive higher levels of PODE, they are more likely to feel supported in dealing with digitally enabled tasks and to regulate their work more effectively. This reasoning is also consistent with evidence from digital work contexts showing that basic psychological need satisfaction is positively associated with perceived productivity [18], and that organizational digital transformation can be linked to stronger employee performance through adaptive psychological states during technological adjustment [19]. Taken together, these findings suggest that higher levels of PODE should be associated with stronger EP.

Perceived organizational digital empowerment and work autonomy

In digital transformation contexts, PODE should be positively associated with WA. The implications of digital technologies depend not only on their availability, but also on whether employees perceive organization-provided digital support as enabling rather than controlling. When digital tools and work arrangements are experienced as facilitating rather than constraining, employees are more likely to feel greater discretion over how they perform and coordinate their work. Recent research suggests that employees’ perceptions of organization-provided digital support can reshape job characteristics, including work discretion and autonomy, and that human-centered implementation and timely digital support help employees experience digital change more positively [20]. Relatedly, evidence suggests that the relationship between digital technologies and autonomy is not automatic but depends on workplace conditions [21].

From an SDT perspective, PODE should be positively related to WA because organization-provided digital support can function as an autonomy-supportive condition that strengthens employees’ sense of volition and choice at work. When employees perceive digital tools and work arrangements as accessible, reliable, and enabling, they are more likely to feel that they can decide how to perform tasks, adjust work methods to situational demands, and manage work more independently. This reasoning is consistent with research on AI-enabled HRM showing that digital systems may function either as autonomy-supportive tools or as autonomy-controlling mechanisms, implying that employees’ empowering perceptions of organization-provided digital support are crucial for stronger work autonomy [22]. It is also consistent with recent evidence that autonomy-supportive organizational environments promote basic psychological need satisfaction and more adaptive employee outcomes [23]. Taken together, these findings suggest that higher levels of PODE should be associated with stronger WA.

The mediating role of work autonomy

WA refers to employees’ perceived discretion over work scheduling, methods, and procedures during task execution [24]. In the present study, WA is proposed as the focal mediator linking PODE and EP. Although SDT identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as three basic psychological needs, WA is treated here as the focal mediator because PODE most directly shapes employees’ room to decide how digitally enabled work is organized and carried out. Compared with competence and relatedness, WA therefore represents the most proximate work-design mechanism through which organization-provided digital support is likely to be associated with employee outcomes in digital work settings.

When employees perceive higher levels of PODE, they are more likely to experience digital tools and work arrangements as enabling rather than constraining. Research on remote and digitally supported work shows that employees’ experiences in digital work settings are strongly shaped by organizational job resources [25], and recent research likewise suggests that advanced technologies may either support or undermine employees’ sense of autonomy depending on whether such technologies are experienced as empowering or controlling [26]. From an SDT perspective, organizational practices implemented in an enabling rather than controlling manner are more likely to support employee autonomy [27]. Accordingly, PODE should be positively associated with WA because it reflects employees’ perceptions that organization-provided digital support offers flexibility, discretion, and guidance in carrying out work.

WA should, in turn, be positively associated with EP. From an SDT perspective, greater autonomy helps employees regulate their effort more effectively, sustain self-endorsed motivation, and adapt their work behaviors to task demands. In digital work settings, greater work autonomy is likely to be associated with stronger employee performance because it enables employees to use digital tools more flexibly to coordinate work and support knowledge sharing and decision-making processes [28]. Diary evidence further shows that daily autonomy positively predicts subsequent task and contextual performance [29], and autonomy has also been found to mediate the relationship between workplace conditions and task performance [30]. Taken together, these arguments suggest that PODE should be indirectly associated with EP through stronger WA.

The moderating role of proactive personality

PP is a relatively stable individual difference that reflects a person’s tendency to identify opportunities, take initiative, and act to influence the surrounding environment [31]. In the present study, PP is proposed to moderate the relationship between PODE and EP. Although PODE provides employees with digital tools, technological support, and supportive work arrangements, such organizational resources are unlikely to be translated into stronger performance to the same extent across employees. Employees differ in whether they interpret these digital conditions as opportunities to learn, act, and improve, or merely as external changes to which they must adapt.

From an SDT perspective, supportive work environments are more likely to foster positive outcomes when employees internalize and utilize contextual support in a self-directed way. Employees high in PP are more likely to notice opportunities, mobilize available resources, and act on them proactively. In the present context, this means that employees high in PP should be more likely to treat organization-provided digital support as a resource they can actively use and personalize in their work, whereas employees low in PP are less likely to engage with such support in a similarly self-directed manner. Accordingly, the same level of PODE should be more strongly associated with EP when employees are higher rather than lower in PP. This reasoning is consistent with recent empirical evidence. Zhang et al. (2022) [32] found that coworker proactive behavior stimulated employees’ autonomous motivation and job performance, and that this pattern was stronger when employees’ own PP was higher. Research in digital transformation settings also shows that PP is positively associated with employees’ digital transformation engagement and job performance [33]. Taken together, these findings suggest that when employees perceive a high level of PODE, those high in PP are more likely to internalize and utilize such digital support in ways that strengthen the positive relationship between PODE and EP.

Research design

Questionnaire design and data sources

Data were collected through a two-wave questionnaire survey from employees working in organizations located in Guangzhou, Haikou, Nanchang, Jiujiang, and Ganzhou, China. The sample included employees from state-owned enterprises, private enterprises, and public institutions, covering managerial, operational, marketing, professional support, and professional/technical positions. Participants were recruited with the assistance of alumni employed in these organizations, who helped distribute the survey links to eligible employees. The sampling approach should therefore be characterized as convenience sampling. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and respondents were informed that all information would be used for academic research only.

To reduce common method bias through temporal separation, data were collected at two time points with a three-week interval. At Time 1 (T1), Questionnaire V1 was used to collect respondents’ demographic information, perceived organizational digital empowerment (PODE), and proactive personality (PP). A total of 600 questionnaires were distributed, and 471 usable responses were returned, yielding a response rate of 78.50%. Three weeks later, at Time 2 (T2), Questionnaire V2 was distributed only to respondents who had completed the first survey. This second-wave questionnaire measured work autonomy (WA) and employee performance (EP). A total of 407 usable responses were returned, representing a retention rate of 86.41% based on the T1 sample.

The two waves were matched using the last four digits of respondents’ phone numbers as a unique identifier. This procedure yielded 386 matched cases. We then excluded cases that showed logically inconsistent responses or exhibited straight-lining, resulting in a final sample of 302 valid responses. This corresponds to a valid rate of 78.23% based on the matched sample. The descriptive statistics of the final sample are reported in Table 2.

Measurement of key variables and scale design

Unless otherwise stated, all study variables were measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). To improve clarity and contextual appropriateness, all scale items were presented in Chinese. Following standard translation procedures, the original English items were translated into Chinese and then back-translated by two bilingual researchers. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and the revised questionnaire was pilot-tested with a small group of employees before formal data collection.

Perceived organizational digital empowerment (PODE). PODE was measured with a four-item scale drawing on Tims, Bakker, and Derks (2012) [34] and contextually adapted to digitalized work settings to capture employees’ perceptions of organization-provided digital tools, technologies, and supportive work arrangements. Sample items included “My organization uses digital technologies to improve employees’ work efficiency” and “My organization introduces AI-related technologies to support employees’ task accomplishment.”

The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the KMO test. As reported in Table 3, the KMO values for PODE, WA, PP, and EP ranged from 0.762 to 0.868, all above the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating that the data were appropriate for factor analysis.

Overall, these results indicate that the measures used in this study demonstrated satisfactory reliability and convergent validity.

Common method bias and discriminant validity

Because all core variables were measured using self-reported data, common method bias was assessed first. Harman’s single-factor test showed that three factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, together accounting for 60.043% of the total variance. More importantly, the first unrotated factor explained 46.763% of the variance, which was below the commonly used threshold of 50%. These results indicate that common method bias was unlikely to pose a serious threat to the findings of this study.

Subsequently, CFA was conducted using AMOS 24.0 to assess the discriminant validity of PODE, WA, PP, and EP. As shown in Table 4, the hypothesized four-factor model provided a better fit to the data than the alternative one-factor, two-factor, and three-factor models. Specifically, the four-factor model showed an acceptable fit, with χ²/df = 2.143, RMSEA = 0.062, CFI = 0.946, TLI = 0.936, and SRMR = 0.042. Following commonly used guidelines, model fit was evaluated using χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. Values of χ²/df below 3, CFI and TLI above 0.90, and RMSEA and SRMR below 0.08 were treated as indicating acceptable model fit. In comparison, the alternative models all exhibited poorer fit. These results support the discriminant validity of the four constructs and further strengthen confidence in the distinctiveness of the study variables.

Model parameter estimation

To further examine the significance of the mediation effect, a Bootstrap procedure with 5,000 resamples was conducted, and the results are reported in Table 9. The indirect effect of PODE on EP through WA was 0.269, and its 95% bias-corrected confidence interval ranged from 0.119 to 0.479, while the 95% percentile confidence interval ranged from 0.116 to 0.478. Because both confidence intervals excluded zero, the indirect effect was significant. In addition, the direct effect of PODE on EP remained significant, with a 95% bias-corrected confidence interval of 0.239 to 0.729 and a 95% percentile confidence interval of 0.219 to 0.706, both excluding zero. These results indicate that WA partially mediates the relationship between PODE and EP. Therefore, H3 was supported.

Moderation effect testing

To further test the moderating role of PP in the relationship between PODE and EP, PROCESS Model 1 was conducted in SPSS 27.0. Following standard practice, PODE and PP were mean-centered before constructing the interaction term. To obtain robust estimates, the analysis was conducted using HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors.

The results showed that PODE had a significant positive effect on EP, with a coefficient of 0.393, SE = 0.062, p < 0.001, and PP also had a significant positive effect on EP, with a coefficient of 0.486, SE = 0.067, p < 0.001. More importantly, the interaction term between PODE and PP was significant, with a coefficient of 0.160, SE = 0.054, t = 2.977, p = 0.003, and a 95% confidence interval of [0.054, 0.266]. In addition, the interaction term explained additional variance in EP, with ΔR² = 0.013, F = 8.862, p = 0.003. These results indicate that PP significantly moderated the relationship between PODE and EP.

At the mean level of PP, the effect was 0.393, SE = 0.062, 95% CI [0.271, 0.516]. At a high level of PP, that is, 1 SD above the mean, the effect increased to 0.503, SE = 0.072, 95% CI [0.362, 0.646]. These results suggest that the positive effect of PODE on EP becomes stronger as PP increases.

Conclusions and discussion

Main findings

The present study generally supports the proposed framework. Overall, the findings indicate that employees’ perceptions of PODE were positively associated with EP, partly through WA, and that this positive association was stronger among employees with higher PP. At the same time, these findings should be interpreted as associational rather than strictly causal, given the short-term two-wave design and the reliance on self-reported measures.

First, the positive association between PODE and EP suggests that the performance-related value of digitalization may depend not only on the presence of digital technologies, but also on whether employees perceive organization-provided digital conditions as supportive and work-facilitating. When employees experience organizational digital resources, technical support, and enabling conditions as accessible, useful, and supportive, they may be better positioned to function effectively in digitalized work settings. This reading fits recent SDT-based work showing that supportive digital management practices are associated with more adaptive employee functioning and better work outcomes [38]. In this sense, PODE may matter because it helps employees engage with digitally enabled work more effectively.

Second, the findings suggest that PODE is linked to EP partly because it is associated with stronger WA. When employees perceive digitally supportive organizational conditions as enabling rather than constraining, they may experience greater discretion, control, and self-direction in how they organize and carry out their work. This pattern is also compatible with SDT, which highlights autonomy-supportive conditions as an important antecedent of self-determined motivation and positive work functioning; meta-analytic evidence further shows that such environments are positively associated with need satisfaction, well-being, and constructive work behaviors [39]. Research in the Chinese digital work context similarly suggests that digital work arrangements are closely related to employees’ autonomy and that such autonomy is an important pathway through which digital work conditions shape employee outcomes [40]. Taken together, these results indicate that PODE is associated with EP partly through employees’ experience of greater work autonomy, rather than through autonomy alone.

Third, the moderating role of PP suggests that the performance-related implications of PODE are not uniform across employees. Employees with higher PP may be more likely to recognize, internalize, and effectively utilize digitally supportive organizational conditions, thereby translating such support into stronger EP. Earlier research on proactive personality points in the same direction by showing that PP strengthens the positive implications of autonomy-related work conditions for employee outcomes [41]. From this perspective, PODE may create opportunity structures, but employees differ in how actively they recognize and use those opportunities.

Finally, the findings should also be interpreted in light of the Chinese context. Empowerment-related practices may not carry the same meaning across national and organizational settings, and the present findings may be especially meaningful in a context in which organizational support and legitimacy shape how such practices are understood [42, 43]. However, because cultural values were not directly measured in the present study, this interpretation should remain contextual rather than definitive.

Theoretical contributions and managerial implications

This study makes three main theoretical contributions.

First, the study strengthens construct clarity in the digital work literature by positioning PODE as a distinct employee-perceived organizational construct. Rather than treating digitally supportive organizational conditions as equivalent to employee-initiated adaptation, broader perceived organizational support, or leader-centered digital leadership, the study clarifies the conceptual space in which PODE operates. By doing so, it provides a clearer theoretical basis for examining how employee-perceived digitally supportive organizational conditions are associated with employee-level outcomes.

Second, the study extends SDT-based research on digital work by specifying work autonomy as a proximal autonomy-related mechanism through which PODE may be associated with employee performance. Although prior research has emphasized the motivational value of autonomy, less attention has been paid to how employee-perceived organizational digital support may be linked to performance through greater discretion and self-direction in daily work. By focusing on WA, the present study identifies a more concrete work-design pathway through which digitally supportive organizational conditions may be linked to employee outcomes.

Third, the study enriches understanding of the boundary conditions and contextual relevance of digital work research by showing that the implications of PODE are contingent rather than uniform across employees. In particular, the study highlights the role of employee dispositions in shaping how digitally supportive conditions are translated into performance-related outcomes. In addition, by testing the proposed framework in China, the study adds context-specific evidence from a non-Western setting and underscores the importance of considering cultural and organizational context when interpreting how employees respond to PODE.

The findings also provide several managerial implications.

First, managers should move beyond a technology-centered view of digital transformation and pay closer attention to employees’ perceptions of PODE. Simply introducing digital tools, platforms, or intelligent systems is unlikely to be sufficient. What matters is whether employees experience these resources as accessible, useful, and supportive in their daily work. Managers should therefore complement digital transformation initiatives with clear communication, practical training, technical assistance, and ongoing feedback so that employees experience digitalization as meaningful support rather than as an abstract organizational agenda.

Second, managers should recognize WA as an important pathway through which PODE may be associated with stronger EP. Digitally supportive organizational conditions are more likely to contribute to positive performance outcomes when employees experience greater discretion in how they organize, schedule, and execute their work. Accordingly, organizations should avoid using digital technologies primarily to intensify monitoring and control. Instead, digital systems should be designed and implemented in ways that support flexibility, decision latitude, and problem solving within clear organizational boundaries.

Third, managers should pay closer attention to differences in PP when designing digital management practices. Because the positive association between PODE and EP was stronger among employees with higher PP, organizations should not assume that all employees will benefit from digitally supportive conditions to the same extent. In recruitment and selection, managers may place greater emphasis on identifying applicants who show initiative, adaptability, and willingness to engage with new technologies. In training and development, organizations may also invest in programs that strengthen proactive problem solving, digital learning, and self-directed work behaviors. At the same time, employees with lower PP may require more structured guidance, coaching, and support to benefit more fully from digitally enabled work environments.

Research limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations, and these also point to promising directions for future research.

First, the generalizability of the findings is constrained by the cultural and sampling context of the study. The data were collected from employees in China, and the sample was obtained through convenience sampling across multiple organizations and sectors. Although this context provides a meaningful setting for examining PODE, the findings should be interpreted with caution when extending them to other national, cultural, or institutional environments. As discussed earlier, employees’ interpretations of empowerment, autonomy, and proactive behavior may vary across cultural settings. Future research could therefore conduct cross-cultural comparative studies to examine whether the relationships among PODE, WA, PP, and EP remain stable across contexts characterized by different levels of power distance, collectivism, or uncertainty avoidance. Future research could also examine whether WA and PP operate differently in more individualistic settings, where discretion and self-initiated behavior may be interpreted differently.

Second, although the study adopted a two-wave design with temporal separation between the measurement of key variables, the design still does not fully establish causal direction. The use of a short-term two-wave panel improves upon a purely cross-sectional design and helps reduce some concerns regarding common method bias. However, the three-week interval may be too short to capture meaningful changes in employee performance, which limits the extent to which the design can speak to true change over time. Accordingly, the present design is better understood as supporting temporal separation and temporal ordering rather than strong causal inference. More specifically, the findings are better interpreted as evidence of temporally separated and temporally ordered associations than as evidence of meaningful within-person change in employee performance or strong causal effects over time. Reciprocal relationships and omitted-variable explanations therefore cannot be ruled out. Future research could strengthen causal inference by using longer panel intervals, multi-wave longitudinal designs, including cross-lagged panel models, daily diary or experience-sampling methods, and experimental or quasi-experimental approaches that allow stronger tests of temporal ordering and underlying psychological processes.

Third, the measures relied primarily on self-reported data, which may introduce subjective bias. This issue is particularly relevant for EP, because even though self-reported performance is commonly used in organizational research, it may not fully capture actual work outcomes and may be influenced by social desirability or self-enhancement tendencies. Similarly, PODE and WA were measured through employee self-reports, which is theoretically meaningful for understanding employees’ perceptions of organizational digital support and subjective autonomy, but still raises concerns about shared method variance. Although temporal separation and statistical checks such as Harman’s single-factor test were used, concerns about common method variance cannot be fully ruled out. Future research could therefore adopt multi-source designs by combining employee reports with supervisor ratings, peer evaluations, or objective performance indicators. Such designs would provide a more rigorous test of whether the observed relationships remain consistent across different measurement sources.

Fourth, the present study focused on WA as the main mediating mechanism and PP as the main moderating condition. Although this focus ensures theoretical parsimony, it precludes the examination of other potentially relevant mechanisms and boundary conditions. In digital work settings, employees’ responses to PODE may also be shaped by variables such as digital self-efficacy, competence satisfaction, psychological safety, leadership style, team climate, or technology-related stress. Future research could extend the present framework by incorporating additional mediators and moderators to develop a more comprehensive understanding of when and how PODE is associated with employee outcomes.

Finally, although this study clarified the distinction between PODE and employee-initiated digital job crafting, further refinement of the construct remains valuable. PODE was conceptualized here as employees’ perceptions of organizationally provided digital resources, support, and enabling conditions, but future studies could examine the construct from a multilevel perspective by combining employee perceptions with organization-level indicators of digital infrastructure, digital support practices, or formal digital transformation initiatives. Such work would help further strengthen construct validity and offer a more integrated account of how digitally supportive organizational conditions are linked to individual experiences and work-related outcomes.

Overall, despite these limitations, the present study provides a useful step toward understanding how PODE is associated with EP, partly through WA and under the boundary condition of PP. Future research that builds on this framework using stronger designs, broader samples, and richer measurement strategies will be well positioned to deepen understanding of digital work in contemporary organizations.
