Muscle synergy-driven ensemble learning framework for individualized stroke gait rehabilitation
Authors: Jaehyuk Lee, Eunchan Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study proposes a novel ensemble machine learning (ML) framework integrating neurophysiological principles from muscle synergy analysis to support clinical decisions in stroke gait rehabilitation. The framework leverages spatial and temporal features of muscle synergies using a hierarchical ensemble model to improve classification performance and interpretability. Muscle synergies were extracted using non-negative matrix factorization from surface EMG recordings of 380 participants, comprising 120 healthy and 260 post-stroke individuals, each contributing one leg. Feature vectors were derived through a bidirectional decomposition process, wherein either the spatial (W) or temporal (H) synergy matrix was fixed to normative patterns obtained from healthy controls. This enabled the extraction of patient-specific deviations in coordination and activation timing, serving as interpretable indicators of stroke-related neuromuscular impairment. Separate classifiers trained on each feature domain were integrated via meta-regression, achieving classification accuracies above 98% across all configurations on the internal test dataset. After training, performance-weighted feature importance values from tree-based models validated the clinical relevance of learned classification criteria. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values quantified sample-specific feature contributions on the test dataset, ensuring individual-level interpretability of predictions. The proposed framework bridges stroke-related neuromuscular impairments and clinical insights, laying a foundation for integrating neurophysiologically grounded ML models into rehabilitation.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Diseases, Health care

Introduction

In clinical settings, precise movement assessment facilitates the early diagnosis, treatment planning, and monitoring of neuromuscular impairments. Consequently, movement analysis has become increasingly crucial, particularly in rehabilitation medicine, as it provides a comprehensive understanding of motor function, optimizes performance, and informs individualized rehabilitation strategies1. Over the past few decades, the development of movement analysis has accelerated, particularly with the advent of muscle synergy analysis (MSA), which employs unsupervised machine learning (ML) techniques to elucidate the neurophysiological control of movement2. A key principle of MSA is to uncover the spatial and temporal coordination of the activation of multiple muscles to produce efficient movement, rather than controlling each muscle independently3. Currently, in the fields of neurological4 and musculoskeletal5 diseases, MSA has provided valuable insights into motor control mechanisms by factorizing coordinated activation into spatial and temporal features.

Among various neuromuscular impairments, stroke has been the most frequently investigated using MSA to identify its gait features, due to its high incidence rate6. Notably, stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide7 and causes persistent post-survival motor impairments that significantly affect the quality of life8. Many studies have elucidated specific patterns of gait dysfunction in stroke patients, such as the phenomenon of synergy merging, in which multiple muscle synergies combine into a single, less efficient, and specific synergy during gait9,10. Recovery from these pathological gait patterns is therefore considered a critical target in stroke rehabilitation to restore more normalized gait.

Several prior studies have applied muscle synergy features, particularly temporal coefficients, in combination with tree-based ML models to assess motor impairment and predict rehabilitation outcomes in stroke patients. For example, Yang et al. demonstrated the utility of temporal muscle synergy features by applying random forest classifiers to characterize impairment severity during sit-to-stand tasks11 and to estimate the effects of short-term rehabilitation12. An et al. further extended this approach to kinetic assessments using handrail force data in severely affected patients13. While these studies focused on temporal features to assess functional states, our study integrates both gait-specific temporal and spatial features, placing particular emphasis on model-level interpretability for pre-rehabilitation decision-making.

By utilizing MSA along with knowledge to understand factorized features, clinicians and practitioners can develop targeted rehabilitation protocols, optimize training strategies, and refine therapeutic interventions to improve gait performance in stroke patients. However, its clinical adoption remains limited due to ambiguities in interpreting and prioritizing synergy features, which often requires substantial practitioner expertise and introduces variability in application14–16. Therefore, in this study, we propose that an ensemble ML framework that highlights clinically relevant synergies may facilitate effective translation into practice. To the best of our knowledge, despite the introduction of various ML models and advancements in computer-aided diagnostic systems designed to reduce subjectivity and improve the consistency in assessments using electromyography (EMG)17,18, the application of MSA for therapeutic decision-making within an ML framework remains extremely rare. In particular, our approach introduces a bidirectional decomposition strategy anchored to normative synergy templates and incorporates SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)-based individualization to enhance interpretability and clinical relevance.

In addition to their use in clinical diagnostics, EMG signals have been widely applied in robotics to train ML algorithms for controlling prosthetic arms and legs. This process typically involves a series of preprocessing steps such as signal filtering, normalization, and feature extraction in both the time and frequency domains. Among these steps, segmentation window size is critical, as it influences both temporal resolution and feature reliability. Shorter windows enhance temporal precision but increase variability, while longer windows stabilize signals but may miss transient events19. Commonly extracted features include root mean square, mean absolute value, median frequency, and wavelet coefficients. These features are then used as inputs for various ML models, including support vector machines, random forests, and deep learning architectures, to predict motor intentions and classify movement patterns. However, these studies have primarily focused on improving the model performance, often overlooking the interpretability of the contributing features or the physiological relevance of the learned representations20,21.

To address these limitations, this study proposed a framework aimed at enhancing the clinical interpretability of EMG data by extracting physiologically meaningful features using MSA. These features were subsequently used to train an ensemble ML model. Feature importance metrics and SHAP analysis were applied to identify synergy components that most influenced the model’s output, enabling individualized prioritization of muscle synergies, which refers to selecting the most relevant features for each patient based on SHAP-derived importance values. Furthermore, a voting-based classification approach was adopted by integrating four distinct tree-based ML models to ensure performance robustness across varying data characteristics. To support reliable interpretation, model-specific weights were assigned based on each model’s predictive performance when aggregating SHAP values and feature importance.

Notably, the spatial and temporal features derived from MSA exhibit distinct structural and statistical characteristics. Separate voting classification models were developed for each feature type and subsequently integrated into a unified ensemble model (Fig. 1). This approach provides a unified framework for decision making by consolidating predictions from both feature sets into a single model.

Overview of the muscle synergy-driven ML framework for stroke gait rehabilitation: from EMG to SHAP-informed therapeutic decision-making.

Accordingly, the proposed framework may offer several stage-specific advantages along the analytical pipeline. First, the use of non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to extract muscle co-activation patterns (spatial features) and activation timings (temporal features) allows clinicians to identify which muscle groups and gait phases may be most influential in each patient. Second, these quantified features can be translated into a preliminary prioritization scheme, guiding therapists toward muscle synergies that the model indicates as clinically significant. Third, because the framework summarizes its outputs using familiar visualization tools such as feature importance scores and SHAP plots, it supports clinical interpretation without requiring expertise in ML, potentially improving usability in practice.

Results

Extracted muscle synergy

Four muscle synergies were extracted from all participants based on the criterion that collectively accounted for over 90% of the EMG variance in healthy controls (variance accounted for, VAF = 0.95 ± 0.03). This number aligns with previously reported synergy structures identified during human locomotion22,23.

Numerous studies on MSA have demonstrated that NMF serves as a robust method for uncovering modular motor control strategies, as it enforces physiological non-negativity constraints and aligns closely with the structure of real-world muscle activation patterns22,23. By grouping co-activated muscles into low-dimensional modules, NMF captures the functional coordination among individual muscles, thereby enhancing the interpretability of complex motor behaviors. In this study, each extracted synergy was explicitly linked to its physiological role based on dominant muscle contributions and their established biomechanical functions during gait.

As summarized in Table1, Synergy 1 (S1) predominantly involves the hamstring muscles, including the biceps femoris (BFM) and semimembranosus (SEM), functioning primarily for deceleration and stabilization of the leg during the terminal swing and early stance phases. Synergy 2 (S2), characterized by strong activation of Rectus Femoris (RFM) and Adductor Magnus (ADD), contributes to knee extension and hip adduction, playing a key role in load acceptance and medial-lateral control during the early stance phase. Synergy 3 (S3), predominantly involving the Gastrocnemius (GCM), is critical for forward propulsion and push-off during terminal stance and pre-swing phases. Finally, Synergy 4 (S4), involving Gluteus Medius (GLU), Vastus Medialis (VMM), and Tibialis Anterior (TIB), supports pelvic stability and stance phase control, particularly during mid-stance24,25..

Moreover, to assess the impact of stroke on synergy organization, we applied a bidirectional decomposition strategy. Extracting the spatial synergy features of stroke patients with fixed temporal features from healthy controls revealed a significant decrease in VAF (0.19 ± 0.16), indicating severely disrupted muscle coordination post-stroke. Conversely, extracting temporal synergy features with fixed spatial patterns resulted in a relatively higher VAF (0.70 ± 0.10). These findings support the hypothesis that spatial features (muscle coordination) are more substantially affected by stroke compared to temporal features (activation timing), consistent with previous clinical observations of altered neuromuscular recruitment patterns in post-stroke gait25,26.

By clearly relating NMF-derived synergy structures to established biomechanical functions and clinical phenomena, our proposed framework significantly enhances interpretability and facilitates clinical decision-making for targeted and individualized gait rehabilitation.

Cross-validation results on the training dataset

Five-fold stratified CV was applied to the training dataset to evaluate the internal performance of each tree-based model, including random forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), LightGBM (LGBM), and CatBoost (CATB), before final ensemble integration. As summarized in Table 2, all four models demonstrated high and stable performance across both spatial and temporal feature types. However, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests revealed no statistically significant performance differences among models within each feature domain. This result suggests that no individual model was consistently superior in the training context. Accordingly, to enhance reliability and integrate the complementary strengths of individual classifiers, we adopted a logistic regression ensemble model (LR-EM). This ensemble model achieved perfect scores (1.0000 ± 0.0000) across all evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), during 5-fold stratified CV.

Discussion

This study proposes an ensemble ML framework that integrates neurophysiological insights from MSA with advanced ML techniques to support clinical decision-making in post-stroke gait rehabilitation.

In our study, NMF successfully extracted four distinct and physiologically interpretable motor modules from healthy controls, accounting for over 90% of the total EMG variance, which is consistent with previous findings in gait-related synergy studies10,27,28. We identified that stroke participants exhibited more spatially diffuse and simplified synergy patterns, along with temporally dispersed and less phasic activation profiles. In addition, the total EMG VAF by the fixed four-synergy was notably reduced in stroke participants compared to healthy controls, suggesting that the normative synergy structure could not fully account for the altered motor patterns. This discrepancy may be explained by prior reports of synergy merging in post-stroke individuals29, and supports the use of synergy decomposition as an explainable and clinically meaningful tool for identifying neuromuscular impairments following stroke30.

To implement the framework, each extracted synergy was decomposed into spatial (muscle weighting) and temporal (activation coefficient) components. These were used to train separate voting-based classifiers, whose outputs were subsequently integrated through a meta-regression layer to form a hierarchical ensemble structure. All tree-based base classifiers achieved over 96% accuracy, reaffirming their suitability for modeling high-dimensional, structured physiological data31. The final ensemble model achieved 98% overall accuracy and an AUC of 1.00 despite class imbalance, underscoring its robustness and generalizability.

SHAP analysis of the final model indicated that spatial features contributed more significantly to classification decisions than temporal features, in line with clinical findings that stroke-related motor impairments are more strongly associated with abnormal muscle coactivation and disorganized recruitment rather than with altered timing patterns32. While spatial synergies may serve as robust markers of neuromuscular dysfunction, temporal features provide complementary insights into phase-specific gait abnormalities. The integration of both types of features enables physiologically grounded and interpretable decision-making within a complex clinical context. To deepen model interpretability, we further analyzed the feature importance rankings generated by the ensemble model.

First, the critical strength of this framework lies in its ability to extract meaningful feature importance values from high-performing models. In the ensemble context, we compute a composite importance score by weighting individual model attributes according to their predictive accuracy. Based on the training dataset used in this study, GCM in the spatial domain emerged as the most critical predictor, particularly its involvement in S3. In the temporal domain, the peak activation value of S3 was the most influential feature. Given that S3 is primarily associated with forward propulsion and gait speed, its impairment has direct implications for reduced walking efficiency in patients with stroke. A previous study reported that paretic propulsion is a key indicator of motor recovery in post-stroke individuals24. Therefore, the selection of S3 as the most influential feature of the ensemble model based on the training dataset supported the physiological validity of the model’s interpretation.

Interestingly, when the trained model was applied to the test dataset, different SHAP-based feature contribution patterns emerged. Specifically, BFM in S1 and number of peaks in the temporal profile were found to contribute most consistently across the patient cohort. This finding aligns with previous reports highlighting BFM’s dual role in swing deceleration and early stance propulsion25. Although GCM in S3 was highly influential during training, its contribution varied considerably in the test set, suggesting that S3 may serve as an indicator of residual lower-limb function depending on individual motor capacity. Such variability may reflect underlying clinical heterogeneity across patients, including differences in stroke severity, lesion location (e.g., cortical versus subcortical), or stage of recovery (acute, subacute, chronic), which are known to affect muscle recruitment patterns and synergy preservation. Meanwhile, S1, characterized by strong involvement of the BFM, appears to be a clinically challenging synergy, as evidenced by its consistently high ranking in both SHAP values and feature importance metrics across datasets26,33.

Second, the proposed framework enables individualized interpretation of neuromuscular control strategies. It leverages SHAP values weighted by model performance to assess the contribution of each feature influencing stroke classification. This provides a data-driven basis for prioritizing rehabilitation targets tailored to each patient’s unique motor coordination profile. This study presents a method for prioritizing rehabilitation targets by identifying synergy components most relevant to gait function in each individual. As shown in Figs. 6 and 7, the contribution of each muscle synergy to the gait impairment varies across individuals. This demonstrates that stroke disrupts the modular organization of motor control in a highly individualized manner. Muscle synergies, which are latent structures governed by the spinal and supraspinal pathways, become less distinct following a stroke. As previously reported, this disruption manifests differently across individuals and includes reduced synergies9,10, abnormal coactivation34,35, and delayed temporal activation36,37. These neurophysiological changes contribute to increased energy expenditure and decreased adaptability during gait38. While healthy individuals typically exhibit four to five distinct synergies for walking, stroke patients often show merged synergies, reducing the number of synergies with variability in the merging patterns39. The individualized SHAP values produced by the proposed ensemble model offer a valuable quantitative basis for the development of individualized motor rehabilitation prescriptions.

To illustrate this individualized approach in practice, Fig. 8 presents SHAP-based feature contributions for two representative stroke participants. For Sample 1 (Fig. 8A), the most influential spatial feature was the TIB across multiple synergies (S1–S4), suggesting excessive or poorly coordinated activation of the ankle dorsiflexor throughout the gait cycle. The dominant temporal feature was the number of activation peaks in S4, indicating fragmented timing likely associated with instability during late stance or pre-swing. These patterns suggest a need to improve distal control and stabilize ankle activation timing through dorsiflexor retraining or timing-based coordination exercises. In contrast, Sample 2 (Fig. 8B) showed BFM in S1 as the top spatial contributor, reflecting overactivation of the hamstrings during terminal swing and early stance. The peak amplitude of S4, associated with push-off, was the most relevant temporal feature and exhibited a low value, pointing to diminished propulsion capacity. Although synergy matrices W and H were each normalized to enable inter-subject comparability, their product WH retains meaningful activation magnitudes across time. This allows peak amplitude features to reflect biologically relevant modulations in muscle recruitment intensity. Prior studies have demonstrated that abnormal peak timing and amplitude in synergy activations are characteristic markers of motor impairment and synergy merging post-stroke29,30,40. For this individual, interventions targeting hamstring modulation and enhancement of plantarflexor output during pre-swing may be prioritized. These case-specific insights highlight the framework’s potential to support precision rehabilitation by linking model explanations to interpretable, phase-specific motor deficits in individual patients.

Finally, in this study, a methodological innovation was introduced by anchoring synergy extraction to those derived from healthy individuals and subsequently extracting each feature of the patients with stroke relative to these fixed references41. This approach allows for the identification of compensatory mechanisms that emerge when stroke patients are assumed to exhibit normal synergies as well as the detection of abnormal synergy structures under the assumption of preserved muscle coordination. Specifically, spatial features were extracted by fixing the temporal activation patterns of the control group, thereby highlighting the altered patterns of muscle recruitment. Conversely, temporal features were extracted by fixing the muscle weightings, thereby enabling the identification of timing-related disruptions in the synergy structure. This bidirectional decomposition strategy offers insights that are difficult to obtain through traditional EMG or kinematic analyses alone, providing clinicians and researchers with an intuitive basis for determining whether rehabilitation should prioritize spatial reorganization or temporal resequencing of motor commands.

Our ensemble framework was designed such that models with higher predictive performance contribute more significantly to the final output. As a result, muscle synergy features identified as important by more accurate models can be regarded as more clinically reliable. In particular, when interpreting the coordination ability of specific muscles related to an individual patient’s gait characteristics, prioritizing the outputs of well-validated models is expected to support the development of more effective and individualized rehabilitation strategies.

Nonetheless, several limitations of this study merit discussion. First, the relatively small sample size and focus on individuals in the chronic phase of stroke recovery may limit generalizability and increase the risk of overfitting, despite the use of stratified CV and ensemble averaging. Although the final ensemble model achieved a perfect AUC of 1.0 on the internal test set, this result should be interpreted with caution, as it may overestimate real-world performance. External validation in larger and more diverse stroke populations, including acute and subacute phases, is needed. Second, the current framework does not incorporate key clinical variables such as stroke subtype, lesion location, or time since onset, all of which influence neuromuscular control and recovery. Including these factors in future models may enhance clinical utility and individualization. Third, this study used a binary classification task (healthy vs. stroke), which, while intentionally simplified to enhance interpretability, may limit clinical granularity. Prior studies have explored multi-class classification based on stroke severity, which may offer more actionable insights. Future work will extend the framework to incorporate functional scores and severity indices for multi-class or multitask modeling. Fourth, anchoring synergy extraction to healthy controls enables physiologically meaningful comparisons but may introduce normative bias by assuming idealized motor patterns. This could reduce sensitivity to atypical but functionally adaptive strategies. Fifth, although the framework demonstrated strong offline interpretability and performance, it has not been tested in real-time settings. Clinical deployment will require optimization for latency, computational efficiency, and robustness to signal variability. Sixth, the use of min–max scaled feature importance and model-weighted SHAP values improves interpretability but may obscure the absolute impact of individual features. Normalization and model averaging can reduce consistency and dilute clinically meaningful distinctions.

In summary, this study presents a clinically applicable ML framework leveraging muscle synergy features. By integrating high-performance classification with interpretable and scalable modules for preprocessing, feature extraction, and model training, the framework provides a quantitative basis for prioritizing rehabilitation targets based on neuromuscular control structure. In future applications, the framework may be further developed into a real-time decision-support system within gait laboratories or wearable EMG devices, assisting therapists in individualized rehabilitation planning. Robustness across diverse clinical settings may be improved by incorporating EMG quality control and integrating multimodal biosignals, including inertial, kinematic, and recently proposed electrostatic field data42,43.

Methods

Data acquisition

EMG data were collected from 380 participants, each contributing one leg. Among them, 120 were right-dominant legs from healthy controls and 260 were paretic legs from chronic stroke patients. Patients were selected if they could walk regardless of age, time since onset, or affected brain regions. Table 4 presents the demographics and functional status of the participants. Healthy controls (mean age: 63.4 ± 6 yrs) were subsequently recruited to be age-matched with the stroke group. An independent t-test confirmed that there was no statistically significant difference in age between the two groups (p> 0.05). Participants who were unable to perform the experiment because of cognitive impairment were excluded. All participants were recruited with the approval of the Institutional Review Board of Korea University (KUIRB-2024-0267-01), and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Surface EMG (Trigno Wireless System, Delsys Inc., USA) was used to record signals from the leg during a 10-meter straight walk at a comfortable pace (Fig. 9). The following muscles were monitored: the rectus femoris (RFM) and vastus medialis (VMM) as knee extensors, the semitendinosus (SEM) and biceps femoris long head (BFM) as knee flexors, the adductor magnus (ADD) as a hip adductor, the gluteus medius (GLU) as a hip abductor, the tibialis anterior (TIB) as an ankle dorsiflexor, and the medial gastrocnemius (GCM) as an ankle plantar flexor. EMG electrodes were placed according to SENIAM guidelines, and the signals were sampled at 2000 Hz.

Proposed framework

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the proposed framework collects raw EMG data, preprocesses it, and extracts muscle synergies. Subsequently, datasets comprising spatial and temporal features are independently trained using four tree-based models: random forest (RF)44, XGBoost (XGB)45, LightGBM (LGBM)46, and CatBoost (CATB)47, which have demonstrated strong performance in prior EMG studies. Given that the present study proposes an early-stage ensemble framework grounded in muscle synergy features, tree-based models were deliberately employed due to their consistently superior performance on typical tabular data and their relatively low burden of hyperparameter tuning48. The trained models generate predictions through a soft voting classification approach, which are then used as inputs for logistic regression to produce the final output, followed by an evaluation process. We selected logistic regression as the meta-classifier primarily to enable interpretable assessment of the relative importance of spatial versus temporal features in the ensemble output. The regression coefficients offer direct insight into which feature domain contributes more significantly to the classification between stroke and healthy gait, aligning with the study’s clinical goal of understanding motor impairment. Upon completion of model training, logistic regression coefficients, weighted feature importance, and SHAP values derived from tree-based models are computed and utilized to support individualized therapeutic decision-making.

Data preprocessing

The EMG signal preprocessing pipeline involves several stages to enhance signal quality and normalize features for subsequent analysis. Initially, a bandpass filter (4th-order, 20–450 Hz) isolates relevant frequency components, followed by rectification and a low-pass filter (4th-order, 6 Hz cutoff) to extract the envelope of muscle activation. The filtered signals are then interpolated to 100 time points per trial using cubic interpolation, ensuring consistent temporal resolution across samples. To further smooth the signals, a Savitzky-Golay filter (window length = 21, polynomial order = 3) is applied. Since filtering can introduce negative values, channel-wise minimum values are identified and offset to ensure non-negativity. Finally, each EMG channel was independently max-normalized across time within each trial to account for differences in baseline amplitude, electrode sensitivity, and muscle-specific activation ranges caused by anatomical and sensor placement variability.

Muscle synergy extraction

Muscle synergies were extracted using NMF49, with the initialization method set to ’random’ and the optimization algorithm based on multiplicative updates, from preprocessed EMG data comprising 380 gait cycles. In healthy individuals, the reconstruction of the original EMG signals using the extracted synergies yielded a VAF exceeding 90%50, confirming the adequacy of the decomposition. To ensure consistency between the datasets of healthy individuals and patients with stroke, the number of synergies for patients was fixed to match that of the healthy group.

Since the order of extracted synergies may vary across individuals, we implemented a two-step alignment procedure based on the Hungarian algorithm51,52 (see Algorithm 1). First, we identified an optimal reference synergy matrix from the healthy group by selecting the individual whose synergy set exhibited the highest average cosine similarity to those of other healthy individuals. This ensured that the reference pattern reflected a typical, unimpaired synergy structure. Then, using this reference, we computed pairwise cosine similarities between each stroke patient’s synergy set and the reference, constructed cost matrices (as negative similarities), and applied the Hungarian algorithm to determine an optimal one-to-one alignment. This approach enabled consistent and interpretable comparison of synergy structures between stroke patients and neurologically healthy controls.

Particularly, to clearly identify the pathological manifestations of spatial and temporal features in stroke patients compared with healthy controls, we employed a bidirectional NMF strategy comprising two complementary decomposition approaches to separately extract spatial and temporal features.

Model construction and optimization

In this study, we developed a robust classification pipeline to distinguish between stroke and normal conditions using spatial and temporal features of muscle synergy. First, The dataset was split into training and testing sets in an 8:2 ratio using stratified sampling to preserve the original class distribution. Specifically, 80% of the data was allocated for training and hyperparameter optimization, whereas the remaining 20% was reserved for model evaluation. The spatial and temporal features were treated as separate inputs to capture the distinct aspects of muscle synergy.

Specifically, as summarized in Table 5, the training dataset for the spatial features consisted of the weight values for eight muscles across synergies, with an input shape of (number of samples, 32). These spatial features were obtained by applying NMF to the EMG data, from which the muscle weighting matrix was derived. For each sample, the resulting muscle weighting vectors were flattened and concatenated to form the final feature vector.

For temporal features, the dataset was composed of three characteristics of the activation profiles (matrix ) of the synergies: 1) the number of activation peaks exceeding 20% of the maximum amplitude, 2) the timing of the peak expressed as a percentage of the gait cycle, and 3) the amplitude at peak activation time. These were computed separately for each of the four synergies, resulting in 12 temporal features in total, with an input shape of (number of samples, 12). Each feature set was used independently to train the four tree-based ML models. Aggregated via Soft Voting, the predicted probabilities serve as inputs for the final logistic regression model for an integrated decision. The logistic regression model yielded coefficients reflecting each feature set’s contribution to the final decision. To ensure optimal performance, hyperparameters for all models were fine-tuned using a grid search. Hyperparameters for all tree-based models were optimized using grid search with 5-fold stratified CV. For the RF classifier, the search space included n_estimators (100–300), max_depth (10–30), min_samples_split (2–10), min_samples_leaf (1–4), and max_features (sqrt or log2). XGB and LGBM were tuned over n_estimators (100–200), max_depth (3–6), and learning_rate (0.01–0.1), with LGBM additionally exploring num_leaves (31–50). CATB was tuned over iterations (100–200), depth (3–6), learning_rate (0.01–0.1), and l2_leaf_reg (1–3). To address class imbalance between stroke and healthy groups, class weighting strategies were incorporated into the model training process. Specifically, class weights were either set to balanced or included as a tunable hyperparameter during grid search for each tree-based classifier. The full list of tuned hyperparameters for each tree-based classifier is presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Model evaluation

To ensure a comprehensive evaluation, model performance was assessed using multiple complementary metrics. Accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly classified samples, served as a general indicator of model performance. To further assess the model’s ability to distinguish between classes, we calculated AUC. As recommended by recent guidelines53, AUC represents the probability that a randomly selected positive sample receives a higher prediction score than a randomly selected negative one. In this study, we designated the healthy control group as the positive class, reflecting both the presence of moderate class imbalance and the clinical aim of identifying preserved neuromuscular control. A higher AUC therefore indicates better discrimination between healthy-like and pathological synergy patterns.

We additionally report precision and recall, which offer more nuanced insight into model performance. Precision is defined as the proportion of true positives among all positive predictions, whereas recall reflects the proportion of actual positives that were correctly identified. Their harmonic mean, the F1-score, provides a balanced performance measure, particularly useful under class-imbalanced conditions. In this study, the F1-score was used as a primary criterion for selecting the best-performing models.

Calculation of feature importance & SHAP values

After completing model training, the feature importance was extracted from each of the four tree-based models. To account for differences in feature importance scales and computation methods across models, the feature importances from each model were rescaled using a min–max normalization technique prior to weighted averaging. Subsequently, weights were assigned to each model’s feature importance based on the performance. Specifically, the average value of all the performance metrics used in this study (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC) was calculated for each model, and the proportion of each model’s average performance was used to determine its weight. Each weighted feature importance was then summed to compute the final feature importance.

Additionally, SHAP values were employed to interpret the model’s output on the test dataset. Given that SHAP is particularly effective in explaining tree-based models, TreeSHAP, an efficient algorithm54 specifically optimized for tree-ensemble methods, was used to compute the feature attributes in this study. SHAP values were first obtained from each individual model and then aggregated using the same performance-based weighting scheme as described above, by multiplying each model’s SHAP values by its respective performance-derived weight and summing the results. This approach enables a robust and computationally efficient estimation of feature-level contributions on a per-sample basis, offering interpretable insights into which muscle synergy components should be prioritized for each patient.
