Development, implementation and evaluation of anxiety management program for women under gynecological surgery with spinal anesthesia: a participatory action research based on the PARIHS framework
Authors: Mahnaz Modanloo, Solmaz Halakou, Homeira Khoddam, Nasrin Nikpeyma, Kobra Rajablou, Shahriar Gholizadeh, Atefeh Sadeghloo, Mohsen Mirsadeghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnxiety reduction is an objective that should be part of each patient’s care plan. The aim of this study was to develop, implement, and evaluate an anxiety management program for women under gynecological surgery with spinal anesthesia based on the participatory action research using PARIHS framework.MethodsThis participatory action research was conducted from 2020 to 2023 in a referral gynecological surgery center in northeastern Iran and followed four phases: (1) problem identification using interviews, observations, literature review, and Numeric Visual Analog Anxiety Scale (NVASA); (2) planning through 16 expert panel and stakeholder sessions to design feasible evidence-based actions; (3) action, implemented in three cycles targeting healthcare providers, the environment, and patients; and (4) evaluation, was conducted by using NVASA measurements across cycles and assessments of communication skills and stress among healthcare providers. Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis for qualitative findings and paired statistical tests for quantitative outcomes.ResultsData analysis from interviews and observations in the first phase of the research process, led to the emergence of 20 subcategories and 6 main categories. A comprehensive literature review further delineated three intervention categories: healthcare provider, patient, and environmental interventions, along with 13 subcategories. In the main evaluation of the effectiveness of the cycles of action phase, mean anxiety score of patients before the interventions were 6.1 ± 1.9, and after the interventions of the health care providers, environment, and patient was 4.66 ± 2.17, 2.86 ± 1.79, and 2.64 ± 1.2, respectively, which had a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001).ConclusionsAnxiety management program based on action research steps has reduced anxiety in patients of study, the application of the anxiety management program with the participation of patients and healthcare providers in the context with a combination of extracted evidence led to the change in the attitude of the stakeholders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04405-9.Keywords: Anxiety, Surgery, Anesthesia, Spinal, Action Research

Background

Preoperative anxiety is a common psychological–physiological condition [1], that experienced by surgical candidates and is characterized by fear, tension, and autonomic arousal. This is a common experience in patients before surgery [2]. It has been reported that 30% of hospitalized patients are stressed even when they do not need surgery, and this value is 60–80% higher in patients who require surgery [3]. Women undergoing gynecological procedures are particularly vulnerable due to concerns related to reproductive function, femininity, and potential loss of body integrity [4]. This is particularly true in the field of gynecology and gynecological surgery, which often involves the partial or total removal of an organ necessarily associated with childbirth or femininity [5].

Multiple factors contribute to preoperative anxiety and its complications in patients undergoing surgery [6]. Studies have reported anxiety-causing factors such as lack of knowledge [7], waiting more than 2 h for surgery [8], surgical lights, warning noises, and surgical instruments [3]. Fear of regional anesthesia and its associated complications, as an unknown procedure to patients, is one of the most important sources of surgery-related anxiety [9]. In this regard, despite the fact that anesthesia and surgical techniques have significantly improved and reached a safer state, patient anxiety before surgery is still a dominant problem [10], all necessary measures should be considered to manage patient anxiety [11].

Anxiety reduction is a goal that should be part of each patient’s treatment plan. The healthcare providers must be prepared to respond to the challenges of anesthesia and surgery [12]. that is considered part of preoperative nursing care [13]. Procedures performed by nurses and other medical staff should be based on responding to the needs, problems, and concerns of patients [14, 15].

Both pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies have been employed to reduce preoperative anxiety [16]. Medicines can reduce the anxiety level of patients. However, alternative interventions without the side effects of drugs, such as cardiovascular and respiratory suppression and lowering of central body temperature, have been introduced [14, 15]. In recent years, emphasis has been placed on the use of non-pharmacological methods to reduce or eliminate anxiety. The provision of non-pharmacological interventions by nurses, due to the noninvasiveness and low cost of these methods, can lead to anxiety reduction, cessation or reduction of medication use, and greater safety than pharmacological methods [13].

Moreover, while numerous clinical trials demonstrate effectiveness of specific interventions [13, 17, 18]. Many lack consideration of contextual factors, including staff readiness, environmental characteristics, and patient preferences—that affect successful adoption and sustainability in real-world settings. Such limitations highlight the need for methodological approaches that incorporate local context, stakeholder engagement, and multiple sources of evidence.

Participatory action research is a practical method that uses the participation of patients, clinical staff, and caregivers to identify problems and potential solutions [14]. This method allows the improvement of processes in nursing practice and, ultimately, the improvement of care outcomes for the patient and the healthcare providers [15]. In clinical trials, despite their implementation strengths, they often do not fully reflect the situation in which they are implemented because they do not use the intuition and experience of clinical individuals in the environment [19]. Thus, there is a need to use evidence-based methods in this regard. Accordingly, the Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (PARIHS) framework was created to highlight the complex interplay of a number of factors that influence the successful implementation of evidence. The Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (PARIHS) framework provides a comprehensive structure for implementing evidence into practice by integrating three core elements: evidence, context, and facilitation. Evidence in this framework encompasses not only research findings but also patient experiences, clinical expertise, and contextual knowledge. Unlike traditional implementation models that emphasize research evidence alone, PARIHS stresses the dynamic interaction between stakeholders and setting. Facilitation purposeful process of enabling and supporting changes central to the PARIHS approach and distinguishes it from other knowledge-translation models [20]. Therefore, it can be very useful as a systematic written method to solve the problem of patient anxiety. The PARIHS framework is an important step in this direction. The aim of this study was to develop, implement, and evaluate an anxiety management program for women undergoing gynecologic surgery with spinal anesthesia based on participatory action research in the context of applying knowledge in health services.

Methods

Design

This study was conducted as a Participatory Action Research (PAR) guided by the PARIHS (Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services) framework. In this study, the participatory action research involved four main phases of problem identification, planning, action and evaluation. The PARIHS framework provided the conceptual structure by integrating evidence, context, and facilitation throughout the study process [21] (Fig. 1).

Implementation of the steps of the action research using the PARIHS framework

Participants and setting

The research setting was the operating room of the referral hospital in the northeast of Iran in Golestan province, which was a gynecological surgery center in the east of the province and had three active rooms. This operating room performs an average of 100–150 elective gynecological surgeries per month.

The research population consisted of the patients who were candidates for elective gynecological surgery and the healthcare providers (BSc anesthesia as nurse anesthetist, surgical technologist, surgical nurses, and anesthesiologists, the head of the hospital, the head of the nursing department, and the head nurse of the operating room department) of this hospital.

Inclusion criteria for the patients were women candidates for elective gynecological surgery and spinal anesthesia, no history of substance abuse and illicit drugs, no known psychiatric illness, and willingness to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria were critical changes in the patient’s hemodynamic conditions before and during the surgery.

Inclusion criteria for the healthcare providers included willingness to participate in the research and willingness to continue participating in the research.

Procedure

The study was conducted in four following phases: problem identification, planning, action, and evaluation. For clarity, the aim, methodological approach, and output of each phase are explicitly described below to demonstrate how each stage informed the next.

Phase 1: Problem identification

This phase aimed to understand the PARIHS framework evidence includes research, patient experience, clinical experience, and information extracted from the research environment to gather evidence that would inform action development. Therefore, data collection was performed in the form of primary evaluation of the context, interviews, observations, and literature review.

Phase 1: Context evaluation

Prior to the start of the study, semi-structured interviews were conducted with the head of the nursing department and other healthcare providers to ensure the existence of the problem and the willingness of the health care providers to participate in the study.

The researcher’s role in examining the research context

The researcher had a history of clinical activity in the research setting and was fully familiar with the setting and the healthcare providers. According to the points mentioned, this setting was considered as a suitable context for implementing changes based on the study objectives (the researcher was an outsider to the study during the implementation of the project in the context).

Based on the PARIHS framework, the first investigation at this phase is to know and understand the context of the research implementation. In the PARIHS framework, context includes culture, leadership, assessment, resources, and infrastructure. In general, the acceptance of context is for the implementation of change.

Phase 1: Interview

For data collection, semi-structured individual interviews were conducted with 16 healthcare providers and 7 gynecological surgery candidates at a prearranged location after obtaining consent from the participants. The purpose of this section is to explore the experiences of healthcare providers in caring for anxious patients around surgery for patients undergoing spinal anesthesia [22]. Two pilot interviews (one patient, one provider) were conducted to assess flow and comprehensibility. Findings resulted in minor wording adjustments.

Prior to the official start of the interview sessions, the purpose and method of the study were explained to the participants and their written informed consent to conduct the research and record the interviews was obtained. They were also given the necessary explanations about the voluntary nature of the study, the confidentiality of the information obtained, and the possibility of withdrawing from the study at any stage. The participants were asked to determine the time and place of the interview according to the conditions and facilities of the hospital in the inpatient setting. The duration of the interview ranged from 25 to 65 min.

Phase 1: Observation

The purpose of the observations was to explain the effect of context on the level of anxiety in patients who were candidates for gynecological surgery under spinal anesthesia. Observations were recorded in the form of partial participation. The 9 steps proposed by Spradley [23] were the basis of the researcher’s observations.

Phase 1: Questionnaire

In the problem identification phase, the Numeric Visual Analog Anxiety Scale (NVASA) was also used to assess patients’ preoperative anxiety by a self-report method [23].This scale was used because it has the capacity to be easily and quickly administered to patients before surgery and it measures anxiety at the moment of assessment.

Phase 1: Literature review

In this study, in addition to extracting problem identification based on clinical experience, in order to extract evidence-based interventions based on research, search and screening of related articles was performed with the aim of reviewing non-pharmacological studies on anxiety management in patients who are candidates for surgery under spinal anesthesia [25].

In order to obtain evidence-based intervention solutions, English keywords such as anxiety, spinal anesthesia, and surgery, as well as their synonyms, were searched in electronic resources and international databases, including Since direct, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Proquest, and Google Scholar search engine, for studies conducted from 2000 to 2021. The results of the retrieved records were stored in EndNote version 18 software. After removing duplicates, articles were screened for the purpose of the search and 31 articles out of 10,450 retrieved records entered the analysis and data extraction stage.

The findings of this phase provided the foundational data for designing the intervention in the subsequent phase.

In this phase, 2 to 6 healthcare providers and the members of the research team participated in each session. The PARIHS framework was used to present evidence-based actions extracted from the studies in the sessions with stakeholders [21].

Phase 3: Action

Actions were implemented in three action cycles [21] (Fig. 2).

Cycles of action research phases

Facilitation has played main role as the main component of the PARIHS framework in the implementation of the cycles of study actions. In line with the implementation of evidence-based actions in the process of facilitation, three key, local and expert facilitators were used in the study. The role of the facilitator is to work with relevant stakeholders to identify or review existing evidence and find solutions to support the implementation of that evidence.

In this study, head of the hospital was the key facilitator for the implementation of the main support actions, including holding meetings related to the study objectives, funding, field visits, and follow-up. In addition, the hospital matron, as a key facilitator in the planning and implementation of actions, had the necessary support to coordinate and follow up on the actions in all three main categories with officials from different departments.

In this study, the operating room supervisor was the local facilitator responsible for planning, follow-up, and implementing the actions.

In this study, members of the research team provided evidence-based information based on the study objectives for the design and conduct of the study during several sessions with relevant officials. In addition, as part of the development of educational workshops (actions based on healthcare providers) were conducted by members of the research team. Therefore, one of the main roles of the researcher in the study of facilitation has been as an expert facilitator.

First cycle of Phase 3: Based on the healthcare providers

Two educational workshops were held on effective interpersonal communication methods, stress management, and relaxation techniques. Lecture, question and answer, role-playing, and group discussion were used as pedagogical methods in the educational workshops.

Training content development prepared by the research team based on validated educational resources and PARIHS facilitation principles. Content validity was confirmed by three faculty experts in nursing, communication, and psychological health.

Workshop Duration & Format: Each workshop: 4 h, held in two sessions. Methods included lecture, group discussion, demonstration, and role-play.

Second cycle of Phase 3: Based on the environment

Changes in the environment of the operating room department of setting included changing the color of the walls of the entrance corridor and the patient waiting area of the operating room to light blue, changing the curtains of the operating room, removing the extra materials installed on the walls of the entrance corridor and the patient waiting area, installing nature and baby posters in the entrance corridor and the patient waiting area, and using natural flowers in the corridor of the staff exit area.

Third cycle of Phase 3: Based on the patient

Installing a television in the waiting area and broadcasting selected images to patients prior to surgery: The average time patients wait for surgery in the waiting room in the research setting is approximately 15 minutes. Two 8-minute videos with images of nature and babies and relaxing music, along with the sounds of nature and the gentle flow of water, were prepared and approved by the research team. During this time, these images were played at the same volume level (level 10) for the patients.

Use of special surgical gowns for patients from the time they enter the operating room until they enter the surgery room: four special operating room gowns were prepared for the patients, and with the order the operating room supervisor, they were used four all elective patients from the time of their admission.

Use of gowns for patients from the time they enter the operating room: To protect the patient's privacy, four operating room gowns in pink and green colors were prepared for the patients. Before entering the entrance area of the operating room, special coats were used for all patients.

Insights gained during implementation phase informed the evaluation strategy applied in the final phase.

Phase 4: Evaluation

In the present study, evaluation was performed in two dimensions of implementing the actions and the effectiveness of each proposed action in the anxiety management program and its overall effect on the patients’ anxiety [21].

The implementation of actions was discussed and evaluated during 3 expert panel sessions and 13 other sessions (with 2–6 subject groups) held during the planning phase. Based on the suggestions made during these sessions, a decision was made to approve and continue the program or to modify the selected action, changing their content and providing new actions to reduce patient anxiety.

The overall effect of anxiety management actions on patients’ anxiety levels was determined using the NVASA: To determine the effectiveness of the actions, a total of 120 patients were selected by convenience sampling and evaluated after implementation of each group of actions. In fact, considering the total number of surgeries per month (an average of 150 surgeries), 20% of the total number of patients were included in the study at each stage. Thus, 30 patients participated before the implementation of the intervention to collect basic information, 30 patients participated after the implementation of the healthcare providers-based actions, 30 patients participated after the implementation of the environment-based actions, and 30 patients participated after the implementation of the patient-based actions.

In order to assess the impact of healthcare providers’ actions on their knowledge and attitudes (considering the participatory nature of the action research method and the inclusion of their perspective in the process of study implementation), two standard patient communication questionnaires by Vakili et al. [24] and BarazPordanjani Pardanjani’s barriers to nurse-patient communication questionnaire [26] were used before and two weeks after the implementation of the interpersonal communication training workshop. Moreover, anesthesiologists’ perception of patients’ anxiety under a regional anesthesia questionnaire developed by Jlala [27] and the Gray-Toft P Anderson the Nursing Stress Scale were used before and two weeks after the stress management and relaxation techniques workshop. Rezaei et al. had translated the questionnaire in their study and the content validity of the questionnaire was obtained through library studies and feedback from nursing and midwifery professors and trainers of School of Midwifery Nursing, Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, Ahvaz. To ensure reliability, the retest method was used and the reliability coefficient was 74% [28].

Final evaluation of the study implementation

At the end of the interventions, a final session was held with the presence of the OR staff in the location of the OR staff lounge to identify strengths, weaknesses, facilitators, barriers, suggestions and future solutions from the perspective of the healthcare providers. The number of participating members was 14 individuals, including an operating room supervisor, a student, five anesthetists, five operating room specialists, and two operating room nurses.

Data analysis method

In order to analyze the data extracted from the interviews, observations, and literature review in the problem identification phase, the conventional content analysis method was used by applying Granheim and Lundman’s approach. Content analysis is a method of analyzing written, verbal and visual messages. This method includes three stages of preparation, planning, and reporting [22, 29].

Integration across phases

Integration occurred at multiple points in the research process. the four phases were dynamically interconnected. Findings from each phase informed subsequent decision-making, consistent with action research methodology.

The results of the comparison showed that before the intervention, 20% of the participants referred to patient anxiety before anesthesia as a (high) concern; but this rate changed to 60% after the intervention. Before the intervention, seven participants (46.7%) stated that they were (often) adequately prepared for the different behaviors of anxious patients, while this value increased to 12 participants (80%) after the intervention (Table 4). There was a significant difference in the accuracy of the patient’s anxiety assessment item before the start of the study and after the intervention. Thus, after the intervention, 70% of participants reported that they correctly assessed patient anxiety most of the time (P = 0.021).

Discussion

This study aimed to develop, implement, and evaluate an anxiety management program for women undergoing gynecological surgery with spinal anesthesia using a participatory action research (PAR) approach guided by the PARIHS framework. The findings show that a structured, context-based, and participatory intervention can effectively reduce preoperative anxiety. Preoperative anxiety has been widely recognized as a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by psychological, interpersonal, and environmental factors [1]. In this study, the data obtained from Phase 1 that most of the gynecological surgery candidates were anxious before the surgery that manifests with physiological and emotional symptoms and can affect the emotions and even the performance of the healthcare providers. Several studies [6, 16, 48] have reported that physiological problems occur in the body as a result of anxiety. In Phase 1, patients reported insufficient information, ineffective communication with healthcare providers, fear of spinal anesthesia, and the unfamiliar operating room environment as major sources of anxiety. Similar factors have been identified in previous study involving gynecological and surgical patients [4].

Considering the prevalence of anxiety in surgical candidates and the subsequent complications, it is necessary to first carefully examine the problem and then implement the necessary measures to manage it. Raineri et al. also emphasized in their study the need for a multifaceted and comprehensive approach to preoperative anxiety management [62].

In this study anxiety levels decreased gradually across the three action cycles. This pattern indicates that anxiety management is more effective when interventions are applied sequentially and in combination. After the first action cycle, which focused on improving healthcare providers’ communication and emotional support, anxiety decreased significantly. This finding is consistent with evidence showing that empathetic communication and reassurance enhance patient trust and reduce preoperative distress [26, 27].

Vogelsang et al. also pointed out that when a patient is admitted to the operating room, the nurse is usually the first person to meet the patient, so it is necessary to greet the patient and create a calm and friendly atmosphere [63].

Further anxiety reduction occurred after the second action cycle, which addressed environmental conditions in the surgical setting. The present findings support the importance of environmental comfort as a key non-pharmacological strategy for anxiety reduction. Because of patient’s condition, including the patient’s loss of self-control during surgery, the operating room setting can create a stressful, frightening, and unreal experience for patients [64]. By creating changes in the internal setting of the operating room and creating a sense of relaxation in the patients, a mental deviation is created in the surgical candidates towards the formation of anxiety. Jamshidi et al. conducted a review of 15 articles to determine the evidence for the impact of the environment on patient health outcomes in the hospital. According to their study, the environmental factors that influenced patient outcomes were grouped into several categories: building format and layout, building floor materials, room characteristics, visibility of medical equipment, type of lighting, and music [65].

In our study, painting the walls of the corridor and patient waiting area and installing nature and baby posters had a significant effect on reducing patient anxiety. In addition, natural plants were used in the entrance corridor in accordance with the environment-based actions. It has been reported that decorating a patient’s room with natural plants has a significant positive effect on physiological responses. It can lead to lower pain, anxiety, and fatigue scores, as well as positive feelings and greater satisfaction [66]. Creating a positive distraction can create a relaxing environment and exposure to color slides with views of nature attracts people’s attention and interest. Dąbrowska et al. reported the role of nature, visual art, sound, and light as positive mental distraction in patients’ experiences in healthcare settings and related psychophysiological outcomes [67].

The greatest reduction in anxiety was observed after the third action cycle, which emphasized patient-centered preparation. Providing individualized education and emotional support helped patients better understand the surgical and anesthetic process. Consistent with earlier studies, structured preoperative education reduces uncertainty, improves coping, and lowers anxiety levels [32–34].

Playing soothing video clips in the waiting area of the operating room has provided mental distraction and reduced anxiety for patients undergoing gynecological surgery. This study used images of nature and babies with soothing nature music. In addition to the positive effect on the patients, the OR team also mentioned that listening to the sounds of nature played a role in improving their mood. Kukreja et al. examined the effect of music types such as jazz, classical, rock, etc. on patient anxiety. The volume level of the music was selected by the patients who listened to it through headphones during surgery, and it was concluded that the anxiety level of the patients in the intervention group was significantly lower [41].

The results of our study showed that the use of gowns for patients undergoing gynecological surgery to maintain coverage, along with other patient-based interventions, had a positive impact on patient anxiety. Ayesha et al. stated in their study that adherence to ethical principles, including respecting patients and preserving their privacy to others, should be observed as it may cause anxiety and discomfort to patients, especially candidates for anesthesia [68]. In the operating room, privacy is more sensitive due to the special conditions of the patients. The special atmosphere of the operating room, including the need for speed and high precision, the rapid entry and exit of patients, the unpredictability of procedures in many cases, and the specific culture that governs the environment, may at the same time affect the moral atmosphere and privacy of patients in the operating room [69].

A major strength of this study is the use of the PARIHS framework. This framework highlights the interaction between evidence, context, and facilitation [20]. Active participation of patients, healthcare providers, and managers ensured that the intervention was practical and aligned with the clinical setting. Previous studies confirm that participatory approaches improve acceptability and sustainability of healthcare interventions [57, 58].

Qualitative findings further supported the quantitative results. Healthcare providers reported improved confidence, better communication, and greater readiness to manage anxious patients. Similar outcomes have been reported in other stut focusing on perioperative and regional anesthesia care [64].

In general, in this study, the application of the anxiety management program with the participation of patients and the healthcare providers in identifying the problem, planning and implementing solutions with the ability to be implemented in the context with a combination of extracted evidence led to change in the attitude in the context and the sustainability of the changes made based on the objectives of the study.

The strength of this study lies in the iterative integration of its four phases. Rather than functioning as isolated components, each phase informed the next, creating a coherent pathway from contextual exploration to intervention development, implementation, and outcome evaluation. This sequential integration enhances the practical relevance and translational value of the findings.

Study limitations

In this study, given the lack of financial resources, the implementation of some measures approved during the problem identification and planning phase, such as changing the curtain at the entrance of the operating room and replacing the door at the entrance of the operating room, was postponed to the future.

Because of the fifth peak of the Covid-19 disease in the research environment and the cancellation of elective surgeries in the hospital, it was impossible to continue the study in the research setting for three months. In fact, because of the workload of the operating room staff and other hospital officials in the research environment, we tried to collect data during hours and days that corresponded to their work activities.

The lack of a control group limits the ability to attribute anxiety reductions solely to the intervention, and results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future research employing randomized or quasi-experimental designs is recommended.

Additionally, some planned actions, such as establishing a separate relaxation room or upgrading multimedia systems could not be implemented due to financial constraints, staff shortages, and time limitations. These contextual barriers are typical in real-world healthcare environments but may limit generalizability.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the development and operationalization of participatory, context-based interventions to manage preoperative anxiety.

Conclusions

One of the goals of care for patients who are candidates for surgery is to prevent and reduce anxiety-provoking conditions. If the needs associated with the problem are not met, it can lead to the formation of subsequent problems. Based on the results of this study, the development, implementation, and evaluation of a comprehensive anxiety management program based on action research steps has reduced anxiety in patients who are candidates for gynecologic surgery with spinal anesthesia. In this study, the application of the anxiety management program with the participation of patients and the healthcare providers in the context with a combination of extracted evidence led to the change in the attitude of the stakeholders and the sustainability of the changes made based on the objectives of the study. Therefore, it is recommended to conduct this type of studies in other clinical departments of the hospital in order to identify the problem based on the context, design, and implementation of the program and based on the real needs of the patients with changes in the health area by involving people and paying attention to the characteristics of the context and relative durability in the environment.
