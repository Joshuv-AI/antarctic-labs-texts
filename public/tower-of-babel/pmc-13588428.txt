Results in a ‘Flash’: Recognizing Misleading Clinical Photography
Authors: Ananya Sharma, Krishna D. Barman, Abir Saraswat
Published: 2026

Photography is an indispensable part of dermatology practice. With the advent of digital marketing and social media, clinical photographs have evolved beyond mere data storage tools into digital information that is actively consumed by both physicians and patients. It is used prominently as a marketing tool to demonstrate treatment results and to attract patients. A 2017 survey of 100 patients showed that patients expressed maximum interest in viewing before-and-after photos on dermatological practice websites.[1] A study assessing the postoperative photographic bias in before-and-after images on Instagram showed that photographic conditions favored the postoperative results in 97% of top posts, and such “biased” accounts were in fact associated with a greater number of followers![2] These results reiterate the expected, that demonstration of ideal, almost “magical” results leads to more patient inflow, as well as public appreciation. In esthetics, this issue is compounded by the beauty industry that pushes air-brushed and edited images of celebrities as an unachievable goal. This has given rise to a pandemic of unrealistic expectations, a self-perpetuating cycle where the patient or consumer is only attracted to images showing drastic transformations, forcing practitioners to demonstrate such results, only for the patient to end up doctor-hopping and chronically dissatisfied. A similar trend is being seen in academic circles, where even scientific journals are unable to ensure consistent and truly representative clinical photography.

Interpreting bias in clinical photography is a skill in itself, and there is little education for either patients or physicians regarding the interpretation of potentially fraudulent “before- and-after” images.

Though consistent positioning and lighting are well talked about, we wish to highlight a few soft points that may drastically alter the perceived results in a matter of seconds, even without blatant photo editing. These can be remembered, observed, and picked up by patients and physicians alike.

Firstly, the resolution of the camera, how well the camera lens is focused on the subject, and the presence of auto-filters can significantly affect the quality of the image. An ever-so slightly blurred picture often gives an overall “smoother” look to the skin.

Second, lighting and the use or lack of a flash. Although the use of flash often helps in better focus of the camera lens, it can alter the depth perception of scars and skin texture in areas where the light is primarily focused [Figure 1a-d]. Meanwhile, tangential lighting can accentuate scars. In hair disorders, vellus and gray hair may be completely camouflaged by a flash; for example, Figure 2 shows a patient of lichen plano-pilaris with gray hair, appearing to show drastic “improvement” in the second image that was taken a few seconds later without flash. Pigmentary disorders, like melasma and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, are especially likely to show nonexistent changes in successive images, merely due to alterations in lighting and shadows.

Third, apart from changes induced by obvious positioning changes, e.g., from upright to recumbent, even a slight tilt of the camera or the direction of the eyeballs can change the apparent contour. A photograph of a person’s face taken front-on from the same level, versus an angle that is 15 to 30 degrees lower, creates a significant change in the perception of the jawline, lips, and chin [Figure 3]. Sommer and Mendelsohn[3] formally analyzed this and found high correlations between neck extension and head protrusion positions with increased jawline definition.

Patient (a) with jaw retracted showing apparently ill-defined jawline and (b) with a well-defined jawline due to jaw protrusion

Another example of subtle positioning is that of a patient with androgenetic alopecia with reduced hair density in the vertex area. Such a patient, when photographed from the top, will project different hair densities at different angles. The length of remnant hair can also be manipulated to modify the apparent improvement [Figure 4]. For under-eye hollowing, a photograph taken with eyes directed upwards accentuates under-eye hollowing compared to a photograph taken on the line of vision.

Fourth, the immediate postprocedure changes that may be photographed as “before” and “after” are exaggerated representations of actual results in some situations. For example, injectables, like under-eye fillers and skin boosters, are often accompanied by slight post-procedure edema, causing fullness, exaggerating the actual final effect that will emerge after a few weeks.

Fifth, a subtle change in expression can completely change image perception. A smiling or pleasant expression instantly gives the appearance of a happier patient and distracts from minor “flaws.” At the same time, a smiling expression accentuates crow’s feet and nasolabial folds.

Other common difference between the “before” and “after” pictures include the subtle use of makeup and the change of background. Even the lip color used can alter the perception of skin tone due to the change in contrast. A bright red color gives the perception of fairer skin, whereas a darker color gives an impression of a darker skin tone.[4] Hairstyles, such as ponytails, can lift the face temporarily.

A few tips for a quick assessment of the validity of “before” and “after” photos include starting the comparison away from the main area of focus. The background and position of the patient should be matched first, followed by observing the direction of light and natural shadows. This is quite evident in Figure 2, where the background of one image is much darker than the other. The tilt of the face can be compared by observing the tip of the nose and/or nostrils to detect slight changes in angle. Ideally, an authentic result should be replicated in multiple viewpoints. A crude guideline for such an assessment can be remembered by the acronym “LAMPS” (Lighting, angles, make-up, position, and shadowing). Other formally proposed scales include SEPIA (Standards for the Evaluation of Photographs in Aesthetics), which uses a nine-point grading system including positioning, facial expression, lighting, background, zoom, makeup and styling, multiple views, timing, and descriptive information.[5]

It is also important to remember that published before-and-after photographs are deliberately selected to show the most striking results and reflect a skewed section of a larger sample, both on social media and in scientific journals. Complications and inadequate results are much less demonstrated.

It would be prudent for both the public and physicians to remember that most common conditions encountered in clinical dermatology are either chronic or relapsing–remitting, while esthetic reality is best enunciated by the statement, “once a scar, always a scar.” Although the NMC (National Medical Commission) RMP (Registered Medical Practitioner) Conduct Regulations, 2023 clearly prohibit RMPs from posting patient photographs or scans on social media, in practice such images continue to be shared by various businesses, individuals who do not fall under these regulations, and at times by RMPs themselves. This article therefore aims to foster a critical and analytical perspective among both physicians and patients to identify misleading clinical photography
