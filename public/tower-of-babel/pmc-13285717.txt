Nanoparticle-mediated inhibition of Yes-associated protein prevents corneal scarring after traumatic injury
Authors: Nae-Won Kang, Kyeongwoo Jang, Hyeonji Kim, Li Jiang, Youngyoon Amy Seo, Euisun Song, Julian Garcia-Sanchez, Danting Lin, Fang Chen, Uiyoung Han, Wendy W. Liu, David Myung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corneal scars develop during the regenerative process following traumatic injuries. Verteporfin (VP), a Yes-associated protein (YAP) inhibitor, has shown promise as an antifibrotic agent, but its application to the ocular surface is limited by unfavorable physicochemical properties. Here, we developed the hyaluronic acid-verteporfin nanoparticles (HA-VP NPs) to increase the apparent solubility of VP and prolong residence on the eye, aiming to promote corneal regeneration and prevent scar formation following injuries. The HA-VP NPs effectively prevented not only the transformation of corneal stromal cells into myofibroblasts, but also the epithelial-mesenchymal transition of epithelial cells by inhibiting the translocation of YAP into the nucleus. HA-VP NPs interacted with CD44 receptors, facilitating endocytosis in corneal stromal cells and contributing to prolonged residence time in corneal defects. In a rat anterior lamellar keratoplasty model, the HA-VP NPs were retained on corneal defects for at least 4 h, resulting in excellent corneal regeneration and significant reduction in scarring.

Introduction

Corneal scars can develop during corneal regeneration following traumatic injuries, often leading to irreversible moderate-to-severe vision impairment [1,2]. When the cornea is injured, quiescent corneal stromal keratocytes differentiate into myofibroblasts by interleukin-1α (IL-1α) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) released from damaged epithelium [3,4]. This differentiation is triggered by transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1), which is secreted by injured and immune cells [1,5,6]. This cytokine induces the translocation of Yes-associated protein (YAP), a transcription coactivator, into the nucleus [7], where it activates the expression of downstream genes associated with myofibroblast formation [8,9]. The resulting myofibroblasts secrete abnormal extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, causing irregular stromal remodeling and corneal haze. In addition, YAP promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), a process in which epithelial cells progressively acquire mesenchymal-like properties while losing their epithelial characteristics and function [10]. EMT is also implicated in scarring and fibrotic progression [11]. Therefore, in addition to stromal cell transformation, epithelial cell transition may also contribute to the development of corneal fibrosis.

Currently, there are no Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved drugs available to prevent or treat corneal scars. However, various chemical agents, such as losartan [12], mitomycin C, steroids [13], and rosiglitazone [14], have demonstrated anti-scarring effects on the cornea. More recently, verteporfin (VP) has emerged as a potential antifibrotic agent. By blocking Engrailed-1 activation, VP has shown efficacy in treating fibrosis across various diseases, including pulmonary fibrosis, systemic sclerosis, hepatic fibrosis, ocular fibrosis, and renal fibrosis [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]].

The topical application of VP for treating corneal defects and preventing scarring presents several challenges. A major limitation is VP's poor water solubility, making it unsuitable for use as a simple aqueous solution (Fig. S1) [23]. To overcome this, VP is encapsulated in liposomes as seen in the commercial product Visudyne®, which was developed to treat age-related macular degeneration with photodynamic therapy [24]. Similarly, for ocular surface applications, VP must be formulated to enhance its apparent solubility. Additionally, the low bioavailability of topical eye drops (∼5%) due to the high clearance rate of the ocular surface is a concern [25,26]. This rapid clearance reduces therapeutic efficacy, making it crucial to develop formulations that can adhere to the corneal surface and increase residence time. Moreover, these formulations should also facilitate corneal regeneration to promote complete wound healing [27,28]. Although a previous HA/VP hydrogel system demonstrated anti-scarring efficacy, it required subconjunctival injection, which may be associated with patient discomfort and procedure-related infection risk [22]. Therefore, a non-invasive topical formulation remains needed to improve clinical practicality and patient convenience while preserving the therapeutic potential of the HA/VP combination.

To translate the antifibrotic potential of VP into clinically viable topical therapy, we engineered self-assembling hyaluronic acid-VP nanoparticles (HA-VP NPs), offering enhanced ocular residence and targeted antifibrotic effects to prevent scarring (Fig. 1A). HA was selected as a structural backbone for its excellent corneal wound healing efficacy, bioadhesiveness, and biocompatibility, making it an ideal candidate for ocular applications [29,30]. Therefore, the HA-VP NPs were designed to not only improve VP's solubility and ocular residence but also to facilitate the wound healing processes. The HA-VP NPs were administered topically to eyes with corneal defects, aiming to provide a patient-friendly treatment method. This nanoparticle formulation may offer several advantages over hydrogel-based eye drops, including more intimate contact with the exposed corneal wound bed, improved drug access to stromal cells within the defect, and active intracellular delivery of antifibrotic agents. Materials and methods

Materials

Hyaluronate amine (HA-NH2; molecular weight (MW) 250 kDa; degree of substitution (DoS) 45%) was obtained from Creative PEGWorks (Durham, NC, USA). VP, N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide sodium salt (sulfo-NHS), 2-mercaptoethanol, and hyaluronidase were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Burlington, MA, United States). Spectra/Por 3 RC dialysis membrane tubing with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 3.5 kDa was obtained from Spectrum. Alpha modification of minimum essential medium (MEM) (1X) with Earle's salts, without ribonucleosides, deoxyribonucleosides, and L-glutamine, was purchased from Corning (NY, USA). GlutaMAX supplement (100X) and MEM non-essential amino acids (NEAA) Solution (100X) were acquired from Gibco (Waltham, MA, USA). The resazurin assay solution was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Milli-Q type 1 ultrapure water was used for the preparation of all aqueous solutions. Syringe filters with a pore size of 0.2 μm were used for the filtration of all aqueous and cell media solutions.

Synthesis of the HA-VP NPs

Table S1 lists the reagents used to prepare the HA-VP conjugates at approximately 10% DoS. The molar ratio between VP and HA-NH2 was adjusted accordingly to prepare the HA-VP conjugate variant with approximately 2% DoS. All reactions were conducted under light-protected conditions. Initially, VP (5.0 mg, 7.0 μmol, 1 eq.) was dissolved in 0.5 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in a 20 mL amber glass vial and stirred for 10 min. Subsequently, 0.1 M of 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid buffer (4.5 mL; pH 5.1) was added, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 10 min. The addition of sulfo-NHS (30.2 mg, 139.1 μmol, 20 eq.) was followed, and the mixture was stirred for 10 min. EDC (13.3 mg, 69.6 μmol, 10 eq.) was subsequently added, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 2 h, after which 2-mercaptoethanol (97.6 μL, 1391.2 μmol, 200 eq.) was introduced and stirred for 15 min. The pH was adjusted to 7.1 by adding 1 M K2PO4 (1 mL; pH 9.7). The addition of HA-NH2 (26.4 mg, 34.8 μmol, 5 eq.) was followed, and the mixture was stirred overnight. The resulting reaction mixture was dialyzed against ultrapure water using dialysis membrane tubing with a 3.5 kDa MWCO for a duration of 3-5 days. Finally, the dialyzed mixture was lyophilized and stored at − 20 °C until further use. The HA-VP conjugates formed HA-VP NPs via spontaneous self-assembly after being dissolved in PBS.

Characterization of the HA-VP conjugate and HA-VP NPs

1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of VP, HA-NH2, and HA-VP conjugate were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 500 MHz spectrometer. The chemical shifts (δ) were reported relative to the residual DMSO signal, which was observed at δ = 2.50 ppm. The HA-VP conjugates were further analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy with a range of 400 – 4000 cm−1, and the absorbance spectra of the HA-VP NPs were measured from 200 to 1000 nm. The background intensities of each group were subtracted. To investigate concentration-dependent absorbance patterns, four different concentrations of the HA-VP NPs were prepared. The VP solution group was prepared by dissolving VP powder in DMSO. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

The shape of the HA-VP NPs was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). To prepare the samples for SEM analysis, a droplet of HA-VP NP suspension was deposited onto aluminum foil-covered SEM stubs. Following air-drying, the samples were sputter-coated with gold using a Leica EM ACE600 Sputter Coater. Morphological characterization was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Apreo S LoVac SEM at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Images were acquired under Optiplan mode utilizing the Trinity Detection System, and size distribution was subsequently calculated from the resulting micrographs. TEM Samples were negatively stained with uranyl acetate before imaging. The zeta potential of HA-VP NPs was measured by NanoBrook Omni particle size and zeta potential analyzer (Brookhaven Instruments; NH, USA).

Biodegradability was evaluated by incubating the HA-VP NPs with hyaluronidase (0, 5, 10, 20, and 50 unit/mL). The mixtures of NPs and hyaluronidase were placed into dialysis bags (MWCO: 3.5 kDa), which were then submerged in 0.1% Triton X-100 solution in PBS. The release medium was collected at 7, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. The collected samples were analyzed using the microplate reader.

Cell culture

Primary human corneal stromal cells (CSCs) were harvested from human donor corneas provided by Lions Eye Institute as previously described [31,32]. The harvested CSCs were positive for CD74 and CD90, and negative for CD45. The cells were cultured in MEM, alpha 1X containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, 1% NEAA solution, and 1% Glutamax. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, and the proliferated cells were passaged at 75% confluency using trypsin-EDTA solution.

Corneal epithelial cells (CECs; ATCC; CRL-11135) were cultured to test the inhibitory effects of HA-VP NPs on EMT. Cells were cultured in keratinocyte-serum free medium supplemented with 5 ng/mL epidermal growth factor, 500 ng/mL hydrocortisone, 0.05 mg/mL bovine pituitary extract, and 5 μg/mL insulin. Cells were incubated under the same conditions as CSCs.

YAP localization and alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression in CSCs

To investigate the intracellular localization of YAP when treated with the HA-VP NPs, CSCs (3 × 105 cells) were seeded on cell culture dishes (density: 1 × 103 cells/mm2) and incubated for one day. Then, the cells were incubated with fresh media containing recombinant human TGF-β1 protein (10 ng/mL; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and the HA-VP NPs (10 μg/mL, based on VP concentration) for 24 h. Three different control groups were prepared as follows: (i) liposome-encapsulated VP (Visudyne®; Bausch and Lomb, Laval, Canada), (ii) VP solution in 1% DMSO, and (iii) HA-NH2 (equivalent to the amount of HA-NH2 in the HA-VP conjugate). After treatment, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min. The fixed cells were then washed, and their membranes were permeabilized using a permeabilizing solution (0.5% Triton X-100 and 5% goat serum) for 30 min at room temperature. For YAP localization analysis, intracellular YAPs were stained with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-YAP (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, Massachusetts, US) for 2 h at room temperature. For α-SMA expression analysis, the cells were stained using Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-α-SMA (1:500; Abcam). The stained cells were washed two times and further stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US). After two additional washes, the cells were analyzed using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

EMT inhibition in CECs

To investigate the inhibitory effect of HA-VP NPs on EMT, we cultured CECs (2 × 104 cells) in each well of 8-well chamber slides (Lab-Tek Inc., MI, USA) and incubated them for one day. Then, the cells were treated with the TGF-β1 (8 ng/mL) and VP formulations (VP solution or Visudyne® or HA-VP NPs; 8 μg/mL, based on VP concentration) and incubated for one day. Control groups included a normal control (without TGF-β1 or any formulations) and a no-treatment control (represented as 0 μg/mL; with TGF-β1 but without formulations). After incubation, the cells were permeabilized and stained with three different primary antibodies (Vimentin, 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology; SNAIL, 1:500, Cell Signaling Technology; N-cadherin, 1:500, Cell Signaling Technology) and secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG, 1:200, Life Technologies Corporation). The stained cells were observed using confocal microscopy.

Cellular uptake

Cellular uptake of HA-VP NPs by CSCs was visualized using confocal microscopy. For competitive inhibition assay for CD44-mediated cellular uptake, the cells (4 × 104 cells) were seeded on the glass bottom dish (D35-20-1.5-N; Cellvis, CA, USA) and incubated for two days. In HA-VP NPs and HA group (i.e., HA-VP NPs in the presence of competitive inhibitor), free HA was pretreated to the media to be 1% (w/v) for 1.5 h, and then HA-VP NPs (0.5, 5, or 50 μg/mL) were added. The treated cells were incubated for 1 h. Then, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS and incubated with Calcein-AM (2 μM) for 30 min. The prepared cells were visualized using confocal microscopy.

To monitor the intracellular intensity of VP, the cells were seeded on the glass bottom dish (1 × 105 cells) and incubated for one day. Then, the cells were treated with Calcein-AM (2 μM) for 30 min, and VP formulations (10 μg/mL based on VP concentration) were added to the cells. The intracellular localization of VP was visualized using live-cell imaging confocal microscopy by detecting autofluorescence of VP. Images were captured at 20, 40, and 60 min. To monitor the spherical-shaped aggregates on the cell surface, images were taken every minute from 40 to 55 min.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

CSCs (1 × 105 cells) were seeded on 12-well cell culture plates and incubated for one day. The cells were then incubated with fresh media containing TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL) and VP formulations (Visudyne® or HA-VP NPs) at various concentrations (2, 10, 20, 100, and 200 μg/mL, based on VP concentration). Control groups included a normal control (without TGF-β1 or any formulations) and a no-treatment control (represented as 0 μg/mL; with TGF-β1 but without formulations). After incubation, RNAs were extracted from the treated cells using Trizol and chloroform. The extracted RNAs were purified using RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, US). RNA concentrations were measured using a Nanodrop One (Thermo Scientific). After adjusting the RNA concentrations to the same level, the RNAs were transcribed to cDNAs using iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, US). Then, TaqMan gene expression master mix (Bio-Rad) and primers for ACTA2 (Thermo Scientific) were added to the cDNAs. RT-qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System with 40 amplification cycles. ACTA2 expression levels were compared to the normal-negative control (i.e., TGF-β1-untreated cells), and GAPDH was used to normalize endogenous RNA expression within the samples. Comparative fold changes in gene expression levels were analyzed by calculating the 2−ΔΔCt.

Animal model

Brown rats (Rattus norvegicus; 150 – 200 g) provided by Charles River Laboratories (San Francisco, CA, US) were used for in vivo experiments. The animal experiments were designed to conform to the ARVO statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were reviewed and approved by the Stanford University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol #: APLAC-32765). To perform anterior lamellar keratoplasty (ALK), a mixture of ketamine (100 mg/mL), xylazine (20 mg/mL), and water (2:1:3, v/v/v) was administered via intramuscular injection at a dose of 1.68 mL/kg. For follow-up studies, rats received a half-dose of the mixture.

The formulations (1 mg/mL as VP) were administered once daily from day 0 to day 3. During all surgical and follow-up studies, proparacaine hydrochloride ophthalmic solution (0.5%; Bausch and Lomb) was applied to the eyes prior to examination. Corneal defects were created by performing ALK on one eye of each rat using a 2.5-mm trephine to make a deep circular cut (40–60% depth of the corneas, based on optical coherence tomography (OCT) images) and a spatula to remove the collagen layers of the stroma. In all in vivo experiments, rats were protected from strong lights to avoid the generation of free radicals.

In vivo fluorescence imaging for corneal residence assessment

To compare corneal residence between the HA-VP conjugate with low content of VP (HA-VPlow) and HA-VP conjugate with high content of VP (HA-VPhigh), the conjugates were labeled with Alexa Fluor 647 NHS ester (Lumiprobe; Maryland, USA). The fluorophore-labeled conjugates were topically administered to the defected eyes of the rats. Fluorescent signals (Ex/Em = 640/689 nm) were detected using an Optical Imaging Lago System (Spectral Instruments Imaging, AZ, US).

The comparison between the Alexa Fluor 647-labeled HA-VP NPs and Visudyne® was conducted under the same experimental conditions, although Visudyne® was not labeled with fluorescent dyes. The intrinsic fluorescence of Visudyne® was detected at different wavelengths (Ex/Em = 675/730 nm). The eye-to-background intensity ratio (EBR) was calculated by comparing the intensity of the eye to that of the body. The treated eyes were enucleated at 4 h, and their intensities were analyzed to confirm ocular residence.

In vivo evaluation of corneal scarring

Corneal defects were assessed using OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) and digital photography. To evaluate stromal exposure, fluorescein solution (1.5%; one droplet) was applied to the eyes, followed by gentle rinsing with balanced salt solution (BSS). Then, the fluorescein-stained areas were visualized under blue light, and the percentage of the stained area was calculated using ImageJ software. As part of post-operative care, ofloxacin ophthalmic solution (0.3%, Bausch and Lomb) was administered to prevent bacterial infections.

To compare therapeutic efficacy, four groups were prepared: sham, HA, Visudyne®, and HA-VP NPs, with the VP concentration set at 1 mg/mL. The sham and HA groups were treated with PBS and HA-NH2 solution (equivalent to the amount of HA-NH2 in the HA-VP conjugate), respectively. The treatment solutions (10 μL) were applied to the defected eyes. Slit-lamp and fluorescein-stained images of the defects were captured on days 0, 1, 2, 3, 7, and 14. Corneal opacity (Haze area) was quantified based on these photos, by measuring the gray scale unit (GSU) of the central cornea with ImageJ. Meanwhile, OCT images were taken on days 0 (before treatment), 7, and 14 to minimize light exposure of VP from the instrument. Epithelial-to-stromal reflectivity ratio (E:S ratio) was calculated based on OCT images using ImageJ. The GSU of the central epithelial and stromal layers was measured, and the values from the epithelium were divided by those from the stroma. The rats were euthanized on day 14, and their eyes were enucleated and cryo-sectioned for immunohistochemical analysis. The thickness of the epithelial layers and entire corneas on day 14 was measured using confocal microscopy.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Expression of corneal biomarkers was evaluated in regenerated corneas harvested on day 14. The cryo-sectioned corneal slices were stained with one of three markers: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-α-SMA (1:500, Abcam), anti-ALDH3A1 (1:500, Abcam), or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-CK-12 (1:100, Abcam). The stained slices were also counterstained with DAPI (1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated anti-phalloidin (1:400, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For slices stained with anti-ALDH3A1, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, Life Technologies Corporation) was used as a secondary antibody. Fluorescent signals were detected using a confocal microscope (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2.263). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc analysis was used for multiple comparisons. For pairwise comparisons, a two-tailed unpaired t-test was used to determine significance between groups. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05; otherwise, differences were labeled as “not significant (n.s.).”

Results

Synthesis and characterization of HA-VP NPs

The HA-VP conjugate was prepared using the EDC/sulfo-NHS coupling reagents in conjunction with HA-NH2, as shown in Fig. 1B. First, the carboxylic acid group in VP was activated with EDC and sulfo-NHS at pH 5.1. The incorporation of sulfo-NHS served to stabilize the O-acylisourea intermediate by forming an ester, thereby preventing the undesirable formation of N-acylurea by-products. Importantly, the use of sulfo-NHS enhanced the solubility of the intermediate species in comparison to NHS. After the activation step, 2-mercaptoethanol was added to quench any excess EDC, which might otherwise react with residual carboxylic acid groups in the HA-NH2. Finally, HA-NH2 was added at pH 7.1. By manipulating the molar ratio between HA-NH2 and VP (Table S1), we synthesized the HA-VP conjugates with different DoS: (i) the HA-VPlow with 2.2 ± 0.1% DoS and 17.8 ± 0.1 mg/g drug content, and (ii) the VPhigh with 10.4 ± 0.8% DoS and 83.0 ± 4.2 mg/g drug content (Table S2).

The conjugation of VP to HA-NH2 was confirmed using various spectroscopic techniques. For 1H NMR spectroscopy, all samples were dissolved in DMSO-d6 to enable a direct comparison between the starting materials and the final conjugate. While the HA-NH2 and HA-VP conjugate exhibited limited solubility in this solvent, they were prepared at sufficiently low concentrations to maintain a solvated state. As shown in Fig. 1C, the characteristic unsaturated proton resonances of VP (ranging from δ 5.2–9.9 ppm; red dotted box) were clearly present in the HA-VPhigh spectrum but absent in the HA-NH2 precursor. While the primary HA backbone signals overlapped with the residual water peak, the characteristic protons at approximately δ 1.2 ppm were preserved in the conjugate (black dotted box), which may imply the successful conjugation of both components.

In the Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra (Fig. 1D), VP showed a unique absorption peak at approximately 1211 cm−1, likely originating from ester (C-O stretching). This peak was present in the HA-VPhigh but absent in the HA-NH2, confirming the successful conjugation of VP to the HA-NH2. The HA-VP conjugates also exhibited unique peaks from HA-NH2, including 1639 cm−1 (N-H bending), 1377 cm−1 (C-H bending), and 1041 cm−1 (C-N stretching). The HA-VP conjugation was also corroborated by absorbance spectroscopy (Fig. 1E). The VP solution displayed characteristic peaks at 356, 434, 576, and 688 nm, while the HA-NH2 solution exhibited negligible absorption peaks within this range. Compared to the VP solution, the HA-VP conjugates generally exhibited broader peaks, with absorbances proportional to the conjugate concentrations.

The physical structure of the HA-VP conjugate was observed using SEM and TEM (Fig. 1F). Due to the amphiphilic nature of the HA-VP conjugates, they self-assembled into sphere-shaped, nano-sized particles (HA-VP NPs). The mean diameter of the HA-VP NPs was estimated to be 111 ± 66 nm based on the analysis of SEM images. In this text, we refer to the material interchangeably as the HA-VP conjugate and HA-VP NPs. The zeta potential of the HA-VP NPs was −19.9 ± 3.4 mV.

The biodegradability of the HA-VP NPs was evaluated using hyaluronidase over 4 days (Fig. 1G). In the absence of the enzyme, the HA-VP NPs exhibited minimal structural degradation (2.2 ± 2.2%). However, the degradation rate increased with higher enzyme concentrations (6.5 ± 5.2% at 5 unit/mL 7.6 ± 8.0% at 10 unit/mL; 10.9 ± 7.4% at 20 unit/mL; 13.7 ± 11.0% at 50 unit/mL on day 4).

To assess the impact of the different VP DoS (HA-VPlow or HA-VPhigh) on corneal residence time, we utilized an in vivo fluorescence imaging technique in a rat ALK model (Fig. 1H and I) [28]. After performing ALK to remove half of the corneal thickness, the HA-VPlow or HA-VPhigh was topically applied to the defected eyes. For this test, the surfaces of these conjugates were functionalized with fluorescent dyes (Alexa Fluor 647) to enable visualization upon excitation. Notably, most of the HA-VPlow was not retained on the eyes 1.5 h after administration. In contrast, the HA-VPhigh-treated eyes exhibited strong fluorescent signals in the center of the eyes, where the corneal defects were located. The signals persisted for 3 h, indicating superior corneal residence by the HA-VPhigh. Quantitative analysis revealed that the relative fluorescence intensity in the eyes was significantly higher in the HA-VPhigh (2.54-fold) group than in the HA-VPlow group. Consequently, the HA-VPhigh was selected for further evaluation.

To evaluate the stability of HA-VP NPs, we compared absorbance calibration curves (0.1 – 200 μg/mL) generated from the same HA-VP solution batch at three time points: Day 0 (D0), Week 1 (W1), and Week 2 (W2), stored at room temperature (Table S3). Statistical analysis using the extra sum-of-squares F-test confirmed that there were no significant differences in the slopes (p = 0.2140) or y-intercepts (p = 0.1026) over the two-week period. The difference in absorbance values was also negligible. These results demonstrate that the HA-VP NPs are stable under the reported storage conditions; otherwise, significant particle aggregation or VP precipitation would have resulted in a measurable shift in the intercept or a decrease in the slope.

HA-VP NPs inhibit the translocation of YAP in CSCs

Human CSCs can differentiate into myofibroblasts when the cornea is injured, leading to the secretion of irregular ECM proteins and the formation of corneal scars [1]. YAP, a transcription coactivator, plays a key role in this transformation [5]. To assess the pharmacological effects of the HA-VP NPs on YAP, we examined YAP localization in CSCs (Fig. 2A). In this study, TGF-β1 was added to all groups to induce CSC differentiation into myofibroblasts and mimic injured conditions [33]. In the no treatment group, YAP intensities were highly concentrated in the nucleus, with a nucleus-to-cytosol intensity ratio (NC ratio) of 4.16 ± 1.71 (Fig. 2B). This localization did not change with HA-NH2 treatment, whereas the VP solution and Visudyne® (FDA-approved VP-loaded liposomal formulation) groups showed reduced nuclear intensity and increased cytosolic intensity, with NC ratios of 1.44 ± 0.29 and 2.09 ± 0.63, respectively. HA-VP NPs suppress α-SMA expression in CSCs

In addition to YAP localization, we investigated the expression of α-SMA to evaluate the transformation of CSCs into myofibroblasts (Fig. 2C) [6]. When TGF-β1 was added, CSCs expressed α-SMA, and HA-NH2 (HA group) alone did not alter this expression pattern. However, treatment with VP-containing formulations (VP solution, Visudyne®, and HA-VP NPs) significantly reduced α-SMA expression. These groups also showed clear VP signals in the cytosol.

The pharmacological activity of the HA-VP NPs was further analyzed by quantifying the expression of the ACTA2 gene, which encodes α-SMA, using RT-qPCR (Fig. 2D). The HA-VP NPs group exhibited concentration-dependent inhibitory effects against ACTA2, with an IC50 value of 2.90 ± 0.20 μg/mL. This value was not significantly different from that of Visudyne® (2.81 ± 0.21 μg/mL). Additionally, we observed negligible cytotoxicity of the HA-VP NPs up to 1000 μg/mL (Fig. S2).

We hypothesized that HA-VP NPs would inhibit EMT by suppressing YAP activation, a key regulator in the transition process [36,37]. The degree of EMT was determined by evaluating the expression levels of three representative biomarkers: Vimentin, Snail, and N-cadherin [[38], [39], [40]]. The untreated cells (i.e., No treatment group) displayed expressions of Vimentin, Snail, and N-cadherin at the edge of cells, indicating a transition to mesenchymal-like cells. HA treatment alone did not significantly alter the expression patterns of these biomarkers. In contrast, both the VP solution and Visudyne® treatments significantly reduced the expression levels of these EMT biomarkers. HA-VP NPs are internalized and accumulate in the CSCs

HA acts as a ligand for the CD44 receptor on the cell membrane, facilitating endocytosis [41]. This receptor is highly expressed when quiescent stromal cells transform into fibroblasts [42]. To verify the CD44-mediated uptake of HA-VP NPs by CSCs, the cells were incubated with HA-VP NPs in the presence or absence of free HA (Fig. 4A). Free HA was used as a competitive inhibitor of HA-VP NP uptake. After 60 min of incubation, VP fluorescence signals were detected inside the cells, indicating the internalization of HA-VP NPs by CSCs. Notably, the intracellular VP intensity was significantly reduced when the cells were pretreated and co-incubated with free HA. This inhibitory effect was also observed across different concentrations of HA-VP NPs (Fig. 4B). In the HA-VP NP-treated groups, intracellular VP intensity increased in a concentration-dependent manner. In contrast, in the groups treated with HA-VP NPs and free HA, the VP intensity reached a plateau between 5 and 50 μg/mL. This limited uptake can be attributed to the saturation of CD44 receptors by free HA and HA-VP NPs, which prevented a further increase in HA-VP NP internalization despite the higher NP concentration. HA-VP NPs prolong the ocular residence time compared to the Visudyne® solution in a rat ALK model

Prolonged residence time of drugs on the ocular surface is critical for improving efficacy, considering the inherently low bioavailability of eye drop formulations (∼5%) [26]. To investigate the residence of the HA-VP NPs in corneal defects, we applied the Alexa Fluor 647-labeled HA-VP NPs to the artificially created corneal defects in rats (Fig. 5A). The fluorescent dye (Alexa Fluor 647) was covalently attached to the surface of the HA-VP NPs for visualization purposes. In this study, Visudyne® was used for comparison, because VP alone is sparingly soluble in aqueous solution. The original purpose and route of administration of Visudyne differ from those of HA-VP NPs. However, as demonstrated in the in vitro studies (Fig. 2, Fig. 3), Visudyne showed inhibitory effects on YAP comparable to those of the VP solution, suggesting that the antifibrotic activity is not compromised by liposomal encapsulation. Therefore, the Visudyne was selected as a control for in vivo evaluation. Immediately after administration (0 h), most of the Visudyne® was not retained on the cornea, and no significant signal was observed on the corneal defects. In contrast, the HA-VP NPs group exhibited a strong signal on the corneal defect, and the intensity remained for 4 h. The intensity was quantitatively compared using the EBR (Fig. 5B). Discussion

Our research aimed to develop HA-VP NPs to increase the apparent aqueous solubility of VP and extend its residence time on corneal defects, as NPs are considered an effective strategy for ocular surface diseases [47,48]. In vitro cell studies employed VP solution and Visudyne® as control groups, as the concentrations used were low enough to be within the solubility limits of VP. However, due to the poor water solubility, the VP solution group was not feasible for use in in vivo animal experiments (Fig. S7). In other words, although VP solution demonstrated pharmacological effects comparable to those of Visudyne® and HA-VP NPs in cell-based studies, it is not a viable option for preclinical or clinical applications due to its poor solubility in aqueous solution. This limitation underscores the need to develop VP formulations for use beyond in vitro studies.

Visudyne®, the FDA-approved liposomal formulation of VP, was originally used in photodynamic therapy to treat age-related macular degeneration by inhibiting choroidal neovascularization [24]. This drug is administered via intravenous injection, and to the best of our knowledge, no research has explored its topical application (i.e., eye drops) for preventing corneal scarring. In this study, we primarily focused on evaluating the efficacy of the HA-VP NPs, while also exploring the potential of this liposomal formulation as an eye drop treatment.

The HA-VP conjugates are amphiphilic polymers consisting of hydrophilic HA and hydrophobic VP. This amphiphilicity drives the self-assembly of the polymers into sphere-shaped NPs [49]. NPs can increase the apparent solubility and stability of hydrophobic molecules, and their hydrophilic surface facilitates interactions between the NPs and biomolecules [[50], [51], [52]]. In the absorbance spectra, the HA-VP NPs exhibited a spectrum similar to that of the VP solution, although their peaks are less sharp. Anchoring VP to the polymer structure appears to influence light absorption. Despite the broader peaks, the absorbance intensities are proportional to the HA-VP NP concentration, suggesting successful conjugation of VP to HA structures.

Myofibroblasts lead to irregular corneal remodeling and haze, induced by TGF-β1 secreted from injured and immune cells [1,6]. During corneal injuries, fibroblasts stimulated by TGF-β1 undergo YAP translocation into the nucleus, upregulating multiple genes related to differentiation, with α-SMA serving as a biomarker for myofibroblast formation [53]. In cellular studies, the VP solution significantly inhibited YAP translocation and α-SMA expression, confirming its pharmacological activities in CSCs. VP is widely regarded as a functional inhibitor of YAP signaling, primarily through suppression of YAP–TEAD-dependent transcriptional ativity. Thus, the reduced nuclear localization of YAP observed here is consistent with functional inhibition of YAP/TEAD signaling [54,55]. Notably, both Visudyne® and HA-VP NPs exhibited inhibitory activities similar to the VP solution. Compared with Visudyne®, HA-VP NPs showed significantly greater inhibition of YAP nuclear localization. This suggests that the pharmacological efficacy of VP is well preserved after conjugation, with increased apparent water solubility.

EMT is a process in which epithelial cells gradually transform into mesenchymal-like cells, losing their epithelial functionality and characteristics. This process plays a role in the development of scarring and fibrosis [34,35]. Thus, alongside the transformation of stromal cells, the transition of epithelial cells may contribute to the development of corneal fibrosis. We evaluated the EMT based on three representative biomarkers: Vimentin, Snail, and N-cadherin. Vimentin, an intermediate filament protein, links the nucleus to the plasma membrane and contributes to the mechanical stability and motility of cells [39]. Snail is a zinc finger protein and a key mediator of EMT [40]. N-cadherin is associated with enhanced migratory and invasive traits, serving as a hallmark of EMT [38]. The significantly reduced expression of all three biomarkers following HA-VP NPs treatment suggests the inhibitory effects of the developed NPs on EMT. Therefore, our results suggest that HA-VP NPs may exhibit anti-fibrotic effects by suppressing both the transition of epithelial cells and the transformation of stromal cells into myofibroblasts.

VP solution, Visudyne®, and HA-VP NPs have distinct mechanisms of cellular uptake. VP solution can diffuse through cell membranes, while Visudyne® (liposomes) may require cellular engulfment and incorporation into vesicles [56]. Another possible mechanism for Visudyne® could involve VP release from the liposome followed by diffusion into cells; however, this possibility is unlikely given the negligible VP release observed (Fig. S8). We hypothesize that HA-VP NPs could be internalized via CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis [41]. The sphere-shaped aggregates observed in Fig. 4 may result from interactions between surface receptors (CD44) and NPs. Following endocytosis, the HA-VP NPs would be biodegraded by intracellular enzymes, releasing free VP molecules to enable their pharmacological effects. Collectively, the cellular studies indicate that the HA-VP NPs are sufficiently biodegraded to show efficacy comparable to VP solution. In addition, HA-VP NPs showed significantly greater inhibition of YAP nuclear localization than Visudyne®, while the EMT findings suggested qualitatively similar or more pronounced inhibitory effects.

Prolonged residence time of VP in corneal defects is crucial for enhanced bioavailability and therapeutic outcomes [26,57,58]. We hypothesized that the HA-based NPs would enhance the local retention of VP on corneal defects through interactions with surface CD44 receptors on fibroblasts and through their innate bioadhesiveness derived from HA [35,59,60]. Because VP is covalently conjugated to the HA backbone, prolonged retention of HA-VP NPs on the ocular surface directly indicates prolonged retention of a VP-bearing construct at the injury site, rather than retention of the carrier alone. Thus, the objective of this system was to increase local VP retention on the injured cornea. As expected, the HA-VP NPs exhibited prolonged corneal residence, remaining on the defects for at least 4 h. The in vivo and ex vivo fluorescence images indicate that the HA-VP NPs are slightly greater in corneal residence than Visudyne®. For the in vivo imaging, we functionalized the HA-VP NPs with a fluorescent dye because the intrinsic fluorescence of VP markedly decreased after conjugation with HA (Fig. S9). Although the dye's excitation/emission wavelengths differ from those of Visudyne® (i.e., the intrinsic fluorescence of VP), their kinetics can be compared by calculating the EBR. We further propose that HA–CD44 interactions contributed to this prolonged retention not only by promoting surface association with exposed stromal/fibroblastic cells, but also by facilitating cellular uptake, which could further reduce rapid precorneal loss and increase local sequestration of VP-bearing NPs within the wound tissue. Because HA is biodegradable, such CD44-mediated uptake may also support intracellular processing of the HA-VP construct after retention and internalization. A higher viscosity of HA-VP NPs than Visudyne® also contributed to the retention (Fig. S10). Therefore, the prolonged ocular residence observed in this study should be interpreted as enhanced local retention of VP at the corneal injury site.

For in vivo evaluation of therapeutic efficacy, we used a rat ALK model mimicking traumatically injured corneas [28]. In OCT images, all rats exhibited mild-to-moderate inflammation after the corneal defect formation, leading to corneal swelling and edema for 7 days (Fig. 6B and S4). The transient increase in haze areas in the HA-VP NPs group during this timeframe may be attributed to the inflammatory response following injury (Fig. D). By day 14, although the sham group showed relieved symptoms, two rats displayed irregular stromal regeneration (rat 3 and 4 in Fig. S4) resulting in cone-shaped corneas, while persistent inflammation and edema remained in another subject (rat 2 in Fig. S4). The corneal neovascularization observed in the corneas on day 14 may be attributed to severe inflammation (Fig. S6). Similarly, one rat in the Visudyne® group showed uncontrollable inflammation and swelling on day 14 (rat 3 in Fig. S4). Interestingly, these symptoms were absent in the HA and HA-VP NPs groups, possibly due to the anti-inflammatory and wound healing effects of HA [28,61]. It is noteworthy that while corneal structures regenerated in both the HA solution and HA-VP NPs groups, the HA solution alone did not reduce haze areas. Only the HA-VP NPs group significantly improved corneal opacity, strongly suggesting their anti-scarring effects derived from VP.

Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the anti-scarring and regenerative effects of the HA-VP NPs. The sham group exhibited high α-SMA expression in the stroma and low ALDH3A1 expression in the epithelium, indicating increased corneal scar formation and reduced transparency. In addition, low expression of CK12 suggests incomplete epithelial healing in the sham group. HA solution eye drops improved epithelial regeneration, likely due to the corneal wound healing effects of HA molecules [62]. However, this treatment was insufficient to prevent corneal scar formation. Visudyne® eye drops tended to reduce myofibroblast transformation, but their anti-scarring effect was not significantly greater than that of the sham and HA groups, as shown in the OCT and slit-lamp images in Fig. 6. Thus, Visudyne® eye drops are not effective in preventing corneal scar formation. In contrast, the HA-VP NPs significantly decreased α-SMA expression and increased ALDH3A1 and CK12 expression, confirming their anti-scarring effects after corneal injury.

Conclusion

We developed VP-conjugated HA (HA-VP) NPs as a potential therapeutic for scarless corneal regeneration following traumatic injuries. The amphiphilic HA-VP conjugates were biodegradable and self-assembled into NPs, significantly increasing the apparent water solubility of VP and enabling its use in vivo. The HA-VP NPs effectively prevented stromal cells from differentiating into myofibroblasts and epithelial cells from undergoing transition to mesenchymal-like phenotypes by decreasing YAP localization to the nucleus. In a rat ALK model, the HA-VP NPs exhibited significantly longer local retention of VP-bearing NPs on corneal defects compared to Visudyne®, presumably leading to improved stromal remodeling and transparency. These findings suggest that the HA-VP NPs are a promising therapeutic approach for achieving scarless corneal regeneration.
