Association of triglyceride-glucose index with presence and development of impaired spirometry: results from Austrian LEAD cohort
Authors: Mohammad Azizzadeh, Emiel F M Wouters, Charmaine J M Lim, Michael Sturm, Robab Breyer-Kohansal, Marie-Kathrin Breyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a marker of metabolic dysfunction. However, evidence regarding its association with lung function (LF) impairment, particularly from longitudinal studies, are scarce. This study aimed to evaluate the association between TyG and both the presence and development of LF impairments.MethodsNine thousand six hundred sixty-eight participants (aged 25–82 yrs; 48% male) who participated in the baseline assessment of the Austrian LEAD cohort and 6,880 individuals with longitudinal follow-up data (mean interval:4.3 yrs) were included in the analysis. Fasting triglycerides and glucose levels were used to calculate the TyG index. Obstructive spirometry, restrictive spirometry, and preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) were defined using both fixed cut-off and lower limit of normal (LLN) criteria. Associations between the TyG index and lung function parameters, as well as the presence of spirometric impairments at baseline, were examined. In addition, the predictive ability of the TyG index for incident spirometric impairment was assessed.ResultsHigher TyG was significantly associated with lower FEV₁, FVC, MMEF₂₅–₇₅, TLC, and RV. Baseline TyG was associated with higher risk of fixed cut-off defined restrictive spirometry [odds ratio (OR): 1.26 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.04–1.53)] and PRISm [OR (95% CI): 1.23 (1.00–1.53)], and showed borderline significance for predicting incident restrictive spirometry [risk ratio (RR) (95% CI): 1.50 (0.95–2.40)] over time. Its predictive ability (area under the curve = 0.63) was comparable to established metabolic syndrome indices, including the International Diabetes Federation, Adult Treatment Panel III, and American Heart Association criteria. Sensitivity analyses using LLN-defined spirometric impairment yielded similar results.ConclusionsTyG index is associated with the presence and development of restrictive pulmonary outcomes. However, its standalone predictive value is limited, and its utility may lie in being an easily accessible index rather than a definitive screening tool for impaired lung health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04361-5.Keywords: Triglyceride-glucose index, Spirometry impairment, Lung function parameters, Longitudinal study

Introduction

Pulmonary function is an important indicator of an individual’s overall health and physical condition. A decline in lung function can negatively affect health related quality of life [1]. Accumulating evidence has shown that reduced pulmonary capacity is associated with early onset of multimorbidity and increased mortality rates [2–4]. Early identification and management of related modifiable factors are important to prevent lung function decline and its subsequent complications. Previous studies have demonstrated an association of metabolic syndrome (MS) and its components including abdominal obesity, hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels with impaired lung function [5–9].

The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index is a marker of insulin resistance and related metabolic dysfunction [10]. It is calculated using fasting glucose and triglyceride levels, parameters routinely measured in clinical practice. Several clinical studies have underscored the significance of the TyG index as a practical indicator for diverse medical conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, fatty liver, kidney diseases, and reproductive system diseases [11]. Metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance can lead to low-grade systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and alterations in lung parenchyma, potentially reducing pulmonary compliance and contributing to restrictive spirometric patterns [12]. Cross-sectional studies have reported associations between the TyG index and spirometry indices [12–14], as well as a mediating role of TyG in the relationship between the dietary inflammation index and the composite dietary antioxidant index with preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) [15]. However, longitudinal studies investigating its association with lung function impairment remain scarce.

The Austrian LEAD (Lung, hEart, sociAl, boDy) study is a longitudinal, observational, population-based cohort initiated in 2012, with follow-up examinations conducted every four years. Using data from the LEAD study, we aimed to: (i) evaluate the association of the TyG index with spirometry and body plethysmography parameters, as well as with the presence of spirometric impairments; (ii) assess the impact of baseline TyG on the development of spirometric impairments over time; and (iii) compare the predictive performance of the TyG index for the development of spirometric impairment with other measures of metabolic dysfunction, including those defined by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [16], the Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III) [17], and the American Heart Association (AHA) [18].

Methods

Study population and subject selection

Data were obtained from the first and second visits of the Austrian LEAD study, a population-based cohort (NCT01727518; http://clinicaltrials.gov) whose design and methodology have been described in detail elsewhere [19]. In brief, the LEAD study investigates respiratory, metabolic, and cardiovascular health. Blood parameters are measured for all participants. In addition, psychological well-being, mental health, health-related quality of life, habits, lifestyle, and medical history are assessed using standardized questionnaires.

At baseline (visit 1), 15,056 participants aged 6–82 years from Vienna and six villages in Lower Austria were recruited between 2012 and 2022. Follow-up examinations are conducted every four years, using the same measurement protocols as at baseline.

All assessments are performed by trained staff at the LEAD Study Centre of the Ludwig Boltzmann Institute for Lung Health, located at the Penzing site of Clinic Ottakring, Vienna, Austria. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee of Vienna (protocol number EK-11-117-0711), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

For the present analysis, we included participants aged ≥ 25 years at visit 1 with complete data for calculating the TyG index in the cross-sectional analysis. For the longitudinal analysis, only those participants who also completed visit 2 were included.

TyG

Serum glucose and triglyceride levels were measured by using an autoanalyzer (Siemens Dimension™ VISTA 1500).

The TyG index was calculated using fasting blood glucose (FBG) and triglyceride (TG) levels according to the formula: Ln (FBG (mg/dl) × TG (mg/dl)/2).

Lung function measures and definitions of impaired spirometry

Post-bronchodilation (BD) spirometry, including forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), and maximal mid-expiratory flow at 25–75% of FVC (MMEF₂₅–₇₅), as well as body plethysmography, including total lung capacity (TLC) and residual volume (RV), were performed in accordance with the international recommendations of the European Respiratory Society (ERS). Spirometry values were expressed as percent predicted based on the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) 2012 reference equations [20], and body plethysmography values were expressed as percent predicted using the LEAD 2024 reference equations [21].

Spirometric patterns were defined using fixed cut-offs as follows: obstructive spirometry was defined as post-BD FEV₁/FVC < 0.70. A restrictive spirometry as post-BD FEV₁/FVC ≥ 0.70 with post-BD FVC < 80% predicted (GLI). PRISm as post-BD FEV₁/FVC ≥ 0.70 with post-BD FEV₁ < 80% predicted (GLI).

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and evidence from previous literature, focusing on factors known to be associated with both metabolic dysfunction and lung function. Demographic variables included age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Smoking status was classified as never smoker or ever smoker, with “ever smoking” defined as having smoked at least 20 packs of cigarettes in total, at least one cigarette per day for one year, or at least two cigarettes per day for six months.

Cardiovascular disease was defined as a self-reported history of any of the following: stroke, peripheral artery occlusive disease (PAOD), myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, cardiac arrhythmia, or carotid calcification. Information on the use of statins and medications for diabetes was obtained from responses to specific questions (Supplementary method S1).

Other indices of metabolic dysfunction

For comparison with the TyG index, metabolic indices were derived based on established criteria from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III), and the American Heart Association (AHA).

All indices were constructed using five components: central obesity, elevated triglycerides, reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, elevated blood pressure, and elevated fasting plasma glucose. Central obesity was defined using sex-specific waist circumference thresholds (ATP III/AHA: >102 cm in men and > 88 cm in women; IDF: >94 cm in men and > 80 cm in women). Elevated triglycerides were defined as ≥ 150 mg/dL or lipid-lowering treatment. Reduced HDL cholesterol was defined as < 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women. Elevated blood pressure was defined as systolic ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥ 85 mmHg or antihypertensive treatment. Elevated fasting glucose was defined as ≥ 110 mg/dL for ATP III and ≥ 100 mg/dL for AHA and IDF. To enable comparability across definitions, all indices were operationalized as continuous sum scores by assigning one point to each abnormal component and summing the total number of abnormalities.

Statistical analysis

Given the absence of universally established clinical cut-offs for the TyG index, participants were categorized using a data-driven approach based on the 75th percentile to identify individuals with relatively elevated metabolic risk within the study population. Participants’ characteristics were described for the total study population and stratified by TyG levels: normal (≤ 75th percentile of the TyG in total population) and high (> 75th percentile). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies with percentages.

For the cross-sectional analysis, multivariable linear regression was used to examine the association between TyG and lung function parameters at visit 1. Binary logistic regression was applied to assess the association between TyG and the likelihood of having PRISm, restrictive, or obstructive spirometry, defined using fixed cut-offs, at visit 1.

For the longitudinal analysis, after exclusion of participants with impaired spirometry at baseline, multivariable Poisson regression with robust error variance was employed to evaluate the association between TyG at visit 1 and the risk of transitioning from non-restrictive to restrictive, non-obstructive to obstructive, and non-PRISm to PRISm patterns.

The predictive performance of TyG was compared with other indices of metabolic dysfunction, including the IDF, AHA, and ATP III criteria, in identifying spirometry impairments. Comparisons were based on Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding areas under the curve (AUCs).

All models were adjusted for potential confounders: age, sex, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), presence of cardiovascular disease, and use of statins and medications for diabetes. Based on the main analysis, we carried out two sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of our results. First, considering that the use of fixed threshold cut-offs may lead to overestimation of spirometry impairment in the older population, we repeated the analysis with a lower limit of normal (LLN) thresholds definition of obstructive spirometry-LLN (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC < LLN), Restrictive spirometry-LLN (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ≥ LLN and FVC < LLN ), and PRISm-LLN (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ≥ LLN and FEV1 < LLN). Second, although the regression models were adjusted for medication use, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding participants using statins or diabetes medications to account for potential confounding due to unmeasured treatment duration and adherence. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (https://cran.r-project.org). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cross-sectional analysis

A total of 9,668 participants from the first visit of the LEAD study, aged 25–82 years and with valid lung function measures and complete data for the calculation of the TyG, were included in the cross-sectional analysis (Supplementary figure S1). Mean ± SD TyG was 8.42 ± 0.58, with higher values observed in males than females across all age categories (Supplementary figure S2).

The median (Q1–Q3) TyG index for the study population was 8.37 (8.02–8.77). Individuals with a TyG value ≤ 8.77 were classified as having normal TyG levels, whereas those with a TyG value > 8.77 were categorized as having high TyG levels. Univariate analyses showed that lung volume and spirometry parameters, except post-BD MMEF25–75% predicted (GLI), were significantly lower in participants with high TyG levels compared with those with normal TyG levels. Furthermore, the prevalence of restrictive and obstructive spirometry, as well as PRISm, defined using fixed cut-offs, was greater among participants with high TyG levels than those with normal TyG levels.

Multivariate linear regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), presence of cardiovascular disease, and use of statins and medications for diabetes, showed that higher TyG as a continuous variable is significantly associated with incrementally lower post-BD FEV₁, FVC, MMEF₂₅–₇₅, TLC, and RV (Fig. 1).

Longitudinal analysis

Among those participants who were included in the cross-sectional analysis, 6,880 participants also took part in the second visit (mean ± SD assessment interval: 4.3 ± 0.7 years) and were included in the longitudinal analysis.

During follow-up, according to the fixed cut-off definitions, 70 participants transitioned from non-restrictive to restrictive, 347 from non-obstructive to obstructive, and 78 from non-PRISm to PRISm.

Adjusted logistic regression models showed that higher TyG at visit 1 is borderline significantly associated with higher risk of developing restrictive spirometry during 2 visits [RRR (95% CI): 1.50 (0.95–2.40); p = 0.07]. Although the risks of PRISm and obstructive spirometry were increased with high TyG, the association was not significant (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis using LLN-based definitions of impaired spirometry

The prevalence of LLN-defined obstructive spirometry, restrictive spirometry, and PRISm was 521 (5.4%), 283 (2.9%), and 233 (2.4%), respectively. In the cross-sectional analysis, participants with high TyG levels showed a higher prevalence of impaired spirometry (LLN definition) compared to those with normal TyG levels (Supplementary table S1). The significant associations between TyG and both restrictive spirometry and PRISm persisted when the analysis was repeated using the LLN definition (Fig. 5).

Cross sectional association of TyG and impaired spirometry (LLN definition) at visit1. Results from a logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, history of cardiovascular diseases, use of statins and medications for diabetes, BMI, and smoking status

In the longitudinal analysis, 46 participants transitioned from LLN defined non-restrictive to restrictive, 180 from non-obstructive to obstructive, and 53 from non-PRISm to PRISm over the follow-up period. Multivariable regression models showed a similar trend for the effect of TyG on the development of impaired spirometry; however, the associations did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the small number of participants who developed PRISm, restrictive, or obstructive spirometry under the LLN definition (Supplementary figure S3). The AUC of TyG for predicting restrictive spirometry based on the LLN definition was comparable to that obtained using the fixed cut-off definition [AUC (95% CI): 0.632 (0.55–0.71); Supplementary figure S4].

Sensitivity analysis excluding participants using statins or diabetes medications

Given that information on the timing of medication initiation and adherence was not available, which may have introduced exposure misclassification and attenuated associations, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding participants using statins or diabetes medications. A total of 1,196 participants on these medications were excluded.

In this restricted sample, the prevalence of obstructive spirometry, restrictive spirometry, and PRISm defined using fixed cut-offs, at visit 1 was 7.3% (n = 615), 3.6% (n = 304), and 2.9% (n = 247), respectively.

In cross-sectional analyses adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), and cardiovascular disease, results were consistent with the main findings. Higher TyG was associated with an increased risk of restrictive spirometry [OR 1.35 (95% CI: 1.08–1.70)] and PRISm [OR 1.30 (95% CI: 1.01–1.67)], while no significant association was observed for obstructive spirometry [OR 1.15 (0.96–1.36)].

In longitudinal analyses, higher baseline TyG showed a borderline association with incident restrictive spirometry [RRR 1.67 (95% CI: 0.97–2.76), p = 0.07], whereas no significant associations were observed for PRISm [RRR 1.14 (0.68–1.90)] or obstructive spirometry [RRR 1.26 (0.97–1.62)].

Discussion

In this large, population-based cohort with a wide age-range, we provide insights into the distribution of the TyG index and the prevalence and incidence of impaired spirometry. We also found that higher TyG level is associated with lower lung function and lung volume parameters, as well as a higher risk of both the presence and development of restrictive spirometry.

The mean TyG level in our study was 8.42, higher than the 8.34 reported by Gil et al. in a younger population and lower than the 8.58 and 8.76 reported by Lopez-Jaramillo et al. and Wu et al., respectively, in older populations [12, 22, 23]. Our finding of higher TyG levels in men compared with women is consistent with previous reports [23, 24]. The association of elevated TyG with reduced spirometric measures aligns with the findings of previous investigation [12, 14, 23]. However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to report an association between the TyG index and lung volume parameters. TyG was negatively associated with both TLC and RV. Previous studies have shown that metabolic syndrome is linked to increased total white blood cells, neutrophils, and lymphocytes [25–28]. Higher TyG as a marker of insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, or dyslipidaemia not only impair airflow by stimulating immune cells and structural cells in the airways [29, 30], but also lead to low grade systemic inflammation, microvascular changes, or remodelling of lung parenchyma (fibrosis, stiffening) that reduce lung compliance or expansion [31, 32]. RV might also decline because the entire lung volume scale is shifted downward. The finding might reflect an insidious, global parenchymal impact of metabolic stress, not solely airway disease.

A few cross-sectional studies have examined the association between TyG and impaired spirometry. Consistent with Wu et al., we found that TyG was associated with restrictive, but not obstructive, spirometry [12]. Gill et al. also reported associations between TyG and FEV1% < LLN and FVC% < LLN, but not FEV1/FVC below the LLN [23]. The significant association of TyG with restrictive but not obstructive spirometry may reflect the influence of metabolic dysregulation on lung compliance and respiratory muscle performance, potentially mediated by adiposity-related mechanical constraints and chronic low-grade inflammation [33]. Although we controlled the effect of BMI in multivariate analysis, BMI does not accurately reflect body composition [34]. Visceral adipose tissue (VAT) is associated with higher TyG [35], and has been shown to have a stronger association with FVC than with FEV1 [36, 37]. Inclusion of direct measures of body composition in future studies assessing the relationship between TyG and lung function could improve understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

Our analysis of baseline TyG levels and the subsequent development of impaired spirometry represents the first longitudinal investigation of this association, as suggested by authors of previous cross-sectional studies. The increased risk of developing restrictive spirometry with higher TyG supports a potential causal role of metabolic dysfunction in the incidence of the restrictive spirometric pattern. This temporal relationship is biologically plausible, as metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance may drive progressive parenchymal remodeling (e.g., through chronic low-grade inflammation and profibrotic processes) and reductions in lung compliance over time, rather than producing an immediate functional deficit, consistent with the mechanisms described above.

Sensitivity analyses using LLN-based definitions of impaired spirometry revealed similar trends in cross-sectional and longitudinal models, though with wider confidence intervals, likely due to the smaller number of participants with restrictive spirometry at baseline and those who developed restriction between baseline and follow-up.

We also evaluated the predictive value of TyG for incident spirometric impairment. The AUC for predicting restrictive spirometry was modest (0.633), comparable to other metabolic dysfunction indices. This suggests that while TyG alone is insufficient as a single screening tool, it may contribute to risk stratification when combined with other clinical variables. Its limited performance in predicting incident obstruction and PRISm further underscores phenotypic differences in the influence of metabolic disturbance on lung function trajectories. Unlike restrictive spirometry, obstructive patterns and PRISm are driven predominantly by airway remodelling, small airway dysfunction, and smoking-related injury, processes that are only weakly influenced by insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation, which likely explains the poor predictive performance of the TyG index for these phenotypes [38].

Besides our findings on the association between TyG and spirometry impairment, results from previous studies on the relationships between TyG and cardiovascular events [39, 40], mortality risk [41], diabetes [42], and central obesity [35] suggest that TyG levels and their changes over time could be regarded as potential marker of multisystem dysregulation on the one hand and healthy aging on the other.

Strengths and limitations

This study has notable strengths, including its large sample size, standardized spirometric assessments, and longitudinal design. The use of post-bronchodilator spirometry improves specificity by reducing misclassification due to reversible airway obstruction; however, it may yield lower prevalence estimates and affect the classification of spirometric patterns. Therefore, comparisons with studies using pre-bronchodilator measurements should be made with caution. Several limitations should also be considered. First, some potential confounding cannot be entirely excluded, particularly regarding dietary factors, physical activity, or unmeasured inflammatory markers. Second, although the longitudinal design partly mitigates concerns about reverse causality, bidirectional relationships cannot be fully excluded; impaired spirometric patterns may themselves contribute to metabolic dysregulation through mechanisms such as reduced physical activity, systemic inflammation, or hypoxia, potentially influencing the TyG index. Furthermore, the relatively small number of incident cases in the longitudinal analysis, particularly for restrictive spirometry, may have limited statistical power and resulted in less stable estimates with wider confidence intervals. This may have contributed to the borderline significance observed in some associations and warrants cautious interpretation of the longitudinal findings. Third, the follow-up duration, while sufficient to detect early transitions, may have been insufficient to capture longer-term effects on lung function impairments. Finally, although TLC is the gold standard for diagnosing restrictive ventilatory impairment, we used spirometry-based criteria to align with common epidemiological practice and ensure comparability with previous studies. Spirometry is more widely available, whereas TLC measurement requires more complex techniques. This pragmatic approach may, however, lead to some misclassification, as reduced FVC does not always reflect true restriction.

Conclusions

Our findings provide evidence that higher TyG levels are associated with reduced lung function and increased risk of restrictive spirometric abnormalities. However, its standalone predictive value is limited, and its utility may lie in being an easily accessible index for metabolic dysfunction rather than a definitive screening tool for impaired lung health.
