A clinical audit of the comprehensive care dentistry model in the internship clinics of a dental school in Pakistan
Authors: Hina Zafar Raja, Muhammad Nasir Saleem, Maryam Mumtaz, Samir Riaz Qazi, Muhammad Sohail Zafar, Muhammad Amber Fareed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComprehensive Care clinics in dental education aim to train doctors in patient- centered, holistic care, focusing on multi-disciplinary treatment provision. There has been a rapid global paradigm shift within the last decade in dental educational models, shifting from departmental training to comprehensive care clinics. This study describes the establishment, feasibility, and outcomes of a pioneer Comprehensive Care Dentistry (CCD) clinic, which was established in a tertiary care Dental Institute in Lahore, Pakistan. The aim is to present the barriers in establishment of Comprehensive Care dental clinics, in dental school of a resource limited country. It also aims to present strengths, weaknesses, and alternative feasible models of this educational system.MethodsThis observational and audit-based study involved the establishment of a comprehensive care dental clinic in a tertiary care dental institute, with systematic documentation and audits of clinical workflows, treatment planning protocols, case allocations and 360-degree feedback from all primary stakeholders, over the first year of clinic’s operation. Data was collected via feedback forms from primary stakeholders, clinical records, and faculty observations. Key performance indicators such as cross infection protocols, patient turnover and satisfaction, material usage, interdepartmental coordination, and patient completion rates were assessed. Hindrances and facilitators to implementation were identified via focused administrative and faculty discourses.ResultsThe clinic provided a structured, patient centered, multidisciplinary approach towards patient care. It had a positive role in enhancing interns’ communication skills, interprofessional coordination and continuity of care. Improved accountability amongst interns, cohesive treatment provision and high patient satisfaction was observed. Challenges identified included lack of faculty coordination and acceptance, variations in treatment plans amongst different consultants and administrative limitations. Overall feasibility, treatment provision and educational value were positively received by all stakeholders.ConclusionThe present study suggested the need to revise dental education modes from discipline-driven numerical requirements to a patient-centred comprehensive care curriculum for the timely professional grooming of dental graduates. CCD positively influences the quality of student learning as well as patient care. Students and patients experience a better learning and treatment experience respectively within this system. In a resource limited country like Pakistan, there are still hindrances in resources and acceptance within faculty for this system but with perseverance and modifications as per feasibility of each dental school, this system may prove to be very successful. Curricular modification may be required for inculcation of this system, within evolving dental education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08179-3.

Introduction

Comprehensive care dentistry (CCD) outlines a seamless integrated clinical dental service that addresses all patients’ dental needs in one clinical setting [1]. The CCD model of dental education is a patient-centred management plan, inculcating holistic patient care in dentistry [1–3]. This concept enables young learners to integrate the concepts of essential dental care planning and inculcate strategies to ensure that dental graduates are well-prepared for their careers as general dental practitioners [1–4]. Comprehensive care dentistry models in different forms have been implemented in dental schools since 1984 [5, 6]. This model manages patients under one roof, mimicking the real-world scenario, with improved interpretation of clinical presentation [3, 7, 8]. Comprehensive care training results in effective time management, skill enhancement, and interpersonal skills [2, 9, 10]. The CCD concept is implemented through a structured curriculum, integrating theoretical knowledge, practical skills, and clinical experience during dental internship clinics and undergraduate years [1–3, 6, 11, 12].

The traditional model of dental education involves discipline-driven numerical requirements, with students allocated to specific specialities, and expected to perform a specified number of procedures [1]. The traditional model may consist of preclinical courses to improve competencies [13, 14]. However, multiple problems exist within this system. The departmental division may be convenient for quota-based learning and evaluation, but such a division has limited practical application where patients present with multiple problems and holistic care [15]. Quota-based subject-specific systems shift the focus from student-centred teaching to procedure-specific learning, leading to underutilization of learning resources, a lack of integration of clinical findings, with frequent referrals/transfers of patients amongst multiple operators in diverse clinical facilities [16]. This prohibits an active role and self-responsibility of students during the learning process [17].

Currently, the Pakistan Medical & Dental Council (PMDC) recommends integration of basic and clinical sciences to facilitate holistic education of undergraduates [6]. This requires the implementation of integrated courses within dental institutes. Integrated curriculum is multi-disciplinary, inter-disciplinary, and trans-disciplinary [18]. These guidelines align with the concept of CCD in dental education, which also provides a multimodality complete treatment to patients [1, 18]. To achieve these requirements, dental schools should train graduates in all aspects of healthcare provision and independent patient management. In addition, dental schools need to work towards developing clinical competence and improving generic competencies i.e. professionalism, communication skills, time management, ethics, integrity, teamwork, problem-solving skills, empathy, critical thinking, creativity, leadership, emotional intellect, research, and life-long learning potential [4]. Simultaneous achievement of these tasks demands a modification in the existing coaching patterns to improve the outcome in all aspects [1].

During the last decade, a gradual shift from conventional to comprehensive care model started taking place in multiple institutes. More than half of the dental schools in North America including Harvard School of Dental Medicine, University of Illinois College of Dentistry, and numerous others in developed countries are currently practising CCD model for undergraduate courses [1–3, 11, 19]. However, in developing countries specific challenges are observed such as lack of resources, awareness, and rigidity within faculty [1]. This reluctance on part of the faculty may be attributed to the perceived fear of lack of departmental autonomy through elimination of departmental boundaries within the comprehensive care system [1, 4, 19]. However, no evidence available has evaluated the factors that may facilitate or hinder the establishment and functioning of the comprehensive model of dentistry in dental schools in developing countries, particularly Pakistan. Therefore, in a leading teaching institute in Pakistan, a clinical model of CCD was implemented. The rationale of this training innovation was to establish a feasible comprehensive care dentistry model in a dental school, that may also overcome the physical and psychological blocks that hinder the amalgamation of clinical applications of diverse specialities. Dental interns were exposed to this program, to prepare the graduates for general dental practice and to foresee the utility of this model for an integrated undergraduate curriculum, followed by a clinical audit.

This study aims to describe the barriers to the establishment of a comprehensive care dentistry clinic in a dental institute of a developing country. It also evaluates the outcomes of the CCD program through rigorous clinical audits, followed by incorporation of certain modifications to overcome the identified challenges.

Methodology

Ethical approval letter was obtained by the Ethical Review Committee of CMH Lahore Medical College, Institute of Dentistry, Case#0.134/ERC/CMH/LMC. CCD was introduced at the Institute of Dentistry, CMH Lahore Medical College, Lahore, in May 2023, for a fresh batch of 75 dental interns. The establishment of the Comprehensive Care Dentistry Clinic was formally approved by the Chief Executive and the administrative leadership of the institution of CMH LMC & IOD. The clinic’s framework, scope of services, and operational protocols were officially reviewed and refined through administrative meetings and internal evaluations. Prior to its official launch, a detailed mock run of the clinic’s functioning was conducted to ensure alignment with institutional standards, patient safety guidelines, and educational objectives. The project was authorized and implemented as part of the college’s strategic development of integrated, patient-centered dental care and clinical training. This program was established in a fully equipped dental clinic with ten functional dental units. To train the institutional clinical faculty as per the international standards of clinical skill assessment, the “Train the Trainers” training program, was initiated before the official functioning of CCD. A change in the rotational schedule of the internship program was devised, incorporating 12 weeks’ rotation in the CCD clinics, six weeks’ rotation in disciplines of Prosthodontics, Operative Dentistry & Endodontics, Oral & Maxillofacial Surgery and Periodontics, and four weeks each in disciplines of Oral Medicine & Diagnostics, Pedodontics and Orthodontics. A clinical audit was performed to evaluate the experiences and perceptions of dental interns, clinical instructors, and clinical management, within a newly established CCD program. Key performance indicators were also identified at the start of the program, which were monitored throughout the functioning of the clinic. These key performance indicators included clinical and professional competency of interns, patient satisfaction, timelines of patient treatment, performance of dental surgery assistants, maintenance of optimal clinical protocols, material usage, patient documentation, performance of the clinical instructors and hindrances faced by clinical management.

Foundation year interns were allocated to CCD Clinic on 3 monthly rotations. The permanent faculty designated for CCD Clinic was as follows: A Professor of Prosthodontics was deputed as Incharge CCD clinic with a Senior Registrar of Prosthodontics appointed as its Clinical Coordinator. The Interns were introduced to the new model on the orientation day. Hands-on training workshops were conducted for instructors and interns on cross-infection protocols, portfolio formulation, dental photography, dental radiology, periodontal assessment, preparation for direct and indirect restorations, basics of implantology with formative assessments, and multidisciplinary comprehensive treatment planning. Interactive sessions for soft skills and generic competencies were also conducted. The program was evaluated through 360-degree feedback to identify strengths and weaknesses for one academic year [8, 10, 20].

During a rotation of 12 weeks, eight groups of interns (3–4 interns in each group) were formed, with the allocation of one dental unit per group. Each member of a group of interns participated as an operator or an assistant alternatively.

A patient referral algorithm (Fig. 1) was developed. All patients presenting to the oral diagnostic department with multiple problems were given two referral options, for either treatment within the CCD clinic, under one roof with the same set of doctors, or via the traditional system of referral to multiple departments, as per the clinical presentations. The fee system of both models was kept identical. Patients were also referred to CCD clinics from departments with long appointment waiting lists. In the CCD clinic, cases were allocated to Interns’ groups, as per the case allocation criteria, (Table 2) adopted from the comprehensive care model practised at Harvard School of Dental Medicine, with cases being classified and allocated as per complexity [9]. At the time of presentation to the CCD Clinic, patients were classified according to these criteria and allotted to interns accordingly. An intern from each clinical batch was appointed as a clinical log management officer, for supervising manual and digital logging of active and inactive cases, adherence to appointment schedules, and missed appointment records with a weekly assessment of complete documentation by all groups to assess the performance of each group. The rationale for assigning duties to interns was to foster a healthy, responsible transition of interns from passive learners to active clinical participants with professional accountability at an early stage. Since these interns worked under supervision of the clinical coordinator, there were reduced operational gaps, without compromising patient safety or documentation precision. Periodic checks were conducted by the Incharge and clinical coordinator to monitor documentation and infection control compliance within CCD Clinic.

Rollover cases were carefully and equally transferred to all members of the new rotational batch to prevent any lag in treatment provision. During the 3 months rotation in CCD Clinic, interns were not only trained in clinical competencies but also guided and encouraged on establishing optimal patient-doctor communication and relationships. Since the rotation in CCD Clinic was longer than other clinical departments, interns had the chance to follow a case from start to finish, learning on dealing with a patient in real world scenarios. These interns were encouraged and facilitated to return to CCD Clinic, within designated time slots to complete or assist with any cases that were not completed within their clinical rotation time frame. Both the new and previous rotational batch interns were encouraged to work together on the completion of rollover cases. This was done to foster an environment of teamwork and long-term maintenance of a patient’s well-being.

A 360-degree feedback model was used to evaluate this program. Feedback was obtained from all stakeholders, including interns, clinical instructors, clinical management faculty, and patients [21]. This feedback was obtained from all patients having had treatment completed in the CCD Clinic. Feedback from interns, and their clinical instructors was obtained on four weekly basis, whereas the feedback from clinical management was obtained quarterly i.e., at the end of each rotation. These validated feedback questionnaires were adopted from another model of comprehensive care dentistry clinics, in practice within South Asia [22] (Table 5). Each questionnaire had a 3-point Likert scale with three domains of feedback. The first domain assessed orientation to the new system of comprehensive care with questions regarding the convenience of CCD Clinic as compared to departments, the convenience of getting treatment performed by the same doctor rather than multiple doctors in different departments, second domain pertained to clinical training, and third domain pertained to clinical management. In addition to the feedback questionnaires, assessment of the interns working in CCD Clinic was also done, by the clinical coordinator. Monthly assessments during three-month rotation within CCD were based upon professionalism, adherence to rules and protocols, clinical performance, and attendance. A score was allocated for satisfactory performance in a specific criterion or none for non-satisfactory performance. Average score of 3 assessment reports were compiled for each intern, with satisfactory performance being (>50%) display of professionalism, adherence to protocols, clinical procedural standards, and punctuality, whereas non-satisfactory (< 50%) score for the same criteria. These reports were made a part of the permanent record files of the interns, within the institution. The above-mentioned key performance indicators were monitored by obtaining feedback, and performing continuous assessments to identify the strengths and weaknesses of this system. Results

The CCD model was implemented in a clinical setting for one year to improve the professional competence of interns of the Institute of Dentistry, CMH Lahore Medical College, Pakistan. Multiple key performance indicators were assessed via 360° multi-source feedback by patients, clinical supervisors, clinical management, and interns, to assess the efficiency of this system. KPIs focussed on the evaluation of the timeline of treatment progression and completion as well as assessments of interns. All groups in the batch were allocated an average of 15 new patients. Approximately each group completed the treatment of seven patients during CCD rotation, whereas the rest were rolled over to the next rotational batch (Fig. 2).

The 360-degree feedback reflected that 91% of patients agreed to the convenience of comprehensive treatment provision in a single clinical facility. While 100% of patients expressed personal satisfaction with the provided treatment. About 73% of patients expressed adequate confidence with this treatment model (Fig. 3).

About 77% of interns were well-oriented to the comprehensive care dentistry model. All the interns (100%) graded it to be a sound system for learning comprehensive treatment planning skills. 62% were content with the clinical supervision, whereas 23% did not voice their opinion and 15% were not satisfied with the extent of clinical supervision. (Fig. 3) 91% of faculty involved in clinical supervision and management agreed on improved patient care via the CCD model whereas 100% agreed on improved competence of interns to plan, diagnose, and comprehensively treat the patients. 30% of clinical instructors reported a lack of consensus in treatment plans. 40% of faculty reported a dearth of opportunities to pursue additional academic responsibilities i.e. pursuance of research and continuing professional development activities owing to the increased workload in the CCD clinic.

In the one-year clinical audit, a cumulative procedural report was generated, evaluating the number of procedures performed monthly (Table 6). A total of 3060 procedures were performed in the CCD clinic from June 2023 to April 2024. The total number of patients treated during this time frame was 2516. The learning approach in this setting differed from the departmental model such that all of the cases treated in the CCD Clinic were completed under one roof. Each patient was treated by a specific group of doctors. The interns were able to treat a case in its entirety, rather than focusing on procedural treatment alone (Table 6).

Discussion

A comprehensive care dentistry program covers essential subjects such as basic sciences, dental materials, oral biology, and clinical dentistry, ensuring that students have a solid foundation in dentistry. Students engage in hands-on training through simulation labs and clinical practice, allowing them to develop essential clinical skills such as patient assessment, diagnosis, and treatment planning. This practical experience is crucial for building confidence and competence in real-world scenarios [23]. The CCD model allows students to learn complete patient care in real-world scenarios, with enhanced decision-making abilities [15, 17]. Case presentations with complete management plans approved by faculty members from multiple specialities improve prompt case analysis and critical thinking skills [1]. The comprehensive care model exposes dental students to supervised holistic approach to patient management in the earlier part of their educational years as compared to the traditional teaching model, which compels dental interns to experience the situation practically in their professional career, without any supervision [1–3, 7–10, 19, 20].

Institute of Dentistry, CMH LMC, Lahore, implemented the CCD model with the allocation of one independent clinical hall for comprehensive care dentistry for a new batch of interns. The clinical rotational schedule was modified to fulfil the number of PMDC required contact hours for internships in multiple specialties to achieve the desirable competencies [16]. Teaching faculty’s knowledge, commitment and standard of care play a pivotal role in the success of an education system [1, 13, 24]. In the present model, interns reported a lack of consensus in treatment planning amongst the supervising faculty. This finding corresponds with the findings reported by Khalifa et al. 1 in a study evaluating the efficacy of a comprehensive care system at the University of Sharjah, United Arab Emirates. They observed that diverse training backgrounds of faculty members lead to differences of opinion. To unify clinical training methodology, standardization and calibration of clinical instruction is recommended [1]. The present model in this review attempted this standardization via the “Train the Trainers” program, where faculty underwent hands-on sessions as per international guidelines of clinical evaluation. Increased practice of pertinent skills enhances the expertise of the specific procedure [16].

Clinical audits aim to identify the strengths and weaknesses of a new policy or system through gauging key performance indicators. The trends of data analysis indicate the extent, direction, and feasibility of the required change. A clinical audit of the comprehensive care dentistry clinic was performed after one complete year of its service to identify the challenges, strengths, and weaknesses of this system. The biggest challenge was the acceptance of this model of dental education within the dental fraternity. This problem was previously reported in multiple dental schools but eventually culminated in the wider application of this concept with designated clinical facilities for comprehensive care [1, 19]. The vision of health professional institutes must complement the changing trends in dental education to meet the needs of society. Strict adherence to old concepts and traditions may lead to a disastrous failure in the form of incompetent graduates, reflecting a downgrade in the quality of healthcare providers [1, 19, 20]. The role of apex bodies is pivotal to reset the perceptions of health professionals empathetically towards patients and to direct the institutional investments for accommodating the infrastructural requirements.

The involvement of multi-disciplinary specialists is essential to teach state of the art comprehensive care to aspiring students [1, 7]. However, a lack of consensus and faculty attendance was observed in treatment planning during clinical sessions and case presentation seminars. This challenge was previously reported in Macy’s study which indicated a lack of official instructions for implementation of new systems can be a plausible reason for their non-compliance. 1, 4, 14. These problems may be mitigated via calibration exercises and consensus meetings amongst the faculty members, but these may be hindered because of the resistance to acceptance of a shift from departmental model towards comprehensive care clinics. The breakdown of departmental boundaries is a change that is necessary albeit slow in acceptance. Literature reflects that generally, a new educational system requires 3 to 5 years for its full incorporation and acceptance [1, 4, 11, 12, 19]. The trends towards integrated teaching are rapidly progressing worldwide that mandates elimination of subject boundaries and mental blocks in certain areas, thus creating a congenial environment for adaptation to contemporary concepts [1, 4, 10, 12]. Successful implementation of this model requires decoding the preconceived notion of faculty through multiple awareness sessions and motivational discourse, prior to the initiation of such ventures, to reduce the untoward resistance during program execution.

Interns reported good orientation to the comprehensive care system via this model, with the development of better treatment planning skills and provision of care, for multi-modality treatments. The tutors supported regular case discussions, evaluations and critical appraisals to enhance students’ critical thinking and patient management abilities [2, 7, 10, 24]. This leads to improved student self-esteem which is an indicator of dental education excellence [1, 24]. This aspect was observed in the daily case presentation seminars depicting progressively increased interest and confidence of interns during their clinical rotation in CCD clinic.

A comprehensive care system presents a philosophy of patient care that places patients’ perspectives and satisfaction at its centre of focus [4]. It is essential for students to learn that patients are the focus of health care. The development of a long term, empathetic relationship with patients is of the utmost importance [25]. This skill is not amenable to be taught easily in a departmental model, whereas it is an inevitable part of the learning experience in comprehensive care clinics, because of the responsibility the doctor feels towards a patient that he or she must manage from start to finish [1, 4, 8, 15, 21, 22, 25]. This naturally results in the satisfaction of the primary stakeholder in healthcare i.e., the patient. Thus improved patient satisfaction is an observed outcome of this system of care in various dental schools globally [4, 11, 14]. Similarly, in the current model, patients were offered CCD management protocol at the time of their first visit to the oral diagnostics of general clinics, thus attaining their verbal consent, followed by written consent in CCD clinic. Patients reported satisfaction with the quality of treatment being provided, with approximately 91% of them considering treatment under a comprehensive care clinical system more convenient as compared to the departmental referral system.

CCD clinic was also observed for the treatment completion timelines. The Pennsylvania Experiment [4] documents reported reduced timelines in comprehensive care settings for treatment completion by students as compared to traditional settings, due to patient-centred protocols. Holmes et al. also reported that students train productively in a comprehensive care system, without strict quota requirements [2, 24]. The 360-degree feedback evaluation reported that the clinical space was inadequate, as per the patient inflow and number of interns at one time. A comprehensive care system requires funds allocation for adequate infrastructural development [2, 4, 11]. Designated faculty members are mandatory for quality assurance for an optimal student-to-supervisor ratio. The overburdening of appointed faculty members with insufficient time to pursue their own academic and research activities can hamper their professional growth and development. Lack of independent infrastructure and insufficient faculty members can hamper the acquisition of this model by already established dental schools.

Keeping in view the advantages and hindrances as observed by the clinical audits within the one year running of Comprehensive Care Dentistry Clinic in a dental school in Pakistan, another feasible model of this system of education was devised to address the problems identified, which may be applicable to other dental colleges with more limited resources. The second model can be implemented without the provision of a designated clinical facility. Two operatories within each departmental clinical hall can be fully equipped with the required armamentarium of general dentistry. A single case for comprehensive assessment and management to the first clinical rotational batch allocated to that specific department shall be allocated to each student, within their respective clinical department. Allocated cases shall be supervised by the identified departmental faculty along with the collaborative faculty of other specialities, designated for comprehensive management cases’ consultation, as per the case mix type.

Operators shall perform patients under a comprehensive care model, within the departments throughout the year, on the designated units within those departments, within given time slots. The cases shall be presented weekly with complete documentation and records to the treatment planning seminars for approval at initiation and treatment completion. This model may prove to be beneficial in encouraging support from all disciplines and improving its acceptance since the CCD dental units will be present within the departmental clinical halls. It may also reduce the workload of designated faculty, providing them with the requisite opportunity to pursue research and academic endeavours that are essential for the growth of institutes.

Limitations

This study was conducted for the comprehensive care dentistry model of education in a single, private dental school. For more widespread assessment and applicability of this educational model, assessments may be conducted at multi-institutional level. Different models of this same concept which may be being practices in various public and private sector dental schools may be compared for feasibility and applicability. Additionally, this audit reports the findings of one year’s operational framework. An audit that is conducted over the span of years may highlight challenges and strengths of this model which may be very valuable in establishing long term feasibility of this model in dental schools, particularly in resource limited countries like Pakistan. Our study was conducted within the framework of a comprehensive care dentistry model in a single private dental school, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. To ensure broader applicability, future studies should adopt a multi-institutional approach, incorporating both public and private sector dental schools. Comparing different adaptations of the comprehensive care model across varied institutional settings may help identify its feasibility, scalability, and contextual challenges. Additionally, this audit reflects only one year of implementation, which may not fully capture the evolving strengths and weaknesses of the model. Longitudinal audits spanning multiple years would provide richer insights into its sustainability, long-term impact on student learning, and institutional integration. This is particularly important for resource-limited contexts such as Pakistan, where economic and infrastructural constraints can significantly influence the feasibility of adopting innovative educational frameworks. Beyond these limitations, practical recommendations emerge from our findings. Faculty development initiatives should emphasize assessment literacy and strategies to overcome reluctance in failing underperforming students. Institutional policies must also foster a culture of transparency and accountability, reducing the stigma associated with reporting poor performance. Finally, collaboration with regulatory authorities such as PMDC and UHS can ensure alignment between educational reforms and accreditation requirements, thereby strengthening the overall validity and acceptance of such models.

Conclusion

The present study suggested the need to revise dental education modes from discipline-driven numerical requirements to a patient-centred comprehensive care curriculum for the timely professional grooming of dental graduates. CCD positively influences the quality of student learning as well as patient care. Students and patients experience a better learning and treatment experience respectively within this system. In a resource limited country like Pakistan, there are still hindrances in resources and acceptance within faculty for this system but with perseverance and modifications as per feasibility of each dental school, this system may prove to be very successful. Curricular modification may be required for inculcation of this system, within evolving dental education.
