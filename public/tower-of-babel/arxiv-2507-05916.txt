On the Effectiveness of Methods and Metrics for Explainable AI in Remote Sensing Image Scene Classification
Jonas Klotz, Tom Burgert, and Begüm Demir

Abstract
The development of explainable artificial intelligence (xAI) methods for scene classification problems has attracted great attention in remote sensing (RS). Most xAI methods and the related evaluation metrics in RS are initially developed for natural images considered in computer vision (CV), and their direct usage in RS may not be suitable. To address this issue, in this paper, we investigate the effectiveness of explanation methods and metrics in the context of RS image scene classification. In detail, we methodologically and experimentally analyze ten explanation metrics spanning five categories (faithfulness, robustness, localization, complexity, randomization), applied to five established feature attribution methods (Occlusion, LIME, GradCAM, LRP, and DeepLIFT) across three RS datasets. Our methodological analysis identifies key limitations in both explanation methods and metrics. The performance of perturbation-based methods, such as Occlusion and LIME, heavily depends on perturbation baselines and spatial characteristics of RS scenes. Gradient-based approaches like GradCAM struggle when multiple labels are present in the same image, while some relevance propagation methods (LRP) can distribute relevance disproportionately relative to the spatial extent of classes. Analogously, we find limitations in evaluation metrics. Faithfulness metrics share the same problems as perturbation-based methods. Localization metrics and complexity metrics are unreliable for classes with a large spatial extent. In contrast, robustness metrics and randomization metrics consistently exhibit greater stability. Our experimental results support these methodological findings. Based on our analysis, we provide guidelines for selecting explanation methods, metrics, and hyperparameters in the context of RS image scene classification. The code of this work will be publicly available at https://git.tu-berlin.de/rsim/xai4rs .



I
Introduction

The development of accurate methods for remote sensing (RS) image scene classification is one of the most important research topics in RS. In recent years,
deep learning (DL)
has emerged as the dominant approach for scene classification problems due to the high capability of DL models to extract and exploit the complex spatial and spectral content of RS images. RS image scenes can contain multiple land-use land-cover (LULC) classes and thus can be simultaneously associated with different class labels (i.e. multi-labels). Accordingly, the existing models are in general capable of assigning multiple LULC class labels to each RS image scene in an archive
[
1

2

Despite their success,

[

[

Recently, Höhl et al.

II
Explanation methods for RS image scene classification

In this section, we methodologically analyze the explanation methods to address the unique challenges posed by the
RS
images.
Most
xAI
research has focused on natural images, which differ significantly from
RS
images
[
19
]
. To this end, we first analyze a range of explanation methods commonly applied in
RS
scene classification regarding these differences.
We focus on local post-hoc explanation methods that generate feature attribution maps, as they are most commonly used in
RS

[
8
]
.
Feature attribution methods can be further grouped into backpropagation-based and perturbation-based methods
[
37
]
. Perturbation-based methods alter model inputs to observe changes in the model output, while backpropagation methods propagate values from the model output to the input space, typically gradients or relevancy scores. We consider three categories: i)
perturbation-based
methods, ii)
relevancy-propagation-based
methods and iii)
gradient-propagation-based
methods.
To provide a comprehensive overview, our analysis includes five popular feature attribution methods covering all categories:
1) two perturbation-based methods (
Occlusion

[
9
]
,
LIME

[
10
]
);
2) two relevancy-propagation-based methods (
LRP

[
11
]
,
DeepLIFT

[
12
]
) and
3) one gradient-propagation-based method (
GradCAM

[
13
]
).
For each category, we first provide a general description, followed by a definition of the explanation methods and a discussion of the limitations considering the differences between
RS
and natural images.

Let us consider a
RS
image scene classification problem where a DL model
f
is trained on a
RS
image archive
\bm{X}_{\mathrm{tr}}
made up of
N
pairs:
(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
for single‐label classification, or
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
for multi‐label classification. For simplicity, we omit the single-label notation.
Each image
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
represents a scene captured by an airborne or satellite sensor, where
C
denotes the spectral bands and
H,W
correspond to spatial dimensions. The model
f
maps an input image
\bm{x}
to a predicted set of labels:

f(\bm{x})=\hat{\bm{y}}

(1)

where
\hat{\bm{y}}
represents the predicted class probabilities or binary assignments.
To interpret the reasoning behind the prediction of a model
f
for an input
\bm{x}
, post-hoc explanation methods, such as feature attribution methods
\Phi
, can be applied:

\Phi(\bm{x},f,\hat{\bm{y}})=\hat{\bm{e}},

(2)

where
\Phi
distributes relevancy scores
r
across
\bm{x}
, resulting in an attribution map
\hat{\bm{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
. In practice,
\hat{\bm{e}}
is often aggregated across spectral bands.
For simplicity, we omit explicit parameterization unless necessary.

II A
Perturbation-based methods

II A 1
Background

Perturbation-based explanation methods explain model predictions by measuring changes in the output between the original and perturbed inputs.
Let
\mathbb{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
be the space of valid inputs (e.g., images) and let
\Theta
be a set of perturbation parameters (e.g., masks, noise levels, patch locations). A perturbation function
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
is defined as:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}:\mathbb{X}\times\Theta\;\to\;\mathbb{X},

(3)

such that for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{X}
and any
\theta\in\Theta
, the output

\tilde{\bm{x}}\;=\;\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},\theta)

(4)

remains in
\mathbb{X}
. In other words,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},\theta)
modifies
\bm{x}
according to the perturbation parameter
\theta
, while still preserving the overall structure such that
\tilde{\bm{x}}
is a valid point in the same space.
We define the category of perturbation-based methods
\Phi_{\text{Perturbation}}
as:

\Phi_{\text{Perturbation}}(f,\bm{x},\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}},\theta)=|f(\bm{x})-f(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},\theta))|.

(5)

Common perturbation functions are blurring
[
38
]
, introducing noise
[
30
]
, or replacing parts of the image
[
39
]
.
For perturbation-based explanation methods, we consider
Occlusion

[
9
]
and
LIME

[
10
]
.

The
Occlusion
method
[
9
]
is defined as follows.
Let each
\theta\in\Theta
specify a region (mask)
\Omega\subseteq\{1,\dots,H\}\times\{1,\dots,W\}
and a constant perturbation baseline
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
We define the perturbation function:

\bigl[\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},\theta)\bigr]_{j,k}\;=\;\begin{cases}\alpha,&\text{if }(j,k)\in\Omega,\\
\bm{x}_{j,k},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6)

Then, we calculate the prediction certainty difference
\Delta s_{c}
as:

\Delta s_{c}(\theta)=f_{c}(\bm{x})-f_{c}\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},\theta)\bigr),

(7)

for each mask
\Omega
. A large positive
\Delta s_{c}(\theta)
means that the perturbation significantly lowered the prediction certainty of the model for class
c
, suggesting that the perturbed features are important. Then, we aggregate the
\Delta s_{c}(\theta)
values to produce an attribution map that assigns high importance to the regions whose occlusion most reduces the model prediction certainty for class
c
.
Thus, the
Occlusion
method
\Phi_{\text{Occlusion}}
is defined as:

\Phi_{\text{Occlusion}}(j,k)\;=\;\frac{1}{\bigl|\{\theta:(j,k)\in\Omega\}\bigr|}\sum_{\theta:(j,k)\in\Omega}\Delta s_{c}(\theta).

(8)

The
LIME

\Phi_{\mathrm{LIME}}
method explains a black-box model
f
by locally approximating it with a simple, interpretable model trained on binary-encoded perturbations of the original input.
Let
S=\{S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{M}\}
be a segmentation of
\bm{x}
into
M
superpixels. Denote by
\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{M}
a binary vector that indicates which superpixels are
present
(1) or
not present
(0). We define the perturbation function:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}:\mathbb{X}\times\{0,1\}^{M}\;\to\;\mathbb{X},

(9)

such that, for each
\theta=\bm{z}
, where:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},\bm{z})_{j,k}\;=\;\begin{cases}[\bm{x}]_{j,k},&\text{if }(j,k)\in S_{m}\text{ ,}\bm{z}_{m}=1,\\
\alpha,&\text{if }(j,k)\in S_{m}\text{ , }\bm{z}_{m}=0,\end{cases}

(10)

and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
is a constant perturbation baseline. By
toggling
each superpixel via
\bm{z}_{m}\in\{0,1\}
, we create perturbed samples in the neighborhood of
\bm{x}
.

LIME
estimates a simple, interpretable model
g
(e.g., a sparse linear model) that approximates
f
on these perturbations near
\bm{x}
. Formally, let:

\Phi_{\mathrm{LIME}}(\bm{x})\;=\;\operatorname{argmin}_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\Bigl[\mathcal{L}\bigl(f,g,\Pi_{x}\bigr)\;+\;\Upsilon(g)\Bigr].

(11)

Here,
\Upsilon(g)
is a regularization term, such as the number of nonzero weights in a linear model or the depth of a decision tree,
\mathcal{L}
is a loss function, and
\Pi_{x}(\tilde{\bm{x}})
denotes a proximity measure that quantifies the distance from a perturbation
\tilde{\bm{x}}
to
\bm{x}
.

II A 2
Limitations

A key component of perturbation-based methods is the perturbation baseline, which is intended to represent the absence of a feature. However, defining an appropriate perturbation baseline is non-trivial. Many
RS
studies simply employ a scalar value
\alpha
, often set to zero, such as in the quantitative evaluation of Kakageorgiou et al.
[
26
]
. However, perturbations with a scalar perturbation baseline can yield
Out-of-Distribution (OoD)
samples
[
40
]
. This is problematic because
DL
models are prone to unreliable predictions when the input does not reside in their learned distribution
[
41
]
. An example case is shown in Fig.
2(a)
, which depicts an RGB image from the
DeepGlobe Multi-Label (DG-ML)
dataset. A rectangular patch is perturbed with the
Occlusion
method, using a black perturbation baseline (
\alpha=0
). Despite occluding a part of the class, the prediction of the model for the class
agricultural land
increased from
f_{c}(\bm{x})=0.90
on the original input to
f_{c}(\tilde{\bm{x}})=0.99
after the occlusion. Even when most of the pixels from the original image are perturbed, as shown in Fig.
2(b)
, the model predicts with high certainty
f_{c}(\tilde{\bm{x}})=0.38
, highlighting the unpredictable behavior introduced by a poorly chosen perturbation baseline.

This challenge is amplified in multispectral
RS
images, where different spectral bands (e.g., near-infrared or thermal) capture distinct information. Common baselines, such as all-zero vectors, can be unsuitable because certain bands rarely contain zero values in operational conditions. For example, in thermal imagery, a zero-valued perturbation baseline is not meaningful, as it does not reflect any typical temperature reading. Similarly, for a near-infrared band, some level of infrared radiation is almost always present, making an all-zero replacement an inadequate representation of the absence of a feature. Consequently, selecting a meaningful perturbation baseline requires more sophisticated approaches that account for the different spectral bands.

Furthermore, the regions within each RS image are associated with different LULC classes, and unlike in natural images, background content does not exist. Let us consider the case where we have a LULC class
c
present in the image and let
A_{c}(\bm{x})
represent the total number of pixels in
\bm{x}
corresponding to class
c
(which can be interpreted as the discrete spatial extent of class
c
within the image).
If the area covered by the perturbation mask
A(\Omega)
of the
Occlusion
method is smaller than
A_{c}(\bm{x})
, the perturbation may fail to obscure a sufficient number of relevant pixels to significantly decrease the prediction certainty. This is depicted in Fig.
2(a)
, where we see a rectangular region that is occluded, which does not reduce
f_{c}(\bm{\tilde{x}})
.
This limitation is less pronounced in natural images, where discriminative features are often unique (e.g., silhouette, object parts). In such cases, occluding typically leads to a noticeable reduction in prediction certainty even if
A_{c}(\bm{x})>A(\Omega)
.

A similar problem occurs for the
LIME
method, where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}
partitions
\bm{x}
in different superpixels and partially occludes them. Here, the prediction remains unchanged, unless most of the superpixels are replaced with the perturbation baseline (see Fig
2(b)
), resulting in a linear model trained primarily on positive class predictions.
This imbalance causes the estimated weights of the linear model to be unreliable, leading to an inaccurate interpretation of feature importance.
Since no single superpixel significantly alters the decision of the model,
LIME
assigns almost equal importance to all superpixels.
However, this relevance is lower than it should be, as the method underestimates the contribution of discriminative features due to the regularization term, potentially leading to wrong interpretations of the reasoning of the model.
Additionally, the more pixels are replaced by the perturbation baseline, the higher the proximity measure
\Pi_{x}
due to the large distance from the original image. As a result, individual pixels are incorrectly attributed as less relevant.

II B
Gradient-propagation-based methods

II B 1
Background

Gradient-propagation-based methods utilize gradient information to generate visual explanations for deep neural network predictions. The gradient-propagation-based explanation method we examine is
GradCAM

[
13
]
, which incorporates gradient information flowing into any convolutional layer, typically the last one due to its high-level semantics and spatial information retention. The attribution map for a specific class
c
is computed by first capturing the gradients of the class score
Y^{c}
with respect to the activations
A^{p}
of convolutional layer
l
. The importance weights
\omega^{c}_{p}
for each feature map are determined by globally averaging the gradients over all spatial locations
(j,k)
:

\omega^{c}_{p}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial Y^{c}}{\partial A^{p}_{j,k}}.

(12)

These weights represent a partial linearization of the network and denote the importance of each feature map
p
for the target class
c
. A weighted combination of these maps followed by a ReLU ensures that only positive influences on the class prediction are visualized:

\Phi_{\mathrm{GradCAM}}^{c}=\text{ReLU}\left(\sum_{p}\omega^{c}_{p}A^{p}\right).

(13)

II B 2
Limitations

Gradient-propagation-based methods exhibit limitations when applied to
RS
imagery, particularly when multiple LULC classes occur in a scene. For instance, in a
RS
image containing five airplanes,
GradCAM
localizes only a single instance
[
42
]
, underscoring its inability to resolve multiple objects of interest at varying scales
[
43
,
44
]
. This failure originates from the reliance of
GradCAM
on a global gradient averaging mechanism, which collapses spatial gradient information into a single channel-wise importance weight. For two distinct objects
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
, activating disjoint regions
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
in
A^{p}
, the gradients
\nabla_{1}
and
\nabla_{2}
are:

\frac{\partial Y^{c}}{\partial A_{j,k}^{p}}=\begin{cases}\nabla_{1},&\forall(j,k)\in R_{1}\\
\nabla_{2},&\forall(j,k)\in R_{2}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(14)

Assuming equal spatial extents (
\lvert R_{1}\rvert=\lvert R_{2}\rvert=N
) and total positions
Z=u\times v
,
GradCAM
computes the channel weight as:

\omega_{p}^{c}=\frac{N}{Z}\,\bigl(\nabla_{1}+\nabla_{2}\bigr).

(15)

When
\nabla_{1}\neq\nabla_{2}
, the larger gradient contributes more to the sum, so the computed weight is more reflective of the larger gradient. However, because
\omega_{p}^{c}
is a scalar applied uniformly across the feature map, the distinct spatial contributions from
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are merged. Consequently, the resulting attribution map
\Phi_{\mathrm{GradCAM}}^{c}
loses the spatial granularity necessary to differentiate between the contributions of multiple objects.

II C
Relevancy-propagation-based methods

II C 1
Background

Relevancy-propagation-based methods, such as
LRP
or
DeepLIFT
, explain the predictions of classifiers by back-propagating a relevancy score throughout the network, to attribute importance to each input feature. For relevancy-propagation-based explanation methods, we consider
DeepLIFT

[
12
]
and
LRP

[
11
]
.

DeepLIFT

[
12
]
attributes the difference in the outputs
f_{c}(\bm{x})-f_{c}\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}\right)
on the differences between the input image
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}
and a perturbation baseline (reference)
\tilde{\bm{x}}\;=\;\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},\theta)
. Specifically,
DeepLIFT
runs forward propagation for both
\bm{x}
and
\tilde{\bm{x}}
inputs. Then, it stores the difference of the weighted activation
\Delta z_{s,t}=w_{s,t}^{(l,l+1)}\left(\bm{x}_{s}^{l}-\tilde{\bm{x}}^{l}_{s}\right)
, where
\bm{x}_{s}^{l}
and
\tilde{\bm{x}}^{l}_{s}
are the activations of the neuron
s
in the
l
layer and
w_{s,t}^{(l,l+1)}
is the weight between neuron
s
and neuron
t
in the next layer
l+1
. Finally, the backpropagation of the difference in the outputs to the final input contributions
\Phi_{\text{DeepLift}}
is achieved with the following rules:

r_{t}^{(L)}=\begin{cases}f_{c}(\bm{x})-f_{c}(\tilde{\bm{x}}),&t=c,\\
0,&t\neq c,\end{cases}

(16)

and

r_{s}^{(l)}=\sum_{t}\frac{\Delta z_{s,t}}{\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}}\Delta z_{s^{\prime},t}}\;r_{t}^{(l+1)},

(17)

and finally

\Phi_{\text{DeepLift}}(f_{c},\bm{x})_{s}=r_{s}^{(1)}\,,

(18)

where
L
is the output layer and
r_{s}^{(1)}
is the contribution of each feature on the input layer. For further propagation rules, we refer the reader to
[
12
]
.

Layer Relevance Propagation (LRP)

[
11
]
assigns a relevance score
r
to each input feature of a network by propagating the output of the model back through the network to the input layer.
LRP
interprets the neural network as a flow graph, redistributing prediction relevance backwards through the layers using specific propagation rules.

Relevance scores
r
for a given layer
l
are propagated to the neurons in the preceding layer as follows:

r_{s}^{(l)}\;=\;\sum_{t}\frac{z_{s,t}}{\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}}z_{s^{\prime},t}}\;r_{t}^{(l+1)}\,,

(19)

where
z_{s,t}
represents the contribution of neuron
s
in layer
l
to the activation of neuron
t
in layer
l+1
, ensuring that total relevance is conserved across layers.
LRP
employs specific propagation rules to distribute relevance back to input features depending on the network architecture and task; see Montavon et al.
[
45
]
for details.
For the final layer, the relevance
r_{t}^{(L)}
is initialized as the output score for the target class
c
. The
LRP
method
\Phi_{\text{LRP}}
for attributing the input space is defined as:

\Phi_{\text{LRP}}\bigl(f_{c},\bm{x}\bigr)_{s}\;=\;r_{s}^{(1)}.

(20)

II C 2
Limitations

As
DeepLIFT
calculates relevance by comparing the output of a model for the input
\bm{x}
and a reference input
\tilde{\bm{x}}
, expressed as
f_{c}(\bm{x})-f_{c}\left(\tilde{\bm{x}}\right)
, it encounters similar issues to perturbation-based methods (see Section
II A 2
).
Sturmfels et al.
[
46
]
investigate the impact of various reference baselines for the
Integrated Gradients
method, which also employs a
difference-from-reference
approach analogous to
DeepLIFT
. Their study visually examines reference baselines such as Gaussian blur, uniform values, blurring, maximum distance, and black baselines. They find that the selection of an appropriate baseline requires domain-specific knowledge to ensure it accurately represents missingness within the data domain. This challenge is amplified in
RS
image scene classification due to the availability of various spectral bands in the data.

A central property of
LRP
and related relevancy-propagation-based methods such as
Integrated Gradients

[
47
]
is
relevance conservation
: the total relevance assigned to the input for class
c
equals the network output,
\sum_{j}r_{j}^{c}=f_{c}(\bm{x})

[
45
]
.
In natural images, discriminative information is typically concentrated within the object of interest.
In contrast,
RS
scenes usually contain several LULC classes, so that a single class may occur in multiple, spatially disconnected regions; the discriminative information is therefore distributed rather than localized.
Because the overall relevance budget is fixed, two classes that yield comparable output scores but occupy differently sized areas receive different relevance densities: the larger region is attributed a lower per-pixel relevance than the smaller region.
Fig.
3
illustrates this issue using an image from the
DG-ML
dataset containing differently sized two classes:
agricultural land
(
c_{\text{major}}
) and
urban land
(
c_{\text{minor}}
).
As the outputs of the model for both classes are similar
(f_{c_{\text{major}}}(\bm{x})\approxeq f_{c_{\text{minor}}}(\bm{x}))
the maximum relevance to be propagated by LRP is similar.
The higher number of relevant pixels for
c_{\text{major}}
results in lower attributed relevance per pixel compared to
c_{\text{minor}}
.
The
r^{c}_{j}
for
c_{\text{major}}
span values in the interval
[0,0.02]
, while the
r^{c}_{j}
for
c_{\text{minor}}
range within
[0,0.18]
.
As attributions are typically normalized between
0
and
1
, this can produce attribution maps that are highly sensitive to outliers.

III
Explanation metrics for RS image scene classification

In this section, we first provide a brief overview of the categorization of explanation metrics. We then introduce selected explanation metrics from different categories and discuss their suitability to estimate the quality of explanations for
RS
image scene classification.
Explanation metrics offer a quantitative way to assess the quality of explanation methods. An explanation can be defined as a
“presentation […] in human-understandable terms”

[
22
]
. Because this definition centers on subjective human interpretation, it inherently lacks a single, absolute ground truth. Therefore, it is not possible to estimate an explanation error, as one would estimate the performance error of a
DL
model
[
24
]
. Furthermore, explanation quality depends on more than the explanation method itself.
When the decision-making process of a model is erroneous, even correctly derived explanations can appear inconsistent or counterintuitive, since explanations inherently reflect the internal logic of the model
[
48
]
.
To address these challenges, researchers have proposed metrics across several categories that capture distinct aspects of explanation quality.
In our study, we consider five different categories of explanation metrics:
Faithfulness
,
Robustness
,
Localization
,
Complexity
and
Randomization
.
Formally, the quality of an explanation is assessed using a metric
\Psi
, which evaluates the effectiveness of an explanation method:

\Psi(\Phi,\bm{x},f,\hat{\bm{y}})=\hat{q},

(21)

where
\hat{q}
is a scalar quality score. For simplicity, we omit explicit parameterization unless necessary.

III A
Faithfulness

III A 1
Background

The first category that we consider is faithfulness, which measures how well explanations reflect the actual decision-making process of the model. A faithful explanation accurately reflects why the model made a specific prediction. Metrics in this category assess this alignment by evaluating whether features identified as important, significantly influence the prediction of the model. Let us consider
\bm{x}
as the input image and
V\subseteq[H]\times[W]
a set of pixels to remove. We define the perturbation function
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},V)

which replaces each feature in
V
with a predefined baseline value. The general faithfulness category
\Psi_{\mathrm{F}}(\bm{x},V)
is defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{F}}(\bm{x},V)=\bigl\lvert\,f(\bm{x})-f\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},V)\bigr)\bigr\rvert.

(22)

Perturbations are commonly applied to the most relevant pixels first (
MoRF
) or the least relevant ones first (
LeRF
).

To evaluate explanation methods for
RS
image scene classification, we consider two faithfulness metrics: i)
Faithfulness Estimate (FE)

[
28
]
, which measures the correlation between feature importance and prediction change, and ii)
Iterative Removal Of Features (IROF)

[
29
]
, which assesses prediction certainty decay when removing the most relevant segments.

Faithfulness Estimate (FE)

[
28
]
quantifies the alignment between attribution magnitude and model sensitivity by measuring the correlation between the total attribution on the top-
K
pixels and the resulting drop in prediction when those pixels are removed. Formally, we define the
FE
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{FE}}
as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{FE}}=\mathrm{corr}_{V:|V|=K}\Bigl(\sum_{(j,k)\in V}\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k},\,f(\bm{x})-f\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},V)\bigr)\Bigr).

(23)

Here,
\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}
denotes the attributed relevancy at pixel
(j,k)
, and
V\subseteq\{1,\dots,H\}\times\{1,\dots,W\}
is the set of the
K
pixel indices with the largest attribution values. The function
\mathrm{corr}
denotes the Pearson correlation coefficient. To compute
\Psi_{\mathrm{FE}}
, one first ranks all pixels by
\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}
, selects the top-
K
indices
V
, and then correlates the summed attributions on
V
with the change
f(\bm{x})-f\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},V)\bigr)
. A higher value of
\Psi_{\mathrm{FE}}
indicates that the explanation method attributes the behavior of the model, i.e., i.e., removing features deemed important by the method results in a significant drop in prediction certainty.

Iterative Removal Of Features (IROF)

[
29
]
measures how the prediction certainty changes when the most relevant features are removed but it considers the
K
most relevant segments rather than pixels. Let us define
\{S_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
as a partition of the image
\bm{x}
into
M
disjoint segments and sort them by their relevance, that:

\frac{1}{|S_{1}|}\sum_{(j,k)\in S_{1}}\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}\;\geq\;\cdots\;\geq\;\frac{1}{|S_{M}|}\sum_{(j,k)\in S_{M}}\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}.

(24)

For each
\kappa=0,1,\dots,K
, define the cumulative removal set
V_{\kappa}\;=\;\bigcup_{m=1}^{\kappa}S_{m},
and the corresponding perturbed input
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},V_{\kappa}).

Finally, the IROF score
\Psi_{\mathrm{IROF}}
for
\bm{x}
is obtained by normalizing each perturbed model output by the original confidence:

\Psi_{\mathrm{IROF}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{\kappa=0}^{K}\frac{f\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x},V_{\kappa})\bigr)}{f(\bm{x})}\,.

(25)

A higher
\Psi_{\mathrm{IROF}}
indicates that removing highly attributed segments causes a rapid drop in prediction confidence, and thus reflects a more faithful explanation.

III A 2
Limitations

Faithfulness metrics inherit several limitations from perturbation-based explanation methods, as both rely on input perturbations to evaluate changes in the prediction certainty of a model. Consequently, they exhibit similar issues to those discussed in Section
II A 2
. For example, like perturbation-based methods, most faithfulness metrics require a carefully chosen baseline to avoid distribution shifts that can affect the predictions of the model.

In addition, the FE metric relies on a strong assumption: that removing a small number of high-attributed pixels will lead to a measurable change in model output. This assumption implicitly presumes that class-relevant information is concentrated in a small subset of pixels.
However, RS scenes often contain multiple spatially disjoint regions associated with the same LULC class. These regions exhibit redundancy, as similar class-relevant features are repeated across the scene.
In such cases, removing a small number of pixels does not produce a meaningful change in prediction, and the attribution sum remains nearly constant across different pixel subsets. As a result, the Pearson correlation used by FE may become numerically unstable, since both numerator and denominator can approach zero, leading to undefined or misleading scores even for faithful explanations.

In contrast to FE, some faithfulness metrics, such as IROF, evaluate the behavior of the model over a sequence of increasingly perturbed inputs. For these trajectory-based metrics, the order in which features are removed plays a critical role in shaping the evaluation outcome. To illustrate this, let us consider a scene in which a class
c_{\text{major}}
occupies most of the image area, whereas a different class
c_{\text{minor}}
covers only a small region. In such images, perturbing pixels belonging to
c_{\text{major}}
do not significantly impact prediction certainty, as multiple pixels or regions contain similar predictive information. Consequently, metrics employing the
MoRF
strategy, which sequentially removes the most relevant pixels first, fail to capture meaningful changes in prediction certainty, as demonstrated by the slow decline of
f(\bm{x})
in Fig.
4
. In contrast, perturbations of the fewer, highly informative pixels associated with
c_{\text{minor}}
lead to a rapid decrease in prediction certainty. This discrepancy results in biased metric outcomes, incorrectly suggesting that explanations for
c_{\text{minor}}
are faithful, while explanations for
c_{\text{major}}
appear less faithful.
The
LeRF
strategy, which removes the least relevant pixels first, provides a better alternative to address this issue. By preserving the crucial predictive features of
c_{\text{minor}}
until later perturbation steps, the
LeRF
strategy ensures a stable prediction certainty and yields a more balanced evaluation of explanation methods.

III B
Robustness

III B 1
Background

The second category we consider is robustness, which assesses the stability of explanations under small input perturbations. Similar, to adversarial attacks on
DL
models
[
49
]
, explanations can also be fooled
[
50
,
51
]
. Thus, ensuring that the explanation is invariant to minimal perturbations in the input space is crucial for critical applications.
We require small perturbations
\delta
such that
\left\|\delta\right\|_{2}<\varepsilon
and expect
\left\|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x})-\bm{x}\right\|_{2}<\varepsilon
for a small positive constant
\varepsilon
, assuming that the model output approximately stayed the same
f(\bm{x})\approx f\left(\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{X}}(\bm{x})\right)
. The category
\Psi_{\mathrm{ROB}}
can be defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{ROB}}(\Phi,\bm{x},\varepsilon)=\max_{\|\delta\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon}\bigl\|\,\Phi(\bm{x})\;-\;\Phi(\bm{x}+\delta)\bigr\|_{2}.

(26)

To evaluate the robustness of explanation methods for
RS
image scene classification, we use i)
Average Sensitivity (AS)

[
30
]
, which measures sensitivity to small perturbations using a Monte Carlo-based approximation and ii)
Local Lipschitz Estimate (LLE)

[
28
]
, which estimates Lipschitz continuity to assess explanation stability under minor input changes.

Average Sensitivity (AS)

[
30
]
evaluates the sensitivity of the explanation method by perturbing samples using a Monte-Carlo-based approximation. The
AS
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{AS}}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathrm{AS}}=\text{avg}_{\bm{x}+\delta\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})\leq\varepsilon}\left[\frac{\|\Phi(\bm{x})-\Phi(\bm{x}+\delta)\|}{\|\bm{x}\|}\right],

(27)

where
\varepsilon
defines the radius of a discrete, finite-sample neighborhood around each input sample
\bm{x}
. This neighborhood denoted as
\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})
, includes all samples that are within a distance of
\varepsilon
from
\bm{x}
. A lower score is indicative of more robustness.

The
Local Lipschitz Estimate (LLE)

[
28
]
estimates the Lipschitz continuity of the explanation, which measures how much the explanation changes with respect to the input under a minor perturbation, meaning the prediction did not change. The
LLE
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{LLE}}
is defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{LLE}}=\max_{\bm{x}+\delta\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}(\bm{x})\leq\epsilon}\left[\frac{|\Phi(\bm{x})-\Phi(\bm{x}+\delta)|_{2}}{|\bm{x}-(\bm{x}+\delta)|_{2}}\right],

(28)

where lower values indicate less change concerning the change in input, which is desirable.

III B 2
Limitations

A key hyper-parameter in this metric category is the noise type. If the introduced noise does not naturally occur in the acquired images, it may generate
OoD
samples, reducing the reliability of the model and thereby affecting the validity of the explanations. When evaluating the robustness of explanation methods, it is important to introduce noise that does not result in
OoD
samples. This consideration also depends on the model architecture, as many models exhibit inherent resilience to certain types of noise. For instance, in
SAR
images, multiplicative noise such as speckle is pervasive
[
52
]
. In contrast, for multispectral images, noise is typically independent of the data and generally additive in nature, such as additive white Gaussian noise
[
53
,
54
]
.

III C
Localization

III C 1
Background

The third category we consider is localization, which measures the spatial accuracy of explanations by comparing them to reference maps or bounding boxes. This comparison can be quantified using a similarity measure
SIM
between the generated explanation map
\Phi(\bm{x})
and a pixel-level reference map
\bm{s}_{gt}
. The category
\Psi_{\mathrm{LOC}}
is defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{LOC}}(\Phi,\bm{x},\bm{s}_{gt},\text{SIM})=\text{SIM}\left(\Phi(\bm{x}),\bm{s}_{gt}\right),

(29)

where
SIM
can be a measure such as Intersection over Union, Pearson correlation, or
SSIM
depending on the nature of the task and reference map.

To evaluate the localization capabilities of explanation methods for
RS
image scene classification, we use i)
Top-K Intersection (TKI)

[
32
]
, which measures the fraction of top-ranked attributions within the pixel-level reference map, and ii)
Relevance Rank Accuracy (RRA)

[
31
]
, which assesses the concentration of high-relevance values within the reference map.

Top-K Intersection (TKI)
, as proposed by Theiner et al.
[
32
]
, measures the fraction of the
K
highest ranked attributions that lie on the binary reference mask
\bm{s}_{gt}
.
Let us define

I_{K}=\{\,i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{K}\}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{gt}=\{\,p\mid\bm{s}_{gt}(p)=1\},

(30)

where
I_{K}
is the set of the top-
K
ranked pixel indices and
S_{gt}
the set of true-positive pixels in the mask.
The intersection of these sets is:

\bm{s}_{1-K}=I_{K}\;\cap\;S_{gt}.

(31)

The
TKI
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{TKI}}
is then defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{TKI}}=\frac{\bigl|\bm{s}_{1-K}\bigr|}{K}.

(32)

This measures how many of the
K
most important features, according to
\bm{e}
, match the pixel‐level reference set
S_{gt}
. If all top-
K
pixels are within the reference mask, then
\Psi_{\mathrm{TKI}}=1
, therefore a higher score is better.

The
RRA

[
31
]
measures the concentration of high-intensity relevance values within the pixel-level reference map. Let
|S_{\text{tot}}|
be the size of the pixel-level reference map mask. Select the
K
highest relevance values, according to
\Phi(\bm{x})
, count how many of these values lie within the actual pixel locations, and divide by
|S_{\text{tot}}|
. This can be expressed as:

P_{\text{top }K}=\left\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{K}\mid R_{p_{1}}>R_{p_{2}}>\ldots>R_{p_{K}}\right\},

(33)

where
P_{\text{top }K}
is the set of pixels with relevance values
R_{p_{1}},R_{p_{2}},\ldots,R_{p_{K}}
sorted in decreasing order until the
K
-th pixel. The
RRA
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{RRA}}
is then computed as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{RRA}}=\frac{\left|P_{\text{top }K}\cap S_{\text{tot}}\right|}{|S_{\text{tot}}|}.

(34)

III C 2
Limitations

In
RS
scene classification, label dependencies arise when the presence or absence of one label influences the likelihood of another, which is a common scenario in
RS
where certain objects (e.g., ships) typically appear in specific environments (e.g., sea). These dependencies are not considered by localization metrics. Furthermore, the reliability of localization metrics heavily depends on accurate and up-to-date pixel-level reference maps. Obtaining such annotations is costly and prone to errors. Inaccuracies or obsolescence in the reference maps can compromise the localization reliability, potentially leading to incorrect assessments. Additionally, most localization metrics become unreliable, when the target class spans a large area of the input image. Let us consider the
TKI
metric in the scenario where we have a single label for the input image. Here, the binary reference mask
\mathbf{s}_{gt}
for the target label becomes a matrix of ones, encompassing all pixels. Thus,
\Psi_{\mathrm{TK}}
inherently achieves its maximum value of 1, regardless of the explanation
\bm{e}
. Since
\mathbf{s}_{gt}
includes every pixel, the intersection
\mathbf{s}_{1-K}=\mathbf{r}_{1-K}\cap\mathbf{s}_{gt}
reduces to
\mathbf{r}_{1-K}
itself, as all top-ranked indices in
\mathbf{r}_{1-K}
necessarily lie within
\mathbf{s}_{gt}
. Consequently, the numerator and denominator of
\Psi_{\mathrm{TK}}
both equal
K
, yielding
\Psi_{\mathrm{TK}}=1
. In this case, the metric cannot differentiate meaningful explanations from arbitrary ones.
This phenomenon is well-documented for simpler localization metrics
[
55
]
, such as the Pointing Game
[
56
]
. However, in
RS
scene classification this also creates challenges for more sophisticated metrics, as in most
RS
scene classification tasks, as classes tend to span larger areas.

III D
Complexity

III D 1
Background

The fourth category we consider is complexity, which quantifies the simplicity and interpretability of explanations, ensuring that they are useful for human understanding. These metrics evaluate properties such as sparsity, entropy, or other measures of complexity in the explanation map
\Phi(\bm{x})
. The category
\Psi_{\mathrm{CMP}}
is defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{CMP}}(\Phi,f,\bm{x},C)=C\left(\Phi(\bm{x})\right),

(35)

where
C
represents a complexity measure, chosen based on the context of interpretability.
To evaluate the complexity of explanations for
RS
image scene classification, we use i)
Sparseness (SP)

[
33
]
and ii)
Complexity (CO)

[
34
]
.

Sparseness (SP)

[
33
]
is a method for evaluating the sparsity of explanations and is defined as the Gini index of the explanation. It is calculated by summing the product of the ranks of the input features and their attributions and dividing by the sum of the attributions. The
SP
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{SP}}
is defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{SP}}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{H}\sum_{k=1}^{W}\bigl(2\,\rho_{j,k}-D-1\bigr)\,\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}}{D(D-1)\,\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{H}\sum_{k=1}^{W}\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}},

(36)

where
\hat{\bm{e}}_{j,k}
is the attribution of pixel
\bm{x}_{j,k}
,
\rho_{j,k}
is the rank of
\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}
among all
D
attributed input features when sorted in non‐decreasing order,
H
and
W
denote the image height and width, i.e.
D=H\times W
. A higher
\Psi_{\mathrm{SP}}
indicates a sparser explanation.

Complexity (CO)

[
34
]
is defined using the Shannon entropy calculation, which measures the amount of uncertainty or randomness in the attribution map. It is calculated by summing the product of the probabilities of the attributions and the logarithm of the probabilities of the attributions. The
CO
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{CO}}
is defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{CO}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{H}\sum_{k=1}^{W}p_{j,k}\,\ln\bigl(p_{j,k}\bigr),

(37)

where

p_{j,k}=\frac{|\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}|}{\sum_{a=1}^{H}\sum_{b=1}^{W}|\Phi(\bm{x})_{a,b}|}

(38)

is the fractional contribution of attribution
\Phi(\bm{x})_{j,k}
, and a higher
\Psi_{\mathrm{CO}}
indicates greater uncertainty (complexity). A uniformly distributed attribution would have the highest possible complexity score.

III D 2
Limitations

Explanation metrics in the complexity category generally assume that an explanation is simpler if it attributes relevance to fewer features for a given prediction. This assumption originates from explaining predictions for natural images, where a class is often defined by a few unique features. There, only a small fraction of the input features contribute to the final prediction. However, classification of
RS
images often relies more on spectral and texture features, a larger set of repetitive features is relevant, which is overlooked by complexity metrics.

A key limitation of the Gini-based
SP
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{SP}}
is its tendency to penalize explanations with uniformly distributed attributions. In a scenario where a class spans nearly the entire image, a comprehensive explanation assigns relevance to multiple regions exhibiting discriminative spectral or textural signals. This uniform distribution is interpreted by
\Psi_{\mathrm{SP}}
as high complexity, thereby misclassifying an accurate explanation as less concise. In contrast, an explanation for a minor class confined to a single region is favored with a low complexity score, even though such concentration does not necessarily improve interpretability.
This limitation is illustrated in Fig.
5
, which shows two explanations for a scene labeled as forest, a class that spans nearly the entire image.
The attribution map from the
Occlusion
method (Fig.
5(c)
) uniformly highlights all forest pixels, yielding a low
SP
value (
\Psi_{\mathrm{SP}}=0.08
). Although most of the input pixels are attributed as relevant, the map remains easy to interpret. By contrast, the LRP map (Fig.
5(d)
) concentrates relevance on a small, localized area, producing a higher sparsity score (
\Psi_{\mathrm{SP}}=0.47
). Here the
SP
metric favors a compact but misleading attribution, mistaking spatial concentration for interpretive simplicity.

III E
Randomization

III E 1
Background

The fifth category we consider is randomization, which tests the reliability of explanations by introducing randomness into model parameters or prediction targets. These metrics evaluate the consistency of explanations under such perturbations to ensure that explanations are not artifacts of specific parameter settings or configurations. The category
\Psi_{\mathrm{RAND}}
is defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{RAND}}(\Phi,f,\bm{x},\mathcal{R},\text{SIM})=\text{SIM}\left(\Phi(\bm{x}),\Phi(\bm{x},f,\mathcal{R})\right),

(39)

where
\mathcal{R}
represents a randomization operation (e.g., model parameter shuffling or target randomization) and
SIM
measures the similarity between the original explanation
\Phi(\bm{x},f)
and the explanation generated under the randomized condition
\Phi(\bm{x},f,\mathcal{R})
.
To evaluate the randomization robustness of explanation methods for
RS
image scene classification, we use i)
Efficient Model Parameter Randomization Test (MPRT)

[
57
]
, which measures explanation complexity under model parameter randomization and ii)
Random Logit (RL)

[
36
]
, which evaluates stability by comparing explanations for target and non-target classes using
Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)
.

Efficient Model Parameter Randomization Test (MPRT)

[
57
]
measures the relative rise in the complexity of the explanation
\Phi\bigl(\bm{x},f_{\mathcal{R}}\bigr)
from a fully randomized model
f_{\mathcal{R}}
for image
\bm{x}
. The
MPRT
metric
\Psi_{MPRT}
is defined as:

\Psi_{MPRT}=\frac{\xi\bigl(\Phi\bigl(\bm{x},f_{\mathcal{R}}\bigr)\bigr)-\xi\bigl(\Phi\bigl(\bm{x},f\bigr)\bigr)}{\xi\bigl(\Phi\bigl(\bm{x},f\bigr)\bigr)}.

(40)

Here,
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{D}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}
denotes a complexity function. To define
\xi
, a histogram‐entropy measure grounded in Shannon entropy is employed. Specifically, the vectorized attributions
\bm{e}=\{\,a_{j,k}\}_{j,k}

are placed into
B
distinct bins; for the
d
-th bin, the frequency
\eta_{d}
is computed and the corresponding normalized probability
\pi_{d}
is

\xi(\bm{e})=-\sum_{d=1}^{B}\pi_{d}\,\log\!\bigl(\pi_{d}\bigr),\quad\text{where}\quad\pi_{d}=\frac{\eta_{d}}{\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{B}\eta_{d^{\prime}}}\,.

(41)

The
Random Logit (RL)
metric
[
36
]
is defined as the
SSIM
over the explanation of the reference label and an explanation of non-target class
\bm{y}^{\prime}
. The
RS
metric
\Psi_{\mathrm{RL}}
is defined as:

\Psi_{\mathrm{RL}}=\operatorname{SSIM}\left(\Phi(\bm{x},f,\hat{\bm{y}}),\Phi\left(\bm{x},f,\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)\right),

(42)

where
\Phi(\bm{x},f,\hat{\bm{y}})
is the explanation generated for the prediction
\hat{\bm{y}}
and
\Phi\left(\bm{x},f,\bm{y}^{\prime}\right)
is the explanation generated for a non-target class
\bm{y}^{\prime}
. Lower values indicate that the explanations are not correlated, which is desirable.

III E 2
Limitations

Due to their minimal sensitivity to variations in input data characteristics, randomization metrics are also robust for
RS
image classification. Metrics like
MPRT
focus solely on how the randomization of model weights affects the outcomes of the explanation method, isolating the influence of the parameters the model has learned.
In contrast,
RL
measures the similarity between explanations for a target class and a non-target class. However, this metric may encounter challenges with label dependencies. For example, if the target class and the non-target class are both influenced by a shared present class, regions associated with the shared class may exhibit high relevance for both classes. In such cases, the attribution similarity can be disproportionately high for both target and non-target classes, because the shared class dominates the explanation outcomes.

IV
Dataset Description and Experimental Design

IV A
Dataset Description

The experiments are conducted using three datasets: 1)
BigEarthNet-S2 v2.0 (BEN-v2.0)

[

IV B
Evaluation Protocol

In the experiments, we evaluate explanation methods and metrics for feature attribution in
RS
image scene classification using the pipeline illustrated in Fig.
1
. We generate explanations by applying different feature attribution methods to the prediction of
DL
models for the considered
RS
datasets. Next, we estimate the quality of these explanations using the considered explanation metrics. Then, the reliability of these metrics is assessed by using the MetaQuantus
[
24
]
framework.

The MetaQuantus framework
[
24
]
considers that the quality of an explanation cannot be objectively measured due to the lack of ground truth. It tests the explanation metrics against two failure modes:
Noise Resilience (NR)
of a metric, which is the resilience against minor perturbations, and
Adversary Reactivity (AR)
, which is the reactivity of the metrics to disruptive perturbations.
A perturbation is considered minor when the predicted class label does not change and disruptive when the predicted class label changes.
To test against these failure modes, two consistency criteria
Intra-Consistency (IAC)
and
Inter-Consistency (IEC)
are utilized.

The IAC criterion measures whether the explanation scores remain similarly distributed after minor perturbations and whether they become dissimilarly distributed following disruptive perturbations. Let
\hat{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the unperturbed metric scores for
N
samples and
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
the corresponding perturbed scores for
k=1,\dots,K
. The IAC score is defined as:

\text{IAC}\;=\;\frac{1}{K}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\,d\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{q}},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}_{k}\bigr),

(43)

where
d
is the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and
0\leq\text{IAC}\leq 1
. A higher value indicates that unperturbed and perturbed metric scores are more similarly distributed.

The IEC criterion evaluates the consistency of metrics in preserving ranking order across multiple explanation methods.
Let
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times L}
be the unperturbed metric scores for
N
samples and
L
explanation methods, averaged over
K
perturbations.
Additionally, let
\overline{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times L}
be the corresponding averaged perturbed metric scores.
We define a binary ranking agreement matrix
\mathbf{U}^{t}\in[0,1]^{N\times L}
, where
t\in\{M,D\}
indicates a minor (
M
) or disruptive (
D
) perturbation.
The IEC score is defined as:

\text{IEC}=\frac{1}{N\times L}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{L}U_{i,j}^{t},

(44)

with
0\leq\text{IEC}\leq 1
.
The entries
U_{i,j}^{t}
capture how consistently the ranking of the metrics agrees between unperturbed
\bar{\mathbf{Q}}
and perturbed
\overline{\mathbf{Q}}^{\prime}
.
For minor perturbations (
t=M
) the ordering of the
L
methods for each sample must remain identical, whereas for disruptive perturbations (
t=D
) the unperturbed score must always ranked higher than its perturbed counterpart.

The
Meta-Consistency (MC)
score combines both intra- and inter-consistency criteria across different failure modes as:

\mathbf{MC}\;=\;\left(\frac{1}{|\bm{m}^{*}|}\right)\bm{m}^{*T}\bm{m},

(45)

where

\bm{m}\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{IAC}_{NR}\\
\mathrm{IAC}_{AR}\\
\mathrm{IEC}_{NR}\\
\mathrm{IEC}_{AR}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{m}^{*}\;=\;\mathbb{1}^{4},

(46)

with the all-ones vector representing an ideal explanation metric that achieves a value of 1 on all criteria. A metric with an
{MC}
score close to 1 indicates strong performance across all criteria tested with higher values reflecting greater overall robustness and reliability.
The MC score is averaged over two types of perturbation: model perturbation and input perturbation. In model perturbation, random noise is added to the parameters, whereas in input perturbation, it is added to the input data.

IV C
Experimental Setup

We have systematically evaluated ten explanation metrics across five widely used feature attribution methods on multi-label
RS
datasets (
BEN-v2.0
,
DG-ML
, and
FBP-ML
). To this end, we trained a ResNet-50 model on
BEN-v2.0
and ResNet-18 models on
DG-ML
and
FBP-ML
, subsequently computing explanations for the respective model predictions.
The ResNet architecture was selected due to its robust performance and demonstrated efficacy when paired with popular explanation methods.

We evaluated the quality of the explanation using the considered metrics and evaluated their reliability with MetaQuantus
[
24
]
on 512 randomly drawn samples, a sample size demonstrated to be sufficient in previous work
[
24
]
. The hyperparameter were set as in the original publication (
k=5
, three iterations). Gaussian noise was chosen for the input perturbation to preserve physical consistency, in line with the recommendations in
[
63
]
.
The choice of model depth does not substantially affect the outcome of the evaluation, as the performance of MetaQuantus has been shown to be largely invariant with respect to model architecture and depth
[
24
]
. For model training, we used the AdamW optimizer, initialized at a learning rate of
10^{-2}
and a weight decay of
0.9
. All experiments were performed on Nvidia H100 80GB GPUs.

We limit our analysis to five explanation methods, as prior work
[
24
]
indicates a minimal influence of the number of methods on the result scores.
Additionally, we used a Random baseline explanation method that samples values randomly from a uniform distribution
U(0,1)
.
For a multi-label case with a label set
\{1,…,K\}
, we apply the same method separately to each label
c\in\{1,…,K\}
to obtain a feature attribution map for each class
c
. Furthermore, as common in the literature, we summed the feature attributions over the channels.

The hyperparameters for the explanation methods were configured as follows. For
DeepLIFT
, the perturbation baseline value was set to
0
. For
GradCAM
, the last convolutional layer was analyzed and the explanations were upscaled by bilinear interpolation. The
LIME
method employed a Euclidean exponential kernel (kernel width 500) as a similarity measure, used linear regression as an interpretable model, and generated 15 superpixels using
SLIC
. For
LRP
, following
[
45
]
, the first third of the network was initialized with the LRP-
\gamma
rule, the second third with the LRP-
\epsilon
rule and the final third with the LRP-
0
rule. Lastly, the
Occlusion
method employed a sliding window of size
(50,50)
with strides of
(10,10)
across the channels and the perturbation baseline was set to
0
.
The quality of the explanations was assessed using 10 explanation metrics implemented via the Quantus framework
[
25
]
with default hyperparameters. Quantus is recognized as the most suitable framework for evaluating explanations
[
64
]
. The explanation metrics were computed over 1024 randomly drawn test samples from each dataset.
The explanation methods are implemented using the Captum framework
[
65
]
with modifications to Captum, Quantus, and MetaQuantus to support multi-label image scene classification.

image scene classification problems, and ii) evaluate the effectiveness of feature attribution methods based on the most reliable explanation metrics.
This ordering ensures that the selection of suitable explanation methods is grounded in metrics whose reliability has been rigorously validated.

emerges as the most versatile explanation method, as it achieves strong performance across multiple evaluation categories. It excels in faithfulness, localization, and randomization, making it particularly well-suited for
RS
image scene classification.

VI
Conclusion and Guidelines

In this study, for the first time in
RS
, we have systematically investigated the suitability of explanation methods and metrics for
RS
image scene classification by combining methodological analysis with extensive experiments on three datasets.
In particular, we analyzed five widely used feature attribution methods:
Occlusion

[
9
]
,
LIME

[
10
]
,
LRP

[
11
]
,
DeepLIFT

[
12
]
and
GradCAM

[
13
]
and ten explanation metrics spanning five categories: faithfulness (
FE

[
28
]
,
IROF

[
29
]
), robustness (
AS

[
30
]
,
LLE

[
28
]
), localization (
RRA

[
31
]
,
TKI

[
32
]
), complexity (
SP

[

]
,

[

]
) and randomization-based (
MPRT

[

]
,
RL

[

]
).

Our methodological analysis shows that perturbation‐based approaches, such as
Occlusion
and
LIME
, are critically dependent on the choice of perturbation baseline. In RS scenes, replacing spectral content with a fixed value can generate
OoD
samples. Furthermore, the redundancy of features across large regions hinders the perturbation function to significantly decrease the prediction of a model, which lowers the reliability of perturbation-based methods.
Gradient‐propagation methods like
GradCAM
are limited in their ability to distinguish between multiple instances of the same object in a scene. Relevancy-propagating methods like
LRP
tend to underestimate the contribution of LULC classes that span larger areas of a scene.
Regarding evaluation metrics, our findings indicate that faithfulness metrics inherit the limitations of perturbation‐based methods and need to be parametrized accordingly. In contrast, robustness metrics demonstrate greater stability. Localization metrics require accurate pixel-level reference maps. Furthermore, localization and complexity metrics are unreliable when a single class spans a large part of the scene. Randomization metrics are often independent of the data.
To support our methodological findings, we conducted two sets of experiments on three
RS
datasets:
BEN-v2.0
,
DG-ML
, and
FBP-ML
. In the first set of experiments, we meta-evaluated ten explanation metrics to assess their reliability. We observe that metrics from the robustness and randomization categories performed well. Conversely, as expected from the methodological analysis, metrics from the faithfulness and localization categories are more unreliable.
In the second set of experiments, we use the most reliable explanation metrics to determine which explanation methods are best suited for
RS
image scene classification. Among the evaluated methods,
GradCAM
emerged as the most robust feature attribution method, demonstrating relatively stable performance across different datasets and evaluation metrics. However, it is important to note that no single method performed optimally across all evaluation criteria.

Following our methodological and experimental analysis, we develop guidelines for selecting suitable explanation metrics and explanation methods for scene classification in
RS
:

1.

We recommend using robustness and randomization metrics due to their reliability across datasets and explanation methods.

2.

Faithfulness metrics should be used cautiously, as they depend on perturbation baselines and may not reliably reflect model behavior. To mitigate this issue, we recommend using the
LeRF
removal strategy, which helps to maintain reliability by gradually removing less relevant features first. Furthermore, we recommend utilizing the mean value as a perturbation baseline.

3.

We suggest using localization metrics only when accurate pixel-level reference maps are available. In particular, we recommend using more advanced localization metrics that also account for the size of the target class. Complexity metrics should be interpreted carefully, as they depend strongly on the spatial extent of the target class. Similarly to localization metrics, complexity metrics could also be modified to account for the target class size.

4.

We recommend using
GradCAM
as a general baseline due to its versatility and ease of interpretation. Among the evaluated methods, it consistently achieves high scores across multiple metric categories.

As a final remark, we would like to note that, due to the lack of ground truth on explanations, it remains not possible to fully validate an explanation metric
[
24
]
. The quantifiable reliability of a metric does not necessarily indicate its validity
[
66
]
. Thus, each evaluation must be critically examined. Future work should focus on refining explanation metrics specifically designed for
RS
image scene classification. In particular, developing perturbation strategies that better accommodate spectral variability and feature redundancy may enhance the reliability of faithfulness metrics and, by extension, perturbation-based explanation methods. Although our study concentrated on feature attribution methods commonly used in
RS
(while being originally designed for natural images), a promising direction for future research is to develop and evaluate explanation techniques tailored specifically to the characteristics of
RS
images.
