Physical integrity and bioefficacy of long-lasting insecticidal nets 18 months after use among Central African Republic refugees in Gado-Badzere, a high malaria burden camp in Cameroon
Authors: Zidedine Nematchoua Weyou, Felicite Djemna Djieyep, Michel Lontsi-Demano, Idriss Nasser Ngangue-Siewe, Genevieve Tchigossou, Rene Ning Teh, Danahé Adanzounon, Helen Kuokuo Kimbi, Irene Ule Ngole Sumbele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Cameroon, long-lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs) are widely distributed as a key malaria control strategy. However, the proper use, maintenance and monitoring of their effectiveness remains a major challenge, especially in refugee settings. This study aimed to evaluate the physical condition and bioefficacy of LLINs used in households at the Gado-Badzere refugee camp in Eastern Cameroon.A cross-sectional study was conducted 18 months after LLINs distribution using a structured questionnaire to evaluate net usage and care. Physical integrity was assessed based on World Health Organisation (WHO) guidelines. WHO cone test was conducted to assess bed net efficacy against mosquitoes. Anopheles gambiae sensu lato mosquitoes were identified to species level using polymerase chain reaction (PCR).Of the 440 LLINs examined, 60.7% were in good condition, 14.5% acceptable and 24.8% deteriorated. Deteriorated nets were significantly more common among males (32.4% vs. 23.2%) and in households with more than five persons (26.1% vs. 21.9%). Significant associations were found between net deterioration and washing practices, including frequent and no washing (36.5% vs. 7.8%), washing period intervals (1–3 months: 53.4% vs. 11.4% for 4–6 months and 31.6% for >6 months) and the use of detergent, soap and bleach/brush (24.8% and 29.7% vs. 83.3%). All 30 LLINs tested were ineffective against field Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations with overall knock-down and mortality rates of 1.5% and 3.1%, respectively. An. gambiae (86.9%) and An. coluzzii (13.1%) were identified as the malaria vector species present in the study area.These findings call for urgent action by humanitarian agencies operating in high-risk settings to strengthen community awareness, with a focus on gender-sensitive approaches to educate CAR refugees on the proper use and care of LLINs. Moreover, given the apparent resistance of An. gambiae s.l. to standard LLIN insecticides, introducing next-generation LLINs and regularly monitoring their physical integrity and bioefficacy are essential to ensure sustained malaria protection in vulnerable refugee populations.Keywords: Malaria, Long-lasting insecticidal net, Bioefficacy, CAR refugees, Gado-Badzere

Introduction

Malaria epidemics have become a frequent occurrence in many parts of the world, with the burden being most severe in sub-Saharan Africa (WHO, 2023). Globally, malaria cases increased by 11 million between 2022 and 2023, with approximately 94% of cases and 95% deaths occurring in the WHO African Region (WHO, 2024). In Cameroon, malaria accounts for 28% of total health facility consultations and 7% of all deaths with children under 5 years of age and pregnant women being the most affected groups (MINSANTE, 2024). To reduce the burden of the disease in the country, vector control operations via the large-scale distribution of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), alongside rapid diagnosis and prompt treatment with artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) have been implemented by the government (PNLP, 2019). Although these measures have resulted to notable declines in the malaria incidence and prevalence in several regions of the country (PNLP, 2021; MINSANTE, 2024), transmission remains persistently high in other settings, particularly among vulnerable populations (Nematchoua Weyou et al., 2024; MINSANTE, 2024).

Malaria incidence is increasingly influenced by climate-related and socio-political drivers. Climate change, particularly rising global temperatures has expanded the geographic suitability for malaria transmission, while extreme weather events disrupt health systems and vector control activities (Charchuk et al., 2016; Marou et al., 2024). These environmental pressures often intersect with humanitarian crises, amplifying malaria risk in vulnerable populations. Conflict-related displacement further exacerbates malaria burden in endemic regions. Forced displacement limits access to preventive and curative health services, increasing susceptibility to malaria among refugee populations (UNHCR, 2022; WHO, 2024).

For decades now, Cameroon has hosted large numbers of refugees in the Central African Region, notably from the Central African Republic (CAR), where civil conflict has displaced over 329,000 refugees into the East Region alone (OCHA, 2023). In malarious areas, exposure to malaria transmission in refugees could be greater than that of surrounding host communities living under normal conditions (Charchuk et al., 2016; Oboth et al., 2019), owing to their vulnerability which is promoted by factors like; social disruption, overcrowding, inadequate shelter, poor nutrition, limited health care facilities and poor environmental management (Biset et al., 2022; Takarinda et al., 2022; Nematchoua Weyou et al., 2024; Debash et al., 2025). Displaced populations in Eastern Cameroon suffer from high malaria burden due to several risk factors to which they are predisposed (Nematchoua Weyou et al., 2024), and one of the most widely used malaria control measures in refugee camps is LLINs. Indeed, recent studies in refugee camps in sub-Saharan Africa have demonstrated the effectiveness of this prevention tool among frequent users (Ahmed et al., 2021; Nematchoua Weyou et al., 2024). However, despite the mass distribution of LLINs in refugee settings, malaria continues to account for up to 50% of all deaths and 16% of child mortalities and remains prevalent in more than 80% of existing refugee camps globally (UNHCR, 2009).

Although LLINs remain the cornerstone of malaria prevention in refugee camps, their operational effectiveness in humanitarian settings may differ substantially from that observed in rural village contexts. Refugees often live in overcrowded shelters constructed from fragile materials, practice frequent washing of nets due to harsh living conditions and face challenges in net maintenance and replacement, all of which can accelerate physical deterioration and reduce insecticidal efficacy (Nyangi et al., 2021; Ngongang-Yipmo et al., 2022; Hiruy et al., 2023; Djoufounna et al., 2024; Nkahe et al., 2024). While several studies in Cameroon have assessed LLIN physical integrity and bioefficacy in stable rural and urban environments (Akono Ntonga et al., 2022; Ngongang-Yipmo et al., 2022; Djoufounna et al., 2024; Efa et al., 2024; Nkahe et al., 2024; Kala Chouakeu et al., 2025), data from protracted humanitarian and refugee settings are scarce. This lack of evidence makes this study relevant given the significant CAR refugee population established in this second largest refugee camp in the country and the reliance of both the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) and humanitarian partners such as the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) on LLINs as a primary prevention tool. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the physical integrity and bioefficacy of LLINs used by the CAR refugees hosted in the Gado-Badzere refugee camp 18 months after distribution. By generating context-specific evidence from a protracted humanitarian setting, this study seeks to inform malaria control strategies, guide LLIN replacement timelines and possible review of policy for improved malaria prevention among high-risk refugee populations.

Materials and methods

Study site

Gado-Badzere refugee camp is located in the Garoua-Boulai Sub-division, Lom and Djerem Division, East Region of Cameroon (5°45′15.9″N, 14°26′0.6″E), approximately 35 km from the Central African Republic border. Malaria control interventions, including LLIN mass distribution, are routinely implemented by governmental and non-governmental organizations operating in the camp. Despite high LLIN ownership, malaria prevalence and parasitaemia remain high in the camp. A detailed description of the camp's geographic location and population characteristics has been published elsewhere (Nematchoua Weyou et al., 2024).

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional survey was carried out in the Gado-Badzere refugee camp 18 months after the January 2023 LLINs mass distribution campaign. The brands of nets distributed included; Interceptor G2, Olyset Plus, DuraNet Plus and Permanet 2.0. To assess the physical integrity of bed nets, all households that owned and used at least one LLIN were included in this study.

Using a structured questionnaire on; if nets were ever washed, washing frequency per year, washing tools and drying places were also evaluated. Bioefficacy tests were conducted on 30 randomly selected LLINs in accordance with WHO/WHOPES guidelines for LLIN bioefficacy assessment (WHO, 2013). The services of a translator were hired to ease communication in the preferred language of refugees (“Fulfulde” or “Sango”). The study proceeded after explaining the study aim and obtaining informed consent from participants.

Assessment of the physical integrity and maintenance of LLINs

All households in possession of at least one LLIN from the last mass distribution in January 2023 were eligible. However, 440 households gave their consent to inspect their nets for physical integrity (i.e 440 LLINs). Sampling was random and included all the 12 sectors of the camp. To minimize selection bias, in households possessing more than one net, selection was performed without prior assessment of the net's physical condition. Consequently nets with visible damage were not preferentially selected and all nets had an equal probability of inclusion.

The physical integrity of nets was assessed in situ by direct observation of hung nets over the sleeping space in households. It was assessed for the presence or absence of holes, the number of holes, the size of holes and face of nets with holes. The number of holes indicates the net's overall usability and effectiveness. The fewer the holes, the better the protection it offers. The hole sizes were classified into four categories as follows: size 1 (0.5–2 cm); size 2 (2–10 cm); size 3 (10–25 cm) and size 4 (>25 cm). It estimates the approximate value of the area occupied by the holes on each net. These categories were used to calculate the proportionate hole indices (pHI) according to WHO recommendations (WHO, 2011) as follows:

pHI = (1 x number of size 1 holes)

+ (23 x number of size 2 holes)

+ (196 x number of size 3 holes)

+ (578 x number of size 4 holes)

On the basis of the pHI values obtained, the nets were classified into three categories as: good if pHI ≤64; acceptable if pHI: 65–642; and deteriorated if pHI >642.

Bioefficacy tests on bed nets using WHO cone bioassays

ITNs substitution in households

Of the 440 LLINs inspected for physical integrity, a subsample of 30 LLINs of different brands was retrieved and substituted with new Interceptor G3 nets. The 30 nets were drawn using stratified sampling by brand from the list of all available nets, with a proportional number of nets selected from each brand. The brands with the wider distribution coverage (more common) contributed more samples, while the brands with limited distribution (rare) contributed fewer samples.

The sides of the nets were labelled before being substituted and kept in separate zip lock plastic bags for bioassays with field collected mosquitoes and susceptible laboratory strains.

Larvae collection and rearing of Anopheles mosquitoes

Anopheles mosquito larvae were identified and collected from rain-filled puddles and hoof prints, shallow pools formed after heavy rain, foot prints and blocked gutters in and around the camp by the dipping method (Service, M.W., 1993). The larvae were transferred into opened plastic containers for transportation to a set up insectarium at the Gado-Badzere health centre. Once in the insectarium, larvae were transferred into large plastic recipients and fed with TetraMin® fish food till the pupa stage. Pupae were collected using a pipette and transferred into cages for them to emerge as adults. The adult mosquitoes were fed with 10% sucrose solution soaked on cotton (Tchuinkam et al., 2011). The rearing process was conducted under standard conditions of 27 °C ± 2 °C and 70% ± 10% relative humidity.

WHO cone bioassays

Cone bioassays with LLINs were conducted as per WHO guidelines (WHO, 2018), using field collected Anopheles gambiae s.l. mosquitoes.

On each side of a hung net, two cones were attached into which were introduced 5 non-blood fed, 3–5 days old female mosquitoes. A cotton ball was used to plug the cone holes and mosquitoes were exposed to the net for 3 min (timed individually for each cone). After exposure, mosquitoes were gently transferred into cups screened with untreated nets and provided with 10% sugar solution soaked on a piece of cotton wool placed on top of the net. A cup screened with an untreated netting (insecticide-free) containing mosquitoes served as the negative control, as confirmed by its source as unimpregnated material. Knockdown and mortality were recorded 60 min and 24 h post exposure respectively.

Mosquitoes were considered alive if they could stand upright and fly in a coordinated manner while, they were considered knocked-down if they could not stand upright and could not fly in a coordinated manner and as dead if it were immobile. WHO (2018) criteria describe the optimal effectiveness of a mosquito net when the knockdown rate was ≥95% and the mortality rate ≥ 80%, minimal effectiveness when mortality ≥50 and knockdown ≥75% and not effective when mortality <50% and knockdown <75%.

After bioassay tests on bed nets using the field mosquito samples, a 25 cm × 25 cm pieces cut was done on the faces of each sampled net. The net pieces from each side was labelled and separately kept in zip lock plastic bags. It was later transported to the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA), Benin for bioassays with the susceptible laboratory strains An. gambiae Kisumu reared and maintained under laboratory conditions. The susceptible An. gambiae kisumu strain were used as positive controls for bioefficacy tests.

Molecular identification of field-tested mosquitoes

After bioassays with field-collected An. gambiae s.l., both live and dead mosquitoes were individually and carefully placed in labelled Eppendorf tubes containing silica gel desiccant using a forceps. It was stored and used for molecular identification of species.

Total DNA was extracted from each mosquito carcass (legs and abdomen) following the Livak protocol (Livak, 1984) from which molecular identification was performed using Short Interspersed Nuclear Element Polymerase Chain Reaction (SINE-PCR) as described by Santolamazza et al. (2008). PCR amplification was carried out in a total reaction volume of 15 μL under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 54 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Data analysis

Data were entered into spread sheets using Microsoft Excel 2016. Analyses were performed using the IBM-Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22 (IBM SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) software. The descriptive statistic was used to evaluate percentages. Independent Chi-square and Fisher's exact tests were used to compare proportions between categorical variables (gender, age groups, education level, occupation, household size, washing of nets and frequency, washing tools and drying place) and the physical integrity of bed nets. Mosquito knockdown and mortality rates were calculated as per WHO (2018) recommendations and summarized into means and standard deviations (SD). Values indicated statistical significance when p < 0.05.

According to the household size, smaller households with 1–5 people had a higher proportion of good nets (70.1%) compared to those with more than 5 people (56.4%).

Following the WHO guidelines, all the 30 tested LLINs irrespective of the brands were inefficient on field mosquito populations with a knockdown rate of 1.5% (n = 23) and mortality of 3.1% (n = 47). Although none of the 4 LLIN brands met WHO bioefficacy criteria, yet no brand performed better than the other. Overall, Interceptor G2 nets and PermaNet 2.0 proved minimal efficacy, while Olyset Plus and DuraNet Plus revealed complete efficiency against susceptible laboratory strains (Table 5).

Anopheline mosquito species composition

Molecular analysis by PCR of 582 randomly selected female Anopheles gambiae s.l. mosquitoes used for bioefficacy tests was conducted for species identification. Two species were identified, An. gambiae (n = 506; 86.9%) and An. coluzzii (n = 76; 13.1%).

Discussion

Malaria remains a significant global health issue, especially in regions with high levels of poverty, poor healthcare infrastructure, and displacement due to conflict or natural disasters (UNHCR, 2024; WHO, 2024). Population migration and resettlement due to armed conflict is considered as a key factor which poses a great challenge in malaria control and elimination, especially in malaria endemic regions. Humanitarian organizations present on the field provide LLINs, one of the most widely used vector control measures in refugee camps. Despite routine LLIN distribution, malaria parasite burden remains high among refugees (UNHCR, 2024; Nematchoua Weyou et al., 2024). The objective of this study was to assess the physical integrity and biological efficacy of LLINs used by CAR refugees in the Gado-Badzere refugee camp in Eastern Cameroon 18 months after the 2023 mass distribution campaign.

LLINs are designed to retain their efficacy against mosquito vectors and preserve their physical integrity for a minimum of 3 years and 20 standard washes (WHO, 2019; WHO, 2015). With regards to bed net integrity after 18 months of use by CAR refugees, almost half of the nets (42%) had holes of distinct sizes, similar to Nyangi et al. (2021) who reported a 49.9% net deterioration during the same period of use in a locality of Kenya. The holes were found mostly on all sides of the nets including the roof, a pattern commonly reported in LLIN durability studies from Cameroon and other African settings. This distribution of net damage observed among CAR refugees is probably linked to frequent washing, type of bedding (commonly bamboo beds and mats), overcrowded households and children causing frequent mishandling of the nets as previously documented in the East, West and Central African contexts (Birhanu et al., 2019; Ahogni et al., 2020; Nkahe et al., 2024). In this study some refugees used the roof side of the nets as storage place, added to the abrasive materials (rough bamboo sticks, iron bars) on which they hang their nets probably increased physical stress and contributed to tearing in this area. Similar roof-side deterioration has been reported in Kenyan and Cameroonian settings, where difficulties in net installation within cramped shelters and abrasive hut construction materials accelerated physical damage (Nyangi et al., 2021; Ngongang-Yipmo et al., 2022). In contrast, other durability assessment studies reported greater loss of integrity on the two larger sides of nets, except the roof due to repeated handling during nightly use and tucking (Mutuku et al., 2013; Boussougou-Sambe et al., 2017; Ngongang-Yipmo et al., 2022; Djoufounna et al., 2024). The uniform deterioration rate of bed nets across all sides, reflected by comparable pHI contributions from the roof and lateral panels, has been reported to be more pronounced in poorer communities (Boussougou-Sambe et al., 2017; Nyangi et al., 2021). Although most refugees possessed bed nets, and more than half (60.7%) of these nets were still classified as being in good physical conditions, the prevalence of malaria was high (40.3%) (Nematchoua Weyou et al., 2024). The finding that 24.8% of nets were deteriorated according to the proportionate hole index (pHI 64–642) suggests a moderate degradation rate and highlighted the need for timely replacement to maintain protective efficacy in this population (WHO, 2013).

Several determinants were associated with damaged or deteriorated conditions of nets. Female refugees were more likely to have bed nets in better physical conditions, compared to males who were more likely to have deteriorated nets. This might be due to the caretaking responsibilities of females to protect their families from malaria and equally the fact that women may often replace or renew their bed nets through antenatal visits, leaving men with older damaged nets. This finding may require aid agencies to equitably distribute bed nets and educating both gender on the proper usage and maintenance of nets. Results from this study also revealed that household size of more than 5 people significantly affects bed net integrity, possibly reporting crowded living conditions in huts which might lead to less careful handling or higher physical stress on the nets. This finding is in line with Hiruy et al. (2023) in Ethiopia who reported that LLINs tend to have shorter functional survival time as family size increased. The frequent washing of bed nets was significantly associated with more deterioration. This behavior is likely influenced by contextual factors common in refugee camps such as makeshift shelters with dirt/mud floors, indoor smoke from cooking with firewood and close-packed living arrangements can accelerate net soiling, motivating more frequent washing than in stable households. Similar associations between washing frequency, intensive use and net damage have been reported in other African settings, including Kenya, Zambia, Benin, Nigeria and Ghana, highlighting that behavioral and environmental factors consistently drive accelerated deterioration (Mutuku et al., 2013; Tan et al., 2016; Santos et al., 2019; Ahogni et al., 2020; Adeneye et al., 2021; Adageba et al., 2022). This result could potentially question the washing practices of bed nets in order to recommend on how to properly care for it. Indeed, bed net integrity was found to be severely tampered with depending on the washing frequencies and the washing tools employed. Frequent washing likely increases bed net deterioration rate due to the recurrent physical stress of washing placed on net fibres and is consistent with reports elsewhere (Akono Ntonga et al., 2022; Nopowo et al., 2020). The highest proportion of deteriorated nets (53.3%) were those that were washed after every 1–3 months, suggesting that too frequent washing may negatively impact on the durability of nets, perhaps due to the physical stress of washing contributing to faster wear and tear of nets. The highest proportion of good nets were those that were washed at every 4–6 months interval with relatively fewer deteriorated nets (11.4%), implying that this interval seems to maintain the best physical integrity of the nets. Although washing the nets after more than every 6 months also maintained good net integrity, the proportion of deteriorated nets remained higher (31.6%) compared to those who wash every 4–6 months. This may suggest that infrequent washing might also lead to net degradation, although not as rapidly as washing more frequently. This could probably be due to delayed maintenance, accumulated dirts or fumes from firewood at the net surface (Ahogni et al., 2020; N'Guessan et al., 2022; Nkahe et al., 2024; Kala Chouakeu et al., 2025). Another factor that showed a positive correlation with bed net deterioration rate was the washing tools. Findings revealed that, majority of nets washed with soap were in good (50.5%) or acceptable (19.8%) conditions, while a smaller proportion were deteriorated (29.7%), implying relative less damage or moderate wear from soap usage. Washing the nets with detergent alone showed a higher percentage of good nets (60.2%) and fewer deteriorated nets (24.8%), indicating that detergents might slightly preserve net integrity better than soap. This finding is contradictory to that obtained by Adeneye et al. (2021) in three rural communities in Ikorodu, Lagos State, Nigeria and Akono Ntonga et al. (2022) in rural and urban Kribi, Cameroon where net deterioration were lower when washed with ordinary soap (lumpy) than those washed with corrosive detergents (powdered soap). The authors attributed this to the fact that, dirty LLINs are soaked in water containing detergent for several minutes or even hours before being washed, therefore, causing chemical reactions that weakens net fibres. In combination with either soap or detergent, participants also used brushes and bleaching agents and this resulted to significantly poorer outcomes with most nets being deteriorated (83.3%) and only 16.7% in good condition with none in acceptable condition. This suggests that using brushes and bleaches probably accelerates or severely damage the nets due to abrasive action. Harsh washing practices of nets were also reported on Olyset Plus nets used in Bertoua (Nkahe et al., 2024). Therefore, to preserve net quality and ensure its continued protection against malaria, educational campaigns before or during bed net distribution is essential to promote guidance on proper washing methods and optimal washing frequency. This could be done by encouraging gentle hand washing using lumpy soap or mild detergent and avoiding abrasive cleaning using brushes and bleaches. In addition, promoting the washing of bed nets within 4 to 6 months interval appears to be optimal for maintaining the net's quality.

It is well known that, even with the presence of holes, bed nets still offers some degree of physical barrier against mosquitoes (WHO, 2013). However, their bioefficacy defined by their ability to repel, incapacitate or kill mosquitoes also remain crucial in protecting CAR refugees from malaria. Biological efficacy tests were conducted on 30 randomly selected LLINs made up of 4 different brands. For nets to be effective, the WHO recommends a mortality threshold of ≥80% in cone bioassays against field mosquitoes (WHO, 2018). In this study, bioassays conducted on all the LLINs against field mosquitoes resulted to mortality rates below the WHO recommended threshold irrespective of the brand, implying that all the nets proved to be ineffective in providing protection against malaria among CAR refugees. This result is similar to that obtained by Ndjeunia-Mbiakop et al. (2025) who reported that none of the bed nets tested in two localities (Belabo and Ouami) in Cameroon's East Region were effective against field populations of Anopheles gambiae s.l. In the refugee context this bed net inefficiency might have been accelerated by the camp environment in which refugees live. Indeed, they live mostly in overcrowded cramped shelters built from precarious materials; the muddy nature of their floor soils their nets faster, requiring frequent washing which probably increases insecticide loss (Brooks et al., 2017). Moreover, in larger household, nets undergo increased mechanical abrasion (touched, pulled and rubbed more frequently by bodies, bedding and hut structures) and more frequent folding, tucking and handling causes faster soiling from sweat, dirt, food residues or children playing with nets, all of which can solubilize or accelerate insecticide loss from bed nets fibres. In addition, this bed net inefficiency could be due to the poor maintenance methods of nets such as very frequent washing which causes rapid degradation of the insecticide concentration or no washing. In this study, nets that were never washed helped to better preserve their physical integrity, but their insecticidal efficacy may have declined due to accumulated dirt, fumes, oils and dust on the nets surface which prevents the active ingredient of the insecticide from being released from the net fibres, as previously reported (Birhanu et al., 2019; Nkahe et al., 2024). In addition to household-level use patterns, conditions during storage and transport prior to distribution may have contributed to accelerated insecticide depletion in bed nets, although these conditions were not directly assessed in the present study.

Another plausible factor which might have affected the poor performance of bed nets used by CAR refugees is the possible resistance of mosquitoes to pyrethroids, which are the main compounds recommended for bed net impregnation (WHO, 2016). Indeed, high resistance intensity of An. gambiae s.l. to pyrethroids has been observed in different settings across the country, with frequencies of the knock down resistance (kdr) allele 1014F and the N1575Y mutation contributing to pyrethroid resistance (Antonio-Nkondjio et al., 2017; Bamou et al., 2021; Piameu et al., 2021; Kala-Chouakeu et al., 2022; Sonhafouo-Chiana et al., 2022). In addition, the overexpression of several P450 detoxification genes such as the Cyp6m2, Cyp6p3, Cyp9k1, Cyp6p4, Cyp6z1 and the Cyp4g16 have been shown to metabolise pyrethroids in An. gambiae s.l. in Cameroon (Tene et al., 2013; Antonio-nkondjio et al., 2015, Antonio-Nkondjio et al., 2017; Bamou et al., 2019, Bamou et al., 2021; Kala-Chouakeu et al., 2022; Sonhafouo-Chiana et al., 2022). Therefore, the high pyrethroid resistance detected in mosquito populations is likely driven by sustained insecticide selection pressure resulting from the extensive use of pyrethroid-treated nets across the country for decades now (Kala-Chouakeu et al., 2022; Ndjeunia-Mbiakop et al., 2025).

Bioassays conducted against laboratory susceptible strain Anopheles gambiae Kisumu instead revealed complete effectiveness with Olyset Plus and DuraNet, meanwhile partial effectiveness was noticed with Interceptor G2 and PermaNet 2.0. This implies that the nets might have lost insecticidal effectiveness or the insecticide on the nets may have quickly degraded over time. The fact that all refugees reported drying their nets under the sun might have also contributed to the rapid loss of the insecticidal properties of the nets as reported elsewhere (Kayedi et al., 2009; Atieli et al., 2010; Nkahe et al., 2024). LLINs are known to be sensitive to prolonged exposure to direct sunlight, particularly ultraviolet radiation, which can degrade insecticide molecules and reduce their bioavailability on net fibres (WHO, 2013). Repeated sun-drying following washing may therefore lead to cumulative insecticide loss through photo degradation and enhanced volatilization, especially under high ambient temperatures typical of refugee camp environments. The sun-drying practice of nets by CAR refugees in this study contrasts with manufacturer and WHO guidance, which recommends drying nets in the shade to preserve insecticidal activity (WHO, 2013). Overall, the findings revealed that both the physical integrity and bioefficacy of LLINs used by CAR refugees declined significantly after 18 months of use, corroborating with reports from Oyweri et al. (2025) within the same period in Western Kenya. Therefore, these findings indicate that all LLINs irrespective of the brand was no longer offering ultimate protection from mosquito bites and malaria transmission, necessitating hasty renewal.

Notwithstanding the insights gained, it is necessary to recognize the study's intrinsic limitations. Although cone assays are a standard and widely used tool for preliminary assessment of bed net efficacy, they primarily capture short-term knock-down and mortality under artificial exposure conditions. The lack of complementary approaches such as the WHO tunnel tests, which better reflect host-seeking behavior in more realistic settings and chemical residue analyses such as HPLC to measure the insecticide remaining on net fibres restricts the interpretation of the findings. Therefore in the absence of these complementary tests, it remains uncertain whether the poor performance of nets against filed An. gambiae s.l. populations is mainly attributable to insecticide resistance, loss of active ingredients or both.

The strength of this study lies in its ability to provide a comprehensive practical finding of LLINs durability, maintenance challenges and insecticide resistance or degradation over time, reflecting realities in refugee settings.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that LLINs used by CAR refugees quickly became more physically damaged and lost chemical bioefficacy against wild An. gambiae s.l. populations after 18 months of use. The factors associated with improper handling of bed nets included; gender, larger household size, too frequent washing and abrasive cleaning methods. An. gambiae and An. coluzzii were identified as malaria vectors in Gado-Badzere. Consequently, to improve bed net longevity, targeted educational campaigns organized by aid agencies could incorporate gender-sensitive approaches to educate CAR refugees on the best practices for maintaining bed nets. Also, given An. gambiae s.l. resistance to standard LLIN insecticides, humanitarian agencies should evaluate LLIN quality and guide the procurement of more durable, advanced formulations. Moreover, LLINs should be regularly distributed in refugee camps to replace damaged nets and maintain full coverage. In addition to vector surveillance, active parasite monitoring is essential for effective malaria control in such vulnerable populations.
