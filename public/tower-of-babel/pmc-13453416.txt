Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts in Gastrointestinal Cancer Metastasis: Mechanisms and Emerging Therapeutic Strategies
Authors: Shasha Gao, Yanru Song, Yining Qiao, Zihan Gao, Jiao Ma, Bingjie Huo, Shenghao Li, Miao Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastasis is the primary cause of mortality across gastrointestinal (GI) cancers, including colorectal, gastric, pancreatic, biliary and esophageal cancers. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) constitute the dominant stromal component of the desmoplastic GI tumor microenvironment (TME) and regulate every stage of metastatic progression. This review systematically summarizes CAF-mediated pro-metastatic mechanisms in GI tumors, including extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, paracrine oncogenic signaling, immune exclusion, angiogenesis, lymphangiogenesis, pre-metastatic niches (PMNs) construction and therapeutic resistance. Notably, CAFs are heterogeneous populations with context-dependent dual functions: certain myofibroblastic CAF (myCAF) subsets restrain tumor progression in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), while inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) drive metastasis and immune suppression. Antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) exert divergent immunomodulatory effects depending on tumor context. Rather than acting as uniformly tumor-promoting stromal cells, CAFs represent heterogeneous and plastic populations whose functions vary by tumor type, spatial niche, and metastatic stage. We further discuss why broad CAF depletion has shown limited clinical success and emphasize the need for subtype-specific, context- aware CAF-targeted strategies.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancers, including colorectal, gastric, pancreatic, biliary and esophageal malignancies, represent a major global cancer burden, with metastasis being the principal cause of cancer-related mortality. Among stromal components, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) have emerged as critical regulators of tumor invasion, dissemination, and colonization.1 Within this milieu, CAFs have evolved from being perceived as passive bystanders to active orchestrators of tumor progression and dissemination. CAFs constitute one of the most abundant stromal cell populations in the TME. Through secretion of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and ECM components, they engage in extensive cross-talk with cancer cells and other stromal elements, thereby sculpting a pro-metastatic niche.2 This reciprocal communication establishes a feed-forward loop that perpetuates tumor aggressiveness. CAFs contribute to metastasis through multiple mechanisms, impacting key hallmarks of cancer including epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and therapy resistance. CAFs secrete factors that directly induce EMT in cancer cells, a critical step for acquiring migratory and invasive capabilities. In pancreatic cancer, Annexin A1 expression in cancer cells is associated with EMT and a CAF-rich microenvironment, linking stromal interaction with aggressive phenotypes.3 Similarly, CAFs in colorectal cancer (CRC) can secrete TGF-β, which downregulates the metastasis suppressor FLRT3 via the SMAD4 pathway to promote EMT.4 Beyond direct signaling, CAFs extensively remodel the ECM, creating a physical scaffold that facilitates cancer cell migration and invasion. In gastric cancer, CAFs-derived hyaluronan and proteoglycan link protein 1 (HAPLN1) promotes tumor invasion through ECM remodeling, with its expression correlating with advanced tumor stage and metastasis.5 This desmoplastic reaction, particularly prominent in cancers like pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), not only provides structural support for invasion but also creates a barrier to drug delivery and immune cell infiltration, contributing to therapeutic resistance.

Recent advancements in high-resolution technologies, such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics, have begun to unravel the profound heterogeneity within the CAF population, revealing distinct subtypes with specialized and sometimes opposing functions. Studies across GI cancers have identified subsets such as myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), each contributing uniquely to the TME. Critically, CAFs are not uniformly tumor-promoting; they exhibit context-dependent and sometimes opposing functions. For instance, in PDAC, broad depletion of α-SMA-positive myCAFs in genetically engineered mouse models has been associated with reduced survival, increased regulatory T-cell infiltration, and the emergence of less differentiated and more aggressive tumors, suggesting that some myCAF populations may exert tumor-restraining functions. apCAFs are characterized by the production of MHC-II complexes and CD74, enabling them to deliver processed antigenic peptides to CD4+ T lymphocytes. Nevertheless, these cells barely express the co-stimulatory markers CD80, CD86 and CD40, a property that drives opposing immunological outcomes. Even though apCAFs carry MHC class II surface proteins, the shortage of co-stimulatory signals allows them to facilitate regulatory T cell generation and foster tumour immune escape under certain conditions. Conversely, apCAFs are also capable of triggering functional CD4+ effector T cell responses in distinct tissue microenvironments. This contradictory dual capacity demonstrates that CAFs are not merely stromal populations that accelerate metastasis. Instead, they constitute a diverse set of immune modulators whose functions shift according to local contextual cues. In CRC, specific iCAF subtypes, like CFD+ iCAFs, are associated with metastasis and poor outcomes, where they secrete factors like SFRP1 to promote cancer stemness and EMT via the FGFR2-HIF1 axis.6 Similarly, in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), spatial and functional analyses have identified myCAF subtypes that are enriched during progression and correlate with poor prognosis, influencing invasion patterns and lymph node metastasis.7 This heterogeneity implies that CAFs can exhibit both tumor-promoting and tumor-restricting properties, making their therapeutic targeting a nuanced challenge. For example, in CRC, the EMT-transcription factor ZEB1 in CAFs suppresses anti-tumor immunity by promoting a myofibroblastic phenotype and restricting inflammatory activation, and its deficiency sensitizes tumors to immune checkpoint inhibition.8 Understanding the context-dependent roles of these CAFs subsets is therefore paramount for developing precise stromal-targeted therapies.

Although previous reviews have summarized CAF biology or CAF-targeted therapies broadly, fewer have integrated CAF heterogeneity with the stepwise metastatic cascade and therapeutic resistance specifically in GI cancers. The translational potential of targeting CAFs in GI cancers is rapidly expanding, with strategies focusing on disrupting CAF activation, neutralizing their secreted factors, and targeting specific surface markers. A prominent target is fibroblast activation protein-alpha (FAP), a cell surface serine protease highly expressed on CAFs. FAP serves not only as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker, with its expression correlating with deeper invasion, poorer differentiation, and metastasis in CRC,9 but also as a therapeutic target for imaging and drug delivery. For instance, 68Ga-FAPI PET/CT has shown high sensitivity in detecting primary and metastatic lesions in lower GI tract malignancies, offering improved staging and radiotherapy planning.10 Furthermore, FAPI-engineered nanoprobes have been developed for targeted delivery and imaging in gastric cancer peritoneal metastasis.11 Other strategies involve neutralizing CAF-derived oncogenic factors. In esophageal cancer, targeting latent TGFβ-binding protein 2 (LTBP2) secreted by CAFs can sensitize cancer cells to chemotherapy by inhibiting integrin α6β4/Src signaling pathway.12 Similarly, in gastric cancer, inhibiting the CAF-secreted lysyl oxidase (LOX) can modulate PD-L1 expression and EMT, potentially overcoming resistance to immunotherapy.13 These approaches highlight the shift towards combination therapies that simultaneously target cancer cells and their supportive stromal ecosystem. Clinically, abundant stromal components within gastrointestinal malignancies are strongly correlated with poor overall survival and delayed diagnosis. This phenomenon is most evident in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and contributes to systemic chemoresistance. To resolve such clinical obstacles, the present review elaborates the regulatory effects of CAFs throughout every phase of gastrointestinal cancer metastasis, and discusses the translational application of these mechanistic findings to develop subtype-specific CAF-targeted interventions. We confine our discussion exclusively to gastrointestinal neoplasms to maintain a tight thematic framework and solid clinical relevance. Additionally, we explore the underlying reasons why CAF-targeted interventions fail to yield consistent clinical responses, and put forward microenvironment-adapted therapeutic regimens. Accordingly, this review centers on gastrointestinal malignancies, integrating cutting-edge research on CAF heterogeneity with the multi-step process of tumor distant dissemination and drug resistance. Unlike previous reviews that provide generalized summaries of CAF biology and corresponding targeted therapies, this work highlights context-dependent functions of distinct CAF subsets during gastrointestinal cancer metastasis. We further illustrate that subtype-tailored, spatially stratified combinatorial treatments are indispensable for successful clinical translation.

Origin, Heterogeneity, and Activation of CAFs

Diverse Cellular Origins of CAFs

In tumors, CAFs constitute a highly heterogeneous stromal population that can either support or restrain tumor progression depending on cellular origin, subtype composition, spatial localization and tumor context (Figure 1). The best-characterized origin is the activation of quiescent tissue-resident fibroblasts. However, the cellular origins of CAFs extend far beyond local fibroblasts, which serves as a key basis for their functional diversity. In certain GI cancers, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) have been identified as a critical source. These circulating mesenchymal stem cells can be recruited to the tumor site by chemokines secreted by cancer cells, such as CXCL12, and subsequently differentiate into CAFs under the influence of the TME.14 For example, in pancreatic cancer, mesenchymal stem cells constitute an important component of the CAF pool.15 Similarly, macrophages present in malignant ascites and pleural effusions of patients with GI cancer can transdifferentiate into fibroblast-like cells, termed macrophage-derived cancer-associated fibroblasts (MDCAFs), which in turn promote tumor growth.16 In pancreatic cancer, the activation of pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) is the dominant and specific origin pathway. Quiescent PSCs store vitamin A, and upon activation by tumor-derived factors, they transform into myofibroblast-like CAFs that are responsible for the excessive deposition of ECM proteins, forming the characteristic dense fibrotic stroma that is a hallmark of PDAC.14 The fibrotic reaction driven mainly by PSC-derived CAFs further contributes to therapeutic resistance by forming physical and metabolic barriers.15 Mouse lineage-tracing studies have further deepened our understanding of this process, revealing that the majority of pancreatic fibroblasts, under both homeostatic and tumorigenic conditions, originate from the fetal splanchnic mesenchyme, suggesting that CAF differentiation follows a persistent developmental program.17 In CRC, IL-11-expressing fibroblasts form a unique subset whose gene signature correlates with poor prognosis, and these cells most likely arise from the activation of local fibroblasts.18 The developmental origin of fibroblasts leaves a lasting imprint: fibroblasts derived from the ectomesenchyme versus the mesoderm maintain distinct HOX gene expression patterns into adulthood and even in pathological conditions such as cancer, which may influence their topological localization and subsequent functions within the TME.19 The multi-origin nature of CAFs, encompassing resident fibroblasts, stellate cells, mesenchymal stem cells, endothelial cells, macrophages, and even epithelial or cancer cells transdifferentiated via processes such as EMT, is the fundamental cause of the functional and phenotypic heterogeneity observed in CAF populations across GI cancers.20 This complexity not only increases the difficulty of developing universal CAF-targeted therapies but also highlights the necessity of in-depth investigations into the specific origins and functions of CAF subsets in different tumor contexts.

Heterogeneity and Functional Subtypes of CAFs

The advent of single-cell transcriptomic technologies has profoundly transformed our understanding of CAFs, revealing that these cells are not a homogeneous population but rather a heterogeneous collection of functionally distinct subpopulations within the TME.21 This heterogeneity, shaped by diverse cellular origins, spatial contexts, and dynamic crosstalk with cancer cells, poses both challenges and opportunities for therapeutic intervention.22,23 In GI cancers, scRNA-seq studies have played a pivotal role in identifying discrete CAF subtypes, each characterized by unique molecular signatures and functional roles in tumor progression.24 Across diverse solid tumors, including those of the digestive tract, three CAF subtypes have been consistently and robustly identified: iCAFs, myCAFs, and apCAFs.25,26 The iCAF subtype displays a prominent secretory phenotype, marked by high expression of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines such as IL-6, IL-11, and CXCL12.27 iCAFs are critical mediators in establishing an immunosuppressive niche by recruiting and reprogramming myeloid cells and suppressing adaptive immune responses, thereby fostering a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory microenvironment.28 In contrast, myCAFs exhibit a contractile and matrix-remodeling phenotype, defined by elevated expression of alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), collagen, and other ECM components.25,29 This subtype is primarily responsible for desmoplasia, producing the dense fibrotic stroma characteristic of cancers such as PDAC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC).30–32 However, this pro-tumorigenic portrayal is not universal. In PDAC, genetic depletion of α-SMA-positive myCAFs or disruption of Hedgehog-driven stroma has been associated with decreased survival, increased regulatory T-cell infiltration, and more aggressive, poorly differentiated tumors, indicating that certain myCAF populations can exert tumor-restraining functions in specific contexts.33 While their precise functions remain to be fully elucidated, apCAFs are implicated in modulating T-cell activity within the TME. These cells express MHC class II and CD74 and can present antigens to CD4+ T cells, yet they often lack classical co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80, CD86, and CD40. Consequently, they may either promote regulatory T-cell responses and immune evasion or, in other contexts, support effector CD4+ T-cell activation.34,35 Their origin remains debated, with evidence suggesting a mesothelial-derived, rather than fibroblast-derived, lineage in some models.35 Thus, both myCAFs and apCAFs illustrate the context-dependent and sometimes opposing roles of CAF subtypes.

CAF subsets do not act independently but instead manifest dynamic phenotypic plasticity and context-dependent functional roles throughout distinct stages of cancer dissemination.36,37 As cancer cells enter the circulation, CAF-derived factors continue to drive metastatic progression. Exosomes and soluble mediators secreted by CAFs prime distant organ sites to form pre-metastatic niches, thereby boosting the efficiency of metastatic colonization.37 For instance, in gastrointestinal cancer models, CAF-secreted IL-6 and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) have been shown to promote the survival and outgrowth of disseminated tumor cells in the liver and peritoneum.30,38 At the site of distant colonization, the interplay between CAF subtypes becomes crucial again: myCAFs support the construction of a permissive stromal framework at metastatic lesions, while iCAFs blunt local anti-tumor immune surveillance, enabling nascent metastases to establish and proliferate.27 This functional specialization is not fixed: CAFs display striking plasticity and can interconvert between subtypes in response to microenvironmental cues including hypoxia, TGF-β, and interleukin-1 (IL-1) signaling.39–41 For example, in pancreatic cancer, cytokine gradients and hypoxia trigger the transition between myCAF and iCAF states, allowing the tumor stroma to adaptively sustain tumor progression under fluctuating conditions.40,42 This intricate, synergistic network of CAF subpopulations, each contributing distinct yet complementary functions across the metastatic cascade, positions them as important, context-dependent regulators of the TME and underscores the urgent need to develop subtype-specific therapeutic strategies to effectively abrogate their tumor-promoting activities.24,43

CAF Activation-Related Signaling Pathways

The activation of quiescent fibroblasts into CAFs is governed by an elaborate and interconnected signaling network, among which the TGF‑β/Smad pathway serves as a core canonical regulator, especially in GI cancers. This pathway is critically implicated in driving myofibroblastic differentiation of CAFs, as evidenced by the robust induction of α-SMA expression.44 Mechanistically, TGF‑β1 and TGF‑β3 isoforms trigger the canonical phosphorylation of SMAD2/3 in fibroblasts; however, the resulting phenotypic outputs, including myofibroblast trans-differentiation and ECM deposition, display striking heterogeneity that is dependent on both the tissue origin of fibroblasts and the specific TGF‑β isoform, with CAFs exhibiting the most pronounced and variable responses.45 Likewise, in CRC, conditioned medium derived from cancer cells promotes the transformation of quiescent fibroblasts into CAFs via ERK pathway activation, which upregulates α‑SMA expression and accelerates fibroblast proliferation and migration.46 The IL‑6/JAK/STAT3 axis constitutes another dominant signaling pathway in CAF regulation. Pro‑inflammatory IL‑6, secreted by both tumor cells and CAFs, mediates epigenetic reprogramming in fibroblasts and sustains the pro‑tumorigenic phenotype of CAFs.47 Moreover, the NF‑κB pathway functions as a major driver of CAF activation in gastric cancer. In this setting, CAFs secrete complement C3 in an NF‑κB‑dependent manner to facilitate EMT, enhance cancer cell motility, and promote immune evasion.48 Notably, persistent NF‑κB activation in gastric CAFs can be induced by cancer cell‑derived exosomes carrying pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), which promotes nuclear PKM2 translocation and p65 acetylation within CAFs.49 A defining feature of CAF activation is the dynamic bidirectional crosstalk between tumor cells and stromal fibroblasts, which forms a self‑sustaining feedforward loop mediated by diverse signaling mediators. Tumor cells actively remodel the TME by secreting soluble factors, cytokines, chemokines, and extracellular vesicles. For example, in CRC, cancer cell‑derived exosomes reprogram quiescent fibroblasts into activated CAFs, and this pro‑tumorigenic phenotype is maintained through sustained activation of the FGFR‑Wnt/β‑catenin signaling axis in fibroblasts.50 Similarly, pancreatic cancer cell‑derived small extracellular vesicles containing ezrin (sEV‑EZR) trigger fibroblast activation by enhancing STAT3 and YAP‑1 signaling, which in turn accelerates cancer cell proliferation and metastatic progression.51 This reciprocal signaling is further exemplified by the Wnt/β‑catenin pathway: cancer cell‑secreted WNT4 activates fibroblasts and promotes EMT and angiogenesis in CRC.52 Additionally, microRNAs such as miR‑1246 act as key paracrine mediators shuttling between CRC cells and fibroblasts. miR‑1246 not only reprograms fibroblasts toward a CAF‑like phenotype but also, upon secretion by activated CAFs, stimulates Wnt/β‑catenin signaling in cancer cells to enhance migratory capacity.53 Collectively, this continuous and reciprocal signal exchange maintains the persistent activation of CAFs and establishes a self‑reinforcing pro‑tumorigenic microenvironment, which represents a key pathological hallmark of aggressive GI cancers.

Context-Dependent Roles of CAFs in Gastrointestinal Cancer Invasion and Migration

Remodeling and Stiffening of the Extracellular Matrix

CAFs function as pivotal orchestrators of pathological ECM remodeling and stiffening within the TME, processes mechanistically linked to GI cancer progression and metastatic dissemination. CAFs synthesize and secrete abundant fibrillar collagens (predominantly type I and III) alongside core ECM constituents including fibronectin and laminin, driving aberrant matrix deposition and excessive cross-linking.54 This structural remodeling is potentiated by cross-linking enzymes such as lysyl oxidase (LOX), directly elevating tissue stiffness, a defining pathological feature of desmoplastic GI cancers.47 For example, mechanistic studies in fibroblast senescence models have demonstrated that upregulated expression of LOX, LOXL1, and transglutaminase 2 (TGM2) correlates with increased Young’s modulus (a quantitative measure of ECM stiffness) in cell-associated matrix.47 Notably, this stiffened fibrotic ECM acts as a dynamic signaling hub rather than a passive structural barrier. The altered biomechanical properties of the matrix initiate bidirectional mechanotransductive crosstalk between stromal and tumor cells. In response to matrix stiffening, CAFs amplify their ECM-remodeling activity: fibroblasts embedded in stiffness-gradient hydrogels exhibit gradient-dependent remodeling of the local ECM, aligning collagen fibers and reshaping the mechanical microenvironment.55 This dynamic interplay establishes a self-sustaining feed-forward loop, wherein CAF-driven ECM deposition augments matrix stiffness, which further promotes fibroblast activation and pathological matrix production.56 In the context of GI tumorigenesis, CAF-mediated ECM remodeling profoundly modulates tumor cell invasive and migratory phenotypes. The dense, cross-linked collagen network generates physical migratory tracks and modulates integrin-dependent adhesion, activating key intracellular mechanotransduction pathways including focal adhesion kinase (FAK) signaling and the Hippo pathway effectors YAP/TAZ in tumor cells, thereby potentiating their motile and invasive capacity.57 These enzymes do not fully eliminate ECM barriers but selectively degrade basement membranes and remodel normal ECM architecture to generate permissive physical channels for tumor cell movement.58 This proteolytic remodeling acts synergistically with de novo ECM deposition, yielding an ECM microenvironment that is structurally supportive of invasion while retaining dynamic malleability. However, CAFs should not be viewed as uniformly tumor-promoting cells, because their functions vary according to subtype, tumor context, spatial localization and disease stage. Collectively, CAF-driven ECM stiffening, aligned fiber architecture, and proteolytic path generation converge to create a pro-metastatic niche that drives GI cancer cell dissemination. Thus, targeting CAF-mediated ECM remodeling and stiffening represents a preclinically supported therapeutic strategy to impede tumor metastasis.59

Regulation of Paracrine Signaling Networks

CAFs act as core signaling hubs in the TME, and their paracrine signaling networks represent important regulators of GI tumor progression and metastatic spread. A major mechanism involves the secretion of diverse growth factors that directly activate corresponding receptors on tumor cells. For example, in gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST), CAFs serve as a critical source of platelet-derived growth factor C (PDGFC).60 PDGFC stimulates PDGFRA signaling in GIST cells, which transcriptionally upregulates the EMT-associated factor SLUG, ultimately promoting tumor growth and distant metastasis.60 This PDGFC–PDGFRA–SLUG paracrine axis illustrates how CAF-derived growth factors directly modulate core oncogenic programs in GI tumor cells. Notably, the CAF secretome is dynamically modulated by TME cues, particularly hypoxia. Hypoxic stress reshapes the secretory profile of CAFs to enhance tumor-promoting activities; in gastric cancer, hypoxia-conditioned CAFs secrete a distinct repertoire of soluble factors that strongly potentiate cancer cell migration and invasiveness.61 CAF-derived exosomes act as specialized conveyors of oncogenic cargo, including functional proteins, non-coding RNAs, and DNA fragments, that are systemically delivered to prime the pre-metastatic niche. This exosomal signaling axis is essential for metastatic progression in CRC, gastric cancer, and other GI malignancies.62 Collectively, this multilayered paracrine network, integrating growth factors, chemokines, cytokines, and exosomal mediators, establishes CAFs as central coordinators of a pro-tumorigenic signaling ecosystem. By orchestrating tumor cell proliferation, directional migration, EMT, and metastatic colonization, these signals fundamentally drive GI cancer progression. Therapeutically, targeting CAF activation or intercepting key paracrine ligand–receptor interactions represents a rational and conceptually compelling strategy to disrupt the supportive TME and suppress metastatic dissemination.

Physical Guidance and Collective Invasion

CAFs exert a pivotal regulatory role in mediating physical guidance and collective invasion of tumor cells, a process highly prevalent in aggressive GI malignancies. A key mechanism underlying this process is that CAFs construct a permissive migratory microenvironment by secreting and reorganizing aligned ECM fibers, thereby forming directional “tracks” or “highways” to facilitate cancer cell movement.63 This structural guidance is further reinforced by the assembly of fibronectin fibrils along the elongated protrusions of CAFs, which serve as stable adhesive substrates for cancer cells primarily via integrin‑α5β1‑mediated binding, supporting efficient migration within three‑dimensional (3D) collagen matrices.63 Notably, most evidence for fibroblast-led collective invasion derives from experimental models, and its relative contribution in human GI cancers may vary by tumor type and stromal architecture. Epithelial‑phenotype cancer cells then follow CAFs as cohesive collective clusters, thereby bypassing the requirement for full EMT.64 This coordinated invasive structure displays distinct front‑rear polarity: CAFs typically act as leader cells responsible for breaching basement membranes and overcoming stromal physical barriers, while cancer cells serve as follower cells.64,65 The efficiency of such collective invasion is dynamically modulated by microenvironmental cytokines and growth factors. For example, epidermal growth factor (EGF) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) promote collective invasion by weakening E-cadherin-dependent cell-cell adhesion, whereas TGF-β may exert inhibitory effects under certain contexts.63 In addition, the genotypic and phenotypic status of stromal fibroblasts substantially determines the heterogeneity of invasive programs, including invasion depth, formation of invasive forks, and migratory persistence of leader cells.65 Emerging evidence indicates that cancer cell-derived exosomes, such as those secreted by colorectal adenocarcinoma cells, can induce the conversion of normal fibroblasts into pro-invasive CAFs by activating the MAPK signaling cascade and other mechanotransduction pathways, thereby establishing a fibroblast-led collective invasion pattern.64 This CAF-mediated physical guidance is not a passive structural support but an active mechanical process. Computational simulations and functional experiments have demonstrated that heterotypic mechanical adhesion between CAFs and tumor cells, coupled with CAF-driven matrix contraction and remodeling, directly directs the outward collective migration of tumor cell clusters from the primary lesion.66 As illustrated in Figure 2, these CAF-driven physical and paracrine mechanisms, including ECM remodeling, matrix stiffening, directional track formation, and mechanical signaling activation, converge to orchestrate the multi-step metastatic cascade from local invasion to distant dissemination. In conclusion, CAFs provide both the structural “rails” via aligned ECM fibers and the propulsive “locomotive” force through matrix degradation and pathfinding activity. Regulation of Immune Cell Recruitment and Functional Reprogramming

CAFs orchestrate the immunosuppressive TME in GI cancers by governing the recruitment, localization, and functional polarization of diverse immune cell subsets. A core mechanism relies on CAF-derived chemokines and cytokines that direct the infiltration of pro-tumorigenic myeloid and lymphoid populations. In CRC, CAFs derived from pericryptal leptin receptor (Lepr)+ stromal cells upregulate melanoma cell adhesion molecule (MCAM) under TGF-β stimulation. MCAM signaling enhances the NF-κB–IL34/CCL8 axis, thereby driving robust recruitment of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs).67 Notably, CAFs represent the primary cellular source of monocyte chemoattractants and differentiation factors in the GI TME, rather than tumor cells themselves. CAF-secreted factors including macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), IL-6, IL-8, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), and CCL2 promote the differentiation of infiltrating monocytes into CD163+ immunosuppressive M2-like macrophages. This process recapitulates physiological monocyte recruitment and differentiation programs in the healthy colon, which are pathologically hijacked by CAFs to sustain immune tolerance.68 In cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), bidirectional crosstalk between cancer cells and CAFs critically modulates immune cell infiltration and functional polarization, with context-dependent effects on tumor progression dictated by disease stage and stromal heterogeneity.69 Pharmacological suppression of hyper-SUMOylation in CCA models reduces CAF abundance and restores the infiltration of anti-tumor immune cells, directly linking CAF density to immune suppression.70 Beyond immune cell recruitment, CAFs directly inhibit the effector functions of anti-tumor immune cells. In experimental models of esophageal carcinogenesis, αSMA⁺/Vimentin⁺ myofibroblasts (MFs), a major CAF subpopulation, are indispensable for establishing a chronic inflammatory and pro-carcinogenic stroma.71 CAFs also secrete a spectrum of soluble immunosuppressive mediators, including TGF-β, IL-10, and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2). The cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2)–PGE2 pathway is a well-characterized effector of tumor immune evasion across GI malignancies.71 In CRC, CAFs can induce T-cell exhaustion. For example, tumor fibroblast-derived FGF2 upregulates SPRY1 expression in CD8+ T cells, impairing T-cell receptor-triggered activation and cytotoxic activity.72 In CRC, CAF-derived fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) induces Sprouty 1 (SPRY1) expression in CD8⁺ T cells, thereby impairing T-cell receptor (TCR)-mediated activation and cytotoxic function, ultimately driving T-cell exhaustion.73 Furthermore, CAFs employ surface molecules to directly suppress anti-tumor immunity. Fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a serine protease highly expressed by CAFs in liver, colon, pancreatic, and gastric cancers, promotes ECM remodeling and positively correlates with M2 macrophage infiltration, consolidating an immunosuppressive TME.74 In ESCC and CRC, CAF-derived WNT2 inhibits dendritic cell (DC) differentiation and DC-dependent T-cell activation via the SOCS3/p-JAK2/p-STAT3 cascade. Targeted inhibition of WNT2 reverses DC dysfunction and synergizes with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.75 In PDAC, macrophage-derived oncostatin M (OSM) stimulates inflammatory gene expression in CAFs, which in turn activate pro-survival and migratory programs in tumor cells and reinforce a pro-tumorigenic immune milieu.76 CAFs also modulate immune checkpoint pathways. In CRC, T-cell immunoglobulin and ITIM domain (TIGIT) represents an emerging immune checkpoint overexpressed in the TME, combinatorial blockade of TIGIT and other inhibitory receptors elicits potent anti-tumor immune responses.77 Additionally, the folate cycle enzyme methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2 (MTHFD2), whose activity is modulated by the TME, promotes cancer immune evasion by upregulating programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression.78 A complementary immunosuppressive mechanism involves physical exclusion of anti-tumor lymphocytes. In CRC, dense intratumoral fibrosis, characterized by abundant αSMA⁺ CAFs and collagen deposition, forms a physical barrier that blocks lymphocyte infiltration into peritoneal metastases, conferring resistance to immunotherapy.79 High stromal content driven by CAFs correlates with poor clinical prognosis and suppressed anti-tumor immunity, manifested by reduced infiltration of CD4⁺ T cells and M1-type anti-tumor macrophages.80 Spatial compartmentalization further amplifies CAF-mediated immune exclusion. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a tumor immune barrier (TIB) composed of SPP1⁺ macrophages and CAFs at the tumor-stroma interface restricts immune cell penetration into the tumor core, thereby determining immunotherapy responsiveness.81 Collectively, through chemokine-guided immune cell recruitment, direct suppression of effector immunity, modulation of immune checkpoint molecules, and physical stromal barrier formation, CAFs function as key orchestrators of the immunosuppressive TME. These multifaceted mechanisms enable tumor immune evasion and drive malignant progression across diverse GI malignancies.

Formation of a Physical Immune Barrier

The dense fibrotic stroma elaborated by CAFs forms a formidable physical barrier that critically hinders immune cell infiltration into the tumor parenchyma, representing a key mechanism underlying the “immune-excluded” phenotype. This barrier is primarily composed of excessively deposited and remodeled ECM, characterized by enhanced collagen cross-linking and pathological accumulation of proteins such as hyaluronic acid, collectively increasing tissue stiffness and forming a dense, fibrous network.82 In GI malignancies, including PDAC and certain subtypes of CRC, this desmoplastic reaction is particularly prominent, with the stromal compartment occasionally accounting for more than 90% of the total tumor mass.14,83 The overproduction of ECM components like collagen I by activated CAFs not only forms a structural scaffold but also collaborates with aberrant, leaky tumor vasculature to elevate interstitial fluid pressure (IFP), creating a synergistic mechanical compression barrier that further restricts the mobility and extravasation of cytotoxic lymphocytes.82,84 Spatial analyses in PDAC have revealed that the organization of periductal fibroblasts dictates leukocyte proximity to malignant epithelium, with increasing fibroblast density corresponding to a graded constraint on immune cell infiltration and elevated collagen I deposition.85 This physical exclusion is a major contributor to immunotherapy resistance, as it prevents CD8⁺ T cells from engaging and eliminating cancer cells, effectively creating an immune-privileged site within the tumor.86,87 Furthermore, CAFs can induce immune exclusion through mechanisms beyond ECM deposition, such as secreting factors that disrupt lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 (LFA-1)-ICAM-1 interactions necessary for T-cell transendothelial migration.88 The clinical consequence is significant, as tumors exhibiting this CAF-driven immune-excluded phenotype, commonly observed in microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC and PDAC, are associated with poor prognosis and markedly reduced response to immune checkpoint blockade therapies.85,89 Therefore, disrupting this CAF-mediated physical barrier is considered a crucial therapeutic strategy to overcome stromal resistance and convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones amenable to immunotherapy.

Regulation of Angiogenesis and Lymphangiogenesis

CAFs serve as pivotal regulators of the TME in GI cancers, exerting a critical influence on tumor progression through their ability to modulate angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis.90 These activated stromal cells secrete a diverse array of pro-angiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), collectively driving the formation of new blood vessels within the tumor.91 This neovascularization process is essential for supplying nutrients and oxygen to the expanding tumor mass and, crucially, provides a direct conduit for tumor cells to enter the systemic circulation, thereby facilitating hematogenous metastasis.92 CAFs promote angiogenesis not only by providing structural support but also by actively regulating signaling crosstalk. For example, CAF-derived exosomes deliver miR-522-3p to endothelial cells. In CRC, exosomal miR-522-3p enhances HUVEC viability, migration, invasion and tube formation by targeting BMP5, representing a key pro-angiogenic mechanism of CAFs.93 Similarly, in ESCC, CAF-derived exosomal circ-0076535 was shown to promote tumor progression, a process intricately linked to the activation of pathways that can support an angiogenic switch.94 Beyond classical angiogenesis, CAFs are equally instrumental in driving lymphangiogenesis, a process vital for lymphatic metastasis, which is a common and critical route of spread for cancers like gastric cancer and CRC.90 CAFs secrete specific lymphangiogenic factors such as VEGF-C and VEGF-D, which induce the proliferation, migration, and tube formation of lymphatic endothelial cells, leading to the expansion and remodeling of the peritumoral lymphatic network.91 This creates channels for tumor cells to disseminate to regional lymph nodes. In ESCC, which exhibits high early lymphatic metastatic potential, CAFs and a high microlymphatic vessel density are associated with poor prognosis.95 Mechanistic studies reveal that CAF-derived exosomes can directly act on tumor-associated lymphatic endothelial cells (TLECs) to promote lymphangiogenesis. Notably, exosomes from CAFs were found to have decreased levels of miR-100-5p compared to those from normal fibroblasts. This downregulation of exosomal miR-100-5p was shown to facilitate lymphangiogenesis by relieving its inhibitory effect on the IGF1R/PI3K/AKT signaling axis in TLECs, thereby promoting their proliferation, migration, and tube-forming capacity both in vitro and in vivo.95 The CAF-mediated regulation of these vascular systems is a hallmark of cancer progression, as corroborated by pan-cancer analyses which identify “inducing angiogenesis” as a core cancer hallmark strongly associated with stromal components like nonmyelinating Schwann cells, which share functional similarities with CAFs in modulating the TME.96 Furthermore, the pro-angiogenic and pro-lymphangiogenic functions of CAFs contribute to establishing an immunosuppressive TME. For example, in cholangiocarcinoma, CAFs are key components of the tumor-immune microenvironment, associated with worse survival due to their role in creating a stiff, poorly immunogenic landscape through extracellular matrix deposition and the release of soluble factors, which can include angiogenic mediators.97 This interplay underscores the multifaceted role of CAFs: by fostering new blood and lymphatic vessel growth, they not only provide physical pathways for metastasis but also shape an environment conducive to immune evasion and treatment resistance. Consequently, targeting CAF-driven angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis, such as by inhibiting specific exosomal miRNAs or their downstream pathways like IGF1R/PI3K/AKT or miR-145-5p/NF-κB, presents a preclinically compelling strategic avenue for developing novel therapeutics aimed at curbing metastasis in GI malignancies.94,95

Mediating the Preparation of Organ-Specific Pre-Metastatic Microenvironments

CAFs are instrumental in orchestrating the formation of organ-specific pre-metastatic niches (PMNs), a critical preparatory step that conditions distant organs for the successful colonization and outgrowth of disseminated tumor cells. This process involves the active secretion of various signaling molecules, including exosomes, cytokines, and growth factors, which remodel the local microenvironment of secondary organs prior to the arrival of metastatic cells.98 Although preclinical KRAS-mutant PDAC mouse models show that primary tumor-derived factors can remodel the lung microenvironment to form weak pre-metastatic niches, clinical data and Fig. 4 consistently demonstrate that the liver is the dominant metastatic site in human pancreatic cancer, and thus represents the primary target of CAF-mediated niche remodeling. More commonly, CAFs contribute to liver pre-metastatic niche formation in gastrointestinal cancers through exosomal transfer, facilitating colonization.99

Exosomes derived from CAFs are particularly crucial vectors for this remote education of pre-metastatic sites. In pancreatic cancer, exosomes from both cancer cells and CAFs facilitate disease progression and organ-specific metastasis, primarily to the liver, with the lungs and peritoneum also being common sites.100 A specific example in CRC metastasis illustrates this mechanism clearly: exosomes derived from CRC cells and carrying integrin beta-like 1 (ITGBL1) are released into the circulation and subsequently taken up by resident fibroblasts in distant organs. These ITGBL1-enriched extracellular vesicles (EVs) activate fibroblasts via the TNFAIP3-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway, prompting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and IL-8. This activation transforms the local stroma into a supportive pre-metastatic niche that is conducive to the growth of subsequently arriving metastatic cells.101 Furthermore, in CRC liver metastasis, cancer cell-derived exosomal HSPC111 reprograms the lipid metabolism of hepatic CAFs by phosphorylating ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY). This leads to acetyl-CoA accumulation and increased H3K27 acetylation, which in turn upregulates CXCL5 secretion from CAFs. CXCL5 not only promotes metastasis but also reinforces exosomal HSPC111 release from CRC cells, forming a feed-forward loop that consolidates the pre-metastatic niche.102 Similarly, in gastric cancer (GC), podoplanin-positive (PDPN+) LTBP1+ CAFs release extracellular vesicles enriched with latent transforming growth factor beta-binding protein 1 (LTBP1). Regulated by YAP signaling, these EVs activate hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) in the liver, converting them into CAF-educated HSCs (CEHs). This activation triggers TGF-β signaling and the subsequent secretion of CCL11, which recruits CCR3⁺ metastatic GC cells to the liver. In this way, a pre-metastatic niche is effectively established to promote hepatic colonization.103

Metabolic reprogramming and inflammatory signaling within the niche are also key functions of CAFs. In PDAC, CAFs and cancer cells expressing CALB2 collaborate with hypoxia to activate an inflammatory fibroblast phenotype. These CALB2-activated CAFs upregulate CALB2 in cancer cells via an IL6-STAT3 signaling loop, and in turn, cancer cell-derived CALB2 activates a Ca2+-CXCL14 inflammatory axis. This cascade promotes metastatic outgrowth and establishes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thereby facilitating PDAC metastasis.104 In HCC, increased matrix stiffness in the primary tumor modulates the cargo of exosomal miRNAs, which are then released to educate the lung pre-metastatic niche. These exosomes reduce glucose uptake and consumption in lung fibroblasts while increasing angiogenesis and vascular permeability, leading to a glucose-enriched niche that supports the colonization and survival of metastatic HCC cells.105 Furthermore, hypoxia in HCC cells enhances the release of exosomes enriched with miR-4508. These exosomes are delivered to lung fibroblasts, where miR-4508 targets and downregulates RFX1. This inhibition results in increased IL17A expression, which activates the p38 MAPK-NF-κB pathway, leading to fibroblast activation and the formation of a lung PMN that promotes HCC pulmonary metastasis.106 These multifaceted mechanisms underscore the central role of CAFs and their secreted factors in meticulously preparing organ-specific pre-metastatic niches, making them compelling targets for therapeutic intervention to prevent metastatic spread. These findings are mainly supported by preclinical models, whereas direct clinical evidence linking specific CAF subsets to organ-specific metastasis remains limited.

Supporting Circulating Tumor Cell Survival and Extravasation

CAFs play a crucial role in supporting the survival of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) during their transit in the bloodstream and in facilitating their extravasation at distant sites. A key mechanism involves the formation of heterotypic clusters between CTCs and circulating CAFs (cCAFs). These heterotypic CTC-CAF clusters exhibit significantly enhanced metastatic potential compared to homotypic CTC clusters or single CTCs.107 The physical association within these clusters provides survival advantages: CAFs can deliver pro-survival signals that protect CTCs from anoikis, shear stress, and immune surveillance in the harsh circulatory environment.108,109 This crosstalk is not merely a passive escort but involves active molecular signaling. CAFs can secrete factors that sustain CTCs, such as laminin-332 (LAM332), which engages integrin ITGA3 on PDAC cells to promote proliferation, suppress apoptosis, and drive the formation of homotypic CTC clusters, collectively enhancing metastatic fitness.110

In pancreatic cancer, specific CAF subtypes secrete factors that can modulate the immune landscape and ECM composition at distant sites, although the precise role of cCAFs in this remote signaling remains an area of active investigation.111,112 The secretion of factors such as CXCL12 by endothelial cells, which is influenced by CAF crosstalk, can further regulate neovascularization and stromal growth at sites of injury or tumor progression, facilitating the establishment of metastases.113 Collectively, through direct clustering and paracrine signaling, CAFs are indispensable partners for CTCs, ensuring their survival during transit through the circulation, facilitating their extravasation, and fostering a receptive environment for metastatic colonization.

CAFs-Mediated Metabolic Reprogramming and Therapeutic Resistance

Metabolic Symbiosis and Nutritional Support

CAFs constitute a vital, heterogeneous compartment within the TME of gastrointestinal cancers. Data from in vitro co-culture systems and animal models support that CAFs regulate tumor progression via metabolic reprogramming and nutrient provision, while clinical specimen analyses only deliver correlative observations without definitive causal proof.114 A central mechanism by which CAFs drive tumor growth and metastasis is the establishment of metabolic symbiosis with malignant cells, widely termed the “reverse Warburg effect”.115 In this metabolic coupling axis, CAFs undergo aerobic glycolysis, a metabolic phenotype featured by robust glucose consumption and massive lactate production even under normoxic conditions.116 Such metabolic rewiring in CAFs generates abundant energy-rich metabolites, including lactate and pyruvate.117 Tumor cells can internalize these metabolites, especially lactate, to fuel mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, which supplies sufficient energy to sustain tumor cell proliferation, survival and invasive capacity.21 This metabolic communication transcends passive substrate exchange; it constitutes a bidirectional regulatory circuit: tumor cells secrete signaling molecules to induce and maintain the glycolytic signature of CAFs, thereby forming a self-amplifying loop that exacerbates malignant behaviors.118 Beyond the canonical lactate shuttle, CAFs also provide vital nutritional support for tumor cells residing in the nutrient-poor, hypoxic core of solid tumors.119 CAFs secrete multiple metabolites indispensable for cancer cell anabolism, such as diverse amino acids and lipids.120 Pancreatic cancer, a typical desmoplastic malignancy sharing pathological similarities with most gastrointestinal tumors, has been investigated extensively in preclinical models. Existing preclinical data reveal that pancreatic CAFs secrete non-essential amino acids and lipid precursors to remodel tumor metabolism; these substrates are essential for biomass synthesis during cell division.120 Relying on the secretory metabolites released by CAFs, tumor cells can overcome metabolic stress and satisfy biosynthetic demands during unrestrained proliferation.121 The metabolic support provided by CAFs is tightly intertwined with their other pro-tumorigenic functions, including ECM remodeling and immune suppression, collectively constructing a permissive microenvironment for metastatic dissemination.122 Targeting this metabolic symbiosis, for example by disrupting lactate transporters or key glycolytic enzymes in CAFs, presents a promising preclinical therapeutic avenue to starve tumor cells and inhibit their metastatic spread.123 A systematic illustration of subtype-specific metabolic pathways across colorectal cancer, esophageal cancer and cholangiocarcinoma is critical for developing precise therapeutic interventions to break the supportive metabolic crosstalk between CAFs and tumor cells. Notably, researchers should clearly distinguish mechanistic conclusions from preclinical experiments and correlative results obtained from human clinical specimens when interpreting relevant metabolic networks.116,119,120

CAF-Driven Physical Barriers and Survival Signaling in Cancer Therapy Resistance

CAFs contribute to chemoresistance in GI cancers by secreting a range of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that activate critical survival pathways in tumor cells.124 A prominent mechanism involved in this process is the activation of the PI3K/Akt and NF-κB signaling axes. For instance, in GC, CAF-derived IL-8 has been identified as a key mediator of oxaliplatin resistance. Activation of the vitamin D receptor (VDR) in GC cells using its ligand calcipotriol has been shown to abrogate this CAF-induced resistance by blocking the IL-8-mediated activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway, thereby restoring chemosensitivity.125 Similarly, in ESCC, exosomes derived from CAFs transfer circular RNA circ_0076535 into cancer cells. This exosomal circRNA acts as a molecular sponge for miR-145-5p, leading to the subsequent activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, which promotes tumor progression and contributes to the development of a therapy-resistant phenotype.94 Beyond direct cytokine signaling, CAFs also modulate cellular metabolism to foster survival. In PDAC, CAFs increase tumor resistance to ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent cell death, by secreting cysteine. This cysteine supports glutathione synthesis in cancer cells, thereby preventing lethal oxidative damage and enhancing survival under therapeutic stress.126 Furthermore, exosomal microRNAs secreted by CAFs play a crucial role in suppressing cell death pathways. In GC, CAFs secrete exosomal miR-522, which is packaged via the USP7/hnRNPA1 axis, particularly upon exposure to cisplatin and paclitaxel. This exosomal miR-522 is transferred to cancer cells, where it inhibits arachidonate lipoxygenase 15 (ALOX15), blocks the accumulation of lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) and ferroptosis, and ultimately leads to the development of acquired chemoresistance.127 These findings collectively underscore that CAFs establish a protective niche through paracrine signaling, which activates intrinsic tumor cell survival cascades and fundamentally diminishes the efficacy of standard chemotherapeutic agents such as 5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin. In addition to paracrine signaling, the dense fibrotic stroma formed by CAFs acts as a physical barrier, impeding the effective penetration of chemotherapeutic drugs. The hallmark desmoplastic reaction, characterized by an excessively dense and fibrotic ECM orchestrated by CAFs, constitutes a formidable physical barrier to drug delivery in GI cancers and directly contributes to chemoresistance.124 This dense stroma, which can comprise over 90% of the tumor mass in cancers like PDAC, mechanically and chemically hinders the perfusion and diffusion of therapeutic agents.128 The stromal architecture is not merely passive; interfacial biophysical cues guide CAF activity and ECM deposition, leading to spatial organization that further isolates tumor cells. Computational modeling of confined co-cultures has revealed that regions of convex curvature enhance edge stress, activating a myofibroblast phenotype in CAFs. This activation drives increased migration and collagen I deposition, resulting in a central “corralling” of cancer cells, which physically sequesters them from therapeutic exposure.128 The clinical consequence of this barrier is evident, as high levels of CAFs and stromal components are correlated with poor therapeutic outcomes.124 Strategies aimed at disrupting this physical barrier are therefore a promising therapeutic avenue in preclinical models. For example, in CCA, a highly desmoplastic cancer, the use of multifunctional iron oxide nanoflowers decorated with gold nanoparticles (GIONF) for photothermal therapy achieved complete tumor regression. The preferential uptake of GIONF by CAFs allowed for their targeted depletion via mild hyperthermia, leading to a significant early reduction in tumor stiffness and normalization of the tumor mechanical environment, thereby overcoming the physical barrier to treatment.129 Similarly, in a PDAC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model, a photothermal-chemotherapeutic agent (Abraxane@MoSe2) was used to induce local hyperthermia. This treatment reduced the number of CAFs (confirmed by decreased α-SMA and vimentin staining), disrupted the stromal architecture, and consequently augmented the synergy of chemotherapy with Abraxane, demonstrating that physical disruption of the stroma can enhance drug efficacy.130 These studies highlight that the CAF-driven fibrotic stroma is a dynamic and targetable physical obstacle, and its modulation is crucial for improving chemotherapeutic drug penetration and efficacy.

CAFs also influence the efficacy of radiotherapy by modulating the DNA damage repair capacity of tumor cells. While the role of CAFs in chemotherapy resistance is well-documented, emerging evidence indicates that they also confer resistance to radiotherapy in GI cancers by regulating tumor cell behavior, including the DNA damage response and pro-survival signaling pathway.124 One key mechanism involved is the CXCL12/CXCR4 chemokine axis. In pancreatic cancer, radiation-resistant cell lines exhibit higher expression of CXCR4 compared to their non-resistant counterparts. The ligand CXCL12, which can be secreted by stromal cells such as fibroblasts, enhances the invasive ability of these resistant cells. Co-culture with fibroblasts or treatment with CXCL12 further augments this invasive phenotype, which can be suppressed by a CXCR4 antagonist. Moreover, irradiation following CXCR4 antagonist treatment has been shown to suppress the colonization capacity of radiation-resistant cells, implicating the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis as a critical mediator of radioresistance.131 Beyond direct signaling, CAFs contribute to a pro-tumorigenic and radioresistant microenvironment through exosomal communication and the induction of cancer stem-like cells (CSCs). In biliary tract cancer (BTC), the protein Transgelin-2 (TAGLN2) is highly expressed in CSCs and in CAFs within the tumor stroma. Silencing of TAGLN2 in BTC cell lines not only reduces chemoresistance but also decreases radioresistance and CSC features such as clonogenicity. Patients with a high stromal TAGLN2 expression index have shorter disease-free survival, suggesting that CAF-derived factors such as TAGLN2 are crucial for maintaining a stem-like, therapy-resistant cell population.132 Furthermore, the inflammatory secretome of CAFs, often referred to as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), can be driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhances tumor progression. In gastric cancer, inflammation-driven downregulation of EZH2 in CAFs maintains the SASP through histone modification (H3K27me3 demethylation). This SASP, mediated via JAK/STAT3 signaling, was shown to enhance peritoneal tumor formation in vivo, and a JAK/STAT3 inhibitor blocked the increase in cancer cell viability induced by senescent CAFs, pointing to a pathway that likely impacts response to various therapies, including radiotherapy.133 Additionally, in the context of pancreatic cancer, neutrophil-derived IL-1β has been found to induce inflammatory CAF polarization, which in turn activates IL-6/STAT-3 signaling in tumor cells. Attenuating this neutrophil-CAF-tumor cell axis not only improves chemotherapy response but also involves modulation of the tumor microenvironment in a way that could influence radiosensitivity.134 These findings illustrate that CAFs promote radioresistance through multifaceted mechanisms, including the activation of specific survival pathways (CXCL12/CXCR4, STAT3), support of CSC populations, and creation of an inflammatory, protective niche that compromises the cytotoxic effects of radiotherapy.

Leading to Resistance in Targeted and Immunotherapy

CAFs are key drivers of therapeutic resistance across gastrointestinal malignancies, acting through dual pathways: neutralizing targeted drug activity and constructing an immunosuppressive microenvironment that blunts immunotherapy efficacy.124 For targeted therapy resistance, CAFs supply compensatory growth and survival signals to help tumor cells bypass drug-inhibited signaling axes. GISTs frequently develop resistance to imatinib, a first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitor. Preclinical evidence identifies a CAF-initiated paracrine cascade: CAF-secreted TGFβ1 upregulates the CCN2/Rack1 axis in GIST cells, further activating the PI3K/AKT cascade to trigger PGK1 phosphorylation and mitochondrial translocation. PGK1-driven metabolic rewiring elevates glycolysis and sustains cancer cell survival, ultimately conferring IM resistance.135 Beyond GISTs, CAFs broadly mediate chemo- and radioresistance throughout gastrointestinal cancers via diverse heterogeneous mechanisms. These include secretion of cytokines, chemokines and growth factors, as well as exosomal transfer of resistance-related microRNAs (miR-92a, miR-106b) to tumor cells. As elaborated in Section 6.2, the dense desmoplastic stroma assembled by CAFs additionally acts as a physical barrier hindering drug delivery. Heterogeneity across distinct CAF subpopulations further complicates efforts to identify universal resistance mechanisms. In pancreatic cancer, an aggressive fibrotic gastrointestinal tumor, stromal CAFs remodel cancer cell biology and blunt chemotherapy responses.136 Large cohort analyses of gastric cancer confirm that high CAF infiltration correlates with weak tumor immunogenicity, frequent immune escape, and poor ICI response.136 CAFs shape this suppressive niche via multiple secreted mediators; for example, stroma-specific SERPINE2 in gastric fibroblasts promotes protumorigenic and immune-inhibitory conditions. Pan-gastrointestinal cancer datasets further validate SERPINE2’s pan-cancer immunoregulatory function, and real-world immunotherapy cohorts support its utility as a biomarker for diminished treatment response, verifying CAF-derived secretomes as direct drivers of ICI resistance. CAF-mediated immune suppression relies on pleiotropic, multi-layered mechanisms. Aberrant Hedgehog (HH) signaling within gastrointestinal tumor microenvironments facilitates immune tolerance. TME inflammatory mediators including IL-6 and IFN-γ, together with macrophages and T cell subsets, modulate HH activity to sustain tolerogenic conditions, wherein CAFs engage in extensive crosstalk with all immune cell lineages.137 In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), CAFs dominate the stromal compartment and suppress anti-tumor immunity to accelerate disease progression. Importantly, CAF heterogeneity introduces functional divergence: certain subsets exert tumor-promoting, immune-inhibitory effects, whereas others restrict malignant progression, ruling out uniform immune-suppressive activity across all CAFs.138 Moreover, the formation of the premetastatic niche is intimately linked to CAF-driven immune remodeling, creating a hospitable environment for disseminated tumor cells and further dampening systemic anti-tumor immunity.139 CAF-mediated immunotherapy resistance can be conceptualized through two non-mutually exclusive phenotypes: the immune-excluded phenotype, in which CD8+ T cells are present in the tumor periphery but physically sequestered within the peritumoral stroma, and the immune-desert phenotype, characterized by scarce T-cell infiltration due to defective priming or trafficking.140 In GI cancers, CAF-derived TGF-β, dense collagen deposition, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure collaboratively create a stiff, immunosuppressive stromal barrier that drives immune exclusion and underlies resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in tumors such as MSS colorectal cancer, PDAC, cholangiocarcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma.141 Clinically, tumors with high CAF infiltration and an immune-excluded signature show reduced response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, whereas strategies that normalize the stroma or deplete pro-tumorigenic CAF subsets can restore T-cell infiltration and enhance ICI activity. To overcome this, rational combination strategies, such as pairing TGF-β inhibitors, FAP-targeted agents, or CXCL12/CXCR4 blockers with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies, are being actively investigated to dismantle the physical and biochemical barriers, converting immunologically “cold” GI tumors into “hot” ones. CAF-mediated immunotherapy resistance is particularly clinically relevant in tumors exhibiting an immune-excluded phenotype, where CD8+ T cells are trapped in the stromal compartment by dense CAF-deposited extracellular matrix rather than absent from the tumor parenchyma (immune-desert phenotype). This distinction carries important therapeutic implications: strategies that deplete or reprogram CAFs to facilitate T-cell infiltration into tumor parenchyma may be more effective in immune-excluded tumors, whereas immune-desert tumors may require additional approaches to generate de novo T-cell responses. Across gastrointestinal malignancies, CAF-driven immune exclusion is most clinically consequential in microsatellite-stable (MSS) colorectal cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma, tumor types that consistently show poor responses to ICIs despite harboring tumor antigens.142–144 In MSS colorectal cancer, CAF-enriched stroma physically restricts T-cell access to malignant epithelium via chondroitin-6-sulfate-mediated mechanisms.89 In PDAC, periductal fibroblast density defines a CD44-dependent stromal checkpoint that determines lymphocyte proximity to tumor cells.85 In these settings, CAF-derived TGF-β and CXCL12 actively contribute to T-cell exclusion while promoting an immunosuppressive milieu.140,141 Consequently, rational combination of CAF-targeted stroma-modulating agents with ICIs represents a clinically rational strategy to convert immunologically “cold” or immune-excluded tumors into T-cell-inflamed phenotypes that are amenable to checkpoint blockade.

Therapeutic Strategies Targeting CAFs in Cancer Treatment

Small Molecule Inhibitors Targeting CAF Activation and Survival Pathways

Targeting the activation and survival pathways of CAFs using small molecule inhibitors has been proposed as a strategy to disrupt the tumor-promoting stromal microenvironment. Among these targeted approaches, inhibitors of the TGF-β signaling pathway have shown potential in preclinical and early clinical studies. As a key regulator of CAF differentiation and activation, TGF-β drives the acquisition of a myofibroblastic phenotype, which is characterized by the expression of α-SMA and excessive production of ECM components. Blocking TGF-β signaling, for instance, with Galunisertib, a selective inhibitor of TGF-β receptor I, has been shown to effectively deactivate CAFs, reduce tumor-associated fibrosis, and enhance the efficacy of conventional chemotherapy in various preclinical models of gastrointestinal tumors. This therapeutic effect has been explored particularly in PDAC and HCC, where a dense, fibrotic stroma is a key contributor to therapy resistance and poor clinical outcomes.

In PDAC, targeting stromal TGF-β signaling can overcome resistance to KRAS inhibitors.145 Furthermore, inhibition of TGF-β signaling in CAFs has been shown to reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment, increasing T cell infiltration and potentially synergizing with ICIs.146 Thus, TGF-β inhibitors aim to normalize the CAF population and the associated fibrotic stroma, thereby improving drug delivery and anti-tumor immunity.

Another critical approach involves targeting fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a cell surface serine protease highly expressed on activated CAFs. FAP inhibitors (FAPIs) are designed to interfere with CAF function and survival. While FAP is a selective marker for CAFs and plays a role in ECM remodeling, clinical trials with FAP-targeted therapies have often shown limited efficacy as monotherapies, frequently necessitating combination with chemotherapy or other agents.147 Notably, the development of FAP inhibitors has advanced significantly in the field of diagnostic imaging: FAPI-based positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) offers high-contrast visualization of the tumor stroma and has been shown to outperform 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) PET/CT in detecting certain malignancies, including PDAC and CRC.148 Beyond diagnostics, FAP inhibitors are being explored for targeted radiotherapy (theranostics).149 However, the functional complexity of CAFs presents a major challenge: simply depleting FAP-positive CAFs may not yield meaningful clinical benefits, as emerging evidence indicates that CAFs also exert tumor-restricting functions under certain contexts. Consequently, current research is shifting towards strategies that modulate CAF phenotypes rather than eliminate them entirely. For instance, FAP-targeted fluorescent probes have enabled detailed in vivo studies of CAF biology, providing insights into their heterogeneous functions within the tumor microenvironment.150 Additionally, FAP-directed nanoplatforms and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are being actively investigated to deliver cytotoxic payloads specifically to pro-tumorigenic CAFs, thereby disrupting their tumor-promoting functions while sparing CAF subpopulations with potential protective roles.151

Inhibitors of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) such as platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) and fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR), which are often aberrantly activated in CAFs, constitute another major class. The multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitor Imatinib, which targets PDGFR among others, has been studied to disrupt CAF survival and function. PDGFR-β signaling, in particular, is crucial for CAF activation and maintenance. Similarly, in CRC, inhibition of the PDGFR pathway can prevent CAF activation induced by CSF1R inhibitors, thereby rescuing therapeutic efficacy.152 However, similar to FAP inhibitors, the efficacy of PDGFR inhibitors as single agents is often limited, and they are more effective in combination regimens. For instance, the PDGFR inhibitor CP-673451 has shown promise in inducing apoptosis in cholangiocarcinoma cells.153 However, a significant challenge is the toxicity profile of FGFR inhibitors, which includes distinct ocular, dermatologic, and metabolic adverse events that require careful management.154–156 Furthermore, resistance to FGFR inhibitors can emerge through mechanisms such as activation of alternative pathways (e.g, MET/Pyk2 axis).157

The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway is another mediator of tumor-stroma crosstalk, particularly in pancreatic cancer. Inhibitors like Vismodegib aim to disrupt the interaction between tumor cells and CAFs. In PDAC, tumor cells secrete Sonic Hedgehog ligand, which activates Hh signaling in stromal cells, leading to desmoplasia. Clinical trials with Hh inhibitors in pancreatic cancer have yielded mixed results, sometimes showing no benefit or even potential detriment, possibly due to the complex, context-dependent roles of the stroma which can sometimes restrain tumor growth.158 This underscores a fundamental challenge in CAF-targeted therapy: CAFs are highly heterogeneous, with subpopulations exerting both pro-tumor and anti-tumor functions. For example, in gastric cancer, distinct CAF subsets like iCAFs and extracellular matrix CAFs (eCAFs) promote tumor progression and immune evasion through different mechanisms.159 Other novel targets include nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (NNMT),160 sirtuin 1 (SIRT1)161 and hypoxia up-regulated protein 1 (HYOU1)162 which are involved in maintaining the CAF phenotype, metabolic reprogramming, and promoting tumor progression. The integration of these small molecule inhibitors with conventional therapies, immunotherapy, or nanotechnology-based delivery systems represents a promising avenue for modulating the tumor microenvironment and improving outcomes in GI tumors.

Drugs Targeting CAF Effector Molecules

Drugs targeting ECM components, such as collagen cross-linking inhibitors (LOXL2 antibody Simtuzumab) or hyaluronidases (PEGPH20), aim to soften the stroma and promote drug penetration, but later-stage clinical trials failed to significantly improve overall survival, suggesting the need for precise patient selection and combination therapies. Targeting the dense and remodeled ECM produced by CAFs represents a direct strategy to overcome the physical barrier to drug delivery in solid tumors. Agents like the lysyl oxidase-like 2 (LOXL2) inhibitor Simtuzumab and the hyaluronidase PEGylated recombinant human hyaluronidase PH20 (PEGPH20) were developed to degrade or prevent the cross-linking of key ECM components, such as collagen and hyaluronic acid, thereby softening the tumor stroma, reducing interstitial fluid pressure, and theoretically enhancing the penetration of chemotherapeutic or targeted agents. The rationale is grounded in the understanding that the TME, particularly in desmoplastic cancers, is a complex mix of stromal cells, fibroblasts, and a stiff ECM that physically impedes treatment efficacy.163,164 However, pivotal late-stage clinical trials for these agents have largely failed to demonstrate significant clinical benefit. The Phase III HALO-301 trial evaluating PEGPH20 in combination with nab-paclitaxel/gemcitabine in hyaluronan-high metastatic PDAC did not meet its primary endpoint of improved overall survival.165 Similarly, the Phase II trial of simtuzumab in combination with gemcitabine showed no improvement in progression-free survival in metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma.166 These clinical setbacks underscore the complexity of the TME and suggest that merely disrupting ECM architecture is insufficient. The lack of efficacy may be attributed to several factors, including the activation of compensatory pathways by CAFs, the heterogeneity of ECM composition across different tumors and patients, and the potential for these therapies to inadvertently promote tumor progression by facilitating cancer cell invasion once the physical barrier is removed. Consequently, future efforts must focus on the precise stratification of patients based on biomarkers of a hyaluronan-rich or highly cross-linked stroma and on rational combination strategies. For instance, combining ECM-targeting drugs with agents that simultaneously inhibit CAF activation or their pro-tumorigenic secretome, or with immunotherapies to counteract potential increased immunosuppression, may yield better outcomes. The experience with these agents highlights a critical lesson in TME-targeted therapy: modulating a single component of this highly adaptive ecosystem often leads to resistance or lack of clinical benefit, necessitating a more integrated and patient-specific therapeutic approach.

Antibodies targeting key cytokines/chemokines secreted by CAFs, such as anti-IL-6 antibody (Siltuximab), anti-CXCL12 antibody (Ulocuplumab), or CXCR4 antagonists (Plerixafor), are being explored to block CAF-mediated pro-metastatic and immunosuppressive signaling pathway. Beyond the physical ECM barrier, CAFs exert a profound influence on tumor progression and therapy resistance through their extensive secretome of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. Targeting these soluble effector molecules is conceptually promising strategy to disrupt the pro-tumorigenic signaling networks orchestrated by CAFs, although most agents in this class remain in early-phase clinical evaluation. For example, IL-6 is a major cytokine secreted by activated CAFs that can promote cancer cell proliferation, survival, and stemness, while also contributing to an immunosuppressive TME by influencing myeloid and lymphoid cell functions.167 The anti-IL-6 monoclonal antibody Siltuximab aims to neutralize this pathway. Similarly, the CXCL12 (SDF-1)/CXCR4 axis is a critical chemokine pathway often upregulated in the CAF-rich TME. CXCL12 secreted by CAFs can directly stimulate tumor cell growth and metastasis, as seen in GIST where CAF-derived platelet-derived growth factor C (PDGFC) promotes metastasis, a process that may involve coordinated chemokine signaling.60 Furthermore, this axis is a potent recruiter of immunosuppressive cells and can sequester T cells away from tumor cells, contributing to T-cell exclusion and dysfunction.163 Agents like the anti-CXCL12 antibody Ulocuplumab or the CXCR4 antagonist Plerixafor (AMD3100) are designed to block this interaction. By disrupting CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling, these drugs aim to inhibit CAF-mediated tumor growth, reduce metastasis, and potentially improve immune cell infiltration and function. This approach is particularly relevant given that CAFs are a key source of factors that drive T-cell exhaustion, a state of dysfunction characterized by upregulated inhibitory receptors like PD-1 and Tim-3.163 Furthermore, targeting upstream regulators of CAF secretome, such as protein SUMOylation in cholangiocarcinoma, can impair CAF growth and alter their protein secretome, disrupting the tumor-stroma cross-talk.70 Thus, intercepting CAF-derived cytokine and chemokine signals represents a multifaceted strategy to cripple the tumor-promoting and immune-evading functions of the TME.

Translational and Cellular Therapies Targeting CAFs

Gene-engineered CAFs or mesenchymal stem cells are being utilized as drug delivery vehicles to target oncolytic viruses or therapeutic genes to tumor sites. This approach leverages the inherent tumor-homing properties of these stromal cells to overcome delivery barriers and enhance therapeutic specificity. For instance, research has demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) can be activated and reprogrammed into CAFs by cues from cancer cells, including exosomes derived from gastric cancer cells.49 These activated cells exhibit a sustained pro-tumorigenic phenotype, making them potential Trojan horses for targeted therapy. The rationale is to modify these cells ex vivo to carry cytotoxic payloads, such as oncolytic viruses that selectively replicate in and lyse cancer cells, or genes encoding prodrug-converting enzymes, and then reintroduce them into the patient. Their natural propensity to migrate to and integrate within the tumor stroma could facilitate the localized release of therapeutic agents, thereby increasing intratumoral drug concentration while minimizing systemic toxicity. This strategy is particularly relevant in GI cancers, where the dense, desmoplastic stroma, largely composed of CAFs, often acts as a physical and biochemical barrier to conventional drug penetration.124 By co-opting the very cells that contribute to this barrier, engineered CAF-based delivery systems offer a investigational method to remodel the tumor microenvironment and improve treatment efficacy.

CAR-T cell therapy targeting CAF-specific antigens, such as Fibroblast Activation Protein (FAP), is under preclinical investigation with the aim of directly eliminating pro-tumorigenic CAFs and reshaping the tumor microenvironment. CAFs are a major component of the TME in gastrointestinal cancers and are key drivers of tumor progression, immune suppression, and therapy resistance.91 FAP is a cell-surface serine protease highly expressed on activated CAFs but with limited expression in normal adult tissues.168 Preclinical studies are exploring the development of FAP-directed CAR-T cells designed to recognize and lyse FAP-positive CAFs. The anticipated outcome of depleting pro-tumorigenic CAFs is multi-faceted: it could disrupt the physical and signaling support for cancer cells, reduce extracellular matrix deposition that impedes drug delivery and immune cell infiltration, and alleviate the immunosuppressive signals that CAFs secrete.169 The development of FAP-targeted vaccines faces significant hurdles, including ensuring sufficient immunogenicity of the peptide, achieving specific targeting of pathogenic CAF subsets without affecting essential normal fibroblasts, and overcoming the inherently immunosuppressive nature of the TME that may inhibit vaccine-induced effector cells. Furthermore, the dynamic and plastic nature of CAFs means that targeting a single antigen like FAP might not address all pro-tumorigenic CAF subpopulations, as evidenced by the identification of diverse CAF subtypes with distinct markers and functions in cancers like gastric and CRC.170 Despite these challenges, early-phase research continues to explore the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of this approach as a component of multi-modal cancer therapy. However, FAP-targeted CAR-T therapy faces significant safety challenges that must be carefully addressed. Preclinical studies have shown that systemic depletion of FAP+ stromal cells can lead to fatal cachexia and bone marrow toxicity.171 The on-target, off-tumor toxicity arises from FAP expression in bone marrow stromal cells and other normal tissues. Future strategies to mitigate these risks include incorporating inducible suicide switches, using tissue-specific promoters, and combining with agents that counteract cachexia. Therefore, future strategies must focus on optimizing target specificity such as identifying combinatorial antigens, restricting CAR-T activity to the tumor site, or using transient effectors, to avoid these severe adverse events. For example, in colorectal cancer, CAFs have been shown to promote an immunosuppressive TME and limit the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.8

FAP peptide vaccines are designed to induce a host immune response against CAFs and are currently in early-stage research. This immunotherapeutic strategy aims to generate endogenous, antigen-specific T cells and/or antibodies that can recognize and eliminate FAP-expressing CAFs within the TME. The concept involves administering FAP-derived peptides, often in combination with immune adjuvants, to prime the patient’s immune system. The goal is to break immune tolerance to this self-antigen and establish a durable, systemic immune response capable of targeting the CAF compartment in established tumors. By eradicating or functionally modulating CAFs, such a vaccine could theoretically reverse many of the tumor-promoting functions of the stroma. In gastrointestinal cancers, CAFs are implicated in creating an immunosuppressive niche, promoting angiogenesis, and facilitating metastasis.92 A successful FAP vaccine could therefore work synergistically with other treatments, such as chemotherapy or immune checkpoint blockade, by remodeling the TME to be less hostile to anti-tumor immunity. For instance, studies have shown that CAFs can suppress T cell proliferation and function, and their depletion or modulation can enhance responses to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade.169 The development of FAP-targeted vaccines faces significant hurdles, including ensuring sufficient immunogenicity of the peptide, achieving specific targeting of pathogenic CAF subsets without affecting essential normal fibroblasts, and overcoming the inherently immunosuppressive nature of the TME that may inhibit vaccine-induced effector cells. Furthermore, the dynamic and plastic nature of CAFs means that targeting a single antigen like FAP might not address all pro-tumorigenic CAF subpopulations, as evidenced by the identification of diverse CAF subtypes with distinct markers and functions in cancers like gastric and colorectal cancer.170 Despite these challenges, early-phase research continues to explore the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of this approach as a component of multi-modal cancer therapy.

Challenges and Future Directions

Precision Targeting Strategies to Address CAF Heterogeneity

The profound functional heterogeneity of CAFs necessitates a paradigm shift from broad stromal depletion to precision targeting strategies that distinguish between pro-tumorigenic and potentially tumor-restraining subpopulations. Future drug development must focus on identifying and exploiting subpopulation-specific markers and targets. Across multiple gastrointestinal cancers, CAFs consist of functionally opposite subtypes: in PDAC, IL‑6‑rich, tumor‑distal iCAFs facilitate tumor growth and immune suppression, while MHC II-positive apCAFs may restrain tumors via CD4+ T cell interaction, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely defined.172 Therefore, a major research direction involves the comprehensive molecular profiling of CAFs to define reliable surface markers or intracellular signaling pathways unique to each functional subset. For example, in gastric cancer, specific CAF subgroups positive for markers like FAP, CD10, and GPR77 have been linked to chemotherapy resistance by inducing EMT and cancer stemness.173 Targeting such specific subpopulations, rather than all α-SMA-positive fibroblasts, could mitigate the tumor-promoting effects observed in some preclinical models where indiscriminate CAF depletion exacerbated cancer progression.83 Emerging therapeutic modalities, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells engineered to recognize CAF-specific surface antigens like FAP, a promising but still preclinical avenue for precision targeting.174 However, the success of such approaches hinges on the identification of targets that are not only specific to the pathogenic CAF subset but also absent or minimally expressed on critical normal tissues, such as pericytes and bone marrow stromal cells, to avoid on-target, off-tumor toxicity.

Spatial multi-omics technologies are indispensable for translating CAF heterogeneity into actionable therapeutic blueprints by resolving the precise anatomical distribution of CAF subtypes and their dynamic interaction networks with tumor and immune cells. The tumor microenvironment is not a homogeneous mixture but a spatially organized ecosystem where cellular crosstalk dictates function. In PDAC, for example, myCAFs are typically found in direct contact with tumor cells in periglandular areas, while iCAFs reside in regions more distant from the epithelium.172 This spatial segregation suggests that different CAF subtypes are educated by distinct microenvironmental cues and, in turn, exert localized effects. Understanding these spatial relationships is critical for designing interventions. For instance, a therapy aimed at disrupting pro-fibrotic myCAFs might need to penetrate the dense peritumoral stroma, whereas an immunomodulatory strategy targeting iCAFs could be effective in the broader stromal compartment. Multi-omics integration further reveals the communication circuits between CAFs and other cells. In gastric cancer, single-cell RNA sequencing has delineated how specific CAF subsets, such as iCAFs and extracellular matrix CAFs (eCAFs), mobilize immune cells by secreting factors like IL-6, CXCL12, and periostin (POSTN) to construct an immunosuppressive niche favorable for invasion.159 By providing a high-resolution map of these ligand-receptor interactions and signaling pathways, spatial multi-omics can identify critical nodal points for intervention. For example, targeting the CXCL10-CXCR3 axis, which mediates CAF differentiation and activation in colon cancer, could be a strategy to disrupt a specific tumor-promoting circuit.175 Furthermore, these technologies can model the evolution of the TME under therapeutic pressure, predicting mechanisms of adaptive resistance. The ultimate goal is to use this spatial and molecular blueprint to guide combination therapies, such as pairing a CAF-subtype-targeted agent with an immune checkpoint inhibitor or chemotherapy, in a rational, spatially informed manner to remodel the tumor microenvironment, overcome stromal barriers to drug delivery, and ultimately improve therapeutic efficacy in GI and other solid tumors.

Optimizing Combination Therapy Strategies

Combining CAF-targeted agents with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy or immunotherapy is a key trend to overcome resistance and improve efficacy in GI cancers. CAFs shape a tumor-promoting TME that drives immunosuppression, angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis, leading to treatment failure. For example, anti-fibrotic drugs plus ICIs can break down both the physical and immunosuppressive barriers formed by CAFs: dense ECM blocks drug penetration and immune cell infiltration, while CAF-secreted factors directly suppress anti-tumor immunity.91 A phase II trial combining lenvatinib and pembrolizumab in well‑differentiated NETs supports this rationale but failed to meet its efficacy endpoint, underscoring ongoing exploration of such synergistic strategies.176 Similarly, the novel tyrosine kinase inhibitor surufatinib, which inhibits VEGFR, FGFR1, and CSF-1R, is designed to concurrently inhibit tumor angiogenesis and modulate tumor-immune evasion, potentially decreasing resistance and making it a candidate for combination regimens.177 Optimal adjustment of timing, sequence and dosage for combined therapies relies on thorough knowledge of CAF dynamics and heterogeneity. Single-cell transcriptomics identifies functionally distinct CAF subsets governing treatment response and resistance, and the dynamic crosstalk between CAFs and immune cells shapes the immunosuppressive TME, making the sequential order of CAF-targeted therapy and immunotherapy extremely vital.91 For example, in CCA, where CAFs are key components of an immune-suppressive TME associated with poor survival, combining therapies that target CAFs or their pathways with ICIs is a logical step forward.97 Advanced imaging techniques, such as dual-tracer PET/CT using [18F]FDG and [68Ga]Ga-FAPI-46, which targets fibroblast activation protein (FAP) on CAFs, can provide superior visualization of tumor volume and may be instrumental in pre-therapeutic staging and in monitoring the dynamic changes in CAF activity during combination therapy, thereby informing optimized treatment schedules.178 This is echoed in GISTs, where [18F] FAPI-42 PET/CT offers complementary value to [18F]FDG PET/CT in predicting treatment response, highlighting the potential of imaging CAF activity to guide therapeutic decisions.179 Ultimately, effective combination strategies may involve targeting specific CAF-derived signaling pathways, such as the Akt/PI3K/mTOR pathway, which has been implicated in fibrotic diseases and could be targeted by agents like sunitinib, or the FGFR signaling axis, which is involved in resistance mechanisms in cancers like GIST and CCA.97,180 The future of optimizing these regimens lies in leveraging a mechanistic understanding of CAF biology, utilizing advanced diagnostic tools to track TME evolution, and designing clinical trials that rationally combine CAF modulation with other therapeutic pillars to dismantle the supportive infrastructure of GI tumors.

Development of Novel Mechanism-of-Action Drugs

The development of novel therapeutic agents that can reprogram CAFs from a tumor-promoting to a tumor-restraining or quiescent phenotype, rather than simply depleting them, represents a promising strategy that warrants rigorous clinical evaluation to maintain tissue homeostasis and improve treatment efficacy in GI cancers.24 This approach, often termed “re-polarization” or “normalization,” aims to disrupt the pro-tumorigenic functions of CAFs while preserving their potential homeostatic roles. For instance, in GISTs, CAFs promote imatinib resistance through a TGFβ1/CCN2/Rack1/PGK1 signaling axis that drives metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells.135 Targeting this CAF-mediated metabolic crosstalk, perhaps by inhibiting key nodes like PGK1 or disrupting the TGF-β1 signaling loop, could reverse the activated, pro-resistance CAF state. Similarly, in gastric cancer, CAF-derived SERPINE2 fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment that contributes to immunotherapy resistance.136 Therapeutic strategies that block SERPINE2 or its downstream effects could potentially “normalize” the CAF population, thereby restoring immune cell function and sensitizing tumors to checkpoint inhibitors. Beyond direct signaling inhibition, epigenetic reprogramming of CAFs is an emerging avenue. The distinct molecular signatures and functional plasticity of CAF subtypes, as revealed by single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, suggest that their phenotypes are governed by reversible epigenetic programs.24 Consequently, agents that modulate these epigenetic landscapes could coax tumor-promoting CAFs into a more quiescent or even tumor-suppressive state. This is particularly relevant in digestive system cancers where CAFs contribute to progression through extracellular matrix remodeling, immunomodulation, and angiogenesis.181 Natural compounds and other small molecules that interfere with key CAF activation pathways, such as TGF-β or Wnt signaling, are under investigation for their potential to reverse CAF activation.181 The rationale for this reprogramming strategy over ablation is strong; indiscriminate CAF depletion has shown limited clinical success and may even be detrimental by disrupting tissue architecture or eliminating potentially beneficial CAF subsets.181 Therefore, the future of CAF-targeted therapy lies in developing precision agents capable of functionally “re-educating” the tumor stroma, thereby reversing its supportive role in metastasis, drug resistance, and immune evasion across GI malignancies.

Concurrently, exploring novel interventions that target the communication hubs between CAFs and other cells in the tumor microenvironment, such as exosomes and gap junctions, is a critical frontier.139 Exosomes, a key subtype of extracellular vesicles (EVs), are pivotal mediators of the intricate cross-talk that regulates liver metastasis and drug resistance in GI cancers.139 For example, in CRC, cancer cell-derived exosomes packaged with miR-99a-5p can reprogram normal lung fibroblasts into activated CAFs by targeting NLRP2 and activating the NF-κB pathway, thereby creating a pro-inflammatory pre-metastatic niche that facilitates metastasis.182 This suggests that repurposing existing kinase inhibitors could disrupt deleterious stromal communication. In pancreatic cancer, tumor-derived exosomes from hypoxic conditions can induce fibroblast activation and premetastatic niche formation in the lungs via ERK1/2-NF-κB signaling, a process that can be inhibited by agents like oleanolic acid.183 Furthermore, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) promote liver micrometastasis by activating hepatic stellate cells into CAFs, indicating that targeting NET formation (e.g, with DNase I) is another strategy to interrupt CAF-mediated metastatic communication.184 The development of drugs that specifically inhibit these communication hubs, whether by blocking EV uptake, neutralizing key CAF-secreted factors like TGFβ or CCL7, or disrupting physical connections like gap junctions, holds immense potential to dismantle the supportive network that CAFs provide to tumor cells.24 By focusing on these dynamic interfaces rather than static cellular targets, next-generation therapies can more effectively disrupt the cooperative ecosystem that drives GI cancer progression and therapy resistance.

As this is a narrative review rather than a systematic review, it has inherent limitations. The available literature on CAFs is highly heterogeneous, with a predominance of preclinical data that may not fully recapitulate human GI cancer biology. Comparing findings across different tumor types is challenging due to variability in CAF and CAF-subtype definitions between studies and the lack of standardized markers for identifying CAF populations. Furthermore, many biologically attractive CAF-targeted strategies discussed here have yet to demonstrate clear clinical benefit in large-scale randomized trials. In particular, broad CAF-depleting approaches, such as Hedgehog-pathway inhibitors or non-selective FAP-targeted cell therapies, have yielded mixed or disappointing clinical results, underscoring the risk of inadvertently removing tumor-restraining stromal subsets. Readers should interpret these preclinical observations with caution, acknowledging the substantial gap between mechanistic discovery and clinical translation.

Conclusion

The role of CAFs in driving metastasis in gastrointestinal cancers represents a significant advance in our understanding of tumor biology. As central orchestrators of the tumor microenvironment, CAFs extend their influence far beyond passive structural support, actively remodeling the extracellular matrix, fostering an immunosuppressive niche, reprogramming metabolic pathways, and conferring resistance to conventional therapies. This multifaceted involvement underscores their position as critical therapeutic targets, yet the path to effective clinical translation is fraught with complexity, primarily due to the profound heterogeneity inherent within the CAF population.

From an expert perspective, the evolution of CAF research reflects a journey from recognizing a uniform stromal component to appreciating a diverse ecosystem of functionally distinct subpopulations. This heterogeneity is not merely academic; it is the fundamental determinant of CAFs’ functional complexity, encompassing both tumor-promoting and, in certain contexts, tumor-restraining activities. The historical challenge of targeting CAFs as a monolithic entity, exemplified by the ambiguous outcomes of broad anti-fibrotic approaches, has given way to a more nuanced strategy. The future lies in precise, subtype-specific targeting of CAFs. While FAP serves as a useful therapeutic anchor and a target for cell therapies, its expression across diverse CAF subpopulations and normal tissues demands better stratification. While FAP remains a valuable therapeutic target and diagnostic biomarker, its utility as a standalone target is limited by CAF heterogeneity, potential off-tumor toxicity, and lack of durable responses as monotherapy. Future strategies should focus on identifying more specific subpopulation markers and rational combination therapies. The development of therapies that selectively deplete or reprogram specific CAF subsets, while sparing others that may have homeostatic or anti-tumor potential, is the next frontier. This precision approach necessitates a parallel investment in discovering robust biomarkers for patient stratification, ensuring that the right CAF-targeted agent is delivered to the right patient.

Balancing the diverse and sometimes contradictory research perspectives on CAF-targeted therapies is crucial. Early enthusiasm for single-agent strategies, such as TGF-β pathway inhibitors or cytokine antagonists, has been tempered by clinical realities showing limited efficacy and potential for adverse remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. These findings do not invalidate the target but highlight the need for strategic integration. The most clinically promising avenue is the rational combination of CAF-modulating agents with established modalities, particularly immunotherapy. The logic is compelling: by dismantling the CAF-mediated physical and immunological barriers, such as dense desmoplastic stroma and exclusion of cytotoxic T cells, these combinations can resensitize GI tumors to immune checkpoint blockade. Similarly, combining CAF-targeted drugs with chemotherapy or radiotherapy may disrupt the protective niche that CAFs provide to cancer cells, thereby overcoming therapeutic resistance. The key is to view CAFs not as a standalone target but as a leverage point to enhance the efficacy of the entire therapeutic arsenal in GI malignancies.

Looking forward, the trajectory of this field must be guided by several interconnected priorities. First, a deeper mechanistic dissection of the molecular drivers of CAF heterogeneity and their dynamic evolution during the metastatic cascade is essential. Single-cell multi-omics technologies will be instrumental in mapping these trajectories. Second, this molecular understanding must fuel the development of next-generation, highly specific pharmacological and possibly cell-based tools to intervene. Third, and perhaps most critically, success hinges on sophisticated clinical trial design that incorporates biomarker-driven patient selection and tests intelligent combination regimens from the outset.

In conclusion, CAFs in gastrointestinal cancers are formidable adversaries in metastasis but also hold the key to novel therapeutic breakthroughs. The challenge is to transform them from ubiquitous facilitators of tumor progression into therapeutic vulnerabilities. This will be achieved not through indiscriminate stromal ablation, but through a sophisticated strategy that respects their heterogeneity, disrupts their pro-metastatic alliances within the tumor microenvironment, and synergizes with complementary treatment modalities. Through these efforts, the tumor-stroma balance may be shifted, ultimately translating our growing knowledge of CAF biology into tangible improvements in outcomes for patients with metastatic GI cancers.
