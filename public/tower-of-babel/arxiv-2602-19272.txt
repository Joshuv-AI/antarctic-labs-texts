Time-iteration methods for controllability
Frédéric Marbach

Abstract
These notes are based on a short course delivered at the Summer School EUR MINT 2025 “ Control, Inverse Problems and Spectral Theory ”, held in June 2025 in Toulouse, France. The course presents three important strategies in control theory, formulated as time-iteration methods, where each time step brings the state of the system closer to the desired target. For linear PDEs, we survey the classical Lebeau–Robbiano method and its more recent developments. This method combines spectral inequalities and dissipation estimates to prove null controllability of a dissipative linear system. For nonlinear PDEs, we reinterpret the Liu–Takahashi–Tucsnak method, which establishes local controllability of a nonlinear system by analyzing the control cost of its linearization. We provide an easily applicable black-box formulation of their method. Finally, for nonlinear ODEs, we present the tangent vectors method, which establishes local exact controllability starting from approximately reachable directions.

A prequel on the controllability of ODEs

Controllability refers to the possibility of choosing a time-varying input in an evolution equation to drive the state towards a desired target.
This self-contained prequel highlights a few basic results on the controllability of ODEs.
It is included as a reference for newcomers, and to fix notations.

For broader context, we refer the readers to classical textbooks on mathematical control theory, for example
[
22
,
69
,
109
]
for ODEs,
[
28
,
116
]
for PDEs, or
[
81
]
for optimal control.

In this section, we fix
n,m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
.

1.1
The rank condition for linear systems

We consider the linear time-invariant control system on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\dot{y}(t)=Ay(t)+Bu(t),

(1.1)

where
y(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state,
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the control, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
constant matrices.

Lemma 1.1
.

For any
T>0
, any initial data
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any control
u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
, there exists a unique mild solution
y\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
to (
1.1
) with
y(0)=y_{0}
given by:

y(t)=e^{tA}y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)A}Bu(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(1.2)

Definition 1.2

(Controllability)
.

Let
T>0
.
System (
1.1
) is said to be
controllable in time
T
when, for any initial state
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and any final state
y_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists
u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that the solution to (
1.1
) with
y(0)=y_{0}
satisfies
y(T)=y_{T}
.

The fundamental result of control theory for linear time-invariant systems, dating back to 1960 in
[
70
, Corollary 5.5]
or
[
76
, Theorem 6 and p. 15]
, is that controllability is equivalent to a simple algebraic condition on the matrices
A
and
B
, which happens to be independent of
T
(and
p
).

Theorem 1.3

(Rank condition)
.

Let
T>0
.
System (
1.1
) is controllable in time
T
if and only if

\operatorname{rank}[B,AB,A^{2}B,\dots,A^{n-1}B]=n.

(1.3)

Proof.

Using (
1.2
), one checks that system (
1.1
) is controllable in time
T
if and only if
\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
\mathcal{R}_{T}
denotes the set of reachable states from the origin:

\mathcal{R}_{T}:=\{y(T)\mid y\text{ is a solution to \eqref{eq:lti_system} with }y(0)=0\}.

(1.4)

Thus
\mathcal{R}_{T}
is the image of the linear operator
\Phi_{T}:L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

\Phi_{T}(u):=\int_{0}^{T}e^{(T-t)A}Bu(t)\mathrm{d}t.

(1.5)

First, assume (
1.3
).

Let us prove that
\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
z\in(\mathcal{R}_{T})^{\perp}
.
Then, for any
u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
,

0=\langle z,\Phi_{T}(u)\rangle=z^{\top}\Phi_{T}(u)=\int_{0}^{T}(B^{\top}e^{(T-t)A^{\top}}z)^{\top}u(t)\mathrm{d}t.

(1.6)

Hence the analytic function
t\mapsto B^{\top}e^{(T-t)A^{\top}}z
from
[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is identically
0
.
Differentiating
n-1
times at
t=T
, we obtain that
(-1)^{k}B^{\top}(A^{\top})^{k}z=0
for
k=0,\dotsc,n-1
.
Thus
z
is orthogonal to the columns of
[B,AB,\dotsc,A^{n-1}B]
, which span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Hence
z=0
, so
(\mathcal{R}_{T})^{\perp}=\{0\}
.

Second, assume that
\operatorname{rank}[B,AB,A^{2}B,\dots,A^{n-1}B]<n
.

Then there exists a nonzero
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, such that
z^{\top}A^{k}B=0
for all
k=0,\dotsc,n-1
.
By Cayley–Hamilton, this holds for all
k\geq 0
.
Then, for any
u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
,

\langle z,\Phi_{T}(u)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{\top}A^{k}B\int_{0}^{T}\frac{(T-t)^{k}}{k!}u(t)\mathrm{d}t=0.

(1.7)

Thus
z\in(\mathcal{R}_{T})^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
.
∎

Let us give two archetypal examples of controllable linear systems with
m=1
.

Example 1.4

(Integrator chain)
.

Consider the system
\dot{y}_{1}=u
,
\dot{y}_{2}=y_{1}
,
\dot{y}_{3}=y_{2}
, …,
\dot{y}_{n}=y_{n-1}
.
This system is of the form
\dot{y}=Ay+Bu
with

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\dotsb&0&0\\
1&0&\dotsb&0&0\\
0&1&\dotsb&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dotsb&1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(1.8)

Hence
B=e_{1}
the first unit vector and
A^{k}B=e_{k+1}
for
0\leq k\leq n-1
.
Thus (
1.3
) is satisfied and the system is controllable in any time
T
.

Remark 1.5
.

Though it may seem like a particular case, it can be proved that any system satisfying the rank condition (
1.3
) can be reduced to the form of
Example

1.4
by
static state feedback
and
linear change of coordinates
.
This reduction is called the
Brunovský normal form
of the system (see
[
21
, Theorem 2]
or
[
69
, Chapter 5, Theorem 2]
).

Example 1.6

(Diagonal system)
.

Consider the system
\dot{y}_{j}=-\lambda_{j}y_{j}+ju(t)
where
\lambda_{j}=j^{2}
.
This system is of the form
\dot{y}=Ay+Bu
with

A=\operatorname{diag}(-\lambda_{1},\dotsc,-\lambda_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
n\end{pmatrix}.

(1.9)

Thus one checks that
[B,AB,\dotsc,A^{n-1}B]=B^{\top}V
where
V
is the Vandermonde matrix associated with
-\lambda_{1},\dotsc,-\lambda_{n}
, which is nonsingular since these values are distinct.
Thus (
1.3
) is satisfied and the system is controllable in any time
T>0
.

We will investigate the null-control cost of this system in
Section

1.3
and use it as a finite approximation of a 1D heat equation controlled from the boundary in
Section

2.6
.

1.2
About the cost of fast controls

Assume that the linear time-invariant system (
1.1
) is controllable.
Hence, for all
T>0
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists at least one control
u
such that the corresponding solution satisfies
y(T)=0
.

In this paragraph, we quantify the cost of such null controllability.
We are interested in a worst-case measure: the largest, over all
y_{0}
in the unit ball, of the minimal size of a control needed to drive the state to zero at time
T
.
It is natural to expect that this cost increases as
T\to 0
.

Understanding the asymptotic behavior of this cost as
T\to 0
is crucial for transferring controllability properties from finite-dimensional ODEs to dissipative PDEs (see
Section

2
), and subsequently from linear PDEs to nonlinear ones (see
Section

3
).

Let
\lvert\cdot\rvert
be the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For
T>0
, define the
null-control cost
as

\mathcal{K}^{p}(T):=\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\lvert y_{0}\rvert=1}\inf\Big\{\|u\|_{L^{p}}\mid u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m}),\enskip y(0)=y_{0}\text{ and }y(T)=0\Big\}.

(1.10)

The following result is due to Seidman and Yong
[
105
]
(see
[
107
]
for
p=2
).

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
0\leq K\leq n-1
be the minimal integer such that

\operatorname{rank}[B,AB,A^{2}B,\dotsc,A^{K}B]=n.

(1.11)

There exists
c>0
such that, as
T\to 0
,

\mathcal{K}^{p}(T)\sim cT^{-K-1+\frac{1}{p}}.

(1.12)

Proof.

Step 1:
Time scaling.

Given
u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
, we introduce
v(s):=u(sT)
.
From the Duhamel formula (
1.2
) at
t=T
, the condition
y(T)=0
is equivalent to
F_{T}(v)=-y_{0}/T
where

F_{T}(v):=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-s(TA)}Bv(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(1.13)

Since
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}(0,T)}=T^{\frac{1}{p}}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}(0,1)}
, we obtain by homogeneity that

\mathcal{K}^{p}(T)=T^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(T)\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(T):=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\lvert z\rvert=1}\inf\big\{\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}}\mid F_{T}(v)=z\big\}.

(1.14)

Step 2:
Decomposition of the state space.

Write
B=(b_{1},\dotsc,b_{m})
.
For
0\leq k\leq K
, define
S_{k}:=\operatorname{span}\{A^{j}b_{l}\mid 0\leq j\leq k,1\leq l\leq m\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
E_{k}
the orthogonal complement of
S_{k-1}
in
S_{k}
, with the convention that
S_{-1}=\{0\}
.
By assumption
E_{0}\oplus\dotsb\oplus E_{K}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
E_{K}\neq\{0\}
.
Let
\mathbb{P}_{k}
denote the orthogonal projection on
E_{k}
.
Thus
\mathbb{P}_{k}A^{j}b_{l}=0
for any
j<k
.

Finally, for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define
\Delta_{T}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{K}T^{-k}\mathbb{P}_{k}z
with inverse
\Delta_{T}^{-1}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{K}T^{k}\mathbb{P}_{k}z
.

Step 3:
Asymptotic expansion of
F_{T}
.

Expanding the exponential in (
1.13
), we see that
F_{T}
scales like
T^{k}
on each
E_{k}
.
More precisely,
\Delta_{T}F_{T}=\Lambda+R_{T}
where, for
v\in L^{p}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{m})
,

\displaystyle\Lambda(v)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{k}(A^{k}b_{l})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(-s)^{k}}{k!}v_{l}(s)\mathrm{d}s

(1.15)

\displaystyle R_{T}(v)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{k}(A^{j}b_{l})}{j!}T^{j-k}\int_{0}^{1}(-s)^{j}v_{l}(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(1.16)

In particular, for
T\leq 1
,

\lvert R_{T}(v)\rvert\leq(K+1)\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\lVert A\rVert^{j}\lvert b_{l}\rvert}{j!}T\lVert v_{l}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq TC_{R}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}}.

(1.17)

Step 4:
The map
\Lambda
is onto from
L^{p}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{m})
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Each
E_{k}
is spanned by the
\mathbb{P}_{k}(A^{k}b_{l})
.
Thus, for each
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exist
\alpha_{0},\dotsc,\alpha_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
z=\sum\mathbb{P}_{k}(A^{k}B\alpha_{k})
.
Moreover, for each
0\leq k\leq K
, there exists
\varphi_{k}\in C^{\infty}([0,1];\mathbb{R})
such that
\int_{0}^{1}(-s)^{j}\varphi_{k}(s)\mathrm{d}s=j!\mathbf{1}_{j=k}
for all
0\leq j\leq K
.
Then
\Lambda(v)=z
where
v(s):=\sum\varphi_{k}(s)\alpha_{k}
.
So
\Lambda
is onto.

In particular, there exists
U\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n};L^{p}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{m}))
such that
\Lambda\circ U=\operatorname{Id}
and
C_{\Lambda}:=\lVert U\rVert<\infty
.

Step 5:
Definition of the constant.

Since
\Lambda
is bounded and
E_{K}\neq\{0\}
,

c:=\sup_{|z|=1,\,z\in E_{K}}\inf\big\{\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}}\mid\Lambda(v)=z\big\}\in(0,C_{\Lambda}].

(1.18)

For all
\varepsilon>0
and
z\in E_{K}
, there exists
v:=V_{\varepsilon}(z)
such that
\lVert V_{\varepsilon}(z)\rVert\leq(c+\varepsilon)\lvert z\rvert
and
\Lambda(V_{\varepsilon}(z))=z
.

Step 6:
Upper bound on the cost.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
T_{\varepsilon}>0
such that, for all
T\in(0,T_{\varepsilon}]
and all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists
v\in L^{p}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that
F_{T}(v)=z
and
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq(c+2\varepsilon)T^{-K}\lvert z\rvert
.

Let
T,\varepsilon>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Decompose
z=z_{K}+z^{\prime}
where
z_{K}\in E_{K}
and
z^{\prime}\in E_{K}^{\perp}
.
To solve
F_{T}(v)=z
, we set
v:=v_{0}+v_{1}+\dotsb
where we define
v_{0}:=V_{\varepsilon}(\Delta_{T}(z_{K}))+U(\Delta_{T}(z^{\prime}))
and
v_{i+1}:=-U(R_{T}(v_{i}))
.
Thus
\lVert v_{0}\rVert\leq(c+\varepsilon)T^{-K}\lvert z_{K}\rvert+C_{\Lambda}T^{-K+1}\lvert z^{\prime}\rvert\leq(c+\varepsilon+C_{\Lambda}T)T^{-K}\lvert z\rvert
.
Moreover
\lVert v_{i+1}\rVert\leq C_{\Lambda}TC_{R}\lVert v_{i}\rVert
.
Thus the series converges normally and
\lVert v\rVert\leq(c+\varepsilon+TC^{\prime})T^{-K}\lvert z\rvert
.
By a telescoping sum argument,
F_{T}(v)=z
.
Choosing
T_{\varepsilon}
small enough, we obtain
\lVert v\rVert\leq(c+2\varepsilon)T^{-K}\lvert z\rvert
.

Step 7:
Lower bound on the cost.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
T_{\varepsilon}>0
such that, for all
T\in(0,T_{\varepsilon}]
, there exists
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
z\neq 0
such that for all
v\in F_{T}^{-1}(z)
, one has
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}}\geq(c-2\varepsilon)T^{-K}\lvert z\rvert
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
By definition (
1.18
) of
c
, there exists
z^{*}\in E_{K}
with
z^{*}\neq 0
such that
\Lambda(v)=z^{*}
implies
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}}\geq(c-\varepsilon)\lvert z^{*}\rvert
.
For
T>0
, set
z_{T}:=\Delta_{T}^{-1}(z^{*})=T^{K}z^{*}
.
Let
v_{T}
such that
F_{T}(v_{T})=z_{T}
.
Thus
(\Lambda+R_{T})(v_{T})=z^{*}
.
Let
w_{T}:=U(R_{T}(v_{T}))
so that
\Lambda(w_{T})=R_{T}(v_{T})
.
Then
\Lambda(v_{T}+w_{T})=z^{*}\in E_{K}
.
Hence
\lVert v_{T}+w_{T}\rVert\geq(c-\varepsilon)\lvert z^{*}\rvert
.
But
\lVert w_{T}\rVert\leq C_{\Lambda}TC_{R}\lVert v_{T}\rVert
.
Hence
(1+TC_{\Lambda}C_{R})\lVert v_{T}\rVert\geq(c-\varepsilon)\lvert z^{*}\rvert=(c-\varepsilon)T^{-K}\lvert z_{T}\rvert
.
For
T\in(0,T_{\varepsilon}]
where
T_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon/(C_{\Lambda}C_{R}(c-2\varepsilon))
, we have found
z_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
z_{T}\neq 0
such that any
v_{T}
satisfying
F_{T}(v_{T})=z_{T}
satisfies
\lVert v_{T}\rVert\geq(c-2\varepsilon)T^{-K}\lvert z_{T}\rvert
.

Step 8:
Conclusion.

We proved that
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}(T)\sim cT^{-K}
.
Recalling (
1.14
) proves (
1.12
).
∎

Example 1.8

(Integrator chain)
.

Recall
Example

1.4
.
Since
B=e_{1}
and
A^{k}B=e_{k+1}
, we have
K=n-1
in (
1.11
).
With the notations of the proof of
Proposition

1.7
, recalling (
1.15
),

\Lambda(v)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e_{k+1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(-s)^{k}}{k!}v(s)\mathrm{d}s

(1.19)

and, as in (
1.18
), since
E_{K}=\mathbb{R}e_{n}
, by homogeneity,

c=\inf\big\{\lVert v\rVert_{L^{p}}\mid\Lambda(v)=e_{n}\big\}.

(1.20)

Take
p=2
to fix ideas.
Then
c
is the minimal
L^{2}
norm of a function on
[0,1]
orthogonal to
1,s,s^{2},\dotsc,s^{n-2}
and such that
\int_{0}^{1}s^{n-1}v(s)\mathrm{d}s=(-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!
.
Using the properties of Legendre polynomials, one can prove that
c=\sqrt{2n-1}\frac{(2n-2)!}{(n-1)!}
.

1.3
About the cost of large systems

In the previous paragraph, we computed the asymptotic behavior as
T\to 0
of the null-control cost for a fixed system of a given size
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
When moving on to PDEs in
Section

2
, we will approximate a PDE by its (increasingly larger) finite dimensional projections.

As an example, consider again the diagonal system
Example

1.6
.
Equip
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the norm
\lVert y\rVert_{h}^{2}:=\sum y_{j}^{2}/j^{2}
(reminiscent of the
H^{-1}
norm for Fourier series).
Define

\mathcal{K}_{n}(T):=\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\lVert y\rVert_{h}=1}\inf_{u\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})}\Big\{\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\mid y(0)=y_{0}\text{ and }y(T)=0\Big\}.

(1.21)

For a fixed
n
, we know by
Proposition

1.7
that
\mathcal{K}_{n}(T)\sim c_{n}T^{-n+\frac{1}{2}}
for some constant
c_{n}
.
This estimate is only valid for
T\leq T_{n}
.
In
Section

2.6
, we will use the following estimate.

Proposition 1.9
.

For all
T>0
and
n\geq 1
,

\mathcal{K}_{n}(T)\leq\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}}{T}}\exp\left(6\sqrt{\frac{n}{T}}\right).

(1.22)

Proof.

Let
T>0
,
n\geq 1
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{h}=1
.
By the Duhamel formula (
1.2
) for the system of
Example

1.6
, we have
y(T)=0
if and only if, for all
1\leq j\leq n
,

\int_{0}^{T}u(t)e^{j^{2}t}\mathrm{d}t=-\frac{y_{0}^{j}}{j}=:b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.23)

Hence
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\lvert b\rvert_{2}=\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{h}=1
is given, and we are looking for a
u\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
of minimal norm satisfying these conditions.
Such a problem is called a
moment problem
, and, starting with
[
37
,
38
]
, the mathematical literature contains many works on such problems where one tries to solve (
1.23
) simultaneously for all
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Here, we focus on the question (and the cost) of the finite moment problem for
1\leq j\leq n
.

By projection on the subspace of
L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
spanned by
e^{t},\dotsc,e^{n^{2}t}
, the control of minimal norm can be written
u(t):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}e^{j^{2}t}
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let us introduce the Gram matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
defined as

M_{ij}:=\int_{0}^{T}e^{(i^{2}+j^{2})t}\mathrm{d}t.

(1.24)

With this notation, solving (
1.23
) for
1\leq j\leq n
is equivalent to the equation
Ma=b
.
To obtain a cost estimate, write

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=a^{\top}Ma=(M^{-1}b)^{\top}M(M^{-1}b)=b^{\top}M^{-1}b.

(1.25)

Since
\lvert b\rvert_{2}=\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{h}
, this proves that
\mathcal{K}_{n}(T)=\lVert M^{-1}\rVert^{\frac{1}{2}}
where, for a matrix
A
, we use the spectral norm
\lVert A\rVert:=\max_{\lvert x\rvert_{2}=1}\lvert Ax\rvert_{2}
.

Starting from (
1.24
), the idea of the proof is to approximate
M
by a Riemann sum:

\int_{0}^{T}e^{(i^{2}+j^{2})t}\mathrm{d}t\approx\frac{T}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{(i^{2}+j^{2})\frac{(k-1)T}{n}}.

(1.26)

Heuristically, we thus have
M\approx\frac{T}{n}UU^{\top}
where we define

U_{ij}=e^{i^{2}(j-1)\frac{T}{n}}.

(1.27)

By
Lemma

1.10
,
\lVert M^{-1}\rVert\leq\frac{n}{T}\lVert U^{-1}\rVert^{2}
.
By
Lemma

1.11
,
\lVert U^{-1}\rVert\leq\sqrt{n}\exp(6\sqrt{n/T})
.
∎

Lemma 1.10
.

For any
n\geq 1
and
T>0
, for the matrices of (
1.24
) and (
1.27
),

\lVert M^{-1}\rVert\leq\frac{n}{T}\lVert U^{-1}\rVert^{2}.

(1.28)

Proof.

Let
D(t):=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i^{2}t})
and
U(t):=D(t)U
.
Then
M=\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{n}}U(t)U(t)^{\top}\mathrm{d}t
.
Indeed, for
1\leq i,j\leq n
, letting
\sigma:=i^{2}+j^{2}>0
, we have

\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{n}}(U(t)U(t)^{\top})_{ij}\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{\sigma(k-1)\frac{T}{n}}\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{n}}e^{\sigma t}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{e^{\sigma T}-1}{e^{\sigma T/n}-1}\frac{e^{\sigma T/n}-1}{\sigma}=M_{ij}.

(1.29)

For a real symmetric matrix

one has
\lVert A^{-1}\rVert^{-1}=\lambda_{\min}(A)=\inf_{\lvert x\rvert_{2}=1}x^{\top}Ax
.

For any

, we have

x^{\top}Mx=\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{n}}x^{\top}D(t)UU^{\top}D(t)x\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{n}}\lambda_{\min}(UU^{\top})\lvert D(t)x\rvert_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{T}{n}\lambda_{\min}(UU^{\top})\lvert x\rvert_{2}^{2}

(1.30)

since
\lvert D(t)x\rvert_{2}\geq\lvert x\rvert_{2}
.
Thus
\lVert M^{-1}\rVert\leq\frac{n}{T}\lVert(UU^{\top})^{-1}\rVert\leq\frac{n}{T}\lVert U^{-1}\rVert^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 1.11
.

For any
n\geq 1
and
T>0
, for the matrix
U
of (
1.27
),

\lVert U^{-1}\rVert\leq\sqrt{n}\exp\left(6\sqrt{n/T}\right).

(1.31)

Proof.

Let
h:=\sqrt{T/n}
.
We see

as the Vandermonde matrix for the nodes
z_{j}:=e^{j^{2}T/n}=e^{y_{j}^{2}}
where
y_{j}:=jh
for

By a classical estimate by Gautschi (see
Lemma

A.6
),

\lVert U^{-1}\rVert\leq\sqrt{n}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}e^{Q_{j}}\quad\text{where}\quad Q_{j}:=\ln\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{1+|z_{k}|}{|z_{j}-z_{k}|}=\sum_{k\neq j}\ln\frac{1+e^{y_{k}^{2}}}{|e^{y_{j}^{2}}-e^{y_{k}^{2}}|}.

(1.32)

We claim that

Q_{j}=\sum_{k\neq j}H_{y_{j}}(y_{k})\leq\frac{1}{h}\int_{h}^{nh}H_{y_{j}}(y)\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{where}\quad H_{x}(y):=\ln\frac{1+e^{y^{2}}}{|e^{x^{2}}-e^{y^{2}}|}.

(1.33)

Indeed, for a fixed
x\geq 0
, for
y\in[0,x)
and
y\in(x,\infty)
, explicitly differentiating

yields

H_{x}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{2ye^{y^{2}}(1+e^{x^{2}})}{(1+e^{y^{2}})(e^{x^{2}}-e^{y^{2}})}.

(1.34)

Thus

is increasing on
[0,x)
and decreasing on
(x,\infty)
.
Hence, for each

we have

, and for each

Summing over

).

For

, using

H_{x}(y)=\ln\frac{1+e^{-y^{2}}}{|1-e^{x^{2}-y^{2}}|}\leq\ln\frac{1+e^{-y^{2}}}{1-e^{-|x^{2}-y^{2}|}}\leq\ln\frac{1+e^{-y^{2}}}{1-e^{-(x-y)^{2}}}.

Thus

(1.36)

Gathering with (

1.4
Small-time local controllability of control-affine systems

We consider the time-invariant control-affine system on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\dot{y}(t)=f_{0}(y(t))+u_{1}(t)f_{1}(y(t))+\dotsb+u_{m}(t)f_{m}(y(t)),

(1.37)

where
y(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state,
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the control, and
f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
vector fields.

We assume that
f_{0}(0)=0
, so that
0
is an equilibrium of the free system (with null control).
We are interested in the local controllability at this equilibrium.
Since this is a local property, it only depends on the values of
f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}
in a neighborhood of the origin.
Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that they are globally Lipschitz.
This will lighten the proofs.
In particular, this prevents finite-time blow-up in the nonlinear ODE (
1.37
).

Lemma 1.12
.

Let
f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, globally Lipschitz, with
f_{0}(0)=0
.
For any
T>0
,
u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a unique mild solution
y\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
to (
1.37
) with initial data
y(0)=y_{0}
.
When needed, we will refer to this solution as
y(t;u,y_{0})
.

Proof.

Let
T>0
,
u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We say that
y\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
mild solution
to (
1.37
) with initial data
y_{0}
when it satisfies the integral formulation:

\forall t\in[0,T],\quad y(t)=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}(f_{0}+u_{1}(s)f_{1}+\dotsb+u_{m}(s)f_{m})(y(s))\mathrm{d}s,

(1.38)

which makes sense for
u\in L^{p}((0,T);\mathbb{R}^{m})
.
Let us prove that such a solution exists.

Define a map
\Theta
from the Banach space
C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself by

(\Theta(y))(t):=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}(f_{0}+u_{1}(s)f_{1}+\dotsb+u_{m}(s)f_{m})(y(s))\mathrm{d}s.

(1.39)

Let
L_{0},L_{1},\dotsc,L_{m}
be the global Lipschitz constants of
f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}
and
L:=\max L_{i}
.
One proves by induction on
k\in\mathbb{N}
that, for all
y,\tilde{y}\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
t\in[0,T]
,

|(\Theta^{k}(y)-\Theta^{k}(\tilde{y}))(t)|\leq\frac{L^{k}(t+\int_{0}^{t}|u|)^{k}}{k!}\lVert y-\tilde{y}\rVert_{C^{0}}.

(1.40)

Since
T
and
u
are fixed, for
k
large enough,
\Theta^{k}
is a contraction and admits a unique fixed-point

by the Banach fixed-point theorem.
Then
\Theta^{k}(\Theta(y^{\star}))=\Theta(\Theta^{k}(y^{\star}))=\Theta(y^{\star})
, so
\Theta(y^{\star})
is a fixed point of
\Theta^{k}
, so is equal to
y^{\star}
.
So
y^{\star}
satisfies (
1.38
).

Uniqueness follows immediately.
If
y
and
\tilde{y}
are two solutions, then
y=\Theta^{k}(y)
and
\tilde{y}=\Theta^{k}(\tilde{y})
which implies that
y=\tilde{y}
since
\Theta^{k}
is a contraction.
∎

1.5
The linear test for control-affine systems

Since small-time local controllability is a local notion, the first natural approach is to attempt to linearize the problem, at the equilibrium state

Given

Nonlinear motions and obstructions

When the linearized system at an equilibrium is not controllable, one must study higher order-terms to determine the controllability of the nonlinear system.

The following example is one where controllability can be obtained using a nonlinear term, using an entirely elementary argument.
The quest for increasingly general sufficient conditions for STLC of ODEs has a long history; see Hermes

Example 1.18

Let

u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
such that

P_{k}\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y},

(2.4)

•

(dissipation of high modes)
for all
T\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in Y
,

P_{k}y_{0}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\lVert\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},0)\rVert_{Y}\leq C_{2}e^{-c_{2}T^{m}k^{b}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(2.5)

During the main time iteration, within each time step of duration
\tau
, we choose a threshold
k\in\mathbb{N}
, control the low modes to zero on
[0,\tau/2]
, then let the energy dissipate on
[\tau/2,\tau]
.
More precisely, the main time iteration relies on the following one-step contraction result.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
2.5
holds, then there exist
C_{3},c_{3}>0
such that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, for all
\tau\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in Y
, there exists
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
such that

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lVert\Sigma_{\tau}(y_{0},u)\rVert_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}e^{-c_{3}\tau^{m}k^{b}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(2.7)

Proof.

Let
C_{1},C_{2},c_{1},c_{2},a,b,m>0
with
a<b
be given by
2.5
.
Let
\tau>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
y_{0}\in Y
.
Define a control
u
on
[0,\tau]
as being given by the first item of
2.5
for a time
\frac{\tau}{2}
, extended by
0
on
[\frac{\tau}{2},\tau]
.
Let
y_{1}:=\Sigma_{\frac{\tau}{2}}(y_{0},u)
.
By
Lemma

2.3
and (
2.4
),

\lVert y_{1}\rVert_{Y}\leq M_{1}\left(\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\right)\leq M_{1}(1+C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}})\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(2.8)

Since
P_{k}y_{1}=0
, (
2.5
) implies that

\lVert\Sigma_{\tau}(y_{0},u)\rVert_{Y}=\lVert\Sigma_{\frac{\tau}{2}}(y_{1},0)\rVert\leq C_{2}e^{-c_{2}(\tau-\frac{\tau}{2})^{m}k^{b}}\lVert y_{1}\rVert_{Y}

(2.9)

We deduce the claimed estimates, with
c_{3}=c_{2}2^{-m}
and
C_{3}=M_{1}C_{2}(1+C_{1})
.
∎

Theorem 2.7
.

Under
2.5
, the abstract linear system
\Sigma
is small-time null-controllable.
More precisely, there exist constants
C_{0},c_{0}>0
such that, for every
T\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in Y
, there exists
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
such that
\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)=0
and

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq C_{0}\exp\left(c_{0}T^{-\sigma}\right)\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}\quad\text{where}\quad\sigma:=\frac{am}{b-a}.

(2.10)

Proof.

Step 1:
Construction of the time and frequency sequences.

We define a sequence of time intervals and frequency cutoffs.
Let
r>1
and
q>1
be two real numbers to be chosen later.
We decompose the interval
[0,T]
into a series of subintervals.
Let

\alpha:=T\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{j}:=\frac{\alpha}{r^{j}}\quad\text{for }j\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.11)

Hence
T=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\tau_{j}
.
We define the sequence of time instants
T_{0}=0
and
T_{j+1}=T_{j}+\tau_{j}
.
The
j
-th step of our procedure will happen on the interval
[T_{j},T_{j+1}]
.

Let
\beta>0
be another parameter to be chosen.
We define the sequence of frequency cutoffs as

k_{j}:=\lfloor\beta q^{j}\rfloor\quad\text{for }j\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.12)

Step 2:
The iterative scheme.

We define a sequence of states
(y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
by
y_{0}
the given initial state and
y_{j+1}:=\Sigma_{\tau_{j}}(y_{j},u_{j})
, where
u_{j}
is the control used on
[T_{j},T_{j+1}]
, given by
Lemma

2.6
.
Hence, by (
2.6
) and (
2.7
),

\displaystyle\lVert u_{j}\rVert_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}k_{j}^{a}}\lVert y_{j}\rVert_{Y},

(2.13)

\displaystyle\lVert y_{j+1}\rVert_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}e^{-\gamma_{j}}\lVert y_{j}\rVert_{Y},

(2.14)

where we introduce the notation

\gamma_{j}:=c_{3}\tau_{j}^{m}k_{j}^{b}-c_{1}k_{j}^{a}.

(2.15)

Step 3:
Choice of the parameters
r,q,\beta
.

Our goal is to ensure that
\lVert y_{j}\rVert_{Y}\to 0
sufficiently fast.
For
\beta\geq 1
, by (
2.12
),
\frac{1}{2}\beta q^{j}\leq k_{j}\leq\beta q^{j}
.
Recalling (
2.11
) for
\tau_{j}
, we obtain

\gamma_{j}\geq(q^{a})^{j}\left(2^{-b}c_{3}\alpha^{m}\beta^{b}\left(q^{b-a}r^{-m}\right)^{j}-c_{1}\beta^{a}\right).

(2.16)

We let
q:=2^{\frac{1}{a}}
so that
q^{a}=2
and
r:=q^{\frac{b-a}{m}}
so that
q^{b-a}r^{-m}=1
.
Also, we tune
\beta
such that
2^{-b}c_{3}\alpha^{m}\beta^{b}=3c_{1}\beta^{a}
.
This corresponds to setting

\beta:=\left(\frac{2^{b}3c_{1}}{c_{3}\alpha^{m}}\right)^{\frac{1}{b-a}}.

(2.17)

With these choices
\beta\to+\infty
as
T\to 0
and one has:

\gamma_{j}\geq 2c_{1}\beta^{a}2^{j}.

(2.18)

Step 4:
Stepwise convergence of the state.

Iterating (
2.14
), we obtain, for any
j\geq 1
,

\lVert y_{j}\rVert_{Y}\leq\exp\left(j\ln C_{3}-2c_{1}\beta^{a}(2^{j}-1)\right)\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(2.19)

Therefore,
\lVert y_{j}\rVert_{Y}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.

Step 5:
Bounding the total control cost.

The overall control
u
is the concatenation of the controls
u_{j}
on
[T_{j},T_{j+1}]
.
Using the bounds (
2.13
) and (
2.19
), we have:

\begin{split}\lVert u_{j}\rVert_{L^{p}}&\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}k_{j}^{a}}\lVert y_{j}\rVert_{Y}\\
&\leq C_{1}\exp(c_{1}\beta^{a}2^{j})\exp(j\ln C_{3}-2c_{1}\beta^{a}(2^{j}-1))\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}\\
&\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}\beta^{a}}\exp\left(j(\ln C_{3}-c_{1}\beta^{a})\right)\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}\\
&\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}\beta^{a}}2^{-j}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}\end{split}

(2.20)

using
2^{j}-1\geq j
and
T
small enough so that
\ln C_{3}-c_{1}\beta^{a}\leq-\ln 2
.

Thus, there exists
T_{0}>0
such that, for all
0<T\leq T_{0}
,

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}((0,T);U)}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lVert u_{j}\rVert_{L^{p}((0,\tau_{j});U)}\leq 2C_{1}e^{c_{1}\beta^{a}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(2.21)

Recalling (
2.11
) and (
2.17
), this entails the cost estimate (
2.10
) since
\beta^{a}\approx(\alpha^{-\frac{m}{b-a}})^{a}\approx T^{-\sigma}
.

Step 6:
Conclusion.

Since
\Sigma
is an abstract linear control system, the associated solution
y(t):=\Sigma_{t}(y_{0},u)
belongs to
C^{0}([0,T];Y)
.
By the composition property (
2.3
),
y(T_{j})=y_{j}
.
We proved that
y_{j}\to 0
.
Since
T_{j}\to T
, by continuity, we conclude that
\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)=y(T)=0
.
∎

Remark 2.10

(Relaxed assumptions on constants)
.

The form of the exponential constants in the assumed estimates (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) is given to fix ideas, and because they match some frequent use cases.
However, the time iteration is robust to many variations of these estimates.
For example, it is common to have polynomial prefactors of the form
T^{-h}
in (
2.4
) (see e.g.
[

, Prop. IV.2.24]
and
Section

In fact, the proof can accommodate for much larger modifications.
Since the time schedule works with

, the result still holds if the estimates (

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\exp\left(c_{1}k^{a}+c_{1}^{\prime}T^{-\theta}\right)\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y},

(2.22)

\displaystyle\lVert\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},0)\rVert_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\exp\left(-c_{2}T^{m}k^{b}+c_{2}^{\prime}k^{a}+c_{2}^{\prime\prime}T^{-\theta}\right)\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(2.23)

Then the cost estimate (
2.10
) holds with
\sigma=\max\{\frac{am}{b-a},\theta\}
.
In fact, up to worsening
b
or
\theta
in the assumptions (
2.22
) and (
2.23
), one can always assume that
\sigma=\frac{am}{b-a}=\theta
.

One can carry out the same proof, starting with adapting
Lemma

2.6
.
In the time schedule, one can keep
q=2^{\frac{1}{a}}
and
r=q^{\frac{b-a}{m}}
and look for
\beta=\rho\alpha^{-\frac{m}{b-a}}
.
The lower-bound (
2.16
) is replaced with

\gamma_{j}\geq 2^{j}\beta^{a}\left(\frac{c_{2}}{2^{b+m}}\rho^{b-a}-(c_{1}+c_{2}^{\prime})-2\theta(c_{1}^{\prime}+c_{2}^{\prime\prime})\rho^{-a}\right).

(2.24)

Choosing
\rho
large enough,
\gamma_{j}\geq 2(c_{1}+c_{1}^{\prime}2^{-\sigma}\rho^{-a})2^{j}\beta^{a}
, and the end of the proof is unchanged.

Remark 2.11
.

Estimate (

.
This can seem to be in sharp contrast with our finite-dimensional estimates of

.
To understand this apparent paradox, one must recall that we will obtain such estimates only in situations where the integer
K
of

.

2.3
The role of spectral inequalities

The time-iteration argument presented in
Section

2.2
is independent of the way that one proves that its assumptions are satisfied.
However, starting with
[
79
]
, most works using this method also rely on a so-called
spectral inequality
to check that the assumptions are satisfied.
Some authors also say
uncertainty principle
, since it is not required that the projections
P_{k}
for
2.5
are orthogonal projections on spectral subspaces of the considered self-adjoint operator.

In this section, we assume that
Y
and
U
are Hilbert spaces, and we consider a family
(P_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of orthogonal projections on
Y
.
For
t\geq 0
, we also define
\mathbb{T}_{t}\in\mathcal{L}(Y)
by
\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}:=\Sigma_{t}(y_{0},0)
(
\mathbb{T}
is the strongly continuous semigroup associated with our system; see
Section

2.9
).
Finally, we assume that there exists an operator
B\in\mathcal{L}(U,Y)
such that, for all
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
and
T\geq 0
,

\Sigma_{T}(0,u)=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{T}_{T-t}Bu(t)\mathrm{d}t.

(2.25)

The existence of an integral representation of
u\mapsto\Sigma_{T}(0,u)
is always true (at least when
p<+\infty
), but
B
is not always bounded from
U
to
Y
(typically not for boundary control); see
[
121
, Theorem 3.9, Problem 3.10]
.
In such a case, there is a notion of admissibility of
B
with respect to
A
(see
[
121
, Section 4]
) and some of the techniques presented here still hold (see
[
115
, Theorem 1.2]
).

The constructions of this section are based on the following
static
assumption (see below for examples), in which neither the time nor the operator
A
(or the semigroup
\mathbb{T}_{t}
) play a role.

Assumption 2.12

(Spectral inequality)
.

There exist
C_{1},c_{1},a>0
such that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\forall y\in Y,\quad\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{Y}\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}\lVert B^{\star}P_{k}y\rVert_{U}

(2.26)

or, equivalently (by a consequence of the closed-graph theorem, see
[
116
, Proposition 12.1.2]
),

\exists D_{k}\in\mathcal{L}(Y,U),\text{ such that }P_{k}=P_{k}BD_{k}\text{ and }\lVert D_{k}\rVert\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}.

(2.27)

2.3.1
The commutative case

In this paragraph, assuming that the projections
P_{k}
commute with the dynamics (i.e. all
t\geq 0
,
P_{k}\mathbb{T}_{t}=\mathbb{T}_{t}P_{k}
), we show that the spectral inequality entails the controllability of the low modes.

Proposition 2.13
.

Under
2.12
, if the projections
P_{k}
commute with the dynamics, then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
T\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in Y
, there exists
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
such that

P_{k}\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq T^{\frac{1}{p}-1}M_{1}C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}

(2.28)

where
M_{1}
is the constant from
Lemma

2.3
.

Proof.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
D_{k}\in\mathcal{L}(Y,U)
be given by
2.12
.
For
T\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in Y
, set

u(t):=-\frac{1}{T}D_{k}\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}.

(2.29)

By
Lemma

2.3
, one has

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq T^{\frac{1}{p}-1}\lVert D_{k}\rVert\cdot\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\lVert\mathbb{T}_{t}\rVert\cdot\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}\leq T^{\frac{1}{p}-1}M_{1}C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(2.30)

By linearity and (
2.25
),

\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)=\mathbb{T}_{T}y_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{T}_{T-t}BD_{k}\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}\mathrm{d}t.

(2.31)

By assumption, the projection
P_{k}
commutes with the semigroup, so, in particular

\forall\tau\geq 0,\quad P_{k}\mathbb{T}_{\tau}P_{k}=P_{k}\mathbb{T}_{\tau}

(2.32)

Applying
P_{k}
to (
2.31
), using
P_{k}BD_{k}=P_{k}
, the semigroup property and (
2.32
),

\begin{split}P_{k}\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)&=P_{k}\mathbb{T}_{T}y_{0}-\frac{1}{T}P_{k}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{T}_{T-t}P_{k}BD_{k}\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=P_{k}\mathbb{T}_{T}y_{0}-\frac{1}{T}P_{k}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{T}_{T-t}P_{k}\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=P_{k}\mathbb{T}_{T}y_{0}-\frac{1}{T}P_{k}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{T}_{T-t}\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}\mathrm{d}t=0.\end{split}

(2.33)

So we have found a control of the expected size, bringing the projection
P_{k}
of the state to
0
.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

The above proof only uses the relation (
2.32
), which is equivalent to the condition that the range of
P_{k}^{\bot}=\operatorname{Id}-P_{k}
is stable by the semigroup (and so slightly weaker than requiring that
P_{k}
commutes with the semigroup; one must only prevent “high-to-low” energy transfers).

2.3.2
The non-commutative case

When the projections
P_{k}
don’t commute with the dynamics, the construction of
Proposition

2.13
no longer yields a null-control for the low modes.
However, the lack of commutation can be compensated by the dissipation to derive the one-step contraction result of
Lemma

2.6
, thus allowing to execute the time iteration of
Theorem

2.7
.

Proposition 2.15
.

Assume that there exist
C_{1},C_{2},c_{1},c_{2},a,b,m>0
with
a<b
such that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the spectral
2.12
and the dissipation condition (
2.5
) hold.
Then there exist
C_{3},c_{3}>0
such that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, for all
\tau\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in Y
, there exists
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
with

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\tau^{\frac{1}{p}-1}M_{1}C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y},

(2.34)

\displaystyle\lVert\Sigma_{\tau}(y_{0},u)\rVert_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}e^{-c_{3}\tau^{m}k^{b}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y},

(2.35)

where
M_{1}
is the constant from
Lemma

2.3
.

Proof.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
D_{k}\in\mathcal{L}(Y,U)
given by
2.12
.
For
\tau\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in Y
, we use the same control as in the proof of
Proposition

2.13
on
(0,\frac{\tau}{2})
, then a null control on
(\frac{\tau}{2},\tau)
:

u(t):=-\frac{2}{\tau}\mathbf{1}_{(0,\frac{\tau}{2})}(t)D_{k}\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}.

(2.36)

By
Lemma

2.3
, we obtain (
2.34
).
By linearity and (
2.25
),

\Sigma_{\tau}(y_{0},u)=\mathbb{T}_{\tau}y_{0}-\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\mathbb{T}_{\tau-t}BD_{k}\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}\mathrm{d}t.

(2.37)

We now write the following decomposition:

BD_{k}=\operatorname{Id}+(BD_{k}-\operatorname{Id})=\operatorname{Id}+P_{k}(BD_{k}-\operatorname{Id})+(\operatorname{Id}-P_{k})(BD_{k}-\operatorname{Id}).

(2.38)

By construction
P_{k}(BD_{k}-\operatorname{Id})=0
.
By linearity and the semigroup property,

\Sigma_{\tau}(y_{0},u)=-\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\mathbb{T}_{\tau-t}(\operatorname{Id}-P_{k})(BD_{k}-\operatorname{Id})\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}\mathrm{d}t.

(2.39)

Hence, by the dissipation condition (
2.5
),

\begin{split}\lVert\Sigma_{\tau}(y_{0},u)\rVert_{Y}&\leq\frac{2}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\frac{\tau}{2}}\lVert\mathbb{T}_{\tau-t}(\operatorname{Id}-P_{k})\rVert(\lVert B\rVert\lVert D_{k}\rVert+1)\lVert\mathbb{T}_{t}\rVert\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq C_{2}e^{-c_{2}(\tau/2)^{m}k^{b}}(\lVert B\rVert C_{1}e^{c_{1}k^{a}}+1)M_{1}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.\end{split}

(2.40)

Thus we obtain (
2.35
) with
C_{3}=C_{2}(C_{1}\lVert B\rVert+1)M_{1}
and
c_{3}=c_{2}2^{-m}
.
∎

2.4
Historical perspective

We briefly recall some contributions regarding the method exposed above, introduced in
[
79
]
, and thus commonly referred to as the “Lebeau–Robbiano method”.

The 1995 paper of Lebeau and Robbiano.

In
[
79
]
, to prove the small-time null controllability of the heat equation posed on a smooth compact Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, the strategy is as follows.

•

Consider
e_{k}
the eigenfunctions of the operator
A
of (
2.1
) (here
A=-\Delta
with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\mathcal{M}
) and
\lambda_{k}
the associated eigenvalues.
Consider
P_{k}
to be the projection on the finite-dimensional space spanned by
e_{1},\dotsc,e_{k}
.

•

This automatically ensures that
\lVert\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},0)\rVert_{Y}\leq e^{-\lambda_{k}T}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}
when
P_{k}y_{0}=0
.
By Weyl’s law, this entails the dissipation property (
2.5
) with
b=\frac{2}{d}
and
m=1
.

•

Using Carleman estimates inspired by
[
98
,
101
]
for the elliptic operator
\partial_{t}^{2}+\Delta
, prove that, for any
\theta>1
, for any
T\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in Y
, there exists
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T)
such that
P_{k}\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)=0
with a cost estimate of the form

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{1}\exp\left(c_{1}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}+c_{1}T^{-\theta}\right)\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(2.41)

This corresponds to our generalized cost assumption (
2.22
) with
a=\frac{1}{d}
by Weyl’s law.

•

Use a geometric time and frequency schedule as exposed above to deduce the small-time null controllability of the full PDE.
By
Remark

2.10
, since
\theta>1
and
\frac{ma}{b-a}=1
, their proof concludes to a cost estimate of the form
C_{0}\exp(c_{0}T^{-\theta})
with
\theta>1
, falling just short of the now-known optimal exponent
1
(see below).

More recent variations and enhancements.

The Lebeau–Robbiano method has been applied in countless settings, and many authors have proposed improvements.
We unfortunately only refer to a few of them, relevant to our exposition, and of which we are aware.

•

In
[
68
]
and
[
80
]
, Jerison, Lebeau and Zuazua started to isolate and study precisely static spectral inequalities of the form of
2.12
, avoiding the lossy estimate (
2.41
).

•

In
[
88
]
, Miller started a first abstraction of the previous results which concerned the heat equation to self-adjoint operators
A
with admissible control operators
B
, obtaining the qualitatively optimal cost as
T\to 0
for the control of a family of diffusion equations.

•

Multiple subsequent works
[
89
,
114
,
115
]
improved the estimates of the cost of controllability, or reduced the assumptions on the control operator
B
.
Miller also remarked in
[
89
]
that commutation is not necessary, only stability of the high modes as in

.

•

In

, Beauchard and Pravda-Starov, in collaboration with Miller, introduced the key novelty that the projections

don’t need to be spectral projections onto the eigenspaces of the operator

This important improvement allows to reuse well-known spectral inequalities for a reference operator and apply it to prove the controllability of another PDE (see

As we prove in

•

Later, it was identified that the method can be extended to time-dependent systems (with time-dependent operators
[

]
, or time-dependent control domains
[

]
).
As mentioned in

•

The recent PhD thesis by Gallaun
[

generalizes these results in multiple directions.
In particular, in the spirit of

See also
[
45
]
for a generalization of
Section

2.3
to the context of Banach spaces.

•

All the references mentioned above work on the dual problem of observability (see
[
116
]
).
In particular, the proofs of
[
89
]
or
[
14
]
rely on a telescoping series argument.
In
Sections

2.2
and

Eventually, we refer to
[
20

for a nice textbook on the Lebeau–Robbiano method containing the proofs of the Carleman inequalities required for the control of the heat equation in bounded domains of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

2.5
Example: interior control of the heat equation on
[0,1]

In this paragraph, we investigate the small-time null controllability of the heat equation on the segment
[0,1]

More precisely, for

3
3

3

equipped with the canonical norm.
We start with the following general well-posedness result.

Proposition 2.16

For any
T>0

and
f\in L^{2}((0,T);H^{-1}(0,1))

\begin{cases}y_{t}-y_{xx}=f&\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,1),\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0&\text{in }(0,T),\\

Moreover, there exists a constant

such that, for every

,
y_{0}
,

\lVert y\rVert_{C^{0}L^{2}}+\lVert y\rVert_{L^{2}H^{1}}+\lVert y\rVert_{H^{1}H^{-1}}\leq C\left(\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}H^{-1}}\right).

(2.45)

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of such a solution to (

) is classical textbook knowledge for linear parabolic PDEs (see for example

The claimed inequality is a classical energy estimate, obtained by multiplying the PDE and integrating by parts.

Multiplying the PDE by

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{1}(yy_{xx})(t,x)\mathrm{d}x=\langle f(t,\cdot),y(t,\cdot)\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}_{0}}.

Integrating by parts in the second term,

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{1}y_{x}^{2}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\leq\lVert f(t,\cdot)\rVert_{H^{-1}}\lVert y_{x}(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert f(t,\cdot)\rVert_{H^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert y_{x}(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

2.5.1
Energy dissipation

Let

To control the low frequencies, we will rely on the following spectral inequality.

(2.50)

2.6

where

.

For any

,
y_{0}\in H^{-1}(0,\pi)
and

, there exists a unique weak solution

(defined by transposition) to (

).

For
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, let
\varphi_{j}(x):=\sin(jx)

(2.54)

Energy dissipation

In particular, as in

2.7
Example: control of the heat equation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a thick set

In this paragraph, we investigate the small-time null controllability of the heat equation set in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(for
d\geq 1
).
We consider

\begin{cases}y_{t}-\Delta y=u\mathbf{1}_{\omega}&\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
y(0,x)=y_{0}(x)&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\end{cases}

(2.58)

where
y_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the initial data and
u\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))
a control, supported in a fixed measurable subset
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This system is well-posed (see your favorite textbook, e.g.
[
51
, Sec. 9]
).
In particular, it defines an abstract linear control system with
Y=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
U=L^{2}(\omega)
and
p=1
.

Lemma 2.21
.

For any
T>0
,
y_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
u\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))
, there exists a unique solution
y\in C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
to (
2.58
).

2.7.1
Energy dissipation and projections

In the absence of control, the Fourier transform of the solution is given for any
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\widehat{y}(t,\xi)=e^{-t|\xi|^{2}}\widehat{y_{0}}(\xi).

(2.59)

In particular, if
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{y_{0}}\subset B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,k)
for some
k\geq 0
, one has

\lVert y(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq e^{-tk^{2}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}.

(2.60)

2.7.2
Spectral inequality

Hence, by
Proposition

2.13
, we must find an inequality of the form

\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{k}\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}.

(2.61)

For (
2.58
) to be small-time controllable, it is natural to require that the control domain
\omega
is well spread-out all over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The modern theory involves the following definition which is now well understood in the control theory community (see e.g.
[
6
,
33
,
120
]
).

Definition 2.22
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
a>0
.
We say that a measurable
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
(\gamma,a)
-
thick
when

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\left|\omega\cap(x+[0,a]^{d})\right|\geq\gamma a^{d}.

(2.62)

It is well-known that band-limited functions cannot have compact support (uncertainty principle).
More quantitatively, for such thick control domains, one has the following important inequality, initially due to Logvinenko and Sereda
[
84
]
with a constant
C^{Na/\gamma}
and enhanced as below by Kovrijkine
[
74
]
.
We give a short self-contained proof in
Section

A.5
.
The heuristic idea of the proof is to split
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into cells of size
a
.
Within each cell, band-limited functions are well-approximated by their Taylor expansion, to which we can apply a Remez inequality.

Proposition 2.23
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that, for any
\gamma\in(0,1]
,
a>0
and any measurable subset
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is
(\gamma,a)
-thick, one has

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left(\frac{C}{\gamma}\right)^{C(Na+1)}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}

(2.63)

for all
N\geq 0
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C})
such that
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{f}\subset B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,N)
.

2.7.3
Small-time null controllability

We recover the following result, due to
[
33
, Theorem 3]
and
[
120
, Theorem 1.1]
(which moreover prove that thickness is in fact also a necessary condition for small-time null controllability).

Theorem 2.24
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
a>0
.
Assume that
\omega
is
(\gamma,a)
-thick.
Then the heat equation (
2.58
) is small-time null controllable.

Proof.

Define
P_{k}
as the projection on the Fourier modes
|\xi|\leq k
.
More precisely, set

P_{k}y:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\xi\mapsto\widehat{y}(\xi)\mathbf{1}_{|\xi|\leq k}\right).

(2.64)

First, by (
2.60
), the dissipation assumption (
2.5
) holds immediately with
m=1
and
b=2
.

Second, since
\omega
is
(\gamma,a)
-thick, by
Proposition

2.23
, for any
y\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}k}\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}

(2.65)

for some constants
C_{1},c_{1}>0
depending on
a
and
\gamma
.
By (
2.59
), it is clear that
P_{k}
commutes with the semigroup.
Written in the Duhamel form (
2.25
), the operator
B:L^{2}(\omega)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the extension by
0
.
Thus
B^{\star}
is merely the restriction to
\omega
operator and
\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}=\lVert B^{\star}P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}
.
Thus, by
Proposition

2.13
, for any
T\in(0,1]
and
y_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists
u\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))
such that
P_{k}\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)=0
and
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq M_{1}C_{1}e^{c_{1}k}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.
Hence (
2.4
) holds with
a=1
.

Thus,
2.5
holds, and the result follows from
Theorem

2.7
.
∎

2.8
Example: 2D Kolmogorov equation

In this paragraph, we investigate the small-time null controllability of a 2D Kolmogorov equation (also called Fokker–Planck equation), based on the hypoelliptic operator
z\partial_{x}-\partial_{zz}
.
PDEs based on this operator are ubiquitous in kinetic theory or boundary layer theory.
We consider:

\begin{cases}y_{t}+zy_{x}-y_{zz}=u\mathbf{1}_{\omega}&\text{in }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
y(0,x,z)=y_{0}(x,z)&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2},\end{cases}

(2.66)

where
y_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the initial data and
u\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))
is a control, supported in a fixed measurable subset
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We have the following well-posedness result (see
[
78
, Section 2]
).

Proposition 2.25
.

For any
T>0
,
y_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
u\in L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))
, there exists a unique solution
y\in C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
to (
2.66
).

Thus, we have an abstract linear control system with
Y=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
U=L^{2}(\omega)
and
p=1
.

To illustrate that the theory exposed in
Section

2.2
can be applied with projections which are not on eigenspaces of the considered operator, we will here use the same projections on Fourier modes as for the heat equation in
Section

2.7
, thus re-using the spectral theory of
Section

2.7.2
involving thick sets and Logvinenko–Sereda inequality.

2.8.1
Energy dissipation

The following decay estimate is also proved in
[
124
, Section 3]
.

Proposition 2.26
.

There exists
c_{0}:=(4-\sqrt{13})/6>0
such that, for any
k\geq 1
and
y_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\widehat{y_{0}}=0
on
B(0,k)
, we have, for any
t\geq 0
,

\lVert y(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq e^{-c_{0}\min\{t,t^{3}\}k^{2}}\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}.

(2.67)

Proof.

We perform a change of variables to follow the characteristics of the Kolmogorov equation.
Set
\phi(t,x,z):=y(t,x+zt,z)
(or conversely
y(t,x,z)=\phi(t,x-zt,z)
).
In particular,
\phi(0)=y_{0}
.

Then, since
\partial_{t}y=\partial_{t}\phi-z\partial_{x}\phi
,
\partial_{x}y=\partial_{x}\phi
and
\partial_{zz}y=\partial_{zz}\phi-2t\partial_{xz}\phi+t^{2}\partial_{xx}\phi
, we get

(\partial_{t}-\partial_{zz}+2t\partial_{xz}-t^{2}\partial_{xx})\phi=0.

(2.68)

Hence we have a PDE with time-varying but constant in space coefficients.
Taking the Fourier transform in both directions
(x,z)\to(\xi,\zeta)
, we get

(\partial_{t}+\zeta^{2}+2t\xi\zeta+t^{2}\xi^{2})\hat{\phi}=0.

(2.69)

Explicit integration leads to

\hat{\phi}(t,\xi,\zeta)=\exp\left(-t\zeta^{2}-t^{2}\xi\zeta-\frac{t^{3}}{3}\xi^{2}\right)\hat{\phi_{0}}(\xi,\zeta).

(2.70)

Writing

|t^{2}\xi\zeta|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}t\zeta^{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}t^{3}\xi^{2}

(2.71)

and optimizing with respect to
\alpha:=(2+\sqrt{13})/3
, we obtain that

t\zeta^{2}+t^{2}\xi\zeta+\frac{t^{3}}{3}\xi^{2}\geq\frac{4-\sqrt{13}}{6}(t\zeta^{2}+t^{3}\xi^{2}).

(2.72)

Since the initial data is supported for
\zeta^{2}+\xi^{2}\geq k^{2}
, we obtain that

\lVert\phi(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq e^{-c_{0}t\min\{1,t^{2}\}k^{2}}\lVert\phi(0)\rVert_{L^{2}},

(2.73)

which is the claimed estimate since, for any
t\geq 0
,
\lVert\phi(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=\lVert y(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}
.
∎

2.8.2
Small-time null controllability

We now recover the following theorem, due to
[
78
]
(when
\omega
has a particular product structure
\omega=\omega_{x}\times\omega_{z}
) and
[
124
]
for general
\omega
.
Both of these references make the stronger assumption on the control set that it contains open balls of fixed radius near all points.

Theorem 2.27
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
a>0
.
Assume that
\omega
is
(\gamma,a)
-thick.
Then the Kolmogorov equation (
2.66
) is small-time null controllable.

Proof.

Define
P_{k}
as the projection on the Fourier modes
|\xi|^{2}+|\zeta|^{2}\leq k^{2}
.
First, by
Proposition

2.26
, the dissipation condition (
2.5
) holds with
m=3
and
b=2
.

Second, since
\omega
is
(\gamma,a)
-thick, by
Proposition

2.23
, for any
y\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C_{1}e^{c_{1}k}\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}

(2.74)

for some constants
C_{1},c_{1}>0
depending on
a
and
\gamma
.
Written in the Duhamel form (
2.25
), the operator
B:L^{2}(\omega)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is the extension by
0
.
Thus
B^{\star}
is merely the restriction to
\omega
operator and
\lVert P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}=\lVert B^{\star}P_{k}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}
.

Contrary to the heat case, here
P_{k}
does not commute with the semigroup.
Nevertheless, by
Proposition

2.15
, the conclusion of
Lemma

2.6
holds with
a=1
.

Thus the result follows from
Theorem

2.7
.
∎

Remark 2.28
.

The Kolmogorov equation (
2.66
) is a particular case of a more general class of hypoelliptic equations, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck equations, studied in
[

]
, of the form

y_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(Q\nabla^{2}y)-\langle Bx,\nabla\cdot y\rangle=u\mathbf{1}_{\omega}.

(2.75)

Our 2D Kolmogorov case is

Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\

(2.76)

which satisfies the condition
\operatorname{rank}(Q^{\frac{1}{2}},BQ^{\frac{1}{2}})=2
.
In particular, the dissipation estimate

2.26
is included in
[
14
, Lemma 3.1]
and
Theorem

2.27
in
[

, Theorem 1.3]
.

2.9
Comments on our formalism

2.9.1
Time continuity and comparison with the usual definition

Our
Definition

2.2
of an
abstract linear control system
slightly differs from the reference one given by
[
121
, Definition 2.1]
.
Indeed, as in
[
102
, Section 3]
, we require time continuity.
Given Banach spaces
Y
and
U
, and
p\in[1,\infty]
, Weiss’ definition of
[
121
]
is:

Definition 2.29
.

An
abstract linear control system
on
Y
is a pair
\Sigma=(\mathbb{T},\Phi)
, where
\mathbb{T}=(\mathbb{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a strongly continuous semigroup on
Y
and
\Phi=(\Phi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a family of bounded operators from
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
to
Y
such that, for all
u,v\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
and
\tau,t\geq 0
,

\Phi_{\tau+t}(u\underset{\tau}{\diamond}v)=\mathbb{T}_{t}\Phi_{\tau}u+\Phi_{t}v.

(2.77)

Recall the definition of a strongly continuous semigroup on
Y
(see
[
94
]
for more details).

Definition 2.30
.

A
strongly continuous semigroup on
Y
is a family
\mathbb{T}=(\mathbb{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of bounded linear operators from
Y
to
Y
such that
\mathbb{T}_{0}=\operatorname{Id}
,
\mathbb{T}_{t+s}=\mathbb{T}_{t}\circ\mathbb{T}_{s}
(for all
t,s\geq 0
), and
\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}=y_{0}
for all fixed
y_{0}\in Y
.

It is almost straightforward to go back and forth between Weiss’ definition and ours.

Lemma 2.31
.

Definition

2.2
and
Definition

2.29
are equivalent for
p\in[1,\infty)
.

When
p=\infty
,
Definition

2.2
is equivalent to
Definition

2.29
with the added constraint that, for any
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
,
t\mapsto\Phi_{t}u
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Proof.

Given an abstract linear control system
\Sigma
in the sense of
Definition

2.2
, it suffices to define
\mathbb{T}_{t}y:=\Sigma_{t}(y,0)
for any
y\in Y
and
t\geq 0
and
\Phi_{t}u:=\Sigma_{t}(0,u)
for any
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
and
t\geq 0
.
One easily checks that
(\mathbb{T},\Phi)
then satisfies
Definition

2.29
(where (
2.77
) follows from (
2.3
)).

Conversely, given an abstract linear control system
(\mathbb{T},\Phi)
in the sense of
Definition

2.29
, it suffices to define
\Sigma_{t}(y_{0},u):=\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}+\Phi_{t}u
.
One easily checks that
\Sigma
is a family of continuous linear maps satisfying (
2.3
) (thanks to (
2.77
)).
The only subtlety is that
Definition

2.2
requires that

is continuous in time for any fixed
y_{0}\in Y
and

.

The continuity of

from

to

is a basic property of strongly continuous semigroups (resulting from the required continuity at

, the uniform boundedness principle and the semigroup property, see
[

).

The continuity of

from

to

(for a fixed

) is more subtle.
When

, one can use that

in

as

, and that translations are continuous in

to prove that

is indeed continuous (see
[
121
, Proposition 2.3]
).
∎

Remark 2.32
.

When

, it appears to be unknown whether the time continuity of

follows from

2.29
(see
[

[

2.9.2
Relation with the evolution equation

The relation between a time-independent uncontrolled evolution equation of the form
\dot{y}=Ay
with initial data
y(0)=y_{0}
and its integrated version
y(t)=\mathbb{T}_{t}y_{0}
is well understood.

A
is called the
generator
of the semigroup
\mathbb{T}_{t}
.
One knows how to define
A
from
\mathbb{T}
, and conversely one knows many sufficient conditions on
A
to ensure that it generates a strongly continuous semigroup
\mathbb{T}
.
We refer to the classical monograph
[
94
]
.

For a controlled equation of the form
\dot{y}=Ay+Bu
and
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
, many results are known.

Given
A
and
B
, the concept of “admissibility” (of
B
with respect to
A
) determines whether they will generate an abstract linear control system.
We refer to
[
121
]
or
[
116
]
.

Conversely, given an abstract linear control system as in
Definition

2.29
, when
p<\infty
, one can construct an operator
B
(see e.g.
[
121
, Thm. 3.9]
).
When
p=\infty
, the existence is not guaranteed (see
[
122
, Remark 3.7]
for a counter-example and
[
110
, Thm. 4.2.1]
for regulated controls).

More generally, one can also consider abstract linear control systems along with observation operators (see e.g.
[
117
]
and
[
67
]
for a survey of admissibility of observation operators).

2.10
Why only null controllability?

For ODEs (such as in
Section

1
) and for some conservative PDEs (such as the semilinear wave equation in
Section

3.2.2
), one can often prove
exact controllability
: for any initial state
y_{0}\in Y
and any target
y_{T}\in Y
, there exists a control
u
steering the system from
y_{0}
to
y_{T}
in time
T
.

However, for the dissipative PDEs studied in this section, one cannot hope for such a result, due to the regularizing effect of the dynamics.
Roughly speaking, as soon as
t>0
, solutions become smoother than the initial data, even with a non-zero control.
Consequently, for fixed
T>0
, the set of states that can be attained at time
T
is usually contained in a proper subspace of the state space
Y
.
In particular, one cannot expect to reach an arbitrary target
y_{T}\in Y
.

Example 2.33
.

Consider the 1D heat equation (
2.42
) with internal control supported in
\omega=(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
.
Let
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
,
T>0
, and
u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\omega)
, and denote by
y
the corresponding solution.
On the uncontrolled region
(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, the function
y
satisfies the
homogeneous
heat equation (since
u\equiv 0
there).
Standard interior regularity (which can be proved by energy estimates and bootstrapping) then yields
y(T)\in C^{\infty}(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.
This rules out exact controllability in the state space
Y=L^{2}(0,1)
.
Note that
y(T)
is actually even smoother than
C^{\infty}
, since it typically enjoys analytic regularity.

Reachable set.

Obtaining a precise description of the
reachable set
at time
T
(i.e. the range of the input-to-state map) is a delicate problem, even in very simple settings.
For instance, for the 1D heat equation controlled from both endpoints, the program initiated in
[
37
]
was only completed much more recently in
[
56
,
57
]
; see also
[
34
, Section 3]
for a brief survey of the many intermediate contributions.
Beyond its intrinsic interest, a sharp characterization of reachable states unlocks new techniques for nonlinear control (see
Section

3.4.2
).

Null controllability and controllability to trajectories.

In more complex PDE models, a common and robust objective is
controllability to trajectories
.
Fix a
reference pair

(\bar{y}_{0},\bar{u})
, and let
\bar{y}
be the associated solution.
One asks whether, for any other initial state
y_{0}\in Y
, there exists a control
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
such that
y(T)=\bar{y}(T)
.
The special case
\bar{y}_{0}=0
,
\bar{u}=0
(hence
\bar{y}\equiv 0
) is the
null controllability
problem:
given
y_{0}\in Y
, can one find
u
such that
y(T)=0
?
This is also the most natural goal in many applications, where the objective is to drive the system to rest.

For
linear
systems, controllability to trajectories is equivalent to null controllability: indeed, if
\bar{y}
solves the system with data
(\bar{y}_{0},\bar{u})
, then the difference
z:=y-\bar{y}
solves the same linear dynamics with initial condition
z(0)=y_{0}-\bar{y}_{0}
and control
u-\bar{u}
; thus
y(T)=\bar{y}(T)
is equivalent to
z(T)=0
.

Operator theoretic definition.

We can formalize these notions using the input-output maps introduced in
Section

2.9
.
For
T>0
, recall that the
semigroup

\mathbb{T}_{T}:Y\to Y
and the
control map

\Phi_{T}:L^{p}((0,T);U)\to Y
are defined by
\mathbb{T}_{T}y_{0}:=\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},0)
and
\Phi_{T}u:=\Sigma_{T}(0,u)
.
By linearity, the final state is given by
y(T)=\mathbb{T}_{T}y_{0}+\Phi_{T}u
.
Thus, with these notations

•

Exact controllability
is the surjectivity condition
\operatorname{Ran}\Phi_{T}=Y
.

•

Null controllability
is the inclusion
\operatorname{Ran}\mathbb{T}_{T}\subset\operatorname{Ran}\Phi_{T}
.

•

Approximate controllability
is the density condition
\overline{\operatorname{Ran}\Phi_{T}}=Y
.

For dissipative systems, since
\mathbb{T}_{T}
maps
Y
into a smaller subspace of smooth functions, the inclusion
\operatorname{Ran}\mathbb{T}_{T}\subset\operatorname{Ran}\Phi_{T}
is much easier to satisfy than
\operatorname{Ran}\Phi_{T}=Y
.

3
From linear to local nonlinear controllability

In this section, we consider a nonlinear control system.
Assuming that its linearization at the null equilibrium is controllable, we will present methods to establish the local controllability of the nonlinear system.
Heuristically, one can think that we consider systems of the form:

\dot{y}=Ay+Bu+N(u,y),

(3.1)

where
N
models a nonlinearity such that
N(0,0)=0
and
DN(0,0)=0
.
For small data, one expects that the contribution of
N
to the dynamics can be estimated perturbatively and that we can transfer the controllability of the linearized system
\dot{y}=Ay+Bu
to (
3.1
).

3.1
Input-output formalism

As in
Section

2.1
, we will use an abstract “input-output” formalism which avoids talking about the objects
A
,
B
and
N
involved in the heuristic system (
3.1
).
Related nonlinear definitions can for example be found in
[
90
, Definition 1.3]
.
As in
Section

2.1
, fix
p\in[1,\infty]
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that
\mathcal{S}
is an
abstract nonlinear control system
when it is a family
(\mathcal{S}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of maps from
Y\times L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
to
Y
such that:

•

for all
y_{0}\in Y
and
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
,
t\mapsto\mathcal{S}_{t}(y_{0},u)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\mathcal{S}_{0}(y_{0},u)=y_{0}
,

•

for all
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{S}_{t}(0,0)=0
,

•

for all
t,\tau\geq 0
,
y_{0}\in Y
and
u,v\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
,

\mathcal{S}_{t+\tau}(y_{0},u\underset{\tau}{\diamond}v)=\mathcal{S}_{t}(\mathcal{S}_{\tau}(y_{0},u),v).

(3.2)

Hence
\mathcal{S}
represents the nonlinear state transition map for the heuristic evolution equation (
3.1
), while
\Sigma
of
Definition

2.2
represents the linear state transition map for its linearized version (
2.1
).
We give multiple concrete examples in the following subsections.

Typically, for well-posed systems in the Hadamard sense, the solution state
\mathcal{S}_{t}(y_{0},u)
depends continuously on
y_{0}
and
u
, but we will not need this property here.

Remark 3.2
.

To avoid burdening the exposition,
Definition

3.1
corresponds to globally well-posed systems.
Since we will be considering only local properties near the equilibrium, it would be sufficient in this section that
\mathcal{S}
be defined only for small enough times, controls and initial states.

3.2
Transferring exact controllability

We start with the easier case where the underlying linear system is exactly controllable.

Definition 3.3
.

For
T>0
, we say that the abstract linear control system
\Sigma
is
exactly controllable
in time
T
when there exists a bounded linear map
L:Y\to L^{p}((0,T);U)
such that, for all
y_{1}\in Y
, the control
u:=L(y_{1})
satisfies
\Sigma_{T}(0,u)=y_{1}
.

Under a natural regularity assumption, the local controllability of the nonlinear system is a direct consequence of the controllability of its linearization.
To lighten the conditions to be checked, we use the notion of strong Fréchet-differentiability.
We refer the reader to
[
104
, Chapter 25]
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
E,F
be Banach spaces and
f:E\to F
.
We say that
f
is
strongly Fréchet-differentiable at
0\in E
when there exists a continuous linear map
Df(0):E\to F
such that

\lVert f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})-Df(0)(x_{1}-x_{2})\rVert_{F}=\underset{x_{1},x_{2}\to 0}{o}\left(\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert_{E}\right).

(3.3)

The following implicit function theorem is proved in
[
104
, Paragraph 25.13]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
E_{1},E_{2},F
be Banach spaces and
f:E_{1}\times E_{2}\to F
such that
f(0,0)=0
.
Assume that
f
is strongly Fréchet-differentiable at
(0,0)
and that
D_{2}f(0,0):E_{2}\to F
is a linear isomorphism.
Then there exists a Lipschitz-continuous map
g:E_{1}\to E_{2}
defined in a neighborhood of
0\in E_{1}
such that, for every
(x,y)
in a neighborhood of
(0,0)\in E_{1}\times E_{2}
,
f(x,y)=0
if and only if
y=g(x)
.
Moreover,
g
is strongly Fréchet-differentiable at
0
and
Dg(0)=-(D_{2}f(0,0))^{-1}D_{1}f(0,0)
.

Remark 3.6
.

Strong differentiability at
0
is stronger than usual Fréchet-differentiability, but weaker than being of class
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of
0
(see
[
92
, end of Section 2]
).
It is tailored for applying implicit function arguments without requiring the knowledge (or the existence) of the derivatives in a neighborhood of the point; only a single derivative at the point suffices.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
E_{1},E_{2},F
be Banach spaces and
f:E_{1}\times E_{2}\to F
such that
f(0,0)=0
.
Assume that
f
is strongly Fréchet-differentiable at
(0,0)
and that
D_{2}f(0,0):E_{2}\to F
has a continuous right inverse
R:F\to E_{2}
.
Then there exists a Lipschitz-continuous map
g:E_{1}\to E_{2}
defined in a neighborhood of
0\in E_{1}
with
g(0)=0
such that, for every
x
in a neighborhood of
0\in E_{1}
,
f(x,g(x))=0
.
Moreover,
g
is strongly Fréchet-differentiable at
0
and
Dg(0)=-R\circ D_{1}f(0,0)
.

Proof.

The strategy is to construct a subspace of
E_{2}
on which the partial derivative becomes an isomorphism, and then apply
Lemma

3.5
.
Let
L_{2}:=D_{2}f(0,0):E_{2}\to F
.
By assumption,
L_{2}
admits a continuous right inverse
R:F\to E_{2}
.
Let us define the operator
\pi:=R\circ L_{2}:E_{2}\to E_{2}
.
Then
\pi
is a continuous projection with
\ker\pi=\ker L_{2}
.
Thus, we can write, as a topological vector sum
E_{2}=\ker L_{2}\oplus\operatorname{im}\pi
.
Moreover,
L_{2}
is an isomorphism from
\operatorname{im}\pi
to
F
.
∎

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
T>0
,
\mathcal{S}
be an abstract nonlinear control system and
\Sigma
be a linear one.
Assume that:

1.

\Sigma
is exactly controllable in time
T
;

2.

\mathcal{S}_{T}:Y\times L^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)\to Y
is strongly Fréchet-differentiable at
(0,0)
, with derivative
\Sigma_{T}
.

Then the nonlinear system
\mathcal{S}
is
locally exactly controllable
in time
T
at
(0,0)
.
That is, there exists
\delta>0
such that for any
y_{0},y_{1}\in B_{Y}(0,\delta)
, there exists
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
such that
\mathcal{S}_{T}(y_{0},u)=y_{1}
.
Moreover, there exists
C>0
such that
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq C(\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}+\lVert y_{1}\rVert_{Y})
.

Proof.

Let
E_{1}:=Y\times Y
,
E_{2}:=L^{p}((0,T);U)
and
F:=Y
.
Define
f:E_{1}\times E_{2}\to F
by

f((y_{0},y_{1}),u):=\mathcal{S}_{T}(y_{0},u)-y_{1}.

(3.4)

By
Definition

3.1
,
f(0,0)=0
.
By the second assumption,
f
is strongly Fréchet-differentiable at
(0,0)
.
By the first assumption and our
Definition

3.3
,
D_{2}f(0,0)
has a bounded right inverse.
Hence, by
Corollary

3.7
, for
y_{0},y_{1}\in Y
small enough, there exists
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
such that
f((y_{0},y_{1}),u)=0
, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{T}(y_{0},u)=y_{1}
.
The Lipschitz-continuity of the inverse given by
Corollary

3.7
gives the estimate of
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}
.
∎

3.2.2
Example: semilinear wave equation

As a PDE example, let us study a 1D semilinear wave equation.
This is both an easy toy model, and relevant for many applications; see

We consider:

\begin{cases}y_{tt}-y_{xx}=G(y)&\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,\pi),\\

G\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
is such that
G(0)=G^{\prime}(0)=0
and
|G^{\prime}(s)|\leq|s|
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
We start with the linear theory before proving the exact controllability of (
3.5
) for
T\geq 2\pi
.

Since (
3.5
) is a PDE of second order with respect to time, the state of the system is actually the pair
(y,y_{t})
.
We therefore introduce the state space
Y:=L^{2}(0,\pi)\times H^{-1}(0,\pi)
and control space
U:=\mathbb{R}
.
We will look for solutions in the solution space

\mathcal{Y}:=C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(0,\pi))\cap C^{1}([0,T];H^{-1}(0,\pi)).

(3.6)

Linear well-posedness is standard (see
[
116
, Theorem 10.9.3 and Section 4.1]
or
[
96
, Theorem 6.6.1]
).

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
T>0
,
(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})\in Y
,
f\in L^{1}((0,T);H^{-1}(0,\pi))
and
u\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
.
There exists a unique weak solution
y\in\mathcal{Y}
to the following linear wave equation

\begin{cases}y_{tt}-y_{xx}=f&\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,\pi),\\
y(t,0)=u(t)&\quad\text{on }(0,T),\\
y(t,\pi)=0&\quad\text{on }(0,T),\end{cases}

(3.7)

with initial data
(y,y_{t})(0)=(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})
.
Moreover, there exists
C_{T}>0
such that

\lVert y\rVert_{C^{0}L^{2}}+\lVert y_{t}\rVert_{C^{0}H^{-1}}\leq C_{T}\left(\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}H^{-1}}+\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert y_{0}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right).

(3.8)

In particular, setting
\Sigma_{s}((y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime}),u):=(y,y_{t})(s)
for
s\in[0,T]
defines an abstract linear control system in the sense of
Definition

2.2
with
p=2
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
T\geq 2\pi
.
There exists a bounded linear map
L:Y\to L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
such that, for all
(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})\in Y
, the control
u:=L(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})
is such that the solution to (
3.7
) with null initial data, control
u
and null source term satisfies
(y,y_{t})(T)=(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We decompose the state on the Hilbert basis of
L^{2}(0,\pi)
given by the eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet Laplacian:
e_{k}(x):=\sqrt{2/\pi}\sin(kx)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
We write
y(t,x)=\sum_{k\geq 1}y_{k}(t)e_{k}(x)
.
Taking the inner product of (
3.7
) with
e_{k}
, we obtain the following cascade of independent harmonic oscillators:

\ddot{y}_{k}(t)+k^{2}y_{k}(t)=b_{k}u(t),\quad\text{with }b_{k}:=k\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}.

(3.9)

We introduce the complex unknown
z_{k}(t):=ky_{k}(t)+i\dot{y}_{k}(t)
which satisfies
\dot{z}_{k}(t)=-ikz_{k}(t)+ib_{k}u(t)
and
z_{k}(0)=0
.
Therefore

z_{k}(T)=ib_{k}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-ik(T-t)}u(t)\mathrm{d}t.

(3.10)

Let
T=2\pi
(if
T>2\pi
, we extend
u
by
0
on
[0,T-2\pi]
).
Hence, solving the control problem
y(T)=y_{1}
and
y_{t}(T)=y_{1}^{\prime}
amounts to finding
u\in L^{2}((0,2\pi);\mathbb{R})
solving the moment problem:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{ikt}u(t)\mathrm{d}t=\frac{e^{ikT}}{ib_{k}}(ky_{1,k}+iy^{\prime}_{1,k})=:a_{k}\in\mathbb{C}

(3.11)

where
(y_{1,k})_{k}
and
(y^{\prime}_{1,k})_{k}
are the coefficients of the target data
y_{1}
and
y_{1}^{\prime}
.
Setting
a_{0}:=0
and
a_{-k}:=\overline{a_{k}}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
we can extend the moment problem to all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Using that the
(e^{ikt})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(0,2\pi)
, we set

u(t):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{j}e^{-ijt}.

(3.12)

Then
u
solves the moment problem by orthogonality of the basis.
By Parseval’s identity,

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|a_{k}|^{2}\leq C\lVert y_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\lVert y_{1}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}^{2}

(3.13)

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

The proof of
Lemma

3.10
relies on Parseval’s identity because the PDE we are studying is exactly the wave equation
y_{tt}-y_{xx}=0
, associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}=k
.
For more general hyperbolic equations, for example if one considers the ubiquitous Sine-Gordon model
G(y)=-\sin y
, one must study the linearized equation
y_{tt}-y_{xx}+y=0
, associated with eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{k^{2}+1}
.
One loses the time-orthogonality of the
e^{i\lambda_{k}t}
on
(0,2\pi)
, so one must replace Parseval’s identity with more powerful tools (see e.g.
[
100
]
), called Ingham inequalities (see
[
65
]
).
A particularly useful version of Ingham’s inequality in such contexts relating the minimal control time with the asymptotic gap of the eigenvalues is due to Haraux in
[
55
, Théorème 4]
.

We can now move to the nonlinear equation (
3.5
).

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
T>0
.
There exists
\delta>0
such that, for all
(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})\in Y
and
u\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert y_{0}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\delta
, there exists a unique weak solution
y\in\mathcal{Y}
to (
3.5
) with initial data
(y,y_{t})(0)=(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})
.
It moreover satisfies

\lVert y\rVert_{C^{0}L^{2}}+\lVert y_{t}\rVert_{C^{0}H^{-1}}\leq 2C_{T}\left(\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert y_{0}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right).

(3.14)

Proof.

Fix
T>0
.
Let
\delta>0
to be chosen later.
Let
(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})\in Y
and
u\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
such that
\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert y_{0}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\delta
.
We define a map
\Theta:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}
as follows.
Given
z\in\mathcal{Y}
, let
y\in\mathcal{Y}
be the solution given by
Lemma

3.9
to (
3.7
) for the source term
f:=G(z)
.

Using the assumptions on
G
,
|G(s)|\leq s^{2}
.
Hence, one has

\lVert\Theta(z)\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C_{T}\left(\delta+\lVert G(z)\rVert_{L^{1}H^{-1}}\right)\leq C_{T}\left(\delta+T\lVert G(z)\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{1}}\right)\leq C_{T}\left(\delta+T\lVert z\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}}^{2}\right).

(3.15)

Thus, given
R>0
, if
z\in B_{\mathcal{Y}}(0,R)
,
\lVert\Theta(z)\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C_{T}(\delta+TR^{2})
.
In particular, with
R:=1/(4TC_{T})
and
\delta:=R/(4C_{T})
, one has
\Theta(z)\in B_{\mathcal{Y}}(0,R/2)
, so
\Theta
preserves
B_{\mathcal{Y}}(0,R)
.

Given
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
\Theta(z_{1})-\Theta(z_{2})
is a solution to (
3.7
) with null initial data and null control, and a source term
G(z_{1})-G(z_{2})
.
Using the assumptions on
G
, for any
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, one has
|G(s_{1})-G(s_{2})|\leq(|s_{1}|+|s_{2}|)|s_{1}-s_{2}|
.
This entails that

\begin{split}\lVert G(z_{1})-G(z_{2})\rVert_{L^{1}(H^{-1})}&\leq T\lVert G(z_{1})-G(z_{2})\rVert_{C^{0}(L^{1})}\\
&\leq T\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{\pi}(|z_{1}(t)|+|z_{2}(t)|)|z_{1}(t)-z_{2}(t)|\\
&\leq T\left(\lVert z_{1}\rVert_{C^{0}L^{2}}+\lVert z_{2}\rVert_{C^{0}L^{2}}\right)\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\rVert_{C^{0}L^{2}}.\end{split}

(3.16)

Hence
\lVert\Theta(z_{1})-\Theta(z_{2})\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq 2RTC_{T}\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}
with our choice of
R
.

Hence
\Theta
is a contraction from
B_{\mathcal{Y}}(0,R)
to itself.
By the Banach fixed-point theorem, it admits a unique fixed-point
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, which is therefore the unique weak solution to (
3.5
).

Moreover, using
z_{2}=0
, we obtain from the previous estimate that, at the fixed-point
y=\Theta(y)
,
\lVert y-\Theta(0)\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}=\lVert\Theta(y)-\Theta(0)\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert y-0\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}
so
\lVert y\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq 2\lVert\Theta(0)\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Using the linear estimate (
3.8
) for
\Theta(0)
proves the nonlinear estimate (
3.14
).
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

For
T>0
, let
\Sigma_{T}:=(y,y_{t})(T)
and
\mathcal{S}_{T}:=(y,y_{t})(T)
be the respective
Y
-valued solutions to (
3.7
) and (
3.5
) at time
T
(depending on
(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})\in Y
and
u\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
).
Then
\mathcal{S}_{T}
is strongly Fréchet-differentiable at
0
with derivative
\Sigma_{T}
.

Proof.

Recalling
Definition

3.4
of strong Fréchet-differentiability, we need to prove that, as
(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})
,
(\tilde{y}_{0},\tilde{y}_{0}^{\prime})\in Y
and
u,\tilde{u}\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
go to
0
,

\lVert w(T)\rVert_{L^{2}\times H^{-1}}=o\big(\lVert y_{0}-\tilde{y}_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert y_{0}^{\prime}-\tilde{y}_{0}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}+\lVert u-\tilde{u}\rVert_{L^{2}}\big)

(3.17)

where
w(t):=\mathcal{S}_{t}((y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime}),u)-\mathcal{S}_{t}((\tilde{y}_{0},\tilde{y}_{0}^{\prime}),\tilde{u})-\Sigma_{t}((y_{0}-\tilde{y}_{0},y_{0}^{\prime}-\tilde{y}_{0}^{\prime}),u-\tilde{u})
.
Thus
w\in\mathcal{Y}
is a solution to (
3.7
) with null initial and boundary data, and a source term
G(y)-G(\tilde{y})
, where we denote by
y,\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
the two nonlinear solutions.
As in (
3.16
), this entails that

\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq TC_{T}(\lVert y\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}+\lVert\tilde{y}\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}})\lVert y-\tilde{y}\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}.

(3.18)

Moreover,
y-\tilde{y}
is a solution to (
3.7
) with initial data
(y_{0}-\tilde{y}_{0},y_{0}^{\prime}-\tilde{y}_{0}^{\prime})
, control
u-\tilde{u}
and source term
G(y)-G(\tilde{y})
.
Combining (
3.8
) and (
3.16
),

\lVert y-\tilde{y}\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C_{T}\left(\lVert y_{0}-\tilde{y}_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert y_{0}^{\prime}-\tilde{y}_{0}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}+\lVert u-\tilde{u}\rVert_{L^{2}}+T(\lVert y\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}+\lVert\tilde{y}\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}})\lVert y-\tilde{y}\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\right).

(3.19)

From the nonlinear estimate (
3.14
),
\lVert y\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}
and
\lVert\tilde{y}\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}
are small when
y_{0},\tilde{y}_{0},y_{0}^{\prime},\tilde{y}_{0}^{\prime},u,\tilde{u}
go to
0
.
Thus we can absorb the right-hand side in the left-hand side, and both estimates entail (
3.17
).
∎

We thus obtain the following local exact controllability result in time
T\geq 2\pi
for (
3.5
).
Such a result is well-known.
Local controllability for general semilinear wave equations was proved in
[
39
]
using the linear theory of
[
100
]
, and the linear test inspired by
[
86
, Theorem 3]
for ODEs.
There are nonlinearities
G
for which one can also prove global controllability results (see
[
125
]
).

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
T\geq 2\pi
.
There exists
\delta>0
such that, for any
(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime}),(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})\in Y
with
\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert y_{0}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}+\lVert y_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert y_{1}^{\prime}\rVert_{H^{-1}}\leq\delta
, there exists
u\in L^{2}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
such that the unique solution to (
3.5
) with initial data
(y_{0},y_{0}^{\prime})
satisfies
(y,y_{t})(T)=(y_{1},y_{1}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We apply the general linear test of
Theorem

3.8
with
p=2
.
We proved that its two assumptions are satisfied in
Lemma

3.10
and
Lemma

3.13
.
∎

Other examples

3.8
(or variations of it) is ubiquitous in the local controllability literature, for many PDEs of different nature.
Some of the most frequent examples are the Korteweg–de Vries equation (see e.g.
[

]
for small-time local controllability when the length of the domain is not “critical”), or the bilinear Schrödinger equation (see e.g.

3.3
Transferring null controllability

We move on to the harder case where the underlying linear system is not exactly controllable as in
Section

3.2
, but only null-controllable.
We present a general abstract result.

3.3.1
A general abstract result

In 2013, Liu, Takahashi and Tucsnak introduced in
[
83
]
a time-iteration argument which allows one to deduce the small-time local null controllability of a nonlinear system from the cost of the small-time null controllability of its linearization at the equilibrium.
This time iteration is also at the heart of a series of recent abstract results
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
by Alabau-Boussouira, Cannarsa and Urbani concerning bilinear parabolic equations (see
Section

3.3.2
).

We present here a streamlined reformulation of these general arguments, which bypasses the historical intermediate “source term method” and makes the approach directly applicable as a black box.
It is straightforward to use in concrete systems and avoids too many assumptions on the operators
A
and
B
of (
3.1
): one only checks the two natural assumptions below.

Assumption 3.15
.

The abstract linear control system
\Sigma
is
small-time null controllable with exponential cost
: for all
T>0
and
y_{0}\in Y
, there exists
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
such that
\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)=0
and

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq C_{L}\exp\left(cT^{-\sigma}\right)\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}

(3.20)

for some given constants
\sigma,c,C_{L}>0
(independent of
T
).

Assumption 3.16
.

The abstract nonlinear control system
\mathcal{S}
is a
nonlinear perturbation
of
\Sigma
: there exist
\gamma>1
and
C_{N}>0
such that, for all
T>0
,
y_{0}\in Y
and
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
,

\lVert\mathcal{S}_{T}(y_{0},u)-\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},u)\rVert_{Y}\leq C_{N}\left(\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\right)^{\gamma}.

(3.21)

Theorem 3.17
.

Under
3.15
and
3.16
, the system
\mathcal{S}
is small-time locally null controllable: for every
T,\eta>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that, for all
y_{0}\in Y
with
\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}\leq\delta
, there exists
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
with
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq\eta
such that
\mathcal{S}_{T}(y_{0},u)=0
.

Proof.

The proof relies on a time iteration.
Within each time interval, we apply the control obtained from the linear approximation.
Reminiscent of Newton’s method, the doubly-exponential convergence of the nonlinear error mitigates the small-time blow-up of the linear control cost.

Step 1:
Time iteration and linear step on each slice.

Partition
[0,T]
into subintervals
[T_{j},T_{j+1}]
, where
T_{0}=0
and
T_{j+1}=T_{j}+\tau_{j}
, with the constraint
\sum_{j\geq 0}\tau_{j}=T
.
We choose

\tau_{j}:=T(1-\rho)\rho^{j}\quad\text{for}\quad\rho:=\gamma^{-\frac{1}{\sigma+1}}\in(0,1).

(3.22)

Given an initial state
y_{0}
, define inductively
y_{j+1}:=\mathcal{S}_{\tau_{j}}(y_{j},u_{j})
, where
u_{j}\in L^{p}((0,\tau_{j});U)
is the control given by
3.15
, such that
\Sigma_{\tau_{j}}(y_{j},u_{j})=0
and

\lVert u_{j}\rVert_{L^{p}((0,\tau_{j});U)}\leq C_{L}\exp(c\tau_{j}^{-\sigma})\lVert y_{j}\rVert_{Y}.

(3.23)

Step 2:
Stepwise nonlinear contraction.

By
3.16
and (
3.23
),

\begin{split}\lVert y_{j+1}\rVert=\lVert\mathcal{S}_{\tau_{j}}(y_{j},u_{j})-\Sigma_{\tau_{j}}(y_{j},u_{j})\rVert&\leq C_{N}(\lVert y_{j}\rVert+\lVert u_{j}\rVert)^{\gamma}\\
&\leq C_{N}(1+C_{L})^{\gamma}\exp(c\gamma\tau_{j}^{-\sigma})\lVert y_{j}\rVert^{\gamma}.\end{split}

(3.24)

Taking logs and dividing by
\gamma^{j+1}
,

\frac{\ln\lVert y_{j+1}\rVert}{\gamma^{j+1}}\leq\frac{\ln\lVert y_{j}\rVert}{\gamma^{j}}+\frac{\ln\left(C_{N}(1+C_{L})^{\gamma}\right)}{\gamma^{j+1}}+\frac{c}{\gamma^{j}\tau_{j}^{\sigma}}.

(3.25)

Summing this telescoping inequality over
j
and recalling (
3.22
) leads to

\frac{\ln\lVert y_{j}\rVert}{\gamma^{j}}\leq\ln\lVert y_{0}\rVert+C_{T}\quad\text{where}\quad C_{T}:=\frac{\ln\left(C_{N}(1+C_{L})^{\gamma}\right)}{\gamma-1}+\frac{cT^{-\sigma}}{(1-\rho)^{\sigma+1}}.

(3.26)

Given a constant
M_{T}>0
, when

\lVert y_{0}\rVert\leq\exp\left(-M_{T}-C_{T}\right),

(3.27)

estimate (
3.26
) yields

\lVert y_{j}\rVert\leq\exp(-M_{T}\gamma^{j})\underset{j\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

(3.28)

Step 3:
Conclusion.

The overall control
u
is the concatenation of the controls
u_{j}
on
[T_{j},T_{j+1}]
.
We prove below that there exists
M_{T}>0
such that, under condition (
3.27
), for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lVert u_{j}\rVert_{L^{p}((0,\tau_{j});U)}\leq 2^{-j}C_{L}\exp(C_{T}+c\tau_{0}^{-\sigma})\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(3.29)

Hence, by the triangle inequality,
u\in L^{p}((0,T);U)
and

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}((0,T);U)}\leq\sum_{j\geq 0}\lVert u_{j}\rVert_{L^{p}((0,\tau_{j});U)}\leq 2C_{L}\exp(C_{T}+c\tau_{0}^{-\sigma})\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{Y}.

(3.30)

Since
\mathcal{S}
is an abstract nonlinear control system, the associated solution
y(t):=\mathcal{S}_{t}(y_{0},u)
belongs to
C^{0}([0,T];Y)
.
By the composition property (
3.2
),
y(T_{j})=y_{j}
.
By (
3.28
),
y_{j}\to 0
.
Since
T_{j}\to T
, by continuity, we conclude that
\mathcal{S}_{T}(y_{0},u)=y(T)=0
.
By (
3.30
), choosing
\lVert y_{0}\rVert\leq\delta
small enough as in the theorem statement, we can ensure that the control is as small as desired.

Step 4:
Proof of (
3.29
).

We fix

M_{T}:=\frac{\ln 2}{\gamma-1}+c\frac{\rho^{-\sigma}-1}{\gamma-1}\tau_{0}^{-\sigma}>0.

(3.31)

From (
3.23
) and (
3.26
), under the smallness hypothesis (
3.27
), for all
j\geq 0
,

\begin{split}\ln\frac{\lVert u_{j}\rVert}{\lVert y_{0}\rVert}&\leq\ln C_{L}+c\tau_{j}^{-\sigma}+(\gamma^{j}-1)\ln\lVert y_{0}\rVert+\gamma^{j}C_{T}\\
&\leq\ln C_{L}+c\tau_{j}^{-\sigma}-(\gamma^{j}-1)M_{T}+C_{T}.\end{split}

(3.32)

Then, using
\frac{\gamma^{j}-1}{\gamma-1}\geq j
and the monotonicity of
s\mapsto\frac{s^{j}-1}{s-1}
on
(1,\infty)
(together with
\gamma>\rho^{-\sigma}=\gamma^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma+1}}
),

(\gamma^{j}-1)M_{T}\geq j\ln 2+c\tau_{0}^{-\sigma}(\rho^{-\sigma j}-1)=j\ln 2+c(\tau_{j}^{-\sigma}-\tau_{0}^{-\sigma}).

(3.33)

Plugging this into (
3.32
) cancels the
c\tau_{j}^{-\sigma}
term and yields

\ln\frac{\lVert u_{j}\rVert}{\lVert y_{0}\rVert}\leq-j\ln 2+\ln C_{L}+c\tau_{0}^{-\sigma}+C_{T},

(3.34)

which proves (
3.29
) and completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.18
.

To avoid burdening the exposition, we stated
Theorem

3.17
for globally well-posed time-invariant control systems.
Nevertheless, it is clear from the proof that

•

the exact same result holds even if the nonlinear system
\mathcal{S}
is only locally well-posed (for example for small enough times, or controls, or initial states),

•

an analogous result holds
mutatis mutandis
for time-dependent systems provided that
3.15
and
3.16
hold uniformly in the considered time interval.

Remark 3.19
.

As highlighted in
[
3
,
4
]
, this construction is entirely explicit (no abstract fixed-point argument is used).
Moreover, tracking the constants in the proof, one obtains a lower bound for the size of the neighborhood
\delta
(which is
\exp(-M_{T}-C_{T})
in our proof) and an upper bound for the control cost
\lVert u\rVert/\lVert y_{0}\rVert
(which is
2C_{L}\exp(C_{T}+c\tau_{0}^{-\sigma})
in our proof).

3.3.2
Historical perspective

We recall the origins of the method and its recent enhancements.

Source term method.

The approach introduced in
[
83
]
has been extensively applied to many PDEs and is commonly referred to as the “source term method”.
In that paper, the strategy can be summarized as follows:

1.

Starting from the small-time null controllability of a linear system
\dot{y}=Ay+Bu
, the authors use the abstract time-iteration argument described above to deduce that any system of the form
\dot{y}=Ay+Bu+f
, where
f
is a given
source term
, is also small-time null controllable, provided that
f\in\mathcal{F}
, a weighted functional space ensuring that
f
decays very fast as
t\to T
.
The associated control and state then also decay very fast as
t\to T
.
Typically, the weights for the source term, the state and the control are of the form
\exp(1/(T-t))
.

2.

For a sufficiently small initial state
y_{0}

This yields a solution of the nonlinear system

The idea of obtaining local controllability for a nonlinear system via a fixed-point argument based on linear controllability in the presence of rapidly decaying source terms had already been used in
[

62

In these works, the first step was carried out by means of Carleman estimates directly handling the source terms, without resorting to time iteration.

A key contribution of

Although

The source term method has been very successfully used in many contexts.
To cite a few: for a Burgers equation with non-local viscosity in

Outside of null controllability (recall

Direct time iteration for bilinear parabolic equations.

The time iteration presented above and dating back to the source term method is also at the heart of the series of recent papers

3.3.3
Example: a bilinear heat equation

In this paragraph we investigate the small-time local controllability of a bilinear heat equation introduced in

Using our abstract result, we give a much shorter proof of its controllability.
We consider the system

\begin{cases}y_{t}(t,x)-y_{xx}(t,x)=u(t)\Gamma[y(t)](x)&\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,\pi)\\

Using a Banach fixed-point argument, one easily proves (see

For every

Assume that, for all

Lemma 3.22

There exists

3.3.4
Example: the viscous Burgers equation

In this paragraph, we investigate the small-time local null controllability of the viscous Burgers equation.

For any

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\eta
such that the unique associated solution to (

.

Let

be the state space and
U:=L^{2}(\omega)
, the control space and

.
The linearized system at the null equilibrium is the controlled heat equation (

By

2.18
, the heat equation is small-time null controllable with exponential cost, so the linearized system satisfies

(with

By

Lemma 3.26
.

There exists
C>0
such that, for any

The proof consists in classical PDE energy estimates: computing the equation satisfied by the difference

3.4
Other approaches

We give a short overview of two other methods which allow to prove the small-time local null controllability of a nonlinear system: an historic one, and a recent perspective.

3.4.1
Uniform controllability of all linearizations

Historically, the first results on the small-time local null controllability of nonlinear parabolic equations relied on the uniform controllability of a family of linearizations.

As an example, consider again the Burgers system (

).
Now, instead of considering only its linearization at

and consider its linearization at

\begin{cases}y_{t}-y_{xx}+\bar{y}y_{x}+\bar{y}_{x}y=u\mathbf{1}_{\omega}&\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,1),\\

y(0,x)=y_{0}(x)&\text{in }(0,1).\end{cases}

(3.48)

This is a family of linear time-dependent

4

Given

3.4.2
Exact controllability on the reachable space

In
[
34
]
, Ervedoza, Le Balc’h and Tucsnak give a modern perspective on the controllability of dissipative systems, by viewing them as being exactly controllable on their reachable space.

To present their ideas, we use the notations of
Section

2.9
for abstract linear control systems.
For
T>0
, recall that the
semigroup

\mathbb{T}_{T}:Y\to Y
and the
control map

\Phi_{T}:L^{p}((0,T);U)\to Y
are defined by
\mathbb{T}_{T}y_{0}:=\Sigma_{T}(y_{0},0)
and
\Phi_{T}u:=\Sigma_{T}(0,u)
.
By linearity,
y(T)=\mathbb{T}_{T}y_{0}+\Phi_{T}u
.

Fix

, introduce the

\operatorname{Ran}\Phi_{T}
and endow it with the following norm:

\lVert z\rVert_{\operatorname{Ran}\Phi_{T}}:=\inf\left\{\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\mid u\in L^{2}((0,T);U)\text{ s.t. }\Phi_{T}u=z\right\}.

(3.49)

With this choice,
\operatorname{Ran}\Phi_{T}
becomes a Hilbert space, and, when
\Sigma

does not depend on

[

Fix any

and set

The key perspective change of
[

]
is that, if one studies

instead of

The key point is that the restriction of

forms a strongly continuous semigroup on

[

or
[

This viewpoint is powerful for perturbation theory (see
[
34

).
In particular, it provides a natural route to treat nonlinear perturbations.
Indeed, if the nonlinearity is sufficiently mild, one can hope that the nonlinear system
\mathcal{S}_{T}
actually defines a

Then the small-time local exact controllability of the nonlinear system follows from the usual linear test argument of

The main difficulty in implementing this program is that one needs a sufficiently explicit characterization of

This can already be delicate for toy linear parabolic models (see
Section

Nevertheless,

4.2
) is small-time locally null controllable?

The following theory gives a formal general framework to answer this question.
The main difficulty is that, since the infinitesimal movements are truly nonlinear, one cannot simply use a linear combination of the associated controls to obtain an exact movement in an arbitrary direction.

4.2
Concatenation estimates

In the (nonlinear) context of tangent vectors, linear combinations of controls will be replaced by time concatenation of controls.
To analyze the effects of such operations, we will need the following classical Grönwall-type estimates (similar to
[
72
, Appendix]
).

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists
C>0
such that, for any
T>0
,
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle|y(T;u,y_{0})-y(T;u,0)|

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(C\left(T+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}\right)\right)|y_{0}|,

(4.3)

\displaystyle|y(T;u,y_{0})-y(T;u,0)-y_{0}|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(T+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}\right)\exp\left(C\left(T+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}\right)\right)|y_{0}|.

(4.4)

Proof.

Let
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
be the Lipschitz constants of
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
and define
C:=\max\{L_{0},L_{1}\}
.
Estimate (
4.3
) follows from the Lipschitz dependence of the solution with respect to the initial data (see
Lemma

B.2
).
Moreover,

\begin{split}\lvert y(T;u,y_{0})-y(T;u,0)-y_{0}\rvert&=\Big|\int_{0}^{T}(f_{0}+u(t)f_{1})(y(t;u,y_{0}))-(f_{0}+u(t)f_{1})(y(t;u,0))\mathrm{d}t\Big|\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}(L_{0}+L_{1}|u(t)|)\lvert y(t;u,y_{0})-y(t;u,0)\rvert\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}(L_{0}+L_{1}|u(t)|)\exp\left(L_{0}T+L_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}\right)\lvert y_{0}\rvert\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(4.5)

using the bound of
Lemma

B.2
for
\lvert y(t;u,y_{0})-y(t;u,0)\rvert
.
We obtain (
4.4
).
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
N\geq 1
, any times
T_{1},\dotsc,T_{N}>0
, any controls
u_{i}\in L^{1}((0,T_{i});\mathbb{R})
for
i=1,\dotsc,N
, and any initial condition
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
T=\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_{i}
and let
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
be the concatenated control defined by
u:=u_{1}\diamond\dotsb\diamond u_{N}
.
Then, the following estimate holds:

\Big|y(T;u,y_{0})-\Big(y_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}y(T_{i};u_{i},0)\Big)\Big|\leq CRe^{CR}\Big(|y_{0}|+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|y(T_{i};u_{i},0)|\Big),

(4.6)

where
R:=T+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}(0,T)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(T_{i}+\lVert u_{i}\rVert_{L^{1}(0,T_{i})})
and
C
is the constant of
Lemma

4.2
.

Proof.

Let us define the flow for the
k
-th segment as
\Theta_{k}(z):=y(T_{k};u_{k},z)
for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let us also define the sequence of states starting from
y_{0}
by
y_{k}:=\Theta_{k}(y_{k-1})
for
k=1,\dotsc,N
.
The final state is thus
y_{N}=y(T;u,y_{0})
.
The quantity we want to estimate is the norm of the error vector

E_{N}:=y_{N}-y_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Theta_{i}(0)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(y_{k}-y_{k-1}-\Theta_{k}(0)\right).

(4.7)

By (
4.4
), each term of the sum is bounded as

\lvert E_{N}\rvert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}CR_{k}e^{CR_{k}}|y_{k-1}|

(4.8)

where
R_{k}:=T_{k}+\lVert u_{k}\rVert_{L^{1}}
.
The next step is to control the growth of
|y_{k-1}|
.
Using (
4.3
),

|y_{k}|=|\Theta_{k}(y_{k-1})|\leq|\Theta_{k}(y_{k-1})-\Theta_{k}(0)|+|\Theta_{k}(0)|\leq|y_{k-1}|\exp(CR_{k})+|\Theta_{k}(0)|.

(4.9)

By unrolling this recurrence relation from
k-1
down to
0
:

|y_{k-1}|\leq\Big(|y_{0}|+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\Theta_{i}(0)|\Big)\exp(CR_{1}+\dotsb+CR_{k-1}).

(4.10)

Using that
R_{i}\geq 0
and
R=\sum R_{i}
proves (
4.6
) with the same constant
C
as in
Lemma

4.2
.
∎

4.3
Tangent vectors

We now give a formal definition for the approximate movements highlighted in
Section

4.1
, in the context of general affine systems as in
Section

1.4
.
Many slightly different definitions exist in the literature (see
[
42
, Definition 1]
,
[
16
, Section 2]
,
[
72
, Section 2]
or
[
58
]
).

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
We say that
\xi
is a
tangent vector of order
k
when there exists a family of
control variations

\{u_{T}\}
such that, for
0<T\ll 1
,
u_{T}\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
with
\lVert u_{T}\rVert_{L^{1}(0,T)}\leq T
and the solution starting from the equilibrium satisfies

y(T;u_{T},0)=T^{k}\xi+\mathcal{O}(T^{k+1}).

(4.11)

Let
K_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the set of all tangent vectors of order
k
and
K_{\infty}:=\cup_{k\geq 1}K_{k}
.

Example 4.5
.

For system (
4.2
), we proved in
Section

4.1
that:

•

\pm e_{1}
are tangent vectors of order
1
with associated control variations
u_{T}(t)=\pm 1
,

•

\pm\frac{1}{3}e_{2}
are tangent vectors of order
2
with
u_{T}(t)=\pm(-4\frac{t}{T}+2)
,

•

\pm\frac{29}{120120}e_{3}
are tangent vectors of order
4
with
u_{T}(t)=\pm(-10(\frac{t}{T})^{3}+6(\frac{t}{T})^{2}+3\frac{t}{T}-1)
.

Since tangent vectors are motions which can be achieved in a truly nonlinear way, there is no reason for
K_{k}
(or
K_{\infty}
) to be a vector space.
However, it enjoys the following elementary properties of monotonicity with respect to
k
and nonlinear convexity, inspired by
[
72
, Section 2]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

The following facts hold:

•

If
k<l
, then
K_{k}\subset K_{l}
.

•

If
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}\in K_{k}
and
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{N}\geq 0
with
\lambda_{1}+\dotsb+\lambda_{N}\leq 1
, then
\lambda_{1}^{k}\xi_{1}+\dotsb+\lambda_{N}^{k}\xi_{N}\in K_{k}
.

Proof.

We prove both statements independently.

•

Let
k<l
and
\xi\in K_{k}
.
Let
\{u_{T}\}
be a family of control variations for
\xi
.
For
0<T\ll 1
, let
v_{T}:=0_{T-T^{\alpha}}\diamond u_{T^{\alpha}}
where
\alpha:=\frac{l}{k}>1
.
Hence
\lVert v_{T}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq\lVert u_{T^{\alpha}}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq T^{\alpha}\leq T
.
Since
f_{0}(0)=0
,

y(T;v_{T},0)=y(T^{\alpha};u_{T^{\alpha}},0)=T^{l}\xi+\mathcal{O}(T^{l+1}).

(4.12)

•

Let
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}\in K_{k}
and
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{N}\geq 0
with
\lambda_{1}+\dotsb+\lambda_{N}\leq 1
.
Let
\{u_{i,T}\}
be families of control variations for the
\xi_{i}
.
For
0<T\ll 1
, let
T_{i}:=\lambda_{i}T
and
T^{\star}:=T_{1}+\dotsb+T_{N}\leq T
.
Define
v_{T}:=0_{T-T^{\star}}\diamond u_{1,T_{1}}\diamond\dotsb\diamond u_{N,T_{N}}
.
Then
\lVert v_{T}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq T^{\star}\leq T
.
By
Lemma

4.3
with
y_{0}=0
,

\begin{split}y(T;v_{T},0)&=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}y(T_{i};u_{i,T_{i}},0)\right)(1+\mathcal{O}(T))\\
&=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{k}T^{k}\xi_{i}+\mathcal{O}(T^{k+1})\right)(1+\mathcal{O}(T))\\
&=T^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{k}\xi_{i}+\mathcal{O}(T^{k+1}),\end{split}

(4.13)

which proves that
\lambda_{1}^{k}\xi_{1}+\dotsb+\lambda_{N}^{k}\xi_{N}\in K_{k}
. ∎

These properties give an intuition of why time rescaling and time concatenation can replace linearity.
In particular, as in
[
43
, Proposition 1.6]
, these properties entail the equivalence of the following formulations of the sufficient condition for controllability of
Theorem

4.9
.

Corollary 4.7
.

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

The origin
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
belongs to the interior of
K_{\infty}
, i.e.
0\in\operatorname{int}K_{\infty}
.

2.

There exist
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}\in K_{\infty}
such that
0\in\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\{\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}\}
.

3.

There exists
k\geq 1
and
r>0
such that
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\in K_{k}
.

Proof.

We will prove the equivalence by showing that
Item

1

\Rightarrow

Item

3

\Rightarrow

Item

2

\Rightarrow

Item

1
.

Proof of
Item

1

\Rightarrow

Item

3
.

Assume
0\in\operatorname{int}K_{\infty}
.
By definition, there exists
r>0
such that
B(0,2r)\subset K_{\infty}
.
In particular
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\subset K_{\infty}
.
Since
K_{\infty}=\cup_{k\geq 1}K_{k}
, by the first item in
Lemma

4.6
, there exists
k\geq 1
such that
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\in K_{k}
.

Proof of
Item

3

\Rightarrow

Item

2
.

Let
k\geq 1
and
r>0
such that
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\in K_{k}
.
For
1\leq i\leq n
, let
\xi_{i}:=+re_{i}
and
\xi_{n+i}:=-re_{i}
.
Then
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{2n}\in K_{\infty}
and
\operatorname{conv}\{\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{2n}\}
is the cross-polytope
\{|y_{1}|+\dotsb+|y_{n}|\leq r\}
.
By Cauchy–Schwarz, it contains
B(0,r/\sqrt{n})
, so
0\in\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\{\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{2n}\}
.

Proof of
Item

2

\Rightarrow

Item

1
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}\in K_{\infty}
such that
0\in\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\{\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}\}
.
Since
K_{\infty}=\cup_{k\geq 1}K_{k}
, by the first item in
Lemma

4.6
, there exists
k\geq 1
such that
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}\in K_{k}
.
By definition, there exists
r>0
such that
B(0,rN^{k})\subset\operatorname{conv}\{\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}\}
.
Hence, for any
\xi\in B(0,r)
, there exist
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{N}
with
0\leq c_{i}\leq 1
such that
\xi=c_{1}\xi_{1}+\dotsb+c_{N}\xi_{N}
and
c_{1}+\dotsb+c_{N}\leq N^{-k}
.
Let
\lambda_{i}:=c_{i}^{\frac{1}{k}}
.
By Hölder’s inequality,
\sum\lambda_{i}\leq N^{\frac{k-1}{k}}(\sum c_{i})^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq 1
.
By the second item of
Lemma

4.6
,
\sum\lambda_{i}^{k}\xi_{i}=\xi\in K_{k}
.
Hence
B(0,r)\subset K_{k}\subset K_{\infty}
.
∎

4.4
Main theorem and time-iteration argument

Before proving the main result
Theorem

4.9
, we give a result corresponding to a single step of the time iteration, where we construct a control bringing an initial state closer to the equilibrium.
Our proofs are inspired by
[
59
, Section 2]
,
[
72
, Appendix]
and
[
26
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Assume that there exists
k\geq 1
and
r>0
such that
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\in K_{k}
.
Then there exists
C>0
such that, for any
z\in B(0,r)
, there exists a time
0\leq T_{z}\leq C|z|^{\frac{1}{k}}
and a control
u_{z}\in L^{1}((0,T_{z});\mathbb{R})
with
\lVert u_{z}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq T_{z}
such that
|y(T_{z};u_{z},z)|\leq C|z|^{1+\frac{1}{k}}
.

Proof.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, we use the notation
e_{n+i}:=-e_{i}
.
For
1\leq i\leq 2n
, let
\{u_{i,T}\}
be a family of control variations associated with
re_{i}
for
0<T\ll 1
.

For
z\in B(0,r)
, we construct a control
u_{z}
that generates a motion approximately equal to
-z
.
We decompose
-z=z_{1}re_{1}+\dotsb+z_{2n}re_{2n}
with
0\leq z_{i}\leq|z|/r
and, for
1\leq i\leq n
,
z_{i}z_{n+i}=0
.

Let
T_{i}:=z_{i}^{\frac{1}{k}}
and
T_{z}:=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}T_{i}\leq n(\frac{|z|}{r})^{\frac{1}{k}}
.
Define
u_{z}\in L^{1}((0,T_{z});\mathbb{R})
with
\lVert u_{z}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq T_{z}
by

u_{z}:=u_{1,T_{1}}\diamond\dotsb\diamond u_{2n,T_{2n}}.

(4.14)

Using
Lemma

4.3
, the final state
y(T_{z};u_{z},z)
satisfies

\Big|y(T_{z};u_{z},z)-\Big(z+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}y(T_{i};u_{i,T_{i}},0)\Big)\Big|\leq CR_{z}e^{CR_{z}}\Big(|z|+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}|y(T_{i};u_{i,T_{i}},0)|\Big),

(4.15)

where
R_{z}:=T_{z}+\lVert u_{z}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq 2T_{z}=\mathcal{O}(|z|^{\frac{1}{k}})
.
The displacement from the origin is

\sum_{i=1}^{2n}y(T_{i};u_{i,T_{i}},0)=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}T_{i}^{k}(re_{i})+\mathcal{O}(T_{i}^{k+1})=-z+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{1+\frac{1}{k}}).

(4.16)

Combining these estimates, we obtain
y(T_{z};u_{z},z)=\mathcal{O}(|z|^{1+\frac{1}{k}})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Assume that
0\in\operatorname{int}K_{\infty}
.
Then system (
4.1
) is
L^{1}
-STLNC.

Proof.

By
Corollary

4.7
, there exists
k\geq 1
and
r>0
such that
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\in K_{k}
.
Thus
Proposition

4.8
provides a map
\mathcal{S}(z):=y(T_{z};u_{z},z)
which acts as a one-step contraction towards the origin.
By iterating this map, we can steer any sufficiently small initial state to zero in finite time.
The result
|\mathcal{S}(z)|=\mathcal{O}(|z|^{1+\frac{1}{k}})
implies that for any contraction factor
q\in(0,1)
, there exists a radius
r_{q}>0
such that for all
z\in B(0,r_{q})
, we have
|\mathcal{S}(z)|\leq q|z|
.
Fix
q=2^{-k}
.

Let
y_{0}
be an initial state in
B(0,r_{q})
.
We define a sequence of states
(y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
by the recurrence relation
y_{j+1}:=\mathcal{S}(y_{j})
for
j\geq 0
.
Then
|y_{j}|\leq 2^{-kj}|y_{0}|
and
y_{j}\to 0
.
The control driving
y_{j}
to
y_{j+1}
is
u_{j}:=u_{y_{j}}
, applied over a time interval of duration
T_{j}:=T_{y_{j}}
.

Proposition

4.8
guarantees that
T_{j}\leq C|y_{j}|^{\frac{1}{k}}
for some constant
C>0
.
The complete concatenated control is given formally by
u(y_{0}):=u_{0}\diamond u_{1}\diamond u_{2}\diamond\dotsb
, and its total duration
T(y_{0})
is

T(y_{0}):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}T_{j}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}C|y_{j}|^{\frac{1}{k}}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}C\left(2^{-kj}|y_{0}|\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}=2C|y_{0}|^{\frac{1}{k}}.

(4.17)

Moreover, one has
\lVert u(y_{0})\rVert_{L^{1}(0,T(y_{0}))}\leq T(y_{0})
.
In particular, by
Lemma

1.12
, the associated solution
y\in C^{0}([0,T(y_{0})];\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuous.
Since we had
y_{j}\to 0
, we get that
y(T(y_{0});u(y_{0}),y_{0})=0
.

As in
Definition

4.1
, let
T>0
.
Let
\delta:=\min\{r_{q},(T/2C)^{k}\}
.
For
y_{0}\in B(0,\delta)
, we can perform the previous construction, and obtain that
\lVert u(y_{0})\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq T(y_{0})\leq T
.
We can therefore use the control
u:=u(y_{0})\diamond 0_{T-T(y_{0})}
to obtain the small-time local null controllability.
∎

Example 4.10
.

Recall system (
4.2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
In
Section

4.1
, we proved that
\pm e_{1}
,
\pm\frac{1}{3}e_{2}
and
\pm ce_{3}
with

are tangent vectors (of respective orders 1, 2 and 4).
Since

imply that this system is

-STLNC.

4.5
How to construct tangent vectors?

Theorem

4.9
deduces controllability from the existence of tangent vectors.
To apply it, one can either perform
ad hoc
system-specific constructions (as in
Section

4.1
), or use general recipes to construct tangent vectors systematically.
We give here a few examples of such arguments.
We start with the easiest systematic motion.

Lemma 4.11
.

One has:
\pm f_{1}(0)
are tangent vectors of order
1
with control variations
u_{T}^{\pm}(t)=\pm 1
.

Proof.

Using
Lemma

B.3
,
y(T;u,0)=(\int_{0}^{T}u)f_{1}(0)+\mathcal{O}((T+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}})\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}})
.
For
u_{T}^{\pm}=\pm 1
, we obtain
y(T;u_{T}^{\pm},0)=\pm Tf_{1}(0)+\mathcal{O}(T^{2})
.
Thus
\pm f_{1}(0)
are tangent vectors of order
1
.
∎

The following well-known result (see
[
42
, Theorem 4]
,
[
58
, Theorem 6]
or
[
16
, Property (P2)]
) is of great importance, and can be transferred to PDEs (see
Section

4.8
).

Proposition 4.12
.

Assume that
f_{0}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Set
A:=Df_{0}(0)
.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

If
\pm\xi\in K_{k}
, then
\pm A\xi\in K_{2k+1}
.

Proof.

We prove that
+A\xi\in K_{2k+1}
; the proof for
-A\xi
is analogous.
By assumption, there exist control variations
\{u_{T}^{\pm}\}
such that
y(T;u_{T}^{\pm},0)=\pm T^{k}\xi+\mathcal{O}(T^{k+1})
.
For
0<T\ll 1
, define

v_{T}:=u_{T^{2}}^{+}\diamond 0_{T-2T^{2}}\diamond u_{T^{2}}^{-}.

(4.18)

Denote by
y_{1}
the state at time
T^{2}
after the first pulse, by
y_{2}
the state at time
T-T^{2}
after the drift segment, and by
y_{3}
the final state.
Using the definition of
K_{k}
,

y_{1}:=y(T^{2};u_{T^{2}}^{+},0)=T^{2k}\xi+\mathcal{O}(T^{2k+2}).

(4.19)

On
[T^{2},T-T^{2}]
, the control
v_{T}
is null and the system evolves according to
\dot{y}=f_{0}(y)
from the initial data
y_{1}
for a duration
\tau=T-2T^{2}
.
By a Grönwall-type estimate (see
Lemma

B.4
),

y_{2}=y(\tau;0,y_{1})=y_{1}+\tau Ay_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}|y_{1}|+\tau|y_{1}|^{2})=T^{2k}\xi+T^{2k+1}A\xi+\mathcal{O}(T^{2k+2}).

(4.20)

Eventually, on the final time interval
[T-T^{2},T]
, by (
4.4
),

y_{3}=y(T^{2};u_{T^{2}}^{-},y_{2})=y_{2}+y(T^{2};u^{-}_{T^{2}},0)+\mathcal{O}(T^{2}|y_{2}|).

(4.21)

Hence, since
y(T^{2};u^{-}_{T^{2}},0)=-T^{2k}\xi+\mathcal{O}(T^{2k+1})
,

y(T;v_{T},0)=y_{3}=T^{2k+1}A\xi+\mathcal{O}(T^{2k+2}),

(4.22)

which proves that
+A\xi\in K_{2k+1}
.
∎

A comforting application of
Proposition

4.12
is to recover the linear test of
Theorem

1.16
.

Corollary 4.13
.

Assume that
A:=Df_{0}(0)
and
b:=f_{1}(0)
satisfy
\operatorname{rank}\{b,Ab,\dotsc,A^{n-1}b\}=n
.
Then the system (
4.1
) is
L^{1}
-STLNC.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.11
,
\pm f_{1}(0)=\pm b
are tangent vectors.
Applying
Proposition

4.12
repeatedly proves that
\pm A^{k}b
are tangent vectors for all
k\geq 0
.
The rank assumption thus implies that
0\in\operatorname{int}K_{\infty}
, so (
4.1
) is
L^{1}
-STLNC by
Theorem

4.9
.
∎

Let us give an example of the interest of

4.12
for a concrete system.

Example 4.14
.

Consider the following variant of system (

):

\begin{cases}\dot{y}_{1}=u\\
\dot{y}_{2}=y_{1}\\
\dot{y}_{3}=y_{2}^{2}+y_{1}^{3}\\
\dot{y}_{4}=y_{3}.\end{cases}

(4.23)

The computations of

remain valid and
\pm e_{1}
,
\pm e_{2}
and
\pm e_{3}
are tangent vectors of respective orders 1, 2 and 4 (the motion along

being truly nonlinear).
Since
e_{4}=Df_{0}(0)e_{3}
,
Proposition

4.12
implies that
\pm e_{4}
are tangent vectors (of order 9).
Hence (
4.23
) is
L^{1}
-STLNC by
Theorem

4.9
.
And we did not need to perform any additional explicit computation as in
Section

4.1
.

Example 4.15

(On the optimality of the order)
.

Proposition

4.12
proves that, given
\pm\xi\in K_{k}
,
\pm A\xi\in K_{2k+1}
.
One can ask if
2k+1
is the least possible order.
Heuristically, one could think that
k+1
could be enough.
We give here a construction showing that the result fails with
2k-1
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Consider the system in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
:

\begin{cases}\dot{y}_{1}=u\\
\dot{y}_{2}=y_{1}^{2p+1}\\
\dot{y}_{3}=y_{2}\\
\dot{y}_{4}=y_{1}^{2p+2}.\end{cases}

(4.24)

Using the control variations
u_{T}(t)=\pm(2-4\frac{t}{T})
, one can prove that
\pm re_{2}
are tangent vectors (for a given
r=r(p)>0
) of order
k:=2p+2
(with remainder
\mathcal{O}(T^{2p+3})
).

Assume that
\pm r^{\prime}e_{3}\in K_{q}
for some
q\geq 0
.
By Hölder’s inequality,

\lvert y_{3}(T)\rvert^{2p+2}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}(T-t)y_{1}^{2p+1}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2p+2}\leq\left(\int_{0}^{T}(T-t)^{2p+2}\mathrm{d}t\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T}y_{1}^{2p+2}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2p+1}.

(4.25)

Thus
\lvert y_{3}(T)\rvert^{2p+2}\leq T^{2p+3}\lvert y_{4}(T)\rvert^{2p+1}
.
If
y(T)=r^{\prime}T^{q}e_{3}+\mathcal{O}(T^{q+1})
, we obtain
T^{q(2p+2)}=\mathcal{O}(T^{2p+3}T^{(q+1)(2p+1)})
so
q\geq 4p+4=2k
.
So one cannot have
\pm r^{\prime}e_{3}\in K_{2k-1}
.

There is still a small gap between the positive result in
K_{2k+1}
and the negative one in
K_{2k}
, which might be settled by more precise investigations.

The constructions presented above are very basic.
One knows many more involved and powerful ways to construct tangent vectors.
Their formulation often involves the language of Lie brackets and properties of the free Lie algebra.
We refer to Hermes
[
60
, Theorem 3.2]
, Sussmann
[
113
, Theorem 7.3]
, Agrachev and Gamkrelidze
[
1
, Theorem 4]
or Krastanov
[
75
, Theorem 2.7]
.

4.6
About continuous controllability

For applications, one may need the controls to depend continuously on the initial data.
This is key in order to go from controllability to stabilization (e.g. with continuous time-varying feedback laws), as identified by Coron in
[
26
]
.
More precisely, let us introduce the following definition.

Definition 4.16

(Continuous
L^{1}
-STLNC)
.

We say that (
4.1
) is
continuously small-time locally null controllable
when, for every
T>0
, there exists
\delta>0
and a continuous map
\mathcal{U}:B(0,\delta)\to L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
with
\mathcal{U}(0)=0
, such that, for every initial data
y_{0}\in B(0,\delta)
,
y(T;\mathcal{U}(y_{0}),y_{0})=0
.

All known examples of
L^{1}
-STLNC systems are in fact continuously
L^{1}
-STLNC.
In fact, all known sufficient conditions for
L^{1}
-STLNC imply the continuous
L^{1}
-STLNC.
However, whether this holds for all systems is still a deep open question in control theory for ODEs.

Open Problem 4.17
.

Let
f_{0},f_{1}\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
f_{0}(0)=0
.
Assume that (
4.1
) is
L^{1}
-STLNC.
Is it also continuously
L^{1}
-STLNC in the sense of
Definition

4.16
.

Going back to our example (
4.2
), we remark that our explicit control variations depended continuously on
T
.
This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.18
.

We say that
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
continuous tangent vector of order
k\geq 1
when it admits a family of control variations
\{u_{T}\}
as in
Definition

4.4
such that the map
T\mapsto u_{T}
is continuous from
(0,\infty)
to
L^{1}((0,\infty);\mathbb{R})
, where we extend each
u_{T}
by
0
.

Defining
K_{k}^{\mathrm{cont}}
as the set of all continuous tangent vectors of order
k
and
K_{\infty}^{\mathrm{cont}}:=\cup_{k\geq 1}K_{k}^{\mathrm{cont}}
, we obtain the same properties as in
Section

4.3
(monotonicity with respect to
k
, nonlinear convexity, …).
All our constructions are continuous.
In particular, one has the following result.

Lemma 4.19
.

Assume that there exists
k\geq 1
and
r>0
such that
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\in K_{k}^{\mathrm{cont}}
.
The maps
z\to T_{z}
and
z\to u_{z}
of
Proposition

4.8
are continuous.

Proof.

All the steps in our construction are continuous.
∎

In the case of continuous tangent vectors, the continuity in
Lemma

4.19
gives rise to a new proof of
Theorem

4.9
, which does not require a time iteration.

Proposition 4.20
.

Assume that there exists
k\geq 1
and
r>0
such that
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\in K_{k}^{\mathrm{cont}}
.
The system (
4.1
) is
L^{1}
-STLNC.

Proof.

Let
r,r_{0}\in(0,1]
to be chosen later.
For a fixed
y_{0}\in B(0,r_{0})
, define a map
\psi_{y_{0}}:B(0,r)\to B(0,r)
by
\psi_{y_{0}}(z):=y(T_{z};u_{z},y_{0})+z
where
T_{z}
and
u_{z}
are constructed in
Lemma

4.19
.
Since
z\mapsto T_{z}
and
z\mapsto u_{z}
are continuous, by
Lemmas

B.1
and
B.2
,
z\mapsto y(T_{z};u_{z},y_{0})
is continuous, and so is
\psi_{y_{0}}
.
We claim that
\psi_{y_{0}}
maps
B(0,r)
to itself.
By Brouwer’s theorem, it thus admits a fixed-point
z^{\star}
.
For this
z^{\star}
, one has
y(T_{z^{\star}};u_{z^{\star}},y_{0})=0
, so we have proved the
L^{1}
-STLNC.

We now prove our claim.
By (
4.3
) and (
4.4
),

\displaystyle\lvert y(T_{z};u_{z},y_{0})-y(T_{z};u_{z},0)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\lvert y_{0}\rvert\leq Cr_{0},

(4.26)

\displaystyle\lvert y(T_{z};u_{z},z)-y(T_{z};u_{z},0)-z\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C(T_{z}+\lVert u_{z}\rVert_{L^{1}})\lvert z\rvert\leq C\lvert z\rvert^{1+\frac{1}{k}}\leq Cr^{1+\frac{1}{k}}.

(4.27)

Since, by
Proposition

4.8
,
\lvert y(T_{z};u_{z},z)\rvert\leq C\lvert z\rvert^{1+\frac{1}{k}}
, we obtain that

\lvert\psi_{y_{0}}(z)\rvert\leq Cr_{0}+Cr^{\frac{1}{k}}r.

(4.28)

Choosing
r<1/(2C)^{k}
then
r_{0}<r/(2C)
, we obtain that for any
y_{0}\in B(0,r_{0})
,
\psi_{y_{0}}
maps
B(0,r)
to itself, which concludes the proof.
∎

The previous proof avoids the time iteration, but it consumes continuous tangent vectors and only delivers (not necessarily continuous)
L^{1}
-STLNC.
This is inherent to the proof method.

Remark 4.21
.

In the previous proof, we found
r,r_{0}>0
and constructed a parametrized map
\psi_{y_{0}}(z):=y(T_{z};u_{z},y_{0})+z
such that, for each
y_{0}\in B(0,r_{0})
,
\psi_{y_{0}}
is continuous and sends
B(0,r)
to itself.
For each fixed
y_{0}
, it thus admits a fixed-point.
The question here is whether this parameter-dependent Brouwer fixed-point can be chosen to depend continuously on the parameter
y_{0}
.

Generally, this is false.
For
(y,z)\in[-1,1]^{2}
, define
f_{y}(z):=y+(1+\lvert y\rvert)z
.
For each
y\in[-1,1]
,
f_{y}
is continuous on
[-1,1]
and sends it to itself.
But, for
y<0
, the unique fixed-point is
-1
, while for
y>0
, the unique fixed-point is
+1
.
This prevents a continuous selection of the fixed-point.

However, as noted in
[
28
, Proposition 11.22]
, repeating the main time iteration of
Section

4.4
, one can transfer the continuity of tangent vectors to the controllability.

Theorem 4.22
.

Assume that there exists
k\geq 1
and
r>0
such that
\pm re_{1},\dotsc,\pm re_{n}\in K_{k}^{\mathrm{cont}}
.
Then system (
4.1
) is continuously
L^{1}
-STLNC.

Proof.

As in the proof of
Theorem

4.9
, let
r>0
be such that the map
z\mapsto\mathcal{S}(z):=y(T_{z};u_{z},z)
, which is continuous by
Lemma

4.19
, satisfies
\lvert\mathcal{S}(z)\rvert\leq 2^{-k}\lvert z\rvert
for all
z\in B(0,r)
.
Given
y_{0}\in B(0,r)
, define the sequences
y_{j}=\mathcal{S}^{j}(y_{0})
as in the proof of
Theorem

4.9
.
For each finite
j
,
y_{0}\mapsto y_{j}
is continuous.
Since the series of associated times and controls converges uniformly for
y_{0}\in B(0,r)
(see (
4.17
)), we conclude that
y_{0}\mapsto T(y_{0})
and
y_{0}\mapsto u(y_{0})
are continuous.
∎

4.7
Quantifying controllability

The relation between the time, the size of the control and the size of the achieved movement is more difficult in this nonlinear context than in
Section

1.2
for linear systems.
However, it gives rise to interesting questions.
For
T>0
, let us define

\mathcal{N}_{T}:=\left\{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\exists u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R}),\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq T,\enskip y(T;u,y_{0})=0\right\}.

(4.29)

Hence
\mathcal{N}_{T}
represents the set of initial states which can be brought back to
0
in time
T
.

With this notation,
L^{1}
-STLNC in the sense of
Definition

4.1
can be equivalently stated as: “for all
T>0
, there exists
\delta_{T}>0
such that
B(0,\delta_{T})\subset\mathcal{N}_{T}
”.

Inspecting the end of the proof of
Theorem

4.9
(and the definition of
\delta
given there), we actually proved the following stronger statement.

Theorem 4.23
.

If
0\in\operatorname{int}K_{k}
, there exists
c>0
such that, for all
0<T\leq 1
,
B(0,cT^{k})\subset\mathcal{N}_{T}
.

Hence, controllability obtained from tangent vectors is automatically “quantified”, in the sense that we have a polynomial estimate on
\delta_{T}
as
T\to 0
.
In fact, all known
L^{1}
-STLNC systems exhibit this property.
This leads to the following deep open question.

Open Problem 4.24
.

Assume that system (
4.1
) is
L^{1}
-STLNC.
Does there exist
c>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that, for all
0<T\leq 1
,
B(0,cT^{k})\subset\mathcal{N}_{T}
?

There are many equivalent ways to present this issue.
From an optimal control perspective, a reformulation is to ask whether small-time local controllability implies that the value function of the minimum-time problem is Hölder continuous at the origin (see
[
73
, Open Problem 1]
).

4.8
Generalization to PDEs

For PDEs, there is a wide range of examples of situations in which the linearized system at an equilibrium “misses” a finite number of directions (and is therefore not controllable).

Obstructions.

In some cases, one can prove that the nonlinear system is not controllable either, due to the presence of some (typically quadratic) “drift”, reminiscent of
Example

1.19
for ODEs, where some given projection of the state cannot be made negative.
This has for example been observed for Schrödinger with Dirichlet
[
18
,
27
]
or Neumann
[
9
]
boundary conditions, for a water-tank system
[
24
]
, for Korteweg–de Vries
[
25
,
93
]
, for Burgers
[
85
]
.

Tangent vectors.

In other cases, one can use the nonlinear terms to recover the finite number of directions missing at the linear order, as in
Example

1.18
with a purely cubic term, or as in
Section

4.1
despite a competition with a quadratic one.
One can use manipulations reminiscent of tangent vectors for ODEs presented in this section.

For PDEs, the current works use slightly different notions of tangent vectors, in order to avoid the exploding cost
\exp(1/T)
of the control of the linear part (see for example
[
19
, Section 2]
).

Nevertheless, some ideas are very similar.
For example, the idea of
Proposition

4.12
to generate new accessible directions has been adapted to several PDEs (see
[
19
, Section 6.4]
or
[
50
, Proposition 4.8]
for Schrödinger with Dirichlet boundary conditions,
[
9
, Section 4.3]
for Schrödinger with Neumann boundary conditions, or
[
23
, Section 4.3]
for Navier–Stokes).

Appendix A
Some inequalities

A.1
Remez inequality in a segment

Our proofs of both spectral inequalities of
Propositions

2.17
and
2.23
rely fundamentally on
Remez inequalities
(see the historical proof
[
97
]
with optimal constant attained by Chebyshev polynomials).
The interest of this inequality for control theory is well-identified (see e.g.
[
7
,
53
,
61
]
).

We give here a direct proof similar to
[
53
, Proposition 3.4]
with a suboptimal constant, but valid for all regular functions.
The usual polynomial case is of course the particular case with vanishing derivative of order
n+1
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\omega\subset[0,1]
be measurable with
|\omega|>0
.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in C^{n+1}([0,1];\mathbb{C})
,

\sup_{[0,1]}|f|\leq\left(\frac{8e}{|\omega|}\right)^{n}\sup_{\omega}|f|+\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\sup_{[0,1]}|f^{(n+1)}|.

(A.1)

Proof.

Let
\delta:=\frac{|\omega|}{2}\frac{1}{n+1}
.
We call a finite set
X\subset\omega
“
\delta
-separated” if
|x-x^{\prime}|\geq\delta
for all
x\neq x^{\prime}\in X
.
Take a maximal (for inclusion)
\delta
-separated set
X
(for example constructed greedily starting from any
\bar{x}\in\omega
).
By maximality,
\omega\subset\cup_{x\in X}[x-\delta,x+\delta]
.
Comparing measures, we obtain that
X
contains at least
n+1
elements.
Thus we found
x_{0}<x_{1}<\dotsb<x_{n}\in\omega
such that
|x_{i}-x_{j}|\geq\delta|i-j|
.

Write the Lagrange interpolation polynomial for
f
at these nodes:

P_{f}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f(x_{j})\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{x-x_{k}}{x_{j}-x_{k}}.

(A.2)

For all
x\in[0,1]
,
|x-x_{k}|\leq 1
and thus, using
n!\geq(n/e)^{n}
,

\frac{|P_{f}(x)|}{\sup_{\omega}|f|}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n}\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{\delta|j-k|}=\frac{1}{\delta^{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{j!(n-j)!}=\frac{1}{\delta^{n}}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\leq\left(\frac{8e}{|\omega|}\right)^{n}.

(A.3)

Finally, by the Lagrange remainder formula, for each
x\in[0,1]
, there exists
\xi_{x}\in[0,1]
such that

f(x)-P_{f}(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi_{x})}{(n+1)!}\prod_{j=0}^{n}(x-x_{j}).

(A.4)

Using again that
|x-x_{j}|\leq 1
, this concludes the proof of (
A.1
).
∎

A.2
Turán inequality

Proof of
Proposition

2.17
.

Using the substitution
y=\cos(\pi x)
, write
f(x)=\sin(\pi x)P(\cos(\pi x))
for a polynomial
P\in\mathbb{R}_{n-1}[y]
.
Note that
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}\leq\sup_{[-1,1]}|P|
.

Given
\omega\subset[0,1]
with
|\omega|=3\delta>0
, set
\omega^{\prime}:=\omega\cap[\delta,1-\delta]
.
Hence
|\omega^{\prime}|\geq\delta
.
The map
\phi(x)=\cos(\pi x)
is a diffeomorphism from
[\delta,1-\delta]
onto its image.
On
[\delta,1-\delta]
, we have
|\phi^{\prime}|\geq 2\pi\delta
.
Let
E:=\phi(\omega^{\prime})
.
It follows that
|E|\geq 2\pi\delta^{2}
.
Let
Z:=\{y\in E;|P(y)|^{2}\leq 2\lVert P\rVert_{L^{2}(E)}^{2}/|E|\}
.
One has
|Z|\geq|E|/2\geq\pi\delta^{2}
.
By the Remez inequality of
Lemma

A.1
applied to
P
and
Z
,

\sup_{[-1,1]}|P|\leq\left(\frac{8e}{|Z|}\right)^{n-1}\sup_{Z}|P|\leq\left(\frac{8e}{\pi\delta^{2}}\right)^{n-1}\frac{1}{\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}\delta}\lVert P\rVert_{L^{2}(E)}.

(A.5)

Finally, the change of variable
y=\cos(\pi x)
on
\omega^{\prime}
yields

\int_{E}P^{2}(y)\mathrm{d}y=\pi\int_{\omega^{\prime}}\frac{f^{2}(x)}{\sin(\pi x)}\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi\delta}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2},

(A.6)

which concludes the proof.
∎

A.3
Remez inequality in multiple variables

The one-dimensional Remez inequality of
Lemma

A.1
easily generalizes to higher dimensions.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\omega\subset[0,1]^{d}
be measurable with
|\omega|>0
.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in C^{n+1}([0,1]^{d};\mathbb{C})
,

\sup_{[0,1]^{d}}|f|\leq\left(\frac{8ed}{|\omega|}\right)^{n}\sup_{\omega}|f|+d^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\sum_{|\alpha|=n+1}\sup_{[0,1]^{d}}\frac{|D^{\alpha}f|}{\alpha!}.

(A.7)

Proof.

Let
x_{0}
be a point where
|f|
attains its maximum
M
on
[0,1]^{d}
,
m:=\sup_{\omega}|f|
and
M^{\prime}
the second term in the right-hand side of (
A.7
).
Assume without loss of generality that
M>M^{\prime}
.

Using spherical coordinates centered at
x_{0}
, let
L_{\theta}
be the ray from
x_{0}
to the boundary of
[0,1]^{d}
along a direction
v_{\theta}\in S^{d-1}
and
0\leq R_{\theta}\leq\sqrt{d}
its length.
For
r\in[0,R_{\theta}]
, let
g_{\theta}(r):=f(x_{0}+r\theta)
.
By the (rescaled) 1D Remez inequality of
Lemma

A.1
applied to
g_{\theta}
,

M=|g_{\theta}(0)|\leq\left(\frac{8edR_{\theta}}{|L_{\theta}\cap\omega|_{1}}\right)^{n}\sup_{L_{\theta}\cap\omega}|f|+\frac{R_{\theta}^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\sup_{[0,R_{\theta}]}|g_{\theta}^{(n+1)}|\leq\left(\frac{8edR_{\theta}}{|L_{\theta}\cap\omega|_{1}}\right)^{n}m+M^{\prime}

(A.8)

Rearranging this inequality, we obtain
|L_{\theta}\cap\omega|_{1}\leq 8eR_{\theta}(m/(M-M^{\prime}))^{\frac{1}{n}}
.

Since
r\leq R_{\theta}
along
L_{\theta}
,

|\omega|=\int_{S^{d-1}}\int_{L_{\theta}\cap\omega}r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\sigma\leq\int_{S^{d-1}}R_{\theta}^{d-1}|L_{\theta}\cap\omega|_{1}\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(A.9)

Substituting the 1D bound and noting that
\int_{S^{d-1}}R_{\theta}^{d}\mathrm{d}\sigma=d\cdot\operatorname{Vol}([0,1]^{d})=d
, we obtain:

|\omega|\leq 8e\left(\frac{m}{M-M^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}\int_{S^{d-1}}R_{\theta}^{d}\mathrm{d}\sigma=8ed\left(\frac{m}{M-M^{\prime}}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

(A.10)

Rearranging for
M
yields the result.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\omega\subset[0,1]^{d}
be measurable with
|\omega|>0
.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
f\in C^{n}([0,1]^{d};\mathbb{C})
,

\sup_{[0,1]^{d}}|f|\leq\left(\frac{16ed}{|\omega|}\right)^{n}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}+d^{\frac{n}{2}}\sum_{|\alpha|=n}\sup_{[0,1]^{d}}\frac{|D^{\alpha}f|}{\alpha!}.

(A.11)

Proof.

Let
M:=\sqrt{2}|\omega|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}
.
We apply
Lemma

A.2
with
n\leftarrow n-1
and a modified subset
\omega^{\prime}:=\left\{x\in\omega;|f(x)|\leq M\right\}
.
First
\sup_{\omega^{\prime}}|f|\leq M
.
Second, since
\frac{1}{2}|\omega|M^{2}=\int_{\omega}f^{2}\geq\int_{\omega\setminus\omega^{\prime}}f^{2}\geq(|\omega|-|\omega^{\prime}|)M^{2}
.
Hence
|\omega^{\prime}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|\omega|
.
Combining these estimates concludes the proof.
∎

A.4
Bernstein inequality

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{C})
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\widehat{f}(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\mathrm{d}x
.
We use the standard multi–index notation: for
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
we set
|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}
and
D^{\alpha}:=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{d}}^{\alpha_{d}}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\#\left\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d};|\alpha|\leq n\right\}\leq(n+1)^{d}.

(A.12)

Lemma A.4
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{f}\subset B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,N)
.
For any multi–index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

\lVert D^{\alpha}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq N^{|\alpha|}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(A.13)

Proof.

Since
\widehat{D^{\alpha}f}(\xi)=(i\xi)^{\alpha}\widehat{f}(\xi)
, the estimate follows from Plancherel’s theorem.
∎

A.5
Logvinenko–Sereda inequality

We prove
Proposition

2.23
.
The thickness condition on the observation subset
\omega
hints at partitioning
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into cells of equal sizes.
Within each cell, we apply our Remez inequality.
We conclude using the fact that the derivatives of band-limited functions grow only geometrically.
Unlike Kovrijkine’s proof of
[
74
]
or
[
120
]
, we neither distinguish good and bad cells, nor use Harnack’s inequality.

Proof of
Proposition

2.23
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
a>0
such that (
2.62
) holds.
Up to working with
g(x):=f(ax)
, which satisfies
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{g}\subset B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,Na)
, we can assume that
a=1
.

For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, set
E_{j}:=j+[0,1]^{d}
and
\omega_{j}:=\omega\cap E_{j}
.
By (
2.62
),
|\omega_{j}|\geq\gamma
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
to be chosen later.
By the Remez inequality of
Lemma

A.3
and
|\omega_{j}|\geq\gamma
,

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(E_{j})}\leq C_{\gamma}^{n}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega_{j})}+d^{\frac{n}{2}}\sum_{|\alpha|=n}\frac{1}{\alpha!}\lVert D^{\alpha}f\rVert_{L^{\infty}(E_{j})},

(A.14)

where
C_{\gamma}:=16ed/\gamma
.
Squaring, using (
A.12
) and the Sobolev embedding of
Lemma

A.5
,

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(E_{j})}^{2}\leq 2C_{\gamma}^{2n}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega_{j})}^{2}+\frac{2d^{3n}}{n!^{2}}(n+1)^{d}2^{d}\sum_{|\alpha|=n}\sum_{|\beta|\leq d}\lVert D^{\alpha+\beta}f\rVert_{L^{2}(E_{j})}^{2}.

(A.15)

Summing over
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we obtain

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq 2C_{\gamma}^{2n}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\frac{2d^{3n}}{n!^{2}}(n+1)^{d}2^{d}\sum_{|\alpha|=n}\sum_{|\beta|\leq d}\lVert D^{\alpha+\beta}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

(A.16)

Since
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{f}\subset B(0,N)
, by Bernstein’s inequality of
Lemma

A.4
and (
A.12
), we obtain

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq 2C_{\gamma}^{2n}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+2\varepsilon_{N}^{2}(n)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

(A.17)

where

\varepsilon_{N}(n):=2^{d}\frac{(ed^{2})^{n}}{n^{n}}(n+1)^{d}(d+1)^{d}N^{d+n}.

(A.18)

Choosing
n_{N}:=\lceil 2ed^{2}N\rceil
, we obtain

\varepsilon_{N}(n_{N})\leq 2^{d}(d+1)^{d}\left(\frac{ed^{2}N}{n_{N}}\right)^{n_{N}}(n_{N}+1)^{2d}\leq C_{d}2^{-2ed^{2}N}(2ed^{2}N+2)^{2d}.

(A.19)

Hence, for
N\geq N_{0}(d)
,
\varepsilon_{N}(n_{N})\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Returning to (
A.17
), we conclude that

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 2C_{\gamma}^{n_{N}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)}\leq 2C_{\gamma}^{2ed^{2}N+1}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\omega)},

(A.20)

which is the claimed estimate (
2.63
).
We recover the optimal dependency on
\gamma
discussed in
[
74
]
.
∎

A.6
Sobolev inequality

In the proof of
Proposition

2.23
, we used the following highly-subcritical Sobolev embedding.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
d\geq 1
.
For all
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{d};\mathbb{R})
,

\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}^{2}\leq 2^{d}\sum_{|\alpha|\leq d}\lVert D^{\alpha}f\rVert_{L^{2}([0,1]^{d})}^{2}.

(A.21)

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
d\geq 1
.
For
d=1
and
x,x^{\prime}\in[0,1]
, by Cauchy–Schwarz

|f^{2}(x)-f^{2}(x^{\prime})|=\left|\int_{x^{\prime}}^{x}2ff^{\prime}\right|\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\lVert f^{\prime}\rVert_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}.

(A.22)

Integrating over
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]
leads to (
A.21
) for
d=1
.

Now let
d\geq 2
and assume that the inequality holds for dimension
d-1
.
Let
x\in[0,1]^{d}
be written as
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})
where
x_{1}\in[0,1]
and
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{d-1}
.

Fix
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{d-1}
.
Applying the base case
d=1
to the function
g(x_{1}):=f(x_{1},x^{\prime})
yields:

\sup_{x_{1}\in[0,1]}|f(x_{1},x^{\prime})|^{2}\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}\left(|f(x_{1},x^{\prime})|^{2}+|\partial_{1}f(x_{1},x^{\prime})|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

(A.23)

To bound the
L^{\infty}
norm over the full domain
[0,1]^{d}
, we take the supremum over
x^{\prime}
:

\begin{split}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})}^{2}&\leq 2\sup_{x^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(|f(x_{1},x^{\prime})|^{2}+|\partial_{1}f(x_{1},x^{\prime})|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\\
&\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sup_{x^{\prime}}|f(x_{1},x^{\prime})|^{2}+\sup_{x^{\prime}}|\partial_{1}f(x_{1},x^{\prime})|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\\
&\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}2^{d-1}\sum_{|\beta|\leq d-1}\left(\lVert D^{\beta}_{x^{\prime}}f(x_{1},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(x^{\prime})}^{2}+\lVert D^{\beta}\partial_{1}f(x_{1},\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}(x^{\prime})}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x_{1}\\
&\leq 2^{d}\sum_{|\beta|\leq d}\lVert D^{\beta}f\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\end{split}

(A.24)

where we applied the inductive hypothesis to
x^{\prime}\mapsto f(x_{1},x^{\prime})
and
x^{\prime}\mapsto\partial_{1}f(x_{1},x^{\prime})
for fixed
x_{1}
.
∎

A.7
Gautschi bound for inverse Vandermonde matrix

Given distinct
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, define the square Vandermonde matrix
V:=(z_{j}^{k-1})_{1\leq j,k\leq n}
.
The following estimate of the norm of the inverse of
V
is proved in
[
47
, Theorem 1]
.

Lemma A.6
.

For any
n\geq 1
and distinct
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, one has

\lVert V^{-1}\rVert\leq\sqrt{n}\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{1+|z_{k}|}{|z_{j}-z_{k}|}.

(A.25)

Proof.

For a matrix
A=(a_{jk})_{1\leq j,k\leq n}
, we introduce its
1
-norm or
column-sum
norm

\lVert A\rVert_{1}:=\max_{\lvert x\rvert_{1}=1}\lvert Ax\rvert_{1}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|a_{jk}|.

(A.26)

In particular, by Cauchy–Schwarz,
\lVert A\rVert=\max_{\lvert x\rvert_{2}=1}\lvert Ax\rvert_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\lVert A\rVert_{1}
.

Let
L_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq n
be the Lagrange basis polynomials for the nodes
z_{1},\dotsc,z_{n}
:

L_{j}(z):=\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{z-z_{k}}{z_{j}-z_{k}}\quad\text{so that}\quad L_{j}(z_{k})=\mathbf{1}_{j=k}.

(A.27)

Writing
L_{j}(z)=\ell^{(j)}_{0}+\ell^{(j)}_{1}z+\dotsb+\ell^{(j)}_{n-1}z^{n-1}
, the vector
\ell^{(j)}
is the
j
-th column of
V^{-1}
since
V\ell^{(j)}=e_{j}
.
For a polynomial
Q(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}q_{m}z^{m}
, define
\lVert Q\rVert_{1}:=|q_{0}|+\dotsb+|q_{n-1}|
.
Then
\lVert z-z_{0}\rVert_{1}=1+|z_{0}|
and
\lVert PQ\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert P\rVert_{1}\lVert Q\rVert_{1}
.
Thus

\lVert L_{j}\rVert_{1}\leq\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{1+|z_{k}|}{|z_{j}-z_{k}|}.

(A.28)

By definition
\lVert V^{-1}\rVert_{1}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\lvert\ell^{(j)}\rvert_{1}
, so (
A.25
) follows by Cauchy–Schwarz.
∎

Appendix B
Well-posedness results

B.1
Control-affine ODEs

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
f_{0},f_{1}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
vector fields with
f_{0}(0)=0
.
We assume that
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are globally Lipschitz, with constants
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
, and globally bounded, with constants
M_{0}
and
M_{1}
.

For
T>0
,
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
t\mapsto y(t;u,y_{0})
be the solution to (
4.1
) with initial data
y_{0}
constructed in
Lemma

1.12
.
We collect standard estimates on this solution.

B.1.1
A priori bounds

Lemma B.1
.

For all
T>0
,
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
,
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\lvert y(t;u,y_{0})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq e^{L_{0}t}\left(\lvert y_{0}\rvert+M_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}(0,t)}\right),

(B.1)

\displaystyle\lvert y(t;u,y_{0})-y_{0}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq e^{L_{0}t}\left(L_{0}t\lvert y_{0}\rvert+M_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}(0,t)}\right).

(B.2)

Proof.

Since
f_{0}(0)=0
, by the integral formulation (
1.38
),

\lvert y(t)\rvert\leq\lvert y_{0}\rvert+\int_{0}^{t}\lvert f_{0}(y)+uf_{1}(y)\rvert\leq\lvert y_{0}\rvert+\int_{0}^{t}L_{0}\lvert y\rvert+M_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\lvert u\rvert

(B.3)

Thus (
B.1
) follows by Grönwall’s inequality.
Similarly,

\lvert y(t)-y_{0}\rvert\leq\int_{0}^{t}\lvert f_{0}(y)+uf_{1}(y)\rvert\leq\int_{0}^{t}L_{0}\lvert y-y_{0}\rvert+L_{0}\lvert y_{0}\rvert+M_{1}\lvert u\rvert,

(B.4)

which entails (
B.2
) by Grönwall’s inequality.
∎

B.1.2
Lipschitz dependence on the parameters

Lemma B.2
.

For all
T>0
,
u,v\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
,
y_{0},z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in[0,T]
,

\lvert y(t;u,y_{0})-y(t;v,z_{0})\rvert\leq e^{L_{0}T+L_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}}\left(\lvert y_{0}-z_{0}\rvert+M_{1}\lVert u-v\rVert_{L^{1}}\right).

(B.5)

Proof.

Let
y
denote the trajectory for the control
u
and initial data
y_{0}
,
z
the trajectory for the control
v
and initial data
z_{0}
.
Then
w:=y-z
satisfies the mild formulation:

w(t)=(y_{0}-z_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}(f_{0}+u(s)f_{1})(y(s))-(f_{0}+v(s)f_{1})(z(s))\mathrm{d}s.

(B.6)

By the triangle inequality,

\begin{split}\lvert w(t)\rvert&\leq\lvert y_{0}-z_{0}\rvert+\int_{0}^{t}\lvert f_{0}(y)-f_{0}(z)\rvert+\lvert u(f_{1}(y)-f_{1}(z))\rvert+\lvert(u-v)f_{1}(z)\rvert\\
&\leq\lvert y_{0}-z_{0}\rvert+\int_{0}^{t}(L_{0}+\lvert u\rvert L_{1})\lvert w\rvert+M_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\lvert u-v\rvert.\end{split}

(B.7)

Grönwall’s inequality entails (
B.5
).
∎

B.1.3
Taylor expansions

Lemma B.3
.

For
T\in[0,1]
and
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{R})
,

y(T;u,0)=\Big(\int_{0}^{T}u\Big)f_{1}(0)+\mathcal{O}((T+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}})\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}}).

(B.8)

Proof.

Let
w(T):=y(T;u,0)-(\int_{0}^{T}u)f_{1}(0)
.
By the mild formulation (
1.38
), since
f_{0}(0)=0
,

\begin{split}\lvert w(T)\rvert&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\lvert f_{0}(y(t;u,0))+u(t)f_{1}(y(t;u,0))-u(t)f_{1}(0)\rvert\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}(L_{0}+L_{1}\lvert u(t)\rvert)\lvert y(t;u,0)\rvert\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(B.9)

Using (
B.1
), we obtain

\lvert w(T)\rvert\leq(L_{0}T+L_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}(0,T)})e^{L_{0}T}M_{1}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{1}(0,T)}.

(B.10)

This entails (
B.8
) for
T\leq 1
.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

Assume that
f_{0}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Let
A:=Df_{0}(0)
.
For
t\in[0,1]
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

y(t;0,y_{0})=y_{0}+tAy_{0}+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}|y_{0}|+t|y_{0}|^{2}).

(B.11)

Proof.

Let
w(t):=y(t;0,y_{0})-y_{0}-tAy_{0}
.
By the mild formulation (
1.38
),

\begin{split}\lvert w(t)\rvert&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\lvert f_{0}(y(s;0,y_{0}))-Ay_{0}\rvert\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\lvert f_{0}(y_{0})-Ay_{0}\rvert+\lvert f_{0}(y(s;0,y_{0}))-f_{0}(y_{0})\rvert\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq t\lvert f_{0}(y_{0})-Ay_{0}\rvert+L_{0}\int_{0}^{t}\lvert y(s;0,y_{0})-y_{0}\rvert\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq t\lvert f_{0}(y_{0})-Ay_{0}\rvert+(L_{0}t)^{2}e^{L_{0}t}\lvert y_{0}\rvert\end{split}

(B.12)

since, by (
B.2
) with
u=0
,
\lvert y(s;0,y_{0})-y_{0}\rvert\leq e^{L_{0}s}L_{0}s\lvert y_{0}\rvert
.

Since
f_{0}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there exists
C_{0}>0
such that
|f_{0}(y_{0})-Ay_{0}|\leq C_{0}|y_{0}|^{2}
when
|y_{0}|\leq 1
.
For
|y_{0}|>1
, write
|f_{0}(y_{0})-Ay_{0}|\leq|f_{0}(y_{0})-f_{0}(0)|+|Ay_{0}|\leq L_{0}|y_{0}|+\lVert A\rVert|y_{0}|\leq(L_{0}+\lVert A\rVert)|y_{0}|^{2}
.
∎

B.2
Burgers equation

We prove
Proposition

3.24
using a classical compactness argument.
To prove the existence of solutions, we will rely on Schaefer’s fixed-point theorem
[
103
]
, which is an easy consequence of Schauder’s fixed-point theorem (see e.g.
[
29
, Chapter 3, Theorem 4.4]
for a short proof), sometimes easier to apply to prove the existence of solutions to PDEs.

Lemma B.5

(Schaefer’s fixed-point theorem)
.

Let
E
be a Banach space and
f
a continuous compact map from
E
to
E
.
Assume that the set

\left\{y\in E\mid\exists\lambda\in[0,1],y=\lambda f(y)\right\}

(B.13)

is bounded.
Then
f
has a fixed point.

We will therefore need the following
a priori
estimates.

Lemma B.6
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
T>0
,
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
and
u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\omega)
, if
y\in\mathcal{Y}
is a solution to

\begin{cases}y_{t}-y_{xx}=\lambda(u\mathbf{1}_{\omega}-yy_{x})&\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,1),\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0&\text{in }(0,T),\\
y(0,x)=\lambda y_{0}(x)&\text{in }(0,1),\end{cases}

(B.14)

then

\lVert y-\lambda z\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C\left(\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)^{2},

(B.15)

where
z\in\mathcal{Y}
is the solution to the linear heat equation (
2.42
).

Proof.

First, let us obtain
a priori
estimates on
y
.
Multiplying (
B.14
) by
y
, integrating over
(0,1)
and using integration by parts, the boundary conditions and Poincaré, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{1}y^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}y_{x}^{2}=\lambda\int_{0}^{1}uy\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}y_{x}^{2}.

(B.16)

Hence,

\lVert y\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert y\rVert_{L^{2}H^{1}}^{2}\leq\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(B.17)

Moreover,
\lVert(y^{2})_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}H^{-1}}\leq\lVert y\rVert_{L^{4}L^{4}}^{2}\leq\lVert y\rVert_{L^{\infty}L^{2}}\lVert y\rVert_{L^{2}H^{1}}
.
Hence, using the PDE, we can estimate
y_{t}
in
L^{2}H^{-1}
.
We conclude that
\lVert y\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C(\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})
.

Let
w:=y-\lambda z\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Then
w
is a solution to

\begin{cases}w_{t}-w_{xx}=-\lambda yy_{x}&\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,1),\\
w(t,0)=w(t,1)=0&\text{in }(0,T),\\
w(0,x)=0&\text{in }(0,1).\end{cases}

(B.18)

By
Proposition

2.16
,
\lVert w\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C\lVert\lambda yy_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}H^{-1}}\leq C\lVert y\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

We can now turn to the proof of the well-posedness.

Proof of
Proposition

3.24
.

Fix
T>0
,
y_{0}\in L^{2}(0,1)
and
u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\omega)
.

Step 1:
Existence.

Let
E:=L^{4}((0,T);L^{4}(0,1))
.
The map
w\mapsto ww_{x}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}(w^{2})
is continuous from
E
to
L^{2}((0,T);H^{-1}(0,1))
.
Let
f:E\to E
be the map which associates to
w\in E
the solution
y\in\mathcal{Y}
given by
Proposition

2.16
to

\begin{cases}y_{t}-y_{xx}=u\mathbf{1}_{\omega}-ww_{x}&\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,1),\\
y(t,0)=y(t,1)=0&\text{in }(0,T),\\
y(0,x)=y_{0}(x)&\text{in }(0,1).\end{cases}

(B.19)

By continuity of
w\mapsto ww_{x}
from
E
to
L^{2}((0,T);H^{-1}(0,1))
and
Proposition

2.16
,
f
is continuous from
E
to
\mathcal{Y}
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{Y}\hookrightarrow E
and this embedding is compact (this follows for example from the Aubin–Lions–Simon lemma, see e.g.
[
108
, Section 9, Corollary 6]
).
Hence
f
is compact as a map from
E
to
E
.
To apply
Lemma

B.5
, it remains to check that the set (
B.13
) is bounded.
Let
y\in E
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
such that
y=\lambda f(y)
.
Since
f\in C^{0}(E;\mathcal{Y})
,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and satisfies (
B.14
).
By
Lemma

B.6
and
Proposition

2.16
,
\lVert y\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq C(M+M^{2})
, where
M:=\lVert y_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}
.
Hence, by Schaefer’s fixed-point theorem, there exists
y\in E
such that
y=f(y)
.
In particular,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Step 2:
Uniqueness.

Consider two solutions
y,\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
to (
3.44
).
Let
w:=y-\tilde{y}
and
z:=y+\tilde{y}
.
Then
w\in\mathcal{Y}
is a solution to

\begin{cases}w_{t}-w_{xx}=-\frac{1}{2}(wz)_{x}&\text{in }(0,T)\times(0,1),\\
w(t,0)=w(t,1)=0&\text{in }(0,T),\\
w(0,x)=0&\text{in }(0,1).\end{cases}

(B.20)

Multiplying by
w
and integrating by parts yields:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{1}w^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}w_{x}^{2}=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{1}z_{x}w^{2}.

(B.21)

By Cauchy–Schwarz, Poincaré and Young, for each
t\in[0,T]
,

\left|\int_{0}^{1}z_{x}w^{2}\right|\leq\lVert z_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert w\rVert_{L^{4}}^{2}\leq\lVert z_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert w\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert w_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\lVert z_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lVert w\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lVert w_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(B.22)

Hence, setting
M(t):=\lVert z_{x}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert w(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq M(t)\lVert w(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(B.23)

Since
z\in\mathcal{Y}
,
M\in L^{1}(0,T)
.
Since
w(0)=0
, Grönwall’s lemma implies that
w\equiv 0
on
[0,T]
.
∎
