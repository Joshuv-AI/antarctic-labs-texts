Association of phase angle with malnutrition and sarcopenia in interstitial lung disease: a cross-sectional analysis of body composition and functional decline
Authors: Lun Zhang, Xiaoyun Xi, Rui Xu, Yang Yang, Tao Duan, Geyi Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsInterstitial Lung Disease (ILD) patients frequently develop malnutrition and sarcopenia. Phase angle (PA), a nutritional biomarker, may predict these conditions, though its clinical utility in ILD remains undetermined. This study evaluated the association of phase angle with malnutrition and sarcopenia in patients with ILD.MethodsA total of 105 hospitalized patients with ILD were prospectively and consecutively enrolled and analyzed in this observational study. Each participant underwent a comprehensive assessment including detailed anthropometric measurements and body composition analysis. Nutritional status was evaluated according to the GLIM criteria to diagnose malnutrition, and sarcopenia was identified based on the AWGS 2019 consensus. Participants were then dichotomized into a low phase angle group (< 5.0°for males; <4.6°for females) and a normal phase angle group (≥ 5.0°for males; ≥4.6°for females) using established cut-off values. Body composition and functional parameters were compared between the two groups. Finally, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with phase angle.ResultsThe prevalence of malnutrition and sarcopenia was 39.0% and 30.4%, respectively. The low PA group demonstrated significantly poorer outcomes across all measured parameters (P < 0.05), including: (1) Body composition: reduced intracellular water, protein, skeletal muscle mass, BMI, and SMI; (2) Physical function: decreased handgrip strength, mid-upper arm circumference, knee extension strength, 6-minute walk distance, 6-meter gait speed, and SPPB scores. Gender-specific analyses revealed consistent patterns. PA strongly correlated with key nutritional and functional markers. PA was significantly negatively correlated with malnutrition (unadjusted OR = 0.307, 95% CI: 0.178–0.531; adjusted OR range: 0.408–0.414, all P < 0.05) and sarcopenia (unadjusted OR = 0.388, 95% CI: 0.230–0.652; adjusted OR range: 0.414–0.543, all P < 0.05).ConclusionIn patients with ILD, Low PA is independently associated with malnutrition and sarcopenia, and shows significant correlations with adverse body composition and functional decline.Keywords: Phase angle, Malnutrition, Sarcopenia, Interstitial lung disease

Background

Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD) is a group of pulmonary disorders affecting the alveolar walls and airspaces, leading to decreased pulmonary function and significant disease burden [1–3]. ILD is characterized by complex etiology, nonspecific clinical manifestations, and diverse radiological and pathological changes, which contribute to diagnostic challenges and delays, severely impacting patients’ quality of life and long-term prognosis [4, 5]. Beyond pulmonary involvement, as the disease progresses, patients with ILD are prone to complications such as malnutrition and sarcopenia due to various factors, including chronic hypoxemia, nutritional and energy imbalances, chronic inflammation, increased respiratory muscle load, reduced phase angle, systemic inflammation, and side effects of corticosteroids and antifibrotic drugs [6–8].

Malnutrition is defined as a state of insufficient energy or nutrient intake or abnormal metabolism, resulting in altered body composition, decreased physiological function, and adverse clinical outcomes [9]. Disease-related malnutrition is highly prevalent in clinical practice [10, 11] and is closely associated with poor prognosis [12]. Previous studies have found that 48.7% of ILD patients suffer from malnutrition, with severe malnutrition being an important predictor of survival [13]. However, malnutrition in ILD patients is often overlooked [14], and there is currently no unified method for assessing malnutrition. Commonly used assessment indicators include mid-arm circumference, body mass index (BMI), fat-free mass (FFM), fat-free mass index (FFMI), and subjective global assessment (SGA) [13, 15, 16]. Variability in assessment methods may lead to difficulties in directly comparing clinical research results and also affects the standardized implementation of nutritional intervention strategies.

In addition to malnutrition, sarcopenia is also frequently observed in ILD patients. Sarcopenia is a progressive and generalized loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength resulting from multiple factors [17]. Studies have reported a prevalence of sarcopenia as high as 39.3% in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) patients, and it is closely associated with patient prognosis and physical performance [18]. Moreover, sarcopenia has been identified as an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality in IPF [19]. Therefore, early detection and intervention of sarcopenia in ILD patients are of particular importance. However, although there are various tools available for screening and diagnosing sarcopenia [20–23], studies specifically focusing on sarcopenia screening and diagnostic methods in ILD patients are currently lacking.

The phase angle (PA) is a classic indicator in the field of nutrition, directly measured by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) as the geometric ratio of reactance to resistance generated when an electrical current passes through the cell membrane. It reflects cellularity, cell membrane integrity, and cellular function [24]. PA decreases with disease, inflammation, malnutrition, and prolonged physical inactivity, and is associated with poor prognosis in various chronic conditions [25–27]. Additionally, the PA is correlated with gender, typically showing higher values in males than in females, which aligns with physiological principles [28].

Previous studies have shown that PA is a novel indicator for assessing health and nutritional status in patients with respiratory diseases, particularly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [29] and COVID-19 [30]. However, research on PA in ILD patients is extremely limited. Some studies have found that IPF patients with low PA have poorer lung function, exercise capacity, and health-related quality of life [31]. Yet, the value of PA in predicting malnutrition and sarcopenia in ILD patients remains unknown.

The present study aims to systematically investigate the clinical value of PA from two dimensions. First, it focuses on elucidating the specific role of PA in assessing the nutritional and functional status of patients with ILD. Second, it innovatively explores the value of PA in identifying ILD patients with comorbid malnutrition or sarcopenia. This work provides novel objective indicators for early detection, thereby enabling timely targeted nutritional support and individualized pulmonary rehabilitation, with significant clinical implications.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient recruitment

This was a prospective observational cross-sectional study. Consecutive patients with ILD were prospectively enrolled from January and December 2023 at the Department of Pulmonary and Critical Care Medicine of the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province. Inclusion criteria were: ① the diagnosis of ILD was confirmed by multidisciplinary consensus in accordance with prevailing diagnostic criteria [32–34]; ② conscious, no communication disorder and able to cooperate with relevant measurements and physical examination in the study; ③ aged 18 to 70 years; ④ clinically stable disease status at the time of enrollment, which was defined by the absence of an acute exacerbation for ≥ 4 weeks prior to enrollment, with no worsening of key respiratory symptoms (e.g., dyspnea, cough), no deterioration in pulmonary function (e.g., stable or no significant decline in FVC or SpO₂), and no escalation of treatment. Exclusion criteria: ① cannot cooperate with the examination; ② patients with malignant tumor, heart failure, hepatic or renal dysfunction, endocrine and metabolic diseases; ③ implanted cardiac pacemaker, cardioverter defibrillator, joint replacement. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province (Approval Letter No.: KHLL2022-YJ 060- (1)). All of the subjects signed the informed consent form.

Patients were first screened using medical records, followed by final enrollment and analysis. A total of 109 patients were enrolled. Among them, 4 patients were excluded due to physical disability, specifically their inability to complete the body composition analysis. Ultimately, 105 patients were included for analysis.

Procedures

Upon admission, comprehensive baseline data were systematically collected, encompassing demographic characteristics, clinical information, and subsequent detailed assessments. These included respiratory evaluations (arterial blood gas analysis and pulmonary function tests), as well as anthropometric measurements, such as height, weight, handgrip strength (HGS), mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), calf circumference (CC), triceps skinfold thickness (TSF), 6-meter gait speed (6MGS), short physical performance battery (SPPB), knee extension strength (KES) and 6-minute walk test (6MWT). The specific measurement methods were as follows:

Pulmonary function tests: Pulmonary function tests were conducted on all participants using the MasterScreen PFT (Jaeger). The procedures were carried out by rigorously trained and certified technicians in accordance with the criteria of the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and the European Respiratory Society (ERS) [35, 36]. The following parameters were recorded: forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV₁) and its percentage of the predicted value (FEV₁%pred), forced vital capacity (FVC) and its percentage of the predicted value (FVC%pred), and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and its percentage of the predicted value (DLCO%pred).

6MWT was performed in a straight 30 m corridor [45]. Participants rested in a seated position near the starting line for 5–10 min, during which blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation were measured; standardized instructions were then provided by the examiner before starting the test. Timing began when the participant started walking from the starting line, and participants were asked to walk back and forth along the corridor continuously for 6 min at their fastest tolerable self‑selected pace; they were allowed to slow down or briefly stop if necessary without stopping the timer, while the examiner provided standardized time announcements and encouragement every minute. At 6 min, the command “stop” was given and the point where the participant stopped was marked; the total distance walked (meters) was recorded as the test outcome, and vital signs as well as subjective ratings of fatigue and dyspnea were reassessed. A 6-minute walk distance (6MWD) of less than 254 m serves as a significant prognostic threshold for patients with ILD, as identified in the ATS/ERS systematic review [46].

All measurements were performed by trained personnel following standardized protocols to ensure measurement reliability and reproducibility.

Body composition analysis and PA

We evaluated human composition determination of body composition and PA using a human composition analyzer (Inbody S10) according to the BIA method. Subjects fast for at least 8 h and empty urine and feces on the morning of the test. Measurement method: Clean the electrode contact surface of the instrument, measure the height and weight of the subject; barefoot, wipe the soles and palms of both hands with the alcohol cotton ball, make the feet and hands contact the silver electrode contact surface of the instrument as much as possible to introduce electric current.

Body composition parameters were measured and recorded using bioelectrical impedance analysis at 50 kHz, including intracellular and extracellular water, protein, inorganic salts, body fat, skeletal muscle mass, basal metabolic rate, visceral fat area, and PA. Based on established cut-off points [47], participants were dichotomized into a low phase angle group (< 5.0°for men; <4.6°for women) and a normal phase angle group (≥ 5.0°for men; ≥4.6°for women).

Malnutrition diagnosis

Nutritional status and the presence of malnutrition were assessed using the GLIM criteria [48]. According to the GLIM framework, the diagnosis of malnutrition is established when a patient meets at least one phenotypic criterion and one etiologic criterion.

Phenotypic criteria: (1) Non-volitional weight loss: >5% within past 6 months, or > 10% beyond 6 months; (2) Low body mass index (BMI): <20 kg/m² if < 70 years (< 18.5 in Asia), or < 22 kg/m² if ≥ 70 years (< 20 in Asia); (3) Reduced muscle mass: assessed via validated methods, e.g. assessed by skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) as determined by BIA, < 7.0 kg/m2 in males and < 5.7 kg/m2 in females.

Etiologic criteria: (1) Reduced food intake or assimilation: energy intake ≤ 50% of requirements for ≥ 1 week, or any reduction for > 2 weeks, or chronic gastrointestinal conditions impairing absorption (e.g., dysphagia, nausea, malabsorption disorders); (2) Disease burden/inflammation: presence of acute disease/injury or chronic condition with inflammatory response (e.g., cancer, COPD, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, interstitial lung disease); clinical judgment suffices, supportive labs optional.

Sarcopenia diagnosis

According to AWGS 2019, sarcopenia is defined by low muscle mass together with low muscle strength and/or poor physical performance in older adults [41]. In practice, low muscle strength is usually identified by handgrip strength (men < 28 kg, women < 18 kg) and/or poor performance (e.g., 6 m gait speed < 1.0 m/s, SPPB ≤ 9, or 5‑time chair stand ≥ 12 s), combined with reduced appendicular SMI assessed by BIA (< 7.0 kg/m2 in males and < 5.7 kg/m2 in females). Participants meeting these criteria were diagnosed as having sarcopenia.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp). Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and compared using independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range [M (P25, P75)] and analyzed using nonparametric tests. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages (n, %), with between-group comparisons conducted by χ² test.

For correlation analysis, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied for normally distributed variables, while Spearman’s rank correlation was used for non-normally distributed data. Stepwise multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to identify independent risk factors for both malnutrition and sarcopenia. The analysis generated odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each significant risk factor.

The variance inflation factor (VIF) method is used to assess for collinearity. If the VIF value exceeds 10, it indicates significant collinearity among the independent variables. If the VIF value exceeds 5, collinearity should also be considered.

A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. All statistical tests were performed with appropriate verification of underlying assumptions, including normality testing using Shapiro-Wilk test and assessment of variance homogeneity where applicable.

The low PA group exhibited significantly lower intracellular water (18.44 ± 3.29 L vs. 21.24 ± 3.68 L, P < 0.001), protein content (7.98 ± 2.13 kg vs. 9.18 ± 1.58 kg, P < 0.001), and skeletal muscle mass (22.06 ± 4.29 kg vs. 25.73 ± 4.80 kg, P < 0.001) than the normal PA group. Regarding respiratory function, the low PA group demonstrated significantly poorer DLCO, while no significant differences were observed in other pulmonary function parameters or the prevalence of respiratory failure between the two groups.

These sex-specific differences highlight the importance of considering gender when evaluating the impact of PA on nutritional and physical status.

Discussion

Our cross-sectional study demonstrates a high prevalence of malnutrition and sarcopenia among patients with ILD, with lower phase angle PA strongly associated with these conditions. These findings extend prior observations in chronic respiratory diseases, where malnutrition and sarcopenia contribute to functional decline and poor prognosis. As a direct marker of cellular integrity and muscle quality derived from bioelectrical impedance analysis [24, 49, 50], PA offers a practical, non-invasive indicator that may enhance early identification of at-risk ILD patients, complementing established assessments of body composition and functional status.

Our study, using the GLIM criteria, revealed a high prevalence of malnutrition in patients with ILD, with 39% meeting criteria for malnutrition. This rate is consistent with prior reports in ILD cohorts, including a 49% prevalence in one series [51]. These findings underscore the substantial burden of malnutrition in ILD, extending beyond traditional metrics such as BMI or subjective global assessment that have dominated earlier nutrition research in this population [17, 52]. Furthermore, our findings revealed that malnourished patients exhibited significantly lower body weight, BMI, and muscle mass, with reduced muscle mass being the most frequent phenotypic criterion, which highlights the value of the GLIM criteria in detecting nutritional deficits that conventional anthropometric measures alone may fail to capture.

Malnutrition exerts profound and multifaceted clinical consequences in patients with ILD. It is associated with immune dysfunction and increased infection risk, thereby contributing to acute exacerbations and all-cause mortality [53, 54]. Moreover, malnutrition impairs exercise tolerance, leading to severe limitations in physical capacity and early-onset dyspnea and fatigue during minimal exertion [51]. Furthermore, malnutrition diminishes health-related quality of life [16, 51, 55]. These data emphasize the importance of early identification and targeted management of malnutrition in ILD. Although the GLIM criteria offer a comprehensive framework for malnutrition diagnosis, more simple and expedient indicators are needed to reflect nutritional status in clinical practice. In this context, bioelectrical impedance‑derived PA, as an integrated indicator of cellular integrity and nutritional status, may offer a practical tool for early risk stratification, which is precisely the focus of the present study.

Epidemiological data on sarcopenia in ILD patients are also limited. Our study found that 30.4% of ILD patients had moderate sarcopenia based on AWGS 2019 criteria. A Japanese cross-sectional study of 56 IPF patients reported a 39.3% sarcopenia prevalence [18], while a survey of elderly ILD patients found a 32.1% prevalence [56]. A meta-analysis indicated that the combined prevalence of sarcopenia in patients with IPF is 26% [57]. Our study’s 30.4% prevalence is comparable to prior findings. Meanwhile, this is also close to the 31.4% prevalence rate in our previous studies on ILD with sarcopenia [58].

The decline in muscle strength and mass weakens respiratory muscle function, thereby increasing the risk of respiratory failure [59]. In our previous studies on functional impairment of respiratory sarcopenia in ILD patients, we found that those with possible respiratory sarcopenia showed significant declines in physical fitness, respiratory function, daily activities, and quality of life [58]. Moreover, sarcopenia is closely associated with systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which contribute to further deterioration of pulmonary and multi-organ function. These pathophysiological alterations adversely affect both overall survival and quality of life in affected patients. A study demonstrates that sarcopenia is associated with significantly higher mortality in ILD patients compared to those without the condition [60].

In this study, patients in the low PA group consistently exhibited significantly lower values across multiple physiological and functional parameters, including body composition (intracellular water, protein, inorganic salt, SMI), nutritional status (BMI, MUAC, TSF, CC), muscle strength (HGS, KES) and physical performance (6MWD, 6MGS, SPPB). Furthermore, multivariate analysis identified phase angle as an independent predictor of both malnutrition and sarcopenia. These findings indicate that the PA holds significant clinical importance and high practicality, with the potential to serve as a non-invasive assessment parameter for patients with ILD in the future. Additionally, this pattern aligns with and extends recent observations in other chronic respiratory diseases. In COPD patients, PA values are significantly lower than those in healthy controls, and a lower PA correlates with progressively worse muscle strength, exercise capacity, and other clinical markers [61–63]. Critically, PA serves as a key predictor in prognostic models for severe physical impairment and as a diagnostic indicator for malnutrition and sarcopenia [61, 63, 64]. In lung cancer patients, PA can predict postoperative complications in surgical cases and is an independent risk factor for mortality in male lung cancer patients [65, 66]. Thus, the consistent association between low PA and impaired nutritional, musculoskeletal, and functional status across ILD and other chronic respiratory diseases underscores PA’s value as a simple, reproducible bedside tool for nutritional risk stratification and early intervention in chronic respiratory diseases.

Conversely, similar associations have been reported in non-respiratory conditions, including poorer survival in gastrointestinal cancer [67], reduced muscle mass in stroke patients [68] and lower hemoglobin levels in hemodialysis patients [69]. Additionally, PA has been shown to predict nutritional status in hemodialysis [70] patients and elderly individuals in convalescent hospitals [71], as well as sarcopenia in patients with cardiovascular disease [72]. Collectively, these findings support PA as a robust indicator of nutritional status, muscle function and functional capacity in chronic disease states.

Meanwhile, gender differences warrant careful consideration when interpreting PA values. PA is consistently associated with sarcopenia, age, and sex, with males typically exhibiting higher PA and lean body mass indices than females, both in healthy adults and in those with chronic diseases [73–77]. Moreover, the prognostic implications of PA often vary by gender, particularly in respiratory conditions. In lung cancer, PA emerges as an independent predictor of mortality in males but not in females, highlighting sex-specific associations that may reflect differential impacts of cachexia or inflammation [66]. In COPD, lower PA is strongly associated with sarcopenia, muscle weakness, reduced exercise capacity, and poorer physical performance, with studies often reporting stronger correlations in males—likely due to higher baseline muscle mass and greater catabolic vulnerability [61–63]. This study also observed gender differences in PA. Gender-stratified PA analysis helps avoid misjudgment caused by a single threshold, suggesting that when applying PA for malnutrition and sarcopenia assessment in patients with ILD, the influence of gender should be fully considered to achieve more precise risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

In summary, this study revealed a high prevalence of malnutrition and sarcopenia among patients with ILD and demonstrated a significant inverse association between PA and these conditions. Lower PA was associated with higher odds of malnutrition and sarcopenia, supporting its potential as an indicator of nutritional and muscular status in this population. These findings suggest that PA assessment via BIA may facilitate the identification of ILD patients at risk for malnutrition or sarcopenia for further evaluation. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to investigate whether PA-guided monitoring can inform timely nutritional support and multidisciplinary interventions to attenuate the progression of malnutrition, sarcopenia, or associated functional decline.

Although this cross-sectional study provides valuable insights into the role of phase angle in assessing malnutrition and sarcopenia in ILD patients, it is inherently limited by its cross-sectional design. Specifically, this study only captured the patients’ condition at the time of enrollment. Consequently, it cannot establish causal relationships. Furthermore, it is unable to evaluate the role of phase angle in predicting the prognosis of ILD patients. Conducted at a single center, this study may be subject to potential selection bias. As an exploratory preliminary study, it precludes definitive conclusions regarding subtype-specific differences. Therefore, future research should involve multicenter, large-scale prospective studies that also account for differences across various types of ILD.

Conclusion

In patients with ILD, a low phase angle is significantly associated with poorer body composition and functional status, as well as a higher prevalence of malnutrition and sarcopenia. There is a significant negative association between PA and both malnutrition and sarcopenia, suggesting that PA may serve as a useful adjunct for identifying individuals at risk of these conditions.
