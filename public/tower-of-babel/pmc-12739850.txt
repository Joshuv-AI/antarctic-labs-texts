Silencing of SlERF.F5 affects tolerance to drought, salt and low-temperature stresses in tomato
Authors: Yanan Chen, Xiaojing Liao, Wenhao Li, Xingyan Cheng, Bo Qi, Jun Gao, Faxiang Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbiotic stresses, including drought, high salinity, and low temperature, present significant challenges to the growth and yield of tomato plants. The AP2/ERF family of genes is integral to plant stress responses; however, the specific role of SlERF.F5 in conferring stress resistance in tomatoes remains to be elucidated.ResultsIn this study, we constructed SlERF.F5-RNAi tomato plants by RNAi technology and treated them with drought, salt and low temperature. Physiological index analysis showed that the stress resistance of SlERF.F5-RNAi plants was significantly enhanced compared with wild-type (WT) plants. Specifically, under stress conditions, the relative water content of the silenced plants was significantly higher; the relative conductivity and malondialdehyde (MDA) content were significantly reduced. In addition, the chlorophyll content of SlERF.F5-RNAi plants was higher. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analyses further indicated that the silencing of SlERF.F5 led to the activation of various stress response genes, with up-regulation observed in genes associated with osmotic regulation (P5CS) and antioxidant defense mechanisms (Cat1 and Apx2). Under conditions of low temperature stress, the expression of the SlCOR gene in SlERF.F5-RNAi plants was upregulated, potentially facilitating the accumulation of hydrophilic COR proteins and thereby mitigating membrane lipid peroxidation damage induced by low temperatures.ConclusionsThis study is the first to establish that SlERF.F5 serves as a negative regulatory in tomato’s response to drought, salinity, and low temperature stress. The silencing of SlERF.F5 enhanced tomato resistance to these abiotic stresses by modulating the expression of stress-responsive genes and preserving cell membrane integrity. These findings not only offer novel insights into the role of ERF family genes in plant stress adaptation, but also identify potential gene targets for the molecular breeding of stress-resistant tomato varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12407-x.Keywords: AP2/ERF, SlERF.F5, Drought, High salt, Low temperature, Tomato

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is one of the most widely cultivated vegetable crops globally [1]. Its fruits are rich in various nutrients, such as vitamin C, lycopene, and potassium [2], making it not only an indispensable ingredient in people’s daily diets but also a crucial raw material in the food processing industry [3]. It plays a pivotal role in the agricultural economic system. However, tomato growth, yield formation, and quality are highly susceptible to abiotic and biotic stresses [4–6].

Among abiotic stresses, drought, salt, and low temperature are the most damaging. Under drought stress, leaf stomatal closure inhibits photosynthesis (reducing net photosynthetic rate), retards growth, and impairs root development [7]. Severe drought leads to loss of cell water potential, plant wilting, and even death [8]. Salt stress induces osmotic stress and ion toxicity. Excessive Na⁺ and Cl⁻ disrupt cellular ion balance, while high external salt concentrations inhibit root water absorption, damage chloroplast structure, reduce photosynthetic pigment content, and affect photosynthetic efficiency [9–11]. Low-temperature stress damages cell membranes, impairs enzyme activity, and causes chilling/freezing injury, further affecting cell division, photosynthetic efficiency, and water transport [12]. Tomatoes are highly sensitive to temperature and light [12, 13], and these abiotic stresses have become key factors restricting the sustainable development of the tomato industry.

The AP2/ERF (APETALA2/ethylene-responsive factor) transcription factor family is a unique class of transcriptional regulatory factors in plants, and it plays a core role in plants’ responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [14–18]. Members contain a conserved 60-amino-acid AP2 domain [19], which forms a β-sheet/α-helix structure to bind GCC-box (AGCCGNC) or DRE/CRT (A/GCCGAC) elements in target gene promoters [20–23]. This binding regulates downstream genes involved in hormone signal transduction, antioxidant defense, and osmotic adjustment [24, 25].

Studies in Arabidopsis thaliana and rice have demonstrated the role of AP2/ERF genes in stress tolerance. For example, For example, Arabidopsis RAP2.6 enhances ABA and stress sensitivity by binding to GCC/CE1 elements [26], while heterologous overexpression of rice OsDREB1A improves Arabidopsis tolerance to salt, drought, and low temperature [27]. Under low K⁺ conditions, the Arabidopsis RAP2.11 gene regulates the expression of AtHAK5 to coordinately respond to low-potassium conditions [28]. Overexpression of LjDREB2B gene enhances the drought stress resistance of Arabidopsis by reducing malondialdehyde content, increasing proline content and antioxidant enzyme activity, and regulating the expression of genes such as AtP5CS1 and AtP5CS2 [29]. The DREB26/ERF12 gene responds to drought stress by regulating epidermal wax biosynthesis [30]. Overexpression of ERF012 enhances the resistance of Arabidopsis to low-temperature, drought, salt, and heavy metal stresses; in contrast, it reduces the plant’s resistance to high-temperature stress [31]. OsERF101 enhances the plant’s resistance to biotic stresses by regulating the recognition of TAL effectors and the activation of Xa1-mediated immune responses [32]. OsERF922 regulates rice tolerance to salt stress by modulating the level of ABA [33]. In tomato, limited studies show that SlMYB15 promotes cold tolerance by activating GSTU43 and ERF-B12 [34]. Antisense Sl-ERF.B.3 transgenic tomato plants enhance the tolerance of tomato to cold stress by reducing cell damage [35]. However, the AP2/ERF family contains 164 members in tomato, and the functions of most genes in abiotic stress responses remain unclear.

SlERF.F5, a member of the tomato AP2/ERF family, has an unknown role in abiotic stress tolerance. In this study, the SlERF.F5 gene was isolated and cloned. Analysis of the expression pattern and promoter of the SlERF.F5 gene indicates that this gene may be involved in the response pathways of tomato to drought, salt, and cold stress. To further investigate the biological function of the SlERF.F5 gene, we obtained SlERF.F5-silenced transgenic tomato plants. We found that silencing of SlERF.F5 significantly enhanced the resistance of tomato seedlings to salt and mannitol. Additionally, under drought, high-salt, and low-temperature stress conditions, silencing of SlERF.F5 improved tomato tolerance to abiotic stresses by altering physiological indices and the expression levels of stress-related genes. These research findings not only fill the gap in studies on the role of SlERF.F5 in abiotic stress responses but also enrich research on the AP2/ERF family in mediating abiotic stress tolerance, providing new genetic resources and a theoretical basis for the molecular breeding of stress-resistant tomatoes.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Wild-type (WT) tomato (Solanum lycopersicum Mill. cv. Ailsa Craig, AC++) and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines seeds were cultivated under sodium light with a photoperiod of 16 h light (28 °C)/8 h dark (18 °C). To detect the response of the SlERF.F5 gene to abiotic stresses, 35-day-old WT tomato seedlings were subjected to stress treatments. For salt stress, the roots of the tomato seedlings were immersed in a 300 mM sodium chloride solution [36]. For drought stress, the tomato seedlings were uprooted, the soil attached to the roots was washed off, and the whole seedlings were placed on dry filter paper. For high-temperature stress, the tomato seedlings were placed in an incubator at 42 °C. For low-temperature stress, the tomato seedlings were placed in an incubator at 4 °C. Three biological replicates were performed for each treatment. After treatment for 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h, respectively, the third leaf from the top of the WT tomato seedlings was collected [37]. Three biological replicates were performed for each treatment. The collected tomato leaves were wrapped in aluminum foil, quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored in a −80 °C freezer.

Construction of SlERF.F5-RNAi vector and plant transformation

The 485 bp sequence of SlERF.F5 was amplified and then ligated into the pHANNIBAL vector in both forward and reverse orientations to form a hairpin structure. Subsequently, through restriction enzyme digestion and ligation, it was integrated into the pBIN19 vector, thus constructing the SlERF.F5-RNAi vector [38]. The constructed SlERF.F5-RNAi vector was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 by the freeze-thaw method and then transferred into WT tomato plants through infection [39]. Finally, the transgenic lines were selected using 60 mg/L kanamycin. The integration of T-DNA in these lines was detected by PCR technology with NPTII-F/R primers (Table S1). The identified transgenic lines can be used for subsequent experimental studies.

Total RNA extraction and quantitative reverse-transcription-PCR analysis

Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) was utilized to extract total RNA from the preserved samples, with the extraction procedure following earlier research findings [40]. After RNA extraction, the integrity of RNA was verified by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis, and clear 28 S and 18 S rRNA bands were observed without obvious degradation. Subsequently, the total RNA concentration was measured using a micro-nucleic acid protein concentration meter (CFX CONNECT, USA). The A260/A280 ratio ranged from 1.8 to 2.0, indicating that the RNA purity was high suitable for subsequent experiments. Reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA was performed using a kit (Promega, Beijing, China).

The quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) experiment was conducted using the CFX96™ Real-Time System from Bio-Rad (USA). The qRT-PCR reaction system was configured as follows: 10 µL of enzyme solution (Promega), 7 µL of distilled water, 1 µL of primers (F + R, with a primer concentration of 10 mM), and 2 µL of cDNA. The qRT-PCR reaction procedure was as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles, 95 °C 5 s, Tm (optimal annealing temperature) 30 s; finally, the melting curve analysis was performed (from 70 °C to 95 °C, the heating rate was 0.5 °C per 2 s) to confirm the specificity of the PCR product [41]. The SlCAC (Solyc08g006960) gene [42], which exhibits stably expressed, was selected as the reference gene, and the 2−ΔΔCT method was used to analyze the expression level of the target gene [43].

Sequence analysis of SlERF.F5

The sequences of ERF proteins in SlERF.F5 and other species were obtained by NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and multiple sequence alignment was performed by DNAMAN software. The phylogenetic tree was constructed by MEGA5 software. The phylogenetic tree was constructed by Neighbor-Joining (NJ) in MEGA 11 software. The Bootstrap analysis was repeated 1000 times to evaluate the confidence of the tree topology.

The 3000 bp sequence upstream of the SlERF.F5 gene promoter was downloaded from the Sol Genomics Network (SGN, http://solgenomics.net/), and the cis-acting elements were further analyzed by the Plant CARE website (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/).

Salt-treated seedling experiments

The tomato seeds of the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines were disinfected and then vernalized in a refrigerator at 4 °C. Subsequently, they were placed in a shaker and incubated at 28 °C with a rotation speed of 120 rpm. After the seeds germinated, they were sown on MS media containing 0, 100, and 200 mM NaCl respectively (35 seeds for each replicate), and then cultured in a light incubator. When growth differences between the seedlings of the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines were observed, the lengths of hypocotyls and roots were measured. Three biological replicates were performed.

Mannitol-treated seedlings experiment

After germination, the seeds of the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines were sown on culture media containing 0, 150 mM, and 300 mM mannitol respectively (35 seeds for each replicate), and then placed in a light incubator for cultivation. When growth differences were observed between the tomato seedlings of the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines, the lengths of hypocotyls and roots were determined. Three biological replicates were performed for the experiment.

Salinity stress tolerance experiment

WT tomato and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines (ERF10, ERF13, and ERF16) seedling were cultivated under sodium light with a photoperiod of 16 h light (28 °C)/8 h dark (18 °C). After about 40 d of cultivation, when the seedlings reach the stage of five leaves and one apical bud, the seedlings of WT and transgenic lines were subjected to salt stress treatment. Tomato seedlings were treated with 100 mM [44], 200 mM [45, 46] and 300 mM [36, 47] NaCl concentrations for preliminary experiments. The results showed that the difference induced by 300 mM NaCl treatment was the most significant. Therefore, we chose 300 mM NaCl for salt stress treatment. They were irrigated with water containing 300 mM NaCl (50 mL per tomato seedling) every two days, and 30 seedlings of each line were treated [36]. Samples were taken from the tomato seedlings of the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines at 0 d, 7 d, and 14 d of treatment, and the chlorophyll content, relative electrical conductivity, relative water content, and MDA content of the leaves from the same position were measured. Each experiment was performed with three biological replicates.

Drought tolerance experiment

When the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi tomato seedlings were cultivated to the stage with five leaves and one apical bud under the same conditions, drought stress treatment was subjected. On the first day of the drought treatment, the tomato seedlings were watered thoroughly, and then drought stress treatment was carried out without watering [48]. After 0 d, 15 d, and 21 d of drought treatment, the chlorophyll content, electrical conductivity, relative water content, and MDA content of the leaves at the same position of tomato seedlings from the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines were detected. Each experiment was conducted with three biological replicates.

Low-temperature tolerance experiment

Tomato seedlings of the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines, which were cultivated under the same conditions, were transferred to an incubator at 4 °C and continuously cultured with unchanged light conditions. Samples were collected from the WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi line tomato seedlings at 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h of treatment, and the chlorophyll content and MDA content of the leaves at the same position in different periods were detected. Each experiment was performed with three biological replicates.

Measurement of water loss rate, relative water content (RWC), relative conductivity, malondialdehyde (MDA) and chlorophyll contents

For the determination of water loss rate, 30 leaves from the same position were selected from each of the WT and three SlERF.F5-RNAi lines, and these leaves were placed on dry filter paper. The leaves were weighed every 30 min until 240 min had elapsed, and the relative water loss rate of the leaves was calculated [36]. Three independent biological replicate samples were set up for each treatment group.

For the determination of relative water content (RWC), the fresh weight (FW), dry weight (DW), and saturated weight (SW) of the leaves need to be measured. Three independent biological replicate samples were set up for each treatment group. The calculation formula is as follows: RWC = [(FW - DW)/(SW - DW)] × 100%.

For the determination of relative conductivity, leaves from the same position of WT and transgenic lines were collected and punched into discs using a hole puncher. Thirty discs per group were placed in a glass test tube, and 10 mL of distilled water was added. After 12 h, the electrical conductivity (R1) was measured. The test tube was then heated in a boiling water bath for 30 min; after cooling to room temperature, the electrical conductivity (R2) was measured again. The relative electrical conductivity was calculated using the formula: Relative electrical conductivity = (R1/R2) × 100%. Three independent biological replicate samples were set up for each treatment group.

The malondialdehyde (MDA) content in leaves was determined using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method [49, 50]. Fresh leaves were fully ground in liquid nitrogen. A 0.2 g aliquot of the ground sample was weighed into a centrifuge tube, followed by the rapid addition of 4 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). After thorough mixing, the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000×g for 5 min. A 1 mL portion of the supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and 4 mL of 10% TCA with 0.5% TBA was added. The mixture was vortexed, incubated at 95 °C for 30 min, and immediately placed in an ice-water bath to stop the reaction. It was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, with three independent biological replicates for each treatment group.

The chlorophyll content in the leaves was extracted with 80% acetone [51]. After 0.1 g of leaves was fully ground, 8 mL of 80% acetone solution was added, and the mixture was soaked in a dark environment for 24 h. During this period, the leaves were gently shaken every 4–6 h until the leaves were completely white. After the extraction was completed, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a 10 mL volumetric flask and diluted with 80% acetone to the scale line. The absorbance of the extract at 663 nm and 645 nm was measured with 80% acetone as the blank control. Chl a = (12.7 × A663-2.69 × A645) × V/(1000 × W); chl b = (22.9 × A645-4.68 × A663) × V/(1000 × W); chl total = Chl a + Chl b. Three independent biological replicates were set up in each treatment group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 software. For evaluating significant differences, Student’s t-test was applied with significance levels set at *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess variations across groups, followed by Tukey’s tests to identify pairwise differences. Treatments showing significant discrepancies (p < 0.05) are denoted by distinct letters. All data represent the mean values of at least three independent biological replicates to ensure reliability.

Results

Molecular characterization of SlERF.F5

Sequence analysis of the SlERF.F5 (Solyc10g009110) gene based on the tomato genome database reveals that the gene has an open reading frame (ORF) of 669 bp in length, encoding a protein composed of 222 amino acid residues. The results of multi-protein sequence alignment using DNAMAN showed that SlERF.F5 is closely related to AP2/ERF transcription factors from other plants. It contains a typical AP2 domain (composed of 3 β-sheets and 1 α-helix), and the amino acid sequence of this domain is highly conserved with only a few amino acid substitutions (Fig. 1 A). The cis-acting element analysis of the promoter region of the SlERF.F5 gene showed that the region contained 12 light response elements (6 G-box, 4 GT1-motif, 2 SP1), 9 ABA response elements (ABRE), 2 MeJA response elements (CGTCA-motif), 2 SA response elements (TCA-element), and 1 ethylene response element (ERE). And drought (MBS), low temperature (LTR), dehydration (DRE core), and related stress response elements. Among them, ABRE and G-box are high-abundance elements, indicating that the promoter may take ABA signal and light signal as the core, integrate hormone regulation and abiotic stress response, and mediate the precise expression of SlERF.F5 gene in stress adaptation and growth and development (Table S2).

The black part in the picture represents the same amino acid in the sequence. The N-terminal amino acid sequence contains three β-sheets and one α-helix structure. B. The phylogenetic tree of tomato SlERF.F5 protein and ERF protein in other species was constructed by adjacency method, and 1,000 replications were constructed via the maximum likelihood (ML) method. The blue box corresponds to the protein in the study.

The phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA (Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis) 5.0 software. A phylogenetic tree of the SlERF.F5 and other ERF proteins was constructed via neighbor-joining and bootstrap analyses with 1000 replicates (Fig. 1B). Phylogenetic analysis based on the selected full-length ERF protein sequence showed that the SlERF.F5 sequence was most similar to SodERF3, followed by StERF4-like, CaERF9, and NtERF4-like. SodERF3 was a typical class II ERF. The expression level of this gene was induced by ABA, salt stress, and wounding, and overexpression of SodERF3 enhances the tolerance of tobacco to drought and osmotic stresses [52]. Currently, there is no research on the StERF4-like, CaERF9, and NtERF4-like genes. Gene structure analysis revealed that SlERF.F5 exhibits an intronless architecture, with a continuous coding sequence (CDS) that lacks the potential to generate structurally diverse proteins via exon rearrangement (Fig. S2), resulting in a relatively fixed protein primary structure. In contrast, SlERF6 and LfERF8-like contain introns (Fig. S2), whose presence enables them to produce multiple splicing variants through exon rearrangement, thereby generating structurally diverse proteins. This difference in gene structure leads to significant divergence in protein sequences between SlERF6, LfERF8-like, and SlERF.F5, ultimately manifesting as a distant evolutionary relationship in the phylogenetic tree. Given that most AP2/ERF family genes are associated with abiotic stress responses and the SlERF.F5 promoter sequence contains stress-responsive elements, we hypothesize that SlERF.F5 may also be involved in abiotic stress responses.

Expression pattern analysis of SlERF.F5

To investigate the function of SlERF.F5 under abiotic stress conditions, the expression level of SlERF.F5 in WT tomato plants after drought, salt, high-temperature, and low-temperature treatments was analyzed using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). As shown in Fig. 2A, the expression level of SlERF.F5 generally exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing after drought treatment; compared with the expression level at 2 h, the expression level of SlERF.F5 was the highest at 24 h. As shown in Fig. 2B, under salt stress, the expression level of SlERF.F5 increased rapidly at the initial stage, reached its peak at 8 h after treatment, and then decreased. This expression pattern suggested that the SlERF.F5 gene may act as a crucial regulator in the salt stress response, being quickly activated at the early stage of stress to modulate the expression of downstream stress-responsive genes, thereby enabling tomato plants to establish early defense mechanisms. The subsequent decrease in expression may be associated with feedback regulation mechanisms that maintain cellular homeostasis and prevent excessive stress responses. As shown in Fig. 2C, the expression level of SlERF.F5 significantly changed over the course of low-temperature treatment. The gene expression peaked at 8 h post-treatment, exhibiting a substantial upregulation compared to the initial level. Expression also increased notably at 2 h. These findings indicated that the SlERF.F5 gene may participate in the early response of tomato to low-temperature stress, undergoing rapid transcriptional activation within 8 h to regulate the expression of downstream stress-responsive genes. As shown in Fig. 2D, during high-temperature stress, the expression level of SlERF.F5 increased slightly at the early stage of treatment, then continued to rise, and reached the maximum value at 12 h after treatment, which was significantly higher than at other time points. These results indicated that the SlERF.F5 gene may play a pivotal regulatory role during the mid-stage of heat stress response in tomato. By activating high-level expression at 12 h, it promotes the coordinated expression of downstream heat-tolerance-related genes, thereby enhancing the plant’s tolerance to high-temperature environments.

WT tomato seedlings were sampled after 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 h of drought, salt, low temperature and high temperature treatment. Three independent biological replicates were set up in each treatment group

Collectively, the above experimental results demonstrate that the SlERF.F5 gene exhibits distinct expression dynamics under various stress treatments, including drought, salt, low temperature, and high temperature. These specific expression patterns indicate that the SlERF.F5 gene was not randomly responsive but rather deeply involved in the process of tomato’s response to different stresses.

Silencing of SlERF.F5 increases seedling tolerance to salt stress

To further study the function of SlERF.F5 gene, different SlERF.F5-silenced lines were obtained. qRT-PCR detection showed that the expression level of SlERF.F5 gene was significantly reduced by 83–98% compared with WT (Fig. S3). Since the seed quantities of the 5th and 15th transgenic lines are insufficient, we have selected the 10th, 13th, and 16th lines for subsequent research. To explore the role of the SlERF.F5 gene in tomato tolerance to salt stress, this study conducted an experiment with gradient concentrations of NaCl. After the seeds germinated, 0 mM, 100 mM, and 200 mM of NaCl were added to the MS medium to observe the growth of seeds from WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines. As shown in Fig. 3A, salt treatment significantly inhibited the hypocotyl length and root length of WT seedlings. In contrast, the growth of seedlings from SlERF.F5-silenced plants was superior to that of WT tomato seedlings. As shown in Fig. 3B, increasing the salt treatment concentration from 100 mM to 200 mM led to a significant shortening of hypocotyl length in both WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines, confirming the inhibitory effect of salt stress on plant growth. Notably, under the same salt concentration, the hypocotyl length of the SlERF.F5-silenced lines was consistently higher than that of the WT plants, indicating stronger salt resistance. A similar pattern was observed for root length (Fig. 3C). In the 100 mM and 200 mM NaCl treatment groups, root growth in the SlERF.F5-silenced lines was less inhibited compared to the WT, with significantly longer root lengths. These results further demonstrate that silencing of SlERF.F5 gene enhances the tolerance of tomato seedlings to salt stress by regulating the growth and development of hypocotyls and roots, effectively alleviating the negative impacts of salt stress.

Silencing of SlERF.F5 increases seedling tolerance to mannitol treatment

Mannitol, a widely used osmotic agent, reduces soil water potential and thereby induces drought stress in plants [53]. To investigate the function of SlERF.F5 gene in the response to osmotic stress, 0 mM (control), 150 mM, and 300 mM mannitol solutions were added to the MS medium, respectively, and the growth of seedlings of the WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines was observed.

As shown in Fig. 4A, during normal growth, the hypocotyl length and root length of seedlings in the SlERF.F5-silenced lines were almost the same as those of WT seedlings. When the mannitol concentration was 150 mM and 300 mM, it was found that compared with normal growth conditions, the hypocotyl length and root length of tomato seedlings were inhibited under stress. As shown in Fig. 4B, with the increase of mannitol treatment concentration from 150 mM to 300 mM, the hypocotyl length of tomato seedlings in both WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines significantly decreased, indicating that high-concentration osmotic stress inhibits plant growth. Notably, under the same mannitol concentration, the hypocotyl length of seedlings in the SlERF.F5-silenced lines was consistently longer than that of WT plants, suggesting stronger resistance to osmotic stress. The root length exhibited a similar trend (Fig. 4C). In the 150 mM and 300 mM mannitol treatment groups, the root length of seedlings in the SlERF.F5-silenced lines was less inhibited than that of WT plants, resulting in longer roots. These results demonstrate that silencing of SlERF.F5 gene enhances the tolerance of tomato seedlings to osmotic stress and plays a crucial role in maintaining the growth of hypocotyls and roots, providing important phenotypic evidence for understanding the osmotic stress response mechanism of tomato.

Silencing of SlERF.F5 reduces the water loss rate of tomato leaves

Building on previous findings that silencing of SlERF.F5 gene significantly enhanced the growth performance of tomato seedlings under salt and osmotic stress, this study aimed to further elucidate the impact mechanism of this gene on plant stress tolerance. A leaf water loss rate measurement experiment was designed. Tomato seedlings of the WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines were cultured for 35 d synchronously (Fig. 5A). Once the plants reached a stable growth stage, leaf samples from the same leaf position were collected, and the leaf water loss rate was measured using the excised-leaf water loss method. As shown in Fig. 5D, there was a significant difference in the water loss rate of detached leaves between WT and SlERF.F5-silenced plants at different time points: throughout the entire measurement period, the leaf water loss rate of the SlERF.F5-silenced lines was consistently lower than that of the WT plants, and the difference between the two became more pronounced as time elapsed. This result indicates that silencing of SlERF.F5 gene can significantly enhance the water retention capacity of tomato leaves.

Silencing of SlERF.F5 increases the resistance of tomato plants to salt stress

To further evaluate the effect of SlERF.F5 gene silencing on the salt stress resistance of tomato plants, WT and SlERF.F5-silenced transgenic lines were subjected to root irrigation with a 300 mM NaCl solution to simulate a high-intensity salt stress environment, and physiological indicators were determined.

As shown in Fig. 6A, after 7 d of salt stress treatment, there was no significant difference in the external growth phenotype between WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines, and all plants maintained a basic growth state. However, when the salt treatment was extended to 14 d (Fig. 6B), the leaves of WT plants showed yellowing and wilting, while the leaves of SlERF.F5-silenced lines remained fresh green, with the plants exhibiting good overall growth. Chlorophyll content is a key indicator reflecting the photosynthetic function and stress tolerance of plants. As shown in Fig. 6C, after 14 d of salt stress treatment, the chlorophyll content in the leaves of the transgenic lines was significantly higher than that in the leaves of WT plants. This indicated that the transgenic lines suffered less damage to the photosynthetic system caused by salt stress and could better maintain photosynthetic physiological functions. Relative electrical conductivity is an intuitive indicator of cell membrane integrity, with lower conductivity indicating less severe damage to the cell membrane. After salt stress treatment (Fig. 6D), the relative electrical conductivity of leaves in the transgenic lines was slightly lower than that of the leaves of WT plants. This suggests that the cell membranes of the transgenic lines have higher stability under salt stress and are less affected by osmotic and ionic toxicity. As shown in Fig. 6E, after salt stress treatment, the relative water content of leaves in the transgenic lines was consistently higher than that in WT plants, indicating that it had a stronger ability to maintain water under salt stress. As presented in Figs. 6F and 7d of salt stress resulted in higher leaf MDA content in transgenic lines than in WT plants, which might be linked to the slight leaf yellowing of transgenic lines. Conversely, after 14 d of salt stress, transgenic lines displayed lower leaf MDA content than WT plants, suggesting that they better maintained cell membrane stability and suffered less damage under sustained salt stress. These results suggested that silencing of SlERF.F5 gene may enhance salt resistance in tomato plants by regulating ion homeostasis, improving osmotic adjustment capacity, and activating antioxidant system activity, thereby alleviating membrane damage and photosynthetic inhibition caused by salt stress.

Silencing of SlERF.F5 increases the resistance of tomato plants to drought stress

To further evaluate the role of the SlERF.F5 gene in drought stress, WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines were subjected to drought stress, and physiological indicators were determined. As shown in Fig. 7A, after 15 d of drought treatment, the leaves of WT plants had exhibited obvious wilting and yellowing symptoms, while the leaves of SlERF.F5-silenced lines began to wilt, but the degree of wilting was milder than that of WT plants. After 21 d of drought treatment (Fig. 7B), the leaves of WT plants turned yellow and abscised, whereas the leaves of SlERF.F5-silenced lines only started to yellow and wilt. The detection of chlorophyll content (Fig. 7C) showed that there was no significant difference in chlorophyll content between WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines at the early stage of treatment. After 21 d of treatment, the chlorophyll content of SlERF.F5-silenced lines was significantly higher than that of WT leaves. After 21 d of drought treatment, the relative conductivity of the leaves of SlERF.F5-silenced lines was slightly lower than that of WT plants (Fig. 7D). Determination of relative water content showed that after 15 d of drought treatment, the relative water content of leaves in SlERF.F5-silenced lines was significantly higher than that in WT plants. However, there was no significant difference between the two groups after 21 d of drought treatment (Fig. 7E). As shown in Fig. 7F, the MDA content in the leaves of SlERF.F5-silenced plants was significantly lower than that of WT plants after drought treatment. These results indicated that silencing of SlERF.F5 gene can significantly enhance the tolerance of tomato plants to drought stress by improving water retention capacity, slowing down the degradation of photosynthetic pigments, and reducing membrane lipid peroxidation damage.

Silencing of SlERF.F5 increases the resistance of tomato plants to low-temperature stress

To elucidate the function of SlERF.F5 gene under low-temperature stress, tomato seedlings of the WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines were subjected to cold stress treatment at 4 °C, and physiological indices were measured. As shown in Fig. 8A, after 4 h of 4 °C cold treatment, there was no significant difference in the growth status of WT and SlERF.F5-silenced tomato seedlings, with both maintaining normal leaf morphology and color. However, when the stress duration was extended to 24 h, the leaves of WT plants began to show typical cold damage symptoms such as obvious wilting and water-soaked freeze lesions, while the SlERF.F5-silenced seedlings still maintained a normal growth state (Fig. 8B). Dynamic monitoring of MDA content reveals the regulatory role of the SlERF.F5 gene in tomato seedling cold resistance. After 24 h of cold treatment, the MDA content in leaves of SlERF.F5-silenced lines was slightly lower than that in WT plants. With prolonged cold stress, MDA accumulation in silenced lines was significantly lower than in WT, indicating less severe membrane lipid peroxidation and stronger membrane stability (Fig. 8C). As shown in Fig. 8D, no significant difference in chlorophyll content was observed between the WT and SlERF.F5-silenced lines, whether after 24 h of 4 °C treatment or under prolonged stress. This indicates that silencing of SlERF.F5 gene did not have an obvious effect on the synthesis or degradation of chlorophyll under low-temperature stress. Although chlorophyll content did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 8D), integrated phenotypic and oxidative damage data suggest that silencing of SlERF.F5 effectively enhances tomato adaptation to cold stress by alleviating membrane lipid peroxidation and maintaining cell structure stability.

The expression levels of stress-related genes in WT and SlERF.F5-RNAi lines under stress conditions

To explore the molecular basis for the difference in stress resistance between WT and SlERF.F5-silenced tomato lines under stress conditions, qRT-PCR was used to analyze the expression levels of stress-responsive genes. The results showed that, compared with WT, the transcriptional levels of the ascorbate peroxidase gene (SlAPX2) (Fig. 9A, G) and catalase gene (SlCat1) (Fig. 9B, H) were significantly upregulated in SlERF.F5-silenced lines after salt stress treatment. The expression of SlCat1 was significantly up-regulated in SlERF.F5 transgenic plants. Given that GCC-box and CRT elements were predicted in the SlCat1 promoter region (Fig. S4), SlERF.F5 may activate the expression of antioxidant genes (SlCat1), thereby enhancing reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity and alleviating oxidative damage under stress conditions. As a rate-limiting enzyme gene in ascorbic acid biosynthesis, the enhanced expression of SlGME2 can promote the accumulation of antioxidant substances, effectively scavenge stress-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS), and alleviate oxidative damage. In contrast, SlP5CS, a key gene in proline synthesis, can drive the massive synthesis of proline when its expression is upregulated, thereby maintaining water balance by regulating cellular osmotic pressure and enhancing cell membrane stability. After salt stress treatment, compared with the WT, the transcriptional levels of SlGME2 (encoding GDP-mannose-3’,5’-epimerase) and SlP5CS (encoding Δ¹-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase) in the silenced lines were both significantly upregulated (Fig. 9C, D, I, J). This suggested that these two genes may be involved in the SlERF.F5-mediated stress resistance regulatory network. SlPR1 works in synergy with the intracellular antioxidant system to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated under stress, thereby alleviating membrane lipid peroxidation damage. SlPR5 reduces cell dehydration damage caused by drought and salt stress by regulating cellular osmotic pressure (promoting the accumulation of proline and soluble sugars). The results showed that compared with the WT, the transcriptional levels of SlPR1 and SlPR5 were significantly upregulated in SlERF.F5-silenced lines. Given that both SlPR1 and SlPR5 promoters contain GCC-Box (Fig. S4), this finding reveals that SlERF.F5 may directly bind to the GCC-Box in SlPR1/SlPR5 promoters to negatively regulate their expression. Collectively, these two genes are involved in the SlERF.F5-mediated stress resistance regulatory network, and their transcriptional modulation by SlERF.F5 is likely dependent on the GCC-Box in their promoters.

To explore the molecular basis of enhanced cold resistance in tomato by silencing of SlERF.F5 gene, this study analyzed the expression of cold-responsive genes in WT and silenced lines after 4℃ stress treatment. The results showed that, compared with WT, the transcriptional level of the cold-responsive gene SlCOR in the silenced lines was significantly up-regulated (Fig. 9M), revealing that SlERF.F5 may be involved in the cold adaptation process of tomato by regulating the expression of SlCOR.

Discussion

Genes of the AP2/ERF family have extensive functions and play crucial roles in plant growth, development, and responses to various stresses. For example, overexpression of SlERF36 in tomato leads to a premature senescence phenotype and causes changes in stomatal density and photosynthesis [54]. SlERF52 is specifically expressed in the abscission zone (AZ) of the flower stalk. Silencing of SlERF52 gene may delay flower abscission by regulating the development of the abscission zone of the flower stalk [55]. SlERF6 plays an important role in fruit ripening by integrating the ethylene and carotenoid synthesis pathways in tomato [56]. Overexpression of Sl-ERF2 gene in transgenic tomato lines leads to premature seed germination and increases ethylene sensitivity [57]. This study for the first time confirmed the function of the AP2/ERF family gene SlERF.F5 in abiotic stress. By analyzing the expression patterns of SlERF.F5 under drought, salt, low-temperature, and high-temperature stresses, it was demonstrated that this gene could be induced by abiotic stresses.

Tomato seedlings were treated with mannitol (simulated drought stress) and salt, and the results showed that silencing of SlERF.F5 significantly enhanced the tolerance of tomato seedlings to these two stresses (Fig. 10). Further stress treatments (drought, salt, and low temperature) confirmed that SlERF.F5-silenced plants exhibited superior resistance to multiple abiotic stresses compared with WT plants. Physiological index analysis revealed distinct differences between SlERF.F5-silenced plants and WT plants under stress conditions: The relative conductivity and malondialdehyde (MDA) content of silenced plants were significantly lower than those of WT plants, indicating that silencing of SlERF.F5 maintained good cell membrane integrity, reduced intracellular substance extravasation, and stabilized the intracellular environment, which is conducive to the normal progress of cellular physiological and biochemical reactions; The chlorophyll content of silenced plants was significantly higher than that of WT plants, suggesting that silencing of SlERF.F5 alleviated stress-induced damage to chloroplast structure and function, enabling stable light energy capture and normal photosynthesis [58]; The relative water content of silenced plants was significantly higher than that of WT plants, which was attributed to the improved cell membrane stability by silencing of SlERF.F5, thereby reducing water loss through damaged cell membranes (Fig. 10).

The schematic diagram of SlERF.F5 regulating tomato salt tolerance, drought tolerance and low temperature tolerance

Further studies on the molecular mechanisms demonstrated that silencing of SlERF.F5 enhances tomato stress resistance by regulating the expression of a series of stress-responsive genes (Fig. 10). qRT-PCR results showed that genes involved in osmotic adjustment, antioxidant defense, and stress response (SlAPX2, SlCat1, SlGME2, SlP5CS, and SlCOR) were significantly upregulated in SlERF.F5-silenced plants compared with WT plants. SlCat1 and SlAPX2 encode catalase (CAT) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX), respectively, which are core components of the plant reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging system [59, 60]. Under salt stress, the elevated expression of these two genes in SlERF.F5-silenced lines may increase CAT and APX enzyme activities [60], thereby accelerating the decomposition of ROS such as H₂O₂ and reducing oxidative stress-induced cellular damage [61]. This is consistent with the lower malondialdehyde (MDA) content—an indicator of membrane lipid peroxidation—observed in SlERF.F5-silenced plants in phenotypic experiments, further confirming that silencing of SlERF.F5 enhances stress resistance by strengthening the antioxidant defense system. Notably, previous studies have reported that the MYB transcription factor SlMYB50 could enhance the scavenging capacity of H₂O₂ and reactive oxygen species (ROS) by upregulating the expression of Cat1 and Apx1, thereby improving tomato stress resistance [36]. Combined with this research result and the phenomenon that the expression of Cat1 and Apx2 genes was significantly upregulated after silencing of SlERF.F5 in this study, it can be inferred that SlERF.F5 may enhance the scavenging capacity of plants for reactive oxygen species under stress conditions by regulating the expression of downstream antioxidant genes (such as Cat1 and Apx2), and ultimately improve the stress resistance level of plants. SlGME2 is a key gene in tomato ascorbic acid (AsA) biosynthesis, catalyzing the conversion of GDP-D-mannose to GDP-L-galactose; its upregulation may promote AsA accumulation and thereby enhance stress resistance [62]. SlP5CS encodes Δ1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase, a rate-limiting enzyme in proline (Pro) synthesis [63]. Increased SlP5CS expression can promote Pro accumulation, which reduces salt ion toxicity, stabilizes intracellular proteins and biological membranes, and ultimately enhances tomato salt tolerance.

As a core regulator of low-temperature response, SlCOR encodes cold-responsive proteins that improve plant cold tolerance by stabilizing biological membrane structures, protecting protein activity, and regulating cell osmotic pressure [64]. Under low-temperature stress, the activated expression of SlCOR in SlERF.F5-silenced lines may induce the accumulation of hydrophilic COR proteins. These proteins can insert into the cell membrane to form a protective layer, alleviating low-temperature-induced membrane lipid peroxidation damage.

This study not only clarifies the negative regulatory role of the SlERF.F5 gene in the multi-stress response of tomato, but also provides a new perspective for understanding the functions of ethylene response factor (ERF) family members in the stress adaptation mechanisms of plants. The research results have important guiding significance for molecular breeding of stress-resistant tomatoes, offering potential gene targets and a theoretical basis for the cultivation of new stress-resistant tomato varieties. However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which SlERF.F5 regulates tomato stress resistance, as well as its interactions with other signaling pathways, still require further in-depth investigation. In the future, techniques such as yeast two-hybrid and chromatin immunoprecipitation can be combined to explore their interactions with upstream and downstream genes, laying a more solid theoretical foundation for the optimization and application of stress-resistant tomato breeding techniques.

Conclusion

This study is the first to confirm that silencing of SlERF.F5 gene can significantly enhance the tolerance of tomato to drought, salt, and low-temperature stresses. It breaks through the previous limitations in understanding the function of this gene and provides a new research perspective for the role of ERF family transcription factors in the regulation of tomato stress resistance. It clarifies that SlERF.F5 may negatively regulate the expression of key genes related to antioxidant defense (SlCat1, SlAPX2), osmotic adjustment (SlP5CS, SlGME2), and low-temperature response (SlCOR), and infers a regulatory pathway of the ‘SlERF.F5-downstream stress resistance gene network’, offering a novel molecular approach for the regulation of tomato stress resistance. Based on the negative regulatory role of the SlERF.F5 gene, this study provides a new molecular target for tomato stress-resistant breeding. Targeted modification of the expression of this gene through gene silencing or CRISPR/Cas9 technology can cultivate new tomato germplasms with enhanced multi-stress resistance, solving the problem of low efficiency in improving stress-resistant traits in traditional breeding.
