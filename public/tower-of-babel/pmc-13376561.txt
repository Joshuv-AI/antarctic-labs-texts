Biogeography and community stability of leaf endophytic diazotrophs in mangrove Kandelia obovata
Authors: Qinghua Hou, Rajapakshalage Thashikala Nethmini, Feng Qi, Lianghao Pan, Xiaofang Shi, Laizhen Huang, Qing He, Qingxiang Chen, Xiaolei Li, Gonglingxia Jiang, Dong Ke, Nan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe leaf endosphere hosts diazotrophs vital for plant health and ecosystem resilience in saline environments. However, the geographic pattern and stability of the diazotrophic community in coastal salt marshes ecosystems are poorly understood. In this study, we examine the biogeographic distribution, assembly processes and community stability of leaf endophytic diazotrophs in the mangrove species Kandelia obovata across multiple regions in China. Our findings reveal that Rhizobiales are the most abundant in the diazotrophic communities. Community assembly is largely governed by stochastic processes, with dispersal limitations playing a dominant role. Soil nutrients emerge as the dominant factor influencing diazotroph composition, surpassing climatic variables and host traits. Notably, soil total sulfur (TS) serves as a key environmental factor shaping the diazotrophic community structure, with low TS environments supporting more stable microbial networks. These findings reveal ecological drivers and adaption strategies of endophytic diazotrophic communities in mangrove ecosystems, emphasizing the influence of nutrients dynamics in leaf endophytic microbial assembly under environmental changes.Subject terms: Molecular biology, Microbial ecology

Introduction

Plants provide a range of ecological niches that support complex microbial communities and play critical roles in promoting plant productivity, health, and resilience in natural ecosystems1. These plant–microbe interactions encompass both epiphytes colonizing external plant surfaces and endophytes residing within plant tissues2. Leaf-associated microbial communities have recently attracted increasing attention owing to their ecological significance and spatial variability3,4. Nethmini et al. 3 identified the dominance of specialist taxa in mangrove leaf endophytic bacterial communities, with temperature acting as a key driver of community dissimilarity across large spatial scales. Similarly, Wang et al. 4 demonstrated that leaf epiphytic bacterial diversity decreased more sharply with altitude than endophytic bacterial diversity in temperate forest ecosystems. These findings underscore the influence of climatic factors on the microbial community structure. Plant traits, such as basal diameter and plant height5,6, as well as essential soil nutrients, including nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur7 are key drivers of microbial colonization and community assembly. However, the relative contributions of plant traits, soil nutrition, and climatic factors to the large-scale structure and resilience of leaf endophytic microbiomes, particularly those involving functional groups, remain poorly understood.

Mangroves are distinct intertidal wetland ecosystems located at the interface of terrestrial and marine environments along tropical and subtropical coastlines8. These ecosystems are characterized by high salinity and fluctuating environmental conditions that profoundly affect ecosystem resilience and the biological processes of the associated microbial communities9. Microbial composition and diversity also strongly influence mangrove ecosystems10. Plants living in mangrove ecosystems host substantial populations of endophytic bacteria within their leaves that contribute to plant development11, enhance tolerance to environmental stressors3, and facilitate key processes, such as nutrient cycling12. Among these bacteria, diazotrophs represent diverse bacterial phylogenetic groups that play a crucial role in biological nitrogen fixation (BNF), a process central to the nitrogen cycle in ecosystems13. In natural terrestrial environments, BNF contributes over 60% of total nitrogen input14, supplying nitrogen to both plants and surrounding microbial communities. The diversity of diazotrophic bacteria identified using the nifH marker gene in environments such as marine sediments15, grassland soils16, and agricultural soils17 is driven mainly by climatic and soil chemical properties17. Environmental factors may considerably alter leaf endophytic microbial communities in woody halophytes18,19. For example, soil P content significantly changes the leaf microbiome species richness and biodiversity of Suaeda salsa (Amaranthaceae) in coastal saline soil18. Furthermore, sulfur also plays a pivotal role in influencing microbial interactions and community structure across various environmental habitats20–22. For instance, studies have found that particularly in nutrient-limited ecosystems like mangroves, elevated sulfur levels—under anaerobic conditions—can limit nitrogen uptake and alter microbial interactions, shaping the community composition22. Moreover, in mangrove sediments, sulfate reduction, a key anaerobic process, has been shown to interact with nitrogen fixation and organic matter decomposition, thus contributing significantly to coastal nutrient cycling22,23. Environmental factors such as sulfur content, along with phosphorus and nitrogen availability, can significantly influence the species richness and biodiversity of the microbiome18,19. However, the impact of soil chemical factors on the distribution and diversity of leaf-endophytic diazotrophic communities in nutrient-limited mangrove ecosystems remains poorly understood.

Microbial community stability, which refers to the ability of an ecosystem to maintain its function and structure despite environmental disturbances15, plays a key role in maintaining ecosystem function and resilience20. Studies have shown that stability is strongly influenced by environmental filters, including temperature, salinity, and nutrient availability24–26. Soil chemical properties play a significant role in shaping the network structure and robustness of microbial communities20,21,27–29. For instance, a study showed that microbial communities in the rice rhizosphere exhibit greater stability at higher pH levels28. In mangrove ecosystems, salinity, nutrient gradients, and local soil conditions have been shown to modulate diazotrophic diversity and network stability24,25. Therefore, we hypothesize that the diversity and stability of the mangrove leaf endophytic diazotrophic microbial community are influenced by key soil chemical properties (S, N, P) determining the community’s resilience to environmental disturbances.

Kandelia obovata is a pivotal species in mangrove restoration due to its ecological significance and capacity to establish and thrive in subtropical environments. Its role in promoting the expansion and resilience of mangrove forests makes it a key species for restoration initiatives aimed at enhancing coastal ecosystem stability. Hence, in this study, 250 leaves of K. obovata were collected to investigate nitrogen-fixing microbial communities in the mangrove leaf endosphere over a broad geographic scale in southern China. We aimed to (a) identify the community composition and diversity of mangrove leaf endophytic diazotrophs over a broad geographic scale, (b) explore community stability and assemblage processes of the leaf endophytic diazotrophic communities and (c) determine the main factors driving the community turnover and resilience of diazotrophic community in the mangrove leaves. This study provides insights into diazotrophic microbial communities in mangrove leaves, highlighting their diversity and stability across a broad geographic range, and contributing to our understanding of their ecological resilience under environmental perturbations and global climate change.

Results

Community compositions, biogeographic distribution and assembly of endophytic diazotrophs in mangrove

From the 68,663 high-quality sequences, a total of 3887 Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were identified. The coverage for each sample exceeded 99.53%, indicating that the vast majority of diazotrophic taxa were effectively captured. Among the diazotrophic communities in K. obovata leaves, Proteobacteria emerged as the dominant phyla (62.02% ~ 94.01%) (Fig. S1A), primarily represented by the Rhizobiales (Fig. S1B) (25.78% ~ 64.04%). At the genus level, Bradyrhizobium was dominant (18.28% ~ 53.88%), followed by Azospirillum (3.62% ~ 11.67%), Marinobacterium (0.22% ~ 9.75%), Azoarcus (0.14% ~ 4.91%, Pseudomonas (0.27% ~ 5.11%), and Pelomonas (1.13% ~ 4.77%) (Fig. 1).

We further analyzed the alpha and beta diversities of the endophytic diazotrophic communities in K. obovata leaves across different geographic regions. Alpha diversity, as measured by the Shannon index, showed significant differences among the sampling sites (ANOVA, p < 0.05), with the highest diversity observed in the Huidong mangrove site (LE-HD-22.83°) and the lowest in the Dongzhaigang mangrove site (LE-DZ-20.00°) (Fig. 2A). Analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) further confirmed significant compositional differences in the diazotrophic communities among the various locations (R = 0.275, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). The beta diversities, measured using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (Fig. S2), exhibited significant distance-decay relationships (p < 0.001), indicating that microbial community similarity decreased with increasing geographic distance.

The β-nearest taxon index (βNTI) analysis indicated that 85.46% of βNTI values fell within the –2 to +2 range, signifying the predominance of stochastic processes in community assembly of K. obovata leaves (Fig. S3A). Dispersal limitation (41.6%), ecological drift (19.01%), and homogenized dispersal (2.13%) collectively accounted for 62.75% of the total assembly processes (Fig. S3B). Furthermore, A significant correlation was observed between βNTI and geographic distance (p < 0.001) (Fig. S3A).

Network topological features and stability of diazotrophic communities in the mangrove leaves of K. obovata along a latitude gradient

To explore the topological characteristics and stability of diazotrophic communities in K. obovata leaves, co-occurrence networks were constructed across a latitudinal gradient (Fig. 3 and Table S1). Networks varied significantly in terms of their complexity. The Baimajin mangrove site (LE-BM-19.72°) and Huidong mangrove site (LE-HD-22.83°) exhibited the highest connectivity, with 171 and 145 nodes and 4080 and 4035 edges, respectively (Fig. 3A and Table S1), indicating highly interactive microbial communities. In contrast, the Ledong mangrove site (LE-LD-18.44°) had 123 nodes and 1772 edges, while the Fuding mangrove site (LE-FD-27.28°) had 86 nodes and 826 edges. Furthermore, the Ledong mangrove site (LE-LD-18.44°) and Fuding mangrove site (LE-FD-27.28°) showed lower network densities (0.233 and 0.181, respectively) and larger diameters (3.749 and 3.742, respectively)(Fig. 3A and Table S1), suggesting a less cohesive community structure. Across the latitudinal gradient, community stability varied among sites, with the Shankou mangrove site (SK-21.57°) exhibiting the highest stability and the Fugong mangrove site (FG-24.39°) showing the lowest (Fig. 3B). Importantly, linear regression analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between network robustness, as measured by natural connectivity and geographic distance (R² = 0.03, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3C). This pattern implies that the leaf endophytic diazotrophic communities in higher-latitude regions may possess greater structural stability.

Key factors driving the diazotrophic community in mangrove leaves

Path model was constructed to analyze the relationships between stability, alpha and beta diversity, as well as environmental factors, including nutrients, soil properties, climate factors, host plant traits and geographic distance (Fig. 4). Climate factors (r = 0.43) and nutrients elements (r = 0.28) had the significant positive effect on alpha diversity (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). Soil properties (r = -0.43) and host plant traits (r = –0.20) had significant negative effect on alpha diversity (Fig. 4A). Soil properties (r = 0.69), geographical distance (r = 0.37) and host plant traits (r = 0.20) had the significant positive effect on beta diversity (p < 0.001), respectively. Climate factors and nutrients elements had significant negative effect (Fig. 4A). Nutrient elements had the strongest and significant negative effect (r = –0.47, p < 0.001) on stability. Soil properties (r = 0.39, p < 0.001) had the significant positive effect on community stability, following by host plant traits (0.36, p < 0.001) and climate factors had indirect effect on stability (Fig. 4A). Nutrient elements had the strongest direct impact on stability (Fig. 4B), Total sulfur (TS) emerged as the most influential factor within nutrient elements (Fig. 4C), contributing substantially to the stability of the microbial community.

Variation partitioning analysis revealed that soil properties, nutrient elements, climatic factors, and host plant traits collectively explained 41.66% of the variation in the diazotrophic community composition in mangrove leaves (Fig. 5A). Soil nutrients contributed the most (16.53%), followed by soil properties (10.31%), climatic factors (8.21%), and host plant traits (3.19%). This indicates that soil nutrients exerted a stronger influence on the leaf diazotrophic community than climatic conditions or host plant characteristics. Based on the mean decrease in Gini index, TS was again identified as the most influential variable, followed by phosphate (PO₄³⁻), dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN), ammonium (NH₄⁺), and total nitrogen (TN) (Fig. 5B). These findings highlight the predominant role of specific nutrient indicators and overall soil chemistry in determining the community structure.

Spearman’s correlation analysis further revealed that the alpha diversity indices of the diazotrophic community (Shannon, Simpson, Richness, and Chao1) were significantly negatively correlated with TS and positively correlated with plant height (KO.High) (Table S2). This suggests that lower TS concentrations are conducive to greater microbial diversity and that taller, more vigorous plants tend to support more diverse diazotrophic communities. Additionally, mean annual evaporation (MAE) showed a significant negative correlation with the Shannon and Simpson indices (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively) and a positive correlation with Richness and Chao1 (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively) (Table S2).

Network structure, community stability and assemblage processes of the diazotrophic community along soil TS gradients

TS was identified as a key influential factor shaping diazotrophic community dissimilarity and stability, as demonstrated by the Mantel test, random forest, Spearman correlation analyses and path analysis. To further investigate its impact, all samples were binned into three groups based on TS concentration: low sulfur (LS, 0.01–0.1 g/kg), middle sulfur (MS, 0.1–0.3 g/kg), and high sulfur (HS, >0.3 g/kg). We then assessed the effects of TS gradients on the community topological characteristics and assembly processes.

Network analysis revealed no network or module hubs in the LS group (Fig. 6A), suggesting a lack of keystone taxa, whose removal has been computationally shown to significantly disrupt microbial structure and function. Furthermore, the proportion of connector nodes increased from the LS group to the HS group (Fig. 6A–C), indicating higher connectivity, but potentially lower stability, in networks with higher sulfur levels. The distribution of nifH ASVs followed a typical power-law pattern across all three groups (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6D–F). However, the LS group exhibited the lowest R² value (Fig. 6D), suggesting a weaker network structure and lower predictability than those of the MS and HS groups.

Pearson’s correlation analysis further revealed significant relationships between TS and network topology (Fig. 6G–I). Specifically, centralization betweenness and modularity increased with TS concentration (p < 0.001, p < 0.05, respectively) (Figs. 6G, H), whereas the number of modules decreased significantly (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6I). The other topological properties did not exhibit any significant correlations (Table S3). Interestingly, in the HS group, both the centralization degree and positive cohesion were negatively correlated with TS (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, respectively), suggesting that high sulfur concentrations may reduce cooperative interactions and the central influence within the network (Table S3).

To evaluate the robustness of co-occurrence networks under different sulfur levels, we applied an attack-based method using natural connectivity as a stability metric. The LS group exhibited the highest network robustness, followed by the MS and HS groups (Fig. 6J), indicating that lower sulfur levels support more stable microbial interactions.

To explore the ecological processes driving diazotrophic community assembly, the β-nearest taxon index (βNTI) was calculated. When comparing across TS groups, βNTI values predominantly fell within the stochastic range (–2 to +2) in the LS and MS groups, whereas values in the HS group were mostly greater than +2 (Fig. 6K), indicating a shift toward deterministic processes under HS conditions.

Discussion

Leaf endophytic bacteria play essential roles in host plant function, particularly by enhancing nutrient acquisition and facilitating nitrogen fixation30–34. In our study, Proteobacteria emerged as the most dominant phylum (Figs. 1 and 3A). This observation is consistent with previous reports highlighting the prevalence of Proteobacteria in the leaf microbiomes of various mangrove species, including K. obovata, Kandelia candel, and Bruguiera gymnorrhiza26,35,36, emphasizing their ability to thrive in the distinct environmental conditions of mangrove ecosystems37.

Notably, within Proteobacteria, the order Rhizobiales dominated (Fig. 1). Rhizobiales are traditionally known as root nodule endosymbionts of legumes, their abundance in leaf tissues may indicate potential recruitment from mangrove sediments. However, alternative mechanisms, including leaf inoculation or seed transfer, could also facilitate their colonization38,39. Studies have shown that mangrove sediments, being rich in nitrogen-fixing Rhizobiales due to their anoxic and nutrient-dense conditions, likely serve as the primary source for these endosymbionts. This is further supported by the dominance of Bradyrhizobium (Fig. 1), a genus adapt in nitrogen fixation via root nodule formation in legumes40, which has also been observed in non-leguminous environments, including forests, agricultural soils, and mangrove systems40–42. Furthermore, synergistic interactions between Bradyrhizobium and Azospirillum reduce salt-induced damage and enhance nitrogen fixation43,44. These cooperative dynamics likely underlie the resilience and productivity of K. obovata in saline environments.

A significant linear distance-decay relationship (p < 0.001) was observed for beta diversities (Fig. S2), indicating that leaf diazotrophic community composition and diversity declined with increasing geographic distance between the sampling sites. This pattern is primarily attributed to the substantial environmental heterogeneity across the study region. The nearly 10° latitudinal gradient (18.44°N–28.35°N) introduced major climatic shifts from tropical to subtropical conditions, including variations in temperature, seasonality, and soil characteristics45,46. These latitudinally driven environmental filters act as strong selective forces, favoring distinct diazotroph taxa at different sites and promoting community turnover47,48. Furthermore, dispersal limitation likely reinforces this biogeographic pattern. While microorganisms have broad dispersal potential, leaf endophytic diazotrophs are constrained by host-specific filters, such as leaf structural and chemical traits, which restrict establishment from the regional species pool49,50. Consequently, distant mangrove populations recruit diazotrophs from locally distinct microbial communities. This interpretation is strongly supported by our non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and ANOSIM analyses, (R = 0.275, p < 0.001) which revealed distinct, site-specific clustering of diazotrophic communities (R = 0.275, p < 0.001). Together, these results underscore the combined roles of environmental selection and dispersal limitation in driving the spatial assembly of leaf-associated microbial communities.

Microbial community assembly shaped by both deterministic and stochastic processes, with their influence varying across ecosystems24. A previous study highlighted the dominant role of stochastic processes in shaping diazotrophic communities during their transition from soil to leaves in mangrove ecosystems26. Similarly, the present study indicated that diazotrophic community assembly was primarily influenced by stochastic processes (Fig. S3A, B), with dispersal limitation emerging as the dominant factor (Fig. S3B). This observation is consistent with previous studies suggesting that the spatial heterogeneity of the leaf endosphere creates a mosaic of microenvironments51. In addition, physical barriers intrinsic to the leaf endosphere may restrict microbial dispersal, thereby amplifying the role of stochastic processes52. Variations in microbial tolerance to stresses at the leaf–air interface further diminish deterministic selection53, suggesting that disruptions to the leaf environment, such as pollution or habitat degradation, could alter microbial assembly and ecosystem function. The relationship between βNTI and geographic distance was further explored, revealing a significant correlation (p < 0.001) between the two variables (Fig.S3A), suggesting that geographic separation plays a significant role in shaping microbial community composition. This finding indicates that geographic distance may influence community assembly processes, highlighting the importance of spatial factors in shaping microbial communities.

Our results indicated that shifts in the leaf diazotrophic community of K. obovata were primarily influenced by soil nutrients, surpassing the climatic factors and host traits (Fig. 4). Path analysis further supported this finding, emphasizing the significant role of nutrients elements, particularly TS, which had the strongest positive effect on community stability (Fig. 5). These findings are consistent with previous reports, indicating that soil properties can indirectly influence phyllosphere microbiomes through plant root-mediated processes54. Some studies have identified soil pH and total phosphorus (TP) as key physicochemical factors influencing the composition of leaf endophytic diazotrophs in forest ecosystems and agricultural bulk soils55,56. However, in our study, TS emerged as a key factor shaping both the community composition and alpha diversity of diazotrophs in K. obovata leaves. Previous research also indicated that sulfur plays a critical role in shaping microbial communities, especially in nutrient-limited environments such as mangrove ecosystems22,55,56. Sulfur, an essential macronutrient, positively affects plant health and microbes in mangrove sediments7,57. Furthermore, studies have shown that elevated sulfur levels combined with anaerobic conditions can restrict nitrogen uptake due to sulfide toxicity and anoxia, which has been observed in several coastal ecosystems58–62. This creates a unique ecological constraint where nitrogen availability in the rhizosphere is often decoupled from plant demand. Sulfur oxidation, coupled with nitrogen fixation, is a key strategy for the survival of salt marsh plants under sulfidic conditions59,63.

Additionally, sulfate reduction is a dominant anaerobic respiration pathway in marine sediments that drives organic matter decomposition, and is closely linked to nitrogen fixation rates in salt marsh ecosystems15,23,59,64. The balance between sulfur and nitrogen cycles is vital for maintaining ecosystem health in mangrove systems65, where these cycles are tightly coupled and influence microbial community structure. As such, TS levels in mangrove soils significantly shape the leaf-associated diazotrophic communities66, suggesting that microbial adaptation to sulfur-nitrogen processes is crucial for ecosystem stability.

Studies have shown that microbial networks characterized by a high number of taxa (nodes) and relatively few highly connected keystone taxa (hubs) are generally considered stable and resilient62. Our results revealed that all groups lacked identifiable network and module hubs. However, the proportion of connector nodes was lowest in the low sulfur (LS) compared to the medium-sulfur (MS) and high sulfur (HS) groups (Figs. 6A, B, and C). These findings suggest that the leaf endophytic diazotrophic community of K. obovata is more stable under low TS conditions, as supported by a robustness analysis indicating enhanced structural stability in LS-associated networks. The LS-associated network exhibited the highest structural stability when subjected to simulated node removal (Fig. 6J). This apparent paradox can be explained by the robustness of decentralized networks: in the absence of central keystone hubs, the network lacks single points of failure, thereby increasing its resilience to species loss or disturbance64. Moreover, TS was negatively correlated with the number of modules but positively correlated with modularity and betweenness centralization (Figs. 6G, H and I), indicating that LS levels promoted a more integrated and centralized diazotrophic network. A well-organized and stable microbial network likely enhances nutrient acquisition from low sulfur soils, promotes leaf development and nitrogen fixation, and has critical functions in the health and productivity of mangrove ecosystems63.

Correlation analyses between the TS concentration and topological parameters of the nifH co-occurrence network revealed in the HS group, increased TS was linked to reduced centralization and cohesion, suggesting that elevated sulfur disrupts network integration (Table S3). In the MS group, TS showed a significant negative correlation with the number of modules (p < 0.01), suggesting that TS variation may destabilize the modular organization. In contrast, within the LS group, increases in TS were positively correlated with both degree centralization (p < 0.001) and clustering coefficient (p < 0.05), suggesting that even minor fluctuations in sulfur levels in low sulfur environments may enhance network centralization and local connectivity. Collectively, these findings underscore that low sulfur conditions are more conducive to microbial community stability in the mangrove leaf endosphere. Therefore, maintaining or restoring low-sulfur environments may be a key strategy for promoting leaf-endophytic diazotrophic communities’ resilience and supporting ecological restoration in mangrove ecosystems. Overall, this study offers novel insights into the diversity and maintenance mechanisms of functional endophytic microbes in halophytic plants, with implications for understanding their ecological roles and adaptive strategies in response to climate change.

Limitations of this study must be acknowledged. We followed standard methods for ecological biogeography studies, which focused on environmental factors such as soil nutrients and plant traits. While plant nutritional status is an important factor that could influence microbial dynamics, it was not included in this study. Future research should incorporate plant nutritional assessments to enhance our understanding of microbial-host interactions. Sulfur levels are identified as a potential main driver in this ecosystem. Therefore, future work should explore sulfur thresholds and assess the role of total sulfur (TS) in regulating the community structure and stability of leaf endophytic diazotrophs, potentially through culture-based methods.

Conclusion

This study provides a large-scale ecological assessment of the structure and assembly mechanisms of leaf endophytic diazotrophs in coastal mangrove ecosystems. The community, dominated by key diazotrophs such as Bradyrhizobium, exhibits nitrogen-fixing potential. The leaf endophytic diazotrophs assembly was driven mainly by stochastic processes, especially dispersal limitations. Total sulfur emerged as a key environmental determinant of shaping community structure. Notably, low-sulfur environments supported more stable diazotrophic interactions, emphasizing the ecological importance of reduced sulfur conditions in maintaining functional and resilient diazotrophic communities. Thus, our study advances in understanding of the ecological drivers of leaf endophytic diazotroph diversity and underscores the influence of soil nutrients on microbiome stability and function in coastal mangrove ecosystems. Given that K. obovata is a foundational species in mangrove restoration and expansion, future research should focus on climate change and environmental factors affecting leaf endophytic diazotrophic community ecosystem function, providing insights for restoration mechanisms facilitating sustainable mangrove management.

Materials and methods

Research area and sample collection

The field survey and sampling process were conducted at 10 coastal mangrove wetland sites in July 2019, encompassing a broad latitudinal (18.44°–24.39°N) and longitudinal range (108.24°–117.92°E) (Fig. S4). Specifically, we established five 5 m × 5 m plots within accessible natural coastal mangrove areas (in the intertidal zone approximately 30–50 m from the shore) at each site, from which five K. obovata trees were chosen following a five-point sampling method. Five fresh leaves from each tree and nearby non-rhizosphere soil were carefully collected in sterile sampling bags for further analysis. We collected 250 individual samples of K. obovata leaves and non-rhizosphere soil from 10 sites in southern China along latitudinal gradients (one site in Zhejiang (YD), two sites in Fujian (FD and FG), two sites in Guangdong (HD and LZ), two sites in Guangxi (SK and ZZ), and three sites in Hainan (BM, DZ, and LD). The freshly collected samples were immediately transferred to the laboratory in refrigerated transportation packaging. Afterward, the leaf surface was cleaned thoroughly with sterile water, freeze-dried, weighed, ground and large debris was removed from the soil samples and thoroughly homogenized. All leaf samples and part soil samples were then stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction was performed. Another two portions of soil were preserved at −20 °C for soil properties and 4 °C for nutrient analysis.

Environmental and biotic parameter analysis

Soil properties, nutrients, climatic data, and host plant traits were analyzed in the laboratory as previously described23. Briefly, soil temperature and pH were determined using a HI98121 pH meter (Hanna, Italy). Additionally, salinity of interstitial sediment water was measured during sampling using a portable salinity tester (PNT3000; TEPS, Germany). Soil texture (sand, silt, and clay) was determined using a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 (Malvern, United Kingdom)65. Soil water extracts were used to measure the SO42– and PO43– levels using ion chromatography (ICS-2100; Dionex, USA). Inorganic nitrogen (NO2–-N, NO3–-N, and NH4+-N) was extracted by shaking in a 2 M KCl solution for 1 h at 200 rpm (soil: solution ratio of 1:4) and measured using a continuous-flow analyzer (SAN + +; Skalar, Breda, the Netherlands). Nutrients (TC, TOC, TN, TS) were analyzed using an elemental analyzer (Vario Macro Cube, Germany). Total inorganic carbon (TIC) was determined by subtracting total organic carbon (TOC) from total carbon (TC). TP was measured using molybdenum blue colorimetry together with flame photometry, as described previously66. Climatic data such (mean annual temperature, mean annual precipitation, MAE, mean annual relative humidity, MAS duration) were obtained from the China Meteorological Data Sharing Service System (http://data.cma.cn/). During the field survey, host plant traits (average height and basal diameter of K. obovata) were assessed within 5 × 5 m plots. TS concentrations were categorized into three groups based on natural breaks in the distribution of TS values across the study samples: low sulfur (LS, 0.01–0.1 g/kg), middle sulfur (MS, 0.1–0.3 g/kg), and high sulfur (HS, >0.3 g/kg) for further data analysis.

High-throughput sequencing

DNA was extracted from mangrove leaves using the GeneJET Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions67. The purity (A260/A280:1.8 ~ 2.0) and concentration (20 ~ 30 ng/μL) of the extracted DNA samples were identified via a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, MA, USA). The nifH gene, responsible for diazotrophic function, was amplified using nifH-F (5ʹ-AAAGGYGGWATCGGYAARTCCACCAC-3ʹ) and nifH-R (5ʹ-TTGTTSGCSGCRTACATSGCCATCAT-3ʹ) primers to investigate the diazotrophic community68. Each PCR reaction mixture consisted of 10 μL SYBR Premix ExTaq (Takara, Dalian, China), 0.2 μL of each primer (20 μM), 1 μL template DNA (20 ~ 30 ng), then diluted with sterile distilled water to 20 μL. PCR amplification of the nifH gene was carried out with denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, 30 annealing cycles at 94 °C for 1 min, 55 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min, followed by elongation at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were then electrophoresed with a 1% agarose gel and recovered using an EZNA Gel Extraction Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). Finally, the purified PCR products were sequenced using a 2 × 250 bp Reagent Kit v2 on an Illumina MiSeq platform.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered by removing mismatched primer sequences, low-quality sequences (quality scores <30 or lengths <275 bp), and barcode sequences. The DADA2 denoising method in QIIME269,70 was used for chimeric filtering, and trimmomatic for quality control. High-quality sequences were merged and denoised into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) without the use of an arbitrary dissimilarity cutoff71. Representative sequences were annotated and taxonomically assigned using both nucleotide sequences and translated amino acid sequences71. Only sequences meeting strict quality standards—including a minimum 275 bp alignment length and verification using local BLASTn searches—were assigned species-level taxonomy. Remaining sequences were classified at the highest taxonomic level supported by confidence criteria as previously described3. ASVs that remained unclassified after this step were excluded from subsequent analyses. The original nifH sequence data were stored in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under BioProject Accession PRJNA1130613.

Data analysis

To compare the community richness and diversity of endosphere diazotrophs between different sampling sites, the alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Simpson, Shannon, and Simpson) were computed using the ‘vegan’ package using R software (version 4.2.0), and the Shannon index was used to indicate the alpha diversity. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and ANOSIM were performed to estimate the beta diversity of diazotrophic communities, which was calculated based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and visualized using the ‘ggplot2’ package72. Linear regression was employed to analyze the variation in the alpha diversity of diazotrophic communities with geographic distance.

To quantify the relative contributions of different environmental variable groups (i.e., soil properties, nutrient elements, climate factors, and host plant traits) to the variation in bacterial α-diversity, variation partitioning analysis (VPA) was conducted. The VPA was performed using the vegan package. The adjusted R² values, which indicate the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by each independent variable group, were visualized as a Venn diagram. Additionally, random forest analysis was employed to assess the importance of nutrient elements, using the random forest package. The “plspm” package was utilized to perform the partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM) analysis.

To evaluate the deterministic/stochastic community assembly processes of the diazotrophic community in the leaf endosphere, the nearest taxon index (NTI) was calculated in each sampling plot in the R ‘picante’ package73. The beta-nearest taxon index (betaNTI) was calculated using 999 randomizations in paired joined plots74. The distribution of betaNTI according to environmental distance was also analyzed to determine the assemblage processes of the diazotrophic communities. To test the assembly mechanisms of the diazotrophic community at different TS ranges, the NTI and betaNTI values were calculated for the paired plots.

To assess the effects of abiotic and biotic factors on diazotroph alpha diversity, linear regressions based on Spearman correlations were conducted between alpha diversity indices and environmental factors. To assess the relationship between diazotrophic community composition and environmental variables, Mantel tests were performed between community composition (Bray-Curtis distance) and environmental data (Euclidean distance) (9,999 permutations) using the vegan R software package.

The co-occurrence network at the ASVs level was constructed with the ‘WGCNA’ package. The Spearman correlation coefficient (r) was calculated to indicate the strength of the correlation between the different taxa. Only correlations with r > 0.7 and p < 0.01 were retained. Network topological parameters were calculated in the ‘graph’ R package75. The Z value (i.e., Zi: within-module connectivity) and P-value (Pi: among-module connectivity) of individual ASVs were calculated to describe the node roles in the ecological network. The threshold values of Zi and Pi were 2.5 and 0.62, respectively. Network nodes were sorted into four subcategories: connectors (Zi<2.5, Pi>0.6), module hubs (Zi>2.5, Pi<0.62), network hubs (Zi>2.5, Pi>0.62), and peripheral nodes (Zi<2.5, Pi<0.62). Keystone taxa were identified as network hubs, module hubs, and connectors76. To discuss the possible impacts of TS on the diazotrophic co-occurrence network, Pearson correlations were calculated between environmental TS and topological parameters. Additionally, Spearman correlations were performed to explore the relationships between network topological parameters and environmental factors. Pearson correlations between the topological parameters of the nifH co-occurrence network and the corresponding sulfur concentrations in different groups were calculated to assess the effects of different soil sulfur contents on the diazotrophic community in K. obovata leaves.

Statistics and reproducibility

R software (version 4.2.0) was used to perform all statistical analyses, supported by the vegan, ggplot2, picante, random forest, and plspm packages. A total of 250 leaf and soil samples were collected as independent biological replicates from different K. obovata trees across 10 mangrove sites, capturing genetic and environmental heterogeneity. Each sample was processed independently for DNA extraction and PCR amplification, with technical replicates included to ensure reproducibility. Alpha diversity indices (Shannon) were calculated using the vegan package. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and visualized through NMDS. Differences in community composition were tested using ANOSIM, PERMANOVA, Mantel/partial Mantel tests. VPA and random forest analyses evaluated the effects of soil properties, nutrients, climate, and host traits on diazotrophic diversity. Community assembly were quantified using the betaNTI with 999 randomizations.

Co-occurrence networks at the ASV level were constructed using the WGCNA package, retaining only strong correlations (r > 0.7, p < 0.01). Network topology metrics and keystone taxa were identified based on Zi and Pi values. Relationships between network characteristics and environmental factors were assessed using Pearson and Spearman correlation analyses. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with additional thresholds indicated as *p < 0.01, **p < 0.001, and ***p < 0.0001.
