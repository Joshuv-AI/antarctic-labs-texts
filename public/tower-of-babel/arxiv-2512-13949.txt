Coherence-Sensitive Readout Models for Quantum Devices: Beyond the Classical Assignment Matrix
Zachariah Malik, Zain H. Saleem

Abstract
Readout error models for noisy quantum devices almost universally assume that measurement noise is classical: the measurement statistics are obtained from the ideal computational-basis populations by a column-stochastic assignment matrix A A . This description is equivalent to assuming that the effective positive-operator-valued measurement (POVM) is diagonal in the measurement basis, and therefore completely insensitive to quantum coherences. We relax this assumption and derive a fully general expression for the observed measurement probabilities under arbitrary completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) noise preceding a computational-basis measurement. Writing the ideal post-circuit stat ρ ~ \tilde{\rho} in terms of its populations x x and coherences y y , we show that the observed probability vector z z satisfies z = A ​ x + C ​ y z=Ax+Cy , where A A is the familiar classical assignment matrix and C C is a coherence-response matrix constructed from the off-diagonal matrix elements of the effective POVM in the computational basis. The classical model z = A ​ x z=Ax arises if and only if all POVM elements are diagonal; in this sense C C quantifies accessible information about coherent readout distortions and interference between computational-basis states, all of which are invisible to models that retain only A A . This work therefore provides a natural, fully general framework for coherence-sensitive readout modeling on current and future quantum devices.

Let
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the vectors of ideal and noisy outcome probabilities, respectively, of a quantum device of
n
qubits, with
N:=2^{n}
, measured in the computational basis. The literature often assumes that there exists a
classical assignment matrix

A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
that maps the
ideal
computational-basis populations of the post-circuit state to the
observed
outcome frequencies, that is,

z=Ax.

(1)

This picture underlies a wide range of readout calibration and error-mitigation techniques, as seen in
18
,
27
,
22
,
11
,
41
and
20
, among others.

However, Eq. (
1
) hides a strong physical assumption: it implicitly assumes that the effective measurement is described by POVM elements that are
diagonal
in the computational basis. Under this assumption, measurement outcomes depend only on the diagonal entries of the ideal density matrix
\tilde{\rho}
, i.e., on classical populations, and are completely insensitive to quantum coherences. In realistic devices, the measurement process is preceded by nontrivial dynamics, including coherent couplings, residual rotations, and possibly correlated noise. There is no reason, in general, for the effective POVM to be diagonal.

In this work, we revisit the assignment matrix model from first principles. Starting from an arbitrary completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) channel acting before a computational-basis measurement, we derive an exact expression for the observed probabilities in terms of the populations and coherences of the ideal post-circuit state. The result takes the form

z=Ax+Cy,

(2)

where,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N(N-1)}
is a vector of coherence parameters (which is formed from the off-diagonal elements of
\tilde{\rho}
) and
C
encapsulates the action these coherences have on the state population.

As would be expected, there is a natural link between the
A
and
C
matrices, and the superoperator matrix representation of the quantum error channel, denoted by
H\in\mathbb{C}^{4^{n}\times 4^{n}}
. Quantum process tomography (QPT) refers to the class of techniques used to reconstruct
H
from measurement data. Therefore, the elements of
A
and
C
can be found via QPT. There has been much work in QPT, for example

,
15
,
39
,
51

N^{2}\times N^{2}
, while the matrices
A
and
C
have sizes
N\times N
and
N\times N(N-1)
, respectively. As such, the computationl burden of finding
A
and
C
is far smaller than that of finding
H
. This cost reduction, of course, comes at the cost of forming the underdetermined system (
2

The standard assignment matrix model is recovered if and only if

II
General framework and derivation

II.1
Setup

We consider a generic circuit on
n
qubits, represented by a unitary
U
acting on an initial state
\rho
. We denote the Hilbert space spanned by the qubits as
\mathcal{H}\sim\mathbb{C}^{N}
, where
N=2^{n}
. The ideal post-circuit state is

\tilde{\rho}=U\rho U^{\dagger}.

(3)

In practice, the state is subject to noise before measurement. We describe this noise by a completely positive, trace preserving (CPTP) channel

\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}),

where
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the space of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. See
(
43
, p. 361)
for a discussion on CPTP error channels. By Kraus’ theorem
(
43
, Theorem 8.3)
, there exist operators
\{E_{a}\}
(which are called Kraus operators) such that

\mathcal{E}(\cdot)=\sum_{a}E_{a}(\cdot)E_{a}^{\dagger},\quad\sum_{a}E_{a}E_{a}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{1}.

(4)

The actual,
noisy
, state prior to measurement is

\rho^{\prime}=\mathcal{E}(\tilde{\rho}).

(5)

We assume that the projective measurement at the hardware level is in the computational basis
\{\ket{k}\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
. The probability of observing outcome
k
is then

z_{k}=\Pr(\text{outcome }k)=\Tr(\ket{k}\bra{k}\rho')\bra{k}\rho^{\prime}.

(6)

It is convenient to “push” the noise channel onto the measurement operators and work in the Heisenberg picture. Recall that a positive operator-valued measure on
\mathcal{H}
is a collection of positive semi-definite operators
\{P_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\sum_{m}P_{m}=\mathbb{1}
(see, e.g.,
(
43
, Section 2.2.6)
for an high-level discussion on POVMs, the reader may also be interested in
13
or
7
for a more rigorous, probabilistic interpretation of POVMs). For example, the measures
\{P_{m}\}
defined by
P_{m}:=E_{m}E_{m}^{\dagger}
form a POVM.

We shall define a POVM to help characterize the error channel, let

F_{k}:=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\ket{k}\bra{k})=\sum_{a}E_{a}^{\dagger}\ket{k}\bra{k}E_{a}.

(7)

Each
F_{k}
is a positive semi-definite operator (as it is a sum of positive semi-definite operators), and the completeness relation

\sum_{k}\ket{k}\bra{k}=\mathbb{1}
together with trace preservation of
\mathcal{E}
implies

\sum_{k}F_{k}=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\mathbb{1})=\mathbb{1}.

(8)

Hence,
\{F_{k}\}
is a POVM. We can then write

\displaystyle z_{k}

\displaystyle=\Tr(|k \rangle\langle k | \rho')

(9)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(|k\rangle\langle k|\sum_{a}E_{a}^{\dagger}\tilde{\rho}E_{a}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\Tr(|k \rangle\langle k | E_{a} \tilde{\rho}E_{a})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\Tr(E_{a} |k \rangle\langle k | E_{a} \tilde{\rho})

\displaystyle=\Tr(F_k \tilde{\rho}),

where the third to fourth equality was obtained by the cyclic property of the trace operation. Indeed, we see that we may use
\{F_{k}\}
to define a discrete probability measure
\Tr(F_{\cdot} \tilde{\rho})
. Now, we shall expand both
F_{k}
and
\tilde{\rho}
in a convenient operator basis and obtain some more insight into the mapping
\tilde{\rho}\rightarrow\rho^{\prime}
.

II.2
Recovering the vector form

We fix the computational basis
\{\ket{\ell}\}
. By definition of
x
, we have
x_{l}=\langle\ell|\tilde{\rho}|\ell\rangle
. Let us denote the off-diagonal elements by

\displaystyle c_{lr}:=\langle\ell|\tilde{\rho}|r\rangle.

(10)

By hermiticity of
\tilde{\rho}
(i.e.,
\tilde{\rho}^{\dagger}=\tilde{\rho}
), we see that
c_{\ell r}=c_{r\ell}^{*}
. Let
c^{R}_{\ell r},c^{I}_{\ell r}
denote the real and imaginary parts of
c_{\ell r}
, respectively. We may accordingly expand the density matrix
\tilde{\rho}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}x_{\ell}\ket{\ell}\bra{\ell}+\sum_{\ell<r}\Big[c^{(R)}_{\ell r}(\ket{\ell}\bra{r}+\ket{r}\bra{\ell})

(11)

\displaystyle+c^{(I)}_{\ell r}i(\ket{\ell}\bra{r}-\ket{r}\bra{\ell})\Big].

The terms in Eq. (
11
) are arranged so that all coefficients are real. We may view (
11
) as a decomposition of
\tilde{\rho}
into the operator basis
\{|\ell\rangle\langle r|\}_{\ell,r}
. A straightforward calculation shows that this basis is orthonormal with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product

\langle B,D\rangle_{HS}=\Tr(B^{\dagger} D).

We expand each POVM element
F_{k}
in the same operator basis:

\displaystyle F_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\alpha_{k\ell}\ket{\ell}\bra{\ell}+\sum_{\ell<r}\Big[\beta^{(R)}_{k,\ell r}(\ket{\ell}\bra{r}+\ket{r}\bra{\ell})

(12)

\displaystyle+\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}i(\ket{\ell}\bra{r}-\ket{r}\bra{\ell})\Big].

The real coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{k\ell}

\displaystyle=\braket{\ell|F_{k}|\ell}\geq 0,

(13)

\displaystyle\beta^{(R)}_{k,\ell r}

\displaystyle=\real\braket{r|F_{k}|\ell},

(14)

\displaystyle\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}

\displaystyle=\imaginary\braket{r|F_{k}|\ell}.

(15)

We let
\beta_{k,\ell r}=\langle r|F_{k}|\ell\rangle
, so
\beta_{k,\ell r}=\beta^{(R)}_{k\ell r}+i\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}
. The positivity of
F_{k}
ensures that these coefficients are not arbitrary, but we will not need their detailed constraints here.

The POVM completeness condition
\sum_{k}F_{k}=\mathbb{1}
implies that

\sum_{k}\alpha_{k\ell}=\sum_{k}\langle\ell|F_{k}|\ell\rangle=1,\quad\forall\ell,

(16)

and

\sum_{k}\beta_{k,\ell r}=\sum_{k}\langle\ell|F_{k}|r\rangle=0,\quad\forall\ell<r,

(17)

which implies that

\sum_{k}\beta^{(R)}_{k,\ell r}=0,\quad\sum_{k}\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}=0,\quad\forall\ell<r.

(18)

To compute
z_{k}=\text{Tr}(F_{k}\tilde{\rho})
, we can rely on the linearity of the trace and the orthogonality of the basis operators under the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. A straightforward, but tedious computation given in Appendix
A
shows that

z_{k}=\sum_{\ell}\alpha_{k\ell}x_{\ell}+2\sum_{\ell<r}\left(\beta^{(R)}_{k,\ell r}c^{(R)}_{\ell r}+\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}c^{(I)}_{\ell r}\right).

(19)

This is the central relation at the level of components.

We now collect terms into a compact vector equation. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the population vector with entries
x_{\ell}
. We shall gather
y\in\mathbb{R}^{N(N-1)}
in a standard lexicographical stacking of the
c_{\ell r}
terms

y:=\begin{bmatrix}c_{0,1}^{(R)}&c_{0,1}^{(I)}&\cdots&c_{N-2,N-1}^{(R)}&c_{N-2,N-1}^{(I)}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(20)

We define the
k
-th row of
C\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N(N-1)}
accordingly

[C]_{k}:=\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{k,0,1}^{(R)}&\beta_{k,0,1}^{(I)}&\cdots&\beta_{k,N-2,N-1}^{(R)}&\beta_{k,N-2,N-1}^{(I)}\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

Then, (
19
) is equivalent to

z=Ax+Cy,

(22)

which is exactly (
2
).

II.3
An alternative characterization

We can also represent the quantum error channel via an
operator-valued
kernel
K:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
defined by

K(s,t):=\sum_{a}E_{a}|s\rangle\langle t|E_{a}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{E}(|s\rangle\langle t|).

(23)

Intuitively, the operator-valued kernel describes how the
ideal
(albeit potentially non-physical) state
|\ell\rangle\langle r|
is mapped under the noise channel. The hope is that we may eventually utilize the rich literature of operator-valued kernels, e.g.,
29
and
28
to bring more insight into our problem.

We first deduce an equivalent condition that results in the assignment matrix relationship (
1
). By our decomposition

\tilde{\rho}=\sum_{\ell}x_{\ell}|\ell\rangle\langle\ell|+\sum_{\ell\neq r}c_{\ell r}|\ell\rangle\langle r|

(24)

introduced in section
II.2
, and starting with (
9
), we see that

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(F_{k}\tilde{\rho}_{k})

(25)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left(\sum_{\ell}x_{\ell}F_{k}|\ell\rangle\langle\ell|+\sum_{\ell\neq r}c_{\ell r}F_{k}|\ell\rangle\langle r|\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}x_{\ell}\sum_{a}\text{Tr}(E_{a}^{\dagger}|k\rangle\langle k|E_{a}|\ell\rangle\langle\ell|)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell\neq r}c_{\ell r}\sum_{a}\text{Tr}(E_{a}^{\dagger}|k\rangle\langle k|E_{a}|\ell\rangle\langle r|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}x_{\ell}\text{Tr}(K(\ell,\ell)|k\rangle\langle k|)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell\neq r}c_{\ell r}\text{Tr}(K(\ell,r)|k\rangle\langle k|).

Then, we see that

z_{k}=\sum_{\ell}x_{\ell}\langle k|K(\ell,\ell)|k\rangle+\sum_{\ell\neq r}c_{\ell,r}\langle k|K(\ell,r)|k\rangle.

(26)

In this case, we recover
Ax=z
if and only if

\operatorname{Tr}[|k\rangle\langle k|K(\ell,r)]=0,\;\text{for all}\;\ell\neq r

(27)

III
The coherence-response matrix and its physical meaning

The new ingredient in Eq. (
2
) is the matrix
C
, which we now discuss in more detail. By construction,
C
is defined so that

(Cy)_{k}=\sum_{\ell<r}\bigg[2\beta^{(R)}_{k,\ell r}c^{(R)}_{\ell r}-2\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}c^{(I)}_{\ell r}\bigg].

(28)

In other words, each row of
C
contains the coefficients
\beta^{(R)}_{k,\ell r}
and
\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}
that specify how the coherences of the state enter into the probability of outcome
k
.

It follows immediately that
C=0
if and only if

\beta^{(R)}_{k,\ell r}=\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}=0

(29)

for all
k
and all
\ell<r
. In turn, this is equivalent to requiring that all POVM elements
F_{k}
are diagonal in the computational basis:

\braket{r|F_{k}|\ell}=0,\quad\forall k,\forall\ell\neq r.

(30)

Thus the condition
C=0
is
precisely
the condition that the measurement be insensitive to coherences of
\tilde{\rho}
in the computational basis. By (
27
), we can see that asking for
diagonal
POVM elements is equivalent to asking for operator-valued kernels
K(\ell,r)
which have
zero
diagonal for any
\ell\neq r
.

The matrix
A
defined above coincides with the usual assignment matrix familiar from readout calibration. When
C=0
, its entries have a simple interpretation:
A_{k\ell}
is the probability of obtaining outcome
k
when the system is prepared in the computational basis state
\ket{\ell}
. When
C\neq 0
, then
A
quantifies the ‘classical’ effects, rather than the coherences.

III.1
Dependence on coherences

Whenever
C\neq 0
, the measured probabilities
z_{k}
depend on the off-diagonal elements of
\tilde{\rho}
. This dependence is linear in the real and imaginary parts
c^{(R)}_{\ell r}
and
c^{(I)}_{\ell r}
, with coefficients determined by the off-diagonal matrix elements of
F_{k}
.

Physically, this means that the measurement is sensitive to the
phase
information encoded in superpositions of computational-basis states. For a given pair
(\ell,r)
, the relative weight of
c^{(R)}_{\ell r}
and
c^{(I)}_{\ell r}
in
(Cy)_{k}
indicates whether the probability of outcome
k
depends more strongly on cosine-like or sine-like components of the relative phase.

III.2
Interpretation as a “non-classicality” measure

From the perspective of readout modeling,
A
captures the purely classical confusion between basis states, while
C
captures all coherent contributions. One can therefore regard
C
(or appropriate norms of
C
) as a quantitative measure of how far the effective measurement is from an ideal classical assignment. In the extreme case
C=0
, the measurement is entirely classical in the computational basis; all deviations from that scenario are encoded in
C
.

In multi-qubit devices, the pattern of nonzero entries in
C
can also reveal structure: which pairs of basis states interfere in which outcomes, whether the measurement couples primarily states that differ by single-qubit flips or more complex patterns, and whether there are signatures of correlated or entangling dynamics in the readout.

IV
Examples

We now illustrate the decomposition for some representative noise channels.

IV.1
Pure dephasing

Consider pure dephasing in the computational basis. For a single qubit, the channel can be written as

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{00}&\lambda\rho_{01}\\
\lambda\rho_{10}&\rho_{11}\end{pmatrix},

(31)

with
|\lambda|\leq 1
. In the Heisenberg picture, the POVM elements are unchanged:

F_{0}=\ket{0}\bra{0},\qquad F_{1}=\ket{1}\bra{1}.

(32)

These are diagonal in the computational basis, so
\beta^{(R)}_{k,\ell r}=\beta^{(I)}_{k,\ell r}=0
and
C=0
. The assignment matrix is
A=\mathbb{1}
. The measurement is insensitive to coherences, as expected for pure dephasing in the measurement basis.

IV.2
Amplitude damping

For single-qubit amplitude damping with parameter
\gamma\in[0,1]
, an operator-sum representation is

E_{0}=\ket{0}\bra{0}+\sqrt{1-\gamma}\ket{1}\bra{1},\qquad E_{1}=\sqrt{\gamma}\ket{0}\bra{1}.

(33)

One finds

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\ket{0}\bra{0})=\ket{0}\bra{0}+\gamma\ket{1}\bra{1},

(34)

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(\ket{1}\bra{1})=(1-\gamma)\ket{1}\bra{1}.

(35)

Both
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
are diagonal. Therefore
C=0
, and

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\
0&1-\gamma\end{pmatrix}.

(36)

Even though amplitude damping is non-Pauli and changes populations, it leaves the POVM diagonal in the computational basis, so measurement remains classical in this sense.

IV.3
Coherent over-rotation before measurement

Now consider a coherent unitary rotation immediately before measurement. For a single qubit, let

U=R_{y}(\theta)=e^{-i\theta Y/2},

(37)

and define a noise channel
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger}
. Then

F_{k}=U^{\dagger}\ket{k}\bra{k}U.

(38)

An explicit calculation yields

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}(\theta/2)&\tfrac{1}{2}\sin\theta\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\sin\theta&\sin^{2}(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix},

(39)

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sin^{2}(\theta/2)&-\tfrac{1}{2}\sin\theta\\
-\tfrac{1}{2}\sin\theta&\cos^{2}(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

The off-diagonal entries are nonzero for
\theta\neq 0
. For a general state

\tilde{\rho}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&c_{01}\\
c_{01}^{*}&x_{1}\end{pmatrix},

(41)

with
c_{01}=c^{(R)}_{01}+ic^{(I)}_{01}
, the probabilities become

\displaystyle z_{0}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})x_{0}+\sin^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})x_{1}+\sin\theta\,c^{(R)}_{01},

(42)

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})x_{0}+\cos^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})x_{1}-\sin\theta\,c^{(R)}_{01}.

(43)

Comparing with Eq. (
2
), we see that

A=\begin{pmatrix}\cos^{2}(\theta/2)&\sin^{2}(\theta/2)\\
\sin^{2}(\theta/2)&\cos^{2}(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix},

(44)

and

C=\begin{pmatrix}\sin\theta&0\\
-\sin\theta&0\end{pmatrix}.

(45)

Thus,
C\neq 0
whenever
\theta\neq 0
.

Physically, this example shows that even a simple coherent misalignment of the measurement axis leads to
C\neq 0
: the measurement probabilities become explicitly sensitive to the real part of the coherence between
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
. If one were to ignore
C
and attempt to model this situation with a classical assignment matrix alone, the resulting model would necessarily be incomplete, because it would predict
z
as a function of
x
only and would miss the dependence on
c^{(R)}_{01}
.

V
Implications for readout modeling

We have shown that, in full generality, the measurement statistics under arbitrary CPTP noise preceding a computational-basis measurement obey the linear relation

z=Ax+Cy,

(46)

where
A
is a column-stochastic assignment matrix acting on the populations
x
, and
C
is a coherence-response matrix acting on the vector of coherences
y
. The classical model
z=Ax
corresponds to the special case
C=0
, i.e., to effective POVM elements that are diagonal in the computational basis.

From a practical perspective, this decomposition highlights a fundamental limitation of models that retain only
A
. Such models can accurately describe pure classical confusion and a subset of noise channels (such as pure dephasing and amplitude damping in the measurement basis), but they cannot capture readout effects that arise from coherent dynamics in the measurement chain. Any pre-measurement unitary rotation, coherent cross-talk, or entangling interaction that generates off-diagonal entries in
F_{k}
will necessarily give
C\neq 0
and hence introduce coherence sensitivity into the measurement.

The matrix
C
itself contains rich information about the device. Its entries encode which pairs of computational-basis states interfere in which measurement outcomes, with what signs and magnitudes, and whether the measurement is more sensitive to the real or imaginary parts of specific coherences. In multi-qubit settings, patterns in
C
may reveal locality structure, correlated errors, or signatures of nontrivial Hamiltonian dynamics during readout.

These observations suggest that a more faithful description of real-device readout should, when appropriate, include both
A
and
C
. In the rest of this section, we shall discuss the computational complexity of obtaining these matrices, solving (
2
), and their comparison to QPT.

V.1
Forming the Systems

In this subsection, we compare the cost of obtaining the elements in
A
and
C
as compared to the full superoperator matrix, i.e., the matrix which solves

\text{vec}(\rho^{\prime})=H\text{vec}(\tilde{\rho}),

(47)

where
\text{vec}(D)
denotes the typical column-stacked vectorization of matrix
D
. In order to find
\tilde{\rho}
, we need the noisy density matrix
\rho^{\prime}
and the superoperator matrix
H
. To find the ideal output distribution
x
, we will need the noisy distribution
z
and the matrices
A
and
C
. We shall compare the cost of obtaining these relevant ingredients.

First, let us compare the cost of obtaining
\rho^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
against
z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The process of obtaining the former is called
quantum state tomography
. This has been extensively studied in the literature, and we briefly review their techniques and costs. For a system of
n
qubits and
n
detectors, one can directly measure
\rho^{\prime}
using at least
4^{n}-1
total measurements, as described in
(
1
, Section 2)
. Beyond the simple measurement techniques outlined in
1
, there is a whole plethora of methods available to estimating
\rho^{\prime}
, each with their own advantages and disadvantages. Some of these methods use linear inversion
52
,
(
42
, Section 2.2.1)
, maximum likelihood estimation
47
, least squares estimation
34
,
19
, and even some machine-learning based approaches
26
,
53
. Regardless of the method we use, one must still obtain
N^{2}
elements to find
\rho^{\prime}
. In contrast,
z
has only
N
entries and can be easily estimated via Monte-Carlo simulations and solely measuring in the computational basis.

Next, let us compare the matrices involved.
H
is an
N^{2}\times N^{2}
matrix while
A
is
N\times N
and
C
is
N\times N(N-1)
. The act of finding
H
(or equivalently, finding the Kraus operators
E_{a}
in (
4
)) is called
quantum process tomography
(QPT). This process involves preparing multiple different ideal states
\tilde{\rho}_{j}
, using state tomography to find the associated noisy states
\rho^{\prime}_{j}
, and then assembling these together to infer the noise channel
\mathcal{E}
(and therefore find
H
). This technique is outlined in, e.g.,
31
,
(
21
, Section 3.2)
,
(
42
, Section 2.2.2)
. Regardless,
N^{4}=16^{n}
elements are required. The matrices
A
and
C
have
N^{3}=8^{n}
elements to be determined. In other words, finding the
H
matrix is a problem that is
2^{n}
times bigger than finding
A
and
C
.

V.2
Solving the Systems

Once the matrices
\rho^{\prime}
and
H
are determined, there is still the problem of solving (
47

VI
Conclusions

We have given a detailed derivation and analysis of the decomposition

z=Ax+Cy

(50)

for computational-basis measurements under arbitrary CPTP noise. The familiar assignment matrix
A
captures the effects of classical confusion between basis states, while the coherence-response matrix
C
captures the contributions arising from off-diagonal components of the effective POVM. The condition
C=0
is equivalent to asking that the POVM
\{F_{k}\}
defined by (
7
) is diagonal in the computational basis. As such, the widely used classical readout model
z=Ax
(as used in
41
,
20
,
18
,
5
, among others) is valid if and only if the measurement is entirely insensitive to coherences of the state in that basis.

By working through explicit examples, we have shown that
C
vanishes for pure dephasing and amplitude damping in the measurement basis, but is nonzero for coherent over-rotations, illustrating how even simple coherent distortions generate coherence-sensitive readout. Indeed, a Pauli-twirled circuit (i.e., the process described in
17
) ideally has a purely Pauli noise channel, and therefore the associated POVM is diagonal in the computational basis. In general, however, we show that
C
contains valuable information about coherent aspects of the measurement process—including hidden basis misalignment and interference between computational-basis states—that is completely invisible in models that retain only
A
.

Of course, one may use the full Liouville superoperator representation of the noise channel (
47
), rather than (
2
), to solve for the ideal state
\tilde{\rho}
. This effectively amounts to resorting to QPT for error characterization, which has been extensively explored; e.g.,
39
,
21
,
1
,
6
,
15
, among others. However, our system (
2
) is of dimensionality
N\times N^{2}
, rather than
N^{2}\times N^{2}
. As
N=2^{n}
, our system is thus exponentially smaller than (
47
), and therefore will be far cheaper to solve.

This, of course, comes at the trade-off of working with a vastly underdetermined system. For future work, we propose to focus on solving a problem of the form (
49
), wherein we minimize a suitable norm while maintaining the physics of the problem. Both the classical results in compressed sensing e.g., those reviewed in
24
and
40
, as well as recent results with machine-learning techniques, such as
25
and
3
, have been successful in solving underdetermined systems, so we expect to find similar success with our problem too.

This framework provides a conceptually simple and mathematically complete foundation for coherence-sensitive readout modeling on quantum devices. We invite future work on the experimental estimation of
C
, on solving (
48
) and (
49
), and on the incorporation of coherence sensitivity into device characterization and error mitigation protocols.
