Schottky-gated transistors using chitosan extracted from marine crab
Authors: Mikio Fukuhara, Tomonori Yokotsuka, Nobuyuki Kobayashi, Masahiko Kumadaki, Nobuhisa Fujima, Toshiyuki Hashida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Amorphous chitosan nanoparticles (AChNPs) are extracted from marine crab shells for use as the active semiconductor material in paper-based electronics. The I–V characteristics of AChNPs exhibit an electron-driven rectifying behaviour at Schottky junctions. Additionally, when used in metal–semiconductor field-effect transistors (MESFETs), the AChNPs demonstrate an amplification output spanning three orders of magnitude and current modulation with an on/off current ratio of approximately 17,000. Hall effect measurements indicate an n-type semiconductor with a carrier concentration of 8.55 × 1010/cm3, which corresponds to a mobility of 15.27 cm2/Vs and an electric resistivity of 4.79 × 106 Ωcm at 298 K. The g value of the electron spin resonance (ESR) peak of AChNPs confirms that the electrons are generated from the aminyl N*-H radical, and photoemission yield spectroscopy reveals a gap energy of 5.21 eV. The proposed diode and MESFET offer substantial promise for future applications of flexible, renewable, and biodegradable paper-based electronic devices with energy storage properties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32796-z.

Introduction

Bioelectronics encompasses new electronic devices and technologies that closely interact with biological systems; the field is advancing rapidly, with a wide range of bioelectronic devices being developed for application in diverse fields, including physics, chemistry, biology, medicine, and pharmacy1,2. Biodegradable chitin is the second most abundant natural compound after cellulose3. Chitosan, derived from chitin, is a mucopolysaccharide containing an amino group at the C-2 position and two hydroxyl groups at the C-3 and C-6 positions. Chitosan has been used in limited medical applications such as wound dressings4, composite biodegradable films5, electrochemical applications such as Zn-coordinated chitosan electrodes for Zn batteries6, and exchange membranes for polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cells7. However, limited research has been conducted on the physical and electrical properties of chitosan nanofibres8–10. We recently reported the semiconducting11,12 and charge-storing13 properties of amorphous chitosan nonofibres (AChNFs), building on our earlier work on supercapacitors14,15 and semiconductors16 fabricated from amorphous cellulose nanofibres (ACFs). In the present study, aiming to develop amorphous chitosan nanoparticle (AChNP)-based transistors, we investigated Schottky gated metal–semiconductor field-effect transistors (MESFETs) utilising AChNPs. The successful fabrication of transistors from natural marine products could enable the development of paper-based electronics with potential applications in portable electronics, transportation, and biocompatible sensing. At first glance, this device appears similar to a chitosan-gated organic field-effect transistor (OFET)17,18, which has a structure combining ionically and electronically conductive polymers, but it is fundamentally different as it does not use ionic conduction.

The I–V characteristics of the Schottky diode were measured at 298 K under direct current (DC) voltage control, from − 50 to + 150 V, using an AChNP sample with approximately 9 wt% bound water and a thickness of 16 μm (Fig. 1a). The I–V curve exhibited clear forward rectification behaviour characteristic of a semiconductor based on electronic conduction. The inset of Fig. 1(a) displays a logarithmic I–V plot, showing a knee voltage (Vk) of 0.36 V. Electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements, the only method for directly observing radicals in organic materials, were performed at 295 K to investigate the origin of the electrons. The ESR spectrum (Fig. 1b) shows a singlet symmetrical signal for chitosan, which indicates the contribution of the unpaired electron in chitosan11,19 and not that of the proton in protonatable polyelectrolyte17,18. The gvalue of the central peak, a key parameter for identifying organic radical species, was 2.0055, consistent with the value of the aminyl radical N*-H group in amorphous chitosan, as reported by Saiki et al.20. and Gryczka et al.21. Hall coefficient measurements revealed an electron mobility of 15. 27 cm2/Vs, carrier density of − 8.55 × 1010 cm−3 and an electric resistivity of 4.78 × 106 Ωcm at 298 K. The Hall coefficient results, measurement geometry, and Hall voltage versus magnetic field plots are shown in Fig. S1 and Fig. S5 of the Supplementary Information (SI). These results clearly indicate that the AChNP active medium behaves as an n-type semiconductor with electronic conductivity, indicating that it is not a proton-capable polyelectrolyte with ammonium groups. In contrast, the most conducting polymers used in OECTs are typically processed through mainstream wet–spinning methods22. The electron mobility is two orders of magnitude higher than that reported for amorphous Si (0.5–1.0 cm2/Vs23) and π-conjugated organic semiconductors (0.08–2.5 cm2/Vs24,25). However, Farka et al.26 reported a relative high value of 40.8 cm2/Vs in p-doped polyethylene-(3,4-dioxythiophene) (PEDOT: sulfate). Using ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) or Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) for measuring the energy gap of AChNPs was difficult due to the large residual capacitance. The energy gap values, measured using an atmospheric photoelectron absorption spectrometer are shown in Fig. 1(d); the gap energy of ChNPs is 5.21 eV. The large power storage capacity of AChNPs is described in S11 of the SI. Note that the withstanding voltage of this sample was 0.46 MV/cm.

Determining structural morphology and surface characteristics by TEM and AFM

In this section, we analyse the structural morphologies and surface characteristics of the AChNP specimens. The wide-field X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Fig. 2a) demonstrates that the specimen comprises an amorphous chitosan phase, characterised by four broad peaks at approximately 8˚, 11˚, 18˚, and 23 ˚27. Figure 2b shows an atomic force microscopy (AFM) image of the surface structure, comprising an average particle size of approximately 7 nm, shown at Fig. S4. In the bright-field TEM image obtained at 120 keV (Fig. 2c) chitosan bundles appear as glucosamine tissue particles with a diameter of 7 nm. The inset of Fig. 2c shows the selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern acquired from the red circular area, indicating the presence of hollow Debye rings. This observation is attributed to the dissolution of chitosan in an acetic acid solution, which separates the molecular chains into individual nanoparticles upon the removal of water. The electrical conductivity of chitosan as a function of water content was evaluated by measuring the amount of water adsorbed by a dry chitosan film over a certain humidification period. The water adsorption curve for the adsorption curve for the chitosan film (Fig. 2d) demonstrates that the water content increases with humidification time and saturates at approximately 9 wt% as bound water12,15. Correspondingly, the electric resistivity decreases parabolically with increasing water content (inset of Fig. 2d).

Complex evaluation of I–V characteristics

To non-destructively analyse the electronic contribution of the sample, AC impedance measurements were performed over the range of 1 mHz to 1 MHz. Figure 3a presents the complex-plane Nyquist plot of the impedance data. The impedance variation with frequency of AChNPs forms a curve that is rate-determined by a mixture of polarisation resistance processes at high frequencies and charge transfer processes at low frequencies. This behaviour suggests that the tissue consists of particles derived from the exoskeleton of crustaceans and cell walls of fungi12. Figure 3(b) shows the frequency dependence (Bode diagram) of the real and imaginary impedance components. We observed a rapid increase in the real impedance from approximately 100 mHz, and a single peak at approximately 7 mHz in the imaginary impedance. This peak is likely due to dielectric dispersion caused by interfacial polarisation Cb28, corresponding to a relaxation time of 0.023 s, calculated using RC = 1/(2πfmax), where fmax (= 6.82 mHz) is the peak frequency in Fig. 3(a). Figure 3(c) depicts a composite equivalent circuit comprising a parallel R1C1 branch, representing the fungal cell wall and crustacean exoskeleton, and a parallel R2C2 branch, corresponding to glucosamine tissue and electrodes. The R1C1 branch exhibits low resistance and relatively high capacitance, associated with power storage characteristics, whereas the R2C2 branch has high resistance and low capacitance, corresponding to semiconductor characteristics.

In the phase angle diagram shown in Fig. 3(d), the low-frequency capacitance behaviour resembles that of a parallel RC circuit. As the frequency decreases, the series capacitance (Cs) decreases to zero, whereas the parallel capacitance (Cp) increases sharply. Cs plays a critical role in determining both the DC I–V characteristics and energy storage capacity of the sample. The relationship between the time constant (RC) and frequency is illustrated in Fig. 3(e). On a logarithmic scale, both RCs and RCp increased almost linearly with decreasing frequency, their values are 542 and 21 s, respectively, at 1 mHz. For practical applications, longer time durations, ranging from 0.1 s to several hours are required.

Electronic behaviours of Chitosan semiconductor MESFETs

The chitosan (with bound water of 6.1 wt%) semiconductor MESFET was fabricated using a gold gate, an aluminium source, and drain electrodes. Details are shown in Fig. S12 in SI. The work function (5.21 eV) of chitosan lies between that of Au (5.10–5.4725 eV)29 and Al (4.06–4.2625 eV)29; hence, it forms a Schottky junction. Figure 4(a) presents a logarithmic plot of the Ids/mm–Vds output characteristics (drain current vs. drain voltage), showing a clear transition from the linear region to the saturation region with increasing Vds at negative gate voltages. Ids/mm was calculated using a gate length of 0.5 mm which is the source-drain distance in Fig. S12(e). The device operates as an n-channel ‘normally on’ (depletion mode) MESFET, exhibiting amplification characteristics. When a positive gate is applied, the Schottky junction becomes forward-biased, as shown in the logarithmic plot in Fig. 4(b). This leads to a large current flow characteristic of enhancement-mode (‘normally-off’) operation. In case of electron-transport OECTs30, the drain current increases when a negative gate voltage is applied and decreases when a positive gate voltage is applied. Therefore, the results in Figs.(a) and (b) prove that AChNPs are clearly electron-driven, and not ion-driven. Figure 4(c) shows the logarithmic I–V transfer characteristics, which correspond to the negligible leakage current Igs from the gate to the drain in Fig. S8(b) of SI. This result indicates that the source–drain current is modulated by the size of the depletion layer, which is created by the gate voltage acting, as a voltage-controlled resistor. In the DC characteristics of FETs, mutual conductance (gm; defined as the change in drain current divided by that in gate voltage) serves as a performance metric for amplification. To improve gm, the drift velocity of electrons passing through the channel region under the gate electrode (Table 1) as well as the gate-channel capacitance must be increased. As the Cs is relatively large in this study (Fig. 3(c)), the amplification effect increases with increasing positive drain voltage (Fig. 4(c). Therefore, the significant threshold voltage shift observed at different drain voltages may attributed to the charge trapping by ChNPs at the gold electrode interface.

The on/off ratio is a crucial parameter in non-volatile memory applications for power electronics with higher energy gaps and breakdown electric fields31. Therefore, the dependence of Ids on Vgs was measured at Vds = 0 V, and the on/off ratio was calculated (Fig. 4(d)). A ratio of 17,545 at Vgs = 6.5 V was obtained, which is relatively high compared with ferroelectric polymer semiconductor devices32. The threshold voltage Vth, is defined as the minimum gate voltage at which the drain current flows. However, because the on/off ratio shown in Fig. 4(d) represents only the static current modulation, further investigations on the frequency-characteristic-induced dynamic characteristics are required.

Calculation of drift current and switching time

We calculated the drift current and switching time of the chitosan semiconductor MESFET and evaluated its potential for electronic device applications. GaAs and GaN, both used in MESFETs, are being extensively studied for potential use in power electronic applications because of their wide bandgap and electric field breakdown values33,34. The on/off switching speed (1/τ in s−1) of the MESFET was determined by the time (τ = W/Vd, where Vd is the drift speed) required by electrons to travel directly under a gate of length W. A shorter gate length and higher drift speed result in higher switching speed. At low voltages, Vd = μeE and τ = W/μeE = W2/μeVds34. When Vds = 1 V, using electron mobility µe = 15.27 cm2/Vs and W = 0.5 mm, we obtained Vd = 305.4 cm/s and τ = 163.7 For a device with W = 1 μm created using a nano-electromechanical system (NEMS) methods, the values of Vd and τ are calculated. Table 1 compares the obtained values with those reported for GaAs and Si35. These results indicate that ChNPs have a strong potential as an electronic material for renewable energy systems and portable (flexible or paper-based) electronics, provided they can be converted into a high-density films with fine particles. Schematic energy-band diagram of the MESFET

Figure 5(a) presents schematic of the chitosann recess gated MESFET with a depletion region of electric double layer. Since an electric double layer is formed at the contact interface between the Au electrode and AChNP as seen from the large second semicircle in Fig. 3(d), it is possible that the depletion layer of AChNP is formed in this electric double layer. The gate/AChNP interface forms a Schottky metal–semiconductor junction, creating a potential barrier. This barrier produces a depletion layer beneath the gate, preventing current flow in that region. However, increasing the negative gate voltage increases Ids owing to an enhancement in number of active channels. In this device, a depletion layer is present even with a zero -gate bias, resulting in‘normally-on’ behaviour. Figure 5(b) depicts the schematic energy-band diagram of the MESFET. This study is the first to report pronounced amplification and a high on/off ratio in transistors derived from marine chitosan. Further studies into the electrical characteristics such as amplification efficiency, rectification, and cyclic stability are essential for practical applications.

In summary, we demonstrated the amplification and on/off ratio effects of nanosized chitosan semiconductor Schottky-gated FETs. The high electron mobility of chitosan plays a significant role in the formation of the depletion layer.

Methods

The 10 wt% AChNP/water dispersion was supplied by Dainichseika Industries. A three-electrode MESFET (Fig. 5a) with a recess-gate structure was fabricated by mechanically attaching Au and Al electrodes to a dense chitosan specimen36. The transistor characteristics were measured at 298 K using a Keithley semiconductor parameter analyser (4200 A-SCS) in a dark box. AC impedance and frequency were analysed using a potentiostat/galvanostat (SP-150, BioLogic Science). The bandgap energy was measured using a photoelectron yield spectrometer (AC-2 S, Rikenkeiki, Tokyo) in the atmosphere by irradiating light from 3.4 to 5.8 eV at an anode voltage of 3 kV.
