Development and validation of a risk-based nomogram for predicting peritoneal dialysis catheter dysfunction in end-stage renal disease patients
Authors: Bin Zhao, Shanshan Guo, Wei Li, Hongqing Shang, Yutao Xing, Jing Liu, Yanwei Hu, Yuzhu Wang, Gang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeritoneal dialysis catheter (PDC) dysfunction significantly impacts patient survival. While individual risk factors are known, a practical tool that integrates multi-dimensional predictors—including surgical, anatomical, and postoperative parameters—for early risk stratification is lacking. This study evaluates open versus laparoscopic catheterization techniques and aims to develop such a predictive model.MethodsA retrospective cohort study analyzed 462 end-stage renal disease patients undergoing first PDC implantation (2022–2024). Data included clinical characteristics, surgical method (open/laparoscopic), and dysfunction events. Multivariate COX regression identified independent risk factors, and a nomogram was developed. Model performance was assessed via ROC and calibration curves, with Bootstrap validation.ResultsAmong 462 patients (324 modeling, 138 validation) with a median follow-up of 30.7 months, laparoscopic catheterization was associated with a significantly lower risk of dysfunction compared to the open approach (18% vs. 27%; hazard ratio [HR] 2.188, 95% CI 1.263–3.791, p = 0.005). Multivariate analysis identified five independent predictors of catheter dysfunction: history of abdominal surgery (HR 6.924, p < 0.001), open catheterization (HR 2.188, p = 0.005), diabetes (HR 2.373, p = 0.016), albumin < 30 g/L (HR 0.865, p < 0.001), and blood potassium < 4 mmol/L (HR 1.479, p = 0.015). The developed nomogram integrating these predictors showed outstanding discriminative performance, with C-indices of 0.953 (95% CI 0.940–0.967) in the modeling cohort and 0.951 (95% CI 0.929–0.972) in the validation cohort. Time-dependent ROC analysis further confirmed its predictive accuracy, with 1- and 2-year AUCs of 0.957/0.979 and 0.921/0.988 in the modeling and validation sets, respectively. Calibration curves showed close alignment between predicted and observed outcomes across both cohorts. The nomogram provides a clinically useful tool for individualized risk assessment and postoperative management.ConclusionsLaparoscopic catheterization reduces dysfunction risk. The presented nomogram is unique in its integration of readily available surgical, comorbidity, and nutritional metrics into a single, visual tool. It facilitates early identification of high-risk patients, thereby aiding individualized surgical planning and targeted postoperative monitoring to improve PDC longevity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00423-025-03945-9.Keywords: Peritoneal dialysis, Catheter survival, Nomogram, Laparoscopy

Introduction

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is an established form of renal replacement therapy. However, catheter-related dysfunction remains a major cause of technical failure and patient morbidity. The management of this complication is complicated by heterogeneous surgical practices and varying patient risk profiles [1–3].Existing literature has identified several clinical and procedural variables that may influence catheter function. These include patient-related factors such as prior abdominal surgery and elevated body mass index, as well as procedure-related factors like surgical technique (e.g., open versus laparoscopic approach) [4–7]. Nevertheless, current risk assessment remains largely empirical. There is a recognized lack of integrative models that can synthesize these disparate elements—surgical, clinical, and biochemical—into a unified framework for individualized risk prediction [8, 9].

To address this unmet need, we developed and validated a novel nomogram that uniquely integrates surgical method with key patient characteristics and dynamic postoperative laboratory values. Our model provides a clinically actionable tool for stratifying individual risk of catheter dysfunction, enabling clinicians to tailor both surgical approach and postoperative surveillance strategies accordingly.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Haidian Hospital (Beijing) between January 2020 and October 2024. We initially screened 495 patients who received peritoneal dialysis (PD) treatment during this period. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 33 patients were excluded, resulting in 462 eligible subjects. These participants were then randomly allocated into two cohorts: a training cohort (n = 324) and a validation cohort (n = 138), using a 7:3 ratio. The complete selection and allocation process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of patient selection

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) completion of ≥ 3 months of continuous PD treatment following catheterization. Exclusion criteria included: (1) previous hemodialysis treatment for ≥ 3 months; (2) history of kidney transplantation; (3) diagnosis of malignant tumors before or within 3 months after catheterization. (4) a documented history of any major catheter-related infection (e.g., peritonitis, tunnel infection).This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Haidian Hospital, Peking University Third Hospital (Approval No. HMEC-XM-2022-14). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and patient privacy and rights were fully protected throughout data collection and analysis.

Data collection protocol

Comprehensive baseline data were collected through structured electronic medical record review. Demographic characteristics included age, gender, height, and body weight. Clinical history documentation focused on diabetes, hypertension, and cerebrovascular events. Laboratory parameters assessed within 3 days prior to catheterization comprised complete serum electrolyte panel (sodium, potassium, calcium, inorganic phosphorus), renal function markers (creatinine, uric acid), hematological parameters (hemoglobin), nutritional indicators (albumin), and lipid profiles (total cholesterol, triglycerides). Twenty-four-hour urine output was precisely measured. Corrected calcium values were calculated using the standard formula: calcium (mmol/L) + 0.02× [40 - albumin (g/L)].

Follow-up and endpoint assessment

Patients were monitored from catheterization until the earliest of these endpoints: mortality, transition to hemodialysis for ≥ 3 months, kidney transplantation, or study conclusion (October 2024). The primary outcome was catheter mechanical dysfunction, rigorously defined according to established criteria [7], including: catheter insertion failure, radiologically confirmed tip migration, adhesion-induced catheter compression, omental wrapping, or catheter distortion. To ensure a specific focus on mechanical causes, episodes of infectious complications (such as peritonitis or tunnel infection) that led to catheter removal were carefully reviewed and excluded from the primary outcome analysis. Assessments were performed at 12 and 24 months post-catheterization. For survival analyses, appropriate censoring was applied for patients who switched to hemodialysis, received transplants, were lost to follow-up, or discontinued PD for over 3 months.

Statistical analysis

For normally distributed data, the intergroup comparisons were carried out via t-tests, whereas for non-normally distributed data [described as M (1/4, 3/4)], non-parametric tests were performed for intergroup comparisons. Furthermore, the counting data were expressed as frequency (percentage), and the χ 2 test was carried out for intergroup comparisons. Patient allocation to the modeling and validation cohorts (7:3 ratio) was performed prior to data analysis using a computer-generated random number sequence to ensure unbiased group assignment. The survival rate and time of patients were assessed via the Kaplan-Meier survival curve. Moreover, the univariate Cox regression model was employed to screen the factors influencing poor catheter function. Variables with p < 0.05 were included in the multivariate Cox regression model to identify the associated variables.For the construction of the multivariate Cox regression model and the nomogram, the treatment of predictor variables was specified as follows: Continuous variables were evaluated for linearity in the log hazard. Serum albumin was confirmed to have an approximately linear relationship with the outcome and was therefore included as a continuous variable, with the hazard ratio (HR) representing the risk change per unit (g/L) increase. In contrast, serum potassium was dichotomized into hypokalemia (yes/no) based on a clinically relevant cut-off value (K⁺< 3.5 mmol/L) to enhance clinical interpretability and because it demonstrated a better model fit in this form. Categorical variables, such as surgical approach and history of diabetes, were included as previously defined. Based on the Cox multivariate analysis, a nomogram was constructed using the rms package in R language according to the hazard ratio (HR) of risk factors. The discriminatory ability of the predictive model in both the modeling and validation cohorts was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) and the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC). Further, the calibration curve was measured to evaluate the model’s accuracy in both cohorts. The ROC rms curve was plotted using the calibrate function of the R package. The closer the curve is to the straight line with a slope of 1, the higher the accuracy of the prediction model. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 20.0 and R 3.6.3 software. The p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Risk factors influencing catheter dysfunction

Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified five independent risk factors for catheter dysfunction in the modeling cohort (Table 2). Notably, a history of abdominal surgery was the strongest predictor (HR = 6.924, 95% CI = 3.208–14.946, p < 0.001). The analysis also confirmed the significant protective effect of the laparoscopic approach, as open catheterization was associated with a more than two-fold increase in dysfunction risk (HR = 2.188, 95% CI = 1.263–3.791, p = 0.005) compared to the laparoscopic method. Other significant risk factors included diabetes (HR = 2.373,95%CI = 1.175–4.794,p = 0.016),hypokalemia (HR = 1.479,95%CI = 1.079–2.027, p = 0.015), and hypoalbuminemia (HR = 0.865, 95% CI = 0.831–0.901, p < 0.001).

Construction and verification of the prediction model

A nomogram was constructed to integrate these five independent predictors for clinical use. To illustrate its application, we calculated the total risk score for a representative patient profile: a patient with a history of abdominal surgery (21 points) and diabetes (9 points), undergoing open catheterization (8 points), with an albumin level of 30 g/L (62 points) and a potassium level of 4 mmol/L (8 points). The summed score of 108 points corresponds to predicted catheter survival probabilities of approximately 94.8% at 1 year and 80.7% at 2 years (Fig. 3).

Nomogram model for predicting 1 and 2-year catheter survival rates in PD patients. 1 = laparoscopic catheterization, 2 = incision catheterization. DM: diabetes mellitus

Model validation

The discriminative ability of the nomogram was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index). In the modeling cohort, the C-index was 0.953 (95% CI: 0.940–0.967), indicating that the model correctly ranked the risk order of patients who would and would not experience catheter dysfunction in 95.3% of randomly selected patient pairs. This high level of discrimination was confirmed in the validation cohort (C-index = 0.951, 95% CI: 0.929–0.972), demonstrating that the model maintains its ability to accurately stratify patients by risk when applied to new patient populations. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis further assessed the model’s predictive accuracy at specific clinical follow-up time points. The areas under the curve (AUC) for predicting 1-year catheter survival were 0.957 and 0.921 in the modeling and validation cohorts, respectively, while the 2-year survival AUC values were 0.979 and 0.988 (Fig. 4A, B). These results indicate that the model provides excellent prediction of both short-term and long-term catheter function. The agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes was assessed using calibration curves. The plots for both cohorts at 1-year and 2-year time points (Figs. 5A-D) demonstrated close alignment with the ideal diagonal line. This indicates that the model’s predicted probabilities accurately reflect the actual clinical outcomes; for instance, among patients predicted to have an 80% probability of 2-year catheter survival, approximately 80% would indeed maintain functional catheters at that time point.

Discussion

This study integrated clinical and surgical data to develop a predictive model for peritoneal dialysis (PD) catheter dysfunction, incorporating five independent predictors: history of abdominal surgery, diabetes, hypoalbuminemia, hypokalemia, and open catheterization. Unlike previous studies that focused on single-factor analyses, our model is the first to unify multidimensional indicators: surgical technique, patient comorbidities, and postoperative nutritional-metabolic status into a single assessment framework. Validation results demonstrated outstanding discriminative ability of the nomogram in both the modeling (C-index: 0.953) and validation (C-index:0.951) cohorts. Furthermore, calibration curves indicated strong agreement between predicted risks and observed outcomes, confirming its reliability as a tool for individualized risk assessment.

First, our study identified a significant association between diabetes and catheter dysfunction (HR = 2.373), which aligns with multiple previous investigations [8–11]. The underlying pathophysiology may involve diabetes-specific mechanisms, including microangiopathy, autonomic dysfunction, and structural alterations of the peritoneal membrane due to advanced glycation end product deposition. Collectively, these changes contribute to impaired intestinal motility and compromised peritoneal function, thereby increasing the risk of catheter malfunction. Our findings are consistent with clinical observations by Nakano et al. [9] and Gadola et al. [11], supporting the implementation of comprehensive management strategies for diabetic PD patients, including regular gastrointestinal function assessment, systematic nutritional monitoring, and intensified surveillance of catheter drainage parameters.

Further analysis revealed that hypoalbuminemia also serves as a key predictor of catheter dysfunction. Beyond the known mechanism of fibrinogen exudation [12, 13], hypoalbuminemia may reflect a systemic inflammatory state, potentially upregulating profibrotic factors such as TGF-β and accelerating peritoneal fibrosis [14, 15]. Additionally, protein-energy wasting has been recognized as a contributor to peritoneal remodeling, underscoring the importance of nutritional intervention.Another important finding is that hypokalemia was confirmed as an independent predictor (HR = 1.479), consistent with reports by Boobés et al. [16]. Potassium deficiency may inhibit intestinal smooth muscle electrophysiology, leading to functional obstruction. Notably, hypokalemia frequently coexists with hypoalbuminemia in the PD population [17], and their interaction may produce a synergistic effect that significantly elevates dysfunction risk. This highlights the need to integrate electrolyte management and nutritional assessment into standardized catheter care protocols.

The inclusion of “previous abdominal surgery history” in our prediction model demonstrates significant clinical relevance, as it reflects underlying physical barriers particularly intra-abdominal adhesions—that may restrict catheter mobility or lead to malposition. The sustained proinflammatory environment following abdominal surgery could further promote the formation of new adhesive bands around the catheter [18]. Although some large-scale cohort studies have not established a significant association between prior surgery and early catheter-related adverse events [19], this discrepancy may stem from variations in population selection, definitions of surgical history, or follow-up duration. Our multifactorial model, through multivariate adjustment, confirms that a history of abdominal surgery significantly increases long-term catheter dysfunction risk (HR = 6.924). These findings highlight the importance of quantitatively evaluating the potential impact of abdominal adhesions when assessing catheter failure risk in this patient subgroup.

The multivariate analysis established laparoscopic catheterization as an independent protective factor against catheter dysfunction (HR = 2.188 for open approach), corresponding to an approximately 55% risk reduction. This finding corroborates existing literature documenting the technical advantages of laparoscopy, including superior visualization for precise pelvic placement [20], controlled adhesion management under direct vision, and reduced surgical trauma [21]. Building upon these established benefits, our study advances the field by integrating surgical approach into a comprehensive risk prediction model. This integration enables personalized decision-making: the model supports prioritizing laparoscopy for high-risk patients ( those with obesity or previous abdominal surgery). By quantitatively weighing surgical options against individual patient characteristics, our model facilitates evidence-based selection of catheterization technique optimized for long-term functional success.

The nomogram developed in this study integrates patient-specific factors, metabolic-nutritional parameters, and treatment-related variables into a unified predictive framework. In this model, continuous predictors such as serum albumin and potassium levels were incorporated as linear terms, which simplifies clinical application and enhances interpretability. Robust discrimination and calibration in both internal and external validation support its potential reliability for clinical use. As a visual tool, it enables rapid risk stratification by allowing clinicians to input routine clinical indicators, thereby providing a quantitative basis for personalized management strategies, such as nutritional optimization, surgical approach selection, and intensified catheter monitoring.Future studies could further refine this model by exploring potential non-linear relationships of continuous variables like serum albumin and by incorporating additional clinically relevant predictors identified in this study. External validation in larger, multi-center cohorts will be crucial to verify the model’s generalizability and to facilitate its future updating and calibration.

However, it must be noted that this study has several limitations: Firstly, due to its retrospective design, despite multivariate analysis being performed to adjust for confounding factors, some unrecorded confounding variables (like patient compliance, details of specific types of abdominal surgeries, and peritoneal function indicators( Clearance × time / Volume of urea, Kt/V) might still have influenced the results. Secondly, the size of the model validation cohort is relatively limited. Moreover, as a single-center study, our nomogram requires external validation in multicenter, prospective cohorts to confirm its generalizability and reliability across different patient populations and clinical settings. Thirdly, the selection of surgical methods also has a certain impact (such as the preferences of different centers and the degree of technical mastery), and should be considered when interpreting the model results. Finally, the model mainly predicts mechanical catheter dysfunction. In the future, the prediction model should be expanded to integrate the impact of infection factors such as peritonitis on the long-term survival of catheters.

Conclusions

In summary, this study successfully constructed and verified a nomogram model for predicting the risk of PDC dysfunction. The model integrated five key clinical predictors: history of abdominal surgery, diabetes, hypoalbuminemia, hypokalemia, and catheterization surgery methods. Furthermore, internal and external validations confirmed the model’s good discrimination and calibration efficiency. Moreover, the model could effectively predict the risk of PDC dysfunction in patients. Future research should investigate other potential prognostic factors that affect the function of PDCs (such as peritoneal transport characteristics, frequency of peritonitis, specific drug use, etc.), and perform model validation and calibration in a larger sample and more heterogeneous population to improve the model’s accuracy and enrich its predictive dimensions. The continuous improvement and clinical implementation of this model can provide personalized risk stratification tools for clinicians, aiding in prospective interventions against catheter selection, surgical method decision-making for catheterization, and long-term follow-up management, decreasing catheter failure rates and increasing the success of PD technology and patient quality of life.
