Perceived prejudice or discrimination in medical care among US adults
Authors: Inimfon Jackson, Robert Yu, Joël Fokom Domgue, Sanjay Shete
Published: 2025

Introduction

Discrimination experiences in the medical setting are associated with poorer quality of care and worse health outcomes. While the eradication of healthcare disparities is essential to achieve the Healthy People 2030 goal, few reports have examined self-reported discrimination in healthcare access using nationally representative data.1,2 To improve our understanding of patients' utilization of healthcare services, it is necessary to investigate the current prevalence of perceived prejudice or discrimination in medical care and identify the factors associated with these disparities among US adults.

Methods

We examined population-based cross-sectional data from the Health Information National Trends Survey 7 (HINTS 7) collected from March through September 2024. HINTS is a nationally representative survey administered to civilian, non-institutionalized adults. Additional information on HINTS survey development, methodology, and design has been previously described.3 This study was considered exempt from IRB approval since the HINTS 7 data are de-identified and publicly available. The study followed EQUATOR reporting guidelines. Details about the study methods are available in the Supplementary material.

The primary outcome was perceived prejudice or discrimination when receiving medical care. This was derived from a response to the survey question, “Have you ever experienced prejudice or been discriminated against when getting medical care?”. Weighted prevalence and 95% CIs were estimated for perceived prejudice or discrimination in medical care for the overall population and by sociodemographic characteristics. The survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to examine the factors associated with perceived prejudice or discrimination in medical care. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4, and a 2-sided P-value <0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

The study included 7119 adults, of whom 3987 were females (weighted percentage 48.8%), 4194 were 50 years or older (48.5%), 3530 were non-Hispanic Whites (NHWs) (60.6%), 880 were Hispanic Whites (11.7%), 66 were Hispanic Blacks (0.6%), and 956 were non-Hispanic Blacks (NHBs) (11.0%). The overall prevalence of perceived prejudice or discrimination when getting medical care was 16.1% (95%CI 14.3-17.9). One fourth [25.9% (95%CI 19.8-32.1)] of NHB individuals experienced perceived prejudice or discrimination when receiving medical care (Figure 1 and Table S1). In the logistic regression analysis, NHB (aOR: 2.27; 95%CI 1.42-3.63) and NH Other (aOR: 2.38; 95%CI 1.19-4.78) individuals had higher odds of reporting perceived prejudice or discrimination when getting medical care than NHW patients. Individuals ≤ 49 years (aOR: 1.69; 95%CI 1.28-2.24) had higher odds of reporting prejudice or perceived discrimination compared to those ≥50 years old. Also, female participants (aOR: 1.99; 95%CI 1.43-2.76) had higher odds of experiencing prejudice or perceived racial discrimination than males. Finally, current (aOR: 2.29; 95%CI 1.55-3.39) and former smokers (aOR: 1.64; 95% CI 1.23-2.20) had higher odds of reporting perceived discrimination or prejudice when getting medical care than never smokers (Table 1).

Discussion

Our study revealed that a sizeable proportion of US adults continue to experience prejudice or discrimination when getting medical care, with some groups, such as younger adults (≤ 49 years), females, smokers, and NHB individuals, having higher odds of experiencing prejudice or discrimination. These findings are consistent with existing literature and highlight the need to understand the drivers of these perceptions in the identified sub-populations. The underlying causes of disparities need to be addressed to improve the quality of care and health outcomes for all patients.

Individuals with a smoking history may experience feelings of stigmatization by their healthcare providers. Women and younger adults may also have different expectations of care, which could influence their perception and reports about discrimination. Interestingly, Hispanic White participants' odds of perceived medical discrimination are almost identical to the odds of non-Hispanic Whites; whereas the Hispanic Black participants' odds of perceived medical discrimination are similar to the odds of non-Hispanic Black participants. Thus, although statistically non-significant, these findings hint that, in a medical setting, discrimination is more likely related to skin color than to language or acculturation. This topic needs to be further investigated, as the number of Hispanic Black individuals in our study was small. Notably, unconscious bias among healthcare providers is a major factor that might contribute to the higher rates of perceived discrimination in medical care.4 Furthermore, a lack of cultural competence negatively impacts patient-physician interactions and leads to miscommunication and distrust between patients and their providers.5

Overall, our results highlight the ongoing discrimination in medical care experienced by adults in the US despite decades of clinical efforts to improve health outcomes. Policy action is urgently needed to dismantle structural barriers that sustain systemic inequities and discrimination in healthcare. Policymakers should prioritize interventions to mitigate implicit bias as a public health issue, diversify the physician workforce, and incentivize health care facilities and staff to implement patient-centered and culturally appropriate practices. Health providers should be encouraged to recognize and manage their own implicit biases, and the medical school and training program curricula should incorporate education about the complexity of health disparities and the role of racial discrimination in driving poorer outcomes. Medical education and continuing professional development are important for teaching physicians the importance of cultural sensitivity in patient care encounters. This could improve the quality of care provided and ensure that healthcare systems are better equipped to meet the needs of an increasingly diverse patient population. Some limitations of our study include the potential for non-response bias; however, rigorous statistical methods and weighting procedures were applied to account for potential bias. The strengths of the study include the use of recent and nationally representative data.
