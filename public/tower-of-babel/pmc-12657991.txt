Predictive engine model incorporating physics based model estimation and machine learning
Authors: Jin-sol Jung, Changmin Son, Andrew Rimell, Rory J Clarkson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGas turbine engines on aircraft are equipped with an Engine Health Monitoring (EHM) system that collects in-service data of various installed sensors. The system is not free from malfunction or deterioration. Hence, the signal can be lost (missing data) or convey faulty information. As the EHM system only captures real-time data during a flight, it implies an important loss of crucial information. It raises an issue regarding data quality and quantity required for adopting Machine Learning (ML) approaches to building predictive engine performance models. Therefore, a Missing Value Imputation process is necessary for training ML model. In this paper, various methods for handling missing data are evaluated including deletion, interpolation, ML model inference, and a physics-based engine performance model. The physics-based engine model was built using commercially available software, Numerical Propulsion System Simulation (NPSS), to provide estimations of those missing data. The results show that incorporating a physics-based model improved the prediction accuracy of the ML-based predictive model and outperformed other methods.Keywords: Engine health monitoring, Machine learning, Missing value imputation, Data qualitySubject terms: Engineering, Aerospace engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Maintaining the safety and integrity of aircraft engine is a challenging task as it is exposed to harsh environments and operating conditions. Those engines undergo a scheduled major overhaul every couple of years, however, sometimes unscheduled maintenance is required due to an urgent issue or unusual performance degradation. In this context, having a capability to forecast engine performance degradation will be beneficial to engine manufacturers and aircraft operators. For the purpose, the collected data from in-service engines becomes crucial information to build predictive engine health model. Most previous research1–22 on predictive engine models used the NASA C-MAPSS dataset23,24 which is based on a simulated turbofan engine data for academic purposes. One of the realistic data source is from Engine Health Monitoring (EHM) system. Sensors which is part of the EHM system, installed in engine, collect data such as pressure, temperature and vibration during each flight. However, it is worth noting that the locations and numbers of sensors are limited due to practical reasons. Additional note is that EHM data is so-called “snap shot” data which collects sensor signals at specific flight conditions, typically at take-off, climb, and cruise (Table 1). It means that the collected sensor signals are sparse dataset while conveys the variation in long-term engine performance such as degradation over engine life. The EHM data of in-service engine has randomness, incompleteness, outliers that is less likely an issue with simulated dataset unless it is intended for a specific purpose. On the other hand, when utilizing real engine data like EHM data to build a predictive model applying ML, the quality of data could play very important role.

The impact of data quality is important topic for the big data and Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) community. An early engagement in data quality streamlines the workflow of ML model. A standard data quality management framework appears to be absent, however, there were some practical approaches to cope with data quality25. When it comes to its completeness, it seems natural to have incomplete data as noted previously. Some argue that small amounts of missing data, less than 10% or 15%, can be removed while having a negligible impact on prediction result26,27. On the other hand, it might require a further management if it exceeds 15% of entire dataset28. During the build-up of the predictive model, it was found that there are missing data in the EHM data that seemed to be of negligible size (Table 2). However, it is worth noting that a single data point of EHM data represents a single flight, which is invaluable.

This paper presents an examination of how data quality management, especially in terms of data incompleteness, affects the performance of the ML-based predictive engine health model. Six different Missing Value Imputation (MVI) methods are introduced and compared on the basis of the prediction accuracy. One of the methods used a physics-based engine performance model representing a Turbofan family engine that the EHM data was acquired from. This physics-based model provides estimated value for missing data. The ML-based predictive model was built using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), one type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), and it was compared with the models applying selected linear and nonlinear algorithms. The findings from present work are valid for a Turbofan family engine as well as other gas turbine engine types. However, the proposed approach will be also applicable to similar power and energy system (See Online Appendix A1 for nomenclature).

Methods

The predictive engine model was built to forecast Turbine Gas Temperature (TGT), one of the key health indices. TGT is measured by a thermocouple positioned between the Intermediate Pressure Turbine (IPT) and the Low Pressure Turbine (LPT) (Fig. 1). There are seven EHM parameters selected as predictor variables, N1, N2, N3, P25, P3, P50, T3, and one target variable, TGT, to build an ML-based predictive model for engine performance. These predictor variables were selected after sensitivity study along with Pearson covariance coefficient analysis (see Online Appendix A2). Missing data are identified when there is no data for a specific parameter, despite the existence of a flight record as indicated by date and time (DATETIME) or flight cycles (CyclesSinceNew). Missing value imputation methods

Missing Value Imputation (MVI) is a method for managing incompleteness in a dataset for ML. Python packages, such as scikit-learn, Tensorflow, and PyTorch, do not have an automated process of managing missing data. Besides, there is no such standard process, and removing rows containing missing data is being employed most often. Alongside this method, there are other MVI methods based on a statistical approach, interpolation, estimation by other ML models, etc. In this paper, six different MVI methods were selected and evaluated as introduced in Table 3 and Fig. 2.

Physics-based engine performance model

In this paper, a physics-based engine performance model was utilized to provide an estimate of missing data. This model was built using Numerical Propulsion System Simulation (NPSS)30. NPSS is software which was originally developed by NASA Glenn Research Center for the development of engine performance models. The model can implement custom performance curves and parameters for each engine component, such as compressors and turbines. This enables the model to better represent real engine performance. However, the model will only represent engine performance if it implements a degradation model of each engine component.

The model was built to represent the three-spool turbofan engine for civil aircraft (Fig. 1). Each module was configured to represent the engine architecture (Fig. 3). The performance curve for each component was implemented in the model. Each component is connected according to the architecture of the real engine in different layers. There is a layer that connects components mechanically. For instance, a Low Pressure Compressor (LPC) is coupled with a Low Pressure Turbine (LPT) via a low-pressure shaft. Another layer is for the main flow that goes through the engine core from the inlet, LPC, Intermediate Pressure Compressor (IPC), High Pressure Compressor (HPC), combustor, High Pressure Turbine (HPT), Intermediate Pressure Turbine (IPT), and LPT. There is a layer for the bypass flow that goes from the LPC to the bypass duct. Furthermore, there is a secondary flow layer that models secondary air systems connecting compressors and turbines, or extracting air from compressors to the cabin. The model runs the engine simulation for given input conditions, such as fuel flow rate, speed, altitude, and pressure, that represents specific flight conditions. The real engine operation is unsteady, and performance deteriorates as it operates. However, the model represents steady-state engine performance without implementing deterioration mechanism yet. In this research, the physics-based model was incorporated as one of the MVI methods. When a missing data is found in the EHM data during preprocessing, this model runs the simulation and provides values to replace the missing data based on the performance estimates.

Long short-term memory

From the literature survey, it was found that the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network has been one of the most widely used neural networks in machine learning approaches3–5,7,9,10,12–14,16,17,20,22,31,32. LSTM networks are a specialized type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN). RNNs are characterized by their chain-like structure, making them suitable for processing sequential (time-domain) data. While theoretically capable of handling long-term dependencies, RNNs often struggle with this in practice, as noted by Bengio et al.33. To address the long-term dependency issue, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber34 introduced LSTM (Fig. 5). This innovation aimed to enhance learning through recurrent back-propagation, which had previously been hindered by insufficient and decaying error backflow. Unlike standard RNNs, which typically feature a simple hyperbolic tangent layer within their repeating modules, LSTMs incorporate four interacting neural network layers. First of all, the prediction results seem not to vary greatly in between different algorithms, linear, nonlinear, and LSTM. Yet more outliers are observed with linear algorithms compared to nonlinear algorithms and LSTM. Linear algorithms show divergent results across different metrics, such as mean RMSE, outlier ratio, and IQR. Two linear algorithms, ‘ridge’ and ‘huber’, deliver the best mean RMSE value, while ‘lasso’ and ‘lassol’ have the least outliers. In IQR, ‘lr’, ‘ridge’, and ‘huber’ show the smallest range among linear algorithms. Among nonlinear algorithms, the results of ‘svmr’ provide the best prediction accuracy consistently with various MVI methods considering mean RMSE and IQR. The outlier ratio seems to be low with ‘ada’ algorithm. LSTM consistently performs well across various MVI methods, considering different metrics, in comparison to other algorithms.

The results show that using the ‘performance’ method for MVI outperformed all other methods. In terms of mean RMSE value, it improved prediction accuracy up to 47% compared to ‘remove rows’ method with one of the linear algorithms. It is improved by up to 46% with the nonlinear algorithms, and 35% with LSTM. It also gives minimum IQR values regardless of the types of the algorithms. It is the only method that improved the prediction accuracy regarding mean RMSE and IQR across all algorithms that are evaluated. This implies that using ‘performance’ method reduces a variance of prediction significantly. Although minimum outlier ratios seems to be varied, the RMSE values of the outliers are lower with ‘performance’ than the others. Another observation is that the ‘remove rows’ method provides the second best prediction accuracy among all MVI methods across different algorithms. While it seems somewhat simple to understand why ‘remove rows’ is widely accepted for handling missing data, this result demonstrates that it performs better than other MVI methods, except for the ‘performance’ method.

Conclusion

EHM system collects sensor measurements from in-service aircraft engine, and often data are lost due to various reasons. It requires an additional effort to process those before feeding into ML-based models. In this paper, the impact of managing missing data in EHM data on prediction accuracy of ML-based predictive model is evaluated. Six Missing Value Imputation (MVI) methods were evaluated including deletion (‘remove rows’), interpolation (‘polynomial’), statistics (‘fillna mean’, ‘rolling’), neural networks (‘kNN’), and a physics-based model (‘performance’).

Among the evaluated methods, the ‘performance’ method, incorporating the physics-based engine performance model, outperformed the other methods. It improved the prediction accuracy of the ML-based predictive model up to 47% of mean RMSE with the linear algorithms, 46% with the nonlinear algorithms, and 36% with LSTM, compared to ‘remove rows’ method. The ‘performance’ method is the only one that consistently improved prediction accuracy regardless of the choice of the algorithm.

The evaluations on other metrics, MAPE, MAE, and , provide a comprehensive understanding of the presented results. Both MAPE and MAE confirm the efficacy of the ‘performance’ method. Meanwhile, values provide how well the model captures the full range and patterns of variability within target TGT, depending on the MVI methods used.

Furthermore, the study highlights that even a small amount of missing data, e.g., less than 1%, needs careful management and can affect prediction performance of ML-based predictive model significantly. The results also suggest the usefulness of integrating ML and physics-based model to improve prediction performance of predictive model.

Although the present study only utilized EHM data at take-off condition, the proposed method can be extended its application to other operating conditions.
