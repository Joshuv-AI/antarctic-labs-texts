Feasibility of ultrasound-mediated blood–brain barrier opening in sheep using sonolucent cranial implants
Authors: Jae Song, Gina Richter-Stretton, Matthew Pelekanos, Corbin Clawson, Siamak Saifzadeh, Craig Simon, Gerhard Leinenga, Rachel de las Heras, Hamish Alexander, Jürgen Götz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug delivery to the brain presents a major hurdle in developing treatments for neurological disorders. A noninvasive strategy addressing this is transcranial focused ultrasound with microbubbles (FUS+MB) that transiently opens the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Because FUS+MB operates within a narrow therapeutic window, the acoustic pressure needs to be adjusted to factor in a highly variable skull attenuation which is difficult to determine in situ. Here, we used in-house and ClearFit® cranial implants made of PMMA as sonolucent, acoustically homogenous material. k-Wave simulations and in vitro attenuation measurements guided us in establishing optimal implant dimensions. Three sheep procedures were performed using the two implants with peak negative pressures (PNPs) ranging from 278 to 601 kPa, while monitoring broadband and harmonic emissions, and systemically injecting a model drug to visualize BBB opening. BBB opening occurred between 395 and 601 kPa, with the PNP correlating with broadband and harmonic emissions. Our study indicates the utility of sonolucent implants for achieving targeted FUS+MB-mediated BBB opening in the large animal model sheep as revealed with the model drug Evans blue. We foresee that cranioplasties will facilitate FUS+MB-based drug delivery especially for diseases such as glioblastomas which require both craniotomy and radiological treatment monitoring.Keywords: Attenuation, Cranioplasty, Focused ultrasound (FUS), Low-intensity ultrasound, Passive cavitation detector, Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA)Subject terms: Translational research, Blood-brain barrier, Diseases of the nervous system, Cancer in the nervous system, Implants, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

The inaccessibility of the human brain continues to present a major challenge for both drug delivery and therapeutic monitoring of brain disorders including oncological and neurodegenerative conditions1,2. Here, we present a feasibility study that combines cranioplasty with focused ultrasound at low intensity (FUS), intended to assist in arriving at more effective treatments of these costly and prevalent disorders.

FUS is an emerging therapeutic modality for a wide range of brain diseases3,4. Different from imaging which uses frequencies in the 2–15 MHz range, FUS operates at lower frequencies. For our preclinical studies in mice, we largely used a transducer operating at 1 MHz5,6, and sub-MHz frequencies in sheep and human study participants7 (and under review). The human and sheep skull are more attenuating than that of the mouse, and attenuation and phase aberration increase with ultrasound frequency8. Therefore, ultrasound imaging of the human brain in neonates is only possible through the fontanelles. In adults, imaging is restricted to limited transcranial windows, or intraoperatively upon removing a portion of the skull or, alternatively, by using sonolucent cranial implants. Recently, these implants have been adopted in clinical practice for multiple indications providing an opportunity for bedside, low-cost simple intracranial imaging without the need for CT and MRI9. For the repair of surgical skull defects, traditionally, autologous bone or custom-made titanium or biocompatible polymer implants are used, thereby achieving anatomical restoration in patients.

FUS can broadly be used in three ways10. (i) When combined with intravenously injected microbubbles (MBs), FUS+MB can be employed to focally open the blood–brain barrier (BBB), with therapeutic effects being achieved by the brain uptake of blood-borne factors11,12. (ii) In a more targeted approach, FUS+MB can effectively deliver therapeutic agents including monoclonal antibodies, viral vectors and chemotherapeutic agents (FUS+MB+drug)13–18. (iii) FUS can further be used on its own, i.e. without including MBs (FUSonly), to induce neuromodulatory effects19,20. To achieve the intended bioeffects in the absence of adverse events, for all three modalities, the FUS parameters must be tightly controlled. However, despite a wealth of data obtained in many preclinical models and an increasing number of clinical trials, the complexity of the three-layered human skull continues to present a challenge for reliably predicting the path of ultrasound waves through the skull and the extent of their attenuation even when CT is used for treatment planning21.

To address this problem, we therefore performed a preclinical feasibility study of FUS+MB in sheep hypothesizing that with acoustically homogeneous low-attenuation cranial implants, FUS can be delivered at a uniform intended in situ pressure over multiple sonication spots without the need of complex skull-based simulations. Several materials have been explored for cranial implants including poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), a transparent synthetic polymer derived from methyl methacrylate22. Sonolucent PMMA-based implants are being commercialized by Longeviti Neuro Solutions under the trade name ClearFit®.

In our study, we first determined the required implant characteristics using k-Wave simulations followed by test bed studies to acoustically characterize in-house PMMA and ClearFit® implants. We then performed in vivo cranioplasty in sheep and performed CT imaging to localize the implant and for image-guided FUS+MB treatment planning. Systemic injection of the model drug Evans blue revealed successful BBB opening in three sheep over multiple spots for a range of acoustic pressures. The study further demonstrated the ease with which a PMMA-based implant procedure can be performed.

Materials and methods

Cranial implants

ClearFit® cranial implants have been obtained from the manufacturer of sonolucent implants, Longeviti Neuro Solutions (Maryland, USA), as part of a research agreement. The implants with a capability for bedside imaging contain PMMA as a component and come in different shapes, sizes and curvatures that can be tailored towards specific applications. For our study, 4-mm-thick flat discs (Cat # 171,709) were employed, with oval dimensions of 4 × 5 cm and beveled edges (Fig. 1a, b). In-house implants made of PMMA (Cast acrylic sheet, Titan Poly Tech Co. Ltd., Thailand) were also generated which, while matching the dimensions of the ClearFit® implants, were 3 mm thick (Fig. 1a, b). The 3-mm-thick PMMA implant is within Longeviti’s design envelope for implant design that was chosen to provide protection similar to or better than native bone. The mechanical testing of the ClearFit® implants included an assessment of impact, flexural and tensile strength, and was also done in real-time and with aged PMMA implant material showing no significant decrease in the mechanical properties over relevant periods of time.

Simulation of ultrasound propagation through an in-house implant

In a clinical setting, the size of the implant would be determined by the clinical need. Using the k-Wave simulation toolbox, the minimum implant diameter that least distorts the ultrasound focus was explored23. A concave transducer was modelled using the specifications of the FUS transducer employed in the below in vitro and sheep studies, i.e., with a 63.2 mm radius of curvature, and a 22 and 64 mm inner and outer diameter, respectively. The transducer was positioned such that the transducer’s backside was located at position 0 mm. Moreover, the center was defined as origin in the simulation, which was done consistently throughout all experiments. As a result, the radiating surface of the transducer was located at around 10 mm (Fig. 2b). The implant with a density of 1,200 kg/m3 and speed of sound of 2,757 m/s was placed at 50 mm. Density and speed of sound of water were 1,000 kg/m3 and 1,500 m/s, respectively. The coupling cone was not modelled in the simulation as it was assumed to not substantially affect ultrasound propagation.

For comparative purposes, focusing through a sheep skull was also explored in the simulation. A CT-scanned sheep skull was imported into the simulation space via resampling using 3D affine transformation, followed by trilinear interpolation since the coordinates of the CT scan and k-Wave simulation mismatched. Next, Hounsfield units of the skull were first linearly converted to density values using the minimum and maximum values of 1,000 and 4,000 kg/m3. Subsequently, speed of sound was linearly converted from the obtained density using the minimum and maximum values24. Absorption was excluded in the simulation considering the use of low frequency ultrasound. Moreover, a piece of skull was simply replaced by water to model a Burr hole or by the implant to model cranioplasty. Attenuation in terms of peak negative pressure (PNP) was measured in each case while varying the diameter from 15 to 40 mm in steps of 5 mm. In all simulations, a 500 kHz, 60 µs long sinusoidal wave with unit amplitude was used as an input, and the grid size was 0.4 mm in all directions.

Ultrasound equipment and test bed measurements

The dominant parameter of an ultrasound waveform that determines therapeutic outcomes in BBB opening and the only parameter affected by an implant is the PNP that becomes reduced to an (intended) internal, i.e. derated PNP. Given the narrow FUS+MB treatment window, attenuation caused by an implant must be known prior to delivering ultrasound to the brain.

To measure attenuation through the two types of implants, an AIMS III tank measuring 0.89 m × 0.51 m × 0.58 m (Onda Corp., USA) was filled with degassed water. Then, a focused ultrasound transducer (H-107, Sonic Concepts, Inc., USA) with an aperture diameter of 64 mm was inserted to measure attenuation in transmission mode. The backside of the transducer was defined as 0 mm in depth and the in-house implant was placed at a 50 mm distance (Fig. 1c), whereas the ClearFit® implant was placed on top of a PMMA sheet in a central opening (Fig. 1d). The transducer was driven at 500 kHz by an arbitrary waveform generator (M2P.6533-X4, Spectrum Instrumentation GmbH, Germany) via a 50 dB power radio-frequency amplifier (240L, Electronics & Innovation, Ltd., USA). 3D scanning was performed using a 1.0 mm diameter needle hydrophone (HNR-1000, Onda Corp., USA) connected to a 3-axis motorized stage from − 4.2 to + 4.2 mm in the x and y plane, and from 55 to 98 mm in the z axis (Fig. 1c, d). The hydrophone was calibrated by the National Physical Laboratory (UK) from 0.1 to 1 MHz in steps of 0.01 MHz using a long duration signal25.

To measure attenuation in detection mode, a flat 6 mm diameter transducer (V323-N-SU, Olympus NDT Inc., USA) was employed to mimic diverging waves produced by a microbubble in response to FUS, and implants were placed at a distance of 120 mm to meet far-field conditions at frequencies ranging from 0.5 to 4 MHz (Fig. 1e). 30 mm below the implant, a 2-D scan was performed while sonicating 100 µs sinusoidal waves at 4 MHz. Once scanning was completed, the hydrophone was moved to the position generating the maximal amplitude, and waveforms were then recorded while increasing the frequency from 0.5 to 4 MHz in steps of 0.25 MHz. The same procedure was repeated using the ClearFit® implant (Fig. 1f).

Feasibility of passive cavitation detection (PCD) through the implants was also assessed. After completing the 3D scan to measure transmission attenuation of the in-house implant, the hydrophone was moved to the pressure peak to visually guide the placing of a silicone flexible tubing (0.8 mm inner diameter, RS Components Pty. Ltd., Australia) (Fig. 1g). Then, the hydrophone was moved out of the ultrasound field. While flowing MBs (Definity®, Lantheus, USA) diluted 1:200 in saline using a syringe pump (Pump 11 Elite, Harvard Apparatus, USA) at 10 mL/h, 2 ms sinusoidal waveforms were excited at 500 kHz with ramping up the amplitude from 20 to 400 mVpp in steps of 10 mVpp. Simultaneously, MB emissions were detected with a PVDF-based passive cavitation detector (Y-141, Sonic Concepts, Inc., USA), positioned at the central opening of the transducer, and sampled with a 16-bit digitizer card (M4i.4421; Spectrum Instrumentation GmbH, Germany) at 40 MHz via a 34-dB pre-amplifier (Model 5662; Olympus NDT Inc., USA). The same procedure was repeated to acquire MB emission in the presence of ClearFit® implant with amplitudes from 5 to 225 mVpp in step of 5 mVpp (Fig. 1h). Both MB emissions were then compared to one acquired without an implant while varying the amplitude from 5 to 200 mVpp in steps of 5 mVpp. A Hanning window was applied to the recorded passive PCD signals, from which higher harmonic (HH) and broadband (BB) emissions were extracted using a custom developed MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., MA, USA) script with the following frequency band where f0 = 0.5 MHz:

Animal experimentation and ethics

Sheep were provided by the Medical Engineering Research Facility (MERF), of the Queensland University of Technology (QUT). They were sourced from a local stockyard. Three four-year-old female Merino cross sheep, #1 (893–16 2152, weight: 60 kg), #2 (893–18 2145, weight: 49 kg), and #3 (893–20 2156, weight: 48 kg) were employed in this study, which was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Queensland University of Technology (QUT ethics approval #3630) and then ratified by The University of Queensland. All experimentation were performed in accordance with QUT and UQ guidelines and regulations, and all authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

General anesthesia was induced with intravenous propofol (3–4 mg/kg), and maintained with 2.5–3% isoflurane in oxygen/air7. Heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, end tidal carbon dioxide, central venous pressure and non-invasive blood pressure were monitored throughout the entire experiment.

Craniotomy was then performed. A U-shaped scalp incision pedicled anteriorly was made and the scalp was retracted with the periosteum elevated as a separate layer to allow for coverage of any air sinus breach. The cranial defect was created by means of two burr holes spanning the superior sagittal sinus with a match head high-speed burr (Stryker, USA), and craniotomy was completed with a standard craniotome (Midas Rex Legends EHS Stylus, Medtronic). The dura remained intact. The 3-mm-thick PMMA (sheep #1) and 4-mm-thick ClearFit® (sheep #2 and #3) implants were securely placed by hitching sutures in the implant and bone edge. The implant bone interface was sealed with bone wax to retain irrigation fluid underneath the implant and thus reduce interference from air bubbles.

A thermoplastic mask (RG461-5SHT, Klarity, US) and moldable cushion (Moldcare RI II HN2 #93,742, Alcare, Japan) were fitted to the sheep head to restrain it throughout the CT scan and FUS+MB procedure. The CT scan was taken as described7 to visualize the location of the implant in the skull as well as for treatment planning in the Brainsight® TMS neuronavigation system (Rogue Research, Canada). Definity® MBs were diluted 1:20 in saline (1 mL MBs in 19 mL saline) and then injected intravenously at a rate of 80 µL/kg/min (i.e. equivalent to an undiluted dosage rate of 4 µL/kg/min). (i) For sheep #1, a separate, freshly prepared Definity® dilution was prepared and injected for each sonication spot using a syringe pump. FUS+MB sonication was initiated 30 s after the MB injection to allow for the circulating MB concentration to peak. Ultrasound parameters were a frequency of 500 kHz, 20 ms pulse length, 1 Hz pulse repetition frequency, and 60 s sonication per spot. To determine a threshold PNP for BBB opening without tissue damage, in sheep #1, three PNPs (278, 395, and 507 kPa, based on an MI of 0.4, 0.55 and 0.7) were applied to 6 spots in total (Fig. 5a). Three PNPs were selected based on the broadly known concept of 0.4 and 0.6 of MIs as opening and tissue damage threshold PNPs, respectively26,27. After each FUS+MB treatment, the MB injection was turned off for 5 min to allow for MB clearance before proceeding to the next sonication spot. (ii) In sheep #2, a PNP of 425 kPa, i.e. higher than the BBB opening threshold found for sheep #1, was applied to 13 spots while continuously infusing Definity® with a drip infusion pump (EN-V5, Enmind, China). The other ultrasound parameters, the MB dilution ratio (1:20) and the infusion rate (80 µL/kg/min) were as for sheep #1. The drip bag was gently squeezed intermittently to resuspend MBs. Baseline PCD responses were only collected at spot 1 prior to MB delivery, and at the final spot 13, 5 min after MB delivery was halted. (iii) In sheep #3, PNPs of 278, 507 and 601 kPa were applied to four spots each arranged in three rows with continuous Definity infusion as done for sheep #2. All FUS+MB treatments were immediately followed by Evans Blue infusion (50 mL of 4% stock [E2129, Sigma-Aldrich, US] in saline infused at 150 mL/h) and heparin injection to prevent blood clots (250 IU/kg, i.v.; Pfizer Australia). Then, the sheep were humanely euthanized via sodium pentobarbitone injection (Lethabarb®, 160 mg/kg; Virbac Australia). The sheep brains were perfused, dissected, and post-fixed in 10% formalin as described previously7. The brains were sliced into 1.5 mm horizontal sections and scanned in a LI-COR Odyssey imaging system (Odyssey Fc, LI-COR, US) at 700 nm for histological analysis of BBB opening.

Histology was conducted on small regions of interest in both sheep brains to assess potential degeneration and erythrocyte extravasation. For that, gross sections were excised and paraffin-embedded and then sectioned at 10 µm thickness. These small sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and imaged at the Queensland Brain Institute’s Advanced Microscopy Facility using an Axio Imager Z1 upright fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss) fitted with an Axiocam 512 color camera at 5 × and 20 × objectives.

Results

3D k-Wave simulation to address edge effects of cranial implants, and test bed measurements of in-house and ClearFit® implants

A confound when delivering discrete sonications through an implant are edge effects which arise where the implant borders the skull. To eliminate this, we first performed a 3D k-Wave simulation of a 3-mm-thick PMMA disc placed in water, varying the disc’s diameter from 15 to 40 mm. We simulated the attenuation of a 500 kHz transducer with the same specifications (e.g., inner and outer diameters and radius of curvature) as the transducer subsequently used in vivo, and first determined the − 1, − 3, and − 6 dB contours of the focus in free-field and then with the implant positioned in the path of the sound wave, observing a focus shift towards the transducer surface due to lensing (Fig. 2a). Upon increasing the implant’s diameter, attenuation became reduced, and the focal size was closer to that observed in free-field, with no further increases seen beyond a diameter of 30 mm. Next, using a PMMA implant 30 mm in diameter, we simulated a cranioplasty condition with an implant inserted into a burr hole, using the burr itself, free-field and an intact skull for reference (Fig. 2b). The simulated attenuation and focus profile for the cranioplasty condition showed good agreement with that of the implant in free-field (Fig. 2b). Attenuation was not significantly altered when the implant diameter was increased beyond 30 mm (Fig. 2c), confirming that a 15 mm edge distance was sufficiently large to eliminate edge effects. Together, this informed us for sheep procedures to use an implant with at least 30 mm in diameter and to locate the focus with a clearance of 15 mm from the boundary of the implant.

To deliver an intended PNP in sheep, because of a narrow FUS+MB treatment window, attenuation caused by an implant must be known. We therefore probed the implants in a water tank. To allow for multiple sonication spots per implant, guided by the above simulation, we selected 4-mm-thick ClearFit® flat discs (Cat # 171,709) with a roughly oval footprint measuring 4 × 5 cm, with beveled edges, as well as matching 3-mm-thick in-house PMMA implants, to determine the resulting transmission attenuation of the ultrasound waves by 3D tank scanning (Fig. 1c, d).

In free-field, 4-mm-wide and 35-mm-long − 6 dB focuses were produced at the geometrical focus of the transducer (Fig. 3a). In the presence of the implants, the focus was shifted upwards by 7 mm, with little change to size and shape. The transmission attenuation calculated from three measurements was 52.5 ± 6.0% for the in-house implant, and 14.4 ± 3.0% for the ClearFit® implant.

We next determined the attenuation by the implants for diverging ultrasound waves, mimicking PCD detection, by using a 6-mm-diameter 2.25 MHz flat transducer (Fig. 1e, f). Detection attenuation by the in-house implant ranged from 15 to 32% and was generally higher at higher frequencies (Fig. 3b). The theoretically obtained attenuation28 showed good agreement with the in-house implant, particularly at lower frequencies. A reason for less agreement at higher frequencies may be attenuation by absorption, which has not been factored into the theoretical considerations. The ClearFit® implants also produced variable attenuation within a similar range as the in-house implant, but with a different pattern for most frequencies. Together, the measurements suggest that it should be feasible to detect MBs through the implants, assuming improved sensitivity of PCD detection due to a significantly lower detection attenuation compared to an intact skull.

In vitro passive cavitation detection through implants

Given our intention to use cranioplasty to improve BBB opening in sheep which requires an interaction of FUS with microbubbles (FUS+MB), we next set up a system to measure cavitation through an implant using a PCD system. We measured two acoustic components, HH and BB emissions, given we have previously shown that both emissions are positively correlated not only with the PNP but also BBB opening5. For that, we flowed Definity® MBs (diluted 1:200 with saline) through a 0.8-mm-wide tube placed at the focal center of a 500 kHz FUS transducer, and measured the intensity of BB and HH emissions as a function of the driving amplitude in the presence and absence of the two implants (Fig. 1g, h).

A representative cavitation spectrum of MB emission detected through the in-house implant revealed strong BB emission, increasing from lower to higher frequencies compared to the baseline spectrum (Fig. 3c). HH emissions were also strongly detected although their magnitude at a higher order approached the level of BB emissions. Based on this observation, frequency bands for quantifying intensities of BB and HH emissions were restricted to 2 to 4 MHz.

In general, the intensities of BB emission for free-field and the two implants showed similar responses, increasing as a function of amplitude (Fig. 3d). More specifically, the free-field intensity was the strongest and the in-house implant the lowest, in agreement with the order of transmission attenuation. The onset amplitude of BB emission and the slope of the intensity increase correlated with transmission attenuation. When we plotted the PCD enhancement (relative to baseline) as a function of PNP, the three responses were similar. One-way ANOVA performed separately at 200, 400, and 600 kPa confirmed that no significant differences exist between the free-field and both implants in detecting BB emission. This suggests that the dominant factor differentiating these responses is transmission attenuation, whereas detection attenuation becomes negligible.

HH emissions for the three conditions fundamentally showed the same response as for BB emission except that the onset amplitude was lower, and intensities became saturated at higher amplitudes (Fig. 3e). The status of the cavitation field would be relevant for distinct PCD responses at different amplitude levels29. When the intensity enhancement was plotted as a function of PNP, intensities in the presence of the implants dropped at high PNPs because of the increased intensities of baseline HH emissions. At 400 kPa, by one-way ANOVA, we found significant differences between free-field and the in-house implant (p = 0.0073), and between free-field and the ClearFit® implant (p = 0.0067). Significant differences were also found at 600 kPa with the corresponding p-values being 0.0014 and 0.0024, respectively (Tukey’s post hockey test). No significant differences were found at 200 kPa. Together, our data suggested the feasibility of using PCD to determine MB emission in sheep sonicated through a cranial implant.

BBB opening in sheep through PMMA implants

Three in vivo cranioplasties were conducted in female Merino sheep, using the 3-mm- thick PMMA implant for sheep #1, and a 4-mm-thick ClearFit® implant, with otherwise identical dimensions, for sheep #2 and #3. After retraction of scalp and periosteum, a defect was created by manual burring of holes to admit a craniotome, before an implant-sized piece of skull was removed and then replaced by the implant (Fig. 4a, b). Sheep #1 received the implant longitudinally, and #2 and #3 transversely. Given these were non-recovery experiments, after placement of the implants, the sheep scalp was not replaced, and the ultrasound probe was applied directly onto the implants for sonication. The implant placed in the longitudinal orientation sat well in the skull defect, whereas the transversely placed implant was more difficult to secure due to the sharp curvature of the skull. We expect this to be a sheep-specific issue, as humans have a less acutely convex skull. To stabilize the head and facilitate treatment planning and image-guided navigation of FUS+MB with the Brainsight® TMS neuronavigation system, a thermoplastic mask was placed over the sheep head for restraint (Fig. 4c).

(b) Different from what has been done in the experimental procedure, here, for illustration purposes, the scalp is replaced. For sheep #1 (Fig. 5), after implanting the in-house PMMA disk, a post-implantation CT scan was obtained, clearly visualizing the implant with a density like that of the brain and other soft tissues (Fig. 5b–d). As noted above, the scalp was not replaced for the procedure, i.e. the ultrasound probe was applied directly onto the implant. Using a diagnostic ultrasound scanner (GE Venue GoTM system and a L4-12 T-RS superficial probe, GE HealthCare, USA), the sheep brain was imaged, which also confirmed good coupling of the probe (not shown).

Before MBs were injected and FUS+MB treatment was initiated, air bubbles beneath the implant were removed. This is not expected to be an issue with chronic implantation. Six sonication spots were planned, with two spots each at 278, 395 and 507 kPa, aimed at determining the PNP threshold for BBB opening (Fig. 5a). Once treatment planning was completed by aligning the sonication volumes with the CT scans (Fig. 5b–d), FUS+MB was performed. Definity® MBs were administered by i.v. infusion for each spot. For sheep #1, the six sonications were administered in a grid-like pattern over the course of one hour, without recoupling of the probe in between. Of note, the inhomogeneity of the ultrasound propagation at the edges of the implant found in the above k-Wave simulation (Fig. 2c) prompted us to apply a 15 mm clearance zone at the perimeter in all three sheep (that was however not experimentally tested). The sheep were infused with a commonly used agent to detect BBB opening, Evans blue, that fluoresces at 700 nm, binds to plasma albumin (68 kDa) and is normally excluded from the brain. This was followed by humane euthanasia of the sheep after 30 min. Brains were harvested, fixed in formalin, sliced horizontally, and imaged in a LiCor fluorescence scanner to observe BBB opening for spots 2, 3, 5 and 6, with spot 3 only showing opening for the dorsal sections. Spot 3 had received a high dose that was partly targeted to the ventricle due to being unable to see brain structures, including ventricles, on the CT scan (Fig. 5e, f). Of note, some dye also seeped a short distance along white matter tracts into nearby areas.

BB and HH emissions were successfully detected at intensities above baseline (Fig. 5g). At the three selected PNPs, average intensities of both emissions from two corresponding spots were comparable. Data points shown on the left were from spots 1, 2, and 3, and those on the right from spots 4, 5, and 6. At 278 kPa, BB emission was more than 10 dB stronger than baseline. Upon increasing the PNP to 395 kPa, the generated BB emission was not only stronger, but also more consistent. In contrast, the HH emission showed only a small intensity enhancement upon increasing the PNP. An increase to 507 kPa only slightly enhanced the intensities of both emissions. Since two BBB openings were observed from spots treated at 395 kPa and none at 278 kPa, we situated the BBB opening threshold between 278 and 395 kPa, based on which 425 kPa was selected for sheep #2.

Visual inspection of 1.5 mm slices revealed small petechiae for all spots except spot 1 (sonicated at 309 and 507 kPa) (Fig. 5h). We counted the number of petechiae per sonication spot in the dorsal 15 slices and found a positive correlation with the PNP. Grouping by PNP, no petechiae were detected in spot 1 and 4 in spot 4; 38 in spot 2 and 77 in spot 5; and 83 in spot 3 and 125 in spot 6. H&E staining was also conducted on 10 µm sections to further assess potential tissue damage. Using a previously established FUS safety grading system ranging from 0 to 330, the few bleeds were scored as petechial hemorrhages (grade 2); however, there was no obvious parenchymal damage as normally seen for grade 2 petechiae (Fig. 5i).

For sheep #2 (Fig. 6), the ClearFit® implant was used, and 13 sonications were performed in an S-shaped pattern, again facilitated by obtaining a CT scan after securing the implant in the skull bone (Fig. 6a, b). Sonication parameters were as for sheep #1, except for a PNP of 425 kPa. The input amplitude to the RF amplifier was accordingly adjusted to generate 425 kPa, reflecting 14% attenuation by the ClearFit® implant. Definity® MBs were infused at the same rate of 80 µl/kg/min, albeit diluted in a saline drip bag. Note that at the selected rate, 37.5 min of infusion is possible while meeting the maximal allowed dose of 150 µL/kg31. Since MBs were delivered by continuous infusion, the baseline could be obtained from spot 1 prior to infusing MBs, and from spot 13 after sonication was completed. During the procedure, MB infusion and sonications were paused after a possible MB-related cardiorespiratory reaction that required pharmaceutical intervention after spot 2. Here, acute hypocapnia, mild hypoxemia, mild hypotension, QRS complex widening on ECG, and bradycardia were observed. This was rectified by administration of vasoactive agents as well as antiarrhythmic and anti-inflammatory medication. Using the remaining MBs in the bag, the infusion was initially recommenced at a slower rate of 50 mL/h to rule out any further MB-related changes to hemodynamics. Once the sheep was assessed to be in a stable condition, MB infusion was continued at the original rate of 235.2 mL/h (80 µL/kg/min), and sonications proceeded. To complete the first two spots and then 11 sonications from spot 3 to 13, 6 min and 27 min were required, respectively, and the total volume of injected MBs was 135.6 mL. BBB opening was again visualized by Evans blue extravasation (Fig. 6c). Openings were detected across multiple slices except for spot 2. The lowest average intensities and the largest standard deviations of BB and HH emissions from this spot may indicate that the failure in BBB opening in spot 2 was likely aligned with abnormal MB delivery (Fig. 6d). BB emission from spots 12 and 13 was more comparable to that from spot 2 than any of the other spots, which differed from the HH emissions. Small areas of diffuse petechiae were seen macroscopically in the sonicated areas (Fig. 6e). 19 ± 12 petechiae were detected per spot except for spot 2, which was without petechiae. We scored the petechiae as low-grade 1, pertaining to scattered or lysed erythrocytes. In agreement, H&E staining failed to detect any erythrocyte extravasation or further tissue damage in the brain (Fig. 6f, g). Notably, susceptibility-weighted MRI sequences, used clinically for hemorrhage detection, lack the ability to detect grade 1 microhemorrhages30.

For sheep #3 (Fig. 7), the same procedure using the ClearFit® implant was performed as for sheep #2 except that 12 spots were treated in a 3 × 4 grid pattern using three PNPs, 278, 507, and 601 kPa (Fig. 7a, b). Definity® MBs prepared in a saline drip bag were initially infused at 50 mL/h while carefully monitoring the sheep vitals. Throughout the 10 min infusion, no significant changes in vitals were observed. Therefore, the infusion rate was increased to 230.4 mL/h (i.e., 80 µl/kg/min). After 1 min, sonication of spot 1 commenced and the remaining 11 spots were treated while MBs were delivered continuously. In total, 29 min was required for the sonications, and 137.7 mL of the diluted MBs were injected in total including the amount given at the slower rate. Two sets of baseline PCD responses using three PNPs were obtained prior to infusing MBs, one at spot 1 and one 5 min after the infusion was stopped upon completion of sonication at spot 12. BBB opening was again verified by Evans blue extravasation (Fig. 7c). No openings were detected in the first row (spot 1 to 4) treated at 278 kPa. In the second row treated with 507 kPa, weak openings were detected across a few slices in spots 6, 7, and 8. In the third 601 kPa row, openings were clearly detected from spots 9 to 12 across multiple slices. BB emissions were enhanced by 5 dB on average at 278 kPa, 34 dB at 507 kPa, and 41 dB at 601 kPa, while HH emissions were enhanced by 31 dB, 43 dB, and 47 dB, for the three PNPs relative to electrical noise, 0 dB (Fig. 7d). Of note, the absolute PCD intensities for sheep #2 and #3 were weaker than for sheep #1, which may be due to the absolute quantity of MBs circulating in the brain, since the MB dose is weight dependent. Sheep #1 weighed 60 kg, while sheep #2 and #3 weighed 49 and 48 kg, respectively. No bleeds were detected, but petechiae were observed with counts increasing in correlation with the PNP (Fig. 7e). Averaging over the four spots per row, 2 ± 2.6 petechiae were found in the first row, 30 ± 7.9 in the second, and 100 ± 36.9 in the third, respectively. The sonication spots with the highest petechia count for each PNP were stained with H&E for further inspection (spot 2 at 278 kPa, spot 7 at 507 kPa, and spot 10 at 601 kPa). No erythrocytes were detected at 278 kPa (data not shown). Erythrocytes were detected at the higher PNPs, with more seen at 601 than 507 kPa (Fig. 7f, g). Notably, most erythrocytes were contained within the lumen of blood vessels, suggesting that they are not due to local blood vessel damage. These were still counted as petechia in our analysis.

Collating BB and HH emissions obtained from three sheep revealed their strong correlation with the PNP, with R2 = 0.82 and p-value = 0.036 for BB emission (Fig. 8a) and R2 = 0.94 and p-value = 0.006 for HH emission (Fig. 8b). Of note, the BB emission was more responsive to PNP changes. PCD responses also showed correlations with histological outcomes. A higher PNP increased the probability of BBB opening (Fig. 8c), as well as petechia counts (R2 = 0.63 and p-value < 0.0001) from spots where opening was verified (Fig. 8d). Overall, no BBB opening was observed in spots treated with 278 kPa (n = 6) whereas significant BB emission was detected at 278 kPa, contradicting the widely believed concept that stable cavitation is the underlying mechanism of safe BBB opening.

Discussion

For safe and effective therapies of the human brain using transcranial FUS, the attenuating skull needs to be overcome in a predictable manner. One way to address this fundamental problem is to create a bony opening and implant the transducer into the skull32, another, as pursued here, is to replace a section with a sonolucent implant of defined and homogenous acoustic properties.

Here, we employed sheep to improve FUS+MB treatments for potential human applications, given the shared three-layered skull structure and guided by our previous work in sheep7. We present an extension of this work by using cranial implants for delivering FUS+MB into the brain. While performing cranioplasty in sheep, we encountered a few technical minor challenges. The sheep’s frontal sinus, implant size and sonication volumes resulted in limited transducer placement options. The contour of the implant was an imperfect match with the sheep cranium resulting in some compression of the underlying brain (not shown). In other relevant large animal or human studies, these issues would not present given the implants are purpose-designed for a better fit in a larger human skull. We also encountered trapped air bubbles underneath the implants and irrigated the space beneath the implant with saline after sealing leaks using bone wax. We believe the poor fit between the sheep skull and the implant contributed to this issue. Of note, with implanted cranioplasties in human subjects there are always a small number of air bubbles which however resolve over time33, and therefore, we anticipate this not being an issue in recovery animals or human subjects.

Before conducting animal experiments, we performed k-Wave simulations to determine the required dimensions of the implant to rule out edge effects, finding that it had to be at least 30 mm in diameter for the given transducer geometry, with the sonication spots (for multiple sonications through one implant) to be located with a clearance of 15 mm from the implant’s boundary. Non-linearity of ultrasound propagation was ignored, as no significant harmonics were detected in tank measurements at the pressure levels used in sheep. The magnitude of the second harmonic was -40 dB compared to the fundamental. Absorption was excluded in the simulation since little heating was expected at our low PNP and duty cycle34, and because PMMA has a similar absorption coefficient as skull35. We determined ultrasound attenuation of the implants at 500 kHz using a converging wave, and a frequency range relevant for PCD detection using diverging waves ranging from 0.5 to 4 MHz. This is the range mostly used in preclinical and clinical transcranial FUS+MB studies. The in-house and ClearFit® implants with a thickness of 3 and 4 mm, respectively, displayed a 52.5% versus 14.4% attenuation in transmission using a 500 kHz transducer, and broadly reflected BBB opening outcome in the three sheep.

In detection mode, both implants displayed similar attenuations ranging from 15 to 32% (in-house) and 11–36% (ClearFit®) over the measured frequency range of 0.5–4 MHz, probably due to wavelength-dependent interference of the implants28. Nonetheless, we posit that these attenuations are considerably lower than through an intact skull, thereby facilitating PCD detection. Indeed, in vitro PCD detection revealed that the implant-caused detection attenuation was negligible (Fig. 3d, e). Moreover, it allowed for detecting PCD responses from a large animal model with an unprecedented sensitivity, revealing that the threshold PNP of BB emission was even lower than that of BBB opening. The data suggests inertial or stable-inertial cavitation as a likely mechanism underlying BBB opening36,37, rather than stable cavitation and related mechanical effects5. Our hypothesis is further supported by a clinical trial using a skull-implanted transducer, SonoCloud (Carthera), that reported 0.85 MPa as the threshold PNP of BBB opening at 1 MHz32,38. Even though the SonoCloud system lacks a PCD, 0.85 MPa is sufficient to cause inertial or stable-inertial cavitation while achieving safe BBB opening, and the in situ PNP estimation for the SonoCloud implant is highly reliable as the transducer was implanted and thus directly interfaced with the brain.

By delivering FUS+MB through implants, we achieved, facilitated by Brainsight® neuronavigation and CT scans, BBB opening as evidenced by Evans blue extravasation for four sonication spots with a PNP of either 395 or 507 kPa in sheep #1 (in-house implant), 12 of 13 sonications at 425 kPa in sheep #2, and 7 out of 8 spots with PNPs of 507 and 601 kPa in sheep #3 (ClearFit® implant). The procedure was repeatable and the BBB opening outcome was highly reproducible as marked by 23 openings of 25 spots with a PNP above the threshold. Our study only involved three sheep procedures which are time-intensive and costly to perform, mirroring the low n numbers routinely observed in non-human primate studies34. We believe our data strengthen the concept of using FUS+MB through implants in human studies.

In sheep #2, spot 2 failed to achieve BBB opening. A likely reason is the reaction of the sheep to Definity® MBs causing a change in hemodynamics that resulted in reduced delivery of the MBs reflected by a weak PCD response, even though normal incident angles were maintained across all 13 sonication spots as confirmed by the Brainsight® system. MB injection in ruminants and pigs is known to result in pulmonary intravascular macrophage activation39. Activation of these phagocytes can lead to a sequence of events, beginning with ingestion of nanoparticles and release of thromboxane A2, resulting in vasoconstriction, bronchoconstriction and pulmonary hypertension, and in some severe cases, anaphylaxis40. During the procedure, the veterinarian confirmed that cardiac functions were stabilized based on ECG, vital signs, and capnography. This is supported by the fact that BB and HH emissions from spot 3 reached intensity and variance levels comparable to those from spot 1. Of note, the MB injection rate in the sheep (3.92 mL/min) was very close to that in the prescribing information for Definity® echocardiography, but the solution was 2.5 times more concentrated to arrive at an equivalent local MB concentration in our infusion protocol as we would have achieved with a bolus injection.

In our study at 500 kHz, we aimed to determine the PNP threshold for BBB opening. Based on Evans blue extravasation detected in two sheep, the threshold PNP was found to be between 278 and 395 kPa, with a corresponding mechanical index (MI) of 0.39 and 0.56, respectively. In our earlier sheep study using 286 kHz and a 1 ms pulse length, the lowest PNP in free-field that produced BBB openings was 1.4 MPa7. Reflecting 60% attenuation at 286 kHz based on our tank scanning7, the derated PNP is reduced to 0.56 MPa (MI = 1.05), which is similar to the lowest BBB opening PNP, 0.48 MPa (MI = 0.96), reported in another team’s sheep study using 250 kHz and a 10 ms pulse length41. As parameters other than the pulse length were quite similar between these two earlier studies, reducing the pulse length as a variable from 10 to 1 ms may be responsible for the slightly higher PNP threshold as shown in a rabbit craniotomy study42. The role that frequency has in determining the PNP threshold for BBB opening remains to be determined.

In human studies, threshold PNPs have been reported at three frequencies ranging from 250 kHz to 1 MHz (two transcranial and one intracranial). Bae and colleagues found 0.18 MPa as the minimum BBB opening PNP at 250 kHz (MI = 0.36)21, whereas a higher MI of 0.48 was required using the 500 kHz NaviFUS system43, and of 0.85 using the 1 MHz SonoCloud system32,38. This suggests that based on these human studies higher frequencies may require a higher MI for BBB opening; however, it should be noted that the PNP in the SonoCloud study had been determined by tank scanning, whereas that in the two transcranial studies were estimates based on simulation.

Overall, we verified the feasibility of BBB opening in combination with sonolucent implants in sheep with potential translatability to humans. Cranioplasty using implants with homogenous acoustic properties facilitated several aspects of the transcranial FUS+MB workflow. 3D tank scanning was sufficient to compensate for transmission attenuation, and the intended PNPs could be repeatedly delivered over multiple spots. This outcome may be predicted by PCD monitoring. Alternative approaches to achieve safe BBB opening transcranially such as target-based acoustic simulation21 or implementation of a PCD-based feedback controller31 were not required. Registration to the neuronavigation system was straightforward and highly precise by using the implant edges as landmarks.

It could be argued that the current study is limited to acute use of PMMA implants, with the question arising how chronic implantation might affect acoustic properties or biointegration. Chronic implantation of PMMA implants is well tolerated by patients and shows good long-term biocompatibility9,44,45. In one clinical retrospective review of 500 cranioplasty patients46, 89 received ClearFit® implants and the major complication rate for these patients was 12.6%, less than the major complication rate for all combined synthetic cranioplasty materials (14.5%) and for autologous bone (30.4%). The implants are chemically and mechanically stable in vivo and do not show any signs of change in acoustic properties. For smaller cranioplasties, bone regrowth can occur at the margins within the first 6–12 months post-operative45. Larger cranioplasties typically do not show the same level of regrowth.

Together, our study demonstrates the utility of both commercially available and in-house implants made of sonolucent, acoustically homogenous materials such as PMMA for achieving targeted FUS+MB-mediated BBB opening in the large animal model sheep as revealed using the model drug Evans blue. Our study also shows that k-Wave simulations are highly informative for deciding on transducer configuration, size of the skull implant to avoid edge effects, and furthermore which inner section of the implant can be used for the transmission of the ultrasound waves into the brain. Given the ease of the procedure, we foresee that cranioplasties will facilitate FUS+MB-based drug delivery especially for diseases such as glioblastomas which require craniotomy/cranioplasty and ongoing treatment monitoring as standard of care.
