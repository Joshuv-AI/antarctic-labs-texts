The Effectiveness of the Revised PEACE Pack With Primary School Students: A Positive Approach to Bullying Reduction and Wellbeing
Authors: Rosalyn H. Shute, Phillip. T. Slee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe PEACE Pack, originally devised to address bullying in secondary (high) schools, has demonstrated efficacy in several countries. The 4th edition now includes social–emotional learning and has been adapted (co‐designed with teachers and students) for primary (elementary) schools, but not previously evaluated with this younger age group. As many young people now acquire personal phones at this age, it covers cyberbullying as well as ‘traditional’ bullying.MethodsThis effectiveness study evaluated the program with 826 students (mean age 9.7 years) in Australia. Wellbeing (level of happiness and number of good friends), frequency of being bullied, and self‐efficacy for coping with bullying were measured before and after the program and at 3‐month follow‐up.ResultsA large effect was found for reduction in bullying frequency for the most severely affected students (bullied once a week or more often before the program, being a quarter of the sample). The percentage of this subsample still being bullied this frequently at 3‐month follow‐up was 33.4%, a reduction of 66.6%. Most other effects of the program were statistically significant but very small, although there was a general pattern of significant linear trends towards ‘improvement’. No evidence was found for a suggested ‘healthy context paradox’.ConclusionsThe PEACE Pack, as modified for primary school students, strongly reduced bullying frequency for the most severely affected students, indicating that the program was fulfilling its core function. In general, other measures improved slightly after the program and were sustained or still improving at the 3‐month follow‐up.So What?The PEACE Pack shows promise as a primary‐school anti‐bullying program that has sustained effects. It includes cyberbullying and social–emotional learning and is designed to be engaging for all students. Being curriculum‐based, it stands to benefit from students' relationships with their regular teachers within the broader school context.

Introduction

School bullying undermines young people's sense of wellbeing (e.g., [1]) and victim self‐efficacy [2], and is associated with poorer mental health (e.g., depression, suicidality) for both victims and perpetrators (e.g., [3, 4, 5]). Furthermore, over the last decade and a half, evidence has accrued that ill effects can be carried into adulthood (e.g., [6, 7]). Despite strong efforts around the world to reduce bullying, outcome effects of programs are generally modest [8, 9]. A recent meta‐analysis found an average of an 18%–19% reduction in bullying perpetration and a 15%–16% reduction in being victimized; however, results vary a great deal, and the reasons are uncertain [8].

Although reduction of bullying is the core measure of program effectiveness, wellbeing is sometimes assessed in terms of levels of anxiety or depression (e.g., [10, 11]). However, wellbeing is not just an absence of mental ill health, but a positive concept [12]. Positive psychological orientations have been identified as important to consider in developing intervention strategies that are effective: “Therefore, the cultivating and building of positive psychological orientations at school should be a priority and certainly a central consideration of bullying interventions and prevention programs” ([4], 1020).

In recognition of this changing approach, Moreno and Jurado [13] carried out a systematic review of programs taking a positive orientation to bullying prevention, concluding that they have a beneficial effect on various aspects of student wellbeing, as well as reducing bullying. However, the review was limited to programs for adolescents. Chicote‐Beato et al. [14], with a specific focus on cyberbullying prevention, noted that few studies have focused on the primary (elementary) years, even though many children acquire phones then, and cyberbullying and ‘traditional’ bullying are often intertwined. Furthermore, bullying often increases in late childhood [15], highlighting the importance of prevention in the primary years.

While some school anti‐bullying programs are presented by external experts (e.g., [10]), peers (e.g., [16]) or online (e.g., [8]), teachers have unique opportunities to contribute to bullying prevention, and to encourage prosocial behaviour [17]. Students have reported preferring to receive interventions from trusted teachers who genuinely care about bullying prevention, rather than from outsiders whose credibility is unknown to them [18]. As Kousholt and Fisker [19] have said, “teachers can exert a positive influence on the social dynamics within children's communities; bullying does not just crop up between various students with various dysfunctions, but the way teachers arrange students' opportunities to form communities is absolutely vital” (p. 596).

While many factors contribute to wellbeing, school is the most important influence after family, and children can report accurately on their subjective wellbeing from the age of 8 [20]. Connectedness, or being integrated into the social life of the school, is an indicator of wellbeing that can be measured in terms of number of close friendships. These are protective against loneliness, depression and victimization, including for bullied children [21], and are positively associated with prosocial behaviour and self‐esteem [22]. Another indicator of wellbeing is level of happiness, which can be defined in terms of frequently experiencing positive emotions [23]. Children in various countries who have ever been bullied or felt excluded at school are less likely to report being happy [24, 25]. Greater happiness is associated with later success in employment, health and social life [26]. It is debated whether this long‐term relationship is causal, but happiness is regarded as an important outcome in itself [27], and potentially increased by programs that address bullying.

We aimed to add to the primary school prevention literature by examining the effectiveness of a program that addresses bullying (including cyberbullying) within a positive psychology framework that concerns human flourishing [28]. The program was originally devised for secondary schools, but an adapted version for primary school students has been pilot‐tested but not previously evaluated. Here, we examine the effectiveness of the program as implemented for Australian students aged 7–11.

The PEACE Pack is a universal program developed in Australia and implemented and evaluated there [29] and, after translation, in a number of other countries (e.g., [30], in Italy; [31], in Greece; [32], in Malta). The acronym stands for Preparation, Education, Action, Coping and Evaluation, describing the process that schools move through in implementing the program. As well as aiming to reduce bullying and increase coping with it, its objective is to improve the social context for bullied children and promote social integration in the school setting. This wider perspective is important, as “no‐one in a social community is untouched by the presence of bullying” ([33], 15); for example, witnessing bullying uniquely contributes to symptoms of anxiety and depression in middle‐school students [34]. The PEACE Pack aims to develop key social and emotional skills when involved in, or witnessing, bullying, namely, self‐awareness, self‐management, responsible decision making, social awareness and relationship skills identified as important in coping [35].

The PEACE Pack is curriculum‐based and designed to be implemented over 8 weeks by teachers who have been intensively trained. It has been co‐developed with students and teachers, who have advised on what works in the classroom. This is important, as students ‘switch off’ if a program is not engaging or focuses on negativity (what not to do) [18]. As a universal program, the PEACE Pack takes account of the fact that many students in a class are likely to feel that bullying is not relevant for them, and the positive and skills‐based focus of the program aims to make it relevant and engaging for all students.

The original program was found to be effective in reducing bullying in Australia [29], and overseas, and there is also some evidence that it promotes positive psychological changes. For example, bearing in mind that bullying victims have low‐self‐efficacy, Guarini et al. [30] found that severe victims increased in self‐confidence after the program was implemented in Italy; students also reported that schoolmates were more likely to intervene in incidents. Murray‐Harvey et al. [36] identified coping with bullying (e.g., making an assertive response) as a positive psychological orientation important to promote in addressing school bullying, and a Greek version of the PEACE Pack found students to use more effective coping strategies after the program and to have greater self‐efficacy for coping with bullying [31]. Although this was not the case for severely bullied students in Malta [32], they felt more positively about school after the intervention. The present study included measures of self‐efficacy/confidence for coping with bullying, as well as bullying frequency, and number of close friendships and happiness as measures of wellbeing. The latter inclusion is in keeping with the systemic and positive psychology orientation of the PEACE Pack and with the recommendation of Hidayati et al. [37] that anti‐bullying programs should include social–emotional learning (SEL).

As well as examining whether various indices of effectiveness improve across the school as a whole, it is important to specifically examine whether the children who are most frequently bullied also benefit from the program, as found by Guarini et al. [30]. Although studies generally present bullying data across an entire school, a majority of students are not frequently bullied or not bullied at all, so overall data on bullying reduction can be misleading, potentially glossing over any effect of a program on the most severely affected students. Furthermore, it is important to include follow‐up measures, to examine whether any changes are sustained over time, as the intention of prevention programs is to achieve long‐term, not temporary, change. Of 79 studies included in Gaffney et al.'s [8] meta‐analysis, fewer than 20 utilized a follow‐up measure, and the length of time between before and after measures was quite variable. Another meta‐analysis, of randomized controlled trials of universal interventions, found that both bullying and mental health measures improved significantly after programs, with small effect sizes and, rather than fading over time, these changes were maintained at follow‐up (mean of 31 weeks later) [38]. For the PEACE Pack specifically, evidence for maintenance of effects after 3 months has been found previously for Greek high school students [31]. While it is certainly the intention that programs will have lasting effects, the possibility of an unintended consequence has recently been raised: that as the classroom environment becomes more positive, the wellbeing of the smaller number of victims may decrease (the ‘healthy context paradox’, e.g., [39]).

Hypotheses

Both for the sample as a whole and for those bullied at least weekly at the start of the program: The frequency of being bullied will reduce from Time 1 (pre‐program) to Time 2 (post‐program). Self‐efficacy for coping with being bullied will increase from Time 1 to Time 2. Happiness will increase from Time 1 to Time 2. Number of good friends will increase from Time 1 to Time 2. These changes will be sustained at follow‐up (Time 3). In accord with the suggested healthy context paradox, the happiness of children still severely bullied after the program will be lowered.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The sample consisted of 826 students aged 7–11 years (M = 9.7 years, s.d. = 1.1) drawn from several South Australian schools that had expressed interest in trialling the program. Overall, 70% of the schools were metropolitan and the remainder were near metropolitan or regional. There were 413 males (53%) and 364 females (47%); gender was missing for 49 (6%), and no student selected the ‘other gender’ option.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was adapted from one previously devised for secondary school students. It explained bullying as deliberate and repeated upsetting or hurting of others in various ways (n.b., whether or not bullying is explicitly described makes no difference to prevalence rates; [40]). Frequency of being victimized was assessed with the question: “How often this year have you been bullied by a student or students at school?” Responses were on a 6‐point scale:1 (bullied every day); 2 (bullied most days); 3 (bullied 1 or 2 days a week); 4 (bullied about once a week); 5 (bullied less than once a week); 6 (never bullied). This 6‐point scale has been widely used in PEACE Pack studies since the 2000s.

Following some questions about what could be done to address bullying (e.g., telling someone, use of school grievance procedures), two questions concerned self‐efficacy. One concerned sense of being able to cope: “Overall, how well do you or would you cope with school bullying?” 1 (very well); 2 (well); 3 (about average); 4 (poorly); 5 (very poorly). The other concerned confidence about knowing what to do: “If YOU were being bullied, do you know how to stop it happening?” 1 (yes); 2 (perhaps); 3 (no).

Happiness was measured using the 4‐item EPOCH (Engagement, Perseverance, Optimism, Connectedness and Happiness) scale [41]. The four items were: I feel happy; I have a lot of fun; I love life; I am a cheerful person. Response options to each were: 1 (almost never, not at all like me); 2 (hardly ever like me); 3 (sometimes like me); 4 (often like me); 5 (almost always, very much like me).

Friendships (as a measure of connectedness) were assessed by the question: “How many good friends do you have at this school?”: 1 (none at all); 2 (one); 3 (two or three); 4 (four to five); 5 (more than five).

Procedure

In line with national guidelines, the South Australian Government was committed to using evidence‐based programs, not only to address bullying, but to promote wellbeing in schools in a broad sense. The Minister of Education invited all public schools in the state to participate in the PEACE Pack program. Ten accepted, the majority being primary or middle schools. Ethics permission for implementing and evaluating the program was gained from Flinders University Social and Behavioural Research Ethics Committee (approval number SBREC 8130), the education authority and the schools. Parents were fully informed about the nature of the project. As the program was regarded as part of the curriculum, parenting consent was opt‐out.

A large‐scale teacher professional development day was run by two experts, for all teachers participating in the program, at the end of the first term in 2018. All were supplied with a printed or digital training booklet and manual. The manual gave detailed lesson plans, including discussion topics, exercises and games, and included a video of various bullying scenarios [42]. The program was delivered over 8 weeks in the second term, with pre‐ and post‐measures taken, and follow‐up measures 3 months later.

The program included content on physical, verbal, social, and cyber bullying. Activities were specifically designed to engage, encourage interest, and promote discussion amongst students and between students and teachers using group and pair discussion and role‐plays. In five lessons, the activities focused on one of the five SEL skills outlined previously. The program included a segment on the qualities of good friends, such as caring, helping, actively listening, and disagreeing without hurting each other, and addressed conflict‐resolution styles and how to work for a win‐win solution.

Student attendance rates were recorded by the teacher and typically involved 1–2 students absent per lesson. If a student was absent for a particular lesson, the teacher would provide them with a short verbal summary of the missed lesson and its focus at the following lesson.

The Education Department was provided with an overall report, and each participating school received its own de‐identified results. For pragmatic reasons (e.g., school unavailability), for some schools, questionnaires were not administered at all three time points.

The analyses were carried out by someone not involved in overseeing or implementing the program, using SPSS 27. Cases with missing data were deleted listwise. For determining whether parametric testing was appropriate, the acceptable limits for distributions' skewness and kurtosis were set at −1.5 to +1.5 [43]. For parametric ANOVAS with repeated measures, effect size is indicated by partial eta squared (Ƞ p 2 ): 0.01 (small effect); 0.06 (medium effect); 0.14 (large effect) [44]. For Friedman's (nonparametric) tests, effect sizes are indicated by Kendall's W, which follows Cohen's guidance of 0.2 (small effect), 0.3 (moderate effect), and 0.5 (large effect).

Results

Frequency of Being Bullied

Whole Sample

Initially, the data were examined to determine whether parametric analysis was appropriate. The distribution was within acceptable limits for Times 1 and 2, but not at Time 3, therefore nonparametric tests were used. A quarter of the sample reported being bullied once a week or more often, while three‐quarters said they were bullied less than once a week or never. Median scores across the 3 timepoints were 5 at Time 1 (less than once a week) and 6 at Times 2 and 3 (never bullied). Friedman's ANOVA (N = 531) yielded ranks of 1.93, 2.01 and 2.05 respectively (chi‐squared = 8.46, d.f. = 2, asymp. sig. = 0.015, Kendall's W = 0.008). That is, there was a significant but very small effect over time, weakly supporting Hypotheses H1 and H5 for the whole sample, i.e., there was an overall small reduction in bullying after the program, sustained at 3‐month follow‐up.

Severely Bullied Subsample

We further examined the data for a subsample, those bullied once a week or more often at Time 1 (n = 133). Although frequency does not necessarily equate to impact, this measure can be taken as an index of severity [32]. The data distribution at all three timepoints had acceptable skewness and kurtosis for parametric analysis, so the general linear model with repeated measures was applied. Means over time were 3.05, 3.97 and 4.25 respectively. The results are shown in Tables 1, 2, 3.

Interpretation of results for frequency of bullying over time, severely affected students.

Taken together, these analyses indicate that, for the most severely bullied students, there was a significant and large effect over time, with a significant linear trend (i.e., a straight‐line trend towards less frequent experiences of bullying), with some degree of levelling off by Time 3. In percentage terms, for this subsample, those reporting being severely bullied over time decreased from 100% to 51.5% to 33.4%, a total drop of 66.6%. The percentage reporting never being bullied increased from 0%, to 14.1% to 18.1%. These results strongly support Hypotheses H1 and H5 for the most severely bullied students, i.e., a large decrease in being bullied after the program, sustained (and still decreasing) by 3‐month follow‐up.

Self‐Efficacy for Coping With Bullying

Whole Sample

Across the three timepoints, all skewness and kurtosis values fell well within acceptable limits; therefore, the General Linear Model with repeated measures was applied (valid N = 493). Multivariate Pillai's Trace = 0.019, F = 4.69, d.f. = 2, 491, p < 0.01, Ƞ p 2 = 0.02 (small effect). For the within‐subjects effect, SS = 6.89, d.f. = 2, MS = 3.45, F = 5.02, p = 0.007, Ƞ p 2 = 0.01 (small effect), with a small but significant linear trend (p = 0.002) and quadratic trend (p = 0.04). M (s.d.) = 2.7 (1.16), for Time 1; 2.61 (1.20) for Time 2; and 2.63 (1.17) for Time 3. This supported Hypothesis H2 (improvement in coping self‐efficacy immediately after the program), and Hypothesis H5 (maintained, indeed still improving, after 3 months, though with some levelling off by then).

Severely Bullied Subsample

All skewness and kurtosis values fell well within parametric limits, therefore the General Linear Model with repeated measures was applied: M (s.d.) = 3.23 (1.17) for Time 1; 3.15 (1.33) for Time 2, and 2.96 (1.25) for Time 3. Valid n = 115. Pillai's Trace = 0.036, F = 2.13, d f. = 2, 133, 2‐tailed p = 0.12, with a significant linear trend (2‐tailed p = 0.04). However, the effect size was small (Ƞ p 2 = 0.04). Hypotheses H2 and H5 were supported, i.e., self‐efficacy for coping with bullying was somewhat increased after the program and this was sustained (and still increasing) at 3‐month follow‐up.

Confidence About Knowing Something to Do to Stop Bullying

Whole Sample

The percentages of students overall who felt they knew how to stop bullying at the three time points were 36%, 44%, and 45%. Those who ‘perhaps’ knew how to stop bullying fell from 38% to 32% then 31%, and the percentage responding that they did not know how to stop bullying reduced from 9% to 5% then 4%. Hence, confidence improved after the program and was maintained at follow‐up.

Severely Bullied Subsample

The pattern was similar, but higher percentages lacked confidence. The equivalent percentages were 28%, 35%, and 38% (for ‘Yes’), 51%, 50%, and 36% (‘Perhaps’), and 19%, 9%, and 4% (‘No’).

Happiness

Whole Sample

For each participant, a mean score between 1 and 5 was derived from the four EPOCH scale items. Skewness was acceptable at all three timepoints, but kurtosis was not at Times 2 and 3; therefore, Friedman's nonparametric test was used (N = 534). Mean ranks over time were 1.89, 2.04, and 2.07 (chi‐square = 11.83, d.f. = 2, asymp. p = 0.003, Kendall's W = 0.011). This indicates a significant but very small effect, weakly supporting Hypotheses H3 and H5 for the whole sample (increased and sustained happiness).

Exploratory analysis found that happiness was correlated with frequency of being bullied at all 3 time points (N = 707, 668 and 685, respectively); Spearman's rho = 0.38, p < 0.001; 0.35, p < 0.001; 0.28, p < 0.001, suggesting a reduction in the strength of the relationship over time.

Severely Bullied Subsample

As only 1 measure of normality slightly exceeded limits, the general linear model was applied and yielded no significant results, failing to support Hypothesis H3 (n = 177). To examine a possible healthy context paradox, for those still severely bullied at Time 3, their happiness was compared with that at Time 1. There was no significant difference and a small effect size (SS = 0.44, d.f. = 1, M.S. = 0.44, F = 1.78, p = 0.18 (n.s.), Ƞ p 2 = 0.01); therefore, Hypothesis H6, that happiness would decrease, was not supported.

Friendships

Whole Sample

The distribution of number of good friends was normal at all three timepoints (N = 718). M (s.d.) = 4.15 (1.03), 4.23 (0.94), and 4.27 (0.97). That is, students reported having at least 4–5 good friends throughout. The General Linear Model with repeated measures yielded a Pillai's Trace of 0.012, F = 3.51, d.f. = 2, 589, p = 0.03, with a significant upward linear trend over time (2‐tailed p = 0.01, Ƞ p 2 = 0.01) (small effect size). This weakly supported Hypotheses H4 and H5, that number of close friends would increase and be sustained over time (it was still increasing at follow‐up).

Severely Bullied Subsample

There was a normal distribution and the General Linear Model with repeated measures yielding a Pillai's Trace of 0.02, F = 1.72, d.f. = 2, p = 0.18 (n.s.) and a small effect size (Ƞ p 2 = 0.02). That is, the effect was small and not statistically significant, indicating little change in number of friends over time. M (s.d.) number of friends at the 3 timepoints was 3.9 (1.14), 4.0 (0.96), and 4.1 (1.02) respectively. The percentage with no friends at all at the 3 timepoints was 3.4, 1.1 and 1.7.

Discussion

The PEACE Pack, a universal intervention for secondary schools based on positive psychology, has been adapted for younger children, but not evaluated previously. In this study with primary‐school aged children in South Australia, the frequency of being bullied and several positive psychological measures of effectiveness were measured, namely, happiness and number of good friends (as indicators of wellbeing), and self‐efficacy/confidence for coping with bullying. Measures were taken immediately before and after the 8‐week program, and at the 3‐month follow‐up.

Before the program, most students (about three‐quarters) reported experiencing no or infrequent bullying, while almost a quarter reported being bullied at least weekly. Across the whole sample there was a small but significant reduction in frequency of being bullied after the program, despite the initial high ceiling (low levels of bullying), and this was maintained at follow‐up. The finding of a significant but small effect is consistent with other studies [9, 38]. It has been suggested that even small effects make mental health programs worthwhile at a population level [45].

For the most frequently bullied students, however, there was a large effect (reduction in victimization) immediately after the program, with a significant linear trend across the three timepoints. This indicates the importance of separately assessing effectiveness for the most severely affected students. The percentage of this subsample, 100% of whom were initially bullied at least weekly, was halved immediately after the program and down to a third at follow‐up, representing a considerable total reduction of 66.6%. The percentage reporting never being bullied increased from 0%, to 14.1% to 18.1%. These figures suggest that the program had bedded‐down in the schools, such that the neediest students experienced further benefit by 3‐month follow‐up.

There was a small but significant linear increase in reported ability to cope with bullying, for the sample as a whole and for the most bullied students specifically, which is in accordance with previous findings for this program with Greek secondary school students [31]. Children's confidence about knowing something to do if they were bullied increased over time, but fewer than a half overall felt sure, and only 38% of the most severely bullied students felt confident. This remaining lack of confidence is not surprising given that students may have witnessed, or experienced, tolerant or ineffective school responses to bullying in the past that undermined their expectations of success (e.g., [46, 47]).

For happiness, there was a very small significant increase across the whole sample, maintained at follow‐up. For the most severely bullied students, however, despite the large reduction in being bullied, there was no increase in happiness; this is consistent with the finding that once a child has been bullied, happiness may be reduced long‐term [24, 25]. Regarding a possible healthy context paradox, there was no evidence of this, in that those still severely bullied after 3 months, amidst a general reduction in bullying, experienced no further lowering of happiness. For the other measure of wellbeing, number of close friends, there was a very small but significant upward trend for the whole sample, despite the initially high numbers of friends reported by the majority, indicating maintenance of the effect. A small significant rise also occurred for the most severely bullied students, although some 60% of these reported having at least 4 good friends prior to the program. This indicates that, although friends generally act as a buffer against victimization, it is no guarantee. Young people may be bullied by their friends [48], although they may be reluctant to consider this as bullying [49], and quality of friendships was not examined in this study. The (already very small) number with no friends at all reduced over time. While few in number, these children need to be identified and provided with psychological assistance ([50]).

Limitations and Future Directions

At the time this study was carried out, the Education Department was not permitting the use of untreated control groups. This was therefore not a tightly controlled efficacy study, but an assessment of real‐world effectiveness with a convenience sample of schools interested in the program. This means that it cannot be ruled out that changes over time were due to factors other than the program, but the overall trend towards improvements is in accordance with PEACE Pack studies with older students. Furthermore, it seems very unlikely that such a sizeable effect for bullying reduction experienced by the most severely affected students would have resulted from some random effect and not from the program.

Further studies in contexts that permit the inclusion of control groups would strengthen confidence that any effects found are due to the program. It would also be useful to: expand measures of wellbeing (including assessing the quality of friendships); add longer‐term follow‐up measures (since there was still evidence of continuing improvement after 3 months); assess SEL skills targeted by the program; and examine whether the program results in a general improvement in school climate. Insights into staff and student experiences that might yield ideas for further program development could be gained through qualitative work, including teacher interviews and focus groups with students following program implementation.

Conclusion

The strongest finding to emerge from this study evaluating the PEACE Pack, as modified for primary school students, was the reduction in bullying frequency for the most severely affected students, indicating that the program was fulfilling its core function. It is also encouraging that there was no evidence of a ‘healthy context paradox’ further affecting the happiness of the still‐frequently‐bullied children. While other effects of the program, including on wellbeing, were very small, there was a consistent pattern of linear trends in the direction of ‘improvement’, indicating that effects were maintained, or still increasing, 3 months after the program ended. Despite the possibility of some programs having harmful effects [51], or leaving still‐bullied students with a drop in wellbeing, there was no evidence that this was the case here.
