Restricted Fubini Rankings and Restricted Unit Interval Parking Functions
Camilo Barreto, Pamela E. Harris, José L. Ramírez, Samuel Ramírez, Julio C. Vasquez

Abstract
. We study three natural types of restrictions on Fubini rankings and unit interval parking functions, which are motivated by their correspondence with ordered set partitions. For each restriction type, we define the corresponding subset of Fubini rankings and unit interval parking functions, establish enumerative results, and provide bijections between the restricted families. We also obtain exponential generating functions and combinatorial interpretations, including connections with exceedances in permutations and with the absence of cyclical adjacencies in set partitions.

05A15, 05A18

1.
Introduction

Throughout, we let
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
and
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Parking functions of length
n
are a set of tuples encoding the parking preference of cars parking on a one-way street that allow all cars to park on the
n
spots on the street.
More precisely, a tuple
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})\in[n]^{n}
has
a_{i}
as the parking preference of car
i
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Cars enter the street in order and parks in the first spot it encounters at or past its preference. If all cars are able to park under this parking scheme, then the tuple is called a parking function.
We let
\mathrm{PF}_{n}
denote the set of parking functions and recall that these were introduced by Konheim and Weiss
[
27
]
who established that
|\mathrm{PF}_{n}|=(n+1)^{n-1}
.

Since their introduction, parking functions have received a lot of attention, given their numerous connections to a variety of areas.
For example, parking functions have connections to hyperplane arrangements
[
15
,
36
]
, computing volumes of flow polytopes
[
4
]
, diagonal harmonins
[
20
]
, counting Boolean intervals in the Weak order of the symmetric group
[
16
]
, the Tower of Hanoi
[
3
]
, and the
Quicksort
algorithm
[
23
]
, among many such connections.
Generalizations of parking functions abound and include cases where the cars are able to back up
[
13
,
10
]
, cars are able to bump cars out of their parking spots
[
22
]
, where the cars have a variety of lengths
[
11
,
19
]
, where the cars have a fixed interval in which they tolerate parking
[
2
,
18
,
21
,
35
]
, and some in which certain spots are unavailable at the beginning of parking
[
1
]
.
There is also work on studying discrete statistics of parking functions to include counting the number of lucky cars, those cars that park in their preference
[
12
,
24
]
, and counting ties, descents, ascents, and fixed points
[
14
,

.
For more on parking functions, we recommend
[

]
and for an article with open problems see
[

]
.

Among subsets of parking functions, unit interval parking functions have been shown to have a rich combinatorial structure.
We recall that unit interval parking, introduced by Hadaway
[
21
]
, are the subset of parking functions in which a car parks either in its preference of in the spot after its preference. We let
\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
denote the set of unit interval parking functions of length
n
.
Hadaway established a bijection between unit interval parking functions and Fubini rankings, which are tuples encoding the results of a competition of
n
players in which ties are allowed.
We let
\mathrm{FR}_{n}
denote the set of Fubini rankings with
n
competitors.
Fubini rankings are in bijection with ordered set partitions, where
a set partition of
[n]
is a collection of pairwise disjoint subsets, which we call
blocks, whose union is precisely
[n]
, while an ordered set partition of
[n]
is simply a set partition in which the order of the blocks matter.
The number of partitions of
[n]
having exactly
k
blocks is the Stirling numbers of the second kind
A008277
, and the number of ordered set partitions of
[n]
having
k
blocks is the Fubini numbers (also known as the ordered Bell numbers)
A000670
.
Given the aforementioned bijections, the number of unit interval parking functions and the number of Fubini rankings is a Fubini number.

In this article, we are interested in the study of
S
-restricted Fubini rankings and
S
-restricted unit interval parking functions.
This is motivated by the connection of Fubini rankings and unit interval parking functions to ordered set partitions, and by the study of
S
-restricted set partitions.
Bényi and Ramírez
[
6
]
studied set partitions with the additional restriction
that the size of each block is contained in a given subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and they call these
S
-restricted set partitions. For more on restricted set partitions see
[

,

,

,

]

We introduce three types of restricted Fubini rankings and three types of restricted unit interval parking functions. We define those next and follow that with a list of our main results.

.

Let

\mathrm{UPF}
Type 1:

Bradt et al.

established a connection between unit interval parking functions and Fubini rankings. In this paper, we explore this relationship in more detail.
Let us begin by giving the technical definition of a Fubini ranking.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\beta=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in[n]^{n}
. We say that
\beta
is a
Fubini ranking
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

There exists at least one index
i\in[n]
such that
b_{i}=1
.

(2)

For every
x\in[n]
, if there are
k>0
entries of
\beta
equal to
x
, then the next possible value on
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}
that is greater than
x
must be
x+k
.

The set of all Fubini rankings of length
n
is denoted by
\mathrm{FR}_{n}
.
The distinct values appearing in a Fubini ranking are called
ranks
.

We illustrate the definition of Fubini rankings next.

Example 2.4
.

Consider
\beta=(1,4,2,2,6,4)
. It satisfies condition (1) of
Definition

2.3
since
1
appears in
\beta
. For condition (2), we verify that each value follows the required pattern: there is one occurrence of
1
, so the next value must be
2=1+1
; two occurrences of
2
, so the next must be
4=2+2
(as
3
is absent); and two occurrences of
4
, so the next must be
6=4+2
(as
5
is absent). If
6
were replaced by
5
, condition (2) would fail, and
\beta
would not be a Fubini ranking.

Note that every Fubini ranking is a parking function, for a proof see
[
21
]
. Next we define ordered set partitions.

Definition 2.5
.

An
ordered partition
of
[n]
into
k
parts (also called blocks) is a sequence
(B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{k})
of disjoint nonempty subsets of
[n]
whose union is
[n]
.
We let
{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
denote the set of all ordered partitions of
n
.

There is a natural bijection between ordered set partitions of
[n]
and Fubini rankings with
n
competitors.

To construct a Fubini ranking from an ordered set partition
B=(B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{k})\in{\mathcal{B}}_{n}
, we associate to it a word
(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n})
defined by setting
b_{i}=1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1}|B_{\ell}|
if and only if
i\in B_{j}
.
For example, the ordered set partition
(\{2,5\},\{1,3,4\},\{7,8\},\{6\})
corresponds to the ranking
(3,1,3,3,1,8,6,6)
. This construction defines a bijection between ordered set partitions and Fubini rankings.

The
Fubini numbers
(or
ordered Bell numbers
) count the number of
ordered set partitions
of a set with
n
elements, that is
\mathrm{Fub}_{n}=|{\mathcal{B}}_{n}|
for all
n\geq 0
. The
Stirling numbers of the second kind
, denoted
S_{2}\left(n,k\right)
, count the number of ways to partition the set
[n]
into
k
nonempty subsets, for more details about set partitions see
[
29
]
.
The Fubini numbers are given by (cf.
[
28
,
26
]
)

\mathrm{Fub}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}k!S_{2}\left(n,k\right).

They can also be expressed using binomial coefficients or as an infinite series:

\mathrm{Fub}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}j^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{m^{n}}{2^{m}}.

The first few Fubini numbers (sequence
A000670
on the OEIS
[
32
]
) are

1,\quad 1,\quad 3,\quad 13,\quad 75,\quad 541,\quad 4683,\quad 47293,\quad 545835,\quad 7087261,\dots.

We recall that the exponential generating function for the Fubini numbers is given by

\frac{1}{2-e^{x}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Fub}_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Moreover, Fubini numbers satisfy the recurrence relation:

\mathrm{Fub}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\mathrm{Fub}_{n-j}.

[

, which helps with the characterization of unit interval parking functions and gives a connection to set partitions.

Definition 2.6

(Definition 2.7 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{PF}_{n}
, and let
\alpha^{\uparrow}=(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{n}^{\prime})
be the weakly increasing rearrangement of
\alpha
. The
block structure
of
\alpha
, denoted by
\alpha^{\prime}=\pi_{1}|\pi_{2}|\cdots|\pi_{k}
, is the partition of
\alpha^{\uparrow}
into consecutive block
\pi_{j}
, where each block
\pi_{j}
starts at the
j
-th entry
a_{i}^{\prime}
of
\alpha^{\uparrow}
satisfying
a_{i}^{\prime}=i
. The elements
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}
are called
blocks
.

We illustrate the block structure of a unit interval parking function next.

Example 2.7
.

Consider
\alpha=(7,5,1,1,5,7,2,8,3)\in\mathrm{UPF}_{9}
. Its weakly increasing rearrangement is
\alpha^{\uparrow}=(1,1,2,3,5,5,7,7,8)
. Identifying the positions where
a_{i}^{\prime}=i
, we obtain the block structure
\alpha^{\prime}=1123|55|778
, with blocks
\pi_{1}=1123
,
\pi_{2}=55
, and
\pi_{3}=778
.

As we showed in
Example

2.2
, a rearrangement of a unit interval parking functions is not necessarily a unit interval parking function. However, certain rearrangements do preserve this property.
The following result, established by Bradt et al.
[
7
]
, characterizes these cases.

Theorem 2.8

(Theorem 2.9 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
and
\alpha^{\prime}=\pi_{1}|\cdots|\pi_{k}
be the block structure of
\alpha
. Then

(1)

A rearrangement
\sigma
of
\alpha
belongs to
\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
if and only if it preserves the relative order of the entries within each block of
\alpha
.

(2)

The number of such rearrangements in
\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
is given by the multinomial coefficient

\binom{n}{|\pi_{1}|,\dots,|\pi_{k}|}.

Remark 2.9
.

From the proof of the previous theorem, we highlight two key observations:

(1)

Each block in the block structure of a unit interval parking function
\alpha
is independent. That is, a car
c_{i}
in
\alpha
finds its parking spot occupied only by other cars within the same block of
c_{i}
.

(2)

The general form of every block
\pi_{i}
in the block structure of some
\alpha\in\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
is
\pi_{i}=kk(k+1)\cdots(k+\ell-2),
where
k=\min\pi_{i}
and
\ell=|\pi_{i}|
.

Bradt et al.
[
7
, Theorem 2.5]
provide a bijection between
\mathrm{FR}_{n}
and
\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
using two special functions that relate the block structure of a unit interval parking function to the different ranks of a Fubini ranking. These functions are defined as follows, see
[
7
, Lemma 2.6]
. The function

(1)

\displaystyle\phi:\mathrm{FR}_{n}\to\mathrm{UPF}_{n}

is defined by
\phi(\beta)=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
, where
\beta=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in\mathrm{FR}_{n}
and

a_{i}=\begin{cases}b_{i},&\text{ if }\hskip 8.5359pt|\{j\in[i]:b_{j}=b_{i}\}|=1;\\
b_{i}+k-2,&\text{ if }\hskip 8.5359pt|\{j\in[i]:b_{j}=b_{i}\}|=k>1.\end{cases}

Similarly, the function

(2)

\displaystyle\psi:\mathrm{UPF}_{n}\to\mathrm{FR}_{n}

is defined by
\psi(\alpha)=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
, for each
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
, where
b_{i}=\min\{a\in\pi_{j}:a_{i}\in\pi_{j}\}
and
\pi_{j}
is the
j
-th block of the block structure of
\alpha
.

Let us give an example of how these functions work.

Example 2.10
.

Consider
\alpha=(2,2,5,3,5,6,1,7,8)\in\mathrm{UPF}_{9}
. The block structure of
\alpha
is
\alpha^{\prime}=1|223|5567=\pi_{1}|\pi_{2}|\pi_{3}
. Applying
\psi
to
\alpha
, we obtain

\psi((2,2,5,3,5,6,1,7,8))=(2,2,5,2,5,5,1,5,5).

Finally, applying
\phi
to
\psi(\alpha)
recovers
\alpha
, as expected.

It is important to note that there exists a correspondence between the blocks in the ordered set partitions and the ranks in the Fubini rankings. The number of blocks is equal to the number of distinct ranks, and the number of elements in a given block corresponds to the number of ties at the associated rank in the Fubini ranking.

Combining
Theorem

2.8
with the fact that
|\mathrm{UPF}_{n}|=\mathrm{Fub}_{n}=|{\mathcal{B}}_{n}|
, Bradt et al. obtain the following identity for the Fubini numbers, see
[
7
, Corollary 2.13]
: For
n\geq 1
, let
(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\vDash n
denote a composition of
n
into
k
parts. Then, as described in

\mathrm{Fub}_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\vDash n}\binom{n}{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}}.

With these preliminary results at hand, we are now ready to introduce restricted unit interval parking functions and restricted Fubini rankings.

3.
Restricted Fubini rankings and Restricted Unit Interval Parking Functions of Type 1

In this section, we introduce the notion of
lucky cars
and describe their role in unit interval parking functions.
This concept is based on the block structure of elements in
\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
as was described by Bradt et al. in
[
7
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{PF}_{n}
. We say that car
i
is a
lucky car
if it parks in its preferred spot
a_{i}
.

For example, in the parking function
\alpha=(5,2,1,1,3,5,4)
, cars 1, 2, and 3 are lucky.
Whenever the parking function is also a unit interval parking function,
the number of blocks in its block structure is the same as the number of lucky cars.
We prove this next.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
. Then, the number of lucky cars is equal to the number of blocks in the block structure of
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
, and let
\alpha^{\uparrow}=(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{n}^{\prime})
be its weakly increasing rearrangement.
Denote the block structure of
\alpha
by
\alpha^{\prime}=\pi_{1}|\cdots|\pi_{m}
.
From
Theorem

2.8
, we know that the relative order of the elements within each block
\pi_{i}
is preserved in
\alpha
.
Moreover, by
Remark

2.9
, the blocks of
\alpha
are independent, and each block
\pi_{i}
has the form
\pi_{i}=(k,k,k+1,\dots,k+\ell-2)
, where
\ell=|\pi_{i}|
is the number of elements in
\pi_{i}
, and
k=\min\pi_{i}
.
Since the first car in each block can always park in its preferred spot,
\alpha
has at least
m
lucky cars.

On the other hand, suppose that cars
i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}
are the
\ell
cars with preferences in
\pi_{i}
, where
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell}
.
Then, their parking preferences are
a_{i_{1}}=k
,
a_{i_{2}}=k,\dots,a_{i_{\ell}}=k+\ell-2
.
Hence, the parking process proceeds as follows: car
{i_{1}}
parks in spot
k
. Car
{i_{2}}
attempts to park in spot
k
, but since it is occupied, it parks in spot
k+1
. Car
{i_{3}}
parks at the next available spot, which is numbered
k+2
, and so on.
Thus, the only lucky car in
\pi_{i}
is car
{i_{1}}
.
Since each block
\pi_{i}
contributes exactly one lucky car, it follows that the number of lucky cars in
\alpha
is exactly the number of blocks, which is
m
, as claimed.
∎

We now introduce our main object of study in this section, which is unit interval parking functions with a number of lucky cars in a given set of integers.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
S
be a subset of positive integers. We define
\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
as the set of unit interval parking functions of length
n
such that the number of lucky cars belongs to
S
. If
\alpha\in\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
, we say that
\alpha
is
S
-restricted unit interval parking functions of type 1
.

For example, consider
\alpha=(1,4,4,1,5,2,7,6)\in\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n}
. The block structure of
\alpha
is
\alpha^{\prime}=112|4456|7
, which, by
Theorem

3.2
, implies that
\alpha
has exactly three lucky cars. Hence,
\alpha\in\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{8,\{1,2,3\}}
. By contrast, let
\beta=(1,3,4,7,4,6,2,7)
. Since
\beta
has six lucky cars, we conclude that
\beta\notin\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{8,\{1,2,3\}}
.

Definition 3.4
.

We define
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
as the set of Fubini rankings with
n
competitors such that the number of distinct ranks belongs to
S
.
The set
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
, is called the set of
S
-restricted Fubini rankings of type 1
.

For example, consider
\gamma=(1,1,1,4,5,6,7,7,9)\in\mathrm{FR}_{9}
, which has 6 ranks, and so
\gamma\in\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{9,S}
, for any
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{+}
provided
6\in S
. However,
\gamma\notin\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{9,\{1,5\}}
, as
6\notin\{1,5\}
.

In the proof of the following result we use the functions
\phi
and
\psi
defined in (
1
) and (
2
), respectively.

Theorem 3.5
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
and any set
S
of positive integers, the set
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
is in bijection with
\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
.

Proof.

Given
\alpha\in\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
, we know from
Theorem

3.2
that the number of blocks in
\alpha^{\prime}
(the block structure of
\alpha
) belongs to
S
. According to
[
7
, Lemma 2.11]
, each block in
\alpha^{\prime}
is mapped by
\psi
to a list of identical entries in
\psi(\alpha)
. These lists determine the distinct ranks in the Fubini ranking
\psi(\alpha)
, which implies that
\psi(\alpha)\in\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
.

Conversely, let
\beta\in\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
. Then, there exist
k
distinct values among the entries of
\beta
for some
k\in S
. Each list of all entries with a unique different value of
\beta
(what we call
competitors in the same rank
) is transformed by
\phi
into a block of the unit interval parking function
\phi(\beta)
. Since all blocks of
\phi(\beta)
are generated in this way, we conclude, by
Theorem

3.2
, that
\phi(\beta)\in\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}
.
∎

For a fixed subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we can give a closed formula for the exponential generating function for
\{|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}|\}_{n\geq 1}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

If
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, then

\sum_{n\geq 0}|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}|\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{k\in S}(e^{x}-1)^{k}.

Proof.

We recall that unit interval parking functions are counted by Fubini numbers, meaning that for all
n\geq 0
, we have
\mathrm{Fub}_{n}=|\mathrm{UPF}_{n}|
. Now, from the bijection given in
Theorem

3.5
, it follows that
|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}|=\sum_{k\in S}k!S_{2}\left(n,k\right)
. Since the exponential generating function (e.g.f.) of the sequence
\{k!\left\{S_{2}\left(n,k\right)\right\}\}_{n\geq 0}
is given by
(e^{x}-1)^{k}
, we obtain that

\sum_{n\geq 0}|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}|\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{k\in S}(e^{x}-1)^{k}.\qed

We now specialize to certain subsets of the integers and give the e.g.f. for those cases. Remember,
that
|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{0,S}|=|\{\emptyset\}|=1
.

Corollary 3.7
.

The closed-form e.g.f. of the counting sequence
\{|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}|\}_{n\geq 0}
for some special choices of
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{+}
are as follows.

(1)

If
S=\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}:k
is even
\}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S}(e^{x}-1)^{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(e^{x}-1)^{2k}=\frac{1}{1-(e^{x}-1)^{2}}.

The first few terms of this sequence correspond to OEIS sequence
A052841
:

1,0,2,6,38,270,2342,23646,272918,3543630,51123782,\dots

(2)

If
S=\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}:k
is odd
\}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S}(e^{x}-1)^{k}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(e^{x}-1)^{2k+1}=\frac{e^{x}-1}{1-(e^{x}-1)^{2}}.

The first few terms of this sequence correspond to OEIS sequence
A089677
:

1,1,7,37,271,2341,23647,272917,3543631,51123781,\dots

(3)

Fix
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. If
S=\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}:k\geq m\}
or
S=\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}:k\leq m\}
, then the corresponding generating functions are

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S}(e^{x}-1)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq m}(e^{x}-1)^{k}=\frac{(e^{x}-1)^{m}}{2-e^{x}}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S}(e^{x}-1)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(e^{x}-1)^{k}=\frac{1-(e^{x}-1)^{m+1}}{2-e^{x}},

respectively.

Proof.

All of the statements are proved by using the geometric series
1/(1-x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}x^{k}
and the finite geometric sum
(1-x^{m+1})/(1-x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}x^{k}
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The sum of the sequences appearing in items (1) and (2) of
Corollary

3.7
yields the Fubini numbers.

We can also obtain some closed formulas for these functions.

Corollary 3.9
.

For all
n\geq 0
, if
S=2\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, then

\displaystyle|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,2{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}|=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sum_{i=0}^{2k}\binom{2k}{i}(-1)^{i}i^{n}.

Proof.

To determine
|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,2{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}|
for all
n\geq 0
, we can first expand
(e^{x}-1)^{2k}
using the binomial theorem for a fixed integer
k
with
2k\leq n
. Doing so yields

\displaystyle(e^{x}-1)^{2k}=\sum_{i=0}^{2k}\binom{2k}{i}e^{ix}(-1)^{2k-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 2k}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{2k}\binom{2k}{i}(-1)^{i}i^{n}\right)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Therefore,

|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,2{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}|=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sum_{i=0}^{2k}\binom{2k}{i}(-1)^{i}i^{n}.\qed

In a similar manner, one can establish the following result using exponential generating functions. However, we present a combinatorial proof instead.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
S\subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be a set of positive integers. Then

|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,S}|=\sum_{m\in S}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}j^{n}.

Proof.

First, by the principle of addition, it is enough to prove that the number of unit interval parking functions in
\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
with exactly
m
lucky cars is given by

\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}j^{n}.

We prove this using the principle of inclusion-exclusion.

By
Theorem

3.2
, a unit interval parking function
\alpha\in\mathrm{UPF}_{n}
has
m
lucky cars if and only if the number of blocks in
\alpha
’s block structure is
m
.
Thus, we seek to count the number of unit interval parking functions whose block structure consists of exactly
m
blocks.
Moreover, by
Theorem

2.8
, it is not necessary for all cars to specify their favorite spot, it suffices to determine which block they prefer, since the cars within each block park in the relative order of their block and the structure of the block (see
Remark

2.9
).

Thus, we count the number of list of block preferences in which every block is chosen by at least one car. In other words, we are counting the size of the set

S_{n}=\{(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in[m]^{n}:\text{for every $1\leq i\leq m$ there exist $1\leq k\leq n$ such that $b_{k}=i$}\}.

Equivalently, we have
|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{m\}}|=|S_{n}|
.

Now, define

A_{i}=\{(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in[m]^{n}:\text{there is no $1\leq k\leq n$ such that $b_{k}=i$}\}

for each
1\leq i\leq m
. Since
S_{n}=[m]^{n}-\bigcup_{n=1}^{m}A_{i}
, and, it follows that

(3)

\displaystyle|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{m\}}|=m^{n}-\left|\bigcup_{n=1}^{m}A_{i}\right|.

Applying the principle of inclusion-exclusion, we obtain

\left|\bigcup_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}\right|=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq m}\left|A_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_{j}}\right|.

To compute
|A_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_{j}}|
, suppose
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq m
and observe that the elements of this intersection are
n
-tuples
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in[m]^{n}
such that
a_{k}\notin\{i_{1},\dots,i_{j}\}
for every
1\leq k\leq n
. This implies
A_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_{j}}=([m]-\{i_{1},\dots,i_{j}\})^{n}
, so its size is given by

|A_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_{j}}|=(m-j)^{n}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\bigcup_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq m}|A_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap A_{i_{j}}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}\leq m}(m-j)^{n}

(4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\binom{m}{j}(m-j)^{n}.

Finally, substituting (
4
) into (
3
), the previous expression for
|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{m\}}|
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{m\}}|

\displaystyle=m^{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\binom{m}{j}(m-j)^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\binom{m}{j}(m-j)^{n}.

Using the symmetry of the binomial coefficient

\binom{m}{j}=\binom{m}{m-j}
for
0\leq j\leq m
, we rearrange the sum to obtain

\displaystyle|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{m\}}|=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-j}\binom{m}{j}j^{n},

which is the desired formula.
∎

3.1.
UPFs without cyclical adjacencies

We consider a class of unit interval parking functions closely related to
\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,2{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}}
. Since unit interval parking functions of length
n
correspond to ordered set partitions of
[n]
(they are both counted by Fubini numbers), the set
\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,2\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
is in bijection with ordered set partitions of
[n]
with an even number of blocks, recalling
Theorem

3.2
. This motivates the introduction of a new class of ordered set partitions, see comments in the sequence
A052841
in the OEIS.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
\bm{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{k})
be an ordered set partition of
[n]
. We say that
\bm{C}
has
no cyclical adjacencies
if, for every
i=1,\dots,k
and every
j=1,\dots,n-1
, the following conditions hold:

(1)

j
and
j+1
do not belong to the same block
C_{i}
, and

(2)

1
and
n
do not belong to the same block
C_{i}
.

The set of all ordered set partitions of the set
[n]
with no cyclical adjacencies is denoted by
\mathscr{O}_{n}
.

For example,
(\{2,4,6\},\{3,5\},\{1\})
and
(\{4,6\},\{3,5\},\{1,3\})
are elements of
\mathscr{O}_{6}
, while
(\{1,2,3\},\{4\},\{5,6\})\notin\mathscr{O}_{6}
.

Let
\mathscr{L}_{n}
be the set of ordered set partitions of
[n]
with an even number of blocks. Given any
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{2k})\in\mathscr{L}_{n}
, we define the relation
\prec
on
[n]
as follows:

•

a\prec b
if
a<n
and
b=a+1
,

•

n\prec 1
.

Whenever
a\prec b
, we refer to
a
as the
cyclical predecessor
of
b
.

For the next theorem, we need the following function
\lambda_{n}:\mathscr{L}_{n}\rightarrow\mathscr{O}_{n}
via the following algorithm:

(1)

Initialize
i=1
and set
B_{2k+1}=\emptyset
.

(2)

If
i\leq 2k
, proceed to step (3), otherwise, go to step (4).

(3)

For each chain of elements in
B_{i}
of the form
j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{t}
(with
t<n
), where
j_{s}\prec j_{s+1}
and
(j_{s},j_{s+1})
forms a cyclical adjacency for
s=1,\dots,t-1
, remove the elements
\{j_{2},j_{4},\ldots,j_{\gamma(t)}\}
from
B_{i}
and place them in
B_{2k+1}
, where

\gamma(t)=\begin{cases}t,&\text{if }t\text{ is even;}\\
t-1,&\text{if }t\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

Increment
i
and return to step (2).

(4)

Finally, define
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})=(B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{2k},B_{2k+1})
.

Clearly,
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})
is a partition of
[n]
. Moreover, every block in
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})
avoids cyclical adjacencies because we remove the adjacencies of the original blocks
B_{1},B_{2},\ldots,B_{2k}
of
\mathbf{B}
in the previous algorithm, and the block
B_{2k+1}
consists of elements from different chains (which are thus nonadjacent) or from chains where adjacency has been explicitly removed. Hence,
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})\in\mathscr{O}_{n}
proving that
\lambda_{n}
is well defined.

Let us give an example of how this function works.

Example 3.12
.

We consider all elements of
\mathscr{L}_{4}
that contain cyclical adjacencies and provide the image under
\lambda_{4}
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}(\{1,2,3\},\{4\})=(\{1,3\},\{4\},\{2\}),\quad\lambda_{4}(\{4\},\{1,2,3\})=(\{4\},\{1,3\},\{2\}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}(\{1\},\{2,3,4\})=(\{1\},\{2,4\},\{3\}),\quad\lambda_{4}(\{2,3,4\},\{1\})=(\{2,4\},\{1\},\{3\}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}(\{1,2\},\{3,4\})=(\{1\},\{3\},\{2,4\}),\quad\lambda_{4}(\{3,4\},\{1,2\})=(\{3\},\{1\},\{2,4\}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}(\{1,3,4\},\{2\})=(\{1,3\},\{2\},\{4\}),\quad\lambda_{4}(\{2\},\{1,3,4\})=(\{2\},\{1,3\},\{4\}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}(\{1,4\},\{2,3\})=(\{4\},\{2\},\{1,3\}),\quad\lambda_{4}(\{2,3\},\{1,4\})=(\{2\},\{4\},\{1,3\}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}(\{1,2,4\},\{3\})=(\{2,4\},\{3\},\{1\}),\quad\lambda_{4}(\{3\},\{1,2,4\})=(\{3\},\{2,4\},\{1\}).

Theorem 3.13
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
, the function
\lambda_{n}:\mathscr{L}_{n}\rightarrow\mathscr{O}_{n}
is a bijection.

Proof.

Injectivity:
Suppose
\mathbf{A}=(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{q})
and
\mathbf{B}=(B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{p})
are distinct elements of
\mathscr{L}_{n}
, meaning there exists some
j\in[1,\max(p,q)]
such that
A_{j}\neq B_{j}
. By following the construction of
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})
and
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})
, at least one of the following must hold:

(1)

The elements of
A_{j}
in
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})
differ from those of
B_{j}
in
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})
.

(2)

The elements of
A_{q+1}
in
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})
differ from those of
B_{p+1}
in
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})
.

Thus,
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})\neq\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})
, proving injectivity.

Surjectivity:
Given any
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{p})\in\mathscr{O}_{n}
, we construct a preimage
\mathbf{B}\in\mathscr{O}_{n}
such that
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{C}
. If
\mathbf{C}
has an even number of blocks, then
\mathbf{C}\in\mathscr{O}_{n}
, and by the algorithm,
\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{C})=\mathbf{C}
.

If
\mathbf{C}
has an odd number of blocks, take the last block
C_{p}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}\}
, where
m<n
(since
\mathbf{C}
does not contain all elements of
[n]
). Move each
c_{l}
to the same block as its cyclical predecessor. The resulting partition has an even number of blocks, and following the algorithm, its image under
\lambda_{n}
is precisely
\mathbf{C}
.

Thus,
\lambda_{n}
is bijective.
∎

3.2.
Exceedances of permutations and unit interval parking functions

Throughout we let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the set of all permutations of
[n]
. We begin with the definition of an exceedance of a permutation.

Definition 3.17
.

Given a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, an

in
\sigma
is an index
j\in[n]
such that
\sigma(j)>j
.

For example, the exceedances in
\sigma=24318576
are at indices
1,2
, and
5
.

Remark 3.18
.

It is possible to show that the number of exceedances among all permutations of
[n]
is
(n-1)\frac{n!}{2}
, see comments in the sequence
A001286
in the OEIS.

Since
\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{n-1\}}
is in bijection with the set of all ordered set partitions of
[n]
with
n-1
blocks, we can define the function
\gamma_{n}:\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{n-1\}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Exc}_{n}
, where
\mathrm{Exc}_{n}
is the set of exccedances in all permutations of
[n]
as follows:
Let

a_{i+1},&\text{ if }j=a_{i};\\

The function

Example 3.20

Where we use the bold number to emphasize the exceedance that is in the image of the ordered partition.

Theorem 3.21

For

,
the function
\gamma_{n}:\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{n-1\}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Exc}_{n}
is a bijection. In particular,
|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{n-1\}}|=(n-1)\frac{n!}{2}
, the number of exceedances of the permutations of
[n]
.

Proof.

Injectivity:
Let
\bm{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{i},\dots,B_{n-1})=(\{a_{1}\},\dots,\{a_{i},a_{i+1}\},\dots,\{a_{n}\})
and
\mathbf{B^{\prime}}=(B^{\prime}_{1},\dots,B^{\prime}_{i},\dots,B^{\prime}_{n-1})=(\{a^{\prime}_{1}\},\dots,\{a^{\prime}_{j},a^{\prime}_{j+1}\},\dots,\{a^{\prime}_{n}\})
two partitions of
[n]
such that
\gamma_{n}(\bm{B})=\gamma_{n}(\mathbf{B^{\prime}})
. Then,

a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{a_{i}-1}a_{i+1}a_{a_{i}}a_{a_{i}+1}\cdots a_{n}=a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}\cdots a^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}_{j}-1}a^{\prime}_{j+1}a^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}_{j}}a^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}_{j}+1}\cdots a^{\prime}_{n}

Note that, since
\gamma_{n}(\bm{B})=\gamma_{n}(\mathbf{B^{\prime}})
, we are looking at the same exceedance (i.e the same entry of the permutation). Hence,
a_{i}=a^{\prime}_{j}
and
a_{i+1}=a^{\prime}_{j+1}
, this means both partitions have the same blocks, but may be not in the same order. However, by the definition of
\gamma_{n}
, since
a_{i}=a^{\prime}_{j}
and
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{a_{i}-1}a_{i+1}a_{a_{i}}a_{a_{i}+1}\cdots a_{n}=a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}\cdots a^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}_{j}-1}a^{\prime}_{j+1}a^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}_{j}}a^{\prime}_{a^{\prime}_{j}+1}\cdots a^{\prime}_{n}
,
i=j
otherwise, the value
a_{i}
would appear in two distinct indices of the permutation, which is a contradiction, and this implies that
\bm{B}=\bm{B^{\prime}}
.

Surjectivity:
Let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}
be a permutation of
[n]
with an exceedance in
j\in[n-1]
, that is
\sigma_{j}>j
. Define a partition of
[n]
,
\bm{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{n-1})
as follows:

\bullet

if
\sigma^{-1}_{j}<j
, then

B_{i}:=\begin{cases}\{\sigma_{i}\},&\textit{ if }i<j\textit{ and }i\neq\sigma^{-1}_{j};\\
\{\sigma_{i+1}\},&\textit{ if }i\geq j;\\
\{j,\sigma_{j}\},&\textit{ if }i=\sigma^{-1}_{j}.\end{cases}

\bullet

if
\sigma^{-1}_{j}>j
then

B_{i}:=\begin{cases}\{\sigma_{i}\},&\textit{ if }i<j;\\
\{\sigma_{i+1}\},&\textit{ if }i\geq j\textit{ and }i\neq\sigma^{-1}_{j}-1;\\
\{j,\sigma_{j}\},&\textit{ if }i=\sigma^{-1}_{j}-1.\end{cases}

In both cases, by the definition of

.

Hence,

is a bijection, and thus

|\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T1}}_{n,\{n-1\}}|=(n-1)\frac{n!}{2},

as desired.
∎

4.
Restricted Fubini rankings and Restricted Unit Interval Parking Functions of Type 2

Definition 4.1
.

We define
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
as the set of Fubini rankings with
n

in

in

belongs to

.
The set
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
is called the set of
S
-restricted Fubini rankings of type 2
.

Intuitively, an element of
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
is a ranking where a tie among

is an element of

.
We give some initial results for restricted Fubini rankings of type 2.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

.

(1)

If

, then
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the set of pemrutations of
[n]
.

(2)

If
S=\{n\}
, then
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}=\{(1,1,\ldots,1)\in[n]^{n}\}
.

(3)

If

\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}=\mathrm{FR}_{n}
, and the e.g.f. of the counting sequence

is given by
\frac{1}{2-e^{x}}
.

(4)

If

, then the e.g.f. of the counting sequence
\{|\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}|\}_{n\geq 0}
is
\frac{1}{2-\cosh{x}}
.

(5)

If

, then the e.g.f. of the counting sequence
\{\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}\}_{n\geq 0}
is
\frac{1}{1-\sinh{x}}
.

Proof.

We consider each case below.

(1)

If
S=\{1\}
, then
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
consists of all permutations of
n
, as only one participant is allowed per position.

(2)

If
S=\{n\}
, then the only element of
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
is the tuple of
n

n
participants must occupy the same position.

(3)

If

, then
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
includes all elements of
\mathrm{FR}_{n}
, as ties of any size are permitted. In this case,
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
is the set of all ordered set partitions of
[n]
, and the e.g.f. of the counting sequence
\{|\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}|\}_{n\geq 0}
is given by
\frac{1}{2-e^{x}}
.

(4)

If

, then
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
is the set of all ordered set partitions of
[n]
only in blocks with an even number of elements, and the e.g.f. of the counting sequence
\{|\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}|\}_{n\geq 0}
is
\frac{1}{2-\cosh{x}}
.

(5)

If
S=\{2k+1\ |\ k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\}
, then
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
is the set of all ordered set partitions of
[n]
only in blocks with an odd number of elements, and the e.g.f. of the counting sequence
\{\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}\}_{n\geq 0}
is
\frac{1}{1-\sinh{x}}
.∎

Next we give a formula for the number of
S
-restricted Fubini rankings of type 2 when
S
is a singleton set.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
S=\{k\}
for some
k
that divides
n
, then
|\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}|=\frac{n!}{(k!)^{n/k}}
.

Proof.

We can arrange
n
elements in
n!
ways. Since each block must have exactly
k
elements, we can split the permutation into consecutive blocks of size
k
. There are
k!
ways to arrange elements within each block of size
k
, since there are
n/k
such blocks, the total number of ways to arrange the elements of a permutation without changing content of the blocks is
(k!)^{n/k}
. By dividing by this number we eliminate the overcounting that arises from permuting the elements within each block, and thus the number of
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
is
\frac{n!}{(k!)^{n/k}}
.
∎

We now give a formula for the number of
S
-restricted Fubini rankings of type 2 for any finite set
S
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{j}\}
be a finite subset of positive integers integers, and let
C=\{\bm{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{j}):\sum_{i=1}^{j}s_{i}c_{i}=n\}
. Then

\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}=n!\sum_{\bm{c}\in C}\binom{\sum_{k=1}^{j}c_{k}}{c_{1},\dots,c_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{j}(s_{i})!^{-c_{i}}.

Proof.

We can arrange
n
elements in
n!
ways. Given a partition tuple
\bm{c}\in C
, we can split the permutation into
\sum_{k=1}^{j}c_{k}
consecutive blocks. To assign sizes to these blocks according to the counts in
\bm{c}
, we simply choose how to group the
\sum_{k=1}^{j}c_{k}
blocks into categories:
c_{1}
blocks of size
s_{1}
,
c_{2}
of size
s_{2}
, and so on. The number of ways to do this is given by the multinomial coefficient:

\binom{\sum_{k=1}^{j}c_{k}}{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{j}}.

Next, we account for internal arrangements within each block. Since a block of size
s_{i}
can be arranged in
s_{i}!
ways, and there are
c_{i}
such blocks, we must divide by
(s_{i}!)^{c_{i}}
to avoid overcounting. Thus, the number of corresponding ordered set partitions is

n!\binom{\sum_{k=1}^{j}c_{k}}{c_{1},\dots,c_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{j}(s_{i})!^{-c_{i}}.

Summing over all valid partition vectors
\bm{c}\in C
gives the total count of
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
.
∎

We have an analogous definition in the case of the unit interval parking functions.

Definition 4.5
.

We define
\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
as the set of unit interval parking functions of length

such that the size of each block belongs to

, where

is a subset of positive integers. If

is an

-restricted unit interval parking functions of type 2

Analogously to the argument given in
Theorem

3.5
, but considering the size of the blocks and the number of ties in each position instead of the number of blocks and the number of different positions, we can state the following theorem.

Theorem 4.6
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
and any set

of positive integers, the set
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
is in bijection with
\mathrm{UPF}^{\mathrm{T2}}_{n,S}
.

5.
Restricted Fubini rankings and Restricted Unit Interval Parking Functions of Type 3

In this section, we introduce a different way of restricting Fubini rankings and unit interval parking functions.

We now impose restrictions on each
rank
of the Fubini ranking according to the entries of a given sequence of positive integers. We make this definition precise next.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{FR}_{n}
have
k
distinct ranks. The
position vector
of
\alpha
is the vector
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})
, where
c_{i}
is the number of entries of
\alpha
that take the value
c_{0}+c_{1}+\cdots+c_{i-1}
for each

, with

.

We illustrate
Definition

5.1
with the following.

Example 5.2
.

Consider
\alpha=(4,2,1,2,4,7,4)
. It is straightforward to check that
\alpha\in\mathrm{FR}_{7}
. Then, given
c_{0}=1
, we have that
c_{1}
the number of occurrences of
c_{0}=1
in
\alpha
, this is
c_{1}=1
. Now,
c_{2}
is the number of occurrences of
c_{0}+c_{1}=2
in
\alpha
, then
c_{2}=2
. Following this procedure we find that
c_{3}=3
and
c_{4}=1
, and then the position vector of
\alpha
is
\bm{c}=(1,2,3,1)
.

Next we define the set of
S
-restricted Fubini rankings of type 3.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{FR}_{n}
have position vector
\bm{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
. A sequence of positive integers
S=(s_{1},s_{2},\dots)
is called a

for
\alpha
if
c_{i}\leq s_{i}
for each

. We denote by
\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T3}}_{n,S}
the set of Fubini rankings of length
n
with block sequence
S
. If
\alpha\in\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T3}}_{n,S}
, we say that
\alpha
is
S
-restricted Fubini rankings of type 3
.

We illustrate
Definition

5.3
with the following.

Example 5.4
.

Let
S=(2,4,6,\dots)
be the sequence of even positive integers. Consider the Fubini ranking
\alpha=(1,3,1,6,3,3,6)
of length 7, which has position vector
\bm{c}=(2,3,2)
. Checking the conditions of
Definition

, we verify that

c_{1}=2\leq 2=s_{1},\quad c_{2}=3\leq 4=s_{2},\quad\text{and}\quad c_{3}=2\leq 6=s_{3}.

Thus, we conclude that

On the other hand, consider the Fubini ranking

we see that

We now give a formula for the number of
S
-restricted Fubini rankings of type 3.

Theorem 5.5
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}
and every sequence of positive integers
S=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots)
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T3}}_{n,S}|=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k})\in M_{S,k,n}}\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}},

where

M_{S,k,n}=\left\{(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k})\mid c_{1}+c_{2}+\cdots+c_{k}=n\text{ and }c_{i}\in[s_{i}]\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq k\right\}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T3}}_{n,S}
be an
S
-restricted Fubini rankings of type 3 with position vector
\bm{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k})
. By definition,
\bm{c}
must be an element of
M_{S,k,n}
, ensuring that
\alpha
has exactly
k
distinct ranks and satisfies the block sequence
S
. To construct such an
\alpha
, we need to assign its
n
entries to the values determined by
\bm{c}
. The number of ways to distribute these
n
elements into blocks of sizes
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k}
is given by the multinomial coefficient
\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}}
.
Finally, summing over all possible values of
k
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Another interesting way to derive
Theorem

5.5
is through exponential generating functions. Let
\bm{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k})\in M_{S,k,n}
. Is known that the exponential generating function of a set of size
c_{i}
is given by
\frac{x^{c_{i}}}{c_{i}!}
for each
i=1,\dots,k
. Taking the product of these functions and summing over all elements of
M_{S,k,n}
and all possible values of

\mathrm{FR}^{\mathrm{T3}}_{n,S}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\bm{c}\in M_{S,k,n}}\frac{x^{c_{1}}}{c_{1}!}\frac{x^{c_{2}}}{c_{2}!}\cdots\frac{x^{c_{k}}}{c_{k}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\bm{c}\in M_{S,k,n}}\frac{x^{n}}{c_{1}!c_{2}!\cdots c_{k}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\bm{c}\in M_{S,k,n}}\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.

Thus, we have proved the theorem once again.
