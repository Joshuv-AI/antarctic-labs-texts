Identification and functional characterization of a novel thioredoxin MpTRX1 from Metschnikowia persimmonesis
Authors: Chang Ho Kang, Jae Hyeok Lee, Yeong Min Lee, Yong Bok Lee, Youngmin Kang, Yeongjun Ban, Ariranur Haniffadli, Endang Rahmat, Chae Oh Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThioredoxins (TRXs) are small, conserved redox-active proteins that play central roles in oxidative stress responses. Here, we identified and functionally characterized a novel thioredoxin, MpTRX1, from the newly isolated yeast Metschnikowia persimmonesis. The full-length MpTRX1 gene was cloned and expressed in Escherichia coli and Saccharomyces cerevisiae to analyze its biochemical and physiological functions. MpTRX1 encodes a 103-amino-acid protein containing a canonical CXXC redox motif, and structural modeling confirmed a conserved thioredoxin fold. Recombinant MpTRX1 exhibited clear disulfide reductase activity in both DTNB (5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid)) and insulin reduction assays. Mutation of either catalytic cysteine residue abolished activity, confirming their essential roles. Moreover, heterologous expression of MpTRX1 in S. cerevisiae enhanced tolerance to hydrogen-peroxide-induced oxidative stress. Although the functional assays were conducted in a heterologous system, these findings demonstrate that MpTRX1 is a bona fide thioredoxin that may contribute to oxidative stress protection in M. persimmonesis. This work provides the first molecular characterization of a protein from M. persimmonesis and establishes a foundation for future studies on its potential ecological and biotechnological applications.Key points• Identification of MpTRX1, a novel thioredoxin from M. persimmonesis.• Recombinant MpTRX1 reduces both chemical and protein substrates.• Overexpression of MpTRX1 enhances oxidative stress tolerance in S. cerevisiae.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13668-y.

Introduction

Epiphytic yeasts inhabit surfaces of plant tissues without causing harm and often contribute to host fitness by promoting growth or enhancing resistance to environmental stress (Hardoim et al. 2008; Ling et al. 2020). Among these, Metschnikowia species are widely distributed and frequently isolated from plant organs such as leaves, fruits, and flowers (Lachance et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2020). Their strong antimicrobial activities against bacterial and fungal pathogens make them attractive as potential biocontrol agents (Oro et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2020). Recent studies have reported that Metschnikowia yeasts exert antifungal effects by producing volatile organic compounds or by secreting killer toxins (Buyuksirit-Bedir and Kuleasan 2022; Iorizzo et al. 2025; Oro et al. 2018). Additionally, some species exhibit biotechnological potential in biofuel production, flavor compound synthesis, and phytochemical biotransformation (Abeln and Chuck 2019; Haniffadli et al. 2024).

Although there is still debate about classification (Macia Valero et al. 2025), Metschnikowia persimmonesis was recently isolated from the calyx of persimmon fruits (Diospyros kaki) and identified as a novel species within the Metschnikowia genus (Kang et al. 2017; Rahmat et al. 2024). It displays strong antagonistic activity against a broad spectrum of plant pathogens, including Botrytis cinerea and Fusarium oxyforum, suggesting promising applications in agriculture as a natural biocontrol agent (Kang et al. 2017; Rahmat et al. 2024). However, despite the rising interest in its practical applications, no gene or protein from M. persimmonesis has been functionally characterized to date. The recent availability of its whole-genome sequence now enables the molecular investigation of genes that may contribute to its unique properties (Rahmat et al. 2021).

Redox regulation is a fundamental cellular process essential for maintaining the intra-cellular redox balance and enabling survival under oxidative stress conditions (Ahsan et al. 2009; Sies 1997; Trachootham et al. 2008). Thioredoxins (TRXs) are a class of small (~ 12 kDa) and highly conserved oxidoreductases that play a central role in cellular redox homeostasis (Holmgren 1979, 1989). They function by catalyzing disulfide bond reduction in substrate proteins via a conserved Cys-Gly-Pro-Cys (CGPC) motif, and thereby participate in DNA synthesis, antioxidant defense, and signal transduction (Arner and Holmgren 2006; Chae et al. 1994; Holmgren 1979; Martin 1995). In fungi such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, two cytoplasmic TRXs (Trx1 and Trx2) have been shown to protect cells from oxidative damage and regulate essential metabolic processes (Garrido and Grant 2002; Grant 2001; Trotter and Grant 2005). However, no TRX homolog has yet been reported or studied in M. persimmonesis.

We identified and characterized a novel thioredoxin gene from M. persimmonesis, which we named MpTRX1. Using a genome-wide search based on conserved thioredoxin domains, we identified candidate genes and selected MpTRX1 based on sequence homology and structural features (Mistry et al. 2021; Rahmat et al. 2021). We cloned the MpTRX1 gene, performed sequence and structural analysis, and examined its biochemical properties through in vitro reductase assays. Furthermore, we evaluated the functional relevance of MpTRX1 in vivo using heterologous expression in S. cerevisiae, focusing on its role in oxidative stress resistance. This work represents the first molecular and functional analysis of a gene from M. persimmonesis and provides a foundation for future studies on its stress response mechanisms and potential applications in redox biology and biotechnology.

Materials and methods

In silico identification and cloning of the MpTRX1 gene

To identify putative thioredoxin genes in M. persimmonesis, a sequence-based homology search was conducted using the Translated Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (TBLASTN) available at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) (Altschul et al. 1997). The amino acid sequence of the con-served thioredoxin domain (Pfam: PF00085; 101 amino acids) was used as a query under default search conditions (Mistry et al. 2021). The domain sequence, retrieved from the Pfam database, was submitted against the whole genome sequence of M. persimmonesis (Taxonomy ID: 2053757, GenBank Accession number: GCA_014905795.1) (Rahmat et al. 2021). Candidate sequences producing significant alignment scores were selected for further analysis. Among eight sequences identified, the one with the highest alignment score (120 bits, e-value 1.5e-32), designated HF325_004745, was selected and named MpTRX1.

Bioinformatic analysis of the MpTRX1 protein sequence and structure

The open reading frame (ORF) sequence of the MpTRX1 gene was translated into an amino acid sequence using the ExPASy Translate tool (https://web.expasy.org/translate/). The deduced MpTRX1 protein sequence was then subjected to sequence homology analysis using the BLASTP algorithm (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), comparing it against thioredoxin sequences from other model organisms, including S. cerevisiae (ScTRX1, NP_010509.1), Homo sapiens (TXN, J04026.1), Escherichia coli (TrxA, VKOH01000030.1), Mus musculus (Txn1, NM_011660.3), Arabidopsis thaliana (AtTRX h1, NM_114963.5), and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (CmCTRX1, AY184797.1). Pairwise sequence identity and similarity percentages were calculated using Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/), and sequence alignments were visualized using BioEdit program (Hall 1999).

To investigate the tertiary structure of MpTRX1, the full-length amino acid sequence was submitted to the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). The structural prediction was performed using the AlphaFold2 deep-learning-based algorithm, which generated a predicted three-dimensional model with per-residue confidence scores (pLDDT) (Jumper et al. 2021). The predicted structure was downloaded and visualized using PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (Schrödinger, LLC) or UCSF ChimeraX. Structural features such as α-helices, β-sheets, and the conserved CXXC redox-active motif were annotated based on visual inspection and alignment with canonical thioredoxin folds from Protein Data Bank (PDB) references (e.g., PDB ID: 2TRX) (Katti et al. 1990).

The location and geometry of the active site cysteine residues (Cys30 and Cys33) were assessed to determine their spatial proximity and orientation for disulfide bond formation and redox reactivity. Structural alignment with known TRX family proteins confirmed that MpTRX1 adopts a conserved thioredoxin fold and redox-active site architecture (Martin 1995; Ren et al. 2009).

Yeast strains and chemicals

The yeast M. persimmonesis was originally isolated and identified by our research group (Kang et al. 2017; Rahmat et al. 2024), and the S. cerevisiae strain W303 (SVL82; MaTα, ade2, his3, leu2, trp1, ura3, can1) was obtained from Horizon Scientific (Waterbeach, Cambridge, UK). Yeast strains were grown at 27 °C in Yeast Extract-Peptone-Dextrose (YPD) medium or YPG medium which replaced glucose in YPD medium composition with galactose (Kang et al. 2015; Rahmat et al. 2024). Chemicals including 5,5′-dithio-bis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), dithiothreitol (DTT), ethylene-diamine-tetraacetic acid (EDTA), 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)−1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), and hydrogen peroxide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Sigma-Aldrich Chemical, Saint Louis, MO, USA).

Construction of expression plasmids

The entire 312 bps coding sequence of the MpTRX1 gene was PCR-amplified using the genomic DNA of M. persimmonesis and the following primer pair: MpTRX1-F (5′-ATGGTCTCTGCTGTCAC-3′) and MpTRX1-R (5′-TTAAGCGTTAGCGGCCA-3′). The PCR products were cloned into the pGEM-T Easy vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The MpTRX1 coding sequence was PCR-amplified again using the following primer pair: MpTRX1 attB1-F (5′-aaaaagcaggccatATGGTCTCTGCTGTCAC-3′)/MpTRX1 attB2-R (5′-agaaagctgggtTAAGCGTTAGCGGCCA-3′) and the gel eluted PCR product was used for third PCR amplification with the primer sets, attB1-F (5′-ggggacaagtttgtacaaaaaagcaggc-3′) and attB2-R (5′-ggggaccactttgtacaagaaagctggg-3′). The DNA fragment obtained from second PCR were cloned into Gateway entry vector pDONR221 using a BP Clonase™ II reaction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To generate MpTRX1M1, MpTRX1M2, and MpTRX1M3 mutants in which one or two cysteine residues of the active site of MpTRX1 are replaced by serines, the pDONR-MpTRX1 DNA (as a template) was PCR-amplified using the Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs, Oshawa, ON, Canada), as described previously (Kang et al. 2019), with a specific primer pairs: MpTRX1CC-F (5′-GCCCTTGTAAAATGATCGCC-3′) and MpTRX1CS-R (5′-CAgAC-CAGGTGGCGAAAAAG-3′) for MpTRX1M1, MpTRX1CS-F (5′-GCCCTTcTAAAATGATCGCC-3′) and MpTRX1CC-R (5′-CACAC-CAGGTGGCGAAAAAG-3′) for MpTRX1M2, and MpTRX1CS-F (5′-GCCCTTcTAAAATGATCGCC-3′) and MpTRX1CS-R (5′-CAgAC-CAGGTGGCGAAAAAG-3′) for MpTRX1M3. The wild-type and mutant gene fragments of MpTRX1 were mobilized to pET44GWa (Kang et al. 2009) to generate pET-MpTRX1-His, pET-MpTRX1M1-His, pET-MpTRX1M2-His, and pET-MpTRX1M3-His, using an LR Clonase™ II reaction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To identify the oxidative-stress tolerance of yeast cells, the wild-type and mutant gene fragments of MpTRX1 were subcloned into the pYES2 vector (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for expression in yeast.

Recombinant protein production and purification

The pET-MpTRX1-His, pET-MpTRX1M1-His, pET-MpTRX1M2-His, and pET-MpTRX1M3-His vectors were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) pLysS cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Recombinant protein production and purification were performed using the HisPur Ni–NTA Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions as described previously (Kang et al. 2009). The purified proteins were dialyzed against 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) before use. For comparative analysis, 6xHis-tagged recombinant E. coli thioredoxin TrxA protein was commercially obtained (Abcam, Cambridge, Cambridgeshire, UK).

Assay for disulfide reductase activity

The disulfide reductase activity of MpTRX1 was evaluated using two different substrates: DTNB and insulin, following a modified protocol based on established methods (Jung et al. 2013). For the DTNB reduction assay, reactions were carried out in a total volume of 0.3 mL containing 50 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.0), 100 mM DTNB, 100 mM NADPH), 0.3 μM thioredoxin reductase, 500 mM EDTA, and purified MpTRX1 proteins. The reaction mixtures were gently mixed, and the change in absorbance at 412 nm (A412) was monitored over time using a DU800 spectrophotometer (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA).

For the insulin reduction assay, MpTRX1 proteins were incubated with insulin as a protein substrate in the presence of 2 mM DTT. Reductase activity was determined by measuring the increase in turbidity caused by insulin reduction, monitored as the change in absorbance at 650 nm (A650).

All assays were conducted with three independent biological replicates, and obtained consistent results, and representative curves are shown.

Survivability and TB exclusion assays

To conduct spot assays, the transformed cells with pYES2-MpTRX1-His (MpTRX1), pYES2-MpTRX1M3-His (MpTRX1M3) or pYES2 vector (vector) were grown in YPD medium overnight (Kang et al. 2019). Then, the concentration of each culture was adjusted to an optical density (OD600) of 1.0. Then, 6 µL aliquots of tenfold serial dilutions were spotted onto YPD or YPG agar plates, incubated at 27 °C, and examined after 2–3 days. For survivability assay, the cells (5 × 107 cells/mL) grown in YPG medium were resuspended in 100 μL distilled water supplemented with (+) or without (−) 50 mM hydrogen peroxide (Garrido and Grant 2002; Kang et al. 2019). Cells were sampled at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min after incubation, and suitable dilutions were plated on YPD agar plates. The number of viable cells or colony forming units (CFU) was counted after 2–3 days of incubation at 27 °C. The cell survival rate (%) of each transformant was calculated as the ratio of the viable cell count at a given time point to the viable cell count at the 0 min time point. Also, MpTRX1 cells (5 × 107 cells/mL) grown in YPD or YPG medium were resuspended in 100 μL distilled water supplemented with or without hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), incubated for 60 min, and visualized by fluorescence microscopy after staining with trypan blue (TB). The TB staining assay was performed as described previously (Kang et al. 2019).

Results

Exploration and cloning of MpTRX1 as a novel TRX gene in M. persimmonesis

To identify thioredoxin (TRX) genes in M. persimmonesis, the Translated Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (TBLASTN) available on the National Center for Bio-technology Information (NCBI) website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was utilized (Altschul et al. 1997). Using default parameters, a TBLASTN search was conducted against the M. persimmonesis genome (TaxID: 2053757) with the Pfam thioredoxin domain (PF00085; 101 amino acids; HMMER3 E-value: 1.5e-32) as the query (Mistry et al. 2021; Rahmat et al. 2021). This search yielded eight sequences with significant alignment scores. These corresponded to six distinct genes (HF325_004745, HF325_004744, HF325_003827, HF325_005047, HF325_006696, HF325_006451), while two sequences originated from a single gene (HF325_005742).

Among the identified candidates, the gene HF325_004745 showed the highest alignment score (120 bits) and was thus selected for further analysis. This gene, designated MpTRX1, is composed of 312 base pairs located on Contig 6, spanning positions 820,151 to 820,462 in the complementary strand. Notably, the sequence does not contain any CTG codons (Fig. 1A). To gain structural insight, the three-dimensional structure of MpTRX1 was predicted using AlphaFold, a state-of-the-art protein structure prediction platform (Fig. 2B, C) (Jumper et al. 2021). The resulting structural model demonstrated a high confidence level and revealed a canonical thioredoxin fold consisting of four α-helices and five β-sheets, a characteristic feature of TRX family proteins (Martin 1995). Notably, the predicted structure includes the conserved redox-active site, the CXXC motif (CGPC), located between residues 30 and 33 (Holmgren et al. 1975; Katti et al. 1990). This motif is positioned at the N-terminal region of the second α-helix (α2), immediately following the β2-strand (Fig. 2C). The cysteine residues at positions 30 and 33 (Cys30 and Cys33) are spatially arranged to facilitate disulfide bond formation and efficient electron exchange, consistent with the functional role of thioredoxins in redox reactions (Arner and Holmgren 2006).

These sequence and structural features strongly support that MpTRX1 possesses the canonical characteristics of a functional TRX protein.

Structural characteristics of MpTRX1

MpTRX1 contains two conserved cysteine residues within its active site, which are presumed to be critical for its reductase activity, as is typical for thioredoxins (Holmgren 1989; Weichsel et al. 1996). To experimentally examine the reductase-related characteristics of MpTRX1, we generated site-directed mutants in which either one (MpTRX1M1 and MpTRX1M2) or both (MpTRX1M3) of these cysteine residues were substituted with serine. For comparative analysis, a commercially obtained recombinant E. coli thioredoxin TrxA protein (EcTrxA) was included as a positive control. The purified proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) under both reducing and non-reducing conditions, transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) to assess the integrity of each recombinant protein (Fig. 3C). Under reducing conditions, all five recombinant proteins—wild-type MpTRX1, the three cysteine mutants, and EcTrxA—migrated as monomers at approximately 12 kDa, consistent with their predicted molecular weights. In contrast, under non-reducing conditions, the analysis revealed that wild-type MpTRX1 and MpTRX1M2 (lacking either of the latter cysteine residues) exhibited additional higher molecular weight bands, indicating dimer and multimer formation via disulfide linkages. By comparison, MpTRX1M1 (lacking either of the preceding cysteine residues) and MpTRX1M3 (lacking both cysteines) displayed only monomeric bands, with minimal or no detectable dimerization.

These findings suggest that the cysteine residues in the active site are not only crucial for electron transfer activity but also facilitate thiol-disulfide exchange that enables inter-molecular interactions—a well-documented mechanism of action in thioredoxin-mediated redox regulation (Arner and Holmgren 2006; Holmgren 1989).

Confirmation of the reductase activity of MpTRX1

To confirm the reductase activity of MpTRX1, both a chemical substrate, DTNB, and a protein substrate, insulin, were employed. As in previous experiments, E. coli-derived EcTrxA, the first-discovered and well-characterized thioredoxin, was used as a positive control (Holmgren 1979; Motohashi and Hisabori 2006).

First, the activity of MpTRX1 was assessed using DTNB as the substrate. The reductase activity of MpTRX1 increased proportionally with increasing protein concentrations (1, 2, and 5 µM). Next, insulin was used as a protein substrate to further examine MpTRX1’s reductase capability. Insulin contains interchain disulfide bonds between its A and B chains, and reduction of these bonds leads to precipitation or conformational changes in the B chain, resulting in increased turbidity of the solution (Holmgren 1979). The turbidity increased in a dose-dependent manner with increasing concentrations of MpTRX1, confirming that MpTRX1 can reduce protein disulfide bonds in a concentration-dependent fashion (Fig. 4B).

To investigate the functional importance of the two conserved cysteine residues within the CXXC active site of MpTRX1, site-directed mutant proteins (MpTRX1M1, MpTRX1M2, and MpTRX1M3), in which one or both cysteines were substituted with serine, were assessed for reductase activity. In DTNB reduction assays, the double mutant MpTRX1M3 (Cys30Ser/Cys33Ser) exhibited no detectable activity, displaying a reduction profile comparable to the control lacking thioredoxin protein (Fig. 4C). Consistently, in the insulin reduction assay, MpTRX1M3 showed negligible activity, equivalent to the control condition containing only thioredoxin reductase (TR) without thioredoxin (Fig. 4D).

Furthermore, the single mutants MpTRX1M1 and MpTRX1M2, in which either Cys30 or Cys33 was replaced by serine, also exhibited minimal reductase activity toward both DTNB and insulin substrates (Fig. 4C, D). These findings indicate that both cysteine residues in the active site are indispensable for the full reductase function of MpTRX1, in agreement with previous reports on the catalytic mechanism of thioredoxin proteins (Arner and Holmgren 2006; Holmgren 1989).

Role of MpTRX1 in the oxidative stress response of yeast cells

Oxidative stress significantly affects yeast cells by inducing cellular damage and triggering adaptive defense mechanisms. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), superoxide anions (O₂⁻), and hydroxyl radicals (OH·), are generated as by-products of normal aerobic metabolism and under various stress conditions (Costa and Moradas-Ferreira 2001; Jamieson 1998). These ROS can damage cellular macromolecules such as proteins, lipids, and DNA, thereby com-promising cellular function and viability.

Thioredoxins (Trx1 and Trx2) in S. cerevisiae are known to play critical roles in maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis during oxidative stress (Garrido and Grant 2002). As small, heat-stable oxidoreductases, they reduce disulfide bonds in target proteins and help preserve the reduced state of other antioxidant systems (Holmgren 1989). Given the demonstrated reductase activity of MpTRX1 in vitro, we sought to investigate its potential in vivo function in the oxidative stress response.

Because molecular tools for genetic studies in M. persimmonesis are not yet fully established, we utilized S. cerevisiae as a model host system. Based on previous reports showing that overexpression of thioredoxin genes can enhance oxidative stress resistance in various organisms (Veal et al. 2007), we examined whether overexpression of MpTRX1 confers similar effects in yeast cells.

To this end, plasmids encoding His-tagged MpTRX1 and the catalytically inactive mutant MpTRX1M3 (with both active-site cysteine residues substituted with serine) were constructed under the control of the GAL1 promoter, and named pYES2-MpTRX1-His and pYES2-MpTRX1M3-His, respectively. These constructs, along with the empty vector, were transformed into S. cerevisiae W303 cells.

Transformants were incubated at 27 °C for 3 h in YPD (containing 2% glucose) or YPG (containing 2% galactose) medium. To assess the functional impact of MpTRX1 expression on oxidative stress tolerance, a spot assay was performed. Cells were grown to stationary phase, serially diluted, and spotted onto glucose or galactose containing plates with or without 11 mM H₂O₂. In the absence of oxidative stress, all strains grew similarly. However, upon H₂O₂ exposure, the MpTRX1-overexpressing strain exhibited markedly improved growth compared to the vector control and MpTRX1M3 strains (Fig. 5B).

To further evaluate the protective effect of MpTRX1, survival of the cells grown in YPG medium was analyzed after exposure to 50 mM H₂O₂ for 0 to 1 h (Fig. 5C). Without oxidative stress, survival remained high in all strains. However, under H₂O₂ treatment, survival of vector and MpTRX1M3 strains dropped to below 30% after 1 h, whereas MpTRX1-expressing cells maintained a significantly higher survival rate of 44 ± 4.59%. This represents approximately 76% greater oxidative-stress resistance compared to vector controls. Statistical analysis (Student’s t-test) confirmed that survival difference between MpTRX1 and vector control strains was significant (p < 0.01).

These results were further supported by trypan blue (TB) exclusion assays. When exposed to 50 mM H₂O₂ for 1 h, MpTRX1-expressing cells grown in YPG medium exhibited a higher proportion of TB-negative (viable) cells compared to the cells grown in YPD medium (Fig. 5D).

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that overexpression of wild-type MpTRX1 significantly enhances oxidative stress tolerance in yeast cells, likely through its redox-regulatory activity.

Discussion

We identified and characterized MpTRX1, a novel thioredoxin from M. persimmonesis. This is the first report demonstrating the molecular and functional properties of a protein derived from this newly sequenced yeast species. Thioredoxins are small, conserved redox-active proteins that play essential roles in maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis by catalyzing thiol-disulfide exchange reactions. The presence of a canonical CXXC motif and the conserved TRX-fold architecture in MpTRX1 confirms its classification as a bona fide thioredoxin (Holmgren 1985).

The primary sequence of MpTRX1 exhibited substantial homology with known thioredoxins from a wide range of organisms, including S. cerevisiae, E. coli, and H. sapiens, indicating evolutionary conservation. Structural modeling further supported this similarity, revealing a TRX-like 3D fold with five β-strands and four α-helices—a hallmark of thioredoxin superfamily proteins.

Biochemical assays verified that recombinant MpTRX1 efficiently reduces DTNB and insulin in a concentration-dependent manner. These are classical substrates used to assess thioredoxin activity, and MpTRX1’s reactivity strongly supports its redox function. Notably, substitution of the conserved cysteine residues within the CXXC motif abrogated enzymatic activity, confirming their essential catalytic role. This is consistent with previous findings in other systems where mutation of either cysteine in the active site impairs electron transfer and disulfide bond reduction (Laurent et al. 1964; Ritz et al. 2000). Interestingly, the reductase activity of MpTRX1 was significantly lower than that of EcTrxA, which served as a positive control. This reduced activity may be attributed to the oligomeric behavior of MpTRX1. SDS-PAGE analysis under non-reducing conditions revealed that MpTRX1 formed a substantially higher proportion of dimeric and multimeric species compared to EcTrxA. Such oligomerization is known to interfere with thioredoxin function by restricting access to the active site or altering the protein’s conformational flexibility, ultimately impairing its catalytic efficiency (Berndt et al. 2008; Jung et al. 2013).

Ectopic expression of MpTRX1 in S. cerevisiae conferred marked tolerance to hydrogen-peroxide-induced oxidative stress, whereas the inactive mutant MpTRX1M3 did not. This in-vivo protection supports the hypothesis that MpTRX1 functions as a bona fide thioredoxin capable of mitigating oxidative damage through its disulfide-reducing activity. Similar results have been observed when thioredoxin genes were overexpressed in other model organisms (Picazo et al. 2019; Ritz et al. 2000; Trotter and Grant 2002).

Importantly, this study provides the first molecular insight into redox regulation in M. persimmonesis, an epiphytic yeast noted for its antimicrobial and biocontrol potential. However, all functional assays in this study were performed in the heterologous host S. cerevisiae. Because genetic tools for M. persimmonesis are still limited, direct evidence for the physiological role of MpTRX1 in its native background is not yet available. Future work using homologous expression or gene-disruption systems will be required to verify whether MpTRX1 confers similar oxidative-stress resistance in M. persimmonesis itself.

In addition, while M. persimmonesis is a symbiotic yeast associated with persimmon (D. kaki), our data do not directly demonstrate its contribution to plant growth or productivity. Nevertheless, by enhancing oxidative-stress tolerance of the yeast, MpTRX1 may indirectly support stable colonization and promote a favorable redox environment that could benefit the host plant. Such potential interactions warrant further investigation in planta, using inoculation or co-culture models, to clarify how yeast-mediated redox buffering influences persimmon physiology and fruit yield.

Conclusion

MpTRX1 is a functional thioredoxin that maintains redox balance and enhances oxidative-stress resistance in yeast cells. This study expands our understanding of redox regulation in non-conventional yeasts and establishes a molecular basis for future exploration of M. persimmonesis as a biocontrol or symbiotic agent in persimmon cultivation.
