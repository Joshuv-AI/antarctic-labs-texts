PAUL: Uncertainty-Guided Partition and Augmentation for Robust Cross-View Geo-Localization under Noisy Correspondence
Zheng Li, Yanming Guo, WenZhe Liu, Xueyi Zhang, Zhaoyun Ding, Long Xu, Mingrui Lao

Abstract
Cross-view geo-localization is a critical task for UAV navigation, event detection, and aerial surveying, as it enables matching between drone-captured and satellite imagery. Most existing approaches embed multi-modal data into a joint feature space to maximize the similarity of paired images. However, these methods typically assume perfect alignment of image pairs during training, which rarely holds true in real-world scenarios. In practice, factors such as urban canyon effects, electromagnetic interference, and adverse weather frequently induce GPS drift, resulting in systematic alignment shifts where only partial correspondences exist between pairs. Despite its prevalence, this source of noisy correspondence has received limited attention in current research. In this paper, we formally introduce and address the Noisy Correspondence on Cross-View Geo-Localization (NC-CVGL) problem, aiming to bridge the gap between idealized benchmarks and practical applications. To this end, we propose PAUL ( P artition and A ugmentation by U ncertainty L earning), a novel framework that partitions and augments training data based on estimated data uncertainty through uncertainty-aware co-augmentation and evidential co-training. Specifically, PAUL selectively augments regions with high correspondence confidence and utilizes uncertainty estimation to refine feature learning, effectively suppressing noise from misaligned pairs. Distinct from traditional filtering or label correction, PAUL leverages both data uncertainty and loss discrepancy for targeted partitioning and augmentation, thus providing robust supervision for noisy samples. Comprehensive experiments validate the effectiveness of individual components in PAUL,which consistently achieves superior performance over other competitive noisy-correspondence-driven methods in various noise ratios.



Introduction

(a)

Cross-View Geo-Location (CVGL) aims to retrieve matching images between UAV and satellite platforms, serving as a fundamental component for various applications such as UAV navigation, event detection, and aerial mapping
(
Zheng, Wei, and Yang 2020
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Lin et al. 2022
)
. Specifically, CVGL commonly involves retrieving the most relevant satellite image from a gallery, given a UAV-captured image as the query, and leveraging the geo-tag (e.g., GPS) of the satellite image for precise localization.

Most existing CVGL methods aim to map heterogeneous image pairs into a unified feature space by maximizing the similarity of positive samples and minimizing that of negative samples
(
Deuser, Habel, and Oswald 2023
)
. These methods typically assume that training samples are strictly aligned, i.e., each UAV image is precisely matched with a satellite image from the same location. However, this assumption often fails in practice.
In real-world CVGL tasks, the data collection process typically consists of two steps: UAV images are acquired through aerial photography, with onboard GPS simultaneously recording the geographic coordinates during image capture. Then, for each set of coordinates, the corresponding satellite image is retrieved from a database to form matched pairs with the UAV images.
However, during data collection, factors such as urban canyons, electromagnetic interference, and adverse weather conditions can introduce GPS noise, resulting in spatial misalignment between UAV and satellite images. Such mismatched pairs are common, especially when UAV GPS signals drift. Consequently, many training samples are only partially aligned, and the resulting noisy correspondences can severely degrade CVGL performance (Figure
1
(c)). This degradation is particularly critical in applications that require high-precision localization, such as navigation, disaster rescue, and security. Despite its practical importance, spatial misalignment caused by GPS drift has received little attention in existing methods, creating a significant gap between current research and real-world requirements.

Inspired by these observations, we formally introduce and define the problem of Noisy Correspondence on Cross-View Geo-Localization (NC-CVGL), which aims to develop robust CVGL models when the training data inevitably contain correspondences with varying alignment quality due to GPS drift. Unlike classical noisy correspondence cases that usually refer to completely incorrect pairs (Figure
1
(a)), the “noise” in NC-CVGL is characterized by systematic spatial offsets rather than entirely mismatched pairs (Figure
1
(b)). To the best of our knowledge, this work is the
first
to systematically define and study this practical yet critical problem. Previous efforts such as SDPL
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
mainly address the issue of non-centered targets in images, whereas our focus is on learning with mixed-quality supervision during training.

To address the NC-CVGL problem, we propose a robust learning framework,
PAUL
(
P
artition and
A
ugmentation by
U
ncertainty
L
earning), fundamentally grounded in uncertainty theory. The core innovation of PAUL lies in systematically leveraging uncertainty principles to both exploit the informative value of noisy samples and mitigate their uncertainty, thereby enhancing model robustness and generalization. Specifically, PAUL applies evidential deep learning to effectively model, represent, and learn uncertainty within both data and feature representations, which strengthens the model’s ability to cope with noisy correspondence conditions. Guided by uncertainty principles, PAUL dynamically partitions the training data and extracts high-confidence information from both “clean” and “noisy” samples for data augmentation and targeted supervision, thus enabling more effective learning from all available data. In contrast to previous NC methods—which typically regard noisy samples as low-value and only exploit structural or contextual information, consequently neglecting or under-utilizing valuable information—PAUL utilizes uncertainty-driven information mining and augmentation to actively identify and enhance valuable regions in noisy data. In this way, PAUL not only enriches supervisory signals, but also fully harnesses the potential of both “clean” and “noisy” data, resulting in significant improvements in model robustness and performance.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

To best of our knowledge, this work serves as a pioneering study in systematically defining the NC-CVGL task and offering a theoretical analysis of alignment noise resulting from real-world GPS drift.

•

We introduce the PAUL framework, grounded in uncertainty theory, which partitions and augments both “clean” and “noisy” data samples, extracts high-confidence information, and, by integrating evidential deep learning, achieves superior noisy data utilization and robustness compared to prior NC methods;

•

Extensive experiments on widely adopted datasets under various noise ratios demonstrate that PAUL achieves superior performance compared to other Noisy-Correspondence-driven methods.

Related Works

Cross-View Geo-Localization

Cross-view geo-localization (CVGL) aims to predict the geographic location of ground-level or UAV images by matching them to geo-tagged satellite views. Early handcrafted approaches
(
Lin, Belongie, and Hays 2013
;
Bansal et al. 2011
;
Castaldo et al. 2015
)
struggled with scale and viewpoint changes; deep learning has since brought significant advances on benchmarks like CVUSA
(
Zhai et al. 2017
)
, CVACT
(
Liu and Li 2019
)
, and Vigor
(
Zhu, Yang, and Chen 2021
)
, using hard negative mining
(
Deuser, Habel, and Oswald 2023
)
, unsupervised or synthesis-based domain bridging
(
Li, Qian, and Xia 2024
;
Toker et al. 2021a
;
Toker et al. 2021b
;
Li et al. 2024
)
, and UAV-oriented datasets
(
Zheng, Wei, and Yang 2020
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Dai et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2024
)
. Part-based architectures further increase robustness
(
Sun, Liu, and Yuan 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
.
However, most approaches still assume well-aligned training pairs, which is unrealistic for real UAV scenarios with frequent misalignment and noisy labels. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to formally describe this challenging task and to propose a tailored framework specifically designed to address this limitation.

Noisy Correspondence

Learning from noisy correspondences is challenging in automatically labeled datasets
(
Huang et al. 2021
)
. Traditional strategies involve small-loss selection
(
Han et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2021
;
Li, Socher, and Hoi 2020
)
, while recent methods explore predictive inconsistency, uncertainty estimation
(
Feng et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2024
;
Zha et al. 2025
;
Zha et al. 2024
)
, re-matching
(
Han et al. 2024
)
, pseudo-pairing
(
Duan et al. 2024
)
, robust objectives
(
Hu et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2022
;
Qin et al. 2024
)
, or soft correspondence
(
Yang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024
)
. Most focus on obvious mismatches or semantic noise, as in cross-modal retrieval.
In CVGL, label noise is more subtle—typically spatial or partial misalignment, not semantic error. Despite its prevalence in real UAV data, most CVGL methods only improve robustness at inference
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
, still relying on clean training data. To our knowledge, we are among the first to explicitly address noisy training correspondences in CVGL, enabling noise-resistant geo-matching.

Data Uncertainty and Evidential Deep Learning

Conventional neural networks lack explicit uncertainty modeling, which is problematic for noisy data
(
Wen et al. 2023
)
. Uncertainty—aleatoric or epistemic—can be estimated with deterministic schemes
(
Sensoy, Kaplan, and Kandemir 2018a
)
, Bayesian inference
(
Gal and Ghahramani 2016
)
, ensembling
(
Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell 2017
)
, or test-time augmentation
(
Lyzhov et al. 2020
;
Gawlikowski et al. 2023
)
. Evidential Deep Learning
(
Sensoy, Kaplan, and Kandemir 2018a
)
deterministically aggregates evidence and quantifies predictive vacuity.
This is especially effective for identifying partially-aligned correspondences which is a key challenge in NC-CVGL.

Methodology

Problem Definition

In the Noisy Correspondence on Cross-View Geo-Localization (NC-CVGL), let
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a set of UAV images and
\mathcal{R}=\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
a set of satellite images, each with geolocation annotations. Due to GPS drift or annotation errors, some pairs
(q_{i},r_{j})
may be spatially misaligned. We measure their spatial overlap using Intersection over Union (IoU)
\mathrm{IoU}(q_{i},r_{j})
, and use thresholds
\tau_{m}=0.39
and
\tau_{s}=0.14
to divide training pairs into:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\left\{(q_{i},r_{j})\mid\mathrm{IoU}(q_{i},r_{j})>\tau_{m}\right\}

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle=\left\{(q_{i},r_{j})\mid\tau_{s}<\mathrm{IoU}(q_{i},r_{j})\leq\tau_{m}\right\},

(2)

where
\mathcal{P}
denotes well-aligned positive pairs, and
\mathcal{N}
includes semi-positive pairs with spatial noise but partial overlap.
For each pair,
y_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
is the observed annotation indicating a matching pair, while
z_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
is a latent variable indicating whether the pair is a noisy semi-positive one (misaligned but overlapping). In practice,
y_{ij}
is observed and used for training, but
z_{ij}
is unobserved.
The goal is to learn an embedding function
f_{\theta}:\mathcal{Q}\cup\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that matching pairs are mapped close in feature space. The training objective is:

f_{\theta}^{*}=\arg\min_{f_{\theta}}\;\mathbb{E}_{(q_{i},r_{j})\in\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{N}}\big[y_{ij}\cdot\ell(f_{\theta}(q_{i}),f_{\theta}(r_{j}),z_{ij})\big],

(3)

where the loss
\ell
depends on the (unobserved) noise indicator
z_{ij}
. Thus, the model must be robust to label noise arising from spatial misalignment.

The Proposed Method

Motivated by the demand to fully utilize both clean and noisy correspondences while mitigating noise impact, our proposed PAUL framework jointly leverages two independently and randomly initialized, identical neural networks and proceeds in three collaborative stages (Figure
2
(a)): (1) Co-divide: probabilistically partitioning clean and noisy pairs; (2) Co-augment: uncertainty-based spatial augmentation; (3) Co-training: dual-network supervision using exchanged datasets.

Co-divide: Collaborative Division and Noise Controllable Sample Selection

We base our model on the Sample4Geo framework. For a training pair
(q_{i},r_{j})
, the embedding features are extracted, and the InfoNCE loss is computed:

\ell_{\mathrm{InfoNCE}}=-\log\frac{\exp(S(q_{i},r_{j})/\tau)}{\sum_{k}\exp(S(q_{i},r_{k})/\tau)},

(4)

where
S(\cdot)
is feature similarity and
\tau
is temperature.

Empirically, the distribution of
\ell_{\mathrm{InfoNCE}}
over all training pairs naturally forms a mixture model, with low losses clustering for clean pairs (high IoU) and high losses for noisy pairs. This inspires us to adopt a soft and probabilistic partitioning by fitting a two-component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) over the loss values:

p(\ell|\theta)=\beta\cdot\mathcal{N}(\ell;\mu_{c},\sigma_{c}^{2})+(1-\beta)\cdot\mathcal{N}(\ell;\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}^{2}),

(5)

where the first component models clean samples and the second noisy samples, and
\beta
is the mixture weight. The EM algorithm is used to update these parameters. Each sample receives a posterior probability of being clean,

w_{i}=\frac{\beta\cdot\mathcal{N}(\ell_{i};\mu_{c},\sigma_{c}^{2})}{\beta\cdot\mathcal{N}(\ell_{i};\mu_{c},\sigma_{c}^{2})+(1-\beta)\cdot\mathcal{N}(\ell_{i};\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}^{2})}

(6)

so that all pairs are simultaneously assigned soft membership to “clean” and “noisy” sets without arbitrary hard thresholds. This naturally allocates noisy (semi-positive) samples for further utilization rather than discarding them.

Co-augment: Uncertainty-aware Region Masking via Evidential Deep Learning

As shown in Figure
2
(b), to address noisy pairs in retrieval tasks without ground-truth labels, we treat each batch of
K
samples as a
K
-way classification problem. For each sample
i
, the similarity logits
\bm{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
(i.e., the
i
-th row of the similarity matrix) are used to compute the evidence
\mathbf{e}_{i}
:

\mathbf{e}_{i}=\exp\left(\tanh\left(\frac{\bm{s}_{i}}{\tau}\right)\right),

(7)

where
\tau
is a temperature parameter and all operations are element-wise. The Dirichlet parameter for sample
i
is then
\bm{\alpha}_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{i}+1
, with concentration
A_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{ik}
. The mean and variance of the resulting pseudo-class probability predictions are:

\mathbb{E}[p_{ik}]=\frac{\alpha_{ik}}{A_{i}},\quad\mathrm{Var}(p_{ik})=\frac{\alpha_{ik}(A_{i}-\alpha_{ik})}{A_{i}^{2}(A_{i}+1)}.

(8)

The
\ell_{\mathrm{EDL}}
(for a batch) is then conveniently split as

\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{EDL}}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\left(y_{ik}-\frac{\alpha_{ik}}{A_{i}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{ik}(A_{i}-\alpha_{ik})}{A_{i}^{2}(A_{i}+1)}\right]

\displaystyle+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\left(\mathrm{Dir}(\bm{\alpha}_{i})\,\|\;\mathrm{Dir}(\mathbf{1})\right)\Bigg\},

(9)

The first bracket is the MSE part, the second is a KL regularizer. The total uncertainty for a sample is
u_{i}=K/\left(\sum_{k}\alpha_{ik}\right)
.

For local masking, the gradient magnitude map for pair
x
is calculated:

H_{x}=\mathrm{Normalize}(\overline{G_{x}}),\;G_{x}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EDL}}}{\partial x}\right|,

(10)

where
\overline{G_{x}}
is channel-wise mean pooling. Using a threshold
\eta
:

M_{x}(u,v)=\mathbb{I}[H_{x}(u,v)>\eta]

(11)

we obtain a spatial mask (largest connected region kept), which zeros out low-confidence areas for subsequent loss computation and model update.

Co-training: Cross-supervised Robust Learning

Following previous NC method
(
Huang et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2023
)
, two independent networks, A and B, are jointly trained in a collaborative manner. At each iteration, each network receives sample selections (clean, augmented, noisy) generated by its peer. Taking the training process of model A as an example for illustration (while model B is trained in a similar way),the training set is defined as:

\mathcal{D}^{A}=\mathcal{D}_{c}^{B}\cup\mathcal{D}_{aug}^{B}\cup\mathcal{D}_{n}^{B}

(12)

The total loss for A is also conveniently expressed by splitting parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}^{A}=\

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{(q_{i},r_{j})\in\mathcal{D}_{c}^{B}\cup\mathcal{D}_{aug}^{B}}\ell_{\mathrm{InfoNCE}}(q_{i},r_{j})}_{\mathcal{L}_{match}}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\mathrm{EDL}}\ \underbrace{\sum_{(q_{i},r_{j})\in\mathcal{D}_{n}^{B}}\ell_{\mathrm{EDL}}(q_{i},r_{j})}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EDL}}},

(13)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{EDL}}
is a trade-off hyperparameter.

Experiments

Datasets

We evaluate our method on two benchmark datasets: the large-scale, publicly available synthetic GTA-UAV dataset
(
Ji et al. 2024
)
, and a real-world dataset that we construct based on the UAV-VisLoc dataset
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
. The GTA-UAV dataset, consisting of 33,763 UAV images with precise GPS annotations, provides both positive and semi-positive samples, enabling comprehensive evaluation in both cross-area and same-area splits.
Notably, to the best of our knowledge, GTA-UAV is currently the only publicly available dataset that contains both positive and semi-positive samples suitable for the NC-CVGL task.
The UAV-VisLoc dataset contains 6,742 pairs of nadir UAV images and corresponding high-resolution satellite maps across 11 regions in China. While the dataset provides only geographic coordinates without fine-grained correspondence, we reorganize the data following the GTA-UAV protocol
(
Ji et al. 2024
)
: images from Changjiang-20 and Taizhou-1 are used for training, and Changjiang-23 and Taizhou-6 are reserved for testing, ensuring no spatial overlap. This partitioning facilitates rigorous and fair real-world evaluation.

Evaluation Protocols

Consistent with established protocols
(
Zhu, Yang, and Chen 2021
;
Dai et al. 2023
;
Zheng, Wei, and Yang 2020
)
, for each UAV query image, we retrieve the top
K

These mechanisms ensure that PAUL does not simply disregard noisy data but instead actively improves their utility and mitigates their negative impact, resulting in a more robust and noise-tolerant representation space compared to previous methods.

Conclusion

This paper investigates the common problem of noisy correspondences on cross-view geo-localization (NC-CVGL), which often arises in practical UAV applications due to annotation errors and spatial misalignment. We analyze the impact of such label noise on existing methods and reveal their lack of robustness. To address this, we propose the PAUL framework, which combines noisy correspondence learning with evidential deep learning to effectively model sample-level uncertainty, thereby enabling better handling of misaligned or ambiguous image pairs. Our approach bridges the gap between idealized benchmarks and real-world applications, significantly improving robustness and reliability across several challenging datasets. In the future, we plan to construct large-scale datasets with real-world noise and further investigate noisy correspondence methods on actual UAV platforms.

Theoretical Foundation: Dempster-Shafer Theory

Our evidential deep learning (EDL) module provides a principled uncertainty quantification, which rigorously aligns with the Dempster-Shafer Theory (DST) of evidence
(
Shafer 2020
;
Sensoy, Kaplan, and Kandemir 2018b
)
. We now present a detailed mathematical justification for this correspondence.

Dempster-Shafer Theory Recap:
DST assigns a
basic belief assignment
(BBA), or
mass function
,
m(\cdot)
over the power set
2^{\Omega}
of the hypothesis space
\Omega=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{K}\}
. For each subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Omega
,
m(\mathcal{A})
quantifies the degree of evidence
exactly
supporting
\mathcal{A}
and not any proper subset. The assignments satisfy:

m(\emptyset)=0,\qquad\sum_{\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Omega}m(\mathcal{A})=1.

For multiclass classification (
K
classes), the mass assigned to singleton
\{h_{k}\}
,
m(\{h_{k}\})
, represents belief directly supporting class
k
, while
m(\Omega)
captures the ”ignorance” or total model uncertainty.

EDL Parameterization as DST:
For each training sample
i
, our model produces non-negative evidence
\mathbf{e}_{i}=[e_{i1},...,e_{iK}]
from similarity logits, following Eq. (
7
). We instantiate a Dirichlet distribution
\mathrm{Dir}(\bm{\alpha}_{i})
with

\alpha_{ik}=e_{ik}+1,\qquad\quad A_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{ik}.

This parameterization admits a direct DST interpretation
(
Sensoy, Kaplan, and Kandemir 2018b
)
: let

\displaystyle m_{i}(\{h_{k}\})

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{ik}}{A_{i}},

(14)

\displaystyle m_{i}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{A_{i}},

(15)

and
m_{i}(\mathcal{A})=0
for all other subsets
\mathcal{A}\subset\Omega
,
|\mathcal{A}|>1
.
It is easy to check that
m_{i}(\{h_{k}\})\geq 0
,
m_{i}(\Omega)>0
, and

\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{i}(\{h_{k}\})+m_{i}(\Omega)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e_{ik}}{A_{i}}+\frac{K}{A_{i}}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}(e_{ik}+1)}{A_{i}}=1.

Belief, Uncertainty, and Predictive Probability:

For each sample
i
and class
k
, the expected probability (belief) is the mean of the Dirichlet predictive:

\mathbb{E}[p_{ik}]=\frac{\alpha_{ik}}{A_{i}},\qquad\textrm{Var}(p_{ik})=\frac{\alpha_{ik}(A_{i}-\alpha_{ik})}{A_{i}^{2}(A_{i}+1)}.

The Dirichlet mean incorporates both accumulated direct evidence (confidence) and epistemic uncertainty. The quantity

u_{i}=m_{i}(\Omega)=\frac{K}{A_{i}}

corresponds to the total uncommitted mass in DST, characterizing the global epistemic uncertainty. Large
e_{ik}
indicates strong support for class
h_{k}
(higher
m_{i}(\{h_{k}\})
, lower
u_{i}
), while
e_{ik}=0,\,\forall k
implies maximum ignorance, i.e.,
m_{i}(\Omega)=1
(the uniform Dirichlet case).

Summary:
Therefore, our network output forms a DST-compatible belief mass assignment over hypotheses: each sample’s predictive Dirichlet encapsulates both belief and uncertainty in a mathematically provable manner, with uncertainty quantification exactly matching the Dempster-Shafer formalism. The loss function
\ell_{\text{EDL}}
(Eq. (
Co-augment: Uncertainty-aware Region Masking via Evidential Deep Learning
)) then enforces both correct probability estimates and appropriate uncertainty penalization, enabling robust learning under noisy labels and spatial misalignment.
