Ring expansion of osmacyclopentadiene affords diverse six-membered osmaaromatics
Authors: Yue Zhao, Bingjie Fu, Yang Li, Di Liu, Wei Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metallaromatics such as metallabenzene and its heteroatom-containing analogues have aroused interests both in experimental and theoretical studies. While various six-membered metallaaromatics have been explored, a general synthetic methodology is still undeveloped. Herein, we report the skeletal editing of five-membered metallacyclopentadiene involving the insertion of the sixth atoms (S, Se, N, C) into the Os–C bond, as a general approach for osmafluoranthene derivatives containing osmathiabenzene, osmaselenabenzene, osmapyridine, and osmabenzene units. These complexes show broad UV-Vis absorptions, and their aromaticity has been authenticated spectroscopically, structurally and computationally.

Introduction

Metallaaromatic compounds are an important category of aromatics, and their syntheses, characterizations, and reactivities have attracted continuing interest from both experimental and theoretical chemists1–8. Fascinating metallaaromatic structures demonstrate interesting aromatic properties and potentials in catalysis and materials9,10. Introduction of main-group heteroatoms (such as O, N, S, Se) further increases the variety of metallaaromatics2,11. Over the past 40 years, diverse analogs of metallabenzene12, such as metallapyryliums13–16, metallapyridines and metallapyridiniums17–23, metallathiabenzenes24–34, metallaselenabenzenes35, have been reported. Although various metallaaromatics have been successfully discovered, synthetic methods remain a key limitation in advancing metallaaromatic chemistry.

The major route for metallathiabenzene and metallaselenabenzene is the insertion of metal with C−S or C−Se bond cleavage, followed by chemical treatments such as oxidation if necessary (Fig. 1a)24–28,32–36. Another method is the oxidation of iridathiacyclohexa-1,3-diene, which is constructed from the reaction of lithium 2,3-dimethylthiapentadienide with IrCl(PEt3)3 (Fig. 1b)29–31. To our knowledge, iridathiabenzene24–31 and rhodathiabenzene32–34 are the limited examples of metallathiabenzenes. Metallacyclopentadienes are important intermediates in both catalysis and synthesis, which contain multi-reactive sites and can undergo various types of reactions, such as insertion, cycloaddition, and transmetalation37–39. In this work, we present the ring expansion of an osmacyclopentadiene structure with the insertion of the sixth atom into the Os–C bond, as a general approach for osmafluoranthene derivatives (Fig. 1c). The first osmathiabenzene and the first crystallographically characterized metallaselenabenzene, as well as osmapyridine and osmabenzene structures, are successfully synthesized.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of osmathiafluoranthene complexes

As shown in Fig. 2a, when osmacyclopentadiene complex 140 was mixed with excess NaSCN, complex 2 was generated first from simple ligand substitution. As the reaction proceeded, complexes 3 and 4 were observed, and at last, no 2 was left. These three complexes (2–4) could be isolated by column chromatography on silica gel. Complex 2 shows similar paramagnetism to 140, and its structure is revealed by high-resolution mass spectroscopy (HRMS) and single-crystal X-ray diffraction study. The Os metal center adopts a distorted octahedral geometry with two trans-oriented PPh3, and the bond distances of Os–C1 (1.970(4) Å), C1–C2 (1.407(5) Å), and C2–C2# (1.426(7) Å) are similar to those of 1 (Fig. 2b)40. A plausible mechanism for the generation of 3 and 4 from 2 was depicted in Fig. 2a, involving nucleophilic attack of the −SCN anion onto the carbon of Os−C in the osmacycle of 2 to intermediate A1, followed by ring expansion through A2 to 4 with S−CN bond cleavage and the generation of −CN. Complex 3 was formed through the replacement of −NCS by −CN. It is noted that the -SCN substituent on an osmabenzene ring can be changed to -SC≡CPh with LiC≡CPh19. The molecular structure of 3 is shown in Fig. 2c, which contains a six-membered osmacycle with good planarity (the mean deviation is 0.0315 Å from the least-squares plane through Os1, C1–C4, S1). The cyanide ligand is trans to the Os−S bond. The distance of Os−S is 2.3099(17) Å, shorter than that of the reported cyclic thienyl ligand coordinated osmium complex (2.3618(12) Å)41. The Os−C4 bond length is 1.956(7) Å, typical of osmaaromatics like osmabenzene and osmapyridine12,17. The C1−C2 (1.385(10) Å), C2−C3 (1.413(10) Å), C3−C4 (1.405(10) Å), and C1−S1 (1.649(7) Å) bonds are delocalized, similar to those of iridathiabenzenes24–31. The reported rhodathiabenzene structures are actually more localized32–34, suggesting weak aromaticity or nonaromaticity, which is confirmed by the aforementioned theoretical study. In the 1H NMR spectrum of 3, the characteristic Os−CH signal was found at 23.71 ppm as a singlet (Table 1), and that of S−CH was the singlet at 10.48 ppm. The extremely low-field shift of Os−CH also resulted from paramagnetism, which was confirmed by magnetic measurements with Evan’s method (Figs. S27–28)42,43. The magnetic moment (Bohr magneton) of 3 at room temperature (μeff = 0.72 μB) is close to the spin-only value for S = 0. The small energy difference (2.98 kcal mol−1, calculated by density functional theory (DFT) studies44–47 in the gas phase) between the singlet ground state (S0) and the lowest triplet state (T1) of 3 contributes to its paramagnetic behaviour. Similar observations were reported for osmapyridine and osmapyridinium complexes, which had the lowest singlet and triplet states close in energy17,20. Its 13C{1H} NMR spectrum showed the corresponding Os−C and S−C signals at 246.1–246.0 ppm and 199.5 ppm, respectively. Complex 3 is the first osmathiabenzene-containing structure. When we mixed 3 with 10 equivalents of NaSCN in THF or DCM/MeOH for 24 h, no 4 was observed. Osmathiafluoranthene 4 has been identified by HRMS and solution NMR. Synthesis of osmaselenafluoranthene complexes

With the successful construction of 3 and 4, we investigated the reaction of osmacyclopentadiene complex 1 with KSeCN. Through a similarly formation mechanism of osmathiafluoranthenes, the first osmaselenabenzene-containing complexes 5 and 6 were obtained (Fig. 3a), which showed the characteristic Os−CH signals at 25.98 ppm (for 5) and 29.63 ppm (for 6) as broad singlets (Table 1), and those of Se−CH at 12.53 ppm (for 5) and 12.04 ppm (for 6) as singlets in the downfield region in the 1H NMR spectra. The 13C{1H} NMR spectrum of 5 displayed the corresponding Os−C and Se−C signals at 243.6–243.5 ppm and 204.2 ppm, respectively. The small energy difference (2.38 kcal mol−1) between S0 and T1 of 5 also results in its paramagnetism. The molecular structure of 5 is confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction, which contains a planar osmaselenabenzene ring (the mean deviation is 0.1176 Å from the least-squares plane through Os1, C1–C4, Se1). As shown in Fig. 3b, the solid structures of osmathiafluoranthene 3 and osmaselenafluoranthene 5 are very similar. The Os−Se bond length is 2.4378(4) Å, and the Os−C4 (1.949(4) Å), C1−C2 (1.406(6) Å), C2−C3 (1.423(6) Å), C3−C4 (1.415(6) Å), and C1−Se1 (1.794(4) Å) bonds are all delocalized in 5, much different from the six-membered iridaselenacycle complex with obvious single and double bond alternations48. Synthesis and structure of osmaazafluoranthene

Treating osmacyclopentadiene complex 1 with azidotrimethylsilane (TMSN3) gave osmapydinium-containing metallafluoranthene complex 7 (Fig. 4). TMSN3 could also act as a nucleophile, and the nitrogen atom possibly inserted into the osmacyclopentadiene unit following a similar pathway as reported through intermediate B1 and B221. Subsequent hydrolysis of intermediate B3 gives the osmapydinium-containing structure49,50. The structure of 7 was also determined by X-ray diffraction. As shown in Fig. 4b, the osmapydinium unit has a good planarity, with a small mean deviation (0.0465 Å) from the least-squares plane through Os1, N1 and C1–C4. The Os–C1 (1.929(6) Å), C4–N1 (1.319(7) Å) and Os1–N1 (2.005(5) Å) bond distances are comparable with the reported ones17,18,20. There are no significant C–C bond alterations in the six-membered ring (C1–C2 1.426(7) Å, C2–C3 1.414(8) Å, C3–C4 1.413(8) Å). The 1H NMR spectrum of complex 7 displayed the characteristic downfield Os–CH signal at 30.14 ppm as a triplet (J = 4.0 Hz). Such a downfield chemical shift indicates the paramagnetism of 7, which was measured by Evans’ method (Figs. S31–32). The small energy difference (2.50 kcal mol−1) between S0 and T1 of 7 also suggests the paramagnetic property17,20. The NH was found at 17.27 ppm as a doublet (J = 8.0 Hz), and the 31P{1H} NMR spectrum showed a singlet at −14.8 ppm. Synthesis and structure of osmafluoranthene

The reactions of osmacyclopentadiene complex 1 with isocyanides were subsequently studied, giving osmabenzene-containing metallafluoranthene 8 and 9 with a fused aza-metallacyclopropene unit (Fig. 5). A possible pathway involved the replacement of one PPh3 ligand by isocyanide, followed by the insertion of isocyanide C atom into the Os–C bond of the osmacyclopentadiene structure, resulting in the expansion to a six-membered osmabenzene unit. The second isocyanide molecule bonded to the metal center as a ligand at last (Fig. 5a). This formation process resembles that of tantallapyridinazirine complexes23. The structure of 8 was confirmed by X-ray diffraction study and is shown in Fig. 5b. The osmabenzene unit also has good planarity, with a small mean deviation (0.0264 Å) from the least-squares plane through Os and C1–C5. The sum of the internal angles is 719.6°, close to the ideal value of 720°. The Os–C1 (2.039(2) Å), Os–C5 (2.070(2) Å) and Os–N1 (2.0924(17) Å) bond distances are comparable with the fused osmaindene structure51. There are obvious C–C single and double bond alterations in the six-membered ring (C1–C2 1.391(3) Å, C2–C3 1.368(3) Å, C3–C4 1.464(3) Å, C4–C5 1.353(3) Å), compared to those of 3, 5 and 7. The 1H NMR spectrum of complex 8 displayed the characteristic Os–CH downfield signal at 12.61 ppm as a doublet (J = 4.0 Hz). Its 31P{1H} NMR spectrum showed a singlet at −11.6 ppm. The Os–C signals of 8 were found at 186.4 ppm as a doublet (J = 20.0 Hz) and 164.2 ppm as a singlet in the 13C{1H} NMR spectrum, consistent with those in the literature51. Aromaticity study

The nucleus-independent chemical shift (NICS)52–55 was performed on model complexes 3’, 5’, 7’, 8’ (in which the PPh3 ligands were replaced by PMe3 for simplicity) to investigate the aromatic property of the above six-membered osmacycles (6MRs). As shown in Fig. 6a, the NICS(1)zz values of 6MRs of 3’, 5’, 7’ and 8’ are −13.7, −13.0, −16.5 and −6.8 ppm, respectively, indicating aromatic 6MRs (whereas positive NICS values correspond to antiaromaticity). For comparison, the NICS(1)zz values of benzene, iridathiabenzene (PEt3 is simplified with PMe3)31 and rhodathiabenzene 32 are −29.8, −17.5, −1.5 ppm, respectively (the optimized structures are shown in Fig. S1). Furthermore, the anisotropy of the induced current density (AICD)56,57 was used to visually demonstrate the aromatic ring currents. As shown in Fig. 6b–e and S2, all the 6MRs of 3’, 5’, 7’ and 8’ exhibit diatropic ring currents in a clockwise direction, supporting their aromaticity. The aromatic stabilization energy (ASE) was also evaluated to further study the aromaticity of the six-membered osmacycles using the “methyl-methylene” isomerization method58,59. As depicted in Fig. S3, the ASE values of model complexes 3”, 5”, 7” and 8” (the naphthalene unit is replaced by two methyl groups) are 20.6, 22.0, 18.5 and 33.9 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating the thermally unfavorable transformations from the aromatic six-membered osmacycles to the non-aromatic isomers.Fig. 6DFT calculations for models 3’, 5’, 7’, 8’.a The computed NICS(1)zz values of 3’, 5’, 7’ and 8’ (numbers in italics, in ppm). b–e AICD plots of 3’, 5’, 7’ and 8’ from π-contribution with an isovalue of 0.025 a.u., and the applied magnetic field points outward the ring plane.

UV–vis spectroscopy

The UV–vis absorption spectra of complexes 3, 5, 7, 9 were obtained in DCM at room temperature (Fig. 7). All complexes show the high-energy absorption bands between 300 and 800 nm (Table S5). To analyze the attribution of the absorptions, TD-DFT calculations were performed on the optimized structures of 3, 5, 7, 9 in DCM medium. For complex 3, the calculated absorption at 445 nm (observed 442 nm) in the visible region is mostly attributed to the transition from the HOMO-6 to LUMO, while the calculated absorption at 554 nm (observed 560 nm) is attributed to the transition from the HOMO-3 to LUMO. Considering the participation of relevant molecular orbitals shown in Fig. S33, it is obvious that the charge transfers occur from dπ (Os) to the fluoranthene unit π* due to metal-to-ligand charge transfer (MLCT), mixed with NCS to fluoranthene unit ligand-to-ligand charge transfer (LLCT)60. There are similar characteristic absorption peaks in 5, the calculated absorption at 442 nm (observed 452 nm) is mostly attributed to the transitions from the HOMO-7 to LUMO, while the calculated absorption at 562 nm (observed 596 nm) is attributed to the transitions from the HOMO-4 to LUMO (Fig. S34). Interestingly, there is about 36 nm (560–596 nm) red-shift of 5 compared to 3, possibly due to the larger radius of the selenium atom, which increases the conjugation of the metallacycle. For complex 7, the calculated absorption at 404 nm (observed 392 nm) is mostly attributed to the transitions from the HOMO to LUMO + 1 and the calculated absorption at 487 nm (observed 491 nm) is attributed to the transitions from the HOMO-2 to LUMO (MLCT and LLCT). The absorption maxima are 340 nm, 396 nm, and 515 nm for complex 9. Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a new synthetic method for six-membered osmaaromatics, which is through skeletal editing with the insertion of the sixth atom, including S, Se, N, and C into a five-membered osmacyclopentadiene ring. The syntheses and characterizations of the first osmathiabenzene- and osmaselenabenzene-containing complexes are demonstrated. This finding enriches metallaaromatic chemistry and can be further applied to construct other metallacycles.

Methods

All manipulations were carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere using standard Schlenk techniques unless otherwise stated. Solvents were purged with a nitrogen flow before use. Other reagents were used as purchased.

Synthesis of complex 2

A mixture of complex 1 (0.200 g, 0.208 mmol) and sodium thiocyanate (0.060 g, 0.74 mmol) in THF (8 mL) was stirred at room temperature for 7 h. A mixture of complex 1 (0.200 g, 0.208 mmol) and sodium thiocyanate (0.060 g, 0.74 mmol) in THF (8 mL) was stirred at room temperature for 18 h. Synthesis complex 7

A mixture of complex 1 (0.150 g, 0.156 mmol) and azidotrimethylsilane (62 μL, 0.468 mmol) in DCE (5 mL) was heated at 80 °C for 2.5 h. Then, the volume of the mixture was reduced to ca. 2 mL, and n-hexane (10 mL) was added to give a reddish-brown solution and black residue. The solution was filtered and evaporated to dryness, giving reddish-brown solids. The solids were washed with toluene (2 mL × 3), then dried under vacuum. Yield: 0.031 g, 20.4%.

Synthesis complex 8

A mixture of complex 1 (0.200 g, 0.208 mmol) and benzyl isocyanide (61 μL, 0.50 mmol) in DCE (10 mL) was stirred at room temperature for 4 h. Then, the volume of the mixture was reduced to ca. 2 mL, and hexane (10 mL) was added to give a red solid. The solid was washed with ether (5 mL × 2), then it was purified by column chromatography on silica gel (eluent: DCM/ether = 20/1). Severe adsorption on silica gel was observed. The red band was collected, and the solvent was evaporated to dryness under vacuum to give a red solid. Yield: 0.045 g, 23.2%.

Synthesis complex 9

A mixture of complex 1 (0.150 g, 0.156 mmol) and methyl isocyanoacetate (70 μL, 0.77 mmol) in DCE (10 mL) was heated at 60 °C for 5 h. After cooling down to room temperature, the volume of the mixture was reduced to ca. 2 mL, and hexane (10 mL) was added to give a red solid. The solid was washed with ether (5 mL × 2), then it was purified by column chromatography on silica gel (eluent: DCM/ether = 10/1). Severe adsorption on silica gel was observed. The red band was collected, and the solvent was evaporated to dryness under vacuum to give a red solid. Yield: 0.042 g, 30.4%.
