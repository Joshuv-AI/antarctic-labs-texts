ChocoLlama: Lessons Learned From Teaching Llamas Dutch
Matthieu Meeus, Anthony Rathé, François Remy, Pieter Delobelle, Jens-Joris Decorte Thomas Demeester, [10pt] Imperial College London Cavell Ghent University, [10pt] Aleph Alpha imec TechWolf

Abstract
While Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding and generation, their performance often lags in lower-resource, non-English languages due to biases in the training data. In this work, we explore strategies for adapting the primarily English LLMs (Llama-2 and Llama-3) to Dutch, a language spoken by 30 million people worldwide yet often underrepresented in LLM development. We collect 104GB of Dutch text ( 32 32 B tokens) from various sources to first apply continued pretraining using low-rank adaptation (LoRA), complemented with Dutch posttraining strategies provided by prior work. For Llama-2, we consider using (i) the tokenizer of the original model, and (ii) training a new, Dutch-specific tokenizer combined with embedding reinitialization. We evaluate our adapted models, ChocoLlama-2, both on standard benchmarks and a novel Dutch benchmark, ChocoLlama-Bench. Our results demonstrate that LoRA can effectively scale for language adaptation, and that tokenizer modification with careful weight reinitialization can improve performance. Notably, Llama-3 was released during the course of this project and, upon evaluation, demonstrated superior Dutch capabilities compared to our Dutch-adapted versions of Llama-2. We hence apply the same adaptation technique to Llama-3, using its original tokenizer. While our adaptation methods enhanced Llama-2’s Dutch capabilities, we found limited gains when applying the same techniques to Llama-3. This suggests that for ever improving, multilingual foundation models, language adaptation techniques may benefit more from focusing on language-specific posttraining rather than on continued pretraining. We hope this work contributes to the broader understanding of adapting LLMs to lower-resource languages, and to the development of Dutch LLMs in particular. We provide open-source access to our models, benchmarks 1 1 1 https://huggingface.co/ChocoLlama and code 2 2 2 https://github.com/ChocoLlamaModel/ChocoLlama .

2.2
Pretraining implementation details

We here further pretrain three distinct LLMs on our entire Dutch dataset for
1
epoch.

First, we develop
ChocoLlama-2-7B-base
, adapting LlaMA-2 7B
[
39
]
to Dutch using its original tokenizer. The total dataset consists of
32
B tokens. We apply continued pretraining (i.e. updating all the parameters) to both the embedding layers as well as the head of the language model. For all layers in between, we apply the parameter efficient fine-tuning method LoRA
[
20
]
to all its available target modules with
r=8
and
\alpha=32
. This leads to
544
M trainable parameters, equivalent to
7.75\%
of all 7B model parameters.

Second, we adapt the tokenizer to one trained exclusively on Dutch data and develop the model
ChocoLlama-2-7B-tokentrans-base
. Specifically, we train a BPE tokenizer on the Dutch portion of the OSCAR corpus
[
36
]
(January 2023 dump) with a vocabulary size of
50,000
tokens. Note that this is larger than the vocabulary size of Llama-2
[
39
]
, which is
32,000
. We realize that changing a component of the LLM as fundamental as the tokens it considers can lead to complete
catastrophic forgetting
of all the language capabilities and knowledge stored in the original LLM. Hence, we apply the model reinitialization technique introduced by Remy et al.
[
32
]
. This technique allows us to map tokens from the Dutch tokenizer to semantically similar tokens from the original, primarily English tokenizer. Initially proposed for masked language models, showing a great performance increase in language adaptation, we now apply this technique to our setup of autoregressive language modeling with Llama-2
[
39
]
. Using this new tokenizer, the total number of tokens is reduced to
22.6
B, or a reduction of
29.4
% compared to the original tokenizer. This is expected, as a Dutch-specific tokenizer (and one with a larger vocabulary) would more efficiently encode the same textual data.
We further consider the same training setup as for
ChocoLlama-2-7B-base
, which now leads to a slightly higher number of trainable parameters (with the increase in vocabulary size), i.e. an equivalent of
11.47\%
of all 7.3B parameters, or 839M in total.

Third and lastly, we also develop
Llama-3-ChocoLlama-base
- applying the same language adaptation strategy to Llama-3 8B
[
2
]
as used for
ChocoLlama-2-7B-base
. This now leads to 1.07B parameters that are trainable, or an equivalent of
11.77\%
of all 8B parameters. While this project was initially exclusively focused on Llama-2
[
39
]
, the new model version was released during the development of
ChocoLlama
and its great performance motivated us to extend our language adaptation efforts to the latest model. We decided to now consider the original tokenizer, and not the Dutch-specific tokenizer. We opt for this setup as the original Llama-3
[
2
]
model already considers an extensive vocabulary size of
128
k tokens. When comparing the vocabulary of each tokenizer, we find that 30.0% of all tokens from the Dutch-specific tokenizer are also in the Llama-3 tokenizer, while this was only 7.9% for Llama-2. We hypothesized that any potential gains from adding Dutch-specific tokens would be now more incremental compared to the risk of catastrophic forgetting, and chose to use the original tokenizer when adapting Llama-3 to Dutch. The total dataset, encoded using the tokenizer of Llama-3, consists of
17
B tokens. This represents a significant reduction compared to the
32
B tokens from the original Llama-2 tokenizer, already suggesting that Llama-3’s capabilities might transfer more effectively to Dutch than Llama-2.

While the observed trends in training perplexity provide insights into the models’ learning progress, it is important to note that the perplexity values are not directly comparable across models due to differences in their tokenization. As pointed out by Mielke
[
27
]
, perplexity is indeed a token-level metric which should be normalized to a common unit before comparing across tokenization schemes. In this work, we normalize perplexity to the number of tokens according to a reference tokenizer, i.e. the original tokenizer of Llama-2
[
39
]
. Let
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}
denote the tokenizer of model
\theta
. For a given reference dataset
D_{\text{ref}}
, let
|\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(D_{\text{ref}})|
represent the number of tokens when the reference dataset is tokenized using
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}
. The normalized perplexity for model
\theta
is then computed as:

\mathcal{P}_{\theta}^{\text{norm}}(D_{\text{batch}})=\mathcal{P}_{\theta}(D_{\text{batch}})\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{T}_{\theta}(D_{\text{ref}})|}{|\mathcal{T}_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(D_{\text{ref}})|}

(1)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
is the perplexity of model
\theta
as evaluated on
D_{\text{ref}}
using its native tokenizer
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}
. For
\theta_{\text{ref}}
we choose Llama-2, while for
D_{\text{ref}}
we simply use the entire pretraining dataset.

Figure
2
(b) illustrates the normalized perplexity throughout training across all models. This normalization now enables a fair comparison of model performance across diverse tokenization schemes.

First, we note that the original Llama-2 7B
[
39
]
model starts off with a moderately low normalized perplexity already. This means, without explicitly being trained on Dutch, the model already has a significant performance in the generation of Dutch natural language. When being further pretrained on Dutch data, as done for
ChocoLlama-2-7B-base
, the perplexity decreases strongly for the first thousands of steps to eventually showing little performance increase closer to the end of the epoch.

Second, we find that the model with token translation,
ChocoLlama-2-7B-tokentrans-base
, starts off with a significantly larger normalized perplexity than the original Llama-2 model. This makes sense, as changing the tokenization, despite the reinitialization of the embeddings as proposed by Remy et al.
[
32
]
, likely has a significant impact on the model performance. Yet, we observe that after a decent amount of steps, the model recovers and its perplexity steadily decreases. Notably, throughout pretraining the normalized perplexity of
ChocoLlama-2-7B-tokentrans-base
catches up with the decreasing perplexity of
ChocoLlama-2-7B-base
, resulting in similar final values for the normalized perplexity at the end of the epoch.

Lastly, we examine the normalized perplexity of the
Llama-3-ChocoLlama-base

We find that the normalized perplexity of the original Llama-3 model (i.e. the beginning of the curve) to be significantly lower than the original Llama-2 models, but also lower than the normalized perplexity of both its Dutch-adapted versions at the end of pretraining. This suggests that Llama-3’s Dutch capabilities are significantly stronger from the start than for the original Llama-2 model. Throughout pretraining, we find the normalized perplexity to steadily decrease further, in a similar fashion as
ChocoLlama-2-7B-base
.

We further optimize the pretrained models for conversational capabilities.

Specifically, we adopt the same strategy as used to align GEITje-7B to GEITje-7B-ultra
[
43
]
. First, we apply supervised finetuning (SFT), utilizing the data made available by Vanroy
[

]
, including translated versions of databricks-dolly-15k
[
8
]
and a cleaned version of the Alpaca dataset
[
37
]
and synthetically generated data using GPT-4
[
28
]
. Next, we apply Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
[
30
]
to the SFT version of all the pretrained models we here develop, now utilizing a Dutch version of the data used to train Zephyr-7B-
\beta

[
41
]
10
10

10

https://huggingface.co/datasets/BramVanroy/ultra_feedback_dutch
. For both the SFT and DPO stage, we apply the same set of parameters to all models as used in GEITje-7B-ultra
[
43
]
, including updating all model hyperparameters. Further, we leverage publicly available code
[

Related work on Dutch language models

In recent years, other language models specifically optimized for the Dutch natural language have been developed. We here provide a brief overview of the developments most relevant to our work and identify a series of models to benchmark the Dutch language capabilities of
ChocoLlama
.

First, prior work has trained masked language models from scratch on Dutch and have showed these to outperform models with multilingual capacity. Delobelle at al.
[
10
]
trained a RoBERTa model
[
26
]
on 39GB of Dutch data from the OSCAR corpus
[
36
]
and achieved state-of-the-art downstream performance compared to the multilingual mBERT
[
13
]
. Interestingly for this work, authors consider both pretraining with the original BPE tokenizer and its vocabulary as well as training a new tokenizer on Dutch data, concluding that also adapting the tokenizer to the target language leads to improved performance. Concurrently, two other Dutch masked language models were developed, BERT-NL
[
4
]
and BERTje
[
9
]
, both also finding increased performance compared to multilingual models.

Further research has investigated methods for ”translating” tokenizers from the original language to a target language by re-initializing the original model’s embeddings based on semantic similarities between tokens. Remy et al.
[
32
]
demonstrated that this approach enables efficient language adaptation for BERT-based
[
13
]
masked language models, a technique we leveraged to develop
ChocoLlama-2-7B-tokentrans-base
. Concurrently with this work, a similar strategy was employed to create tweety-7b-dutch
[
33
]
, a trans-tokenized adaptation of Mistral 7B
[
21
]
to Dutch.

Most relevant to our work, autoregressive LLMs have also been finetuned to Dutch. GEITje-7B
[
34
]
is a full-parameter finetuned model starting from Mistral 7B
[
21
]
further trained on 10 billion tokens of Dutch text. Further versions of GEITje-7B have been developed, optimized for conversational behaviour leveraging synthetic (translated) instructions and conversations made available by Vanroy
[
42
]
. The best performing model of this suite is reportedly GEITje-7B-ultra
[
43
]
, which applies direct preference optimization
[
30
]
on a supervised finetuned version of the base model. Further, models have also been developed without continued pretraining on Dutch data and just leveraging Dutch instruction datasets. Recent examples include Reynaerde-7B-Chat
[
11
]
, a QLoRA
[
12
]
finetuned version of the instruction-tuned version of Mistral 7B v0.3
[
21
]
, and ReBatch/Llama-3-8B-dutch
[
31
]
, a QLORA
[
12
]
and ORPO
[
19
]
fine-tuned version of Llama-3-8B
[
2
]
.

Finally, a leaderboard has been established to track and compare the Dutch language capabilities of newly developed LLMs

https://huggingface.co/spaces/BramVanroy/open_dutch_llm_leaderboard

6
Discussion

Our work on adapting Llama-based LLMs to Dutch provides several valuable insights about the challenges and opportunities in language adaptation, particularly for lower-resource languages.

Successful language adaptation for Llama-2.

Our approach to adapt the primarily English Llama-2 to Dutch proved successful. To our knowledge, parameter-efficient finetuning methods such as LoRA
[
20
]
, are not typically applied to the scale used in this project. Specifically, we apply LoRA during continued pretraining on 32B Dutch tokens, just updating around 10% of the overall model parameters to teach a primarily English model Dutch. Our results demonstrate that efficient parameter updates can effectively adapt a model to a new language.

Our experiments also indicate that modifying the tokenizer can significantly enhance performance across benchmarks. Replacing the tokenizer with one fully trained on Dutch, combined with the embedding initialization strategy suggested by Remy et al.
[
32
]
, and continued pretraining using LoRA, resulted in
ChocoLlama-2-7B-tokentrans-instruct
surpassing
ChocoLlama-2-7B-instruct
on ChocoLlama-Bench. We find this especially meaningful given the inherent risk of catastrophic forgetting when changing the tokenizer, as exemplified by the large initial perplexity during training (see Figure
2
(b)). These findings suggest that, for adapting models to domains or languages that differ substantially from the original training data, modifying the tokenizer—especially when paired with careful weight reinitialization—can be a promising approach. Moreover, we find that changing the tokenizer more efficiently compresses the same amount of Dutch text in 29.4% less tokens, making it more efficient for language adaptation and less costly at inference time.

Finally, we observed that the posttraining phase on limited Dutch data effectively enabled conversational behavior in the model.

Developing state-of-the-art LLMs.

Our work also revealed the increasing challenges in developing state-of-the-art models for specific languages or domains. While our adaptation technique worked well with Llama-2, we found ourselves unable to surpass concurrent work based on Mistral-7b, which possessed stronger multilingual capabilities from the start. More strikingly, during our development process, the release of Llama-3 demonstrated performance that exceeded our Dutch-specific model, highlighting the rapid pace of advancement in multilingual capabilities of foundation models.

We argue that these findings lead to an important observation about the relative value of continued pretraining versus posttraining. When we applied our adaptation approach to Llama-3, which already possessed strong Dutch language capabilities, we found that our continued pretraining on Dutch data followed by posttraining did not outperform Meta’s instruction-tuned Llama-3 model. This suggests that when the base model already has significant capabilities in the target domain or language, the impact of continued pretraining may be less significant than high-quality instruction tuning.

Benchmarking for lower-resource languages.

Lastly, our experience highlights the complexities of benchmarking language models in lower-resource languages. Although we initially used existing benchmarks for Dutch, we empirically observed that these benchmarks may not consistently or accurately assess a model’s ability to generate fluent Dutch text. To address this, we introduced ChocoLlama-Bench as a new benchmark focused on evaluating Dutch language generation. We empirically find this benchmark to offer a more comprehensive and reliable evaluation of Dutch language capabilities, and we hope its open-source availability will facilitate robust benchmarking of future models.

However, we note that our benchmark assumes the availability of evaluation models with strong proficiency in the target language. We here use GPT-4o for assessment, which may introduce its own biases and limitations. This underscores, more broadly, the challenge of developing reliable evaluation metrics for language models in lower-resource languages.

7
Conclusion

The successful language adaptation for Llama-2 teaches us that parameter-efficient finetuning methods at scale enable language adaption. Further, when the target domain is sufficiently distinct from the original training data, switching the tokenizers with embedding reinitialization could offer a promising approach.

Looking ahead, as foundation models become increasingly multilingual, the focus of language adaptation may need to evolve as well. Future efforts could benefit from targeted adaptations focusing on specific linguistic nuances or cultural elements, likely during posttraining, rather than broadly focused language adaptation during continued pretraining.

Beyond language adaptation, our findings might also apply to the adaptation of LLMs to specialized domains (e.g. biomedical or legal text). While large models trained on vast amounts of public data are likely to outperform domain-specific models for general use cases, some level of adaptation (during pretraining or by incorporating expert feedback in posttraining) will likely enhance the model’s performance in more specialized use cases. We hypothesize that such improvements might even be achievable with smaller models. Although large models are typically the first choice due to their capacity to store vast knowledge, smaller models, when adapted effectively, might also perform well in niche tasks while being more cost-effective.

Lastly, we emphasize that the development of more robust and comprehensive evaluation frameworks for non-English languages remains essential for advancing model accuracy and relevance in diverse contexts.
