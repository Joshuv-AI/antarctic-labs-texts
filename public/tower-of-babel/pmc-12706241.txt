Identifying long-term healthcare and sociodemographic risk factors for lower extremity amputation: a 10-year national registry-based case–control study in Denmark
Authors: Pia Søe Jensen, Thomas Kallemose, Klaus Kirketerp-Møller, Helle Gybel Juul-Larsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify early-occurring healthcare and sociodemographic risk factors associated with lower extremity amputation (LEA) by analysing health trajectories up to 10 years before amputation.DesignA national, observational, registry-based matched case–control study.SettingThe Danish universal healthcare system, using national health registers.ParticipantsWe included 2551 individuals who underwent first-time LEA in 2017–2018 and matched each to two control groups: (1) The Community Controls Group representing the average population who were matched on age, sex and municipality (n=12 748) and (2) a Diabetes Mellitus/Peripheral Arterial Disease (DM/PAD) Control Group matched on age, sex and DM or PAD duration (n=12 478) representing a high-risk population.Primary outcome measuresPresence of healthcare, sociodemographic and medication-related risk factors associated with LEA was evaluated across three time periods leading up to amputation: the Immediate (0–2 years prior), Early (2–5 years prior) and Long-term (5–10 years prior) risk period.ResultsPolypharmacy and antibiotic use—particularly dicloxacillin targeting Staphylococcus aureus—were strongly associated with LEA across all time periods. Dicloxacillin was prescribed on average 7.8 years prior to major amputation, with long-term ORs of 2.99 (95% CI 2.51 to 3.56) and 2.07 (95% CI 1.75 to 2.46) compared with community and DM/PAD controls. Opioid and paracetamol use also showed strong associations. Individuals with LEA were more likely to live alone and have lower educational attainment. Frequent dental visits were inversely associated with risk.ConclusionsThis study identifies characteristics associated with LEA, including long-term exposure to dicloxacillin and opioid analgesics, alongside polypharmacy and socioeconomic disadvantage. These factors were detectable up to 10 years before amputation and may serve as early indicators for risk identification and guide targeted general practitioner interventions.

Introduction

Lower extremity amputation (LEA) is a severe and irreversible event associated with substantial physical, psychosocial and functional impact, including reduced ambulation, independence and social participation.1 2 Individuals who undergo LEA are frequently characterised by advanced age, male sex and multimorbidity—particularly diabetes mellitus (DM) and peripheral arterial disease (PAD)—as well as complex pharmacological treatment.3 Socioeconomic disadvantage is strongly associated with both increased risk of LEA and poorer outcomes, reflecting persistent health inequalities.4 5 LEA is commonly categorised as either major (above or below the knee) or minor (below the ankle) amputation.

In Denmark, approximately 1400 adults undergo LEA each year, with major amputations being most prevalent,6 with an incidence of 2.3 adults undergoing major LEA per 100 000 adults.7 Cardiovascular diseases, most notably diabetes and PAD, constitute the predominant risk factors for LEA.3 National registry data report diabetes and PAD in 49% and 70% of individuals who undergo LEA, respectively.6 Consistent with international evidence, people living with diabetes exhibit a significantly higher risk of LEA compared with individuals without diabetes.8 Despite advances in surgical and medical care, post-amputation survival remains poor, with mortality rates exceeding 30%, 60% and 80% at one, five and ten years, respectively.9 Although recent Danish data suggest modest improvements in survival, long-term outcomes remain suboptimal,10 underscoring the need for effective preventive strategies.

Previous research suggests that both diabetes and PAD often remain undiagnosed in the years preceding amputation.6 Even when PAD is recognised, adherence to evidence-based secondary prevention, such as statin therapy, remains inadequate,6 despite evidence supporting the effectiveness of intensive lipid-lowering treatment in reducing the risk of limb loss.11 The risk factor profile for PAD, including diabetes, smoking, dyslipidaemia, hypertension and physical inactivity, is well established.12 However, symptom onset is often insidious or atypical, and factors such as multimorbidity and low health literacy contribute to delayed clinical presentation, which frequently does not occur until the stage of critical limb-threatening ischaemia or the development of chronic leg ulcers with mixed arterial and venous pathology.12 Notably, a substantial proportion of individuals undergoing LEA have had repeated healthcare encounters in the years preceding amputation, indicating that risk factors are not consistently recognised or acted on in clinical practice.6

In Denmark, diagnostic evaluation and vascular intervention are typically initiated following referral to orthopaedic outpatient clinics, with referral influenced by demographic and contextual factors such as age, prior surgery, marital status and place of residence.13 Earlier vascular assessment, such as ankle-brachial index testing, may facilitate revascularisation or medical interventions that could reduce the risk of limb loss.14 Timely identification in primary care remains challenging due to absent or atypical clinical symptoms, low health literacy and systemic failures to recognise individuals at risk—particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups—further challenging general practitioners (GPs), who are often the first point of contact, in identifying those at risk.15

Despite frequent healthcare engagement, many individuals are not identified as being at risk for LEA until disease progression precludes limb salvage.6 This suggests insufficient systematic identification of early risk factors and limited understanding of risk trajectories across diagnostic groups. The aim of this study is to identify early-occurring healthcare and sociodemographic risk factors associated with LEA. By retrospectively analysing healthcare data during the 10 years preceding amputation and comparing individuals with and without diabetes and/or PAD, this study seeks to inform earlier and more targeted intervention strategies in both primary and secondary care.

Materials and methods

We conducted a national, observational, registry-based case–control study using data from Danish national registries. Individuals who had undergone LEA between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2018 were identified as cases. The timeframe (2017–2018) was chosen to ensure data completeness prior to logistical changes in the national registries implemented in 2019 and to avoid potential data bias related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Two control groups were matched to each case on different criteria: (1) Age, sex and municipality and (2) Age, sex and duration of diabetes and/or PAD. The study is reported in accordance with the STROBE checklist, using the extension for case-control studies.16

Study setting and data sources

The Danish national public healthcare system is tax-funded, providing free and uniform healthcare to all citizens. Each Danish citizen is assigned a unique social security number (CPR number) at birth, which is used to register all contacts with the healthcare system. The Central Person Registration (CPR) number enables linkage at the individual level across different national registers.

Data were extracted from five national registers: the national patient register, which contains information on all contacts with Danish hospitals, including data on outpatient visits and emergency department contacts;17 the National Prescription Registry, which contains information on prescriptions dispensed, excluding over-the-counter medication;18 the Danish Health Service Register for Primary Care, which contains information on health services provided by GPs, registered medical specialists, physiotherapists, dentists, psychologists, chiropractors and podiatrists;19 the Danish Civil Registry, which provides information on gender, date and place of birth and citizenship;20 the Migration Register, which contains information on the date of immigration and emigration21 and the Population Education Register, that provides information on educational attainment.22 The Danish national health and administrative registers are characterised by high data completeness and validity, ensuring reliable linkage and robust longitudinal analyses.23

Study population

We conducted a matched case-control study of individuals ≥18 years of age as of 1 January 2017 in Denmark with a valid CPR number. We excluded individuals without continuous residency in Denmark during the 20 years preceding inclusion, individuals who had previously undergone LEA or were amputated due to bone cancer, congenital malformations, deformities and chromosomal abnormalities or trauma. Cases

Cases with first-time LEA were identified using data from the National Patient Registry. We included cases who had undergone LEA between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2018. The definition of LEA has been described in detail in Jensen et al.6 LEA was defined as one of the following surgical procedures: hip exarticulation or trans femoral amputation (ie, above the knee amputation (AKA)), knee disarticulation or trans tibial amputation (ie, below the knee amputation (BKA)), foot amputation or toe amputation (For detailed information, see online supplemental material 1). AKA and BKA were defined as major amputation where foot and toe amputations were defined as minor amputation. If a case had more than one procedure recorded on the same day, the most proximal procedure was identified as the index amputation.

Controls

Two control groups were established, with up to five controls selected per case from the study population. The first control group was matched by age (within a 2 year range), sex and municipality and is referred to as the Community controls. The second control group was matched by age (within a 2 year range), sex and duration of diabetes and/or PAD before the index date (within a 2 year range) and is referred to as the DM/PAD controls. Diagnosis of diabetes and/or PAD was defined based on ICD-10 and ATC codes in accordance with the Chronic Condition Measuring Guide24 (online supplemental material 2 for detailed description).

Outcome

The primary outcome for the study is development of LEA (yes/no), defined as described under the ‘cases’ section.

Risk factors

Risk factors were categorised into three risk periods defined by time intervals: the Immediate risk period (0–2 years before index amputation, excluding the index hospitalisation), the Early risk period (2–5 years before index amputation), and the Long-term risk period (5–10 years before). Each period captures risk factors within distinct temporal ranges to improve the risk assessment.

Chronic conditions

Hypertension was defined by a registered ICD10 code (I109 I110 I119 I120 I129 I130 I131 I132 I139 I150 I151 I152 I158 I159). Mental and behavioural disorders due to psychoactive substance use were defined by a registered ICD10 code (F10-F19). For the analysis, the diagnosis of atherosclerosis was only identified in cases where a diabetes diagnosis was absent.

Medical treatment

From the National Prescription Registry, we included the following variables: methadone use was defined by ATC-code (N07BC02). Opioid use was defined by ATC codes (N02A, R05DA04). Paracetamol use was defined by ATC code (N02B). Codeine use was defined by ATC code (R05DA04). Corticosteroids for systemic use were defined by ATC code (H02A). Dicloxacillin use was defined by ATC code (J01CF01). Neuropathic pain was registered with an ATC code for gabapentin (N03AX). Use of diuretics was defined by ATC code (C03AA). Use of anti-thrombotic medication was defined by ATC code (B01A). Use of anti-hypertensive medication was defined by ATC codes (C02DB, C02CA, C08, C09). Cholesterol-lowering medication was defined by ATC codes (C10AA, C10AB, C10AD, C10AX, C10B). Use of beta-blockers was defined by ATC code (C07).

Healthcare utilisation

From the Danish Health Service Register for Primary Care, we included data on healthcare utilisation, including number of contacts with GPs, dentist, podiatrist, otorhinolaryngologist and ophthalmologist. In addition, the number of hospital outpatient visits was retrieved from the National Patient Register.

Sociodemographic data

Sociodemographic data were obtained regarding household size (i.e. whether the individual was living alone). Lifetime events were defined as becoming widowed or divorced.

Descriptive variables

Sociodemographic data were obtained regarding age, sex, marital status and the highest level of education. Multimorbidity was defined as two or more chronic conditions based on the Chronic Condition Measuring Guide.24 The Chronic Condition Measuring Guide contains 83 chronic conditions based on ICD10 and ATC codes. Polypharmacy was defined as individuals registered with five to nine prescribed medications and hyper-polypharmacy was defined as ten or more prescribed medications. Distal blood pressure measurement, percutaneous transluminal angioplasty and bypass surgery was retrieved from the National Patient Register (See online supplemental table S3).

Associations for risk factors and LEA within each sample were analysed by logistic regression models, with separate models for each risk factor and risk period.

Models fitted for the cases and Community Controls samples were adjusted for age, sex and municipality, and models fitted for the cases and DM/PAD controls samples were adjusted for age, sex, duration of diabetes and the duration of PAD.

Several healthcare utilisation variables exhibited skewed distributions; therefore, the following variables were dichotomised into yes/no categories. (1) For variables in the model with Community Controls (for all risk periods): contact with GPs, dentist, podiatrist and ophthalmologist and (2) For variables in the model with DM/PAD controls: contact with GPs (the Immediate risk period, and the Early risk period), dentist (the Immediate risk period, the Early risk period and the Long-term risk period) and ophthalmologist (the Immediate risk period, and the Early risk period).

Model goodness of fit was examined using a sample-size adjusted Hosmer–Lemeshow test. If the model did not fit, a covariate was removed, and model fit was reassessed iteratively until acceptable fit was achieved. Model estimates are presented as ORs with 95% CIs and p values. Level of statistical significance was set at 0.05 and all p values within each risk factor, group and risk time period were corrected for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction. Results from the models investigating the association between health-related and socioeconomic variables and the risk of LEA were corrected by multiplying the p value with eight tests performed. Results from the models investigating the association between prescription medication use and the risk of LEA were corrected by multiplying the p value with 12 tests performed. This represents the Bonferroni correction, that is, the p value for each test must be equal to its alpha divided by the number of tests performed.

Data management, matching and descriptive analyses were done using SAS V.9.4 software package for Windows (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). R V.4.1.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for statistical analysis and generation of figures.

Results

Between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2018, we identified 2551 individuals in Denmark who underwent first-time LEA. The Community Control group included 12 748 individuals while the DM/PAD Control group comprised 12 478 individuals. See figure 1 for a detailed flowchart of the inclusion and matching process.

Table 1 presents the characteristics of individuals with first-time LEA alongside the two matched control groups. Of the individuals with LEA, 1246 (49%) underwent major, and 1305 (51%) underwent minor amputation. Individuals with a major LEA had a median age of 73 years (IQR 65–81 years); 474 (38%) were diagnosed with diabetes, and 500 (40%) were diagnosed with PAD (without concurrent diabetes) before the time of amputation. In this subgroup, distal blood pressure measurement was performed in 872 individuals (70%), percutaneous transluminal angioplasty in 132 individuals (11%) and bypass surgery in 233 individuals (19%) within a 10-year period preceding LEA. Notably, the prevalence of distal blood pressure measurements was markedly higher in the LEA cohort (70%) compared with the DM/PAD cohort (11%), highlighting a significant discrepancy in diagnostic practices between these patients groups.

Polypharmacy was highly prevalent among individuals with LEA: 73% were exposed to polypharmacy, and 30% to hyper-polypharmacy within 6 months preceding LEA. In comparison, 30% of the Community control group were exposed to polypharmacy and 4% to hyper-polypharmacy. For the DM/PAD control group, 41% were exposed to polypharmacy and 8% to hyper-polypharmacy.

Table 2 presents the association between prescription medication use and LEA. Overall, prescription medication was associated with significantly higher odds of undergoing LEA. This association was observed for both major and minor LEA and strengthened over the three risk periods preceding amputation. The strongest association was observed with exposure to the antibiotic Dicloxacillin, with OR 2.99 (95% CI 2.51 to 3.56) compared with the Community cohort and OR 2.07 (95% CI 1.75 to 2.46) compared with the DM/PAD cohort during the 10-year period preceding LEA. This association consistently showed elevated ORs across all time intervals and control groups, ranging from 11 to 21 and peaking within the 2 years immediately prior to LEA. On average, individuals with LEA received Dicloxacillin 7.8 years prior to the index amputation. Additionally, exposure to pain management medication, including opioid and paracetamol, was also associated with a higher risk of undergoing LEA. In the second analysis including the DM/PAD controls, this association persisted consistently across all three risk periods.

Discussion

This study compared the 10-year healthcare trajectory of individuals undergoing LEA with two control groups: a representative sample of the general Danish population and a high-risk cohort diagnosed with diabetes or PAD. The aim was to assess the strength of the associations between potential risk factors and subsequent LEA. Polypharmacy emerged as a significant risk factor. Notably, the use of antibiotics targeting Staphylococcus aureus—likely prescribed for skin and soft tissue infections—was associated with the highest risk of undergoing LEA. Likewise, the use of analgesics, including opioids and paracetamol, demonstrated a strong association with later amputation. Sociodemographic characteristics were also strongly linked to LEA. Among these, living alone had the strongest association to LEA across both control groups. Conversely, frequent visits to a dentist were associated with lower odds of LEA, possibly reflecting greater engagement with preventive healthcare and self-care.

Risk factors

The characteristics of this cohort are comparable to previous descriptions of individuals with LEA in terms of demographic and health-related conditions.25 26 The high prevalence of chronic diseases27 and the frequent use of multiple medications is well-documented, underscoring polypharmacy as a significant risk factor for amputation, particularly with respect to dosage and duration of medication use prior to the amputation.6 Importantly, the results demonstrate that for more than half of the medications prescribed within the scope of this study, individuals exhibited significantly higher odds of undergoing LEA compared with the control group being diagnosed with DM and/or PAD during the 2 year period preceding the amputation.

In this study, we found that exposure to the antibiotic agent Dicloxacillin was associated with the highest odds of LEA across all time periods and both control groups. Although national health registries do not specify the indication for treatment, it is plausible that prescriptions reflect management of leg ulcer infections.28 Dicloxacillin is primarily used to treat S. aureus, the predominant pathogen in leg ulcer infections.29 This finding aligns with previous research showing that S. aureus is the leading cause of soft tissue and bone infections, both key contributors to impaired wound healing and the second most common indication for amputation.30 Our findings indicate a pronounced association between exposure to dicloxacillin and subsequent risk of LEA, observable several years prior to the amputation, both in comparison to the general population and individuals with DM or PAD. On average, individuals received dicloxacillin almost 8 years prior to amputation, highlighting a potentially relevant clinical window for early identification of patients at elevated risk.

The clinical relevance of this finding aligns with the observation of Salm et al who identified S. aureus as the most prevalent pathogen in leg ulcers regardless of diabetes status,29 supporting the biological plausibility of prolonged infectious processes contributing to amputation risk. Furthermore, Jensen et al have shown that individuals undergoing LEA primarily had interactions with their GP in the years preceding the amputation,6 suggesting antibiotic prescribing patterns in primary care could indeed be leveraged as an early signal for identifying high-risk patients. Given the extended time interval between initial antibiotic use and eventual LEA, it is reasonable to hypothesise that dicloxacillin exposure, when reflecting underlying chronic or recurrent infections, could represent a modifiable risk indicator suitable for future inclusion in predictive models.

The second strongest association with LEA was observed for opioid and paracetamol use, which highlights the complexity of pain management in this population.12 This is particularly relevant, given the underlying arterial insufficiency, during the immediate risk period (0–2 years before amputation), when critical limb ischaemia may manifest.31 The consistent association across all risk periods underscores the need to evaluate the long-term pain trajectories and may require a multidisciplinary response across healthcare sectors. Additionally, we observed substantial discrepancies in the use of distal blood pressure measurements between cohorts, which is concerning given their critical role in diagnosing PAD, particularly among individuals with asymptomatic or atypical symptoms.12 The low utilisation in high-risk populations, including individuals with hypertension or diabetes—among whom microvascular complications are common32—suggests a systemic underuse of diagnostic procedures, potentially contributing to delayed identification.

In line with previous findings,33 this study also showed that socioeconomic factors, such as living alone and lack of dental visits, were associated with an increased risk of LEA. These variables may indicate limited self-care capacity or engagement with preventive services. Body mass index (BMI) was not available in the registry data, although it may serve as a surrogate for both income and health literacy. Interestingly, a US registry-based study found higher LEA risk among non-obese individuals compared with those with severe obesity (BMI >30), in a general population cohort with a 71% prevalence of obesity, despite the presence of diabetes or PAD.34 In this study, higher income was associated with lower risk of LEA,34 supporting findings suggesting that low socioeconomic status increases the risk of LEA even in the presence of diabetes and PAD.35 These findings underline the importance of considering socioeconomic factors as potential contributors to the overall risk profile of individuals susceptible to LEA.

General practitioner and predictive models

The GPs serve as the primary point of contact in the Danish healthcare system in the years preceding LEA. Evidence suggests that individuals with LEA have limited hospital contact until approximately 3 years prior to amputation.6 GPs play a crucial role in the early identification of conditions like diabetes and PAD. Despite the severe prognosis associated with PAD, the condition remains disproportionately under-recognised and under-managed compared with coronary artery disease. A contributing factor is the asymptomatic presentation—particularly in people with diabetes-related neuropathy, which complicates timely risk identification.36 Non-invasive diagnostic tools such as the toe-brachial index and toe systolic blood pressure are increasingly used in general practice, but their diagnostic accuracy remains uncertain, highlighting the need for improved screening strategies and timely referral pathways.37 Recently, predictive models—particularly those employing machine learning (ML)—have shown promise in identifying individuals at risk of amputation.38 So far, ML models focus on predicting diabetic foot ulcers or post-amputation outcomes, with few explicitly modelling LEA as the primary outcome.39 Some models aim to identify amputation risk among individuals with PAD by focusing on pre-surgical data and medical history,40 while others apply tools such as the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index to predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes.41 A Danish study, only including individuals with diabetes, demonstrated that predictive ML models incorporating national registry data, local clinical records and personal tracking data could identify risk factors for diabetic foot ulcers and amputation—particularly when including socioeconomic variables.33 This supports our findings and highlights the potential of integrating registry data with clinical indicators such as smoking, nutritional status and physical activity, in general practice, particularly given the long latency between early warning signs like antibiotic use and LEA, to enhance early identification of high-risk individuals by GPs, facilitating timely preventive strategies.6

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study include the large nationwide sample, the high-quality registry data collected prospectively over two decades, and complete national follow-up, which minimises selection and information bias. This study has several limitations. First, the absence of clinical variables such as smoking status, alcohol use, BMI and physical activity limits the comprehensiveness of the risk profile. Second, diagnostic coding from primary care and data on medication administered during hospitalisations were unavailable, potentially affecting the classification of comorbidities. Although diagnoses identified during GP consultations are not linked to ICD-10 codes in Danish registers, the inclusion of dispensed prescription data partially mitigates this limitation. Third, the indication for antibiotic prescriptions is unknown, and although dicloxacillin is typically used to treat skin and bone infections, including leg ulcers, this assumption introduces a risk of misclassification. Finally, data were drawn from 2017 to 2018, a relatively short and slightly older period, but this timeframe provided the most recent complete national registry linkage. We therefore consider the data representative of the current clinical and epidemiological contexts.

Conclusions

This study identifies characteristics associated with LEA, including polypharmacy, high rates of antibiotic use targeting Staphylococcus aureus, opioid and paracetamol prescriptions as well as indicators of socioeconomic disadvantage. These factors, detectable up to 10 years before the event, were associated with LEA, indicating that modifiable risk indicators are identified long before amputation becomes inevitable. The findings also demonstrate that individuals with and without diabetes, PAD or both differ in their medication use and risk exposure, underscoring the value of stratified risk assessment in high-risk populations. Socioeconomic factors such as living alone and low engagement with preventive healthcare services, including dental care, were also associated with elevated risk and should be considered in future risk models.
