Blended adapted sailing therapy with telerehabilitation for rare skeletal disorders: protocol for a pilot randomised controlled feasibility trial in Italy (PaS2-EXTEND)

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRare skeletal disorders (RSDs) cause lifelong functional impairment, chronic pain and reduced quality of life. Evidence-based rehabilitation strategies remain underdeveloped, particularly for adolescents and young adults. We previously demonstrated preliminary feasibility of a 5-day adapted sailing intervention but observed benefit attenuation at 3-month follow-up. This pilot trial evaluates feasibility, acceptability and safety of intensive adapted sailing therapy followed by home-based telerehabilitation maintenance versus telerehabilitation alone.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective, randomised, assessor-blinded, parallel two-arm pilot feasibility trial. 24 participants aged 12–30 years with confirmed RSDs will randomise with a 1:1 allocation ratio to: (1) 5-day adapted sailing therapy followed by 3-month telerehabilitation (n=12) or (2) 3-month telerehabilitation alone (n=12). Primary outcomes assess feasibility through recruitment efficiency (≥80% eligible patients enrolled), intervention adherence (≥75% sessions completed), participant retention (≥80% at 6-month follow-up), and safety (zero serious adverse events attributable to interventions). Secondary outcomes include sensor-based motor function (balance, gait, upper extremity mobility via inertial measurement units) and patient-reported outcomes (health-related quality of life, functional capacity, kinesiophobia, pain), measured at baseline, 3-month and 6-month follow-up. Exploratory analyses will estimate preliminary between-group effect sizes. Statistical analysis uses intention-to-treat principles with linear mixed-effects models.Ethics and disseminationThe study received approval from the Area Vasta Emilia Centro Ethics Committee (363/2025/Sper/IOR) on 7 July 2025. The study is ongoing, and data collection is expected to be completed by March 2026. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed open-access publications, conference presentations, patient organisation partnerships and plain-language summaries.Trial registration numberNCT07102875.Keywords: Randomized Controlled Trial, Wearable Devices, Patient Reported Outcome Measures, Rare Diseases, Bone diseases, Feasibility Studies

Introduction

Rare skeletal disorders (RSDs) comprise a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of conditions, including diseases of bone (skeletal dysplasias and dysostoses), rare connective tissue disorders with skeletal involvement and rare metabolic bone diseases affecting bone and cartilage development, as classified within the Orphanet nosology and characterised by lifelong structural deformities, skeletal fragility, progressive mobility impairments, chronic pain and profound psychosocial consequences.1,4 In this protocol, the term ‘rare skeletal disorders’ is used consistently throughout. Despite significant advances in genetic diagnostics and orthopaedic surgical management, evidence-based rehabilitation strategies for RSDs remain critically underdeveloped, particularly during vulnerable transitional periods from paediatric to adult care systems.5,8

Conventional outpatient physiotherapy inadequately addresses rare disease needs due to geographic barriers requiring specialist centre access, fragmented care pathways, limited clinician expertise, insufficient psychosocial integration and reduced access to socially engaging motor learning environments.9,11 Individuals with RSDs commonly experience kinesiophobia due to skeletal fragility and fracture history, leading to activity avoidance patterns that paradoxically increase functional decline.12 13 These gaps underscore the need for innovative rehabilitation frameworks aligned with the WHO’s International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), which emphasises holistic assessment spanning body function/structure, activity, participation and contextual factors.14 15

This study operationalises ICF framework through a blended rehabilitation model addressing three interdependent domains (figure 1): body functions and structures (musculoskeletal impairments quantified via sensor-based assessments), activities and participation (functional capacity and social engagement) and contextual factors (environmental adaptations and personal factors including self-efficacy and motivation). This approach is justified by the shared functional phenotype across RSD diagnoses, including reduced mobility, balance deficits and kinesiophobia, which form the common targets of intervention regardless of underlying aetiology.

Experience-based interventions that embed therapeutic aims within meaningful, socially engaging activities in naturalistic environments may address several limitations of conventional clinic-based physiotherapy.16 17 Adapted sailing exemplifies this principle by integrating dynamic postural control demands, whole-body coordination challenges, upper extremity strengthening, aerobic conditioning and teamwork within ecologically valid aquatic environments. Systematic reviews document consistent improvements in psychological well-being, quality of life, physical function, self-efficacy and social integration following sailing-based interventions across diverse clinical populations.18,21

Adapted sailing potentially activates motor learning principles: variable practice in unpredictable conditions promotes motor schema development through contextual interference and naturally optimises challenge points.22,24 Sailing satisfies Self-Determination Theory’s core needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness), enhancing intrinsic motivation.25 26 Successfully managing challenges provides mastery experiences strengthening self-efficacy and reducing kinesiophobia.27 28

To evaluate this experiential approach, we conducted a single-arm pilot study (NCT06397443) in eight adolescents with RSDs. The 5-day adapted sailing intervention proved feasible (100% retention/adherence), safe (zero serious adverse events (AEs)), with preliminary improvements in proprioception, postural control and quality of life. However, single-arm design precluded causal inference, and benefits showed concerning attenuation at 3-month follow-up, revealing a critical gap: the need for structured maintenance strategies to consolidate intensive intervention gains.29

To address this maintenance challenge, the present study tests a blended rehabilitation model combining intensive adapted sailing with subsequent home-based telerehabilitation. Telerehabilitation delivers supervised programmes via videoconferencing with effectiveness comparable to in-person care while reducing transportation and access barriers.30,33 This approach strategically leverages motor learning stages: intensive sailing enables cognitive-stage skill acquisition through massed practice, while telerehabilitation supports distributed practice during associative/autonomous consolidation.22,24 This pilot randomised controlled trial evaluates fundamental feasibility parameters, recruitment capacity, intervention adherence, participant retention and comprehensive safety profiles, essential for definitive trial design in this skeletally fragile population.34 35

The current protocol (PaS2-EXTEND) represents the first randomised controlled evaluation of blended adapted sailing therapy combined with telerehabilitation maintenance for RSDs. The primary objective is to rigorously evaluate feasibility through predefined thresholds for recruitment efficiency (≥80% of eligible individuals enrolled), intervention adherence (≥75% of prescribed sessions completed), participant retention (≥80% completing 6 month follow-up) and safety (zero serious AEs (SAEs) attributable to interventions). Secondary objectives include providing preliminary effect size estimates with 95% CIs to inform definitive trial design. Given the pilot feasibility design, all secondary efficacy analyses are explicitly exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Methods and analysis

Study design

PaS2-EXTEND is a prospective, randomised, multisite, assessor-blinded, active-comparator-controlled, two-arm parallel-group pilot feasibility trial. The protocol adheres to Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2025 guidelines and Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) extension for pilot and feasibility trials.35 36 The complete SPIRIT checklist is provided in online supplemental file S1.

The protocol (version 2.1) received approval from the Area Vasta Emilia Centro Ethics Committee (CE-AVEC 363/2025/Sper/IOR) on 7 July 2025 (online supplemental file S2). The trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07102875) on 14 July 2025, before initiation of participant recruitment on 15 July 2025. A protocol amendment (EM293/2025_363/2025/Sper/IOR_EM1) clarifying data management was approved 15 September 2025. Participant recruitment commenced on 15 July 2025, immediately following ethical approval and prospective trial registration. Data collection is anticipated to be completed by 31 March 2026, following the final participant’s 6-month follow-up assessment.

Participants are randomised with 1:1 allocation ratio to:

Group 1 (Experimental): 5-day adapted sailing therapy at one of two sites (Marina di Ravenna or Palermo), immediately followed by 3-month home-based telerehabilitation (n=12).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: (a) Any sex; (b) Clinical and/or molecular genetic diagnosis of a RSD, consistent with the Orphanet classification, confirmed by a CeMaRS specialist physician; (c) Age 12–30 years inclusive at enrolment; (d) No orthopaedic surgery within 6 months preceding enrolment, unless deemed clinically irrelevant to study participation by the principal investigator; (e) Ability to understand and comply with study procedures; (f) Signed informed consent obtained from the participant (≥18 years) or a legal guardian with written assent from the minor participant (<18 years) and (g) Adequate Italian language comprehension for completion of patient-reported questionnaires.

Exclusion criteria: (a) Severe or unstable medical conditions that could interfere with safe participation (eg, uncontrolled cardiac or respiratory disease, active oncological disease); (b) Cognitive or neurological impairments that may hinder adherence to study procedures; (c) Participation in similar intensive supervised rehabilitation programmes within 3 months preceding enrolment; (d) Contraindications to virtual or sailing-based activities, including severe motion sickness, vertigo or vestibular disorders, or photosensitive epilepsy; (e) Musculoskeletal injuries (eg, fractures or major sprains) occurring within 12 months preceding enrolment; (f) Any condition or circumstance that, in the opinion of the investigator, may compromise participant safety or study compliance; and (g) Decline or inability to provide informed consent.

Note on mobility and ambulatory status: Participants across the full spectrum of mobility levels are eligible, including those using wheelchairs or assistive devices; independent ambulation is not required. Eligibility is determined on an individual basis by the principal investigator in accordance with the criteria above. Participants’ current mobility level (independent ambulation/assistive device use/wheelchair use) is recorded at baseline as a descriptive variable.

Sample size determination

Following methodological guidance for pilot feasibility research in rare disease populations, the sample size of n=24 participants (12 per group) was determined through two considerations34 35 37:

Secondary consideration: Information for future trial design

Complementary power calculations (G*Power 3.1.9.739 40) indicate that n=24 provides adequate sensitivity for detecting moderate-to-large effect sizes. For repeated-measures analysis with two groups, three time points, within-subject correlation r=0.5, and α=0.05 (appropriate for exploratory pilot analyses), n=24 provides 80% power to detect f=0.28 (approximately Cohen’s d≈0.56).41 42

These power calculations demonstrate that our sample size is informative for future trial planning; not to justify hypothesis testing. CIs will be wide, and we will not draw conclusions based on statistical significance testing of secondary outcomes.

Furthermore, this sample size is consistent with published pilot feasibility trials in rare disease rehabilitation.43 44

Recruitment strategy

Recruitment commenced on 15 July 2025, immediately following ethical approval and ClinicalTrials.gov registration, through multiple channels: (a) direct clinical recruitment during routine specialist appointments at CeMaRS, (b) registry-based recruitment via CeMaRS rare disease registry database search, (c) digital outreach through institutional social media channels and (d) patient organisation partnerships through UNIAMO-FIMR, the Italian umbrella patient organisation for rare disease and disease-specific advocacy organisations, which will disseminate study information through their networks and newsletters.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

An independent biostatistician generated the randomisation sequence with 1:1 allocation using computer-generated random numbers (Microsoft Excel RAND function). The sequence was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by administrative staff not involved in recruitment, intervention, or assessment. On completion of baseline assessment confirming eligibility and informed consent, the outcome assessor contacted designated administrative staff, who opened the next envelope and communicated allocation directly to the participant.

Simple randomisation was selected because RSD diagnostic heterogeneity makes meaningful stratification challenging. Baseline characteristics will be reported with standardised mean differences (SMDs) for key prognostic factors; if substantial imbalances emerge (SMD >0.5), sensitivity analyses will include these variables as covariates.

An independent data monitor audited 20% of randomisation envelopes (n=5) at study midpoint, verifying sequential ordering, absence of tampering and documentation of opening dates. No procedural deviations were identified.

Blinding

Complete participant and intervention provider blinding is infeasible due to the experiential nature of sailing. Multilevel blinding procedures minimise detection bias45:

Level 1-Outcome assessor blinding (primary bias mitigation): All staff conducting instrumental outcome assessments using inertial measurement units (IMUs) at all time points are blinded to group allocation. Two trained assessors (biomedical engineers) conduct all IMU measurements. Procedures to maintain blinding: assessors receive explicit training on blinding importance and are instructed not to inquire about participants’ activities; assessment rooms contain no sailing-related materials; participants receive written reminder cards before each assessment not to discuss study activities; separate administrative systems for scheduling prevent assessor access to intervention calendars.

Acknowledged unblinding risks: Group 1 participants may have sun exposure from sailing; travel-related scheduling gaps may differ; inadvertent participant disclosure; assessor inference from functional performance patterns. Detection bias is considered low risk for IMU measures due to objective sensor-derived data, standardised protocols minimising assessor discretion, and automated data extraction.

Level 2-Patient-reported outcome administration: Self-administered questionnaires are completed independently in private assessment rooms without assessor presence. However, participants are necessarily unblinded, creating high risk of performance bias. This is addressed through: (1) objective IMU measures designated as primary secondary outcomes, (2) pre-specified sensitivity analyses conducted separately for objective vs subjective outcomes and (3) transparent reporting distinguishing objective from subjective outcomes.

Level 3-Statistical analyst blinding: The biostatistician conducting all analyses is blinded to group allocation until completion of planned analyses. The dataset is locked and group assignments randomly re-coded as ‘group A’ and ‘group B’ by independent data manager. Unblinding occurs only after Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) finalisation, execution of all planned analyses, and preparation of complete results.

Blinding verification: At final assessment, outcome assessors complete brief questionnaire asking which intervention they believe participants received. Proportion of correct guesses exceeding chance (>50%) indicates blinding failure.

Phase II: Telerehabilitation maintenance

Following sailing therapy and postintervention assessment, group 1 participants transition to home-based telerehabilitation maintenance following identical structure to group 2, initiating within 7 days to minimise gap between phases.

Group 2 (Active comparator): Telerehabilitation alone

Duration: 3 months (12 weeks)

Frequency: Twice-weekly sessions (24 total prescribed).

Session structure: 40 min per session via institutional GDPR-compliant videoconferencing platform.

Personnel: Sessions supervised in real-time by licensed physiotherapists (n=6 trained in protocol) with ≥5 years’ musculoskeletal rehabilitation experience and RSD management training.

Session components: (1) Warm-up (5 min) with active mobilisation including marching in place with arm oscillations, shoulder circles and reaches, trunk rotations; (2) Specific exercises (30 min) with targeted strengthening, coordination, balance, proprioceptive exercises following progressive overload principles and (3) Cool-down (5 min) with static stretching major muscle groups held 20–30 s, diaphragmatic breathing.

Real-time safety monitoring: Presession screening (pain level, acute symptoms, sleep quality, medication changes), continuous during-exercise visual assessment via videoconference, mid-session check at 15 min (pain, fatigue, concerning symptoms), immediate modification criteria (pain increases>2 points and exceeds 6/10 absolute level, dizziness/chest pain/severe shortness of breath, visible severe compensatory patterns, technical difficulties), post-session assessment (final pain/fatigue levels, difficulty rating, AE query).

Concomitant care and prohibited interventions

Participants are instructed to maintain their usual medical care and medications throughout the study period.

Permitted: Routine follow-up with orthopaedic specialists or physiatrists; analgesic medications as prescribed; assistive devices if already in use at baseline; participation in non-intensive recreational activities.

Prohibited: Initiation of new intensive rehabilitation programmes (≥3 sessions/week); new physiotherapy or occupational therapy interventions; participation in other clinical trials of rehabilitation interventions.

All concomitant medications, therapies and significant medical events documented in structured logs are completed at each assessment time point, with verification against medical records where discrepancies noted.

Intervention fidelity monitoring

Sailing therapy: Study coordinators directly observed 25% of sailing sessions at each site using standardised checklists documenting protocol elements (session duration, instructor–participant interactions, safety implementation, debriefing completion).

Telerehabilitation: All sessions were recorded (with consent) for quality assurance. Random 20% sampling underwent structured review by independent physiotherapists using fidelity checklists assessing protocol adherence, appropriate progression and supervision quality.

Outcome measures

The study employs hierarchical outcome structure distinguishing primary feasibility objectives from exploratory efficacy endpoints.

Primary outcome: Feasibility assessment (composite endpoint)

Evaluated at study completion (t3, 6-month follow-up):

Acceptability

Recruitment efficiency: Proportion of eligible patients successfully enrolled, calculated as (number enrolled/number eligible)×100%. Data source: comprehensive screening logs documenting all patients assessed for eligibility, eligibility determination outcomes, consent outcomes, documented reasons for declination. Target threshold: ≥80% enrolled.

Participant retention: Proportion of randomised participants completing final assessment, calculated as (Number completing t₃ assessment/Number randomised)×100%. Participants who withdraw consent, are lost to follow-up, or die are considered non-retained. Participants missing t₃ window but completing delayed assessment within 30 days are considered retained. Target threshold: ≥80% retention at t₃.

Safety

AEs are systematically documented using ICH-GCP E6(R3) guidelines and adapted CTCAE V.5.0.46 47 SAEs meet ICH-GCP criteria including death, life-threatening events, hospitalisation or persistent disability; RSD-specific examples include fractures, vertebral compression, joint dislocations and severe pain requiring emergency evaluation. Mild AEs (MAEs) are self-limiting events requiring only self-care (eg, transient nausea, muscle soreness, minor bruising). Each AE is graded for causality and severity using CTCAE-adapted criteria (grades 1–5).

Interventions are considered safe for definitive trial progression if: zero SAEs definitively/probably attributable to interventions, upper 95% CI bound for SAE rate <15% and MAE incidence comparable to similar exercise interventions.

Comprehensive AE classification and grading details are in online supplemental file 4.

Secondary outcomes: exploratory efficacy assessment

All secondary outcomes are explicitly exploratory and hypothesis-generating given pilot feasibility design. Analyses focus on effect size estimation with wide CIs rather than significance testing. Results inform future definitive trial design but should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence of efficacy.

Outcome hierarchy: Among secondary outcomes, we prioritise motor functionality (objective sensor-derived IMU measures) and health-related quality of life (EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level, EQ-5D-5L) based on: (1) motor function represents objective quantification minimising self-report bias, (2) health-related quality of life is a universally relevant patient-centred outcome, (3) established responsiveness of both to rehabilitation interventions. Other patient-reported outcomes are considered supportive/exploratory.

I. Objective motor functionality assessment via IMUs

Validated wearable sensor system comprising five wireless IMUs integrated with pressure platform (Euleria Lab System, Euleria Health S.r.l., Rovereto, Italy).48 Both systems communicate with the software via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) and operate at 60 Hz sampling frequency, enabling synchronised data acquisition and real-time visualisation. The IMU system provides angular resolution of 0.1° with dynamic accuracy within ±2°, meeting recommended thresholds for clinically relevant motion analysis. IMUs are positioned on specific anatomical landmarks using adjustable elastic bands (figure 4). Prior to each assessment, participants undergo a three-second standing calibration while wearing the IMUs and, when specified, positioned on the pressure platform.

Sensor configuration for kinematic and postural assessment. Placement of inertial measurement units (IMUs) and pressure platform used in the study. The schematic shows a neutral human model standing on the pressure board with IMUs positioned on the sternum, proximal humeri, wrists, thighs, proximal tibiae and feet. This standardised sensor setup enabled comprehensive collection of kinematic and postural data across all experimental conditions.

IMU offers practical advantages including portability enabling standardised assessments across multisite settings, reduced participant burden compared with marker-based motion capture, established validity and reliability for quantifying balance and gait parameters in musculoskeletal populations.49 50

Standardised assessment protocol

All IMU assessments are conducted in quiet, temperature-controlled room (20°C–22°C) with consistent lighting and firm, level floor. Participants wear comfortable athletic clothing and athletic shoes (or remain barefoot for balance assessments based on individual preference, recorded for consistency). Single trained assessors, blinded to group allocation, conducted all measurements for a given participant across time points to minimise inter-rater variability.

For participants who are unable to safely perform standing-dependent or gait-dependent tasks, these specific assessments (eg, standing balance, gait analysis and Timed Up and Go) will be recorded as not applicable rather than adapted, in order to preserve measurement validity. Seated assessments (eg, trunk and upper extremity tasks) will be performed as per protocol, and the pattern of missing data will be documented and accounted for in the statistical analysis.

Assessment duration and sequencing: The complete IMU battery has an estimated duration of approximately 30 min, including 1 min standardised rest intervals between subassessments. Subassessments are administered in the following prespecified order, progressing from least to most physically demanding: (1) Bipodal Balance Assessment; (2) Trunk Mobility Assessment; (3) Fall Risk Predictors Assessment; (4) Gait Analysis and (5) Upper Extremity Mobility Assessment. This sequence was designed to minimise carry-over fatigue effects on earlier assessments and to group postural/static tasks before dynamic locomotor and transition tasks.

Pain and fatigue monitoring: Given the skeletal fragility and pain burden characteristic of the study population, the assessor verbally asks the participant about pain, fatigue and willingness to continue at each rest interval between subassessments. The assessment is discontinued at any point if the participant wishes to stop or if the assessor observes signs of acute discomfort. Subassessments completed before discontinuation are included in analyses; incomplete subassessments are documented as missing data with the reason recorded in the case report form (CRF).

Bipodal balance assessment: A single IMU positioned on the sternum via elastic chest strap; participant standing on pressure board (58.4 cm × 36.4 cm). Protocol: 30 s trial standing quietly with eyes open (focusing on a wall-mounted target at eye level, 3 m away), followed by a 30 s trial with eyes closed. Measured Parameters: Centre of Pressure (COP) trajectory metrics including total path length (mm), sway area (mm²), mediolateral/anteroposterior displacement ranges (mm), mean velocity (mm/s); IMU-derived trunk acceleration (deg/s2) and angular velocity (deg/s) patterns. Clinical Significance: Assesses postural control, sensory integration and balance stability.

Trunk mobility assessment: Five IMUs positioned bilaterally on the shanks (anteromedial surface, proximal third), on the thighs and on the sternum. Participants perform trunk extension (five repetitions) while sitting on a chair and trunk flexion (five repetitions) in standing position. Measured parameters: trunk flexion/extension angle (deg). Clinical significance: Evaluates seated trunk mobility, an essential component of postural control and functional stability.

Gait analysis: Two IMUs positioned bilaterally on feet (dorsum). Protocol: Participants walk at self-selected comfortable pace along 10 m straight walkway. Three trials recorded, 1 min rest between trials. Measured parameters: spatiotemporal gait variables including stride length (m), stride time (s), cadence (steps/min), stance/swing phase durations (% gait cycle), double support time (s), gait velocity (m/s), stride-to-stride variability. Clinical significance: Quantifies walking efficiency, symmetry and consistency.

Fall risk predictors assessment: Two IMUs positioned on the sternum and right thigh for the sit-to-stand and the Timed Up and Go tests, and on both feet for the 180° rotation, step test and figure-of-8 walk test. For the sit-to-stand exercise, participants begin sitting on a chair and stand up five times. In the Timed Up and Go, participants stand up from a chair, walk 3 m, turn around, walk back and sit down again. For the final exercises, participants are asked to perform an 180° turn, alternately step onto a 15-cm-high step (4 repetitions per foot) and walk following a figure-of-8 pattern around two obstacles positioned 3 m apart. Measured parameters: temporal and kinematic metrics relevant to dynamic balance and transition control. Clinical significance: evaluating walking ability, including curved-path walking, balance performance and fall risk.

Upper extremity mobility assessment: Five IMUs positioned on sternum, bilateral wrists (dorsal surface), bilateral humeri (lateral aspect, mid-shaft). Protocol: repeated scapular retraction with resistance band (monolateral and bilateral, 10 repetitions); isometric scapular retraction with resistance band (monolateral and bilateral, 10 repetitions). Scapular retraction under elastic resistance was selected because it replicates the pulling and hauling movements central to adapted sailing, enabling assessment of kinematic quality (smoothness, coordination, bilateral symmetry) under standardised functional loading conditions rather than maximum ROM. A single fixed resistance band grade (MoVeS Band Latex, green, heavy resistance, 1.5 m precut length; MVS In Motion, Roeselare, Belgium) is used for all participants at all time points and documented in the CRF. Measured parameters: range of motion (deg) in sagittal/frontal planes, movement velocity/smoothness (jerk metrics), bilateral symmetric indices, coordination patterns. Clinical significance: Evaluates upper extremity function critical for activities of daily living.

Data processing: Raw sensor data processed using Euleria Lab proprietary algorithms, extracting biomechanical parameters. Primary outcome variables included COP path length and sway area (balance), gait velocity and stride length variability (walking function), and shoulder range of motion in sagittal abduction planes (upper extremity function). Within the IMU parameter set, postural control (COP path length and sway area) and upper extremity kinematics (movement smoothness) are considered the highest-priority outcomes, reflecting the two primary physical demands of adapted sailing: continuous dynamic balance in an unpredictable aquatic environment and repeated upper extremity effort involved in managing sails, ropes and steering. Trunk mobility, gait and fall risk transition metrics are reported as complementary outcomes.

II. Patient-reported outcome measures

All patient-reported outcomes are administered via paper questionnaires in quiet private assessment room during scheduled visits. Participants complete questionnaires independently. Research personnel are available to answer questions about questionnaire format but do not influence response content.

Standardised administration order (designed to minimise fatigue effects): (1) EQ-5D-5L, 2–3 min, (2) Paediatric Outcomes Data Collection Instrument (PODCI, 8 to 10 min), (3) Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK, 3–4 min), (4) Pain Visual Analogue Scale (VAS-Pain, 1 min) and (5) Behavioural, Emotional and Social Skills Inventory-45 items (BESSI-45, 5–8 min). Total completion time: ~25–30 min.

Health-related quality of life: EQ-5D-5L

EQ-5D-5L is a generic HRQoL measure with validated Italian translation and value set.51 52 This instrument was selected for its generic applicability across diverse RSD diagnoses, availability of Italian population-based utility values enabling health economic evaluation, brief administration time (2–3 min) minimising participant burden, widespread use in rehabilitation trials facilitating cross-study comparisons and age-appropriate versions.

The instrument assesses five dimensions; each rated on five-level scales: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, anxiety/depression. Additionally, VAS (EQ-VAS) ranging from 0 (worst imaginable health) to 100 (best imaginable health) captures global self-rated health.

Three scoring methods are employed. The descriptive system generates a five-digit code (eg, ‘21132’) representing responses across dimensions. The utility index provides a single summary score (range −0.59 to 1.00, where 1.00=perfect health, 0=death, negative values=states worse than death) by applying Italian population-based value sets to descriptive responses.52 The EQ-VAS is a vertical 0–100 scale anchored by ‘worst imaginable health’ (0) and ‘best imaginable health’ (100), where participants mark their current perceived global health state.

For clinical interpretation, we adopt 0.05 as the reference minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the utility index, consistent with published MCIDs ranging 0.03–0.08 across various populations.53

Functional outcomes: PODCI

The PODCI is a validated self-report measure designed for children and adolescents with musculoskeletal disorders, with established utility in RSD populations including osteogenesis imperfecta and skeletal dysplasias.54 55 This instrument was selected for its disease-specific validation in paediatric musculoskeletal populations, comprehensive coverage of functional domains relevant to RSDs and demonstrated responsiveness to rehabilitation interventions.

The PODCI comprises 83 items across six domains: upper extremity function (hand/arm tasks including writing, utensil use, dressing, grooming), mobility/physical function (walking, stair climbing, transfers, wheelchair use), sports and physical functioning (participation in physical education, sports, recreational activities), pain/comfort (frequency, severity and interference with daily activities), happiness/satisfaction (satisfaction with physical appearance, peer relationships, overall quality of life) and global functioning (overall physical health and function). Items assess frequency or difficulty of specific activities over the past week using varied response scales.

Scale scores are standardised to 0–100 range, where higher scores indicate better function, less pain or greater satisfaction. For clinical interpretation, we adopt 10 points as the reference MCID, consistent with published MCIDs of 5–10 points across PODCI scales in paediatric musculoskeletal populations.54 Age-appropriate versions are selected based on participant age, with the same version maintained across all time points. Participants self-report without parent-proxy completion, with reading assistance provided when needed.

Fear of movement: TSK

The TSK-13 (13-item version) is a validated self-report measure of kinesiophobia (fear of movement/reinjury) with validated Italian translation.56 Kinesiophobia is particularly relevant in RSD populations where skeletal fragility creates legitimate injury concerns that may generalise to maladaptive activity avoidance. Strong associations are documented between kinesiophobia and disability/reduced rehabilitation outcomes in musculoskeletal conditions.57 The scale requires brief administration (3–4 min) and is sensitive to change following rehabilitation interventions.58 Responses use a 4-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree to 4=strongly agree). The total score is calculated as the sum of all items (range 13–52), with higher scores indicating greater kinesiophobia. A score ≥37 is typically considered clinically significant kinesiophobia warranting psychological intervention.59 The MCID is estimated at 4–5 points based on various musculoskeletal populations.58

Pain intensity: VAS

The VAS consists of a 100 mm horizontal line anchored by ‘no pain’ (0 mm) and ‘worst pain imaginable’ (100 mm), representing the gold-standard unidimensional pain measure.60 61 It demonstrates strong psychometric properties (reliability, validity, responsiveness) across age ranges including paediatric and adolescent populations, is cross-culturally applicable without translation requirements, and is sensitive to change following rehabilitation interventions. Administration is brief (<1 min): participants place a single vertical mark on the 100 mm line to indicate pain intensity, and the distance from 0 mm to the mark is measured in millimetres, yielding a score of 0–100. Score interpretation: 0 mm indicates no pain; 1–30 mm mild pain; 31–70 mm moderate pain; 71–100 mm severe pain. A change of 10–13 mm on the 100 mm VAS is considered clinically meaningful for chronic pain populations (MCID).62 63 Participants are instructed to rate their ‘typical pain level over the past 48 hours’ rather than current momentary pain to minimise situational fluctuations and improve reliability.

Psychosocial functioning: BESSI-45

BESSI-45 is a multidimensional self-report questionnaire assessing psychosocial competencies increasingly recognised as critical rehabilitation outcomes beyond traditional physical function measures, particularly relevant for adolescents and young adults navigating educational, vocational and social transitions.64,66

The BESSI-45 comprises five key skill domains: self-management (organisation, responsibility, self-discipline, persistence), social engagement (sociability, assertiveness, energy level), cooperation (respect, empathy, trust, prosocial behaviour), emotional resilience (emotional stability, stress tolerance, optimism) and innovation (creativity, curiosity, open-mindedness). Participants rate perceived competence using a 5-point Likert scale (from 1=not at all well to 5=extremely well) in response to the prompt: ‘How well do the following statements describe you?’

Domain subscale scores are calculated as the mean of 9 items per domain (range 1.00–5.00), with higher scores indicating greater perceived competence. Domain-specific scores are preferred over total scores for interpretability. The BESSI-45 demonstrates strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.75–0.85 across domains), test-retest reliability (r=0.70–0.80 over 6-week intervals), and convergent validity with established personality and competence measures.64

As the MCID has not yet been established for the BESSI-45, we consider changes ≥0.5 points (equivalent to one half-response category shift) as potentially meaningful, recognising this threshold as exploratory.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will follow prespecified SAP finalised in February 2026 prior to database lock (scheduled March 2026). The current protocol describes planned analyses; detailed implementation specifications will be documented in final SAP.

Three analysis populations are defined a priori: (1) ITT population (primary): all randomised participants analysed according to allocated intervention regardless of adherence, protocol deviations, or study completion status, preserving randomisation benefits and providing unbiased effect estimates under missing-at-random (MAR) assumptions; (2) per-protocol (PP) population (sensitivity): participants completing ≥75% of prescribed sessions (group 1: ≥3 of 4 sailing days and ≥18 of 24 telerehabilitation sessions; group 2: ≥18 of 24 telerehabilitation sessions) and completing the 6-month assessment (t₃), to estimate intervention effects under optimal adherence; (3) Safety population: all participants receiving ≥1 intervention session, analysed according to intervention actually received.

The primary outcome is feasibility, assessed through four parameters: (1) Recruitment efficiency: proportion of eligible patients enrolled, reported with 95% exact binomial CIs using Clopper-Pearson method; success criterion ≥80%. (2) Adherence: proportion of prescribed sessions completed, calculated per participant and summarised as mean±SD, median (IQR), and proportion achieving ≥75% threshold with 95% exact binomial CIs; success criterion: mean ≥75% in both groups and ≥80% of participants achieving ≥75% individual adherence. (3) Retention: proportion of randomised participants completing t₃ assessment with 95% exact binomial CIs; success criterion≥80%. (4) Safety: AE incidence rates (events per 100 participant-days with 95% Poisson CIs), calculated separately for SAEs, moderate AEs (MAEs), and overall AEs; proportions of participants experiencing ≥1 event reported with 95% exact binomial CIs; between-group comparisons using Fisher’s exact tests interpreted descriptively; success criterion: zero SAEs definitively or probably attributable to interventions. The composite feasibility assessment deems progression to definitive trial appropriate if all four success criteria are met.

Secondary outcomes are analysed exploratorily and hypothesis-generating with emphasis on effect size estimation rather than hypothesis testing, given the pilot design and limited power.

Continuous variables will be summarised as mean±SD with 95% CIs for normally distributed data, or as median (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Normality will be assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests alongside visual inspection of Q-Q plots and histograms. Categorical variables will be reported as frequencies and percentages with 95% exact binomial CIs.

Continuous outcomes measured repeatedly are analysed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) with fixed effects for group (group 1 vs group 2), time (categorical), and group-by-time interaction (primary parameter of interest), plus random intercepts for participants. LMMs handle the unbalanced design (t₁ available only for Group 1) and provide valid inference under MAR assumptions via likelihood-based estimation without requiring explicit imputation. Between-group differences in change from baseline are estimated at each time point with 95% CIs. Model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals) are evaluated via diagnostic plots; if violated, transformations or non-parametric alternatives will be considered. Within-group analyses for group 1 compare baseline (t₀) to immediate post-sailing (t₁) using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, providing mechanistic insights into acute sailing effects (recognising lack of control comparison precludes causal inference). Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) with 95% CIs (computed via bootstrap, 1000 iterations) are calculated for all comparisons, contextualised against published MCIDs where available, and used to inform future trial sample size calculations (using lower CI bound to avoid pilot effect size inflation).

Sensitivity analyses include: (1) adjusted LMMs incorporating prespecified covariates (baseline outcome value, age, sex, diagnosis category, study site); (2) PP population analyses; (3) complete-case analyses and (4) pattern-mixture models assuming missing-not-at-random scenarios (dropouts assumed 0.5 SD and 1.0 SD worse outcomes than completers) to assess robustness. Given the exploratory pilot design, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons is applied; instead, all p values are reported exactly as continuous measures of evidence strength. For exploratory secondary analyses, a relaxed two-sided α=0.10 threshold is used (rather than conventional α=0.05) to increase sensitivity for detecting promising signals warranting further investigation in adequately powered trials, while acknowledging increased Type I error risk.67 Comprehensive reporting of all results with exact p values, effect sizes and CIs enables readers to judge evidence strength. Baseline characteristics are compared between groups using SMDs; |SMD|>0.5 flagged as potentially meaningful imbalance requiring covariate adjustment in sensitivity analyses. Given the small sample size, no formal subgroup analyses stratified by diagnosis category or mobility stratum are planned. These variables serve exclusively as descriptive characteristics of sample composition and as prespecified covariates in adjusted sensitivity analyses. Any apparent between-subgroup differences observed in this pilot should be considered hypothesis-generating only and interpreted with extreme caution.

All statistical analyses will be conducted using R (V.4.3.1 or higher, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Wien, Austria). Complete annotated R analysis scripts will be archived with timestamps before database lock and made publicly available via Open Science Framework on publication.

Data monitoring and interim analyses

This pilot feasibility trial does not have a formal Data Monitoring Committee given the small sample size (n=24), short duration (6-month follow-up), low-risk interventions and intensive safety monitoring already integrated into the study design. Safety oversight is provided through systematic AE documentation, quarterly independent audits, and immediate reporting of SAEs to the Ethics Committee. No interim analyses are planned; all analyses will be conducted after study completion.

Data collection and management

Structured paper CRFs will be completed at each assessment time point and entered into Research electronic data capture (REDCap) platform (V.14.0.2) by the research team.68 REDCap, hosted on encrypted institutional servers, incorporates range checks, logic validation, and required field alerts to ensure data quality. 20% of CRFs will undergo independent double data entry with discrepancies resolved via source document review. A research monitor will conduct quarterly audits of 10% of CRFs, targeting <1% discordance between electronic and paper records. Weekly query reports will flag out-of-range values and missing data, with resolution within five business days.

Electronic data will be stored on institutional servers with daily automated backups, version control and complete audit trails. Paper source documents will be stored in locked cabinets in restricted access areas. Both electronic and paper records will be retained for 15 years poststudy completion per Italian regulatory requirements (Legislative Decree 211/2003). A separate password-protected linkage file connecting participant names to study IDs will be maintained on an encrypted server with access restricted to the principal investigator and study coordinator only.

Database soft lock is scheduled for March 2026 following the final participant’s 6-month assessment plus 30-day safety window, after which no further data entry will be permitted. Following completion of pre-specified data cleaning, the database will undergo hard lock, and the final analysis dataset will be exported. After hard lock, allocation codes will be revealed to the biostatistician, who will conduct all planned analyses with groups masked as A/B. Final unblinding and results interpretation will occur only after completion of all prespecified analyses.

Data Availability Statement

Deidentified individual participant data underlying published results will be made publicly available 6 months after publication of primary outcomes manuscript. Shared data will include demographic and baseline clinical characteristics; primary feasibility outcome data (recruitment, adherence, retention, AEs); secondary outcome measures (IMU parameters, PRO scores) at all time points; group allocation; data dictionary defining all variables.

Access mechanisms: Researchers may request data by contacting the corresponding author and submitting: (1) a research proposal (maximum two pages), (2) a signed data use agreement and (3) ethics approval documentation if the secondary analysis constitutes human subjects’ research. Requests are reviewed within 30 calendar days; approved requests receive data within 60 days via secure file transfer.

Supporting documents shared: Study protocol (this document), SAP (finalised prior to database lock) published as supplement, blank CRFs, Ethics Committee approval letters, informed consent templates, SPIRIT checklist (online supplemental file S1), analysis code (R scripts) on request.

Timeline and duration: Data sharing commences for 6 months postpublication; data remain available for a minimum of 5 years (extendable to 10 years).
