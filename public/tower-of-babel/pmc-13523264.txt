Grade-specific associations between insomnia and depressive symptoms in adolescents: a cross-lagged panel network analysis
Authors: Qian Chen, Si-Yang Liu, Shi-Hao Ma, Xiao-Song Shen, Wan-Jun Cheng, Gu-Bing Wang, Zong-Kui Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsomnia and depression frequently co-occur during adolescence, posing significant challenges to adolescent mental health. However, the direction of their association remains inconsistent in the literature and may show grade-specific patterns. Grounded in psychopathology network theory and informed by the developmental psychopathology perspective, this study examined symptom-level associations between insomnia and depression to better characterize these association patterns.MethodsThis study employed cross-lagged panel network analysis to explore symptom-level interactions between insomnia and depression from Grades 5 to 12, capturing both contemporaneous (within-timepoint) associations and temporal (cross-timepoint) predictive associations. A large cohort of 175,950 adolescents (MAge = 14.35, SD = 2.14; 52.72% female) completed the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) at two time points.ResultsThe findings showed both relatively stable and grade-specific patterns of association between insomnia and depression. In the contemporaneous networks, Motor descriptively showed the highest strength centrality in most grades, although the symptom with the highest centrality differed in some grades. In the temporal networks, descriptive centrality results indicated that Sad Mood had the highest out-expected influence in most grades, except for Anhedonia in Grade 6 and 8 and Suicide in Grade 12; Daily Function descriptively had the highest in-expected influence in most grades, except for Fatigue in Grade 10 and 11.ConclusionThese findings highlight several symptoms in the insomnia–depression network, with some variation across grades. By moving beyond total-score approaches, this symptom-level perspective provides exploratory insights that may shed light on inconsistencies in prior findings regarding the directionality of the insomnia–depression association and offer a more nuanced understanding of insomnia–depression relationships during adolescence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13034-026-01106-4.

Introduction

Adolescence is a crucial stage of development, marked by major psychological and physiological changes [57]. During this time, shifts in emotions and cognitive development can make adolescents more vulnerable to mental health challenges [9]. Among these challenges, insomnia and depression are of particular concern as they represent two of the most prevalent and impactful features of adolescent psychopathology [1, 27, 51]. Previous research has indicated that adolescence is considered as the initial onset of depression, and its prevalence is continuing to rise [9, 26]. For example, in the United States, the prevalence of adolescent depression rose from 8.1% in 2009 to 15.8% in 2019 [61]. Recent studies in China also reported a prevalence rate of 19.85% [53]. Insomnia is another common issue among adolescents [3, 18, 63]. Reduced sleep duration and decreased slow-wave sleep during adolescence are strongly associated with an increased rate of insomnia. Its prevalence is comparable to that of other major mental health conditions, such as depression. Notably, insomnia and depression frequently co‑occur among adolescents [4, 32, 49], yet the processes linking them remain insufficiently understood, underscoring the need to investigate their underlying mechanisms. In particular, longitudinal evidence is mixed: some studies find bidirectional effects, others detect a one-way pathway, and a few report that bidirectionality depends on developmental stage [1, 36, 46]. Taken together, these findings indicate the need for more fine-grained, symptom-level analyses.

Psychopathology network theory posits that mental disorders are not caused by a single latent factor but emerge from dynamic feedback loops among interacting symptoms, providing a framework for quantifying complex symptom–symptom relations [10, 25]. This perspective offers a useful symptom-level lens for revisiting the mixed longitudinal findings on insomnia and depression, whereas traditional total scores tend to average over symptom differences and fail to reveal the relatively central pathways involved. Building on this framework, approaches such as cross-lagged panel network (CLPN; [21, 23]) analysis can help identify which specific symptoms may be relatively more central in the insomnia–depression symptom network. However, the field still lacks systematic evidence comparing symptom-to-symptom relations across multiple grade levels. Therefore, exploratory examination of grade-specific interactions may help clarify symptom-level associations and provide insight into inconsistencies observed in prior research. Since individual symptoms are more directly observable than latent disorders, this approach may inform hypotheses about symptoms of interest for future studies.

Insomnia and depression among adolescents

Insomnia disorder usually refers to feeling dissatisfied with sleep despite having sufficient opportunities for rest. It often includes symptoms such as difficulty falling asleep, trouble staying asleep, or waking too early and being unable to fall back asleep [2]. It is important to note that evaluating the impact of insomnia in adolescents should take into account potential differences across different stages of development. In fact, the DSM-5 also highlights that insomnia disorder must be distinguished from sleep pattern changes that occur as part of normal developmental processes [2]. Specifically, the overall sleep needs of adolescents evolve as their sleep architecture matures, such as a reduction in slow-wave sleep [13, 24]. Moreover, as they mature physiologically, the rate at which sleep pressure accumulates during wakefulness slows down [31]. Furthermore, beyond physiological development, grade-specific academic pressures may also chronically restrict adolescents’ sleep [7, 14]. In school-based adolescent research, grade level is frequently used as a practical grouping variable because it aligns students within the same educational context [47, 58]. Compared with age alone, grade level may better capture shared school-related experiences—such as academic demands, school transitions, and daily schedules—that are closely related to adolescents’ sleep and depression [26, 40].

Given the high co-occurrence of insomnia and depression observed among adolescents in clinical settings, an increasing number of studies have focused on the close association between the two [45]. For example, Orchard et al. [49] found that nearly 92% of adolescents suffering from depression reported experiencing sleep-related issues. Adolescents with depression experience significantly more wakefulness in bed, including longer sleep onset latency, increased wake after sleep onset, more frequent awakenings, and reduced sleep efficiency [32, 42]. However, despite these robust cross-sectional findings, research on the underlying mechanisms and direction of association between insomnia and depression remains inconsistent. On the one hand, insomnia disorder is often regarded as a precursor of internalizing symptoms, especially during adolescence [8, 26]. The recently proposed Sleep to Internalizing Pathway in Young Adolescents (SIPYA) Model [1] suggests that insomnia disorder disrupts the intra- and inter-network dynamics of the default mode network, making adolescents more susceptible to repetitive negative thinking and thereby increasing their vulnerability to depression. Longitudinal studies also have shown that sleep problems can predict the onset, persistence, and recurrence of depressive symptoms. For example, a longitudinal study on the developmental trajectory of depression in children and adolescents found that sleep problems could predict depression relapse [20]. On the other hand, Cognitive Models of Insomnia suggest that depression may disrupt with sleep by fostering rumination and negative automatic thoughts [30, 44]. For instance, Lovato et al. [43] found that depression might act as a precursor to poor sleep, rather than the converse. Moreover, prior research has highlighted a vicious cycle between insomnia and depression; however, the presence of this cycle appears to vary across age groups. For example, Marino et al. [46] identified a bidirectional cross-lagged relationship between insomnia and depressive symptoms in adolescents aged 10 to 12, rather than after age 13. This heterogeneity further highlights the potential value of symptom-level analysis, which may provide an exploratory approach to better understand associations between insomnia and depression.

Grade-specific analysis of cross-lagged panel network models

From a developmental psychopathology perspective, symptom configurations may differ across school grades, potentially reflecting a combination of developmental and grade-linked contextual influences [16]. Consequently, symptom-level associations may differ across grades even when overall symptom levels are comparable, meaning that analyses based on the full sample may obscure grade-specific patterns. Such variability is consistent with the broader developmental and symptom-level heterogeneity observed in psychiatric disorders. Clinical research indicates that the same disorder can manifest with different symptoms constellations [5], and not all adolescents with sleep disturbances develop depression [46]. Taken together, these considerations underscore the importance of examining grade-specific symptom patterns.

Cross-sectional network analysis has been used in previous studies to explore symptom-level patterns of insomnia and depression in adolescents. For example, Wang et al. [60] found that sad mood is a bridging symptom in the network of anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances among Chinese high school students. In another study, Wang et al. [59] showed that sleep disturbances had the strongest association with anhedonia in adolescent major depressive disorder. However, these studies are cross-sectional and limited to single time points. CLPN analysis offers an innovative approach to capturing and mapping the complex relationships between symptoms of insomnia and depression over multiple time points. This method not only identifies symptom interactions within contemporaneous networks but also highlights which symptoms may have stronger predictive associations with other symptoms in temporal networks [21, 23]. Overall, examining both contemporaneous and temporal networks of symptoms may provide exploratory insights into their immediate interactions and predictive dynamics, enhancing our understanding of the interplay between insomnia and depression.

Current study

Using CLPN analysis, this study focuses on symptom-level dynamics to examine the associations between insomnia and depression among adolescents from Grades 5 to 12. The present study aims to: (1) explore the interactions within the depression-insomnia association network in contemporaneous networks to provide insights into symptom co-occurrence patterns; (2) investigate predictive associations between insomnia and depression symptoms in temporal networks; and (3) uncover grade-specific variations in these associations. By identifying grade-specific symptom patterns between insomnia and depression, this study helps clarify mixed findings in prior research and provides a more fine-grained basis for future research.

Method

Participants

Data were collected as part of the broader Research Project on the Prevention and Treatment of Common Mental Health Issues among Adolescents (Project No. CFTC-BJ01-2303063). Each participant completed two assessments six months apart, at T1 (September–October 2022) and T2 (March–April 2023), and remained in the same grade during this period. The survey was conducted with the support of the Lvluo Mental Care Platform (https://www.lvluoxinli.com). It received thorough review and approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the corresponding author’s institution. Data were collected from 208,828 adolescents attending primary, junior high, and senior high schools across participating schools in 13 provinces of China. Within each participating school, the survey was administered as a school-wide census to all eligible students during regular school hours. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and for those under 14, both the participants and their guardians provided consent. Because the survey platform required all items to be completed before submission, no missing data were present in the primary variables. Data cleaning procedures excluded cases with atypical response times, contradictory responses, duplicate submissions, failures on validity check items, or patterned responding. After applying these procedures, a total of 175,950 students were retained for the final analysis (84.26% valid response rate), with the reduction in sample size mainly reflecting data quality filtering.

Measures

Basic demographic information, including grade, sex, age, and boarding status, was measured.

Insomnia severity index (ISI)

The ISI is a 7-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess the nature, severity, and impact of insomnia, including both nighttime and daytime components [48]. In particular, it has been recognized for its strong psychometric validity [6, 15]. In this study, we utilized the Chinese version of the ISI, which has demonstrated strong reliability and validity in Chinese adolescent populations [15]. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (e.g., 0 = no problem to 4 = very severe problem), with higher scores indicating greater severity of insomnia. In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.84 and 0.85 at two different time points, respectively.

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 is a depression screening tool developed based on the nine diagnostic criteria from the DSM-5 [2, 33]. In this study, we used the Chinese version of the PHQ-9, which has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese adolescents [34]. The scale consists of nine items rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27, where higher scores reflect more severe depressive symptoms. In this study, Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.87 across both time points.

Analysis procedure

Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were performed for all ISI and PHQ-9 item scores. The informativeness of each symptom was assessed (indicated by the SDs of individual symptoms) and possible item redundancy was evaluated. These analyses were conducted using the describe function and the goldbricker function from the R package psych [54] and networktools [52], respectively. In addition, sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the impact of removing conceptually overlapping items.

Next, cross-lagged panel network analyses were performed for the entire sample and for each grade separately. Specifically, the contemporaneous and temporal networks were estimated in R 4.4.2. The contemporaneous networks were estimated using the graphical Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBICglasso) within the Gaussian Graphical Model framework [21, 23]. Centrality metrics (e.g., edge weight, strength, and expected influence) were used to assess the influence of nodes within the network [21, 23]. The temporal network was estimated using LASSO regularization with 10-fold cross-validation [55]. In the temporal network, two centrality metrics: in-Expected Influence values (in-EI, indicating the extent to which a symptom is influenced by others within the network) and out-Expected Influence values (out-EI, representing the degree to which a symptom affects other symptoms) were employed [55]. Sex, grade (for the whole-sample analysis), and boarding status were included as covariates, as these variables are directly and consistently associated with adolescents’ sleep and depression symptoms [38, 40]. Specifically, each symptom variable was regressed on these covariates prior to network estimation, and the resulting residuals were used in subsequent analyses.

Moreover, the random 5000 “nonparametric” type and 5000 “case-drop” type bootstraps were performed to test the accuracy and stability for both contemporaneous and temporal networks [37]. Correlation stability coefficients (CS-coefficients) greater than 0.5, along with narrow confidence intervals for each edge weight, were regarded as indicators of network stability and accuracy [22, 55].

Finally, to examine differences in network structure (e.g., distributions of edge weights) and node strength across adjacent grades in contemporaneous networks, Network Comparison Tests were conducted with 5,000 permutations. P-values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure due to multiple tests [56].

Results

Sample characteristics and preliminary analyses

Notably, all items demonstrated SDs greater than 0.25, reflecting an adequate level of informational variability. Moreover, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding the PHQ-9 Sleep Problems item because of its conceptual overlap with ISI items. Both the contemporaneous and temporal networks remained highly similar across the overall and grade-specific analyses. Overall, the main symptom patterns and associations were largely preserved, suggesting that the estimated network connections were unlikely to be driven by item overlap. Together, these findings support the inclusion of all items in subsequent analyses. Correlation analyses indicated that item correlations ranged from r = − 0.14 to r = 0.68. Accuracy and stability of network

Network accuracy and stability analyses demonstrated good robustness across all analyses. For the overall sample, the contemporaneous networks demonstrated high stability (CS-coefficients > 0.75), and the narrow confidence intervals for edge weights indicate high accuracy (Fig. S2–S3). The temporal network was also stable, with both in-EI and out-EI CS-coefficients of 0.75 and narrow confidence intervals for edge weights (Fig. S4–S5). Similar patterns were observed in grade-specific analyses: contemporaneous networks for Grades 5–12 exhibited good stability and accuracy (Fig. S6–7), and temporal networks within each grade also demonstrated reliable stability and accuracy (Fig. S8–9).

Grade-specific contemporaneous networks

Grade-specific contemporaneous networks showed both similarities and descriptive differences across grades. For Grade 5, Sleep Onset consistently exhibited the greatest node strength centrality at both T1 and T2. Motor was the node with the greatest strength centrality in Grades 6, 7, 9, and 10. In Grade 8 and Grade 11, the most central nodes at T1 were Dissatisfaction and Sleep Problems, respectively, while at T2, Motor was the most central node for both grades. In Grade 12, the node with the greatest strength was Suicide at T1 and Sleep Worry at T2 (See Fig. S14 for details). To further examine whether these contemporaneous networks differed between grades, Network Comparison Tests were conducted between adjacent grades at T1 and T2 separately (see Table 2). Network invariance tests indicated significant structural differences (ps < 0.05) at T1 for the comparisons between Grades 5–6, 6–7, 8–9, and 10–11. At T2, significant structural differences were observed between Grades 6–7, 8–9, and 11–12. Node strength invariance tests showed a few significant differences in node strength. Among the nodes with the greatest centrality in each grade, only Dissatisfaction in Grade 8 at T1 showed a significant centrality difference between Grades 8 and 9 (p < 0.01).

Discussion

This study used CLPN analysis to examine grade-specific associations between insomnia and depressive symptoms among adolescents. First, the findings revealed a distinction between contemporaneous co-occurrence patterns and temporal predictive associations. Both networks further suggested that associations between insomnia and depressive symptoms were concentrated in a small number of symptoms and symptom pathways rather than broadly distributed across the network. Second, the results showed both relative stability and grade-related variation in symptom centrality across grades. Overall, these findings may help explain why previous studies have reached inconsistent conclusions regarding the association between insomnia and depressive symptoms. A symptom-level perspective may therefore provide a more nuanced understanding of how insomnia and depressive symptoms are linked across grades during adolescence.

Contemporaneous networks

In the contemporaneous networks, except for the first semester of Grade 8, Motor showed the highest strength centrality in most grades between Grades 6 and 10. This pattern indicates that Motor was more strongly statistically connected with other symptoms at the same time point, rather than implying causal priority or clinical primacy. Motor represents a measurement dimension of the PHQ-9 item “Moving or speaking slowly, or restlessness”, which is both a symptom and a diagnostic criterion for major depressive disorder [2]. Our finding aligns with previous studies highlighting the relevance of psychomotor symptoms in the manifestation and progression of depressive disorders [35, 50] and their association with insomnia [39, 62]. From a developmental psychopathology perspective, one possible interpretation is that, during adolescence, psychomotor symptoms may co-occur with emotional and sleep-related symptoms, thereby showing relatively strong contemporaneous associations within the network. Notably, Motor had a comparatively low mean level (M = 0.19 at T1), despite its relatively high centrality. This raises the possibility that its central position may reflect not only substantive interconnections among symptoms but also item distributional characteristics, such as relatively low base rates in community samples compared with clinical samples. In addition, the regularization procedure used in network estimation may preferentially retain stronger associations while shrinking weaker edges toward zero [55], which may also influence the resulting centrality pattern. Future research using experimental designs and samples with comparable clinical status is needed to further examine the role of motor symptoms within the symptom network.

Furthermore, the contemporaneous networks showed some variation across grades in node centrality, suggesting potential grade-related heterogeneity in symptom organization. The clearest statistically supported difference was observed for Dissatisfaction, whose node strength differed significantly between Grades 8 and 9 at T1. Previous research has suggested that the middle school years often coincide with substantial physical, psychological, academic, and contextual changes [40]. Accordingly, the observed differences between Grades 8 and 9 may reflect developmental and grade-linked contextual influences on symptom organization within the contemporaneous network. However, because the present study did not directly examine these mechanisms, this possibility warrants further investigation. That said, several descriptive differences in the most central nodes across grades did not reach statistical significance. For example, descriptive contrasts in the most central nodes between Grades 5 and 6, Grades 10 and 11, and Grades 11 and 12 did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that the centrality values of these highly connected nodes were relatively close to one another. Moreover, the NCT identified several other significant differences in node strength across grades, although not all of these involved the most central nodes in their respective networks. Taken together, these findings suggest some grade-related heterogeneity in contemporaneous symptom organization, although not all centrality differences reflect robust between-grade distinctions. From a developmental psychopathology perspective, broader developmental changes, including biological maturation, academic pressure, and shifts in the socioemotional environment, may contribute to these between-grade differences [16, 19, 58]. These patterns highlight specific symptoms that may warrant closer attention in future research on grade-related differences in symptom organization.

Temporal network

Taken together with the contemporaneous findings, the temporal network may help explain why total-score studies have sometimes reached contradictory conclusions about directionality: only a few lagged links connected the insomnia and depression clusters, and these links ran in two opposite directions.The centrality results indicated an asymmetrical pattern: relatively high out-EI was concentrated among depressive symptoms, whereas relatively high in-EI was distributed across both depressive and insomnia-related symptoms. However, this asymmetry at the symptom level may not translate directly into total-score associations. Consider that adolescents with high overall depression scores may not score highly on the symptoms that occupy more central positions in the insomnia–depression network. When elevated total scores are driven primarily by symptoms occupying more peripheral positions in the network, the aggregate association between insomnia and depression may appear weakened, masked, or even reversed across studies.

Specifically, Sad Mood descriptively showed the highest out-expected influence in the overall temporal network and in Grades 5, 7, 9, 10, and 11, whereas Anhedonia showed the highest out-EI in Grades 6 and 8, and Suicide showed the highest out-EI in Grade 12. This pattern suggests possibly distinct roles for these symptoms across grades. Given that Sad Mood and Anhedonia are core symptoms of depression [2, 12], this pattern indicates that they may play particularly influential roles in the network. , This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that adolescents with depression tend to exhibit higher levels of insomnia symptoms [41, 43]. Furthermore, Suicide showed higher out-EI in Grade 12, which may be related to the increased academic pressure of the critical final year of high school. Previous studies have indicated that academic stress is significantly associated with increased suicidal ideation and decreased sleep quality among adolescents, particularly during this period [21, 58]. Taken together, these findings are consistent with Cognitive Models of Insomnia, which suggest that depression-related cognitive processes, such as rumination and negative automatic thoughts, may disrupt sleep [30, 44]. In contrast, they are less directly supportive of the SIPYA Model [1], which proposes that sleep problems prospectively contribute to depressive symptoms. One possible explanation is that SIPYA involves cumulative neurodevelopmental changes and multiple intermediate mechanisms—such as disrupted brain network dynamics and repetitive negative thoughts—that may not be fully captured by the six-month interval examined here. That said, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously, as centrality indices do not allow causal inference.Accordingly, the finding that Sad Mood, Anhedonia, and Suicide appeared to be potentially important symptoms should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Further research is needed to determine the stability of this finding across samples, measures, and developmental stages.

Furthermore, Daily Function was descriptively the most impacted node in most grades and overall sample, except for Grades 10 and 11, where Fatigue appeared as the most impacted node. This pattern suggests that they may be particularly susceptible to influence from other symptoms within the network, potentially reflecting downstream functional and somatic consequences. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that somatic symptoms are prominent features of adolescent depression [29]. From a practical standpoint, Daily Function and Fatigue may therefore warrant closer attention when monitoring the sleep–depression symptom network. Future research could explore whether insights from symptom network analyses may help inform adolescent mental health screening and monitoring efforts, potentially contributing to earlier identification of symptom patterns. . Similarly, given the non-causal nature of CLPN models, this finding also requires further investigation.

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be noted. First, the present study was not preregistered. Given the exploratory nature of network analyses, future research may benefit from preregistering analytic strategies to enhance transparency. Second, the interpretation of the CLPN results should be approached with caution. On the one hand, although centrality metrics can help identify relatively central symptoms in the network, they do not indicate causal influence or definitive intervention targets [11, 17]. Therefore, the findings should be considered as hypothesis-generating rather than as direct clinical recommendations. On the other hand, CLPN models have inherent methodological limitations, such as the assumption of stationarity and the inability to fully disentangle between-person and within-person effects [28]. Future research may benefit from applying more advanced methods and causal designs to further examine these associations. Third, although the present study relied on well-validated instruments, previous studies have also applied these measures to symptom network analyses [62]. However, as clinical screening instruments are primarily developed for screening purposes, item-level network interpretations should be approached with caution. Future studies could incorporate alternative measures or objective sleep measures such as polysomnography to further clarify these findings. Fourth, the study sample consisted of Chinese adolescents and the sample sizes across grades were not perfectly balanced, which may limit the generalizability and stability of the findings. Future research could include cross-cultural samples and more balanced grade distributions. Fifth, the present study used a six-month lag interval, which may be more suitable for capturing relatively longer-term associations but may not fully reflect shorter-term symptom dynamics that unfold over days or weeks. Future studies employing shorter or multiple assessment intervals, as well as longer longitudinal designs, may help clarify both short- and long-term temporal relationships within the sleep–depression network. Sixth, this study focuses on the relationship during school days, and exploring the differences between school days and rest days in future research could deepen the understanding of the insomnia-depression network among adolescents. Finally, the present study examined only insomnia and depression symptoms. Future studies could incorporate additional variables, such as anxiety, stress, and sibling relationship quality, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of symptom interactions during adolescent development.

Conclusions

This study used cross-lagged panel network analysis to examine symptom-level associations between insomnia and depressive symptoms among adolescents in Grades 5 to 12, at both the overall and grade-specific levels. In the contemporaneous networks, Motor descriptively showed the highest strength centrality in most grades, while also revealing a certain degree of heterogeneity across grades. In the temporal networks, Sad Mood showed the highest out-expected influence in the overall sample and descriptively in most grades, , whereas Daily Function showed the highest in-expected influence in the overall sample and descriptively in most grades, but not in Grades 10 and 11. Taken together, these findings suggest that associations between insomnia and depression may be concentrated in a limited number of symptom pathways and may show some grade-related variation. A symptom-level perspective may therefore provide a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between insomnia and depression during adolescence and offer useful directions for future research.
