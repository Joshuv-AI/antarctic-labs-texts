Neutrophil extracellular traps associated with severity and prognosis of community-acquired pneumonia
Authors: Yaqi Wang, Huaiya Xie, Luo Wang, Junping Fan, Ying Zhang, Siqi Pan, Qiaoling Chen, Wangji Zhou, Xueqi Liu, Aohua Wu, Hong Zhang, Lan Song, Jihai Liu, Jinglan Wang, Xinlun Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to verify whether neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) was related to the severity of respiratory tract infections (RTI) and establish a prognostic model for severe respiratory infections. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated from RTI patients for RNA sequencing. Weighted Graph Co-expression Network Analysis was performed to identify gene modules and analyze the correlation between gene modules and clinical features. The prognostic model was established by LASSO regression. The concentration of IL-8 in plasma was measured by Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The gene expression levels in PBMC were detected by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). In total, 120 patients with RTIs were enrolled between July 2022 and March 2024. Enrichment analysis showed that NETs formation was enhanced during the early stages of pneumonia and sepsis. IL-8 had the strongest correlation with NETs formation, and the concentration of IL-8 in the plasma of patients with sepsis was significantly higher than that in patients with pneumonia and healthy controls (p < 0.001). NET-related genes were positively correlated with the SOFA score and 7-category ordinal scale. The expression level of MPO and PADI4 in sepsis patients were higher than that in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) alone and healthy controls. The AUC of the prognostic prediction model for severe CAP composed of two genes (PADI4-CD177) showed the best performance, with an AUC of 0.917. This study confirmed that NETs formation was activated in RTI and the 2-gene signature provided a rapid and highly accurate biomarker for predicting the prognosis of severe CAP, which awaits further verification in a prospective cohort.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-02021-2.Keywords: Transcriptome, Community-acquired pneumonia, Sepsis, Neutrophil extracellular traps

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is an important cause of mortality worldwide and imposes a significant burden on the economy and society. The mortality rate increases significantly when the disease progresses to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) or sepsis [1–3]. Although the PSI and CURB-65 can assess the severity of pneumonia, they have limitations in predicting the prognosis of patients [4]. Other laboratory indicators, such as procalcitonin and C-reactive protein (CRP), have only moderate accuracy in predicting the clinical outcomes of patients with pneumonia; therefore, it is still necessary to further explore the biomarkers that can accurately predict the prognosis of patients with CAP [5]. Additionally, pathogens that cause pneumonia include fungi, bacteria, and viruses. Therefore, it is imperative to identify the common host immune response against different pathogens for the development of potential therapeutic targets.

As the first line of defense of immune response, neutrophils play an important role in immunity against infections [6]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which are reticular structures released by neutrophils containing citrullinated histones, myeloperoxidase (MPO), neutrophil elastase, and DNA, are involved in the pathophysiological processes of both infectious and non-infectious diseases [7]. NET production can be induced by various pathogens, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Aspergillus fumigatus and Staphylococcus aureus [8]. Since the outbreak of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in late 2019, the role of NETs in severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection has been extensively studied and NETs formation has been correlated with COVID-19 severity [9, 10]. During influenza viral infection, neutrophils were recruited to the lung, which released massive NETs, and participated in the progression from pneumonia to ARDS [11]. Excessive release of NETs caused acute lung injury in mice infected with the influenza virus and Staphylococcus aureus [12]. Whether NETs can be used as prognostic markers of severe respiratory infections remains controversial. Some studies have shown that NETs were significantly associated with mortality in severe CAP and could be used as a prognostic marker for COVID-19, influenza and sepsis [13–18]. However, other studies have shown that although the concentration of NETs in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of patients with ARDS caused by pneumonia or in the peripheral blood of patients with COVID-19 pneumonia was increased, it was not associated with mortality [19, 20]. In addition, quantitative methods for NETs, including measurement of cell-free DNA, concentration of MPO-DNA or NE-DNA complexes detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and determination of citrullinated histone formation, are complex and time-consuming and have not been used in clinical practice [21, 22].

To address these limitations and controversies, we focused on identifying a stable, RNA expression-based prognostic signature. Here, we collected peripheral blood from patients with acute respiratory tract infection (RTI) for transcriptome analysis to clarify the role of NETs in respiratory infections from the following aspects: whether the enhancement of NETs formation is a common host immune response caused by different respiratory pathogens, the expression levels of NET-related genes in patients with respiratory infections of different severities, and the prognostic value of NET-related genes in patients with severe pneumonia. We also focused on the signaling pathways and immune mediators that may have regulatory relationships with NETs.

Methods

Patients

Patients were enrolled from Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) in Beijing, China, between July 2022 and March 2024. Patients with suspected RTI were identified using nucleic acid testing or chest CT. Patients ≥ 18 years of age with upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) were recruited if the viral nucleic acid test of the nasopharyngeal swab (NP) was positive and the onset of the disease was less than 3 days. The diagnosis of CAP must meet the diagnostic criteria of the guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of adult community-acquired pneumonia in China (2016 Edition) [23], while patients with sepsis were diagnosed according to the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3) [24]. Sepsis 3.0 diagnostic criteria were used to classify patient severity. Only patients with CAP and sepsis whose onset was less than 7 days were included.

Clinical data, including demographics (age and sex), clinical information (comorbidities and oxygen therapy), and laboratory results (routine blood test, C-reactive protein, etiological cultivation, and respiratory pathogen nucleic acid test), were obtained from the electronic medical record system. Patients with URTIs were followed up for seven days, whereas those with pneumonia or sepsis were followed up for six months, and disease prognosis information was collected. Participants were evaluated for disease progression or remission based on 7-category ordinal scale at each follow-up [25]. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of PUMCH (I-24PJ1218). Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients. All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments.

NP swab sample collection

On the first day of enrollment, NP swabs were collected to determine the type of infectious pathogen. For COVID-19 patients, remnant NP swab samples were obtained from the Medical Laboratory to exclude bacterial infections. RNA extraction, nucleic acid amplification, and target gene detection were performed according to manufacturer’s instructions.

PBMC sample collection

Patients with RTI who were enrolled underwent blood collection and disease status assessment on the first day of enrollment. Patients with CAP or sepsis were also followed up and had their blood collected at the 6th month after enrollment as controls. After collecting blood samples, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were separated by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation from 2 mL blood within 4 hours, added TRIzol, frozen in −80 ° C freezer, and stored in liquid nitrogen until use.

Library Preparation and sequencing

RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on a Bioanalyzer2100 system (Agilent Technologies). mRNA was purified from total RNA and the library were generated through NEBNext® Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA), then purified with AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA) and the quality of the library was assessed on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Finally, the library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

Transcriptome analysis

Quality control, reads mapping and reads quantification

The average sequencing depth was 20 M reads per sample. After library sequencing, clean reads were obtained by removing reads containing adapters and low-quality reads from the raw data. An index of the reference genome GRCh38 was built, and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 v2.0.5 [26]. The alignment rate was greater than 85% for all samples. Gene frequencies were determined using featureCounts (version 2.0.0) in the Subread package [27].

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis

The Sva R package was used to remove batch effects (Supplementary Fig. S1). DEGs were compared between the two groups using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.42.0). The resulting p values were adjusted using Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach to control for the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted p value (FDR) < 0.05, found by DESeq2, were assigned as differentially expressed. DESeq2 employs a Negative Binomial Generalized Linear Model combined with an Empirical Bayes approach to stabilize variance estimates across genes, which is particularly beneficial when analyzing subgroups with limited sample sizes. The CPM of each gene, which was defined as counts of exon models per million mapped reads, were calculated using the edgeR R package (version 4.0.16). To eliminate potential confounding effects from gender, age, and underlying diseases, these factors were adjusted as covariates in the statistical analysis.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.10.0). Pathways with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. The results of GO and KEGG analyses were used for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to identify the upregulated and downregulated pathways in RTIs.

NETs and other signaling pathway scores

The NETs gene set was obtained from a published study, and NETs scores were calculated using the AUCell R package (version 1.24.0), which assessed the proportion of NET-related genes overexpressed in each sample [28]. Gene sets of other signaling pathways, including response to type I interferon, response to type II interferon, interleukin 6 (IL-6) production, interleukin 1 (IL-1) production, interleukin 8 (IL-8/CXCL8) production, and complement and coagulation cascades, were extracted from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and were all significantly enriched (FDR < 0.05) in the acute phase of respiratory tract infections [29]. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was used to calculate the gene-set enrichment score for each sample, and correlation analysis was performed for NETs formation and other pathways [30].

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

The clinical data of the participants and log2 (CPM + 1) values were used to perform WGCNA to describe the association patterns of genes in different groups of RTIs. Different gene modules can be identified by WGCNA and associated with clinical phenotype data to identify potential biomarker genes or therapeutic targets [31].

Protein-Protein interaction (PPI) network

DEGs of interest were input into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to build a PPI network showing protein-protein interactions with a combination score > 0.4, which was exported to Cytoscape. The CytoNCA plug-in was used to calculate the betweenness centrality (BC) of genes to identify core genes.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model

To verify whether NET-associated genes enriched in the modules of WGCNA could predict the prognosis of patients with severe community-acquired pneumonia, the gene expression matrix of GSE196399 was used to establish a prediction model for NET-associated genes [32]. LASSO regression was used to filter the genes and construct models, which avoid redundancy and collinearity. The optimal λ was determined through 5-fold cross-validation to ensure that the predictive performance of the selected model is optimal and stable. Patients in GSE196399 were divided into survival and non-survival groups according to their clinical outcomes, and 70% and 30% of the patients were randomly divided into test and validation sets, respectively.

Cytokine detection

IL-8/CXCL8 concentration in the plasma of RTI patients and healthy control were measured using appropriate enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Multi sciences, China).

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA of RTI patients were extracted from PBMC using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ES Science, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The purity and concentration of RNA were detected by Nanodrop 2000. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). The mRNA level was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara). Only technical replicates with a cycle threshold standard deviation less than 0.5 were accepted for subsequent relative quantification. Melt curve analysis for all primer sets consistently showed a single, sharp peak at the expected temperature. The expression level of the target gene was normalized by GAPDH and ACTB. The primer sequences used in the study were shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Two grouping methods were used in this study. To explore the common immune response caused by different pathogens, patients were divided into viral infection; non-viral infection, including bacterial or fungal infection and clinical diagnosis of CAP but not detectable respiratory viral infection; and co-infection (viral and other pathogens co-infected) groups. Unclear diagnoses of CAP and patients with RTI who did not detect a definite pathogen were assessed for CAP by two experienced physicians (X.T. and H.X.). The infectious pathogens of patients with sepsis were detected through sputum or bronchoaspiration specimens. Patients with pneumonia who were followed up for six months served as the control group. These patients were confirmed to have returned to a normal life state through follow-up and had not taken antibiotic drugs. When there were more than three samples in each group, a comparison was performed between groups.

The X2 test, ANOVA, and Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test were used to compare baseline characteristics of participants with different disease severity. Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages). Spearman’s rank correlation correlation coefficients were calculated between NET-related genes and clinical features to assess the potential correlations.

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and the significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Between July 2022 and March 2024, 120 patients with respiratory tract infections were enrolled, including 45 patients with URTI, 59 with CAP, and 16 with sepsis (Fig. 1). The influenza virus and SARS-CoV-2 were the most common viral infections. 52% of patients with CAP and sepsis had at least one comorbidity, and the most common comorbidity was hypertension (29.3%), followed by diabetes mellitus (13.3%) and cardiovascular disease (12.0%). Ten patients with sepsis needed to receive high-flow oxygen inhalation or mechanical ventilation when enrolled, and the median SOFA score was 3.5 (IQR: 2.00, 7.25). All patients with pneumonia had a seven-category ordinal scale of 1 at 6 months follow-up, and the samples obtained at the 6 months were used as a control group in subsequent analyses. During follow-up, three patients with sepsis died (Table 1, Supplementary Table S2).

NETs signature in viral pneumonia and viral sepsis patients

By comparing the transcriptomes of patients with different respiratory viral infections in the acute phase and the control group, NETs formation was enhanced in patients infected with SARS-CoV-2; however, this phenomenon was not observed in patients with URTI caused by the influenza virus. However, pneumonia caused by different viruses, including SARS-CoV-2 and influenza virus, show enhanced NETs formation in the early stages of the disease (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Because NETs formation was a common immune response in viral pneumonia, we compared all patients with viral pneumonia and sepsis to controls. There were 869 and 1454 up-regulated expressed genes and 176 and 483 downregulated genes in the acute phase of viral pneumonia and viral sepsis, respectively, using the 6-month follow-up of viral pneumonia as the control group (Fig. 2a-b). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that NET formation was significantly enhanced during the acute phase, and more genes were differentially expressed during sepsis (Fig. 2c). MPO, CAMP, ELANE and CD177 were significantly upregulated in patients with sepsis or pneumonia, whereas PADI4 were upregulated only in patients with sepsis (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. S3). GO enrichment analysis showed that the defense response to viruses and interferon signaling were enhanced in the early stage of viral sepsis, and the production of interleukins, including IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10, was also upregulated (Fig. 2e). Importantly, neutrophil-related immune responses were significantly enriched in patients with viral sepsis. The interferon-stimulated genes, CXCL8 and IL10 were upregulated in both viral pneumonia and viral sepsis (Fig. 2f).

Common NETs formation enhancement in RTI patients of different pathogens

According to the species of respiratory pathogen, patients were divided into respiratory viral infection, non-viral infection (including bacterial infection and clinical diagnosis of CAP, but no respiratory virus detected), and co-infection groups. Compared to the control group, there were 1020, 476, and 2507 DEGs in the viral pneumonia (n = 31), non-viral pneumonia (n = 24), and co-infected pneumonia groups (n = 4), respectively, and 2084, 3746, and 3831 DEGs in the viral sepsis (n = 7), non-viral sepsis (n = 5), and co-infected sepsis groups (n = 4), respectively (Supplementary Fig. S4). RTI with different severities caused by different pathogens showed enhanced NETs formation, which is not affected by age, sex or underlying diseases (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. S4). The correlation between NETs formation and the implicated signaling pathways, especially inflammatory cytokines, was also examined. IL-8 production was most closely related to NETs formation (ρ = 0.93, p < 2.2e-16). IL-1 (ρ = 0.89, p < 2.2e-16) and IL-6 (ρ = 0.87, p < 2.2e-16) production were also correlated with NETs formation, implying that there might be a regulatory relationship between NETs and multiple interleukins (Fig. 3b). The concentration of IL-8 in the plasma of patients with sepsis was significantly higher than that of community-acquired pneumonia alone and healthy controls (HC vs. CAP vs. sepsis: 1.66 [IQR 1.38 to 1.74] vs. 1.92 [IQR 1.48 to 3.30] vs. 6.32 [IQR 4.54 to 17.6], p < 0.001), and was positively correlated with the SOFA score across all groups (ρ = 0.61, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Table S3).

NETs scores were calculated based on published gene set [28]. The NETs score of patients with sepsis was significantly higher than that of patients with CAP (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3d).

NETs formation related to RTI severity

WGCNA was used to identify gene modules and analyze the correlations between modules and clinical phenotypes during the early phase of the disease. Fifteen modules were identified by analyzing the gene expression profiles of patients with pneumonia (n = 59) and sepsis (n = 16), and most NET-related genes were enriched in the MEtan module. Although the ME_yellow module wass also associated with neutrophil chemotaxis, enzymes released by NETs such as MPO and ELANE were all enriched in the ME_tan module. Therefore, subsequent analyses are based on the ME_tan module (Supplementary Fig. S5). Correlation analysis showed that the MEtan module was positively correlated with neutrophil percentage (ρ = 0.4, p = 0.02), seven-category ordinal scale (ρ = 0.36, p = 0.002), and the presence of sepsis (ρ = 0.36, p = 0.002) (Fig. 4a). The PPI network was constructed using MEtan modules, and the top five genes identified by BC were CTSG, CEACAM6, CEACAM8, LTF, and MMP8, among which CEACAM8 was a marker of neutrophil activation (Fig. 4b) [33].

Correlation of NETs and RTI severity. (a) WGCNA analysis of transcriptome data of pneumonia and sepsis patients. (b) PPI network of MEtan gene module in WGCNA analysis. (c) Correlation analysis of NETs related gene and clinical characteristics. (d) The expression level of PADI4 and MPO

Further correlation analysis of clinical information with essential genes related to NETs, including MPO, ELANE, CAMP, CD177, and PADI4, showed that PADI4 and CD177 were most positively correlated with SOFA and seven-category ordinal scale, and that ELANE and MPO were also associated with sepsis occurrence (Fig. 4c). The expression levels of PADI4 and MPO in PBMC of patients with sepsis were significantly higher than those in CAP (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Table S4).

Prognostic model of CAP based on NETs related genes

Since NETs formation was enhanced in patients infected with different respiratory pathogens and correlated with disease severity, we further explored whether NETs genes could be used as prognostic markers for severe pneumonia. Based on the top five genes with the highest BC in the ME_tan gene module and the genes essential for NET formation, a prognostic model was established for a severe community-acquired pneumonia cohort (GSE196399) using LASSO regression. The AUCs of the prognostic signatures composed of one to five genes were greater than 0.7, among which the AUC of one gene (CD177) was 0.85, and 2 gene-signature (PADI4 and CD177) performed best, with an AUC of 0.917 (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. S6).

Prognostic model of severe CAP. (a) Prognostic model of severe CAP based on NETs related genes and top 5 BC genes in MEtan gene module through Lasso regression. (b) Prediction of transcript factors of NETs related genes and top 5 BC genes in ME_tan gene module

To further clarify the regulatory network of the included genes, prediction of transcription factors showed that both ELF1 and RARA could regulate CEACAM6, CEACAM8, CAMP, MPO, and LTF, whereas CEBPB only regulated CAMP and MPO expression. In addition, ETS1 simultaneously regulated CAMP and CD177 expression, whereas RFX6 regulated MPO, PADI4, and CEACAM6 (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the transcriptomic data of PBMC from patients with RTIs of varying severities to explore the shared immune response to different pathogens and establish a prognostic model for severe CAP. The results showed that enhancement of NETs formation was a common phenomenon in different respiratory tract infections and was positively correlated with disease severity. In addition, NETs formation was related to a variety of cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8, as well as the Toll-like receptor pathway, providing clues to the regulatory network of NETs. Finally, the AUCs of the prognostic models of severe CAP based on one to five NET-related genes were steadily greater than 0.7, with an optimal AUC of 0.917 based on only 2 genes.

NETs released by neutrophils play an important role in pathogen elimination [8, 34]. We found that RTI with different pathogens could result in an increased release of NETs, which was consistent with the results of a previous study in which patients with severe CAP were compared with healthy controls, indicating the critical role of NETs in severe CAP [32]. However, we applied the sepsis 3.0 criteria to classify the severity of RTI patients and divided them into pure viral infection, non-viral infection, and co-infection groups according to the infectious pathogens. It was further confirmed at the transcriptome level that enhanced formation of NETs was a common immune response in patients with pneumonia or sepsis, regardless of the type of pathogen. Most previous studies have focused on severe pneumonia or ARDS; however, this study covered patients with RTIs ranging from URTI to sepsis. Interestingly, although NETs formation in different upper respiratory tract viral infections was inconsistent, NET formation was significantly activated when the disease develops into pneumonia.

The concentration of NETs correlated with the severity of RTI, and appropriate NETs production helped eliminate pathogens, whereas excessive NETs formation could contribute to tissue damage, which was consistent with our findings [35]. The enhancement of NETs formation in patients with early-stage sepsis was more obvious than that in patients with CAP, and WGCNA analysis and correlation analysis both showed that NETs formation was positively correlated with the SOFA score and 7-category ordinal scale. Some studies have focused on the correlation between NETs and the prognosis of RTI patients, but there are still some controversies [13, 19]. Prognostic prediction models for severe CAP were established using NET-related genes and the top five genes of BC in the MEtan module. The best AUC reached 0.917 by 2-gene signature (CD177, PADI4), which provided a possible new prognostic prediction marker for patients with severe CAP. CD177 + neutrophils could release NETs [36]. PADI4 encodes the PAD4 protein, which is responsible for histone citrullination during NETosis and is essential for NETs formation. Previous studies have shown that a PAD4 inhibitor or PADI4 gene knockout significantly reduced the degree of acute lung injury [37]. However, other studies have shown that PAD4 knockout affected bactericidal function in mice; therefore, it is vital to determine the appropriate concentration of NETs that play a positive immune role [38]. While CD177 is consistently recognized as a key neutrophil activation marker associated with severity in septic shock and ARDS [39–41], the prognostic utility of PADI4 has been primarily confirmed in mechanistic models, where its enzymatic activity drives NETs formation and contributes to sepsis-induced acute lung injury [42]. Our finding that PADI4 is a powerful prognostic factor in a general clinical CAP cohort extends the observation previously limited to severe septic shock patients, thus bridging the gap between molecular pathogenesis and clinical outcome prediction in CAP [43]. Biomarkers based on transcriptome sequencing can predict the prognosis of patients with respiratory infections. However, gene signatures related to NETs have not yet been established [44, 45]. Unlike the current ELISA method to measure the concentration of MPO-DNA and NE-DNA representing NETs, we established a prognostic model based on the level of RNA expression, which is expected to be tested by polymerase chain reaction in the future. However, whether our prognostic model is superior to laboratory tests needs to be verified in a large cohort.

Notably, there were significant NETs markers in the PBMC of patients with pneumonia and sepsis, which indicated the presence of low-density neutrophils (LDNs), according to previous transcriptome sequencing results of PBMC from patients with sepsis [46]. LDNs exhibit a lower buoyant density due to nuclear and cytoplasmic changes and therefore co-localize with the PBMCs [47]. While the majority of normal, mature neutrophils were pelleted out, pathological states like severe CAP and sepsis were characterized by the emergence of a distinct, activated subset known as LDNs [48]. Recently, LDNs have been widely reported in the cases of COVID-19 and sepsis [49–51]. Compared with normal-density neutrophils, these cells produce more NETs and have a stronger pro-inflammatory ability [52]. Moreover, current studies suggested that LDNs may reflect excessive activation of neutrophils [49, 53]. Additionally, LDNs were positively correlated with multiple organ failure and the SOFA score; therefore, whether the proportion of LDNs in the peripheral blood is a promising marker of disease severity or prognosis needs to be further verified in large cohorts [54].

In addition to pathogens, various factors can induce NETs formation, such as IL-8, IL-1, type I interferons and coagulation. In this study, NETs formation in patients with pneumonia and sepsis was positively correlated with the production of IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8, with IL-8 showing the strongest correlation. IL-8 is involved in the chemotaxis and activation of neutrophils, and stimulates the production of NETs [55, 56]. The current study showed that the concentration of IL-8 in the BALF was significantly correlated with sepsis mortality [57]. In COVID-19 related studies, it has been found that neutrophil-derived IL-8 can form a positive feedback loop to continuously activate NETs release and that targeting IL-8-CXCR1/CXCR2 attenuates NETosis [58]. IL-8 binds with high affinity to CXCR2 and CXCR1 on circulating neutrophils, inducing their rapid and overwhelming recruitment to the site of inflammation [59]. By inhibiting neutrophil recruitment and reducing the levels of inflammatory biomarkers (like MPO) in the lungs, CXCR2 antagonists offer a promising adjunctive therapy for ARDS to limit disease progression and improve outcomes [60, 61]. While promising, a CXCR2 antagonist has not yet received regulatory approval for ARDS, and trials are ongoing. Our results showed that the expression level of CXCL8 and NETs formation were significantly higher in patients with sepsis than in those with CAP. Therefore, IL-8 may be involved in the development of pneumonia in patients with sepsis via NET activation. IL-1β is mainly produced by macrophages and associated with the prognosis of patients with sepsis, which has been proven as a mediator to stimulate the production of NETs in other diseases, such as atherosclerosis and systemic lupus erythematosus [62–64]. However, it has not been confirmed that IL-1β was involved in disease progression by promoting NETosis in severe RTI. Although type I interferon can stimulate neutrophils to produce NETs, in our study, a correlation between type I interferon and NETs formation was only observed in patients with pneumonia or viral infection, indicating that type I interferon may not be the key factor causing NETs formation in patients with sepsis but may play a role in viral infection [64]. In general, multiple cytokines are associated with NETs release, and the cytokines that mediate the involvement of NETs in the progression of RTI require further study. Furthermore, recent research highlighted that NETs were central to driving sepsis-induced coagulopathy providing a prothrombotic scaffold (DNA and histones) that accelerates coagulation. NETs directly activate platelets and the intrinsic clotting pathway while damaging the endothelium, forming a lethal cycle of immunothrombosis [65, 66].

Our study has some limitations. First, this was a single-center study and the sample size was small, particularly for patients with sepsis and patients with definite bacterial infection or co-infection. To identify gene signatures and key signaling pathways that can predict the prognosis of severe CAP in the early stages of the disease, only patients with CAP or sepsis patients within one week of disease onset and those with URTI in the acute stage were included. Although a small number of patients with sepsis were enrolled, the number of patients infected with different pathogens was similar, which avoided bias caused by sample size to a certain extent. Furthermore, many confounders can influence the expression level of NETs, including medication (steroids, antibiotics), smoking, and sex/age, but after adjusting for age, gender and comorbidities, NETs formation was still upregulated in patients with RTI. Second, NETs release obtained from the RNA-sequencing of PBMC may be influenced by LDNs. This finding required further confirmation through in vitro experiments or single-cell sequencing data in future studies. However, the external validation cohort utilized whole blood transcriptome sequencing, which partially confirmed the stability of the prognostic model to some extent. Most importantly, larger prospective cohorts are still needed to validate this model in different samples and patients with sepsis etiologies. Finally, bulk transcriptome sequencing limited our analysis of immune cell subtype-specific molecular signatures and pathophysiology. However, immune infiltration analysis showed neutrophil scores correlated with respiratory infection severity. Notably, the regulatory mechanism between IL-8 and NETs release remains to be further verified. Although the expression of NETs-related genes was detected, direct detection of NETs indicators will be performed in future.

In conclusion, our study indicated that NETs formation was a common immune response in pneumonia or secondary sepsis, regardless of the pathogen species. Importantly, NETs formation correlated with RTI severity, and prognostic prediction models for severe CAP were established with good performance. Establishing prognostic markers for respiratory infections based on host gene expression could provide rapid biomarkers for detection. Several cytokines are closely associated with NETs formation. Establishing a regulatory network between NETs and cytokines is expected to provide new intervention targets for the treatment of severe respiratory tract infections. The NETs signature and related regulatory relationships proposed in this study require further validation using large cohorts and in vitro experiments.
