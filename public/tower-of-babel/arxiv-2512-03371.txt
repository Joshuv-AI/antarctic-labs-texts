Local categories: a new framework for partiality
MARCELLO LANFRANCHI, JEAN-SIMON PACAUD LEMAY

Abstract
Restriction categories provide a categorical framework for partiality. In this paper, we introduce three new categorical theories for partiality: local categories, partial categories, and inclusion categories. The objects of a local category are partially accessible resources, and morphisms are processes between these resources. In a partial category, partiality is addressed via two operators, restriction and contraction, which control the domain of definition of a morphism. Finally, an inclusion category is a category equipped with a family of monics which axiomatize the inclusions between sets. The main result of this paper shows that restriction categories are 2 2 -equivalent to local categories, that partial categories are 2 2 -equivalent to inclusion categories, and that both restriction/local categories are 2 2 -equivalent to bounded partial/inclusion categories. Our result offers four equivalent ways to describe partiality: on morphisms, via restriction categories; on objects, with local categories; operationally, with partial categories; and via inclusions, with inclusion categories. We also translate several key concepts from restriction category theory to the local category context, which allows us to show that various special kinds of restriction categories, such as inverse categories, are 2 2 -equivalent to their analogous kind of local categories. In particular, the equivalence between inverse (restriction) categories and inverse local categories is a generalization of the celebrated Ehresmann-Schein-Nambooripad theorem for inverse semigroups.

It is important to stress that
\mathsf{L}
is only an assignment on objects and does not involve any assignment on morphisms. In particular,
\mathsf{L}
does not extend to a functor. Similarly,
\eta
is not a natural transformation.

In a local category,
\mathsf{L}M
can be regarded as a total space in which

embeds via the inclusion
\eta_{M}\colon M\to\mathsf{L}M
. With this interpretation,
[L.1]
establishes that the enlargement
\mathsf{L}\mathsf{L}M
of the enlargement
\mathsf{L}M
of

is just itself and that the inclusion morphism
\eta_{\mathsf{L}M}
corresponds to the identity;
[L.2]
makes
\eta_{M}
into a proper inclusion; finally,
[L.3]
establishes that the pre-image
P
of
M
along
f
exists in
\mathbb{C}
and is embedded into

. An important class of objects in a local category are those which coincide with their enlargement.

Definition 3.3
.

A
total object
in a local category is an object

which coincides with its enlargement, that is,
M=\mathsf{L}M
, and
\eta_{M}=\mathsf{id}_{M}
.

Here are now some examples of local categories.

Example 3.4
.

Every category is trivially a local category where for each object
M
we set
\mathsf{L}M\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=M
and
\eta_{M}=\mathsf{id}_{M}
. In this case, every object is total.

Example 3.5
.

Given a restriction monoid
M
, consider the category
\mathbf{L}[M]
of restriction idempotents of
M
, which is the category whose objects are elements
a\in M
such that
a=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mua\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, while a morphism

from
a
to

is an element

such that
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mux\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=a
and

. In particular, the identity morphism of an object

is represented by

itself. Thus,
\mathbf{L}[M]
comes with a local structure, where for each object

,
\mathsf{L}a\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=1
is the unit of

and
\eta_{a}\colon a\to 1
is

.

Example 3.6
.

Consider the category
\mathsf{PARSET}
whose objects are pairs
(A,U)
formed by two sets such that
U\subseteq A
and whose morphisms

are functions

defined between the subsets. Thus,

is a local category, where
\mathsf{L}(A,U)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(A,A)
and
\eta_{(A,U)}
is the inclusion function

.

Example 3.7
.

Consider the category
\mathsf{PARTOP}
whose objects are pairs
(A,U)
formed by two topological spaces such that

and in which the topology of

is the induced topology, and whose morphisms

are continuous functions

is a local category, where
\mathsf{L}(A,U)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(A,A)
and
\eta_{(A,U)}
is the inclusion function

.

Example 3.8
.

Consider the category
\mathsf{IDEMRING}
whose objects are pairs
(R,e_{R})
formed by a unital and commutative ring

and an idempotent element

, that is,

, and whose morphisms

\mathsf{L}(R,e_{R})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(R,1_{R})

4
Restriction categories are equivalent to local categories

In this section, we provide one of the main results of this paper: restriction categories are
2
-equivalent to local categories. This justifies the concept that restriction categories and local categories are two sides of the same coin, with one encoding partiality using morphisms and the other using objects.

We begin by onstructing a local category from a restriction category. So given a restriction category
\mathbb{X}
, define the category
\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]

.

between two restriction functors

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu(Fa)\varphi_{A}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\displaystyle=\penalty\ \mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu(Fa)\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\varphi_{A}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

(

\displaystyle=\penalty\ \mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muFa\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

(

\displaystyle=\penalty\ Fa

(

\displaystyle(Fa)\varphi_{A}(Ga)=(Fa)\varphi_{A}

This shows that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{L}[F](f))(\mathbf{L}[\varphi]_{(B,b)})

(

\displaystyle=\penalty\ (F\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\varphi_{A}(Gf)

(FU,(Ff)\varphi_{N})
. To compute the other composition, notice that, since
\varphi
is total, the following diagram is a pullback:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces FU}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces GU}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces FM}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces GM}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F\eta_{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{M}}

Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}[\varphi]_{M}(\mathbf{R}[G](U,f))=(FM,\varphi_{M})(GU,Gf)=(FU,\varphi_{U}(Gf))

However, since
\varphi
is natural, we can replace
\varphi_{U}(Gf)
with
(Ff)\varphi_{N}
and conclude that
\mathbf{R}[\varphi]
is a total natural transformation.
∎

Bringing this together, we obtain our
2
-functor from local categories to restriction categories.

Proposition 4.16
.

\mathbf{R}_{\mathsf{lax}}\colon\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}\to\mathsf{RCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
and
\mathbf{R}\colon\mathsf{LCAT}\to\mathsf{RCAT}
are
2
-functors.

Now that we have made
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{R}
into
2
-functors, we not only show that
\mathbf{R}_{\mathsf{lax}}\dashv\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{lax}}
and
\mathbf{R}\dashv\mathbf{L}
form two
2
-adjunctions, but also that these adjunctions are
2
-equivalences. Let us start by defining the unit and the counit of the adjunction. Consider a restriction category
\mathbb{X}
and let us unwrap the definition of
\mathbf{R}[\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]]
:

​[Objects]:

An object of
\mathbf{R}[\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]]
consists of a pair
(A,a)
formed by an object
A
of
\mathbb{X}
together with a restriction idempotent

of
\mathbb{X}
. However, since

should be total in
\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]
,

. Thus, objects of
\mathbf{R}[\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]]
, really are pairs

, where

;

​[Morphisms]:

A morphism

of
\mathbf{R}[\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]]
consists of a restriction idempotent

of
\mathbb{X}
together with a morphism

of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]
. In particular,
a=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
;

​[Restriction]:

The restriction idempotent of a morphism

is given by
\eta_{(A,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)}\colon(A,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\to(A,\mathsf{id}_{A})
, that is, by
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\colon(A,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\to(A,\mathsf{id}_{A})
.

Proposition 4.17
.

For every restriction category
\mathbb{X}
, the restriction category
\mathbf{R}[\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]]
is isomorphic, as a restriction category, to
\mathbb{X}
. Moreover, this isomorphism is natural in
\mathbb{X}
.

Proof.

From the previous discussion, we can construct a functor
N\colon\mathbb{X}\to\mathbf{R}[\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]]
which sends every object

to
(A,\mathsf{id}_{A})
and every morphism

to
f\colon(A,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\to(B,\mathsf{id}_{B})
. Finally, it is easy to see that this functor is an isomorphism of categories, which is natural in
\mathbb{X}
and preserves the restriction structure.
∎

Let us now unwrap the definition of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]]
for a local category
\mathbb{C}
:

​[Objects]:

An object of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]]
is a pair
(A,U)
formed by a total object

of
\mathbb{C}
together with an object

of
\mathbb{C}
, such that
\mathsf{L}U=A
;

​[Morphisms]:

A morphism

of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]]
consists of an object

of
\mathbb{C}
subject to the condition
\mathsf{L}W=A
, together with a morphism

. The morphism

needs also to meet two further conditions: (1)

should be total in
\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]
and (2)
(W,f)(V,\eta_{V})=(W,f)
. Condition (1) implies that

, while condition (2) implies the existence of a unique morphism

such that
\tilde{f}\eta_{V}=f
. Therefore, a morphism of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]]
, really is a morphism
\tilde{f}\colon U\to V
;

​[Local]:

The
\mathsf{L}
-assignment of

sends each

to
(A,A)
, and
\eta_{(A,U)}
is
\eta_{U}\colon U\to A
.

Proposition 4.18
.

For every local category
\mathbb{C}
, the local category
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]]
is isomorphic, as a local category, to
\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, this isomorphism is natural in
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

From the previous discussion, we can construct a functor
E\colon\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]]\to\mathbb{C}
which sends an object
(A,U)
of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]]
to
U
and a morphism

to
\tilde{f}\colon U\to V
. It is not hard to see that such a functor is natural in
\mathbb{C}
, and that it preserves the local structure. Furthermore, we can also define an inverse of

which sends an object
M
of
\mathbb{C}
to
(\mathsf{L}M,M)
and a morphism

to the morphism

. However, since

, this functor, which is also a local functor, defines an inverse to

. Thus,

is a natural isomorphism of local categories.
∎

Finally, we can prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.19
.

The two
2
-functors
\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{lax}}
and
\mathbf{R}_{\mathsf{lax}}
form a
2
-equivalence
\mathsf{RCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}\simeq\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
. Moreover, this
2
-equivalence restricts to a
2
-equivalence
\mathsf{RCAT}\simeq\mathsf{LCAT}
.

Proof.

To prove that
\mathbf{L}_{\mathsf{lax}}
and
\mathbf{R}_{\mathsf{lax}}
form a
2
-equivalence, one only needs to show that the two natural isomorphisms of Propositions
4.17
and
4.18
satisfy the triangle identities. Let us start with the first triangle equation:

\displaystyle E_{\mathbf{L}}\circ\mathbf{L}[N]=\mathsf{id}_{\mathbf{L}}

First, notice that,
\mathbf{L}[N]
sends an object
(A,a)
of
\mathbf{L}[\mathbb{X}]
to
(N(A),N(a))=((A,\mathsf{id}_{A}),a)
and a morphism

to
f\colon((A,\mathsf{id}_{A}),a)\to((B,\mathsf{id}_{B}),b)
. Thus,
E_{\mathbf{L}}\circ\mathbf{L}[N]
sends each
(A,a)
to
(A,a)
and each
f\colon(A,a)\to(B,b)
to itself. Now, let us prove the second triangle equation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}[E]\circ N_{\mathbf{R}}=\mathsf{id}_{\mathbf{R}}

For starters,
N_{\mathbf{R}}
sends an object
M=\mathsf{L}M
of
\mathbf{R}[\mathbb{C}]
to
(M,\mathsf{id}_{M})
and a morphism
f\colon M\to N
to
f\colon(M,\mathsf{id}_{M})\to(N,\mathsf{id}_{N})
. Finally,
\mathbf{R}[E]
sends each
(M,\mathsf{id}_{M})
to
M
and each
f\colon(M,\mathsf{id}_{M})\to(N,\mathsf{id}_{N})
to
f\colon M\to N
.
∎

5
Concepts of local categories

Restriction categories offer a language for partiality on morphisms by introducing concepts like total morphisms or the compatibility between parallel morphisms. The goal of this section is to translate these concepts for local categories, giving us a language of partiality on objects. For starters, let us recall some key concepts in restriction category theory.

Definition 5.1
.

In a restriction category
\mathbb{X}
:

•

[
6
, Proposition 6.3]
Two parallel morphisms
f,g\colon A\to B
are
compatible
, written as
f\smile g
, when
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mug=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mug\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5muf
;

•

[
6
, Definition 6.7]
A
family of compatible morphisms
consists of a family
\mathscr{F}
of morphisms, which are pair-wise compatible, that is,
f\smile g
, for each
f,g\in\mathscr{F}
;

•

[
3
, Section 2.1.4]
A morphism
g\colon A\to B

bounds
a morphism
f\colon A\to B

from above
, written as
f\leq g
, when
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mug=f
.

Two parallel morphisms
f
and
g
are compatible when, restricting
g
to the domain of definition of
f
, that is, by considering the morphism
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mug
, it amounts to restricting
f
to the domain of definition of
g
. Moreover,
f\leq g
when, by restricting
g
to the domain of definition of
f
, we get
f
. We now introduce the analogue concepts in the language of local categories.

Definition 5.2
.

In a local category
\mathbb{C}
:

•

Two objects
M,N
are
compatible
, written as
M\smile N
, when
\mathsf{L}M=\mathsf{L}N
;

•

An object
N

bounds
an object
M

from above
, written as
M\leq N
, when
M\smile B
and there exists a morphism
m\colon M\to N
such that
m\eta_{N}=\eta_{M}
;

•

An
extendible morphism
is a morphism
f\colon M\to N
such that there exists a morphism
\tilde{f}\colon\mathsf{L}M\to\mathsf{L}N
, called an
extension
of
f
, making the following diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{L}M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{L}N}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{N}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{f}}

commutative and a pullback diagram of
\mathbb{C}
.

Two objects
M
and
N
of a local category are compatible when they are embedded in the same enlargement. Moreover,
M\leq N
when
M
is embedded into
N
. Finally, a morphism

is extendible when there exists a morphism
\tilde{f}\colon\mathsf{L}M\to\mathsf{L}N
between the enlargements of

and

which restricts to

.

Compatibility between morphisms in a restriction category is reflexive and symmetric; however, in general, it fails to be transitive. The next lemma shows that compatibility between objects of a local category defines instead an equivalence relation.

Lemma 5.3
.

The binary relation
\smile
of compatibility between objects of a local category is an equivalence relation.

Proof.

It is immediate to see that every object
A
is compatible with itself. Moreover, if
A\smile B
, then
\mathsf{L}A=\mathsf{L}B
, then
B\smile A
, and finally, if
A\smile B
and
B\smile C
, then
\mathsf{L}A=\mathsf{L}B=\mathsf{L}C
, thus,
A\smile C
.
∎

We now show that the relation
\leq
defined between objects of a local category is a partial order.

Definition 5.4
.

A category
\mathbb{C}
admits a
partial order on objects
when
\mathbb{C}
comes equipped with a binary relation
\leq
which satisfies the following conditions:

​[PO.1]:

For every object
A
,
A\leq A
;

​[PO.2]:

For each
A
,
B
, and
C
,
A\leq B
and
B\leq C
implies
A\leq C
;

​[PO.3]:

For each
A
and
B
,
A\leq B
and
B\leq A
implies
A\cong B
.

Lemma 5.5
.

In a local category
\mathbb{C}
,
A\leq B
if and only if
\mathsf{L}A=\mathsf{L}B
and there exists a unique morphism
m\colon A\to B
such that
m\eta_{B}=\eta_{A}
. Moreover, such
m
is monic.

Proof.

Since
m\eta_{B}=\eta_{A}
is monic,
m
is monic as well. Moreover, if
m^{\prime}\colon A\to B
satisfy the equation
m^{\prime}\eta_{B}=\eta_{A}=m\eta_{B}
, since
\eta_{B}
is monic,
m^{\prime}=m
.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

Every local category
\mathbb{C}
has a partial order on objects.

Proof.

For every
A
in
\mathbb{C}
,
A\leq A
, since
\mathsf{id}_{A}\eta_{A}=\eta_{A}
. Consider now
A\leq B
and
B\leq C
Thus, there exist
m\colon A\to B
and
m^{\prime}\colon B\to C
such that
m\eta_{B}=\eta_{A}
and
m^{\prime}\eta_{C}=\eta_{B}
. Thus,
mm^{\prime}\eta_{C}=m\eta_{B}=\eta_{A}
. Thus,
A\leq C
. Finally, suppose that
A\leq B
and
B\leq A
. Thus, there exist
m\colon A\to B
and
m^{\prime}\colon B\to A
such that
m\eta_{B}=\eta_{A}
and
m^{\prime}\eta_{A}=\eta_{B}
. However, using that
\eta_{A}
and
\eta_{B}
are monic, we also conclude that
mm^{\prime}
and
m^{\prime}m
must be the idenities on
A
and
B
, respectively. Thus,
A\cong B
.
∎

Next, we shall extend the partial order between objects of a local category to morphisms. First, let us introduce the following concepts.

.

In a local category
\mathbb{C}
:

•

Two morphisms

and

, when their domains are compatible

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota_{A}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\wedge B}}

consists of a family

of morphisms, which are pair-wise compatible, that is,

A morphism

Compatibility between morphisms of a local category defines a coherence relation, that is, reflexive and symmetric, which, however, fails in general to be transitive;
\leq
defines a partial order on morphisms.

Lemma 5.8
.

The binary relation
\smile
of compatibility between morphisms of a local category is a coherence relation.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Lemma

.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

The relation
\leq
defined on morphisms of a local category is a partial order.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Proposition
5.6
. We leave the reader to spell out all the details of the proof.
∎

We now prove that compatibility and partial order in the restriction sense correspond to compatibility and partial order in the local sense.

Lemma 5.10
.

Consider two parallel morphisms

in a restriction category
\mathbb{X}
. Then

and

are compatible in
\mathbb{X}
if and only if

.

. Consider an object

to
(\ast,\mathsf{id}_{\ast})
. However, the morphism

\displaystyle(A,\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\xrightarrow{f}(B,\mathsf{id}_{B})\xrightarrow{!_{B}}(\ast,\mathsf{id}_{\ast})

\mathbb{X}

∎

Definition 6.5
.

A

in a local category
\mathbb{C}
consists of a total object
\ast
of
\mathbb{C}
which is terminal in
\mathbb{C}
.

Definition 6.6
.

A

between two object

and

consists of an object
M\times N

A

To further justify these definitions, we now show that a restriction category has restriction products if and only if its associated local category does.

(

We may now finally prove the main result of this section that a restriction (resp. local) category is Cartesian if and only if its associated local (resp. restriction) category is Cartesian. To this end, we denote by
\mathsf{CRCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
the
2
-subcategory of
\mathsf{RCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
spanned by Cartesian restriction categories and restriction functors which preserve restriction terminal objects and restriction products, and similarly we denote by
\mathsf{CLCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
the
2
-subcategory of
\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
spanned by Cartesian local categories and local functors which preserve local terminal objects and local products.

Theorem 6.9
.

Cartesian local categories are Cartesian objects in the
2
-category
\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
.

Moreover, the
2
-equivalence of Theorem
4.19
restricts to a
2
-equivalence
\mathsf{CRCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}\simeq\mathsf{CLCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
.

Proof.

Let us first prove that Cartesian objects in
\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
are precisely Cartesian local categories. Start by considering a Cartesian local category
\mathbb{C}
. By forgetting the local structure, we define the functors
\ast\colon\mathds{1}\to\mathbb{C}
and
\times\colon\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
, which are right adjoints to the terminal functor
!\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathds{1}
and the diagonal functor
\Delta\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
in the
2
-category
\mathsf{CAT}
of categories, respectively. Since the
2
-morphisms of
\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
are just natural transformations, we only need to check that
\ast
and
\times
are local functors. First, notice that the terminal local category
\mathds{1}
is the terminal category
\{\mathds{1}\}
equipped with the trivial local structure (see Example
3.4
). Thus, by requiring
\ast
to be a local functor is to require the following equations to hold

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}(\ast(\mathds{1}))=\ast(\mathsf{L}(\mathds{1}))=\ast(\mathds{1})

\displaystyle\eta_{\ast(\mathds{1})}=\ast(\eta_{\mathds{1}})=\ast(\mathsf{id}_{\mathds{1}})=\mathsf{id}_{\ast(\mathds{1})}

which is to say that the terminal object must be total in
\mathbb{C}
, that is,
\ast=\ast(\mathds{1})
is a local terminal object. Note that the functors
\ast
and
\times
necessarily preserve the pullbacks of the maximal inclusions since right adjoints preserve limits. Consider now the product functor
\times
. Requiring
\times
to be local amounts to requiring the following equations to hold, for every
M
and
N
in
\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}(M\times N)=(\mathsf{L}M)\times(\mathsf{L}N)

\displaystyle\eta_{M}\times\eta_{N}=\eta_{M\times N}

which is precisely to say that
M\times N
is a local product. Conversely, consider a Cartesian object
\mathbb{C}
of
\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
. The forgetful
2
-functor
\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}\to\mathsf{CAT}
which forgets the local structure, preserves adjunctions, thus, the underlying category of
\mathbb{C}
is Cartesian. Moreover, since the right adjoints
\ast
and
\times
of the terminal local functor
!
and of the diagonal local functor
\Delta
, respectively, are local functors, the terminal object of
\mathbb{C}
must be a local terminal object and the products of
\mathbb{C}
must be local products. This proves that Cartesian objects of
\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
are precisely the Cartesian local categories.

Finally, by
[
16
, Proposition 6.1.7]
, every
2
-equivalence
{\mathbf{K}}\simeq{\mathbf{J}}
between two categories preserves adjunctions, thus, in particular, it lifts to a
2
-equivalence between the
2
-categories of Cartesian objects and Cartesian preserving
1
-morphisms
\mathsf{CART}[{\mathbf{K}}]\simeq\mathsf{CART}[{\mathbf{J}}]
. Thus, the
2
-equivalence
\mathsf{RCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}\simeq\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
of Theorem
4.19
lifts to a
2
-equivalence
\mathsf{CART}[\mathsf{RCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}]\simeq\mathsf{CART}[\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}]
. However, we have already shown that
\mathsf{CART}[\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}]
is the
2
-category of Cartesian local categories. Finally, as discussed in
[
5
, Section 4.1]
,
\mathsf{CART}[\mathsf{RCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}]
is the
2
-category of Cartesian restriction categories.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

In
[
5
]
, a full theory of restriction limits was explored. So far, we have not yet developed a full theory of local limits, that is, the correct notion of limits for local categories which would correspond to restriction limits under the
2
-equivalence
\mathsf{RCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}\simeq\mathsf{LCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
. This turned out to be more subtle and complicated than one might expect, so leave this for future work.

Lemma 7.1
.

8
Inverse local categories

Inverse categories were introduced in
[
17
]
as a multi-object version of inverse semigroups and inverse monoids. Concretely, an inverse category consists of a category for which every morphism
f\colon A\to B
admits a morphism
f^{\circ}\colon B\to A
such that
ff^{\circ}f=f
and
f^{\circ}ff^{\circ}=f^{\circ}
. As observed in
[
3
, Section 2.3.2]
, inverse categories can be seen as a special class of restriction categories, where
ff^{\circ}\colon A\to A
defines the restriction idempotent of a morphism
f\colon A\to B
. Informally, inverse categories are the restriction version of a groupoid. Inverse categories find applications in computer science. In particular, they have been employed in
[
11
]
as a categorical language for inverse computation.

In this section, we introduce inverse local categories, which are those local categories which correspond to inverse categories under the
2
-equivalence of Theorem
4.19
. For starters, recall the definition of an inverse category.

Definition 8.1
.

[
3
, Section 2.3.2]

In a restriction category
\mathbb{X}
, a
restriction isomorphism
is a morphism
f\colon A\to B
for which there exists a (necessarily unique) morphism
f^{\circ}\colon B\to A
such that
ff^{\circ}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
f^{\circ}f=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf^{\circ}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
. An
inverse restriction category
is a restriction category
\mathbb{X}
in which every morphism is a restriction isomorphism.

Remark 8.2
.

In the literature, restriction isomorphisms are also known as partial isomorphisms
[
3
, Section 2.3.2]
.

We now introduce the analogue of inverse categories for local categories.

Definition 8.3
.

In a local category
\mathbb{X}
, a
local isomorphism
is a morphism
f\colon A\to B
for which there exists an object
V\leq B
and a morphism
f\up V\colon A\to V
such that
(f\up V)m=f
and
f\up V
is an isomorphism, where
m\eta_{B}=\eta_{V}
. An
inverse local category
consists of a local category whose morphisms are all local isomorphisms.

To justify this definition, let us show that restriction isomorphisms in a restriction category correspond to local isomorphisms in the associated local category.

9
Join local categories

Join restriction categories were introduced in
[
7
]
. Concretely, the join of two compatible morphisms
f,g\colon A\to B
of a restriction category, that is, partially-defined morphisms that agree on the intersection of their domains, can be allegedly interpreted as the morphism obtained by gluing together
f
and
g
along the intersection of their domains. Join restriction categories were further studied in
[
12
]
; they also found application in tangent category theory
[
1
,
8
]
.

In this section, we introduce join local categories, which are those local categories that correspond to join restriction categories under the
2
-equivalence of Theorem
4.19
. We begin by recalling the notion of join restriction categories.

Definition 9.1
.

[
7
, Definition 10.1]

In a restriction category
\mathbb{X}
the
join
of a family
\mathscr{F}
of compatible morphisms
f\colon A\to B
of
\mathbb{X}
is the (necessarily unique) morphism
\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}f\colon A\to B
subject to the following conditions:

•

For every
g\in\mathscr{F}
,
g\leq\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}f
;

•

If a morphism
h\colon A\to B
, satisfies
f\leq h
, for every
f\in\mathscr{F}
, then
\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}f\leq h
.

A
join restriction category
consists of a restriction category
\mathbb{X}
subject to the following conditions:

​[JR.1]:

Every family
\mathscr{F}
of compatible morphisms of
\mathbb{X}
(including the empty family) admits a join;

​[JR.2]:

The join of a family
\mathscr{F}
of compatible morphisms is stable under pre-composition, explicitly:

\displaystyle g\left(\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}f\right)=\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}(gf)

​[JR.3]:

The join of a family
\mathscr{F}
of compatible morphisms is stable under post-composition, explicitly:

\displaystyle\left(\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}f\right)h=\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}(fh)

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
\mathbb{X}

Lemma 9.4
.

In a local category
\mathbb{C}
satisfying
[JL.1]

\displaystyle\left(\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}f\right)h=\bigvee_{f\in\mathscr{F}}(fh)

\mathscr{F}
of compatible morphisms

and a morphism

of
\mathbb{C}
. For every

Partial categories: an operational approach to partiality

In Section
3
, we introduced local categories as a new categorical framework to encode partiality. We made a case that local categories are suitable to describe
partiality on objects
, in contrast with restriction categories, which are designed to encode
partiality on morphisms
. In this section, we introduce a third approach to reasoning around partiality: partial categories. The objects and morphisms of a partial category shall not be interpreted as partial objects or partial morphisms, instead, the objects should be regarded as resources whose access is not constrained and morphisms as processes that consume their input resources in their entirety. Partiality in a partial category is instead captured by a partial order
\leq
on the objects and by two operators,
restriction
and
contraction
, that control the access of the processes to the correct amount of resources. In particular, the restriction operator
restricts
the access of a process
f\colon A\to B
to a resource
U\leq A
, while the contraction operator
contracts
a process
f\colon A\to B
to a new process
f\up V\colon A\up_{f}V\to V
which ensures that the outputs land inside a resource
V\leq B
.

.

.

11
Inclusion categories: partiality via inclusions

In the previous section, we introduced partial categories as an operational context for controlling partiality. With Lemmas
10.9
and
10.10
, we proved that in every partial category there is a special family of monics, obtained by restricting identities
\mathsf{id}_{A}\colon A\to A
to each
U\leq A
, and that the restriction and the contraction operators are entirely determined by those monics. In particular, the restriction of a morphism
f\colon A\to B
to

coincides with the composition

where
m\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathsf{id}_{A}\down U\colon U\to A
and the contraction of a morphism

onto

coincides with the pullback of
f
along
m\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathsf{id}_{B}\down V\colon V\to B
. In this section, we isolate the properties of this family of monics by introducing inclusion categories. We then show that inclusion categories are
2
-equivalent to partial categories.

Definition 11.1
.

An

on a category

​[I.1]:

\mathscr{N}

​[I.2]:

\mathscr{N}

\mathbb{C}
;

​[I.3]:

For each pair of parallel monics

,
m\in\mathscr{N}
and
m^{\prime}\in\mathscr{N}
implies
m=m^{\prime}
;

​[I.4]:

\mathscr{N}
is stable under composition, that is, for each
m\colon A\to B
and
m^{\prime}\colon B\to C
where
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathscr{N}
, thus,
mm^{\prime}\in\mathscr{N}
;

​[I.5]:

\mathscr{N}
is a display system, that is, for every
m\colon A\to B
of
\mathscr{N}
and any morphism
f\colon C\to B
, the pullback

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

The monics of an inclusion system are called

. An

consists of a category equipped with an inclusion system.

Remark 11.2
.

[

establishes a

-categories. An
\mathscr{M}
-category is a category equipped with a family

Here are some examples of inclusion categories.

Example 11.3

Every category admits a trivial inclusion system, whose only inclusions are identities.

.

The next example shows that not every inclusion category is a local category (in a non-trivial way).

Example 11.6
.

.

Example 11.7
.

.

Example 11.8
.

(Example

) is an inclusion category whose inclusions

.

.

The fundamental theorem of partiality

Every local category
\mathbb{C}
carries a canonical inclusion system, whose inclusions are those monics
m\colon U\to A
between two objects
U\leq A
of
\mathbb{C}
such that
m\eta_{A}=\eta_{U}
. In general, an inclusion category does not carry a local structure (see Example
11.5
). In this section, we isolate the exact condition which makes inclusion categories and partial categories
2
-equivalent to restriction and local categories: boundedness. We then use this characterization to prove the main result of this paper: restriction categories, local categories, bounded partial categories, and bounded inclusion categories are all
2
-equivalent to each other. As an application, we revisit the definition of an inverse local category and connect this to the Ehresmann-Schein-Nambooripad theorem for inverse categories. We begin by defining bounded partial categories and bounded inclusion categories.

Definition 12.1
.

A partial category
\mathbb{C}
is
bounded
if for every object
A
of
\mathbb{C}
there exists a unique object
\mathsf{L}A
such that
A\leq\mathsf{L}A
and
\mathsf{L}A
is maximal in the sense that
\mathsf{L}A\leq B
implies
B=\mathsf{L}A
.

Definition 12.2
.

An inclusion category
\mathbb{C}
is
bounded
when for every object
A
of
\mathbb{C}
, there exists a unique inclusion
\eta_{A}\colon A\to\mathsf{L}A
, called the
maximal inclusion
of
A
, such that if there exists an inclusion
x\colon\mathsf{L}A\to B
, then
\mathsf{L}A=B
.

We now show that the
2
-equivalence of Theorem
11.14
restricts to a
2
-equivalence between bounded partial categories and bounded inclusion categories. To this end, we denote by
\mathsf{BPCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
(
\mathsf{BPCAT}
) the
2
-subcategory of
\mathsf{PCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
(
\mathsf{PCAT}
) of bounded partial categories, partial functors which preserve the boundedness, that is,
F\mathsf{L}A=\mathsf{L}FA
, and partial (total) natural transformations. Similarly, we denote by
\mathsf{BNCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
(
\mathsf{BNCAT}
) the
2
-subcategory of
\mathsf{NCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
(
\mathsf{NCAT}
) of bounded inclusion categories, inclusion functors which preserve the boundedness, that is,
F\eta_{A}=\eta_{FA}
, and inclusion (total) natural transformations.

Theorem 12.3
.

The
2
-equivalences of Theorem
11.14
restricts to a
2
-equivalence
\mathsf{BPCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}\simeq\mathsf{BNCAT}_{\mathsf{lax}}
and a
2
-equivalence
\mathsf{BPCAT}\simeq\mathsf{BNCAT}
.

Proof.

Consider a bounded partial category
\mathbb{C}
. Thus, for every object there exists a unique maximal object
\mathsf{L}A
which bounds
A
from above, that is,
A\leq\mathsf{L}A
. Let
\eta_{A}
be the contraction
\mathsf{id}_{\mathsf{L}A}\down A\colon A\to\mathsf{L}A
of the identity on
\mathsf{L}A
to
A
. Suppose that there exists an inclusion
\mathsf{L}A\to B
in
\mathrm{N}[\mathbb{C}]
. In particular,
\mathsf{L}A\leq B
. Thus,
B=\mathsf{L}A
. Now, suppose that
m^{\prime}\colon A\to\mathsf{L}^{\prime}A
is another maximal inclusion for
A
in
\mathrm{N}[\mathbb{C}]
. Thus,
A\leq\mathsf{L}^{\prime}A
. Consider
B
such that
\mathsf{L}^{\prime}A\leq B
, thus, there exists an inclusion
\mathsf{L}^{\prime}A\to B
, thus, since
m^{\prime}
is also maximal,

. Therefore,
\mathsf{L}^{\prime}A
is another maximal object which bounds
A
from above. Thus, by the uniqueness of
\mathsf{L}A
,
\mathsf{L}^{\prime}A=\mathsf{L}A
. This, together with
[I.3]
, proves that
\eta_{A}
is the unique maximal inclusion of
\mathrm{N}[\mathbb{C}]
and therefore that
\mathrm{N}[\mathbb{C}]
is bounded.

Conversely, consider a bounded inclusion category
\mathbb{C}
. Thus, every object
A
of
\mathbb{C}
admits an object
\mathsf{L}A
and a unique maximal inclusion
\eta_{A}\colon A\to\mathsf{L}A
. Thus,
A\leq\mathsf{L}A
in
\mathrm{P}[\mathbb{C}]
. Suppose that
\mathsf{L}A\leq B

x\colon\mathsf{L}A\to B
. Thus, by maximality of
\eta_{A}
,
B=\mathsf{L}A
. Suppose now that
A\leq\mathsf{L}^{\prime}A

\mathsf{L}^{\prime}A\leq B

\eta_{A}
,

The next step is to compare bounded inclusion categories with local categories. We first show that every local category carries a canonical inclusion system.

In this paper, we have introduced three new categorical approaches to reason around partiality: local categories, in which partiality is on objects, instead of being on morphisms as per restriction categories; (bounded) partial categories, in which partiality is encoded operationally, via the restriction and the contraction operators; (bounded) inclusion categories, in which partiality is described by a class of monics, which generalize inclusions of sets. We may now state the main result of this paper: that all these four approaches for describing partiality, restriction categories, local categories, bounded partial categories, and bounded inclusion categories are equivalent, allowing one to move from one approach to another one, as desired.
