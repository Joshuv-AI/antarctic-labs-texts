Design of Cysteine Functional Polyesters through Michael Addition
Authors: Marta Chrószcz-Porębska, Sylwia Waśkiewicz, Tomasz Gołofit, Agnieszka Gadomska-Gajadhur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The use of amino acid molecules to tailor polymer properties is gaining traction in the field of biomedical applications like tissue engineering. Following this trend, in this work, L-cysteine (Cys) and N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) were used to modify linear polyesters of butenedioic acid, specifically polymaleates and polyfumarates, via thio-Michael addition. The covalent attachment of amino acids via their thiol groups was evidenced by NMR and FT-IR analysis. NMR-based calculations also revealed that polymaleates showed a higher degree of addition (AD) than polyfumarates, with nearly 100% AD achieved using Cys. Further characteristics of the thermal properties and solubility of the obtained adducts revealed that polymaleates are more promising for such applications, as they showed high thermal stability (> 200 ℃), a low glass transition temperature (< 0 ℃), and solubility in many organic solvents. The use of polyfumarate adducts is limited due to their insolubility in the majority of organic solvents. Considering the low molecular weight of the obtained polyesters, it was concluded that the obtained polymaleate adducts could not serve as standalone components of tissue engineering scaffolds. However, it did not exclude them from being used as modifiers for common polylactide or poly(ɛ-caprolactone) scaffolds, as they showed solubility in the same organic solvents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26707-5.

Introduction

Developing functionalised polymers with appropriate chemical and physical properties for biomedical applications is a challenge of modern materials science1–3. Several strategies are employed to tailor polymer properties4,5. Among them, post-polymerisation modification has gained increasing attention from scientists worldwide6–8. A prominent method within this strategy is the thio-Michael addition, which offers selectivity, mild reaction conditions, and compatibility with a wide range of functional groups9–11. This method is significant for grafting biologically active molecules, such as amino acids or peptides, onto the polymer backbone12–15.

In recent years, grafting of L-cysteine (Cys), a thiol-containing amino acid, has attracted attention due to the multifunctionality of its chemical structure16. Cys possesses a unique thiol (-SH) group that plays a crucial role in disulfide bond formation17. Therefore, the introduction of Cys molecules may influence the polymer’s hydrophilicity, charge distribution, and degradation rate, thereby enabling customisation for specific applications such as biomedical engineering16. It is crucial in tissue engineering, where hydrophilic scaffolds with tailored degradation rates are desirable18–21. The introduction of the Cys moiety could also enhance the polymer’s ability to interact with biological systems through disulfide and hydrogen bonding, as well as redox reactions. It is particularly relevant in drug delivery systems, where redox-sensitive linkers are exploited to release drugs intracellularly in response to the reductive environment of the cytoplasm22–24. Furthermore, Cys-modified polymers have shown potential in biosensing, due to the affinity of Cys molecules for metal ions and biomolecules25,26.

An interesting polymers for thio-Michael’s modifications are linear polyesters of butenedioic acid. Butenedioic acid is a dicarboxylic unsaturated acid with two geometric isomers, maleic and fumaric, which differ in the arrangement of their carboxyl groups around the carbon-carbon double bond. In maleic acid, the carboxyl groups are positioned on the same side of the double bond, resulting in a Z configuration. In contrast, fumaric acid has the carboxyl groups on opposite sides of the double bond, resulting in the trans configuration. The undoubted advantages of polybutenedioates are their biodegradability27. Fumaric acid occurs in nature as an intermediate in the Krebs cycle and is converted to maleic acid by the enzyme fumarase28. They are also known for their tunable mechanical properties, and thus found application in many life areas such as unsaturated resins29,30, surfactants31, disposable packing materials32, bone cement composites33,34, drug delivery systems35, and tissue engineering scaffolds36,37.

Despite the advantages of polymaleates and polyfumarates, their application in biomedical fields can be limited by their inherently poor tissue adhesion. This limitation stems from the lack of functional groups on the polymer backbone that can interact with biological tissues. However, this drawback can be effectively addressed by exploiting Michael addition reaction. The unsaturated double bonds in the polyester backbone act as Michael acceptors, allowing for the selective addition of highly reactive nucleophiles, such as thiols or amines, to form stable and covalent linkages. This approach can enhance the cell adhesion of polyesters, increase cell affinity, and control degradation profiles, thereby broadening their utility in the biomedical field.

The research hypothesis of the present study is that polyesters of butenedioic acid can be modified by grafting L-cysteine and N-acetyl-L-cysteine via thio-Michael addition, and that the resulting adduct shows physicochemical properties suitable for use in tissue engineering applications. The research hypothesis was verified for polyesters of maleic anhydride and diethyl fumarate with linear diols of three, six, and nine methylene moieties (-CH2) (Fig. 1) by measuring the molecular weight (MW), the degree of addition (AD), thermal stability (Td), glass transition temperature (Tg), and solubility.

This study introduces a new class of biodegradable polyesters of butenedioic acid functionalized with Cys and NAC via thio-Michael addition, provides insight into how the incorporation of Cys moieties affects the physicochemical properties of the resulting materials, and presents an environmentally friendly synthetic approach that avoids the use of catalysts and toxic solvents while generating no harmful by-products. It represents the initial stage of research aimed at developing L-cysteine-functionalized modifiers for polymeric scaffolds, and it will be continued by evaluating the physicochemical and mechanical properties, as well as the cytotoxicity and cell proliferation of scaffolds incorporating the obtained adducts.

Results

Six linear polyesters of butenedioic acid and linear 1,3-propane, 1,6-hexane, and 1,9-nonanediol were synthesised via melt polycondensation. Figure 1 shows the chemical structure of the synthesised polyesters. Their chemical structure was confirmed by 1H NMR (Fig. 2) and 13C NMR (Fig. 3). The NMR spectra of 1,3-propanediol polyesters were chosen as representative spectra of synthesised polyesters.

Representative 1H NMR spectra of synthesised polyesters: (a) poly(propylene maleate) and (b) poly(propylene fumarate).

As shown in Fig. 2a, multiple signals within the 6.2 to 6.8 ppm range can be observed on the poly(propylene maleate) (PPM) spectrum. They correspond to the double-bond protons. Those signals are separated and can be assigned to the double bonds of polymaleates (6.3–6.6 ppm) and polyfumarates (6.65–6.8 ppm). There is also a low-intensity signal at 6.23 ppm, corresponding to the unreacted maleic anhydride. The remaining signals, located at 1.9 and 4.2 ppm, correspond to the respectively -CH2- and -CH2-O- moieties of the diol segment. Low-intensity signals observed at 1.75 and 3.45 ppm derived from the -CH2- and -CH2-O- end groups. Figure 2b shows the 1H NMR spectra of poly(propylene fumarate) (PPF). There is a single peak located at 6.82 ppm, assigned to the polyester double bond. The remaining signals observed at 2.1 and 4.3 ppm derived from the -CH2- and -CH2-O- moieties of the diol segment, and the low-intensity signals at 1.9 and 3.7 ppm correspond to the end groups.

13C NMR spectra of PPM (Fig. 3a) also showed multiple signals within the ranges of 128–136 and 160–170 ppm, which correspond to the double bonds and carbonyl groups. Their multiplicity also confirmed partial isomerisation of polymaleates to polyfumarates. On the contrary, a single signal is visible on the PPF spectrum (Fig. 3b) in each range. This indicates that isomerisation did not occur. On both PPM and PPF spectra, signals are observed at 32 and 63 ppm. They correspond to -CH2-O and -CH2-groups of the diol segment.

The dispersity index (Ɖ) did not exceed 2.0 except for polyesters of 1,9-nonanediol, which were 2.32 for polymaleate and 4.99 for polyfumarate.

As polyfumarates were synthesised from fumarate diester, they were not subjected to this analysis. The ED of the studied polymaleates ranged from 87.41% to 88.85%. Obtained values were statistically similar (p > 0.05), with an average of 87.95%.

After being synthesised, polyesters were subsequently subjected to Michael addition with L-cysteine (Cys, A1) and N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC, A2). The chemical structure of the obtained Michael adducts was confirmed by 1H NMR (Fig. 4) and FT IR (Supplementary Fig. S2 online) analysis.

Figure 4a and b show the 1H NMR spectra of the PPM adduct with Cys (PPMA1) and NAC (PPMA2), respectively. No signals within the 6.2 and 6.8 ppm range are observed on the PPMA1 spectrum, indicating the complete Michael addition. On the contrary, signals from double bond protons are still observed on the PPMA2 spectra, suggesting the incomplete addition. As shown on PPMA1 spectra, signals from Cys -CH2- and -CH < groups can be found around 3 and 3.8 ppm, respectively. The same signals are visible on the PPMA2 spectrum but differ in chemical shift, appearing at 3.0 and 4.45 ppm. Additional signals, from NAC CH3- and -NH- groups, are visible at 1.8 and 8.25 ppm, respectively. A high-intensity signal at 6.8 ppm can be found on the 1H NMR spectra of PPF adduct with Cys (PPFA1, Fig. 4c) and NAC (PPFA2, Fig. 4d), suggesting incomplete Michael addition. Moreover, they did not reveal the presence of signals deriving from Cys and NAC, implying low addition efficiency.

Representative 1H NMR spectra of synthesised Michael adducts: (a) poly(propylene maleate) adduct with L-cysteine,(b) poly(propylene maleate) adduct with N-acetyl-L-cysteine, c) poly(propylene fumarate) adduct with L-cysteine, and d) poly(propylene fumarate) adduct with N-acetyl-L-cysteine.

The FT IR analysis verified whether the addition reaction proceeds via the thiol (-SH) or amine (-NH2) group. The obtained spectra of Cys, NAC, and PPM/PPF adducts are shown in Supplementary Figure S2 online. As shown, the Michael addition occurred via the SH group. The peak at 2551 cm− 1, deriving from the stretching vibration of the S-H group, is observed in the Cys and NAC spectra. The same peak disappeared after the Michael addition, as shown by the PPM and PPF adduct spectra, indicating the thio-Michael based addition.

Based on the 1H NMR spectra of polyesters and their Michael adducts, the degree of addition (AD) was calculated (Fig. 5). The AD of Cys adducts ranged from 58.8 to 98.9%. Those results are significantly higher (p < 0.05) than those for NAC adducts, which ranged from 62.5 to 80.2%. The AD of polymaleates was also significantly higher (p < 0.05) than that of polyfumarates, except for PNM adduct with NAC (PNMA2), which shows similar (p < 0.05) AD to that of PPF and PNF adducts with Cys.

Further, the thermal properties of polymaleates and their adducts were evaluated. As shown in Table 2, polymaleates and polyfumarates showed similar thermal stability (Td50%), with an average of up to 392 ℃. However, the onset values (Td5%) were, on average, 52% lower for polymaleates. The modification with Cys and NAC slightly decreased the thermal stability of polymaleates as adducts with Cys and NAC showed an average Td50% value of 381 and 356 ℃, respectively. No significant increase in Td50% values was observed for polyfumarates adducts. Significant changes were observed in Td5% values for polymaleates, as it decreased by 100% after modification. Table 2 also shows the results for glass transition temperature (Tg). Polymaleates showed Tg values between − 50 to and − 30 ℃. Polyfumarates showed 2-fold higher values, ranging from 42 to 50 ℃. Although the Tg values of polymaleates and polyfumarates were significantly different, they exhibited the same trend in change, with the Tg value decreasing as the diol segment lengthened. The modification with Cys and NAC resulted in a decrease in Tg values of polymaleates. Still, it did not exert significant changes in Tg values of polyfumarates, except for PPF adducts, whose values increased.

Finally, thio-Michael adducts were tested for their solubility in multiple organic solvents. As shown in Fig. 6, unmodified polymaleates showed solubility in most tested solvents, except for n-hexane, ethanol, and distilled water. The modification with Cys noticeably reduces their solubility. PPMA1 adducts showed solubility only in distilled water, PHMA1 in chloroform, DMF, and DMSO, whereas PNMA1 in dichloromethane, chloroform, THF, DMF, and DMSO. Adducts with NAC showed similar solubility to unmodified polymaleates, except for toluene, diethyl ether, and 1-butanol, in which they were insoluble. The thio-Michael modification did not affect the solubility of polyfumarates. Unmodified polyesters as well as their adducts with Cys and NAC showed solubility only in dichloromethane and chloroform, except for the PPF adduct with Cys, which was insoluble in all of the tested solvents.

Discussion

The post-polymerisation functionalisation of unsaturated polyesters represents a versatile approach to tailoring polymer properties for advanced biomedical applications such as tissue engineering scaffolds or drug delivery systems. The Michael addition reaction offers high chemoselectivity, mild reaction conditions, and compatibility with various functional groups. In this context, linear polyesters derived from butenedioic acid (maleic or fumaric acid) offer a reactive platform due to the presence of backbone double bonds. This study investigates the grafting of L-cysteine (Cys), a bifunctional amino acid bearing both thiol (-SH) and amine (-NH2) groups, onto butenedioic acid polyester backbones via Michael addition. The incorporation of Cys and its N-acetylated derivative (NAC) is expected to enhance hydrophilicity, introduce potential sites for further bioconjugation, and improve adhesion to tissue proteins via hydrogen bond formation. These materials were not previously described in the literature, highlighting the novelty of the present paper.

Polyesters of butenedioic acid were obtained by polycondensation with linear diols of three, six, and nine methylene (-CH2) moieties. The polymaleates, derived from maleic anhydride, represent the cis(E)-configured polyesters, whereas the polyfumarates, obtained from diethyl fumarate, correspond to the trans(Z)-configured isomers.

The polycondensation of maleic anhydride to polymaleates was controlled through titration analysis, which monitored changes in the number of free acid (acid number, AN) and ester groups (ester number, EN) in the polymer sample. As the polycondensation proceeded, the concentration of free carboxylic groups in the polymer decreased, while the concentration of ester groups increased, indicating that the acid groups underwent esterification. The conversion of the acid group to the ester group was confirmed by calculating the esterification degree (ED), which in the final products was higher than 87%, indicating a high degree of conversion.

As confirmed by 1H NMR (Fig. 2) and 13C NMR (Fig. 3) analysis, the polycondensation of maleic anhydride, apart from the formation of polymaleates, results in the formation of polyfumarate fraction, as it is known that polymaleates are prompted to undergo isomerisation to polyfumarates at elevated temperatures38–40. Signals of the polymer end groups were also detected on NMR spectra, indicating a low molecular weight (MW) of the obtained polyesters. It was further confirmed by the GPC analysis, which revealed that the MW of polymaleates did not exceed 4000 g/mol, indicating an average number of repeating units of 14. Polyfumarates showed around 10-fold higher MW than polyfumarates, which did not exceed 40,000 g/mol. It can be attributed to the longer polycondensation time of polyfumarates. As they showed higher stability than polymaleates, it was necessary to lengthen the polycondensation reaction to achieve a satisfactory conversion of diethyl fumarate to polyester, which was reflected in a higher molecular weight. Although the polycondensation reaction of polyfumarates lasted for 30 h, around 10% of diethyl fumarate remained unreacted. Therefore, it was necessary to purify the obtained product from the unreacted ester. Analysing the GPC spectra (Supplementary Fig. S1 online), it can be seen that both polymaleates and polyfumarates consist of several fractions that vary in molecular weight (MW). The contribution of fractions with different MW in the polyesters increased with the lengthening of the diol segment, as confirmed by the dispersity index (Ɖ) values. The Ɖ values were also higher for polyfumarates than for polymaleates, which can be attributed to the longer polycondensation time. The obtained polyesters were also characterised by their esterification degree (ED). After being purified, the ED of polyfumarates can be recognised as 100%. The ED of polymaleates was lower than that of polyfumarates, averaging 88%. It can be attributed to the formation of polyfumarates, which are insoluble in the titration media.

The modification of polyesters was performed by grafting Cys and NAC onto the polymer backbone. Michael’s addition was chosen for this purpose as it is characterised by simplicity. Modifications were performed in a mixture of water and an organic solvent – acetone for polymaleates and chloroform for polyfumarates - at room temperature for 24 h. To achieve the highest degree of addition (AD) possible, Cys and NAC were used in a 4-fold molar excess. This prompted the need to purify the adducts from traces of unreacted amino acids. The purification process relies on the differences in water solubility between the Michael adducts and Cys/NAC. It involved multiple shaking of the products with a fresh portion of distilled water, followed by centrifugation, decantation of the water containing dissolved Cys/NAC, and removal of residual water under reduced pressure. As the applied purification method does not involve toxic organic solvents, it agrees with the “green” chemistry trends, which is its significant advantage.

The occurrence of Michael’s addition was confirmed by analysing the intensity of double bond signals on the 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 4). When the AD was near 100%, double bond signals were almost invisible on the studied spectra, as was the case for the PPM adduct with Cys (Fig. 4a) and the remaining polymaleate and Cys adducts. The effectiveness of Michael’s addition depended on the type of polyester and grafting agent used. Polymaleates were characterised by higher AD than polyfumarates, in the case of both Cys and NAC grafting. It is visible in Fig. 4c and d, where a high-intensity double bond signal is observed, together with no signals from the Cys/NAC -CH < and -CH2- groups, which can be seen in the polymaleates spectra (Fig. 4a and b). Michael’s addition procedure can explain it. The reaction between polyfumarates and Cys/NAC was performed in a mixture of two immiscible solvents – water and chloroform. Therefore, the reaction occurred on a phase boundary, which reduced the probability of effective collision between the reactants, reflected in lower efficiency. It was also observed that the NAC grafting was characterised by lower efficiency than Cys grafting, which can be explained by the effect of steric hindrance exerted by an additional acetyl group. Further analysis of the obtained adducts revealed that the performed Michael addition occurred via the thio (-SH) group, as indicated by the disappearance of the S-H bond stretching vibration signal at 2551 cm− 1 in the FT-IR spectra (Figure S2). It also implies that the applied purification method was effective, as the signal of the S-H bond will be visible if any traces of Cys/NAC remain in the analysed sample.

After being purified, the obtained adducts were characterised for their thermal properties and solubility.

Polymaleate adducts turned out to be less thermally stable than the unmodified polyesters (Table 2). This can be attributed to the incorporation of Cys and NAC moieties, which undergo decomposition at around 200 ℃41,42. It was not observed for polyfumarate adducts, as they showed thermal stability similar to that of the unmodified polyesters. This is likely due to the low AD values for polyfumarate adducts, which suggests that the number of Cys/NAC molecules grafted onto the polymer backbone was insufficient to cause a decrease in thermal stability. It was also observed that the functionalisation lowered the initial decomposition temperature of polymaleates. As it was not observed for polyfumarates, it can be concluded that it is rather associated with the water that remains in the analysed samples, as it was easier to completely dry powdered polyfumarate adducts than the resinous polymaleates.

The glass transition temperature (Tg) of polyfumarates was higher than that of polymaleates (Table 2). It agrees with the literature data and results from the differences in the spatial configuration of their repeating units43,44. Moreover, it was observed that the Tg of both polyfumarates and polymaleates decreased with the lengthening of the diol segment, which is also in agreement with the literature, as the lengthening of the repeating units results in greater chain mobility45. The thio-Michael addition increased the Tg of polymaleates, while no significant change was observed for polyfumarate, except for the PPF adducts. This is due to the decrease in the polymaleate chain mobility caused by the additional Cys or NAC molecule grafted onto the polyester chain46–48. This effect was not observed for polyfumarates, where the AD was lower. PPF adducts made an exception. However, they showed the highest AD among all polyfumarates. Despite the differences in the Tg, both polymaleates and polyfumarates adducts can be used to fabricate materials intended for use in the human body. Polymaleates exhibited lower Tg values than the human body temperature, while polyfumarates showed higher values. This indicates that the physical and mechanical properties of these polymers are expected to remain stable under physiological conditions, at temperatures typical of the human body (~ 37 °C).

Grafting Cys/NAC molecules onto the polymaleate chains influenced their solubility in organic solvents (Fig. 6). It was prominent for Cys adducts, which lost their solubility in most organic solvents. The most significant solubility change was observed for PPM and Cys adduct, which was only soluble in distilled water. The observed behaviour likely results from the combined influence of two factors: the length of the diol segment and the presence of hydrophilic amine and carboxylic groups introduced through the L-cysteine moiety. PPMA1, an adduct of PPM and Cys, was synthesised using 1,3-propanediol, which contains the shortest alkyl chain among the diols studied and therefore exhibits the highest hydrophilicity among the synthesised polymaleates49. Moreover, PPMA1 displayed the highest AD (98.9%), resulting in the greatest number of hydrophilic carboxylic and amine groups grafted onto the polymer chains. The combination of these features accounts for the observed water solubility of the PPMA1 adduct. The remaining polymaleates adducts with Cys were insoluble in distilled water, as they consisted of polymer chains composed of more hydrophobic aliphatic hexane and nonanediol segments. Polymaleate adducts with NAC remained soluble in most of the tested organic solvents and were insoluble in distilled water. It was likely due to the less hydrophilic effect of the NAC molecule and lower AD compared to the Cys adducts. In the case of polyfumarates, no change in solubility was observed due to the Michael addition. Like unmodified polyfumarates, adducts showed solubility only in chloroform and dichloromethane, which may restrict their use in the fabrication of tissue engineering scaffolds.

Materials and methods

Polyester synthesis

Polymaleates

Poly(propylene maleate) (PPM), poly(hexylene maleate) (PHM), and poly(nonylene maleate) (PNM) were synthesised according to the following procedure.

Maleic anhydride (0.30 mol, 29.42 g) and diol (0.30 mol, from 22.83 to 48.08 g depending on the alkyl chain length) were placed in a 100 mL round-bottom flask. The flask was placed on a combined hot plate and magnetic stirrer and heated via an oil bath to 140 ℃. The reaction was carried out for 7 h. After 2 h of the reaction, the polycondensation by-product – water, was removed from the reaction mixture using a vacuum pump. The obtained product was in the form of a liquid resin.

Polyfumarates

Poly(propylene fumarate) (PPF), poly(hexylene fumarate) (PHF), and poly(nonylene fumarate) (PNF) were synthesised according to the following procedure.

Diethyl fumarate (0.30 mol, 51.65 g) and diol (0.30 mol, from 22.83 to 48.08 g depending on the alkyl chain length were placed in a 100 mL round-bottom flask. The flask was placed on a combined hot plate and magnetic stirrer and heated via an oil bath to 140 ℃. The reaction was carried out for 30 h. After 5 h of the reaction, the polycondensation by-product – ethanol, was removed from the reaction mixture using a vacuum pump. The obtained product was then cooled to room temperature and dissolved in 300 mL of dichloromethane. Then, it was precipitated in 600 mL of diethyl ether and kept at −20 ℃ for 24 h. Finally, it was filtered under reduced pressure and dried for 24 h at room temperature, followed by an additional 24 h at 40 ℃. The obtained product was in the form of a white powder.

Thio-michael addition

L-cysteine and N-acetyl-L-cysteine were used to modify polyesters via thio-Michael addition.

50 wt% solution of polymaleates (15 g) in acetone was placed in a 250 mL glass beaker and mixed (200 rpm) with a 10 wt% water solution of Cys/NAC for 24 h at room temperature. The molar ratio of Cys/NAC in relation to the number of double bonds in polyester was 4:1. The solvents (acetone and water) were then removed on a rotary evaporator under 30 mbar. The crude product was purified from the unreacted Cys/NAC. For this purpose, it was agitated with 100 ml of distilled water and centrifuged for 20 min at 6000 rpm. The water, now a solution of Cys/NAC, was removed by decantation. The purification procedure was repeated three times with a fresh portion of distilled water. Pure Michael’s adducts were dried under reduced pressure (500 mbar) at 60 ℃. The final product was in the form of a viscous resin. PPMA1, being water-soluble, could be purified due to its slower dissolution rate than free Cys. However, this process was associated with a low yield, as a significant portion of PPMA1 dissolved along with Cys. The purification yield of the remaining adducts was high (> 90%).

The thio-Michael addition with polyfumarates followed the same procedure, except that the solution of polyesters was prepared in chloroform, and its concentration was reduced to 20 wt%. The obtained product was in the form of a white powder.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT IR)

FT-IR spectra were obtained using the ALPHA spectrometer (Bruker, Berlin, Germany) with the Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) technique, collecting 32 scans over the 4000–400 cm⁻¹ spectral range.

Gel permeation chromatography/Size exclusion chromatography (GPC/SEC)

The molecular weights (MW) and dispersity index (Ɖ) were determined using size exclusion chromatography (SEC) with an Agilent 1260 Infinity system (Santa Clara, CA, USA). The setup included an isocratic pump, an autosampler, a degasser, a thermostated column, and a differential refractometer (MDS RI Detector). Data acquisition and analysis were performed using Addon Rev. software (version B.01.02, Agilent Technologies). MW values were calculated based on calibration with linear polystyrene standards (580–128 900 g/mol). The analyses were conducted at 30 °C using dichloromethane (HPLC grade) as the mobile phase, with a 0.8 mL/min flow rate.

Thermogravimetry (TG)

The TG measurements were performed utilising the SDT Q600 analyser (TA Instruments, Eschborn, Germany). A sample weighing approximately 10 mg was heated from room temperature up to 500 ℃, with a heating rate of 10 K/min and a nitrogen flow of 100 mL/min.

The obtained thermograms were analysed using TA Instruments Universal Analysis 2000 software.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The Q2000 DSC analyser (TA Instruments, Eschborn, Germany) was used to determine the glass transition temperature of polyesters and thio-Michael adducts. A sample weighing approximately 5 mg was placed in standard aluminium crucibles, cooled from room temperature to – 80 ℃, and then heated to 100 ℃, with a heating rate of 10 K/min and a nitrogen flow of 50 mL/min.

The glass transition temperature (Tg) was taken as the midpoint of the transition region using the TA Instrument Universal Analysis 2000 software.

Solubility

A polymer sample weighing 0.1 g was dissolved in 1 g of the following solvents: n-hexane, toluene, diethyl ether, ethanol, dichloromethane, tetrahydrofuran (THF), chloroform, ethyl acetate, 1,4-dioxane, methanol, acetone, acetonitrile), 1-butanol, dimethylformamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), distilled water. After 24 h of shaking using a vibrating shaker (Heidolph 545−10000-00, Schwabach, Germany), the polymer’s solubility was visually assessed.

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as average values of five measurements, with corresponding standard deviations (SD), except for the molecular weight and thermal properties, which were measured only once. To assess the statistical significance of the results, a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test with a Mann-Whitney U post-hoc test was performed using Statistica 13.1 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) software, with a significance level (p) of 0.05.

Conclusion

The primary objective of this paper was to synthesise amino acid–polymer conjugates and evaluate their potential for use in tissue engineering scaffold manufacturing. To this end, L-cysteine (Cys) and N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) were grafted onto the linear polyesters of butenedioic acid using a thio-Michael addition. The Cys and NAC molecules grafted onto the polymer chain can be used as anchoring points for peptide conjugation, thereby enhancing adhesion to biological tissues.

Obtained by melt polycondensation with linear diols, polyesters of maleic anhydride and diethyl fumarate were characterised by relatively low molecular weights. This indicates that their adducts with Cys and NAC cannot be used as standalone components of tissue engineering scaffolds due to insufficient chain entanglement, resulting in inadequate structural integrity. However, their further characteristics revealed that they could be used as a modifier of tissue engineering scaffolds based on polylactide (PLA) and poly(ɛ-caprolactone) (PCL). Among the obtained materials, adducts based on polymaleates proved to be promising, as they exhibited a high degree of addition, high thermal stability, a low glass transition temperature, and solubility in many organic solvents, similar to those in which PLA and PCL are soluble. The latter feature is critical as scaffold manufacturing methods often rely on the polymer solubility. Polyfumarates present certain limitations, primarily due to their solubility in chloroform and dichloromethane, as well as their low degree of addition. Therefore, polymaleate adducts were selected for further study, in which they will be used to modify PLA and PCL-based scaffolds.
