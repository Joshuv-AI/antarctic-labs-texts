The role of stretching protocols in post-fatigue performance and flexibility among soccer players
Authors: Mojtaba Iranmanesh, Elham Hosseini, Roya Bigtashkhani, Aida Sabouri, Mohammad Alghosi, Mohammad Alimoradi, Farzaneh Saki, David George Behm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare the efficacy of static stretching (SS), slow dynamic stretching (SDS), and fast dynamic stretching (FDS) on restoring flexibility, balance, and performance following soccer-specific fatigue. Forty male soccer players (age: 21.0 ± 2.4 years) completed the study. Participants performed a soccer-specific fatigue protocol followed by one of four conditions (SS, SDS at 50 bpm, FDS at 100 bpm, and a control condition (CC)). Measures included knee range of motion (ROM) (Modified Thomas and passive knee extension tests), dynamic balance (Y-Balance Test), biomechanics during a countermovement jump (CMJ) (assessed via OpenCap), 20-m sprint speed, and Illinois agility test performance. The findings indicate that the SS condition showed the greatest improvements in knee flexion (d = 0.51–0.98) and extension (d = 0.45–0.52) ROM. The SDS condition demonstrated superior performance in CMJ jump (highest knee flexion increase, lowest knee valgus, fastest take-off time; d = 0.43–1.89), sprint speed (d = 0.57–0.71), and agility (d = 0.80–0.92). Although dynamic balance improved over time, there were no significant differences between the stretching conditions (p > 0.05). Additionally, the FDS protocol resulted in the smallest gains across all measured outcomes, particularly under fatigued conditions. SDS is most effective for enhancing knee joint mechanics, sprint, and agility recovery post-fatigue, while SS is optimal for ROM restoration. Stretching protocol selection should be contingent on the intended recovery outcome. These findings support incorporating such targeted interventions to optimize athletic performance.

Introduction

Soccer is the world’s most popular sport, engaging millions of athletes across all competitive levels1,2. Although soccer provides substantial physical and psychological benefits, it also presents a considerable risk of injury, particularly to the lower extremities, which constitute more than 70% of all soccer-related injuries, most frequently involving the hamstrings (37%), ankle (25%), and knee (17%)3,4. Such injuries often lead to lengthy rehabilitation, performance decline, and in severe cases, career interruption1. Soccer is a contact sport with high-intensity movements including sprinting, jumping, and change of direction that may increase an athlete’s risk of injury4. Generally, soccer players improve their physical fitness and performance by sprinting, ball dribbling, and other high-intensity workouts5.

While training contributes to enhanced performance, fatigue can have serious implications. In particular, fatigue impairs muscle strength, speed, and agility. This leads to decreased performance, with more frequent shooting and passing errors6. Furthermore, players who are exhausted have challenges keeping optimal posture and balance, which increases their risk of injury during rapid movements7. Additionally, fatigue affects cognitive function, leading to slower reaction times and decision-making, which could make it more difficult to perform and respond to technical and tactical challenges during a match8,9. Effective recovery techniques are essential for ensuring optimal athletic performance and reducing the risk of injury in soccer. Additionally, recent literature suggests a variety of recovery strategies for athletes, including stretching, massage, foam rolling, and heat therapy10,11.

Different recovery stretching techniques have been investigated, including passive static, active static, dynamic, and proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation12. Static (SS) and dynamic stretching (DS) are two of the most common stretching techniques. The most important difference between these protocols is that SS involves the athlete maintaining a fixed, stretched position of the musculotendinous unit for a determined duration, whereas DS involves fast or slow movements through a full range of motion (ROM) that are related to sports performance13,14. Evidence suggests that DS may exert a more positive impact on performance than SS, as it improves muscle activity, sprinting performance, agility, balance, ROM, and recovery12. For instance, in elite soccer players, DS optimized repeated sprint performance, whereas SS was detrimental, slowing total sprint time versus DS (26.3s vs. 24.9s)15.

Additionally, DS enhances vertical jump performance, in contrast to detrimental effects reported with prolonged SS (e.g., > 60 s per muscle group)16–19. A systematic review found that DS improved jump performance by 2.1%, while SS led to a 1.3% decrease, with impairments worsening to 2.6% when stretches were held for ≥ 60 s17. SS is intended to facilitate recovery by allowing muscles to relax and increasing ROM, while DS is also intended to increase ROM and reduce the risk of injury by increasing blood circulation and improving performance12,13. Although some studies support the benefits of DS in improving performance14,20, others have found no significant difference when comparing DS and SS effects on athletic performance19,21–23. As a result of the limited and inconsistent evidence, the main research question is whether different stretching protocols impact recovery and performance in soccer players following functional fatigue. Consequently, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of SS, slow dynamic stretching (SDS), and fast dynamic stretching (FDS) on vertical jump performance, flexibility, balance, speed, and agility in soccer players after a functional fatigue protocol. Finally, the objective is to identify the most effective stretching technique for the purpose of maintaining or improving the performance of soccer players experiencing exhaustion. Based on the prior literature, it was hypothesized that SS would induce the greatest increase in ROM while DS would provide the greatest performance improvements.

Results

Knee flexion and extension in the modified Thomas test (MTT) and passive knee extension test (PKET)

Knee flexion ROM (MTT) decreased across all stretching protocols, with the largest drop in SS. SDS and FDS showed smaller decreases, with SDS outperforming SS. Statistical analysis showed significant effects of time (F = 70.86, p < 0.001, η² = 0.64) and a time × condition interaction (F = 4.25, p < 0.001, η² = 0.09), but no main effect of condition (F = 2.01, p = 0.14, η² = 0.04). Post-intervention pairwise comparisons indicated significant decreases for SS vs. control condition (CC) (5.48 º, p < 0.001, d = 0.98, 95% CI 7.26 to 3.69), SDS vs. CC (2.01 º, p = 0.03, d = 0.34, 95% CI 3.87 to 0.14), SS vs. SDS (3.46 º, p = 0.002, d = 0.51, 95% CI 5.63 to 1.30), and SS vs. FDS (5.22º, p < 0.001, d = 0.92, 95% CI 7.04 to 3.41) (Table 2). Knee extension ROM (PKET) increased in all protocols, with SS producing the greatest gains. Significant effects of time (F = 27.56, p < 0.001, η² = 0.41) and time × condition interaction (F = 3.30, p < 0.001, η² = 0.07) were found, but no main effect of condition (F = 0.85, p = 0.46, η² = 0.02). Post-intervention pairwise comparisons showed significant increases for SS vs. CC (3.16 º, p = 0.002, d = 0.52, 95% CI 5.11 to 1.20), SS vs. SDS (2.0 º, p = 0.006, d = 0.45, 95% CI 3.39 to 0.60), and SS vs. FDS (2.84 º, p = 0.003, d = 0.50, 95% CI 4.65 to 1.02) (Table 2).

Dynamic balance in the Y-balance test (YBT)

Dynamic balance performance showed a significant decline following the fatigue protocol across all conditions. Statistical analysis revealed a significant main effect of time (F = 6.47, p = 0.003, η² = 0.14), but no significant main effect of condition (F = 0.60, p = 0.61, η² = 0.01) or time × condition interaction (F = 0.57, p = 0.075, η² = 0.01). (Table 2).

Knee flexion, knee valgus, jump height, and take off time in the countermovement jump (CMJ) test

Knee flexion angle improved in all protocols, with SDS showing the largest gains, followed by FDS. Statistical analysis showed significant effects of time (F = 554.23, p < 0.001, η² = 0.93), condition (F = 24.10, p < 0.001, η² = 0.38), and a time × condition interaction (F = 5.96, p < 0.001, η² = 0.13). Post-intervention pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences for SDS vs. CC (3.85º, p < 0.001, d = 1.40, 95% CI 2.97 to 4.72), FDS vs. CC (1.30 º, p = 0.01, d = 0.39, 95% CI 0.23 to 2.36), SDS vs. SS (3.42 º, p < 0.001, d = 1.89, 95% CI 2.84 to 4.00), FDS vs. SS (0.87 º, p = 0.03, d = 0.34, 95% CI 0.05 to 1.68), and SDS vs. FDS (2.55 º, p < 0.001, d = 1.27, 95% CI 1.91 to 3.19) (Table 2). Knee valgus increased after fatigue in all conditions. Post-intervention, stretching reduced knee valgus compared to control, with SDS showing the greatest reduction. Statistical analysis revealed significant effects of time (F = 306.10, p < 0.001, η² = 0.88), condition (F = 12.30, p < 0.001, η² = 0.24), and their interaction (F = 27.67, p < 0.001, η² = 0.41). Post-hoc comparisons indicated significant reductions for SS vs. CC (− 1.17°, p < 0.001, d = 1.18, 95% CI − 1.48 to − 0.85), SDS vs. CC (− 1.41°, p < 0.001, d = 0.90, 95% CI − 1.91 to − 0.91), FDS vs. CC (− 0.67°, p = 0.002, d = 0.53, 95% CI − 1.07 to − 0.27), and SDS vs. FDS (− 0.73°, p = 0.009, d = 0.43, 95% CI − 1.28 to − 0.19). (Table 2). Jump height decreased in all conditions post-fatigue, with significant effects of time (F = 38.33, p < 0.001, η² = 0.49) and condition (F = 3.45, p = 0.01, η² = 0.08), but no interaction (F = 0.71, p = 0.63, η² = 0.01) (Table 2). Take-off time increased after fatigue. Post-intervention, SS had the longest times, while SDS and FDS showed better recovery. Statistical analysis showed significant effects of time (F = 60.72, p < 0.001, η² = 0.60), condition (F = 14.44, p < 0.001, η² = 0.27), and their interaction (F = 16.99, p < 0.001, η² = 0.30). Post-intervention pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences for SS vs. CC (0.24 s, p < 0.001, d = 0.95, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.33), SDS vs. SS (−0.29 s, p < 0.001, d = 1.44, 95% CI −0.35 to −0.22), and FDS vs. SS (−0.30 s, p < 0.001, d = 1.25, 95% CI −0.38 to −0.22) (Table 2).

Speed in the 20-m sprint test

Sprint speed declined after fatigue in all groups. Post-intervention, all stretching protocols improved speed versus CC, with SDS showing the largest improvement. Statistical analysis showed significant effects of time (F = 40.86, p < 0.001, η² = 0.51), condition (F = 13.91, p = 0.004, η² = 0.26), and their interaction (F = 2.44, p = 0.02, η² = 0.05). Post-intervention pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences for SS vs. CC (−0.18 s, p = 0.04, d = 0.33, 95% CI −0.36 to −0.006), SDS vs. CC (−0.30 s, p < 0.001, d = 0.71, 95% CI −0.44 to −0.16), FDS vs. CC (−0.13 s, p = 0.01, d = 0.39, 95% CI −0.25 to −0.02), and SDS vs. FDS (−0.16 s, p < 0.001, d = 0.57, 95% CI −0.25 to −0.07) (Table 2).

Agility in the Illinois agility test (IAT)

Agility decreased in all conditions post-fatigue. After intervention, SDS showed the best recovery, whereas SS and FDS performed worse than CC. Statistical analysis revealed significant effects of time (F = 131.63, p < 0.001, η² = 0.77), condition (F = 10.48, p < 0.001, η² = 0.21), and their interaction (F = 14.22, p < 0.001, η² = 0.26). Post-intervention pairwise comparisons showed significant differences for SS vs. CC (2.24 s, p < 0.001, d = 0.87, 95% CI 1.42 to 3.07), FDS vs. CC (2.30 s, p < 0.001, d = 0.70, 95% CI 1.25 to 3.36), SDS vs. SS (−2.93 s, p < 0.001, d = 0.92, 95% CI −3.94 to −1.91), and SDS vs. FDS (−2.99 s, p < 0.001, d = 0.80, 95% CI −4.17 to −1.80) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study shows that SDS is the most effective protocol for post-fatigue recovery, significantly improving CMJ, sprint, and agility. By contrast, SS was more effective for restoring knee ROM, while FDS provided the least benefits. These findings suggest that SDS should be prioritized for post-exercise recovery when the goal is to optimize performance, whereas SS may be more suitable for flexibility restoration.

The findings demonstrated that SS produced significantly greater improvements in knee flexion/extension ROM post-fatigue (51.7 ± 3.8°, 53.7 ± 3.9°) compared to both SDS (55.1 ± 5.1°, 55.7 ± 2.0°) and FDS (56.9 ± 3.5°, 56.5 ± 3.8°). This supports previous research suggesting that SS enhances flexibility. Proposed mechanisms include improved musculotendinous compliance, reduced neural excitability, and increased stretch tolerance24,25. These mechanisms likely allow for more sustained elongation of the muscle-tendon unit, particularly effective under fatigue-induced stiffness. Similar outcomes have been reported by Wang (2013), who found that SS produced superior acute gains in knee extension ROM compared to DS26. However, all protocols showed comparable improvements in hamstrings/quadriceps flexibility. This contrasts with studies reporting DS’s superiority for hamstrings flexibility19,27, suggesting that DS’s benefits may be muscle-specific rather than universal. This discrepancy may be due to our fatigue protocol altering muscle responsiveness, or to the controlled 30-second stretch duration, which avoided the performance impairment often seen in longer protocols. Mechanistically, SS’s advantage for knee extension likely stems from greater plastic deformation during prolonged holds28, while DS’s rhythmic contractions may be less effective at overcoming fatigue-induced stiffness in biarticular muscles29. Practically, SS may be best suited for restoring flexibility after intense play, whereas dynamic methods are preferable for maintaining explosive performance.

YBT scores decreased after fatigue for SS and FDS, whereas SDS enabled the best recovery of dynamic balance. No significant differences were found among stretching conditions after the intervention, although SDS was slightly more effective in improving YBT performance post-fatigue (Control: 97.3 ± 6.4, SS = 96.2 ± 2.8, SDS = 97.6 ± 2.9, FDS = 97.2 ± 4.9). This aligns with findings by Daneshjoo et al. (2024), who reported that SDS more effectively restores post-fatigue balance by enhancing neuromuscular control11. SDS may act through enhanced proprioceptive input, increased corticospinal excitability, and optimal muscle-tendon stiffness. The controlled and deliberate movements of SDS stimulate muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs, improving proprioception and kinesthetic awareness30,31. Rhythmic contractions may also prime corticospinal pathways and counteract fatigue-related reductions in motor control32. Unlike SS, which can decrease stiffness through viscoelastic creep, SDS helps maintain muscle-tendon stiffness, enabling quicker postural corrections11,24. Furthermore, the differences between SDS and FDS despite varied movement speeds suggest that the quality of movement, rather than its velocity, is more influential for restoring balance. From a performance perspective, incorporating SDS into recovery routines could improve both movement efficiency and injury resilience in post-fatigue conditions.

SDS yielded the best recovery of jump height, take-off time, and knee stability, confirming its role in restoring explosive power post-fatigue (jump height: 37.0 ± 1.4 cm; knee flexion: 80.8 ± 1.1°; knee valgus: 17.7 ± 1.5°; take-off time: 0.9 ± 0.1 s). These findings reflect superior recovery of both explosive force production and movement quality, which are essential to optimal CMJ performance. Fatigue typically impairs neuromuscular function by reducing muscle spindle sensitivity and delaying motor unit recruitment, leading to compromised proprioception and joint stability33. SDS promotes neuromuscular reactivation and the stretch-shortening cycle, thereby optimizing coordination and rapid force generation11,32. Conversely, SS produced the lowest jump height, confirming its negative impact on explosive performance13. This outcome aligns with the findings of Robbins and Scheuermann (2008), whose study on the effects of SS on vertical jump height showed a decrease in jump height following the SS protocol34. Additionally, Behm et al. (2016), who suggested that the viscoelastic creep induced by SS may reduce force transmission efficiency19. FDS, due to its higher speed, seems less effective, likely because of suboptimal motor control11. These results reinforce that movement quality, which is characterized by controlled range, tempo, and proprioceptive feedback, may be more critical than movement speed in enhancing jump mechanics under fatigued states.

SDS was most effective in restoring agility and maintaining sprint performance post-fatigue (IAT: 16.6 ± 2.1 s; 20-m sprint: 3.8 ± 0.3 s). SS had limited benefits, while FDS occasionally reduced motor control, offering no meaningful improvements under fatigued conditions. These findings align with prior evidence that SS, while increasing muscle-tendon unit compliance, does not sufficiently activate the central nervous system to optimize performance in explosive or complex motor task19. In contrast, SDS likely promotes performance recovery through enhanced corticospinal excitability, improved muscle spindle sensitivity, and increased neuromuscular readiness32. Controlled dynamic movements, such as those used in SDS (unlike FDS), activate the stretch-shortening cycle in a coordinated manner, which has been shown to improve rate of force development and intermuscular coordination11. This neuromechanical priming effect is critical in post-fatigue conditions, where central drive and proprioceptive acuity are often diminished33. By gradually elevating physiological and neuromechanical readiness, SDS supports oxygen delivery, metabolic efficiency35, and joint stability during fatigue. Furthermore, agility tasks such as the IAT require rapid deceleration, reacceleration, and direction changes, which are closely tied to proprioceptive function and joint stability36. SDS appears to effectively reestablish joint position sense and reactive control by stimulating both intrafusal (muscle spindle) and extrafusal (alpha motor neuron) pathways31. Compared to the faster-paced FDS (100 bpm), SDS’s slower cadence (50 bpm) may allow more precise motor unit recruitment and muscle coordination, which are particularly beneficial when movement quality is prioritized over speed. Despite, FDS decreased agility time (19.6 ± 2.8 s), which possibly occurred due to diminished movement control and incomplete neuromuscular integration during faster, less deliberate stretches. High-speed DSs can sometimes elicit stretch reflex activity that disrupts fine motor control, especially under fatigue, when joint stability is already compromised11. This reinforces the concept that stretching quality, and not merely movement type, plays a critical role in post-fatigue recovery. Taken together, these results support the superiority of SDS in preparing the neuromuscular system for multidirectional and reactive tasks under fatigued conditions. Its capacity to enhance sensorimotor integration, preserve explosive strength, and stabilize joint mechanics makes it particularly valuable for soccer players, whose performance relies heavily on rapid and controlled directional changes.

In conclusion, SS is most effective for restoring hamstrings and quadriceps flexibility, while SDS outperforms both SS and FDS in recovering performance measures, including jump mechanics, sprint, and agility. These findings emphasize the importance of context-specific stretching protocols, particularly under fatigue, to optimize recovery and athletic performance.

Method

Participants

This study employed a randomized, counterbalanced, within-subjects (repeated-measures) design. A sample size of 40 male soccer players was calculated utilizing G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.4) for a mixed repeated measures ANOVA, with α = 0.05 and effect size of 0.30, achieving a statistical power of 0.9. Forty male semi-professional soccer players (aged 18–25 years) with ≥ 3 years of regular training (≥ 3 sessions/week) participated in this study (March 24–April 2, 2025). Inclusion criteria were a BMI of 21–25 kg/m², no history of lower limb injury or disease in the past six years, and no participation in structured lower limb stretching routines. The narrow BMI range was chosen to reduce variability in body composition, which may affect performance outcomes, though it limits generalizability.

Procedure

Forty-eight hours prior to testing, participants attended a 2-hour familiarization session, which included study explanations, completion of consent forms, and baseline anthropometric measurements. The experimental protocol (Fig. 1) comprised a baseline assessment following a standardized warm-up, a soccer-specific functional fatigue protocol (SAFT90) performed to maximal exhaustion, an initial post-fatigue test, one of four randomly assigned interventions: SDS, FDS, SS, or CC, and a second post-test conducted 5 min after the intervention. Randomization was computer-generated All testing sessions lasted approximately 30 min, were conducted in the morning (8:00–11:00 AM) under standardized environmental conditions, and the four experimental conditions were separated by 48-hour washout periods. Participants were asked to maintain consistent diet and sleep, with adherence checked via self-reported logs before each session.

Fatigue protocol

The SAFT90 was developed using time-motion analysis data from English Championship matches (Prozone®) to simulate the fatigue patterns experienced during soccer gameplay. The test involves a 20-meter shuttle run with four poles that participants maneuver around by walking, jogging, side-stepping, and sprinting (Fig. 2). Participants begin by moving backward or side-stepping around the first pole, then proceed forward through the course while navigating the remaining three poles. Participants followed verbal instructions from an audio compact disc (CD) to maintain movement intensity and perform various activities during the SAFT90 test. The audio CD played a 15-minute activity protocol that was randomly and repeatedly looped to cover the full duration of a standard soccer match. The protocol included different movement speeds—standing, walking, jogging, running, and sprinting—based on the average demands of each activity. The 90-minute test was split into two 45-minute halves, each containing three 15-minute segments. After completing the first half, participants took a 15-minute break before continuing with the second half37. All participants completed the full protocol, reaching voluntary exhaustion, which was verified using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion Scale. Strong verbal encouragement was provided throughout to ensure maximal effort.

Stretching protocol

All stretching interventions were supervised and guided by a member of the research team. After the fatigue protocol, the hamstrings stretching routine included two exercises: standing hamstrings stretch, where participants stood with feet hip-width apart, kept their knees extended, and flexed forward at the hips to lower their head toward the floor while wrapping their arms around their lower legs; and a leg lift stretch, performed by lifting one straight leg toward the torso from a standing position. For the quadriceps, stretching involved a standing quadriceps stretch, where participants balanced on one leg, flexed the opposite knee, and gently pulled the heel toward the gluteals while using a wall for support, as well as a forward lunge, executed by taking a large step forward, lowering the pelvis, and flexing the front knee as much as possible while maintaining hip-width stance (Fig. 3). These stretches targeted flexibility in the lower body muscles. All stretching protocols (SDS, FDS, and SS) utilized similar exercises; however, in the SDS and FDS protocols, muscles were stretched dynamically, whereas in the SS protocol, exercises were maintained statically once reaching the maximum ROM, which was determined as the point of maximum tolerable stretch reported by the participant without pain. Each stretch was performed for 30 s with two repetitions (one for each leg), according to guidelines indicating that SS exceeding 60 s can impair performance38. Additionally, FDS stretching occurred at 100 beats per minute, whereas SDS was conducted at 50 beats per minute. Furthermore, participants assigned to the CC (without stretching) performed a resting period equivalent in duration to that of the stretching interventions, after which they completed the post-tests in the identical order. Rest intervals between repetitions and exercises was 10 s. Also, all participants had a five-minute rest period between the stretching protocols and measurements11,23.

MTT and PKET

The MTT involves the subject sitting at the edge of an examination table, raising one knee to the chest while allowing the other leg free. The healthcare professional subsequently supported the subject to relax while keeping the knee flexion of the affected leg. The healthcare professional examined the contralateral knee extension, indicating tension in the quadriceps muscles39,40. The PKET was performed to evaluate hamstrings length and total knee joint mobility in subjects. Initially, each subject was placed supine on an examination table, with one leg stretched straight and the opposite hip flexed at approximately 90 degrees. An examiner held the thigh of the flexed leg just proximal to the knee with one hand while holding the ankle with the other hand. The examiner subsequently extended the knee joint to its full ROM, carefully observing for any symptoms of discomfort or pain during the procedure. After reaching maximum knee extension ROM (the point just before the onset of pain or discomfort), the position was maintained temporarily to allow accurate measurement41,42. A standard 180° clear plastic universal goniometer (Ghamatpooyan, Tehran, Iran) was utilized to assess the angle of knee flexion (for MTT) knee extension (for PKET), with the axis placed at the lateral epicondyle of the femur, the fixed arm aligned with the greater trochanter, and the moving arm directed towards the lateral malleolus. These tests were conducted three times, and the mean of the resulting data was documented39–42. The test-retest reliability was 0.92 for the MTT and 0.98 for the PKET43,44.

YBT

The YBT involved individuals standing on one leg in the center of a Y-shaped device, with the other leg positioned behind them. The assessment required extending as far as possible in three directions: anterior, which is forward; posteromedial, which is diagonally backward toward the midline; and posterolateral, which is diagonally backward away from the midline, while maintaining balance on the standing leg. Each participant performed three attempts in each direction, and the greatest distance reached was recorded. Distances were measured from the center of the YBT platform to the farthest point reached by the foot. Reach distances were normalized to leg length using the formula (reach distance divided by leg length times 100), where leg length was measured from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus45. One failed attempt, defined as touching down with the reaching foot or losing balance, was allowed per direction. The intraclass correlation coefficient reported for this test was 0.9146.

CMJ

The CMJ must be tested methodically in order to be evaluated with OpenCap software. To complete the CMJ, participants were asked to stand with their feet shoulder-width apart, squatted quickly to their preferred knee flexion depth, and then jumped vertically with maximal speed and effort, and finally landing comfortably. This test was conducted using two iPhones strategically positioned to film the jumps from multiple viewpoints. OpenCap examines video footage to deliver insights into essential knee kinematic characteristics, including “knee flexion” and “knee valgus”, as well as “jump height” and “take off time”47–49. This comprehensive evaluation assists in evaluating an athlete’s biomechanics and pinpointing potential injury risks or opportunities for performance enhancement. OpenCap’s markerless motion capture system has demonstrated reliability in estimating vertical ground reaction forces and other kinematic variables, rendering it an invaluable instrument for field-based biomechanical assessment50. The program enables comprehensive assessment of movement strategies in jumping exercises, essential for optimizing athletic performance and improving injury prevention strategies47,48,50. The test-retest reliability reported for this test was between 0.62 and 0.9351.

20-m sprint and IAT

The 20-meter sprint test and the IAT were conducted using Smart speed timing technology (Fusion Sport, Australia). Participants began from a standing position behind the starting line and sprinted at maximal velocity to the finish line, while timing gates automatically recorded sprint time52. For the IAT, cones were arranged in a rectangular pattern following the standard protocol. Upon a start signal, participants sprinted from behind the line to the first cone, weaved through the cones, and returned to the start line. The Smart speed system recorded split times at multiple checkpoints along the course. Each participant completed two trials for both tests, with the best performance used for analysis45,53. A 2-minute rest interval was provided between trials to minimize fatigue. Standardized verbal encouragement was given throughout testing to promote maximal effort while ensuring consistency across participants. All assessments were performed outdoors on natural grass under stable environmental conditions. The test-retest reliability values reported were 0.98 for the 20-m sprint and 0.96 for the IAT54,55.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS Version 26 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) was used for statistical analysis. The Levene’s Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to determine if the scores were normally distributed and if the differences between conditions were homogeneous (p > 0.05). For all dependent variables, mixed repeated measure ANOVA was performed with time (pre-test vs. post-fatigue vs. post-stretching protocols) and conditions (SDS vs. FDS vs. SS vs. CC) as factors. Significant time × condition interactions were followed by post-hoc Bonferroni tests for pairwise comparisons. The effect sizes for all parameters were assessed using partial η², with values of 0.02, 0.13, and 0.26 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively56. Additionally, the magnitude of differences for pairwise comparisons was evaluated using Cohen’s d effect size for normally distributed data, interpreted as follows: trivial (< 0.20), small (0.20 to 0.59), moderate (0.60 to 1.19), large (1.20 to 1.99), and very large (≥ 2.0)57. A significance level of 0.05 was used for all statistical parameters.
