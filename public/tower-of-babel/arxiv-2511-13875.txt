Market competition and poverty dynamics: Short and long run effects across financial development levels
Mohamed Chaouch, Thanasis Stengos

Abstract
. This paper investigates how market competition influences poverty dynamics using a functional econometric framework that captures both contemporaneous and lagged effects. Using annual data for 48 countries from 1991–2017, we estimate function-on-function regressions linking poverty headcount ratios to market concentration and other macroeconomic indicators. The results show that, based on the entire sample, stronger competition initially increased poverty during structural adjustment phases, but its adverse impact weakened after 2010 as economies adapted and efficiency gains emerged. The estimated bivariate surfaces reveal that the effect of competition on poverty often persists over multiple years (around 5 years), highlighting the importance of intertemporal transmission. Then, functional clustering based on market capitalization (MCAP) uncovers strong heterogeneity: pro-poor 5-years lagged effect of competition in low- and medium-MCAP economies, while it remains insignificant to weakly negative in high-MCAP countries. Overall, the findings underscore the value of functional data methods in uncovering evolving and lag-dependent poverty–competition linkages that static panel models fail to capture.

1.
Introduction

Reducing poverty and promoting shared prosperity remain central objectives of development policy, yet the channels through which structural reforms influence poverty dynamics are still imperfectly understood. Over the past three decades, many low- and middle-income countries have implemented product-market liberalization, privatization, and deregulation, often combined with trade opening, in ways that have substantially strengthened competition in domestic markets. These reforms are typically justified on efficiency grounds: by reducing market power and barriers to entry, stronger competition is expected to compress markups, reallocate resources toward more productive firms, stimulate innovation, and ultimately support higher growth. Whether such efficiency gains translate into lower poverty, however, depends on how the gains and losses from competition are distributed across households and along the income distribution.

Competition affects poor households through several intertwined channels. On the consumption side, lower markups and reduced market power can generate substantial price reductions in markets for food, energy, transport, medicines, and other basic goods. Because poor households devote a large share of their budgets to these necessities, even modest price cuts can have large welfare effects
(
2
;
10
;
11
, e.g.)
. On the production side, increased competition can spur entry and expansion of more efficient firms, raise aggregate productivity, and potentially increase labor demand and wages. At the same time, competitive pressure can displace incumbent firms, destroy jobs in previously protected sectors, compress wages in segments with weak bargaining power, and push marginal producers into low-productivity informal activities. In environments characterized by pervasive informality, limited access to finance, and weak social protection, these transitional costs can be large and may dominate the longer-run efficiency gains in the short and medium run
(
3
)
.

Recent theoretical work has sharpened this ambiguity by embedding competition into general-equilibrium frameworks with heterogeneous agents, imperfect labor markets, and explicit ownership structure. In a vertically differentiated setting with consumers who can be workers and shareholders,
7
show that moving from monopoly to duopoly can either reduce or increase the headcount poverty rate, depending on the position of the poverty line, the dispersion of preferences and productivities, and the share of profits accruing to poor versus rich households. When ownership of firms is concentrated among richer households and the poor obtain most of their income from wages and informal earnings, intensified competition tends to reduce profits for the rich while lowering prices and sometimes raising wages, so that poverty can fall. When poor households also rely on small-scale business profits that are threatened by more efficient entrants, or when labor-market adjustment is slow, poverty may instead increase. This literature emphasizes that the impact of competition on poverty is inherently state-dependent and mediated by the distribution of assets, the structure of labor and product markets, and the depth of financial systems, rather than being unconditionally pro-poor.

A parallel policy-oriented literature reaches similar conclusions using case studies and distributional simulations. World Bank and OECD reports document that a lack of competition in markets for essential goods disproportionately harms poor consumers, whereas tackling cartels or anti-competitive regulations in sectors such as food, transport, and pharmaceuticals can generate meaningful poverty reductions through the price channel
(
3
)
. For South Africa, for example,
2

Similar evidence from Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa shows that cartels and regulatory restrictions in food and transport markets have raised prices by 30–50%, with disproportionate impacts on poor households
(
3
)
.

Despite this growing interest, empirical evidence that directly links competition to poverty outcomes remains relatively limited and fragmented. Much of the existing work either focuses on sector-specific price effects or treats competition as part of broader trade and regulatory reforms. Moreover, most empirical analyses rely on static or near-static frameworks—typically reduced-form panel regressions—that link annual poverty indicators to contemporaneous or one-period-lagged measures of competition, imposing constant effects over long periods of time. Such specifications are ill-suited to capture the idea that both competition and poverty evolve gradually as part of longer-term development trajectories, and that the impact of competition on poverty may change sign along the reform process. For example, reforms that intensify competition may initially raise poverty through job losses and business closures but later reduce it as efficiency gains are passed on to consumers and as labor markets and institutions adjust. The timing and magnitude of these effects are likely to differ systematically across countries depending on their level of financial development and their institutional capacity to manage structural change.

In this paper we contribute to this debate by studying how competition affects poverty over time and across levels of financial development in a large cross-country setting. Using annual data for 48 countries over the period 1991–2017, we combine internationally comparable poverty headcount ratios at different international poverty lines with a Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) of market concentration as a proxy for product-market competition and a set of macroeconomic controls, including GDP per capita, government expenditure, trade openness, inflation, and growth, as well as a measure of financial development (market capitalization over GDP). We model poverty and competition as smoothly evolving trajectories and estimate how their relationship varies both over calendar time and across lags. We further ask whether the competition–poverty relationship differs across clusters of countries with low, medium, and high financial development, recognizing that the ability of poor households to benefit from competition depends critically on access to credit, the breadth of asset ownership, and the existence of mechanisms to insure against shocks. In doing so, we speak directly to recent theoretical work on competition and poverty and to policy debates on competition policy as an instrument for inclusive growth.

2.
Literature review

The empirical literature on competition and poverty spans several strands that differ in their focus and methodological approach. A first strand consists of sectoral studies that analyze how changes in competition in markets for basic goods affect the real incomes of poor households. These papers typically combine household expenditure surveys with detailed price data and simulate how prices would change under more competitive market structures, for instance, under the elimination of cartels or marketing boards.
4
examine the abolition of Madagascar’s vanilla marketing board and find that increased competition in the vanilla market lowered poverty among smallholders.
19
analyzes food policy in Indonesia and shows that price distortions in staple food markets have sizable poverty impacts. More broadly, World Bank tools such as the Welfare and Competition approach developed by
14
simulate the distributional effects of reductions in market concentration in food and other essential sectors, showing that poor households gain disproportionately from lower prices. These studies are typically partial-equilibrium and price-channel focused: they take quantities and factor incomes as given, or treat them in a highly stylized manner, and concentrate on the direct consumption-side impact of price changes.

A second, closely related strand examines local market concentration and poverty using micro or meso-level data.
15
study retail market concentration in Mexico, using a municipality-level panel with detailed measures of concentration in the retail sector. They find that higher local concentration is associated with higher poverty, and that simulated reductions in concentration through more competition would have non-trivial poverty-reducing effects. Their work resonates with other studies documenting that market power in downstream sectors such as retail and logistics can erode the real incomes of the poor, even when upstream production is competitive. In a similar spirit, case studies on Kenya and other African countries show that trade and regulatory policies that shield domestic incumbents in sugar and maize from competition keep consumer prices well above world levels, with adverse effects on income and poverty among net consumers
(
1
;
3
)
. Methodologically, these contributions typically estimate reduced-form regressions of poverty or welfare indicators on measures of local concentration, controlling for fixed effects and standard observables, sometimes complemented by policy simulations.

A third strand focuses on the broader relationship between competition, market power, and poverty using cross-country or national panel data.
16
uses a panel of countries to analyze how market concentration at the national level is related to poverty, employing fixed-effects regressions where headcount poverty ratios at different lines are regressed on measures of product-market concentration and macroeconomic controls. His findings indicate that higher concentration is associated with higher poverty, particularly in countries with weaker institutions and lower financial development. In parallel, World Bank and OECD flagship reports synthesize a range of country experiences and econometric evidence, arguing that competition policy can support shared prosperity by lowering prices, improving access to jobs and inputs, and unlocking productivity growth, while highlighting that the poverty impact of competition is mediated by complementary policies and institutions
(
3
;
12
)
. Empirically, these studies predominantly rely on linear panel models with country and time fixed effects, sometimes allowing for interaction terms (for example between competition and financial development or inequality), but generally treating the coefficients as constant over time.

A more structural strand of the literature employs computable general equilibrium (CGE) models or analytically tractable general-equilibrium frameworks to study how changes in market structure and competition affect poverty through both price and income channels. Early CGE studies embed competitive or oligopolistic product markets into multi-sector models calibrated to social accounting matrices and household survey data, and simulate the effects of tariff reforms, changes in markups, or the dismantling of marketing boards on poverty and inequality. More recently,
7
develop a vertically differentiated general-equilibrium model with heterogeneous households who act as consumers, workers, and shareholders, and compare poverty outcomes under monopoly and duopoly. They show that the effect of increased competition on poverty depends on the poverty line, the distribution of preferences and productivity, and, crucially, the ownership structure. Their calibration and simulation exercises illustrate cases where moving from monopoly to duopoly is poverty-reducing and others where it is poverty-increasing, highlighting the importance of context. In their empirical companion analysis, they estimate static panel regressions of poverty headcounts at multiple lines on measures of concentration and macro controls, finding suggestive evidence that lower concentration tends to reduce poverty but with substantial heterogeneity across income groups and regions.

Across these strands, two methodological features stand out. First, poverty and competition are almost always modeled as discrete-time variables observed annually or at similar frequencies, and the effect of competition on poverty is assumed to be linear and time-invariant (conditional on observables). Even when dynamics are introduced through a small number of lags or through error-correction terms, the structure is highly constrained and does not allow for rich, time-varying effects. Second, the link between competition and poverty is predominantly estimated in a scalar-on-scalar framework, in which a single measure of competition at date
t
(or
t-1
) is related to a single poverty statistic at date
t
, without modeling the joint evolution of their trajectories. As a result, existing empirical approaches are ill-equipped to capture the possibility that competitive reforms are initially poverty-increasing but eventually poverty-reducing, that their effects propagate over several years, and that the shape of this propagation mechanism differs systematically across development and financial regimes.

By contrast, the approach we pursue in this paper is explicitly dynamic and trajectory-based. We treat competition and poverty as smoothly evolving processes and use a functional econometric framework to estimate a time-varying and lagged relationship between them, while allowing this relationship to differ across clusters of countries defined by their financial-development paths. In doing so, we build on the economic competition–poverty literature reviewed above, while introducing tools that are better suited to capture the long-run, state-dependent dynamics that theoretical and policy discussions emphasize but standard empirical models largely abstract from. Specifically, our framework allows us to trace how the impact of competition on poverty changes over the course of reforms, to quantify the horizon and intensity of lagged effects, and to compare these patterns systematically across low-, middle-, and high–financial-development regimes. The resulting evidence provides a richer characterization of the competition–poverty nexus and clarifies the conditions under which pro-competitive reforms are most likely to be poverty-reducing.

3.
Data

In this paper we employ an annual panel dataset spanning the years 1991 to 2017 and covering a broad set of developing and developed countries. The empirical analysis is based on macroeconomic indicators and competition metrics constructed to examine the relationship between market structure and poverty incidence at the national level.

The dependent variable in this study is the poverty headcount ratio, measured at three international poverty lines: $1.90, $3.20, and $5.50 per day (in 2011 PPP-adjusted dollars). These indicators, drawn from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators (WDI), represent the proportion of the population living below the specified income thresholds and serve to quantify extreme, moderate, and upper-bound poverty across countries and years.

The primary explanatory variable is the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI), a standard measure of market concentration derived from firm-level sales or value-added data. Higher values of the HHI indicate more concentrated markets, i.e., lower levels of competition. In the context of this study, the HHI is aggregated at the national level using firm data sourced from Orbis and Worldscope databases. The index captures the overall structure of competition in each economy and is assumed to reflect the potential for firms to exercise market power.

To control for macroeconomic confounding factors that might influence poverty levels, we include a set of standard covariates. These are: log GDP per capita (as a proxy for average income), trade openness (exports plus imports as a share of GDP), inflation rate (measured as the annual percentage change in the Consumer Price Index), government consumption as a percentage of GDP, market capitalization (stock market capitalization over GDP), and annual GDP growth. All variables are sourced from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators, except for the HHI, which is computed from firm-level databases. Table

4.
Methodology

4.1.
Functional Data Analysis

Contrary to the existing literature, our approach based on FDA allows for competition and poverty to be modeled as smooth trajectories and allow their interrelationship to vary flexibly over time. In particular, no prior study appears to estimate a time‐varying slope function
\beta(t)
or a slope surface
\beta(s,t)
that quantifies how competition at time
s
affects poverty at time
t
. Such an approach would permit detection of immediate, delayed or persistent effects, as well as variation in magnitude and sign of the effect over the full time horizon.

To do that, we propose to use a function–on–function linear regression model of the form

(4.1)

Y_{i}(t)=\alpha(t)+\int_{\mathcal{T}}\beta(s,t)\,X_{i}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\varepsilon_{i}(t)\quad i=1,\dots,n,

where
Y_{i}(t)
denotes the poverty trajectory of country
i
, and
X_{i}(s)
denotes the competition trajectory observed over a time interval
\mathcal{T}
. This model allows
\beta(s,t)
to capture both contemporaneous (
s\approx t
) and lagged (
s<t
) effects of competition on poverty. Additionally, we incorporate functional covariates—such as GDP per capita, inflation rate, trade openness, government spending and market capitalisation—to control for macro‑economic dynamics.

FDA provides a principled framework for modeling information that varies continuously over a domain such as time, space, or frequency. In contrast to classical multivariate or longitudinal approaches that treat repeated measurements as finite-dimensional vectors—one for each time point—FDA views each observation as a smooth function
X_{i}(t)
, with
t\in\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}
. This perspective allows the analyst to exploit the inherent continuity and smoothness in the data, capturing complex temporal patterns, long-range dependencies, and smooth dynamics that are often obscured in traditional discrete-time models
(
13
;
6
)
.

Letting
\{X_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote a sample of functional covariate, and
Y_{i}(t)
a corresponding functional response, the general form of the functional linear model (FLM) is given by:

Y_{i}(t)=\alpha(t)+\int_{\mathcal{T}}\beta(t,s)X_{i}(s)\,ds+\varepsilon_{i}(t),

where
\alpha(t)
is a smooth intercept function,
\beta(t,s)
is a bivariate coefficient surface that captures how the value of
X_{i}(s)
influences
Y_{i}(t)
, and
\varepsilon_{i}(t)
is a mean-zero stochastic error process. This formulation accommodates both contemporaneous and lagged effects by allowing the relationship between
X_{i}
and
Y_{i}
to vary smoothly across both dimensions.

A simplified version of this model is the scalar-on-function regression:

Y_{i}=\alpha+\int_{\mathcal{T}}\beta(s)X_{i}(s)\,ds+\varepsilon_{i},

in which a scalar response is regressed on a functional predictor.

Models described above have gained increasing traction in economics as many economic
processes naturally evolve over continuous time and exhibit smooth dynamics that are poorly
captured by discrete or static models.
Applications span a wide spectrum of economic domains. For example, FDA has been used to
analyze lifetime income trajectories, enabling researchers to study heterogeneous earnings paths
and long-run mobility patterns rather than relying on cross-sectional income snapshots

(
5
)
. In financial econometrics, functional methods have been
applied to model volatility surfaces, where treating volatility as a continuous function reveals
smooth temporal variation and structural patterns that traditional GARCH-type models overlook

(
9
)
. FDA has also proven valuable for term-structure modeling:
functional dynamic factor models treat yield curves as evolving functions, leading to improved
forecasting and interpretation of interest-rate movements
(
8
)
. Moreover,
functional approaches have been used to investigate income inequality dynamics, where hybrid
models and functional representations of income evolution allow the separation of persistent
inequality from transient shocks across the life cycle
(
18
)
.

Importantly, FDA is particularly well suited to our application, where both poverty and competition are measured over time and are likely governed by smooth latent dynamics. It enables us to model the effect of competition on poverty not as a fixed scalar coefficient, but as a function that evolves across time and accounts for potential lag structures. This temporal flexibility is crucial for uncovering when and how competition exerts its strongest influence on poverty alleviation, especially in contexts characterized by institutional inertia or delayed policy effects. Moreover, FDA methods such as Functional Principal Component Analysis (FPCA)
(
20
)
allow estimation even in the presence of sparsely or irregularly observed curves—common in macroeconomic panel data.

4.2.
Smoothing sparse functional data

In many applied econometric contexts—especially those involving macroeconomic or policy indicators—functional variables are recorded sparsely and irregularly over time. Variables such as poverty incidence or market competition (e.g., the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index) are typically observed only at annual frequency, often with missing values and uneven coverage across countries. Classical functional data smoothing methods, which assume dense and regularly spaced observations, are ill-suited to such settings.

To address this, we adopt the
Principal Analysis by Conditional Expectation
(PACE) framework introduced by
20
, which enables consistent estimation of functional trajectories from sparse and noisy data.

Let
X_{i}(t)
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, denote independent realizations of a square-integrable stochastic process
X(t)
defined on a compact interval
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}
. In our application, each
X_{i}(t)
corresponds to the time trajectory of a country-level variable, such as poverty or competition, observed at subject-specific time points
\{t_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{N_{i}}
. The observations are modeled as

\widetilde{X}_{ij}=X_{i}(t_{ij})+\delta_{ij},

where the errors
\delta_{ij}
are i.i.d. with
\mathbb{E}[\delta_{ij}]=0
and
\text{Var}(\delta_{ij})=\sigma^{2}<\infty
. PACE reconstructs the latent function
X_{i}(t)
by estimating its conditional expectation given the noisy and sparse data
\{(t_{ij},\widetilde{X}_{ij})\}
, using the Karhunen–Loève expansion:

X_{i}(t)=\mu(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\xi_{ik}\phi_{k}(t),

where
\mu(t)
is the mean function,
\phi_{k}(t)
are the orthonormal eigenfunctions of the covariance operator, and
\xi_{ik}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda_{k})
are uncorrelated scores capturing individual deviations from the mean.

The estimation proceeds in three main steps. First, the mean function
\mu(t)
is estimated using local linear or kernel smoothing:

\widehat{\mu}(t)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}K_{h}(t_{ij}-t)\widetilde{X}_{ij}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}K_{h}(t_{ij}-t)},

where
K_{h}(\cdot)=K(\cdot/h)
is a kernel function and
h
is the bandwidth. Second, the covariance surface
G(s,t)=\mathrm{Cov}(X(s),X(t))
is estimated from the pooled raw covariances of the demeaned observations. For each subject
i
, define the centered observation

\widetilde{X}_{ij}^{c}=\widetilde{X}_{ij}-\widehat{\mu}(t_{ij}),

and construct raw covariance pairs

C_{ij\ell}=\widetilde{X}_{ij}^{c}\,\widetilde{X}_{i\ell}^{c},\;\text{for}\;j\neq\ell.

The covariance surface is then estimated using a two–dimensional kernel smoother:

\widehat{G}(s,t)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq\ell}K_{h_{1}}(t_{ij}-s)\,K_{h_{2}}(t_{i\ell}-t)\,C_{ij\ell}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq\ell}K_{h_{1}}(t_{ij}-s)\,K_{h_{2}}(t_{i\ell}-t)},

where
K_{h_{1}}
and
K_{h_{2}}
are kernel functions with bandwidths
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, respectively. This smoothed covariance estimator excludes diagonal elements
j=\ell
, which contain additional measurement noise and would otherwise bias the surface upward. The resulting surface
\widehat{G}(s,t)
provides a consistent estimate of the population covariance operator
G(s,t)
under sparse and irregular designs
(
20
)
.

Spectral decomposition of the smoothed covariance operator yields the empirical eigenfunctions
\widehat{\phi}_{k}(t)
and eigenvalues
\widehat{\lambda}_{k}
. Third, the principal component scores are estimated via conditional expectation:

\widehat{\xi}_{ik}=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left(\xi_{ik}\mid\widetilde{X}_{i1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{iN_{i}}\right)=\widehat{\lambda}_{k}\widehat{\phi}_{k}^{\top}(\mathbf{t}_{i})\widehat{\Sigma}_{i}^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}-\widehat{\mu}(\mathbf{t}_{i})),

where
\mathbf{t}_{i}=(t_{i1},\ldots,t_{iN_{i}})^{\top}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}=(\widetilde{X}_{i1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{iN_{i}})^{\top}
, and
\widehat{\Sigma}_{i}
is the estimated covariance matrix of the vector of noisy observations
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
of subject
i
, accounting for both signal and noise. The final reconstructed trajectory is given by:

\widehat{X}_{i}(t)=\widehat{\mu}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\xi}_{ik}\widehat{\phi}_{k}(t),

with
K
chosen to explain a desired percentage of the total variance.

From a computational standpoint, the PACE algorithm is implemented in the R package
fdapace
. The core function
FPCA()
provides automatic bandwidth selection, score estimation, and trajectory reconstruction, with built-in handling of sparse or irregularly spaced functional data. The package supports both dense and sparse designs.

In our study, PACE is applied to reconstruct the temporal trajectories of all macroeconomic variables, ensuring consistent smoothing even in the presence of missing or irregularly spaced data. This preprocessing step is crucial to enable accurate estimation of functional linear regression models, which rely on well-recovered curves to assess the evolving impact of competition on poverty.

4.3.
Market capitalization profiles-based clustering and clusters characteristics

An important feature of the empirical strategy in
7
is the stratification of countries based on their financial market structure. In particular, the authors classify countries according to their
average market capitalization
over the 1991–2017 period, thereby distinguishing economies with deep capital markets from those with bank-based or less developed financial systems. This categorization is interpreted as a proxy for differences in ownership structure, which is theorized to condition the impact of competition on poverty.

To account for potential heterogeneity, the authors split the sample into two subsamples: countries with high market capitalization and countries with low market capitalization, using a data-driven threshold for the average MCAP. Separate regressions are then estimated for each group to examine how the competition–poverty relationship varies across different financial regimes.

To explore potential heterogeneity in the competition–poverty relationship across countries with different financial market structures, we applied an unsupervised classification method to the smoothed market capitalization trajectories over the period 1991–2017. Specifically, we implemented a functional
k
-means clustering algorithm on the market capitalization curves obtained via penalized B-spline smoothing. This approach allows us to group countries based on the entire temporal profile of their financial development, rather than relying on static or arbitrarily defined thresholds (as is the case in
7
). The optimal number of clusters was determined to be three, based on visual inspection, interpretability, and stability of the resulting groups.

The functional
k
-means clustering of market capitalization trajectories reveals three distinct groups of countries that differ systematically in the depth and maturity of their financial markets (see Figure
3(a)
). These clusters—labeled High MCAP, Medium MCAP, and Low MCAP—reflect different stages of financial development, institutional capacity, and integration into global capital markets. Table
3
summarizes the country composition of each cluster.

Cluster 1 – Medium MCAP Economies

Countries in this group exhibit moderately deep and expanding financial markets, where MCAP-to-GDP ratios typically range between 40% and 80%. Their MCAP trajectories (red line in Figure
3(b)
) are relatively stable, showing gradual deepening during the 1990s and early 2000s, followed by mild convergence. The corresponding mean poverty trajectory (middle curve in Figure
3(b)
) lies between those of high- and low-MCAP economies, showing a substantial reduction in poverty until the mid-2000s, after which progress slows.

These countries represent upper-middle-income and emerging market economies with growing but incomplete financial inclusion. Capital markets are large enough to influence macroeconomic dynamics but remain somewhat vulnerable to volatility and institutional weaknesses. The observed poverty pattern suggests that financial expansion and competition have supported poverty alleviation, though the benefits have been uneven across time and social groups.

Cluster 2 – High MCAP Economies

High-MCAP economies possess mature, liquid, and globally integrated capital markets, often with MCAP levels exceeding 100% of GDP. Their financial trajectories (green line in Figure
3(b)
) are stable with mild cyclical fluctuations, consistent with well-developed institutions, sophisticated financial intermediation, and a broad investor base.

The mean poverty trajectory (bottom curve in Figure
3(b)
) shows consistently low poverty levels throughout the sample period, confirming that financial deepening and competition coincide with high income levels, advanced social protection, and inclusive credit systems. These economies illustrate the steady-state regime in which financial development has already reached a threshold beyond which additional competition exerts limited influence on poverty outcomes. In such contexts, market efficiency gains are typically offset by redistributive fiscal mechanisms and labor-market adaptability.

Cluster 3 – Low MCAP Economies

This group is characterized by shallow and volatile financial markets, with MCAP-to-GDP ratios rarely exceeding 30%. Their MCAP trajectories (blue line in Figure
3(b)
) show low starting levels and limited growth over time, indicating persistent structural bottlenecks such as weak investor protection, limited market liquidity, and dependence on bank-based financing.

The corresponding mean poverty trajectory (top curve in Figure

The co-movement between low MCAP and high poverty suggests that limited financial intermediation constrains the transmission of growth and competition effects to the poor, amplifying inequality and vulnerability to external shocks.

4.4.
Functional Linear Model

4.4.1.
The model

Let
Y_{i}(t)
denote the functional response corresponding to the log-transformed poverty headcount curve for country
i
, observed over a time domain
t\in\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}
. For each country
i=1,\dots,n
, we observe
p
functional predictors
X^{(j)}_{i}(s)
,
j=1,\dots,p
, defined on the same or possibly different domain
s\in\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}
. In our empirical setting, the predictors include the trajectories of market concentration (HH), GDP per capita (GDP), government expenditure ratio (G), trade openness (Trade), inflation rate (INF), and growth (Growth). Following
7
we transformed all predictors using Logarithm function except Growth.

The multiple functional linear model (MFLM) is given by:

(4.2)

Y_{i}(t)=\alpha(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\beta_{j}(s,t)X_{i}^{(j)}(s)\,ds+\varepsilon_{i}(t),

where:

•

\alpha(t)
is a smooth intercept function,

•

\beta_{j}(s,t)
is a bivariate coefficient surface quantifying the effect of predictor
j
evaluated at time
s
on the response at time
t
,

•

\varepsilon_{i}(t)
is a zero-mean random error process with finite variance.

This model allows the effect of each covariate to vary across both
s
and
t
, capturing dynamic and possibly lagged associations between competition-related macroeconomic indicators and poverty over time.

Remark 4.1
.

A useful and widely applied special case of model (
4.2
) arises when the influence of each functional covariate on the response is assumed to be instantaneous, that is, when the coefficient surface satisfies
\beta_{j}(s,t)=0
for all
s\neq t
.
Under this restriction, the effect of the predictor
X_{i}^{(j)}(s)
on the response
Y_{i}(t)
operates only when both functions are evaluated at the same time point,
s=t
.
Consequently, the bivariate coefficient surface
\beta_{j}(s,t)
reduces to a univariate coefficient function
\beta_{j}(t)=\beta_{j}(t,t)
, yielding the so-called
concurrent functional linear model
:

(4.3)

Y_{i}(t)=\alpha(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{j}(t)X_{i}^{(j)}(t)+\varepsilon_{i}(t).

This formulation captures contemporaneous relationships between the functional response and functional predictors, assuming no dynamic or lagged dependence across time.
In empirical applications such as ours, where both poverty and macroeconomic indicators evolve over the same temporal domain, the concurrent specification provides a parsimonious yet informative representation of how time-varying covariates jointly explain the evolution of poverty.

4.4.2.
Estimation

To estimate the parameters in model (
4.2
), the functions
Y_{i}(t)
,
X_{i}^{(j)}(s)
, and
\beta_{j}(s,t)
are expanded in suitable basis systems (e.g., B-splines, Fourier, or functional principal components). Let us suppose the functional response and each functional predictor are represented as:

Y_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{y}}c_{ik}\psi_{k}(t),\quad X_{i}^{(j)}(s)=\sum_{l=1}^{K_{x}}d_{il}^{(j)}\phi_{l}(s),

where
\{\psi_{k}(t)\}
and
\{\phi_{l}(s)\}
are basis functions, and
c_{ik},d_{il}^{(j)}
are the corresponding coefficients.

The bivariate coefficient surface
\beta_{j}(s,t)
is then approximated using a tensor-product basis:

\beta_{j}(s,t)=\sum_{l=1}^{K_{x}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{y}}\theta_{lk}^{(j)}\phi_{l}(s)\psi_{k}(t),

where
\theta_{lk}^{(j)}
are parameters to be estimated.

Substituting into (
4.2
) and exploiting orthogonality, estimation reduces to solving a penalized least squares problem:

\min_{\Theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|Y_{i}(t)-\alpha(t)-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\int\beta_{j}(s,t)X_{i}^{(j)}(s)\,ds\right\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}\iint\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\beta_{j}(s,t)}{\partial s^{2}}\right)^{2}ds\,dt,

where the penalty terms control smoothness in the coefficient surfaces.

The estimation is typically carried out using functional regression tools such as
pffr()
from the
refund
R package
(
17
)
.

5.
Empirical results

5.1.
Benchmark Model: Static Regression Framework (
7
)

Before introducing the our results based on functional regression models, it is useful to recall the benchmark empirical framework adopted by
7
.

Their analysis investigates how market competition, financial development, and their interaction affect poverty levels across countries and over time, using a panel data approach with country and time fixed effects.

The estimated baseline model takes the form:

(5.1)

\text{Pov}_{it}=\alpha_{i}+\lambda_{t}+\beta_{1}\,\text{HHI}_{it}+\beta_{2}\,\text{MCAP}_{it}+\beta_{3}\,(\text{HHI}_{it}\times\text{AVMCAP}_{i})+\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\mathbf{Z}_{it}+\varepsilon_{it},

where
\text{AVMCAP}_{i}
is the average MCAP for country
i
calculated over the period of study,
\mathbf{Z}_{it}
is a vector of control variables including GDP per capita, government expenditure, trade openness, inflation, and output growth.

The estimation is conducted using panel fixed effects with robust standard errors clustered at the country level.
The coefficients
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, and
\beta_{3}
capture, respectively, the direct effects of competition and financial development, and their joint interaction on poverty.

Across the three poverty thresholds ($1.9, $3.2, and $5.5),
7
report the following main empirical results:

(1)

The coefficient on market concentration (
\beta_{1}
) is positive and statistically significant at the lower poverty thresholds, implying that greater competition (lower HHI) is associated with lower poverty.
This suggests a pro-poor role of competition at the extensive margin.

(2)

The coefficient on financial development (
\beta_{2}
) is negative and significant, confirming that deeper financial markets are associated with lower poverty rates.

(3)

The interaction term (
\beta_{3}
) is negative and significant for the lowest poverty line ($1.9), but weaker at higher thresholds.
This indicates that the poverty-reducing effect of competition is amplified in countries with more developed financial systems.

(4)

Control variables behave as expected: higher GDP per capita and trade openness are negatively associated with poverty, while inflation and government expenditure show mixed effects depending on the poverty threshold.

Overall, these results suggest that both competition and financial development contribute to poverty reduction, and that their interaction has a reinforcing effect, particularly for extreme poverty.
However, the static specification in equation (
5.1
) assumes constant coefficients over time, thus providing only average effects across the entire sample period.
In contrast, our functional regression framework introduced below relaxes this assumption by allowing the effects of competition, financial development, and their interaction to vary smoothly over time.

5.2.
Analysis of the dynamic contemporaneous effect of competition on poverty

Figure

\beta_{\text{HHI}}(t)

\beta_{\text{HHI}}(t)
convey the direction and strength of competition’s effect on poverty: a positive slope implies that higher concentration (less competition) increases poverty, indicating a pro-poor role for competition; a negative slope implies that higher concentration reduces poverty, suggesting that competition is anti-poor; and coefficients close to zero reflect an economically insignificant contemporaneous association.

The full-sample estimates (red curve) remain negative for most of the sample period, with the strongest magnitude in the early 2000s and gradual convergence toward zero thereafter. This pattern suggests that, on average, greater competition (lower HHI) coincided with higher extreme poverty—an anti-poor relationship. Such a finding is consistent with transitional dynamics in developing and emerging economies, where liberalization and intensified competition initially led to firm exits, labor market disruptions, and short-run welfare losses before efficiency gains were realized. The temporal attenuation of this negative effect indicates a gradual adjustment as economies adapted to more open and competitive environments.

The heterogeneity across clusters is economically meaningful. In the medium-MCAP cluster (green dashed line), the slope fluctuates around zero and becomes slightly positive after 2000, suggesting a neutral to mildly pro-poor relationship. In these economies—characterized by intermediate financial depth and ongoing institutional development (e.g., Brazil, Poland, Turkey, Thailand)—moderate financial deepening appears to have mitigated the short-term dislocation effects of competition, allowing some diffusion of its efficiency gains toward poverty alleviation.

In the high-MCAP cluster (blue dashed line), encompassing financially mature economies such as the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia, the slope is sharply negative in the early 1990s but rises toward zero in subsequent years. This trajectory indicates that, while early competitive pressures were associated with temporary increases in poverty—likely due to structural labor-market adjustments—these effects dissipated as robust financial systems and social protection mechanisms absorbed the adjustment costs. Over time, competition’s adverse impact weakened substantially, approaching statistical insignificance.

By contrast, the low-MCAP cluster (purple dashed line) displays persistently negative slopes throughout the period, with a pronounced trough around the mid- 2000s. In countries with shallow financial systems and weak institutional capacity (e.g., Bangladesh, Tunisia, Zambia), intensified competition appears to have consistently exacerbated poverty. In such settings, limited access to finance and underdeveloped safety nets may have amplified the vulnerability of low-income households and small enterprises to market liberalization.

Taken together, the results reveal a clear financial-development gradient in the competition–poverty nexus. In low-MCAP economies, competition remains systematically anti-poor; in medium-MCAP economies, its effects are largely neutral or weakly pro-poor; and in high-MCAP economies, the adverse short-run effects of competition attenuate over time. The aggregate (full-sample) slope therefore masks substantial cross-sectional heterogeneity, highlighting the importance of conditioning the analysis of competition and welfare on financial and institutional development.

Beyond market competition, the estimated functional coefficients provide additional insights into how macroeconomic conditions influence poverty across financial-development clusters and poverty thresholds. Across all models, the sign, magnitude, and temporal stability of the slopes highlight marked differences in the transmission of economic and policy variables to welfare outcomes.

GDP per capita.

The estimated coefficient for GDP per capita is negative for the medium- and high-MCAP clusters, indicating that income growth contributes to poverty reduction in financially developed and moderately developed economies. The effect is most pronounced at the lowest poverty threshold ($1.9) and weakens as the poverty line increases, consistent with diminishing marginal welfare gains once extreme poverty declines. In contrast, the coefficient is persistently positive for the low-MCAP cluster, suggesting that income growth has not translated into poverty alleviation in financially shallow economies. This finding implies that in less developed financial systems, the benefits of growth may be unevenly distributed or absorbed by higher-income segments, reflecting structural barriers to inclusive development.

Government expenditure.
The estimated coefficients for public spending are negative in the early years across most clusters, confirming the expected poverty-reducing effect of fiscal policy. In high- and medium-MCAP economies, the slopes gradually move toward zero or become mildly positive after the mid-2000s, indicating a decline in fiscal effectiveness as these economies achieved higher income levels and diminishing returns from additional expenditure. By contrast, in low-MCAP countries, the coefficients remain persistently negative over the entire period, suggesting that government spending continues to play a crucial role in alleviating poverty. In these financially shallow economies, public expenditure likely compensates for weak private-sector credit and limited access to finance, directly supporting consumption and social safety nets. Overall, the results highlight that the welfare impact of fiscal policy is strongest and most sustained in low-MCAP contexts, while its marginal effectiveness declines as financial and institutional systems mature.

Trade openness.
The estimated coefficients for trade openness reveal heterogeneous effects across financial-development clusters. In low-MCAP economies, the slopes become increasingly negative after 2000, indicating that greater openness is associated with poverty reduction. This pattern suggests that integration into global markets has supported employment creation and income diversification in financially shallow economies. By contrast, in medium- and high-MCAP countries, the slopes fluctuate around zero, implying that trade exerts an unstable or economically insignificant contemporaneous effect on poverty. Overall, the results indicate that the pro-poor benefits of trade liberalization are concentrated in less financially developed economies, while in more advanced systems the gains appear to have already materialized or been offset by structural adjustments in production and labor markets.

Inflation.
The estimated slopes for inflation display heterogeneous patterns across clusters. In low- and medium-MCAP economies, the coefficients are weakly negative or close to zero, indicating that moderate inflation either coincides with mild poverty reductions or exerts no significant contemporaneous effect. This may reflect the fact that in financially shallow environments, episodes of moderate inflation are often associated with growth and fiscal expansion phases that temporarily boost employment and income. By contrast, in high-MCAP economies, the slope turns positive in several periods, implying that higher inflation tends to increase poverty. In these financially mature contexts, inflation primarily erodes real purchasing power among low-income households, as asset holders and formal-sector workers are better able to hedge or adjust nominal incomes. Overall, these findings suggest that the welfare implications of inflation depend critically on financial depth and the ability of households to protect real incomes against price volatility.

Interaction between competition and financial development.

The estimated interaction coefficient
\beta_{\text{HHI}\times\text{MCAP}}(t)
displayed in Figure

In other words, in countries with deeper financial systems, the marginal effect of competition on poverty reduction becomes stronger. The magnitude of the interaction effect peaks around the early 2000s and declines gradually thereafter, suggesting that the complementarity between competition and financial development was most pronounced during the period of rapid financial liberalization and market integration. Toward the end of the sample, the interaction term approaches zero, implying a weakening of this reinforcing mechanism as financial systems matured and additional market deepening yielded diminishing welfare returns. Overall, the positive and time-varying pattern of
\beta_{\text{HHI}\times\text{MCAP}}(t)
highlights that competition and financial development act as complementary channels for poverty alleviation, particularly in the early stages of financial expansion.

6.
Conclusion

This paper introduces a functional econometric framework to analyze how market competition affects poverty dynamics over time and across heterogeneous financial systems. By treating both poverty and competition as continuous trajectories, our approach departs from conventional panel models that assume constant parameters and contemporaneous effects. The function-on-function regression models reveal rich temporal structures and lagged dependencies that would be obscured under static specifications.

Three main insights emerge. First, the impact of competition on poverty is inherently time-varying and nonlinear. Stronger competition initially coincides with higher poverty—reflecting short-run adjustment costs from liberalization—but becomes poverty-reducing once economies adapt and efficiency gains diffuse. Second, financial development conditions the competition–poverty relationship. In low-MCAP economies, where markets are shallow and access to credit limited, competition exerts strong pro-poor effects once structural bottlenecks ease. In contrast, in high-MCAP economies, where financial inclusion and safety nets are already mature, the marginal role of competition is negligible. Third, the interaction between competition and financial development is positive and dynamic, confirming that deeper financial markets magnify the poverty-reducing impact of competition, especially during phases of rapid financial expansion.

Our approach allows the uncovering of smooth, time-localized effects, to identify when policy reforms exert their greatest influence, and to disentangle short-run disruptions from long-run structural benefits. In the context of the competition–poverty nexus, this framework demonstrates that policy evaluation should not rely on static averages but on evolving temporal responses. Future research could extend this work by integrating functional quantile regression to assess distributional effects across income groups, or by embedding spatial functional dependence to capture cross-country diffusion in competition and welfare dynamics. Overall, the evidence suggests that competition, when supported by financial deepening and institutional strengthening, can be a powerful instrument for inclusive and sustained poverty alleviation.
