SURVEY BEHAVIOR AND COGNITION ACROSS THE LIFE COURSE: EVIDENCE FROM THE PANEL STUDY OF INCOME DYNAMICS
Authors: MALAK B. KALASHO, ESTHER M. FRIEDMAN, THOMAS F. CROSSLEY
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cognitive abilities across adulthood are important pre-cursors to dementia. The primary way to capture cognitive abilities is via expensive and time-consuming standardized cognitive tests. We explore the extent to which cognition can be inferred from indicators of survey response behavior (SRB), such as the time to complete a questionnaire and the incidence of contradictory responses. We use nationally representative data from a mixed-mode supplement to the Panel Study of Income Dynamics collected in 2016. This Wellbeing and Daily Life supplement includes rich cognitive data capturing both verbal and quantitative reasoning, and paradata from the supplement (e.g., time to complete). We assess the ability of survey response behavior to improve prediction of cognitive ability after accounting for standard sociodemographic variables typically available in surveys. We explore this from age 30 and across the life course. We find that SRB measures provide modest but consistent explanatory power across a wide range of ages. The explanatory power of SRB measures in our data is less than that of education, except at the oldest ages. SRB measures contain useful additional information, but models that include basic demographics, education and SRB measures leave much of the variation in cognitive ability unexplained. Which SRB measures best predict cognitive ability is consistent across cognitive measure but differs by age. Errors (i.e., contradictory responses) and response times are the best predictors at working ages but skips and backups are more predictive at older ages.

INTRODUCTION

An estimated 6.9 million Americans aged 65 and above are living with Alzheimer’s disease, a number projected to nearly double to 13.8 million by 2060 (Alzheimer’s Association 2024). An even greater proportion of the population lives with mild cognitive impairment (Rajan et al. 2021) that may eventually lead to dementia. Cognitive abilities across adulthood are important precursors to dementia and are associated with a range of behaviors and experiences that shape life-course health, well-being, and disparities. The primary way to capture cognitive outcomes such as these is through standardized cognitive assessments. However, these assessments are expensive, time-consuming, and burdensome to respondents. In addition, many such assessments have only been validated for specific age groups (e.g., age 65 and above), are not comparable across subpopulations, or are not appropriate across survey modes. Because of this, many studies do not collect such cognitive data regularly, if at all.

We explore the extent to which cognition can be inferred from indicators of survey response behavior (SRB) routinely available across studies at no additional burden to respondents. Survey completion is a cognitively demanding task that involves reading skills, decision-making, and memory. Indicators of how well respondents perform on the task of survey completion itself, with regard to speed, accuracy, and efficiency, may therefore capture more general cognitive skills; indeed, there is some evidence of this from a relatively recent body of work.

A number of papers have studied response times and cognition in large surveys. Junghaenel et al. (2022) and Schneider et al. (2023) show that response times to general survey items are associated with cognitive test scores. Both papers use data from the Understanding America Study, a web-based survey based on probability sample (and so nationally representative). In contrast, Sanders et al. (2025) and Nichols et al. (2025) study the time taken to complete a cognitive test imbedded in a survey. Both study broad samples of older adults, the National Social Life, Health, and Aging Project and the Longitudinal Aging Study in India. Sanders et al. (2025) show that the time taken to complete the Montreal Cognitive Assessment in a computer-assisted personal interview (CAPI) interview is associated both with the score and subsequent changes in the score. Nichols et al. (2025) show that time to complete components of the Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol is not only predictive of the score but also has predictive power for dementia over and above the assessment score.

A handful of studies have used large probability samples to explore how survey response behaviors (other than response times) relate to cognitive outcomes. These include a number by Stefan Schneider and colleagues (Schneider et al. 2021, 2022, 2024). These use measures of low-quality reporting on scales administered in aging studies, usually as part of pen and paper self-completion “leave behind” questionnaires. SRB measures include item nonresponse, Guttman errors, outliers, and poor fit in IRT models. These measures predict cognition both concurrently and prospectively and also predict mortality. Schneider et al. (2024) show that low-quality responses have more predictive power for cognition when survey items are more complex. This helps to distinguish a true association with cognition from an association driven by satisficing or lack of survey effort. de New and Schurer (2023) and Hedengren and Stratmann (2012) focus specifically on rates of item nonresponse, which they show is associated with both cognitive and noncognitive abilities (notably conscientiousness). They also show that it as useful predictor of earnings, even after controlling for standard demographic variables and education.

We build on this emerging body of work and extend the evidence base in several ways. First, we explore the ability of surveys to predict cognition not merely at older ages but across the life course. To the best of our knowledge, the only other papers to do so are Junghaenel et al. (2022), which considers rich information on response times, and de New and Schurer (2023) and Hedengren and Stratmann (2012), both of which study item nonresponse rates. We replicate their results on new data and, while we have less granular information on response times than Junghaenel et al. (2022), we extend the analysis to a wider range of SRB measures. Second, we focus on web respondents. As the web is becoming an increasingly common survey mode, it is important to understand how SRB measures from web surveys predict cognition. Junghaenel et al. (2022) and Schneider et al. (2023) study samples from a nationally representative web survey (the UAS), but study multiple aspects of response times. We study a different survey and extend the analysis to multiple SRB measures. Other papers studying a range of survey response behaviors are based on “leave behind” pen and pencil self-completion surveys (Schneider et al. 2022, 2024) or CAPI interviews (Nichols et al. 2025) or a mix of the two (de New and Schurer 2023). We look at a variety of measures of survey response behaviors and explicitly compare these measures to see which ones matter most overall, across cognitive outcomes, and for different periods of the life course. Finally, this study focuses in more detail on how much additional variance is explained by SRB measures, given a set of sociodemographic conditioning variables available in almost all surveys; most of the existing literature focuses instead on effect sizes (or raw correlations) and statistical significance.

To do this, we use nationally representative data from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID), in particular a mixed-mode supplement on Wellbeing and Daily Life collected in 2016 by web and paper (with a small number by telephone), to assess the well-being of U.S. adults ages 30 and over (Freedman 2017). This supplement includes rich cognitive data capturing both verbal and quantitative reasoning. Paradata from the supplement (e.g., item skips, section backup, section timeout, and average response time) were also made publicly available. To assess errors in response, we calculated simple measures of inconsistent responses across survey items capturing daily activities and well-being, available in the supplement. We merged data from the supplement to those from core PSID data to capture even more detailed information on respondents’ demographics at a concurrent time point and on results of the AD8© dementia screener across the 2017, 2019, and 2021 survey waves to explore the predictive power of survey response behaviors over three time points.

Specifically, we address the following three research questions:

R1. How much unexplained variation in cognition is captured by indicators of survey response behaviors?

R2. Do indicators of survey response behavior predict cognition at younger ages, and if so, is the amount of otherwise unexplained variation captured by survey response variables comparable to older ages?

R3. What types of survey response behaviors (i.e., skips, backups, timeouts, response time, errors) provide the most information, and does this vary over the life course?

DATA AND METHODS

Data

This study uses data from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics, the world’s longest-running nationally representative household panel survey (PSID 2023). The PSID has conducted annual interviews with U.S. families from 1968 to 1997 and biennially thereafter. We primarily use data from the 2016 Wellbeing and Daily Life Supplement, which focused on respondents’ wellbeing, personality traits, cognition, and everyday skills (Freedman 2017). In the main PSID surveys, the respondent is a reference person and provides information about themselves, their spouse or partner, and/or other members of the household. The supplement was fully self-administered and allowed both reference persons and spouses or partners to be respondents. Individuals were eligible for the supplement if they were at least 30years old by December 31, 2015 and were a household head or spouse/partner in the 2015 main survey (Freedman 2017). Individuals whose main survey interviews were conducted through proxies (n = 124) or in Spanish (n = 413) were not eligible for the supplement. Of the 10,689 individuals who met these criteria, 8,341 completed the survey (response rate= 78 percent). The survey was self-administered primarily via web (n = 6,182) or paper-and-pencil interviewing (PAPI) (n = 2,077), with a small number administered by telephone (n = 82). Additionally, we use data from the PSID main surveys in the years 2017, 2019, and 2021 (PSID 2025) to look at dementia status, and also take advantage of streamlined socio-demographic data (i.e., race and education) from the PSID SHELF file (Daumler et al. 2025; Pfeffer et al. 2025).

Sample Inclusion Rules

We restrict our sample to respondents who completed the survey via web administration because we have a richer set of SRB measures for web respondents. In Appendix F, please see the supplementary data online, we provide a pooled analysis of both web and PAPI respondents, utilizing two measures of SRB applicable to both modes.

We also exclude a small number of respondents with interview breakoffs (n = 18), as well as those with missing values for race and/or education (n = 15). Finally, we also omitted two respondents who provided uniform responses, or “straight-lined,” in any of the sections used to derive a measure of survey response behavior. Our primary analysis sample, therefore, includes a total of 6,147 adults aged 30 and above.

Cognitive Measures

Verbal Reasoning and Numeracy.

The supplement includes four cognitive assessments, one that evaluates verbal reasoning, and three that evaluate numerical ability. The latter include a set of numerical calculations related to health, a set of missing number questions, and a set of “story problems”. The story problems are simple financial calculations. A complete list of items is presented in Appendix A (supplemental texts S1–S4, please see the supplementary data online). Verbal reasoning is assessed through a six-item sentence completion test, where respondents select the most suitable missing word in a statement. The three health calculations cover time management of medication schedules and interpretation of numerical health information. The missing numbers task is a six-item test on identification of missing values in a number series. The story problems task includes six items, including calculating change, discounts, and compound interest. We combine the three numeracy tasks. Raw sum scores for verbal reasoning and numeracy are presented in table 1.

For our analysis, we construct cardinal measures of ability for verbal reasoning and numeracy using Item Response Theory (IRT). IRT is a set of models that infers an individual’s latent ability from their responses to multiple test items (Park 2019). It estimates individuals’ location on a standardized distribution of ability values while accounting for additional item-level parameters that may impact the probability of answering an item correctly. To estimate our cognitive scores, we use a two-parameter logistic (2-PL) model, which predicts the probability of a correct answer using discrimination and difficulty parameters. Item discrimination refers to how effectively an item differentiates between respondents with varying ability levels, while item difficulty refers to the level of ability required to have a probability of 50 percent of answering an item correctly. The probability that an individual with cognitive ability θ answers an item Y correctly is defined as: P(Y=1∣θ,a,b)=exp(a(θ−b))1+exp(a(θ−b)), where a is the item discrimination parameter and b is the item difficulty parameter. Unlike the raw sum scores, the IRT-estimated scores provide measures that reflect varying item characteristics, recognizing, for example, that a correct answer on a difficult item indicates a higher ability than a correct answer on an easier item. Unweighted distributions of the IRT-estimated cognitive ability values for verbal reasoning and numeracy are shown in Appendix B (figures S1 and S2, please see the supplementary data online).

As we will be exploring the extent to which SRB measures explain (in a statistical sense) variation in verbal and numerical ability across the life course, it is important to document how variation in those dimensions of ability evolves with age. Note that while we will refer to differences across age as age differences, the supplement is a cross-sectional survey (at a single point in time), and respondents to the supplement of different ages are also drawn from different birth cohorts. Figure 1 shows the mean IRT-estimated cognitive scores and variance across age using kernel-weighted local polynomial smoothing. For numerical ability, both the mean score and the variation to be explained are flat across age until above 80years. For verbal ability, mean scores rise slightly with age, but the variation across individuals of the same age declines with age. This is important to keep in mind as we interpret differences in the predictive power of SRB measures across ages.

AD8 Dementia Screen.

Starting in 2017, the PSID incorporated the Eight-Item Informant Interview to Differentiate Aging and Dementia (AD8) screen in its main surveys for adults aged 65 or older (Freedman et al. 2019). The AD8 identifies individuals who are at risk for dementia by assessing changes in memory, orientation to time, judgement, and daily function (Galvin et al. 2005). For example, some questions inquire about changes in repeating stories, recalling appointments, and managing complex financial matters. The AD8 measure in the PSID is a mix of self and proxy responses, with spouses, partners, or other family members providing proxy responses when the reference person is not the respondent. A cutoff score of 2 or more “yes” responses is used to categorize individuals with possible dementia. A complete list of AD8 items in the PSID is presented in Appendix A (supplemental text S5, please see the supplementary data online). We utilize the AD8 measure from the main surveys conducted in the years 2017, 2019, and 2021, focusing on respondents who were 65 or older by time of the supplement interview in 2016. The percentage of proxy responses in these years ranges between 33 and 35 percent. The fractions of respondents with scores of 2 or more on the AD8 are reported in table 1, ranging between approximately 14 and 17 percent.

Survey Response Behavior (SRB) Measures

We developed five measures of SRB using data from seven sections of the supplement, spanning 119 total items. Sections used for constructing cognitive measures were not used for constructing SRB measures. Our measures include both paradata and general survey response metrics:

Item skip is the count of item nonresponse, categorized as 0, 1, 2, and 3 or more skips. The prevalence of 3 or more skips is less than 6 percent.

Section backup is the count of times a respondent backs up during a section, categorized as 0, 1, 2, and 3 or more backups. The prevalence of 3 or more backups is less than 3 percent.

Section timeout is the count of times a respondent times out during a section, categorized as 0 and 1 or more timeouts. The prevalence of one or more timeouts is less than 2 percent.

Log average response time is the total response time, in seconds, across the seven sections divided by the total number of items and log-transformed due to skewness. Log-transforming average response times is common in the literature (Junghaenel et al. 2022).

Error is the count of contradictory responses, categorized as 0, 1, and 2 or more errors. For example, a respondent makes an error if they report being simultaneously happy and sad “all” or “most” of a particular day. The items used to identify these errors are listed in Appendix C (supplemental text S6, please see the supplementary data online). While the prevalence of any error is 14%, the prevalence of more than one error is less than 2 percent.

Summary statistics for all survey response behavior measures are provided in table 2.

Additional Controls

We use four demographic controls: age, race, sex, and education. Age is recorded as the respondent’s age in years as of December 31, 2015 (the date used to determine age eligibility for the supplement). We create age groups (30 to 49, 50 to 64, 65 to 74, and 75 and above) as well as overlapping age windows (e.g., 30–50, 35–55, 40–60, etc.) for our analysis. Sex is categorized as either male or female. Race is classified into the categories non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and other. Education is categorized based on the highest level completed: did not complete high school; completed high school but did not attend college; attended college but did not obtain a bachelor’s degree; obtained a bachelor’s degree but no postgraduate degree; and obtained a postgraduate degree. Sample characteristics are summarized in table 1.

Analytic Methods

To assess how much unexplained variation in cognition is captured by indicators of survey response behavior, we used a series of Ordinary Least Squares regressions and focused on the marginal predictive power of the indicators. Specifically, we examined our cognitive outcomes (i.e., verbal reasoning, numeracy, dementia status in 2017, 2019, and 2021), with demographic variables and added SRB measures as predictors across several linear regression models. We assessed the additional explanatory power of SRB measures by calculating partial R-squared values relative to a base model including only demographic variables that would be available in most surveys. Specifically, for cognition measure θ, basic demographic variables Xi and SRB measures Zi, we estimate a base model: θ=Xiα+εi, and an augmented model: θ=Xiα+Ziβ+ui.

The partial R-squared RZ∣X2 is defined as: RZ∣X2=RZ,X2−RX21−RX2, where RZ,X2 is the R-squared measure in the augmented model, and RX2 is the R-squared measure in the base model. Thus, it is the increase in the R-squared measure when SRB measures are added, divided by one minus the R-squared measure when just basic demographic variables are accounted for. An R-squared measure has several possible interpretations. Our preferred interpretation is that the R-squared is the squared correlation between the outcome variable (here, θ) and a linear regression model’s prediction of that variable. This is the natural multivariate extension of the simple correlation between an SRB measure and a cognitive measure that is reported in much of the literature. It also does not require homoscedasticity. A partial R-squared is then the normalized change in that squared correlation when additional predictor variables are added to the model. Each model was run across 10 overlapping age windows to evaluate the marginal predictive power of the SRB measures across the life course. The AD8 is a binary outcome, so for that outcome, we are estimating a linear probability model. For comparison, we also estimated logistic regression models and found that predicated values and marginal effects of explanatory variables were very similar.

For each cognitive outcome, our baseline model includes race and sex as predictors. We then add education as an additional predictor and determine its partial R-squared for each age window. This gives us a benchmark partial R-squared to compare SRB measures to. We next run models with our baseline demographics and the full set of SRB measures, calculating the partial R-squared of SRB measures for each age window. This allows us to compare SRB measures and education as incremental predictors of cognitive abilities. We then run models that included race, sex, education, and survey response behaviors to determine the joint marginal predictive power of SRB measures while controlling for education. These calculations are presented graphically below, with plots of partial R-squared values across the age windows.

We applied the same method to evaluate which individual SRB measures provide the most information. Using a base model with race, sex, and education as predictors, we ran five additional models, each incorporating one SRB measure. We calculated the partial R-squared for each survey response behavior to assess its unique contribution to explaining variance in cognitive outcomes. This analysis was repeated and plotted across the overlapping age windows. All analysis uses the PSID-supplied weights and accounts for complex sampling (strata and clustering).

R-squared measures can be sensitive to outliers and to model misspecification. We undertook various checks to make sure that we were not under-estimating the predictive power of SRBs. With respect to outliers, the AD8 is binary, and the IRT-estimated verbal and numerical ability scores do not suffer from outliers. The distributions of the estimated verbal and numerical ability scores are presented in Appendix B (figures S1 and S2, please see the supplementary data online). Turning to independent variables, most of our SRB variables are categorical. The one possible concern is with the logarithm of average response time. Inspection of “qq” plots (which plot the quantiles of this variable against the quantiles of a normal distribution) did not indicate the presence of outliers or significant departures from normality. We also calculated DFFITS influence statistics for our regressions. These did not indicate the presence of unusual influential observations, except perhaps in our oldest age window (75 and older).

To check whether model misspecification is leading us to underestimate the predictive power of SRBs, we compared our preferred specifications to specifications that arise from using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Lasso) to choose from a rich set of possible SRB variables (and interactions both between those variables and between those variables and our basic demographic variables). Full details are in Appendix G, please see the supplementary data online. We find that the total R-squared from the Lasso-selected models never exceeds the total R-squared from our standard specification including both demographics and SRBs. From this we conclude that it is unlikely that we are underestimating the explanatory power of SRBs because of model specification errors decreasing the model R-squared.

FINDINGS

Marginal Predictive Power of Survey Response Behaviors for Cognition

We first consider RQ1 and RQ2. Figure 2 presents the partial R-squared of education and of SRB measures (both relative to the base model) at different ages. It also presents the partial R-squared of SRB measures when added to a model that includes both our baseline demographics and education. Panel (a) presents results for Verbal Reasoning, Panel (b) for Numerical reasoning and Panel (c) for the AD8. The full results of the estimated models are presented in Appendix D (Tables S1–S3, please see the supplementary data online). Table 3 presents p-values from tests of the (joint) importance of the SRBs in our estimated models and are presented for a selection of age windows.

For verbal reasoning, the partial R-squared of SRB measures is consistently around 2–4 percent across working ages and then rises with age for our older respondents, to 7.8 percent for the oldest age window we consider. The first row of table 3 indicates that SRBs are statistically significant at p < .05 in all age windows.

For comparison, the partial R-squared of education is higher among our youngest adults (30–50years of age) at 10.6 percent but then declines gradually with age and is lower than for SRB measures in our oldest group. The marginal explanatory power of SRB measures is unaffected by the inclusion of education in the model: while there is less unexplained variance when education is included in the model, SRB measures explain about the same fraction of the remaining unexplained variation in either case. This is true at all ages.

For numeracy (figure 3), the partial R-squared of SRB measures begins at 4.6 percent for our youngest respondents and then declines slowly across working ages to 2.2 percent before rising back to 4.5 percent for the oldest respondents. The second row of table 3 indicates that SRB measures are statistically significant at p < .05 at younger and older ages, but not in the 65–74 age window.

Again, for comparison, the marginal explanatory power of education is stable at about 11–13 percent right across the life-course, only declining to 8.3 percent for the oldest group of respondents. Thus, for numeracy, education has greater marginal explanatory power at all ages. However, as with verbal reasoning, the marginal explanatory power of SRB measures (as a fraction of the unexplained variance) is not diminished by the inclusion of education in the model.

Finally, we consider the AD8 screener (figure 4). As the screener is administered only to respondents over 65, figure 4 begins at this age and only considers three age windows (with age increasing from left to right). For AD8, the marginal explanatory power of SRB measures rises with age from 65 and is greater than the marginal explanatory power of education at all of the age ranges considered. Figure 4 only presents the results for AD8 measured in the 2017 main survey (one year after the supplement from which the SRB measures are drawn). The figures for AD8 measured in the 2019 and 2021 surveys are very similar, and can be found in the Supplemental Materials, Appendix E (figures S3 and S4, please see the supplementary data online). As with our other two measures of cognitive ability, the marginal explanatory power of SRB measures is not diminished by the inclusion of education in the model. Table 3 indicates that SRB measures are statistically significant predictors among respondents ages 75 and older for the 2017 AD8 screener.

Comparing Marginal Predictive Power of Individual Survey Response Behavior Variables

We now consider which SRB measures offer the most predictive power for cognitive abilities, and whether this varies across the life-course (RQ3). Figures 5–7 display the partial R-squared of five different SRB measures when each is individually added to a base model of race, sex, and education. Figure 5 considers verbal reasoning, figure 6 considers numerical ability and figure 7 considers dementia status (as determined by the AD8 screener) in 2017.

Figures 5 and 6 show that the explanatory power of individual SRB measures for verbal reasoning and numerical ability is modest, below 2.5 percent for all measures at all ages below 70. There is an age pattern which is consistent across verbal reasoning and numerical ability. At younger ages, the strongest predictors of cognitive ability are response time and errors (contradictory responses). At older ages, the strongest predictor is instead backups, with skips being a somewhat strong predictor for verbal reasoning. The decline in the marginal explanatory power of mean response time with age stands in contrast to Junghaenel et al. (2022), which finds that response times are more strongly correlated with cognitive measures at older ages.

For dementia status (AD8) in 2017, the strongest predictors are skips, errors, and backups, as shown in figure 7. This is consistent with the other two cognitive measures, as we only observe dementia status at about age 65. Again, figure 7 only presents the results for AD8 measured in the 2017 main survey. The figures for AD8 measured in the 2019 and 2021 surveys show the same SRBs as the strongest predictors across age, though the explanatory power of backups is stronger in these years. The figures can be found in Appendix E (figures S5 and S6, please see the supplementary data online).

The importance of backups in predicting cognitive outcomes at the oldest ages is important when comparing our main results, for web respondents, and the results for pooled web and pencil and paper respondents (in Appendix F, please see the supplementary data online). As the backup measure is only available for web respondents, the pooled data show a much smaller increase in the marginal explanatory power of SRB measures at older ages.

SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION

A growing body of research indicates that SRB is linked to both current and future cognitive functioning and dementia across a variety of countries and contexts and different measures of SRB and cognition. SRB could potentially enhance the estimation of cognitive functioning in surveys lacking formal cognitive assessments. Building upon this emerging area of work, our research is differentiated by its focus on the predictive capability of SRBs for cognitive abilities not just in older adults but throughout the life course, including among individuals under age 50 who have received little attention in the literature.

Our findings demonstrate that SRB measures offer modest yet consistent explanatory power and show that this is true across different age groups, including adults ages 30–50. Although incremental explanatory power of SRBs is generally less than that of education (except for verbal ability and AD8 in older individuals). SRB measures still provide valuable information. We find that SRB measures enhance model fit, although a significant portion of cognitive variation remains unexplained after incorporating basic demographics, education, and measures of SRB in models.

We also investigate which specific SRB measures are most relevant for predicting cognition across the life course. Our results show that errors and response times are the best predictors during working ages, whereas skips and backups become more indicative at older ages. Errors prove to be strong predictors of dementia, though they are not as effective for verbal and numerical abilities in older individuals.

We find that SRBs are better predictors of verbal reasoning than numeracy. We speculate that this may be because verbal reasoning skills are more critical to the ability to answer surveys.

Not all studies have measures of cognitive functioning. Even the PSID used in this work only collects detailed cognitive functioning periodically as part of supplemental studies, not in the main interview. We show the potential benefit of using SRB measures along with other socio-demographic factors to better capture cognitive functioning over the life course. For instance, while education is typically more strongly associated with cognition than is SRB, the influence of education diminishes with age, eventually being outweighed by SRB for verbal reasoning in the oldest age group. Furthermore, the addition of SRB measures does not diminish its marginal explanatory power when education is included in the models.

Like all studies, ours has several limitations that should be noted. One advantage of this study is that we examine SRBs in a mixed-mode survey, which expands upon much of the current research by including respondents answering by web. While this is an important addition to the literature due to the increasing prevalence of online surveys, web respondents may differ from those responding via other modes of survey collection. To account for this, we do report results for pooled web and pen-and-paper respondents in our supplemental analyses, but as the mode was not randomly allocated, we cannot credibly study how the predictive power of SRB measures differs by survey mode. We also had fewer measures available from the paper survey that could be compared to the web. Future work is needed to explore SRBs and cognition across modes and in mixed-mode surveys.

Second, the data we have on response times are not as granular as in some other studies. We only had access to section-level (not item-level) timings. We therefore examined the average response time across items, even though prior work suggests that differences across items might affect response times differently (Hess et al. 2013; DiTrapani et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2018). Junghaenel et al (2022), in particular, show that respondent reaction to the time intensity of an item is a strong additional predictor of cognition.

Finally, prior research has shown that SRB measures are more predictive of cognition when drawn from more complex items (Schneider et al. 2024). Both because we only have timing data at the section level and because of the narrow content domains of the supplement, we found that we did not have variation in complexity or difficulty in which we could attempt to replicate this finding.

In sum, our findings show that even after accounting for standard socio-demographic characteristics, the SRB measures available to us provide incremental yet valuable explanatory power throughout the life course. Although this contribution is generally less than that of education, it becomes more pronounced among the oldest respondents. With the total explanatory power of our full models ranging from 15 to 30 percent—depending on age group and cognitive measure—SRB measures enhance model fit but nonetheless leave a substantial degree of cognitive variance unexplained. This suggests that SRBs could have some utility in augmenting models that necessitate cognitive information, when that information is absent or insufficient for a given study. De New and Schurer (2023) demonstrate that even a weak proxy can be useful in reducing omitted variable bias in a regression model. As suggested by Junghaenel et al. (2022) and Schneider et al. (2024), the incremental explanatory power of response times, in particular, may be greater when more granular timing information is available, or where items in the survey instrument have greater variation in complexity or reading difficulty.

With most longitudinal surveys already quite lengthy, SRBs are one way to gather additional data with no added burden to respondents. As web and mixed-mode survey technologies increase in prevalence in the future, the integration of SRB information will become more mainstream and seamless. Future work is needed to further explore other possibilities of SRBs for augmenting survey data over the life course.
