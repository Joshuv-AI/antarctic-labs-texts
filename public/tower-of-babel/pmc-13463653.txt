Interruption in treatment and its risk factors among adults living with HIV in Uganda: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Saadick Mugerwa Ssentongo, William Okello, Hamidah Kakooza Mulungi, Peter Sekiranda, Christine Ninsiima, Bonniface Oryokot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to determine the prevalence of Interruption in treatment (IIT) and its risk factors in Katakwi District, Uganda.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in three antiretroviral therapy clinics. Poisson generalized estimating equations were used to identify factors associated with interruption in treatment.ResultsIIT was high at 68.2% and was associated with being aged 25–49, lacking social support from family and friends, being on ART for 0-1 years, and having a non-suppressed viral load. However, lower IIT rates were observed among those living within 5 km of the facility.ConclusionThere is a need to strengthen client-tracking systems and client-adherence literacy to reduce this IIT.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-026-00898-x.

Introduction

Interruption in treatment (IIT) is defined as failure to reinitiate care after a gap of 28 days or more from the last expected clinical contact or treatment refill, and is a critical global challenge with attrition rates of up to 30% annually [1, 2]. It leads to severe consequences like advanced HIV disease, viral resistance, persistent viremia, HIV transmission, and high AIDS related mortality [3]. This challenge is acute in Sub-Saharan Africa, which carries a heavy HIV burden. The region also experiences high IIT rates between 23% and 70% [3]. This undermines the efforts to achieve epidemic control.

Despite Uganda’s success in scaling up ART to over 96.1% of those who know their status. Its IIT remains at 20%, well above the acceptable 2% threshold [4]. Common drivers include unstable income, medication forgetfulness, stigma, and lack of social support. In response, Uganda has implemented interventions such as physical locator forms, differentiated service models for care, and improvements in client literacy.

Katakwi district has one of the highest numbers of clients in care (5,600) in the Teso Sub-region. In January-March 2024, it reported a high IIT of 4% with a low return to treatment rate (67%), highlighting a significant local challenge. However, there is limited evidence on the drivers of this IIT. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of IIT and its predictors in Katakwi District, Uganda, to inform targeted intervention.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted among PLHIV aged ≥ 18 years in Katakwi District, Uganda, from 15th July 2024 to 1 st August 2024.

Study population

The Study population was PLHIV aged ≥ 18 years who had been on ART with at least one clinical contact.

Study setting

The study was conducted at three high-volume ART clinics in Katakwi District: Katakwi General Hospital, Magoro Health Center III, and Ngariam Health Center III. These health facilities were selected purposively because they provide care to 73% of the district’s PLHIV.

As of the end of the January-March 2024 quarter, Katakwi district had a total of 5,600 PLHIV. The district is located approximately 350 km northeast of Kampala City, Uganda. As of 2024, the district has a total population of 234,705 [5]. The district has a semi-arid climate, and the major economic activities are cattle-keeping and subsistence farming, indicative of a low-income population. These socioeconomic and geographic factors are expected to cause significant challenges to clinic attendance. Each health facility had an ART clinic in charge, supervised by the district HIV focal person, who is the technical lead, and a trained HIV care and treatment provider.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were all PLHIV aged ≥ 18 years receiving care from three high-volume sites, in care between October 2023 and June 2024. Exclusion criteria included having a history of psychiatric illness, having been transferred out of facilities, and being too ill to participate.

Sampling procedures

Participants active in care were identified through the Open Electronic Medical Record System (EMRS). Their paper-based charts were cross-checked in real time to ensure data accuracy. A list of all eligible clients was compiled to form a sample frame. Participants were selected using a consecutive sampling technique. All eligible clients with appointments during the study period were interviewed; those with forward appointments were called back, and those who had treatment interruptions were followed up and interviewed.

Data collection

Trained research assistants collected data from these identified individuals through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire.

Study variables

The dependent variable was clients who had experienced Interruption in treatment (IIT), a binary variable defined as PLHIV with no clinical contact for 28 days or more after the last expected clinical contact or clinical appointment [1].

Independent Variables included socio-demographic characteristics (age, Sex, Employment status, religion), duration on ART, Mode of care, distance to clinic, HIV status Disclosure, receiving support from family/friends, and viral load status.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel, validated for correctness, and exported to STATA version 17 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). We summarised categorical variables as proportions and continuous variables as means and standard deviations, or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for skewed distributions. The association between interruption in treatment and categorical variables was assessed using the Chi-square test, and that between interruption in treatment and numerical variables using Student’s t-test. We fitted Poisson generalised estimating equations (GEE) with robust standard errors, accounting for clustering at the health facility level. The model included all variables with p < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis and clinically relevant variables identified in the literature, adjusting for each other. The overall model fit was significant (p < 0.001). All Results were presented as adjusted Risk Ratios (aRRs) with a 95% confidence interval. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for analysis.

Prevalence of interruption in treatment

Among the PLHIV, 68.2% (n = 242) (CI: 63.1%−72.8%) had experienced IIT. Of these, 55.0% (n = 133) had interrupted for 30 days, 28.1% (n = 68) for 60 days, 14.0% (n = 34) for 90 days, and 2.9% (n = 7) for more than 90 days.

Factors associated with interruption in treatment

In multivariate analysis, clients aged 25–49 had a 5% higher rate of IIT compared to those aged ≥ 50 years (aRR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.004–1.09). The IIT rate was 27% higher among clients with no family social support (aRR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.21–1.33). Adult PLHIV with a non-suppressed viral load had an 11% higher rate of IIT (aRR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06–1.17), while those on ART for 0–1 years had a 28% higher IIT rate compared to those on ART for > 5 years (aRR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.05–1.56). Conversely, clients living within 5 km of the facility had a 14% lower rate of IIT than those living > 10 km away (aRR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.80–0.93), as shown in Table 2.

Discussion

This study found a high IIT prevalence of 68.2%, consistent with findings from Nigeria [1] and Ethiopia [6] but it is higher than the rates in Kenya [7], Tanzania [8]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in the definition of IIT, with some using a 90-day definition compared to the current 28-day definition, differences in study populations’ characteristics, and follow-up efforts. However, the high IIT emphasises the need to scale up evidence-based retention initiatives, including client tracking systems with active phone calls and physical follow-ups, appointment reminders, attaching a community health worker per client for accountability, operating clinic hours that favour clients who are returning to Care, providing adequate health education and expansion of community outreaches to bring ART services closer to clients.

We found that clients aged 25–49 had a 5% higher IIT rate than those aged > 50 years, consistent with studies from Swaziland [9] and France [10]. This is likely because individuals in this age group face higher HIV related stigma, difficulties with disclosure, and economic pressure [11]. These findings highlight the need for age-specific, tailored retention strategies, including flexible appointment scheduling, peer support groups, disclosure support for young people to trusted individuals, and stigma-reduction interventions for PLHIV in this age group [12].

At the interpersonal level, having social support from family and friends was associated with lower rates of IIT. This highlights the importance of social support, interpersonal relationships, and community networks in improving continuity of care by providing reminders, escorting clients to the facility, creating a stigma-free environment, and assisting with clinic visits and drug refills [13]. These findings support the integration of family-centred HIV care approaches, including disclosure counselling and partner sensitisation sessions to utilise social support as a retention facilitator.

Having a non-suppressed viral load was associated with a higher likelihood of IIT, consistent with findings in South Africa [14] and the USA [15]. This is logically consistent, as treatment interruption leads to missed doses and viral rebound, while adherence challenges can also lead to clients suffering from advanced HIV-related diseases, resulting in missed clinic visits [16]. Therefore, these findings highlight the importance of intensive adherence support and closer monitoring of clients with high viremia levels.

Clients on ART for 0–1 years were more likely to interrupt treatment as compared to those on ART for > 5 years, consistent with studies from South Africa [17] and China [18]. This is often due to initial drug toxicities, adverse effects, high HIV-related stigma, and difficulties in accepting the HIV diagnosis [19]. This early period of diagnosis presents a critical window of vulnerability, during which intensive support is most needed. These findings support assigning expert clients to all newly initiated clients to provide peer mentorship, facilitate psychological adjustment, and promote adherence during the first year of ART [12].

Beyond individual factors, structural determinants also affected continuity of care. Clients living within 5 km of the facility were less likely to interrupt treatment compared to those living more than 10 km away, consistent with the literature, indicating that proximity facilitates better access to ART services [20]. This suggests that eliminating barriers, like transport costs and long distances through expansion of community differentiated ART models, including community ART groups and drug pick up points, could structuralbarriers and reduce the likelihood of IIT.

Limitations and strengths

Due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, we could not establish causality and the temporal sequence of associations. The study was conducted in selected facilities, affecting the generalizability of the findings to similar settings. However, a key strength is that it was a multicenter study that engaged clients directly rather than using routine data, which can introduce misclassifications.

Conclusions

This study found a high interruption in treatment associated with being aged 25–49, lacking social support, having a non-suppressed viral load, and being new on ART for 0 to 1 year. Living close to the facility (< 5 km) was protective. These findings highlight the interplay between individual and health-system barriers to continuity of care. The results emphasise the need for patient-centred interventions, including scaling up community-differentiated models to bring ART services closer to patients, establishing age-specific peer support groups, and assigning community health workers to newly diagnosed clients to ensure a smooth transition to long-term ART. Addressing these determinants is key to retaining clients in care and achieving epidemic control in Uganda.
