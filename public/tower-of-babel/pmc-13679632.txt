Cancer-associated fibroblasts-derived CAV1 promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition and stemness in bladder cancer through activation of the LIF/STAT3 signaling pathway
Authors: Qi Wang, Dengdian Wang, Haihong Jiang, Jie Min, Lin Hu, Jiatong Yin, Xiansheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CAV1 induces EMT and stemness in bladder cancer.•CAV1 drives tumor progression and recurrence.•CAV1 activates the LIF/STAT3 signaling pathway.•Blocking LIF/STAT3 reverses CAV1-induced effects.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Cancer-associated fibroblasts, CAV1, Cancer stem cells, LIF

Introduction

Bladder cancer constitutes a major urological malignancy, and its rising incidence worldwide has imposed a substantial healthcare burden [1,2]. Despite advances in early diagnosis and surgical management, bladder cancer remains challenging due to its high recurrence rate and persistent risk of progression to muscle-invasive disease [1,3]. In recent years, immunotherapy represented by PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors, along with the emergence of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) such as enfortumab vedotin (EV), targeting Nectin-4, and sacituzumab govitecan targeting Trop-2, has significantly improved survival outcomes for some patients [4,5]. Therefore, uncovering the mechanisms responsible for bladder cancer recurrence and progression is crucial for developing improved therapeutic strategies.

The paradigm of cancer research has shifted from a cancer cell–centric perspective to a more holistic investigation of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [6,7]. The TME is a dynamic and heterogeneous ecosystem comprising not only malignant cells but also immune infiltrates, vascular endothelial cells, stromal components such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and a complex extracellular matrix [8,9]. The functional state of immune cells is tightly linked to metabolic reprogramming, with mitochondrial dynamics playing a central role in coordinating inflammatory and immune responses [10,11]. Increasing evidence underscores that these non-malignant components actively shape tumor behavior rather than serving as passive bystanders [12,13]. It is worth noting that the complex intercellular communication within the TME also plays a significant role in the development of treatment resistance [14,15]. Among the various stromal cell types, CAFs have emerged as key orchestrators of tumor progression [16,17]. Their ability to remodel tissue architecture and create a tumor-permissive niche highlights their functional importance across many tumor types. In bladder cancer, the enrichment of CAFs is similarly closely associated with higher pathological grades, advanced clinical stages, and poorer patient survival outcomes [18]. These clinical associations, together with mounting mechanistic evidence, underscore the central role of CAFs in driving an aggressive disease phenotype. Therefore, targeting CAFs or their secreted signaling networks has emerged as a promising new direction for anticancer therapy.

Caveolin-1 (CAV1) is a key functional protein of caveolae structures in the cell membrane [19]. Recent evidence indicates that Cav-1 deficiency disrupts BDNF/TrkB signaling in retinal ganglion cells, thereby compromising RGC survival and contributing to glaucoma pathogenesis [20]. It not only participates in membrane transport and signal transduction but also plays complex roles in various cancers [21]. CAV1 exhibits context-dependent functions, acting as a tumor suppressor in early-stage cancers but promoting progression when expressed in the stromal compartment of advanced tumors [22]. However, the role of CAV1 in bladder cancer has mainly been investigated in tumor cells, while its expression, function, and regulatory mechanisms in CAFs remain largely unexplored. Therefore, this study aims to define the contribution and molecular basis of CAF-derived CAV1 in bladder cancer progression.

Materials and methods

Data collection and analysis

The bladder cancer single-cell RNA sequencing dataset (GSE130001) was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database and includes samples from two patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer [23]. Following standard quality control and principal component analysis (PCA), cell types were annotated based on well-established marker genes with reference to the TISCH database [24]. Genes associated with ferroptosis were retrieved from the FerrDb database [25]. Stemness‑related genes were derived from 30 stemness‑relevant gene sets available in the Molecular Signatures Database [26]. Transcriptomic sequencing data and corresponding clinical information for bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA) were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Immune cell infiltration abundance across 13 independent bladder cancer datasets was obtained using the BEST (Biomarker Exploration of Solid Tumors) online platform (https://rookieutopia.hiplot.com.cn/app_direct/BEST/), which provides standardized estimates of tumor immune infiltration levels [27].

Clinical samples and immunofluorescence

Commercial bladder cancer tissue microarrays (ZL-BlaU961; 60 tumors and 36 matched adjacent normal tissues; Shanghai Zhuoli Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were subjected to immunofluorescence staining using primary antibodies against CAV1 and α-SMA (Affinity Biosciences, USA), followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining [28]. Images were acquired with a Leica fluorescence scanning system (five fields per section), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using HALO software. All staining results were independently evaluated by two pathologists with appropriate positive and negative controls.

Cell culture and transfection

Human bladder fibroblasts (CP-H073) and bladder cancer cell lines (T24 and UMUC3) were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. CAFs were generated by co-culturing fibroblasts with bladder cancer cells (1:2) in a Transwell system for 48 h and validated by increased expression of CAF marker genes. The induction protocol was adapted from previously established methods for CAF activation via tumor–stroma paracrine interactions [29]. CAV1- and STAT3-specific siRNAs (GenePharma, Shanghai) were used for gene silencing, and recombinant human LIF (20 ng/mL; MedChemExpress, USA) was applied for 24 h where indicated.

Cellular immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked before incubation with anti-STAT3 and anti-CD44 primary antibodies, followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured spectrophotometrically. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (EnzyArtisan, China). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on a real-time PCR system. Relative gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [30]. Primer sequences were as follows: CAV1 forward 5′-CATCCCGATGGCACTCATCTG-3′, reverse 5′-TGCACTGAATCTCAATCAGGAAG-3′; STAT3 forward 5’-GGTGCCTGTGGGAAGAATCA-3', reverse 5’-ACATCCTGAAGGTGCTGCTC-3’; GAPDH forward 5′-CAAGGCTGAGAACGGGAAG-3′, reverse 5′-TGAAGACGCCAGTGGACTC-3′.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes for Western blot analysis. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against N-cadherin, Vimentin, β-actin, CD44, CD133, OCT4, SOX2 (Proteintech, China), CAV1, LIF, JAK2, p-JAK2, STAT3, and p-STAT3 (Affinity Biosciences, USA). Following washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system and visualized with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell chambers (Corning, USA), with Matrigel-coated inserts used for invasion assays. After 24 h, migrated or invaded cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and quantified by counting cells in five randomly selected microscopic fields.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at low density and cultured for 7 days. Colonies were then fixed, stained with crystal violet, and those containing more than 50 cells were counted to determine colony formation efficiency.

Tumor sphere formation assay

Cells were cultured in ultra-low attachment plates with serum-free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with B27, EGF, and bFGF for 7 days. Tumor spheres (>50 μm) were counted under a microscope, and sphere formation efficiency was calculated.

In vivo xenograft tumor model and immunohistochemistry

To investigate the in vivo role of the CAV1–LIF axis in tumor progression, a subcutaneous xenograft model was established using 4–6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (purchased from Anhui Zhongsheng Suyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). T24 bladder cancer cells (5 × 10⁶) were mixed with CAFs and suspended in a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel (Corning) and serum-free medium, then subcutaneously injected into the right dorsal flank of each mouse to establish xenograft tumors. Stattic (MedChemExpress, China) was dissolved in 10% DMSO and 90% corn oil for in vivo administration. Body weight was monitored weekly to assess systemic toxicity. At the endpoint, tumor tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm. After antigen retrieval and blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CAV1, LIF, p-STAT3 (Affinity Biosciences, USA), and Ki-67 (Proteintech, China). Signals were detected using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized with DAB, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining.

Statistical analysis

The R software (version 4.4.1) was used for statistical analysis. All experiments were performed in triplicate and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while differences among multiple groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell analysis identifies different cell types and key genes in bladder cancer samples

Following quality control and normalization, sequencing depth positively correlated with intracellular UMI counts and mitochondrial gene content (Fig. 1A). Unsupervised clustering partitioned cells from both samples into nine distinct clusters (Fig. 1B). These clusters were further annotated into four major cell types, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myofibroblasts, based on canonical marker genes (Fig. 1C and D). Differential expression analysis of fibroblasts identified 447 DEGs, intersecting these genes with stemness- and ferroptosis-related genes identified nine candidate genes (Fig. 1E). The distribution of this signature across the four cell types is shown in a t-SNE projection and quantified in the corresponding point plot (Fig. 1F and G).

CAV1 expression and its clinical significance in bladder cancer

Pan-cancer analysis revealed that CAV1 was upregulated in six cancer types and downregulated in ten others, including BLCA (Fig. 2A). In the TCGA-BLCA cohort, paired tumor-normal analysis revealed that CAV1 expression was significantly lower in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal samples (Fig. 2B). We further evaluated the association between CAV1 expression and clinicopathological characteristics. CAV1 expression differed significantly according to pathologic T stage, gender, histologic grade, subtype, smoking, and pathologic stage (Fig. 2C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with elevated CAV1 expression had significantly poorer overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) compared to those with low CAV1 expression (Fig. 2D). Univariate Cox regression analysis identified pathologic T stage, N stage, M stage, pathologic stage, age, and CAV1 expression as significant risk factors, while papillary subtype was a protective factor (Fig. 2E). Multivariate analysis further demonstrated that pathologic N stage and CAV1 expression were independent predictors of poor prognosis (Fig. 2F).

CAV1 correlates with fibroblast infiltration and exhibits heterogeneous expression in cancer-associated fibroblasts

MCPcounter analysis across 13 bladder cancer datasets revealed that fibroblast infiltration was positively correlated with CAV1 expression (Fig. 3A and B). Single-cell analysis further demonstrated that CAV1 and ACTA2 exhibited overlapping but distinct expression patterns within the fibroblast population, with CAV1 predominantly restricted to a subset of ACTA2-positive fibroblast (Fig. 3C). GSEA of fibroblasts stratified by CAV1 expression showed that high CAV1 was associated with enrichment of multiple Hallmark pathways, whereas fibroblasts with low CAV1 expression were enriched in allograft rejection and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (Fig. 3D). In the TCGA-BLCA cohort, CAV1 and ACTA2 expression were significantly positively correlated at the bulk level (Fig. 3E). Immunofluorescence staining of 60 tumor and 36 adjacent normal tissues confirmed co-localization of CAV1 with α-SMA-positive fibroblasts, with significantly elevated CAV1 expression in tumor tissues; however, α-SMA levels remained unchanged, and no significant correlation between CAV1 and α-SMA was observed at the protein level (Fig. 3F and G), indicating that CAV1 expression is restricted to a specific CAF subpopulation rather than reflecting overall CAF abundance.

CAFs enhance the malignancy of bladder cancer cells via EMT and stemness

Immunofluorescence confirmed Collagen I expression (Fig. 4A). We then established an indirect co-culture system of CAFs with bladder cancer cell lines to simulate intercellular interactions within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 4B). Following indirect co-culture, the malignant phenotype of bladder cancer cells was significantly enhanced. Transwell assays showed that the migration and invasion abilities of cancer cells co-cultured with CAFs were markedly increased (Fig. 4C). In addition, colony formation assay indicated a significant boost in proliferative capacity (Fig. 4D), while tumorsphere formation assay demonstrated enhanced stemness (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis of EMT-related markers showed that co-culture promoted EMT in bladder cancer cells, as evidenced by increased expression of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 4F). Meanwhile, protein expression levels of key stemness markers, including CD44, CD133, OCT4, and SOX2, were also significantly elevated (Fig. 4G).

Knockdown of CAV1 in CAFs attenuates their pro-tumorigenic effects on bladder cancer cells

To investigate the role of CAV1 in CAFs, siRNA-mediated knockdown was performed to inhibit CAV1 expression (Fig. 5A). RT-qPCR confirmed that both siRNA sequences effectively reduced CAV1 mRNA levels compared with the control group (Fig. 5B). Functional experiments indicated that compared with the control group CAFs, the migration and invasion abilities of bladder cancer cells were significantly reduced after co-culture with two CAV1-knocked-down CAFs (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the plate colony formation experiment confirmed that the proliferation ability of cancer cells also significantly decreased (Fig. 5D and E). The tumor stem cell spheroid formation experiment further showed that the self-renewal and stemness characteristics of cancer cells were inhibited (Fig. 5F and G).Western blot analysis showed that after co-culture with CAV1-knocked-down CAFs, and the expression of EMT markers N-cadherin and Vimentin decreased (Fig. 5H). Additionally, protein expression of key stemness markers, including CD44, CD133, OCT4, and SOX2, was markedly decreased (Fig. 5I).

Bladder cancer CAFs promote malignant progression via the CAV1-LIF-STAT3 axis

Previous studies have shown that in pancreatic cancer, CAV1 in CAFs promotes chemoresistance by regulating LIF secretion and subsequently activating the STAT3 signaling pathway in cancer cells [31]. To elucidate the downstream mechanism of CAV1 in bladder cancer, we analyzed the TCGA-BLCA dataset and found that LIF was significantly upregulated in the CAV1-high group and positively correlated with CAV1 expression (Fig. 6A and B). Western blot confirmed that CAV1 knockdown in CAFs significantly reduced LIF protein expression, indicating that CAV1 regulates LIF secretion from the stromal compartment (Fig. 6C). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that recombinant LIF (rLIF) treatment induced nuclear translocation of STAT3 (green) and increased expression of CD44 (red), a known downstream target of STAT3 signaling, in control cells (Fig. 6D). We next examined the LIF-STAT3 axis in bladder cancer cells. RT-qPCR confirmed efficient STAT3 knockdown using two independent siRNAs (Fig. 6E). Western blot further demonstrated that LIF activated JAK2-STAT3 signaling, whereas STAT3 knockdown abolished LIF-induced STAT3 phosphorylation (Fig. 6F). Functionally, LIF significantly enhanced migration, invasion, and colony formation in control cells, while STAT3 knockdown substantially attenuated these pro-tumor effects (Fig. 6G and H).

Targeting STAT3 suppresses CAF-promoted tumor growth in vivo

To investigate the functional role of STAT3 in CAF-mediated tumor promotion in bladder cancer, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model with four experimental groups. Tumor growth of T24 cells was compared between the monoculture group and the CAF co-inoculation group. Consistent with the in vitro findings, CAFs markedly enhanced tumor growth in vivo (Fig. 7A). Notably, pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 in the presence of CAFs effectively abrogated this CAF-driven tumor acceleration. Tumors in the STAT3 inhibition group exhibited significantly reduced final volumes, and tumor weights, reaching levels comparable to those of T24 cells alone without CAFs (Fig. 7B and C). IHC analysis demonstrated enhanced STAT3 activation, indicated by increased p-STAT3 staining, in CAF-inoculated tumors, while STAT3 inhibition reversed this effect. No significant alterations in CAV1, LIF, or Ki-67 expression were detected among the groups (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

Bladder cancer is a common malignancy of the urinary tract, with a steadily increasing global incidence [1,32]. Although surgery and immunotherapy have improved outcomes, high recurrence and progression rates remain major challenges [33].

In bladder cancer, increased enrichment of CAFs is strongly associated with higher pathological grade, advanced clinical stage, and poorer patient survival [34,35]. Consistent with these clinical observations, single-cell transcriptomic profiling delineated the cellular ecosystem of bladder cancer tissues at high resolution and revealed a marked enrichment of CAV1 in CAFs. CAF stimulation induced a pronounced EMT, characterized by decreased epithelial markers, increased mesenchymal proteins, and acquisition of spindle-like cellular morphology. We observed upregulation of key stemness-related transcription factors and surface markers, suggesting that CAF-driven signaling not only promotes EMT but also reinforces cancer stem cell-like properties. These findings collectively reinforce the concept that CAFs act as a central orchestrator of tumor cell plasticity. By modulating both EMT and cancer stemness, CAFs create a permissive microenvironment that facilitates tumor progression, therapeutic resistance, and metastatic dissemination [36,37].

The TME is increasingly recognized as a dynamic ecosystem in which stromal cells, metabolic networks, immune components, and tumor-associated microorganisms cooperatively regulate tumor initiation and progression. For example, mitochondria, as central regulators of cellular metabolism, play essential and context-dependent roles in metabolic reprogramming within the TME, thereby influencing cell fate determination, intercellular communication, and therapeutic responses [38,39]. In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that the tumor-associated microbiota actively shapes the TME through metabolic regulation, immune modulation, and direct interactions with stromal and tumor cells, further contributing to malignant progression and treatment resistance [40,41]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CAV1 expression within CAFs promotes tumor progression by remodeling the stromal compartment and regulating the secretion of pro-tumorigenic cytokines and extracellular matrix components [31,42,43]. As a membrane-associated scaffolding protein, CAV1 participates in lipid raft organization, vesicular trafficking, and multiple signaling pathways, thereby contributing to stromal remodeling, therapeutic resistance, and poor clinical outcomes across various malignancies [44]. Through these diverse functions, CAV1 has been implicated in tumor progression, stromal remodeling, therapeutic resistance, and unfavorable clinical outcomes across several malignancies [45]. Silencing CAV1 in CAFs markedly impaired their pro-tumorigenic effects on bladder cancer cells, including reduced migration, invasion, proliferation, and stem-like traits. Concomitantly, CAV1 deficiency abrogated CAF-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition and diminished the expression of stemness-associated transcription factors, indicating a loss of tumor cell plasticity. Collectively, these findings position CAV1 as a key regulator of CAF-mediated tumor–stroma crosstalk and underscore its potential as a therapeutic target for inhibiting bladder cancer progression.

In multiple solid tumors, LIF has been shown to promote EMT, enhance migratory and invasive capabilities, and maintain cancer stem cell properties [46,47]. Its tumor-promoting effects are largely mediated through activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway. Clinical and large-scale genomic analyses have further shown that elevated LIF/LIFR expression is strongly associated with higher pathological grade, advanced stage, and poor prognosis across multiple tumor types [48,49]. Our results show that CAF-derived CAV1 regulates LIF expression, which in turn activates the JAK2/STAT3 signaling cascade in tumor cells. Exogenous recombinant LIF markedly promoted bladder cancer cell migration, invasion, and proliferation, whereas STAT3 silencing completely abrogated these effects, indicating that LIF-driven tumor-promoting activity is dependent on STAT3 signaling.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, most experiments were performed using established cell lines; therefore, further validation in patient-derived organoids or primary CAFs is needed to better reflect the heterogeneity of bladder cancer. Second, the upstream regulatory mechanisms governing CAV1-mediated modulation of LIF transcription and secretion remain unclear and warrant further investigation. Notably, although CAV1 expression was reduced in bulk transcriptomic datasets, single-cell and immunofluorescence analyses revealed its preferential enrichment in a distinct CAF subpopulation. This apparent discrepancy likely reflects cell type–specific expression patterns of CAV1 as well as inherent differences between bulk tissue profiling and single-cell or spatial resolution analyses. Finally, although STAT3 is known to regulate multiple immunological processes within the tumor microenvironment, its potential crosstalk with immune cell populations was not explored in this study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings deepen the understanding of CAF–tumor cell communication in bladder cancer and identify CAF-derived CAV1 as a crucial upstream driver of both EMT and cancer stemness through the LIF/STAT3 axis.
