Age-stratified analysis of euploidy rates in Progestin-Primed Ovarian Stimulation (PPOS) utilizing micronized progesterone or dydrogesterone versus GnRH analogues
Authors: Rang Liu, Xinyi Pan, Yutong Wang, Rui Xiang, Shaohong Zhuang, Xiaoting Diao, Cong Fang, Xiaoyan Liang, Haitao Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposesThis study aims to analyze the euploidy rates of PPOS utilizing micronized progesterone and dydrogesterone in comparison to conventional controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) protocols in pre-implantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) cycles stratified by age cohorts.MethodsThis was a retrospective study including 2897 PGT-A cycles from a single reproductive medicine center. Study groups were categorized by different COS protocols, including PPOS (micronized progesterone), PPOS (dydrogesterone), GnRH-agonist, or GnRH-antagonist, and further sub-grouped into < 35 years, 35–37 years, 38–40 years, and > 40 years. Data about baseline characteristics, protocols and embryonic outcomes were analyzed. Multivariate regression analysis and adjustment by inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were performed to investigate the correlation between COS protocols and euploidy rate per biopsied blastocyst.ResultsBefore stratified analysis and adjustment, the PPOS (dydrogesterone) group had significantly higher age, lower AMH level and fewer AFC than the PPOS (micronized progesterone), the GnRH-a and GnRH-Ant groups. Euploidy rate per COC, per MII oocyte and per biopsied blastocyst were lowest in the PPOS (dydrogesterone) group, followed by the PPOS (micronized progesterone) group. In subgroup analysis, the euploidy rate showed great disparity in different age groups and declined sharply every three years. All COS protocols were statistically comparable with respect to euploidy rate across all age cohorts. Regression analysis and IPTW adjustment revealed age as the sole factor affecting euploidy rates, with ovarian stimulation protocols showing no association.ConclusionsPPOS protocols using micronized progesterone or dydrogesterone were statistically comparable with conventional COS regimens in euploidy rates across all different age groups. Considering the significant impact of age, the necessity of refined age stratification for a more rigorous discussion about the aneuploidy risk of different COS protocols was emphasized.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01834-9.Keywords: Controlled ovarian stimulation, Progestin-primed ovarian stimulation, Micronized progesterone, Dydrogesterone, Pre-implantation genetic testing, Euploidy

Introduction

Controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) is a cornerstone of assisted reproductive technology (ART), primarily aimed at inducing multifollicular development [1]. A notable limitation of COS is the occurrence of a premature luteinizing hormone (LH) surge, which is instigated by elevated plasma estradiol concentrations resulting from multiple growing follicles [2]. In decades, gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues such as GnRH antagonists (GnRH-ant) or agonists (GnRH-a) are widely used to suppress endogenous LH surges, and nowadays GnRH-ant has been recommended as the standard and first-line COS protocol for patients regardless of ovarian response [1]. Despite their established efficacy and safety, conventional protocols are constrained by the requirement for multiple injection doses, associated high costs, the necessity for frequent clinic visits, and the risk of adverse effects, including ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) [3, 4]. In 2015, Kuang et al. introduced the progestin-primed ovarian stimulation (PPOS) protocol based on medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) to approach the pituitary suppression and inhibit the premature LH surge [5]. The utilization of oral progestins in PPOS makes it an attractive alternative in certain patient populations for being patient-friendly, cost-effective, and associated with a reduced risk of OHSS. These attributes render PPOS particularly advantageous in scenarios where freeze-all protocols are planned, such as oocyte donation, fertility preservation, and pre-implantation genetic testing (PGT) cycles [6].

With the expanding deployment of PPOS in clinical practice over the past few years, safety aspects have garnered increasing attention and debate. In an attempt to explore whether progestins influence oocyte or embryo development, the risk of aneuploidy in PPOS cycles has been extensively discussed in the literature. While several studies suggested that PPOS seemed not to increase the incidence of aneuploid embryos compared to conventional COS protocols [7–10], other investigations have yielded inconsistent results, especially when study populations were stratified by maternal age [11–13]. However, the majority of these studies are limited by the sample sizes of pre-implantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) cycles and exhibit heterogeneity in patients’ age and progestin types included, encompassing MPA, dydrogesterone, and micronized progesterone. Recently, pharmacovigilance data have raised questions about the safety of dydrogesterone compared to micronized progesterone regarding birth defects [14]. A more thorough and straightforward comparison of dydrogesterone and micronized progesterone in terms of embryo ploidy could possibly supply some information for the comprehensive assessment of their safety profiles.

This study aims to address these gaps by conducting a retrospective analysis of PGT-A cycles in a relatively large sample size from a single reproductive center. The investigation focuses on the effects of PPOS employing either micronized progesterone or dydrogesterone on oocyte and embryo outcomes, with an emphasis on the embryo euploidy rate. It is anticipated that our findings could offer helpful insights for optimizing COS strategies in PGT-A cycles, potentially contributing to the improvement of clinical outcomes for infertile patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and subjects

This retrospective study was conducted at the Reproductive Medicine Center of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between October 2018 and October 2024. The study included women aged 20–49 years who were proposed to undergo pre-implantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) cycles for advanced maternal age, recurrent implantation failure, or recurrent pregnancy loss and treated with one of the three COS protocols: long GnRH-agonist protocol, GnRH-antagonist protocol, or PPOS protocol. Patients or cycles were excluded if (1) using other protocols including non-standard long protocols, natural/modified natural cycles, mild stimulation, luteal-phase stimulation, etc.; (2) with oocyte donation or using thawed embryo for biopsy; (3) with missing core clinical or laboratory data; (4) with cancelled oocyte retrieval (Fig. 1). Patients’ demographic characteristics and cycle information were reviewed and analyzed including age, type of infertility, duration of infertility, body mass index (BMI), ovarian reserve (i.e., anti-Müllerian hormone, AMH and antral follicle count, AFC), gonadotropin (Gn) dosage, stimulation duration, hormone level on trigger day and the outcomes of oocyte retrieval, insemination, embryo development, and embryo biopsy. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (approval number: 2025ZSLYEC-151).

Flowchart of the study

Controlled ovarian stimulation protocols

For the PPOS protocol, 200 mg/day of oral micronized progesterone (Utrogestan, Besins Healthcare, France) or 20 mg/day of oral dydrogesterone (Duphaston, Abbott Biologicals, Netherlands) was administered daily with Gn from the second or third day of the menstrual cycle until the trigger day. For the GnRH-a protocol, patients received a long-acting dose of GnRH-a (triptorelin acetate, Decapeptyl, Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Germany) during the mid-luteal phase of the preceding menstrual cycle. After approximately 14–20 days, pituitary downregulation was confirmed by ultrasound and serum hormone levels, and then Gn was given. For the GnRH-ant protocol, ovarian stimulation was started on the second or third day of the menstrual cycle. A GnRH-ant (cetrorelix acetate, Cetrotide, Merck Serono, Switzerland) was introduced on the fifth or sixth day of ovarian stimulation or when the leading follicle reached 14 mm in diameter.

In all protocols, the initial Gn dose was determined based on the patient’s age, BMI, AFC and AMH level. During ovarian stimulation process, ovarian response was monitored by transvaginal ultrasound and serum hormone measurements. The Gn dose was adjusted based on ovarian response. Final oocyte maturation was triggered with 10, 000IU of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) (Livzon Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) and/or 0.1–0.2 mg triptorelin acetate (Decapeptyl, Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Germany) when at least one follicle reached ≥ 18 mm or two follicles reached ≥ 17 mm. Oocyte retrieval was performed 36–38 h after the trigger under transvaginal ultrasound guidance.

Oocyte insemination, embryo culture, and pre-implantation genetic testing for aneuploidy

Retrieved cumulus oocyte complexes (COCs) were fertilized by conventional IVF or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) according to semen parameters. For ICSI, cumulus granulosa cells were stripped, and MII oocytes were injected 4 h after retrieval. Fertilization status was assessed 16–18 h later by the presence of two pronuclei (2PN). Embryos were cultured up to the fully expanded blastocyst stage and graded on day 5 and 6. According to the Gardner criteria, blastocyst was scored by the degree of expansion, ranging from stage 1 (early blastocyst) to stage 6 (hatched blastocyst), and as well as the morphology of trophectoderm (TE) and inner cell mass (ICM), graded from C (very few cells) to A (many cells, tightly packed and cohesive) [15]. Trophectoderm biopsy was performed for blastocyst graded ≥ 3 BC on day 5 or day 6 by laser-assisted aspiration of approximately 5–10 trophectoderm cells. Biopsied blastocysts were vitrified afterwards. Whole genome amplification (WGA) was conducted for biopsied cells, and comprehensive chromosome analysis was performed by next-generation sequencing (NGS). The results for successful testing were recorded as euploid, aneuploid, or mosaic.

Outcomes and statistics

The primary outcome was the euploid rate per biopsied blastocyst. Secondary outcomes included the euploid rate per COC and the euploid rate per MII. The number of oocytes retrieved, the number and rate of fertilization, the number and rate of MII, the number of blastocysts formed, blastocysts biopsied and euploid blastocysts were also recorded.

Kolmogorov-Smirnova tests were conducted to explore whether the data followed a normal distribution. Continuous variables were presented as medians (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The post-hoc test was used to determine the significant difference between the two groups. For differences among groups, a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In post hoc testing, Bonferroni correction was used and a P-value < 0.0083 (0.05/6) was considered statistically significant. The analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, Chicago, IL, USA).

Multivariate linear regression analysis was used to assess the association between COS protocols and the euploidy rate per biopsied blastocyst, adjusting for potential confounders such as female age, AMH, AFC, BMI and hormone level on trigger day. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed to address potential confounding and minimize the selection bias using arguments of propensity score (PS) and average treatment effect (ATE). Covariate balance was confirmed with standardized mean differences (SMD) < 0.1 [16]. The propensity model includes covariates in the baseline characteristics for weighting: age (as a categorical variable), type of infertility, duration of infertility, BMI, AMH (as a categorical variable), and AFC. The association between COS protocols and the euploidy rate per biopsied blastocyst was then re-assessed using a weighted regression model. The analysis results were presented as β values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The regression analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1, http://www.r-project.org).

Results

A total of 2897 PGT-A cycles were included in the analysis, divided by different COS protocols. The baseline characteristics of each treatment group were summarized in Table 1. The distribution of PPOS (micronized progesterone), PPOS (dydrogesterone), GnRH-ant, and GnRH-a protocols in cycles was 1417, 566, 535, and 379, respectively. Significant differences were observed in overall age, BMI, AMH levels, AFC, gonadotropin (Gn) dosage, stimulation duration, and hormonal profiles on the trigger day (all P < 0.05), except for infertility type. The highest female age was observed in the PPOS (dydrogesterone) group (40 [37, 42] years), followed by the PPOS (micronized progesterone) group (39 [33, 42] years). Both PPOS groups exhibited significantly higher ages compared to the GnRH-Ant (35 [30, 41] years) and GnRH-a (36 [31, 39] years) groups. When female age was stratified into categories of < 30 years, 31–34 years, 35–37 years, 38–40 years, and > 40 years, all subgroups were comparable (all P > 0.05). Ovarian reserve indicators, including AMH and AFC, demonstrated trends consistent with age among groups. AMH levels were significantly lower in the micronized progesterone group (1.96 [1.10, 3.33] ng/mL) and the dydrogesterone group (1.53 [0.86, 2.67] ng/mL) than the GnRH-a group (2.48 [1.73, 3.50] ng/mL) and GnRH-Ant group (2.45 [1.10, 5.05] ng/mL) (P < 0.001). Similarly, AFC was lower in the PPOS (micronized progesterone) (9 [6, 14]) and PPOS (dydrogesterone) (8 [5, 12]) compared to the GnRH-Ant (10.7 [6, 17]) and GnRH-a (10 [8, 14]) groups (P < 0.001).

Total Gn dosage was highest in the GnRH-a group (3000 [2400, 3750] IU), followed by PPOS (dydrogesterone) group (2700 [2250, 3300] IU), PPOS (micronized progesterone) (2400 [2025, 3000] IU) and GnRH-Ant (2400 [1800, 3000] IU) groups (P < 0.001). Stimulation duration was longer in the GnRH-a group (11 [10, 12] days) compared to the other groups, which were all with a median of 9 days (P < 0.001). On the trigger day, estradiol (E2) levels were highest in the GnRH-a group (2870 [1905, 4341.5] pg/mL) and lowest in the PPOS (dydrogesterone) group (2054 [1200, 3221] pg/mL) (P < 0.001). LH levels were markedly suppressed in the GnRH-a group (1.22 [0.86, 1.83] IU/L) compared to other groups (P < 0.001). Progesterone (P) levels were significantly elevated in the PPOS (micronized progesterone) group (8.77 [5.84, 12.90] ng/mL) relative to the PPOS (dydrogesterone) group and conventional protocols (P < 0.001), which conformed with pharmacological properties.

Significant differences were observed in all oocyte and embryological outcomes among the PPOS (micronized progesterone), PPOS (dydrogesterone), GnRH-Ant, and GnRH-a groups (all P < 0.05) (Table 2). The median number of oocytes retrieved was significantly lower in both PPOS groups (micronized progesterone: 9 [5, 14]; dydrogesterone: 8 [5, 13]) compared to the GnRH-Ant (11 [6, 18]) and GnRH-a (13 [9, 17]) protocols (P < 0.001). Similarly, the number of mature metaphase II (MII) oocytes was lower in the PPOS groups (micronized progesterone: 7 [4, 11]; dydrogesterone: 6 [4, 10]) than in the GnRH-Ant (9 [5, 14]) and GnRH-a (10 [6, 14]) groups (P < 0.001). For the MII rate, PPOS (micronized progesterone) exhibited the highest rate (79.95%), which was comparable with GnRH-a protocols (78.69%) but significantly higher than PPOS (dydrogesterone) (76.24%). Analysis of fertilization outcomes revealed that the median number of 2 pronuclei (2PN) zygotes was lower in the PPOS groups (micronized progesterone: 5 [3, 9]; dydrogesterone: 5 [3, 8]) than in the GnRH-Ant (6 [3, 12]) and GnRH-a (8 [5, 12]) groups (P < 0.001). The 2PN rate of PPOS (micronized progesterone) (62.19%) was comparable with PPOS (dydrogesterone) (62.27%) and GnRH-Ant (62.37%) groups, but significantly lower than in the GnRH-a group (64.49%). Blastocyst development and biopsied outcomes differed across the groups. The median number of blastocysts formed was highest in the GnRH-a group (4 [2, 6.5]) and lowest in the PPOS (dydrogesterone) group (2 [1, 4], P < 0.001). Consistently, the number of biopsied blastocysts was significantly lower in the PPOS (dydrogesterone) group (1 [0, 3]) compared to the PPOS (micronized progesterone) (2 [0, 3]), GnRH-Ant (2 [0.5, 4]), and GnRH-a (3 [1, 4]) protocols (P < 0.001). The median number of euploid blastocysts per cycle was lower in the PPOS (dydrogesterone) (0 [0, 1]) than in the PPOS (micronized progesterone) (0 [0, 2]), GnRH-Ant (1 [0, 2]) and GnRH-a (1 [0, 2]) groups (P < 0.001). The euploidy rates per COC, per MII oocyte and per biopsied blastocyst were lowest in the PPOS (dydrogesterone) group (7.78%, 9.87%, and 42.42%, respectively), followed by the PPOS (micronized progesterone) group (10.28%, 12.76%, and 46.27%, respectively). The euploidy rates in the GnRH-Ant (12.04%, 15.17%, and 54.52%, respectively) and GnRH-a group (10.45%, 15.89%, and 52.67%, respectively) were comparable.

To further investigate the effect of maternal age on euploidy outcomes from four COS protocols, a stratified analysis was conducted based on four age groups: < 35 years, 35–37 years, 38–40 years, and > 40 years (Fig. 2). Although all three euploidy rates were comparable between PPOS protocols (micronized progesterone and dydrogesterone) and conventional COS treatment in all age subgroups (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1), the results highlighted an age-related decline in euploidy rates across PPOS (micronized progesterone), PPOS (dydrogesterone), GnRH-Ant, and GnRH-a, with PPOS (dydrogesterone) appearing to be the most negatively affected by increasing maternal age. The euploidy rate per COC across four treatment protocols declined gradually from 14.52%-16.15% in the < 35 years group to as low as 2.68%−3.51% in the group > 40 years (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the euploidy rate per MII oocyte dropped gradually from 18.49%-20.16% in the younger group to 3.28%–4.51% in the advanced-age group (> 40 years) (Fig. 2B). The notable age-related decline also occurred in euploidy rates per biopsied blastocyst, decreasing from 59.72%-63.91% in the < 35 years group to 16.90%–24.74% in the > 40 years group (Fig. 2C).

Linear regression analysis and IPTW adjustment were used to address the potential confounders and explore the correlation of COS protocols and the euploidy rate per biopsied blastocyst (Table 3). Multivariate linear regression analysis showed only age was considered a significant influencing factor for the euploidy rate per biopsied blastocyst, and negative effect strengthened as age increased (35-37y: β −0.083, 95% CI −0.136 to −0.044; 38-40y: β −0.285, 95% CI −0.294 to −0.211; >40y: β −0.462, 95% CI −0.449 to −0.360, all P < 0.001). IPTW analysis adjusted the covariates to be balanced (Supplementary Table 2) and further revealed that PPOS (micronized progesterone), PPOS (dydrogesterone) (β 0.008, 95% CI −0.037 to 0.051, P = 0.759), GnRH-antagonist (β 0.007, 95% CI −0.038 to 0.050, P = 0.776), and GnRH-a (β 0.041, 95% CI −0.009 to 0.080, P = 0.120) were statistically comparable for the euploidy rate per biopsied blastocyst (Table 3).

Discussion

PGT-A represents a significant advancement in ART, particularly for individuals of advanced maternal age, those with a history of recurrent pregnancy loss, or those experiencing repeated implantation failure. This technique facilitates the identification and selection of euploid embryos for transfer, intending to optimize implantation rates and subsequent live birth outcomes [17]. While ART has revolutionized infertility treatment, there is growing concern about potential iatrogenic factors that may influence embryo ploidy, including ovarian stimulation [18]. Hitherto, the impact of different COS protocols, particularly PPOS, on embryo ploidy remains controversial. The question of whether PPOS increases the risk of aneuploidy compared to traditional GnRH-based protocols is still under investigation. This study comparatively analyze the aneuploidy risk associated with different types of progestin and conventional ovarian stimulation protocols, with a specific focus on age-stratified differences. By retrospectively analyzing 2897 PGT-A cycles, our study demonstrated that PPOS using micronized progesterone, dydrogesterone and GnRH analogues were comparable regarding the euploidy rate. Meanwhile, notable variations in euploidy rates dependent on age were observed across all COS protocols.

Analysis of baseline characteristics revealed a significantly higher age in the PPOS group compared to the conventional stimulation group, with a greater proportion of advanced maternal age patients. This observation was similar with some previous studies [11, 19], suggesting a clinical preference towards adopting PPOS in older patients. Patients with advanced maternal age [20], diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) [21], or poor ovarian response (POR) [22] could benefit from PPOS to have satisfactory embryological and clinical outcomes. For these patients, oocyte banking strategies with multiple stimulation cycles are often considered, where fresh embryo transfer is not contemplated. With non-inferior efficacy in oocyte retrieval and embryo formation [20, 23] compared to conventional protocols, PPOS presents its superiority in the scenario where “freeze-all” is adopted. With a high risk of premature luteinizing hormone surges, aged or DOR patients benefit from the efficient suppression of endogenous LH surges by the sustained elevated progesterone levels [5, 24], thereby reducing cycle cancellation rates. Furthermore, for aged patients with an indication of PGT, which is associated with substantial cumulative costs resulting from repetitive stimulation cycles and embryo biopsies, PPOS offers a potentially more economical alternative and a more flexible and simplified oral regimen, thereby alleviating some of the financial and physical burden on these patients.

The hormone profiles on trigger day showed relatively lower E2 and higher LH levels in the PPOS protocols compared with GnRH analogue groups, consistent with findings from related prior studies [11, 19]. More specifically, the dydrogesterone group exhibited the lowest E2, the highest LH, and the lowest P level. The reduced E2 could reflect an aging ovarian reserve and diminished granulosa cell function, given that this group had the oldest population at baseline. The precise mechanism by which progesterone regulates the LH surge remains complex and not fully understood. Although high progesterone levels have long been shown to suppress LH, several lines of evidence suggest that mildly elevated progesterone at the end of the follicular phase may facilitate LH secretion [25]. Despite the pharmacologic masking of progestin bioactivity by the tested P level, dydrogesterone appeared more potent in stimulating LH secretion at the preovulatory time point while exerting milder pituitary suppression. Additionally, euploidy rates per COC, per MII, and per biopsied blastocyst were lowest in the dydrogesterone group. At first glance, this inferiority could be attributed to the oldest age in this group; however, differences persisted across most age-stratified subgroups, particularly for those aged < 40, although without statistical significance. Likewise, a retrospective study comparing PPOS with dydrogesterone versus GnRH-antagonist reported a trend toward lower euploid rates in the dydrogesterone groups within the < 35, 35–37, and 38–40 subgroups [26]. Another randomized controlled trial comparing dydrogesterone and GnRH-antagonist found a slightly lower euploid rate (per injected oocyte) in the dydrogesterone group (12.5% vs. 16.0%), without statistical significance [27]. Evidence directly comparing micronized progesterone and dydrogesterone remains limited. More definitive conclusions are expected from high-quality randomized clinical trials focusing on different progestin comparisons.

It was observed that the disparities of laboratory outcomes and biopsy outcomes across four COS protocols conformed to the inherent age discrepancies among groups. Given the profound impact of maternal age on euploidy rates [28], subgroup analysis by age stratification would be more rigorous and scientific to discuss the differences between groups. Inconsistent conclusions were formed in previous studies discussing the aneuploidy risk of PPOS in younger and older populations [12, 13]. Pai et al. analyzed the euploidy rate of MPA cycles in young (< 38 years) and elder (≥ 38 years) sub-groups, and found that the euploidy rate of PPOS was significantly lower than the GnRH-ant group in the older population (5.4% vs. 26.7%, P = 0.006), while the difference was without significance in the young group [12]. Conversely, using an age cut-off value of 35, Wan et al. argued that in the older group, the euploidy rate per biopsied blastocyst of PPOS (MPA) was significantly higher than the GnRH-ant protocol (54.94% vs. 40.88%). The young group did not show a significant difference [13], which was in accordance with Pai’s study.

A stratified analysis using three-year age intervals was conducted in this study. Our analysis demonstrated no statistically significant differences in euploidy rates per retrieved oocyte, per MII oocyte, or per biopsied blastocyst between micronized progesterone, dydrogesterone, and conventional protocols across all age strata. However, a precipitous age-dependent decline in euploidy rates was observed. For example, a striking 10–20% difference was observed between patients aged 38–40 years and those > 40 years. The multivariate regression with IPTW adjustment identified maternal age as the only known significant influencing factor of euploidy rate per biopsied blastocyst. These findings underscore that a coarse age categorization may be insufficient for the statistical power in comparative protocol analyses, necessitating a fine age stratification to enable valid comparisons. Our data further emphasize the temporal urgency in treating advanced-age patients, where prolonged treatment intervals may exacerbate aneuploidy risks through continued ovarian aging. Optimal outcomes require expeditious acquisition of sufficient euploid embryos within compressed timelines. Notably, in younger patients (< 35 years), PPOS protocols also demonstrated comparable euploidy rates to conventional regimens. Given its cost-effectiveness and operational advantages in freeze-all cycles, PPOS represents a practical and potentially beneficial option for most IVF patients.

It is noted that most prior investigations evaluating PPOS exclusively used a single progesterone type, such as MPA, micronized progesterone, or dydrogesterone. Our study concurrently discussed the clinical efficacy of both micronized progesterone and dydrogesterone as medications for ovarian stimulation in a relatively larger scale, with a focus on the embryo chromosome ploid state. These two types of progestins have different chemical structures and functional groups. Progesterone is characterized by a steroid backbone composed of three cyclohexane rings and one cyclopentane ring. Its progestogenic effects are primarily mediated via the 3-keto functional group and the C4-C5 double bond [29]. Micronization enhances the per os bioavailability of progesterone, enabling the effective action as a natural progesterone. Sustained administration of micronized progesterone results in a significant and prolonged elevation of serum progesterone levels for days [30]. By contrast, dydrogesterone represents a stereoisomer of progesterone that features distinct structural modifications, with a methyl group at the C10 position in the α-configuration, and a hydrogen atom at C9 in the β-orientation. Additionally, dydrogesterone contains an extra double bond between C6 and C7. These structural alterations confer a bent molecular conformation with increased rigidity compared to native progesterone [29]. As an artificial non-androgenic progesterone without relevant androgenic, estrogenic, or mineralocorticoid activity, dydrogesterone has a higher affinity for the progesterone receptor, associated with a higher potency, absorption, and bioavailability than the natural progesterone [31]. Recently, it was reported that dydrogesterone exposure increased the risk of birth defects, mainly including hypospadias and congenital heart defects, based on the World Health Organization pharmacovigilance database [14, 32]. Our data analysis suggested that both types of progesterone appeared to be comparable from statistical aspect in their efficacy for obtaining euploid blastocysts across different age subgroups. This finding could possibly provide additional data supporting the safety profile of micronized progesterone and dydrogesterone from an embryological perspective. The systematic long-term safety of all different types of progesterone as medications for ovarian stimulation requires further data and discussion.

Our study was mainly limited in its retrospective design from a single center. Due to its retrospective nature, the sample size was not predetermined by a formal power analysis but was contingent upon the availability of all eligible patients or treatment cycles during the study period. While this approach encompasses the entire available cohort, it raises the possibility that the study may be underpowered to detect smaller, yet clinically relevant, effects. This underscores the need for cautious interpretation of the negative findings. Moreover, unknown bias may still come from the different concrete treatment protocols during ART, which were individualized and dependent on the specific clinical scenario. However, this shortage was more or less alleviated by the relatively large sample size of cycles included for analysis and statistics design of the IPTW method for multicategory treatments comparison, which simulated a randomized clinical trial and created a pseudo-population with adjusted covariate distribution among groups. A multi-center study with a prospective design is expected to further test the above findings.

In summary, this study demonstrated that the euploidy rates of ovarian stimulation using micronized progesterone, dydrogesterone and conventional regimens were statistically comparable in different age subgroups. Our results also implicated the necessity of refined age stratification for a more rigorous discussion about the aneuploidy risk of different COS protocols, considering the significant impact of age.
