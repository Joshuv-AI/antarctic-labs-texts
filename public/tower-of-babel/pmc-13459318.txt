Association of epicardial fat volume with early outcomes in patients undergoing cryoablation for atrial fibrillation
Authors: Mustafa Uçar, Nurullah Çetin, Mustafa Özcan Soylu, Muhammed İkbal Şaşmaz, Fatma Can
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpicardial fat is a metabolically active visceral adipose tissue that has been implicated in atrial remodeling and atrial fibrillation (AF) pathophysiology. Although increased epicardial fat volume has been associated with AF presence and recurrence after radiofrequency ablation, its role in predicting early outcomes after cryoballoon ablation (CBA) remains incompletely defined. This study aimed to evaluate the association between computed tomography (CT)–derived epicardial fat volume and early arrhythmia recurrence following CBA for paroxysmal AF.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 61 consecutive patients who underwent CBA for paroxysmal AF. Epicardial fat volume was quantified on pre-procedural non-contrast cardiac CT using automated segmentation with predefined attenuation thresholds (− 30 to − 190 HU). Patients were followed for 6 months after a 3-month blanking period. AF recurrence was defined as any documented atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia lasting ≥ 30 s. Clinical, echocardiographic, laboratory, and procedural variables were compared between patients with and without recurrence. Univariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to identify predictors of recurrence. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed including age, body mass index, and epicardial fat volume.ResultsDuring follow-up, arrhythmia recurrence occurred in 14 patients (23.0%). Patients with recurrence were older and had higher rates of hypertension and coronary artery disease. Epicardial fat volume was significantly higher in patients with recurrence compared with those without recurrence (333.5 ± 82.1 vs. 201.2 ± 66.8 cm³, p < 0.001). In univariate logistic regression analysis, epicardial fat volume was a strong predictor of recurrence (OR 1.02 per cm³, 95% CI 1.01–1.04, p < 0.001), along with age, body mass index, left atrial diameter, coronary artery disease, and lower hemoglobin levels. ROC analysis demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability of epicardial fat volume for predicting recurrence (AUC = 0.891), outperforming body mass index (AUC = 0.836). No major procedural complications were observed.ConclusionIncreased CT-derived epicardial fat volume is strongly associated with early atrial arrhythmia recurrence following cryoballoon ablation for paroxysmal AF. Epicardial fat volume demonstrated excellent predictive performance and may provide incremental value beyond conventional clinical and echocardiographic parameters. Pre-procedural assessment of epicardial fat volume may help identify patients at higher risk for recurrence and guide individualized ablation strategies and follow-up.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia and represents a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 study, more than 59 million individuals are affected by AF, with a steadily increasing prevalence over the past decade [2]. Despite advances in treatment, AF management remains challenging due to its heterogeneous pathophysiology and variable clinical course. Clinically, AF is classified as paroxysmal, persistent, long-standing persistent, or permanent based on episode duration and response to therapy [3].

Current AF management strategies include rate control and rhythm control approaches. Rhythm control can be achieved using antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) or catheter ablation. However, AAD therapy is often limited by modest efficacy, adverse effects, and poor long-term tolerability. Consequently, contemporary guidelines recommend catheter ablation as a Class I indication for rhythm control in patients with symptomatic paroxysmal AF, aiming to maintain sinus rhythm and prevent disease progression [4].

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is the cornerstone of catheter ablation for paroxysmal AF and can be performed using point-by-point radiofrequency ablation, cryoballoon ablation (CBA), or the recently introduced pulse-field ablation [5]. Among these modalities, CBA has gained widespread adoption due to its procedural simplicity, shorter procedure times, and reproducible lesion formation [6–8]. Using cryothermal energy generated by nitrous oxide cooling to approximately − 60 °C, CBA produces circumferential lesions around the pulmonary vein antra. The efficacy and safety of CBA in paroxysmal AF have been well established in multiple randomized trials and real-world studies [6, 9, 10].

Epicardial fat (EF) is a metabolically active visceral adipose tissue located between the visceral pericardium and the myocardium. It is predominantly distributed along the atrioventricular and interventricular grooves and around the right ventricle [11]. EF should be distinguished from paracardial fat, which lies outside the parietal pericardium, and from pericardial fat, which includes both compartments [12]. Accumulating evidence suggests that EF plays a role in atrial remodeling through local inflammatory, fibrotic, and autonomic mechanisms, thereby contributing to AF initiation and recurrence [13, 14]. Epicardial fat volume can be assessed using transthoracic echocardiography, computed tomography (CT), or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. Although various thresholds have been proposed, epicardial fat thickness > 5 mm or volume > 125 mL have been suggested to be associated with increased cardiovascular risk [15].

Given the potential role of EF as a local arrhythmogenic substrate, this study aimed to evaluate the association between CT-derived epicardial fat volume and early post-procedural outcomes in patients undergoing cryoballoon ablation for paroxysmal AF.

Materials and methods

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 61 consecutive patients who underwent cryoballoon ablation for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation at the Celal Bayar University Cardiology Department between September 2022 and October 2024. Clinical characteristics, procedural data, and follow-up information were obtained from the institutional electronic medical record system.

Patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent CBA for paroxysmal AF were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included persistent or permanent AF, moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease, metabolic or systemic conditions known to precipitate AF (e.g., hyperthyroidism or severe anemia), use of medications affecting atrial electrophysiology, presence of a common pulmonary vein ostium on thoracic CT, failure to achieve complete pulmonary vein isolation, or incomplete clinical or imaging data.

Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Cryoballoon ablation procedure

All procedures were performed under deep sedation using a third-generation Arctic Front Advance cryoballoon catheter (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Following transseptal puncture, a FlexCath Advance steerable sheath (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was positioned in the left atrium.

Ablation was initiated at the left superior pulmonary vein and continued in a clockwise fashion, concluding with the right superior pulmonary vein. Cryoapplications were delivered for 240 s to the left pulmonary veins and 180 s to the right pulmonary veins. If a temperature of − 40 °C was not achieved within the first 60 s, an additional application was performed. Applications were terminated early if the balloon temperature dropped below − 55 °C.

During ablation of the right-sided pulmonary veins, continuous phrenic nerve pacing was performed from the junction of the superior vena cava and right subclavian vein using a decapolar catheter (WEBSTER™, Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA), with close monitoring of diaphragmatic excursion. All procedures were performed in sinus rhythm; electrical cardioversion was applied if AF occurred during the procedure.

Procedural success was defined as complete electrical isolation of all pulmonary veins, confirmed by demonstration of both entrance and exit block in each vein using a circular mapping catheter at the end of the procedure [16].

Computed tomography and epicardial fat volume assessment

Pre-procedural coronary computed tomography angiography was performed using a dual-source multidetector CT scanner (Somatom Drive, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) to evaluate pulmonary vein anatomy. For the assessment of epicardial fat volume, non-contrast cardiac CT images were acquired during end-inspiratory breath-hold with a slice thickness of 3 mm as part of the standard calcium scoring protocol. The upper anatomical boundary was defined at the level of the pulmonary artery bifurcation, and the lower boundary at the left ventricular apex and diaphragm.

All CT images were transferred to a dedicated workstation (Syngo®.Via, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) for analysis. Epicardial fat was defined as the adipose tissue located between the myocardium and the visceral layer of the pericardium. Quantitative assessment of epicardial fat volume was performed by an independent radiologist who was blinded to the clinical and procedural outcomes. Epicardial fat segmentation was performed using semi-automated software, with manual adjustment of the pericardial contours when necessary.

Epicardial fat volume was automatically segmented using predefined attenuation thresholds ranging from − 30 to − 190 Hounsfield units (HU), in accordance with commonly accepted CT-based criteria for adipose tissue identification in the literature [17, 18]. The software automatically calculated the total epicardial fat volume, which was expressed in cubic centimeters (cm³).

All pre-procedural CT scans were performed within one week prior to the ablation procedure, minimizing the potential impact of temporal changes on epicardial fat volume measurements.

Follow-up and definition of recurrence

The first three months following the ablation procedure were considered a blanking period, during which atrial arrhythmia recurrences were not classified as procedural failure.

Atrial fibrillation recurrence was defined as any documented episode of atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia lasting ≥ 30 s after the blanking period [19, 20].

Post-procedural medical therapy was applied according to institutional protocol during the blanking period. Patients without structural heart disease received Class IC antiarrhythmic drugs together with beta-blockers, whereas patients with left ventricular ejection fraction < 45% were treated with amiodarone plus beta-blockers.

All patients received standard post-procedural medical therapy, including antiarrhythmic drugs during the blanking period, according to institutional protocol.

At the 6-month follow-up, all patients underwent 12-lead electrocardiography and 24-hour Holter monitoring. Additional Holter monitoring was performed in symptomatic patients. Recurrence was assessed based on electrocardiographic documentation or Holter recordings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality and expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between patients with and without atrial fibrillation recurrence were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were less than 5.Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As most variables were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were used where appropriate. Univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify variables associated with atrial fibrillation recurrence after cryoballoon ablation. In addition, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed including clinically relevant variables (age, body mass index, and epicardial fat volume), taking into account the limited number of events to avoid model overfitting. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of epicardial fat volume and body mass index for predicting recurrence, and optimal cut-off values were determined. The optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden index. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 61 patients who underwent cryoballoon ablation for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation were included in the study. During the 6-month follow-up period, arrhythmia recurrence after the blanking period was documented in 14 patients (23.0%), while 47 patients (77.0%) remained free from recurrence.

Patients with recurrence were significantly older than those without recurrence (66.4 ± 6.1 vs. 57.8 ± 11.9 years, p = 0.012). All patients in the recurrence group were female, whereas male sex was observed only in the non-recurrence group (p < 0.001). Hypertension and coronary artery disease were significantly more frequent among patients with recurrence (both p < 0.001), while the prevalence of diabetes mellitus did not differ significantly between groups.

Left atrial diameter was significantly larger in patients with recurrence compared to those without recurrence (42.1 ± 3.4 vs. 38.7 ± 4.1 mm, p = 0.006). Body mass index was also markedly higher in the recurrence group (30.3 ± 3.5 vs. 25.5 ± 3.4 kg/m², p < 0.001). Left ventricular ejection fraction tended to be lower in patients with recurrence; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.066).

Baseline laboratory parameters, including fasting glucose, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, LDL-cholesterol, and triglyceride levels, were comparable between the two groups. Hemoglobin levels were significantly lower in patients with recurrence (11.54 ± 1.12 vs. 12.86 ± 1.64 g/dL, p = 0.007). The presence of coronary artery disease was also a strong predictor of recurrence (OR 9.25, 95% CI 2.39–35.81, p = 0.001).

The mean epicardial fat volume in the overall study population was 231.5 ± 89.6 cm³ and higher epicardial fat volume was significantly associated with AF recurrence, with each 1 cm³ increase conferring a 2% increase in recurrence risk (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.04, p < 0.001). Similarly, higher body mass index was associated with an increased risk of recurrence (OR 1.40 per kg/m², 95% CI 1.16–1.70, p = 0.001).

Left atrial diameter was also a significant predictor of recurrence (OR 1.28 per mm, 95% CI 1.06–1.55, p = 0.012), whereas higher hemoglobin levels were inversely associated with recurrence risk (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.33–0.87, p = 0.012).

Hypertension could not be reliably evaluated in regression analysis due to complete separation, as all patients with recurrence had hypertension.

A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed including age, body mass index, and epicardial fat volume. In this model, epicardial fat volume remained an independent predictor of atrial fibrillation recurrence (OR 1.024 per cm³, 95% CI 1.009–1.039, p = 0.002). Epicardial fat volume measured by computed tomography was significantly higher in patients with recurrence compared with those without recurrence (333.5 ± 82.1 vs. 201.2 ± 66.8 cm³, p < 0.001).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that epicardial fat volume was a strong predictor of early recurrence after cryoballoon ablation, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.891 (95% CI: 0.783–0.998, p < 0.001). An epicardial fat volume cut-off value of approximately 150 cm³ provided high sensitivity with acceptable specificity for predicting early recurrence. Clinically, this threshold may help identify patients at increased risk of post-ablation arrhythmia recurrence who could benefit from closer rhythm surveillance and more intensive follow-up strategies.

Body mass index also showed predictive value for recurrence (AUC = 0.836, 95% CI: 0.735–0.937, p < 0.001), although its discriminative performance was inferior to that of epicardial fat volume. ROC curves for epicardial fat volume and body mass index are presented in Fig. 2.

Regarding procedural safety, no major complications such as cardiac tamponade, stroke, atrio-esophageal fistula, or phrenic nerve palsy were observed. Minor vascular access-site complications occurred in 2 patients (3.3%), both presenting as hematoma, and were managed conservatively without the need for surgical or percutaneous intervention.

Discussion

In this retrospective study of patients undergoing cryoballoon ablation for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, increased CT-derived epicardial fat volume was associated with early arrhythmia recurrence. Patients with recurrence had higher epicardial fat volume, larger left atrial diameter, higher body mass index, and lower hemoglobin levels. Moreover, epicardial fat volume demonstrated good discriminative ability for predicting early recurrence, outperforming body mass index in ROC analysis.

Epicardial fat is increasingly recognized as an active paracrine and endocrine organ rather than a passive fat depot. It secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, and profibrotic mediators that may directly influence the adjacent atrial myocardium, promoting electrical and structural remodeling [13–15]. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated a relationship between increased epicardial or pericardial fat and the presence, burden, and severity of atrial fibrillation [13, 14]. Wong et al. reported that greater pericardial fat volume was associated not only with AF severity but also with poorer outcomes after catheter ablation, supporting the concept that epicardial adiposity contributes to arrhythmogenic substrate persistence [14].

Most data examining the impact of epicardial fat volume on ablation outcomes originate from radiofrequency ablation cohorts. However, cryoballoon ablation differs fundamentally in lesion formation, energy delivery, and tissue interaction. Despite these differences, our findings suggest that epicardial fat volume similarly influences outcomes after cryoballoon ablation. This observation aligns with emerging evidence indicating that epicardial fat volume is a predictor of recurrence irrespective of ablation modality, likely reflecting its upstream role in atrial remodeling rather than procedural factors alone [13–15].

Importantly, our study exclusively included patients with paroxysmal AF, while those with persistent or permanent AF were deliberately excluded. Persistent AF is characterized by more advanced atrial fibrosis, electrical heterogeneity, and structural remodeling, which may overshadow the contribution of epicardial fat volume to recurrence risk [19]. By restricting our cohort to paroxysmal AF, we aimed to evaluate the role of epicardial fat volume in an earlier disease stage, where modifiable risk factors and local arrhythmogenic substrates may have greater prognostic relevance. This approach enhances the internal validity of our findings but may limit extrapolation to patients with more advanced AF phenotypes.

Similarly, patients with a common pulmonary vein ostium were excluded, although this anatomical variant is not considered an absolute contraindication for cryoballoon ablation. Common ostium anatomy has been associated with altered balloon-tissue contact, variable lesion geometry, and potentially different procedural success rates [6, 21]. Excluding these patients allowed for a more homogeneous procedural population and minimized confounding related to pulmonary vein anatomy.

An additional finding of clinical relevance is the discriminative performance of epicardial fat volume in ROC analysis. An epicardial fat volume cut-off of approximately 150 cm³ identified patients at higher risk for early recurrence with relatively high sensitivity. However, this cut-off value should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size and the fact that it was derived from the same dataset, which may result in optimistic estimates. Body mass index was also associated with recurrence, although its discriminative ability was lower than that of epicardial fat volume.

Epicardial fat volume remained independently associated with recurrence even after adjustment for age and body mass index. This finding suggests that epicardial fat volume may not merely reflect general obesity but could have direct local effects on the atrial substrate contributing to arrhythmogenesis. However, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

With the recent introduction of pulse-field ablation (PFA), which selectively targets myocardial tissue while sparing surrounding structures, the interaction between epicardial fat volume and ablation efficacy warrants further investigation [5]. Given the association between high epicardial fat volume burden and recurrence after thermal ablation modalities, it is conceivable that PFA may offer advantages in patients with extensive epicardial adiposity. However, this hypothesis remains speculative and should be addressed in future comparative studies.

Finally, procedural safety in our cohort was favorable. No major complications such as cardiac tamponade, stroke, atrio-esophageal fistula, or phrenic nerve injury were observed, and minor vascular complications were successfully managed conservatively. These findings are consistent with prior reports confirming the safety profile of cryoballoon ablation in paroxysmal AF [6–9].

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective and single-center design limits causal inference and may introduce selection bias. Second, the relatively small sample size, particularly the limited number of patients with arrhythmia recurrence, restricts statistical power and precluded robust multivariable regression analysis due to issues such as complete separation for certain variables (e.g., hypertension).

Third, arrhythmia recurrence was assessed using scheduled electrocardiography and 24-hour Holter monitoring, which may underestimate asymptomatic or intermittent episodes. This may have led to underestimation of the true recurrence rate and potential outcome misclassification. Continuous rhythm monitoring was not available. Fourth, epicardial fat volume was assessed using a single CT-based method and attenuation threshold range; although this approach is consistent with widely accepted literature standards, variability across imaging protocols may affect generalizability.

Additionally, only patients with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation were included, while those with persistent AF were excluded, limiting extrapolation of the findings to other AF subtypes. Patients with common pulmonary vein ostium anatomy were also excluded, although this anatomy is not an absolute contraindication for cryoballoon ablation, potentially affecting external validity. Finally, the study focused on early outcomes, and long-term recurrence beyond six months was not evaluated.

An additional noteworthy finding was that all recurrence cases occurred in female patients. This imbalance is likely related to the small sample size and may reflect statistical instability or confounding by associated clinical variables, rather than a true sex-specific effect.

Despite these limitations, the study provides clinically relevant insights into the association between epicardial fat and early recurrence after cryoballoon ablation and supports the need for larger, prospective studies to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

In this retrospective single-center study of patients undergoing cryoballoon ablation for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, increased CT-derived epicardial fat volume was associated with early atrial arrhythmia recurrence after the blanking period. Patients with recurrence had higher epicardial fat volume, body mass index, larger left atrial diameter, and lower hemoglobin levels compared with those without recurrence.

Epicardial fat volume demonstrated good discriminatory performance for predicting early recurrence and remained independently associated with recurrence after adjustment for age and body mass index.

These findings suggest that epicardial fat may contribute to atrial remodeling and arrhythmogenesis. However, given the limited sample size and observational design, the results should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger prospective studies.
