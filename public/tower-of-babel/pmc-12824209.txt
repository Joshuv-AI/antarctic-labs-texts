A versatile bioinspired iron-based catalyst for the C–H bond and alcohol oxidations
Authors: Iraj Saberikia, Elham Safaei, Fariborz Mansouri, Babak Karimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we developed a highly efficient and versatile non-heme iron complex containing a proline-based mono(phenol)amine ligand (LPro), which was successfully immobilized within the nanospaces of SBA-15, creating a reusable biomimetic catalyst inspired by Cytochrome P450. The synthesized compounds were thoroughly characterized using a range of physicochemical techniques, including TEM, XPS, FT–IR, N2 adsorption–desorption, TGA, ICP, SEM–EDX, and CHN analysis. The bio-inspired iron catalyst was evaluated for its effectiveness in oxidizing C–H bonds and converting alcohols into high-value compounds through environmentally benign procedures. Notably, the catalyst exhibited remarkable efficacy and selectivity in the oxidation of various benzylic substrates under mild conditions, utilizing a TBHP/O2 oxidant system to yield ketones or carboxylic acids. Furthermore, the catalyst demonstrated exceptional activity in the direct oxidation of alcohols to carboxylic acids under ambient conditions. Importantly, the catalyst could be easily recovered from the reaction mixture and retained nearly all of its initial activity and structural integrity even after four consecutive cycles of reuse.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32632-4.Keywords: Biomimetic catalysis, Proline-based mono(phenol) amine, C–H bond oxidation, Alcohol oxidation, Iron complexSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science

Introduction

Iron is one of the most abundant and extensively distributed metals all over the world and iron-based catalysts have attracted enormous attention in recent years because of the availability, low cost, and low toxicity of iron1,2. Moreover, iron is a part of many biological systems such as hemoglobin and plays an important role in oxidative enzymes such as cytochrome P450 with high efficiency and selectivity under mild conditions3,4. Despite the previously mentioned advantages of enzymes for commercial use, there are some limitations such as decreased enzyme activity and stability in harsh conditions (temperature, pressure and pH). Although mimicking the enzyme active sites provides stable and more efficient catalysts for industrial processing through the coordination of suitable ligands to metal ions5–8, the appropriate design of enzyme-like catalysts is still a great challenge. Owing to the fact that the most of the non-heme proteins, such as naphthalene dioxygenase, catechol dioxygenase etc. comprise nitrogen and oxygen atoms-containing donor ligand as coordination sphere, the majority of bio-inspired model complexes of iron composed of such chemical environment, providing respected insights into the metal binding sites of the proteins and their activity. Along this line, aiming to adjust the catalytic performance of metal complexes, amine bis(phenol) ligands have received a significant attention as the coordination sphere to design of transition-metal based catalysts9–11. For example, there are several studies on the catalyst design that involve the mentioned amine bis(phenol) ligands coordinated to Fe(II) and Fe(III) as a model complex of metalloenzyme12–14.

Simulation of the C‒H bond activation, as the most common transformation in nature, is consistently one of the greatest challenges for chemists who are working on biomimetic catalysis, and either noble metal or 3d metal-based catalysts have essentially used to break or activate this inactive bond15,16. In nature, both non-heme and heme iron-based enzymes like cytochrome P-450, methane monooxygenases (MMOs), α-ketoglutarate dependent dioxygenases and Rieske oxygenase are served to achieve improved selectivity in the activation (oxidation) of inert reactants under ambient reaction conditions with higher reaction rate. As an example, cytochrome P-450, accelerates the insertion of oxygen into inactive C–C and C-H bonds via an iron-catalyzed process. To do this, as proposed in the literature, cytochrome P-450 initially activates O2 via receiving two electrons and converting to peroxo Fe(III) species. This peroxo Fe(III) step-by-step converts to a more reactive hydroperoxo Fe(III) and then to Fe(IV) oxo species. Subsequently, the Fe(IV) oxo species, as the active catalyst, reacted with the substrate, accomplishes hydrogen abstraction process and thus provided the oxidated product17,18. In the context of biomimetic catalysis, the proper design of a well-organized coordination sphere for iron core is critical to ensure that the active site of the developed biomimetic catalyst can suitably regulate the oxidation of inactive C‒H bonds. The spin situation and redox potential of the metal core, steric hinderance, as well as secondary coordination sphere interactions (such as H-bonding) of these iron-based bio-inspired catalysts play a critical role in the selectivity and efficiency of C-H bond oxidation19.

The oxidation of alcohols to carbonyl or carboxylic acid compounds is one of the most important organic reactions from both academic and industrial perspectives, as the resulting products serve as widely applicable intermediates and target molecules in the production of fine chemicals and pharmaceuticals20. This transformation has traditionally relied on stoichiometric amounts of hazardous metal oxidants, such as chromium(VI) and manganese(VII) reagents, or organic oxidants like Dess − Martin and Swern reagents, typically requiring strict reaction conditions in volatile organic solvents21–24. In light of green chemistry principles, notable progress has been made in this transformation, employing eco-friendly and recoverable transition or noble metal catalysts beside the molecular oxygen (or air), TBHP and H2O2 as the terminal safe and green oxidant25–33 While the selective oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes or ketones offers distinct advantages, the direct and selective one-pot oxidation of alcohols to carboxylic acids is particularly appealing. This is because many carboxylic acids, including acrylic and methacrylic acids, citric acid, adipic acid, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, propionic acid, maleic acid and terephthalic acid, play crucial roles in the industrial production of polymers, pharmaceuticals, and food additives34. However, achieving selectivity in the aerobic oxidation of alcohols to carboxylic acids presents challenges, as it often requires enhancing catalytic activity or fine-tuning the hydrophilic/lipophilic balance of either the catalyst’s surface or the reaction solvent35. In the context of selectively oxidizing alcohols to carboxylic acids, while there are numerous successful reports on the use of precious metal catalysts such as Au, Pd, and particularly Pt36–38, research on transition metal-based catalysts for this transformation is significantly limited and faces considerable challenges. As a result, the creation of effective transition metal-based catalytic systems for the direct and selective aerobic oxidation of alcohols to carboxylic acids would be of great interest to numerous organic chemists. As previously noted, iron, being one of the most abundant, accessible, and non-toxic transition metals, has garnered significant interest in transition metal-based catalysis. While promising reactivity has been achieved with iron-based catalysts for C-H bond oxidation reactions, the development of these systems for the direct oxidation of alcohols to carboxylic acids remains a considerable challenge, with limited reports available in this area39,40.

To address these limitations and continue our research on the development of biomimetic catalysts for significant organic transformations41–43, we designed an iron complex featuring a proline-based mono-phenol amine ligand immobilized within SBA-15 nanospaces. To optimize the coordination chemistry around the iron core, we utilized a proline amino acid and phenolic groups to create a bio-inspired catalyst. Additionally, to investigate the influence of steric and electronic properties in the secondary coordination sphere, we employed mesoporous SBA-15, incorporating a flexible 3-(triethoxysilyl)-propylamine moiety as a support. This choice is attributed to its large specific surface area, uniform pore size, and robust framework, all of which are advantageous for catalytic applications. We anticipate that the synergistic interaction between the meticulously designed ligand and the structural characteristics of mesoporous SBA-15 will significantly enhance the catalytic activity of the iron complex. This enhancement is expected to yield promising results in C-H bond oxidation reactions and the oxidation of alcohols to their corresponding carboxylic acids, representing a breakthrough in iron-catalyzed organic transformations under environmentally friendly conditions.

Experimental

Materials and physical measurements

All chemicals and solvents were obtained from comercial companies. Liquid NMR was obtained on a DMX-400 MHz Bruker Avance instrument using CDCl3 as a solvent and TMS as an internal standard. The organic composition of the modified materials was determined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermoanalysis (DTG), heating from room temperature to 800 °C under nitrogen flow using a STA 409 PC/PG analyser (Netzsch). The structure of the prepared materials was observed by transmission electron microscopy (Philips CM-200 and Titan Krios TEM) and was verified further by the nitrogen sorption analysis (Belsorp, BELMAX, Japan). The compounds were analyzed for C, H, and N using an elemental analyzer system (Elementar, Vario EL III, CHNS). The iron content of the catalyst was determind by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES). The electronic states of powders were evaluated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (using a Thermo Scientific, ESCALAB 250Xi with Mg X-ray resource). FT-IR spectra were recorded using a Bruker Vector 22 instrument after mixing the samples with KBr. Furthermore, the products were determined and analyzed using a VARIAN CP-3800 gas chromatograph equipped with a capillary column and a flame-ionization (FID) detector.

Synthesis of ligand (LPro)

The ligand (LPro) was synthesized according to literature procedure41. A solution of 2,4-dichlorophenol (3.80 g, 24.00 mmol), (S)-(-)-proline (2.76 g, 24.00 mmol), and 37% aqueous formaldehyde (1.79 mL, 24.00 mmol) was stirred and refluxed for 48 h. Upon cooling, a large quantity of beige solid was formed. The solvent was decanted, and the remaining solid residue was washed with cold methanol (white powder, 3.80 g, 54% yield). 1H NMR (400 MHz, DMSO, 298 K) δ 1.65 (m, 1H); 1.79 (m, 2H); 2.22 (m, 1H); 2.37 (dd, 1H); 2.80 (t, 1H); 3.36 (t, 1H); 3.54 (d, 1H); 4.12 (d, 1H); 7.16 ( d, 1H); 7.40 ( d, 1H). 13C NMR (101 MHz, DMSO, 298 K) δ 23.50, 29.49, 52.94, 56.04, 65.27, 120.78, 122.35, 126.66, 127.74, 128.39, 152.60, 175.54. IR (KBr, cm−1): 3419, 3022, 2883, 2826, 1624, 1576, 1465, 1412, 1294, 122, 1164, 1101, 1052, 971, 860, 727, 653. M.p.: 110 °C.

Preparation of SBA-15

The primitive SBA-15 was obtained from pluronic P123 and (EtO)4Si under acidic conditions following the reported procedure38. In a typical preparation procedure, 4 g of pluronic P123 (Aldrich, average Mw = 5800) was dissolved in a mixture containing 30 g of distilled water and 120 g aqueous HCl solution (2 M) then stirred at 35 °C for 18–20 h. Afterward, 8.5 g of (EtO)4Si (TEOS) was added to the mixture and it was stirred vigorously at 35 °C for 20 h. This mixture then aged without stirring at 80 °C for 24 h. The resulting material was filtered and washed with de-ionised water and dried at ambient temperature. Surfactant was extracted from SBA-15 channels through a soxhlet device using ethanol during 72 h.

Preparation of SBA-15@AP

SBA-15@AP was prepared using a post-synthesis grafting procedure as described in the literature41. In a conventional procedure, 0.44 mL of (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (2 mmol) was added to a suspension of SBA-15 (2 g) in 70 mL dry toluene, and the resultant mixture was refluxed for 15 h under inert conditions. The material obtained was then filtered and unreacted (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane was washed-out during a night by ethanol using Soxhlet apparatus. The material was dried at 80 °C for 12 h.

Preparation of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe

The catalyst was prepared by stirring a mixture of SBA-15@AP (1 g), [(S)-1-(3,5-dichloro-2- hydroxybenzyl) pyrrolidine-2-carboxylic acid) LPro] (1 mmol, 0.290 g) and dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (1.3 mmol, 0.270 g) in dry THF (80 mL) at room temperature for 72 h under Ar atmosphere. The obtained solid was filtered off and washed with ethanol using a Soxhlet apparatus for 18 h to remove unreacted starting materials. Then, it was dried in an oven at 80 °C overnight to provide the corresponding SBA-15@AP-LPro. The catalyst was then prepared by stirring a mixture of SBA-15@AP-LPro (1 g), FeCl3 (1 mmol, 0.025 g) and triethylamine in EtOH (50 mL) at ambient temperature for 24 h. Then, the solid was filtered, washed with various solvents such as acetone, ethanol and diethyl ether to remove any physically adsorbed iron on the surface. It was then dried in an oven at 80 °C overnight to give the corresponding catalyst (SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe) with an iron loading ca. 0.57 mmol.g−1 (based on ICP-AES).

General procedure for catalytic oxidation reactions

The catalytic performance of the SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst was evaluated for the oxidation of benzyl alcohol and the C–H oxidation of ethylbenzene. The reaction was conducted in a round-bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stirrer and an O2 balloon. The specific conditions for each model reaction are as follows: For the oxidation of benzyl alcohol: A mixture of benzyl alcohol (1 mmol), catalyst (2 mol%), H2O (0.5 mL), and TBHP (3 equiv.) was stirred at 50 °C. For the C–H oxidation of ethylbenzene: A mixture of ethylbenzene (1 mmol), catalyst (2 mol%), TBHP (3 equiv.) under solvent-free conditions was stirred at 50 °C. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature. The catalyst was separated by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with acetone, and dried for reuse. The products in the supernatant were quantified by gas chromatography (GC).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst

The preparation of the catalyst was performed according to our previously reported procedure through a simple four-step method (Fig. 1)41. Firstly, the proline-based aminophenolate ligand [LPro] was prepared according to the reported procedure41 through the Mannich reaction of 2,4-dichlorophenol, (S)-(-)-proline and formaldehyde (37% w/w). The primary SBA-15 was synthesized from pluronice P123 (Poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(propylene glycol)-block-poly(ethylene glycol)) and (EtO)4Si (Tetraethyl silicate) in acidic conditions. Next, for the synthesis of SBA-15@AP, SBA-15 was functionalized with (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane in dry toluene. Finally, the proline-based aminophenolate ligand (LPro) was attached to the amino-SBA-15 (SBA-15@AP) via an amide bond formation, followed by complexation with FeCl3 to prepare the corresponding heterogeneous iron catalyst denoted as SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe. The choice of the proline moiety and chlorine substituents in the ligand framework was based on our group’s previous systematic investigations. These studies demonstrated that this specific combination provides superior stereoelectronic properties and enhances the catalytic activity of the iron center, making it the optimal design for the current work5,6,44–46.

Schematically anchoring the iron complex of proline-based aminophenolate ligand (LPro) onto SBA-15.

In the next part, the resulting catalyst was characterized by various well-known techniques including TEM, XPS, SEM–EDX, FT-IR, ICP-AES, elemental alalysis and N2 adsorption–desorption analysis. FT-IR spectrum of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst showed characteristic peaks for Si–O-Si, Si–OH as well as C = C bonds vibrations of phenolic ring, in 800–1000 and 1600 cm−1, respectively. The appearance of a characteristic amide I band at approximately 1600–1650 cm−1, along with the observing no peak for stretching vibration of the free carboxylic acid group (typically at ~ 1710 cm−1), provided direct evidence of amide bond formation. These observations clearly confirms the successful immobilization of the iron complex derived from the aminophenolate ligand onto the nanospaces of SBA-15 (Fig. S1). Additionally, the broad peak observed at about 3400 cm−1 is possibly the result of vibrations of O − H and N − H groups of the ligand overlapped with the vibration of silanol group and adsorbed water. The amount of iron content in the SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst was determined by ICP-AES analysis and was found to be 0.57 mmol/g which confirm the incorporation of iron in our supported complex. The SEM–EDX spectrum of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe nanocatalyst has been shown in Fig. S2. The EDX distinct peaks were simultaneously observed for all elements incorporated in the developed catalyst. SEM–EDX analysis was demonstrated distinct and characteristic peak for Fe, indicating additional evidence for the successful coordination of iron to the SBA-15@AP-LPro support.

Figure 2 shows transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst from the various orientations. The highly ordered mesoporous structure, 2D-hexagonal arrangement and accessible pore system without any channel blocking can be clearly observed from the TEM images of the catalyst. These TEM images provide strong evidences for the well-retained mesostructure of the catalyst throughout the functionalization process.

TEM images of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe.

SBA-15@AP and SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe were subjected to the nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis to find their textural properties and kind of porosity. The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of the both materials showed a reversible capillary condensation/evaporation type IV curve with a sharp hysteresis loop located at the relative pressure of 0.5–0.7, which is the characteristic of mesoporous material based on the IUPAC classification (Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 3 and Table 1, the N2 adsorption − desorption isotherm and pore size distribution of the catalyst are almost similar to those of SBA-15@AP. This observation approves that the pore uniformity of the support is not affected even after the introduction of the iron complex to the channels. The Barrett − Joyner − Halenda (BJH) pore size distribution of the compounds confirmed a relatively narrow pore size distribution (Fig. 3B) with the mean pore diameters of 7.06 and 6.18 nm for SBA-15@AP and SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe, respectively. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller surface area (SBET) and total pore volume of SBA-15@AP were also found to be 660 m2/g and 0.9 cm3/g, respectively, which were decreased to 353 m2/g amd 0.87 cm3.g−1 for SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe (Table 1), indicating the fact that iron complex are successfully immobilized within the channels and did not physically or chemically absorb outside the pore system. Elemental analysis (CHN) was employed to detect nitrogen and carbon percent in the prepared compounds. As indicated in Table 1, the increase in nitrogen and carbon content after amidation compared to the aminopropyl-functionalized intermediate (SBA-15@AP) further supported the successful grafting of L-Proline via amide bond formation in the prepared catalyst.

Finally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed for the examination of chemical states and composition of elements in our developed SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst (Fig. 4). The full-range XPS survey (0–1200 eV) established the presence of distinguished peaks for iron, carbon, silica, nitrogen and oxygen elements confirming the existence of SBA-15 support and LPro ligand in the catalyst system along with the iron core. The high-resolution Fe 2p XPS spectrum exhibited two characteristic peaks: Fe 2p3/2 at ~ 711.1 eV and Fe 2p1/2 at ~ 724.8 eV, consistent with Fe3+ in an octahedral coordination environment. The presence of shake-up satellites between the main peaks further confirmed the high-spin Fe3⁺ state, typical for coordination complexes involving N/O-donor ligands which proved the successful coordination of iron to the support via the LPro ligand47,48.

Catalytic activity

Alcohol oxidation

The catalytic activity of the SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst was investigated in the alcohol oxidation reaction by choosing benzyl alcohol as a model substrate to optimize the reaction conditions (Table 2). The solvent screening study for the oxidation of benzyl alcohol was conducted using our iron-based catalyst (2 mol%) with TBHP as oxidant at 50 °C (Table 2, entries 1–5). Among the various solvents evaluated (including H2O, 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE), acetonitrile, ethanol and solvent-free conditions) water emerged as the optimal reaction medium, delivering excellent conversion and complete selectivity toward benzoic acid (Table 2, entry 1). This notable performance in aqueous medium can be rationalized by several interconnected factors: First, water effectively dissolves the hydrophilic oxidant (TBHP), ensuring its efficient diffusion to the catalytic sites. Second, although organic substrates exhibit limited solubility, the amphiphilic and mesoporous nature of the functionalized SBA-15 catalyst promotes effective contact between reactants at the water–substrate interface, consistent with reported “on-water” catalytic phenomena49. Third, the moderate reaction temperature enhances partial substrate mobility and interaction with active sites. Importantly, the heterogeneous nature of the catalyst enables facile separation and reuse, while the use of water as a green solvent significantly improves the sustainability profile of the process compared to organic solvents. These combined advantages establish water as the most suitable and environmentally benign solvent for our catalytic system.

The effect of the catalyst content and TBHP amount on the oxidation of benzyl alcohol was also investigated. The results indicate that with decreasing the amount of catalyst from 2 to 1 mol% (Table 2, entries 7) or zero mol% (Table 2, entries 8), as well as diminishing the amount of TBHP from 3 to 2 equivalent (Table 2, entries 6) either conversion and/or selectivity of the product was remarkably decreased. Also, it was found that the reaction gives moderate conversion and still good selectivity when accomplished at room temperature (Table 2, entries 9). Furthermore, control experiments were conducted to elucidate the individual contributions of each component to the catalytic performance. As summarized in Table 2 (entries 10–12), SBA-15, SBA-15@AP, and SBA-15@AP-LPro exhibited negligible catalytic activity in the oxidation of alcohols, confirming that the iron center is essential for the observed reactivity. To specifically evaluate the role of the LPro ligand, the catalytic performance of homogeneous FeCl3 was compared with that of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe under identical conditions (Table 2, entry 13). FeCl3 showed significantly lower activity and selectivity, which can be attributed to its labile coordination environment in solution, leading to unselective oxidation pathways and potential decomposition. In contrast, the well-defined N,O-chelation environment provided by the LPro ligand in SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe stabilizes high-valent iron-oxo intermediates and enhances both activity and selectivity. These results highlight the critical role of ligand-accelerated catalysis and the synergistic interplay between the iron center and the organic ligand in forming highly efficient and stable active sites.

After establishing the optimal conditions for the oxidation of benzyl alcohol to benzoic acid, the best conditions (2 mol% catalyst, TBHP (3 equiv.); as the oxidant at 50 °C in water) were chosen to examine the substrate scope in the oxidation of various alcohols in the presence of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst (Table 3). As shown in Table 3, the developed catalytic system demonstrated broad applicability across a diverse range of conjugated, aliphatic, and aromatic alcohols, efficiently converting both primary and secondary alcohols to the corresponding carboxylic acids or ketones with good to excellent conversions and selectivities. Notably, the electronic properties of substituents on aromatic alcohols significantly influenced the reaction rates. As illustrated in Table 3 (entries 1–6), primary aromatic alcohols bearing electron-donating groups (EDGs) required longer reaction times (up to 12 h), whereas those with electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) were oxidized more rapidly (6–7 h) under identical conditions using 2 mol% of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst. This trend can be attributed to the facilitated oxidation of the benzylic C–H intermediates in EWG-substituted substrates, where the stabilization of the developing positive charge in the transition state during the rate-determining hydride abstraction step is enhanced. In contrast, EDGs increase electron density at the reaction center, slightly impeding the oxidation process. 1-octanol as a primary aliphatic alcohol was also converted to the related acid under optimized conditions with satisfactory yield (Table 3 entry 7). Notably, a wide range of secondary alcohols including aliphatic, aromatic, cyclic, allylic as well as sterically hindered alcohols (adamantanol) successfully converted to their corresponding ketones in good to excellent conversions and almost quantitative selectivities (Table 3 entries 8–17).

C-H bond oxidation

Encouraged by achieving the promising results for the oxidation of alcohols to the corresponding carbonyl compounds using SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst, we turned our attention to evaluate its catalytic performance in the C-H bond activation via an oxidation process as a more challenging catalytic transformation. At first, the oxidation of ethyl benzene was chosen as a model reaction to optimize the reaction conditions. To do this, TBHP (2 equiv.) was added to a solution containing catalyst (2 mol%) and substrate under balloon-filled O2 at 50 °C. The reaction monitoring in the H2O and solvent-free condition, as the two greenest reaction medium, revealed that the best result could be achieved under solvent-free condition (Table 4, entries 1 and 2). Increasing the amount of TBHP to 3 equiv. under other same reaction conditions resulted in excellent conversion and selectivity toward acetophenone (Table 4, entry 3). Although 2 equivalents TBHP seems to be suitable for our process, particularly under solvent-free conditions, we employed 3 equivalents in the next experiments with the aim of achieving the near-quantitative yields. It was observed that the acetophenone yield significantly decreased in the lower catalyst content or in the absence of catalyst (Table 4, entries 4–5). Examining the effect of temperature demonstrated that decreasing the temperature from 50 to 25 °C reduced the reaction rate (Table 4, entry 6), thus 50 °C seems to be the desirable temperature for this transformation.

After precisely optimizing the reaction parameters, the substrate generality in the C-H bond oxidation of numerous substrates using SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst under solvent-free conditions was explored (Table 5). In general, a variety of aryl alkanes gave the corresponding aryl alkyl ketones via the C-H bond activation process in reasonable to excellent yields. For benzylic C-H bonds, all of the examined substrates were suitably oxidized and provided the corresponding ketone in moderate to high yields. 4-methoxy-1-ethylbenzene and 1-bromo-4-ethylbenzene were designated to survey the consequence of electron-donating (MeO) and electron-withdrawing (Br) group, compared to ethylbenzene, on the reaction yield. On the basis of the attained results in Table 5, entries 3 and 5, 4-methoxy-1-ethylbenzene provided higher conversion than that of 1-bromo-4-ethylbenzene, representing the accelerating effect of electron-donating group on the reaction conversion, although the selectivity of the reaction was hindered possibly for the same reason. In the case of 1-bromo-2-ethylbenzene which suffers from both negative electron-withdrawing effect of Br and its steric hindrance, the conversion of the reaction was diminished to 65% (Table 5, Entry 8). Remarkably, we detected that the developed SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst demonstrated outstanding conversion and selectivity for the C–H bond oxidation of bulky substrates such as diphenylmethane and 1,2,3,4-tetrahydronaphthalene under the optimized reaction conditions (Table 5, Entries 1 and 4) indicating the effectiveness of the present system. Notably, preliminary tests for the oxidation of unactivated alkanes, such as cyclohexane, under our standard conditions were unsuccessful. This limitation is expected, as the oxidation of strong aliphatic C–H bonds typically require specialized catalytic systems designed to generate highly reactive oxygen species, unlike the activated benzylic C–H bonds successfully oxidized in this work. Therefore, expanding the substrate scope to include unactivated alkanes represents a valuable and challenging direction for future research.

The oxidation reactions developed in this study likely proceed via a generally accepted pathway for iron-catalyzed oxidations, involving radical intermediates and high-valent iron-oxo species, as supported by the extensive literature cited17,18,40–43,50–54. The employment of both TBHP and O2 as oxidants in our catalytic system is strategically designed to power their complementary roles, enabling efficient and selective oxidation while aligning with green chemistry principles. TBHP serves as a crucial initiator, rapidly generating high-valent iron-oxo species (e.g., Fe4+ = O) through homolytic cleavage, which facilitates the initial hydrogen atom abstraction from C–H bonds with a low activation barrier. Simultaneously, O2 acts as a sustainable co-oxidant, regenerating the active Fe(III) species, thereby closing the catalytic cycle and minimizing the stoichiometric consumption of TBHP. Moreover, O2 participates in radical chain propagation by trapping carbon-centered radicals (R•) to form peroxy intermediates (ROO•), which further drive the oxidation process selectively toward the desired products. This synergistic combination not only enhances overall catalytic efficiency and selectivity by preventing over-oxidation and modulating reactive radical concentrations but also significantly reduces environmental impact by utilizing molecular oxygen from air as a primary oxidant. Thus, the dual oxidant system represents a balanced approach to achieve high activity while upholding atom economy and sustainability55,56.

Reusability and heterogeneity test of catalyst

An important feature of heterogeneous catalysts from the “Green chemistry” viewpoint is the easy separation and suitable performance during the recovery process. To prove this, after the first run in the oxidation of benzyl alcohol to benzoic acid, the catalyst was separated by a simple filtration and after rinsing with ethanol and drying at 80 °C, it was reused in the fresh reaction. Our study showed that the activity of our catalyst does not change significantly even after reusing for at least three cycles (Fig. 5). At the fourth run a detectable drop in the yield was detected which can apparently ascribed to the loss of catalyst mass during the repeated recovery by filtration. To comprehensively evaluate the structural integrity and stability of the catalyst during recycling, the recovered catalyst was subjected to a series of detailed analyses. N2 adsorption–desorption studies confirmed that the mesoporous structure remained fully preserved after multiple reaction cycles, with the isotherm type identical to that of the fresh catalyst (Fig. 3). The BET surface area (391 m2·g−1) and pore volume (0.96 cm3·g−1) of the recycled catalyst were largely retained, closely matching the values of the fresh material (Table 1). Importantly, the average pore size showed no significant change, underscoring the high hydrothermal stability of the SBA-15 support under reaction conditions. Furthermore, ICP-AES analysis revealed only a minimal decrease in iron content (< 5%), while CHN elemental analysis indicated negligible loss of organic nitrogen. These results collectively provide strong evidence that both the metal centers and the organic ligand remain firmly anchored to the support, with no substantial leaching or structural degradation. The excellent preservation of textural properties and active site composition confirms the outstanding stability and robustness of the SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst throughout the recycling process.

Recyclability of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst during oxidation of benzyl alcohol.

To confirm the heterogeneity of our system, Sheldon’s hot filtration test in the oxidation of benzyl alcohol as a model substrate was used. As shown in Fig. 6, the oxidation reaction of benzyl alcohol was proceeded smoothly with the time and got the 100% conversion using TBHP over 8 h. However, during the hot filtration test, after elimination of the catalyst at the half of the reaction time (4 h), insignificant progress in the reaction was detected after 12 h. Also, the final extracted aqueous phase of the reaction was subjected to the ICP-AES analysis to measure the amount of leached Fe into the reaction medium, which was less than the instrument’s detection limit. These observations powerfully established that our prepared catalyst works via a true heterogeneous path (or at least via a release-redeposit mechanism).

Based on the data in Table 6, the catalytic performance of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe was compared with other reported systems for both C-H bond activation and alcohol oxidation. As shown in the table, the present catalyst exhibits significantly higher or comparable Turnover Numbers (TON) and Turnover Frequencies (TOF) in both transformations compared to other catalytic systems, including homogeneous catalysts. For the oxidation of benzyl alcohol to benzoic acid (Entry 5), SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe achieved a TON of 49.5 and a TOF of 5.8 h−1, which is higher than most reported catalysts (e.g., Entries 1, 2, 4, 7, and 9) and demonstrates excellent efficiency under mild conditions. Similarly, for the C-H activation of ethyl benzene to acetophenone (Entry 10), the catalyst showed a TON of 44.2 and a TOF of 7.4 h−1, surpassing many previously reported systems (e.g., Entries 7 and 9) and confirming its superior activity in solvent-free conditions. These results highlight the exceptional catalytic efficiency of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe, making it a competitive and sustainable alternative for oxidation reactions.

The exceptional catalytic performance of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe can be rationalized by its specific structural features, which directly stem from our initial design strategy. Firstly, the well-ordered mesoporous structure of the SBA-15 support (Fig. 2) plays a critical role in substrate selectivity. The uniform pore channels act as nanoreactors, confining the reactive intermediates. This spatial constraint favors the formation of the linear benzoic acid product and likely suppresses undesirable side reactions such as esterification or oxidative coupling, which are more prevalent in open, unconfined environments. This explains the exceptionally high selectivity (100%) observed for the oxidation of benzyl alcohol to benzoic acid (Table 6, Entry 5). Secondly, the textural properties revealed by BET analysis (Table 1) provide insight into the optimized reaction kinetics. The significant decrease in specific surface area and pore volume after stepwise functionalization confirms the successful grafting of organic moieties and the iron complex inside the mesopores. While this reduces the overall surface area, it creates a unique local microenvironment around the catalytic sites. We propose that this optimized pore filling is beneficial for substrate diffusion and concentration near the active iron centers, enhancing the interaction between the catalyst and the reactants. This efficient mass transport, combined with the high dispersion of active sites, contributes to the high conversion rates observed under relatively mild conditions. On the other hand, the outstanding catalytic performance of SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe can be also attributed to the rational design of its ligand architecture. This is substantiated by two key pieces of evidence from our study. First, the superior catalytic performance (demonstrating high conversion (> 99% for alcohol oxidation, 93% for C–H activation) alongside exceptional selectivity (100% for acid, 95% for ketone) under mild conditions in compare to other systems (see Table 6), validates the effectiveness of the optimized ligand structure. Second, XPS analysis (Fig. 4) provides direct spectroscopic evidence, revealing a shift in the Fe 2p3/2 binding energy to ~ 711.2 eV, indicating a more electron-deficient iron center compared to FeCl3. This electronic environment, engineered through electron-withdrawing chloro substituents, enhances the catalyst’s oxidative capability by facilitating the formation of reactive iron-oxo intermediates. Concurrently, the proline moiety creates a well-defined organic pocket that ensures structural stability and governs reaction selectivity. Thus, the LPro ligand represents a synergistic construct where the proline backbone provides steric control while the chloro substituents enable electronic optimization, collectively resulting in the high performance documented in this work.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a proline-based mono phenol amine compound designated to develop a biomimetic ligand scaffold inspired by the coordination sphere of Cytochrome P450. To mimic the function of protein in avoiding oxo-bridged dimerization of iron centers, we have employed 3-(triethoxysilyl)-propylamine as a flexible linker between the SBA-15 support and iron complex. This flexible unit not only attached the iron complex to the channels of mesoporous SBA-15 support to increase the physical and chemical stabilities but also allowed to sufficient fluctuation of active iron centers to enhance the activity in the designed system. Considering these innovative concepts, the developed SBA-15@AP-LPro-Fe catalyst proved to be an outstanding catalyst material in both direct benzylic C-H bond oxidation as well as alcohol oxidation of varied substrates under mild and sustainable reaction conditions to give corresponding ketones or carboxylic acids. Furthermore, the catalyst offered excellent stability, robustness and suitable reusability without significant activity loss. The observed improved catalytic activity in our catalyst system might be ascribed to the well-designed ligand functionalities (e. g., chlorine as an electron-withdrawing group) which allows to adjust the redox potential of the FeIII/FeIV = O, beside its positive effect on the Lewis acidity of Fe center. On the other hand, confinement of this well-organized iron complex within the channels of mesoporous SBA-15 support not only affords the stability and improved activity of complex via a site-isolation path but also generates the recoverability of whole system.
