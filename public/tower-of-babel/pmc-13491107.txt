Physician Opt-Out and Patient Pathways to Medical Aid in Dying at an Academic Medical Center
Authors: Eliana Ward-Lev, Megan Cvitanovic, Holly K. Tabor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case series evaluates patterns of physician participation in medical aid in dying among patients with terminal illnesses in California.

Introduction

Medical aid in dying (MAID) was legalized in California in 2016. To qualify, individuals with decision-making capacity who are terminally ill must complete 2 requests to an attending physician and 1 to a consulting physician.1 Participation in MAID is optional for institutions and clinicians, who can opt out entirely or for a specific case.1,2 While studies have reported challenges finding willing physicians,3 none, to our knowledge, have examined physician participation and opt-out patterns. This analysis addresses that gap.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective case series study at Stanford Health Care (SHC) using electronic health record (EHR) and ethics consultation data following the case series reporting guidelines.4 Patients were included if they made a first request for MAID between June 2016 and June 2021 and had an attending and/or consulting physician at SHC. Preliminary analysis was completed in 2024, and further analysis, including confirmation of preliminary results, was completed in February to April 2026. Patients did not need to complete the MAID process to be included. Extracted data included demographics, terminal diagnosis, clinicians involved in MAID and overall care for the patient’s terminal illness, evidence of clinician opt-out and documented reasoning, and outcome (eg, medication ingestion).

At least 2 research team members reviewed each EHR. Discrepancies were resolved collaboratively. When possible, missing data were resolved through communication with involved physicians. This study was approved by the Stanford Institutional Review Board, and a waiver of consent and HIPAA authorization were obtained. Descriptive statistics were performed with Excel version 16.111 (Microsoft Corp).

Results

A total of 130 patients made a first request for MAID in the study period (Table). The most common diagnoses were cancer (115 patients [88.5%]) and neurologic disease (10 patients [7.7%]), with cardiopulmonary disease having the fewest diagnoses (5 patients [3.8%]).

A total of 49 physicians (39 Stanford physicians [79.6%]; 10 non-Stanford physicians [20.4%]) served as attending physicians. Most were from oncology (17 physicians [34.7%]) and primary care (16 physicians [32.7%]) (Table). Almost half of Stanford physicians served as attendings only once (18 physicians [46.2%]), and 4 Stanford physicians (10.3%) served as attendings for 6 or more patients, accounting for 37.8% of patients (49 patients). One oncologist served as an attending for 24 patients (18.5%), half of whom were not his own. Non-Stanford physicians, including 2 primary care clinicians and 8 community MAID clinicians, served 17 patients (13.1%), with 1 physician acting as an attending 7 times.

Of the 16 physicians who opted out, most were oncologists (12 physicians [75.0%]). No neurologists opted out. Of the 24 patients (18.5%) who had documentation of a physician opt-out, nearly all had cancer (23 patients [95.8%]). Reasons for opting out included not “endorsing” or not wanting to participate in MAID (9 physicians [53.6%]), being a new clinician to the patient (1 physician [6.3%]), “ethical concerns” (1 physician [6.3%]), and believing the patient was likely to live longer than 6 months (3 physicians [18.8%]).

Patients connected with an attending physician through several pathways. The most direct occurred when patients made a request to the specialist treating their terminal disease and that specialist agreed to serve as the attending. This was the case for all patients with neurologic disease. In contrast, only 52 patients with cancer (45.2%) had their primary oncologist serve as their attending physician (Figure). Instead, 48 patients with cancer (41.7%) pursued MAID with a physician with whom they had no prior relationship.

Discussion

In this single-center, retrospective case series study of patients pursuing MAID, we found (1) variability in pathways to identifying an attending physician; (2) high opt-out rates in oncology; (3) frequent participation of attendings who had no prior relationship with patients; and (4) concentration of prescribing among few physicians. To our knowledge, this provides the first detailed description of opt-out patterns and patient pathways to MAID.

These findings carry potential consequences for institutional planning and equitable access to MAID. Variability in MAID pathways could create a fragmented system, which poses challenges for institutions seeking to streamline processes. High opt-out rates require patients to find a new attending, which may delay MAID access and impose an additional burden when patients are seriously ill.5 Additionally, when patients are directed to an unfamiliar attending physician, the relationship may lack the longitudinal context to support end-of-life decision-making. Concentration of prescribing places a disproportionate time and emotional burden on a small group of physicians, often without compensation, and creates a fragile system in which the departure of high-volume attendings could disrupt access.

Our dataset likely underestimates these challenges, as it only includes patients who successfully found an attending physician and does not capture instances when opt-out was not documented. Additional limitations include the use of data from a single academic medical center, limiting generalizability, and incomplete EHR documentation. Future research should examine physician- and institutional-level factors associated with difficulty identifying MAID attendings, as well as mitigation strategies such as creation of referral pathways and support for frequent MAID prescribers.
