Extensive humerus and scapula hydatidosis: A unique case report and comprehensive review of management strategies
Authors: Sadegh Saberi, Hamed Naghizadeh, Nima Bagheri, Hana Saffar, Zeinab Mohseni Afshar, Seyyed Saeed Khabiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceBone hydatidosis is a rare manifestation of Echinococcus infection, presenting significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case report describes the successful management of an extensive hydatid disease involving the entire humerus and scapula.Case presentationA 44-year-old male presented with progressive right arm pain and swelling. Introduction

Hydatid disease, caused by Echinococcus tapeworms, primarily affects the liver and lungs in humans [1]. However, in rare cases, it can involve other tissues, including bone [2]. Bone hydatidosis, accounting for less than 4 % of symptomatic cases, presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its nonspecific presentation and complex management requirements [3].

Bone involvement in hydatid disease can occur through hematogenous spread, lymphatic circulation, or direct extension from adjacent infected tissues. Unlike soft tissue cysts, bone lesions often lack a fibrous capsule, making surgical management challenging. The spine is the most commonly affected site, followed by the pelvis and long bones [4].

Diagnosis of bone hydatidosis is frequently delayed due to its indolent nature and nonspecific radiological features, often mimicking other bone lesions [5]. Management typically involves a combination of surgical intervention and long-term antiparasitic therapy, but the optimal approach for extensive bone involvement remains debated [6].

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE guidelines [7].We present a unique case of extensive hydatid disease involving the entire humerus and scapula in a 44-year-old man. This case report aims to highlight the complexities of diagnosis and management in extensive bone hydatidosis and to share insights from our approach combining total humerus endoprosthesis, scapular curettage, and prolonged medical therapy.

Case report

A 44-year-old male livestock worker from northwest Iran presented to our orthopedic department with a complex clinical history spanning four years. The patient's initial complaint, dating back five months prior to his first visit, was progressive right arm pain and swelling. Radiographs at that time revealed multiple lytic lesions throughout the humerus with associated soft tissue density in the mid-arm region (Figs. 1A and B). Following multidisciplinary consultation, a comprehensive treatment strategy was formulated. The patient was initiated on preoperative Albendazole (400 mg BID) for six weeks, followed by surgical intervention.

The intraoperative technique involved an extended anterolateral approach in the arm area, between the deltoid and pectoralis major, to access the head of the humerus and the shoulder joint in the proximal area, and the brachialis was approached to the humerus bone in the distal area. The neurovascular area of the arm was meticulously protected during the operation, and precautions were taken to avoid leakage of bone and cyst secretions. Due to the size of the lesion, the soft tissue cyst was initially entered with a large gauge needle, and after injecting diluted povidone‑iodine, suction was performed. (Fig. 4-A) We performed this irrigation and debridement similarly, with a window in the proximal part of the humerus(Fig. 4-B). There was no pathology within the shoulder joint, and the humerus head and glenoid cavity were healthy. To approach the scapula region, an oval-shaped window was opened on the anterior wall of the glenoid neck. The scapula bone was entered for irrigation with diluted povidone‑iodine and extensive curettage, followed by the placement of orthopedic cement inside the cavity.Fig. 4The intraoperative photograph depicts (A) the injection of diluted povidone‑iodine into the cystic tissue, as well as (B) the utilization of a proximal humerus bone window for the administration of antiscolex.Fig. 4

A complete humerus resection and a MUTARS total humerus prosthesis were implanted. The patient's wound was closed after hemostasis, irrigation, and debridement (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). Postoperatively, the arm was immobilized in a sling for two weeks, followed by initiation of pendulum exercises. Albendazole therapy (400 mg BID) was continued for an additional three months. The patient reported significant pain reduction and improved functional capacity at a three-year follow-up. The shoulder range of motion improved to 60° abduction and 70° forward elevation, though a slight anterior shoulder protrusion was noted (Fig. 7). Despite recommendations for further surgical intervention to address residual motion limitations, the patient declined, expressing satisfaction with his current functional status. This decision underscores the importance of balancing aggressive treatment with patient preferences and quality-of-life considerations in managing such complex cases.

Discussion

Hydatid disease, caused by Echinococcus species (primarily E. granulosus), is a parasitic infection typically manifests as cystic lesions in the liver and lungs. However, in rare instances, it can affect other tissues, including bone [8]. Bone involvement, accounting for less than 4 % of symptomatic cases, presents unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges that warrant careful consideration [9]. Bone hydatidosis, also known as echinococcal osteomyelitis, results from the spread of Echinococcus granulosus larvae to osseous structures. The spine is most commonly affected, followed by the pelvis, long bones, skull, and ribs [10]. The parasite can reach bones through various routes: hematogenous spread [11], lymphatic circulation [12], or direct extension from adjacent infected tissues [13]. Notably, bone lesions can occur without concomitant lung or liver involvement [14], which can further complicate diagnosis.

In our case, the extensive involvement of the humerus and scapula likely resulted from hematogenous or lymphatic spread or potentially direct extension from nearby soft tissues. The unaffected glenohumeral joint suggests independent involvement of the two bones rather than contiguous spread, highlighting the unpredictable nature of this disease's dissemination.

Diagnosis of bone hydatidosis is often challenging due to its nonspecific presentation and imaging features. While highly sensitive and specific for hepatic hydatid cysts (80–100 % and 88–96 %, respectively), serological tests lose efficacy in extrahepatic involvement [15]. False-negative results are common in isolated bone hydatidosis, as these lesions are typically primary and not associated with visceral involvement [[16], [17], [18]]. This underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in endemic areas, even in the face of negative serological results.

The dense nature of bone tissue prevents the formation of large, spherical cysts with fibrous capsules characteristic of hepatic and pulmonary hydatidosis. Instead, bone lesions tend to be irregularly shaped and lack encapsulation, complicating surgical management [19]. This unique feature of bone hydatidosis necessitates a different approach to diagnosis and treatment compared to soft tissue involvement.

Radiographically, bone hydatidosis can mimic other osseous lesions, often leading to initial misdiagnosis as a tumor. The disease progression typically follows a pattern: beginning as a well-defined lytic lesion, it evolves into a cystic appearance with loculation, reactive sclerosis, and bone expansion. As the lesion grows longitudinally, it causes cortical thinning and eventual soft tissue extension. Notably, periosteal reaction and new bone formation are absent, and calcification is more common in the soft tissue component than within the bone itself [20]. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) plays a crucial role in diagnosing and assessing disease extent due to its ability to delineate the characteristic multiseptated cystic lesions permeating the medullary cavity and compromising cortical integrity [21]. In our case, MRI was instrumental in revealing the full extent of bone and soft tissue involvement, guiding our surgical planning and approach.

Management of bone hydatidosis requires a multifaceted approach combining surgical intervention and medical therapy. Standalone medical treatment is generally considered insufficient [22], emphasizing the need for a comprehensive treatment strategy. The cornerstone of successful management and recurrence prevention involves three key elements: 1) Perioperative anthelmintic therapy, 2) Wide surgical resection of the lesion, and 3) Intraoperative use of scolicidal agents.

While wide resection is preferred over intralesional curettage [23], anatomical constraints in areas such as the spine and pelvis may limit the extent of resection. This highlights the need for tailored surgical approaches based on the specific anatomical location and extent of disease.

Various scolicidal agents have been employed intraoperatively, including hypertonic saline, 5 % silver nitrate, ethanol, povidone‑iodine, and polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. The choice of a scolicidal agent can impact both the immediate success of the surgery and the long-term recurrence risk.

Our surgical approach aimed to minimize disease spread, reduce recurrence risk, and prevent anaphylaxis. We utilized diluted povidone‑iodine as a scolicidal agent, administering it through a large-gauge needle for soft tissue cysts and through strategically placed windows in the humerus and scapula for osseous lesions. This technique allowed for effective irrigation and debridement while maintaining structural integrity where possible.

Using a staged approach - first addressing the soft tissue component, then the osseous lesions - allowed for better control of potential contamination and reduced the risk of anaphylaxis. This methodical approach may be particularly beneficial in cases of extensive involvement like ours.

Reconstruction following resection presents another challenge in bone hydatidosis. Options include cement filling, allografts, or prosthetic implantation. The choice of reconstruction method depends on the extent of bone loss, the anatomical location, and the patient's functional requirements.

Given the extensive humeral involvement, we performed a wide resection of the humerus and associated soft tissue, followed by reconstruction with a total humerus prosthesis. This approach allowed for the complete removal of the affected bone while maintaining upper limb function. We opted for wide curettage and PMMA filling for the scapula to preserve its structure, as the glenoid remained uninvolved. This differential approach to the humerus and scapula demonstrates the importance of tailoring the surgical strategy to the specific characteristics of each involved bone.

A comprehensive review by Steinmetz et al. reported a 17 % overall recurrence rate across all treatment modalities, with a 15 % recurrence rate even after “total” resection [29]. This highlights the importance of meticulous surgical technique and long-term follow-up. The high recurrence rate, even after apparently complete resection, underscores the insidious nature of this disease and the potential for microscopic spread beyond visible lesions.

Regarding anthelmintic therapy, some sources recommend initiating treatment three months preoperatively and continuing for an extended period postoperatively [29,38], we adopted a shorter preoperative course due to the patient's severe symptoms. Postoperatively, we administered a three-month course of Albendazole at a reduced daily dose.

Anthelmintic therapy's optimal duration and dosing in bone hydatidosis remain subject to debate. Future research should aim to establish evidence-based guidelines for medical management, considering factors such as disease extent, surgical approach, and patient-specific factors.

At five-year follow-up, our patient remains disease-free and maintains satisfactory function, although he declined further corrective surgery for prosthetic subluxation. This outcome demonstrates that even in cases of extensive involvement, a combination of aggressive surgical management and appropriate medical therapy can lead to favorable long-term results.

The patient's decision to forego additional corrective surgery highlights the importance of balancing optimal medical outcomes with patient preferences and quality of life considerations. This patient-centered approach is crucial in managing chronic conditions like bone hydatidosis, where treatment can be prolonged and potentially involve multiple interventions.

In conclusion, bone hydatidosis presents a significant clinical challenge requiring a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach. Wide resection, when feasible, combined with appropriate reconstruction techniques such as tumor prostheses, has shown promising outcomes. Perioperative anthelmintic therapy and intraoperative scolicidal measures are crucial adjuncts to surgical management.

The case presented here demonstrates that a carefully planned and executed treatment strategy can lead to successful outcomes even with extensive involvement of multiple bones. However, the high recurrence rates reported in the literature underscore the need for long-term follow-up and continued research into more effective treatment modalities.

Future research should focus on optimizing surgical techniques, improving medical therapies, and developing novel approaches to both diagnosis and treatment. By advancing our understanding and managing this rare but complex disease, we can continue improving outcomes and quality of life for patients affected by bone hydatidosis.
