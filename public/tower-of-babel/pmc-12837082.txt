Traumatic nerve root injury due to unilateral facet fracture of the cervical spine caused by a direct external force; a case report

Background

The incidence of unilateral facet fracture of the cervical spine is approximately 10% of all cases of spinal trauma [1]. According to previous reports, the mechanism of unilateral facet fracture is usually a flexional and rotational force on the cervical spine with high energy [2]. For treatment, conservative treatment is often employed for cases without instability, and surgical intervention is considered if there is significant displacement, instability, or neurological deterioration. Herein, we report an extremely rare case of traumatic nerve root injury due to a unilateral facet fracture of the cervical spine caused by a direct external force.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old Japanese man had been injured by a direct hit from an iron object on the right side of the posterior neck at a construction site and was transported to our hospital by ambulance. At the initial visit, a neurological examination revealed severe paresthesia of the C8 area and motor weakness of finger extension and abduction on the right side (Manual Muscle Testing (MMT) grade 1). Deep tendon reflexes were normal. Computed tomography (CT) axial imaging revealed a fracture line from the T1 transverse process to the facet joint of C7/T1 on the right side. CT sagittal reconstruction imaging revealed unilateral facet fracture of T1 and foraminal stenosis of C7/T1 on the right side due to the T1 upper facet bony fragment (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a slight hematoma in the posterior soft tissue; however, no injury to the C7/T1 intervertebral disc, supraspinous or interspinous ligaments, or spinal cord was observed (Fig. 2).

We diagnosed the patient with a C8 nerve root injury on the right side due to a unilateral facet fracture of C7/T1 on the right side caused by a direct external force. In the present case, we diagnosed no rotational instability because the cause of the injury was a direct external force. After a thorough discussion of the treatment options, the patient chose conservative management over surgery. Thus, the patient was treated conservatively with Philadelphia collar immobilization for 3 months. Short-term follow-up imaging was done with X-rays to confirm the absence of dislocation, as there was no initial evidence suggesting instability. His paresthesia in the C8 area on the right side disappeared 1 month after the injury. In addition, his motor weakness of finger extension and abduction gradually improved within 2 months after the injury. An X-ray image revealed no progression of the C7/T1 facet dislocation (Fig. 3A). At the final follow-up 1 year after the injury, his paresthesia or motor weakness was ultimately improved, and CT revealed a complete union of the facet bony fragment (Fig. 3BC).

Discussion and conclusion

Unilateral facet fractures of the cervical spine account for approximately 10% of all spinal injuries [1]. Previous studies have indicated that this type of fracture typically occurs due to a high-energy mechanism involving flexion and rotational forces applied to the cervical spine [2]. In addition, unilateral facet fracture is sometimes associated with intervertebral disc or ligamentous injuries, leading to instability at the facet joint [3]. A previous report has warned that even minimally displaced unilateral facet fractures can progress to facet dislocation, resulting in severe neurological impairment [4]. By contrast, the present case is extremely rare, as the injury mechanism involved a direct external force rather than a flexion-rotation injury. MRI findings revealed no intervertebral disc or interspinous ligament injury. For the treatment of unilateral facet fracture, whether surgical or conservative treatment should be chosen is controversial. However, recent reports suggest that surgical treatment yields better outcomes than conservative treatment [1, 5]. Previous studies have identified several risk factors for the failure of conservative treatment, including the presence of neurological symptoms, intervertebral disc or ligamentous injuries, vertebral artery injury, bony fragments measuring ≥ 1 cm or occupying ≥ 40% of the lateral mass height on CT, and severe obesity (body weight exceeding 100 kg) [6–8]. In the present case, although the patient presented paralysis indicative of nerve root injury at the initial visit, MRI revealed no intervertebral disc or posterior ligamentous injury, and CT confirmed that the size of the bony fragment was less than 1 cm. Given that the injury was caused by direct external trauma rather than a rotational mechanism, conservative treatment was chosen, ultimately leading to a favorable clinical outcome.

While spinal cord injuries are more commonly associated with these fractures, nerve root injuries in the context of unilateral facet fractures are less well-documented. Most studies focus on spinal cord injuries, which are more commonly associated with these fractures. We have referred to studies on spinal cord injury outcomes, as they provide valuable insights into managing neurological deficits in similar cases [4]. However, the exact outcomes for nerve root injuries remain less well-defined in the literature, highlighting the need for further research in this area.

Moreover, the potential for late presentations of complications, such as instability or progression to dislocation, should also be considered. While initial treatment may seem successful, complications can emerge at a later stage, requiring surgical treatment. In the present case, the patient did not show signs of instability or dislocation during follow-up. However, careful long-term monitoring with X-ray observations was conducted to ensure no delayed complications occurred, especially considering the presence of nerve root involvement. Regular follow-up is essential for detecting any potential late complications, particularly in patients at risk for instability.

In this case, the patient responded well to conservative treatment, and no neurological deterioration was observed during follow-up. As there was no significant deterioration in neurological function, surgical intervention was not required. In general, if there is no significant improvement in neurological function within 4–6 weeks of conservative treatment, or if neurological deterioration is observed, surgical intervention may be considered. However, in this case, surgical intervention was not required due to the favorable response to conservative treatment.

In conclusion, the present case suggests that conservative treatment can be a viable option in managing unilateral facet fractures of the cervical spine, even in the presence of nerve root injury, provided there is no rotational instability and no intervertebral disc or posterior ligamentous injury. However, careful patient selection and long-term follow-up are essential. It is important to note that this report involves a single case, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. While conservative treatment proved effective in this instance, the generalizability of these results is limited by the small sample size. Larger cohort studies are necessary to establish statistical significance and confirm whether conservative treatment is consistently effective, particularly in patients with nerve root involvement.
