CanDo (Canadian Donor Milk) randomised controlled trial: pasteurised human donor milk supplementation in the well-baby unit – protocol
Authors: Maryam Razaghi, Deborah L O’Connor, Jyotsna Shah, Tom Leibson, Kim Dart, Nicole Da Silva, Erica O’born, Alex Kiss, Debbie Stone, Sharon Unger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMother’s milk is the gold standard for feeding newborns. Despite lactation support while in hospital, supplementation rates remain high in Canadian well-baby units at 35–50%. When supplementation is needed, the choice between formula milk and pasteurised human donor milk (donor milk) remains uncertain with a lack of clinical trials to inform this practice. This study aims to compare the effect of supplementing mother’s milk with donor milk versus formula in infants at higher risk for supplementation (infants of diabetic mothers, infants born small for gestational age or with a birth weight less than 2.5 kg and late preterm infants born between 350/7 and 366/7 weeks gestation).Methods and analysisThis is an ongoing, open-label, single-centre, randomised controlled trial conducted at Mount Sinai Hospital, Toronto, Canada. A total of 112 infants (56 per group) will be randomised to receive donor milk or infant formula as a supplement to mother’s milk during their initial hospital stay, when supplementation is deemed necessary by the family and/or healthcare team. The primary outcome is exclusive human milk feeding at 4 months of age. Secondary outcomes include any or exclusive human milk feeding at 1, 2 and 3 months; infant growth and health indicators and breastfeeding self-efficacy. Exploratory outcomes encompass infant temperament; parental mental health (assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory and Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale); milk cortisol concentrations; and informal milk sharing comparing donor milk and formula supplementation. Follow-up includes monthly telephone assessments and a virtual or in-person visit at 4 months post partum. Data will be analysed using intention-to-treat principles.Ethics and disseminationThe CanDo trial has received ethics approval from the Mount Sinai Hospital Research Ethics Board and the University of Toronto. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conference presentations and stakeholder engagement with hospital and public health decision-makers. Findings will address a critical evidence gap regarding the use of donor milk supplementation in well-baby units and may inform future clinical practice and policy in newborn feeding.Trial registration numberNCT06315127.Keywords: PAEDIATRICS, Clinical Trial, Infant

Introduction

The recommended food for newborns is mother’s milk, offering numerous health benefits for both infant and mother.1 2 The WHO and Health Canada recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months for all infants.3 4 The WHO goes on to state that if mother’s milk is unavailable or insufficient in volume, both preterm and term infants should be fed donor milk.5 The WHO has not formally graded the strength of evidence for this recommendation across all infant populations, and data remain limited outside the very low birth weight (VLBW) group. While there is emerging evidence for the use of donor milk in populations other than VLBW infants6 this gap underscores a need for further research.

Historically in Canada and other high-income countries, formula has been the predominant supplement when one is required for any infant during the postpartum hospital stay. As human milk banking has grown, the interest in and the practice of providing donor milk to infants in a well-baby unit has increased.7–11 There are more than 360 000 infants born annually in Canada12 of whom approximately 90% are admitted to well-baby units13 where typical supplementation rates are about 35–50%, particularly for high-risk infants.14 15 Data from the USA indicate that formula supplementation in early life among breastfed infants remains a common practice. In 2022, 22.9% of breastfed infants were supplemented before 2 days of age, and 33.9% were supplemented with formula by 3 months.16 In parallel, the availability and use of donor human milk in the US well-baby (Level I) hospital units has increased significantly, rising from 25.2% of hospitals in 2018 to 40.5% in 2024.17

Supplementation of mother’s milk may be either medically indicated or not. Medically indicated reasons may be categorised into maternal reasons, such as severe illness or specific medications, and infant reasons. Infants may require supplementation for such reasons as hypoglycaemia, jaundice or excessive weight loss that are unresponsive to breastfeeding and when there is insufficient expressed colostrum for supplementation. In addition to these clinical indications, supplementation practices in the early postnatal period may also be influenced by institutional factors, including the timing of care (eg, time of day and day of week) and variation in staff practices or training, which may affect the availability of breastfeeding support and consistency in clinical decision-making. There are multiple reasons for non-medically indicated supplementation including maternal fatigue, a perception of low milk supply or parental wishes.18 19 Formula supplementation is associated with a reduction in exclusive human milk feeding later in infancy as early introduction of formula within the first days after birth is linked to significantly shorter breastfeeding duration and earlier cessation across populations14 20 21 while the use of donor human milk caries elevated financial cost and inequitable distribution within Canada and globally.22 23

Current evidence and knowledge gaps

There is emerging evidence of impact of using donor milk versus formula as a supplement during initial hospitalisation on adherence to breastfeeding guidelines post-discharge. A recent systematic review identified 11 studies involving more than 10 000 moderate, late preterm and early-term infants who required supplementation when their mother’s own milk was insufficient.6 Only two were randomised controlled trials (RCTs). The first was an early RCT by Saarinen et al24 that compared donor milk and infant formula primarily in relation to cow’s milk allergy risk. It did not assess breastfeeding continuation or growth outcomes, and findings were not directly applicable to supplementation practices in well-baby units. The second, more recent pilot RCT by Pithia et al,25 reported unclear effects on breastfeeding and short-term growth outcomes and was underpowered (n=32) to detect clinically meaningful differences. The remaining nine studies were observational, comprising mainly retrospective cohort designs11 26–32 and one cross-sectional population-level study.33 Overall evidence quality was low due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcome definitions, lack of blinding, reliance on historical comparisons and limited control for confounders. Some cohort studies suggested that supplementation with donor human milk may improve exclusive breastfeeding rates at hospital discharge,26–28 while others reported no difference compared with infant formula.11 33 Across observational studies, findings related to growth, hypoglycaemia, morbidity and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission were inconsistent.29–32 Importantly, no study reported adverse health effects associated with donor milk use in this population.34 Because most available data come from retrospective cohorts or cross-sectional reports, and the two existing randomised trials were either small or methodologically limited, the current literature does not establish superiority of donor milk over infant formula when a supplement is required for a breastfed infant. Limited evidence suggests a potential improvement in exclusive breastfeeding at 4–6 months with donor milk supplementation with no clear difference at earlier time points; although the findings are inconsistent and the certainty remains very low. In view of limited high-quality evidence, we have launched a RCT evaluating the impact of donor human milk compared with infant formula as a supplement to mother’s milk in well-baby units, with the aim of contributing meaningful data to support clinical practice.

Hypothesis and objectives

Hypothesis

It is hypothesised that when infants admitted to a well-baby unit require supplemental feeding, the receipt of donor milk during the initial hospitalisation will enhance parents’ perceived value of human milk feeding and thereby increase both the duration and exclusivity of human milk feeding after hospital discharge.

Primary research question

When an infant admitted to the well-baby unit requires supplemental feeds, does the provision of donor milk, compared with formula supplementation, improve exclusive human milk feeding rates at 4 months post partum?

Secondary research questions

When an infant admitted to the well-baby unit requires supplemental feeds, does the provision of donor milk, compared with formula supplementation, affect exclusive or any human milk feeding rates at 1, 2 and 3 months post partum, length of initial hospital stay, infant health outcomes, growth parameters and scores on the breastfeeding self-efficacy scale?

Exploratory research questions

When an infant admitted to the well-baby unit requires supplemental feeds, does the provision of donor milk, compared with formula supplementation, affect infant temperament, parental mental health, milk cortisol concentrations or engagement in informal milk sharing following hospital discharge?

Methods and analysis

In this paper, the term human milk encompasses both mother’s milk and donor milk. We use mother’s milk in alignment with the literature35 with the intention that this term be inclusive of all parents who provide their own milk for their infants. Donor milk refers specifically to pasteurised human donor milk obtained from an accredited milk bank.

Exclusive breastfeeding is defined as the provision of only human milk, whether directly from the breast or expressed mother’s milk, without any additional fluids (including water) or solids, except for vitamins, minerals and medicines (including rehydration solutions).

Exclusive human milk feeding is defined as the provision of only human milk, whether directly from the breast or expressed mother’s milk, or donor milk, without any additional fluids or solids, except for vitamins, minerals and medicines (including rehydration solutions).

Study design and setting

The CanDo trial is a pragmatic, open label, RCT with two arms (donor milk vs formula supplement). The intervention lasts for the duration of the postpartum hospitalisation, and the primary outcome, exclusive human milk feeding rate, is assessed at 4 months post partum via either an in-person or virtual visit, based on family preference and to maximise patient retention. This is an intention-to-treat study, and follow-up continues even in cases where a supplement is anticipated, and the infant is randomised but does not receive it. The trial is being conducted at Mount Sinai Hospital in Toronto, Canada, which has over 6000 annual admissions to the well-baby unit.

Participants, eligibility criteria and consent

Families are recruited either prenatally or on admission to the well-baby unit if their infants are considered at increased risk of requiring supplementation. This population was selected based on a historical supplementation rate of approximately 80% at Mount Sinai Hospital, ensuring relevance to routine clinical practice.

In the well-baby unit, formula supplementation is the current standard of care when supplementation is required, and donor milk is not routinely available outside of specific indications in the NICU. During the consent process, parents are provided with information about both feeding options and the rationale for the study, including the current uncertainty regarding impact on breastfeeding outcomes. Participation in the study is entirely voluntary. Parental feeding preferences are discussed, and only families who are comfortable with either donor milk or formula supplementation and willing to be randomised are enrolled in the study.

Inclusion criteria: infants admitted to the well-baby unit at Mount Sinai Hospital whose parent(s) intend to feed mother’s milk and who are at increased risk for supplementation for any of the following reasons:

Randomisation and allocation

Prior to the start of the study, a unique randomisation schedule was generated by the study statistician. The computer-generated randomisation schedule is securely stored in a locked room in a sealed binder, with each participant’s allocation placed in separate envelopes. Importantly, to maintain blinding of randomisation, the study coordinator accesses the randomisation schedule one envelope at a time following participant enrolment. This approach ensures that the study coordinator cannot see whether a participant will receive donor milk or formula as a supplement until the point of assignment. The study allocation is randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio, using random blocks of 2 or 4. Furthermore, to ensure balanced allocation across key participant characteristics, the randomisation schedule includes stratification by infant’s sex and whether the infant is born to a diabetic mother or whether they are either SGA or with a birth weight <2.5 kg or late preterm. Infants born to diabetic mothers who also meet another criterion (eg, late preterm) are randomised within the diabetic group. Following randomisation, group allocation is communicated with the family and healthcare team by the study coordinator. In the case of twins, the first baby is randomised, and the group allocation is kept the same for the second twin as multiple secondary outcomes are based on the parents.

Feeding intervention

Donor milk and formula supplementation procedures

The intervention is administered throughout the infant’s hospitalisation at any time that supplementation to mother’s milk is confirmed by the clinical team with the request being initiated by either the clinical team or the infant’s family. At the recruitment site, the typical length of stay in hospital for these patients ranges from 36 to 72 hours.

All donor milk used in this study is processed and supplied by the Rogers Hixon Ontario Human Milk Bank, located at Mount Sinai Hospital. Milk from this milk bank is pooled from multiple donors to ensure nutritional consistency and is pasteurised using the Holder method (62.5°C for 30 min), in accordance with guidelines set by the Human Milk Banking Association of North America.36 Following pasteurisation, donor milk is stored at −20°C. For the purposes of this study, donor milk is thawed and aliquoted into twelve 10 mL syringes, which are transported daily by study staff to the well-baby unit. Donor milk aliquots are stored in a dedicated refrigerator located in the medication room of the well-baby unit, specifically allocated for the CanDo trial. This setup allows ready nursing access to donor milk when it is needed, thus ensuring timely supplementation for infants in the study. A donor milk log sheet with lot identification numbers for each batch of donor milk is maintained at the dispensing refrigerator.

For infants randomised to receive formula supplementation, the nursing team provides a standard cow’s milk-based infant formula designed for term infants, with a caloric density of 20 kcal/oz. Formula handling and administration follow the local well-baby unit protocols to ensure safety and consistency.

Indications for supplemental feeding

This study follows a pragmatic approach, with the clinical team in the well-baby unit determining the need for infant feed supplementation based on the condition of the mother–infant dyad. Decisions regarding the timing and duration of supplementation are made at the bedside, reflecting routine clinical practice. Supplementation is provided during the postpartum hospitalisation whenever deemed necessary by the care team or as requested by the infant’s family following consultation with their medical care team.

For the purposes of this study, reasons for supplementation are grouped into two broad categories:

Medical indications include neonatal conditions requiring additional nutritional support, such as hypoglycaemia, hyperbilirubinaemia, excessive weight loss, poor weight gain, poor feeding or acute illness. Supplementation was also initiated in response to lactation-related challenges, including low milk supply or feeding concerns identified by clinical staff. Supplementation was always with mother’s milk first and the alternative feed (either donor milk or formula) second.

Non-medical indications encompass maternal postpartum complications (eg, postoperative pain or fatigue following caesarean delivery) and parental decisions influenced by perceived insufficient colostrum or low breastfeeding confidence. In some cases, supplementation may be initiated due to a combination of factors including parental anxiety, perceived infant hunger or reported lack of breastfeeding confidence.

General feeding guidelines, including ad libitum, on-demand mother’s milk feeding as per the parent’s plans, are in place. Management of hypoglycaemia is according to the recommendations of the Canadian Paediatric Society and in accordance with Mount Sinai Hospital’s hypoglycaemia protocol including routine glucose monitoring for all infants born <37 weeks gestation, <10th percentile birth weight for gestation, >90th percentile birth weight for gestation or those born to mothers with diabetes. The infant is provided a supplement (dextrose gel followed by supplemental feed) for any glucose <2.6 mmol/L within the first 72 hours after birth.37

Safety monitoring

Safety monitoring for infants in the donor milk group includes infant weight loss >7% in 24 hours or >10% in 48 hours, or if the infant’s blood glucose remains <2.6 mmol/L for two consecutive checks. In either of these cases, the infant exits the feeding intervention but remains eligible for continued follow-up if the parents are in agreement and subsequent supplemental feeding decisions are guided by clinical care and may include donor milk or formula depending on the clinical context.

Sample size estimation

The current exclusive breastfeeding rate in Toronto at 4 months of age is 28%.38 As there are no randomised trials in this population reporting exclusive breastfeeding rates at 4 months, our sample size assumptions were informed by cohort studies showing improved breastfeeding outcomes following the introduction of donor milk. In one retrospective review following donor milk supplementation in late preterm and term newborns (>35 weeks gestational age), the rate of exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months of age increased from 15% to 58%.27 We therefore assumed that donor milk supplementation would result in higher exclusive human milk feeding rates compared with formula supplementation and considered an increase from 28% to 56% to be a clinically significant and plausible effect size. This was used as an estimate of the expected between-group difference for sample size calculation. With 80% power and an alpha level of 0.05, we require 47 infants in each of the two study arms. We anticipate, based on our prior work, a 15% loss to follow-up to 4 months of age; hence 56 infants are required for each study arm or 112 infants in total. As the estimate is based on indirect evidence the true effect size may differ; the present study will nonetheless help inform future trials in this area.

Differences in supplementation will be further discernible as this study focuses on a group that are at elevated risk for supplementation with historical data at Mount Sinai Hospital documenting an approximate supplementation rate of 80% for infants of diabetic mothers and those born SGA or at a late preterm gestation.

Data collection and security

Study measures at birth are assessed in-person during the initial hospitalisation or extracted from patient charts. Outcomes at 1, 2 and 3 months are conducted by telephone call. The 4-month visit is conducted by telephone, video call or in-person according to the parent(s)’ preference. When two parents are available, both parents are asked to separately complete the study questionnaires. The progress through the trial is outlined in figure 1. All study data, including hospital and study visit information, are collected using the REDCap platform (Research Electronic Data Capture) and are securely stored on the Mount Sinai server. Access to this data is restricted to authorised study team members to maintain confidentiality and ensure data protection.

Summary of study follow-up and timing.

Infant feeding data collection

During the birth hospitalisation, all supplemental feeds are documented, including the number of supplemental feeds per day, and the duration of supplementation until discharge. Following discharge, detailed infant feeding data are collected at each follow-up using a structured questionnaire (online supplemental appendix 1). Parents are asked to report, over the preceding 2 weeks (or since the last study contact), the average number of feeds per day and the type of feeding, including direct breastfeeding, expressed breastmilk, formula or other fluids. If breastfeeding is discontinued, the date and reason are recorded. This data allows assessment of the duration, frequency and intensity of supplementation, including early in-hospital exposure and transitions in feeding patterns over time post-discharge39 40

The outcome measurements

Primary outcome

Exclusive human milk feeding

Exclusive human milk feeding at 1, 2, 3 (secondary outcomes) and 4 months (primary outcome) postpartum is assessed by parent report of infant feeding practices using a validated questionnaire developed for this purpose41 (online supplemental appendix 1). Participants are asked whether the infant is receiving any human milk (yes/ no) and about exclusive human milk feeding, defined as the provision of only human milk with no additional fluids (including water) or solids except for vitamins/minerals and medicine (including rehydration solutions). Based on previous research, we explore human milk feeding ‘at’ each time point (within the previous 2 weeks of the interaction) as well as ‘for’ each time interval (eg, from hospital discharge to 4 months).42 The primary outcome will be analysed by comparing the proportion of infants receiving exclusive human milk feeding at 4 months post partum between the donor milk and formula groups. Feeding outcomes from 1 to 4 months will also be examined longitudinally, with time-specific analyses at 1, 2, and 3 months treated as secondary outcomes.

Other secondary outcomes

Demographic details

Socio-demographic data (education level, socioeconomic indicators, racial identity, etc) are collected from parents at the time of trial registration using a questionnaire designed for this purpose. Infant birth data (birth weight, gestational age, mode of delivery) are extracted from patient charts. The validated, 5-item Infant Feeding Intentions scale is administered to parents during the newborn hospital stay in order to quantitatively measure the intensity of the plan to exclusively feed their infants human milk for the recommended duration of 6 months.43 Socio-demographic characteristics, baseline clinical variables and Infant Feeding Intentions scores will be summarised descriptively and reported as baseline characteristics for each study group.

Breastfeeding self-efficacy (breast self-efficacy scale)

At monthly intervals post partum, we administer the validated Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale-Short Form, which consists of 14 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘not at all confident’ to ‘always confident’.44 A parent’s perception of their ability to breastfeed has been shown to predict both duration and exclusivity of breastfeeding.45 Breastfeeding self-efficacy scores will be compared between the donor milk and formula groups at each time point, and changes over time will be explored.

Infant growth and overall health

Birth and daily infant weight are abstracted from the patient chart, along with all glucose measurement results and unanticipated transfers to the NICU. As donor milk in preterm infants has been linked to slower weight gain,46 unanticipated excessive weight loss is an important safety signal for the use of donor milk. Anthropometric measures including weight, length and head circumference are measured at birth and monthly until study completion (4 months of age) using standardised procedures including length boards and non-stretchable tape measures for in-person visits. For virtual visits, parents are provided with tape measures as well as information from the Hospital for Sick Children’s ‘About Kids Health’ website on how to measure their infant’s weight and length at home.47 This information is available in print format in multiple languages as well as in audio and video files. These instructions have already been successfully implemented in virtual clinics at The Hospital for Sick Children. Additional information is provided on measuring the head circumference, which is repeated during virtual visits. In the event that there is an observed decline in growth z-score aligned with WHO growth standards during any of the follow-up visits (at 1, 2, 3 and 4 months old), recommendations are provided for the family to see their healthcare provider for repeated measurement and consultation as appropriate.

At each monthly data collection point, parents are asked to report on a variety of health-related indicators for their infant that are aligned with exclusive breastfeeding. These include any re-hospitalisations, visits to physicians (virtual or in-person), emergency rooms, walk-in clinics or allied health professionals (including lactation consultation) and prescription and non-prescription medication use.

Growth will be assessed using mean z-scores based on WHO growth standards, with Fenton growth charts applied for late preterm infants where appropriate, and health outcomes will be evaluated using healthcare utilisation measures, including hospital readmissions, emergency department visits, walk-in clinic visits, other primary care visits and medication use.

Exploratory outcomes

Infant temperament (Infant Behaviour Questionnaire)

Infant temperament is assessed at monthly intervals with the Infant Behaviour Questionnaire - Revised, Very Short Form, which is a survey of 37 items spanning three broad scales including negative emotionality, positive affectivity and orienting/regulatory capacity.48 Our hypothesis is that infant temperament can reciprocally impact human milk feeding outcomes (eg, a baby with colic may be more likely to receive formula supplementation whereas the use of formula may also impact the baby’s temperament). Infant temperament scores will be examined in relation to feeding outcomes and compared across study groups.

Parental stress, anxiety and depression

The relationship between donor milk and parental mental health has been highlighted as an area requiring further study.49 Two well-established self-reported assessment tools are being employed to measure parental mental health at 2 and 4 months. For families with two parents, both are asked to independently complete the same questionnaires. Anxiety is measured with State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) which has been used to assess trait (20 items) and state (20 items) anxiety. This tool has been validated in different populations including postpartum women.50 The STAI has been used to differentiate anxiety from depressive syndromes and to generally assess caregiver distress in research.51 Depression is being measured using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale which has been classically described for screening for postpartum depression in mothers using a 10-point scale.52 There is evidence for its validity in fathers53 and therefore all parents are invited to complete this screening. The measurement of these outcomes will allow us to explore how the interaction of donor milk and parental mood may impact the duration and exclusivity of human milk feeding at 4 months. Measures of anxiety and depression will be compared between groups and explored in relation to feeding outcomes.

Milk cortisol

Milk cortisol is known to be associated with maternal distress and may play an important role in programming infant response to parental stress and may further be related to infant growth.54 55 We collect human milk samples for cortisol analysis at 2 and 4 months post partum, to align with the timing of data collection on parental stress, anxiety and depression. Participants collect milk samples using kits and training provided by study personnel. The milk samples are collected in the morning, within 2 hours after waking up and preferably between 06:30 and 08:30, to account for the diurnal rise in cortisol levels during this time.56 Participants are instructed to store the samples in a freezer at −20°C, preferably in a deep freeze. Milk samples are shipped to the laboratory via courier or given directly to study personnel by participants who opt for an in-person visit at 4 months post partum. Cortisol concentrations will be quantified in batch using an ELISA assay. Cortisol concentrations will be compared between groups and examined in relation to parental mental health and infant outcomes.

Informal milk sharing

The prevalence of informal milk sharing is assessed at each postpartum time point as part of the infant feeding practices questionnaire. Specifically, we ask parents to indicate the number of times per day their infant was fed expressed human milk using a bottle, supplemental nurser or other methods, and whether the milk was expressed by the parent themselves (constitutes exclusive breastfeeding) or by someone else (constitutes informal milk sharing). The prevalence of informal milk sharing will be described and compared between groups over time.

Statistical analysis plan

The primary analysis for this trial will follow an intention-to-treat approach. Descriptive statistics will be calculated for all variables of interest. Characteristics at birth will be expressed as n (%) or mean±SD, unless otherwise noted and will be summarised descriptively by study group and stratification variables (risk condition and infant sex). Unadjusted and adjusted models will be run to compare primary, secondary and exploratory outcomes. The primary outcome of exclusive human milk feeding will be compared between groups using appropriate regression models for binary outcomes (eg, logistic regression), with adjustment for prespecified stratification factors. Secondary and exploratory outcomes will be assessed using linear and logistic regression models based on the nature of the outcome measure.

Longitudinal outcomes (eg, feeding practices, breastfeeding self-efficacy and growth measures across follow-up visits) will be analysed using mixed-effects or generalised estimating equation models to account for repeated measurements within infants, including terms for group, time and group-by-time interaction where appropriate. These approaches allow inclusion of all available data without excluding infants with intermittent missing data points.

In addition, exploratory analyses will describe outcomes among infants whose actual supplementation differs from their assigned intervention, including changes driven by clinical circumstances (eg, NICU admission requiring alternative supplementation), to better reflect how feeding decisions evolve in clinical practice.

The models will be adjusted for risk condition (being born to a diabetic mother or late preterm/low birth weight (<2.5 kg)/SGA or both) and infant sex. Additional baseline variables, such as mode of delivery and parity, may be considered in adjusted analyses if clinically relevant or if there is evidence of imbalance between groups. Sensitivity analyses may be conducted to assess the robustness of findings, including adjustment for in-hospital supplementation where appropriate.

Missing data will be handled using mixed-effects models where appropriate; if missingness exceeds 5% for key covariates or appears non-random, multiple imputation and sensitivity analyses will be considered. The p value for these analyses will be adjusted to account for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni adjustment). Data will be interpreted according to p<0.05, including after adjustment for multiple comparisons where applicable. There will be no interim analysis, all data will be analysed at the completion of all follow-up.

Study steering committee and data monitoring

The trial is overseen by a study steering committee consisting of the principal investigators, co-investigators, study coordinator, a milk bank representative and a parent advisor. This committee is responsible for decisions related to the organisation and conduct of the study and meets two times a month to review progress, address challenges and ensure that ethical and scientific standards are maintained. A separate data monitoring committee is not established because the intervention is considered minimal risk and takes place in a setting where donor milk use is standard practice. Instead, a data and safety monitoring plan is in place to protect participant safety. Under this plan, any serious adverse events, such as a donor milk recall, will be reported to the steering committee within 24 hours for immediate review and action, including withdrawal of affected infants if necessary.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public are not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research. However, a parent advisor provides input to ensure that communication of study objectives and materials are clear and relevant from a caregiver’s perspective. The advisor does not participate in data collection or analysis but offers feedback to make the research questions understandable and meaningful for families.

Ethics and dissemination

The CanDo trial has received ethics approval from the Mount Sinai Hospital Research Ethics Board (reference number: 23–0119 A) and the University of Toronto (reference number: 45450). Trial registration at ClinicalTrials.gov was completed prior to the beginning of recruitment (NCT06315127). Written informed consent is obtained by trained study personnel from a parent or guardian prior to the entry of any infant into the study. All data will be managed confidentially in accordance with institutional and national privacy standards, with access restricted to authorised study personnel. Any important changes to the study protocol, such as modifications to eligibility criteria or study procedures, are communicated promptly to the steering committee and amendments are submitted to the research ethics board. All changes are documented, dated and version-controlled to ensure transparency.

Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, scientific and clinical conferences and stakeholder engagement with hospital and public health leaders. Results will address a critical evidence gap regarding donor milk supplementation in well-baby units and may guide future clinical practice, policy and paediatric nutrition guidelines. Results are expected to influence the allocation of donor milk in Canada and internationally. Public outreach will include parent-focused platforms.

This RCT aims to study the potential health impacts of donor milk use in the well-baby unit. At present, 5 of 52 well-baby units in Ontario (our province in Canada) offer donor milk supplementation for the populations in this study. It is anticipated that positive results from this trial could be used to advocate for greater equity in accessing donor milk in all well-baby units. On the other hand, null or negative trial results may call into question the use of donor milk for this population of infants and may suggest redirection of funds to other programmes within a well-baby unit. Our proposed research takes a broad approach with the primary outcome, exclusive breastfeeding, being an important determinant of public health. The selected secondary and exploratory outcome variables will provide evidence for the importance of nutrition on early parent–child interactions and subsequent infant health, growth and development. The exploratory measures assess a wide range of parent–child interactions including measures of infant temperament and parent mental health. These outcomes are increasingly being recognised as critical in early infant development and family well-being and will be evaluated as potential outcome variables for future research. Human milk cortisol levels will be analysed to further study the potential biological underpinnings of the link between parental stress and infant health.

This is a pragmatic feeding trial whereby families and their healthcare teams make all supplementation decisions. This represents the everyday experience of a well-baby unit; however, we cannot eliminate the possibility of bias from the unblinded nature of this study. This lack of blinding is intentional, as awareness of the supplementation type is central to understanding how donor milk may influence parental perceptions, decision-making and experiences related to infant feeding, effects that would not be captured under blinded conditions.

There remains a possibility that knowledge of receiving donor milk or infant formula may influence exclusive breastfeeding outcomes. While this study aims to provide valuable insights into the supplementation of parental milk with donor human milk in infants at higher risk of requiring supplementation in a well-baby unit, conducting the trial at a single centre may limit the generalisability of findings to broader healthcare settings. As the study focuses on short-term outcomes up to 4 months, it serves as an initial step toward understanding the longer-term developmental and metabolic effects of donor milk compared with formula.
