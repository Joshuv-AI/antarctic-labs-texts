Beyond Troponin
Authors: Francesco Cardinali, Tommaso Recchioni, Daniele Faccenda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKey words: ablation, atrial fibrillation, electrophysiology, myocardial ischemia

Pulsed-field ablation (PFA) has rapidly entered contemporary electrophysiology as a nonthermal ablation modality for atrial fibrillation, given its selective effect on myocardial tissue and the consequent limited injury to surrounding structures.1,2 As worldwide clinical experience expands, new clinical challenges are emerging. The case reported by Pathiranage et al3 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports highlights one of them, focusing on a patient presenting with chest pain and markedly elevated high-sensitivity troponin I (hs-TnI) shortly after PFA procedure for atrial fibrillation, thereby mimicking acute coronary syndrome (ACS).3

The clinical message is timely. Troponin elevation after catheter ablation is not unexpected, and PFA should not be labeled as “biomarker silent” simply because it is nonthermal. Its mechanism relies on high-voltage electric fields that induce irreversible electroporation, resulting in myocardial lesions through disruption of cell membrane integrity while limiting damage to the surrounding structures.4 The same process that enables effective lesion formation may also result in substantial biomarker release, thus complicating the evaluation of postprocedural chest pain.

Furthermore, interpreting the degree of troponin elevation may not be straightforward. In the current case, hs-TnI was markedly elevated at presentation and then progressively declined. This peculiar kinetic pattern, combined with the absence of ischemic electrocardiographic changes, preserved left ventricular systolic function without regional wall motion abnormalities, and coronary computed tomography angiography showing no obstructive coronary artery disease, supported procedure-related myocardial injury rather than acute myocardial infarction. Nevertheless, the initial value was clinically striking and appropriately triggered an ACS work-up.

Recent biomarker studies have provided meaningful insights into this topic. Serial hs-TnI assessment has shown that PFA may produce even greater troponin release than radiofrequency ablation.5 Moreover, biomarker kinetics appear to vary according to the PFA platform and the timing of blood sampling.6 A recent head-to-head comparison of 3 PFA systems, including the Medtronic PulseSelect platform used in the reported case, revealed substantial differences in high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) release across technologies.7 However, hs-TnI and hs-cTnT values are not numerically interchangeable, and direct comparisons across assays should be avoided.

This distinction is particularly relevant here. The reported hs-TnI value of 12,221 ng/L, if interpreted as an approximately 24-hour postprocedural measurement, appears higher than the values described in the literature at similar time points for other PFA platforms. Nevertheless, the exact timing of biomarker sampling and the lack of platform-specific hs-TnI data for Medtronic PFA should temper conclusions regarding how unusual this elevation truly was.

The broader lesson is that troponin after PFA must be interpreted beyond the number. In the immediate postablation period, the absolute biomarker value alone has limited specificity. A more reliable assessment requires integration of the procedural context, time from ablation, biomarker trajectory, symptoms, electrocardiography, and imaging when clinically indicated (Figure 1). In this case, the downward troponin trajectory provided additional diagnostic value: rather than showing the progressive rise expected in the majority of ACS cases, levels were already markedly elevated at presentation and subsequently declined. Indeed, the direction of biomarker change, whether upward or downward, may ultimately prove more diagnostically informative than the absolute value itself and therefore warrants explicit consideration when evaluating postprocedural chest pain in the era of PFA.

From a practical standpoint, the challenge is to avoid 2 opposite mistakes. Chest pain after ablation should not be dismissed, especially in older patients with cardiovascular risk factors or known coronary artery disease. At the same time, routine troponin testing and reflexively equating substantial troponin elevation with myocardial infarction may lead to overdiagnosis, unnecessary invasive investigations, prolonged hospitalization, and patient anxiety. PFA may thus require clinicians to recalibrate their indications for and interpretation of postprocedural biomarker testing.

Future research should define accurate biomarker ranges after different PFA systems using standardized sampling at clinically meaningful time points, ideally including baseline, early postprocedural, 24-hour, and 48-hour measurements. Studies should also assess whether troponin release correlates with lesion burden, ablation durability, atrial substrate, or clinical outcomes. Whether the magnitude of troponin release could eventually serve as a surrogate for lesion burden or procedural efficacy represents an intriguing translational question; however, current evidence remains insufficient to support such an application, and dedicated prospective studies are warranted before any clinical inference can be drawn. Importantly, platform-specific data are needed, because “PFA” should not be treated as a single homogeneous procedure.

The case by Pathiranage et al3 is valuable not because it establishes a fixed troponin threshold after PFA, but because it highlights the evolving diagnostic landscape created by new ablation technologies. In the era of PFA, clinicians must move beyond the absolute troponin value and interpret myocardial injury through the integrated lens of mechanism, biomarker kinetics, and clinical context.
