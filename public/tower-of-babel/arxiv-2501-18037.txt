A Comprehensive Framework for Statistical Inference in Measurement System Assessment Studies
Banafsheh Lashkari and Shojaeddin Chenouri

Abstract
Measurement system analysis aims to quantify the variability in data attributable to the measurement system and evaluate its contribution to overall data variability. This paper conducts a rigorous theoretical investigation of the statistical methods used in such analyses, focusing on variance components and other critical parameters. While established techniques exist for single-variable cases, a systematic theoretical exploration of their properties has been largely overlooked. This study addresses this gap by examining estimators for variance components and other key parameters in measurement system assessment, analyzing their statistical properties, and providing new insights into their reliability, performance, and applicability.



keywords

Gauge R&R studies; measurement system analysis; variance components; signal-to-noise ratio; estimation; inference; convergence; confidence interval.

1
Introduction

Maintaining and continuously improving quality is essential across all industries, as critical decisions regarding processes and products depend heavily on the accuracy and reliability of the measurement data
(
Montgomery 2020
)
. However, measurement data often deviate from the true values due to a range of factors, including the limitations of the measurement devices, operator proficiency, environmental conditions, inherent item variability, and fluctuations across cycles, days, or machines
(
Wheeler and Lyday 1989
;
Taver 1995
)
. As such, a comprehensive understanding of these error sources is essential to ensure that decisions based on the data are sound and reliable
(
Smith, McCrary, and Callahan 2007
)
.

All components involved in the process of data collection–including instruments, operators, methods, software, environmental conditions, and the broader measurement environment–constitute the measurement system
(
Automotive Industry Action Group 1995
;
Montgomery 2020
)
. A measurement system analysis serves to quantify the variability in measurement data attributable to these components
(
Montgomery and Runger 1993a
)
. The primary objectives of this analysis are to assess the measurement system’s contribution to overall data variability, identify specific sources of variability within the measurement system, and assess its suitability for the intended application
(
Montgomery and Runger 1993b
;
Burdick, Borror, and Montgomery 2003
;
Majeske 2012
)
.

The standard approach to assessing a measurement system
(
Montgomery and Runger 1993a
;
Burdick, Borror, and Montgomery 2003
)
involves randomly selecting a sample of units from the process and having each operator, or more broadly, each measurement system, perform repeated measurements on these units. Here, a
unit
refers to the object or item being measured. Data from this experiment are typically analyzed using a two-way model, which decomposes the measurement error into two primary components: the systematic error attributed to operator differences (reproducibility) and the error arising when the same measurement system is used multiple times over a short time period (repeatability).

Measurement systems that are unaffected by operator bias or human factors such as skill, knowledge, or attitude are commonly referred to as measurement systems with no operator effect. These systems are typically automated, relying on machine-controlled processes like automated gauges or scanners to conduct measurements. Such systems are particularly important in industries like smart manufacturing
(
Suriano et al. 2015
;
Yang et al. 2021
;
Majeske 2012
)
, aerospace, and the automotive sector
(
Drouot et al. 2018
;
Browne, Mackay, and Steiner 2009
)
. By assuming the operator effect is negligible or non-existent, a simplified one-way model can be employed to capture repeatability. Notable studies in measurement system assessment that utilize one-way models include those by
Browne, Mackay, and Steiner (2009)
;
Browne, Steiner, and MacKay (2010)
;
Weaver et al. (2012)
.

The field of measurement system assessment has experienced significant methodological advancements, particularly for the evaluation of single characteristics. The guidelines and standards developed by
Automotive Industry Action Group (2003)
provide a comprehensive framework to perform measurement system analyses and interpret the results. The practical considerations involved in designing and implementing measurement system studies are examined by
Montgomery and Runger (1993a)
, while
Burdick, Borror, and Montgomery (2003)
offer a detailed review of methodologies for evaluating measurement system capability, with a specific focus on the ANOVA procedure. Furthermore, alternative plans to the standard measurement system assessment plan are proposed by
Browne, MacKay, and Steiner (2010)
;
Stevens et al. (2010)
;
Stevens et al. (2013)
.

The scope of measurement system analysis methodologies has also expanded to include setups involving non-numerical scales. For instance,
Van Wieringen and De Mast (2008)
and
Danila, Steiner, and MacKay (2010)
present frameworks for assessing measurement systems operating on binary scales, where units are classified as pass or fail.
Similarly,
Culp et al. (2018)
propose a methodology for analyzing measurement systems with ordinal measurements, while
Osthus et al. (2021)
develop an approach for assessing repeatability and reproducibility in systems using count data.

Despite the field’s significant methodological breadth, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there remains a lack of documented theoretical analysis of these methods and their properties in the literature. This paper addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive theoretical investigation of measurement system assessment studies for univariate data. Specifically, we examine statistical properties and considerations related to measurement system analysis parameters, focusing on the one-way random effects model under a balanced standard plan. While the one-way model is valued for its simplicity, our analysis uncovers complexities in certain aspects. Nonetheless, the insights gained from this investigation offer a solid foundation for extending these theoretical analyses to more complex scenarios, such as two-way analysis of variance, advanced models, and diverse data types, including multivariate and functional data. This paper, therefore, concentrates on the one-way random effects model, which often suffices for many practical measurement system assessment applications.

This paper is organized as follows: Section
2
presents the univariate one-way ANOVA model, which serves as the statistical framework for evaluating measurement system variability. Section
3
outlines key parameters for assessing measurement systems and defines the foundational notation used throughout the paper. These parameters are analyzed in greater detail in subsequent sections. Section
4
, provides a preliminary analysis of the univariate one-way ANOVA model, laying the groundwork for a deeper understanding of the measurement system. DSection
5
outlines various techniques for estimating the relevant parameters. A comprehensive examination of the statistical properties associated with these parameters, including estimator bias, sampling variance, hypothesis testing, convergence properties, and confidence intervals, is presented in Section
6
. Conclusions are drawn in Section
7
. Finally, the appendix contains detailed proofs that support the analysis of the paper.

2
The univariate one-way model

We consider a setting where an experiment is conducted with a randomly selected sample of
a>2
units drawn from a population of units. Each unit in the sample is measured
r>1
times, with the order of measurements determined randomly. See, e.g.,
(
Automotive Industry Action Group 1995
, Section 3)
. It is assumed that the measurand remains constant across repeated measurements. The model used to express the measured values is,

\displaystyle Y_{ij}=\mu+U_{i}+\epsilon_{ij},\quad\textrm{for \;}i=1,\ldots,a,\textrm{ and }\,j=1,\ldots,r,

(1)

where
Y_{ij}
is the
j
th repeated measurement for the
i
th unit,
\mu
is a general mean parameter,
U_{i}
represents the random main effect of unit
i
, and
\epsilon_{ij}
represents the random measurement error associated with the
j
th replicate measurement taken from unit
i
. All
U_{i}
’s are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{u})
random variables, and all
\epsilon_{ij}
’s are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})
random variables. Furthermore, for
i
fixed, we treat each
U_{i}
and
\epsilon_{ij}
as being mutually independent.

Under the one-way model of (
1
), while the random variables
U_{i}
’s and
\epsilon_{ij}
’s are uncorrelated, the measurements
Y_{ij}
’s are not uncorrelated for all
i
and
j
cases. Specifically, for repeated measurements
j
and
j^{\prime}
of the same unit
i
, the covariance is given by,

\displaystyle\textrm{Cov}(Y_{ij},Y_{ij^{\prime}})=\sigma^{2}_{u}\quad\textrm{for \;}j\neq j^{\prime}\,,

(2)

whereas for measurements taken from different units, the covariance is,

\displaystyle\textrm{Cov}(Y_{ij},Y_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})=0\quad\text{for \;}i\neq i^{\prime},\text{ and any\;}j,j^{\prime}.

(3)

As
Y_{ij}
’s are normally distributed random variables, the zero covariance of (
3
) signifies the independence of measurements taken from different units. In contrast, the repeated measurements made on the same unit are dependent.
In view of (
1
), the variance of a single measurement
Y_{ij}
is,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{t}=\sigma^{2}_{u}+\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,.

(4)

The variance quantities
\sigma^{2}_{u}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
which sum to the total measurement variance
\sigma^{2}_{t}
are referred to as variance components.

3
Parameters for assessing measurement system

This section reviews fundamental parameters for measurement system analysis, tailored specifically to the one-way random effects model.

The percentage R&R ratio, as defined by AIAG
(
1995
)
, is given by

\displaystyle\%\textrm{R\&R}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\sigma_{\epsilon}}{\sigma_{t}}$}}\,100\%\,.

(5)

According to the guidelines set by AIAG
(
1995
)
, a measurement system is considered acceptable if the R&R ratio is below
10\%
. For ratios between
10\%
and
30\%
, the measurement system’s acceptability will depend on contextual factors such as the importance of the application and the cost of implementing improvements. If the R&R ratio exceeds
30\%
, corrective actions to enhance the measurement system are strongly recommended.

Larsen (2002)
and
Burdick and Larsen (1997)
note the signal-to-noise ratio as,

\displaystyle\textrm{SNR}=\frac{\sigma_{u}}{\sigma_{\epsilon}}.

(6)

The AIAG
(
1995
)
and
Burdick, Borror, and Montgomery (2003)

employ a version of (
6
) scaled by
\sqrt{2}
, often referred to as the gauge discrimination ratio. This ratio assesses whether the measurement system’s resolution is adequate to effectively monitor the feature of interest. According to AIAG guidelines, an acceptable value for the gauge discrimination ratio is
5
or greater, while a value below
2
indicates that the measurement system is unsuitable for process monitoring. Steiner and MacKay
(
2005
)
propose more moderate criteria, suggesting that an SNR value exceeding
3
, as defined by (
6
), indicates a valid measurement system. Conversely, a value below
2
is considered unacceptable.

An alternative ratio to compare the quality of a measurement system is the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), defined as
(
Mader, Prins, and Lampe 1999
;
Majeske and Andrews 2002
)
,

\displaystyle\textrm{ICC}=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{u}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{u}}{\sigma^{2}_{u}+\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}.

(7)

It reflects the correlation in repeated measurements on the same subject
(
Majeske and Andrews 2002
)
, and
Donner and Eliasziw (1987)
regard the intra-class correlation coefficient as a measure of reliability. When the variability of the measurement system is negligible relative to that of the units, the ICC approaches one, indicating high reliability. Conversely, when the measurement system’s variability is substantial compared to the variability of units, the ICC decreases, signifying lower reliability.

The AIAG
(
1995
)
uses the precision-to-tolerance ratio (PTR) as an additional approval criterion. PTR is defined as the ratio of the width of the probability distribution of repeated measurements on the same unit to the tolerance width of the process. If
{UL}
and
{LL}
represent the upper and lower specification limits of the process, the PTR is expressed as

\displaystyle\textrm{PTR}=\frac{\kappa\,\sigma_{\epsilon}}{UL-LL}\,,

(8)

where
\kappa
is a scaling factor commonly set to
5.15
or
6
, corresponding to capturing
99\%
and
99.73\%
of a normal distribution with variance
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
, respectively.

The literature suggests varying thresholds for acceptable PTR values, typically ranging from
0.1
to
0.3
. For instance,
Montgomery and Runger (1993a)
recommend a PTR value of
0.1
or less for a measurement system to be considered adequate.
Mader, Prins, and Lampe (1999)
propose a maximum PTR value of
0.2
, while
Barrentine (2003)
suggests that a PTR value not exceed
0.3
. However,
Montgomery and Runger (1993a)
caution against over-relying on PTR. They argue that a measurement system’s ability to detect meaningful variability in units is more important.
Moreover, they emphasize that metrics such as %R&R and SNR provide more comprehensive insights into the measurement system’s performance than PTR alone.

Throughout this paper, the quality of the measurement system is assessed using the ratio of the two variance components,

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{u}}{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}.

(9)

The parameters introduced earlier to evaluate the measurement system—namely, the percentage R&R ratio,
SNR
, and the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC)—are related to
\rho
through

\%\textrm{R\&R}=\left(1+\rho\right)^{-1/2}\times 100\%
,
\text{SNR}=\rho^{1/2}
, and
\textrm{ICC}=1/\left(1+\rho^{-1}\right)
.

A smaller value of
\rho
suggests that measurement error is the dominant source of variation, whereas larger values of
\rho
indicate that measurement error contributes less significantly to the overall variability in the observed data. It is important to note that the recommended range for
\rho
can vary depending on the specific context. For instance, as pointed out by
Steiner and MacKay (2005)
, improving the measurement system is advisable if
\rho
falls below approximately
4
.

4
Preliminary analysis

In the context of the one-way model (
1
), the two sources of variation are the units (
U
) and the measurement errors (
\epsilon
). These sources represent the underlying factors or components that contribute to the variation observed in the measurement data (
Y
).

Two sums of squares that are the basis for the analysis of the variance components of the one-way model (
1
) are,

\displaystyle SS_{u}

\displaystyle=r\sum_{i=1}^{a}(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2},

(10)

\displaystyle SS_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}\,,

(11)

where
\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}Y_{ij}
is the mean of the measurements of the
i
th unit, and
\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}=\frac{1}{ar}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}Y_{ij}
is the overall mean of measured data. There are two means of squares,

\displaystyle{MS}_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{r}{a-1}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2},

(12)

\displaystyle MS_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a(r-1)}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}\,.

(13)

The total corrected sum of squares is defined as

\displaystyle SS_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}.

(14)

This total sum of squares can be partitioned into,

\displaystyle SS_{t}=SS_{u}+SS_{\epsilon}\,.

(15)

This classification based on the source of variation is known as the analysis of variance (ANOVA). The ANOVA classification associated with the one-way model of (
1
) is demonstrated in Table

.

With the normal distribution of

SS_{u}
and

are two independent statistics where,

\displaystyle\frac{SS_{u}}{\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{u}\right]}\sim\chi^{2}_{a-1}\,,\quad\mbox{and }\quad\frac{SS_{\epsilon}}{\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{\epsilon}\right]}\sim\chi^{2}_{a(r-1)}\,.

(16)

The expected mean of squares terms are,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{u}\right]=\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}+r\sigma^{2}_{u}\quad\mbox{and }\quad\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{\epsilon}\right]=\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,.

(17)

The distributions of
SS_{u}
and
SS_{\epsilon}
given in (
16
) lead to the result that

\displaystyle F=\frac{{MS_{u}}/{\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{u}\right]}}{{MS_{\epsilon}}/{\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{\epsilon}\right]}}={(1+r\rho)^{-1}}\frac{MS_{u}}{MS_{\epsilon}}

(18)

has a central
F
-distribution with
a-1
and
a(r-1)
degrees of freedom.

To initiate a measurement system study,
Montgomery and Runger (1993a)
recommend starting with an initial analysis to identify the factors contributing to the variation in the measured data. This can be achieved by conducting a hypothesis test for
\sigma^{2}_{u},
. Once the significance of this variance component is statistically confirmed, the variance components can then be estimated.

5
Point estimation

The estimation of variance components for the one-way model has been extensively studied, with a rich body of literature available on the topic. For a thorough review of methods in this area, readers are directed to references such as
Klotz, Milton, and Zacks (1969)
;
Sahai (1979)
;
Khuri and Sahai (1985)
;
Robinson (1987)
;
Searle, Casella, and McCulloch (1992)
. This section provides a concise overview of several established techniques for estimating variance components within the context of the one-way model defined by Equation (
1
). Specifically, we explore the ANOVA-based estimators, the uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator (UMVUE), and the maximum likelihood estimator. Additionally, we employ the commonly used plug-in estimator to derive estimates for ratios of variance components, which are integral to measurement system assessment studies.

5.1
ANOVA estimation

A common approach for estimating variance components is the ANOVA method, which is conceptually similar to the method of moments. In this procedure, each sum (or mean) of squares has an expected value that is a linear function of the variance components. The ANOVA estimation method solves these equations for
\sigma^{2}_{u}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
, substituting the expected mean squares with their observed values. Consequently, the ANOVA estimators for
\sigma^{2}_{u}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
are expressed as,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}(MS_{u}-MS_{\epsilon})\,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}=MS_{\epsilon}\,.

(19)

These estimators are unbiased. By substituting the ANOVA estimators from (
19
) into (
9
), the plug-in estimator of
\rho
is given by,

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}-1\right).

(20)

One drawback of the ANOVA estimator is its potential to yield a negative estimate for the variance component
\sigma^{2}_{u}
. Specifically, the estimator
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
, as defined in (
19
), will produce a negative value whenever
MS_{u}<MS_{\epsilon}\,
. The likelihood of this occurrence depends on the characteristics of the data. For the one-way model in (
1
), the probability of this event can be expressed as,

\displaystyle\Pr\left({MS_{u}}<{MS_{\epsilon}}\right)=\Pr\left(F<{(1+r\rho)^{-1}}\right),

(21)

where the random variable
F
follows an
F
-distribution with
a-1
and
a(r-1)
degrees of freedom.

The probability defined in (
21
) depends on the sample size
a
, the number of measurement replications
r
, and the ratio of variance components
\rho
. A lower probability of
MS_{u}<MS_{\epsilon}
is preferred, as it indicates a higher likelihood that the ANOVA estimates of variance components align with the parameter space requirements, specifically
\sigma^{2}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{0+}
, and
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, where
\mathbb{R}^{0+}
and
\mathbb{R}^{+}
denote the sets of non-negative real numbers and positive real numbers, respectively.

To better understand the likelihood of this scenario, Figure
1
illustrates the relationship between the probability of
MS_{u}<MS_{\epsilon}\,
and
\rho
across four sample sizes
(
a=5,10,20,30
)
and three values of replications (
r=2,3,6
), a total of
12
plans. As shown in Figure
1
, increasing the number of measurements, i.e.,
N=ar
, whether by increasing the sample size
a
or the number of replications
r
, reduces the probability of
MS_{u}<MS_{\epsilon}\,
. Generally, larger values of
\rho
are more advantageous as they correspond to a lower likelihood of
MS_{u}<MS_{\epsilon}\,

(
Majeske and Andrews 2002
)
.

A negative estimate of variance can be interpreted in different ways
(
Thompson 1962
)
. It may indicate that the variance component is not significantly different from zero, suggesting the possibility of simplifying the model. This interpretation can be statistically validated through a hypothesis test for the null hypothesis
H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma^{2}_{u}=0\,
.

Insufficient data is another potential cause of negative variance estimates, highlighting the need to incorporate more data. In the context of measurement system assessment, baseline (or historical) data are often readily available and can be leveraged to enhance analysis. The use of baseline data is recommended in the literature
Steiner and MacKay (2005)
, and its advantages are quantified by
Stevens et al. (2013)
.

5.2
Non-negative ANOVA estimation

When considering
\sigma^{2}_{u}=0\,
, the ANOVA estimator of
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
is calculated as follows,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}=\frac{SS_{t}}{ar-1}\,.

(22)

This expression for
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}
can also be written as
(MS_{u}\mathrm{df}_{u}+MS_{\epsilon}\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon})/\left(\mathrm{df}_{u}+\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}\right)
, which represents a pooled variance estimator combining
MS_{u}
and
MS_{\epsilon}\,
.

In cases where
MS_{u}<MS_{\epsilon}\,
, it can be demonstrated that
{SS_{t}}/({ar-1})<MS_{\epsilon}\,
. Conversely, if
MS_{\epsilon}<MS_{u}\,
, then
MS_{\epsilon}<{SS_{t}}/({ar-1})
.
Utilizing these relationships, the non-negative ANOVA estimators of variance components are expressed as follows,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}=\max\left(0,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\left(MS_{u}-MS_{\epsilon}\right)\right)\,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}=\min\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{SS_t}{ar-1}$}}\,,MS_{\epsilon}\right).

(23)

These non-negative ANOVA estimators of variance components are no longer unbiased. However, they exhibit smaller mean square errors compared to their traditional ANOVA counterparts, as noted by
Lee and Kapadia (1992)
.

The plug-in estimator of
\rho
using the non-negative estimators of variance components is expressed as,

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{NANOVA}}}=\max\left(0,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}-1\right)\right).

(24)

5.3
Uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimators

The UMVUEs of variance components have been developed for the one-way model. Let
\bm{\theta}=(\mu,\,\sigma^{2}_{u},\,\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})^{\top}
represent the vector of unknown parameters in the model (
1
). Suppose the observed data of the measurements on unit
i
are arranged as
\bm{y}_{i}=(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{ir})^{\top}
. The likelihood function of
\bm{\theta}
, as set up in
(
Searle, Casella, and McCulloch 1992
, Chapter 3)
, is expressed by

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=c(\sigma^{2}_{u}\,,\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})\exp\left\{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{-1}{2\sigma^2_\epsilon}$}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{a}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}(y_{ij}-\overline{y}_{i\cdot})^{2}}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2(\sigma^2_\epsilon+ r \sigma^2_u)}$}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{a}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}(\overline{y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}}\!-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{ar(\mu- \overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot} )^2}{2(\sigma^2_\epsilon+ r \sigma^2_u)}$}}\right\}

(25)

where

c(\sigma^{2}_{u}\,,\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}ar}(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})^{-\frac{1}{2}a(r-1)}(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}+r\sigma^{2}_{u})^{-\frac{a}{2}}\,,

and
\overline{y}_{i\cdot}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}y_{ij}
and
\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot}=\frac{1}{ar}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}y_{ij}\,
.

From the structure of the likelihood function, it can be deduced that the joint distribution of
Y_{ij}
’s belongs to the exponential family and the vector
(\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot},MS_{u},MS_{\epsilon})^{\top}
serves as a jointly sufficient and complete statistic for the parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
. By utilizing an unbiased estimator of
\bm{\theta}
, and applying the Lehmann-Scheffé theorem (1950, 1955), this estimator becomes the UMVUE for
\bm{\theta}
, depending solely on
(\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot},MS_{u},MS_{\epsilon})^{\top}
. It is noteworthy that the ANOVA estimators of variance components, as outlined in (
19
) are unbiased since
\mathrm{E}[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}]=\sigma^{2}_{u}
and
\mathrm{E}[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}]=\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
. Consequently, the UMVUEs for variance components
\sigma^{2}_{u}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
coincide with their respective ANOVA estimators
(
Graybill and Wortham 1956
)
.

5.4
Maximum likelihood estimation

The maximum likelihood estimation method is underpinned by a strong theoretical foundation. Taking logarithm of
\mathcal{L(\bm{\theta})}
, the log-likelihood function of
\bm{\theta}
is,

\begin{split}{l}(\bm{\theta})=&-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{a(r-1)}{2}$}}\ln(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{a}{2}$}}\ln(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}+r\sigma^{2}_{u})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{a}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}(y_{ij}-\overline{y}_{i\cdot})^{2}}\\
&-\frac{1}{2(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}+r\sigma^{2}_{u})}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{a}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}(\overline{y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}-\frac{ar(\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot}-\mu)^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}+r\sigma^{2}_{u})}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{ar}{2}$}}\ln(2\pi)\,.\end{split}

(26)

The maximum likelihood estimation involves maximizing the likelihood function over the parameter space.
In (
26
), the parameter
\mu
appears in the quadratic term
(\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot}-\mu)^{2}
, and its parameter space is
\mathbb{R}
. The log-likelihood function
{l}(\bm{\theta})
attains its maximum when
{\mu}=\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot}\,
.

By calculating the partial derivatives of
{l}(\bm{\theta})
with respect to
\sigma^{2}_{u}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
, and solving the resulting score equations, the maximum likelihood estimators for the variance components are obtained as,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\big(\beta^{-1}MS_{u}-MS_{\epsilon}\big)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}=MS_{\epsilon}\,,

(27)

where the scaling factor
\beta
is defined as
\beta=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{a}{a-1}$}}\,
.

The estimators in (
27
) are considered as the maximum likelihood estimators under the condition that both
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
and
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}
lie within the parameter space of
\sigma^{2}_{u}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
. The estimator
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}=MS_{\epsilon}
is inherently positive, and therefore it satisfies the parameter space requirements for
{\sigma}^{2}_{\epsilon}
. However, the estimator
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
, as defined in (
27
) remains non-negative only if the condition
{MS_{u}}\geq\beta{MS_{\epsilon}}
holds.

When
{MS_{u}}<\beta{MS_{\epsilon}}
, the log-likelihood function
{l}(\bm{\theta})
achieves its maximum at the boundary of the parameter space, leading to the following estimators,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}=0\,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}=\frac{SS_{t}}{ar}\,\cdot

(28)

n this case, the estimators provided in (
28
) serve as the maximum likelihood estimators for
{\sigma}^{2}_{u}
and
{\sigma}^{2}_{\epsilon}
, respectively
(
Searle, Casella, and McCulloch 1992
, Chapter 3)
.
By combining the relations (
27
) and (
28
), the maximum likelihood estimators for
{\sigma}^{2}_{u}
and
{\sigma}^{2}_{\epsilon}
can be expressed as follows,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}=\max\left(0\,,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\left(\beta^{-1}MS_{u}-MS_{\epsilon}\right)\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}=\min\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{SS_t}{ar}$}},MS_{\epsilon}\right).

(29)

Substituting these maximum likelihood estimators for the variance components into the equation for
\rho
, the maximum likelihood estimator for
\rho
is given by,

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}=\max\left(0\,,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\left(\beta^{-1}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}-1\right)\right).

(30)

5.5
Other estimation methods

Other approaches are available for estimating the variance components. Two notable methods include restricted maximum likelihood
(
Thompson 1962
;
Patterson and Thompson 1971
)
and the Bayesian framework.

The restricted maximum likelihood method is an extension of the maximum likelihood estimation technique. It aims to estimate model parameters by maximizing the likelihood of the observed data while incorporating constraints on these parameters. This method is particularly advantageous as it provides less biased results compared to standard maximum likelihood estimation. Under the assumption of normality for the random effects and balanced study designs, restricted maximum likelihood estimators are identical to the non-negative ANOVA estimators
(
Corbeil and Searle 1976
)
.

Within the Bayesian framework, variance components are treated as random variables with associated prior distributions. Bayesian estimation uses Bayes’ theorem to derive a conditional probability distribution, known as the posterior distribution, which integrates prior knowledge with observed data. This posterior distribution serves as the foundation for estimating the parameters of interest, often requiring numerical methods to obtain solutions
(
Searle, Casella, and McCulloch 1992
, Chapter 3)
.

Prominent examples of Bayesian estimation include the works of
Tiao and Tan (1965)
;
Klotz, Milton, and Zacks (1969)
;
Portnoy (1971)
, which investigate Bayes estimators of variance components in the context of the one-way model. Additionally, studies by
Rajagopalan and Broemeling (1983)
;
Fong, Rue, and Wakefield (2010)
, extend these methods to more general linear models. The versatility of the Bayesian approach is further demonstrated by
Weaver et al. (2012)
, who illustrate its application across a wide range of measurement system analyses.

6
Theoretical properties

The estimation of measurement system study parameters necessitates a thorough understanding of their statistical properties. In this section, we conduct an in-depth analysis of the statistical properties of variance components and their ratio within the context of a measurement system assessment study. This exploration offers valuable insights into the fundamental characteristics and overall performance of the measurement system.

6.1
The expected values and bias

In our theoretical analysis, we begin by investigating the expected value and bias associated with the estimation of variance components and their ratio. Our focus centers on conducting a detailed examination of these properties using estimators derived from the ANOVA, non-negative ANOVA, and maximum likelihood methods.

A. ANOVA estimations

The ANOVA estimators of variance components, as provided in (
19
), are unbiased since
\mathrm{E}[MS_{u}]=r\sigma^{2}_{u}+\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
and
\mathrm{E}[MS_{\epsilon}]=\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
. However, this unbiasedness does not extend directly to the estimator of
\rho
as defined by (
20
). The expected value of
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}
according to (
20
) is expressed as follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\mathrm{E}\left[\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}\right]-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}(1+r\rho)\mathrm{E}[F]-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}},

(31)

where
F
is a random variable that has a central
F
-distribution with
\mathrm{df}_{u}=a-1
and
\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}=a(r-1)
degrees of freedom. Note that the expectation of
F
is
\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}/(\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}-2)
where
\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}>2
. Subsequently, the expected value of
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}
can be expressed as,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}-2}\left(\rho\,\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{2}{r}$}}\right).

(32)

The difference between the expected value of an estimator and the true value of the parameter it estimates is defined as the bias of the estimator. For
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}
, the bias is given by,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bias}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}\right]=\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}\right]-\rho=\frac{2(1+r\rho)}{r(\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}-2)}

(33)

This equation reveals that
\widehat{\rho}_{{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}
consistently exhibits a positive bias. However, the bias diminishes as the sample size or the number of replications increases, eventually approaching zero.

The relative bias, expressed in percentage, provides a measure of an estimator’s accuracy. For the estimator of
\rho
, it is calculated as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}\,\right]}{\rho}-1\right)\times 100\,.

(34)

illustrates a comparison of the relative bias of
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}
with respect to
\rho
for various study plans, characterized by different combinations of sample sizes and replication measurements.

It is worth noting that any reasonable measurement system typically exhibits
\rho>1

(
Browne, Steiner, and MacKay 2010
)
. Consequently, our study focuses on the range of
\rho>1
, including four distinct sample sizes,
a=5,10,20,30
. For each sample size, three different numbers of replication measurements are considered,
r=2,3,6
. As shown in Figure

, increasing either the sample size or replications reduces the bias in the estimation of
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}
.

B. Maximum likelihood and non-negative ANOVA

Identifying the bias of maximum likelihood and non-negative ANOVA estimators can be challenging since their estimations of
\sigma^{2}_{u}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
depend on the relationship between
MS_{u}
and
MS_{\epsilon}
. Specifically, the expected values of
\sigma^{2}_{u}
,
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
, and
\rho
are influenced by the probability that the estimates lie on the boundary of the parameter space. This probability using the maximum likelihood estimation method is determined by,

\displaystyle p_{{}_{\circ}}

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}<\beta\right)=\Pr\left(F<\beta{(1+r\rho)^{-1}}\right),

(35)

where
F
is the random variable, as characterized in (
18
).
The expected values of the maximum likelihood estimators
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
and
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}
are determined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\right]=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\left(1-p_{{}_{\circ}}\right)\mathrm{E}\left[\beta^{-1}MS_{u}-MS_{\epsilon}\,\middle|\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}\geq\beta\right],

(36)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}\right]=\left(1-p_{{}_{\circ}}\right)\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{\epsilon}\middle|\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}\geq\beta\right]+\frac{p_{{}_{\circ}}}{ar}\mathrm{E}\left[SS_{t}\middle|\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}<\beta\right],

(37)

and the expected value of
\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}
is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\left(1-p_{{}_{\circ}}\right)\mathrm{E}\left[\beta^{-1}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}-1\,\middle|\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}\geq\beta\right].

(38)

We can determine the expectation of
\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}
through,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\left(1-p_{{}_{\circ}}\right)\mathrm{E}\left[\beta^{-1}(1+r\rho)F-1\mid F\geq\beta(1+r\rho)^{-1}\right].

(39)

The distribution of
\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}
can be verified as a truncated
F
-distribution with
a-1
and
a(r-1)
degrees of freedom. A more explicit expression for the moments of this distribution is provided by
Nadarajah and Kotz (2008)
, which involves the use of the Gauss hypergeometric function.

The expected values of the maximum likelihood estimators
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
,
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}
, and
\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}
cannot be derived analytically due to the complexity of the underlying equations. As a result, determining the bias of these estimates analytically is challenging, necessitating numerical methods for evaluation.

The properties of the estimators from the non-negative ANOVA and maximum likelihood methods are quite similar in model (
1
). Specifically, by substituting
\beta
with
1
in equations (
35
) and (
39
), we can first determine the probability that the non-negative ANOVA estimations lie on the boundary and then compute
\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{NANOVA}}}\right]
.

6.2
Sampling variance

A. ANOVA estimations

The variances of the mean of squares terms
MS_{u}
and
MS_{\epsilon}\,
, which are proportional to chi-squared distributions with degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{u}=a-1
and
\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}=a(r-1)
, are given as follows
(
Searle, Casella, and McCulloch 1992
)
,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left[MS_{u}\right]=\frac{2\left(\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{u}\right]\right)^{2}}{a-1}\quad\mbox{and }\quad{\mathrm{Var}}\left[MS_{\epsilon}\right]=\frac{2\left(\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{\epsilon}\right]\right)^{2}}{a(r-1)}\,\cdot

(40)

Assuming the independence of
MS_{u}
and
MS_{\epsilon}
under normality, the variances of
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
and
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon},}
using the ANOVA estimation method, for a sample size of
a
and replication
r
can be expressed as follows,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{2}{r^2}$}}\left(\frac{(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}+r\sigma^{2}_{u})^{2}}{a-1}+\frac{\sigma^{4}_{\epsilon}}{a(r-1)}\right),

(41)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon\,}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,\sigma^{4}_{\epsilon}}{a(r-1)}.

(42)

The variance of
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}
, as defined in (
20
), can be computed by,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r^2}$}}\,\mathrm{Var}\left[\frac{MS_{u}}{MS_{\epsilon}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+r\rho)^{2}}{r^{2}}\,\mathrm{Var}\left[F\right],

(43)

where the random variable
F
is defined in (
18
). The variance of random variable
F
is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[F\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}^{2}\left(\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}+\mathrm{df}_{u}-2\right)}{\mathrm{df}_{u}\left(\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}-2\right)^{2}\left(\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}-4\right)},

(44)

where
\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}>4
, and the variance does not exist otherwise. By substituting (
44
) in to (
43
), the variance of
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}
is obtained as,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{ANOVA}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2(1+r\rho)^{2}\,\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}^{2}\left(\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}+\mathrm{df}_{u}-2\right)}{r^{2}\mathrm{df}_{u}\left(\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}-2\right)^{2}\left(\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}-4\right)}.

(45)

B. Maximum likelihood and non-negative ANOVA

First, we analyze the variance of
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
and
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}
using their maximum likelihood estimations. For
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
, the variance is given by,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left[\big(\,\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\,\big)^{2}\right]-\left(\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r^2}$}}(1-p_{{}_{\circ}})\mathrm{E}\left[\left(\beta^{-1}MS_{u}-MS_{\epsilon}\right)^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}\geq\beta\right]-\left(\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\right]\right)^{2},

(46)

where
\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\right]
is provided in (
36
).

For
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}
, the variance is expressed as,

\displaystyle\!\!\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\left[\big(\,\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}\,\big)^{2}\right]-\left(\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{{}_{\circ}})\mathrm{E}\left[MS_{\epsilon}^{2}\,\middle|\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}\geq\beta\right]+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{p_{_\circ}}{a^2r^2}$}}\,\mathrm{E}\left[SS_{t}^{2}\middle|\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{MS_u}{MS_\epsilon}$}}<\beta\right]-\left(\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}\right]\right)^{2}.

(47)

where
\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}\right]
is detailed in (
37
). Similarly, the variance of
\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}
can be determined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}\right]=\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho\,}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}^{2}\right]-\left(\mathrm{E}\left[\,\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}\right]\right)^{2},

where,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho\,}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r^2}$}}(1-p_{{}_{\circ}})\mathrm{E}\left[\left(\beta^{-1}(1+r\rho)F-1\right)^{2}\mid F\geq\beta(1+r\rho)^{-1}\right],

(48)

and
\mathrm{E}\left[\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}\right]
is defined in (
39
).

The finite-sample variance properties of
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
,
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}
and
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{NANOVA}}}
, using non-negative ANOVA estimators, can be derived in a similar manner by setting
\beta
to
1
.

To compare the finite-sample variances of
\rho
estimations, a relative standard error (SE) is calculated and expressed as a percentage using the formula

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var\left[\,\widehat{\rho}\,\right]}}}{\rho}\times 100\,.

(49)

illustrates the relative standard error (SE) as a function of

6.3
Test of hypothesis

Point estimates provide valuable insight into the magnitude of assessment parameters in a measurement system study. However, they offer limited information regarding the precision of these estimates. For instance, if the estimated variability of the measurement system exceeds expectations, hypothesis testing can determine whether it is significantly larger than a specified threshold. Similarly, a small point estimate for the signal-to-noise ratio (or the parameter
\rho
) may suggest an inadequate measurement system. Statistical hypothesis tests allow for probabilistic evaluations, enabling the assessment of the significance of these parameters
(
Burdick 1994
;
Montgomery and Runger 1993a
)
.

To evaluate the statistical significance of estimated values and facilitate informed decision-making regarding the measurement system, we will examine three specific hypothesis tests within the framework of model (
1
), as outlined below.

i)

H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma^{2}_{u}=0
vs
H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma^{2}_{u}>0\,
,

ii)

H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma_{\epsilon}\leq\sigma_{{}_{0}}
vs
H_{a}\,:\,\sigma_{\epsilon}>\sigma_{{}_{0}}\,
,

iii)

H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\rho\leq\rho_{{}_{0}}
vs
H_{a}\,:\,\rho>\rho_{{}_{0}}\,
.

An essential question in measurement system analysis is whether variability in measurement data can be attributed to differences among units
(
Montgomery and Runger 1993a
)
. To investigate this, a hypothesis test can be conducted for
\sigma^{2}_{u}
with the null hypothesis
H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma^{2}_{u}=0\,
.

We use the statistic
{MS_{u}}/{MS_{\epsilon}}
to assess whether the variance component
\sigma^{2}_{u}
is negligible compared to
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
. Under hypothesis
H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma^{2}_{u}=0
, the statistic
{MS_{u}}/{MS_{\epsilon}}
follows an
F
-distribution with
\mathrm{df}_{u}
and
\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}
degrees of freedom. A large observed value of this statistic provides evidence that the variance component
\sigma^{2}_{u}
significantly contributes to the variability in the data. The
p
-value for testing
H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma^{2}_{u}=0
is calculated as follows,

\displaystyle p\mbox{-value}=1-\Pr\left(F<\frac{r\sum_{i=1}^{a}(y_{i\cdot}-\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}/(a-1)}{\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(y_{ij}-\overline{y}_{i\cdot})^{2}/(N-a)}\right),

(50)

A frequent question in measurement system assessment studies is whether the variability of the measurement system remains below an acceptable threshold
(
Burdick and Larsen 1997
)
. To address this, a hypothesis test can be conducted with the null hypothesis
H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma_{\epsilon}\leq\sigma_{{}_{0}}
and the alternative hypothesis
H_{a}\,:\,\sigma_{\epsilon}>\sigma_{{}_{0}}\,
, where
\sigma_{{}_{0}}
denotes the specified threshold for acceptable variability.

To test the hypothesis
H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\sigma_{\epsilon}\leq\sigma_{{}_{0}}
,the test statistics
SS_{\epsilon}/\sigma^{2}_{{}_{0}}
is employed. Under the condition
\sigma_{\epsilon}=\sigma_{{}_{0}}
, this statistic has the same distribution as
W=SS_{\epsilon}/\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}
, which is chi-square distributed with
\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}
degrees of freedom. Typically, a large observed value of
SS_{\epsilon}
provides evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis
\sigma_{\epsilon}>\sigma_{{}_{0}}
. The corresponding
p
-value for the test can be computed as

\displaystyle p\textrm{-value}=1-\Pr\left(W<\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{\sigma^2_0}$}}\,{\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(y_{ij}-\overline{y}_{i\cdot})^{2}}\right).

(51)

In measurement system analysis, it is often necessary to test whether the relative magnitude of the variance components attributed to the units and measurement system exceeds a specified threshold, denoted as
\rho_{{}_{0}}

(
Donner and Eliasziw 1987
;
Steiner and MacKay 2005
)
. For this purpose, the null hypothesis is formulated as
H_{{}_{0}}\,:\,\rho\leq\rho_{{}_{0}}\,
, with the alternative hypothesis
H_{a}\,:\,\rho>\rho_{{}_{0}}\,
.

For this hypothesis test, the test statistic
(1+r\rho_{{}_{0}})^{-1}MS_{u}/MS_{\epsilon}
is employed. Under the null hypothesis
\rho=\rho_{{}_{0}}
, this statistic follows an
F
-distribution with degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{u}
and
\mathrm{df}_{\epsilon}
. A large observed value of
(1+r\rho_{{}_{0}})^{-1}MS_{u}/MS_{\epsilon}
provides evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis
H_{a}\,:\,\rho>\rho_{{}_{0}}\,
. The
p
-value for this test is computed as

\displaystyle p\mbox{-value}=1-\Pr\left(F<\frac{r\sum_{i=1}^{a}(y_{i\cdot}-\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}}{(1+r\rho_{{}_{0}})\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(y_{ij}-\overline{y}_{i\cdot})^{2}}\right).

(52)

Leveraging the result of Theorem

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{\sigma_{22}}}}{\sqrt{a\,}}$}},\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{\sigma_{22}}}}{\sqrt{a\,}}$}}\right].

\displaystyle\sqrt{a}\left\{\log\big(\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}\,}\big)-\log\big({\sigma}^{2}_{u}\big)\right\}\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\sigma_{22}}{\sigma^4_u}$}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{CI}_{\sigma^{2}_{u},\,\log}=\left[\exp\left\{\log(\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}})-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{z_{1-{\alpha}/{2}} \sqrt{\widehat{\sigma_{22}}}}{\widehat{\sigma^2_{u}}\sqrt{a\,}}$}}\right\},\exp\left\{\log(\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}})+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{z_{1-{\alpha}/{2}} \sqrt{\widehat{\sigma_{22}}}}{\widehat{\sigma^2_{u}}\sqrt{a\,}}$}}\right\}\right].

7
Conclusion

This paper presents a comprehensive theoretical investigation of the statistical methods used in measurement system assessment, focusing on the univariate one-way random effects model. We analyze several estimators for variance components and key parameters in measurement system assessment.

The ANOVA-based estimator, while widely used, inherently has the potential to produce negative estimates—a limitation that becomes less pronounced with increasing SNR or the number of measurements
N=ar
. Our analysis also examines the bias and sampling variance properties of the estimators for variance components and the SNR. Due to the complexity of the underlying equations, deriving the bias and variance analytically for the maximum likelihood estimators is infeasible. However, the ANOVA-based SNR estimator consistently exhibits a positive bias, which highlights a key characteristic of its performance. Numerical studies revealed that the maximum likelihood estimator of the SNR maintained an approximately zero bias when the replication number
r
equals
3
, regardless of the sample size–a desirable feature for reliable measurement system assessments. Moreover, a comparative analysis demonstrated that
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}
consistently exhibited lower SE compared to
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{NANOVA}}}
across study plans with a total of
60
measurements. Notably, the smallest relative standard error for
\widehat{\rho}_{\rule{0.0pt}{4.82224pt}{\tiny\mbox{MLE}}}
was observed under a study plan with a sample size of
20
and
3
measurement replications.

We investigated the asymptotic behavior of the maximum likelihood estimators across three scenarios, defined by variations in the sample size and/or the replication number. To assess the statistical significance of the point estimates and support informed decision-making regarding the measurement system, we explored procedures for conducting specific hypothesis tests and constructing confidence intervals.

The one-way model serves as a simple yet powerful framework for many practical applications. The insights gained from this work establish a theoretical foundation for expanding the investigation to more complex models, multivariate data, functional data, and advanced experimental designs.

Proof of convergence properties

We proceed to establish the convergence properties of the estimators introduced in Section

.

Proof of Theorem
1

(a) Recall that
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}=MS_{\epsilon}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a(r-1)}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}
. The random variable
Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}
can be expressed as
\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}
where
\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\epsilon_{ij}
. Assuming that the
\epsilon_{ij}
’s are independent random variables with zero mean and finite second moment
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
, it can be shown that the second moment for the random variable
\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}
is
\mathrm{E}\left[(\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot})^{2}\right]=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{r-1}{r}$}}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
.
Since all
\epsilon_{ij}
’s are identically distributed too, random variables
\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{1j}-\overline{\epsilon}_{1\cdot})^{2}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{2j}-\overline{\epsilon}_{2\cdot})^{2},\ldots
are independent, identically distributed, and have a finite mean,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot})^{2}\Big]=(r-1)\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon},\quad\mbox{for }\;i=1,\ldots,a.

(61)

By the strong law of large numbers, we have,

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot})^{2}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s}}(r-1)\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon},

(62)

as
a\rightarrow\infty
, for any fixed
r
. This completes the proof for part (a).

(b) The maximum likelihood estimate of
\sigma^{2}_{u}
is given by
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\big(\beta^{-1}MS_{u}-MS_{\epsilon}\big)
, which can also be expressed as,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{ar(r-1)}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}.

The random variable
\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}
can be expressed as
\mu+U_{i}+\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}
. Assuming that all
U_{i}
’s and all
\epsilon_{ij}
’s are i.i.d., it follows that the random variables
\overline{Y}_{1\cdot}
,
\overline{Y}_{2\cdot},\ldots
are identically distributed and independent with mean
\mu
and the variance of
\sigma^{2}_{u}+r^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
. Using the consistency of the second sample moment for
\overline{Y}_{1\cdot}
,
\overline{Y}_{2\cdot},\ldots
we have,

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s}}\sigma^{2}_{u}+\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\sigma^2_\epsilon}{r}$}},

(63)

as
a\to\infty
. From part (a), we also have that
MS_{\epsilon}\to\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
almost surely as
a\to\infty
. Combining these results, we conclude

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s}}{\sigma}^{2}_{u}>0,\;\;\textrm{ as }a\rightarrow\infty,

(64)

which means that the estimator
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
converges almost surely to the true value of
{\sigma}^{2}_{u}
as
a\rightarrow\infty
, value of
r
.

(c) To prove this result, we begin with the equation,

\displaystyle\sqrt{a\,}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}-{\sigma}^{2}_{\epsilon}\\
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}-{\sigma}^{2}_{u}\end{array}\!\!\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{a\,}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a(r-1)}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\\
\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{ar(r-1)}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{u}\end{array}\!\!\right).

This equation can be expressed as the sum of two vectors,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{(r-1)}$}}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\\
\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{-1}{r(r-1)}$}}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{r}\end{array}\!\!\right)\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\left(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{u}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\sigma^2_\epsilon}{r}$}}\end{array}\!\!\right).

Using the multivariate central limit theorem, the first term satisfies,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\\
\frac{-1}{r(r-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}}{r}\end{array}\!\!\right)\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\tau_{1}^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-r^{-1}\\
-r^{-1}&r^{-2}\end{array}\right)\right),

as
a\to\infty
, where
\tau_{1}^{2}
is the variance of
\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r-1}$}}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(Y_{ij}-\overline{Y}_{i\cdot})^{2}
, given by

\displaystyle\tau_{1}^{2}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{(r-1)^2}$}}\,\text{Var}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot})^{2}\Big].

(77)

For the second term, the asymptotic distribution of the sample variance gives,

\displaystyle\sqrt{a}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{u}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\sigma^2_\epsilon}{r}$}}\right)\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\mathcal{N}(0,\,\tau_{2}^{2})

(78)

as
a\to\infty
, where
\tau_{2}^{2}
is the variance of
(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\mu)^{2}
, expressed by,

\displaystyle\tau_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{Var}\left[\left(U_{i}+\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}\right)^{2}\right].

Under the one-way model (
1
) with normality, the variances
\tau_{1}^{2}
and
\tau_{2}^{2}
simplify to.

\displaystyle\tau_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma^{4}_{\epsilon}}{r-1},

(79)

\displaystyle\tau_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2(\sigma^{2}_{u}+r^{-1}{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}})^{2}.

(80)

Additionally, the two random vectors presented in (
A
) are independent. Combining these results leads to the desired asymptotic distribution for the joint estimator. This completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
3

(a)
The random variables
\epsilon_{ij}
’s are i.i.d.. Using the consistency of the second sample moment,

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot})^{2}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s}}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}

(81)

as
r\to\infty
and for any fixed number of units. By summing the above result for all units, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{ar}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot})^{2}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s}}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,\quad\textrm{ as }r\rightarrow\infty\,{\textrm{ and }}\forall a\,.

(82)

Therefore,
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s}}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
as
r\to\infty
.

(b) From the asymptotic normality of the sample variance, we have,

\displaystyle\sqrt{r\,}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}\right)^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\right)\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\mathcal{N}(0,\,\tau_{\epsilon}^{2}),\;\;\textrm{as }r\to\infty.

(83)

Since
\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{1j}-\overline{\epsilon}_{1\cdot})^{2}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\epsilon_{2j}-\overline{\epsilon}_{2\cdot})^{2},\ldots
are independent random variables that are normally distributed as
r
increases, their sum will also be normally distributed. Thus, using the properties of the normal distribution, we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{ar}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{ar}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{a}\left(\epsilon_{ij}-\overline{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}\right)^{2}-\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\right)\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\mathcal{N}(0,\,\tau_{\epsilon}^{2}),\;\;\textrm{as }r\to\infty.

(84)

which concludes the result.

(c) To prove this part, we start by expressing the maximum likelihood estimate of
\sigma^{2}_{u}
as,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{a}$}}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^{2}-\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{r}$}}MS_{\epsilon}

(85)

We know that the random variable
\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^{2}/(\sigma^{2}_{u}+r^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})
follows a chi-squared distribution with
a-1
degrees of freedom assuming the normal distribution of random effects. Therefore,
a^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\overline{Y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^{2}
has a gamma distribution with shape parameter
{(a-1)}/{2}
and scale parameter
2(\sigma^{2}_{u}+r^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})/a
.
Moreover, part (b) of this theorem implies that
MS_{\epsilon}=O_{p}\left(r^{-{1/2}}\right)
, which allows us to express (
85
) as

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}={\textrm{Gamma}}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{a-1}{2}$}},{2a^{-1}\left(\sigma^{2}_{u}+r^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\right)}\right)+o_{p}\left(1\right).

(86)

Here,
\textrm{Gamma}(\kappa,\theta)
denotes the gamma distribution with shape parameter
\kappa
and scale parameter
\theta
. Since
r^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
converges to zero as
r\to\infty
, we have that
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
converges in distribution to a gamma distribution with shape parameter
{(a-1)}/{2}
and scale parameter
2\sigma^{2}_{u}/a
, as
r\to\infty
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\xrightarrow{\text{d}}\textrm{Gamma}\left(\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{a-1}{2}$}},{2a^{-1}\sigma^{2}_{u}}\right).

(87)

which is the expected convergence result.

Proof of Theorem
4

In the limit case where the sample size
a
tends to infinity, Theorems
1
and
2
establish that
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}
converges to
\sigma^{2}_{u}\,
,
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}
converges to
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
, and
\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\scriptsize\mathrm{MLE}}}
converges to
\rho
. Furthermore, as
\beta\to 1
, the estimates obtained through the maximum likelihood method and (non-negative) ANOVA method become identical.

When the replication number
r
approaches infinity the estimates take different forms. Using the maximum likelihood method,
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\to\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\overline{y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^{2}/a
,
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,}\to\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
, and
\widehat{\rho}_{{}_{\scriptsize\mathrm{MLE}}}\to\sum_{i=1}^{a}(\overline{y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^{2}/(a\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})
.
Under the (non-negative) ANOVA method, the estimates take slightly different forms:
\widehat{\sigma^{2}_{u}}\to\sum_{i=1}^{a}\left(\overline{y}_{i\cdot}-\overline{y}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^{2}/(a-1)
,
