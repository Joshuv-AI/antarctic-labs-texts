Impact of 24-hour urine output trajectories on 28-day mortality in people with urinary tract infection: based on the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Huanlong Du, Dongzhi Wang, Jinhai Shao, Jiabin Wu, Feng Zhang, Lingna Du, Xiaolin Zhu, Yefei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrinary tract infection (UTI) is a common clinical infection that can lead to sepsis, infectious shock, and death when severe. Urine output is a critical indicator of renal function and hemodynamic status, and its trajectory may hold vital value for clinical outcomes in people with UTI.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between 24-hour urine output trajectories and 28-day mortality in people managing UTI.MethodsThe data of individuals living with UTI were identified from the MIMIC-IV database. Latent class trajectory modeling (LCTM) classified urine output trajectories during the first 24 h post-ICU admission. The fitness of the model was evaluated using Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Sample Adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (SABIC), Entropy and Likelihood Ratio. Survival curves, Cox regression (adjusted for potential confounding factors: age, sex, race, marital status, vital signs, laboratory indicators, and all other available important clinical covariates), and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to analyze associations between trajectory groups and 28-day mortality (adjusted for all confounding factors). Effect modifications were explored by subgroup analyses.ResultsLCTM ultimately identified three types of urine output trajectories: Class 1 (5.57%): first decreased and then rebounded urine output; Class 2 (11.60%): continuous slow and stable increase in urine output, reflecting good renal perfusion and fluid balance; Class 3 (82.83%): continuous low urine output (0–1 mL/kg/h), with mortality rates of 92.6%, 94.8%, and 88.0%, respectively. Class 2 was significantly associated with reduced 28-day mortality risk compared to Class 3 group (HR = 0.479, 95%CI: 0.269–0.855, P = 0.013). Schoenfeld residual analysis confirmed that the model satisfied the proportional hazards assumption, and Bootstrap internal validation showed that the core risk ratio estimation was stable, which jointly supported the robustness of the main findings of this study. According to the stratified analysis, increased urine output during hours 12–24 post-admission correlated with lower mortality (HR < 1, P < 0.05). RCS revealed a nonlinear relationship between urine output during hours 6–12 and survival (P-non-linear = 0.0280).ConclusionDynamic 24-hour urine output changes significantly correlate with 28-day mortality in those with UTI, which underscores the clinical importance of urine output monitoring for optimizing treatment.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13233-6.Keywords: Urine output trajectory, Urinary tract infection, 28-day mortality, Acute kidney injury

Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is a prevalent bacterial infection involving inflammatory responses from microbial invasion of the urinary system, ranging from cystitis to pyelonephritis [1]. Recent epidemiology reports approximately 4.49 billion UTI cases and 300,000 deaths worldwide in 2021 [2], imposing a substantial public health burden. As one of the most common acquired infections in hospitals, UTI can lead to infection dissemination and complications. Although most patients have a favorable prognosis with timely treatment, some develop severe conditions like sepsis, acute kidney injury (AKI), and multi-organ dysfunction [3, 4], which can increase mortality risk. Early identification of risk factors for disease progression and prognosis is thus crucial for targeted intervention of people with worse conditions.

Urine output, a key indicator of renal function and hemodynamics, holds nonnegligible clinical value in critically ill patients [5]. A retrospective study indicated that dynamic 24-hour urine output changes correlate with AKI incidence in sepsis patients [6]. In other critically ill populations, urine monitoring predicts AKI 12 h in advance, with sustained oliguria or rapid decline associated with 83%–156% increased AKI risk [6, 7]. These findings are highly relevant for UTI management, as UTI is a major source of sepsis and AKI, with > 30% of severe UTI patients progressing to sepsis [8]. Dynamic urine output changes may serve as early warning signs of clinical deterioration in UTI. Nevertheless, systematic studies linking specific trajectories to outcomes are lacking.

Latent class trajectory modeling (LCTM) is a person-centered analytical method that simulates heterogeneous growth trajectories by identifying latent subgroups. In this study, we leveraged LCTM to classify 24-hour urine output trajectories in people with UTI from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database, aiming to provide evidence for risk stratification and personalized treatment.

Methods

Data source

Data were extracted from MIMIC-IV v2.2 (released January 2023), containing records of > 400,000 admissions [9]. Since this study was conducted entirely using third-party, anonymous, publicly available databases, it was exempt from the requirement for individual patient consent and approval from an ethics committee (https://mimic-iv.mit.edu/).

Study population

The International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes were utilized to identify people diagnosed with UTI in the MIMIC-IV database (data covers 2008–2019) (ICD 9/10 codes: 0980, 5950, 5951, 5952, 5953, 5954, 59581, 59001, 59010, 59011, 5902, 5903, 59080, 59081, 5909, 5970, 59780, 59781, 59789, 59800, 59801, 5990, N390, O8620, O8621, O8622, O8629, N340, N341, N342, N343, N30, N300, N3000, N3001, N301, N3010, N3011, N302, N3020, N3021, N303, N3030, N3031, N304, N3040, N3041, N308, N3080, N3081, N309, N3090, N3091, N99521, A5400, A562) [10]. In addition, to ensure the specificity of diagnosis, we adopted a composite diagnostic criterion, which further requires patients to have microbiological evidence of positive urine culture based on ICD coding screening.

Exclusion: (1) Patients whose urine culture did not isolate any microorganisms; (2) Patients who were not admitted to the ICU for the first time. To avoid observation bias and duplicate counting issues caused by multiple enrollments of the same patient, this study sorted all ICU hospitalization records of each patient in ascending order of ICU admission time (intime), and assigned a sequence number to each hospitalization (rn = 1 represents the earliest admission), retaining only ICU hospitalization records with rn = 1 (i.e. the first ICU admission). At the same time, the first ICU admission must occur during the UTI-related hospitalization period. By following the above steps, we ensured that each patient analyzed corresponds to only one independent ICU hospitalization event (3). Patients whose ICU stay was less than 1 day; (4) Patients under 18 years old; (5) Records of abnormal values exceeding the 99.9th percentile of the average urine output; (6) Missing continuous urine output records within 24 h after admission to the ICU. The exclusion process of patients is shown in Table S1. The final cohort comprised 2,173 UTI patients (Fig. 1).

Exposure and outcome

The urine output data in this study were sourced from ICU nursing records in the MIMIC-IV database. The urine output of critically ill patients was continuously monitored through indwelling catheterization and recorded by nurses on an hourly basis, which ensured data continuity, including night shifts.

We analyzed the trajectory of urine output changes at the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th 6-hour intervals within 24 h after admission to the ICU. The selection of this interval is mainly based on two considerations. Firstly, the interval has consistency with the diagnostic window of the international KDIGO-AKI guidelines [11], which makes the results of the trajectory model have direct clinical staging significance. Secondly, compared with the commonly used 4-hour or 12-hour intervals in clinical practice, this interval can achieve the best balance between monitoring sensitivity and data stability, which is beneficial for constructing robust trajectory models. Patients with incomplete urine output records within 24 h were excluded. For an individual with missing record points within a very small number of intervals, multiple interpolation was used to complete them. Urine output was normalized as mL/kg/h during each time period, i.e., urine output during the first 6 h = total urine output during the first 6 h / (body weight × 6).

The outcome was 28-day mortality during ICU admission.

Covariates

Covariates included demographics, vital signs, laboratory parameters, treatments, severity scores, comorbidities, and types of infectious bacteria (Table 1). For variables with a missing value ratio of less than 20%, this study used a multiple imputation algorithm (Mice package, RF mode) based on random forest (RF) for prediction and inference, with the RF method in the “Mice” package used to handle other missing variables. Abnormal records with a urine output of 99.9th percentile were excluded for tail reduction processing. The selection of this method is mainly based on the following considerations. Clinical data often present complex nonlinear relationships and non-normal distributions. RF, as a non-parametric algorithm, can effectively handle the commonly existing nonlinear relationships and complex variable interactions in clinical data. Moreover, RF is insensitive to data distributions (such as non-normal distributions). Compared to traditional mean imputation or linear regression-based imputation, RF imputation can better maintain the original correlation between variables and reduce bias caused by parameter models. The filling of RF helps to make the subsequent Cox regression estimates closer to the true values. Although the standard error may be slightly underestimated, the issue has been largely alleviated through multiple imputation and merging with Rubin’s rules, which can accurately reflect the uncertainty introduced by the imputation process. For indicators with multiple measurement results, the most severe value was selected.

Statistics for choosing the best number of classes

Given that urine output is a core indicator reflecting renal function status, this study did not exclude patients with chronic kidney disease or those receiving dialysis treatment. The kidney disease and renal replacement therapy defined in the baseline variables are based on ICD coding and are used for statistical adjustment in subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were represented by median (IQR), and intergroup comparisons were made via Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages (%), and were compared by chi square test. This study constructed a Group-Based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM) to identify the dynamic trajectory of urine output over time within 24 h of admission to the ICU (in the trajectory fitting stage, the model did not include any covariates and only modeled based on the time series of standardized urine output values). The number of trajectory groups was determined by constructing 1 to 5 sets of models, and the selection of the best fitting model combined statistical goodness of fit and clinical interpretability, based on the following criteria: (1) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample size corrected BIC (SABIC) values were minimized; (2) The logarithmic likelihood value was the highest; (3) Entropy > 0.7 indicated high classification certainty; (4) The average posterior probability of each trajectory group was greater than 70%, ensuring accurate individual classification; (5) On the basis of meeting the above statistical standards, efforts were made to ensure that the proportion of samples in each group was greater than 5% to maintain statistical stability while also balancing the clinical rationality of the trajectory pattern [12].

28-day survival condition of people with UTI was revealed via Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival curves and compared by log-rank tests. The associations between trajectories/time-specific urine output and 28-day mortality were assessed by Cox proportional hazard models. We used Schoenfeld residual test to test the Proportional Hazard (PH) hypothesis for both global and individual variables using the cox.zph function in the survival R package. In addition, we also employed non-parametric Bootstrap method (1000 resampling with replacement) for internal validation to evaluate the stability of risk estimation. In the model adjusted for all confounding factors, interaction tests and subgroup analyses were conducted for variables, including Escherichia coli (E. coli) infection, Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) infection, urinary catheter use, and antibiotic treatment. Stratified analysis and restricted cubic splines (RCS) demonstrated relationships between 24-hour urine output after admission to ICU within different time and 28-day mortality (Crude: Unadjusted; Model 1: Adjusted for age, gender, race, marital status; Model 2: Fully adjusted for all covariates (Table S2)). All statistical analyses were conducted using the R package (v 4.4.1). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

24-hour urine output trajectories

Based on the changes in urine volume levels within 24 h after admission to the ICU, this study fitted 1 to 5 sets of GBTM models. Based on the comprehensive statistical analysis of the goodness of fit and clinical interpretability, three groups were ultimately determined as the optimal models. The model performed well in all indicators (AIC = 20332.86, BIC = 20401.06, SABIC = 20362.94, entropy = 0.92, average a posteriori probability (AvePP) > 0.80), and met the preset criteria of greater than 5%. The model had good classification quality and robust results (Table 1). In addition, based on this, the three types of urine volume trajectory models ultimately determined in this study (Class 1 indicated delayed recovery, Class 2 indicated good recovery, and Class 3 indicated sustained oliguria) clearly corresponded to clinical phenotypes with pathological and physiological significance. The trajectory models can avoid excessive model complexity while ensuring sufficient quantities of each type. Therefore, they were selected as the final analysis scheme (Fig. 2).

The mean posterior probabilities for Class 1, Class 2, and Class 3 were 91.16%, 88.66%, and 97.74%, respectively (Table S3). All mean values exceeded 70%, indicating reliable classification results.

Baseline characteristics

A total of 2,173 UTI patients were included in this study and categorized into 3 latent classes based on their 24-hour urine output trajectories. Compared to Class 1 and Class 2, patients in Class 3 had considerably higher 28-day mortality (P = 0.002). Furthermore, they had higher age, mean blood pressure, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II, potassium concentration, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine levels, and rates of RRT, and lower chloride concentration (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Association between 24-hour urine output trajectories and mortality risk in UTI patients

K-M survival curves (Fig. 3) revealed significant differences in 28-day survival rates among the different urine output trajectory groups (Log-rank test, overall comparison between groups: P = 0.002). Specifically, the survival rate of Class 2 was markedly higher than that of Class 3 (P = 0.001), while no statistically significant differences were observed between Class 2 and Class 1 (P = 0.372) or between Class 1 and Class 3 (P = 0.133). The overall trend suggested that Class 2 patients may have a better 28-day survival prognosis.

Kaplan–Meier indicates the association between changes in urine volume and 28-day survival of patients presented with UTI. Class1 (blue, N = 121): a trend of first decrease and then increase; Class2 (pink, N = 252): a trend of continuous and slow increase; Class3 (green, N = 1800): a sustained low level

The results of the Cox regression analysis were presented in Table 3. In the model adjusted for all confounding factors, Class 2 was associated with a remarkably reduced mortality risk of 52.1% compared to the reference group (Class 3) (HR = 0.479, 95%CI: 0.269–0.855, P = 0.013)（Table 3). We used Schoenfeld residuals to test the PH hypothesis for three stepwise adjusted models in Cox regression analysis. The results showed that no statistically significant deviations were found in the global test of all models and the specialized test of urine output trajectory variables (P > 0.05), indicating that the models meet the PH hypothesis. The PH test results are summarized in Table S4.

Since the trajectory classification itself ensured its internal stability and reliability through strict fitting indicators and posterior probabilities, and the subsequent Cox model corresponded to the PH hypothesis, the current analysis results were believed to have good robustness. The internal validation results for the stability of risk estimation showed that the HR of Class 2 vs. Class 3, after bootstrap bias correction, was 0.475 (95% CIprobability: 0.221–0.792), which was highly consistent with the original estimate. Association between urine output during different periods within 24 h after admission to the ICU and 28-day mortality risk in UTI patients

Stratified analysis was conducted on the association between 28-day survival status of UTI patients and urine output at different stages within 24 h after admission to the ICU and with all confounding factors adjusted (Fig. 4). The results demonstrated that urine output during the 3rd (12–18 h) (HR = 0.724, 95%CI: 0.582–0.900, P = 0.004) and 4th (18–24 h) (HR = 0.587, 95%CI: 0.457–0.754, P < 0.001) 6-hour intervals was predominantly associated with decreased 28-day mortality in UTI patients.

Forest plot of association between urine volume in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th 6-hour periods within 24 h after admission to the ICU and 28-day mortality in patients with UTI

The RCS results revealed that after adjusting for all confounders, urine output during the 2nd (6–12 h) 6-hour interval after admission to the ICU exhibited a significant association with 28-day survival status (P-overall = 0.0273) and displayed a distinct nonlinear relationship (P-non-linear = 0.0280). For the 3rd (12–18 h) and 4th (18–24 h) intervals, a remarkable association between urine output and 28-day survival status was observed (P-overall < 0.05), but no nonlinear relationship was detected (P-non-linear > 0.05) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

According to the fit results of the model, we identified three classes of urine output trajectory in people with UTI. The slow-increasing trajectory (2mL/kg/h; Class 2) was associated with appreciably reduced mortality risk. We also found the remarkable association between urine output during different periods within 24 h after admission to the ICU and 28-day mortality risk in UTI patients. Schoenfeld residual analysis confirmed that the model corresponded to the proportional hazards assumption. Bootstrap internal validation showed a stable core risk ratio estimate, which collectively supported the robustness of the main findings of this study. After adjusting for all confounding factors, the urine output during the 12–24-hour period (the 3rd to 4th 6-hour intervals) was markedly associated with a decrease in mortality rate. Among them, the urine output changes during the 6–12-hour period (the 2nd 6-hour interval) showed a non-linear relationship with the mortality rate. These findings suggested that dynamic monitoring of urine output was vital in the prognosis assessment of individuals with UTI.

The reduced mortality risk in UTI patients linked to the Class 2 trajectory (persistent slow increase) aligned with prior research. A study of sepsis reported a nonlinear inverse correlation between 24-hour urine output and ICU mortality, indicating a 3% mortality reduction per 50 mL increase when urine output was below 1,663.5 mL [13]. Urine production is closely related to systemic circulation, metabolism, and prognosis [14]. Studies in hemodialysis patients show that preserved residual renal function mitigates fluid overload and complications like CKD-associated pruritus, left ventricular hypertrophy, and uncontrolled hypertension [15, 16]. In our study, stable urine output growth likely indicated intact renal functional reserve and effective renal perfusion in UTI patients, enabling maintained body fluid balance and metabolic waste clearance. Moreover, UTI typically triggers inflammatory responses that can impair kidneys via renal vasoconstriction, direct damage to renal tubule epithelial cells, and complement activation [17]. This urine output pattern may reflect moderate inflammation levels and therapeutic responsiveness, preventing severe infection-induced microcirculatory dysfunction and multi-organ injury.

Regarding divergent urine output patterns between Class 2 and Class 3, similar results have been documented [5]. Reduced urine output signifies early renal injury, heightening AKI risk [18, 19]. Additionally, abnormal urine output may stem from fluid imbalance, drug toxicity, or electrolyte disturbances. Clinically, interpreting urine output changes requires comprehensive consideration of individual pathophysiological contexts.

Subgroup analyses revealed that Class 2 was associated with significantly lower mortality risk in patients without E. coli infection, without K. pneumoniae infection, with urinary catheter use, and receiving antibiotic therapy. E. coli and K. pneumoniae are predominant UTI pathogens, and antibiotics form the cornerstone of UTI intervention [20, 21]. Their absence or targeted use represents standard strategies to improve prognosis and reduce complications. Although urinary catheterization is a well-established iatrogenic UTI risk factor [22], Class 2 still conferred significant prognostic advantages in patients using urinary catheters. It suggested that sustained slow-increasing urine output may indicate favorable local inflammatory control and renal status, providing reliable prognostic insights even in high-risk subgroups like patients using a urinary catheter.

We observed that urine output during 12–24-hour post-admission remarkably correlated with reduced mortality. While specific guidelines for UTI patients are lacking, shared pathophysiological mechanisms with sepsis, where UTI is a common etiology, make sepsis management principles clinically relevant. Source control is critical in sepsis, with international guidelines recommending intervention within 12 h of diagnosis [23]. This principle is reflected in our findings: early appropriate antibiotics likely clear urinary pathogens effectively, reducing endotoxin release and systemic inflammation-induced renal damage [24, 25]. Such timely interventions directly improve urine output trajectories beyond 12 h, manifesting as the significant association between 12 and 24 h urine output and mortality reduction. Notably, defining optimal source control windows for UTI, particularly distinguishing obstructive and non-obstructive cases, requires high-quality evidence and remains a key research direction.

Limitations still exist in this study. First, as a single-center retrospective study, selection bias may exist. Second, urine output is influenced by unmeasured confounders (e.g., diuretic use), potentially affecting trajectory interpretation. Thirdly, although this study has adjusted for a range of demographic, clinical, and treatment variables, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out. In the future, if more comprehensive information on potential confounding factors is collected and more refined prospective studies are designed, it will help further validate the findings of this study. In addition, due to the inherent characteristics of retrospective databases, we were unable to stratify patients based on the specific location of UTI (such as simple cystitis, pyelonephritis, or urogenic sepsis). Future research can further validate the findings of this study by clarifying the stratification of infection sites. Finally, routine clinical urine measurement methods may introduce inaccuracies in clinical and digital aspects. Thus, multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate our findings.

Conclusion

In this study, we elucidated critical associations between dynamic urine output characteristics and clinical outcomes in individuals with UTI by analyzing 24-hour urine output trajectories. The results demonstrated that a persistently slow-increasing urine output pattern (Class 2 trajectory) correlates markedly with improved prognosis, offering a novel objective indicator for clinical assessment. Additionally, we established the distinct clinical significance of urine output changes during specific intervals, particularly highlighting the prognostic value of urine output recovery during hours 12–24. These findings deepened our understanding of urine output monitoring in prognostication and provide an evidence-based foundation for individualized therapeutic decision-making in people with UTI.
