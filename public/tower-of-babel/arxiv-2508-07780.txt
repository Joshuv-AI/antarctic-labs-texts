Reconstructing the dielectric properties of melanoma in 3D using real-life melanoma model
Georg Kyhn, Eric Lindström, Larisa Beilina

Abstract
The paper presents performance of the adaptive domain decomposition finite element/finite difference method for reconstruction of the dielectric permittivity and conductivity functions for 3D real-life melanoma model using measurements of the backscattered electric field at the boundary of the investigated domain. We present several gradient-based reconstruction algorithms which use optimization approach to find stationary point of the Lagrangian. Our computational tests show qualitative and quantitative reconstruction of dielectric permittivity and conductivity functions using realistic model of malign melanoma at 6 GHz in 3D.



Keywords—
Maxwell’s equations, finite element mesh, adaptive finite element method, finite difference method, coefficient inverse problems, microwave imaging, malign melanoma, dielectric properties of skin

The mathematical model

In this work, the spatial domain of interest
\Omega
is a bounded, convex and a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with smooth boundary
\Gamma
.
The time domain of interest is the interval
(0,T)
for end time
T>0
.
Denote the space time domains
\Omega_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\Gamma_{T}:=\Gamma\times(0,T)
.
The problem is restricted to linear, isotropic and non-dispersive materials.

In order to utilize the strengths of both the Finite Difference (FD) and the Finite Element (FE) methods, the domain
\Omega
is decomposed into the subregions
{\Omega_{\mathrm{FDM}}}
and
{\Omega_{\mathrm{FEM}}}
such that
\Omega={\Omega_{\mathrm{FDM}}}\cup{\Omega_{\mathrm{FEM}}}
and
\overline{\Omega}_{\mathrm{FEM}}\subset\Omega
with
\partial{\Omega_{\mathrm{FEM}}}\subset{\Omega_{\mathrm{FDM}}}
.
For details of the domain decomposition, we refer the reader to
[
6
]
.
Given constants
\overline{\varepsilon}>1
and
\overline{\sigma}>0
, we assume

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x)=1,

\displaystyle\sigma(x)=0,

\displaystyle\text{for $x$ in }{\Omega_{\mathrm{FDM}}},

(1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x)\in[1,\overline{\varepsilon}],

\displaystyle\sigma(x)\in[0,\overline{\sigma}],

\displaystyle\text{for $x$ in }\Omega\setminus{\Omega_{\mathrm{FDM}}},

holds for the relative electric permittivity
\varepsilon\in C^{2}(\Omega)
and the electric conductivity
\sigma\in C^{2}(\Omega)
.
From Maxwells equations, utilizing Ohm’s, the stabilized problem can be derived.

Forward problem:

Find
E
such that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{tt}E+\sigma\partial_{t}E-\Delta E-\nabla\nabla\cdot\left(\varepsilon-1\right)E

\displaystyle=0\ \text{in }\Omega_{T},

(2a)

\displaystyle E(\cdot,0)=f_{0},\ \partial_{t}E(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=f_{1}\ \text{in }\Omega,

(2b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}E+\partial_{t}E

\displaystyle=0\ \text{on }\Gamma_{T},

(2c)

hold for given
\varepsilon,\sigma,f_{0}
and
f_{1}
.

Above,
E:{\Omega_{T}}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the electric field and
f_{0},f_{1}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
are initial conditions.
Note that the Gauss Flux theorem was introduced to the forward problem as a stabilizing term in order to mitigate the possibility of spurious solutions arising due to the use of
P1
-elements in the FEM - see details in
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
16
]
.
The first order absorbing boundary condition (
2c
), is included since by (
1
), the model equation (
2a
) becomes the wave equation on
{\Omega_{\mathrm{FDM}}}
and
\Gamma\subset\overline{\Omega}_{\mathrm{FDM}}
.
We can also state the inverse problem.

Inverse problem:

Find
\varepsilon(x)
and
\sigma(x)
where (
1
) holds for given bounds
\overline{\varepsilon}
and
\overline{\sigma}
such that the following function
E_{\mathrm{obs}}
is known at
\Gamma_{T}
:

E\approx E_{\mathrm{obs}},\hskip 8.50012pt\text{on }\Gamma_{T}.

(3)

Usually,
E_{\mathrm{obs}}
are measurements made at the boundary
\Gamma_{T}
which incorporate noise as well.

3
The Lagrangian and optimality conditions

Due to the fact that the inverse problem is an ill-posed problem, see
[
3
,
19
]
, we use an optimization approach and minimize the respective Tikhonov functional,
J
.
Let

\begin{split}J(E,\varepsilon,\sigma)&:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(E-E_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)^{2}\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}z_{\delta}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\frac{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{0})^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\gamma_{\sigma}}{2}\int_{\Omega}(\sigma-\sigma^{0})^{2}\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(4)

Here,
\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}
is the delta-function equal to one where
E_{\mathrm{obs}}
is observed and zero elsewhere,
z_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smoothing function, and
\gamma_{\varepsilon},\gamma_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}
are the regularization parameters for
\varepsilon
and
\sigma
, respectively.
Let
H^{1}(\Omega_{T})
be the usual Sobolev space, and define the space
H^{1}_{T}(\Omega_{T})=\left\{v\in[H^{1}(\Omega_{T})]^{3}:v(\cdot,T)=\partial_{t}v(\cdot,T)=0\right\}
.
In order to minimize (
4
), we introduce the Lagrangian
L
on weak form as

\begin{split}L(E,\lambda,\varepsilon,\sigma)&=J(E,\varepsilon,\sigma)-\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon(x)\lambda(x,0)f_{1}(x)\mathrm{d}x\\
&-\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\lambda\partial_{t}E\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\sigma\int_{0}^{T}\lambda\partial_{t}E\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\lambda\partial_{t}E\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\lambda\nabla E\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\lambda\nabla\cdot\left(\varepsilon-1\right)E\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(5)

Here,
\lambda\in H^{1}_{T}(\Omega_{T})
is the Lagrangian multiplier.
We can define the domain of the Lagrangian as
\mathcal{U}:=[H^{1}(\Omega_{T})]^{3}\times H_{T}^{1}(\Omega_{T})\times L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
L:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then, to find the minimum of (
4
), we need to find a stationary point
u\in\mathcal{U}
of the Lagrangian such that
DL(u)(\tilde{u})=0
for all
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}
.
Here,
DL(u)
is the Fréchet derivative of
L
at
u
.
Since the differentiated Lagrangian can be split into the sum of the partial derivatives, we can consider one term at a time, when finding zeroes. Thus, we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{split}0=&\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial E}(u)(\tilde{E})=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(E-E_{\mathrm{obs}})\tilde{E}\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}z_{\delta}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\\
&-\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon(x)\partial_{t}\lambda(x,0)\tilde{E}(x,0)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\lambda\partial_{t}\tilde{E}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x\\
&-\int_{\Omega}\sigma\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\lambda\tilde{E}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}\lambda\tilde{E}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\lambda\nabla\tilde{E}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\lambda\nabla\cdot\left(\varepsilon-1\right)\tilde{E}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0=&\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda}(u)(\tilde{\lambda})=-\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon(x)\tilde{\lambda}(x,0)f_{1}(x)\mathrm{d}x\\
&-\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\tilde{\lambda}\partial_{t}E\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\sigma\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{\lambda}\partial_{t}E\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\tilde{\lambda}\partial_{t}E\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\tilde{\lambda}\nabla E\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\tilde{\lambda}\nabla\cdot\left(\varepsilon-1\right)E\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0=\ &\frac{\partial L}{\partial\varepsilon}(u)(\tilde{\varepsilon})=\gamma_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{0})\tilde{\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\varepsilon}(x)\lambda(x,0)f_{1}(x)\mathrm{d}x\\
&-\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\lambda\partial_{t}E\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\lambda\nabla\cdot\tilde{\varepsilon}E\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}0=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\sigma}(u)(\tilde{\sigma})&=\gamma_{\sigma}\int_{\Omega}(\sigma-\sigma^{0})\tilde{\sigma}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\tilde{\sigma}\int_{0}^{T}\lambda\partial_{t}E\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

(9)

Note that finding a stationary point in the
\tilde{\lambda}
direction is equivalent to solving the forward problem.
In the same way, from (
6
), the adjoint problem can be derived.

Adjoint problem:

Given
\varepsilon,\sigma
and
E
, find
\lambda
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varepsilon\partial_{tt}\lambda&-\sigma\partial_{t}\lambda-\Delta\lambda-\left(\varepsilon-1\right)\nabla\nabla\cdot\lambda\\
&=\ -(E-E_{\mathrm{obs}})\delta_{\mathrm{obs}}z_{\delta},\end{split}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(10a)

\displaystyle\lambda(x,T)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\lambda(x,T)=0,

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(10b)

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\lambda

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\lambda,

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{T},

(10c)

where
E_{\mathrm{obs}}
are observed values on the boundary.

4
Algorithms for solution of MCIP

In our numerical simulations we are using
two different gradient-based algorithms: conjugate gradient algorithm (CGA)
and adaptive CGA (ACGA).
ACGA uses adaptive
finite element method in space which significantly improves reconstructions of

\varepsilon,\sigma
obtained on the initially non-refined mesh by CGA.

Let us define the following functions which are obtained from optimal conditions

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\varepsilon}(u)(\tilde{\varepsilon})=0
and
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\sigma}(u)(\tilde{\sigma})=0

and which we will use in the conjugate gradient algorithm (CGA):

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &g_{\varepsilon}(x):=[\gamma_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{0})-\lambda(x,0)f_{1}\\
&-\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\lambda\partial_{t}E~\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\cdot\lambda\nabla\cdot E~\mathrm{d}t](x),\end{aligned}

(11)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} g_{\sigma}(x)&:=[\gamma_{\sigma}(\sigma-\sigma^{0})+\int_{0}^{T}\lambda\partial_{t}E\,\mathrm{d}t](x).\end{aligned}

(12)

In (
11
) we have used that
\nabla\cdot(\bar{\varepsilon}E)=\nabla\bar{\varepsilon}E+\bar{\varepsilon}\nabla\cdot E\approx\bar{\varepsilon}\nabla\cdot E.

Let
\varepsilon^{m}
and
\sigma^{m}
be the coefficients computed on the iteration
m
of the CGA,
and
E^{m}:=E(\varepsilon^{m},\sigma^{m})
,
\lambda^{m}:=\lambda(E^{m},\varepsilon^{m},\sigma^{m})
,
g_{\varepsilon}^{m}:=g_{\varepsilon}^{m}(x)
where
E(\varepsilon^{m},\sigma^{m})
and
\lambda(E^{m},\varepsilon^{m},\sigma^{m})
are the solutions of the forward and adjoint problems, respectively.
We introduce CGA in a continuous setting but the same algorithm
applies in the discrete setting.
The conjugate gradient algorithm is formulated as follows.

Conjugate Gradient Algorithm (CGA):
For iterations
m=0,\dots,M
perform following steps.

0.

Initialization: set
m=0
and choose initial guesses
\varepsilon^{0}
,
\sigma^{0},\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{0},\gamma_{\sigma}^{0},\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{0},\alpha_{\sigma}^{0}
.

1.

Calculate
E^{m}
,
\lambda^{m}
,
g^{m}_{\varepsilon}
and
g^{m}_{\sigma}
.

2.

Update the dielectric permittivity as:

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{m+1}

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon^{m}+\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{m}d_{\varepsilon}^{m},

(13)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012ptd_{\varepsilon}^{m}

\displaystyle:=-g_{\varepsilon}^{m}+\beta_{\varepsilon}^{m}d_{\varepsilon}^{m-1},\beta_{\varepsilon}^{m}:=\frac{\|g_{\varepsilon}^{m}\|_{\Omega}^{2}}{\|g_{\varepsilon}^{m-1}\|_{\Omega}^{2}},

(14)

3.

Update conductivity as:

\displaystyle\sigma^{m+1}

\displaystyle:=\sigma^{m}+\alpha_{\sigma}^{m}d_{\sigma}^{m},

(15)

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012ptd_{\sigma}^{m}

\displaystyle:=-g_{\sigma}^{m}+\beta_{\sigma}^{m}d_{\sigma}^{m-1},\beta_{\sigma}^{m}:=\frac{\|g_{\sigma}^{m}\|_{\Omega}^{2}}{\|g_{\sigma}^{m-1},\|_{\Omega}^{2}},

(16)

where
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
,
\alpha_{\sigma}
are chosen step sizes with
d_{\varepsilon}^{0}:=-g_{\varepsilon}^{0}
,
d_{\sigma}^{0}:=-g_{\sigma}^{0}
.

4.

Compute new optimal step-sizes as

\begin{split}\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{m+1}=-\frac{(g_{\varepsilon}^{m},d_{\varepsilon}^{m})}{\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{m}(d_{\varepsilon}^{m},d_{\varepsilon}^{m})},\hskip 8.50012pt\alpha_{\sigma}^{m+1}=-\frac{(g_{\sigma}^{m},d_{\sigma}^{m})}{\gamma_{\sigma}^{m}(d_{\sigma}^{m},d_{\sigma}^{m})}.\end{split}

(17)

5.

Compute new regularization parameters for any
p\in(0,1)
via iterative rules of
[
2
]
as

\begin{split}\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{m+1}=\frac{\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{0}}{(m+1)^{p}},\hskip 8.50012pt\gamma_{\sigma}^{m+1}=\frac{\gamma_{\sigma}^{0}}{(m+1)^{p}}\end{split}

(18)

6.

Terminate the algorithm if either
\|\varepsilon^{m+1}-\varepsilon^{m}\|<\eta_{\varepsilon}^{1}
or
\|\sigma^{m+1}-\sigma^{m}\|<\eta_{\sigma}^{1}
and
\|g_{\varepsilon}^{m}\|<\eta_{\varepsilon}^{2}
or
\|g_{\sigma}^{m}\|<\eta_{\sigma}^{2}
, where
\eta^{1}_{\varepsilon}
,
\eta^{2}_{\varepsilon}
,
\eta^{1}_{\sigma}
and
\eta^{2}_{\sigma}
are tolerances chosen by the user. Otherwise, set
m:=m+1
and repeat the algorithm from step 1).

The main idea of the adaptive local mesh refinement used in this work
is that the finite element mesh
K_{h}
should be refined in such elements where
both functions

|h{\varepsilon}_{h}|,|h{\sigma}_{h}|
achieve its maximum
values:

\begin{split}|h{\varepsilon}_{h}|\geq\widetilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}\max\limits_{K_{h}}|h{\varepsilon}_{h}|,\\
|h{\sigma}_{h}|\geq\widetilde{\beta}_{\sigma}\max\limits_{K_{h}}|h{\sigma}_{h}|.\end{split}

(19)

Here,
\widetilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1),\widetilde{\beta}_{\sigma}\in(0,1)
are
numbers which should
be chosen computationally, and
h=h(x)
is a piecewise-constant mesh function
representing the local diameter of the elements and is defined as

h|_{K}=h_{K}~~~\forall K\in K_{h}.

(20)

The mesh refinements recommendations (
19
) are based on a posteriori error estimates for the
errors
|\varepsilon-{\varepsilon}_{h}|
,
|\sigma-\sigma_{h}|
in the reconstructed functions
\varepsilon,\sigma
,
respectively. The proofs of a posteriori error estimates
can be derived using technique of
[
7
]

and
is
topic of the ongoing research. We formulate our adaptive conjugate
gradient algorithm as follows below.

Adaptive Conjugate Gradient Algorithm (ACGA):
For mesh
refinements
i=0,\dots,N
perform the steps below.

0.

Choose initial
spatial mesh
K^{0}_{h}
in
{\Omega_{\mathrm{FEM}}}
.

1.

Compute
\varepsilon_{h,i}
,
\sigma_{h,i}
on mesh
K^{i}_{h}
according to the CGA algorithm.

2.

Refine locally such elements in the finite element mesh
K_{h}^{i}
where

\begin{split}|h{\varepsilon}_{h,i}|\geq\widetilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon,i}\max_{K\in K_{h}^{i}}|h{\varepsilon}_{h,i}|,\\
|h{\sigma}_{h,i}|\geq\widetilde{\beta}_{\sigma,i}\max_{K\in K_{h}^{i}}|h{\sigma}_{h,i}|.\end{split}

Here,
\widetilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon,i}\in(0,1),\widetilde{\beta}_{\sigma,i}\in(0,1)

are constants chosen by the user.

3.

Construct the new mesh as
K_{h}^{i+1}
and interpolate
\varepsilon_{h,i}
,
\sigma_{h,i}
as well as measurements
E_{\rm obs}
onto it.

4.

Terminate the algorithm if either
\|\varepsilon_{h,i+1}-\varepsilon_{h,i}\|<\theta_{\varepsilon}^{1}
or
\|\sigma_{h,i+1}-\sigma_{h,i}\|<\theta_{\sigma}^{1}
and
\|g_{\varepsilon}^{m}\|<\theta_{\varepsilon}^{2}
or
\|g_{\sigma}^{m}\|<\theta_{\sigma}^{2}
, where
\theta^{1}_{\varepsilon}
,
\theta^{2}_{\varepsilon}
,
\theta^{1}_{\sigma}
and
\theta^{2}_{\sigma}
are tolerances chosen by the user. Otherwise, set
i:=i+1
and repeat the algorithm from step 1).

We note that our mesh refinement is performed only in space and not in
time to be able smoothly run the ADDFE/FDM algorithm
for solutions of the forward and adjoint problems - see details of
this algorithm for our model problem in
[
6
]
. We also note that
numbers
\widetilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon,i},\widetilde{\beta}_{\sigma,i}
in ACGA algorithm should be chosen computationally depending
on the obtained results of reconstruction. If these numbers are
chosen close to zero then almost all elements in the mesh
K_{h}^{i}
will
be refined, and opposite, if
\widetilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon,i}\approx 1,\widetilde{\beta}_{\sigma,i}\approx 1
then the mesh will be not refined
at all.

6
Conclusions

The work presents performance of CGA and ACGA algorithms for reconstruction of
dielectric properties of malign melanoma placed in the homogeneous domain.
Our computational tests show qualitative and quantitative
reconstruction of the relative dielectric permittivity function of
malign melanoma measured at 6 GHz.
Future work is related to the reconstruction of dielectric properties of malign melanoma placed inside skin with dielectric properties of skin corresponding to the real values of MM presented in the Table 1 and Figure
2
.
