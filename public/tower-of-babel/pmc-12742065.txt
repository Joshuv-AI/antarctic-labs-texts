An exploratory qualitative study on financial toxicity in cancer patients of Pakistan: implications, patient coping strategies and future direction
Authors: Wajiha Ahmad, Shahid Muhammad Iqbal, Amna Shahbaz, Beenish Ihsan, Muhammad Amir, Muhammad Nauman Jamil, Jawad Akbar Khan, Qurratul Ain Jamil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore the impact of financial toxicity (FT) faced by cancer patients in Bahawalpur, Pakistan, identify their coping strategies and provide patient-driven recommendations to mitigate the FT.DesignA qualitative study design was used, and thematic analysis was employed to analyse the data.SettingPatients were selected from two tertiary care hospitals located in Bahawalpur, Pakistan.ParticipantThirty patients were selected using a purposive and convenience sampling method. Data were collected between April and June 2024. Patients who provided consent, were 18 years or older and were receiving cancer treatment were included in the study.ResultsMost participants were between 40 and 60 years old, and 66.6% were male. The study highlights severe FT, characterised by financial instability, psychological distress and family lifestyle disruptions. Patients employed various strategies, such as adjusting healthcare decisions, mobilising financial resources and seeking financial aid to cover treatment costs. Novel insights revealed the inadequacy of existing government health card programmes, which focus on direct medical expenses but fail to cover indirect costs, further exacerbating FT. Patients reported significant challenges in accessing government aid programmes due to administrative barriers. Participants highlighted the need for additional support mechanisms to address these gaps effectively, including enhanced accessibility, broadening financial assistance and integrated financial counselling.ConclusionThis study is the first to explore FT in Pakistan’s healthcare system. It reveals gaps in support mechanisms and highlights the need for comprehensive policy interventions. Addressing these challenges holistically can improve patient outcomes and quality of life.

Introduction

Financial toxicity (FT) refers to the financial burden cancer places on patients and their families due to the high costs of treatment.1 2 Unlike many chronic diseases, cancer often involves expensive and prolonged therapies, leading to substantial out-of-pocket expenses.3 4 Beyond direct medical costs, FT adversely affects household finances, often forcing families to make difficult choices that can lead to debt, reduced access to care and diminished quality of life.5 It has far-reaching consequences, affecting patients physical and mental health and ultimately compromising treatment outcomes.1 6

Globally, FT is a well-recognised challenge, particularly in high-income countries. For example, studies in China and the USA reported that over half of cancer patients experience treatment-related debt, while in Australia, one-third of cancer patients report moderate to high levels of FT due to the disease.5 7 8 Patients having FT may delay their medical appointments, discontinue prescribed medications and endure high levels of stress, anxiety and depression.911 However, while these challenges are well documented in high-income settings, less is known about FT in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the healthcare system often lacks adequate financial protection and access to care.

In Asia, FT is an escalating issue in countries such as India and Malaysia, where limited insurance coverage and out-of-pocket costs leave patients vulnerable to FT. For instance, more than half of Malaysian cancer-affected households are affected by catastrophic health expenditure, spending more than 30% of their annual income on the treatment.12 13

In Pakistan, the healthcare system is a mix of private and public models where government hospitals offer subsidised basic services, while private facilities provide specialised and expensive care.14 Public expenditure on health remains under 2% of gross domestic product (GDP), and most patients depend on out-of-pocket payments to cover treatment.15 To strengthen financial protection, the government has launched the Sehat Sahulat Programme (SSP), also known as the National Health Card, which provides inpatient cover of up to one million Pakistani rupees per family each year at selected hospitals.1 However, coverage mostly addresses direct medical costs, and many cancer patients still face considerable indirect expenses such as accommodation, travel and income loss. As a result, despite these initiatives, a large part of the population, particularly those from low-income groups and rural areas, remains vulnerable to catastrophic health expenditures and the subsequent effects of FT.

The majority of people in Pakistan cannot afford private health insurance,16 relying on public hospitals that often lack advanced treatments and are plagued by long waiting times. Patients seeking specialised care in private facilities incur significant out-of-pocket costs,14 while those in rural areas incur additional costs for travelling and accommodation.17 These financial pressures often compel patients to adopt coping strategies, such as reducing essential household spending, incurring debt or selling assets.10 18 Such measures increase household stress and significantly affect patients’ quality of life.

Despite increasing awareness of the financial burden of cancer treatment costs worldwide, there is limited research on the financial challenges faced by cancer patients in Pakistan. Current studies and interventions are designed mainly for high-income settings, raising concerns about their applicability in LMICs like Pakistan, where the healthcare system and socio-economic conditions differ significantly. This knowledge gap highlighted the need for localised research to understand the broader socio-economic implications of FT, financial coping strategies adopted by patients and patient-driven recommendations to mitigate the FT driven by treatment costs. This study focuses on the experiences of cancer patients in Bahawalpur, Pakistan. The findings will contribute to developing practical solutions and policy interventions, ultimately guiding efforts to mitigate FT and improve the quality of life and successful treatment outcomes for cancer patients in Pakistan and other LMICs.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A qualitative design was employed, using in-depth, semi-structured, face-to-face interviews to gather detailed insights following Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) guidelines.

This study was conducted at two major hospitals in Bahawalpur, Pakistan: Bahawal Victoria Hospital, a tertiary care teaching hospital,19 20 and Bahawalpur Institute of Nuclear Oncology, a specialised cancer centre under the Pakistan Atomic Energy Commission. Both hospitals serve the diverse needs of Bahawalpur’s population, which spans 45 588 square kilometres and includes approximately 11.46 million people, of whom 78% live in rural areas.21 The hospitals were chosen for their role in providing cancer care to rural and semi-urban patients, thereby addressing the healthcare inequities in these regions.

Participants

A total of 30 participants were approached, and none of them refused to participate in this study. The inclusion criteria for the study were (1) cancer patients aged 18 years or older, (2) patients currently hospitalised or receiving treatment and (3) patients willing to give informed consent. Patients unable or unwilling to participate in interviews were excluded from the study. To ensure a sample that reflects a range of perspectives, purposive sampling was employed, capturing variations in cancer types, treatment stages and demographics. Following this, convenience sampling was used to approach participants at the hospital, allowing for practical recruitment of those who consented to participate.

Data collection

The researcher, WA, is a female PhD student trained in qualitative studies, and QAJ is her supervisor. The interview scheme was developed following an extensive literature review and consultation with three senior experts in oncology and pharmacy practice to minimise bias. The interview guide was tested with three cancer patients who met the inclusion criteria but were not part of the final study sample. The reason for the pilot study was to evaluate the relevance, clarity and flow of the questions, as well as the duration of the interviews. Feedback from these participants and experts helped identify redundant and ambiguous questions, which were then refined to ensure a clearer structure and progression of questions, thereby ensuring that the interview guide accurately captured patients’ experiences of FT.

Authors WA, SMI and QAJ, along with all authors, thoroughly discussed and finalised the interview schema before data collection commenced. The semi-structured interview guide used in this study is provided as online supplemental file 1. WA first contacted the participants in the hospital offices and explained that the survey was part of WA’s PhD research. By presenting its objectives, WA gave them a general overview of the significance of this study.

After participants expressed interest in the study, WA fixed the interview timing to suit their convenience. On the day of the interview, the WA again described the project’s aim and assured them of the confidentiality of the data. These two meetings helped to establish the rapport. All interviews were conducted in a private room at the hospital, ensuring participants had adequate time to share their experiences without feeling rushed. No third person was in the interview room to ensure participant confidentiality and comfort. WA conducted the interviews. WA’s involvement as the primary researcher is due to its relevance to her PhD research. All interviews were audio recorded after getting the participants’ consent. Each session lasted between 20 and 25 min, with an average duration of 21 min. The main interview questions were as follows:

How does FT impact you?

What strategies have you employed to cope with FT?

What did you need to cope with FT?

All interviews were conducted in a single session, with no follow-up interviews. Immediately after each interview, post-interview reflection notes were taken. To protect the participants’ original perspectives, the transcripts were not returned to them for review and comment. After reviewing the interview transcripts and analysing the data, interviews were stopped if thematic saturation was reached. Data were collected from April to June 2024.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transferred to the computer from the mobile. Google Drive is used to store and keep data safe. No software was used for analysis. Recordings were anonymised, using codes (eg, P1, P2) to ensure confidentiality. All interviews were conducted in Urdu and subsequently translated into English by the first author, who is fluent in both languages. To ensure translation accuracy and preserve the original meanings, the audio interviews and transcripts were cross-checked by bilingual co-authors. Any ambiguities or discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus within the research team. This process minimised the risk of interpretation bias. Data analysis followed a thematic approach following the six-step framework proposed by Braun and Clarke. The first author, WA, familiarised herself with the transcripts, making notes on critical observations and generating initial codes inductively to capture meaningful patterns in participant responses. Codes were then grouped into categories and refined into broader themes that represented the main outcomes of the study: the impact of FT, coping strategies and recommendations to mitigate FT.

Co-authors reviewed each interview’s content independently to confirm the relevance, consistency and representation of participant experiences within the codes and themes. Weekly meetings enabled the research team to resolve disagreements and reach a consensus on the final themes. Participants were not asked to provide feedback on the findings to maintain the authenticity of their initial viewpoints.

Rigour

Researchers thoroughly reviewed qualitative literature to ensure rigour and uphold neutrality during interviews. Participants were assured that their responses would remain confidential and would not affect their treatment. Objectivity was maintained throughout, with all participants encouraged to respond in depth, capturing authentic insights into their experiences.

Results

A total of 30 participants were included in the study. Most participants were between 40 and 60 years old; male respondents comprised 66.7% of the sample. Almost all participants reported earning less than PKR 50 000 per month. For comparison, the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics indicated that the average monthly income in Pakistan was PKR 24 028 in 2021. Although overall wage levels have probably risen since then, this suggests that most participants in our 2024 sample come from low-income households.22 This demographic profile highlights the predominantly rural patients coming from larger households, potentially intensifying the financial impact of cancer treatment costs. To enhance both the depth and clarity of our qualitative results, we have included online supplemental file 2, which features additional participant quotations for each main theme and its corresponding subthemes.

The study’s findings highlighted three primary areas: the impact of FT on patients and family, the coping strategies employed by patients and the recommendations to mitigate the FT resulting from the financial burden of treatment costs.

Part 1: Impact of FT on patients and family

The financial burden of cancer treatment profoundly impacts patients and their families. Participants shared how cancer-related expenses, income loss and psychological stress compounded the challenges they faced, affecting their financial stability, emotional well-being and daily household management.

Financial instability

Due to the physical toll of cancer treatment, many participants found it challenging to maintain their previous work schedules; this inability to work reduced income and increased reliance on savings or family support.

After starting treatment, I couldn’t keep up with my job. The fatigue was overwhelming, and I had no choice but to take unpaid leave. (P23)

Some participants experienced job loss due to prolonged absences or inability to fulfil work requirements. This loss often left them feeling financially vulnerable and concerned about future employment opportunities.

I was let go after missing too many days for treatment. Now, I’m not just dealing with cancer but also figuring out how to support my family without a job. (P8)

Psychological and emotional distress

Financial stress and the inability to maintain social connections caused depression among most of the participants. Many reported decreased social activities, further isolating them during a difficult time.

I stopped going out with friends because I couldn’t afford it. Staying home all the time just made me feel more alone and depressed. (P7)

Most participants expressed guilt when they depended on their family for financial support. This dependence often led to a sense of burden and shame.

I feel terrible for relying on my family. They’re doing so much, and I wish I could contribute instead of taking from them. (P13)

Due to the financial burden of cancer treatment, many participants reduced spending on personal care and items like clothing, which impacted their self-esteem and sense of normalcy.

I can’t remember the last time I bought new clothes. It’s such a small thing, but it affects my feelings. Cutting back on my grooming and clothes makes me feel like I’ve lost part of my identity. (P26)

Family financial and lifestyle disruption

Participants reported cutting back on food and other household essentials to manage healthcare costs. These reductions often impacted the entire family, adding stress and guilt for the participant.

Basic things, like buying enough groceries, have become a struggle. Every trip to the store reminds me of our financial limits. We’ve had to stretch every meal and buy only the cheapest food. (P23)

The financial burden of treatment impacted the ability to provide for dependents, adding another layer of emotional burden. Participants felt distressed about not being able to afford essentials for their children.

I have to explain to my children why we can’t buy the things they need. It’s painful, and I feel like I’m failing as a parent. It breaks my heart when my kids need something, and I can’t provide it. (P19)

Participants found it challenging to accommodate extra healthcare expenses, like specialised foods, clothing for comfort during treatment and increased utility bills for at-home care.

We must keep the house warmer because I get cold so easily now, but the heating bills are piling up. (P21)

The special foods I need for my diet are expensive, and it’s hard to fit them into our already tight budget. (P21)

Part 2: Coping strategies to manage FT

Adjusting healthcare decisions to manage costs

Participants often made difficult choices to manage healthcare costs, such as delaying or skipping medical treatments or opting for alternative healthcare options. These adjustments allowed them to prioritise immediate financial obligations but often came with health trade-offs.

I had to postpone my follow-up appointments. It was a tough choice, but the cost was too high. (P29)

Instead of going to a clinic, I used local herbal treatments. It’s not ideal, but it’s what I can afford. (P17)

Participants reduced non-essential household expenses to allocate more funds towards healthcare. Many shared how they cut back on luxuries, dining out or regular purchases to stay financially afloat.

Family outings are a thing of the past. Every penny counts for medical bills now. (P11)

Mobilising financial resources to cover healthcare costs

Some participants adjusted their work arrangements in response to FT. For instance, some moved to part-time work to balance caregiving responsibilities, while others hired help for their businesses to manage operations more efficiently.

Hiring someone to manage the shop means I can take rest while keeping the business running. (P21)

Doing part-time jobs has helped, but making ends meet with reduced income is hard. (P18)

Participants turned to various income-generating strategies to cope financially, including selling personal assets and agricultural land, using savings, borrowing money and taking out loans.

I had to sell some jewellery to cover a recent bill. It’s hard for me, but we needed the money. (P6)

I took a small loan to pay for the surgery, hoping we can pay it back soon. (P10)

Accessing financial assistance for treatment

Some participants sought financial aid through government programmes, which helped offset healthcare costs and provided a temporary cushion.

I took government assistance, such as the Sehat card. It helped, even though it doesn’t cover everything. (P18)

Applying for financial aid from Bait ul Maal was challenging and time-consuming, but getting some help is a relief. (P22)

Participants also contacted community funds and charitable organisations for financial relief.

The BACAS fund (cancer trust) provided free treatment; we’d be lost without them.(P15)

Part 3: Recommendations to mitigate FT

Participants provided insights into potential solutions to ease the FT. These recommendations focused on government and institutional support and professional financial and medical guidance.

Government and institutional support

Many participants highlighted the need for subsidised public transportation and ration cards to ensure patients receive essential nutrition and to reduce the financial burden of frequent medical visits.

Traveling to the hospital multiple times is exhausting and costly. If public transport were free for patients, it would ease so much of that burden. (P19)

Ration cards could help us afford healthier food, which is crucial for my recovery. Many families are struggling with basic needs, so it would be great if it also included items for my kids.(P14)

Participants preferred direct transfers of health card funds to patients’ bank accounts in a lump sum to ensure timely and accessible healthcare funding.

Receiving the money all at once in our bank account would help us plan better and avoid delays in getting the necessary treatments. (P23)

Long wait times for administration and medical appointments were a significant source of stress for patients. Participants suggested reducing these wait times and consolidating services under one roof for more efficiency.

The administration should run the system better, so we don't have to wait so long (P5)

Having everything in one place would save our time and money and reduce the back-and-forth that patients must endure.(P17)

Integrating counselling into treatment

Many participants recommended that they need the help of financial counsellors, who could help with budget planning and managing the high costs associated with cancer care.

A financial counsellor could help us to figure out how to manage medical expenses without sacrificing other basic needs.(P13)

Pharmacist counselling was suggested to help patients access affordable medications and manage side effects, ultimately improving physical and financial health.

Managing medication costs and side effects is a big challenge. Having a pharmacist guide, would reduce both my financial and health-related stress.(P30)

Discussion

This study offers a unique perspective on the complex challenges and strategies related to FT among cancer patients in Pakistan, where the government provides a ‘Health Card’ programme.1 This programme offers free cancer treatment coverage for up to 1 million Pakistani rupees to mitigate the financial burden on patients. This initiative is part of Pakistan’s effort to advance universal health coverage (UHC) under the SSP, aligned with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.8, which aims to ensure financial risk protection and access to essential health services. However, our findings reveal that, despite this financial assistance, cancer patients continue to face significant FT due to associated indirect costs, such as travel, accommodation and missed work, which the health card does not cover. This study contributes novel insights into the limitations of such support programmes and underscores the need for a more comprehensive approach that addresses direct and indirect treatment costs. In Pakistan, where over 30% of the population lives below the poverty line and where inflation continues to erode purchasing power,23 by examining FT in the context of Pakistan, this study highlights areas where the healthcare system can further enhance support for cancer patients.

Our study also reveals a demographic imbalance that must be understood within the socio-cultural context. The predominance of male respondents does not reflect cancer prevalence but rather the gendered norms in financial decision-making and healthcare navigation, as men commonly manage household finances, making them more accessible for recruitment.24 25 Likewise, the absence of participants over 60 years is linked to behavioural and structural barriers, as older patients in low-resource areas often avoid prolonged treatment due to frailty, comorbidities, mobility changes or family preference for home-based palliative care.26 27 These demographic patterns are crucial to interpreting FT in this context, as age and gender influence healthcare-seeking behaviour and financial responsibility.

Our study reveals that loss of income due to cancer treatment significantly exacerbates FT, as patients often face reduced earnings or unemployment. This finding is consistent with previous studies documenting that FT frequently arises from lost income during treatment.28 29 However, our study provides added insight into how unemployment or reduced work hours directly affect patients’ ability to maintain even basic household expenses, heightening their vulnerability to FT. The impact on household stability underscores the importance of employment support systems during treatment. The psychological toll of FT was also notable in this study, with patients describing depression, lowered self-esteem and feelings of guilt for relying on family for financial support. Similarly, emotional challenges linked to FT have been reported in prior studies.3033 Our findings highlight a deeper emotional strain resulting from reduced spending on personal care and necessities, which affects patients’ sense of dignity and independence. These effects are likely compounded by the stigma and discomfort some patients feel in seeking financial help from family members, pointing to a need for more accessible external support options.

Patients in this study reported significant burden on their household budgets, frequently reducing spending on food, clothing and other essentials. Similar findings from previous studies have shown that cancer patients tend to cut back on basic needs to manage costs associated with treatment.5 10 31 Our study further emphasises that these cutbacks affect patients and their families, as they may compromise household nutrition, health and stability. This has long-term implications for achieving Sustainable Development Goal(SDG) targets (SDG 3 (good health and well-being) and SDG 1 (no poverty)). The added stress of managing healthcare costs on top of daily expenses highlights a critical area where financial assistance programmes could help mitigate FT.

To manage the financial burden of cancer treatment, participants employed various strategies to adjust their financial priorities. Many patients reported delaying or skipping treatments and opting for alternative healthcare options to reduce costs. Although this behaviour is consistent with previous studies that observe similar compromises among cancer patients.4 5 Our findings demonstrate the emotional and health-related risks associated with these strategies, as delaying care can lead to a worse health prognosis. Additionally, patients’ choices to modify household expenses, including cutting back on social and recreational activities, have been reported in other studies.10 31 33 Our findings reveal a direct link between increased feelings of isolation and a decline in quality of life, suggesting the need for interventions that address both financial and emotional needs. The study found that patients implemented various strategies to adjust their employment and income. Some participants moved to part-time work, hired temporary staff to sustain their businesses, or found ways to supplement their income by selling personal assets or borrowing money. This aligns with previous findings where patients similarly modified employment and sought additional income to manage FT.5 31 33 34 However, our study suggests that relying on family loans or liquidating assets often leaves patients feeling financially insecure about their future, with some expressing concerns about long-term recovery due to depleted savings. The added emotional toll of dependency, which may affect patients’ self-esteem and sense of independence, underscores the importance of financial education and planning support for patients undergoing cancer treatment.

Access to financial assistance programmes, such as government aid and charitable support, emerged as crucial in managing costs and reducing financial burden for many participants. However, unlike studies that focus solely on the availability of such resources.5 30 31 34 35 Our findings reveal significant challenges in the accessibility of these resources. Patients describe burdensome administrative processes, reimbursement delays and upfront payment requirements. These barriers not only add stress to patients but also complicate their ability to maintain consistent access to essential resources for ongoing treatment and recovery.

Our findings suggest several actionable recommendations to mitigate FT among cancer patients. Governmental and institutional support could be enhanced through subsidised public transport,31 ration cards for essential items and direct transfers of health card funds to patients’ accounts. While suggested in other studies, these forms of support have unique relevance here as participants directly expressed challenges with upfront costs and complex reimbursement processes.3638 Moreover, integrating financial counselling and pharmacist support within healthcare settings could help patients manage FT more effectively because doctors often do not have time to guide them.39 This study highlights the need for more accessible counselling, where patients can receive practical advice on managing treatment-related expenses and making informed decisions about medication costs, which may ease the financial burden. Such intervention could align with Pakistan’s broader UHC commitments and strengthen health system responsiveness as emphasised in the National Health Vision and the global SDG agenda. Future research should assess the long-term impact of FT and the effectiveness of such interventions.

Significance

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first qualitative study exploring the FT of cancer patients in Pakistan, capturing the financial needs of patients from low-income levels. It highlights how Pakistan’s economic challenges and limited healthcare resources intensify FT, emphasising the need for expanded public health support and inclusive policies. The findings provide critical insights to guide healthcare reforms and develop comprehensive support programmes for cancer patients in low-income settings.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is its reliance on patients’ recollection of past experiences, which may lead to incomplete or inaccurate reports due to memory lapses. Additionally, since the research was conducted in a single city, the findings are not statistically generalisable; instead, they provide detailed, contextually rich insights into the lived experiences of cancer patients facing FT in resource-limited settings. Nevertheless, these insights can assist policymakers and researchers in designing larger-scale, quantitative and multicentre studies to further explore and validate the identified patterns.

Conclusion

The study concludes that financial burden remains a critical barrier to cancer care, contributing to FT in Pakistan, despite the presence of government initiatives such as the health card. The findings suggest a need for more comprehensive support that addresses direct and indirect treatment costs. Enhanced policy interventions, such as subsidies on transportation, expanded healthcare benefits and integration of counselling in treatment, could significantly reduce FT. Addressing these suggestions is essential to improve patient treatment outcomes and quality of life, supporting Pakistan’s efforts toward a more inclusive and effective healthcare system.
