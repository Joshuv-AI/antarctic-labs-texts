Predictive Scaling Laws for Efficient GRPO Training of Large Reasoning Models
Datta Nimmaturi, Vaishnavi Bhargava, Rajat Ghosh, Johnu George, Debojyoti Dutta

Abstract
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) for complex reasoning tasks requires substantial computational resources, particularly when employing reinforcement learning approaches such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). This computational burden necessitates more efficient training strategies to make reasoning-capable models accessible to a broader research community. We address this challenge by developing a predictive framework that enables better understanding and optimization of the GRPO training process for reasoning tasks. Through comprehensive experiments across multiple model architectures—Llama 3B/8B and Qwen 3B/7B parameters—we systematically compare various fine-tuning techniques and derive empirical scaling laws that predict model reward trajectories during GRPO training. Our scaling law incorporates three key factors: model size, initial performance, and training progression, enabling accurate prediction of performance plateaus and convergence phases. Our experimental results reveal consistent sigmoid-shaped learning curves across all tested models, characterized by three distinct phases: initial slow progress, rapid improvement, and eventual plateau. Notably, we observe that training beyond the convergence point (approximately 40% of an epoch) yields minimal performance gains while consuming significant computational resources. The derived scaling law accurately predicts these phase transitions, enabling informed resource allocation decisions. This work provides a quantitative framework for optimizing GRPO training efficiency in reasoning tasks. By predicting performance plateaus in advance, researchers can make data-driven decisions about training duration, avoiding wasteful over-training while maintaining model quality. Our approach generalizes across different model sizes and architectures, suggesting broader applicability to reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning strategies for reasoning-capable LLMs.



Introduction

The development of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 and Gemini has revolutionized natural language generation tasks, yet their capacity for structured, multi-step reasoning remains a significant limitation. To address this gap, Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have emerged as a specialized class of models that integrate advanced techniques including Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, reinforcement learning, and reasoning-focused datasets to enhance logical problem-solving capabilities.

Unlike traditional LLMs that concentrate computational resources primarily during training to optimize next-token prediction, LRMs strategically reallocate substantial computational resources to test-time inference. This paradigm shift enables more deliberate and structured reasoning processes during model deployment, representing a fundamental change in how we approach language model optimization.

Current Approaches to Reasoning Enhancement

Contemporary reasoning enhancement strategies can be broadly categorized into two complementary approaches: output-focused and input-focused methods.

Output-Focused Approaches

Output-focused approaches generate multiple candidate solutions for a given input and employ various selection mechanisms to identify the optimal response. These methods include:

•

Majority voting
leverages collective agreement by selecting the most frequently occurring answer among sampled outputs.

•

Best-of-N sampling
generates N candidate responses and selects the highest-scoring solution according to a reward model, which may be either an outcome reward model focusing on final correctness or a process reward model evaluating the complete reasoning trajectory. However, process reward models require frequent retraining to prevent reward hacking, introducing additional computational overhead.

•

Beam search with process rewards
extends this concept by maintaining multiple high-probability reasoning trajectories in parallel, scoring each for reasoning quality rather than mere likelihood. This approach enables exploration of diverse intermediate chains of thought.

•

Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)
systematically explores the space of possible reasoning steps to identify high-reward paths, though it often encounters scalability challenges due to large branching factors, necessitating sophisticated pruning strategies.

Input-Focused Strategies

Input-focused strategies modify how inputs are processed to promote structured reasoning:

•

Fine-tuning models to prioritize reasoning tokens
alters their proposal distribution to favor tokens that initiate structured reasoning processes.

•

Re-ranking outputs based on reasoning quality metrics
helps select answers demonstrating superior logical consistency.

•

Prompt updating techniques
dynamically adjust prompts to guide models toward more systematic reasoning steps.

A notable complementary approach is STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner), which trains models to generate their own reasoning traces and uses these traces for self-improvement through fine-tuning. STaR combines supervised learning with reinforcement learning rewards to encourage generation of correct intermediate steps, fostering models capable of structured internal reasoning.

Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning

The computational and data requirements of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) have motivated the adoption of reinforcement learning (RL) approaches for reasoning model development. While SFT heavily relies on expensive, high-quality labeled datasets and may suffer from catastrophic forgetting, RL enables models to learn through trial-and-error reward signals, discovering optimal strategies for achieving specific reasoning objectives.

Recent advances in RL-based reasoning include several notable contributions:

Group Relative Policy Optimization

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
Shao et al. 2024
is a recent reinforcement learning algorithm designed to improve the efficiency of LLM fine-tuning for reasoning tasks. Traditional Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
Schulman et al. 2017
is an actor-critic method that requires training a separate value (critic) model to estimate baselines, introducing significant computational and memory overhead. GRPO eliminates the need for this critic model by estimating baselines from
group-relative advantages
. Specifically, for each input question, GRPO samples a group of outputs and computes the baseline as the average reward within this group, then calculates advantages relative to this baseline. This reduces resource requirements while maintaining stable and effective policy optimization.

•

GRPO Formulation

For each question
q
, GRPO samples a group
G=\{o_{1},o_{2},\ldots,o_{G}\}
of outputs from the old policy
\pi_{\theta_{old}}
and optimizes the policy
\pi_{\theta}
by maximizing:

\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{old}}(O|q)}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\Big(\min\Big(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{i}|q)}A_{i},\\
\text{clipped}\Big(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{i}|q)},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\Big)A_{i}\Big)\\
-\beta D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta}||\pi_{ref})\Big)\Bigg].

(1)

Here
A_{i}
denotes the advantage computed from a group of rewards
\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{G}\}
:

A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\text{mean}(\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{G}\})}{\text{std}(\{r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{G}\})},

(2)

and

D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta}||\pi_{ref})=\frac{\pi_{ref}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}-\log\frac{\pi_{ref}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}-1.

(3)

Note that
\epsilon
and
\beta
are parameters controlling the clipping range and KL penalty, respectively.

Furthermore, GRPO supports both
outcome supervision
, where only final outputs are rewarded, and
process supervision
, where intermediate reasoning steps receive rewards, providing denser and more informative training signals for reasoning-focused tasks. Empirical results show that GRPO enhances performance compared to PPO while using fewer computational resources, making it especially practical for large reasoning model training.

In the broader spectrum of RL techniques for LLMs, GRPO complements:

•

Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT)

Yuan et al. 2023
: Fine-tuning on outputs filtered by correctness, using offline sampled data.

•

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Rafailov et al. 2024
: Optimizing policies based on pairwise preferences without explicit reward models.

•

Online RFT

Agnihotri and Pandey 2025
: Similar to RFT but with online sampling from the current policy for dynamic adaptation.

•

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

Schulman et al. 2017
: An actor-critic RL approach optimizing expected rewards with a value baseline and KL constraints for stability.

These methods differ in data source (offline vs. online sampling), reward specification (rule-based, preference-based, or model-based), and optimization approach. GRPO stands out by combining the sample efficiency of online RL with the computational simplicity of baseline-free algorithms, enabling scalable and cost-effective training of reasoning-capable language models.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

•

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)

Hu et al. 2021
is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that inserts small trainable low-rank matrices into pre-trained model weights, enabling task adaptation without updating all parameters and significantly reducing memory requirements.

LoRA augments linear projections with an additional factorized low-rank projection. Given a standard projection
Y=XW
where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}
and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}
, LoRA computes:

Y=XW+sXL_{1}L_{2},

(4)

where
L_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r}
and
L_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o}
are the low-rank adapter matrices, and
s
is a scaling factor.

•

QLoRA

Dettmers et al. 2023
is an extension of LoRA that integrates 4-bit quantization with LoRA adapters to further reduce GPU memory usage while maintaining full fine-tuning performance. Specifically, QLoRA stores the base model weights in 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) format and backpropagates through them into LoRA adapters using 16-bit precision, recovering full fine-tuning performance at a fraction of the memory cost. It also introduces:

–

Double Quantization
: Further compresses quantization constants to reduce storage overhead.

–

Paged Optimizers
: Uses unified memory to prevent out-of-memory errors when training large models.

These innovations enable fine-tuning of models up to 65B parameters on a single 48GB GPU without degradation.

Recent work, such as Tina et al.
Wang et al. 2025
, explores combining GRPO with LoRA for efficient reasoning model training. In this paper, we extend this idea by employing
LoRA with GRPO on quantized models
, enabling scalable reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning of large reasoning models within practical compute budgets.

Research Motivation and Contributions

Despite the promising results of RL-based reasoning model training, the computational costs remain prohibitive for many research groups. Current training practices often rely on heuristic stopping criteria, potentially leading to either premature termination or wasteful over-training. This paper addresses these challenges by developing a predictive framework for understanding and optimizing GRPO training efficiency.

Our key contributions include:

•

A comprehensive empirical analysis of GRPO training dynamics across multiple model architectures and sizes

•

Derivation of scaling laws that predict model reward trajectories during training

•

Identification of consistent learning phases that enable optimal stopping criteria

•

A reproducible experimental framework using parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques

Methodology

Experimental Setup

To ensure reproducibility and accessibility, we designed our experiments using minimal computational resources while maintaining scientific rigor. Our approach combines LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) with GRPO to fine-tune open-source models on publicly available datasets.

Model Selection

We selected four representative models spanning different sizes and architectural families:

•

Llama family
Quantized Llama-3B [unsloth/llama-3.2-3b-instruct-unsloth-bnb-4bit] and Llama-8B [unsloth/meta-llama-3.1-8b-instruct-unsloth-bnb-4bit]

•

Qwen family
: Quantized Qwen-3B [unsloth/qwen2.5-3b-instruct-unsloth-bnb-4bit] and Qwen-7B [unsloth/qwen2.5-7b-instruct-unsloth-bnb-4bit] parameters

This selection enables systematic analysis of both architectural differences and scaling effects while maintaining computational feasibility.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

We employ LoRA for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of quantized models, which provides several advantages:

•

Reduced memory requirements
: Quantization significantly decreases memory consumption

•

Modularity
: Training only a small portion of parameters enables easy toggling of reasoning behavior

•

Computational efficiency
: Substantial reduction in training time and resource requirements

Training Algorithm

Our training process utilizes GRPO with the following key characteristics:

•

Baseline estimation
: Group-based baseline calculation eliminates the need for a separate critic model

•

Reward optimization
: Direct optimization of reasoning task rewards

•

Policy constraints
: Proximal policy optimization principles maintain training stability

Dataset

We utilize the OpenThoughts dataset
Guha et al. 2025
called Math-lightEval
Hendrycks et al. 2021
, which provides high-quality reasoning examples across diverse problem domains. This dataset choice ensures:

•

Reproducibility
: Publicly available and well-documented

•

Quality
: Carefully curated reasoning traces

•

Diversity
: Broad coverage of reasoning task types

Training Setup

We run all our trainings on single Nvidia A100 with 40GB VRAM. We use unsloth
Han, Han, and team 2023
for hardware efficient training and open-r1
Hugging Face 2025
as reference code for our experiments. A very big problem in performing Reinforcement Learning for LLMs is that one needs to run auto regressive inference to generate samples to then later evaluate. This becomes a bottleneck for any RL training on LLM. To help with that, vLLM
Kwon et al. 2023
is used as the preferred inferencing engine. This creates an additional memory requirement to load and store the model’s weights, KVCache and Activations thus putting additional pressure on GPU RAM. In our observations, more than 90% of the time is spent generating sample completions. Both the memory requirement and training time go up as the sequence length and number of completions go up. So to make sure that we stay within the constraints, we choose 8192 as the maximum sequence length with 8 completions per prompt.

Evaluation Methodology

Our evaluation framework focuses on reward progression throughout training, enabling detailed analysis of learning dynamics. We track model performance at regular intervals and identify key transition points in the learning process.

Our experimental results reveal remarkably consistent learning patterns across all tested models, regardless of size or architectural family. Every model exhibits a distinct sigmoid-shaped (S-shaped) learning curve characterized by three well-defined phases:

1.

Initial Phase
(0.0 to 0.1 normalized training steps): A period of slow initial progress where reward increases minimally, suggesting the model is learning basic task structure and reward signal interpretation.

2.

Rapid Growth Phase
(0.1 to 0.2 normalized training steps): A steep and rapid increase in reward, representing the most efficient phase of learning. The inflection point consistently occurs around the 0.2 mark across all models.

3.

Plateau Phase
(0.2 onwards): The rate of improvement slows dramatically, and model performance converges to a stable maximum reward level, indicating diminishing returns from continued training.

Scaling Law Fitting and Analysis

To quantify the observed learning patterns, we fitted a comprehensive scaling law equation across all datasets. The empirical scaling law takes the form:

R(t)=\alpha\cdot rinit+\beta\cdot s+\frac{\gamma}{1+\exp(-\delta\cdot(t-t_{0}))}

where:

•

R(t)
is the reward at normalized training step
t

•

r_{init}
is the initial reward specific to each model configuration

•

s
is the model size parameter (in Billions)

•

\alpha
is the initial scale factor

•

\beta
is the model scale factor

•

\gamma
is the sigmoid amplitude factor

•

\delta
is the sigmoid steepness factor

•

t_{0}
is the inflection point

The fitted global parameters across all datasets are:

•

\alpha=1.009
(initial scale)

•

\beta=2.500
(model scale factor)

•

\gamma=0.401
(sigmoid factor)

•

\delta=39.875
(steepness factor)

•

t_{0}=0.1
(inflection point)

Theoritical Reward Scaling Law

We observe that the progression of reward during training follows a consistent power-law behavior with respect to training steps. The normalized reward difference can be modeled as:

\frac{R_{\max}-R_{t}}{R_{\max}-R_{\min}}=N_{t}^{-\alpha}

(5)

where:

•

R_{t}
denotes the reward at step
t
,

•

R_{\max}
and
R_{\min}
are the maximum and minimum observed rewards during training,

•

N_{t}
is the training step

•

\alpha\approx 0.3
is an empirically determined exponent.

This relation implies a decelerating improvement in reward as training progresses, reflecting a regime of diminishing returns, as mentioned in
Schulman et al. 2017
too. The power-law structure offers a compact means of modeling learning dynamics and may aid in predicting saturation points or optimizing training schedules.

Model Size Impact on Performance

Our results demonstrate a strong correlation between model size and final achievable performance:

•

Llama family
: Llama-8B plateaus at approximately 20.8 reward, while Llama-3B reaches only 8.7 reward

•

Qwen family
: Qwen-7B achieves approximately 19.8 reward, compared to Qwen-3B’s 8.4 reward

This 2.4× performance difference between larger and smaller models within each family suggests that parameter count is a dominant factor in determining reasoning capability.

Architectural Consistency

Remarkably, models of similar size across different architectural families achieve comparable performance levels:

•

Larger models
: Llama-8B (20.8) and Qwen-7B (19.8) show similar final performance

•

Smaller models
: Llama-3B (8.7) and Qwen-3B (8.4) converge to comparable reward ranges

This observation suggests that model scale is more influential than architectural differences in determining reasoning performance under GRPO training.

Training Timeline Consistency

Despite significant differences in final reward achievement, all models follow remarkably similar training timelines:

•

Rapid learning initiation
: All models begin steep improvement at approximately 0.1 normalized steps

•

Plateau onset
: Performance stabilization occurs around 0.2 normalized steps across all models

•

Phase duration
: The rapid growth phase spans approximately 0.2 normalized steps for all models

This temporal consistency implies that while the magnitude of learning is determined by model size, the relative speed and phases of learning are universal across the tested architectures.

Computational Efficiency Gains

Our findings enable significant computational savings through informed training decisions:

•

Early stopping
: Training can be terminated at the 0.2 or 0.3 normalized step mark without meaningful performance loss

•

Resource allocation
: Computational resources can be redirected from over-training to exploring different model configurations

•

Cost reduction
: Substantial reduction in training costs while maintaining model quality

Resource planning

While our study provides valuable insights into GRPO training dynamics, several limitations should be acknowledged:

•

Dataset specificity
: Results are based on the OpenThoughts dataset
Hendrycks et al. 2021
and may not generalize to all reasoning task types

•

Model scope
: Our analysis focuses on specific model families and sizes

•

Training configuration
: Results may vary with different hyperparameter settings or training configurations

Future work should explore:

•

Extension to larger model scales and additional architectural families

•

Validation across diverse reasoning datasets and task types

Integration with other parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques

Conclusion

This work presents a comprehensive analysis of GRPO training dynamics for reasoning-capable language models, revealing universal learning patterns that enable more efficient training strategies. Our key findings include the identification of consistent sigmoid-shaped learning curves across different model sizes and architectures, the derivation of predictive scaling laws for training optimization, and the demonstration of significant computational savings through informed stopping criteria.

The practical implications of this research extend beyond theoretical understanding to actionable insights for the research community. By providing a quantitative framework for predicting training progression, we enable researchers to make data-driven decisions about resource allocation and training duration. The potential for substantial computational savings while maintaining model quality represents a significant advancement in making reasoning-capable LLMs more accessible to a broader research community.

Our findings suggest that the paradigm shift toward test-time computation in reasoning models can be effectively supported by more efficient training strategies. As the field continues to evolve, the predictive framework presented here offers a foundation for further optimizations in reinforcement learning-based model development.

The universality of learning patterns across different model architectures and sizes suggests that these insights may generalize to other reasoning enhancement approaches beyond GRPO. This work contributes to the broader goal of developing more efficient and accessible methods for creating reasoning-capable AI systems.
