Forgetting as a Feature: Cognitive Alignment of Large Language Models
Hien Tran, Quinten Steenhuis, Alexandros Christoforos, Chadbourne Davis

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are often evaluated against ideals of perfect Bayesian inference, yet growing evidence shows their in-context updates resemble discounted filters that systematically forget past evidence. Rather than viewing this forgetting as a limitation, we reframe it as a cognitive feature. Drawing inspiration from psychological studies on human memory decay, we model LLM inference as a probabilistic memory process governed by exponential forgetting curves. To evaluate this perspective, we introduce a benchmark suite spanning temporal reasoning, concept drift, and associative recall, enabling direct comparisons between LLM forgetting dynamics and human behavioral data. Our analysis uncovers striking parallels: LLMs exhibit forgetting rates consistent with human-like efficiency trade-offs between stability and adaptability. Building on these insights, we propose probabilistic memory prompting, a method that shapes evidence integration to mimic human memory traces, yielding improved performance on long-horizon reasoning tasks. This work bridges machine learning and cognitive science, positioning forgetting not as a weakness but as a guiding principle for adaptive reasoning systems.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as versatile general-purpose learners, demonstrating remarkable few-shot generalization through in-context learning and sustained progress across diverse domains

,
1
,

]
. Yet, despite their impressive capabilities, recent studies suggest that LLMs do not operate as ideal Bayesian reasoners with perfect memory. Instead, they exhibit systematic
forgetting

While prior work has largely framed such forgetting as a form of deficiency—manifested in catastrophic forgetting during continual training or information loss in pre-training dynamics—its broader implications remain underexplored.

Recent investigations have begun to quantify and analyze forgetting in LLMs. For example, Liao et al.
[

,
12
,
11
]
reveal that models gradually lose access to previously seen knowledge during pre-training, echoing the phenomenon of
knowledge decay
. Other efforts examine forgetting in continual fine-tuning scenarios, where task-specific performance diminishes over sequential updates. However, these perspectives typically regard forgetting as a problem to be minimized or eliminated, overlooking the possibility that forgetting itself may serve as a
functional adaptation mechanism
. In cognitive psychology, forgetting is not only inevitable but beneficial: it enables humans to prioritize relevant evidence, adapt to non-stationary environments, and balance the competing demands of stability and plasticity.

In this work, we embrace a new perspective:
forgetting as a feature rather than a flaw
. We conceptualize LLM inference as a probabilistic memory process governed by exponential decay, drawing parallels to well-documented human memory curves
[
21
,
13

cognitive alignment
, suggesting that LLMs’ memory dynamics may reflect rational trade-offs between retaining stability and enabling efficient adaptation. Building on this insight, we introduce a benchmark suite designed to evaluate LLM forgetting in tasks spanning temporal reasoning, concept drift, and associative recall, alongside human behavioral baselines. Through this comparative lens, we uncover striking similarities between LLMs and humans in the efficiency of memory decay.

Finally, we operationalize these insights into a practical method we term
probabilistic memory prompting
, which shapes evidence integration in LLMs according to human-like forgetting traces. This approach not only improves long-horizon reasoning but also provides a cognitively motivated pathway to aligning LLM inference with adaptive principles observed in human memory.

relate work

Forgetting and Memory Dynamics in Neural Models.

The phenomenon of forgetting has long been regarded as a limitation in machine learning systems, particularly in continual and lifelong learning settings where maintaining previously acquired knowledge is critical
[
4
,
9
,
19
]
. Conventional approaches typically aim to mitigate forgetting through mechanisms such as parameter regularization, rehearsal buffers, or architectural isolation. However, recent empirical observations suggest that large language models exhibit structured and systematic patterns of forgetting even during inference, rather than purely random degradation. These behaviors indicate that forgetting may not merely be a defect of optimization or capacity, but rather an emergent property related to how models manage uncertainty, relevance, and temporal information. Such observations motivate a rethinking of forgetting as a potentially functional mechanism that enables models to adapt to evolving contexts and reduce interference from outdated or irrelevant information.

Cognitive Perspectives and Probabilistic Modeling of Memory.

In cognitive science, forgetting has long been understood as an adaptive process that supports efficient reasoning by balancing stability and flexibility
[
17
,
1
,
18
]
. Human memory does not retain all past information uniformly; instead, it exhibits graded decay shaped by relevance, recency, and contextual importance. Inspired by these principles, recent computational perspectives have begun to reinterpret neural inference as a form of approximate Bayesian reasoning under resource constraints, where memory decay emerges naturally from rational trade-offs between accuracy and efficiency. Within this view, forgetting is not a failure to preserve information, but a mechanism that enables robust generalization and rapid adaptation. Building on this perspective, our work models forgetting as a controllable and quantifiable component of inference, and leverages it as a tool for improving long-horizon reasoning. By explicitly aligning model behavior with cognitively motivated memory dynamics, we bridge the gap between human-like memory processes and large-scale neural inference.

We model the LLM’s sequential inference as an approximate Bayesian process characterized by exponential forgetting. Let
\theta
be latent world-state parameters and
\mathcal{D}_{1:t}
be observations up to time
t
. We posit that LLMs deviate from ideal Bayesian inference by applying a temporal discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1]
, or a
memory decay parameter
, to past evidence. This yields our
cognitively-aligned Bayesian update
[
14
,
10
]
:

p_{t}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{1:t})\propto\underbrace{p(D_{t}\mid\theta)}_{\text{Likelihood}}\cdot\underbrace{\big[p_{t-1}(\theta\mid\mathcal{D}_{1:t-1})\big]^{\gamma}}_{\text{Discounted Prior}}.

(1)

Here,
\gamma=1
recovers exact Bayesian inference, while
\gamma\to 0
leads to a memoryless state reliant only on the current likelihood. This formulation captures the stability-plasticity trade-off.

To quantify the model’s intrinsic forgetting, we calibrate an optimal discount factor,
\gamma^{*}
, by minimizing the KL divergence between the LLM’s observed predictive distribution
\hat{p}_{\text{LLM},t}
and our theoretical model
p_{\text{Bayes},t}
:

\gamma^{*}=\arg\min_{\gamma\in(0,1]}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\text{KL}}\left(\hat{p}_{\text{LLM},t}\|p_{\text{Bayes},t}(\cdot;\gamma)\right).

(2)

C=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}

w_{i}=\frac{\exp(-\lambda(t-i))}{\sum_{j=1}^{t}\exp(-\lambda(t-j))},\quad\forall x_{i}\in C,

3.4
Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate PMP against two baselines: standard full-context prompting and a recency-based truncation ("sliding window"). The evaluation spans three categories: (i)
Synthetic Non-Stationary Probes
, including biased die, shifting-mean Gaussian process, and variable recall delay tasks; (ii)
Human-Aligned Memory Tasks
, designed to mirror human memory experiments for direct comparison of forgetting curves and patterns; and (iii)
Long-Horizon Reasoning Benchmarks
, covering NLP tasks requiring long-term dependency understanding such as multi-hop QA (e.g., HotpotQA) and sequential planning. Performance is measured using predictive KL divergence, task-specific accuracy under distribution shifts, and curve-fitting error against established human forgetting laws. We hypothesize that PMP will demonstrate superior robustness under concept drift and stronger alignment with human memory dynamics.

The "reflect-and-correct" procedure actively repairs the reasoning path, salvaging potentially correct solutions that would have otherwise been discarded. This ability to dynamically identify and rectify errors in-situ is the primary driver behind the performance gains we observe, confirming that correction is a more powerful mechanism than mere rejection.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we revisit the role of forgetting in large language models and argue that it should be viewed not as a deficiency, but as a functional mechanism aligned with principles of human cognition. By framing model inference as a form of probabilistic memory updating, we show that the apparent loss of past information can be interpreted as an adaptive process that balances stability and plasticity under limited computational resources. This perspective enables a principled understanding of how large models manage long-context reasoning and respond to non-stationary information.
Building on this insight, we introduce a cognitively motivated framework that explicitly models forgetting through controllable memory decay.
