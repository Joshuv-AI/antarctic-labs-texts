Small-signal stability of power systems with voltage droop
Jakob Niehues, Robin Delabays, Anna Büttner, Frank Hellmann

Abstract
The stability of inverter-dominated power grids remains an active area of research. This paper presents novel sufficient conditions for ensuring small-signal stability in lossless and constant R / X R/X grids with highly heterogeneous mixes of grid-forming inverters that implement an adapted V V – q q droop control. The proposed conditions can be evaluated in the neighborhood of each bus without information on the rest of the grid. Apart from the presence of V V – q q droop, no additional assumptions are made regarding the inverter control strategies, nor is dynamical homogeneity across the system assumed. The analysis is enabled by recasting the node dynamics in terms of complex frequency and power, resulting in transfer functions that directly capture the small-signal frequency and amplitude responses to active and reactive power imbalances. These transfer functions are directly aligned with typical design considerations in grid-forming control. Building on an adapted small-phase theorem and viewing the system as a closed feedback loop between nodes and lines, the derived stability conditions also yield new insights when applied to established inverter control designs. We demonstrate in simulations that our conditions are not overly conservative and can identify individual inverters that are misconfigured and cause instability.

VI
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we derived fully decentralized small-signal stability conditions for power grids under the assumption of
V
-
q
droop and homogeneous
R/X
ratio for the lines. The preceding results provide a simple characterization of small-signal stability of heterogeneous grids in terms of transfer matrices between power mismatch on the input side, and frequency and voltage velocity on the output side. Such transfer function-based specifications are natural for the design and specification of decentralized power grid control strategies, and could potentially be directly encoded in grid codes
[
33
]
. This is especially interesting as the transfer functions we are concerned with can be measured experimentally
[
22
]
.

The type of conditions derived here are robust in the sense that, if the numerical range of a nodal transfer matrix is bounded away from zero for all
s
on the contour (see proof), a perturbation of the transfer matrix of
H_{\infty}
norm smaller than the bound, can not make the system unstable. However, as we have to assume an exact droop relationship, this robustness does not yet easily extend to actual system parameters.

As the complex frequency approach
[
19
]
can also capture load models and grid-following control, as shown in
[
34
]
, we expect that our results can be adapted to load models. A starting point for an extension to line dynamics is given in
[
28
]
. An alternative approach would be to use the observation in Appendix B of
[
21
]
that line dynamics in the case of a homogeneous R/X ratio are essentially a low-pass filter on the nodal power flow that can be absorbed into the node dynamics.

The most significant challenge for our approach is to accurately account for non-droop-like reactions to voltage amplitude deviations. This also prevents us from directly applying the theory to conventional models in the presence of losses. Naively adding in additional voltage dynamics on the nodal side fails due to the sectoriality constraints. Similarly, models that do not have a pass-through like
\delta_{n}
in (
13
) fail our conditions at infinite imaginary

Phase stability preliminaries

Our results are based on the Generalized Small Phase Theorem of Chen
et al.

[
23
]
. We prove a straightforward proposition stating that if the transfer matrices of the system under consideration have a block structure, the global stability conditions can be decomposed into local conditions. An immediate application are networked systems that consist of node and edge variables that are coupled according to a graph.

Using this proposition we give a precise statement of the stability conditions for a power grid of general grid-forming grid actors with

-
q
droop as introduced above.

For completeness, we begin by recalling the Small Phase Theorem of

W

[

39
]

\displaystyle=\left\{{\bm{z}}^{\dagger}{\bm{M}}{\bm{z}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\,,\penalty\ {\bm{z}}^{\dagger}{\bm{z}}=1\right\}\,,

(31)

\displaystyle W^{\prime}({\bm{M}})

\displaystyle=\left\{{\bm{z}}^{\dagger}{\bm{M}}{\bm{z}}\penalty\ |\penalty\ {\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\,,\penalty\ {\bm{z}}^{\dagger}{\bm{z}}>0\right\}\,.

(32)

When the numerical range lies in a half complex plane, we introduce the notion of

Assume that 0 is not in the interior of

The phase center is defined as

Linear form of power grids with

To make use of Proposition

In this section, we show that the power grid can be represented as an interconnected feedback system of two transfer matrices:
{\bm{T}}^{\rm nod}\#{\bm{\mathcal{T}}^{\rm net}}
.

{\bm{T}}^{\rm nod}
includes all nodal transfer matrices from
\hat{q}_{n}
and
p_{n}
to
\varrho_{n}
and
\omega_{n}
, as in (
2

{\bm{\mathcal{T}}^{\rm net}}
represents the network structure and the physics of the coupling, as it takes
\bm{\varrho}
and

and

The fundamental assumption we make is that the nodes can be modeled as voltage sources that react to conditions in the grid. This assumption is most natural in the context of grid-forming actors, such as power plants or grid-forming inverters.

C-A

Complex frequency notation

As noted above, every node has a complex voltage (representing a balanced three-phase voltage)
v_{n}=v_{d,n}+jv_{q,n}
:

\displaystyle v_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=V_{n}(t)e^{j\varphi_{n}(t)}=e^{\theta_{n}(t)}\,,

(39)

and a complex current
\imath_{n}
. The latter is given in terms of the former through the admittance matrix
\bm{Y}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\imath}(t)=\bm{Y}\cdot\bm{v}(t)=-j\bm{L}\cdot\bm{v}(t)\,.

(40)

The matrix
\bm{L}:=je^{-j\kappa}\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a real, symmetric, positive definite Laplacian.
We show the proof for lossless grids, where
\kappa=0

V
.

We use a power-invariant transformation from
ABC

S_{n}(t)=v_{n}(t){\overline{\imath}}_{n}(t)=p_{n}(t)+jq_{n}(t)
with active power
p_{n}(t)
and reactive power
q_{n}(t)
.

Milano
[
19
]
suggests writing the nodal dynamics through the time derivative of the complex phase
\theta_{n}
, the complex frequency
\eta
:

\displaystyle\eta_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{\theta}_{n}(t)\,,

(41)

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\eta_{n}(t)v_{n}(t)

(42)

\displaystyle=(\varrho_{n}(t)+j\omega_{n}(t))v_{n}(t)\,.

(43)

We will drop the explicit time dependence
(t)
from now on. By considering both, the complex equation and the complex conjugate equation,

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{n}

\displaystyle=\eta_{n}v_{n}\,,

(44)

\displaystyle\dot{\overline{v}}_{n}

\displaystyle={\overline{\eta}}_{n}{\overline{v}}_{n}\,,

(45)

we can switch back and forth between complex and real picture, using a linear transformation. The velocities
\varrho_{n}
,
\omega_{n}
,
\eta_{n}
and
{\overline{\eta}}_{n}
are related by:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{n}\\
{\overline{\eta}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&j\\
1&-j\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{n}\\
\omega_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{U}\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{n}\\
\omega_{n}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(46)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{n}\\
\omega_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
-j&j\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{n}\\
{\overline{\eta}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{U}^{\dagger}\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{n}\\
{\overline{\eta}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(47)

Note that
\bm{U}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\bm{U}^{\dagger}
, thus
\bm{U}/\sqrt{2}
is a unitary matrix. This means that under
\bm{U}
as coordinate transformation, all pertinent properties of linear dynamical systems are retained.

A system of grid-forming actors

We are interested in conditions that guarantee small-signal stability of a heterogeneous system of grid-forming actors, without strong assumptions on their internal structure. As noted above, we assume that we can model the nodes as voltages reacting to the grid state. We assume that the voltages react in a smooth, differentiable manner, and that
V_{n}>0
. Thus,
\omega_{n}
and
\varrho_{n}
are defined, and can be chosen as the nodal output variable. Using
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
as the input that the nodal actor sees from the grid, we can write the general form of a node’s behavior in terms of three functions
r_{n}
,

and
{\bm{f}}^{\bm{x}}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\varrho_{n}

\displaystyle=r_{n}(\varphi_{n},V_{n},p_{n},q_{n},\bm{x}_{n})\,,

(48)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}

\displaystyle=o_{n}(\varphi_{n},V_{n},p_{n},q_{n},\bm{x}_{n})\,,

(49)

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}_{n}

\displaystyle={\bm{f}}^{\bm{x}}_{n}(\varphi_{n},V_{n},p_{n},q_{n},\bm{x}_{n})\,.

(50)

Here,
\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{var}}}
are internal states of dimension
n_{\text{var}}
that reflect the inner workings of the grid actor, and are not visible directly in the output
v
. Examples include generator frequencies, inner-loop DC voltages, or the

- and
q
-components of internal AC quantities.

We make two assumptions on the form of the functions
r_{n}
,

and
{\bm{f}}^{\bm{x}}_{n}
: I) Following
[
20
]
, we assume that the nodal dynamics does not explicitly depend on

. This assumption is justified by symmetry considerations and the desire to not introduce harmonic disturbances into the grid. II) We assume that the reaction to a deviation in the voltage mirrors that of a deviation in the reactive power. That is, we assume that near the operation point,
r_{n}
,

and
{\bm{f}}^{\bm{x}}_{n}
only depend on
\hat{q}_{n}=q_{n}+\alpha_{n}V_{n}
for some real

rather than on both
q_{n}
and
V_{n}
separately. With these assumptions we have:

\displaystyle\varrho_{n}

\displaystyle=r_{n}(p_{n},\hat{q}_{n},\bm{x}_{n})\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}

\displaystyle=o_{n}(p_{n},\hat{q}_{n},\bm{x}_{n})\,,

(52)

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}_{n}

\displaystyle={\bm{f}}^{\bm{x}}_{n}(p_{n},\hat{q}_{n},\bm{x}_{n})\,.

(53)

C-C

The linearized nodal response

We define the coefficients of the Jacobian as

\displaystyle J_{n}^{\omega p}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\partial o_{n}}{\partial p_{n}}\,,

\displaystyle J_{n}^{\varrho\hat{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial r_{n}}{\partial(\hat{q}_{n})}\,,

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{n}^{xx}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{f}_{n}^{x}}{\partial\bm{x}_{n}}\,,

etc.

(54)

We now want to look at the linear response of the nodal subsystem around an operating point
v_{n}^{\circ}
,
i_{n}^{\circ}
. We assume that the operating point satisfies
\varrho_{n}^{\circ}=\omega_{n}^{\circ}=\dot{\bm{x}}_{n}=0
. Write
\Delta p_{n}=p_{n}-p_{n}^{\circ}
and
\Delta\hat{q}_{n}=q_{n}-q_{n}^{\circ}+\alpha_{n}(V_{n}-V_{n}^{\circ})
and assume that
{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\circ}=\bm{0}
.
The linearized nodal dynamics are then

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\bm{J}_{n}^{xp}\Delta p_{n}+\bm{J}_{n}^{xq}\Delta\hat{q}_{n}+\bm{J}_{n}^{xx}\bm{x}_{n}\,,

(55)

\displaystyle\varrho_{n}

\displaystyle=J_{n}^{\varrho p}\Delta p_{n}+J_{n}^{\varrho\hat{q}}\Delta\hat{q}_{n}+\bm{J}_{n}^{\varrho x}\bm{x}_{n}\,,

(56)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}

\displaystyle=J_{n}^{\omega p}\Delta p_{n}+J_{n}^{\omega\hat{q}}\Delta\hat{q}_{n}+\bm{J}_{n}^{\omega x}\bm{x}_{n}\,.

(57)

which we stack as

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{x}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\bm{J}_{n}^{xqp}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\hat{q}_{n}\\
\Delta p_{n}\end{bmatrix}+\bm{J}_{n}^{xx}\bm{x}_{n}\,,

(58)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{n}\\
\omega_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\bm{J}_{n}^{\varrho\omega\hat{q}p}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\hat{q}_{n}\\
\Delta p_{n}\end{bmatrix}+\bm{J}_{n}^{\varrho\omega x}\bm{x}_{n}\,.

(59)

The nodal transfer matrix from
\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\hat{q}_{n}&\Delta p_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}
to
\begin{bmatrix}\varrho_{n}&\omega_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}
is then just

\displaystyle-{\bm{T}}_{n}(s)

\displaystyle=\bm{J}_{n}^{\varrho\omega\hat{q}p}+\bm{J}_{n}^{\varrho\omega x}(s-\bm{J}_{n}^{xx})^{-1}\bm{J}_{n}^{xqp}\,.

(60)

We can summarize the transfer matrices of all nodes in
{\bm{T}}^{\rm nod}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\varrho}\\
\bm{\omega}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle={\bm{T}}^{\rm nod}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\hat{q}}\\
\Delta\bm{p}\end{bmatrix}\coloneqq\bigoplus_{n}{\bm{T}}_{n}(s)\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\hat{q}}\\
\Delta\bm{p}\end{bmatrix}.

(61)

C-D

The linearized network response

To obtain the full linearized equations, we need the response of
\Delta p_{n}
and
\Delta\hat{q}_{n}
to variations in the complex angle
\theta_{n}
around a given power flow with
\theta_{n}^{\circ}
.

This is most easily given in terms of a variant of the complex power and the complex couplings introduced by
[
21
]
. We define

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq q_{n}+jp_{n}\,,

(62)

to mirror the definition of the complex frequency
[
19
]
. In terms of the usual complex power, this is
\sigma_{n}=j\overline{S}_{n}
. This complex power can be expressed in terms of the Hermitian matrix
\bm{{K}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
of complex couplings
[
19
,
21
]
:

\displaystyle{K}_{nm}

\displaystyle={\overline{v}}_{n}L_{nm}v_{m}\,,

(63)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}{K}_{nm}\,.

(64)

These quantities have a very simple derivative with respect to the complex phases of the system:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{K}_{nm}}{\partial\theta_{h}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{hm}{K}_{nm}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{K}_{nm}}{\partial{\overline{\theta}}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{hn}{K}_{nm}\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma_{n}}{\partial\theta_{h}}

\displaystyle={K}_{nh}\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma_{n}}{\partial{\overline{\theta}}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{nh}\sigma_{n}\,.

(66)

The linearization of
\sigma_{n}
around an operating state of the system with complex couplings
{K}^{\circ}_{nm}
and complex power
\sigma^{\circ}_{n}
is then given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}\approx\sigma^{\circ}_{n}+\sigma_{n}^{\circ}\Delta{\overline{\theta}}_{n}+\sum_{m}{K}^{\circ}_{nm}\Delta\theta_{m}\,

(67)

or, in vector notation,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\sigma}\\
\Delta\bm{{\overline{\sigma}}}\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{K}}^{\circ}&[\bm{\sigma}^{\circ}]\\
[\bm{{\overline{\sigma}}}^{\circ}]&\bm{{\overline{{K}}}}^{\circ}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\theta}\\
\Delta\bm{{\overline{\theta}}}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(68)

As the nodal dynamics depend on
\Delta\hat{q}_{n}
and
\Delta p_{n}
, as inputs, we now consider

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{n}+\alpha_{n}\Delta V_{n}=\Delta\hat{q}_{n}+j\Delta p_{n}\,,

(69)

for the output of the edge dynamics.
Together with
\Delta V_{n}\approx V_{n}^{\circ}\frac{1}{2}(\Delta\theta+\Delta{\overline{\theta}})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\sigma}+\bm{\alpha}\Delta\bm{V}\\
\Delta\bm{{\overline{\sigma}}}+\bm{\alpha}\Delta\bm{V}\end{bmatrix}\approx\bm{J}^{\text{net}}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\bm{\theta}\\
\Delta\bm{{\overline{\theta}}}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(70)

with the transfer matrix

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{\text{net}}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{K}}^{\circ}+\frac{1}{2}[\bm{\alpha}][\bm{V}^{\circ}]&[\bm{\sigma}^{\circ}]+\frac{1}{2}[\bm{\alpha}][\bm{V}^{\circ}]\\
[\bm{{\overline{\sigma}}}^{\circ}]+\frac{1}{2}[\bm{\alpha}][\bm{V}^{\circ}]&\bm{{\overline{{K}}}}^{\circ}+\frac{1}{2}[\bm{\alpha}][\bm{V}^{\circ}]\end{bmatrix}\,.

(71)

Note that as
\bm{K}^{\circ}
is Hermitian, and so is
\bm{J}^{\text{net}}
. Further, we see from (

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{1}&-\bm{1}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}
is a zero mode of the network response

At this point, we can see the necessity of incorporating the

-
q
droop into the network response. Without the presence of the

would be indefinite and thus not amenable to sectorial analysis.

\varrho_{n}
and
\omega_{n}

Recall that

\displaystyle\Delta\dot{\theta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\eta_{n}\,,

(72)

\displaystyle s\Delta\theta_{n}

\displaystyle=\eta_{n}\,,

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{n}\bm{U}\,,

With this we can write the network response from a deviation in

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathcal{T}}^{\rm net}}(s)

Sectoriality of the nodal transfer matrix

Each
{\bm{T}}_{n}(s)
of the form (

matrix. Here, we give conditions that ensure that it is strictly accretive, meaning the numerical range is contained in the open right half plane:
\underline{\phi}>-\pi/2
and
\overline{\phi}<\pi/2

Is gives especially concise conditions for sectoriality.

Lemma 4
.

A complex
2\times 2
matrix
{\bm{T}}_{n}(s)
is strictly accretive, hence sectorial, if and only if its four entries [see (
2
)] fulfill (
3
) and (
4
):

\displaystyle\Re({T}^{\omega p}_{n})+\Re({T}^{\varrho\hat{q}}_{n})

\displaystyle>0\,,

(77)

\displaystyle\Re({T}^{\omega p}_{n})\cdot\Re({T}^{\varrho\hat{q}}_{n})

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{4}\left|{T}^{\omega\hat{q}}_{n}+\overline{T}^{\varrho p}_{n}\right|^{2}\,.

(78)

Proof.

If the numerical range
W
of
{\bm{T}}_{n}
(see (
31
)) is contained in the right-hand side, the real part of
W({\bm{T}}_{n})
has to be strictly positive:
\Re(W({\bm{T}}_{n}(s)))>0
. The real part of the numerical range is given by the numerical range of the Hermitian part of

, which we denote
\bm{\hat{T}}_{n}(s)=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{T}}_{n}(s)+{\bm{T}}_{n}(s)^{\dagger})

The numerical range of a Hermitian matrix is on the real axis. It is strictly positive if and only if the matrix is positive definite. The two by two matrix
\bm{\hat{T}}_{n}(s)
is positive definite if and only if its determinant and its trace are positive. Expressed in terms of the matrix elements of
{\bm{T}}_{n}(s)
these conditions are (
3
) and (
4
).
∎

Edge-wise decomposition and analysis of the network response

We now return to the network response. Our goal is to show that under the condition that [see (
5
)]

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}\geq\alpha^{\text{theory}}_{n}\coloneqq 2\sum_{m}\tilde{Y}_{nm}\frac{V_{m}^{\circ}}{\cos(\varphi_{n}^{\circ}-\varphi_{m}^{\circ})},

(79)

we can decompose the network response into frequency wise semi-stable and semi-sectorial edge contributions.

Lemma 5
.

\bm{J}^{\text{net}}
can be decomposed into edge-wise contributions
\bm{J}_{e}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{J}^{\textnormal{net}}=\bm{B_{+}}^{\dagger}\bigoplus_{e}\bm{J}_{e}\bm{B_{+}}\,,

(80)

if we introduce an edge-wise decomposition
\alpha^{\prime}_{nm}
of
\alpha_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}=-2V^{\circ}_{n}\sum_{m\neq n}L_{nm}\alpha^{\prime}_{nm}.

(81)

Proof.

The fundamental strategy is to collect the terms that represent each edge.
In each of the four blocks of
\bm{J}^{\text{net}}
, the off diagonal matrix elements naturally have an edge associated to them. The diagonal elements of
\bm{K}^{\circ}
can be written as a sum of edge-wise contributions
K^{\circ}_{nn}=-|V^{\circ}_{n}|^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}L_{nm}
. The
\bm{\sigma}^{\circ}
can be written as
\sigma^{\circ}_{n}=\sum_{m\neq n}K^{\circ}_{nm}-|V^{\circ}_{n}|^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}L_{nm}
. We then introduce a similar decomposition for
\frac{1}{2}\bm{\alpha}
times
\bm{V}^{\circ}
, writing
\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{n}V_{n}^{\circ}=-|V^{\circ}_{n}|^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}L_{nm}\alpha^{\prime}_{nm}
. Now, the contributions to the matrix elements of
\bm{J}^{\text{net}}
associated to an edge
e=(n,m)
all live on the rows and columns associated to
n
and
m
. Thus, we can place them in a
4\times 4
matrix
\bm{J}^{e}

using the matrices
\bm{P}_{e}
of (
28
) that pick out exactly those rows and columns.

To collect these edge-wise contributions, we introduce
C^{\prime}_{nm}\coloneqq\frac{{\overline{v}}_{n}^{\circ}}{v_{n}^{\circ}}(1+\alpha^{\prime}_{nm})-\frac{{\overline{v}}_{m}^{\circ}}{v_{n}^{\circ}}
. Then we can succinctly write the four by four matrix of elements originating from a single edge as
\bm{J}_{e}=-L_{nm}\bm{R}^{\dagger}\bm{\tilde{J}}_{e}\bm{R}
with

\displaystyle\bm{\tilde{J}}_{e}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1+\alpha^{\prime}_{nm}&C^{\prime}_{nm}&-1&0\\
{\overline{C}}^{\prime}_{nm}&1+\alpha^{\prime}_{nm}&0&-1\\
-1&0&1+\alpha^{\prime}_{mn}&C^{\prime}_{mn}\\
0&-1&{\overline{C}}^{\prime}_{mn}&1+\alpha^{\prime}_{mn}\end{bmatrix}.

(82)

and
\bm{R}:=\text{diag}({\overline{v}}_{n}^{\circ},v_{n}^{\circ},{\overline{v}}_{m}^{\circ},v_{m}^{\circ})
.
With this (
80
) can be verified by straightforward calculation, collecting all terms associated to each edge.
∎

As
\bm{J}^{\text{net}}
, and the
\bm{J}_{e}
, are Hermitian, their numerical range is on the real axis. They are (semi-)sectorial, if and only if they are (semi-)definite. In the phase stability theorems, it is assumed that the transfer matrix
G(\epsilon^{+})
has phase center zero. From (

\bm{J}^{\text{net}}
and thus
\bm{J}_{e}
have to be positive semi-definite.

Lemma 6
.

\bm{J}_{e}
is positive semi-definite, hence semi-sectorial, if

\displaystyle|\varphi_{n}^{\circ}-\varphi_{m}^{\circ}|<\frac{\pi}{2}\,\quad\forall\,e=(n,m)\in\mathcal{E},

(83)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}_{nm}\geq\frac{V^{\circ}_{m}}{V^{\circ}_{n}\cos(\varphi_{n}^{\circ}-\varphi_{m}^{\circ})}-1\,.

(84)

Proof.

This can be verified with a straightforward calculation, e.g., using the Schur complement lemma.
∎

The edge-wise decomposition of
\alpha_{n}
leaves us with the freedom to weight the
\alpha^{\prime}_{nm}
freely, as long as they satisfy (
81

The tightest bound is achieved by weighting them proportional to the bounds derived in (

Lemma 7

{\bm{\mathcal{T}}^{\rm net}}(s)
can be decomposed into semi-stable frequency-wise sectorial

\displaystyle{\bm{\mathcal{T}}^{\rm net}}(s)=\bm{\tilde{U}}^{\dagger}\bm{B_{+}}^{\dagger}\bigoplus_{e}\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s)\bm{B_{+}}\bm{\tilde{U}}\,,

if
\alpha_{n}\geq\alpha_{n}^{\textnormal{theory}}

Proof.

The

(86)

According to Lemma

The factor

As

Preliminaries

Let us recall two properties of
W^{\prime}
that will prove useful later on.
First, it follows from the definition of
W^{\prime}
that

\displaystyle W^{\prime}({\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{M}}{\bm{B}})

\displaystyle\subseteq\left(W^{\prime}({\bm{M}})\cup 0\right)\,,

(87)

for any
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
{\bm{B}}
of appropriate size, and therefore,

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}({\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{M}}{\bm{B}})

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\phi}({\bm{M}})\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\phi}({\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{M}}{\bm{B}})

\displaystyle\geq\underline{\phi}({\bm{M}})\,.

(88)

Second, for a block diagonal system
{\bm{M}}=\bigoplus_{e}{\bm{M}}_{e}
, the numerical range is the convex hull of the blocks’ numerical ranges
[
37
, Property 1.2.10]
:

\displaystyle W({\bm{M}})

\displaystyle={\rm Conv}\left(W({\bm{M}}_{1}),...,W({\bm{M}}_{E})\right)\,.

(89)

Thus, if
\bm{M}
is semi-sectorial,

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}({\bm{M}})

\displaystyle=\max_{e}\overline{\phi}({\bm{M}}_{e})\,,

\displaystyle\underline{\phi}({\bm{M}})

\displaystyle=\min_{e}\underline{\phi}({\bm{M}}_{e})\,.

(90)

With this toolbox, we are now ready to prove our main result.
The proof of Proposition
3
relies on the four following Lemmas.

Lemma 8
.

Let
{\bm{T}}_{1},...,{\bm{T}}_{N}
be stable transfer functions. Then
{\bm{T}}(s)=\bigoplus_{n}{\bm{T}}_{n}(s)
is stable.

Proof.

The transfer function
{\bm{T}}(s)
is stable, because the set of its poles is the union of the poles of its blocks.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
{\bm{T}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{T}}_{N}
be frequency-wise sectorial transfer functions. Then,
\bm{T}(s)=\bigoplus_{n}T_{n}(s)
is frequency-wise sectorial if and only if

\displaystyle\max_{n}\overline{\phi}\left({\bm{T}}_{n}(s)\right)-\min_{n}\underline{\phi}\left({\bm{T}}_{n}(s)\right)

\displaystyle<\pi\,,

(91)

for all
s\in j[0,\infty]
, cf. (
35
).

Proof.

Due to (
89
), we have that
W(\bm{T})
is the convex hull of all
W({\bm{T}}_{n})
.
Therefore, if (
91
) is satisfied for all,
W(\bm{T})
is contained in a sector of angle
\delta(\bm{T})<\pi
.
Furthermore, as none of the
W({\bm{T}}_{n})
contain the origin,
W(\bm{T})
does not contains the origin.
We conclude that
{\bm{T}}
is frequency-wise sectorial.
Similarly, if
{\bm{T}}
is frequency-wise sectorial, then none of the
W({\bm{T}}_{n})
contains the origin, and they all lie in a sector of angle smaller than
\pi
and (
91
) holds.
All of the above holds for any
s\in j[0,\infty]
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 10
.

Let
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{1},...,\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{E}
be semi-stable transfer functions and let us define
\bm{\mathcal{T}}(s)=\bigoplus_{e}\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s)
.
Let
{\bm{B}}
be a complex matrix of appropriate dimensions.
Then both
\bm{\mathcal{T}}(s)
and
{\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(s){\bm{B}}
are semi-stable.

Proof.

The transfer function
\bm{\mathcal{T}}(s)
is semi-stable, because the set of its poles is the union of the poles of its blocks.
As the matrix
{\bm{B}}
cannot introduce new poles, the poles of
{\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(s){\bm{B}}
form a subset of the poles of
{\bm{T}}(s)
.
Therefore,
{\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(s){\bm{B}}
is semi-stable.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{1},...,\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{E}
be frequency-wise semi-sectorial transfer functions and let us define
{\bm{T}}(s)=\bigoplus_{e}\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s)
.
Assume further that

\displaystyle\max_{e}\overline{\phi}(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s))-\min_{e}\underline{\phi}(\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s))

\displaystyle\leq\pi\,,

(92)

for all
s\in j\mathbb{R}\setminus j\Omega
,
where
j\Omega
is the union of the poles of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{1},...,\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{E}
that lie on the imaginary axis, cf. (
36
).
Assume that
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{1},...,\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{E}
are all frequency-wise semi-sectorial, and assume furthermore that they are semi-sectorial along the indented imaginary axis avoiding the poles of all
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s)
for indents smaller than some finite
\epsilon^{*}
.
Finally, assume that
{\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(s){\bm{B}}
has constant rank along this indented imaginary axis for some constant complex matrix
{\bm{B}}
of appropriate dimensions.
Then
{\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(s){\bm{B}}
is frequency-wise semi-sectorial.

Remark:

\bm{\mathcal{T}(s)}
is covered with
\bm{B}=\bm{I}
.

Proof.

First observe that if a meromorphic
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s)
has constant rank
r
on a contour, it has constant rank on any infinitesimal deformation of the contour. A matrix of rank
r
has a minor of order
r
with non-zero determinant, and the determinants of all minors of order larger than
r
are zero. As the minors are meromorphic functions, they are either identically zero, or their zeros are isolated points. Thus the rank can only change at isolated points of the meromorphic function. As the rank is constant on the contour, none of these points can be on the contour and we can deform the contour avoiding these points.

Take an
\epsilon<\epsilon^{*}
such that for all
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon
, the imaginary axis with
\epsilon^{\prime}
indentation at
j\Omega
does not hit a rank changing point of any
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s)
,
e\in\{1,...,E\}
.

By assumption, for all
e\in\{1,...,E\}
,

\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{e}(s)
is semi-sectorial and has constant rank on this
\epsilon
-indented imaginary axis (contour).

Combining (
87
), (
89
), and (
92
), semi-sectoriality of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{1}(s),...,\bm{\mathcal{T}}\!\!_{E}(s)
implies semi-sectoriality of
{\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(s){\bm{B}}
, for
s\in j\mathbb{R}
.

Furthermore, by assumption,
{\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(s){\bm{B}}
has constant rank along the
\epsilon
-indented imaginary axis.

Altogether, the above implies that
{\bm{B}}^{\dagger}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(s){\bm{B}}
is frequency-wise semi-sectorial, which concludes the proof.
∎
