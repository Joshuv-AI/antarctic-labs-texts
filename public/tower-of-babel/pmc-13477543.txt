Creating compassionate workplaces: A mixed-methods-based integrated framework on last aiders in the workplace
Authors: Melanie Joshi, Julia Strupp, Helena Kukla, Golrokh Esmaili, Karin Ohler, Raymond Voltz, Georg Bollig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSociety often perceives work and private life as separate spheres which leads to uncertainty about issues such as serious illness, dying, death, and grief within workplace settings. Last Aid Courses (LAC) are established to reduce uncertainty and insecurity towards these issues in the public, but not designed for use in the workplace.ObjectivesTo develop an integrated framework specifically designed for the workplace context to foster compassionate workplaces.MethodsAfter performing 102 LAC in different workplace settings, a mixed-methods design was employed, comprising (a) an online survey among participants (n = 611) including closed questions and open free-text questions, and (b) five focus groups with former course participants (n = 24).ResultsThe vast majority of respondents (94.5%) considered both the topics addressed and the conduction of LAC in the workplace as highly relevant. This relevance was reflected in increased confidence in dealing with the subject matter (96,0%) and a high willingness to recommend the course to others (98.3%). Participants expressed the need for stronger integration of workplace-related topics, particularly concerning grief management, communication training, and labor-law-related aspects. The qualitative data revealed three interrelated levels: 1. Personal level: Dealing with those affected, consisting of shared experience, trust, and the impact of grief on employees; 2. Company level: Structural factors, including (a lack of) managerial support, attitude, and established structures; 3. Development level: Conceptual input encompassing characteristics of potential last aiders in the workplace, ideas for institutionalization, best practice, being integrated into the company structure, support for potential future last aiders, and barriers.ConclusionOur findings confirm the high relevance of the topic within workplace settings and demonstrate that LAC enhance confidence in dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief. The complexity of the issue highlights that sustainable implementation requires workplace-specific approaches. Personal and structural dimensions are closely intertwined and form part of a framework aimed at strengthening workplace-based confidence in responding sensitively to serious illness, dying, death, and grief.

Background

In Germany, with a population of approximately 83,6 million, around one million died in 2025. Of these, 28% were between 15 and 67 years old, indicating a high likelihood that they were employed at the time of death.1,2 Previous findings estimate that about 50% of all bereaved individuals were employed during their time of grief, and about 10% of organization’s employees are affected by grief at any given time. 3 Due to demographic changes, the likelihood of being affected by either serious illness, caring for another person or the death of a loved-one increases. As one response, Last Aid Courses (LAC) for non-professionals have been developed using a community-based approach. These courses are designed to provide essential knowledge and practical skills to enhance the public dialogue about serious illness, dying, death, and grief, to improve confidence in dealing with these issues, and to promote compassionate support within communities.4,5 However, they have not been deployed in the workplace setting.

Historically, industrialization contributed to a separation between work and private spheres. 6 Consequently, topics such as serious illness, dying, death, and grief, traditionally embedded in private life, are often not associated with the workplace.7,8 On one hand, serious illness and grief affect individuals on the physical, emotional, cognitive, social, and spiritual level shown in fatigue, lack of concentration, feelings of anger and guilt, tearfulness, memory lapses, and depression which lead to a significant negative relationship between grief and job behavior and work engagement.3,7,9,10 On the other hand, social and interpersonal interactions as well as psychological and emotional experiences significantly affect grieving individuals. Given that working people spend a substantial part of their lifetime in the workplace, where social interactions occur, these issues inevitably intersect with the professional environment.3,10

Unexpected sick leave may create additional challenges for managing workloads within companies. 3 However, the costs of failing to address these issues may be even higher, potentially leading to increased stress-related absenteeism or presenteeism, were employees are only present, but not productive, accidents at work, loss of business reputation and decreased employee satisfaction, or even termination by the employee.3,11,12

In a labor market characterized by a lack of skilled workers, 13 addressing topics such as serious illness, dying, death, and grief may be crucial for employee satisfaction and serve both, companies and their employees for retain staff.

Existing workplace health policies such as mental health first aid or occupational reintegration management for employees returning to work after an extended illness address related issues, but either limited to a single theme or focus primarily on rehabilitation, rather than providing an integrated framework for dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief. Similarly, previous studies focus on returning to work after serious illness14–18 or chronic disease19,20 and grief.21,22 In contrast to this, the concept of compassionate workplaces is embedded within the framework of Public Health Palliative Care (PHPC), which views end-of-life care as a shared societal responsibility extending beyond healthcare services.23,24 It is closely linked to the Compassionate Communities approach, which promotes the involvement of communities and everyday social institutions, including workplaces, in supporting people affected by serious illness, death, and loss.25,26 Compassionate workplaces are organizational settings that acknowledge and actively support employees experiencing serious illness, caregiving responsibilities, death, dying, or grief through compassionate policies, practices, and workplace cultures. Noticing suffering, understanding the source of distress, showing empathic response and helping relieve suffering are be seen as key factors for compassionate workplaces.10,27,28

The aim of our study was the feasibility, acceptance and possible effects of LAC in the workplace within the overall aim to develop a framework for sensitively dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace based on robust empirical data which addresses the questions of whether and how companies deal with serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace; what measures may support or challenge employees in such situations, and what potential future training of last aiders may need in order to fulfill their roles in the workplace.

Methods

Study design

This study was part of a larger mixed-methods-study, the Last Aiders in the workplace (LAUT)-study including a nationwide representative cross-sectional online survey 2 to examine sensitive approaches to serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace, with the overarching aim of developing a suitable integrated framework. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Board of the Medical Faculty at the University of Cologne (#23-1443_1) and was registered with the German Register for Clinical Trials (DRKS00033422). It will be reported in consolidation with STROBE and COREQ guidelines.

To achieve our research objective, we needed a low-threshold approach to engage with the company’s employees. Therefore, we cooperated with Letzte Hilfe Deutschland gGmbH (the non-governmental organization LAST AID Germany), who provide LAC designed for the broad public to empower people to support the dying and their loved-ones with basic knowledge and compassion. 4 Letzte Hilfe issued a call on their website inviting individuals interested in becoming LAC instructors for the project and providing workplace-based LAC. The training was provided free of charge; however, participants committed to delivering at least two workplace-based LAC within a company. The LAC were in accordance to the national course rules for LAC (https://www.letztehilfe.info/), conducted by two course instructors, one of whom needed to be a health care professional, the other be experienced in the topic area (e.g. grief counselor, hospice volunteer). Based on their specific guidelines, Last Aid Germany trained 125 persons to be LAC instructor. Training for course instructors was conducted by two certified LAC trainers. Due to a high demand of LAC in the workplace, we additionally included 71 already certified LAC instructors.

LAC instructors for the project were then asked to identify suitable companies, which would subsequently receive the LAC free of charge. In cases where instructor faced challenges in recruiting companies or finding a second course instructor, they were supported by the project coordinator (GE) who maintained an overview of all course instructors, course participants and companies.

For the overall study, we implemented a project advisory board consisting of experienced representatives of companies with different backgrounds who supported the development of recruitment strategies and provided input for the conceptual work.

Study procedure, setting and recruitment

The study aimed at the working population in Germany. Public relations efforts were used to raise awareness of the project (e.g., a television report on the first LAC conducted in a company, local press coverage) among companies, and existing networks (e.g. a mailing list for equal opportunity officers) were utilized. We implemented 102 LAC in 75 small to large companies and administrations (e.g., brewery, magazine editorial office, national bank, communal association, pet supply store) in nine German federal states between February 1st 2024 and August 8th 2025. An online survey was implemented after the courses, available via QR-Code only for participants of the LAC. In addition to the online survey, focus groups were seen as appropriate method to create synergizing effects on brainstorming of the concept of a last aider in the workplace. Since participants of the focus group were from different parts of Germany, we chose an online format via zoom to enable participation. MJ (M.A.), a registered nurse and social scientist, experienced in qualitative methods, moderated and led the discussion and GB (M.D.), a palliative care senior resident experienced in LAC training, observed group interaction and took field notes. Participants received a shopping-voucher of 15 Euros as a thank you.

Instruments

Since no validated instrument was available to capture workplace‐related experiences with serious illness, dying, death, and grief, an internal project team (HK, JS, TL) developed a cross-sectional questionnaire tailored to the study’s objectives. The initial pool of topics and potential items was generated through a structured brainstorming process informed by a targeted review of the literature. Subsequently, the multidisciplinary research group—bringing together expertise from health sciences, psychology, palliative care, and sociology—reviewed and refined the questionnaire items through several rounds of discussion until consensus was reached. The draft questionnaire underwent pre-testing with four members of the target group using a think-aloud approach (Charters 2003) to assess clarity, relevance, and completeness. Feedback from these sessions led to several iterative revisions, during which problematic items were reworded or reorganized. Following pilot-testing with seven former participants of the LAC, the instrument was finalized, with the main adjustments focusing on enhancing the precision of item formulations and the overall coherence of the questionnaire. The final instrument included closed questions (n=20) and open-ended free-text questions (n=10) aimed at an insight of willingness, needs and structures to act as a last aider in the workplace (a person to be approached when staff was affected by serious illness, dying, death, and grief). Other segments of the questionnaire comprised exploring possible challenges for an implementation of a last aider in the workplace, relevance of the themes concerning the workplace and relevance of the LAC for the workplace. It was designed to take an average of about 20 minutes to complete but has yet not been validated.

Focus groups were moderated using a semi-structured interview guide (see Annex I) designed by MJ, which was piloted by four members of the multiprofessional team as critical friends and discussed with GB. Only small adjustments were made.

Participants, inclusion, and data collection

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were at least 18 years old, currently employed and former participants of the LAC, able to complete a questionnaire in German, and provided written informed consent. Exclusion criterium was non-participation in the LAC provided in the company. No follow-up was planned; however, all participants were asked in the online survey whether they were willing to participate in the focus group discussion and leave their contact details. Potential participants (n=116) were then contacted with a link to a dedicated project website, which contained a hidden page listing available focus group dates for selection. The webpage was accessible only through this link to ensure that only invited individuals could join the focus group panel. There was no relationship to nor knowledge of the researchers prior to the study commencement since focus group participants were recruited from the online survey and presentation of the researchers were limited to a short introduction of the study objective.

Data processing and analysis

Closed questions were analyzed descriptively concerning frequencies using SPSS 29.0.0.0. Due to the descriptive design, no exposures, predictors, confounders nor effect modifiers were defined. The answers were summarized to a dichotomous format (e.g. “agree” including the response option “fully agree” and “agree”, “disagree” held the options “completely disagree and disagree”).

We analyzed open-ended free-text questions using a qualitative-quantitative approach in Microsoft Excel 2024, allowing for coding and assessing frequencies. Multiple coding was applied whenever a statement matched more than one category. Coding was primarily carried out by MJ and secondary by a non-related research team member (AW) for consistency. Only codes holding more than 10% of the total code frequency in the respective topic will be reported.

MAXQDA 2022 was used for qualitative analysis. The content of the focus group discussion was videorecorded and transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis 29 by MJ and reviewed by GB. We considered inductive thematic analysis, with its interpretive yet not overly abstract approach, to be appropriate, as the focus was on workplace experiences and expectations, with the aim of contributing to the development of a concept for compassionate workplaces. Transcripts were not returned to participants nor discussed with them. Data saturation was reached as repetitions of themes, but no new relevant categories arose from the last focus group.

Quantitative analysis

Course content

Nearly half of the participants (43.2%) reported that they would have benefited from the knowledge provided by the LAC in situations that arose prior to attending. A large majority (94,5%) considered the topics covered by the LAC to be (very) important for the workplace.

Most participants (96,0%) agreed (fully) to have gained confidence in dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief after the LAC. A total of 86,8% believed that the course would have a positive impact on the workplace. 98,3% would recommend the LAC to their colleagues, 93,8% agreed (fully) that a designated last aider responsible for supporting employees on these topics should be implemented in their workplace, and 63,7% can picture themselves in this role.

Course framework

The courses had a mean of 11 participants. Of these, a majority of 88,5% considered the group size adequate. Only few found the group too small (4,3%) or too big (7,2%).

The courses were designed to be open, allowing managers to participate as well and 70,3% of participants reported that managers attended. Consequently, we were interested in examining whether they perceived this as a barrier to participation. Participants of the mixed courses did not feel a barrier in a majority (57,0%) of cases, however, 4,0% did feel a barrier and 9,3% did not know.

Regarding the content, 75,4% found the amount of theoretical input adequate, 18,4% preferred slightly more, and 2,7% wanted significantly more theoretical input. Only 3,5% wished to have had less theoretical input. 4,9% wished for much more practical, 31,1% for little more practical input. 62,2% were satisfied with the amount of practical input.

Qualitative analysis

Free-text analysis

We asked participants which topics were of utmost relevance concerning serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace. Most statements related to communication with affected colleagues (75 out of 154 responses; 48,7%):“The question arises: how do I deal with colleagues who are caring for sick/dying people in their immediate environment or who have lost a loved one? How do I interact with these people in the right way? How do we work together?” (Participant-ID 1181)

“The question arises: how do I deal with colleagues who are caring for sick/dying people in their immediate environment or who have lost a loved one? How do I interact with these people in the right way? How do we work together?” (Participant-ID 1181)

Another topic was serious illness, disability and mental strain (35/154; 22,7%). Additionally, dealing with affected colleagues within the team in the work context was reported (25/154; 16.2%), encompassing issues such as respecting privacy, reduced work efficiency, and the risk of errors arising from strain:“Their thoughts are not on their work, and mistakes may occur. Colleagues do not know how to deal with those affected. Tears cause uncertainty.” (Participant-ID 1253)

“Their thoughts are not on their work, and mistakes may occur. Colleagues do not know how to deal with those affected. Tears cause uncertainty.” (Participant-ID 1253)

Legal, organizational, and financial issues were also considered relevant workplace-related topics (21/154; 13,6%):“How much grief is acceptable? How often can the affected person take time off work? What do colleagues and employers have to cope with and endure, and how much does the death affect everyday working life?” (Participant-ID 321)

“How much grief is acceptable? How often can the affected person take time off work? What do colleagues and employers have to cope with and endure, and how much does the death affect everyday working life?” (Participant-ID 321)

In situations which arose before the LAC, participants wished they had known how to communicate with affected co-workers (83/107; 77,6%):“That I don’t need to be afraid when I'm at a loss for words and still want to be there for my colleague.” (Participant-ID 426)

“That I don’t need to be afraid when I'm at a loss for words and still want to be there for my colleague.” (Participant-ID 426)

Also, a wish for knowledge about professional/voluntary contact points (20/107; 18,6%) existed. The open-ended free-text question regarding what participants would need to adequately fulfil their potential future role as a last aider in the workplace was most frequently answered with a need for further and/or continuing training (331/414; 80,0%). Of those, some participants specified topics they wished to be included in a potential future training, namely legal aspects (9/414; 2.2%) and psychology, communication, or grief counselling (30/414; 7.2%).

Beyond training, participants expressed a desire for further measures to enable them to effectively fulfill a potential future role as last aider in the workplace, including networking opportunities and/or collegial exchange (44/414; 10,6%), sufficient time resources (47/414; 11,4%), and a mentor or contact person and/or access to supervision (45/414; 10, 9%).

When asked about the structural framework, participants indicated that last aiders in the workplace should have a reduced workload or work hours, or the possibility of a leave of absence to fulfil their role (266/311; 85,5%), with some noting potential challenges related to time recording. Another need was access to a separate room for private conversations or a quiet space for grief support (47/311; 15.1%).

In response to a free-text question regarding barriers at the management level for implementing a last aider in the workplace, participants most frequently identified a lack of time and human resources (182/332; 54,8%):“How would that work in everyday working life? When would which type of leave be necessary? That varies from day to day.” (Participant-ID 289)

“How would that work in everyday working life? When would which type of leave be necessary? That varies from day to day.” (Participant-ID 289)

A similarly big barrier was a prevailing organizational culture, which entirely disregarded personal issues in the workplace 135/332 (40,7%), which was described by one participant as:“When I asked if we could take the course for employees, the response was, “What does that have to do with our work?” Even at the management level, there seems to be a lack of willingness to address the issue.)” (Participant-ID 244)

“When I asked if we could take the course for employees, the response was, “What does that have to do with our work?” Even at the management level, there seems to be a lack of willingness to address the issue.)” (Participant-ID 244)

However, nearly a quarter of participants (76/332; 22,9%) reported perceiving no barriers within their company. From the perspective of co-workers, the main barriers for a last aider in the workplace were thematic reluctance and rejection, often linked to major inhibitions such as shame, fear, or concerns about discrimination (145/327; 44,3%):“Employees may be afraid to show their emotions and be vulnerable when dealing with such a private and intimate issue as grief for loved ones.” (Participant-ID 1610)

“Employees may be afraid to show their emotions and be vulnerable when dealing with such a private and intimate issue as grief for loved ones.” (Participant-ID 1610)

Participants also reported potential trust-related barriers, including concerns about being too close to colleagues in the workplace or, conversely, about trusting previously unknown colleagues, as well as doubts regarding confidentiality (66/327; 20.2%). Additional challenges included prioritizing work due to pressure and limited time resources (35/327; 10,7%), as well as low or lacking acceptance and appreciation for the last aider role (41/327; 12,5%).“The team may have to substitute my work when I am busy as last aider in the workplace; no understanding for last aiders in the workplace, ‘nonsense.’” (Participant-ID 1177)

“The team may have to substitute my work when I am busy as last aider in the workplace; no understanding for last aiders in the workplace, ‘nonsense.’” (Participant-ID 1177)

However, a part of the participants (60/327 (18,3%) saw only slight or no barriers concerning their colleagues.

Generally, participants wished the course to have a more specific thematic link to the workplace without mentioning specifying topics (33/81; 40,7%), more input about dealing with grief (26/81;32,1%) and/or information about (labor) law (13/81;16,0%). Beyond thematic content, some participants suggested incorporating more practical methods such as role play, discussion, and exchange (15/81; 18,5%). Nevertheless, the majority (72%) reported that they did not feel any topics were missing.

Personal level

The overarching theme covers both the perspective of those affected and the perspective of those not affected - how they interact with those affected or how they would like to interact with them. Two types of scenarios were identified: In the first case, those affected deal with their issues quietly and do not want sympathy. This seems to be a relief for others, as there are clear instructions on how to behave as a colleague. In the second case, those affected do not talk about their issues and are not approached either. They experience speechless and are relieved when someone addresses them.

One theme is the shared experience of realizing that many co-workers have gone through similar experiences and the sense that they are not alone in facing common challenges such as serious illness, dying, death, and grief, thereby overcoming speechlessness: “In the aftermath of this death, I noticed that in my team […] each of us has experience with end-of-life care, the death of a close relative, a sibling, in other words, someone really close to us, and we've never talked about it. And I think overcoming this inability to talk about it is so important to me.” (Focus group 3, pos. 12)

“In the aftermath of this death, I noticed that in my team […] each of us has experience with end-of-life care, the death of a close relative, a sibling, in other words, someone really close to us, and we've never talked about it. And I think overcoming this inability to talk about it is so important to me.” (Focus group 3, pos. 12)

Trust through discretion, experience, competence and/or familiarity within the (long-standing) team as contributing factors were mentioned as two different yet important features: “[…] it helps to know that the person has—I don't know—already done volunteer work in that field or taken a really in-depth course. I think something like that can build a little trust. Or, as I said at the beginning, if someone has already had a lot of that kind of experience in their personal life, that might also build trust.” “I have been working with my colleagues for a very long time, and that is also beneficial.” (Focus group 2, pos. 82)

Or, as I said at the beginning, if someone has already had a lot of that kind of experience in their personal life, that might also build trust.”

“I have been working with my colleagues for a very long time, and that is also beneficial.” (Focus group 2, pos. 82)

Impact of grief on those affected referred to both those affected and their colleagues or managers describing experiences of grief in the workplace: “Because I’ll miss that person too when they're no longer sitting in their place tomorrow. And I'll have to deal with that somehow. And I think that in a chain like this, the links aren't really forged together yet.” (Focus group 2, pos. 39)

Company level: Structural factors

This overarching theme encompasses experiences with corporate culture as well as the desire for change in corporate culture regarding the topics of serious illness, dying, death, and grief. It relates more to attitudes, cultural norms, and patterns of cooperation within the company than to specific practical aspects, such as institutionalization.

(Lacking) managerial support highlights the importance of managerial involvement and illustrates the effects that insufficient support can have within a company:” We have quite a few managers, and that's where it starts. They have to understand that they need to make employees available [as last aiders] in the first place. Because it's no use if I, as an employee, say, ‘Hey, I feel capable of seeing this through and really helping others,’ and my department head says, ‘I don’t think much of that, I don’t support it.’” (Focus group 5, pos. 129)

” We have quite a few managers, and that's where it starts. They have to understand that they need to make employees available [as last aiders] in the first place. Because it's no use if I, as an employee, say, ‘Hey, I feel capable of seeing this through and really helping others,’ and my department head says, ‘I don’t think much of that, I don’t support it.’” (Focus group 5, pos. 129)

Closely connected to this, the theme of (moral) stance encompasses elements of corporate culture, including openness toward and the de-stigmatization of serious illness, dying, death, and grief, along with an acknowledgment of their importance. “[…]when a company like that decides to train a last aider, that's also intentional. And I think that intentionality is always so important.” (Focus group 3, pos. 41)

“[…]when a company like that decides to train a last aider, that's also intentional. And I think that intentionality is always so important.” (Focus group 3, pos. 41)

On the one hand, established structures reflect a desire for clear routines and designated points of contact providing guidance on what to do or whom to approach when oneself or a colleague is affected by serious illness, dying, death, or grief. On the other hand, existing corporate structures can make it more difficult to address these issues in a personal and appropriate way.“[…] I'm also on the staff council, I have to say, how I learned that there are regulations on how to deal with the death of employees. And if someone dies as a retiree, then it's not mentioned in the obituary, nor is it mentioned in any way on the intranet […] that just doesn't happen, then.” (Focus group 3, pos. 33)

“[…] I'm also on the staff council, I have to say, how I learned that there are regulations on how to deal with the death of employees. And if someone dies as a retiree, then it's not mentioned in the obituary, nor is it mentioned in any way on the intranet […] that just doesn't happen, then.” (Focus group 3, pos. 33)

Development level: Conceptual input

This overarching theme includes both personal and structural aspects required for the implementation of a sustainable framework.

Characteristics of a last aider should be empathy and the ability to listen or make offers to talk. Some participants wished someone with personal experience:“And my experience, […] is that people who have grieved themselves […] are more empathetic because they know what doesn’t feel good, exactly, and what does feel good. And I think that anyone who signs up for this will already have some kind of connection to the topic.” (Focus group 5, pos. 57)

“And my experience, […] is that people who have grieved themselves […] are more empathetic because they know what doesn’t feel good, exactly, and what does feel good. And I think that anyone who signs up for this will already have some kind of connection to the topic.” (Focus group 5, pos. 57)

Ideas for institutionalization refer to both systematic and personal aspects. While employees value the personal engagement of managers, such engagement should not be the decisive factor on how serious illness, dying, death, and grief are addressed in the workplace. However, some specifications should be in place, such as place of retreat, mandatory seminars for managers, but also a low-threshold offer as knowledge of a contact person via corporate communication: “[…] well, most companies and firms have an intranet […], that the people who do it [last aiders] are also introduced, with a personal introduction of the person, which I think is very important, not just a phone number. And yes, where they can be found. So practical things too, such as what issues you can contact these people […] about.” (Focus group 4, pos. 29)

Best Practice examples include positive measures at both the personal and corporal level. Within a smaller circle, several initiatives were taken, such as memorial table, a condolence book or card for relatives, holding conversations with affected colleagues or within the team. External practices involved consultation of (external) professional help, having corporal health management with free sessions associated with the context of burden and a professional external network. Within the companies measures like obituary notice in case of death of an employee, offering LAC, a bus for employees to visit the funeral or an assigned contact person for any issues was mentioned: “And for me, a last aider is similar to a confidant. In our company, we have a confidant who deals with all kinds of issues, and people can come to her with anything, be it minor ailments, personal matters, work-related issues, or anything else.” (Focus group 1, pos. 38)

Being integrated into the company was a theme discussing disadvantages and advantages of a contact person, i.e. last aider within the company or an external solution for dealing with the themes: “On the other hand, if someone in the company is committed to the issue, they naturally also know the structures of the company to a certain extent and can assess to a certain extent what is possible and what is not, or which people can still be approached. That would then be the advantage of [the last aider] having a certain insider knowledge of the company structures. But as a company, we cannot offer that 24/7 with internal staff.” (Focus group 3, Pos. 44)

Support for last aiders confirms the answers of the open-ended free-text questions on needs for last aiders of the survey, which is mostly temporal support, i.e. exemption and emotional support through collegial exchange or supervision:“So that all these skills are gathered in one, two, or three people in a central position within a company, who are then also given the resources to do this work, who receive support through supervision themselves or through collegial case consultation.” (Focus group 1, pos. 34)

Barriers of sensitive dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace were identified across three domains. First, company size appeared to play a certain role, as cultivating a culture of compassion seemed easier to achieve within smaller organizations. Second, gender aspects were highlighted, as men in Germany are more likely to hold managerial positions while at the same time being less likely to participate in programs addressing these topics. This was discussed as one of the challenges in implementing a compassionate workplace. Third, the cultural tendency to separate work from private life, and to keep the personal sphere distinct from the professional one, was also seen as a significant barrier. Additionally, a general reluctance to discuss topics such as serious illness, dying, death, and grief was an overall topic, which also depended on the culture of the company and in the top management: “And it really depends on who you have—let’s say—at the top of an organization, who's in charge […] And if you have someone there who takes it too personally or who can’t find the words themselves, then there's no obituary [in the intranet].” (Focus group 3, Pos. 35)

Discussion

This study examined whether and how companies deal with serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace; what measures may support or challenge employees in such situations, and what potential future last aiders may need in order to fulfill their roles in the workplace. Based on this information, our overall aim was to design a framework for workplaces to address these topics.

Analysis of the focus group discussions revealed considerable variation in how companies address serious illness, dying, death, and grief. Some organizations already have structures, such as corporate health management, that allow these themes to be integrated as an additional domain. Smaller companies, however, may not require such formal structures, while larger organizations with multiple regional branches may lack mutual points of contact, making a “one-size-fits-all” approach neither practical nor effective.

The high perceived relevance and positive impact of the LAC, although not (yet) adjusted to the specifics of the workplace itself, underscore the need for structured support in addressing serious illness, dying, death, and grief. The findings suggest that such training not only increases individual confidence but also may contribute to a more compassionate workplace culture, since the realization of shared experiences already fostered compassion and the majority believe that the course would have positive impact for them in the workplace. However, there are topics for LAC-participants, that were lacking in the course as legal, organizational and financial information.

The data also highlights that fostering both personal and structural preparedness is crucial for sustainable implementation, particularly in workplaces with varying sizes, cultures, and resources.

However, the above-mentioned differences stress the need for a flexible framework that integrates and combines measures across multiple levels. Such a framework should allow for differentiated interventions, tailored to the intensity of needs of the affected individuals, and provide support that is guided by knowledge, skills, and best practices. By considering both organizational context and individual requirements, the framework should enable sustainable and sensitive dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief in diverse workplace settings.

With demographic change leading to a reduced number of professional and informal caregivers who will be able to care for those with serious illnesses, alongside an increasing number of people facing the end of life, professional support in dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief cannot be a feasible option for everyone. Findings of an Australian study show only 6,4% of high risk for prolonged grief disorder. 30 However, as everyone may need support during such challenging times, greater societal engagement is required to ensure that only those with complex needs requiring professional services are referred to them. 31 Based on the Adult Bereavement Care Pyramid, 32 we created an integrated framework for sensitively dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace, including both, laypersons and professionals (see Figure 2) which was presented, critically commented in a project advisory board meeting and adapted. The Adult Bereavement Care Pyramid was chosen as a template, as it holds different levels of needs represented also in our data. As referred to in the focus group discussions, the (top) management is crucial for implementing an integrated framework for dealing sensitively with serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace for (moral) stance, managerial support and intentionality to provide structures to enable such an interaction. This is in line with findings from a meta-analysis showing that, leader as consideration as well as leader initiating structure have impact on perceived organizational support. 33 Figure 2.Hierarchy of themes of focus group discussion.

The levels represent the intensity and impact of the topics and involvement of an individual in the workplace. They correspond to the degree of specialization, as well as to the affected person’s needs, the types of support offered, and the knowledge and skills of those providing that support.

Each level is dynamic and may shift upward or downward over time as needs change, since grief and its impact is highly individual, including complicated grief or hold mild symptoms10,22 The private context encompasses sources of support beyond the workplace - such as family, friends, or cultural and faith communities—while the company culture shapes the overall, lived attitude toward serious illness, dying, death, and grief within the company, key to become a compassionate workplace. The first two levels represent involvement of all employees (level 1) of which some are more experienced than others (level 2), as highlighted with the focus group results on shared experiences, trust, and impact of grief on those affected. Between them and the internal or external professional level (level 4) as described in the themes best practice and being integrated into the company, the Company Last Aider (level 3) emerged as a new resource of a concept for the workplace. This position would mirror the role of the (mandatory) Company First Aider implemented in every company with one or several persons, depending on company size in Germany. 34 Derived from the wish for a further and/or continuing training and results of both the answers to the open-ended free-text questions in the online-survey and focus group discussions lead to an advanced curriculum for Last Aiders in the workplace (see Annex II) based on a basic course (e.g. a LAC) with four basic modules and three advanced modules tailored to suit the needs of persons in the workplace. The course curriculum addresses the Core Skills of the European Association for Palliative Care (EAPC) for education. The results from our study showed the need for knowledge on communication with co-workers affected by serious illness, disability, mental strain and/or grief and dealing with those affected within the team. These topics resulted in a module “Open dialogue in the workplace”. Another aspect of the results was the relevance of legal, organizational and financial topics for the workplace as well as knowledge of professional/voluntary contact points or support networks which were integrated into another module “Providing support in the workplace”. The concern to feel overwhelmed while performing as last aider and the expressed wish for networking-possibilities, collegial exchange or a mentor/contact person or possibilities for supervision were answered in the module “Self-care”.

Conclusion

The implementation of Last Aid in the workplace courses and implementation of Company Last Aiders in open-minded companies can be a first step on the way to a public health policy that ensures sensitive dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief in the workplace thereby fostering compassionate workplaces.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its broad focus on the workplace context, addressing topics that are relevant across different sectors and organizational sizes. The multi-methods design, combining an online survey with focus group discussions, allowed for both quantitative and qualitative insights, enhancing the depth and validity of the findings.

However, several limitations should be considered. Although our themes may be seen as relevant for all workplaces and our acquisition was widely spread, the results may be based on individuals and/or companies that have an interest in the theme or being already involved in company-wide establishment of structures for dealing with serious illness, dying, death, and grief. Additionally, self-selection of participants and availability may have influenced the relevance and impact of the LAC. Due to the descriptive analysis in a study with an explorative approach, we did not do a sample size calculation. The questionnaire although pilot tested, has not been validated within this study which we recommended in future research. Future prospective studies could give an insight into differences in company size and effectiveness concerning the new modules as an intervention or implementation study.
