TiO2-based multifunctional catalytic systems for green and sustainable organic transformations: a comprehensive review
Authors: Rohinee D. Hoval, Santosh T. Shinde, Anandrao A. Kale, Nanasaheb S. Gaikwad, Girish S. Yedase, Digambar B. Bankar, Kaluram G. Kanade, Bhausaheb Dhokale, Dinesh P. Amalnerkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Titanium dioxide (TiO2) has emerged as a cornerstone of semiconductor nanomaterials known for its excellent chemical stability, low toxicity, and highly tuneable physicochemical properties. TiO2-based nanocatalysts have been employed to drive a wide range of organic transformations under green and sustainable conditions. By leveraging large surface area, strong oxidative potential, tunable band gap, and efficient charge separation, these materials serve as effective catalysts for the selective oxidation, reduction, and coupling reactions, often delivering high conversions with good reusability/turnover frequency. Therefore, owing to its tunable properties and versatile applicability, TiO2-based nanocrystalline materials have a significant potential to promote various useful organic transformations with high yields and good reusability. In this review, we discuss how the tunability of TiO2 allows for superior selectivity and reusability, ultimately positioning it as an indispensable tool in the transition toward circular and sustainable chemical manufacturing.

Introduction

Organic transformations are the fundamental backbone of chemical manufacturing, offering efficient synthetic pathways for the construction of complex molecular architectures for pharmaceuticals,1 fine chemicals,2 agrochemicals,3–5 and advanced functional materials.6 Despite their utility, many traditional organic synthetic protocols still depend on hazardous reagents, challenging reaction conditions, and non-renewable feedstocks, imposing significant environmental and economic burden. The escalating demand for the development of effective, selective, and reusable catalytic systems has been spurred by the growing need for environmentally friendly, sustainable chemical processes.7–9 Green chemistry relies heavily on catalysis, as it not only accelerates reaction kinetics but also governs yield, chemo-selectivity, and atom economy, facilitating the synthesis of target compounds in cleaner, more environmentally responsible settings. The pursuit of advanced catalytic systems that combine high intrinsic activity, long-term stability, and minimum ecological footprint remains a challenge in modern chemical synthesis.10–12

Recent advances in catalysis have been made possible by nanotechnology. Unlike their bulk counterparts, nano-catalysts exhibit enhanced reactivity and selectivity, a direct result of their high surface-to-volume ratios, engineered morphologies, and tuneable electronic structures.13 Nano-catalysts effectively bridge the gap between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis, combining the advantages of both, namely the high efficiency of the former with the robust separation and recyclability of the latter.14 In particular, metal and metal-oxide nanocatalysts have garnered considerable interest owing to their distinct redox properties and superior surface chemistry, which enable the activation of small molecules that drive a variety of chemical transformations.15–25

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) has emerged as one of the most extensively studied semiconducting oxides in heterogeneous catalysis, owing to its remarkable synergy of physicochemical and structural properties (Fig. 1). TiO2 is a highly adaptable material for catalytic applications owing to its high surface area, customizable shape, cost-effectiveness, chemical inertness, and nontoxicity.26–29 TiO2 primarily exists in three crystalline polymorphic forms: anatase, rutile, and brookite, each exhibiting distinct catalytic and optical properties, which are briefly discussed in Table 1. The anatase phase is widely favoured for photocatalytic and surface-driven organic transformations due to its relatively large surface area, greater density of surface hydroxyl groups, and enhanced charge carrier mobility, all of which contribute to superior catalytic performance. The rutile phase, as the thermodynamically most stable form, possesses a high refractive index, excellent thermal stability, and strong light absorption capacity, making it well suited for high-temperature catalytic and photothermal applications. Although less common, the brookite phase has garnered increasing attention for its unique surface structure and efficient charge-transfer characteristics, particularly when combined with anatase or rutile, often resulting in a synergistic enhancement of catalytic efficiency.30–34

Despite all of these advantages, the photosensitivity of pristine TiO2 is primarily limited to the ultraviolet region due to its broad bandgap (3.0–3.2 eV). Many studies have focused on doping TiO2 with various metals (Fe, Cu, Ag, Au, Pd) and nonmetals (N, S, C) to alter its electronic structure and overcome this limitation. Metal dopants usually introduce impurity energy levels within the bandgap, promoting more efficient electron–hole separation and charge-carrier recombination.35,36 Non-metal dopants, on the other hand, usually change the valence band structure, expanding light absorption into the visible spectrum. These particular changes not only increase TiO2's photocatalytic activity but also increase its suitability for a variety of organic transformations in both thermal and photochemical settings.37,38

Because of these unique features, TiO2 has been widely used in diverse applications (Fig. 2). Owing to its high refractive index, brightness, and superior UV shielding capabilities, it is commonly used as a white pigment in the paint and pigment industries.39,40 TiO2-based photocatalysts have been used in environmental remediation to purify water and air by efficiently breaking down organic contaminants through photocatalytic reactions.41,42 TiO2-based nanostructures have an important role in the energy industry for photocatalytic hydrogen production, lithium-ion batteries, and dye-sensitized solar cells.43,44 Significant progress in biomedical applications, including implants, antimicrobial coatings, and drug delivery systems, has been made possible by TiO2's biocompatibility and antibacterial qualities.45,46 Also, self-cleaning and anti-fogging surfaces have been designed to leverage TiO2's light-induced hydrophilicity, and its semiconducting properties enable applications in sensors, UV detectors, and electronic devices. This good mobility highlights TiO2's significance as a multipurpose nanomaterial, bridging both fundamental understanding and real-world applications.47,48

Applications of titanium dioxide.

Beyond these traditional applications, TiO2-based nanocatalysts have recently shown significant potential in facilitating a wide range of organic transformations (Fig. 3). Both TiO2 and modified TiO2 systems have been utilized for oxidation,49 reduction,50 condensation,51 esterification,52 and coupling reactions53 under thermal and photocatalytic conditions. TiO2 is a desirable material for green chemical synthesis due to its high selectivity, recyclability, and environmental compatibility. Furthermore, by promoting charge transfer and reducing electron–hole recombination, functional modifications, including doping, heterojunction formation, and the addition of metal nanoparticles, have significantly increased its catalytic efficiency.29

Applications of titanium dioxide in various catalytic reactions.

Despite these developments, there are still several barriers in the way of fully utilizing TiO2's catalytic potential for organic synthesis. Rapid charge-carrier recombination frequently decreases photocatalytic efficiency, and its broad bandgap limits visible-light absorption. Making TiO2 nanostructures with ideal shape, electrical characteristics, and surface chemistry is necessary to overcome these limitations. Therefore, the purpose of this review is to provide an overview of recent developments in the production, modification, and use of TiO2-based nano-catalysts for organic transformations. Understanding how structural tailoring and compositional control affect catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability needs particular attention. To guide the practical development of next-generation TiO2 nanocatalysts for efficient and sustainable organic synthesis, this review identifies current challenges and proposes new research directions.

This review provides a more comprehensive and insightful understanding of TiO2-based catalytic systems, along with an updated synopsis of research published since 2000. Earlier reviews have generally focused on specific reactions or individual catalytic properties, whereas in this review, TiO2 is discussed as a multifunctional material that can act in photocatalytic, thermocatalytic, and support roles within a single framework. Special attention has been given to explaining how various modification techniques, such as doping, heterojunction creation, and surface engineering, can affect catalytic activity and reaction pathways. This review article provides a more relevant interpretation of previous research by classifying the literature by structure–activity relationships and precisely identifying the role of TiO2 across various organic systems. Therefore, our work offers a clearer path for the logical design and future development of effective and sustainable TiO2-based catalytic systems rather than merely updating earlier publications.

Synthetic methods for titanium dioxide

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) can be synthesized using a variety of techniques that employ distinct chemical pathways and precursors (Fig. 4). These techniques have been thoroughly investigated over the last few decades, employing organometallic compounds, titanium alkoxides, and inorganic titanium salts as starting materials.58–60 Researchers and industrial chemists have found these methods quite attractive for their ease of use, affordability, and minimal waste generation.

Various synthetic methods for the preparation of titanium dioxide.

The physicochemical properties of TiO2, including particle morphology, grain size, surface area, and crystalline phase (anatase, rutile, or brookite), are highly affected by several factors, including pH, reaction temperature, solvent type, precursor reactivity, and hydrolysis/condensation conditions. TiO2 with a controlled microstructure and unique surface chemistry can be produced by carefully optimizing these conditions.

The synthesis of high-purity TiO2 nanoparticles with consistent particle size, increased surface area, superior thermal stability, and accurate phase control is the main benefit of these synthetic methods. Furthermore, depending on the reaction conditions and the chosen synthesis route, this method's adaptability enables the synthesis of TiO2 in a variety of morphologies, including nanoparticles, nanorods, nanospheres, and thin films. In conclusion, the most commonly used catalytic material in the organic transformations reported in this article remains nanocrystalline titanium dioxide.61–64

TiO2-based catalytic systems used in various useful organic transformations

Recent developments in organic transformations catalysed by titanium dioxide and its doped derivatives are reported in this review. With a focus on its effectiveness as a sustainable and independent catalytic system for the synthesis of organic molecules, the catalytic role of TiO2 in supporting a range of chemical processes has been thoroughly examined. TiO2, both pure and doped, exhibit exceptional catalytic activity without the need for further supports, highlighting its potential as an adaptable heterogeneous catalyst. To clarify how titanium dioxide and its doped forms affect reaction rates and selectivity in organic transformations, the underlying reaction mechanisms have also been examined.

Oxidation reactions

Selective oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes and ketones using TiO2-based systems

A significant modification in green chemistry is the selective oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes or ketones, which can be conducted under milder conditions and prevents excessive oxidation to carboxylic acids. For this reason, TiO2-based catalysts have been carefully studied; research indicates that structural modification, dopant selection, and reaction conditions have a major impact on conversion, selectivity, and reaction processes. The oxidation processes mediated by TiO2 have been shown to have catalytic pathways (Scheme 1). For example, Luca Samiolo et al. demonstrated that N-doped TiO2 outperforms undoped TiO2 under visible light irradiation for the oxidation of benzyl alcohol and 4-pyridinylmethanol to their corresponding aldehydes. Investigating how nitrogen doping transforms the material provides insight into this increased activity. Nitrogen helps the material to absorb more visible light by creating new energy levels within the band gap. In addition, it creates Lewis acid sites on the surface, which are crucial for reactant interaction. The system's overall activity is eventually increased by such modifications, which facilitate alcohol molecules' ability to adsorb onto the surface and become activated (Scheme 2).65 Daijiro Tsukamoto et al. reported another effective modification involving the WO3/TiO2 composite, in which TiO2 particles are partially coated with WO3. When alcohols are oxidized in water under λ > 350 nm light, this arrangement yields very good aldehyde selectivity. The enhanced selectivity is due to WO3 preventing aldehydes from adhering to the TiO2 surface. So, the generated aldehyde does not remain on the surface for long, which ultimately improves selectivity by preventing further oxidation (Scheme 3).66 Jyotsna Tripathy et al. synthesised polydopamine-coated TiO2 nanotubes, which provide another approach for visible light-driven selective oxidation. As a sensitizer in this system, visible light is absorbed by polydopamine, which then moves electrons into TiO2's conduction band. Additionally, the photogenerated holes participate in the transformation of benzyl alcohol into benzaldehyde. Reactive oxygen species are generated when electrons are transferred and react with dissolved oxygen (O2), thereby aiding the reaction (Scheme 4).67 Systems supported by metal nanoparticles also show great promise. For example, it has been demonstrated that under aerobic vapour-phase conditions, Au nanoparticles supported on mesoporous TiO2 increase the oxidation rate of benzyl alcohol. High selectivity for benzaldehyde is achieved in these systems due to improved catalytic efficiency resulting from uniform dispersion and reduced Au particle sizes (Scheme 5).68 Additionally, Akbar Mahdavi-Shakib et al. exposed crystal facets of TiO2 supports, which play a crucial role in catalytic activity. In Au/TiO2 catalysts, samples with a high percentage of {101} facets exhibit faster reaction rates than samples with a predominance of {001} facets. A more favourable contact at the Au–TiO2 interface, which makes the catalyst function more effectively, is probably the cause of this enhanced performance. Furthermore, these characteristics might promote electron–hole separation, thereby increasing the overall reaction rate.69

Oxidation of olefins to epoxides using TiO2-based catalysts

The selective oxidation of olefins to epoxides is an essential step in organic synthesis because epoxides are valuable intermediates in the synthesis of polymers, fine compounds, and pharmaceuticals. Compared to conventional homogeneous oxidation methods, which often require hazardous oxidants or generate substantial waste, heterogeneous TiO2-based catalysts offer a more eco-friendly, recyclable alternative. Numerous investigations have demonstrated that the crystalline phase of TiO2, surface changes, and the type of oxidant used significantly affect epoxidation performance, including both activity and selectivity. For example, Sang-Ho Chung et al. demonstrated that TiO2 is an effective catalyst for the epoxidation of dicyclopentadiene. Specifically, anatase TiO2 exhibits nearly twice the conversion of the rutile phase, demonstrating its exceptional catalytic capabilities. Active oxygen species such as Ti–OOH are produced when TiO2 interacts with H2O2 and are essential for promoting epoxide synthesis. These reactive species improve the productivity of the epoxidation process. TiO2 is a highly effective catalyst due to these factors, and its performance is highly dependent on its structure, making it especially useful for olefin epoxidation (Scheme 6).70 Similarly, Subhashree Mishra et al. reported that cobalt oxide dispersed on TiO2 (Co3O4/TiO2) functions as an efficient catalyst for the epoxidation of styrene and cyclohexene, achieving over 85% conversion with high selectivity toward the corresponding epoxide products under mild reaction conditions. Titanium dioxide serves as a stable support in this system, ensuring that the cobalt oxide active sites are widely distributed. Strong metal-support interactions and uniform Co3O4 dispersion on TiO2 are responsible for the increased catalytic activity and selectivity. Density Functional Theory (DFT), which offers insights into intermediates and rate-determining processes, is used to further clarify the reaction mechanism and energetics (Scheme 7).71 Matteo Guidotti et al. reported titanium-grafted mesoporous silica catalysts that utilize H2O2 for the efficient and sustainable epoxidation of cyclohexene. Under mild conditions, the widely distributed Ti sites yield high conversion and selectivity, offering an eco-friendly alternative to traditional oxidants.72 Léa Gonçalves et al. demonstrated that titanium dioxide functionalized silicon carbide (SiC–TiO2) serves as an effective heterogeneous catalyst for epoxidation reactions. Titanium dioxide provides the catalytically active sites in this system, while silicon carbide (SiC) serves as a thermally conductive, chemically inert support. On the SiC surface, the TiO2 functionalization creates isolated Ti active centres and enhances dispersion. Ti sites activate the oxidant (usually H2O2) during the reaction to produce Ti-peroxo species, which are in charge of selectively transferring oxygen to the olefinic CC bond, resulting in the creation of epoxides. Heat dissipation and catalytic durability are improved by SiC's strong thermal conductivity and stability. Due to strong support-active phase interactions, the TiO2/SiC system exhibits better activity, selectivity, and reusability.73

A significant advancement in the field was reported by Changjun Yang et al., who explored the epoxidation of cyclooctene over anatase TiO2 using H2O2 as the oxidant. Their research revealed that inactive Ti-η2 peroxide species gradually accumulate on the catalytic surface, causing the catalyst to deactivate. Nevertheless, they discovered that these inactive species can be successfully eliminated by subjecting the system to UV radiation (λ > 350 nm). This procedure helps restore the active Ti sites, thereby preserving the catalyst's activity. All things considered, this method offers a practical means of preventing deactivation and ensuring stable, long-term performance in H2O2-driven epoxidation processes (Scheme 8).74 Similarly, Xiaoli Wei et al. reported the synthesis of mixed-oxide catalysts such as Al2O3–ZrO2–TiO2, which demonstrated remarkable activity in the epoxidation of soybean oil, achieving approximately 86% conversion. The results of this study show that adding TiO2 improves the composite's catalytic efficiency by improving its surface acidity and enhancing its capacity to activate oxygen. Improved oxygen activation enables oxygen species to participate more effectively in the reaction, while increased surface acidity promotes greater adsorption of reactant molecules. These combined effects result in increased overall catalytic efficiency.75

Oxidation of sulphides to sulfoxides or sulfones

It is commonly known that titanium dioxide is an effective catalyst for the oxidative desulfurization of organosulfur compounds. In a study by Miaoguo Zuo et al., TiO2 synthesised via the sol–gel method was used in combination with hydrogen peroxide as the oxidising agent, achieving an exceptional dibenzothiophene removal efficiency of approximately 99.6%. The effects of key operational factors, such as catalyst dosage, reaction temperature, and the H2O2-to-DBT molar ratio, were systematically assessed in this work. The results led to the proposal of a thermocatalytic reaction pathway in which reactive oxygen species, specifically peroxo-titanium intermediates, generated on the TiO2 surface drive the breaking of the sulfur–carbon bond (Scheme 9).76 In another report, Mahshid Zarrabi et al. reported the photocatalytic oxidative desulfurization of dibenzothiophene in alkane media using C/TiO2@MCM-41 nanocomposites. Upon exposure to visible light, the TiO2 component create photogenerated holes. It imposed charges on carriers, valence-band holes and surface-bound hydroxyl radicals were the main oxidative species responsible for converting DBT.77 Recent progress in this field has been comprehensively reviewed by Zahraa A. Hamza et al., which outlines the catalytic efficiency, operational stability, and structural tuning of TiO2-based catalysts used in oxidative desulfurization. The review covers a broad spectrum of TiO2 morphologies, including nanoparticles and mesoporous structures, highlighting their significance in catalytic applications. It goes on to explain how alteration techniques, including elemental doping and composite design, might enhance the material's physicochemical characteristics and catalytic efficiency, thereby improving oxidative desulfurization (ODS) performance.78

Reduction reactions

Reduction of nitroarenes to amines

The selective reduction of nitroaromatic compounds to their corresponding amines, which serve as essential intermediates in the pharmaceutical, agrochemical, and dye industries, has been thoroughly investigated using titanium dioxide as an efficient catalyst. Swapna Challagulla et al. investigated the photocatalytic reduction of nitroarenes using TiO2 and conducted a comparative assessment of the anatase and rutile phases. Their results showed that under UV irradiation, anatase TiO2 demonstrated better catalytic activity, effectively converting nitrobenzene to aniline. As a sacrificial hole scavenger, methanol increased the process efficiency. Anatase TiO2's more favourable conduction band position and lower electron–hole recombination rate were identified as the reasons for this enhanced performance. Overall, the work conclusively shows that TiO2's photocatalytic performance is significantly influenced by its crystal phase (Scheme 10).79 Kazuya Imamura et al. demonstrated the photocatalytic reduction of nitrobenzene to aniline over TiO2 under ultraviolet irradiation, employing saccharides as alternative hydrogen sources in place of molecular hydrogen. They have stated that monosaccharides like glucose performed better as hydrogen donors than disaccharides, which exhibited slower response rates. The suggested process states that protons and electrons are generated when photogenerated holes on the TiO2 surface oxidize saccharides. After that, these species are used to reduce nitrobenzene. Saccharides promote the reduction process by acting as electron donors, without altering the overall reaction pathway.80 Leila Ghasemi et al. developed a β-cyclodextrin functionalized TiO2 photocatalyst for the efficient reduction of nitroaromatic compounds in aqueous media under blue LED irradiation. Under combined ultrasonic photocatalytic conditions, the improved catalyst demonstrated good product yields and significantly faster reaction speeds. Furthermore, the material exhibited outstanding reusability over multiple cycles, demonstrating the usefulness and eco-friendliness of this catalytic system (Schemes 11 and 12).81

Hydrogenation/semi-hydrogenation of alkenes and alkynes

Kazuya Imamura et al. established that alkenes can be selectively hydrogenated to their corresponding alkanes in alcoholic TiO2 suspensions under light irradiation, without requiring molecular hydrogen. The catalytic behaviour of TiO2 is considerably altered when metal co-catalysts are used. The hydrogenation of styrene to ethylbenzene was not demonstrated by pristine TiO2. However, methanolic solutions containing Pd–TiO2, Pt–TiO2, and Rh–TiO2 underwent almost complete conversion within 30 minutes upon exposure to UV light, with Pd–TiO2 showing the highest efficiency. These results unequivocally show that whereas pure TiO2 is inert in this reaction, the addition of appropriate metal co-catalysts enables effective photocatalytic hydrogenation of CC bonds under mild, hydrogen-free conditions without altering the basic reaction pathway (Scheme 13).82 Building upon this approach for alkynes, a recent study by Xincheng Li et al. demonstrated the selective photocatalytic semi-hydrogenation of phenylacetylene to styrene using a Ni2P/TiO2 catalyst under visible-light irradiation. The results of this study show that Ni2P nanoparticles supported on TiO2 enable effective hydrogenation under mild, environmentally friendly conditions while maintaining good selectivity for the intended products. This performance demonstrates how effectively TiO2 catalyses alkyne hydrogenation, expanding its use beyond traditional noble metal-based systems without altering the basic reaction behaviour (Scheme 14).83

Photocatalytic hydrogenation of styrene over metal-TiO2.

TiO2-supported Ni2P catalyzed semi-hydrogenation of alkynes.

Jing Zhao et al. further investigated the influence of support composition and cocatalyst architecture through a Pd–Cu2O/TiO2 system. The selective semi-hydrogenation of alkynes under mild reaction conditions was facilitated by Cu2O-associated hydride species via controlled hydrogen activation and hydrogen spillover. These results show that TiO2-based mixed-support composites may efficiently control both selectivity and hydrogenation activity. This indicates that altering the catalytic performance is largely dependent on the interaction between TiO2 and other support elements.84 From a theoretical perspective, Qiang Wan et al. examined defect-engineered anatase TiO2 and assessed its potential for catalysing the hydrogenation of acetylene (C2H2) to ethylene (C2H4) using density functional theory (DFT) investigations. According to computational results, TiO2 can facilitate the selective hydrogenation of small alkynes under specific surface-defect conditions. These observations imply that hydrogenation processes using non-metal- or low-metal-loaded TiO2 catalytic devices may be feasible.85 More recently, investigations by Melissa Cely-Pinto et al. on photocatalytic semi-hydrogenation demonstrated that both Cu@TiO2 and bimetallic CuPd@TiO2 catalysts are capable of converting alkynes to alkenes using methanol as the hydrogen donor. Although Cu@TiO2 exhibited slower reaction rates relative to Pd-based catalysts, the incorporation of Pd in CuPd@TiO2 enhanced the reaction kinetics by approximately 50% while preserving high selectivity (>95%) and displaying notable tolerance to reaction time. These results highlight a viable strategy for developing cost-effective hydrogenation catalysts with reduced noble metal content (Scheme 15).86

Photocatalytic reduction of CO2 to value-added fuels

Shunji Xie et al. reported that a Pt–TiO2/MgO nanocomposite significantly enhanced the photoreduction of CO2 in the presence of H2O, resulting in methane formation under UV irradiation. A faster rate of CO2 conversion to CH4 was linked to the enhanced interfacial basicity caused by the addition of MgO. This study demonstrated that precise engineering of the Pt/TiO2/MgO interface enhances charge separation, improves CO2 adsorption and activation, and facilitates selective hydrocarbon production. These improvements collectively help overcome the inherent limitations of bare TiO2 (Scheme 16).87 Shiyue Qin et al. reported that CuO–TiO2 composites, using methanol as a sacrificial reagent, can photocatalytically reduce CO2 to value-added products such as methyl formate under light irradiation. This study demonstrated the potential of metal-TiO2 systems for CO2 photoreduction and emphasised the critical role of heterojunction formation (CuO–TiO2) in promoting charge separation and enhancing catalytic performance.88 Longfu Wei et al. focused on the synthesis of TiO2-based heterojunction and hybrid photocatalysts to overcome the intrinsic limitations of TiO2. A wide variety of heterojunction topologies, including p–n, non-p–n, Z-scheme systems, TiO2-metal, TiO2-carbon, and facet-engineered structures, were covered in their thorough review. The importance of these various designs in enhancing photocatalytic performance was highlighted. The study further clarified that factors such as appropriate band alignment, efficient heterointerface engineering, and well-designed composites improve charge separation and reduce recombination losses under both UV and visible light irradiation. Without altering the basic chemical route, more charge carriers are available for reactions, greatly improving CO2 photoconversion into usable fuels.89

Photocatalytic reduction of CO2 to value-added fuels.

Chenxu Yin et al. reported a significant advancement using atomically thin two-dimensional (2D) TiO2, an ultrathin sheet material that operates efficiently without noble metal cocatalysts. With a high percentage of liquid products and over 98% selectivity toward alcohols, this 2D TiO2 demonstrated exceptional photocatalytic activity for CO2 reduction. The study demonstrated that by combining severe thinning of TiO2 with surface defect engineering, pure TiO2 can serve as an effective photocatalyst for converting CO2 to fuels, eliminating the need for expensive metal additives.90 Bhavya Jaksani et al. developed a metal–organic framework (MOF) derived CuO/TiO2 composite catalyst that exhibited exceptionally high methanol production from CO2 in an AI-assisted continuous-flow photoreactor. Their results showed that an improved reactor design, combined with TiO2 and MOF-derived CuO, can significantly increase CO2-to-fuel conversion efficiency. This study demonstrates a viable and workable method for producing fuel from solar energy.91 Ying Liu et al. performed a first-principles density functional theory (DFT) study of Cu/TiO2 photocatalysts, in which Cu nanorods supported on TiO2 were used to explore possible reaction pathways for CO2 reduction. The results obtained revealed that the first hydrogenation step and subsequent hydrogenation operations are the most energetically favorable for the synthesis of methanol (CH3OH). This study provides a comprehensive (atomic-level) explanation of how hydrogen species adsorbed on the surface and the electronic structure at the Cu–TiO2 interface affect the products formed during the reaction (product selectivity). Additionally, it offers vital direction for the rational design of effective photocatalysts.92

C–C bond formation reaction

Aldol condensation reaction

Aldol condensation is an important chemical synthesis reaction that is frequently used to create carbon–carbon bonds. Because of its unique surface characteristics and catalytic activity, titanium dioxide has become a successful heterogeneous catalyst for this process. Fan Lin et al. provided molecular-level insights into C–C bond formation on anatase TiO2 by preparing facet-controlled TiO2 with predominant {101} or {001} surfaces. Their research showed that Lewis acid–Brønsted base site pairings are responsible for acetone aldol condensation, with the {001} facet exhibiting greater catalytic activity than the {101} facet (Scheme 17).93 Theoretical insights were provided by Yuntao Zhao et al., who employed density functional theory (DFT) and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) to investigate aldol condensation on TiO2 (101) in the aqueous phase. According to their findings, formaldehyde and acetone may condense energetically, and water greatly lowers the energy barrier for the dehydration stage. These results imply that TiO2-catalyzed C–C coupling processes could effectively employ liquid-phase feedstocks derived from biomass (Scheme 18).94 Brandon Elliott Oliphant et al. investigated TiO2 catalyzed aldol condensation of mixed oxygenates, including acetaldehyde, acetone, and butanone. Reactions involving mixed substrates resulted in cross-condensation products such as 3-penten-2-one, whereas self-aldol condensation of separate molecules formed dimers. Acetaldehyde was found to preferentially adsorb onto the TiO2 surface, thereby reducing competing chemical pathways and favouring the production of crotonaldehyde as the predominant product. This work shows that in complex carbonyl reaction systems, TiO2 plays a critical role in controlling product selectivity, enabling the selective synthesis of specific products (Scheme 19).95 Hayder Alalwan and Alaa Alminshid examined the adsorption of acetone on hydrated and dehydrated TiO2 surfaces, demonstrating that it undergoes self-condensation followed by dehydration to yield mesityl oxide. Their results demonstrate the critical role of water and surface hydroxyl groups in directing the process toward condensation rather than oxidation, thereby offering mechanistic support for aldol condensation on TiO2 nanoparticles.96

Henry (nitroaldol) reaction

Abdelhfid Aqil et al. developed topologically tunable chitosan TiO2 hybrid materials as cooperative acid–base catalysts for the nitroaldol condensation of nitroalkanes with aldehydes. These hybrids enabled synergistic catalysis, as the TiO2 component provided Lewis acidic sites, while the chitosan's amino groups served as basic sites. The study indicated that the hybrid's morphology, whether it be aerogel, cryogel, or film, had a major impact on catalytic efficiency, with aerogel forms reaching about 98% conversion in three hours. This work demonstrates how TiO2-based hybrid materials can efficiently mediate C–C bond formation under heterogeneous conditions, highlighting the importance of acid–base cooperative mechanisms, surface accessibility, and porosity in governing catalytic activity, even though the reaction examined was a nitroaldol rather than a classical aldol condensation (Scheme 20).97

Knoevenagel condensation

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) has emerged as a versatile heterogeneous catalyst for Knoevenagel condensation, owing to its surface acid–base properties that promote deprotonation of active methylene groups and carbonyl activation. Mostafa Sayed et al. developed an Ag@TiO2 nanocomposite that efficiently catalyzed the condensation of various aromatic aldehydes with active methylene compounds in ethanol at 65 °C, delivering high yields of isolated products. Pd-doped TiO2 systems have attracted extensive attention and constitute the majority of studies on heterogeneous catalysts for the Heck reaction over the past few decades. According to the literature, TiO2's high surface area, strong metal-support contacts, thermal stability, and photocatalytic electron-transfer characteristics make it an excellent support for palladium. These catalysts consistently exhibit excellent recyclability, high activity, and outstanding selectivity in C–C coupling. Leah O. TiO2 acts as a support material with a large surface area, which helps spread the palladium (Pd) active sites evenly across its surface. The catalyst effectively promoted the Mizoroki–Heck coupling of bromobenzene and styrene, achieving 89.7% conversion under optimized conditions. This work demonstrates the potential of Pd-supported TiO2 nanofiber catalysts as effective heterogeneous systems for Heck reactions (Scheme 25).101 Suresh B. According to their study, the catalyst effectively promotes the reaction upon exposure to visible light, producing high yields and maintaining activity across multiple reuse cycles. The potential of Pd/TiO2 as a heterogeneous photocatalyst for sustainable, environmentally friendly Heck reactions is highlighted in this work (Scheme 26).102 Mahmoud Nasrollahzadeh et al. reported TiO2-supported Pd nanoparticles (TiO2@Pd) as efficient, ligand-free, and reusable heterogeneous catalysts for the Mizoroki–Heck coupling of aryl halides with alkenes. This study highlighted TiO2's potential as a dependable and reusable support by showing that solid-supported Pd on TiO2 can compete with homogeneous Pd catalysts under ligand-free conditions, even for challenging substrates such as aryl chlorides (Scheme 28).105

Titanium dioxide has emerged as a highly adaptable heterogeneous catalyst and support for biodiesel production due to its chemical stability, large surface area, and variable acid–base characteristics. Under mild solar irradiation, these photocatalysts achieved a yield of approximately 89.5%, highlighting their potential for sustainable and energy-efficient biodiesel production.113 They increase catalytic activity while consuming less energy by taking advantage of TiO2's semiconducting qualities to encourage photogenerated charge transfer.

Alkylation and acylation reactions

Beckmann rearrangement

The Beckmann rearrangement is a crucial commercial process that transforms ketoximes into amides, including cyclohexanone oxime into ε-caprolactam. Heterogeneous solid acid catalysts are being developed because conventional homogeneous acid catalysts frequently face issues such as corrosion, difficult separation, and environmental concerns. Because of their stability, adjustable acidity, and reusability, TiO2-based materials, especially modified or mixed oxides, have demonstrated great potential. Dongsen Mao et al. reported that B2O3-modified TiO2–ZrO2 mixed oxides efficiently catalyse the vapour-phase Beckmann rearrangement of cyclohexanone oxime, achieving high conversion and selectivity.119 Hashem Sharghi and Mona Hosseini Sarvari demonstrated that solid TiO2 can catalyze the solvent-free, one-step Beckmann rearrangement of various aldehydes and ketones in the presence of hydroxylamine hydrochloride. In this process, the in situ-generated oximes are converted into the corresponding amides or lactams under simple heating. The reactions afforded generally good yields for both aromatic and aliphatic substrates. This study provides evidence for TiO2 as a mild and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional strong-acid Beckmann conditions (Schemes 35 and 36).120

Green multicomponent reactions

Mannich type reaction

One of the important multicomponent transformations that forms C–C/C–N bonds and is frequently used in the synthesis of β-aminocarbonyl compounds is the Mannich reaction. Greener, heterogeneous alternatives are important because traditional Mannich processes rely on homogeneous acids or bases. In this sense, TiO2's variable surface acidity, stability, and ecologically friendly properties have made it a viable solid catalyst. J. N. Sangshetti et al. reported that nanocrystalline TiO2 efficiently catalyses a one-pot, three-component Mannich-type reaction at room temperature. In their study, 4-hydroxycoumarin, an aromatic aldehyde, and a secondary amine were combined in ethanol to form 3-(α-aminobenzyl)-4-hydroxycoumarin derivatives in good to excellent yields. The catalyst demonstrated excellent reusability with minimal loss of activity, highlighting nanocrystalline TiO2 as a mild, heterogeneous, and environmentally friendly catalyst for C–C bond-forming reactions (Scheme 37).121 M. Z. Kassaee et al. reported that anatase phase TiO2 nanoparticles act as an efficient heterogeneous catalyst for a one-pot, three-component, solvent-free Mannich reaction involving an aldehyde, an amine, and a ketone, yielding a range of β-aminocarbonyl compounds with good stereoselectivity. The reaction proceeds at room temperature using only 20 mol% of TiO2 nanoparticles and typically completes within approximately 2–6 h under optimized conditions. Notably, the catalyst is easily recoverable and reusable, maintaining high activity over four consecutive cycles. The authors additionally reported that the yields and diastereoselectivity with TiO2 nanoparticles are superior to those with bulk TiO2 or other nanoparticle catalysts studied, including Al2O3, SiO2, ZrO2, and ZnO (Scheme 38).122 Yuanyong Yao et al. reported that solid-acid SO42−/TiO2 efficiently catalyses a three-component Mannich reaction at ambient temperature. In their study, dimethyl malonate, an aromatic aldehyde, and an aromatic primary amine were reacted in the presence of sulphated TiO2 to afford the corresponding β-amino esters in good yields. They proved that SO42−/TiO2 had far more catalytic activity than other sulphated metal oxide catalysts and that the calcination temperature, which affects the material's Lewis and Brønsted acidity, had a substantial impact on its performance. The potential of sulphated TiO2 as an efficient heterogeneous acid catalyst for moderate, room-temperature C–C bond-forming reactions is highlighted in this work (Scheme 39).123 Similarly, Masoud Samet et al. developed a straightforward three-component Mannich-type reaction involving aromatic aldehydes, amines, and ketones. This method effectively produced a variety of β-amino ketones in high yields and with high stereoselectivity. The mild, solvent-free conditions of the reactions provide a practical and eco-friendly method for the synthesis of physiologically significant β-amino carbonyl compounds (Scheme 40).124

Ugi type α-acylamino amides synthesis

One of the most important multicomponent transformations frequently used for the rapid synthesis of α-acylamino amides is the Ugi reaction. The development of heterogeneous catalysts has recently attracted interest as a means to improve the sustainability of this reaction. Because of its great reusability and surface acidity, titanium dioxide has become an effective and environmentally safe catalyst in this context. Thus, TiO2-mediated Ugi reactions offer a useful and environmentally friendly substitute for traditional catalytic systems.

Carlos Vila and Magnus Rueping reported a visible-light-driven heterogeneous catalyst for oxidative multicomponent reactions, using titanium dioxide as a recyclable catalyst. In their study, TiO2 served as both a heterogeneous catalyst and a photocatalyst when exposed to visible light, enabling efficient C–H functionalization through multicomponent reaction pathways. The authors stressed that TiO2 provides surface Lewis-acidic sites and generates photocatalytically produced electron–hole pairs, which aid in the activation of chemical intermediates. This dual catalytic activity significantly improved the efficiency of multicomponent transformations, such as Ugi reactions.

Since additional homogeneous acids or hazardous metal catalysts were required, the process showed broad substrate compatibility and operated under mild conditions. It's interesting to note that the TiO2 catalyst can be easily recovered by simple filtration and reused without loss of activity. The study emphasized the advantages of TiO2 in terms of sustainability, usability, and adherence to green chemistry principles. Additionally, the visible light-driven approach required less energy than conventional thermal activation. Overall, Vila et al. showed that TiO2 is a heterogeneous catalyst for multicomponent reactions that is both efficient and environmentally safe, offering a strong basis for advancing TiO2-mediated Ugi conversions (Scheme 44).128

Summary and future perspectives

Nanocrystalline TiO2-based catalysts have gained considerable attention in organic synthesis due to their wide applicability and sustainable nature. As summarized in Table 2, these catalysts have been successfully employed in numerous organic transformations, including multicomponent reactions, condensations, cross-coupling reactions, and redox processes. Many of these reactions proceed efficiently under mild, solvent-free, or aqueous conditions, highlighting the green chemistry potential of TiO2-based systems. The inherent chemical stability and non-toxic nature of TiO2 further enhance its suitability for environmentally benign synthesis. In addition, the low cost and easy availability of TiO2 make it attractive for both academic and industrial research.

The data presented in Table 2 clearly demonstrate that structural modification of TiO2 significantly improves catalytic performance. Metal doping, surface functionalization, and composite formation often lead to higher yields and shorter reaction times. Magnetic TiO2-based catalysts enable easy separation and recovery, which is particularly beneficial for repeated use. Functionalized TiO2 catalysts bearing acidic or ionic liquid moieties show excellent activity in multicomponent and condensation reactions. TiO2-supported noble metal catalysts, especially Pd/TiO2 systems, dominate carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions such as Suzuki–Miyaura, Heck, and Sonogashira couplings. These systems often outperform conventional homogeneous catalysts in terms of operational simplicity.

Photocatalytic applications of TiO2, also highlighted in Table 2, introduce an additional sustainable dimension to organic synthesis. Light-driven reactions reduce energy consumption and eliminate the need for harsh reagents. Visible-light-responsive TiO2 materials expand the scope beyond UV-based processes. Such photocatalytic transformations align well with the principles of green and energy-efficient chemistry.

Recyclability studies summarized in Table 3 strongly support the practical relevance of TiO2-based nanocatalysts. Most catalysts retain good to excellent activity over multiple catalytic cycles. In many cases, TiO2-based systems can be reused for four to ten consecutive runs with only a marginal decrease in yield. Magnetic TiO2 composites exhibit particularly high recyclability due to their facile recovery with an external magnet. Pd- and Ag-supported TiO2 catalysts generally exhibit low metal leaching, resulting in stable performance over repeated cycles. These findings emphasize the robustness of TiO2-based catalytic platforms.

However, Table 3 also reveals that some catalysts suffer gradual deactivation after repeated reuse. This loss in activity may arise from surface fouling, nanoparticle agglomeration, or partial loss of active sites. Such observations highlight the importance of understanding catalyst deactivation mechanisms. Improved catalyst design is necessary to maintain long-term activity. Advanced characterization techniques can help establish structure–performance–stability relationships.

Future research should focus on enhancing the durability and regeneration capability of TiO2-based catalysts, as suggested by the recyclability trends in Table 3. Rational nano-engineering, defect control, and heterojunction design may further improve catalytic stability. Expanding the scope of TiO2-based catalysts to asymmetric and enantioselective transformations remains an important challenge. Greater attention should also be paid to scaling up reactions reported in Table 2 for industrial applications. Life-cycle and sustainability assessments will be crucial for real-world implementation. Overall, the combined insights from Tables 2 and 3 clearly demonstrate that nanocrystalline TiO2-based catalysts are promising, recyclable, and sustainable platforms for future organic synthesis.

Conclusion and future perspectives

TiO2-based nanocrystalline catalysts have emerged as highly versatile ecologically heterogeneous systems for a wide range of organic transformations, including multicomponent reactions, coupling reactions, oxidations, reductions, and heterocyclic synthesis. As evident from Table 2, the Ag–TiO2 Nanocomposite, Pd–TiO2-350, Pd–Ni/TiO2, La2O2CO3–TiO2 and TiO2–P25 are under relatively mild and environmentally friendly conditions, such as aqueous media and even solvent-free systems. In addition, the recyclability data summarized in Table 3 demonstrate that many TiO2-based catalysts can be reused multiple times with only a gradual decline in activity, highlighting their potential for sustainable applications.

A closer look at the reported recyclability studies shows that catalytic performance tends to decline after several reuse cycles, indicating that deactivation remains a concern. This loss in activity could be due to factors such as surface blockage, particle aggregation, or a gradual reduction in the number of active sites. However, most studies do not explore these issues in detail, leaving the exact reasons for catalyst deactivation insufficiently understood.

Another key limitation is the absence of standardized methods for estimating the reported catalytic performance and recyclability. Differences in reaction conditions, catalyst loading, and recycling procedures make it difficult to directly compare results across different studies. Establishing uniform evaluation protocols would significantly improve the reliability and reproducibility of reported data.

For metal-supported TiO2 systems, the possibility of metal leaching is also a concern, mainly for applications requiring high purity. While recyclability is often demonstrated, a detailed analysis of catalyst stability and metal loss are not always provided, which raises questions about long-term applicability.

From a photocatalytic perspective, TiO2 is a semiconductor with a wide band gap, which limits its activity under visible light and remains a major bottleneck for practical applications. Although strategies such as doping and composite formation have shown some improvement, achieving efficient utilization of solar energy is still a significant challenge.

In addition, most studies remain limited to small-scale laboratory settings. Practical issues such as catalyst recovery, scalability, and reactor design have not been sufficiently explored. Furthermore, despite being described as “green,” many systems lack thorough evaluation in terms of cost, energy consumption, and environmental impact through techno-economic analysis (TEA) or life-cycle assessment (LCA).

Overall, TiO2-based heterogeneous catalysts have gained considerable attention as effective tools for developing greener and more efficient synthetic methods. Their use in multistep and one-pot reactions not only simplifies reaction processes but also helps in reducing waste generation. Another important advantage is their good reusability and stability, as many TiO2-based systems retain their catalytic performance over multiple cycles, supporting their relevance in sustainable chemistry. Considering these benefits, TiO2-based nanocatalysts offer a practical combination of environmental compatibility, cost-effectiveness, and ease of handling. These features make them attractive candidates for potential industrial applications in organic synthesis. With continued research and development, the role of TiO2 in this field is likely to grow, opening new opportunities for broader application.

Future perspectives

Moving forward, research in this field should focus on gaining deeper insight into catalyst deactivation mechanisms through advanced characterization techniques. Developing standardized guidelines for performance evaluation and recyclability studies will also be essential for meaningful comparison across different catalytic systems.

Efforts should be directed toward designing more stable and leaching-resistant catalysts, particularly for metal-supported TiO2 materials. At the same time, improving visible-light responsiveness through bandgap engineering and heterostructure design will be critical for advancing photocatalytic applications.

To enable real-world implementation, greater emphasis should be placed on scale-up studies, including continuous-flow processes and practical reactor systems. Finally, integrating techno-economic and environmental assessments into future research will provide a more realistic understanding of the sustainability and industrial feasibility of these catalytic systems.
