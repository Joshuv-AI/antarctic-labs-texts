Biventricular impella support in acute antibody-mediated cardiac allograft rejection with severe hemodynamic compromise
Authors: Ye In Christopher Kwon, Kelli Fox, Jay Patel, Richard Cooke, Hamang Patel, Inna F Tchoukina, Krishnasree Rao, Melissa Smallfield, Keyur B Shah, Vigneshwar Kasirajan, Zubair A Hashmi, Josue Chery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute antibody-mediated cardiac allograft rejection (AMR) can lead to SCAI Stage D cardiogenic shock, frequently necessitating escalation to extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support. However, biventricular Impella (BiPella), configured with Impella 5.5 and Impella RP Flex, may offer an alternative, less invasive cardiac unloading and support.Case PresentationWe present a 28-year-old male nine-months post-heart transplantation who developed refractory shock from AMR after immunosuppression nonadherence. Background

Acute antibody-mediated cardiac allograft rejection (AMR) represents a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in heart transplantation (HT) recipients and is associated with hemodynamic compromise in 10–47% of cases [1]. Management typically requires rapid escalation from inotropes and vasopressors to mechanical circulatory support (MCS), most often venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) [1]. Although effective and rapidly deployable, VA-ECMO is invasive and carries risks including high anticoagulation requirements, bleeding, and limb ischemia [1, 2]. Furthermore, in advanced biventricular failure due to antibody-mediated rejection, VA-ECMO often provides inadequate left ventricular (LV) unloading and may compromise coronary perfusion [3]. The retrograde aortic flow generated by VA-ECMO increases LV afterload, predisposing to LV distension, elevated wall stress, and pulmonary congestion, thereby limiting myocardial recovery and frequently necessitating adjunctive strategies to adequately decompress the LV. Here, we present biventricular Impella support (BiPella), consisting of concurrent Impella 5.5 and Impella RP Flex, as a less invasive and effective alternative bridge to recovery in a patient with acute AMR and severe hemodynamic compromise.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old male presented with one week of progressive fatigue, intractable nausea, vomiting, and new-onset chest pressure. He reported medication nonadherence over the preceding several weeks. On arrival, he was in atrial flutter with rate controlled ventricular response. Physical examination revealed signs of volume overload, including elevated jugular venous pressure and peripheral edema, but no fever or focal infectious findings. The patient had a history of orthotopic HT nine months earlier for end-stage nonischemic dilated cardiomyopathy refractory to maximal guideline-directed medical therapy. At the time of initial transplant evaluation, the patient underwent formal psychosocial assessment and was deemed an acceptable candidate with adequate insight and social support per our institution’s protocols. Three months before presentation, his graft function was preserved with LVEF 60–65% and surveillance endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) showing 1R acute cellular rejection (ACR) without pathologic evidence of AMR. He was maintained on a tacrolimus-based immunosuppression regimen but had documented nonadherence in the weeks preceding admission.

The differential diagnosis included acute AMR, acute cellular rejection (ACR), delayed graft dysfunction related to immunosuppression nonadherence, arrhythmia-related decompensation, and infection. Acute AMR was diagnosed based on new donor-specific antibodies, rapid hemodynamic deterioration with biventricular failure, and exclusion of infectious causes. ACR was considered less likely given a recent surveillance biopsy showing only 1R changes, and arrhythmia or isolated medication nonadherence alone could not account for the severity of graft dysfunction.

Initial evaluation demonstrated lactic acidosis (3.0 mmol/L; reference 0.5–2.0 mmol/L) and markedly elevated brain natriuretic peptide (3,302 pg/mL; reference < 100 pg/mL), along with laboratory findings consistent with early end-organ hypoperfusion, including mild transaminase elevation (aspartate aminotransferase 63 U/L, reference 0–50 U/L; alanine aminotransferase 127 U/L, reference 0–50 U/L) and KDIGO stage 2 acute kidney injury (creatinine 2.01 mg/dL from a baseline of 1.02 mg/dL). Hepatic synthetic function remained preserved with total bilirubin 0.9 mg/dL and INR 1.1, and urine output was maintained at approximately 0.8–1.0 mL/kg/hr, Tacrolimus trough level was 7.6 ng/mL (goal 8–10 ng/mL), reflecting marginally subtherapeutic exposure in the setting of reported medication nonadherence. Electrocardiogram showed rate controlled atrial flutter. He was started on pulse dose methylprednisolone (1 g intravenously daily for three days) and dobutamine 2.5 mcg/kg/min with his chronic immunosuppression regimen.

Right heart catheterization on dobutamine demonstrated markedly elevated filling pressures (RA mean 20 mmHg, RV 38/20 mmHg, PCWP 24 mmHg) with preserved cardiac output (7.81 L/min) and reduced thermodilution index (2.2 L/min/m²). We suspect that despite the preserved cardiac output, the cardiac index was reduced when adjusted to body surface area, reflecting relative circulatory insufficiency and known limitations of thermodilution measurements in the setting of atrial arrhythmia and increased filling pressures. Pulmonary artery pressure was 38/24 mmHg (mean 29 mmHg), yielding a transpulmonary gradient of 5 mmHg and calculated PVR of 0.6 Wood units, without evidence of fixed pulmonary hypertension. Subsequently, transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed severely reduced LVEF of 25–30%, LV global longitudinal strain of -3%, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion of 0.66 cm, and RV S’ of 5.2 cm/sec, further suggesting severe biventricular dysfunction (Fig. 1). The patient proceeded to decompensate hemodynamically while on dobutamine at 5 mcg/kg/min, developing atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (rate up to 145 bpm), which precluded further inotrope escalation. Concurrently, lactate and creatinine levels peaked at 4.1 mmol/L and 2.12 mg/dL, respectively. The decision was made to proceed with percutaneous surgical implantation of BiPella configured with the Impella RP Flex and 5.5 (Abiomed Inc., Danvers, MA, USA). He was deemed a non-candidate for retransplant due to medication noncompliance.

TTE on admission revealed biventricular dysfunction (LVEF 25–30%) with GLS − 3.0%

Through a 4-cm infraclavicular incision, the right axillary artery and vein were exposed and controlled. For right ventricular support, the right internal jugular vein was cannulated under ultrasound and transesophageal echocardiographic guidance, and a 20-Fr sheath was advanced into the right atrium. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a Swan–Ganz catheter was advanced into the distal right pulmonary artery, followed by placement of a guidewire into the P4 segment. The Impella RP Flex was then advanced over the wire into position within the right pulmonary artery and initiated at a flow rate of 2.2 L/min, providing immediate right-sided circulatory support. After confirmation of stable right ventricular unloading, systemic heparinization (intraoperative ACT > 250 s) was administered. A 10-mm vascular graft was then anastomosed in an end-to-side fashion to the right axillary artery to preserve distal upper-extremity perfusion. Using an AL1 catheter and J-wire, the aortic valve was crossed under fluoroscopic guidance, and the J-wire was exchanged for the Impella 5.5 guidewire. The Impella 5.5 was positioned with the inflow 5.2 cm below the aortic annulus and the outflow above the valve, then initiated at P6 with satisfactory unloading. The axillary graft was trimmed, secured, and the incision closed in standard fashion (Fig. 2). Concurrent human leukocyte antigen testing revealed new donor-specific antibodies, further confirming the immunologic etiology of his biventricular failure.

Postoperatively, the patient underwent immunomodulatory therapy including three doses of anti-thymocyte globulin, three cycles of plasma exchange each followed by intravenous immunoglobulin, and a four-dose course of eculizumab to address antibody-mediated injury. Systemic anticoagulation was achieved with intravenous unfractionated heparin during Impella support, targeting an anti–factor Xa level of 0.3–0.5 IU/mL and an aPTT of 60–80 s. On postoperative day 3, he returned to the operating room for exploration of a localized right axillary artery bleeding site, which was controlled surgically with subsequent placement of a wound vacuum-assisted closure device. No further bleeding occurred thereafter. There was no evidence of clinically significant hemolysis, thromboembolic events, or device-related infection during the support period.

The Impella 5.5 was weaned over six days and explanted after demonstration of left ventricular recovery, followed by weaning and explantation of the Impella RP Flex two days later. Dobutamine was discontinued two days thereafter. Transthoracic echocardiography prior to discharge demonstrated recovery of left ventricular function with an LVEF of 45–50% and GLS of − 10.9% (Fig. 3). Aside from a transient lactate elevation to 2.3 mmol/L that resolved without intervention, the remainder of the hospital course was uncomplicated. The patient received multidisciplinary support including transplant psychiatry, social work, and transplant pharmacy counseling, and was discharged on postoperative day 30 asymptomatic and ambulatory.

Discussion

Acute AMR remains a major clinical challenge in HT recipients. In milder cases, therapy centers on combination regimens aimed at halting immune-mediated injury. However, severe presentations complicated by refractory cardiogenic shock often necessitate MCS as a bridge to both immunologic and hemodynamic recovery. VA-ECMO has been the most frequently described modality in this setting; however, it does not provide reliable left ventricular (LV) unloading and therefore often requires adjunctive venting strategies such as left atrial or direct LV drainage. Hybrid configurations such as VA-ECMO combined with Impella 5.5 (“ECpella”) can improve LV unloading compared with VA-ECMO alone [4]. Yet, ECMO’s non-physiologic retrograde aortic perfusion and non-pulsatile systemic flow raises LV afterload. Coronary and end-organ perfusion may thus be less optimized under these systems, whereas BiPella utilizing an Impella 5.5 for LV support in conjunction with a temporary right ventricular assist device (e.g., Impella RP Flex or ProtekDuo) preserves anterograde pulmonary and systemic flow, maintaining a more natural pulsatile pressure [4, 5]. Retrospective data also suggest that simultaneous BiPella implantation, rather than staged placement, confers better survival, lower filling pressures, and higher cardiac output, with relatively low complication rates such as hemolysis or thrombosis [6].

With respect to invasiveness, neither ECpella nor BiPella requires sternotomy, thereby avoiding mediastinal adhesions and reducing the need for prolonged mechanical ventilation [7]. A key distinction, however, is that the BiPella configuration can be implanted via the axillary artery and internal jugular vein, entirely avoiding femoral cannulation [5]. This groin-free approach reduces the risk of limb ischemia and large-bore arterial complications and facilitates earlier extubation and mobilization. Although select ECMO configurations may permit ambulation, this is typically more technically challenging and resource intensive. In our experience, patients supported with BiPella can often be extubated and ambulating within 24 h of device placement. While fully ambulatory Impella systems do not yet exist, axillary and internal jugular access permits upright activity and early ambulation while preserving lower-extremity mobility and near-normal range of motion. Reports of Impella 5.5 with ProtekDuo support describe rapid weaning from mechanical ventilation followed by active ambulation within days of device implantation [5].

By contrast, VA-ECMO often necessitates deeper sedation, prolonged intubation, and patient immobilization and is associated with higher bleeding and infectious risks related to large-bore cannulation, extracorporeal circuitry, and systemic anticoagulation. These are major concerns that are particularly relevant in HT recipients undergoing intensive immunosuppressive therapy [6]. In the context of primary graft dysfunction or severe acute AMR with rapid hemodynamic compromise but preserved pulmonary function and manageable pulmonary vascular resistance, percutaneous BiPella support represents a viable alternative to VA-ECMO–based strategies, providing effective biventricular unloading with pulsatile microaxial flow while minimizing surgical trauma and device-related complications. This experience underscores the importance of selecting the least invasive yet physiologically appropriate MCS strategy in patients requiring concurrent, intensive immunomodulatory therapy.

Elevated pulmonary pressures and RV afterload have been linked to poor survival in BiPella recipients [6]. In most cases, LV support is introduced first. However, because our patient had markedly impaired RVSF, RV support was initiated pre-emptively to maintain LV preload once the Impella 5.5 was activated. Rapid LV unloading can otherwise shift the septum leftward, distort RV geometry, and precipitate RV failure. In shock states with high right atrial pressures and venous congestion, early preservation of forward right-sided flow may also be advantageous before full biventricular support is established. Moreover, during axillary grafting, valve crossing, and wire exchanges, transient hemodynamic instability is common. During these maneuvers, baseline RV support can help stabilize LV filling and systemic pressures. Importantly, high RV afterload can compromise RV support flow, reflect advanced disease, or represent fixed pulmonary vascular resistance, thereby limiting the efficacy of BiPella. In such circumstances, VA-ECMO with LV venting may be more appropriate. Thus, careful evaluation of pulmonary vascular load indices is critical when selecting candidates for BiPella therapy.

Conclusions

Although Impella support is well established for cardiogenic shock, its use as a primary biventricular strategy before VA-ECMO in refractory shock due to acute allograft rejection is rarely reported. Evidence gaps remain, including the absence of randomized comparisons of BiPella versus VA-ECMO in this setting and the lack of standardized anticoagulation protocols for rejection-associated coagulopathy. Moreover, patients with profound hypoxemia, severe vasoplegia requiring very high flows, persistent arrythmias, or in cardiogenic shock still require VA-ECMO escalation. However, this case illustrates that percutaneous BiPella can serve as a rapid and effective bridge to recovery while potentially avoiding the higher risks associated with VA-ECMO.
