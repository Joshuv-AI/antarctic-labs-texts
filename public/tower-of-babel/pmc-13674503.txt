Charge-mediated remote epitaxy of Ga2O3 via MoS2
Authors: Gyeong Ryul Lee, Dong-Gwan Kim, Wonwoo Suh, Yoonho Choi, Donghan Kim, Mohammad M. Afandi, Juhui Oh, Hongsik Park, Celesta S. Chang, Hyun S. Kum, Taehoon Lee, Roy B. Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Remote epitaxy of the corundum phase α-Ga2O3 is demonstrated through a domain-free, transfer-free monolayer of MoS2 template synthesized on sapphire. Despite the intrinsic nonidealities of MoS2—specifically its thickness and polar bonding characteristics—for remote epitaxy, single-crystalline α-Ga2O3 films are epitaxially grown on polycrystalline MoS2 with crystallographic fidelity comparable to those grown directly on bare sapphire. This observation substantiates that the substrate lattice potential can still be effectively transmitted through the MoS2 layer, facilitating epitaxial registry at the growth front. The resulting α-Ga2O3 films exhibit crystal quality and surface morphology on par with those grown directly on bare sapphire. Moreover, a centimeter-scale, crack-free α-Ga2O3 membrane was successfully exfoliated using a Ni stressor, underscoring the mechanical compatibility and scalability of the remote epitaxial process. Structural disordering observed with more-than-bilayer MoS2 further corroborates the remote epitaxy mechanism. First-principles calculations reveal that interfacial interactions between the bottom S atoms of MoS2 and the Al atoms of sapphire induce spatially modulated charge distributions on the MoS2 surface, creating energetically favorable adsorption sites for Ga species. Collectively, these findings establish a mechanistic framework for charge-mediated remote epitaxy across polar interfaces and expand the design space for heterogeneous integration of transferable wide bandgap Ga2O3 membranes in next-generation power electronics and optoelectronic devices.

INTRODUCTION

Since the inception in 2017, remote epitaxy has emerged as a transformative growth technique, rapidly expanding from initial demonstrations on III-V arsenide and nitride materials to encompass complex oxides and other materials (1–3). This advancement has enabled novel device architectures through heterogeneous integration, showcasing the technique’s versatility and potential (3–6).

Remote epitaxy employs a 2-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) material—most commonly graphene—as an interlayer between a single-crystalline substrate and the epitaxial layer grown atop it. The term “remote” reflects the underlying conceptual framework, which integrates lattice transparency, electrostatic potential penetration through atomically thin 2D materials, and vdW bonding at the interface. Together, these effects enable epitaxial registry without the formation of strong covalent bonds. This framework distinguishes remote epitaxy from both conventional and vdW epitaxy by balancing substrate influence and interlayer decoupling, thereby facilitating exfoliation and substrate reuse (7).

To date, mechanistic studies of remote epitaxy have focused predominantly on graphene as it is regarded as an ideal 2D material for this growth technique (8, 9). However, from the perspectives of material compatibility and process scalability, it is essential to investigate alternative vdW materials to enable broader adoption and practical implementation of this novel epitaxial technique. Recently, the remote epitaxy of ZnO nanowires on MoS2-covered ZnO nanowires was reported, demonstrating the feasibility of non-graphene-based remote epitaxy (10). Notably, the interaction between zinc and sulfur resulted in polarity inversion in the ZnO nanowires with respect to the underling ZnO nanowires, illustrating how a 2D material can influence the growth based on the nature of its constituent atoms (11). Liu et al. has demonstrated remote epitaxy of VO2 using an ultrathin amorphous Al2O3 deposited by atomic layer deposition, suggesting that remote epitaxy can be facilitated by an atomically thin layer of non-vdW materials as well (12).

As remote epitaxy continues to evolve in sophistication and versatility, there will be more materials that can be fabricated as single-crystalline membranes, offering a high degree of integration flexibility. This is particularly important for materials like gallium oxide (Ga2O3), a wide bandgap semiconductor considered vital for next-generation high-power electronics. Ga2O3 suffers from inherently low thermal conductivity, which significantly limits its performance. To enable efficient heat dissipation, Ga2O3 typically needs to be transferred onto a thermally conductive substrate via direct wafer bonding followed by substrate removal (13), or through complex ion-cut techniques (14). Remote epitaxy and exfoliation of Ga2O3 not only enable more efficient thermal management but also expand its applicability as part of the broader family of wide bandgap semiconductors compatible with heterogenous integration (4).

In this work, we demonstrate centimeter-scale remote epitaxy of corundum α-Ga2O3—a metastable polymorph of Ga2O3—on MoS2-coated sapphire (α-Al2O3) and elucidate the remote epitaxy mechanism using 2D materials beyond graphene. Because metastable α-Ga2O3 can be grown and stabilized on sapphire (α-Al2O3) due to their shared corundum structure (15), its remote epitaxy would not be feasible without the substrate acting as a seed layer. This makes the α-Ga2O3/sapphire system an ideal platform for investigating MoS2-based remote epitaxy. From a practical perspective, α-Ga2O3 is also a promising wide-bandgap semiconductor for next-generation high-power electronic devices, alongside monoclinic β-Ga2O3 (16–18). In this study, polycrystalline MoS2 was directly synthesized on sapphire using a scalable solution-based process, followed by Ga2O3 growth via a simple mist chemical vapor deposition (CVD) under atmospheric pressure. Notably, no sophisticated high-vacuum epitaxy or deposition techniques were required, underscoring the robustness and processability of MoS2-based remote epitaxy. The overall process flow for the MoS2-based remote epitaxy of Ga2O3 is depicted in Fig. 1.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Continuous and uniform polycrystalline MoS2 for remote epitaxy

For remote epitaxy, it is critical that the 2D material is continuous and exhibits uniform thickness (or a well-controlled number of layers). The most extensively studied method for synthesizing MoS2 involves sulfurization via thermolysis using MoO3 and S powders. However, this approach often yields triangular MoS2 domains that overlap, making precise control over layer number and surface coverage challenging (19). While recent studies have introduced processes capable of producing MoS2 with uniform thickness and minimal domain overlap, many of these methods incorporate buffer layers (20), rendering them unsuitable for remote epitaxy.

In this study, a solution-based process was employed, wherein ammonium tetrathiomolybdate ((NH4)2MoS4) precursors were spin-coated onto sapphire and subsequently reduced to MoS2 film through thermal processing. To control the thickness uniformity and coverage, the precursor concentration and solvent mixture were optimized (see Methods for further details). Details of MoS2 synthesis and optimization process can be found in our previous report (21). The thickness uniformity and coverage of the synthesized MoS2 were characterized by Raman spectroscopy, in which the number of layers was determined by the spacing (∆mode) between the E2g and A1g peaks. Polycrystalline MoS2 films synthesized with precursor concentrations of 0.008, 0.012, 0.024, and 0.048 M exhibited ∆mode values of ∼20.91, ∼22, ∼23.12, and ∼ 25.31 cm−1, corresponding to mono-, bi-, tri-, and five-layer MoS2, respectively (fig. S1A). A top-view image of monolayer MoS2 taken by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) in Fig. 2A shows the continuous layer without any triangular domains over a wide area. Furthermore, Raman mapping with diffraction grating with 600 grooves over a 30- by-30 μm2 area (Fig. 2B) revealed an average ∆mode of ∼21 cm−1, with the maximum and minimum ∆mode measured at 21.3 and 16 cm−1, respectively. These values are well within the established range for monolayer MoS2 (20, 22–25). However, thicker MoS2 exhibited more non-uniform thickness (fig. S1B), which could have affected the Ga2O3 growth. We also confirmed that these MoS2 layers do not oxidize during α-Ga2O3 growths by mist CVD at atmospheric pressure (fig. S1, C and D).

α-Ga2O3 grown on mono- and multi-layer MoS2

α-Ga2O3 was grown at 450°C using mist CVD at a typical growth rate of 0.75 μm/hour. The structural characteristics of the films varied with increasing MoS2 layer thickness, as indicated by the x-ray diffraction (XRD) 2θ-ω scans in Fig. 2C. On monolayer MoS2, both XRD and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) (Fig. 2D) confirmed the formation of highly oriented single-phase α-Ga2O3, similar to α-Ga2O3 grown directly on sapphire. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the α-Ga2O3 surface (Fig. 2E) showed no discernible morphological differences compared to growth on bare sapphire. Thus, both XRD and SEM analyses indicate that α-Ga2O3 on monolayer MoS2 exhibits structural characteristics nearly identical to those on bare sapphire. Figure 2C shows that the α-phase is present even on bi- and tri-layer MoS2. However, the crystal quality quickly deteriorates (see Fig. 3H), suggesting the growth process is vastly different from the monolayer sample. Details of the growth study are discussed later. Growth on tri-layer MoS2 resulted in hexagonal ε-Ga2O3, coexisting with α-Ga2O3, as observed in Fig. 2C. On five-layer MoS2, the film predominantly exhibited the ε-phase in the form of large hexagonal islands (Fig. 2F).

According to our density function theory (DFT) calculations, electrons in S atoms are drawn to Al atoms in certain configurations, creating charge-depleted regions on the surface S atoms. This substrate-induced charge redistribution on the MoS2 surface is preserved only in the monolayer case. Therefore, when the charge-modulated adsorption landscape is preserved (monolayer case), α-phase nucleation is energetically favored. In contrast, when this interaction is weakened in multilayer MoS2, the positional correlation between adsorption sites and the substrate lattice is lost, reducing the energetic preference for α-phase formation and resulting in a shift in phase tendency toward ε-Ga2O3. The details of the DFT calculations are further discussed below.

At the early stage of growth, we noticed that the nucleation on MoS2-on-sapphire was distinctively different from that on bare sapphire and strongly dependent on the number of MoS2 layers. After 2 to 5 minutes of growth on bare sapphire, rhombohedral-shape α-Ga2O3 nuclei were formed and started coalescing on sapphire (Fig. 3, A and B). In comparison, the surface of Ga2O3 on monolayer MoS2 (Fig. 3, C and D) indicates that the nuclei were forming on top of the already coalescing surface, suggesting faster nucleation and growth than the bare sapphire case. Despite the faster nucleation, the well-aligned nuclei suggested the process was still governed by the underlying sapphire. With increasing MoS2 thickness, fewer nuclei were observed, and their shapes became increasingly random, likely due to the disordered Ga2O3 layer underneath (Fig. 3, E and F). In the case of the five-layer MoS2, the Ga2O3 film grew as a film, but its phase could not be determined by XRD, suggesting the film was very disordered (fig. S2, A and C). Prolonged growth on this disordered Ga2O3 eventually led to the formation of large ε-phase islands (fig. S2, B and C), a phenomenon previously observed in other studies where the ε-phase emerged on an amorphous layer of TiO2 (26). We attribute the difference in nucleation rate, shown in Fig. 3, A and C, to the change in density of nucleation sites for Ga.

Experimentally, nucleation on monolayer MoS2 is characterized by a lower density of well-aligned nuclei, along with secondary nucleation occurring on already coalescing surfaces. In contrast, increasing the MoS2 thickness leads to a reduction in nucleation density and more randomly distributed nuclei. Based on our observation and DFT calculations, we propose that the charge-depleted regions on the growth surface create fewer nucleation sites for Ga. This reduction in site density enhances the adatom mobility, thereby promoting the nucleation of new Ga2O3 microcrystals on top of the coalescing surface, as observed in Fig. 3C. On multilayer MoS2, the absence of nucleation sites makes the initial nucleation process more difficult and random.

MoS2 could have altered the nucleation kinetics, but our observations suggest that the overall growth rate remained relatively constant with the monolayer MoS2. There was no morphological change due to the MoS2 layer, but faster nucleation could have attributed to slight increases in the roughness, as confirmed atomic force microscopy (AFM) (fig. S3, A and B). Additionally, AFM imaging of Ga2O3 grown on tri-layer MoS2 (fig. S3C) shows a much rougher surface with significant morphological changes, consistent with the observed change in the nucleation process.

The α-Ga2O3 films with thicknesses of 0.5 ∼ 1.5 μm grown on sapphire by our mist CVD typically exhibits full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) values of 40 ∼ 70 and 900 ∼ 1300 arcsec for the (0006) and (101¯4¯) reflections in rocking curve mode, respectively. These values are comparable to those reported in the literature (27). The relatively low FWHM values for the on-axis (0006) reflection suggest that the domains are highly oriented along the c-axis, with minimal tilt. In contrast, the higher FWHM values for the (1014¯) reflection can be interpreted as a significant density of edge- and mixed-type dislocations typically with a Burgers vector be=13〈21¯1¯0〉 (28), where the dislocations are mainly produced by the mismatches in lattice constants and coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) between α-Ga2O3 (CTE ∼ 9.35 × 10−6°C−1) and sapphire (CTE ∼ 8.11 × 10−6°C−1) (29).

Due to the large lattice mismatch of approximately 4.6% between α-Ga2O3 and sapphire, the critical thickness of α-Ga2O3 on sapphire is expected to be less than 10 nm (29, 30). But unlike conventional epitaxy, remote epitaxial films are possibly less sensitive to the lattice mismatch, as they can relax elastically due to their weak vdW interaction with the underlying 2D layer (31). The strain mapping analysis via cross-sectional high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) scanning transmission microscopy (STEM) suggests that α-Ga2O3 is essentially strain-free near the interface (fig. S4). While more macroscopic reciprocal space mapping (fig. S5) indicates that the films are fully relaxed on a film scale, a traditional covalent interface would typically retain significant residual strain near the interface due to the lattice and CTE mismatches. Specifically, the CTE mismatch would be expected to induce localized tensile strain during the cooling process. The observation that the Ga2O3 remains strain-free while coupled to the substrate suggests interfacial “slippage” facilitated by van der Waals (vdW) interactions. This localized relaxation—occurring despite the substrate’s thermal contraction—provides compelling evidence for a remote epitaxial mechanism and demonstrates the mechanical decoupling afforded by the MoS2 interlayer.

On average, however, the α-Ga2O3 films on monolayer MoS2/sapphire exhibited a slight increase in FWHM values of both (0006) and (101¯4¯) reflections compared to those grown directly on sapphire (see Fig. 2, H through J). In the remote epitaxy of GaN on graphene/SiC, S. Lee et al. reported a reduction in crystal quality, which was primarily attributed to the requirement of a thick, low-temperature GaN buffer to achieve full film coalescence (32). This suggests that the low surface energy of vdW surface modifies the growth kinetics. Given that there is no buffer layer in our case, it is possible that the nucleation process becomes more dependent on the growth conditions, which influence the crystal quality.

Although beam-induced damage of MoS2 precluded high-magnification imaging of the defect core structures, the current STEM image in fig. S6 clearly indicates that the dislocations are generated within the Ga2O3 layer rather than being extended from the substrate. The threading dislocation density is estimated to be on the order of 1010–1011 cm−2, which is in good agreement with the dislocation density estimated by the off-axis FWHM values obtained from XRD. This observation is important, as it suggests that the defect density is affected by the initial nucleation and coalescence kinetics on the MoS2 surface. Additionally, it remains possible that localized imperfections, such as grain boundaries inherent in polycrystalline MoS2, act as preferential sites for defect nucleation. Although a direct spatial correlation between the MoS2 grain boundaries and Ga2O3 defects could not be established within the examined regions, previous study on remote epitaxy has indicated that 2D template defects and grain boundaries can significantly alter the chemical reactivity and surface potential, thereby degrading the overlayer crystallinity (33). Therefore, the grain boundaries in polycrystalline MoS2 may have contributed to the slightly lower crystal quality compared to growth on a bare sapphire substrate.

This kinetics-dependent growth is further supported by growth experiments in which the growth temperature was varied from 450 to 470°C. Given the phase control is temperature-sensitive (34), the explored growth window may have been limited to achieve optimal results. Nevertheless, within this range, increasing the temperature led to overall decrease in FWHM values. Specifically, the discrepancy in off-axis FWHM values between the bare sapphire and MoS2 samples was reduced (fig. S7). Overall, the crystal quality of remote epitaxial films is possibly more sensitive to growth conditions due to the weakly interacting vdW surface.

When bilayer MoS2-on-sapphire was used as the substrate, the FWHM values increased significantly, exceeding 1000 and 2000 arcsec for the (0006) and (101¯4¯) reflections, respectively, suggesting a substantial degradation in crystal quality (Fig. 3, H and I and table S1) due to the transition from remote to vdW epitaxy. Cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of Ga2O3 grown on bi- and tri-layer MoS2 are presented in fig. S9, A and B. In both cases, fast Fourier transform patterns from the imaged areas reveal amorphous Ga2O3 intermixed with the α-phase. As the MoS2 thickness increased further, the crystal quality of Ga2O3 continued to deteriorate progressively. For the ε-phase grown on five-layer MoS2, the FWHM broadening became so excessive that reliable measurements could not be obtained.

To examine the interface regions in greater detail, cross-sectional scanning TEM (STEM) was performed on single-crystalline α-Ga2O3 grown on monolayer MoS2/sapphire. The low-magnification STEM image (Fig. 4A) shows a laterally continuous MoS2 layer over an extended region. Combined with the Raman mapping results (Fig. 1B), this observation strongly suggests the solution-processed monolayer MoS2 forms uniformly over a wide area without detectable pinholes or structural discontinuities. The crystallographic alignment was further confirmed by selected area electron diffraction (SAED) acquired along the [1¯21¯0] zone axis (Fig. 4B), showing two well-aligned sets of diffraction spots from α-Ga2O3 and sapphire, thereby confirming the epitaxial relationship between the film and the substrate.

More importantly, the high-magnification STEM image of the interface (Fig. 4C) reveals that the single-crystalline α-Ga2O3 appears to have grown in direct registry with the sapphire, despite the presence of the intermediate MoS2 layer. Notably, the polycrystalline nature of MoS2 did not compromise the single-crystallinity of the Ga2O3 epilayer. Strain mapping of the α-Ga2O3 above MoS2 also shows no localized high-strain regions associated with structural defects within the analyzed region (fig. S4). Additionally, atomic-resolution HAADF and annular bright-field (ABF)-STEM images (fig. S8, A and B), along with their corresponding integrated intensity profiles (fig. S8C), suggest that the initial layer on the MoS2 interlayer is the Ga atom layer, which corroborates the DFT predictions. The monolayer nature of MoS2 was further confirmed by the two sharp peaks of Sulfur and a single Molybdenum peak in energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) analysis, also shown by a sharp intensity in the line profile extracted across the interface (Fig. 4D). Collectively, this comprehensive analysis of the interface region provides structural evidence for remote epitaxy of α-Ga2O3 on MoS2.

As the layer count increased from monolayer to multilayer configurations (bi-, tri-, and five-layers), the Ga2O3 structure became progressively more disordered (fig. S9). Both the XRD and STEM analyses consistently demonstrated that monolayer MoS2 facilitates remote epitaxy—a conclusion further validated by successful mechanical exfoliation and DFT calculations discussed in subsequent sections.

Exfoliation of α-Ga2O3

One of the key features of remote epitaxy is that the epitaxial film can be easily exfoliated with minimal mechanical force, which can be typically induced by depositing a thick layer of Ni (1, 35). By applying a Ni stressor, we successfully exfoliated a centimeter-sized α-Ga2O3 film from a monolayer MoS2-on-sapphire substrate without any damages (Fig. 4, E to G). The exfoliated α-Ga2O3 membrane was crack-free and devoid of any structural defects or features, as expected from remote-epitaxially grown films. The substrate side was similarly clean, with negligible traces of Ga, indicating that no Ga2O3 remained on the surface (fig. S10). These results indicate that the exfoliation occurred at the weakest point – the interface, which was held together by relatively weak vdW interactions.

Understanding growth mechanism

Experimental results demonstrated that high-quality single-crystal α-Ga2O3 films were successfully synthesized via remote epitaxy using a polycrystalline monolayer of MoS2 as a 2D interlayer. Considering previous remote-epitaxy studies that employed single-crystalline and monatomic graphene (1, 31, 33), the polar-bonding nature of the intervening MoS2 layer suggests that a distinctly different remote-epitaxy mechanism may be required to explain the present system. It is well known that graphene-based remote epitaxy relies on the “lattice transparency” of a one-atom-thick carbon layer, which allows the atomic potential of substrate to influence the growing film despite the presence of graphene (3, 36). Due to the weak vdW potential of monolayer graphene, it cannot fully screen the stronger electrostatic field of a polar substrate, enabling adatoms to register to the substrate lattice. In contrast to the purely in-plane sp2 bonds with no permanent dipole for graphene, Mo atoms in MoS2 have out-of-plane 4d orbitals that overlap with 3p orbitals of sulfur, forming polar covalent bonds oriented perpendicular to the MoS2 monolayer (37–41). Furthermore, MoS2 is not as “electrically transparent” as graphene due to its greater thickness.

To elucidate the underlying mechanism enabling freestanding single-crystal α-Ga2O3 growth via MoS2 interlayer, we conducted DFT calculations. Figure 5A presents one of the lowest-energy heterostructure models identified in this study, consisting of an atomically optimized Al2O3 substrate, a monolayer MoS2, and an epitaxial α-Ga2O3 overlayer. As a first step, we examined the optimal MoS2/Al2O3 interface configurations in the absence of the α-Ga2O3 layer to identify the most stable interfacial arrangements. Multiple low-energy configurations with varying relative in-plane orientations were found, as illustrated in Fig. 5B and fig. S11, all differing in formation energy by only ∼3 meV. Despite these small energy differences, the electronic charge distribution within the MoS2 layer varied significantly depending on the specific interfacial alignment. Notably, configurations I and II (see Fig. 5B) exhibited pronounced charge depletion on the top surface of the MoS2 layer, influenced by the underlying Al2O3 substrate. This depletion does not occur uniformly across all top sulfur atoms (referred to as Stop in Fig. 5C), but rather selectively, depending on the geometric alignment among the Stop atoms, the lower sulfur atoms (Sbott), and the topmost aluminium atoms (Altop) of the Al2O3 substrate. More precisely, significant charge depletion occurs when the Altop, Sbott, and Stop atoms are nearly colinear, as shown in Fig. 5C. When the charge depletion regions are formed, the charge accumulation regions also appear (see Fig. 5B). Deviations from this ideal colinear atomic alignment result in reduced charge depletion - or even local charge accumulation when the deviation is substantial.

Given that the degree of collinearity is closely related to the Altop-Sbott distance (fig. S12), a shorter Altop-Sbott distance also correlates strongly with increased charge depletion on Stop, indicating that the strong influence of Altop is mediated via Sbott to the MoS2 surface. This substrate-induced charge redistribution is not unique to a single configuration; similar trends are also observed across other Al2O3/MoS2 heterostructure models (fig. S13). Consequently, the majority of Altop atoms on the Al2O3 substrate effectively transmit their positional information through the MoS2 layer, generating a patterned array of charge-depleted Stop atoms on its surface, as schematically visualized in Fig. 5F. Remarkably, this pattern mirrors the crystallographic symmetry of the underlying Al2O3 substrate. Such charge polarization on Stop atoms is observed only in monolayer MoS2 and is absent in the bilayer MoS2 structures examined (fig. S14), indicating that the substrate ‘transparency’ necessary for remote epitaxy may operate only in monolayer MoS2.

The next question is how this Al2O3-induced charge redistribution in the Stop atoms correlates with the single-crystal growth of the Ga2O3 epitaxial layer. To investigate this, we calculated the adsorption energies of Ga adatoms on the MoS2 surface. We found that stronger adsorption occurs at sites exhibiting greater charge depletion (see comparison in Fig. 5, D and E). A similar adsorption trend is observed for the hydrated Ga(H2O)3 complex (fig. S15), which has been proposed as one of the initial adsorbing Ga species in the mist CVD process (42). From a kinetic perspective, higher adsorption energy implies longer residence times for Ga adatoms at these Stop sites, which can facilitate nucleation. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that, during the growth of the epitaxial Ga2O3 layer, nucleation preferentially occurs at the charge-depleted Stop sites due to their stronger interaction with Ga adatoms. If this assumption holds, then the positions of Gabott atoms should align closely with these charge-depleted regions to enable stable single-crystal growth. To test this, we compared the atomic positions of Gabott in two Ga2O3 polymorphs (α and ε phases) with the locations of charge-depleted Stop atoms, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 5, F and G. The corresponding strain values for various growth orientations of each phase are also shown in Fig. 5H. This analysis reveals that α-Ga2O3 grown along the (001) direction exhibits the best alignment with the charge-depleted sites and the lowest interfacial strain, making it the more favorable epitaxial phase on the MoS2 layer. This finding is consistent with our experimental observations. Ultimately, it is this localized charge redistribution on the MoS2 surface, induced by Al2O3-MoS2 interactions, that governs the crystallization kinetics of the Ga2O3 epitaxial layer. Despite being significantly thicker than graphene, MoS2 functions as a “transparent” 2D layer in a similar manner, transmitting the structural information of the substrate to the growing film and thereby enabling the remote epitaxial growth of single-crystal α-Ga2O3.

Finally, the small differences in formation energy among the various Al2O3-MoS2 interface configurations (tables S2 and S3) suggest that the difference in energy barriers for MoS2 nucleation on the Al2O3 substrate are similarly small across these configurations. Consequently, MoS2 grains with orientations other than configuration I, the lowest-energy configuration, can form with non-negligible probabilities, resulting in the experimentally observed polycrystalline MoS2 layer. However, despite the presence of varied in-plane orientations within this polycrystalline MoS2 film, the charge redistribution at the MoS2-substrate interface remains highly selective and consistently tied to the positions of the Altop atoms on the Al2O3 substrate, as shown in Fig. 5B and fig. S13. This indicates that the underlying substrate can still impose a spatially patterned electrostatic influence across different MoS2 grains. Therefore, a plausible explanation for the observed single-crystal α-Ga2O3 epitaxial growth on polycrystalline MoS2 is the preferential nucleation and subsequent growth of Ga2O3 at these substrate-imprinted, charge-depleted sites, regardless of the grain orientation of the underlying MoS2.

As outlined in note S1, this charge-mediated remote epitaxy is not expected to occur on an O-terminated sapphire surface, as such termination would promote direct covalent bonding between O and S. Although no thermal treatment was applied to the sapphire substrate prior to spin-coating the precursor solution, the spectra taken by x-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) from monolayer MoS2 on sapphire (see fig. S16, A to C) predominantly indicate stoichiometric MoS2, with no significant chemical interaction between MoS2 and the sapphire surface.

The only deviation is found in the S 2p signal in fig. S16B, which exhibits a peak at 168 ∼ 169 eV corresponding to S-O bonding. The oxygen in these S–O species likely originates from the sapphire surface or from adsorbed hydroxyl groups, which can be corroborated by Al-OH bonding shown in fig. S16C. The localized formation of S-O bonds may have influenced the remote epitaxy process, the subsequent exfoliation behavior, as well as the crystal quality of epitaxial α-Ga2O3.

To elucidate the role of S-O bonding, remote epitaxy was attempted on monolayer MoS2 synthesized on O2-plasma-treated sapphire (see note S2 and fig. S17 for more details). The plasma treatment was utilized to deliberately induce surface hydroxylation. Notably, α-Ga2O3 growth was entirely suppressed on the monolayer template following the surface treatment. These results, corroborated by DFT calculations, substantiate the viability of charge-mediated remote epitaxy of single-crystalline α-Ga2O3 on MoS2.

We successfully demonstrated remote epitaxy of centimeter-scale α-Ga2O3 using polycrystalline MoS2 as a 2D vdW layer. Given that metastable α-Ga2O3 can only be grown on substrates with matching crystal structure, the monolayer MoS2 was sufficiently thin to allow the substrate to dictate the nucleation process. However, as the MoS2 thickness increased, the nucleation process was no longer controlled by the substrate, resulting in a more disordered Ga2O3 film. Our DFT calculations revealed that the lowest energy configuration for Ga adsorption is achieved when Al directly aligns with the bottom S and Ga aligns with the top S. This results in an Al(δ+)-S(δ−)-S(δ+) arrangement that forms the basis for Ga2O3 nucleation. The concept of charge-mediated remote epitaxy can be extended to a wide range of materials, enabling rational material selection based on inter- and intra-atomic interactions to achieve optimal growth and exfoliation.

Once α-Ga2O3 is fabricated as a free-standing membrane, it can be integrated into a variety of platforms at the materials level. As previously mentioned, remote epitaxial α-Ga2O3 can significantly simplify thermal management. Furthermore, this free-standing Ga2O3 enables the development of an additional key device class - power electronics - that can be seamlessly integrated with silicon and other compound semiconductors, such as gallium arsenide and gallium nitride. This advancement paves the way for multifunctional electronic systems capable of performing logic, communication, and power management on a single chip (43–45). Traditionally, integration of diverse devices is only possible at the chip level, which increases the overall footprint and introduces latency issues. The high degree of flexibility afforded by remote epitaxy offers exciting opportunities for advancing next-generation wide-bandgap semiconductor technologies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

2D polycrystalline MoS2 synthesis

Polycrystalline MoS2 was synthesized by sulfurization of a precursor solution coated on c-plane sapphire substrates. Prior to spin coating, a substrate was sequentially cleaned by sonication in acetone, isopropanol (IPA), and deionized water. The precursor solution contained (NH4)2MoS4, dissolved in a mixed solvent system of dimethylformamide, n-butylamine and ethanolamine (21). This solvent combination was employed to ensure the uniform coating of the precursor solution by controlling the viscosity and preventing the formation of MoS42− clusters during dissolution (46).

After spin coatings, samples were soft-baked at 150°C for 30 minutes to remove solvents. The synthesis involved two annealing steps: reduction of (NH4)2MoS4 to MoS3 under N2/H2 flow at <1 Torr and 300°C for 10 minutes, and conversion to MoS2 using molten sulfur under N2 atmosphere at 600–700 Torr and 900°C for 1 hour.

α-Ga2O3 thin film growth

The α-Ga2O3 thin film was grown using the mist CVD method. Mist CVD is a deposition technique in which the precursor solution is atomized into a mist and transported to the reactor, typically under atmospheric pressure, to facilitate the growth of single-crystal Ga2O3 (42). In this study, a 0.02 M solution of gallium acetylacetonate (Ga(acac)3) was prepared in deionized water with added HCl to enhance solubility. The solution was then atomized using a 1.7 MHz ultrasonic transducer and delivered into the reactor by N2 carrier gas. To prevent oxidation of MoS2 during the temperature ramp-up, an N2 ambient was established in the reactor prior to growth. Typical growth temperature and rate for α-Ga2O3 were 450°C and approximately 700 nm/hour, respectively. To investigate the influence growth conditions on remote epitaxy on MoS2, the growth temperature was varied from 420 to 470°C, and the film thickness was controlled in the range of 100 to 700 nm.

α-Ga2O3 thin film exfoliation

The exfoliation of the α-Ga2O3 thin film was carried out using a Ti/Ni stressor. First, the sample with the grown Ga2O3 thin film was cleaned sequentially with acetone, IPA, and deionized water. A 50 nm Ti adhesion layer was then deposited using an e-beam evaporator to aid in the deposition of the stressor layer. Subsequently, a 3.54 μm Ni stressor layer, which induces tensile stress to facilitate separation, was deposited using DC sputtering. Afterward, a thermal release tape was applied to exfoliate the α-Ga2O3 thin film. Additionally, SEM EDS analysis was performed on both the exfoliated thin film and the remaining substrate.

Material characterizations

Material characterization employed multiple techniques: Raman spectroscopy for MoS2 thickness determination (Renishaw inVia Reflex) and mapping (HORIBA LabRAM HR Evolution), x-ray diffraction for structural analysis (Panalytical X’pert3 MRD systems), and comprehensive surface analysis including SEM (JEOL JSM-6701F), EBSD (Hitachi SU8230), and AFM (Park Systems NX20). Cross-sectional microstructure was examined using TEM/STEM following FIB specimen preparation, with high-resolution imaging at 200 kV (FEI Titan G2) and analytical microscopy at 300 kV (Thermo Fisher Themis Z). XPS analysis of the MoS2 on sapphire surface was performed using a NEXSA system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The excitation source was mono chromatic Al Kα (energy: 1486.6 eV) with a spot size of 400 μm. For fitting of both Mo and O peaks were fitted with symmetric Voigt functions.

Computational details

The first-principles calculations were performed using the projected augmented wave (PAW) plane-wave basis, implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) (47). An energy cutoff of 520 eV was employed and the atomic positions were optimized until the maximum force on each of them was less than 0.01 eV/Å. The hetero-structures of Al2O3 and MoS2 were modeled using k-point grids of 8x8x1. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional (48) was employed for geometrical optimization and electronic structure calculations. For the van der Waals corrections, Grimme’s DFT-D3 method (49) based on a semiempirical GGA-type theory was utilized. The slab models had dangling bonds on the vacuum surface, which were terminated with pseudo-hydrogen atoms with appropriate fractional charges to prevent the generation of surface states. To determine the vacuum level, dipole corrections were introduced to compensate for the artificial dipole moment at the open ends (20 Å vacuum space along the c-axis) arising from the periodical boundary condition imposed in these calculations.
