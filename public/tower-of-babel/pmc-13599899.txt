Decentralised oncology trials: decentralisation as a tool, not the goal
Authors: Jordan E Cohen, Natalie Taylor, Angela C Webster, Jia Liu
Published: 2026

The way we design and deliver trials is evolving. Decentralised clinical trials (DCTs), defined most simply, are studies where components are performed away from a central research facility, providing a unique pathway with the potential to improve the accessibility of cancer research for patients.1

However, as enthusiasm for DCTs grows alongside efforts to improve equity in cancer care, decentralisation risks being misconstrued with patient-centred trial design despite limited evidence on patient-reported experiences of oncology DCTs. The study by Akala et al2 provides insights into the status of DCT implementation in oncology within the UK. Their review examined the DCT landscape by triangulating the current literature on DCT implementation, the regulatory status governing DCT use and findings from a national survey on DCT awareness across UK clinical trial units. Akala et al highlighted a major evaluation gap in oncology DCTs, with only eight trials across five countries having systematically evaluated the implementation of oncology DCTs.2 Patients living with cancer are a complex, heterogeneous group and decentralisation of trials may present challenges not experienced in non-cancer cohorts.

The question moving forward should no longer be whether oncology trials can be decentralised, but rather how decentralised elements are best selected, evaluated and resourced to support patient-centred design and improve equity in trial participation, while maintaining safety, robust trial quality and site feasibility.

First, decentralisation should seek to fundamentally improve patient-centred trial design, rather than simply act as its own endpoint. Each decision for decentralised element inclusion needs to be thoroughly considered, whether for patient benefit, to improve recruitment, as an operational measure, to improve cost efficiency, for environmental benefit, or some combination of these. The SHORT-FOX study was designed to enable patients to have local chemotherapy with telehealth reviews, reducing travel burden and associated costs, with greater uptake in regional patients.3 However, assumed convenience to investigators may not always align with patient preference. A Canadian survey by Richards et al demonstrated 79% of respondents found having options for the study location important, but 39% still preferred in-person interaction even at the cost of more travel.4 Similarly, a recent Japanese survey by Kawakami et al found a strong willingness for DCT element use, while simultaneously showing that preferences for face-to-face traditional care remained.5 Patient-centred decentralisation requires careful consideration and would be strengthened by consumer trial codesign and flexibility in empowering patients to choose their preferred method of care (table 1).

Second, DCT elements must be evaluated prospectively, rather than reviewed retrospectively, focusing on quantitative trial operation alone. Implementation studies so far have focused largely on data completeness, patient retention and protocol adherence to assess trial performance when DCT elements are integrated. An evidence-based assessment of trial implementation is paramount to capture both site operational feasibility and patient experience. Future evaluations must review measures of acceptability, uptake, patient and caregiver burden, site and local clinician workload, digital exclusion, equity, resource usage including environmental impact, sustainability, and uncaptured barriers through rigorous qualitative analysis. Both patient and clinician preferences need to be measured directly rather than inferred by researchers. Prospective mixed-methods evaluation, including using studies within a trial, can capture variation in patient and investigator preferences that conventional trial metrics alone may miss, helping identify acceptability concerns despite a seemingly otherwise successful DCT implementation. Remote evaluations may be time-saving for some but create unnecessary technological stress for others; local treatment may reduce travel but create uncertainty in care responsibilities between different teams; home monitoring may be convenient for some but unknowingly undesirable for others.

Third, patient-centred decentralisation cannot simply shift complexity elsewhere in the healthcare system. Centralisation of trials provides efficiency by concentrating expertise and resources around the site, meaning any move towards decentralisation should ensure its benefits justify any additional cost or reduced efficiency. Governance surrounding DCTs is multilayered, complex and variable between countries. The updated ICH E6(R3) has provided a foundation that patient and healthcare professional perspectives should inform trial design, quality measures should be collected prospectively and trial processes should reflect the level of risk and importance of the data rather than create avoidable work.6 Implementing this falls to all who design trials, from single-site investigator-initiated studies, through to multinational pharmaceutical industry trials. There is the opportunity to design trials, while supporting sites implementing these to allow for flexible participation and DCT element use, rather than placing the onus upon individual trial units to manage local operational complexities. Current UK guidelines, while having their own limitations, similarly support the principle that sponsors have a responsibility to engage patients, to assess technologies and risk and to account for investigator oversight.7 More globally, the US FDA guidance reinforces this notion of sponsor responsibility for the design, coordination and monitoring of decentralised activities.8

Akala et al2 have demonstrated that the enthusiasm for the decentralisation of oncology trials risks outpacing the evidence on how best to implement DCTs. The goal of oncology trials is to improve care options for patients with access to investigational medicines forming part of this care spectrum. The future is not maximally decentralised trials; it is deliberately designed, flexible trials that use decentralisation when it improves patient experience without compromising feasibility, safety or trial integrity.
