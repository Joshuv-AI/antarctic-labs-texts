Development and temporal validation of a multimodal post-test risk stratification model for pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings
Authors: Yingjie Zhou, Peng Liu, Hanbing Jia, Zi Wang, Xiaojing Zhou, Haiyan Li, Jia Zhang, Ci Liu, Xuejun Liu, Weijun Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To develop and temporally evaluate a multimodal post-test classification framework integrating prenatal imaging and genetic findings in pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings. We retrospectively screened 826 pregnancies undergoing routine prenatal screening or diagnostic testing. After exclusion of 8 pregnancies with insufficient follow-up information and 72 with incomplete data on the final model predictors, 746 pregnancies were included in the final complete-case analysis, including a normal pregnancy group (n = 641) and an abnormal pregnancy group (n = 105). Clinical characteristics and testing results were compared between groups. According to the enrollment period, 546 pregnancies were used for model development and internal validation, and 200 pregnancies from the same institution during a later time period were used as a temporally separated internal validation cohort. Variables independently associated with abnormal pregnancy status classification were identified using multivariable logistic regression, and a nomogram was constructed. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Ultrasound abnormalities (OR = 74.36, 95% CI: 21.75–254.30), abnormal karyotypes (OR = 12.59, 95% CI: 5.55–28.56), and pathogenic CNVs (OR = 19.47, 95% CI: 9.35–40.54) were independently associated with abnormal pregnancy status classification within this post-test framework. The framework demonstrated favorable apparent classification performance, with AUCs of 0.917 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.867–0.961) in the training set and 0.906 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.822–0.977) in the testing set. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the included variables were closely related to the criteria used for outcome classification, which may have led to optimistic estimates of apparent performance. Calibration was acceptable, and DCA suggested potential net benefit across threshold probabilities of 0.05–0.85. In the temporally separated internal validation cohort, the framework achieved an AUC of 0.969 (95% CI: 0.918–1.000), with a sensitivity of 0.950 and a specificity of 0.972; however, this result should be interpreted within the context of a single-center post-test clinical workflow and requires further multicenter evaluation. The multimodal post-test classification framework integrating ultrasound, karyotype, and CNV findings showed favorable apparent classification performance in this cohort of pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings. Its potential value lies in supporting structured post-test risk communication and multidisciplinary discussion, particularly in cases with complex or discordant prenatal findings. This framework should not be interpreted as a primary screening tool or as an independent predictor of the natural course of pregnancy. Further prospective multicenter evaluation is needed before broader clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53358-x.

Introduction

Adverse prenatal findings—including fetal structural anomalies, chromosomal abnormalities, monogenic disorders, and intrauterine fetal demise—represent major contributors to perinatal mortality, long-term disability, and global disease burden. Recent estimates from the World Health Organization indicate that congenital anomalies occur in approximately 5–6% of all pregnancies worldwide, with 25–30% directly attributable to chromosomal or genetic abnormalities1. In China, national surveillance data similarly report a prevalence of major birth defects of approximately 5–6%2. As the proportion of pregnancies in women of advanced maternal age continues to rise and environmental risk factors become increasingly complex, early and accurate identification of pregnancies at increased risk has become an important public health priority.

Current prenatal diagnostic modalities provide valuable but incomplete insights into fetal status. Ultrasonography is a non-invasive, real-time, first-line screening modality that can detect 70–80% of major structural anomalies, but it cannot directly define their underlying genetic etiology3,4. Conventional karyotyping has long been considered a standard cytogenetic method for detecting aneuploidies and large structural rearrangements, although its resolution is insufficient to identify abnormalities smaller than 5–10 Mb5,6. Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) and low-pass whole-genome sequencing for copy number variation analysis (CNV-seq) have substantially improved the detection of pathogenic CNVs, adding an additional 6–10% diagnostic yield in fetuses with ultrasound abnormalities7. However, CNV-based methods are limited in their ability to detect balanced rearrangements, low-level mosaicism, or single-nucleotide variants8,9. NIPT is highly effective for screening common aneuploidies, but its clinical value for detecting rare CNVs or monogenic disorders remains limited10. Taken together, no single modality is sufficient to capture the full spectrum of prenatal structural and genetic abnormalities.

To address these limitations, several recent studies have attempted to develop multivariable classification or risk stratification models. However, most have relied on dual-modality combinations, such as ultrasonography with NIPT or CMA, and their external validation performance has generally been moderate, with reported AUC values ranging from 0.85 to 0.9411. A few studies have explored integrating ultrasonography, karyotyping, and CMA, but these models have mostly remained at the internal validation stage, with AUCs of 0.89–0.94, and have rarely quantified the relative contributions of different diagnostic modalities12,13. Therefore, there remains a need for a more integrated and carefully validated framework that combines imaging, cytogenetics, and CNV analysis in pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings.

In this context, the present study integrated prenatal ultrasonography, chromosomal karyotyping, and CNV analysis into a multimodal logistic regression model for post-test risk stratification among pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings. After screening 826 eligible pregnancies and excluding 80 cases due to insufficient follow-up information or incomplete predictor data, 746 pregnancies were included in the final complete-case analysis, of which 546 cases were used for model development and internal validation, and an additional 200 cases from the same institution but a later time period were used as a temporally separated internal validation cohort. Several representative clinical vignettes are provided in the Supplementary Material only as descriptive examples of multimodal prenatal assessment in real-world practice. These special cases were not part of model development, validation, recalibration, or formal evaluation of the proposed algorithm, and they should not be interpreted as evidence of model performance in individual patients. Rather, they are included only to illustrate the clinical complexity and interpretive heterogeneity often encountered in prenatal care. The aim of this study was to develop and validate an integrated quantitative framework to support more standardized post-test risk stratification and risk communication in pregnancies with known abnormal prenatal findings, rather than to serve as a primary screening tool or an independent predictor of the natural course of pregnancy.

Materials and methods

Research subjects

This study initially screened 826 pregnant women who underwent routine prenatal screening and/or prenatal diagnosis at our hospital between January 2019 and December 2024. After exclusion of cases with insufficient follow-up information or incomplete data on the final model predictors, 746 pregnancies were included in the final complete-case analysis to support pregnancy status classification based on prenatal findings and available post-diagnostic clinical records. The participants underwent one or more of the following investigations according to clinical indication: ultrasound screening, serological screening, NIPT, amniotic fluid cell analysis, chromosomal karyotyping, CNV-seq, and, in selected cases, WES. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee. All enrolled pregnancies underwent routine prenatal ultrasound screening, which served as the entry point of the diagnostic pathway. Additional imaging or genetic tests were performed based on clinical indications.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: singleton or twin pregnancy with a gestational age ≥ 12 weeks; completion of at least one prenatal imaging or genetic test; availability of sufficient follow-up information for final pregnancy status classification; and provision of informed consent to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria consisted of participants with serious comorbid conditions that could substantially affect pregnancy assessment, such as malignant tumors or organ failure; those lacking key prenatal examinations or follow-up information required for final classification; and those exposed to experimental interventions or medications known to affect fetal development.

Inclusion of typical cases

Several representative clinical vignettes from the study cohort are provided in the Supplementary Material solely as descriptive contextual examples of multimodal prenatal assessment in real-world clinical practice. These special cases were not incorporated into model development, variable selection, validation, recalibration, or formal evaluation of algorithm performance. The proposed nomogram was not retrospectively applied to these cases, and they should not be interpreted as individual-level demonstrations of model validity or clinical utility. We acknowledge that, because these vignettes were not presented as formal algorithm-application examples, their contribution to the main analytical objectives of the study is limited. Accordingly, they are intended only to illustrate the complexity of integrating imaging and genetic findings in prenatal care rather than to provide direct evidence supporting model performance.

Instruments and methods

Ultrasound examinations were performed using the GE Voluson E10/E8 diagnostic system equipped with an abdominal convex probe (frequency 2–5 MHz), following the ALARA principle. Fetal MRI examinations were conducted using the GE Signa 1.5T MR imaging system to assess the anatomical sites of fetal lesions.

With informed consent, approximately 10 mL of peripheral blood was collected from each pregnant woman after 12 weeks of gestation for cfDNA extraction to assess the risk of fetal trisomies.

With informed consent, fetal samples (including amniotic fluid, chorionic villi, or umbilical cord blood) were obtained through procedures such as amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling. Concurrently, peripheral blood samples from the pregnant woman and her spouse were collected as parental controls.

Abnormality

Imaging abnormalities were defined based on prenatal ultrasonography and, when clinically indicated, fetal MRI. Structural abnormalities were categorized according to the affected organ system, while soft markers, fetal growth abnormalities, and amniotic fluid abnormalities were evaluated according to standardized prenatal imaging guidelines. All imaging findings were reviewed by experienced fetal medicine specialists. For model construction, imaging abnormalities primarily referred to fetal structural abnormalities identified on ultrasound; isolated soft markers without associated structural abnormalities were not classified as imaging abnormalities.

An abnormal NIPT result was defined as a high-risk result exceeding laboratory-specific thresholds for common aneuploidies. Non-informative results or those with low fetal fraction were not classified as abnormal. CNVs were interpreted according to established ACMG guidelines14, and only pathogenic or likely pathogenic CNVs were considered abnormal for model construction. For the purposes of final clinical grouping, genetic test findings were interpreted according to their clinical significance rather than treated as equivalent to adverse outcome classification solely on the basis of technical abnormality.

Outcome definition

For the purposes of this study, pregnancies were categorized into a normal pregnancy group and an abnormal pregnancy group based on the integrated clinical assessment of prenatal imaging findings, genetic test interpretation, and available follow-up or postnatal information documented in the medical records.

The abnormal pregnancy group included pregnancies with clinically significant structural abnormalities and/or pathogenic or likely pathogenic genetic abnormalities considered relevant to fetal outcome.

Pregnancies were classified as normal when no clinically significant structural abnormality was identified at birth or follow-up and no confirmed clinically significant genetic disorder was established. Therefore, final group assignment was not determined by any single technical test result alone. A small number of pregnancies in the normal group had abnormal karyotype or CNV reports that were not ultimately considered clinically significant for final outcome classification, such as variants of uncertain significance (VUS), inherited balanced chromosomal rearrangements, benign chromosomal variants (for example, pericentric inversion of chromosome 9 [inv(9)]), or other findings insufficient to support classification into the abnormal pregnancy group. In contrast, aneuploidies or genetic abnormalities with established clinical significance, such as trisomy 21 or 47,XXY (Klinefelter syndrome), were classified into the abnormal pregnancy group even in the absence of major structural abnormalities detected at birth or follow-up.

To improve the transparency of abnormality classification, structural abnormalities were further categorized according to the major ultrasound-detected system involved, and the corresponding karyotype and CNV findings were summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

The abnormal pregnancy group was not further stratified according to subsequent clinical trajectory (for example, termination of pregnancy versus postnatal complications), because detailed trajectory-level outcome data were not consistently available in this retrospective dataset.

To further clarify the distinction between technical abnormalities, clinical significance, and final pregnancy status classification, illustrative examples of representative findings and their corresponding classification within the study framework are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Inclusion information and grouping situation

Pregnant women were categorized into two groups: a normal pregnancy group and an abnormal pregnancy group. The following information was collected for each case: maternal age, paternal age, menstrual regularity, mode of conception (including IVF), gestational age at examination, gravidity, parity, number of fetuses, nuchal translucency (NT) measurement, ultrasound findings, MRI findings, non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) results, karyotype results, and CNV results.

Because this was a retrospective clinically indicated testing cohort, not all participants underwent the same downstream investigations. The availability of karyotype analysis, CNV testing, fetal MRI, or WES depended on ultrasound findings, screening results, family history, and clinical decision-making. Therefore, missingness was likely related to clinical indication rather than being completely random. We did not perform multiple imputation for missing data. Instead, a complete-case analysis was conducted based on the final model predictors.

A total of 826 pregnancies initially met the eligibility criteria. Of these, 80 were excluded, including 8 pregnancies with insufficient follow-up information for final pregnancy status classification and 72 with incomplete data on the final model predictors. Consequently, 746 pregnancies were included in the final complete-case analysis. According to the enrollment period, 546 pregnancies were assigned to the earlier-period model-development cohort and were randomly split into a training set (n = 383) and a testing set (n = 163), while 200 pregnancies from a later period within the same institution were used as a temporally separated internal validation cohort. Accordingly, the proposed model should be interpreted as applicable primarily to pregnancies with complete post-test multimodal data rather than to an unselected general prenatal screening population. A participant flow diagram illustrating case screening, exclusions, and cohort allocation is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/). Baseline characteristics were compared between the normal pregnancy group and the abnormal pregnancy group. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors associated with abnormal pregnancy status. Variables retained in the multivariable analysis were incorporated into the final model, which was presented as a nomogram.

The model was developed as a post-test quantitative risk stratification tool in pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings, rather than as a primary screening or standalone diagnostic model. Because the included predictors were conceptually related to the criteria used for final group classification, model performance metrics should be interpreted as measures of post-test risk stratification performance within this specific clinical context rather than as evidence of independent predictive ability. Calibration curves were generated using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to assess agreement between predicted probabilities and observed group status. Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC). In addition, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the potential clinical net benefit of the model across different threshold probabilities. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic information of study participants and comparison between groups

A total of 746 pregnant women were included in this study, of whom 641 (85.9%) were classified into the normal pregnancy group and 105 (14.1%) were classified into the abnormal pregnancy group. The baseline characteristics and prenatal examination results of the two groups are summarized and compared below.

Regarding baseline characteristics, Gestational age at index ultrasound examination in the abnormal pregnancy group compared with the normal group [21.73 (19.00–24.00) weeks vs. 23.25 (19.00–27.00) weeks, P < 0.001], reflecting earlier detection and referral in pregnancies later classified into the abnormal pregnancy group. Additionally, the proportion of women with more than three pregnancies was higher in the abnormal group [26.67% (28/105) vs. 15.29% (98/641), P = 0.004]. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in maternal age [30.00 (27.00–33.00) years vs. 30.00 (28.00–33.75) years], paternal age [30.40 (28.00–34.00) years vs. 31.00 (28.00–34.00) years], parity composition (multiparas: 68.49% vs. 65.71%), number of fetuses (multiple pregnancies: 1.25% vs. 3.81%), menstrual regularity (regular: 97.19% vs. 96.24%), or NT screening abnormality rate [16.19% (17/105) vs. 10.92% (70/641)] (all P > 0.05).

Among the prenatal diagnostic indicators, ultrasound structural examination showed the most pronounced differences between groups, with an abnormal rate of 48.57% (51/105) in the abnormal pregnancy group, compared with 0.62% (4/641) in the normal group (P < 0.001). Genetic testing indicators also differed significantly: the abnormal group had a NIPT abnormality rate of 37.10% (39/105) versus 8.74% (56/641) in the normal group (P < 0.001); karyotype analysis abnormal rates were 49.50% (52/105) versus 3.43% (22/641) (P < 0.001); and CNV abnormalities were most pronounced, occurring in 73.30% (77/105) of the abnormal group compared with 4.52% (29/641) of the normal group (P < 0.001). No significant difference was observed in fetal MRI abnormality rates between the two groups [1.90% (2/105) vs. 5.00% (32/641), P = 0.160]. The baseline characteristics and prenatal test results are summarized in Table 1.

Multicollinearity testing indicated that the variance inflation factor (VIF) values for each variable in the model were close to 1 (ultrasound diagnosis: 1.02; karyotype results: 1.03; CNV results: 1.02), well below the commonly used threshold of 5. This suggests that there is no significant multicollinearity among the three predictor variables, and each independently contributes to the classification of abnormal pregnancy status. Accordingly, the estimated regression coefficients are stable and reliable.

A more detailed classification of structural and genetic abnormalities is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Because some fetuses presented with abnormalities involving multiple organ systems, the ultrasound categories in this table are not mutually exclusive; therefore, the numbers in each category do not sum to the total number of abnormal pregnancies shown in Table 1. The table presents the distribution of ultrasound-detected abnormality patterns and the corresponding karyotype and CNV findings for each category.

It should be noted that final outcome grouping was based on integrated clinical significance rather than any single genetic test result alone; therefore, a small number of abnormal karyotype or CNV findings were retained in the normal pregnancy group when they were not considered sufficient to establish a clinically significant abnormal pregnancy outcome, such as variants of uncertain significance, inherited balanced rearrangements, or benign chromosomal variants.

Results of multivariate logistic regression analysis

Using abnormal pregnancy status classification as the dependent variable (Table 2), and based on clinical considerations, variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis (Table 3) were included in a multivariate logistic regression model. A stepwise selection method was applied for further analysis. The results demonstrated that abnormal ultrasound findings (OR = 74.36, 95% CI: 21.75–254.30, P < 0.001), abnormal karyotype results (OR = 12.59, 95% CI: 5.55–28.56, P < 0.001), and abnormal CNV detection (OR = 19.47, 95% CI: 9.35–40.54, P < 0.001) were independently associated with classification into the abnormal pregnancy status group within this post-test risk stratification framework.(Table 4).

Nomogram construction and ROC analysis

The model was developed as a post-test quantitative risk stratification tool in pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings. A nomogram was constructed based on three independent predictors identified in the multivariable logistic regression analysis: ultrasound findings, karyotype results, and CNV results (Fig. 1). Each variable level corresponded to a specific point value, and the total score was used to estimate the corresponding risk probability. This ROC analysis summarizes the discriminatory performance of the model in the overall development sample, whereas the training- and testing-set results are presented separately in the internal validation analysis below.

ROC curve analysis is shown in Fig. 2, with an AUC of 0.925 (95% CI: 0.891–0.958). The optimal cutoff value determined by the Youden index was 0.122, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.876 and a specificity of 0.924. These findings suggest that the multivariable model had favorable discriminatory performance in the study population.

Internal validation of the post-test classification framework

A total of 746 pregnancies were included in the final complete-case analysis. Of these, 546 pregnancies from an earlier period were used for model development and internal validation and were randomly divided into a training set and a testing set, while an additional 200 pregnancies from the same institution but a later time period were used as a temporally separated internal validation cohort.

ROC curve analysis with 1,000 bootstrap resamplings was performed to assess model discrimination (Fig. 3). The AUC was 0.917 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.867–0.961) in the training set and 0.906 (bootstrap 95% CI: 0.822–0.977) in the testing set, suggesting similar discriminatory performance in the two datasets.

A nomogram was constructed from the multivariable logistic regression model based on three predictors: ultrasound findings, karyotype results, and CNV results (Fig. 4). Among these predictors, abnormal ultrasound findings had the largest contribution to the total score, followed by CNV abnormalities and abnormal karyotype results.

Calibration curves showed acceptable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed group status in both datasets (Fig. 5). The Brier scores were 0.0507 and 0.0425 in the training and testing sets, respectively, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test P values were 0.9321 and 0.2354, suggesting no substantial lack of fit.

Decision curve analysis showed that the model provided higher net benefit than the two extreme strategies across threshold probabilities of approximately 0.05–0.80 in the training set and 0.05–0.85 in the testing set (Fig. 6). These results suggest that the model may have potential value for post-test risk stratification in pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings.

Temporal validation analysis

Further evaluation of model classification performance was conducted using a temporally separated internal validation cohort from the same institution. Among the 200 cases, the model correctly identified 19 abnormal cases (true positives) and misclassified 1 abnormal case as normal (false negative). It also correctly classified 175 normal pregnancies (true negatives) and misclassified 5 normal pregnancies as abnormal (false positives). According to the confusion matrix, the overall accuracy was 0.97 (95% CI: 0.936–0.989), which was higher than the No Information Rate (NIR, P = 0.000148). The Kappa coefficient was 0.847. In addition, the model showed a sensitivity of 0.950, a specificity of 0.972, a positive predictive value of 0.792, a negative predictive value of 0.994, and a balanced accuracy of 0.961. McNemar’s test yielded P = 0.221, suggesting no significant asymmetry in misclassification types (Table 5).

In the temporal validation cohort, the discriminative ability of the model was evaluated in a temporally separated sample from the same institution. ROC curve analysis showed a high apparent discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.969 (95% CI: 0.918-1.000; Fig. 7). The optimal cutoff value determined by the Youden index was 0.349, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.95 and a specificity of 0.972. However, because this validation cohort was derived from the same institution and shared a similar diagnostic workflow and clinical context, the observed performance may be optimistic and should not be interpreted as evidence of broad external generalizability. Further validation in larger multicenter populations is required.

Calibration curves based on five equal-frequency bins were used to assess agreement between predicted probabilities and observed group status in the temporal validation cohort (Fig. 8). The calibration line of the model was generally close to the reference line, suggesting acceptable agreement between predicted and observed values. In the low-risk range, predicted probabilities were similar to the observed event rates. In the medium-to-high probability range, observed event rates were slightly higher than the predicted probabilities. Overall, the calibration results suggest acceptable agreement between predicted and observed outcomes in the temporal validation cohort, although broader transportability remains to be established.

In the temporal validation cohort, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to assess the potential net benefit of the model across a range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 9). Across threshold probabilities of approximately 0.05–0.90, the model showed higher net benefit than the two extreme strategies. This pattern was more apparent in the low-to-moderate threshold range. Overall, the DCA results in the temporal validation cohort were generally consistent with those observed in the internal validation analyses and suggest potential value of the model within similar post-test clinical settings, although broader clinical applicability remains to be established.

Discussion

The present study developed and validated a multivariable logistic regression model integrating ultrasound findings, chromosomal karyotype results, and CNV findings in pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings. In this dataset, ultrasound-detected structural abnormalities, abnormal karyotype results, and pathogenic CNVs were identified as independent predictors in the model. The model showed favorable apparent discriminatory performance in the internal validation datasets and in the single-center temporal validation cohort; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the predictors are conceptually related to the criteria used for final outcome classification, which may have contributed to optimistic estimates of model performance. Accordingly, the model is best understood as a post-test risk stratification framework for structured risk communication within this specific clinical context rather than as an independent predictive tool.

Main research findings

Although the model developed in the current study included only three core variables, it showed favorable apparent discriminatory performance in both the internal validation datasets and the single-center temporal validation cohort, with AUCs of 0.906–0.917 and 0.969 (95% CI: 0.918-1.000), respectively. Among the included predictors, ultrasound-detected structural abnormalities showed the largest effect size (OR = 74.36), suggesting that this variable contributed most strongly to the model in our dataset. The nomogram, calibration analysis, and decision curve analysis further supported the overall performance of the model and its potential value for risk stratification. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution because the final outcome classification partially overlapped conceptually with the included predictors, and this may have contributed to optimistic estimates of apparent discrimination. Further validation in larger and more heterogeneous populations is required. The large effect size observed for ultrasound abnormalities should be interpreted within the context of this clinically enriched post-test cohort. Because ultrasound findings were also clinically relevant to final pregnancy status classification, this association may partly reflect incorporation bias and predictor–outcome overlap rather than an independent causal or prognostic effect.

Clinical utility

Importantly, the proposed nomogram is not intended to replace established prenatal diagnostic procedures. Instead, it serves as a post-test risk stratification tool that integrates heterogeneous prenatal information into an individualized quantitative estimate. This type of quantitative assessment may facilitate more standardized prenatal counseling, improve communication of uncertainty to expectant parents, and support stratification of follow-up and perinatal management intensity, particularly in cases with discordant or inconclusive findings.

The potential added value of the nomogram lies primarily in its ability to translate heterogeneous prenatal findings into a structured numerical estimate. In routine practice, such a tool may be helpful in complex cases with multiple abnormal findings, where qualitative interpretation alone may vary across clinicians or centers. We therefore view this model as a complementary post-test risk stratification tool rather than a standalone replacement for expert clinical judgment. Supplementary clinical vignettes were included only to provide descriptive clinical context for the multimodal diagnostic pathway. They were not used for model development, validation, or formal evaluation of algorithm performance.

In clinical practice, the proposed model is intended to support structured post-test counseling and multidisciplinary discussion rather than replace expert clinical judgment. For example, pregnancies with relatively high model-derived classification probabilities may warrant intensified counseling, closer follow-up, additional targeted evaluation, and more detailed discussion of perinatal management options. In contrast, pregnancies with relatively low model-derived classification probabilities in the context of discordant or uncertain findings may be managed with standard follow-up and careful reassessment rather than immediate escalation of intervention. These examples are intended to illustrate possible clinical use scenarios rather than fixed decision thresholds.

At the same time, we acknowledge that the supplementary clinical vignettes included in this study were not designed as formal demonstrations of algorithm application. Because they were not prospectively selected or retrospectively scored using the nomogram, they provide only limited incremental value to the main analytical results and should be interpreted solely as supplementary contextual material.

Comparison with previous studies

Currently, most prenatal risk prediction models are based on single- or dual-modality approaches, often combining imaging findings (e.g., soft markers or structural abnormalities) with NIPT, or integrating ultrasound with CMA; however, evidence from external validation and broader generalizability remains limited. Models combining ultrasound soft markers with NIPT have generally reported external validation AUCs ranging from 0.85 to 0.93. For instance, Norton et al.15 reported in a large multicenter study that a trisomy screening model achieved an external validation AUC of 0.89–0.92. Although sensitivity was high, the model was primarily designed for common aneuploidies and could not account for CNVs and other submicroscopic abnormalities. Similarly, Zhang et al.16 found that an extended NIPT model achieved an external validation AUC of 0.87, with relatively limited predictive ability for rare CNVs. Models integrating ultrasound structural abnormalities with CMA have also shown potential value, but their reported performance varied across studies. Most studies reported internal validation AUCs of 0.88–0.94, whereas external validation AUCs were generally lower, ranging from 0.85 to 0.8817,18. This variation may be related to population heterogeneity, differences in ultrasound interpretation, and the inherent limitations of specific testing modalities. In addition, CMA may identify variants of uncertain significance (VUS), which can complicate clinical interpretation and limit the practical applicability of such models. Although some studies attempted to integrate ultrasound, karyotyping, and CMA19,20, with reported AUCs of 0.89–0.94, most were limited to internal validation and did not quantify the relative contributions of the included modalities.

In this context, the present study developed a unified post-test classification framework integrating imaging, cytogenetics, and molecular genetics. In our dataset, the model showed high apparent discrimination in the temporal validation cohort from the same institution (AUC = 0.969). Previous evidence has shown that multimodal models can achieve good overall discrimination. For example, in a multimodal meta-analysis by Shreeve et al.21, the combined AUC was 0.92, decreasing to 0.87–0.90 on external validation, and the International Society for Prenatal Diagnosis (ISPD) reported that the median AUC of multimodal clinical models is approximately 0.9122. However, because our validation was performed within a single-center temporally separated cohort rather than across independent institutions, this result should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive evidence of superior transportability. The high apparent performance observed in the temporal validation cohort may reflect similarities in patient characteristics, case mix, and diagnostic workflows rather than true generalizability. Rather than indicating superiority over prior models, our findings suggest that integrating ultrasound findings, karyotype results, and CNV analysis within a single framework may offer a potentially useful basis for post-test risk stratification in similar clinical settings.

Compared with these previously reported ranges, the performance observed in our temporal validation cohort from the same institution appears encouraging. However, given differences in study design, population characteristics, outcome definitions, and validation strategies across studies, direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. Rather than indicating definitive superiority, our findings suggest that integrating ultrasound findings, karyotype results, and CNV analysis within a single model may provide a more comprehensive basis for post-test risk stratification.

Moreover, in the temporal validation cohort, the model showed an overall accuracy of 97% and a specificity of 0.972. To support model stability and reduce the risk of overfitting, variable independence was assessed, and all included predictors showed variance inflation factors of less than 5. Among the included predictors, structural abnormalities detected by ultrasound showed the largest effect size within the multimodal framework (OR = 74.36), exceeding the effect sizes reported for soft markers in some previous studies23,24. This finding suggests that ultrasound-detected structural abnormalities contributed most strongly to model performance in our dataset, which is clinically plausible given their importance as direct phenotypic manifestations of fetal developmental disturbance. Nevertheless, the magnitude of this association should be interpreted cautiously and requires further confirmation in larger and more heterogeneous populations.

Clinical significance and potential application

The present model is based on three routinely available indicators and therefore has the practical advantage of relative simplicity in data acquisition and implementation. Decision curve analysis suggested that the model may provide potential net benefit across a broad threshold range of approximately 0.05–0.85, indicating that it could be useful for post-test risk stratification in clinically abnormal pregnancies. Its potential value lies mainly in supporting more standardized quantitative communication and multidisciplinary discussion, rather than replacing expert case-specific interpretation.

In addition, because ultrasound-detected structural abnormalities showed the largest contribution in the current model, our findings are consistent with the clinical importance of structural abnormalities as a major indication for further invasive prenatal evaluation25,26. However, this observation should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive, and detailed phenotype-genotype correlation remains essential in individual cases.

The clinical implications of false-positive and false-negative classifications should also be considered. In this post-test setting, a false-positive classification may lead to intensified counseling, additional imaging or genetic investigations, increased parental anxiety, and potentially unnecessary escalation of multidisciplinary management. Conversely, a false-negative classification may result in underestimation of fetal risk, insufficient counseling, delayed referral, or missed opportunities for closer surveillance and delivery planning. Therefore, the proposed model should not be used in isolation. Instead, it should be interpreted together with detailed phenotypic assessment, expert genetic interpretation, family history, and shared decision-making.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study conducted within a clinically indicated testing pathway. Model development was based on complete-case analysis, and additional downstream investigations, including karyotype analysis, CNV testing, MRI, and WES, were not performed uniformly across all pregnancies but were selected according to ultrasound findings, screening results, family history, and clinical judgment. Therefore, missingness was likely informative rather than completely at random, and selection bias cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the model should be interpreted as applicable primarily to pregnancies with complete multimodal post-test data rather than to an unselected general prenatal screening population. We did not perform variable-wise missingness profiling, formal comparisons between included and excluded cases, or multiple imputation, and these issues should be addressed in future prospective studies.

Second, although a temporally separated validation cohort was included, it was derived from the same institution and shared a similar diagnostic workflow and clinical context. Therefore, the very high apparent discrimination observed in this cohort should be interpreted cautiously and should not be regarded as evidence of broad external generalizability. This high apparent performance may partly reflect similarities in patient characteristics, case mix, and diagnostic workflows within the same institution rather than true transportability across settings. Further multicenter validation in more heterogeneous populations is required.

Third, an important conceptual limitation is that the model was developed in pregnancies already characterized by clinically meaningful prenatal findings, and the final outcome classification partially overlapped conceptually with the predictors included in the model. This predictor–outcome overlap may have introduced circularity and contributed to optimistic estimates of apparent model performance. Accordingly, the present framework should be interpreted as a post-test risk stratification model for structured risk communication rather than as an etiologic, screening, or natural-course prognostic model.

Fourth, the abnormal pregnancy group was not stratified according to detailed post-diagnostic trajectories. Because termination of pregnancy is itself a post-diagnostic clinical decision influenced by fetal findings, parental preferences, counseling, and clinician judgment, and because detailed trajectory-level data were not consistently available, termination-related bias could not be formally evaluated. This should be taken into account when interpreting the observed associations and the apparent performance of the model.

In addition, the current model did not incorporate higher-dimensional omics approaches, such as whole-exome sequencing or whole-genome sequencing, which may lead to underestimation of risk in some pregnancies affected by monogenic disorders. Ultrasound assessment is also operator-dependent, and formal interobserver agreement analysis was not available in this retrospective study. Finally, although the variables included in the model are clinically relevant, some of the genetic tests may be associated with substantial cost. Formal cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond the scope of the present study but will be important for evaluating the feasibility of broader clinical implementation. In addition, although several supplementary clinical vignettes were provided to reflect the complexity of real-world prenatal assessment, these cases were not incorporated into the model framework and did not formally demonstrate algorithm application. Their interpretive value is therefore descriptive rather than analytical, which should be considered when assessing their relevance to the primary study objectives.

Future studies should therefore focus on prospective, multicenter validation in larger and more diverse populations, while also exploring the integration of higher-dimensional omics data and more advanced imaging approaches to further refine post-test classification and structured risk communication. In addition, formal cost-effectiveness analyses will be important to assess the feasibility and sustainability of implementing this model in routine clinical practice across different healthcare settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a single-center retrospective multimodal post-test risk stratification framework integrating ultrasound, karyotype, and CNV findings in pregnancies with abnormal prenatal findings. The model showed favorable apparent performance in internal and temporal validation within the same institution. However, it should be interpreted as a complementary tool for structured risk communication and multidisciplinary discussion in pregnancies with complete multimodal post-test data, rather than as a primary screening tool or an independent predictor of the natural course of pregnancy. Further prospective multicenter validation is required before broader clinical application.
