Surveillance of low‐grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasms for progression to pseudomyxoma peritonei: Results from a structured surveillance programme
Authors: Enda Hannan, Lorena Martin Roman, Lukas O'Brien, Anna Mueller, Oonagh Staunton, Conor Shields, John Aird, Jurgen Mulsow
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimLow‐grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (LAMN) of the appendix is a rare tumour that can progress to pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP). There is a lack of standardization of surveillance following resection of LAMN as the progression rate to PMP is unclear. The aim of this study was to evaluate the rate of progression following resection of LAMN to PMP in a structured surveillance programme.MethodData for all patients referred for LAMN surveillance from 2013 to 2021 were retrospectively collected. The surveillance regime consisted of annual CT and tumour markers for a 5‐year period. Patients who progressed to PMP were identified.ResultsOf the patients enrolled in surveillance following appendicectomy and LAMN diagnosis (65.1% female, median age 56 years), 83 had completed at least 1 year of surveillance (median follow‐up 24 months). Of these, 6% (n = 5) showed disease progression during follow‐up. The median time to progression was 23 months. Survival analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in progression with regards to T staging (p = 0.39), margin positivity (p = 0.11) or appendiceal perforation (p = 0.26). No patients with Tis disease developed PMP. A statistically significant difference in progression was seen in patients with M1b staging (p = 0.021) and in those with mucin beyond the right iliac fossa at diagnosis (p = 0.04).ConclusionThe observed progression rate justifies the necessity of postappendicectomy surveillance in patients with LAMN, with the risk of progression being highest within the first 3 years of diagnosis. The described surveillance programme allows for early detection of subclinical progression to PMP.

INTRODUCTION

Low‐grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (LAMN) is a rare clinical entity, with approximately 2000 cases diagnosed annually in the United States [1]. It is an unusual form of malignancy that is characterized by neoplastic epithelial cells which produce cytoplasmic mucin that is secreted into the lumen of the appendix, resulting in a dilated and mucus‐filled appendix [1, 2, 3, 4]. The term LAMN was first introduced by Misandri et al. to describe a pathological entity that lacks overt malignant features but is associated with extra‐appendiceal spread; it was subsequently recognized as part of the Peritoneal Surface Oncology Group International (PSOGI) classification for appendiceal neoplasia [3, 4]. LAMN does not metastasize by lymphatic or haematogenous means, but instead demonstrates pushing invasion as opposed to infiltrative invasion [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. If granted access to the peritoneal cavity, such as by means of appendiceal perforation, it can result in the deposition of mucin (often with epithelial cells) along peritoneal surfaces, resulting in pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

PMP is characterized by the progressive accumulation of mucinous ascites [6]. Despite a relatively indolent nature, it may be fatal if left untreated and is associated with severely debilitating symptoms in its advanced stages [6]. The incidence of PMP in western populations is 1.8 per million population [7]. The current standard of care of PMP is cytoreductive surgery (CRS) with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) [6, 8]. With this treatment modality, overall 5‐ and 10‐year survival rates have been reported at 75% and 63%, respectively [7]. In patients with advanced disease a complete surgical clearance may not be achievable and there is significant risk of morbidity following CRS/HIPEC. Thus, surveillance strategies that facilitate earlier detection and intervention would be of value [7, 9].

LAMN is frequently diagnosed incidentally, either by means of a radiologically or endoscopically abnormal appendix or as an unanticipated histopathological result following appendicectomy [2, 9]. Although appendicectomy is widely considered curative for LAMN, this rare pathological finding does pose an unanswered clinical dilemma [2, 10]. Given that LAMN is a precursor lesion for PMP, many clinicians perform routine surveillance due to concerns about disease recurrence and subsequent development of PMP [7]. However, what is currently unclear in the literature is the frequency with which patients will develop PMP following previous diagnosis and treatment for LAMN [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. There also exists significant discordance in surveillance practices following surgical resection of LAMN [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7].

In 2013, a surgical programme for the management of patients with peritoneal malignancy, including PMP, was established in Ireland [11]. As part of this service, a surveillance programme for patients diagnosed with LAMN evolved. This has offered the unique opportunity to report on a nationwide experience of rates of progression of LAMN to PMP, as well as to identify potential factors that may reflect a higher risk of progression. Such information may be valuable in guiding and informing surveillance practices.

METHOD

Study design and patient selection

A retrospective observational cohort study of all patients with a histopathological diagnosis of LAMN following surgical resection who were subsequently referred for inclusion in the surveillance programme was performed over an 8‐year period (July 2013 to July 2021). Patients were identified by review of a prospectively maintained database of patients discussed at the peritoneal malignancy multidisciplinary team meeting (MDM). Following this, a detailed review of patient charts and electronic medical records was performed. Demographic, intraoperative, postoperative, histopathological and surveillance data were collected. All patients of any age referred with a histopathological diagnosis of LAMN who were enrolled in the surveillance programme were included in the study. Patients who had appendiceal pathology other than LAMN were excluded from the study. Patients with LAMN who had not completed at least 1 year of surveillance were excluded from survival analysis.

Surveillance regime

Prior to enrollment in surveillance, all patients were discussed at the peritoneal malignancy MDM. LAMN was defined according to the PSOGI classification and was staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [12]. The surveillance programme for patients with LAMN was modelled after similar protocols used in two specialist centres in the United Kingdom and was as follows [7]: annual clinical reviewannual serum tumour markers for 5 years: carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19‐9 and cancer antigen 125annual CT of the abdomen and pelvis for 5 years.

Upon completion of the 5‐year surveillance window, patients would continue to undergo an annual clinical review, with a CT scan performed every 2 years until completion of 10 years of surveillance. Patients who demonstrated any clinical, biochemical or radiological anomaly were rediscussed at the MDM, and those who were identified as having progressed to PMP were considered for CRS/HIPEC.

Study outcomes

The primary endpoint for this study was the occurrence of progression to PMP following a prior histopathological diagnosis of LAMN after surgical resection. PMP was defined as radiological evidence of one or more new mucinous lesions in the peritoneal cavity on CT scan. The time to progression was also noted, with this defined as the time from surgical resection of the LAMN until the time of diagnosis of PMP. The presence of certain pathological features, such as appendiceal perforation, resection margins status, extra‐appendiceal mucin and AJCC staging, were also examined in the context of progression [12].

Statistical analysis and ethical approval

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 23 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and RStudio Desktop (RStudio, Boston, MA, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated and presented as a number and percentage for categorical variables, with median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Survival analysis was performed including the patients with at least 1 year's follow‐up using the Kaplan–Meier method. The log‐rank test was used for between‐group comparison. Approval for this study was granted by the local hospital clinical audit committee.

RESULTS

Patient demographics and clinical data

Table 1 summarizes the patient demographics and clinical data. A total of 83 patients with a histopathological diagnosis of LAMN after surgical resection were referred to the surveillance programme from 29 different hospitals over an 8‐year period. Of these, 65.1% (n = 54) were female and the median age was 56 years. The median time of follow‐up was 24 months. Most patients (79.5%, n = 66) were symptomatic with abdominal pain at the time of initial diagnosis. The indication for surgery in most patients was either a diagnosis of acute appendicitis (43.4%, n = 36) or an incidental finding of an appendiceal mucocele on CT (27.7%, n = 23). The histopathological diagnosis was made following appendicectomy (79.5%, n = 66), caecectomy (3.6%, n = 3), right hemicolectomy (8.3%, n = 7) or as part of a gynaecological surgery (8.3%, n = 7). Most patients (79.5%, n = 66) underwent surgery by a laparoscopic approach.

Pathological biochemical data

The pathological biochemical data are also summarized in Table 1. Most patients presented without appendiceal perforation (67.5%, n = 56) and had resection margins that were clear of mucin or epithelial cells (84.3%, n = 70). Appendiceal perforation was defined as a macroscopically identifiable full‐thickness defect in the wall of the appendix documented in the histopathological report. With regards to AJCC staging, most (57.1%, n = 48) had a histopathological stage of T4a, representing a LAMN with invasion of the serosal surface with acellular mucin or cellular tumour [12]. A majority also had M0 staging (71.4%, n = 60), representing the absence of intraperitoneal acellular mucin. Eleven patients (13.1%) had M1a staging, with intraperitoneal acellular mucin present, while four (4.8%) had M1b staging due to a finding of intraperitoneal mucin containing tumour cells. Of those who did have an associated finding with intraperitoneal mucin, 10 patients (11.9%) had mucin confined to the right lower quadrant while five (6%) had mucin in the pelvis. Inconsistent availability of tumour marker data prevented further analysis on their role in detecting progression.

Progression to PMP

Of the 83 patients included in this study, five (6%) showed progressive disease (see Table 2). Of these, three (60%) were female with a median age of 48 years. The median time to progression was 22 months (range 8.05–32.4 months). The indication for initial appendicectomy in these patients was either for a suspected diagnosis of acute appendicitis (n = 2, 40%) or an incidental finding of a radiologically abnormal appendix (n = 3, 60%). Two patients underwent laparoscopic appendicectomy, while the remaining three patients were managed by open appendicectomy, laparoscopic caecectomy and exploratory laparotomy, respectively. Intraperitoneal mucin was noted in three of the five patients at their initial surgery. Two patients had an AJCC histopathological staging of T4a, one had T3 staging and the remaining two did not have AJCC staging determined in their histopathological report. One patient who progressed had M1a staging in the context of a T4a LAMN. One patient who progressed had a positive margin in a T4a LAMN. One patient had a perforated appendix in the context of T3 staging. In patients without extra‐appendiceal mucin at the time of diagnosis of LAMN, the progression rate was 3.3% (n = 2/60), while 20% (n = 3/15) of patients with extra‐appendiceal mucin progressed to PMP.

Two patients with progression underwent CRS/HIPEC, with the final histology for one of these being high‐grade mucinous carcinoma peritonei. One patient underwent diagnostic laparoscopy 21 months following diagnosis of LAMN after open appendicectomy, revealing a small amount of acellular mucin in the pelvis that was aspirated at the time of laparoscopy. Given the low volume of isolated disease, this patient opted for surveillance over CRS/HIPEC and remains disease‐free to date on surveillance 7 years from diagnosis. In the remaining two who demonstrated progression, the overall disease volume remained low and, having considered the options, these patients were managed with ongoing surveillance. To date, both patients undergoing surveillance remain alive with stable disease on serial imaging (follow‐up duration 25 and 49 months, respectively).

Survival analysis

Survival analysis was conducted using a Kaplan–Meier curve with comparison by log‐rank test and demonstrated no statistically significant difference in progression to PMP with regards to AJCC T staging (p = 0.39), margin positivity (p = 0.11) or appendiceal perforation (p = 0.26) (Figures 1 and 2). A statistically significant difference in progression was seen in patients with AJCC M1b staging (p = 0.021) and in those with mucin beyond the right iliac fossa at diagnosis (p = 0.04).

DISCUSSION

The current study presents the results of a surveillance programme for the development of PMP following resection of appendiceal LAMN. As a result of the surveillance regimen, five subclinical progressions to PMP, representing 6% of the cohort, were detected in a timely manner, allowing for early surgical intervention where appropriate at a point when disease burden is likely to be significantly lower than at a time when the development of symptoms would prompt investigation. Two of the five patients, representing 2.4% of the entire cohort, proceeded to CRS/HIPEC while the other three patients were managed conservatively and their low‐volume disease has remained stable. Risk factors for progression were found to be M1b staging and the presence of mucin beyond the right iliac fossa at initial presentation. It is noteworthy that one of the patients who subsequently underwent CRS/HIPEC was found to have high‐grade peritoneal disease. To our knowledge, no adverse events have occurred as a result of surveillance and there were no delayed, missed or incorrect diagnoses of PMP in this cohort. The observed progression rate would appear to justify the necessity for surveillance, with just over 1 in 20 patients showing disease progression. Given that just over half of the patients who demonstrated progression were safely managed without surgical intervention, it may seem reasonable to suggest that diagnosis of progression in this cohort is not clinically relevant. However, it is entirely possible that such patients may progress to a stage where CRS/HIPEC should be considered in the future, and an awareness of their current status may allow for a timelier detection of such a progression at a subclinical stage by means of surveillance, thus facilitating intervention at an earlier time with a lower volume of disease.

An interesting observation of the current study is the timing of progression to PMP. In this cohort, all progressions occurred within a 3‐year window of diagnosis, suggesting that this may be the interval at which risk is highest. To our knowledge, only one other study has specifically reported on the observed time interval between diagnosis to progression in a structured surveillance programme, with two progressions amongst a cohort of 41 patients, which occurred at 18 and 22 months, respectively [13]. Another important observation is that the presence of perforated LAMN or AJCC T4a disease did not confer a statistically significant higher risk of progression to PMP than in those without these factors. It has previously been suggested that, in the presence of perforated LAMN, patients should be considered directly for CRS/HIPEC [14, 15]. Our experience would suggest that these patients can safely be surveilled. Similarly, some centres offer ‘pre‐emptive’ CRS/HIPEC for T4a LAMN [16, 17]. The Chicago Consensus Working Group lists both surveillance and surveillance and CRS/HIPEC as options for T4a LAMN, although the pre‐emptive utilization of CRS/HIPEC is not supported by high‐quality evidence [18].

The management of a margin‐positive LAMN is similarly controversial. In our study, a small number of patients with margin‐positive LAMN were surveilled without re‐excision and without subsequent documentation of disease recurrence or progression. However, the modest number of patients with this pathological finding in our cohort makes drawing a definitive conclusion on this difficult. Another observation of our study was that no progressions to PMP were observed in patients with AJCC‐staged Tis LAMN, suggesting that such patients are at particularly low risk. This has been observed in other surveillance programmes, with one cohort that consisted of more than 80% of patients with Tis staging reporting no progressions to PMP [2].

In our study, a group of 15 patients with localized intraperitoneal mucin at the time of LAMN resection were included in the surveillance programme. Such patients were offered surveillance on a case‐by‐case basis after a detailed discussion on the potential risks and benefits of such a strategy following MDM discussion. Three of the 15 patients in this group demonstrated progressive disease during the follow‐up period, but only one of these patients proceeded to CRS/HIPEC, with the other two opting for ongoing surveillance. The remaining 12 patients with intraperitoneal mucin remain progression‐free to date (median follow‐up 30.5 months, range 17–61 months). It is interesting to note that of these 12 patients five underwent an oophrectomy at the time of initial surgery due to ovarian involvement, suggesting that these patients had low‐volume PMP at the time of presentation but have not demonstrated disease progression to date (median follow‐up 27 months, range 17–45 months).

Management of patients with localized intraperitoneal mucin at time of diagnosis has proved controversial. The American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons suggest that appendicectomy with cytoreduction of periappendiceal peritoneum in those with acellular mucin localized to the right iliac fossa may be considered, with the addition of HIPEC in cases with cellular mucin due to the higher risk of peritoneal metastases [19]. Meanwhile, the Chicago Consensus stratifies management of M1a/b LAMN into three categories: acellular mucin localized to the RIF, widespread acellular mucin and cellular mucin [8]. For the latter two categories, CRS/HIPEC should be considered. For the former, both observation and CRS/HIPEC are considered viable options [18]. The Colorectal and Peritoneal Oncology Centre in Manchester, meanwhile, stratifies patients into lower‐risk ‘type 1’ lesions and higher risk ‘type 2’ lesions based on invasion beyond subserosa, the presence of perforation and peri‐appendiceal mucin, with LAMN 1 typically undergoing surveillance and LAMN 2 being offered CRS/HIPEC [7, 17]. Careful patient selection and appropriate counselling is essential if surveillance is to be offered over CRS/HIPEC in such patients.

To date, only a small number of studies have reported rates of progression to PMP following appendicectomy for LAMN. These studies are retrospective in nature, with modest patient numbers and mostly conducted in single centres. Guaglio et al. reported a 14‐year experience with 41 patients enrolled in surveillance, demonstrating a progression rate of 4.9% in a programme that consisted of 6‐monthly physical examination, CT scan and tumour markers for 5 years, and then yearly thereafter [13]. Both observed progressions in this study occurred within 2 years, one with and one without appendiceal perforation and extra‐appendiceal mucin, respectively [13]. Foster et al. reported on surveillance for 22 patients by 6‐monthly CT scan and tumour markers, with laparoscopy performed in the event of radiological disease or routinely after 12 months without radiological disease [14]. A progression rate of 23% was observed. All patients who progressed initially demonstrated appendiceal perforation and extra‐appendiceal mucin at diagnosis [14]. However, this study was limited by the absence of AJCC staging data [14]. Gupta et al. observed no progressions to PMP amongst 39 surveilled patients; however, the vast majority of this cohort had low‐risk Tis disease, and the surveillance methodology lacked standardization [19]. In contrast to this, more than half of our enrolled patients had T4a staging. Wong et al. examined 64 patients undergoing postappendicectomy surveillance; however, a high proportion of these also had Tis disease [20]. Two patients in that study progressed to PMP, both of whom had T4aM1b staging [20]. Tiselius et al. reported no progressions amongst 41 patients after LAMN resection; however, this study also lacked AJCC staging data [21]. In contrast, a study from the Anderson Cancer Center, Texas reported a PMP rate of 20% amongst 98 patients, but also lacked information regarding T staging [22].

The number of patients enrolled in the current study is larger than most similar studies and includes a wide range of relevant pathological data that are lacking in many prior publications. It was observed that patients with T4a disease, appendiceal perforation and positive margins can be safely surveilled, and that patients with Tis disease appear to pose a low risk of progression. The risk of progression also would appear to be highest within the first 3 years of diagnosis. This study is not without limitations. It is retrospective in nature with a relatively modest cohort of patients, and the availability of certain data variables, such as tumour markers, was limited. Given that patients were referred from multiple different centres, it is impossible to ascertain the impact that different surgical approaches and techniques may have had on progression outcomes. At the beginning of our surveillance programme, appendiceal pathologies were initially reviewed by local pathology departments from the referring centre rather than in our own centre. However, we now review all LAMN specimens in our own centre with a histopathologist with a special interest in appendiceal pathology. In addition, specific pathological data were missing in approximately 10% of patients. With regards to our utilization of tumour markers as part of the surveillance programme, it is important to note that these markers do lack specificity for PMP. However, it has been demonstrated that they do offer reasonable sensitivity in such a context, with up to 76% of patients who progress to PMP demonstrating an elevation in at least one of the utilized tumour markers. Our study also did not specifically examine the potential impact of tumour size. Lastly, our follow‐up period of 24 months is short. While our centre is the only one to offer such a programme in Ireland, inviting and encouraging nationwide referral for LAMN surveillance, transferring such patients to our care is not mandatory, and thus it is possible that some patients are being managed locally in their centre of diagnosis. Nonetheless, our study demonstrates that postappendicectomy surveillance for LAMN is a safe, viable and effective strategy, allowing for timely detection of subclinical progression to PMP to facilitate earlier intervention. The observed progression rate justifies the necessity for surveillance in this group of patients with a rare pathology. Larger collaborative studies would help to inform the intensity and duration of the surveillance offered to these patients.

CONCLUSION

The observed progression to PMP rate justifies the necessity of posttreatment surveillance in patients with LAMN, with the risk of progression being highest within the first 3 years of diagnosis. Patients with cellular mucin outside the appendix and those with mucin beyond the right lower quadrant appear to be at highest risk. Patients with Tis stage appear to be a very low risk and our current surveillance regime is likely to be too intensive for this group. A small cohort of carefully selected patients with extra‐appendiceal mucin at initial diagnosis were safely surveilled following appropriate counselling. This study provides valuable information which, if complimented by data from other centres, may inform the most optimum surveillance strategy for patients following resection of LAMN.
