Ruptured thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm treated with physician-modified endograft and in situ laser fenestrations to limit aortic coverage
Authors: Samie Cloutier, Jean-François Blair, Laura M Drudi, Stéphane Elkouri, Nathalie Beaudoin, Philippe Charbonneau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe management of thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms has shifted toward endovascular repair over recent decades. Fenestrated and branched custom-made devices remain largely restricted to elective settings, whereas off-the-shelf branched endografts may be used in urgent cases but are limited by specific anatomic constraints and its fixed design. We report the case of a 73-year-old woman with an extent II thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm presenting with suspected contained rupture 1 month after a first-stage frozen elephant trunk procedure. Emergent endovascular repair was performed using a physician-modified endograft incorporating two branches and three in situ laser fenestrations. The procedure was successful with an uneventful postoperative recovery.Keywords: Fenestrated branched endovascular aortic repair, Physician-modified endograft, Thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, In situ laser fenestration, Ruptured thoracoabdominal aneurysm

The management of thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms (TAAAs) has evolved substantially over decades, with a progressive shift toward endovascular techniques.1 In the elective settings, custom-made devices (CMDs) is the gold standard for complex aortic repair.2, 3, 4 However, manufacturing delays ranging from 2 to 10 weeks limit their use in patients presenting with symptomatic or ruptured aneurysms.5,6 When instructions for use are met, off-the-shelf branched endografts offer a readily available alternative for urgent repair, although the need for extensive aortic coverage may increase the risk of spinal cord ischemia (SCI).7 In situ laser fenestration (ISLF) represents a favorable option, but this requires aortic wall apposition of the graft at the level of the target vessels, and may present inherent risk of a type III endoleak. Finally, physician-modified endografts (PMEGs) remain a valuable alternative8 and represent the closest available alternative to CMDs when carefully planned and constructed.9 This report presents the case of a contained rupture of a complex Crawford extent II TAAA.

Case report

A 73-year-old female patient with a medical history of hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and atrial fibrillation treated with apixaban had previously undergone a frozen elephant trunk (FET) procedure as the first-stage treatment for an 85-mm Crawford extent II TAAA repair at another center. One month after the initial stage, the patient experienced mild thoracic chest pain while remaining hemodynamically stable. A computed tomography angiography demonstrated a 5-mm aortic diameter progression associated with a left pleural effusion of 20 Hounsfield units (Fig 1, A). The patient was subsequently transferred to our institution. The initial management strategy consisted of a staged procedure within the same hospital admission, with thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) deployed within the FET and extending to Ishimaru zone 5, followed by a planned fenestrated branched endovascular aortic repair within 2 weeks (Fig 1, B). After the first-stage TEVAR, the patient's symptoms completely resolved; however, 3 days later, she developed mild recurrent thoracic pain while remaining hemodynamically stable. A computed tomography angiography was urgently repeated and revealed a slight increase in the left pleural effusion. Despite an effusion of the same radiodensity as previously reported, the possibility of a contained rupture could not be excluded. Therefore, a diagnostic thoracentesis was performed and revealed fresh blood, confirming the diagnosis. The patient was then emergently taken to the hybrid operating room. A lumbar cerebrospinal fluid drain was placed before the procedure. PMEG based on a Cook Zenith-Alpha-36-161 (Cook Medical) thoracic endograft was prepared. Two 7-mm polytetrafluoroethylene surgical graft branches were sutured for the celiac trunk (CT) and the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), using locking sutures with Ethibond 4-0 (Ethibond Excel suture) and Gore-Tex CV-3. Gold pigtail markers were added on the PMEG to facilitate orientation (Fig 2). The modified graft was then resheathed, and the device was deployed using fusion imaging guidance. To optimize the landing zone and preserve as many lumbar arteries as possible, the distal sealing was achieved in the infrarenal aorta over a length of 40 mm in an aorta measuring 30 mm of diameter. A thoracic molding balloon was subsequently used for graft apposition. ISLFs for renal arteries were created using a 0.9-mm Turbo Elite laser catheter (Spectranetics). For each fenestration, a 0.014-inch BMW guidewire was advanced, followed by progressive dilatation of the fenestration with a 2-mm cutting balloon and a 4-mm monorail balloon. The wire was exchanged for a 0.035-inch wire to advance the bridging stents. The right renal and both left renal arteries were stented with 7 × 23-mm, 5 × 22-mm, and 5 × 22-mm BeGraft stents (BentleyInnoMed), respectively. The visceral branches were then completed with deployment of an 8 × 37-mm BeGraft plus stent in the SMA and an 8 × 57-mm BeGraft stent in the CT. Final angiography demonstrated satisfactory results, with preserved perfusion of all five visceral vessels and no evidence of dissection or endoleak (Fig 3). A chest tube was placed before leaving the operating room, and 800 mL of blood was drained during the first 24 hours. The patient was monitored in the intensive care unit with a target mean arterial pressure of ≥90 mm Hg. The cerebrospinal fluid drain was removed 48 hours after surgery. The patient had an uneventful recovery and was discharged home on postoperative day 7.

Aortic diameters were 30 mm at the level of the FET, 80 mm at the maximum aneurysm size, 42 mm at the level of the celiac trunk (CT) and superior mesenteric arteries (SMAs), 31 mm at the renal arteries, and 30 mm at the infrarenal level.

Discussion

This case illustrates limitations of diagnostic imaging in a suspected symptomatic TAAA. Regarding the pleural effusion, the low Hounsfield unit value did not allow us to clearly distinguish between an expected postoperative effusion following the recent FET procedure and a contained rupture of the thoracoabdominal aneurysm. Multidisciplinary discussion with radiology and cardiac surgery initially favored a postoperative effusion. Diagnostic thoracentesis was essential to confirm the urgency of the situation and orient the patient to an urgent repair.

Also, this case highlighted the flexibility of using PMEGs and ISLF in the urgent setting of a complex contained-rupture TAAA. The patient was recovering from a recent FET, and therefore an endovascular approach was selected as the best option. Gore TAMBE (W.L. Gore) and Cook Medical T-Branch have the advantage of being off-the-shelf and therefore rapidly available for urgent procedures, although those endoprostheses are designed to obtain sealing in the iliac arteries with an additional bifurcated component that does not allow preservation of the lumbar arteries in the infrarenal aortic segment.10 SCI risk increases considerably when spinal arterial collateral pathways are not sufficient in patients having an extensive coverage from a one-stage procedure, and the need to keep as much segmental arteries as possible is crucial.11 In an urgent situation, aneurysm exclusion from the circulation is mandatory, and therefore, staging options such as proximal TEVAR or minimally invasive segmental artery coil embolization are not possible. In our case, we therefore decided to select a landing zone in the infrarenal aorta to maintain patency of the large lumbar arteries. Using a thoracic endograft sized for appropriate oversize, the combination of PMEG and ISLF was considered a favorable option to obtain sealing few centimeters below renal arteries, preserving the lumbar arteries and allowing sufficient endoprosthesis length if a distal extension was required in the case of a type Ib endoleak.

The segmental artery laser preservation technique, which was previously described by our group,12 is a strategy that could have been used to preserve flow to the lumbar arteries. However, in this situation, an alternative strategy of landing above the lumbar arteries was selected.

Because contact between the endograft and the aortic wall at the renal level was anticipated, ISLF was chosen as the most reliable option to obtain technical success, especially to overcome potential rotation issues associated with PMEG fenestrations for the two left renal arteries with diameters of less than 5 mm. Moreover, fenestrations seem to offer superior long-term patency over branches for small target vessels. For the CT and SMA, standard wall PTFE branches were used to allow stronger stability and decrease the risk of complications associated with bridging stents.

The limitation of this strategy remains the risk of a type III endoleak associated with ISLF and PMEG when compared with CMDs. Also, those cases should be reserved for high-volume centers with a multidisciplinary and advanced endovascular aortic program.

Conclusions

PMEGs combined with in situ ISLF may provide a valuable strategy to reduce aortic coverage length and potentially mitigate SCI during urgent TAAA repair. In selected cases, strong clinical suspicion may outweigh equivocal imaging findings and guide urgent intervention.
