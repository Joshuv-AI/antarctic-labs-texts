Generating Fit Check Videos with a Handheld Camera
Bowei Chen, Brian Curless, Ira Kemelmacher-Shlizerman, Steven M. Seitz

Abstract
. Self-captured full-body videos are popular, but most deployments require mounted cameras, carefully-framed shots, and repeated practice. We propose a more convenient solution that enables full-body video capture using handheld mobile devices. Our approach takes as input two static photos (front and back) of you in a mirror, along with an IMU motion reference that you perform while holding your mobile phone, and synthesizes a realistic video of you performing a similar target motion. We enable rendering into a new scene, with consistent illumination and shadows. We propose a novel video diffusion-based model to achieve this. Specifically, we propose a parameter-free frame generation strategy, as well as a multi-reference attention mechanism, that effectively integrate appearance information from both the front and back selfies into the video diffusion model. Additionally, we introduce an image-based fine-tuning strategy to enhance frame sharpness and improve the generation of shadows and reflections, achieving a more realistic human-scene composition.

1.
Introduction

With the rise in popularity of video platforms like Tiktok and Instagram Reels, self-captured video has become a major industry.
However, taking high quality,
full-body
videos of yourself is not straightforward.
The most common solution is to use a tripod-mounted camera, but this introduces its own friction: creators must carry extra gear, guess at their framing without a reliable viewfinder, and often retake videos multiple times to achieve a desirable result
(
Anderson 2018b
;
Anderson 2018a
;
Peters 2013
)
.
Alternatively, you could use a full-length mirror – this enables full-body capture with a handheld mobile phone without tripod, but with awkward framing, limited ability to move (the viewpoint moves with you), and constraints on location – you’re restricted to filming in front of a mirror.
These limitations highlight the need for a more convenient, user-friendly solution for high-quality, full-body video self-capture.

Motivated by this, our goal is to enable full-body selfie video capture with a handheld mobile phone, plus a mirror for taking two (front+back) selfie stills, with support for virtual backgrounds, for a wide range of applications but with a particular focus on “fit check” videos (influencers trying on outfits) popular on social media.
Specifically, we seek to provide the following capabilities:

•

full-body self-capture with a handheld mobile phone that appears as if the camera was mounted on a tripod

•

video output generated from image captures (
i.e
.
, mirror stills) – eliminating the need to record actual full-body video

•

accurate rendering of both the front and back of the subject

•

flexible body motion,
e.g
.
, walks, twirls, etc.

•

virtual backgrounds with consistent shading

In short, we seek to provide the video you would have achieved with a tripod-mounted camera and lots of practice in your desired environment.

Our solution is to separately capture your appearance (in a mirror), your movement (from phone sensors), and a compatible background, and then
compose
them together into a desired video (Fig.
1
). All steps are designed to be easy and quick to achieve with a standard handheld mobile phone.

Solving this problem involves several challenges.

First, we need to generate a video from two mirror stills (selfies) and motion information; the video should maintain temporal consistency, support human poses that differ from those in the input selfies, and accurately preserve the subject’s outfit and face – all rendered from a stationary third-person viewpoint.

Second, the motion capture process must be simple and achievable using only a handheld phone – this is especially challenging, as your body pose is high-dimensional and not fully observable from the phone that you are carrying in your hand.
Third, you should be composited into the new background with realistic shading, shadows, and reflections.

Our solution for motion self-capture is to leverage the inertial sensor (IMU) present on today’s mobile phones. Because your full body pose and motion are not typically fully observable from your handheld phone, we rely on a database of pose sequences and seek to retrieve the best match from IMU data.
Specifically, we use the IMU orientation and trajectory time-sequence to retrieve the top-k closest motions from our motion database for you to choose from.
Once a motion is selected, we retrieve the top-k backgrounds with the closest ground plane orientation to that of the selected motion’s background, ensuring compatibility between the motion and the background (Fig.
1
, top right).

Given the motion and background, a straightforward approach to generating fit check videos is to design a human image animation model – based on video diffusion models – that takes as input two mirror selfies, a target motion represented in skeleton format, and a background image.

However, simply adapting existing techniques
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Tu et al. 2024
)
presents two key challenges. First, it fails to effectively utilize information from the back selfie, leading to inaccuracies in the generated outfit. Second, it produces lower-quality frames with blurriness and unnatural human-scene composition (
e.g
.
, weak reflections on a reflective floor).
To address these issues, we propose a novel, parameter-free frame generation strategy and a multi-reference attention mechanism.

These components are essential for enabling the model to effectively integrate information from multiple reference images without introducing additional network parameters, in contrast to ReferenceNet
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
.

Furthermore, we propose an effective image-based fine-tuning strategy using a synthesized, task-specific image dataset to enhance the frame quality, improving sharpness, reflections, and shadow rendering, while preserving temporal consistency.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

We demonstrate the ability to capture full-body, fixed-viewpoint fit-check videos from handheld mobile phones. Our approach supports customized motion capture and matching, and virtual background with reasonable shading and shadows.

•

We design a parameter-free frame generation strategy, as well as a multi-reference attention mechanism, to effectively integrate multiple reference images into human animation methods based on video diffusion models.

•

We introduce an effective fine-tuning strategy to enhance the quality of generated frames, improving sharpness, reflections, and shadow rendering.
This fine-tuning strategy is architecture-agonistic and can also be adapted to other human animation models with different model architectures.

Related Work

Since we are the first to introduce this novel application, there are no existing papers that directly apply to our task. However, we review relevant research below and discuss the differences between their methods and our approach.

Diffusion Model for Human Image Animation.

Human image animation aims to animate a single static human image to generate a human video.
In this section, we review diffusion-based approaches, as they have showed superior generation quality compared to GAN-based methods
(

Diffusion-based approaches for human image animation can be categorized into two main types.
The first category
(

;
Hu et al. 2023
;

;

;

;

;

;
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Wang et al. 2025c
)

builds on top of an image diffusion model such as Stable Diffusion (SD)
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
, where additional temporal layers, appearance and pose encoding strategy are designed based on the image diffusion model to ensure the temporal consistency, appearance and pose fidelity.
For example, Animate Anyone
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
introduced ReferenceNet to inject reference image features into SD and employed a pose guider (skeleton-based) to ensure accurate motion. Training occurred in two stages: first, SD was fine-tuned on image pairs without temporal constraints; second, the added temporal layers (initialized by AnimateDiff

Champ

extended this approach by incorporating SMPL-based pose signals
(
Pavlakos et al. 2019
)

The second category
(
Tu et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Tan et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024d
;

)
, which provides stronger temporal consistency.
StableAnimator
(

)
proposed an SVD-based pipeline with an identity-aware appearance controller to generate human videos with improved identity preservation, conditioned on skeleton input. MimicMotion
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
introduced a confidence-aware pose guidance mechanism (in skeleton format) and regional loss amplification to enhance video quality and reduce distortions. However, these methods tend to produce frames that are slightly blurrier than image diffusion-based approaches.

In summary, both methods assume a single front-facing reference image without customized motion matching.
For our task, we build on MimicMotion, as it has demonstrated strong appearance and temporal consistency, and has been widely adopted in the community
(
Datou 2024
;
Kijai 2024
)
.
We redesign the frame generation strategy and attention mechanism and introduce a fine-tuning stage, allowing the pipeline to leverage multiple reference images and generate higher-quality, sharper frames.

Although our approach builds on MimicMotion, the proposed pipeline can also be applied to other video diffusion-based methods with minimal or no modifications.

3.
Method

We start by formally define our task. As input, the user captures two full-body mirror selfies – front view
I_{fr}
and back view
I_{bk}
– and records motion using a mobile phone’s IMU. This motion is used to retrieve a target pose sequence,
P_{1:N}
, where
N
is the sequence length. Finally, a target background
I_{bg}
is retrieved.
Given these inputs, we generate a fit check video,
\hat{V}_{1:N}
, with the person following the target poses within the target background (Fig.
2
right).

Training Data for Human Animation
.
Since no existing dataset contains paired mirror selfies and fit check videos, we construct our dataset using only fit check videos, which consist solely of third-person views.
Each training pair consists of an input set
\{I^{\prime}_{fr},I^{\prime}_{bk},I^{\prime}_{bg},{P}^{\prime}_{1:T}\}
and its corresponding ground-truth (GT) video
V^{\prime}_{1:T}
. Here,
V^{\prime}_{1:T}
comprises
T
consecutive frames sampled from a fit check video
V_{1:N}
(
N\geq T
), while
{P}^{\prime}_{1:T}
represents the corresponding extracted poses in DWPose
(
Yang et al. 2023
)
format. The background image
I^{\prime}_{bg}
is extracted under the assumption of a static camera, with occluded regions (not visible across all frames) inpainted using Stable Diffusion
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
. The front and back view images,
I^{\prime}_{fr}
and
I^{\prime}_{bk}
, are randomly sampled from
V_{1:N}
and have their backgrounds removed using
(
Ravi et al. 2024
)
, as the original background is not needed during testing.

3.1.
Naive Method

The naive method adapts an existing human animation method to integrate an additional reference image and a background image.

Model Design
.

Our method builds upon MimicMotion
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, an SVD-based human animation model that employs a latent video diffusion framework with a 3D UNet and dedicated feature encoders for pose and appearance.
While the original MimicMotion takes a single reference image and a pose sequence to generate a video, it is not designed to handle multiple reference images or changing backgrounds.
To address this, our naive method modifies feature encoders to extract features from the extra reference image (back view) and background image, integrating them into the UNet.
We found the naive method is robust enough to handle selfie input despite being trained on third-person view data. Therefore, we do not need to introduce additional modules to address the domain gap between selfie and third-person views, allowing us to keep the overall method lightweight and reduce training complexity. We will first describe the training process before detailing our modifications.

Model Training
.
We adopt the EDM diffusion framework
(
Karras et al. 2022
)
for model training. Specifically, we begin with a clean latent obtained from the ground-truth frames, denoted as
z_{0}=E_{v}(V)
, where
V=[I^{\prime}_{fr},V^{\prime}_{1:T}]_{t}
,
[\cdot,\cdot]_{t}
is temporal concatenation, and
E_{v}
is the VAE encoder.
Here,
I^{\prime}_{fr}
is treated as the first ground-truth frame.
This follows the base model SVD, an image animation method trained to generate the first frame identical to the reference image.
We use
I^{\prime}_{fr}
instead of
I^{\prime}_{bk}
as MimicMotion assumes a front-facing input, and we continue training from their pretrained model.

Next, we apply the forward process to produce a noisy latent
z_{s}=z_{0}+\sigma_{s}^{2}\epsilon
, where
\sigma_{s}
is the noise level and
\epsilon
is Gaussian noise.
We update our model parameters
\Theta
by minimizing the loss:

(1)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_{s}\sim p(\mathbf{z};\sigma_{s}),\sigma_{s}\sim p(\sigma)}\left[\lambda_{\sigma_{s}}\left\|F_{\Theta}(\mathbf{z}_{s};\sigma_{s};\mathbf{c})-\mathbf{z}_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],\\

where
p(\sigma)
is the noise level distribution during training,
p(\mathbf{z};\sigma_{s})
represents the distribution of data with noise level
\sigma_{s}
, and
\lambda_{\sigma_{s}}
is the weight for the loss at denoising timestep
s
.
\mathbf{c}=\{f_{v},f_{im},f_{p}\}
comprises features extracted by different encoders: the VAE encoder
E_{v}
, the image encoder
E_{im}
, and the pose encoder
E_{p}
.
The function
F_{\Theta}
is defined based on the preconditioning strategy
(
Karras et al. 2022
;
Salimans and Ho 2022
)
:

(2)

F_{\Theta}(\mathbf{z}_{s};\sigma_{s};\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{c}_{\text{skip}}(\sigma_{s})\mathbf{z}_{s}+\mathbf{c}_{\text{out}}(\sigma_{s})\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{c}_{\text{in}}\mathbf{z}_{s};\mathbf{c}_{\text{noise}}(\sigma_{s});\mathbf{c}),

where
\epsilon_{\theta}
represents the 3D UNet, and
\mathbf{c}_{\text{in}}
,
\mathbf{c}_{\text{out}}
,
\mathbf{c}_{\text{skip}}
, and
\mathbf{c}_{\text{noise}}
are preconditioning functions. During training, we initialize from the pretrained checkpoint of MimicMotion and update
\epsilon_{\theta}
and
E_{p}
jointly using Eq.
1
. Please refer to the left box “Training: Video Dataset” in Fig.
2
.
The naive method passes only the front images and other ground truth frames (along with their corresponding poses) through the solid purple arrows, and does not generate the back image (bottom, purple border) in the output.

Feature Integration
. We now describe our modification of how the conditioning features
\mathbf{c}=\{f_{v},f_{im},f_{p}\}
are computed and integrated into
\epsilon_{\theta}
.

First,
f_{v}=[E_{v}(I^{\prime}_{fr}),E_{v}(I^{\prime}_{bk}),E_{v}(I^{\prime}_{bg})]_{c}
is obtained by passing
I^{\prime}_{fr}
,
I^{\prime}_{bk}
, and
I^{\prime}_{bg}
through the VAE encoder
E_{v}
, where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{c}
is channel-wise concatenation.

This feature is duplicated along the temporal axis, channel-concatenated with the noisy latent, and then fed into the UNet. Second, the image features are extracted as
f_{im}=E_{im}(I^{\prime}_{fr})+E_{im}(I^{\prime}_{bk})
, where we use addition instead of concatenation, as it improves results in practice. Here,
E_{im}
is implemented as a CLIP encoder
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
, and its output feature
f_{im}
is integrated into the UNet via cross-attention layers (same as SVD). Third, pose features are extracted as
f_{p}=E_{p}([P^{\prime}_{fr},P^{\prime}_{1:T}]_{t})
, where
E_{p}
is implemented the same as the pose encoder in MimicMotion and
P^{\prime}_{fr}
is the DWPose of
I^{\prime}_{fr}
. Finally,
f_{p}
is integrated into
\epsilon_{\theta}
by adding them to the output of UNet’s first convolution layer.

Reference Image Augmentation
.
During training,
I^{\prime}_{fr}
and
I^{\prime}_{bk}
are sampled from the same video source as the GT sequence, resulting in similar lighting and shading between the reference images and GT sequence. However, at test time, we generate videos in a new background with different lighting conditions, using selfies as inputs.
To address this, we augment training data by adjusting the color tone of
I^{\prime}_{fr}
and
I^{\prime}_{bk}
to match randomly sampled backgrounds from our background retrieval database using a pretrained image harmonization network
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
(details in supplementary). This works better than using image relighting networks
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
for augmentation, which excessively alter shading in reference images, making it harder for the model to preserve clothing patterns.

Model Inference
.
During inference, we replace
\{I^{\prime}_{fr},I^{\prime}_{bk},I^{\prime}_{bg},{P}^{\prime}_{1:T}\}
with
\{I_{fr},I_{bk},I_{bg},{P}_{1:N}\}
.
Starting from a random noise, the model progressively denoises it to obtain a clean latent, which is then decoded by the VAE decoder to generate frames. We additionally remove the first frame because it is the same as
I_{fr}
.
Since this process only produces
T+1
frames, we adopt the progressive latent fusion strategy from
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
to generate
N
frames (
N\geq T
). This method segments the sequence into overlapping segments with
T+1
frames, denoises each segment, and fuses them to reconstruct a N-length video
\hat{V}^{1:N}
.
Further, to preserve identity, we refine the face in generated frames using a pretrained face refinement method
(
Contributors 2024
)
, with the face in
I_{fr}
as the reference.

3.2.
Frame Generation Strategy

The goal is to improve the naive model by effectively integrating
I_{bk}
for accurate back-view reconstruction. One approach is adding a ReferenceNet
(
Hu et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, a 2D UNet similar to Stable Diffusion, to extract features from both front and back images and inject them into the 3D UNet via self-attention layers. As shown in Fig.
3
(c), this design captures text on the back (albeit blurry) but introduces an unwanted white pattern around the hood. Further, using ReferenceNet increases the model’s parameters and training time by around 1.5 times, making it computationally inefficient.

We observe that the clothing pattern in the front view is accurately reconstructed, while the primary difference in handling
I_{fr}
and
I_{bk}
is that
I_{fr}
is always generated as the first frame. This may stem from the video model’s strong prior for frame-to-frame consistency, enforcing
I_{fr}
features to propagate effectively over time.
Motivated by this, we propose a simple, parameter-free frame generation strategy (Fig.
2
left). Our insight is that enforcing the front and back views as the first frames leverages the video model’s consistency, ensuring key visual details, such as clothing patterns, remain coherent throughout the sequence.
Specifically, we treat the back-view image as the second ground-truth frame, enhancing the naive approach by setting
V=[I^{\prime}_{fr},I^{\prime}_{bk},V^{\prime}_{1:T}]_{t}
and
f_{p}=E_{p}([P^{\prime}_{fr},P^{\prime}_{bk},P^{\prime}_{1:T}]_{t})
, where
P^{\prime}_{bk}
represents the DWPose of
I^{\prime}_{bk}
.
During inference, the model generates
T+2
frames at once (through multiple denoising steps), and we discard the first two frames.

As shown in Fig.
3
(d), this parameter-free strategy substantially improves back-view accuracy over the naive method (b) and achieves results comparable to ReferenceNet (c), but with fewer parameters, reduced training time, and no hood artifacts. However, the text in (d) remains blurry and unreadable.

3.3.
Multi-Reference Attention

To better reproduce patterns from input images, we design a multi-reference attention mechanism that leverages our proposed frame generation design, inspired by
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
.
Our key idea is to modify the 3D UNet’s spatial self-attention layers to better integrate features from multiple reference images into other frames (see Fig.
4
).
Specifically, we define a feature map from a self-attention layer as
x_{1:(T+2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(T+2)\times H\times W\times C}
, where H, W, and C represent the height, width, and channel size of the feature map, respectively. Based on our frame generation design, we extract the reference image features as
x_{fr}=x_{1}
and
x_{bk}=x_{2}
, both having dimensions

1\times H\times W\times C
.
Then we duplicate
x_{fr}
and
x_{bk}
along temporal axis T times. Next, we augment
x_{3:(T+2)}
(
i.e
.
, feature maps of other frames, excluding
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
) by concatenating them width-wise with
x_{fr}
and
x_{bk}
.
Further, we concatenate
x_{1}
with
[x_{fr},x_{fr}]_{w}
and
x_{2}
with
[x_{bk},x_{bk}]_{w}
, both along the width dimension, for batch processing, where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{w}
is width-wise concatenation.
Finally, we apply self-attention and extract the first third of the output feature map along the width dimension as the output.
The key difference between our design and
(
Hu et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
is that they apply self-attention using reference features extracted from ReferenceNet, whereas our approach utilizes features extracted directly from the 3D UNet. Our design offers two advantages: (1) it ensures that
x_{fr}
,
x_{bk}
, and
x_{3:T+2}
share the same feature space, enabling effective fusion, and (2) it is more efficient, as it avoids introducing additional network parameters by eliminating the need for ReferenceNet.

3.4.
Image-Based Fine-Tuning

To further enhance the quality of generated frames, we propose an image-based fine-tuning strategy, involving: (1) constructing a task-specific high-quality image dataset, and (2) strategically fine-tune our model on this dataset, while preserving temporal coherence.

We do not use high-quality video datasets for fine-tuning due to two main challenges: (1) it is difficult to obtain fit check videos (with both front and back views) that are of higher quality than our training videos, and (2) motion blur, which is common in videos, can negatively impact frame sharpness.
Therefore, we instead construct an
image dataset consisting of image pairs tailored for our task (
e.g
.
, front and back images of the same individual). Next, we will discuss the dataset construction process and describe how to fine-tune our trained video model using the image dataset. Fig.
2
(middle) visualizes our fine-tuning strategy.

Constructing Image Dataset for Fine-Tuning
.
Our goal is to construct training pairs, each consisting of an input set
\{I^{\prime}_{fr},I^{\prime}_{bk},I^{\prime}_{bg},P^{\prime}_{gt}\}
and a corresponding ground-truth image
I^{\prime}_{gt}
. Obtaining real-world images
I^{\prime}_{fr}
,
I^{\prime}_{bk}
, and
I^{\prime}_{gt}
of the same person within identical backgrounds is challenging; thus, we opt for synthetic dataset creation.

To construct each training pair, we first collect real front-view images (
I^{\prime}_{fr}
) from the web, specifically selecting those with visible reflections or shadows, as we find this further helps enhance the model’s capability to generate realistic reflections and shadows.

Next, we employ a pretrained image reposing method (the first stage of
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
) to synthesize the corresponding back-view images (
I^{\prime}_{bk}
).
We set the ground-truth
I^{\prime}_{gt}
as either
I^{\prime}_{fr}
or
I^{\prime}_{bk}
, obtaining
I^{\prime}_{bg}
by inpainting the foreground and shadow/reflection regions of
I^{\prime}_{gt}
.

Fine-Tuning Video Models on Image Dataset
.

We fine-tune our trained model using constructed image pairs, where the ground-truth image
I^{\prime}_{gt}
and its corresponding pose
P^{\prime}_{gt}
are duplicated
T
times to match the required input sequence length.
In practice, this duplication strategy leads to better temporal consistency compared to fine-tuning with non-duplicated sequences (
i.e
.
, setting
T=1
).
Inspired by
(
Chen et al. 2024b
)
, we fine-tune only the spatial layers of the model to preserve its temporal coherence.

During fine-tuning, we select
I^{\prime}_{fr}
as GT 80% of the time, and
I^{\prime}_{bk}
20% of times. This balance is chosen because
I^{\prime}_{bk}
is synthetically generated and may contain artifacts, while completely omitting it would lead to model collapse, causing the model to generate only front views, even for back-facing poses.
As part of data augmentation, we randomly shift and scale
I^{\prime}_{gt}
,
P^{\prime}_{gt}
and
I^{\prime}_{bg}
, filling undefined regions with white pixels.

Fine-tuning on high-quality sharp images with shadows and reflections for just 30 minutes enhances frame quality, improving both sharpness and human-scene composition (
i.e
.
, shadow and reflection generation).
As shown in Fig.
3
(f), it makes the text sharper.

Note that, our fine-tuning strategy is architecture-agonistic and can also be adapted to other human animation models.

See supplementary for examples of shadow and reflection improvements.

3.5.
Motion and Background Retrieval from IMU

Motion Acquisition
.
A motion generation model could be trained to map recorded IMU data to motion, but existing methods have key limitations: (1) they require multiple IMUs or additional motion capture devices
(
Van Wouwe et al. 2024
;
Yi et al. 2022
;
Du et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2022
;
Yuan et al. 2023
)
, but we use only a single phone, or (2) they suffer from foot sliding and jittering due to extremely sparse input
(
Mollyn et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Tevet et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
, such as a single IMU.
Given these challenges, we opt for motion retrieval.
Existing motion retrieval methods
(
Petrovich et al. 2023
;
Bica et al. 2024
;
Fujiwara et al. 2024
;
Shashank et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024b
)
mainly rely on text inputs, which are unsuitable for our needs.
Training an IMU-to-motion retrieval model is difficult due to the lack of large-scale paired mobile phone IMU and motion datasets. Synthesizing such data from existing sources
(
Mahmood et al. 2019
)
introduces a gap between training and inference, as mobile phone IMUs are noisy and optimized for other functionalities.

Instead, we propose an IMU-based motion retrieval approach that matches recorded orientation and translation to a motion database composed of diverse fit check motions.
The key idea is to retrieve the top-k closest matches by computing dynamic time warping (DTW) distances between the recorded and candidate motions’ orientation and translation (details in supplementary).

Background Acquisition
.
The retrieved motion must be compatible with the virtual background, ensuring natural alignment with the ground plane.
Given the retrieved motion, we select the top-k backgrounds whose ground plane orientation closely matches that of the original background of retrieved motion (details in supplementary).
Alternatively, users can provide a preferred background, as long as the ground plane aligns with the motion.

4.
Experiments

In this section, we focus on evaluating the human animation component.
Please see implementation details and evaluation of motion retrieval in supplementary.

Datasets.

We collect a dataset of 1,590 fit check videos from the web, each featuring a single person with both front and back views. The average video length is 9.5 seconds, and we subsample every 4 frames, yielding 72 frames per video on average. The dataset is split into 1,441 training and 149 test videos.
We estimate body orientation per frame using a pretrained SMPL-based detector
(
Li et al. 2022
)
, classifying frames as front view (330°–30°) or back view (150°–210°).
For fine-tuning, we collect 122 front-view images from the web, forming a fine-tuning dataset with 122 training pairs.
Furthermore, we also evaluate on widely used datasets, TikTok
(
Jafarian and Park 2021
)
and UBC Fashion
(
Zablotskaia et al. 2019
)
.

To evaluate our selfie task, we create a self-capture dataset with front and back mirror selfies from 8 individuals wearing various outfits, totaling 24 captures.
We record a real “ground truth” fit check video for each capture, maintaining the same clothing and a nearly identical background image.

Baselines.

To our knowledge, no existing work directly addresses our task. So we adapt four human animation methods as baselines:
(1) Animate Anyone
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
, built on Stable Diffusion, employs ReferenceNet to extract features from a single reference image and fuse them into the UNet. To extend this approach, we utilize ReferenceNet to extract features from both the front and back images, integrating them into the UNet. Additionally, the background image is encoded by the VAE and incorporated into the UNet in the same manner as noisy latents, aligning with our design.
(2) Champ
(
Zhu et al. 2024
)
builds on Animate Anyone with additional pose signals (e.g., SMPL). We modify it similarly while preserving its pose input.
(3) StableAnimator
(
Tu et al. 2024
)
.
(4) MimicMotion
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
Both (3) and (4) are SVD-based methods and are adapted the same as the naive method, differing only in the lack of reference image augmentation.
All baselines share our input setup (except Champ with additional pose input). For fair comparison, we initialize each model with its pretrained checkpoint and continuing training them on our dataset. More details are in supplementary.

Comparison with Baselines.

First, we present a qualitative comparison with baselines on our selfie dataset in Fig.
7
. Specifically, SD-based baselines Animate Anyone (b) and Champ (c) leverage back selfies using ReferenceNet to enhance the back view generation, albeit with artifacts, outperforming SVD-based baselines (d) and (e) in this aspect. However, these SD-based baselines suffer from poorer temporal consistency, introducing background jitter and artifacts.
Additionally, these baselines fail to produce realistic ground reflections, reducing realism, while our method generates reasonable reflections, demonstrating the effectiveness of the shadow and reflection enhancement in the fine-tuning stage.
In summary, our method achieves accurate outfit rendering, realistic reflections, and strong temporal consistency through novel designs within a video diffusion-based framework.

Our model outperforms all baselines across all metrics. 5.
Limitations

Our method has several limitations.
First, it struggles to generate realistic shadows when compositing subjects into sunlit backgrounds (Fig.
8
).
Second, due to limitations of the adopted video diffusion model SVD, it fails to accurately render fine details like hands and intricate patterns.
Third, the background in generated frames might have slight color shift compared to the input background due to the use of VAE and the color shift between frames in training videos.
These issues can be mitigated by using a more advanced video diffusion model as the base.
In addition, the proposed motion capturing method does not consider arm movements due to the limit of single phone IMU.
Incorporating additional IMUs (
e.g
.
, from a smartwatch) alleviate this limitation.
