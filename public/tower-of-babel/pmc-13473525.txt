Sequencing PARP inhibitors in breast cancer: lessons learned and emerging insights
Authors: Kenan Aloss, Mallory I Frederick, Saima Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) have emerged as an important single-agent targeted therapeutic option for a subpopulation of breast cancer patients. While PARPi combinations have shown synergy in the pre-clinical setting, concomitant regimens have shown limited clinical benefit. Optimizing treatment schedules is therefore needed to improve outcomes. Sequential dosing of PARPi with cytotoxic, targeted, or immune therapies can enhance efficacy by sustaining DNA damage, suppressing DNA repair, and modulating the immune response. This approach also decreases toxicity by exploiting differences in replication stress between normal and cancer cells. Here, we discuss the rationale for improved efficacy and enhanced tolerability with sequential PARPi approaches, supported by pre-clinical and clinical evidence.Subject terms: Cancer, Drug discovery, Oncology

Introduction

Over the past fifty years, the survival of breast cancer patients has improved partly due to advancements in therapeutic approaches. Breast cancer patients have long been tested for germline genetic variations, yet only recently has a targeted therapeutic approach emerged for patients with germline mutations in BRCA1/2 (gBRCAMUT). By targeting poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP)-1/2, PARP inhibitors (PARPi) have changed the treatment paradigm for patients with early triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), the most aggressive breast cancer subtype. One such PARPi, olaparib, has demonstrated an improvement in overall survival (OS) in mainly TNBC patients with gBRCAMUT1. Given such clinical success, the full potential of PARPi needs to be further investigated, building from a better understanding of its mechanism of action as a single-agent and interaction with other therapies.

PARPi are well known for impairing DNA repair via two mechanisms of action. As a DNA damage sensor, PARP-1 is recruited to single-strand breaks, where it synthesizes PAR chains on itself (autoPARylation) or adjacent target proteins, favoring the recruitment of DNA repair factors and release of PARP-1 from DNA2. Synthetic lethality results from PARPi targeting the catalytic activity of PARP-1 in cells with mutations in BRCA1/2 (BRCAMUT). Here, unrepaired single-strand breaks lead to double-strand breaks, resulting in genomic instability and cell death3,4. A second mechanism is PARP-DNA trapping, in which PARP-1 fails to dissociate from the damaged DNA, generating cytotoxic lesions5–7. This may arise from either inhibition of auto-PARylation and electrostatic repulsion from the negative charges of PAR chains, or allosteric changes in PARP-1 structure increasing DNA-binding affinity. PARP-DNA trapping correlates with PARPi potency, distinguishing individual PARPi: talazoparib and niraparib have higher potencies in comparison to olaparib and rucaparib, while veliparib is the least potent PARPi due to its limited ability to trap PARP-DNA8,9.

Efficacy of PARPi has also been demonstrated beyond the BRCAMUT context10. As monotherapies, PARPi have demonstrated activity in tumors that exhibit BRCAness, a phenotype similar to BRCAMUT tumors, mainly characterized by deficiencies in homologous recombination (HR)11. Furthermore, PARP enzymes are increasingly recognized as regulators of multiple cellular processes. PARP-1/2 have been implicated in chromatin remodeling, regulation of transcription, and stress responses12,13. PARPi have demonstrated a broader range of tumor-suppressive activity including angiogenesis, immune modulation, cell migration and chemokine signaling, in addition to the development of bone, brain, and lung metastasis. These developments underscore our evolving understanding of PARPi mechanisms, shifting from a DNA damage-exclusive effect to targeting multiple hallmarks of cancer.

Despite their clinical success, the therapeutic benefit of PARPi remains limited by intrinsic and acquired resistance. The objective response rate (ORR) of PARPi in metastatic breast cancer patients was reported at 60%10. Mechanisms associated with acquired resistance include: secondary mutations that restore the open reading frame of HR mutations, including reversion mutations in BRCA1/2; protection of replication forks; and alterations that decrease PARP-trapping efficiency such as loss of PARG or PARP-1 mutations2. Additionally, phosphorylation of PARP-1 by a receptor tyrosine kinase, the mesenchymal epithelial transition receptor (c-MET), increases PARP-1 activity and diminishes PARPi binding to PARP-114. Such adaptive responses highlight the complexity of PARPi resistance and emphasize the need to identify molecular vulnerabilities that can be exploited.

To address these challenges, PARPi are increasingly being applied in combination approaches. The combination of PARPi with platinum-based therapies has been shown to synergistically increase DNA damage and cell death15. By co-targeting kinases involved in the DNA damage response (DDR), including ATR checkpoint kinase, WEE1 G2 checkpoint kinase (WEE1), and checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1), synergistic antitumor effects have also been achieved16,17. Similarly, targeting immune checkpoints and disrupting compensatory oncogenic or metabolic pathways have been investigated in combination with PARPi. Importantly, the order and timing of sequential PARPi combination therapies may influence treatment efficacy. Here, we investigate the rationale, pre-clinical evidence, and clinical progress in the development of sequential therapeutic strategies involving PARP inhibitors, emphasizing how treatment sequencing may influence efficacy and mitigate toxicity in breast cancer.

Rationale for sequential combination therapy

Drug combinations have been evaluated using various dosing regimens (Fig. 1)18,19. Classically, clinical trials have evaluated three types of dosing regimens: concomitant, where all drugs are administered concomitantly; sequentially, where if two drugs (Drug A and B) need to be administered, Drug A is given first, and Drug B is given second, with Drug B administered upon completion of Drug A; or in alternation, where Drug A and B are sequenced, but in a repeated manner20,21. Sequential therapy results in less toxicity and can improve survival, particularly in comparison to concomitant therapy.

More recently, a modification of the sequential approach, commonly used with targeted therapies, is a lead-in sequential approach (pre-treatment), wherein Drug A is given first, with Drug B given at a later time-point, while Drug A is maintained10,22,23. Overall, sequential therapies allow for Drug A to induce vulnerabilities in the cancer cells, rendering the cancer cells more sensitive to Drug B. If resistance to Drug A induces an acquired vulnerability to Drug B, alternate dosing schedule can control drug-sensitive and drug-resistant cancer cell populations over a longer period of time. Furthermore, intermittent drug dosing includes a pause during therapy24, allowing for regression of drug-resistant cancer cells and recovery of non-cancerous cells between treatments to mitigate toxicity. Finally, combinations with lead-in sequential therapies and intermittent dosing have also been employed25.

A key rationale for drug sequencing is exploiting the dynamic nature of tumor adaptation and drug resistance mechanisms. Tumor cells exposed to cytotoxic or targeted agents often activate compensatory signaling networks, such as DNA repair, survival, or checkpoint pathways, that confer resistance to ongoing therapy but may simultaneously create a new therapeutic liability. For example, replication stress induced by PARPi or platinum compounds increases reliance on the G2/M checkpoint, rendering cells more sensitive to subsequent WEE1 inhibition26. Conversely, concurrent inhibition can trigger excessive DNA damage and mitotic catastrophe, leading to normal tissue toxicity. Thus, sequencing allows selective targeting of tumor-specific adaptive states while minimizing collateral injury. As previously highlighted18, by challenging current combination paradigms, we can shift toward cancer treatment schedules that exploit transient vulnerabilities in tumor cells, improving quality of life for patients while maintaining toxic effects on cancerous cells.

Mechanistic studies demonstrate that sequential exposure can rewire apoptotic and DNA damage signaling networks to enhance tumor cell death. In TNBC, EGFR inhibition remodels apoptotic signaling and suppresses pro-survival pathways, thereby sensitizing cells to subsequent DNA-damaging agents; this sensitization is lost with concurrent exposure, which fails to induce the same adaptive rewiring27. Similarly, in BRAF-mutant melanoma, predictable induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in response to MAPK inhibitors can be exploited to sensitize cells to a histone deacetylase inhibitor, vorinostat, with a sequenced approach28. In both cases, early adaptive resistance mechanisms are converted into exploitable liabilities by timing the second therapy after these changes occur.

Another important mechanistic rationale comes from studies with cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitors. These agents disrupt cell-cycle progression and DNA repair, but their efficacy depends critically on drug scheduling. In TNBC cells, the pan-CDK inhibitor, roscovitine, showed synergy with doxorubicin only when roscovitine was administered first, by inducing G2/M arrest, while increasing DNA double-strand breaks but decreasing recruitment of HR proteins29. Consistent with the sequence dependence, in pancreatic cancer, administering taxol prior to a CDK4/6 inhibitor (CDK4/6i) prevented genomically aberrant cells from re-entering the cell cycle, and was associated with downregulation of DNA repair gene expression and HR, resulting in persistent DNA damage30.

Sequential strategies have also proven effective in modulating the tumor microenvironment. The TONIC trial highlights that short-term pre-treatments with either cisplatin or doxorubicin improve TNBC patient responses to anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapies due to modification of the tumor microenvironment31. Pre-clinical models support this observation, demonstrating that limited DNA damage exposure triggers type I interferon (IFN) signaling and antigen presentation through cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathway activation, thereby sensitizing tumors to subsequent immunotherapy32–34. These findings show that short, controlled DNA damage can reverse immune-evasive phenotypes, creating a temporal window in which immune checkpoint blockade is more effective.

Together, these studies demonstrate that therapy-induced resistance is not fixed but evolves along defined temporal trajectories. Sequential treatment strategies leverage this plasticity, using precise timing to intercept cellular resistance mechanisms and convert them into therapeutic liabilities. As a result, rationally designed sequences have the potential to enhance tumor control while reducing systemic toxicity.

Pre-clinical evidence of PARPi in sequential approaches

Sequential administration of PARPi with other anticancer agents has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy in breast cancer. In contrast to currently applied regimens, sequential approaches can maintain or even increase efficacy while minimizing toxicity. Sequential PARPi improves anticancer efficacy

Increasing DNA damage and apoptosis

Sequencing PARPi with DNA damaging agents can amplify DNA damage and apoptotic cell death by exploiting the timing of cellular responses such as DNA damage and repair. One of the best characterized examples involves the sequential combination of talazoparib and sacituzumab govitecan (SG), an antibody-drug conjugate currently approved for metastatic HER2-negative breast cancer patients35,36. Conjugated to an anti-TROP2 antibody, SG delivers the topoisomerase I inhibitor (TOPi) SN-38 directly to tumor cells, inducing topoisomerase cleavage complexes that block DNA transcription and lead to DNA breaks. PARP-1 can resolve these complexes, thereby mitigating the cytotoxic effects of TOPi, having an instrumental role in drug resistance37. However, treating BRCA wild-type (BRCAWT) TNBC cells with sequential SG followed by talazoparib resulted in sustained topoisomerase cleavage complexes and persistent DNA damage, which would otherwise resolve within hours in the absence of PARPi. Consequently, the sequential combination increased apoptosis compared to monotherapy with SG or talazoparib24. Analogously, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, pre-treatment with the ATR inhibitor, ceralasertib, increased DNA damage and restored sensitivity to PARPi in BRCAMUT cells with acquired resistance to olaparib, and demonstrated synergy in intrinsically DDR-proficient models. On the contrary, in DDR-deficient models mainly due to BRCAMUT, sequencing with olaparib-first followed by ceralesertib resulted in significantly higher synergy38.

Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of treatment sequencing in enhancing DNA damage and PARPi efficacy. In cells with intrinsic or acquired resistance, initiating treatment with SG or an ATR inhibitor prior to PARPi restored sensitivity, whereas BRCAMUT models benefitted from PARPi-first sequential strategy.

Decreasing DNA repair efficiency

Sequencing anti-PARP therapy provides the potential to leverage the cancer cell’s vulnerability and ability to repair DNA damage. Sustained treatment with PARPi demonstrated downregulation of RAD51 and other DNA repair proteins39. PARPi also have reduced HR factor availability, thus inducing BRCAness. Alternatively, therapeutics that target epigenetic pathways, cell cycle checkpoint, and DNA repair, can also induce BRCAness, thereby sensitizing BRCA1/2-proficient cells to PARPi40,41.

Our group previously evaluated the combination of talazoparib and carboplatin in a panel of TNBC cell lines and three mouse models15. Concomitant combination experiments demonstrated synergy in 92% of TNBC cell lines, regardless of BRCA1/2-mutation status, with dose-reduction indices ranging from 1.5 to 3.8 for talazoparib, and 2.1 to 7.1 for carboplatin. We compared three combination strategies, including PARPi-first, carboplatin-first, and concomitant dosing. Both concurrent and sequential combinations resulted in similar effects on cell proliferation, replication fork speed, and short-term DNA damage responses in TNBC cells. However, the PARPi-first sequential combination significantly inhibited migration, invasion, and lung metastasis compared to the concomitant treatment in a BRCAWT orthotopic xenograft. Although the underlying mechanism requires further investigation, whole transcriptomic analysis of the metastatic lungs revealed that sequential talazoparib followed by carboplatin downregulated DNA repair pathways including HR and mismatch repair, which can increase PARPi sensitivity. In contrast, the concomitant combination upregulated chemokine signaling and VEGF pathways, which can promote a proinflammatory response and tumor progression. Thus, sequencing PARPi with carboplatin can play an important role in preventing the development of distant metastasis.

Several pre-clinical studies have evaluated CDK inhibitors in combination with PARPi in breast cancer. A pan-CDK 1,2,5,9 inhibitor, dinaciclib, increased sensitivity to PARPi in BRCAMUT and BRCAWT TNBC cell lines and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models with intrinsic and acquired resistance to PARPi. By downregulating c-myc and RAD51 expression, dinaciclib inhibits HR, resulting in synergy when used concomitantly with PARPi42,43. CDK1 plays an important role in cell-cycle progression and checkpoint activation via phosphorylation of BRCA1 and BRCA1 foci formation. Targeting CDK1 was shown to sensitize BRCA1-proficient TNBC cells to PARPi, but not their BRCA1-deficient counterparts44,45. Sequential administration of the CDK1 inhibitor RO3306 followed by PARPi was also shown to sensitize BRCAWT TNBC cells to PARPi by impairing HR repair by decreasing BRCA1 phosphorylation and RAD51 levels. This insufficient DNA repair led to sustained DNA damage, G2/M arrest, and mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis compared to PARPi monotherapy.

CDK4/6i induce cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase, and have been shown to be synergistic with PARPi in BRCAMUT and BRCAWT TNBC cells46. In BRCAMUT TNBC cells, the concomitant combination downregulated HR and increased DNA damage47. The sequential administration of PARPi-first for six days followed by CDK4/6i improved the antiproliferative effects of PARPi in BRCAMUT TNBC cell lines, and in a BRCAMUT cell line with acquired resistance to PARPi47,48. The reverse sequence with CDK4/6i-first was less effective than the PARPi-first combination, which may be attributed to the G1 arrest induced by the CDK4/6i47. However, another important context are hormone receptor-positive cells with acquired resistance to CDK4/6i or anti-hormonal therapy which demonstrate an increased expression of PARP-1 or cAMP-specific phosphodiesterase 4D (PDE4D)49,50. Inhibition of PDE4D was shown to induce BRCAness, DNA damage, PARP-1 trapping, and transcriptional repression to restore sensitivity to anti-hormonal therapy or CDK4/6i. Therefore, combining anti-hormonal therapy or CDK4/6i with PARPi demonstrated synergy in cell lines and mouse models by inducing DNA damage, G1 arrest, and apoptosis, regardless of BRCA1/2-mutation status. Furthermore, gBRCA2MUT tumors with an intrinsic heterozygous loss of RB1, and, with therapeutic pressure of CDK4/6i, develop biallelic inactivation of RB1 and resistance to CDK4/6i51. These cells demonstrated a distinct sensitivity to PARPi. However, PARPi treatment can induce BRCA-reversion mutations, a known mechanism of PARPi resistance, which sensitize cells to CDK4/6i, favoring the sequencing of upfront PARPi use in BRCA2MUT tumors, prior to CDK4/6i.

Overall, these findings highlight that rational sequencing of PARPi with platinum agents or CDK inhibitors can exploit tumor DNA repair capacity to enhance PARPi response. By functionally impairing HR and sustaining DNA damage, these sequential approaches offer promising strategies to overcome acquired resistance in BRCAMUT tumors and expand PARPi efficacy to BRCAWT TNBC (Fig. 3).

Altering immune response: an emerging mechanism

In addition to their effects on DNA repair, PARPi also modulates anti-tumor immune responses. PARPi upregulates PD-L1 (Programmed Death-Ligand 1) expression and promotes the accumulation of cytosolic DNA to activate the cGAS-STING pathway32,52. PARPi efficacy has also been shown to be dependent on recruitment of CD8+ T-cells53,54. Concomitant administration of olaparib and the WEE1 inhibitor, AZD1775, increased cGAS levels, which in turn activated the cGAS-STING pathway, stimulating production of type I IFNs, to induce antitumor immunity in murine models of BRCAWT TNBC55.

Interestingly, sequential dosing with olaparib followed by AZD1775 (administered as 7 days of olaparib followed by 7 days of AZD1775, in alternating cycles) similarly activated the cGAS-STING pathway in ovarian cancer cells, evidenced by upregulation of downstream effectors such as TRAF-associated NF-κB activator (TANK)-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and IFN regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), along with increased expression of type I interferon response genes (CXCL10 and CCL5), which promote T-cell recruitment26. However, the sequential regimen resulted in superior tumor growth inhibition compared to the concomitant combination in both wild-type and Trp53/BRCA1-deficient mice. This effect was diminished after CD8 depletion, suggesting a key role of T-cells in mediating the efficacy of the sequential regimen. Consistently, sequential combination promoted tumor infiltration and activation of CD8+ T-cells while preserving their proliferative capacity. The sequential approach was associated with significantly less DNA damage and cell death in CD8+ T-cells, with an increase in T-cell cytokines including TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-2, compared to the concomitant approach. The sequential administration of olaparib and AZD1775 improved tumor growth inhibition with either chimeric antigen receptor T-cells or an immune checkpoint inhibitor. Taken together, these findings suggest that optimizing treatment schedules, with sequential PARPi and WEE1 inhibition, enhances antitumor efficacy by promoting anti-tumor immune responses while preserving T-cell function.

Sequential PARPi decreases toxicity

Concomitant administration of PARPi and other DNA damaging agents in breast cancer is often limited by overlapping toxicity. For instance, concomitant administration of carboplatin and olaparib has been associated with hematological, hepatic, and renal toxicities in a breast cancer mouse model56. Therefore, a sequenced dosing administration may mitigate toxicities and improve therapeutic outcomes.

We recently evaluated the cytotoxicity of different combinations of talazoparib and carboplatin in two human normal cell lines including breast epithelial cells and bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells15. The concomitant combination induced the highest levels of DNA damage and caspase-dependent apoptosis. In contrast, the talazoparib-first approach followed by carboplatin led to decreased DNA damage and cell death compared to the concomitant combination, indicating less toxicity. Despite similar hematological toxicities, mice treated with the concomitant combination demonstrated the greatest toxicity with the least weight gain. Interestingly, in a BRCAMUT mouse model, the carboplatin-first combination approach was associated with the greatest weight gain. Similarly, a carboplatin-first approach with a 48-hour delay prior to olaparib in a BRCAMUT TNBC PDX model was associated with reduced bone marrow toxicity in comparison to the concomitant approach57.

Analogously, a sequential treatment with SG followed by talazoparib synergistically reduced viability of TNBC cells. However, such synergy was not observed in normal cells24. In mice, tumor-specific delivery of SG limits its accumulation in non-tumor tissues58, thereby creating a therapeutic window for a sequential administration of PARPi, minimizing overlapping toxicities. Sequential PARPi and WEE1 inhibition also decreased toxicity in comparison to the concomitant approach in ovarian cancer59.

The sequential strategy leverages the intrinsic differences in basal replication stress between normal and malignant cells. Cancer cells have higher endogenous replication stress than normal cells60. When PARPi were combined with inhibitors of WEE1 or bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) domain, both sequential and concomitant dosing increased replication stress and DNA damage in tumor cells, ultimately promoting cell death. In contrast, normal cells exposed to sequential therapy had minimal increase in replication stress and DNA damage compared to the concurrent treatment59,61. Importantly, when the basal replication stress of normal cells was experimentally induced to levels comparable to those in cancer cells, sequential treatment induced significant increase in DNA damage and subsequent cell death59,61.

These findings highlight the critical role of low endogenous replication stress in protecting normal cells from toxicity following sequential treatment. By reducing DNA damage and apoptosis in normal cells, treatment sequencing broadens the therapeutic window and enhances the tolerability of PARPi-based combinational regimens.

Clinical evidence of PARPi in sequential approaches

As orally available targeted therapeutics, PARPi are ideal candidates to be used in combination. As discussed above, PARPi impair DNA repair, sustain DNA damage, and induce immune responses. Consequently, PARPi have been widely evaluated in clinical trials using various dosing strategies with different agents, including platinum compounds, antibody-drug conjugates, cell cycle checkpoint inhibitors and immune therapies. PARPi and platinum compounds

The complementary effects of PARPi and platinum compounds on suppressing DNA repair and enhancing DNA damage have provided strong rationale for exploring their concomitant combination. Despite clinical benefits observed with the concomitant combination, concerns with toxicity have prompted the evaluation of various sequencing strategies.

Phase I clinical trials

While many of the earlier Phase I trials tested PARPi amongst patients with several lines of prior chemotherapy, the use of different dosing strategies can still be informative. The recommended phase II doses (RP2D) for veliparib in combination with chemotherapy ranged from 100 to 250 mg twice daily62–64. Somlo et al. evaluated the concomitant administration of veliparib and carboplatin in BRCAMUT metastatic breast cancer. The combination demonstrated an ORR of 56% with an acceptable toxicity profile. Nonetheless, 75% of these patients required dose delays or reductions due to cytopenias. Interestingly, veliparib was also tested in a lead-in sequential manner, whereby carboplatin-based chemotherapy was administered in the first cycle, with veliparib only starting in the second cycle. While Appleman et al. employed an intermittent dosing of veliparib on days 1–7 of a 21-day cycle from cycle 2 onwards, Gray et al. dosed veliparib continuously from cycle 2. Both studies showed similar response rates at 69%, for breast cancer patients with intermittent veliparib dosing, and BRCAMUT ovarian cancer patients with continuous dosing. The addition of veliparib in the second cycle was not associated with a clinically significant increase in myelosuppression and toxicity was comparable for patients receiving the veliparib combination and chemotherapy alone.

Phase I trials with olaparib also demonstrated potential in combination with carboplatin65,66. Safety and efficacy were demonstrated with an intermittent dosing of olaparib capsules at 400 mg twice daily (days 1-7) in concomitant combination with carboplatin in BRCAMUT breast and ovarian cancer patients. A sequential combination was also evaluated comparing a 1-day lead-in with either olaparib or carboplatin first, with the dosing scheme reversed in cycle 2 in women cancers. Olaparib tablets were administered intermittently for 7/21 days. Here, the maximum tolerated dose was 200 mg twice daily. While the ORR was also shown to be 69% for BRCAMUT patients, correlative studies revealed that the carboplatin-first approach was associated with a 50% increase in olaparib clearance, partly due to intracellular sequestration.

Fewer studies have reported the combination of talazoparib with carboplatin67,68. One trial evaluated the continuous dosing of talazoparib with a one-day lead-in with various carboplatin schedules. The combination was associated with unacceptable hematologic toxicity, which was more prominent amongst gBRCAMUT patients. Intermittent dosing of talazoparib for days 1-3 or 1-7 was also evaluated concurrently with paclitaxel and carboplatin. The RP2D of talazoparib was established at 250 mcg but there was significant myelosuppression. Talazoparib has a longer half-life at 90 + /-58 h, suggesting that leveraging of sequential and intermittent dosing needs to further explored.

Phase II/III clinical trials

The phase III BrighTNess trial investigated the efficacy of veliparib in concurrent combination with carboplatin and paclitaxel, followed by anthracycline-based chemotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting for TNBC patients1,25. Improvements in pathological complete response (pCR) and event-free survival (EFS) were observed with the veliparib/carboplatin/paclitaxel combination group, yet these effects were attributed primarily to the action of carboplatin alone. These results are not surprising, however, since the trial used a very low dose of veliparib (50 mg twice daily), the PARPi with the lowest potency.

The combination of veliparib with carboplatin plus paclitaxel was also evaluated in the phase III BROCADE3 trial in gBRCAMUT patients with locally advanced/metastatic breast cancer, but with a different dosing regimen of veliparib25. Here, the starting dose of veliparib was 120 mg orally twice daily, administered with a 2-day run-in, prior to the administration of carboplatin and paclitaxel, and in an intermittent fashion (first 7 days of 21-day cycle). The veliparib group demonstrated a significant improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) (14.5 vs 12.6 months; hazard ratio (HR) 0.71, 95% CI 0.57-0.88, p = 0.0016) with comparable adverse events as the chemotherapy arm. While this did not translate into a statistically significant improvement in OS, the numerically longer median OS of 32.4 months versus 28.2 months in the veliparib and control arms, respectively, suggest a clinical benefit69. Therefore, the BROCADE3 trial results highlight the importance of the dosing regimen.

The role of olaparib in combination with paclitaxel plus carboplatin was also investigated in the neoadjuvant setting of TNBC patients in the phase II/III PARTNER trial57,70. Patients were randomized to either receive a sequential combination with olaparib-first (2-day lead-in), versus paclitaxel plus carboplatin-first (3-day lead-in), versus control chemotherapy, all of which was followed by anthracycline-based chemotherapy. Olaparib was administered at 150 mg twice daily at an intermittent dosing schedule for 13 of 21 days. The trial was stopped early by the independent data monitoring and safety committee. The study was divided into two cohorts. In the gBRCAMUT cohort (N = 108)57, imbalances in baseline characteristics were identified with a greater proportion of node-positive patients in the control arm in comparison to chemotherapy-first group (42.2% versus 23.1%). There was no difference in the pCR rates between the three treatment arms, however the chemotherapy-first arm demonstrated an improvement in OS in comparison to control (log-rank p = 0.039). The olaparib-first combination arm was dropped early and had only 22 patients. A post-hoc analysis demonstrated poorer OS with the olaparib-first arm in comparison to control (log-rank p = 0.008), yet the number of events in the two groups were equivalent. The chemotherapy-first arm demonstrated higher toxicity with the proportion of grade ≥3 adverse events at 76.9% versus 60.0% in the control arm. In the BRCAWT cohort (N = 531)70, there was no difference in pCR, EFS or OS between the chemotherapy-first combination arm versus control arm. Patients who received the chemotherapy-first combination demonstrated slightly more grade ≥3 adverse events (64.2% versus 58.7%) in comparison to control.

Despite the favorable prognostic outcome observed with the chemotherapy-first approach, the results of the PARTNER trial need to be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size of the gBRCAMUT cohort. Compounded with the lack of correlation between pCR and OS, the findings remain unclear. It is important to note that the dosing of olaparib was 150 mg twice daily, which is half the dose of olaparib when used in monotherapy1,71. To better understand the true potential of a PARPi in this context, a higher dose of olaparib could have been used with important dose reductions in carboplatin and paclitaxel. Furthermore, with current neoadjuvant therapy including immunotherapy-based regimens in TNBC, one questions if the use of olaparib can support de-escalation of one or more chemotherapeutic agents in this context. Indeed, the results of the GeparOLA trial also suggested the potential of replacing carboplatin with olaparib72.

Overall, these platinum-based combination trials suggest that in BRCAMUT breast cancer patients, a durable response with acceptable toxicity can be achieved with a higher-dose, low-potency PARPi, veliparib, using a lead-in sequential approach. While a platinum-based chemotherapy-first approach followed by a more potent PARPi, olaparib, may offer a clinical benefit, toxicity is still a concern. Further studies are needed to maximize efficacy while minimizing toxicity for the more potent PARPi using sequential and intermittent dosing approaches in the neoadjuvant setting.

PARPi and Antibody Drug Conjugates (ADCs)

Despite strong pre-clinical evidence supporting the mechanistic synergy between PARPi and TOPi73, their concurrent combination has been associated with significant hematological toxicities in breast cancer patients74,75. TOPi-based antibody-drug conjugates, such as SG and datopotamab deruxtecan, represent a rational strategy for combination therapy with PARPi due to tumor-targeted delivery76,77. However, SG monotherapy has been associated with 14-18% higher rates of grade ≥3 neutropenia than standard chemotherapy in TNBC patients, suggesting the need for strategic dosing in combination78,79. A phase Ib clinical trial assessed the safety of rucaparib in concurrent dosing with SG in six cancer patients, including TNBC80. Grade ≥3 neutropenia occurred in 83% of patients, and grade ≥3 anemia in 16.7%. These findings underscore the need for optimized dosing approaches to ameliorate overlapping adverse effects.

The efficacy and safety of sequential versus concurrent administration of PARPi and SG were evaluated in a phase Ib clinical trial with metastatic TNBC patients (N = 30)24. In the sequential arm, patients received SG on days 1 and 8 followed by talazoparib on days 15 to 21 of each 21-day cycle. In the concurrent arm, SG was administered on days 1 and 8, with daily talazoparib of each 21-day cycle. Sequential treatment achieved an OS of 11.1 months and a median PFS of 7.6 months, without dose-limiting toxicities. In contrast, concurrent dosing resulted in OS of 4.3 months, a PFS of 2.3 months, and was associated with severe myelosuppression. Olaparib was also evaluated in a sequenced administration with a nanoparticle TOPi, CRLX01 in a phase I clinical trial in patients with advanced solid tumors81. Olaparib was administered 48 hours after each biweekly dose of CRLX01 (days 3-13 and 17-26 of 28-day cycles). This sequential regimen demonstrated encouraging clinical activity and safety profile, further expanding the potential for sequential PARPi.

PARPi and cell cycle checkpoint inhibitors

The clinical translation of PARPi combined with cell cycle checkpoint inhibitors has been challenging due to overlapping toxicities and modest therapeutic benefits in breast cancer. The phase II VIOLETTE clinical trial evaluated two olaparib-based concomitant combinations in metastatic second or third line TNBC patients (N = 273)82. The combination of olaparib and the ATR inhibitor, ceralasertib, failed to demonstrate improvement in PFS compared to olaparib monotherapy, although a manageable safety profile was reported. However, olaparib plus a WEE1 inhibitor, adavosertib, was discontinued early due to a high incidence of grade ≥3 toxicities (78%).

While the EFFORT trial (N = 80) demonstrated efficacy with the concomitant administration of olaparib and adavosertib combination in PARPi-resistant ovarian cancer83, a dose-expansion phase I trial demonstrated limited antitumor activity in 130 patients with solid tumors84. However, greater tolerability was demonstrated with the sequential combination of olaparib followed by adavosertib in a smaller phase Ib trial with DDR-aberrant advanced solid tumors, including PARPi-resistant breast tumors85. Although promising results, larger trials are needed to confirm the efficacy and safety of the sequential approach with WEE1 inhibition.

A special consideration needs to be made for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative and gBRCAMUT breast cancer patients. As monotherapies, olaparib demonstrated a 9.4% improvement in invasive disease-free survival (iDFS) and 4.4% improvement in OS with 6-year follow-up in HER2-negative gBRCAMUT patients86, while CDK4/6i demonstrated up to a 1.8% improvement in OS and 4.9-6.5% improvement in iDFS with 4-7 years follow-up in hormone receptor-positive patients87,88. The ASCO guidelines have suggested that since CDK4/6i can be administered 12-16 months post-endocrine therapy initiation or definitive surgery, very motivated patients could be offered PARPi-first, followed by CDK 4/6i89. Metastatic patients with gBRCAMUT treated with CDK4/6i have demonstrated a poorer prognosis compared to BRCAWT patients90,91. In a real-world study of 81 gBRCAMUT hormone receptor-positive advanced breast cancer patients, patients who progressed with a CDK4/6i as first-line treatment, subsequent PARPi therapy significantly extended PFS to 11.8 months in comparison to other treatment groups: 3.5 months with endocrine therapy, 5.2 months with single-agent chemotherapy, and 9.5 months with polychemotherapy (p < 0.001)92. In another cohort (N = 41), patients who failed to respond to CDK4/6i demonstrated a more pronounced benefit to subsequent PARPi therapy (Odd’s Ratio (OR) = 8.87, 95% CI 2.84-27.98, p = 0.00002)51. These findings suggest that PARPi should be prioritized in hormone receptor-positive gBRCAMUT advanced breast cancer patients.

PARPi and immune checkpoint inhibitors

PARPi have been shown to upregulate PD-L1 expression in pre-clinical breast cancer models, providing a rationale for combining PARPi with PD-L1 or PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitors52. In metastatic or advanced TNBC patients, a phase II clinical trial (N = 55) showed that the concomitant combination of niraparib and pembrolizumab achieved improved responses amongst patients with BRCAMUT tumors compared to BRCAWT tumors, with an ORR of 47% versus 11% and a median PFS of 8.3 months versus 2.1 months93. In the phase I/II JAVELIN PARP Medley basket trial (N = 223), the combination of avelumab plus talazoparib achieved an ORR of 18% and a median duration of response (DOR) of 11.1 months in TNBC patients, with an improved ORR of 34.8% and DOR of 15.7 months in hormone receptor-positive and DDR-altered breast cancer94. In a phase II randomized controlled trial in the neoadjuvant setting, the addition of durvalumab and olaparib to standard neoadjuvant paclitaxel substantially improved pCR rates compared to paclitaxel alone from 27% to 47% in TNBCs, and from 22% to 64% in high-risk hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative tumors95. Importantly, across these studies, combining PARPi with anti-PD-L1 or anti-PD-1 therapy was generally well tolerated, with grade ≥3 hematologic toxicities not exceeding 35%.

In the gBRCAMUT metastatic breast cancer cohort (N = 34) of the MEDIOLA phase 1/2 trial, the impact of a sequential treatment of daily olaparib for 4 weeks followed by combined daily olaparib and durvalumab administered every 4 weeks was assessed23. At week 28, this regimen exhibited promising activity with 50% disease control and median DOR of 9.2 months. Moreover, treatment was well tolerated, with grade ≥3 hematological toxicities remaining below 12%. These findings indicate encouraging clinical benefits from sequential PARPi and immune checkpoint inhibitors; however, randomized trials are required to determine whether this strategy improves outcomes compared with standard-of-care treatments in breast cancer.

The KEYLYNK-009 study was a phase 2 trial that evaluated the role of olaparib plus pembrolizumab versus chemotherapy plus pembrolizumab in advanced TNBC patients who had clinical benefit with chemotherapy induction of carboplatin and gemcitabine plus pembrolizumab. Patients receiving olaparib plus pembrolizumab demonstrated similar PFS and OS and less treatment-related adverse events in comparison to the patients receiving chemotherapy, further supporting the role olaparib as an approach to de-escalate the use of chemotherapy96.

Advances in selective PARP-1 inhibitors

One avenue for future investigation remains the sequential combination with PARP-1 selective inhibitors. Several PARP-1 selective inhibitors have emerged, demonstrating up to 2000-fold greater selectivity for PARP-1 in comparison to PARP-297. One such inhibitor, saruparib, has shown less hematologic toxicity than the first-generation PARP inhibitors, and the potential for 10-fold dose reduction with complete responses in concomitant combination with either carboplatin or ceralasertib in pre-clinical BRCAMUT TNBC models98,99. Since saruparib has also demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity and safety in a phase 1/2a clinical trial100, there is potential for use of PARP-1 selective inhibitors in sequential combination to enhance anti-PARP-1 efficacy and decrease toxicity. Another selective PARP-1 inhibitor, AZD9574, which inhibited tumor growth in an intracranial BRCAMUT breast cancer metastasis model, also demonstrated efficacy in combination with temozolomide in a glioblastoma HR-proficient PDX model101. Based on survival and hematologic toxicity, use of AZD9574 would allow for use of a lower dose of temozolomide. Therefore, the use of a selective PARP-1 inhibitor could support decreasing the dose of the combination agent, which could help mitigate toxicity while maintaining efficacy.

Conclusions

The clinical success of PARPi has been challenging due to intrinsic and acquired resistance, identifying biomarkers to select best efficacy, and dose-limiting toxicities. Although PARPi demonstrate synergistic activity with multiple anti-cancer agents in breast cancer, the clinical translation of concomitant combinations has been limited by overlapping toxicities or lack of survival benefits. These challenges highlight the need to optimize treatment schedules to enhance efficacy while reducing toxicity.

Emerging pre-clinical evidence increasingly supports sequential PARPi-based regimens as a promising strategy to achieve the balance between efficacy and toxicity. Two studies which demonstrated superior anti-tumor efficacy with PARPi-first sequential combination versus the concomitant approach, were combinations with carboplatin or a WEE1 inhibitor. Sequential PARPi dosing can sustain DNA damage, suppress DNA repair efficiency, and modulate antitumor immune responses, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to subsequent agents while sparing normal cells due to lower baseline replication stress. Beyond improving responses in BRCAMUT sensitive cells, several sequential PARPi strategies have shown potential to overcome acquired resistance in BRCAMUT cells and to expand the therapeutic benefit of PARPi to BRCAWT cancers.

Clinical studies support the therapeutic advantages of sequential PARPi-strategies with intermittent dosing. While PARPi-first combination showed durable responses with carboplatin and improved tolerability with WEE1i, sequential SG followed by PARPi combination demonstrated longer survival and decreased myelosuppression in comparison to the concomitant combination. Additionally, there is potential for the role of PARPi in BRCAMUT tumors with progression to CDK4/6i. Selective PARP-1 inhibitors further represent an important strategy for dose-reduction of the PARPi and combination agent to maintain efficacy and minimize toxicity. Collectively, these results suggest that optimized sequencing may help address limitations that have historically constrained the clinical application of PARPi combinations.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations need to be addressed. Many pre-clinical and clinical studies compare sequential regimens only to monotherapies or standard chemotherapy rather than to concomitant administration, limiting direct assessment of their relative advantages. In addition, some sequential strategies have been evaluated only in-vitro, without validation in animal models. Clinical trials have often used multi-drug combinations with PARPi, commonly with full-dose chemotherapy, which may limit the full potential of PARPi.

Taken together, sequential PARPi-based strategies may offer a more effective and tolerable approach than conventional concomitant combinations. With an improved understanding of the mechanistic underpinnings of PARPi-based combination approaches, these insights will inform the design of clinical trials. In these studies, PARP-1 selective inhibitors can be incorporated with sequential and intermittent schedules to first, identify optimal dosing of PARPi and combination agent based on and safety profiles, and second, improve efficacy in specific clinical contexts. Ultimately, sequential PARPi combination approaches will have the potential to improve outcomes and quality of life for patients with difficult-to treat breast cancers.
