Research note: Effects of Eucommia ulmoides seed meal on egg quality, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal health in laying hens
Authors: Hui Ma, Lijun Wang, Jia Feng, Yuna Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe source of protein feed is a critical factor influencing egg quality. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of Eucommia ulmoides seed meal (EUSM), as a novel protein feed ingredient, on egg quality, antioxidant capacity, and intestinal health in laying hens. Four hundred and fifty 50-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens were randomly assigned to five dietary treatments: a control diet (0% replacement) and diets in which 10%, 20%, 30%, or 40% of soybean meal protein was replaced with EUSM. Results showed that EUSM supplementation linearly decreased yolk color (P < 0.001). Eggshell strength was significantly improved at 10 - 30% replacement, exhibiting a quadratic response (P < 0.001). Serum superoxide dismutase activity and calcium concentration increased, with significant quadratic trends (P < 0.001). Regarding intestinal health, replacement at 10% increased villus height in the duodenum and upregulated the expression of Claudin, ZO1, and Occludin in the duodenum and jejunum (P < 0.05). However, replacement at ≥ 20% progressively reduced villus height in all intestinal segments (P < 0.001) and decreased tight junction protein expression in proximal segments, although ileal ZO1 and Occludin remained elevated at 20% replacement. In conclusion, replacing 10% of soybean meal protein with EUSM improves eggshell strength, antioxidant status, and intestinal barrier function, making it a promising alternative protein source for laying hen diets. Higher inclusion levels (≥20%) compromise intestinal morphology and should be avoided. These findings support the potential of EUSM to reduce soybean meal dependency in the layer industry.Keywords: Egg quality, Eucommia ulmoides seed meal, Intestinal health, Laying hen, Protein source

Introduction

The global poultry industry plays a pivotal role in fulfilling the protein demand of the growing human population (Abbas et al., 2022). However, volatility in protein-rich feed ingredients along with changes in poultry product prices have attracted the attention of researchers and poultry stakeholders to explore alternative economic protein sources (Abbas et al., 2023). The most widely studied alternatives to soybean meal to date are cottonseed meal, rapeseed meal, and peanut meal (Wangui et al., 2025). The application of cottonseed meal is limited by the presence of the antinutritional factor gossypol and an unbalanced amino acid profile. Rapeseed meal contains high levels of glucosinolates and fiber, which can negatively affect nutrient digestibility and feed intake. Aflatoxin contamination remains a major challenge for the use of peanut meal. Therefore, although these conventional oilseed meals have been extensively studied, none has emerged as a universally satisfactory alternative to soybean meal. The development of novel protein feed resources continues to attract widespread attention from industry professionals.

Eucommia ulmoides is a prized traditional Chinese medicinal plant whose bark, leaves, and seeds contain diverse bioactive compounds, including lignans, flavonoids, iridoids, steroids, and terpenoids. Eucommia ulmoides leaves, bark, and their extracts have been demonstrated, as functional feed additives for poultry, to positively modulate egg quality and the physiological status of laying hens. Eucommia ulmoides seed meal (EUSM), a byproduct of oil extraction from Eucommia ulmoides seeds, contains 30.53% crude protein (He et al., 2026). It is also rich in bioactive phytochemicals, including chlorogenic acid, aucubin, and flavonoids, which numerous studies have shown to possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties (Peng et al., 2024). Chlorogenic acid and flavonoids enhance eggshell thickness by improving antioxidant capacity and intestinal barrier function in laying hens. Aucubin has been shown to increase blood calcium and phosphorus levels and improve bone health in mice, which may explain how Eucommia ulmoides extract improves eggshell quality in laying hens. Our previous research has identified EUSM as a potential novel protein feed resource for laying hens; however, its regulatory effect on egg quality remains unclear (He et al., 2026). In light of the aforementioned research gaps, this study was conducted to evaluate the effects of varying inclusion levels of EUSM on intestinal health and egg quality in laying hens. The findings are intended to provide a scientific basis for the potential application of EUSM as a novel functional ingredient in laying hen diets, distinguishing it from well‑studied conventional alternatives to soybean meal.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and animal treatment

A total of 450 fifty‑week‑old Hy‑Line Brown laying hens with similar body condition and health status were randomly assigned to five dietary treatment groups using a computer‑generated random number sequence (SPSS 25.0). Each group comprised six replicates with 15 birds per replicate (n = 6 per treatment). The trial spanned 13 weeks, including a 1-week pretest period and a 12-week formal test period. During the formal phase, the control group (CON) received a basal diet, while four experimental groups (EUSM10, EUSM20, EUSM30, EUSM40) were fed diets in which EUSM replaced 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% of soybean meal protein on an isonitrogenous basis, respectively. The diet formula (Table 1) was formulated according to NRC recommendations (1994) and Chinese Feeding Standard of Chicken (NY/T 33-2004). Water and feed were provided ad libitum. The relative humidity was kept at 60%-70% and the room temperature was maintained at 23-27°C during the trial.

Measurement of egg quality

At the end of wk 12, three eggs were randomly chosen from each replicate to analyze egg quality. Shell thickness, shell strength, albumen height, yolk color and Haugh Units were measured by using Egg Tester Ultimate TM (Orka Food Technology Co., Ltd., Tel Aviv, Israel).

Serum biochemical indices

Blood samples were collected from laying hens at the trial termination. One healthy hen per replicate was randomly selected after overnight fasting. Approximately 8-12 mL of blood per bird was drawn from the wing vein into labeled vacutainer tubes. Following clotting at 37°C in a water bath, serum was separated by centrifugation (3,500 rpm for 10 min), transferred into cryotubes, and stored at -20°C for subsequent analysis. Serum concentrations of total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), uric acid (UA), calcium (Ca), and inorganic phosphorus (IP), alongside enzymatic activities of catalase (CAT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), were quantified using a 3100 Automatic Analyzer (Hitachi, Japan).

Intestinal histology and intestinal barrier

At the end of 12-week experimental period, one bird was selected from each replicate and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Duodenum, jejunum and ileum were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were processed, trimmed and embedded in paraffin by routine methods. Paraffin sections were cut into 5 µm and stained with hematoxylin-eosin. Ten non-overlapping microscopic fields per section were selected to assess the villus height and crypt depth using a light microscope (Ni-U, Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Mucosal samples were gently scraped with glass slides and stored at -80°C for immunoglobulins and gene expression analysis. Total RNA was extracted from intestinal mucosa using Trizol reagent (AG21102, Hunan Accurate Biological Co., Ltd., Hunan, China). Reverse transcription and real‑time PCR were performed following standard protocols. The primer sequences for Claudin (F: CTAGTGATGAGGCAGATGAA, R: AGATAGGTCGGAAGCAGAT), ZO‑1 (F: GGAGATTCCGAGGTTTGCGT, R: TGAGAAACCCAGCTTCCCGA), Occludin (F: GGCGGAGGGCCACCA, R: GCTGCTCCAGTAGTAGGAGC), and the endogenous control β‑actin (F: ACACCCACACCCTCTGCATGAA, R: TGCTGCTGACACCTTCACCATTC) were used. The PCR reaction conditions were: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The mRNA expression levels were calculated using β-Actin as endogenous control by the 2-∆∆ct method.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). When a significant overall effect was detected (P < 0.05), means were compared using Duncan’s multiple range test. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts were performed to evaluate linear and quadratic trends of the response variables with increasing EUSM inclusion levels. Results are presented as means with pooled standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Effects of EUSM on egg quality and serum biochemistry

As shown in Table 2, dietary replacement of soybean meal protein with EUSM had no significant effect on shell thickness, albumen height, and haugh units (P > 0.05). However, shell strength was significantly influenced (P < 0.001), with EUSM10, EUSM20, and EUSM30 groups showing higher values than the control and EUSM40 groups. A significant quadratic trend was detected for shell strength (P < 0.001). Yolk color decreased progressively with increasing EUSM inclusion (P < 0.001 for both linear and quadratic trends). SOD activity was significantly increased by all EUSM levels compared with the control (P < 0.001), with significant linear and quadratic trends (P < 0.001). Serum calcium concentration showed a significant response (P = 0.013) and a quadratic trend (P = 0.002); the highest calcium level was observed in the EUSM20 group. Other parameters (CAT, TAOC, GSH, TP, UA, APK, IP, ALB) were not significantly affected (P > 0.05).

The improvement in eggshell strength, despite unchanged shell thickness, suggests that EUSM enhanced shell ultrastructure or calcium utilization rather than simply increasing shell thickness. Serum calcium concentration exhibited a quadratic dose‑response relationship, with the highest level observed in the EUSM20 group — an effect that may be attributed to aucubin, a key component of EUSM previously reported to regulate blood calcium levels (Yang et al., 2022). Furthermore, the significant elevation of SOD activity in all EUSM groups indicates enhanced systemic antioxidant capacity, which may protect the uterine mucosa from oxidative damage, thereby contributing to shell strength improvement (Peng et al., 2022). Interestingly, the quadratic trends for shell strength, serum calcium, and SOD activity revealed a non‑monotonic dose response: all parameters increased from control to moderate substitution levels (EUSM10 - EUSM30) but declined at the highest level (EUSM40). This decline may be due to excessive antinutritional factors interfering with mineral absorption or intestinal function, consistent with the reduced villus height observed in the EUSM40 group. Yolk color decreased progressively with increasing EUSM inclusion, which is likely attributable to the melanin content in EUSM. Melanin, a high‑molecular‑weight pigment formed from phenolic compounds, has been proposed as a seed predator deterrent (Nguyen et al., 2023). When present in the diet, melanin may interfere with carotenoid absorption or deposition, leading to paler yolks. This represents a major limitation for EUSM application in the layer industry. No significant changes were observed in other serum parameters, indicating that EUSM at tested levels did not impair liver or kidney function.

EUSM modulated intestinal morphology and the expression of tight junction proteins

In the duodenum, villus height was significantly altered (P < 0.001) with linear and quadratic trends (P < 0.001); the highest villus height was in EUSM10, while the lowest was in EUSM40. Crypt depth decreased with EUSM inclusion (P = 0.034, linear P = 0.024), and the villus height/crypt depth ratio (V/C) also differed significantly (P = 0.049). In the jejunum, villus height decreased in a linear and quadratic manner (P < 0.001), with the control group having the highest value and EUSM40 the lowest. In the ileum, villus height showed significant linear and quadratic trends (P < 0.001), decreasing progressively with higher EUSM levels. V/C in the ileum exhibited a significant quadratic trend (P = 0.022). In the duodenum, EUSM10 significantly upregulated the expression of Claudin, ZO1, and Occludin compared with the control (P < 0.05). In the jejunum, ZO1 and Occludin were highest in the EUSM10 group (P < 0.05). In the ileum, ZO1 expression was highest in EUSM20 and Occludin highest in EUSM20 as well, both with significant overall effects (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed for jejunal Claudin or ileal Claudin (P > 0.05).

These contrasting effects can be largely attributed to the antinutritional factors in EUSM, as reported in our previous study. Total saponins (4.72%) are known to exert biphasic actions: low doses stimulate epithelial maturation and enhance tight junction expression (Bafundo et al., 2021; He et al., 2026), consistent with EUSM10 results; high doses damage membrane integrity and increase permeability, explaining the villus atrophy at EUSM40 (Shen et al., 2008). Regional differences along the intestine support this interpretation. In the duodenum, direct exposure to high concentrations of saponin inhibitor produced a clear threshold effect (positive at 10%, negative at ≥ 20%). In the ileum, where microbial metabolism may partially detoxify these compounds, tight junction protein upregulation persisted at 20% substitution, even though villus height was already reduced - indicating a compensatory maintenance of barrier function despite morphological damage. We conclude that moderate EUSM substitution is safe, but higher levels compromise intestinal health due to cumulative effects of saponins. Future studies should directly quantify these antinutritional factors in the diets and assess their metabolic fate along the gut.
