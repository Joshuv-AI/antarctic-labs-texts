Non-Markovianity in collision models with initial intra-environment correlations
Graeme Pleasance, Angel E. Neira, Marco Merkli, Francesco Petruccione

Abstract
Collision models (CMs) describe an open system interacting in sequence with elements of an environment, termed ancillas. They have been established as a useful tool for analyzing non-Markovian open quantum dynamics based on the ability to control the environmental memory through simple feedback mechanisms. In this work, we investigate how ancilla-ancilla entanglement can serve as a mechanism for controlling the non-Markovianity of an open system, focusing on an operational approach to generating correlations within the environment. To this end, we first demonstrate that the open dynamics of CMs with sequentially generated correlations between groups of ancillas can be mapped onto a composite CM, where the memory part of the environment is incorporated into an enlarged Markovian system. We then apply this framework to an all-qubit CM, and show that non-Markovian behavior emerges only when the next incoming pair of ancillas are entangled prior to colliding with the system. On the other hand, when system-ancilla collisions precede ancilla-ancilla entanglement, we find the open dynamics to always be Markovian. Our findings highlight how certain qualitative features of inter-ancilla correlations can strongly influence the onset of system non-Markovianity.

2
Basic collision model

To fix the notation, we start by outlining the basic setup used to describe an open system
{S}
interacting with a memoryless environment of uncorrelated ancillas
[
23
]
. In such a model the environment
E
consists of
n
identical ancillas
{A}_{j}
(
j=1,\ldots,n
) initialized in the state
\rho_{E}(0)=\rho_{{A}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho_{{A}_{n}}
, where the Hilbert spaces of the system and the
j
-th ancilla are denoted as
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{{A}_{j}}
, respectively. Since the ancillas are identical, the index
j
is simply a label for a copy of the same Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
. The system and environment interact through pairwise collisions between
{S}
and each
{A}_{j}
, where each collision is described by a unitary operator
U
acting on
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
. We shall denote by
U_{{S}j}
the equivalent actions on
{S}
and the ancilla
{A}_{j}
when several ancillas are present, i.e. when the total Hilbert space is
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{{A}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{{A}_{n}}
, and

\rho\mapsto\mathcal{U}_{{S}j}\rho=U_{{S}j}\rho U^{\dagger}_{{S}j}

(1)

the collision map between
{S}
and
{A}_{j}
acting on density matrices
\rho
of the system plus
n
ancillas.

Let us define the single-collision map

\rho_{S}\mapsto\Lambda[\rho_{S}]=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}[U(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{A})U^{\dagger}].

(2)

It is a completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map on the set of all system density matrices. For an initially factorized state
\rho(0)=\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho^{\otimes n}_{A}
, the reduced system state after
n
collisions
\rho_{S}(n)=\mathrm{Tr}_{{A}_{1}...{A}_{n}}[\mathcal{U}_{{S}n}\cdots\mathcal{U}_{{S}1}\rho(0)]
is obtained by iterating
n
times the map
\Lambda
,

\rho_{S}(n)=\Lambda^{n}[\rho_{S}(0)].

(3)

This implies the system dynamics to be Markovian insofar that the composite map
\Lambda_{n}\equiv\Lambda^{n}
implementing the evolution
\rho_{S}(0)\mapsto\rho_{S}(n)=\Lambda_{n}\rho_{S}(0)
is divisible, i.e.,
\Lambda_{n}
can inherently be written as a composition of CPTP maps
[
52
,
53
]
. At the same time, the memoryless property of Eq. (
3
) may also be understood from fact that it describes the repeated interaction between
{S}
and a
single
ancilla
{A}
, whose correlations with
{S}
can be erased at each step without affecting the future system evolution (that is, no memory of the system state is retained across successive collisions).

3
Non-Markovian collision model with correlated ancillas

As previously noted, the realization of non-Markovian dynamics relies on modifying the basic setup above according to one of several schemes. Two common schemes employed in the literature are:

(i)

AA collisions: After
{S}
has collided with the ancilla
{A}_{j}
, and before
{S}
collides with the next one
{A}_{j+1}
, the two ancillas
{A}_{j}
and
{A}_{j+1}
interact via a unitary operator
W_{j+1,j}

[
38
,
39
,
40
,
46
,
47
,
49
,
34
]
. As such,
A_{j+1}
already carries memory of the system state before colliding with
S
. In this scheme one can also collide groups of more than two ancillas; the total ‘length’ of each group (number of connections between first and last ancilla) is called the memory depth
[
46
]
.

(ii)

Intra-environment correlations: The initial environment state
\rho_{E}(0)
is replaced by one that is nonfactorizable, reflecting classical or quantum correlations between ancillas
[
24
,
42
,
25
,
44
]
.

In the current work, we introduce a different approach to the latter scheme (ii) that closely resembles (i), in which correlations between ancillas are generated operationally. In particular, we consider initial states in which overlapping groups of
L
ancillas are correlated in sequence before colliding with
S
(see Fig.
1
). We proceed to formalize the model as follows. First, let
W
define a unitary acting on any fixed number of
L\geq 1
ancillas, and let
W_{[j+L-1,j]}
denote
W
acting on a string of
L
ancillas with indices
j,j+1,\ldots,j+L-1
(while acting as the identity on all other ancillas). We further denote by
{\mathcal{W}}_{[j+L-1,j]}
and
{\mathcal{W}}
the actions of
W_{[j+L-1,j]}
and
W
lifted to density matrices of
{S}
and
n
ancillas,

{\mathcal{W}}\rho=W\rho W^{\dagger},\qquad{\mathcal{W}}_{[L+j-1,j]}\rho=W_{[L+j-1,j]}\rho W_{[L+j-1,j]}^{\dagger}.

(4)

Thus, the initial states we consider are given as

\rho(0)=\rho_{S}(0)\otimes{\mathcal{W}}_{[n+L-1,n]}\cdots{\mathcal{W}}_{[L+1,2]}{\mathcal{W}}_{[L,1]}\,\big(\rho_{A}^{\otimes n+L-1}\big).

(5)

By setting
L=1
, it is clear that (
5
) reduces to the same class of product states associated with memoryless CMs. In this regard, we refer to
L
as the
correlation length
of the environment in analogy to the memory depth introduced in
[
46
]
(see further discussion below). We remark that it is also feasible to allow for more general types of operations
\mathcal{W}
represented by non-unitary CPTP maps.

Starting from the initial condition (
5
), the full system-reservoir state evolves as

\displaystyle\rho(n)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{{S}n}\cdots\mathcal{U}_{{S}1}\big(\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\mathcal{W}_{[n+L-1,n]}\cdots\mathcal{W}_{[L,1]}\,\rho^{\otimes n+L-1}_{A}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{{S}n}\mathcal{W}_{[n+L-1,n]}\cdots\mathcal{U}_{{S}1}\mathcal{W}_{[L,1]}\big(\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho^{\otimes n+L-1}_{A}\big),

(6)

such that the reduced system state after
n
collisions reads

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(n)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}_{{A}_{1}...{A}_{n+L-1}}\big[\mathcal{U}_{{S}n}\mathcal{W}_{[n+L-1,n]}\cdots\mathcal{U}_{S1}\mathcal{W}_{[L,1]}\big(\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho^{\otimes n+L-1}_{A}\big)\big]

\displaystyle\equiv\Lambda_{n}[\rho_{S}(0)].

(7)

In contrast to (
3
), the map
\Lambda_{n}
is generally non-Markovian since it is not guaranteed to be factorizable into products of CPTP maps.

Remark.

The operators
U
and
W
commute exactly when
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{W}
do. In this case, we have

{\mathcal{U}}_{{S}n}\cdots{\mathcal{U}}_{{S}1}{\mathcal{W}}_{[n+L-1,n]}\cdots{\mathcal{W}}_{[L,1]}={\mathcal{W}}_{[n+L-1,n]}\cdots{\mathcal{W}}_{[L,1]}{\mathcal{U}}_{{S}n}\cdots{\mathcal{U}}_{{S}1},\quad[U,W]=0.

(8)

When tracing over the ancilla degrees of freedom in (
3
), the action of each
\mathcal{W}
now disappears since these superoperators represent a conjugation with a unitary. It then follows that the reduced system dynamics is independent of the intra-environment correlations provided these are generated by an operator
W
that commutes with the
{S}-{A}
interaction.

Mapping onto Markovian composite collision model

Following the ideas of Ref.
[

4.2
Effect of AA entanglement on the system non-Markovianity

Measure of non-Markovianity.
We now introduce a suitable quantifier of non-Markovianity based on the trace distance measure proposed in
[
54
]
. The trace distance quantifies the distinguishability of any pair of density matrices
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
, and is defined as

\mathcal{D}(\rho^{1},\rho^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\|\rho^{1}-\rho^{2}\|_{1},

(23)

where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is the trace norm
\|X\|_{1}=\mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}
. The states
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
are maximally distinguishable when their trace distance equals one, which occurs if and only if
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
are orthogonal. In a Markovian process, the distinguishability between any pair of initial system states
\rho^{1,2}_{S}(0)
only ever decreases with increasing collision number
n
. This specifically amounts to a continual loss of information from the system to the environment. On the other hand, non-Markovian effects are identified when information flows from the environment back to the system, corresponding to a temporary increase in the trace distance between successive collisions.

For the discrete evolution
n\mapsto\rho_{S}(n)
, this behavior is captured by the the Breuer-Piilo-Laine (BLP) measure
[
54
,
55
]

\mathcal{N}_{BLP}=\max_{\rho^{1}_{S}(0),\rho^{2}_{S}(0)}\sum_{n\in S_{+}}\Big[\mathcal{D}\big(\rho^{1}_{S}(n+1),\rho^{2}_{S}(n+1)\big)-\mathcal{D}\big(\rho^{1}_{S}(n),\rho^{2}_{S}(n)\big)\Big],

(24)

which by definition is maximized over every choice of initial state pair
\{\rho^{1,2}_{S}(0)\}
. The sum is taken over the set

\displaystyle S_{+}

\displaystyle=\big\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\mathcal{D}\big(\rho^{1}_{S}(n+1),\rho^{2}_{S}(n+1)\big)-\mathcal{D}\big(\rho^{1}_{S}(n),\rho^{2}_{S}(n)\big)>0\big\}.

(25)

As such, (
24
) is non-zero if and only if there is some initial state pair for which the trace distance temporarily increases. For the all-qubit CM considered above, it is possible to evaluate the trace distance as

\mathcal{D}(\rho^{1}_{S}(n),\rho^{2}_{S}(n))=\sqrt{(p_{1}-p_{2})^{2}+|D(n)|^{2}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}},

(26)

implying the set
S_{+}
to be independent of the initial states
\rho^{1,2}_{S}(0)
, i.e.

n\in S_{+}\,\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\,|D(n+1)|>|D(n)|.

(27)

Hence, both a necessary and sufficient condition for non-Markovianity in this case is for
|D(n)|
to increase when going from collision
n
to
n+1
.

For qubits, the maximal value in (
24
) is achieved for pairs of states that are antipodal points on the Bloch sphere
[
56
]
. The trace distance between any pair of antipodal states can be parametrized in terms of a single parameter
\eta\in[0,1]
,

\mathcal{D}\big(\rho^{1}_{S}(n),\rho^{2}_{S}(n)\big)=\sqrt{\eta+|D(n)|^{2}(1-\eta)},\qquad\rho^{1}_{S}\perp\rho^{2}_{S}.

(28)

The maximization in (
24
) is then a maximization over
\eta
:

\mathcal{N}_{BLP}=\max_{\eta\in[0,1]}\sum_{n\in S_{+}}\left(\sqrt{\eta+|D(n+1)|^{2}(1-\eta)}-\sqrt{\eta+|D(n)|^{2}(1-\eta)}\right).

(29)

4.3
Interchanging the order of SA and AA collisions

As we have seen in the previous section, AA entanglement is necessary for the system evolution (
17
) to exhibit memory effects. We further show this occurrence of non-Markovianity is due to the fact that the ancillas are entangled
before
they collide with the system.

For this, we proceed to evaluate the dynamical map (
16
) in the opposite case, where consecutive ancillas are entangled
after
the first member in each pair collides with
{S}
. In particular, this coincides with the CCM representation of the open dynamics depicted in Fig.
3
(b). The dynamical map in this case reads

{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\prime}[\vartheta]=\mathrm{Tr}_{{A}_{1}}\big({\rm e}^{-i\varepsilon\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\otimes\sigma^{(2)}_{z}\otimes\sigma^{(1)}_{z}}\left(\left[{\rm e}^{-i\tau\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma^{(1)}_{x}}\vartheta{\rm e}^{i\tau\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma^{(1)}_{x}}\right]\otimes\rho_{A}\right){\rm e}^{i\varepsilon\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}\otimes\sigma^{(2)}_{z}\otimes\sigma^{(1)}_{z}}\big).

(36)

We show in Sec.
6.5
that the corresponding decoherence function is given by

D^{\prime}(n)={\rm e}^{-2i\tau}\big(\cos(2\tau)-i\sin(2\tau)\cos(2\varepsilon)\big)^{n-1},\quad n\geq 1.

(37)

Hence,
|D^{\prime}(n)|=(\cos^{2}(2\tau)+\sin^{2}(2\tau)\cos^{2}(2\varepsilon))^{\frac{1}{2}(n-1)}
, which is a function of
n
that never increases, since
\cos^{2}(2\tau)+\sin^{2}(2\tau)\cos^{2}(2\varepsilon)\leq 1
. We conclude that the system dynamics is Markovian for all values of the parameters
\tau
and
\varepsilon
. This illustrates how reversing the order of SA and AA operations can profoundly affect the non-Markovianity of the system evolution.

5
Conclusions

In summary, we have derived a new class of non-Markovian CMs with correlated environment states, whose correlations are generated through applying a sequence of unitary (or CPTP) operations to successive groups of ancillas. For these types of CMs, we have demonstrated that the open dynamics admits a general mapping onto a CCM, in which the memory part of the environment is incorporated into an enlarged Markovian system. In particular, the size of this memory part is quantified by the correlation length characterizing the range of intra-environment correlations between ancillas. This shares an analogous interpretation with the memory depth from Ref.
[
46
]
, which, for a separate class of CMs featuring AA collisions, quantifies the range of system-environment correlations that must be accounted for in the construction of the system dynamical map. Notably, the CCM corresponding to our setup displays key differences to that of
[
46
]
regarding the arrangement of the memory and non-memory parts and their mutual interactions with the system.

We have further analyzed the extent to which entanglement can influence the non-Markovianity of the open dynamics of an all-qubit CM with correlated ancilla pairs. By examining the nature of correlations shared between ancilla pairs representing the interacting portion of the environment, we have found AA entanglement to be necessary (although not sufficient) for the emergence of non-Markovian behavior. At the same time, the order in which SA and AA collisions occur has been shown to play a key role in determining the non-Markovianity of the open dynamics. A more detailed analysis of the role of AA entanglement in the emergence of non-Markovianity will be presented elsewhere
[
61
]
.

Looking ahead, the CM developed here could be used to study the thermodynamics of open systems interacting with nonthermal environments, whereby ancillas initially prepared in thermal states are correlated in sequence. Understanding how such correlations impact the performance of thermal devices—taking into account the energetic cost in generating such correlations—may have applications in the design of quantum heat engines and refrigerators
[
62
]
. Finally, one could also extend our work in the direction of Refs.
[
45
,
24
,
25
]
by formally characterizing the non-Markovian dynamics simulable by a CM with a correlation structure determined by (
5
). For an open two-level system, it has been demonstrated that any CP- or P-indivisible Pauli channel can be simulated by a CM with suitably correlated ancillas
[
24
,
25
]
. While the precise connection between non-Markovianity and intra-environment correlations is not yet fully understood, such work could shed further light on the applicability of CMs for simulating general non-Markovian open dynamics.

\mathcal{S}_{k,l}^{2}=\mathcal{I},\qquad{\mathcal{U}}_{{S}1}\mathcal{S}_{2,1}\mathcal{S}_{3,2}\cdots\mathcal{S}_{L,L-1}=\mathcal{S}_{2,1}\mathcal{S}_{3,2}\cdots\mathcal{S}_{L,L-1}{\mathcal{U}}_{{S}L},

(42)

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the identity map on
{\mathcal{H}}_{A}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
. The second identity relates to the fact that transferring the state of
A_{L}
to
A_{1}
, colliding
S
with
A_{1}
, and then shifting the state of
A_{1}
back to
A_{L}
, is in effect the same as colliding
S
with
A_{L}
. Using the explicit form of
{\mathcal{M}}
as per (
9
),

{\mathcal{M}}[\vartheta]=\mathrm{Tr}_{A_{1}}\Big({\mathcal{U}}_{{S}1}{\mathcal{W}}_{[L,1]}(\vartheta\otimes\rho_{A})\Big),

we apply (
42
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}[\vartheta]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{{A}_{1}}\Big({\mathcal{U}}_{{S}1}\mathcal{S}_{2,1}\mathcal{S}_{3,2}\cdots\mathcal{S}_{L,L-1}\mathcal{S}_{L,L-1}\cdots\mathcal{S}_{3,2}\mathcal{S}_{2,1}{\mathcal{W}}_{[L,1]}(\vartheta\otimes\rho_{A})\Big)

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}_{{A}_{1}}\Big(\mathcal{S}_{2,1}\mathcal{S}_{3,2}\cdots\mathcal{S}_{L,L-1}{\mathcal{U}}_{{S}L}{\mathcal{W}}_{[L,1]}^{\prime}(\vartheta\otimes\rho_{A})\Big),

with
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}_{[L,1]}=\mathcal{S}_{L,L-1}\cdots\mathcal{S}_{2,1}\mathcal{W}_{[L,1]}
. Next we use that

\mathrm{Tr}_{{A}_{k}}\Big(\mathcal{S}_{k+1,k}\,\rho_{S}\rho_{{A}_{1}}\cdots\rho_{{A}_{k}}\rho_{{A}_{k+1}}\cdots\Big)=\rho_{S}\cdots\rho_{{A}_{k}}\rho_{{A}_{k+2}}\cdots=\mathrm{Tr}_{{A}_{k+1}}\Big(\rho_{S}\cdots\rho_{{A}_{k}}\rho_{{A}_{k+1}}\cdots\Big).

(44)

By linearity and applying this relation
L-1
times to the last line of (
43
), we then get (
13
). This completes the proof of Proposition
1
.
\square

6.3
Proof of Proposition
2

For the model outlined in Sec.
4
, in which both
S
and each
A
are qubits, we may express
{\mathcal{M}}
in the following ordered operator basis

\big\{e_{00},e_{01},e_{02},e_{03},\quad e_{10},e_{11},e_{12},e_{13},\quad e_{20},e_{21},e_{22},e_{23},\quad e_{30},e_{31},e_{32},e_{33}\big\},

(45)

where

e_{kl}=e_{k}\otimes e_{l},

(46)

and

e_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul},\quad e_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{x},\quad e_{2}=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{y},\quad e_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sigma_{z}.

(47)

(To simplify the notation we shall use the same
e_{k}
to denote basis operators of either
S
or
A
.) The operator sets
\{e_{j}\}
are mutually orthonormal with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product

\langle e_{k}|e_{l}\rangle\equiv\mathrm{Tr}[e^{\dagger}_{k}e_{l}]=\delta_{k,l},

(48)

such that

\langle e_{kl}|e_{mn}\rangle=\langle e_{k}\otimes e_{l}|e_{m}\otimes e_{n}\rangle=\langle e_{k}|e_{m}\rangle\,\langle e_{l}|e_{n}\rangle=\delta_{k,m}\delta_{l,n}.

(49)

Accordingly, when expressed in the basis (
45
), the map
\mathcal{M}
takes the form of a
16\times 16
matrix with matrix elements

\boldsymbol{M}_{kl,mn}\equiv\langle e_{kl}|\boldsymbol{M}|e_{lm}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}[(e_{k}\otimes e_{l})^{\dagger}{\mathcal{M}}[e_{m}\otimes e_{n}]],

while in the same basis, the composite state
\vartheta\mapsto|\vartheta\rangle
of
S+A
is represented as
16\times 1
column vector. The coefficients
\rho_{m}
parameterizing
\rho_{A}
may also be computed from the inner product (
48
) as

\rho_{m}=\sqrt{2}\langle e_{m}|\rho_{A}\rangle=\sqrt{2}\mathrm{Tr}[e_{m}^{\dagger}\rho_{A}],

(50)

with
\rho_{0}=1
.

After much algebra, the matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
can be shown to take the form

\boldsymbol{M}=\begin{pNiceArray}.

(51)

Here, the empty blocks have null entries, and the matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{2}
are given by

\boldsymbol{A}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
\rho_{1}\cos(2\varepsilon)&0&0&-i\rho_{2}\sin(2\varepsilon)\\
\rho_{2}\cos(2\varepsilon)&0&0&-i\rho_{1}\sin(2\varepsilon)\\
\rho_{3}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cos(2\varepsilon)&-i\rho_{3}\sin(2\varepsilon)&0\\
0&\rho_{1}&0&0\\
0&\rho_{2}&0&0\\
0&\rho_{3}\cos(2\varepsilon)&-i\sin(2\varepsilon)&0\end{pmatrix}.

(52)

The structure of (
54
) into the
4\times 4
blocks corresponds to the four groupings of the ordered basis indicated by a space in (
45
).

The matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
has a simple eigenvalue
1
, and only one other eigenvalue
0
of algebraic multiplicity
3
and geometric multiplicity
2
, implying it is nondiagonalizable. However, powers of
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
are straightforward to calculate,

\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
\rho_{1}\cos(2\varepsilon)-i\rho_{2}\rho_{3}\sin(2\varepsilon)&0&0&0\\
\rho_{2}\cos(2\varepsilon)-i\rho_{1}\rho_{3}\sin(2\varepsilon)&0&0&0\\
\rho_{3}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\qquad n\geq 2.

(53)

Hence, the
n
-th power of (
54
) is then

\boldsymbol{M}^{n}=\begin{pNiceArray},\qquad n\geq 2,

(54)

where the
8\times 8
matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
is defined as

\boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pNiceArray}.

(55)

Using the above expression for
\boldsymbol{M}^{n}
, we next show that the decoherence function
D(n)
can be evaluated in terms of powers of the matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{1,2}
, (
20
).

Lemma 1
.

The decoherence function
D(n)
in (
18
) is given by

D(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{3}([\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{30,3m}-[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{20,3m})\rho_{m}.

(56)

Proof.
In the representation introduced above, we can evaluate the reduced density matrix
\rho_{S}(n)=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}\mathcal{M}^{n}[\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{A}]
by acting
\boldsymbol{M}^{n}
on the column vector
|\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{A}\rangle
. Since
\mathrm{Tr}_{A}[e_{kl}]=\sqrt{2}e_{k}\delta_{l,0}
, the parts of the image of
\boldsymbol{M}^{n}
proportional to
e_{kl}
all vanish except if
l=0
, and hence we do not need all matrix elements of
\boldsymbol{M}^{n}
to determine
\rho_{S}(n)
. Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l=0}^{3}\mathrm{Tr}_{A}[e_{kl}]\langle e_{kl}|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}|\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{A}\rangle

(57)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=0}^{3}e_{k}\langle e_{k0}|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}|\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{A}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=0}^{3}e_{k}\sum_{l,m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{k0,lm}\langle e_{lm}|\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{A}\rangle.

Next we have from (
50
),

\langle e_{lm}|\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{A}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\rho_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[e_{l}^{\dagger}\rho_{S}],\qquad l,m=0,\ldots,3,

which combined with (
57
) yields

\rho_{S}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{3}e_{k}\sum_{l,m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{k0,lm}\rho_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[e_{l}^{\dagger}\rho_{S}].

(58)

We can further simplify this expression using (see (
53
), (
54
))

[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{00,lm}=\delta_{l,0}\delta_{m,0},\qquad[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{10,lm}=\delta_{l,1}\delta_{m,0},

(59)

so that

\rho_{S}(n)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e_{0}+e_{1}\mathrm{Tr}[e_{1}\rho_{S}]+\sum_{k=2,3}e_{k}\sum_{l,m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{k0,lm}\rho_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[e_{l}^{\dagger}\rho_{S}].

(60)

The initial state
\rho_{S}
represented in the
\sigma_{x}
basis reads

\rho_{S}=\begin{pmatrix}p&z\\
z^{*}&1-p\end{pmatrix},\qquad(\sigma_{x}{\rm-basis})

(61)

with
p\in[0,1]
and
|z|^{2}\leq p(1-p)
. Let us then also represent the reduced density matrix (
60
) in the same basis,

\rho_{S}(n)=\begin{pmatrix}p&\alpha_{3}(n)-\alpha_{2}(n)\\
\alpha_{3}(n)+\alpha_{2}(n)&1-p\end{pmatrix},\qquad(\sigma_{x}{\rm-basis})

(62)

where

\alpha_{k}(n)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{l,m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{k0,lm}\rho_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[e_{l}^{\dagger}\rho_{S}],\quad k=2,3.

(63)

In terms of the parameters
p
and
z
, we have

\mathrm{Tr}[e_{l}^{\dagger}\rho_{S}]=\sqrt{2}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tfrac{1}{2},&l=0\\
p-\tfrac{1}{2},&l=1\\
-i{\rm Im}\,z,&l=2\\
{\rm Re}\,z,&l=3\end{array}\right.

(64)

The reduced system dynamics is thus entirely determined by the off-diagonal matrix elements of (
62
) depending on
\alpha_{2}(n)
and
\alpha_{3}(n)
(in the
\sigma_{x}
basis). We now define

\alpha_{3}(n)-\alpha_{2}(n)=D(n)z,

(65)

where
D(n)
is a function of
n
which is independent of
z
. In particular, both off-diagonal elements of
\rho_{S}(n)
must vanish for all
n
if
z=0
. It then follows that
\alpha_{2}(n)=\alpha_{3}(n)=0
if
z=0
. From (
54
) and (
64
), the terms with
l=0,1
in (
63
) can also be seen to vanish, and so

\alpha_{k}(n)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{l=2,3}\ \sum_{m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{k0,lm}\rho_{m}\mathrm{Tr}[e_{l}^{\dagger}\rho_{S}],\quad k=2,3.

(66)

Using again (
64
) we obtain

\alpha_{k}(n)=\bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{k0,3m}\rho_{m}\bigg){\rm Re}\,z-i\bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{k0,2m}\rho_{m}\bigg){\rm Im}\,z,\quad k=2,3.

(67)

For the left side of (
65
) to be a multiple of
z
, we have the constraint

\sum_{m=0}^{3}([\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{30,3m}-[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{20,3m})\rho_{m}=-\sum_{m=0}^{3}([\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{30,2m}-[\boldsymbol{M}^{n}]_{20,2m})\rho_{m}

(68)

and the decoherence function
D(n)
becomes (
56
). This completes the proof of Lemma
1
.
\square

Next we evaluate the matrix elements appearing in the sum (
56
). We first obtain from (
54
),

D(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{3}([\boldsymbol{B}^{n}]_{4,m+4}-[\boldsymbol{B}^{n}]_{0,m+4})\rho_{m},

(69)

where
\boldsymbol{B}
is given in (
55
). We observe that
\boldsymbol{B}
is a block circulant matrix and that it can be block-diagonalized according to

\boldsymbol{B}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}&\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}\\
\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}&-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{+}&0\\
0&\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{-}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}&\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}\\
\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}&-\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}\end{array}\right)

(70)

where
\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}_{4}
are
4\times 4
identity matrices, and

\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\pm}=\cos(2\tau)\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\pm i\sin(2\tau)\boldsymbol{A}_{2}.

(71)

Let us denote the Jordan decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\pm}
by

\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{\pm}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\pm}\boldsymbol{D}_{\pm}\boldsymbol{P}_{\pm}^{-1},

(72)

where
\boldsymbol{P}_{\pm}
are invertible matrices and
\boldsymbol{D}_{\pm}
are the Jordan blocks. It follows from (
70
) and (
72
) that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}&\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\\
\boldsymbol{P}_{+}&-\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}&0\\
0&\boldsymbol{D}_{-}^{n}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{P}_{+}^{-1}\\
\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}&-\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{P}_{+}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}\\
\boldsymbol{P}_{+}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{P}_{+}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}\end{array}\right).

In view of (
69
), we use (
6.3
) to get

\boldsymbol{B}_{0,m+4}=\frac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{P}_{+}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}^{-1}]_{0m}-\frac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\boldsymbol{D}_{-}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}]_{0m}

(83)

and

\boldsymbol{B}_{4,m+4}=\frac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{P}_{+}\boldsymbol{D}_{+}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{+}^{-1}]_{0m}+\frac{1}{2}[\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\boldsymbol{D}_{-}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}]_{0m}.

(84)

Substituting the above into (
69
) leads to

D(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{P}_{-}\boldsymbol{D}_{-}^{n}\boldsymbol{P}_{-}^{-1}]_{0m}\rho_{m}=\sum_{m=0}^{3}[\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{-}^{n}]_{0m}\rho_{m},

(85)

which gives precisely (
20
). This concludes the proof of Proposition
2
.
\square

6.4
Derivation of (
33
) and (
35
)

In this section, we derive limiting cases of the decoherence function (
85
), assuming each ancilla is initialized in the state
\rho_{A}=|+\rangle\langle+|_{A}
, (
22
). Since for this state
\rho_{1}=1
and
\rho_{2}=\rho_{3}=0
, from (
52
) we have

\displaystyle\cos(2\tau)\boldsymbol{A}_{1}-i\sin(2\tau)\boldsymbol{A}_{2}

(86)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\cos(2\tau)&-i\sin(2\tau)\cos(2\varepsilon)&0&0\\
\cos(2\tau)\cos(2\varepsilon)&-i\sin(2\tau)&0&0\\
0&0&0&-i\cos(2\tau)\sin(2\varepsilon)\\
0&0&-\sin(2\tau)\sin(2\varepsilon)&0\end{pmatrix},

such that combining (
85
) and (
86
) yields

D(n)=\left[\boldsymbol{C}^{n}\right]_{00}+\left[\boldsymbol{C}^{n}\right]_{01},\qquad\boldsymbol{C}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(2\tau)&-i\sin(2\tau)\cos(2\varepsilon)\\
\cos(2\tau)\cos(2\varepsilon)&-i\sin(2\tau)\end{pmatrix}.

(87)

The matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
can be diagonalized as

\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{P}\begin{pmatrix}z_{+}&0\\
0&z_{-}\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1},

(88)

where the eigenvalues are
z_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\big(e^{-2i\tau}\pm\Delta\big)
, and
\Delta=\sqrt{\cos(4\tau)-i\cos(4\varepsilon)\sin(4\tau)}
. Furthermore, the matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
and its inverse
\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}

read

\boldsymbol{P}=\begin{pmatrix}q_{+}&q_{-}\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\kappa}\begin{pmatrix}1\ &-q_{-}\\
-1\ &q_{+}\end{pmatrix},

(89)

with

q_{\pm}(\varepsilon,\tau)=\frac{\kappa}{2\Delta}(e^{2i\tau}\pm\Delta),\qquad\kappa(\varepsilon,\tau)=q_{+}-q_{-}.

(90)

Let us for now assume that
\varepsilon,\tau\neq\frac{\pi}{4}
, implying
\cos(2\varepsilon)\cos(2\tau)\neq 0
. We can then proceed to calculate powers of
\boldsymbol{C}
using (
88
). In doing so, the decoherence function (
87
) becomes
D(n)=\frac{1}{\kappa}(q_{+}(1-q_{-})z_{+}^{n}-q_{-}(1-q_{+})z_{-}^{n})
, which can be rewritten as

D(n)=\zeta_{+}(\varepsilon,\tau)\left(\dfrac{{\rm e}^{-2i\tau}+\Delta}{2}\right)^{n}-\zeta_{-}(\varepsilon,\tau)\left(\dfrac{{\rm e}^{-2i\tau}-\Delta}{2}\right)^{n},

(91)

where

\zeta_{\pm}(\varepsilon,\tau)=\frac{1}{\Delta}\Big(1-\frac{e^{2i\tau}\mp\Delta}{2\cos(2\varepsilon)\cos(2\tau)}\Big)\frac{e^{2i\tau}\pm\Delta}{2}.

(92)

Note that since
|\Delta|^{4}=\cos^{2}(4\tau)+\sin^{2}(4\tau)\cos^{2}(4\varepsilon)\leq 1
, with equality if and only if
\cos(4\varepsilon)=\pm 1
, we have
|\Delta|<1
for
\varepsilon\neq\frac{\pi}{4}
, and therefore
\frac{1}{2}|e^{-2i\tau}\pm\Delta|<1
. It follows from (
91
) that
|D(n)|\leq Ce^{-\gamma n}
for some
C,\gamma>0
. This implies initial coherences of
S
to decay exponentially in the collision number
n
whenever
\varepsilon\neq\frac{\pi}{4}
.

Next we consider the following two limits of (
91
), which are well defined given that
D(n)
is a continuous function of both
\varepsilon
and
\tau
:

(i)

As
\varepsilon\rightarrow\frac{\pi}{4}
, we obtain
\zeta_{+}(\pi/4,\tau)=1
and
\zeta_{-}(\pi/4,\tau)=0
, giving

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow\pi/4}D(n)=(\cos{(2\tau)})^{n}.

(93)

(ii)

As
\tau\rightarrow\frac{\pi}{4}
, we obtain for
n\geq 1
,

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\pi/4}\zeta_{+}(\varepsilon,\tau)\left(\frac{{\rm e}^{-2i\tau}+\Delta}{2}\right)^{n}=0,\quad\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\pi/4}\zeta_{-}(\varepsilon,\tau)\left(\frac{{\rm e}^{-2i\tau}-\Delta}{2}\right)^{n}=-(-i)^{n}\cos({2\varepsilon}),

such that

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\pi/4}D(n)=e^{-i\pi n/2}\cos(2\varepsilon)\qquad n\geq 1.

(94)

Hence, we recover the two expressions (
33
) and (
35
).

6.5
Derivation of (
37
)

The decoherence function
D^{\prime}(n)
can be derived following the same procedure used to obtain
D(n)
in Sec.
6.3
, where
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
can be similarly represented as a
16\times 16
matrix. For the sake of brevity, we omit the full derivation and only present the intermediate results needed to construct the solution for
D^{\prime}(n)
. The matrix representation of (
36
) in the basis (
45
) is found to be

\boldsymbol{M}^{\prime}=\begin{pNiceArray},

(95)

where

\boldsymbol{A}_{1}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{A}_{2}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&0&-i\rho_{2}\sin{(2\varepsilon)}&0\\
0&0&-i\rho_{1}\sin{(2\varepsilon)}&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(96)

and

\boldsymbol{B}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{(2\tau)}&0&0&0\\
\rho_{1}\cos{(2\varepsilon})\cos{(2\tau)}&0&0&-i\rho_{2}\sin{(2\varepsilon)}\\
\rho_{2}\cos{(2\varepsilon)}\cos{(2\tau)}&0&0&-i\rho_{1}\sin{(2\varepsilon)}\\
\rho_{3}\cos{(2\tau)}&0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{B}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&0\\
0&\rho_{1}\cos{(2\varepsilon)}&0&0\\
0&\rho_{2}\cos{(2\varepsilon)}&0&0\\
0&\rho_{3}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(97)

By again following the same steps outlined in the proof of Proposition
1
, we obtain

D^{\prime}(n)=\sum_{m=0}^{3}\left[(\boldsymbol{B}_{1}-i\sin{(2\tau)}\boldsymbol{B}_{2})^{n}\right]_{0m}\rho_{m},

(98)

which depends only on the matrices
\boldsymbol{B}_{1,2}
. To further simplify this expression one can set
\rho_{1}=1
,
\rho_{2}=\rho_{3}=0
, corresponding to when each ancilla is initalized in the state
\rho_{A}=|+\rangle\langle+|_{A}
, (
22
). We then get (
D^{\prime}(0)=1
)

D^{\prime}(n)={\rm e}^{-2i\tau}(\cos{(2\tau)}-i\sin{(2\tau)}\cos{(2\varepsilon)})^{n-1},\quad n\geq 1,

(99)

giving (
37
).
