Quaternionic Pole Placement via Companion Forms and the Ackermann Formula
Michael Sebek

Abstract
We present an extension of state-feedback pole placement for quaternionic systems, based on companion forms and the Ackermann formula. For controllable single-input quaternionic LTI models, we define a companion polynomial that right-annihilates its companion matrix, characterize spectra via right-eigenvalue similarity classes, and prove coefficient-matching design in controllable coordinates. We then derive a coordinate-free Ackermann gain expression valid for real target polynomials, and state its scope and limitations. Short examples demonstrate correctness, practical use, and numerical simplicity.



I
Introduction

Quaternions provide a natural extension of real and complex numbers: like
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathbb{H}
is a division algebra, but—crucially—multiplication is
noncommutative
. This changes some rules in systems and control. Eigenvalues must be interpreted carefully (right vs. left) and—on the right—they are defined only up to
quaternionic similarity classes
. Determinants and a full Cayley–Hamilton theorem do not carry over in the usual way, and even polynomial evaluation is order-dependent, so
right and left zeros need not coincide
. As a result, several classical tools are not yet fully developed—or are simply missing—when one works directly over
\mathbb{H}
; see, e.g., foundational results on quaternionic LTI stability
[
19
]
and linear algebra
[
21
]
.

Quaternion models are a natural choice whenever states or operators are genuinely hypercomplex. Beyond rigid-body attitude kinematics, representative areas include:
spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}

quantum dynamics
[
25
]
and qubit gate control—e.g., flatness-based single-qubit gates
[
5
]
;
NMR/MRI pulse design—e.g., quaternion descriptions of selective-pulse propagators
[
6
]
;
Polarization/bivariate signal processing and colour imaging—e.g., Quaternion-MUSIC for DOA–polarization
[
16
]
, quaternion Fourier transforms for colour images
[
22
]
, and a recent overview
[
13
]
;
and learning and computation with hypercomplex variables—e.g., quaternion neural controllers
[
23
]
, quaternion CNNs for speech
[
18
]
, and foundational links to quantum computing
[
15
]
.

Other interpretations of quaternions are already used in control of special classes of systems—for example, unit quaternions as rotation parameters, complex
2{\times}2
representations, or real four-vector embeddings. In this note, we adopt the
hypercomplex algebraic
viewpoint, where signals, states, and operators naturally reside in
\mathbb{H}
. This keeps the calculus aligned with the modeling (no repeated conversions to
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
), makes spectral notions explicit (right eigenvalues and their similarity classes), and meshes naturally with companion constructions used in design.

The primary contribution of this work is to lift standard real/complex state-space methods to quaternionic systems. We tackle a basic, practical task:
state-feedback pole placement
for single-input, controllable LTI models over
\mathbb{H}
. The results rest on two pillars. First, we develop quaternionic
companion forms
together with a quaternionic
companion polynomial
that right-annihilates its companion matrix—playing the role of characteristic/minimal polynomials over fields—and thereby provide a clean, determinant-free framework in controllable (companion) coordinates. Second, we give two equivalent design routes: (i)
coefficient matching
in the controllable companion form, and (ii) a
coordinate-free Ackermann-type
expression based only on the controllability matrix and a desired companion polynomial. We also specify the exact scope: the Ackermann route applies when the target polynomial has
real (central) coefficients
, which suffices to prescribe any combination of real classes and complex-conjugate classes of right eigenvalues.

These workflows resemble their
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{C}
counterparts—companion form, one-shot gain, and an Ackermann-style formula—but they differ in substance: over
\mathbb{H}
, right eigenvalues (the poles) are
similarity classes
(not points), polynomial evaluation is side/ordering-sensitive, and the Ackermann step is valid only for
real (central)
coefficients. Our design preserves the familiar flow while making these quaternionic nuances explicit.

The note is organized as follows. Section II reviews quaternion algebra and matrix preliminaries; Section III states the quaternionic LTI model. Section IV introduces the controllable companion form and its companion polynomial. Section V formulates pole placement over
\mathbb{H}
. Section VI presents coefficient matching in controllable coordinates; Section VII derives the quaternionic Ackermann formula and delineates its scope; and Section VIII concludes.

II
Quaternions and Matrices

Quaternions

A quaternion is a
hypercomplex
scalar
q=a+b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}

with
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
, and imaginary units
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}
satisfying

\mathbf{i}^{2}=\mathbf{j}^{2}=\mathbf{k}^{2}=\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\mathbf{k}=-1
(hence
\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{j}\mathbf{i},

\mathbf{j}\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{i}=-\mathbf{k}\mathbf{j},

\mathbf{k}\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{j}=-\mathbf{i}\mathbf{k}
). The set of all quaternions, denoted
\mathbb{H},

is a 4-dimensional associative but
noncommutative
division algebra over
\mathbb{R}.
For
q\in\mathbb{H},
denote its real and imaginary parts by
\operatorname{Re}(q)=a
and
\operatorname{Im}(q)=b\mathbf{i}+c\mathbf{j}+d\mathbf{k}
, respectively; the conjugate is
\bar{q}=a-b\mathbf{i}-c\mathbf{j}-d\mathbf{k}
and the norm is
|q|=\sqrt{q\bar{q}}.
Throughout we use the scalar (hypercomplex) model of
\mathbb{H}
; see
[
21
]
for a more detailed introduction.

Similarity and classes

Two quaternions
q,r
are
similar
, written
q\sim r,
if
q=\alpha^{-1}r\alpha
for some nonzero
\alpha\in\mathbb{H}.

Similarity preserves
\operatorname{Re}(\cdot),

\left|\cdot\right|,
and
\left|\operatorname{Im}(\cdot)\right|
and partitions
\mathbb{H}
into equivalence classes
\left[\cdot\right]
: real quaternions are isolated classes
\left[a\right]=\{a\}
; If
\operatorname{Im}(q)\neq 0,
then
[q]

is a 2-sphere
\{\mathrm{Re}(q)+u\,\left|\operatorname{Im}(q)\right|:u\in\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}\},\left|u\right|=1\}
that meets the standard complex line
\{a+b\mathbf{i}:\,a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}

in exactly two points
\operatorname{Re}(q)\pm i\,\left|\operatorname{Im}(q)\right|.

For example,
[\mathbf{k}]
contains all imaginary units, including
\pm\mathbf{i}.

Quaternionic matrices

For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbb{H}^{m\times n}
denote the set of

m\times n
matrices with entries in
\mathbb{H}.
We view column vectors as a right
\mathbb{H}
-module and use the standard conjugate transpose
A^{*}=\overline{A}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Noncommutativity shows up strongly: entrywise conjugation and transpose
do not
distribute over products in the usual way: in general

\overline{AB}\neq\bar{A}\bar{B}

and
(AB)^{\mathsf{T}}\neq B^{\mathsf{T}}A^{\mathsf{T}}
. By contrast, the mixed identity holds:
(AB)^{\ast}=B^{\ast}A^{\ast}.

Likewise,
(A^{\top})^{-1}=(A^{-1})^{\top}

need not
hold over
\mathbb{H}
(as transpose may destroy invertibility), whereas
(A^{*})^{-1}=(A^{-1})^{*}
does hold. See e.g.
[
21
]
for further subtleties.

Rank and invertibility

For
A\in\mathbb{H}^{m\times n}
, the quaternionic rank, denoted
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{H}}A
,
is the maximal number of
right–independent
columns of
A

(i.e., if
\sum_{j}a_{j}c_{j}=0
with coefficients
c_{j}\in\mathbb{H}
on the
right
,
then all
c_{j}=0
). Equivalently, it is the maximal number of
left–independent
rows. A square matrix

A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
is invertible iff
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{H}}A=n,
see
[
21
]
.

Right eigenvalues and spectrum

For
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
, a
right eigenpair
is
(v,\lambda)

with
v\neq 0
and

\displaystyle Av

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v\,\lambda,\qquad v\in\mathbb{H}^{n},\ \lambda\in\mathbb{H}.

(1)

Scaling
v
on the right by
\alpha\neq 0\in\mathbb{H}

replaces
\lambda
by
\alpha^{-1}\lambda\alpha.

So, right eigenvalues are defined up to quaternionic similarity.
Therefore, the right spectrum is the set of similarity classes

\sigma_{\mathscr{r}}(A):=\{[\lambda]:\exists v\neq 0\text{ such that }Av=v\lambda\}\subset\mathbb{H}/\sim,

which is invariant under matrix similarity:
A\mapsto S^{-1}AS

[
21
]
.
When a scalar is needed, we choose the unique complex representative in each class with a nonnegative imaginary part and call it the
standard eigenvalue.

(Left eigenvalues, defined by
Av=\mu v
, are
not
preserved by matrix similarity and will not be used unless explicitly stated.)

Polynomials and evaluations

Let
\mathbb{H}[\lambda]
denote the ring of quaternionic polynomials
p(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}\lambda^{k}

with
p_{k}\in\mathbb{H}
, where the indeterminate
\lambda

commutes with all coefficients.
Evaluation at a quaternion
q\in\mathbb{H}
, however, is order–dependent:

p(q)_{\mathscr{r}}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}q^{k}

is the
right value
and

p(q)_{\ell}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{k}p_{k}
is the
left value
.

Left and right zeros

A
right zero
of
p
is
q\in\mathbb{H}
such that
p(q)_{\mathscr{r}}=0;

a
left zero
satisfies
p(q)_{\ell}=0
.
Left and right zeros differ only by similarity.
If
p(q)_{\ell}=0
, then there is some
\alpha\neq 0

with
p(\alpha^{-1}q\alpha)_{\mathscr{r}}=0
, and conversely
[
9
]
.
If all
p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
(central coefficients), then

p(q)_{\mathscr{r}}=p(q)_{\ell}
for all
q
and the left zeros equal the right zeros.

Isolated and spherical zeros

A quaternionic polynomial has either
n
(not necessarily distinct) right zeros or infinitely many.
A nonreal zero is either
isolated
(a single point of its class) or
spherical
(the entire class
[q]
, a 2-sphere).
The
spherical case
occurs exactly when the real quadratic
\chi_{q}(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}-2\,\mathrm{Re}(q)\,\lambda+|q|^{2}

divides
p(\lambda);
Real zeros are always isolated since
[r]=\{r\}.

With real coefficients, all zeros are either real (hence isolated) or spherical
[
20
]
.

Notation

We view column vectors as a right
\mathbb{H}
-module.
Let
e_{i}
denote the
i
th canonical basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
, i.e., the column with a
1
in position
i
and zeros elsewhere; in particular
e_{n}=[0,\ldots,0,1]^{\top}
.
Since these entries are real (central), right/left multiplication with scalars is unambiguous when
e_{i}
appears.

Quaternionic state–space systems

We study continuous-time LTI state–space models over

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Ax(t)+Bu(t),

Stability

Asymptotic (internal) stability is defined exactly as in the real/complex case: with

for all

.
Equivalently, (

Controllability

As in the real/complex case, controllability means the ability to steer the state from any

to any

in finite time by a suitable input.
In the single–input setting, the controllability matrix is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[\,B\;\;AB\;\;\cdots\;\;A^{n-1}B\,\bigr]\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n},

(3)

and the pair
(A,B)
is completely controllable iff
\mathcal{C}
is invertible over

(equivalently, iff

]

IV
Companion Form and Companion Polynomial

In classical (real/complex) linear systems, two key tools are the controllable companion form and the characteristic or minimal polynomial. For quaternionic systems, the controllable companion form still applies. However, characteristic and minimal polynomials do not carry over directly, because there is no simple determinant on quaternions. Instead, we can work with a quaternionic companion polynomial.

The

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}+a_{1}\lambda+\cdots+a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\lambda^{n}\in\mathbb{H}[\lambda].

Compared with the classical

Remark 2
.

This

The right spectrum of the companion matrix consists of similarity classes formed by the right zeros of the companion polynomial.

Theorem 3

(
Right zeros form the right spectrum of

Let

\displaystyle\lambda,\ \ldots,\ (A_{c}v)_{n-1}=\lambda^{n-1},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(a_{0}+a_{1}\lambda+\cdots+a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1})=\lambda^{n}.

From the first

\displaystyle x_{0}\mu,\ x_{2}=x_{1}\mu=x_{0}\mu^{2},\ \ldots,\ x_{n-1}=x_{0}\mu^{n-1}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle{\bigcup}_{i}\,[\lambda_{i}],

The left zeros of

The companion polynomial can actually be associated with any cyclic quaternionic matrix

(

(

Remark 4
.

Hence, the companion polynomial shares some properties of the minimal/characteristic polynomial even in quaternions, but not all! In particular, it

Remark 5
.

Having associated a companion polynomial to a controllable pair

V
Control Problem: State-Feedback Pole Placement in Quaternionic Systems

We consider single-input LTI quaternionic state–space models (
2
) with

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Ax(t)+Bu(t),

(24)

with
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
.
We use static state feedback

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-Kx(t),\qquad K\in\mathbb{H}^{1\times n},

which yields the closed-loop dynamics

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=A_{\mathrm{cl}}x(t),\qquad A_{\mathrm{cl}}\coloneqq A-BK.

In the real or complex case, the method assigns
n
(not necessarily distinct) eigenvalues of
A_{\mathrm{cl}}
. In the quaternionic setting, the situation is different: spectra must be interpreted through
right eigenvalues
(
1
) which are determined only up to quaternionic similarity (i.e.,
\lambda\sim\alpha^{-1}\lambda\,\alpha
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}
). Consequently, pole placement specifies the
closed-loop right spectrum
as a multiset of such similarity classes (each class being finite or infinite), or, equivalently, by choosing canonical complex representatives

\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{C},\quad\operatorname{Im}\lambda_{k}\geq 0,\quad k=1,\dots,n,

counted with algebraic multiplicity. Under the usual controllability assumptions, the feedback gain
K
can then be designed to realize the desired right-spectrum classes of
A_{\mathrm{cl}}
.

Problem formulation

(
Pole placement over
\mathbb{H}
)
.

Given
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
, and desired targets

\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{H}
(or their canonical complex representatives),
find
K\in\mathbb{H}^{1\times n}
such that, for the closed loop
A_{\rm cl}=A-BK
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathscr{r}}(A_{\mathrm{cl}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle{\bigcup}_{i}\,[\lambda_{i}],

with similarity classes
[\lambda_{k}]
taken with the prescribed multiplicities.

Typically, the
\{\lambda_{k}\}
are chosen as the
right zeros
of a desired monic
closed-loop companion polynomial
a_{d}(\lambda)
.
When
a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}[\lambda]
, nonreal roots appear in conjugate pairs and encode one spherical class each.
For continuous time systems, pick representatives in the open left half–plane;
\operatorname{Re}
sets decay rate and
\left|\operatorname{Im}\right|
sets oscillation frequency.

VI
Quaternionic Pole Placement via Controllable Companion Form

In the real/complex setting, state feedback is obtained by transforming a controllable pair to companion form and matching coefficients. Over
\mathbb{H}
, the same workflow applies for single-input controllable systems: use the
companion polynomial
—read from the last row of the companion matrix and computed over
\mathbb{H}
—and interpret poles as
right-eigenvalue similarity classes
. Pole placement then reduces to a coefficient update in controller coordinates, followed by mapping the gain back through the similarity transformation. Crucially, this construction avoids determinants and characteristic/minimal polynomials—as well as Cayley–Hamilton and adjugates—which do not carry over cleanly to
\mathbb{H}
.

Theorem 5

(
Pole placement via controllable companion form
)
.

Assume
(A,B)
with
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
is controllable, and let

(A_{c},B_{c})
be its (lower) controllable companion form via
T
so that

A_{c}=T^{-1}AT
,
B_{c}=T^{-1}B
.
Write the last row of
A_{c}
as
\left[-a_{0},\dots,-a_{n-1}\right]
and let

a(\lambda)=a_{0}+a_{1}\lambda+\cdots+a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\lambda^{n}
be the companion polynomial.
Given any monic

\displaystyle a_{d}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{0}+d_{1}\lambda+\cdots+d_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\lambda^{n},

(25)

there exists a unique feedback

, such that the right spectrum of

equals the multiset of right zeros (similarity classes, with multiplicities)
of

. Moreover

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{c}\,T^{-1},

(26)

\displaystyle K_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big[d_{0}-a_{0}\ \ d_{1}-a_{1}\ \ \cdots\ \ d_{n-1}-a_{n-1}\big].

(27)

Proof.

In controllable coordinates, with
K_{c}:=KT
, we have

Writing

reads

\displaystyle(a_{0}+k_{0})+(a_{1}+k_{1})\lambda+\cdots+(a_{n-1}+k_{n-1})\lambda^{n-1}+\lambda^{n}.

Setting

yields
a_{\mathrm{cl}}(\lambda)=a_{d}(\lambda)
.
By the previously established spectral property of the companion form, the right zeros of

a_{\mathrm{cl}}
are the right eigenvalues of
A_{\mathrm{cl},c}
, and therefore of
A_{\mathrm{cl}}
.
Uniqueness follows since the coefficient update
k_{i}=d_{i}-a_{i}
is bijective and
K\mapsto K_{c}=KT
is
bijective (invertible

). Finally

.
∎

VII
Quaternionic Ackermann Formula

In this section, we show that the Ackermann formula carries over to quaternionic systems. Unlike the companion-form coefficient-update method, it is not fully general: it applies when the desired closed-loop companion polynomial has
real coefficients
. Practically, this means we assign right eigenvalue classes (spherical similarity classes) with chosen multiplicities—still broad enough for design, but not literally unrestricted. Algorithmically, the recipe is identical to the classical case (same controllability matrix selection and one-shot gain computation). At the same time, the proof is different in substance: over
\mathbb{H}
we cannot appeal to the determinant or Cayley–Hamilton, so we justify the formula via the controllable companion relations and a central (real-coefficient) polynomial calculus.

Theorem 6

(
Quaternionic Ackermann formula
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
be such that the pair
(A,B)
is controllable with
\mathcal{C}=\left[B,AB,\ldots,A^{n-1}B\right]\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n}
. Let

a_{d}(\lambda)=d_{0}+d_{1}\lambda+\cdots+d_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\lambda^{n}\in\mathbb{R}[\lambda],

(33)

be the desired monic closed-loop companion polynomial with real coefficients and let
e_{n}=[0,\dots,0,1]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the last canonical basis vector. Then the state-feedback row vector

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{C}^{-1}a_{d}(A),

(34)

with
u=-Kx
yields the closed-loop matrix
A-BK

whose right spectrum equals the similarity classes induced by the right roots of
a_{d}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathscr{r}}(A_{\mathrm{cl}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}[\lambda_{i}],

(35)

where
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are the right roots of
a_{d}(\lambda)
(listed with multiplicity).

Remark 6
.

The Ackermann step is valid only when
a_{d}\in\mathbb{R}[\lambda]
, because real scalars lie in the center of
\mathbb{H}
, ensuring that
a_{d}(A)
is unambiguous. In this case: closed-loop poles are real or occur as complex-conjugate pairs; left/right zeros coincide; and any nonreal root
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
actually stands for the entire spherical class
[\gamma]\subset\mathbb{H}
(choosing
\gamma,\bar{\gamma}
assigns that class).

For an
n
th-order single-input design, each real class uses one degree and each nonreal class uses two; with
r
real and
s
nonreal classes,
2s+r\leq n.

The following Lemma is important in the Ackermann formula proof.

Lemma 1

(
Polynomial Intertwining
)
.

Let
p(\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}[\lambda]
be a polynomial with real coefficients. If an invertible matrix
T
satisfies

\displaystyle AT

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\!A_{c}

(36)

\displaystyle p\!\left(A\right)\!T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Tp\!\left(A_{c}\right).

(37)

Proof.

From
AT=TA_{c}
it follows by induction that

\displaystyle A^{k}T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle TA_{c}^{\,k}\quad(k=0,1,\dots).

(38)

Write
p(\lambda)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}\lambda^{k}
with

p_{k}\in\mathbb{R}.
Then

\displaystyle p(A)T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}A^{k}T=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}T\!A_{c}^{\,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}A_{c}^{\,k}=Tp(A_{c}),

where we used that real coefficients lie in the center
Z(\mathbb{H})=\mathbb{R}
and commute with

T
and with powers of
A_{c}.

∎

Remark 7
.

The lemma generally fails if

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}

and

VIII
Conclusion

This note developed a quaternionic framework for state-feedback pole placement. We (i) formalized controllable companion forms over
\mathbb{H}
and their companion polynomials, which right-annihilate the companion matrix, and (ii) gave two practical designs: coefficient matching in controllable coordinates and a coordinate-free Ackermann construction valid for real (central) targets. The method keeps the convenience of field-based workflows while addressing quaternionic specifics—companion polynomial instead of characteristic/minimal, right-eigenvalue similarity classes, and order-sensitive polynomial evaluation.

Although we focused on single-input systems, extension to multi-input appears straightforward via block companion/Brunovsky forms and the same central-polynomial calculus—mainly algebraic bookkeeping. Future work will focus on observer duals, robustness, the numerical conditioning of quaternionic companion computations, and output-feedback designs.
