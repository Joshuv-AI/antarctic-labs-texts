On Positive Integers n n with ϕ ⁡ ( n ) = 2 3 ⋅ ( n + 1 ) \phi(n)=\frac{2}{3}\cdot(n+1)


Abstract
While solving a special case of a question of Erdős and Graham Steinerberger asks for all integers n n with ϕ ⁡ ( n ) = 2 3 ⋅ ( n + 1 ) \phi(n)=\frac{2}{3}\cdot(n+1) . He discovered the solutions n ∈ { 5 , 5 ⋅ 7 , 5 ⋅ 7 ⋅ 37 , 5 ⋅ 7 ⋅ 37 ⋅ 1297 } n\in\{5,5\cdot 7,5\cdot 7\cdot 37,5\cdot 7\cdot 37\cdot 1297\} and found that any additional solution must be greater than 10 10 10^{10} . He conjectured that there are no such additional solutions to this problem. We analyze this problem and prove: • Every solution n n must be square-free. • IF p p and q q are prime factors of a solution n n then p ∤ ( q − 1 ) p\nmid(q-1) . • Any solution additional to the set given by Steinerberger has to have at least 7 prime factors. • For any additional solution it holds n ≥ 10 14 n\geq 10^{14} .

Any solution is square-free

Theorem 1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer with
\phi(n)=\frac{2}{3}\cdot(n+1)
. Then
n
is square-free.

Proof.

We want to find all positive integers
n
which solve equation (
1
). Obviously,
n=1
is not a solution. Thus, from now on let be
n>1
. As

\displaystyle\phi(n)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\prod_{p\mid n}\frac{p-1}{p}

(
1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\prod_{p\mid n}\frac{p-1}{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)

(2)

or

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=\frac{2\prod_{p\mid n}p}{3\cdot\prod_{p\mid n}(p-1)-2\prod_{p\mid n}p}=:\frac{N}{D}.

(3)

First observe that for every
p\mid n
the left hand side of (
2
) is
\leq 1-\frac{1}{p}
. Thus, would
n
be divisible by 2 or 3, the product on the left side of (
2
) would be at most
1-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{2}{3}
, hence smaller than the right hand side of (
2
). Thus, we know for all primes
q\mid n
that
q\geq 5
.

Any such prime divisor
q
of
n
clearly divides the left hand side of (
3
) and the numerator
N
of the right hand side of (
3
), but
N
only once. So it does not divide the denominator
D
. Thus, the only possible non-trivial common divisor of
N
and
D
is 2. Since
n
must be divisible by at least one odd prime
D
is even, hence
\mathop{gcd}(D,N)=2
. Now
n
is an integer, which means that
D\mid N
. Hence,
D\mid 2
. Since also
2=\mathop{gcd}(D,N)\mid D
and
D=\frac{n}{N}>0
it follows
D=2
. Thus,

3\cdot\prod_{p\mid n}(p-1)-2\prod_{p\mid n}p=2

and
n=\prod_{p\mid n}p
is square-free.
∎

Now we can reformulate the problem: Find all finite sets
\emptyset\neq Q\subset\mathbb{P}\setminus\{2,3\}
of primes with

\displaystyle 3\cdot\prod_{q\in Q}(q-1)-2\prod_{q\in Q}q

\displaystyle=2.

(4)

In this setting we have

\displaystyle\prod_{q\in Q}q

\displaystyle=n.

(5)

Remark that with
n=\prod_{q\in Q}
it follows from (
4
) that
2\equiv 0-2n\equiv n\pmod{3}
, hence
n\equiv 5\pmod{6}
. So the restriction to such
n
by Steinerberger does not loose any other solutions of (
1
).

Corollary 2
.

Let
n
be a positive integer with
\phi(n)=\frac{2}{3}\cdot(n+1)
and let
p
and
r
be two primes with
pr\mid n
. Then
p\nmid(r-1)
.

Proof.

From equation (
4
) we now that there is a set
\{p,r\}\subseteq Q\subset\mathbb{P}\setminus\{2,3\}
of primes with

. Obviously,
p
divides
n
and
r-1
divides
\prod_{q\in Q}(q-1)
, but from (
4
) we know that both products are relatively prime, because
n
is odd. Hence,
p\nmid(r-1)
.
∎

3.3
A generalized variant of the case
k=2

The method of factoring a certain term to find all solutions of a problem which was used in the previous Section
3.2
can be generalized:

Lemma 5
.

Let
A>B\geq 2
be positive integers and
q_{1}<q_{2}
be two primes with

\displaystyle A\cdot(q_{1}-1)\cdot(q_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=B\cdot q_{1}\cdot q_{2}+2.

(6)

Then

\displaystyle((A-B)q_{1}-A)\cdot((A-B)q_{2}-A)

\displaystyle=AB+2(A-B).

(7)

If
A=2a
and
B=2b
are both even it follows

\displaystyle((a-b)q_{1}-a)((a-b)q_{2}-a)

\displaystyle=ab+a-b.

(8)

Proof.

Let
A,B,q_{1},q_{2}
be as described in the lemma. We are interested in solutions of

\displaystyle A\cdot(q_{1}-1)\cdot(q_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=B\cdot q_{1}\cdot q_{2}+2.

As in the previous section we equivalently restate this equation as

\displaystyle(A-B)\cdot q_{1}q_{2}-A\cdot(q_{1}+q_{2})+A-2

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(A-B)^{2}q_{1}q_{2}-A\cdot(A-B)(q_{1}+q_{2})+(A-B)(A-2)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle((A-B)q_{1}-A)\cdot((A-B)q_{2}-A)

\displaystyle=A^{2}-(A-B)(A-2)

\displaystyle=AB+2(A-B).

If
A=2a
and
B=2b
this can be simplified further to

\displaystyle((a-b)q_{1}-a)((a-b)q_{2}-a)

\displaystyle=ab+a-b.

In the previous Section
3.2
we had
A=3
and
B=2
, in future settings the second variant will become useful. Hence, in the general situation we can find all solutions by factoring
AB+2(A-B)
or

, respectively. Since both factors on the left side of (

) are congruent to
-a\pmod{a-b}
only factorizations

with
f_{1}\equiv-a\pmod{a-b}
have to be considered. (Then it is automatically
f_{2}\equiv-a\pmod{a-b}
, too.) The same is true for equation (
7
).
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
A>B\geq 2
be positive integers and let
k\geq 1
be a fixed integer. Furthermore, let

a set of primes with
|Q|=k
. Then the equation

\displaystyle A\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}(q_{i}-1)

\displaystyle=B\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}+2.

(9)

has only finitely many solutions.

Proof.

Let
A>B\geq 2
,
k
, and
Q
as in the lemma. Further let
Q=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{k}\}
with
q_{1}<q_{2}<\dots<q_{k}
primes. With

equation (

) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{B}{A}+\frac{2}{A\cdot n}

and for even

and
B=2b
to

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{a}+\frac{1}{a\cdot n}.

(10)

Suppose

for a positive integer

. Then

, hence

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{b}{a}+\frac{1}{a\cdot n}

Thus, if

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{m+i}}\right)

\displaystyle>\frac{b}{a}+\frac{1}{a\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{m+i}}

So, only finitely many cases for

can assume one can substitute this value into equation (

4
Implementation and computer-assisted generated results

The work flow as demonstrated in Section
3.6
for solving the case
k=4
can be generalized for larger values of
k
and automated. Thus, an implementation of these algorithms and the use of computers could lead to better results. With this in mind, we wrote a C++ program which uses the findings of in particular Theorem
1
, Corollary
2
, and Lemmas
6
and
7
. Some optimizations and adjustments for better calculations are being applied, in particular

•

Since we are interested in more than one situation in these calculations, if a given integer is prime, we precalculate the set of the first million primes for quick lookups.

•

For further primalty testing in the first step trial division is implemented which is later supplemented by a Fermat probable prime test. To identify the composite integers
n<2^{55}\approx 3.6\cdot 10^{16}
passing this test we use a scaled down version of the table of all Fermat pseudoprimes
<2^{64}
given by Feitsma citeFeitsma. Since, we mostly do not need to consider larger integers this is sufficient. And, we excluded the pseudoprimes with small factors from this list, too, as we find them with the initial trial dividing.

•

As the calculation comes down to factoring integers we utilize trial division and Pollard’s
p-1
factoring method. (As most integers we have to factor are
<10^{15}
we do not bother to implement more sophisticated methods.

•

Instead of calculating products
\prod_{q\in Q}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)
directly we work with its logarithm: The factors
\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)
are for larger values of
q
nearly 1, and thus, can’t be expressed with high precision. But
\log\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)
tends to 0, hence the representation error when using floating point arithmetic is much lower.

•

To neglect rounding errors while using floating point arithmetic we use safe guards: If we have to guarantee that a value
a
has to be smaller than a given limit
L
, than we only use values of
a
with
a<L\cdot(1-\epsilon)
with a suitable (small)
\epsilon>0
.

•

As the different subcases during the computation are independent of another, we use OpenMP to parallelize these task on different CPU cores. (But this algorithm is not designed to use the parallelism of a GPU, since the algorithm branches in many ways. Thus, no SIMD (same instruction multiple data) constructs can be used.)

With these implementation we get

Theorem 11
.

The are no solutions
n
to equation (
4
) with exactly five or six prime factors. Thus, every additional solution, not already listed by Steinerberger, has to have at least seven different prime factors.

Proof.

The program found no solutions with
k=5
and
k=6
, respectively. (However, it reproduced the known solutions for
k\leq 4
.) The computation proceeded in a few seconds on a standard desktop PC.
∎

As the number of branches to consider increases by a large amount and a greater number of larger integers has to be factored a search for solutions with exactly
k=7
prime factors is out of reach in a reasonable computing time. However, if one restricts the search and forces
n
to be below a given limit, these tasks become feasible, again.

To do so we observe two small findings:

Lemma 12
.

Let
n=\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}
be a solution with
k\geq 3
,
5\leq q_{1}<\dots<q_{k}
primes and
q_{1},\dots,q_{j}
already known. Furthermore, let
L\geq n
be an upper limit on
n
.

•

Then we have

q_{j+1}\leq\sqrt[k-j]{\frac{L}{q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{j}}}.

•

With
b=\prod_{i=1}^{k-2}q_{i}
and
a=\frac{3}{2}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k-2}(q_{i}-1)
it is

ab+a-b<(a-b)^{2}\cdot\frac{L}{b}.

Proof.

The first observation follows directly from the definition of
(q_{i})
as a monotonically increasing sequence.

For the second one we recall from equation (
4
) that

\displaystyle 3\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}(q_{i}-1)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}+2

holds. Set
A:=3\prod_{i=1}^{k-2}q_{i}
and
B:=2\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k-2}q_{i}
. Then both products are even and with
a:=\frac{A}{2}
and
b:=\frac{B}{2}
we get

\displaystyle a\cdot(q_{k-1}-1)\cdot(q_{k}-1)

\displaystyle=b\cdot q_{k-1}\cdot q_{k}+1.

From this we get as in Section
3.3

\displaystyle((a-b)q_{k-1}-a)\cdot((a-b)q_{k}-a)

\displaystyle=ab+a-b.

As
a-b\geq 1>0
and
a>0
it is

\displaystyle ab+a-b

\displaystyle<(a-b)^{2}q_{k-1}q_{k}=(a-b)^{2}\cdot\frac{n}{b}\leq(a-b)^{2}\cdot\frac{L}{b}.

∎

This can be used to cut branches in the computation:

•

If at any point in the computation we get
q_{j+1}\leq q_{j}
from Lemma
12
then there is no solution in this case and it can be terminated.

•

If one has to factor an integer
ab+a-b
in the algorithm which does not fulfill the second observation of Lemma
12
then this factorization cannot lead to a solution with
n\leq L
. Hence, we do not complete this factorization and cut this branch.

In this way we can exclude the existence of solutions
n\leq L
with exactly
k
prime divisors even for larger values of
