Acral Pigmentation in Peutz-Jeghers Syndrome: Dermoscopic Findings and Treatment with the Q-Switched Nd:YAG Laser
Authors: Hyeonwoo Shin, Keunyoung Hur, Jee-Soo Lee, Moon-Woo Seong, Je-Ho Mun
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS; MIM 175200) is an autosomal dominant multiple-organ cancer syndrome. It is characterized by brown macules distributed in the perioral skin, oral mucosa, hands and feet, and hamartomatous gastrointestinal polyps that can eventually lead to intestinal obstruction, abdominal pain, bleeding, and anemia. Patients with PJS are at a higher risk of ovarian, testicular, breast, lung, and pancreatic cancers. This predisposition is due to the pathogenic variant in serine/threonine kinase 11 (STK11) gene located on chromosome 19p13.3. Here, we present the dermoscopic findings, histopathologic features of acral pigmentation, and DNA sequencing results of the patient with PJS. We also report a successful removal of acral pigmentation using the Q-switched Nd:YAG laser (QSNYL) treatment. Our results suggest that QSNYL therapy could be a treatment option for acral pigmentation in patients with PJS.

INTRODUCTION

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS; MIM 175200) is an autosomal dominant multiple-organ cancer disorder caused by a mutation in serine/threonine kinase 11 (STK11) gene located on chromosome 19p13.31. This syndrome is characterized by the presence of brown macules distributed in the oral mucosa and acral skin. Patients with PJS have a higher risk of developing cancers and hamartomatous gastrointestinal polyps that can lead to intestinal obstruction, abdominal pain, bleeding, and anemia. Here, we present a successful treatment of acral pigmentation using a Q-switched Nd:YAG laser (QSNYL), as well as dermoscopic findings, in a patient with PJS.

CASE REPORT

A 23-year-old female, referred to our department for acral pigmentation, had pigmented lesions on her lips since early childhood. Clinical examination also revealed multiple brown lentiginous macules on her fingers (Fig. 1). Dermoscopic examination showed parallel ridge patterns (PRPs), while pathological analysis demonstrated increased melanin pigmentation within basal keratinocytes; however, atypical melanocyte proliferation was not observed (Fig. 2).

Genomic DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood using the Gentra Puregene Blood kit (Gentra Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All coding exons and flanking intronic regions were amplified by PCR using primers specific for STK11. The amplified product was directly sequenced with an ABI PRISM 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems) using the BigDye Terminator v.3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems). Direct sequencing revealed a heterozygous c.465-2A>G mutation in STK11 (2015 ACMG/AMP classification: pathogenic variant) (Fig. 3). The lesions were irradiated with QSNYL (Pastelle; WONTECH) at a wavelength of 532 nm with a fluence of 1.1 J/cm2 and a spot size of 3 mm. Two treatment sessions were performed at 6-week intervals. Pigmentation was successfully removed without any side effects (Fig. 1).

DISCUSSION

PJS is characterized by brown to black discrete, flat, oval pigmented lesions on the mucosal regions and areas of the hands or feet, and generally measure less than 5 mm in diameter2. STK11 is a tumor suppressor gene and is involved in the regulation of diverse processes, including cellular growth, cell polarity, energy metabolism, and apoptosis. Although the exact mechanism underlying the effect of the mutation on mucosal and acral pigmentation remains to be elucidated, several lines of evidence suggest that STK11 plays a vital role in melanocyte biology and suppresses melanocyte transformation3.

Dermoscopy is a useful tool for the diagnosis and management of acral pigmentation45. Although PRP is considered to be a highly sensitive and specific sign for acral melanoma, it should be noted that PRPs could also occur in non-melanomic conditions as well6. Non-melanomic PRPs could be caused by drug-induced pigmentation, congenital acral nevus, exogenous pigmentation, and pigmentation associated with systemic diseases, including PJS and Laugier-Hunziker syndrome. To the best of our knowledge, dermoscopic findings of acral pigmented macules associated with PJS have been scarcely reported in the literature, with only three case reports, including our study78. The dermoscopic features of PRPs in PJS are attributed to the deposition of melanin, primarily in the crista profunda intermedia in PJS7.

Although previous studies have reported the successful treatment of mucosal macules in PJS with laser therapy9, there is a scarcity of reports regarding the management of acral pigmentation in PJS. Since the presence of multiple pigmentations on fingers and lips causes cosmetic discomfort, treatment is necessary to improve the quality of life of affected individuals. Since 532 nm QSNYL primarily targets epidermal pigmentation with the destruction of melanin, the outcome was satisfactory to our patient.

In conclusion, we report the dermoscopic findings and the mutation profile of a patient with PJS. Our study revealed that QSNYL therapy is a successful treatment option for acral pigmentation in patients with PJS.
