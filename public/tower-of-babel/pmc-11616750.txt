Astilbin exerts anti-hypersensitivity by regulating metabolic demand and neuronal activity in rodent model of neuropathic pain
Authors: Qiru Wang, Dongxia Duan, Chao Luo, Jinlu Huang, Jinbao Wei, Yang Zhang, Ke Zhang, Tong Zhou, Wei Wang, Shaoxin Yang, Le Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAstilbe chinensis, is a traditional Chinese medicine commonly employed for pain management. However, its primary active ingredient remains a subject of debate.MethodsSpinal nerve ligation (SNL) and formalin-induced pain models were employed. Network pharmacology and bioinformatics were utilized to identify targets. Verification was performed through spinal cord double immunofluorescence staining, quantitative PCR and whole-cell recording techniques.ResultsIn experiments conducted on neuropathic rats, both systemic and intrathecal administration of astilbin, an essential constituent, exhibited a noteworthy and dose-dependently decrease in chronic and acute pain behaviours. The ED50 value, which represents the dose at which 50% effectiveness is achieved, was measure at 7.59 μg, while the Emax value, indicating the maximum attainable effect, was found to be 60% of the maximal possible effect (% MPE). Forty-two shared targets were identified, enriching the metabolic and synaptic pathways in the network pharmacology analysis, as confirmed by transcriptomic analysis. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed a strong correlation between the anti-nociceptive effects of astilbin and neuronal metabolic processes. Spinal functional ultrasound (FUS) analysis indicated increased spinal blood flow intensity and changes in metabolism-related enzyme activity, including stearoyl-CoA desaturase (Scd), 17beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (Hsd17b7) and sterol 14alpha-demethylase (Cyp51) in neuropathic rats, pretreatment with astilbin decreased formalin-induced blood flow in acute pain. Bath application of astilbin dose-dependently inhibited neuronal activity by reducing the frequency and amplitude of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) without affecting miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs).ConclusionsIn summary, this study provides evidence that astilbin alleviates pain by modulating neuronal metabolic processes and synaptic homeostasis.Keywords: Astilbin, network pharmacology, WGCNA, metabolic homeostasis, synaptic transmission

Introduction

Chronic pain involves elevated glutamatergic transmission, with spinal glutamate release increasing by 3–7 times [1,2]. Plastic changes in neuronal activity within the spinal dorsal horn are believed to facilitate the augmentation of pain signalling, necessitating substantial energy expenditure and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) consumption. Neural electrical activity involves the activation of specific proteins and ion channels, which establish the molecular basis of electrical excitability, accompanied by substantial ATP consumption at synapses [3,4]. In neurons, the mitochondrial oxidation pathway is the primary ATP synthesis pathway that dynamically responds to energy demands, notably during pain, by coordinating blood flow with the cardiovascular system to target specific substances at synaptic junctions [5,6]. Blood flow crucially supports the supply of nutrients for tissue repair [7]. Previous research has indicated that the regulating of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes influences synaptic neurotransmitter release, which is pivotal in analgesia. Evidence suggests that conventional compounds, such as astilbin enhance mitochondrial membrane potential and vitality while maintaining endogenous ATP levels [8].

Astilbin, a flavonoid extracted from Astilbe chinensis, exhibits promising activity and possesses numerous pharmacological properties beneficial to human health in clinical settings [9]. Studies have indicated that astilbin significantly reduces injury-induced hypersensitivity in mice [10]. It exerts significant neuroprotective effects by ameliorating neuronal death and oxidative stress during disease progression in vivo [11]. As a functionally connected network, astilbin significantly increases the proliferation and migration ability of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and blood vessel formation, promoting blood perfusion and vascular activity in response to pathological ATP demand [12]. Therefore, improved blood perfusion levels help to elevate tissue metabolic requestments. In mice fed with a high-fat diet (HFD), astilbin reduces body weight, insulin resistance, inflammation, hepatic lipid droplet aggregation and triglyceride accumulation. It also reversed signal alterations in glutamate metabolism-related pathways and enhanced both hepatic ATP transduction and xanthine metabolism pathways [13]. Taken together, these results provide strong evidence that astilbin exerts potential neuroprotective effects by modulating metabolic homeostasis.

In the present study, we examined the underlying targeted molecular or endogenous processes by which astilbin primarily participates in the regulation of nociceptive behaviours in both chronic and acute pain. However, this topic has not yet been fully explored. We hypothesized that a single intrathecal administration of astilbin would promote neuronal protection by decreasing synaptic glutamate release and plasticity, leading to strengthened functional connectivity synaptic dynamics of blood vessel guided metabolic supply and ultimately suppressed pain sensations. To systematically test this hypothesis, network pharmacology was used to predict the targets and intracellular processes involved in the action of astilbin. To identify the key genes regulating behavioural sensitization in neuropathic rats and distinguish them from other detected genes, we assessed the profiles of these genes and key gene modules, as well as the underlying targets associated with astilbin-induced antinociceptive effects, using RNA-Seq and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Furthermore, we tested the vasodilatory effects of astilbin on vessel intensity and plasticity, both in vivo and in vitro. In the present study, we revealed that a single intrathecal injection of astilbin produced dose- and time-dependent antinociception in both spinal nerve ligation (SNL) and formalin-induced pain by promoting spinal metabolic processes involving enzymatic activity, highlighting the interactions between spinal vessel-mediated ATP production and neural activity in nociceptive transformation and transduction.

Materials and methods

Animals and drugs

The chemical formula of astilbin is C21H22O11. It belongs to the flavonoid family and contains seven hydroxyl groups that can form hydrogen bonds with targets.

All experimental animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Welfare Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (lot: 10902) and were carried out following National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 in this study [14]. All the experiments were conducted using specific pathogen free (SPF) male Wistar rats (9 weeks, weighing between 120 and 150 g) obtained from the SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rats were housed in the Shanghai Mental Health Center Animal Care Facility (Shanghai, China) for at least 1 week prior to the start of behaviour testing and surgery. Animals had ad libitum access to food and water and were on a light/dark cycle of 12 h/12 h (light cycle: 08:00–20:00) in an air-conditioned room with a set temperature of 18–22 °C with 55% ± 5% humidity. Experimenters were blinded to treatment groups in behavioural experiments and randomized to treatment groups using a random number generator. Multiple treatment groups were always found within any individual cage of animals. Antinociceptive effects were assessed in SNL and formalin-induced rats and assigned to the following groups: sham represented the exposed spine nerves without ligation group, SNL represented the SNL model group, and SNL + astilbin (Mecklin, Shanghai, China) represented the different doses (dose: 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 μg in 10 µL artificial cerebro-spinal fluid (ACSF) of astilbin-treated model groups. After 14 days of SNL modelling, rats in the SNL + astilbin groups received intrathecal astilbin treatment at 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 μg. Meanwhile, rats in the sham group and SNL group were administered an equivalent volume of ACSF (10 µL) followed by a 15 μL ACSF flush. For intraperitoneal injections, astilbin was dissolved in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, 9004-32-4). After injections, paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) was recorded at before and 1, 2 and 4 h post-intrathecal injection. For formalin-induced tonic pain, the rats were divided into three groups: the formalin + astilbin (30 and 100 μg) groups and the formalin group. The former group was treated with intrathecal astilbin followed by formalin administration, whereas the other group was treated with the same volume of ACSF (10 µL) followed by a 15 μL ACSF flush. Rats were first given intrathecal astilbin (30 and 100 μg) or ACSF injections, followed by subcutaneous formalin injections on the dorsal side of the hindpaw. The duration of nociceptive behaviours was immediately and manually counted every 10 min, up to 90 min following formalin injection.

Spinal nerve ligation

SNL were performed as previously described [15]. Briefly, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane. An incision was made through the skin between the spinal column and the left iliac crest, and the longissimus lumborum muscle, articular processes of L4-S1, and fascia above L6 were removed to expose the L5 and L6 spinal nerves. The nerves were tightly ligated using a 6-0 silk thread. The wound was sutured strictly with 4-0 silk sutures in two layers and cleaned. In sham-operated rats, the surgical procedure was identical to that in the L5/L6 SNL group but without L5 and L6 SNLs. Rats that exhibited motor deficiencies during surgery were excluded from the study.

Intrathecal catheterization

After the rats were placed under deep anaesthesia using inhaled isoflurane (R510-22, RWD, Guangzhou, China, 4–5% induction, 1–2% maintenance), the spinal cord was catheterized [16]. First, we carefully transected the area between the fifth and sixth lumbar vertebrae, cut the intervertebral soft tissue, and exposed the atlantooccipital membrane. Second, a small slit was cut in the membrane, through which the catheter polyethylene tubing (PE-10: 0.28 mm i.d. and 0.61 mm o.d., Advanced Biotechnologies Inc., South Dennis, MA) was inserted into the rat’s subarachnoid space. The sudden movement of the rat tail indicated that the catheter had hit the spinal cord, confirming the correct cannulation direction. The following day, successfully operated rats underwent bilateral hindlimb paralysis after lidocaine followed by flushing with 15 μL ACSF.

Behavioural assessment of nociception

To determine mechanical allodynia in the SNL-induced model of persistent pain, we used the von Frey test to measure the nociceptive threshold [17]. The rats were housed individually in a plastic cage with a metal grid to allow access to the plantar surface of the hind paws. After an acclimatization period of 1 h, each von Frey filament was pushed against the surface of the hindpaw, and paw withdrawal responses were considered pain perception using a 2290 CE electrical von Frey hair (IITC Life Science, Woodland Hills, CA). Paw withdrawal threshold was determined after 4 h. In all the cases, the investigator was blinded to the drug treatment. Allodynia was defined as a PWT of 8 g [18].

For acute pain analysis, at 40 min after the intrathecal injection of astilbin, the rat was injected subcutaneously in the left hind paw with 50 μL of 5% formalin (221008, Yulu, Shangrao, China) and immediately passed into a clear Plexiglas observation chamber with a mirror embedded in a 45° angle beneath the floor to record animal behaviour and study formalin-induced pain. The number of flinches in the injected paw was recorded as a measure of the nociceptive response for 60 s at 10 min intervals during 90 min observations. The recorded behaviours were divided into two phases, namely, 0–10 and 10–90 min after formalin injection as the first and second phases of formalin-induced pain, respectively [19].

Network pharmacology analysis

Network pharmacology was used to explore the potential targets and mechanisms of action of astilbin on pain. Using the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), the Canonical SMILES string for astilbin was obtained and uploaded to SwissTargetPrediction (www.SwissTargetPrediction.ch) to identify the potential targets of astilbin. The astilbin-related genes were collected from several databases, including GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), TTD database (https://db.idrblab.org/ttd/), OMIM database (http://omim.org/) and DisGeNET database (https://www.disgenet.org), by searching the key term ‘pain’. Next, the astilbin-pain interactive genes were screened by Venny2.1.0 (Venn’s diagrams drawing tool) and then input to the STRING database (http://string-db.org/) for protein interaction analysis. The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was uploaded to Cytoscape 3.9 (https://cytoscape.org/), followed by the top 10 hub targets obtained by degree ranking using the cytoHubba plugin. The Gene Ontology (GO) database was used to identify the biological mechanisms underlying high-throughput genomic or transcriptome data, including molecular function (MF), biological processes (BPs), and cellular components (CCs). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database was used to determine the function and biological relationships between potential targets. The analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler_v4.8.1 software package of the R platform (https://rstudio.com).

Molecular docking

To analyse the binding affinities and modes of interaction between astilbin and its potential targets, CB-dock2, a tool based on the AutoDock Vina molecular docking procedure for protein–ligand blind docking, integrating cavity detection, docking and homologous template fitting was employed [20]. The 3D molecular structure of astilbin was obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Protein crystal structures were obtained from RCSB Protein Database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and saved in PDB format. For the docking analysis, the potential proteins and astilbin were subjected to CB-dock2. CB-dock2 retrieved from the protein–ligand complexes database for template ligands with high topology similarity (FP2 ≥ 0.4). The similarity between the query protein and proteins complexed with the selected template ligands was calculated. The complexes with more than 40% sequence identity (and pocket RMSD ≤4 Å) in the template ligand binding site were retained for the subsequent template-based cavity detection and molecular docking. Finally, the potential binding sites of the query ligand were ranked according to the Vina score (kcal/mol). If the Vina score, or binding energy, was less than −1.2 kcal/mol or −5 kJ/mol (1 kcal = 4.184 kJ), the docking results were considered reliable, and the minimum binding energy was selected for docking analysis. Lower docking energy indicates stronger binding capacity.

RNA isolation, qPCR and RNA‑sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the ipsilateral spinal cord using the TRIzol reagent (9108; Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) and purified using the miRNAeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). cDNA was obtained using a cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (RR036A, Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) using a LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master Kit (04887352001, Roche Molecular Diagnostics, Alameda, CA). Each reaction was performed in triplicate. The relative quantification of genes was normalized to those of GAPDH in the same cDNA samples and expressed as fold change compared with the internal standard via the 2−ΔΔCT method. The forward primers were 5′-CATCACTGCCACCCAGAAGACTG-3′ and 5′-ATGCCAGTGAGCTTCCCGTTCAG-3′ for reverse GAPDH. The forward primers were 5′-GCAAGCTCTACACCTGCCTCTT-3′ and 5′-CGTGCCTTGTAAGTTCTGTGGC-3′ for reverse stearoyl-CoA desaturase (Scd). The forward primers were 5′-TCCTCTGACCAAATACGCGAGC-3′ and 5′-TGGTCCTGACACGCTTTTCCAG-3′ for reverse 17beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (Hsd17b7). The forward primers were 5′-ATGGTACTGCTGGGCTTGCTGCAGT-3′ and 5′-TCCGCATTCAGGTCTTCGTTC-3′ for reverse sterol 14alpha-demethylase (Cyp51).

Transcriptome libraries were constructed and sequenced at LC-BIO Bio-tech, Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols. About 1 μg of RNA per sample was used to purify poly (A) RNA using Dynabeads Oligo (dT). Poly(A) RNA was fragmented into small pieces and reverse-transcribed to generate cDNA. After a series of treatments, we obtained an average insert size of 300 ± 50 bp for the final cDNA library, and an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 was used to perform 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing (PE150).

Weighted gene co‑expression network analysis

The co-expression network was built using the WGCNA package in R, which provided topological properties, including the construction of scale-free networks, co-expression matrix, adjacency function and calculation of different node coefficients. The soft threshold (β) was set to 8 based on the scale-free topology criterion using the function Pick Soft Threshold. The adjacency matrix was used to calculate the topological overlap matrix (TOM) and the corresponding dissimilarity (1 − TOM), which were used as important biological indicators of co-expression network interconnection and distance for hierarchical gene clustering. The dynamic tree cut algorithm was used to identify the gene modules. Finally, the module eigengenes (MEs) of the gene modules were calculated and clustered hierarchically. Similar modules were combined with shear height and marked with different colours, and specific modules were identified based on module feature relationships. Heatmaps of the clustered module features were drawn, and module membership (MM) values (correlation coefficient between gene significance (GS) and module significance) and GS values (correlation coefficient between gene and clinical features) were calculated [21,22].

Spinal functional ultrasound (FUS) imaging

After the rat was placed under deep anaesthesia with isoflurane, laminectomy was performed between the 12th thoracic vertebra and the second lumbar vertebra [23]. This window allowed the entire ultrasonic probe (14 mm) to be positioned in the sagittal plane. Following laminectomy, the rat was placed on a ‘spinal cord’ stereotaxic frame with the lumbar rachis suspended. Images were obtained after the rats were stable and lightly anesthetized (respiratory rate, approximately 120 breaths/min) for 1 h. The rat was kept at 37 °C during the surgical procedure and the imaging session. Approximately, 2 mL of ACSF was gently pumped into the spinal cord and the laminectomy cavity was filled with foetal ultrasound gel (Comper, Minhao Technology, Shenzhen, China). The ultrasonic probe was positioned just above the window using a 3-axis motor system attached to the ultrasound probe. NeuroScan live acquisition software was used to drive the probe connected to an ultrasonic ultrafast scanner. Data were analysed by ΔF/F following intrathecal injections [24].

Electrophysiological recording

Laminectomy was performed on the rats to remove lumbar spinal enlargement, as previously described [25,26]. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane. The L3–L5 spinal cord was removed quickly and placed in ice-cold oxygenated (95% O2 and 5% CO2) high sucrose ACSF containing: 234 mM sucrose, 3.6 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 12 mM glucose, 2.5 mM CaCl2 and 25 mM NaHCO3. Spinal cord slices of 300 µm thickness were prepared with VT1200S Vibratome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), transferred to a storage chamber, and incubated in an oxygenated ACSF: 125 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2 and 10 mM d-glucose, with pH 7.3 at 32 °C for 30 min. The slices were transferred to an incubator with ACSF at room temperature for at least 1 h before recording [27].

For miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) recording, the sodium channel, subtype A of GABA receptors, and glycine receptors were inhibited by the bath application of 0.5 µM tetrodotoxin (TTX, Aladdin, Shanghai, China), 100 µM picrotoxin (PTX, Sigma, St. Louis, MO) and 1 µM strychnine (Sigma, St. Louis, MO). A borosilicate glass-recording electrode (BF150-86-10, Sutter, Sacramento, CA) was filled with a K+-based internal solution containing the following: 130 mM K-gluconate, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 1.1 mM EGTA, 10 mM sodium phosphocreatine, 5 mM QX314, 4 mM Na2-ATP, 0.4 mM Na-GTP and 1 mM MgSO4. The pH was adjusted to pH 7.2 with KOH. The neurons were voltage-clamped at −70 mV.

For the miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs) recording, the electrode was filled with a high Cl− solution containing (in mM): 140 mM CsCl, 1.1 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM MgCl2, 3 mM MgATP and 0.3 mM Tris-guanosine triphosphate; the pH was adjusted to pH 7.4 with CsOH. The kainate, α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazole propionic acid receptor (AMPAR) and N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) activities were blocked by a bath application of TTX (0.5 µM), 6-cyano-7-nitroquinoxaline-2,3-dione (CNQX, Sigma) (20 µM) and 5-phosphono-d-norvaline (d-AP5, Tocris, Ellisville, MO) (50 µM) when the neurons voltage-clamped at −70 mV. Data were acquired using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Axon Instruments, Foster City, CA) and a 1550 B digitizer (Axon Instruments, Foster City, CA) and recorded for 5 min for analysis. Series resistance was monitored throughout the experiments, and neurons with access resistance (Ra) <30 MΩ or membrane resistance (Rm)/Ra >10 were chosen for subsequent analysis. Data were filtered at 2 kHz, sampled at 10 kHz, and analysed using pClamp 10.3 software (Axon Instruments, Foster City, CA). Data analysis was performed using the axo-graph analysis package. A semi-automated sliding template protocol was employed to analyse both classes of miniature data. This detection criterion was determined by optimizing the scaling factor and ensuring the quality of fit. Events were identified when the criterion surpassed a pre-set threshold level. Additionally, the algorithm seamlessly adjusted for any variations in recording noise, considering only amplitudes that were at least three times the standard deviation of the noise (3σ). Each event identified by the template was individually evaluated and accepted for analysis based on two criteria: they must not overlap, and the records must exhibit a stable baseline (2.5 ms) both before and after mIPSC/mEPSCs rising and decay phases. Once accepted, the mIPSCs/mEPSCs were aligned at their onset and averaged. The peak amplitude, rise time (measured over 10–90% of the peak amplitude) and decay time constant (calculated over 20–80% of the decay phase) were computed using the AxoGraph analysis software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining involved tissue fixation with paraformaldehyde and dehydration with 30% sucrose for structural preservation. Permeabilization with Triton X-100 facilitates antibody penetration. A blocking solution minimized nonspecific binding, and a primary antibody (NeuN, ab104224; glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ab4674; ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1), ab178846; Scd, 28678-1-AP; CYP51, A13485) was applied against the protein of interest. After washing, a fluorophore-labelled secondary antibody was introduced to visualize the target protein by fluorescence microscopy. Samples are subsequently mounted with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)-containing medium for nuclear staining, enabling precise examination and analysis of protein localization within spinal slices.

Statistical analysis

Before statistical analysis, all data were tested for Gaussian distribution assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test after transforming to logarithms. Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (Version 7.0, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). Differences were analysed by unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test, and one-way or repeated measures two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Sidak’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were processed for visualization using CorelDraw 2020 (Corel Corporation, Ottawa, Canada).

Results

Astilbin attenuated mechanical allodynia and formalin-induced pain

To evaluate the antinociceptive effects of astilbin on pain states, we first evaluated mechanical (von Frey test) pain sensitivity in rats with SNL and formalin-induced pain rats by systematic injections. Compared to the sham group, SNL rats exhibited hypersensitivity following innocuous mechanical stimuli in the ipsilateral hind paws, and intraperitoneal injection of astilbin significantly inhibited neuropathic pain behaviour and exerted maximum anti-hypersensitivity at 1 h (Figure 1(A), F(2, 56) = 1195, p < 0.0001, *p < 0.05 compared with the SNL group, #p < 0.05 compared with the sham group, two-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Sidak’s post-tests). Pretreatment with astilbin for 1 h remarkably suppressed the formalin-induced tonic flinching response, but not the acute nociceptive response (Figure 1(B), F(1, 100) = 54.54, p < 0.0001). Intrathecal administration of 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 μg astilbin in 10 μL volume remarkably suppressed PWT in neuropathic rats in a dose-dependent manner at 1 h post-intrathecal injection, while a similar dosage of astilbin did not significantly prolong PWT in ipsilateral hindpaws of sham rats at 1 h after injections (Figure 1(C), mechanical allodynia F(6,116) = 677.7, p < 0.0001, *,#p < 0.05 compared with the SNL group, #p < 0.05 compared with the sham group; two-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Sidak’s post-tests). Dose–response analyses showed that the ED50 and Emax values of astilbin to inhibit mechanical allodynia were 7.59 μg and 60% MPE, as calculated 1 h after injection (Figure 1(D)). While intrathecal administration of 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 μg astilbin did not affect PWT in sham rats (Supplementary Figure 1(A), F(5, 96) = 7.134, p < 0.0001).

To test whether astilbin was engaged in inflammatory pain, we administered 50 µL of 5% formalin under the skin of the right hind paws in naïve rats. This injection induced a significant pain-related behaviour characterized by flinching and licking of the injected paw. The number of formalin-induced flinches was quantified manually for 60 s at 10 min intervals until 90 min (Figure 1(E)). Intrathecal injections of astilbin (30 μg and 100 μg) were conducted for 60 min before the formalin injection. In the acute phase (phase I, up to 10 min after formalin injection), no difference was observed in the number of flinches between the control and astilbin-treated groups. In the chronic phase (phase II, 10–90 min after formalin injection), as the dose of astilbin increased, the number of flinches in the astilbin-treated group decreased significantly in a dose-dependent manner, peaking at 30, 40 and 50 min compared to the control group during the 90-minute observation period (anti-nociceptive behaviours were observed at 30, 40 and 50 min, Figure 1(E); p < 0.05, *p < 0.05, 100 μg treated group compared with the control group, #p < 0.05 30 μg treated group compared with the control group). Collectively, our study demonstrated that intrathecal injections of astilbin significantly alleviated chronic neuropathic and inflammatory pain in rats.

The network pharmacology inferred that metabolic-related pathways involved astilbin-induced anti-hypersensitivity effects in pain

Network pharmacology was conducted to investigate the potential targets and signalling pathways of astilbin-induced anti-hypersensitivity. First, we obtained the structures of astilbin from PubChem and uploaded them to the SwissTargetPrediction database to identify putative targets. A set of pain-related targets was established by extraction from OMIM, GeneCards, TTD and DisGeNET databases. A total of 42 target proteins were obtained from the Venn diagram using the intersection of astilbin targets and pain-related genes (Figure 2(A)). A STRING database was used to predict potential interactions between target candidates at the protein level, and an astilbin-pain-target interaction network was visualized using Cytoscape to reflect the relationship of intersecting genes between pain and astilbin. We screened the top 10 hub targets ranked by degree from 42 intersecting genes, and the results revealed the involvement of tumour necrosis factor (TNF), hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1A), amyloid beta precursor protein (APP), estrogen recetor 1 (ESR1), matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP2), proto-oncogene receptor tyrosine kinase (MET), mitogen-activated protein kinase 14 (MAPK14), beta-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1, beta-secretase 1 (BACE1), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG) and disintegrin and metalloproteinase 17 (ADAM17) (Figure 2(B)).

Functional enrichment analysis of the 42 candidate targets was conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R software. KEGG pathway analysis showed that astilbin-induced antinociceptive effects were mainly associated with pathways related to cellular metabolism, such as metabolic processes and endocrine resistance, and a variety of signalling pathways, such as the sphingolipid, MAPK and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) signalling pathways (Figure 2(C)). Candidate targets were analysed, and the top eight BPs, CCs and MFs were selected for visual analysis. The top eight significantly enriched GO terms were closely related to the activity of astilbin on pain, including oxygen levels, neuron projection membranes, metallopeptidase activity and carbonate dehydratase activity. They were associated with the pain-related activities of astilbin, which were mainly involved in the metabolic processes of pain (Figure 2(D)).

Astilbin regulated the expressions of metabolic process in neuropathic pain

Network pharmacology results suggested that the antinociceptive mechanism of astilbin may be related to metabolic and immune responses. Therefore, we performed RNA transcriptome sequencing of the sham, SNL and astilbin-treated groups (n = 4) to further explore the specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and signalling pathways that mediate pain following astilbin intervention. The mRNA profiles of the spinal cord tissues of rats in the SNL, astilbin-treated and sham groups were determined using mRNA-seq. We compared the DEGs of the SNL versus the sham group and the SNL versus the astilbin-treated group and showed the results using a heatmap. Both SNL and astilbin triggered the expression of numerous DEGs (Figure 3(A,D)). Based on the criteria |log2FC| > 1 and p < 0.05, 21,999 genes, including 12,041 upregulated and 9958 downregulated genes, were identified in the comparison between SNL group and sham group. To determine the possible gene functions and enrichment pathways of DEGs related to neuropathic pain, we performed GO functional and KEGG enrichment analyses of DEGs, which indicated that DEGs were mainly associated with metabolic processes and immune response (Figure 3(B,C)). Similarly, in the SNL versus astilbin-treated group, 22,080 DEGs were identified, with 11,163 upregulated and 10,917 downregulated genes. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed that the astilbin-treated group was associated with changes in metabolism-related genes and signalling pathways, including fatty acid biosynthetic processes, metabolic pathways, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and synaptic processes (Figure 3(E,F)). Therefore, the RNA profile suggested that astilbin might regulate metabolism-related genes and signalling pathways in neuropathic pain.

WGCNA inferred that metabolic process involved in astilbin-induced antinociception

We established sham operation, neuropathic pain and astilbin-treatment groups. Fourteen days post-operatively, the pain thresholds of the rats were recorded, and measurements were taken again. Following the operation, L3–L5 were isolated, and transcriptome sequencing was performed to gather raw data. WGCNA performed a similarity categorization analysis of the relationship between behavioural features such as PWT, nerve injury, pain, with gene expression according to a similar mathematical distribution [28]. WGCNA incorporates the categorized similarly expressed genes into a gene set, which was originally obtained from 23,565 detected genes in L3–L5 samples from the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. Repeatability checks were performed until the data set and the internal structure of the data were stabilized by deleting non-expressed genes. The clustering system tree and corresponding module diagrams were obtained using a Dynamic Tree Cut (Figure 4(A)). Twenty-three thousand five hundred and sixty-five validated genes were selected for subsequent analysis. The 25 modules obtained from the cut were distinguished by different colours, among which the module with the highest number of genes was light-yellow. Of the four categories of biological characteristics, PWT was used as a clinical reference, whereas ligations, pain and anxiety did not achieve statistical significance or reflect individual biological characteristics (Figure 4(B)). The completed cluster dendrogram showed that the light-yellow module was most closely associated with the PWT (correlation coefficient of 0.61), and the light-yellow module was selected for subsequent analysis (Figure 4(C)). GO functional analysis was performed on the light-yellow module, resulting in BP changes, mainly enriched in biosynthetic and metabolic progresses (Figure 4(D)). The three most popular signalling pathway databases, namely, KEGG, Reactome and WikiPathways, were used to identify signalling pathways enriched in the light-yellow module. The results revealed high enrichment of metabolism-related signalling pathways, such as the lipid biosynthetic process, cholesterol metabolic process and steroid metabolism (Figure 4(E– G)). Therefore, these data collectively demonstrated that astilbin may regulate metabolic pathways that exert antinociceptive effects in rats.

Intrathecal astilbin increased the hemodynamic activity and metabolic biomarkers in neuropathic rats

The spinal cord is a highly vascularized organ containing a dense network of capillaries in both the dorsal and ventral horns. This dense capillary network is responsible for meeting high tissue energy demands [23]. To illustrate the hemodynamic activity of the spinal cord, we performed a laminectomy of the lumbar enlargement using FUS imaging. Astilbin perfusion was performed in both the sham and SNL groups, and FUS imaging were recorded. Sham rats exhibited no hemodynamic response after astilbin perfusion (Figure 5(A)), whereas SNL rats showed a significant increase in hemodynamic activation in lamina I/II following astilbin treatment (Figure 5(B)). The amplitude of the response induced by astilbin in the SNL group was greater than that in the sham group, suggesting that astilbin modulates local energy demand by recruiting blood vessel-induced perfusion and metabolic supply (Figure 5(C,D)). Furthermore, we observed a robust hemodynamic response in formalin-induced an acute pain behaviour (Figure 5 (E)). Pretreatment with a single dose of astilbin completely abolished formalin-induced hemodynamic response (Figure 5(F)). Scd, Hsd17b7 and Cyp51 are key mediators in the regulating of lipid, cholesterol and sterol metabolism activity in cellular processes. We demonstrated that intrathecal astilbin remarkably restored Scd (Figure 5(G), F(2, 20) = 8.450, p = 0.0022), Hsd17b7 (Figure 5(H), F(2, 15) = 5.854, p = 0.0132) and Cyp51 (Figure 5(I), F(2, 20) = 8.450, p = 0.0007) mRNA spinal cord following neuropathic pain. Co-staining with neuronal (NeuN), astrocyte (GFAP), microglial (Ibal) and synaptic (Bassoon) markers revealed that Scd was mainly colocalized with bassoon in dorsal horn I–V laminae (Supplementary Figure 2(A–D)). In contrast, Cyp51 was surrounded with synaptic biomarker Bassoon (Supplementary Figure 2(E–H)). Additionally, our protein-level analysis revealed a marked enhancement in Cyp51 and Scd expressions following astilbin administration, consistent with our mRNA-level findings (Figure 5(J,K): Cyp51: F(2, 15) = 6.441, p = 0.0096; Figure 5(L,M): Scd: F(2, 15) = 11.23, p = 0.0010, one-way ANOVA, the data were presented as means ± SEM).

Astilbin had highly stable binding capacity with Cyp51, Hsd17b7 and Scd

To evaluate the affinity of astilbin for its targets, we performed molecular docking analysis. The binding poses and interactions of astilbin with the three proteins were determined using CB-dock2 and the binding energy for each interaction was calculated. The results showed that astilbin bound to its protein targets via visible hydrogen bonds and strong electrostatic interactions. Cyp51, Hsd17b7 and Scd had low binding energies of −9.3, −9 and −7.7 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating highly stable binding (Figure 6(A–C)). As shown in Figure 6(A), Cyp51 and astilbin formed hydrogen bonds with F139, L134, T136 and H257. As shown in Figure 6(B), Hsd17b7 and astilbin interacted with T140, L93, G94 and I14. As shown in Figure 6(C), Scd and astilbin form hydrogen bonds with N148, Y254, L82 and M79.

Astilbin significantly alleviated SNL-induced neuronal activities

Previously, our network pharmacology, transcriptomics and WGCNA data suggested that astilbin exerts antinociceptive effects by regulating neuronal metabolic pathways following neuropathic pain. Furthermore, we demonstrated that astilbin increases hemodynamic activation and metabolic perfusion in SNL-induced neuropathic pain, subsequently regulating neuronal activity (Figures 4 and 5). Since astilbin has the potential to regulate metabolic homeostasis induced by nerve injury, it is expected that neuronal activity will be favourably modulated after astilbin treatment. Electrophysiological recording was used to assess the neuronal activity. We recorded mEPSCs in the spinal gyrus of rats with neuropathic pain and sham rats. In the mEPSC recordings, both the frequency and amplitude were significantly higher in the SNL group than in the sham group following nerve injury. With the bath of 10–100 μM astilbin, the increase in amplitude and frequency of mEPSCs was decreased in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7(A–E), frequency: F(5, 62) = 71.41, p < 0.0001; amplitude: F(5, 62) = 24.23, p < 0.0001, the cumulative distribution analysis was performed using data from all attached neurons in each group). We observed no alterations in the frequency or amplitude of mEPSCs in sham rats following astilbin treatment (Figure 7(A–E)). In mIPSC recordings, a bath application of 100 μM astilbin did not significantly affect the frequency and amplitude after the application of d-AP5, CNQX and TTX in both the sham and SNL groups (Figure 7(F–J), frequency: F(3, 38) = 19.28, p < 0.0001; amplitude: F(3, 38) = 2.017, p = 0.1279). Overall, our results showed that astilbin alleviated spinal glutamatergic transmission in rats with SNL-induced neuropathy.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that astilbin treatment markedly regulates various cellular processes, including metabolic activity, calcium signalling and neural plasticity. The module associated with the antinociceptive effects of astilbin was correlated with metabolic processes, suggesting that astilbin may exert its effects by regulating energy homeostasis and mitochondrial capacity during pain states. Furthermore, astilbin was found to induce positive perfusion of blood, restore nerve injury-induced metabolic alterations by regulating Cyp51, Hsd17b7 and Scd activity in neuropathic rats, and significantly decrease nerve injury-induced glutamatergic transmission during neuropathic pain. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that astilbin may be involved in mitochondrial capacity and ATP supply by controlling synaptic glutamate release and vessel perfusion during neuropathic pain.

Blood flow guides oxygen and nutrients, as well as glucose, biologically active molecules, and other substances required for metabolism, and maintains the function of the organism by the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) of mitochondria. As the basis of the electrical activity of nerves, ATP production is the material basis for driving nerve activity. In this study, we demonstrated that astilbin effectively alleviated chronic neuropathic and formalin-induced pain in the second phase. Potential targets of astilbin were identified using network pharmacology, molecular docking, transcriptome sequencing and WGCNA. These data collectively annotated to metabolism-related pathways, may be involved in the antinociceptive effects. In addition, spinal tissue blood flow imaging indicated that astilbin significantly enhanced blood perfusion in neuropathic rats, suggesting that the astilbin-induced antinociceptive effects involve metabolic pathways and require energy.

Network pharmacology was used to screen the potential targets of astilbin-induced antinociceptive effects, and 42 pain-related targets were enriched, which were mainly annotated to energy metabolism-related signalling pathways, including AGE–RAGE, MAPK and sphingolipid signalling pathways (Figure 2(C)). Interactions between AGEs and their receptors (RAGE) play a crucial role in promoting inflammatory responses. In particular, the AGE–RAGE axis has potential as a biomarker for clinical endpoint prediction of neuropathic pain [29]. Further annotation revealed that these pathways were primarily enriched in axons, membranes of projection neurons, protein phosphatase binding and carbonate dehydratase activity. In the transcriptome sequencing analysis, we categorized the DEGs after astilbin intervention as metabolic pathways, biosynthesis, cytokine receptors and other signalling pathways (Figure 3(E–F)), which were also annotated in network pharmacology. We also verified the binding affinity and mode of interaction astilbin with Cyp51, Hsd17b7 and Scd. In addition, we correlated gene expression with antinociceptive behavioural phenotypes using WGCNA, and the results showed that the highest correlation was in the light-yellow module with KEGG-annotated signalling pathways, which were mainly dominated by metabolic pathways. These data suggested that astilbin functionally exerted anti-hypersensitivity effects by regulating metabolic pathways. In chronic pain, ROS are the metabolites of OXPHOS in the mitochondria. On the one hand, excessive mitochondrial fission leads to mitochondrial fragmentation, resulting in ROS production [30]. In contrast, mitochondrial activity is crucial for neuronal firing and behavioural phenotypes. Mitochondrial alterations are associated with muscle weakness, neurological deficits and visual disturbances [31]. Mutated dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) causes the mitochondria to collapse into perinuclear clusters containing highly interconnected networks. The deficiency of DRP1 leads to mitochondrial elongation and attachment to mitochondrial tubule attachment [32]. Mutations in the DRP1 gene or abnormal DRP1 expression are strongly associated with nociceptive hypersensitivity by interfering with mitochondrial dynamics, and DRP1 mutations lead to painful behaviours [33]. The elongated mitochondria gradually accumulate oxidative damage and transform from elongated tubules to large globules, leading to neurological disorders and pain [34]. Thus, mitochondrial homeostasis is critical for maintaining organismal sensation. In addition, using molecular docking techniques, we observed a region of astilbin binding at greater depth [35]. Validation experiments further demonstrated the binding of proteins, such as Scd, Hsd17b7 and Cyp51 with the astilbin in response to metabolic processes (Figure 5(E,F)). Collectively, our docking assessment revealed the interactions between astilbin and metabolism-related processes in neuropathic pain from docking assessment.

Energy homeostasis at the synaptic sites is the fundamental basis of neural activity. Single neuronal activity consumes more ATP and requires mitochondrial homeostasis during pain compared to naïve rats. We observed a robust increase in the intensity of blood flow in the spinal cord after astilbin injection in neuropathic rats, accompanied by perfusion of substances necessary for metabolic processes, which did not occur in sham rats (Figure 5(A–D)). Blood vessel perfusion guides the delivery of oxygen and nutrients and regulates endothelial cell migration and proliferation in response to mitochondria-controlled cellular metabolism [36]. As the core of the intracellular oxidative metabolism, mitochondria play an integral role in sensing inflammatory or otherwise hypoxic environments that guide gene transcription to ensure adequate nutrients and oxygen perfusion via angiogenic vascular extension [37]. Increased vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) stimulates the mobilization of mature endothelial cells in existing vessels and the maturation of circulating endothelial precursor cells (CEPs), which guide tissue perfusion and ATP production [38]. Thus, astilbin plays an essential role in the regulation of energy homeostasis. Because energy homeostasis promotes neural activity, whole-cell patch-clamp recordings demonstrated that astilbin inhibited SNL-induced glutamatergic neurotransmission after the reversal of energy homeostasis by inhibiting the frequency and amplitude of mEPSCs in a dose-dependent manner. The spinal cord is an important region for pain integration. Changes in the blood flow intensity and synaptic plasticity in this region were selectively observed. Collectively, these data suggest that blood vessels can be synergistically activated in response to synaptic energy demand.

As shown in Figure 4(D), a single intrathecal injection of astilbin increased the spinal blood flow intensity in SNL rats within 25–50 min, returning to baseline 50 min after injection. However, the analgesic effects of astilbin peaked 60 min after injection and persisted thereafter. This mismatch could be attributed to the complexity of the pharmacological actions involved. Although an increase in the spinal blood flow intensity likely reflects an immediate vascular response to the drug, the delayed onset and sustained analgesic effects suggest the involvement of more intricate biochemical and physiological processes. Furthermore, the differences between acute pain models, such as formalin-induced nociceptive responses, and chronic pain models, such as SNL-induced neuropathic pain, underscore the multifaceted nature of the effects of astilbin. These findings highlight the need for further research to elucidate the temporal dynamics and underlying mechanisms to optimize the therapeutic potential of astilbin in managing chronic pain condition.

Conclusions

Our results illustrated that, in the context of neuropathic pain, treatment with astilbin is essential for the activation of mitochondria-controlled cellular metabolism. Our data provided evidence that astilbin plays a remarkable antinociceptive role by enhancing vessel perfusion and supporting metabolic processes that are critical for neuronal activity in the spinal cord (Figure 8).

Schematic diagram displaying the role and mechanism of astilbin in neuropathic pain. Nerve injury resulted in spinal sensitization of glutamate release and alterations of neural plasticity, which were associated with behavioural hypersensitivity in neuropathic rats. Single intrathecal astilbin remarkably alleviated SNL and formalin-induced pain phenotypes. Upon application, astilbin induced spinal transcriptional and cellular alterations such as metabolic enzymes of Cyp51, Hsd17b7 and Scd, which participated in mitochondrial activity. Meanwhile, intrathecal astilbin promoted the vasodilating effect mediated by AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) in pathological states, enabling an increased substance supply at synaptic sites. This led to decrease spinal excitatory synaptic transmission and pain behaviours.
