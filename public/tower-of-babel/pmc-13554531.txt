Changes in Glomerular Filtration Rate in Transgender Individuals Receiving Hormone Therapy: A Longitudinal Study
Authors: Diana Carolina Alfonso Vergel, Oscar Mauricio Muñoz, Laura Viviana Galindo, Darío A. Parra Prieto, Paula Violeta Rodríguez Segura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe changes that occur over time in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of transgender individuals receiving hormone therapy, and the factors that influence these changes, have not yet been fully evaluated.MethodsThis is a longitudinal study based on a retrospective cohort of transgender people treated at Hospital Universitario San Ignacio in Bogotá, Colombia. Changes in eGFR (calculated using the CKD-EPI 2021 formula for both sexes) were analyzed over 36 months. The impact of age, BMI, previous gender affirmation surgery and hormone therapy was analyzed using a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model.Results166 transgender patients were included in the study (Median age 29 years, 94 women and 72 men). For transgender women, eGFR did not change over time; however, estrogen therapy was associated with a higher eGFR (+17.2 ml/min/1.73 m2, p<0.05). In transgender men, both CKD-EPI 2021 formulas showed a significant decrease in GFR over time. The GEE model showed a significant change in eGFR ranging from -0.63 to -0.79 per month (p < 0.05), depending on the formula used. A significant impact of age on eGFR was demonstrated in both groups.ConclusionThis study suggests that transgender women did not experience significant changes in eGFR over time. Estrogen use showed a possible positive modyfing effect on eGFR. In transgender men, an overall decrease in eGFR was evident over time. Our findings emphasize the need for periodic renal assessment in transgender individuals using the same evaluation formula, and highlight the need to optimize the available diagnostic tools.

Introduction

Globally, an estimated 25 million transgender individuals exist, 1 including over 1 million in the United States alone. 2 This population exhibits a higher prevalence of comorbidities, such as mental health disorders, cardiovascular disease, and complications related to hormone therapy, 3 highlighting the need for precise clinical assessment tools. 4

A critical challenge in their care is the evaluation of renal function. Conventional formulas for estimating glomerular filtration rate (GFR), such as CKD-EPI, incorporate sex assigned at birth as a key variable. 5 However, this approach may be inadequate for transgender individuals, as hormone therapy significantly alters body composition and serum creatinine levels. While studies in cisgender populations demonstrate that sex hormones influence chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression, 6 their impact on transgender individuals without CKD has not been evaluated.

This knowledge gap is particularly relevant because changes in muscle mass induced by hormones directly affect traditional renal biomarkers (e.g. creatinine). There are currently no guidelines on which sex variable (male or female) to use in eGFR equations for transgender patients. 6

Our study provides a longitudinal analysis of eGFR changes in a cohort of transgender individuals receiving hormone therapy at a Colombian referral center, and evaluates the factors associated with changes over time, considering male and women versions of the CKD-EPI 2021 equation.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a longitudinal study based in a retrospective cohort of people managed at the Transgender Clinic of the Hospital Universitario San Ignacio (Bogotá, Colombia), between January 2019 and June 2024. Inclusion criteria were transgender adults (>18 years), receiving gender-affirming hormone therapy, with at least 6 months of endocrinological follow-up. Non-binary individuals, those with pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD), HIV infection, active malignancies, current use of nephrotoxic medications (e.g., NSAIDs, aminoglycosides) and with comorbidities affecting renal function (diabetes mellitus, chronic uropathies, uncontrolled hypertension (≥140/90 mmHg) were excluded.

Clinical Protocol and Variables

Patients were followed quarterly during the first year, with hormonal titration to achieve therapeutic estradiol/testosterone levels according to the goals for gender affirmation therapy recommended by the Colombian Consensus on Transgender Therapy. 7 Additionally, they were accompanied by a multidisciplinary evaluation team (psychiatry, endocrinology, surgery).

For the present study, a standardised tool was created to systematically collect data from medical records, including anthropometric parameters (weight, height, body mass index BMI), serum creatinine, eGFR (CKD-EPI 2021), type of hormone therapy (transdermal/oral estrogens, injectable testosterone, antiandrogens), and surgical interventions. Renal function was assessed at the time of admission to the transgender clinic and at follow-up appointments performed at 6, 12, 24 and 36 months.

Statistical Analysis

As we decided to include data for the entire available population in the analysis, a sample size calculation was not performed.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median [IQR], according to normal distribution. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate the assumption of normal distribution of the data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%).

To estimate the trend over time of the estimated eGFR using CKD-EPI, a longitudinal analysis was performed using generalized estimating equation (GEE). This approach considered the autocorrelation of repeated observations within the same patient and allowed us to evaluate how the average of the response variables changed as a function of each of the covariates studied. We assumed the premise that no variations in glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) would occur during the 36-month follow-up period. A multivariable GEE was employed to identify the coefficients of each covariate (age, BMI, type of hormone used, previous gender affirmation surgery). The exchangeable correlation structure was utilised. The time model with a significant contribution (p-value <0.05) and the lowest quasi‐likelihood under the independence model criterion (QIC) represented the best fit for the data. Sensitivity analyses included unstructured and independent structures, with no significant changes of results. All analyses were performed in STATA® v.17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station. Texas, USA).

A statistically significant change in BMI was observed in transgender women after initiating hormone therapy (22.3 to 23 kg/m2, p<0.05). In transgender men there were no significant variations.

Of the participants, 60.5% of transgender women and 82.4% of transgender men achieved the recommended estradiol and testosterone levels (100–200 pg/ml and 50–55 ng/dl, respectively), as indicated by international guidelines. Some transgender women (22.2%) were not receiving estrogen therapy for various medical reasons, but were instead receiving antiandrogen therapy.

Transgender Women

Similarly, for the eGFR using the female variable, the average value went from 94 ml/min/1.73m2 to 93 ml/min/1.73m2, which was not significant (p=0.78). Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the change over time of the eGFR in a group of individuals seen at the transgender clinic of a referral hospital. We found that transgender women did not have significant changes in eGFR, while transgender men had an overall decrease in eGFR, although it was not possible to determine whether this finding was directly related to testosterone use or other confounders. The results were consistent regardless of whether male or women versions of the CKD-EPI 2021 equation were used.

Although the influence of hormone therapy on renal function is not clear, its impact on physical changes and body composition, especially muscle mass, is known.8,9 This directly affects creatinine production, thus altering eGFR. In our study, most transgender women were classified as normal weight (79%), as were half of the transgender men (50%). Weight changes during the follow-up period for transgender women was 3 kgs, and for transgender men was 1 kg, which were not clinically significant and do not seem to explain the variations in eGFR. These results contrast with population-based studies that found that 24% of transgender individuals were overweight and 18% obese before initiating hormone therapy. 10 Similar findings were reported in studies conducted in the Netherlands and the United States, where higher rates of obesity were observed in this population undergoing hormone therapy. 11 However, it is important to emphasize that weight and BMI alone cannot explain body composition, so it is not possible to identify by these methods whether the variation is due to fat gain or muscle mass gain.

On the other hand, it has been described in cisgender men that decrease testosterone levels is associated with adverse outcomes such as low muscle mass, frailty, arterial stiffness, and cardiovascular events in elderly, diabetic, obese, or liver disease populations. 12 However, in our study, no changes related to testosterone levels were observed in transgender men. We do not know whether this could be influenced by prior unrecorded testosterone use, which was not possible to determine because of clinical record limitations. This information is relevant, as it has been observed that testosterone use can increase muscle mass and decrease subcutaneous fat mass, leading to an increase in body mass index. 13 An increase in creatinine between 0.0005 and 0.001 mg/dL has been documented in transgender men, possibly associated with increased muscle mass. 14

In our study, the use of either of the two sex variables used for eGFR did not alter the general trends observed; and in both groups, a progressive decrease in eGFR was observed with increasing age, which is to be expected and coincides with what has been described in cisgender populations. However, in the same patient, eGFR could vary by more than 10 ml/min/1.73 m2 depending on the variable used (male or female), this is of clinical relevance since a difference of this magnitude classifies the patient within a different stage of renal disease; additionally, this change could even be interpreted in the same patient as progression of renal disease if the variable that will be used during the follow-up of renal function during hormone therapy is not clearly defined.

When the same formula is used over time, a clear trend is observed, in our study: in transgender men they evidenced a progressive decrease, while in transgender women eGFR tends to be stable. Our study does not allow us to determine which option is the most appropriate, so further research is required to answer this question.

In general, it has been suggested that eGFR calculation should consider the identification and gender expression of each patient. However, there is no definitive consensus. 15 The KDIGO 2024 guideline recommends calculating eGFR using the CKD-EPI 2021 equation without using the gender variable or using cystatin C as a biomarker, since this is not related to muscle mass. 16

Taking this into account, the problem may lie in the biomarker rather than the formula, given the correlation between creatinine and muscle mass. However, even when using cystatin C, Van Eeghen et al reported that in transgender women, cystatin C decreased by 0.069 mg/L (95 % CI, 0.049 to 0.089), corresponding to an increase of 7 ml/min/1.73 m2 in eGFR. In transgender men, however, cystatin C increased by 0.052 mg/L (95 % CI, 0.031 to 0.072), corresponding to a decrease of 6 ml/min/1.73 m2 in eGFR. Similarly, creatinine concentrations decreased by 0.065 mg/dL (95 % CI, -0.076 to -0.054) in transgender women and increased by 0.131 mg/dL (95 % CI, 0.119 to 0.142) in transgender men, data like those found in our study. 17 While these findings reinforce the potential usefulness of cystatin C as a sex-independent marker in these cases, they also indicate changes in eGFR towards decreased renal function in the male transgender population and improved renal function in the female transgender population. These changes are not correlated with body mass index or biomarkers.

Our results showed that transgender women do not present statistically significant variations in eGFR over time; however, when adjusting for estrogen use, a higher eGFR was observed. In relation to estrogens, some studies suggest that they could have a renoprotective effect due to the presence of receptors in mesangial, endothelial and epithelial cells of the kidney, which influence the synthesis and activity of cytokines, growth factors, vasoactive mediators and the regulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). 18 Despite the absence of comprehensive evidence from human studies, the analysis indicates that individuals who use estrogen exhibit a statistically significant increase in eGFR, irrespective of the variable utilized for eGFR calculation. This observation may be indicative of a potential modifying effect. Previous studies have also identified that the use of oral contraceptives with estrogens could be associated with microalbuminuria, macroalbuminuria and increased blood pressure. 19 However, the evaluation of these data within the context of our population was precluded, since only 10% of patients underwent a urinalysis during follow-up. Further studies are required to clarify this topic.

In transgender men, there was a statistically significant decrease in eGFR for the two calculations performed in the CKD-EPI 2021 formula. The relationship between this and the administration of testosterone as part of gender reassignment treatment is unclear. This is because our study lacks information on testosterone levels and their relationship with renal function. Previous studies in cisgender populations have shown that sex hormones, such as contraceptives, postmenopausal hormone therapy and testosterone replacement therapy, are implicated in the development and progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, the effects of these hormones on individuals receiving gender-affirming hormone therapy are unclear. 6 Additionally, it has been documented that men with CKD experience a faster decline in eGFR than women, potentially due to sex hormones. 20

Finally, our data showed a reduction in eGFR of approximately 0.7–0.9 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year, which is similar to the value observed in cisgender adults. In healthy cisgender adults, it has been reported that the normal glomerular filtration rate (GFR) declines by an average of 0.96–1.2 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year in women and men, respectively, after reaching its peak in young adulthood (around 35–40 years of age). 7

Some limitations must be recognized in the present study. Its retrospective design and the collection of data from medical records resulted in loss of information. Anthropometric data were limited to weight and BMI, without evaluating the body composition of transgender patients, which limits the analysis of the impact of hormone therapy and could represent a confounding factor in the results. About hormone therapy, it was not possible to establish the exact timing, nor to establish precise doses or duration to correlate with eGFR at follow-up. This is relevant since there is in vitro evidence of changes induced by supra-physiological levels of testosterone in relation to apoptosis of tubular proximal cells related to the Fas/Fas L pathways and activation of caspases. 21

Additionally, the evaluation of renal function in some cases was irregular, and there is very little data on albuminuria, considering also that creatinine and eGFR may not detect early changes in renal function. Future studies are needed, using different, earlier and more accurate biomarkers of renal injury in this context. Finally, due to the follow-up time, it is not possible to determine whether the findings are maintained beyond the three-year follow-up.

This study is the first Latin American investigation to evaluate renal function in the transgender population over time. The study used the CKD-EPI 2021 formula, taking into account biological sex and the gender that patients aspire to through hormone therapy. It is important to note that there is currently a lack of clarity on this matter, and the level of recommendation is limited.

Furthermore, patients undergoing hormone therapy for gender transition must be aware of the potential risks associated with this treatment, which have been shown to adversely affect renal function. Further studies are required to ascertain the most effective method of evaluating kidney function in this demographic, thereby facilitating a more precise, equitable and suitable assessment and offering opportunities for prevention and treatment.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that there were no substantial alterations in eGFR over time in the transgender women population. As indicated in the cisgender population, age was identified as a factor associated with the progressive decline in renal function. In transgender women, estrogen use exhibited a potential modifying effect on eGFR, as indicated by a statistically significant increase. Conversely, in transgender men, an overall decrease in eGFR was observed. However, it was not feasible to ascertain whether this finding is directly associated with testosterone use or other confounding factors. The findings underscore the significance of preserving uniformity in eGFR calculation, thereby advocating for the consistent utilization of the same sex in the CKD-EPI 2021 formula for each patient during clinical follow-up, until more robust evidence and specific tools for this population become available. The investigation further underscores the necessity to explore additional biomarkers, including cystatin C and NGAL (Neutrophil Gelatinase-Associated Lipocalin), which may facilitate a more precise evaluation of kidney function in transgender individuals.
