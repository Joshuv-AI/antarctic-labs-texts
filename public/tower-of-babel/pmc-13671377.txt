Managing and Preventing Placement‐Based Fatigue in Radiography and Allied Health Students: A Narrative Review
Authors: Yalindi Binduhewa, Vicki Braithwaite, Therese Gunn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClinical placement plays a key role in the education of allied health students by providing real‐world experiences required to develop professional competence and confidence. For radiography students, extensive unpaid clinical placements form a substantial component of professional training. Despite their educational value, clinical placements expose students to physical, emotional, and financial stressors that may contribute to fatigue. This narrative review aimed to investigate current and potential support systems, alternatives to placement, and healthy coping strategies available to radiography and allied health students to assist in the prevention and management of placement‐based fatigue. A structured literature search of PubMed, EMBASE, ScienceDirect, and CINAHL databases was undertaken, supplemented by grey literature searching. The limited literature supports that mindfulness and resilience workshops as well as exercise and therapeutic touch are potential interventions for managing placement‐based fatigue. These practices were found to decrease fatigue along with fatigue‐related symptoms such as stress and emotional exhaustion. The availability of appropriate financial aid systems was also identified as a solution to preventing placement‐based fatigue. Integrated simulated placement was additionally found to be a possible alternative to placement, but potentially difficult to implement. The limited evidence suggests that the management and prevention of placement‐based fatigue in allied health students have several potential interventions. However, more research needs to be conducted to study the effects of these interventions, particularly on radiography students and third‐ and fourth‐year allied health students to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of these interventions.

Introduction

Allied health degrees in Australia include mandatory Work Integrated Learning (WIL) as a core component of each course. WIL is often placement‐based and conducted in partnership with the universities' industry and clinical partners [1]. Although simulation activities such as online virtual reality programs, working with anthropomorphic phantoms, and role‐playing are all valuable modes of learning, clinical placement provides students with integral firsthand experience that cannot be acquired otherwise [2]. For radiography students, clinical placement facilitates the development of confidence in working with imaging equipment, communicating with patients, working as part of a multi‐disciplinary team, and understanding the workflow of medical imaging (MI) departments—all fundamental skills for a graduate radiographer [2].

Medical radiation practice programs in Australia are developed in accordance with the Medical Radiation Practice Accreditation Standards [3]. These standards are based on the professional capabilities developed by the Medical Radiation Practice Board of Australia (MRPBA), which outline “the minimum professional skills, knowledge, and attributes” required to safely practice as a medical radiation practitioner [4]. To meet the accreditation standards, placements are organised so that students gain experience in public and private settings, hospital and clinic settings, rural/regional settings, as well as in a variety of shift types, where practicable [3]. Placement blocks vary between 1 to 36 weeks in length and usually consist of 32 to 40‐h weeks, during which students are also required to complete academic assessments in the form of case studies, reflections, and competencies [2]. Placement and assessment are employed as evidence of meeting the professional capabilities. The international average of total clinical placement hours for diagnostic radiography programs is 1397 [5]. At some Australian universities, over 2000 h of placement are required, significantly more than the 1000 and 800 h required for social work and nursing degrees respectively [6]. Currently, most allied health placements are unpaid.

One of the biggest contributors to health student attrition is the inability to cope during placement due to physical, emotional, and financial stressors [7]. A survey of fourth year radiography students identified managing assessment, negative interactions with clinical staff, and adapting to high‐pressure environments as a few of the most commonly experienced placement‐based stressors [8]. Some other challenges include feeling unprepared and intimidated, fear of making mistakes, bullying, and even sexual harassment [9]. Financial stressors also play a huge role in the wellbeing of students, with a study on Australian nursing students revealing that 73% of the students found their placement stressful due to financial strain [10]. Grant‐Smith and de Zwaan's ACEN report on WIL wellbeing identified additional placement‐related financial stressors, including reduced employment income, loss of employment, increased debt, and reliance on support from others while undertaking placement [11]. Stress has been shown to have a positive correlation with fatigue [12], and likewise, a positive correlation between fatigue and sleep quality [13]. In a study by Abdali et al., 44.8% of the surveyed nursing students reported poor sleep quality, which is conceivable considering the variety of stressors students face while on placement [13]. Trying to manage these stressors, particularly with minimal assistance from educational institutions and clinical sites, leaves students with little time and energy for good nutrition, exercise, sleep, and socialisation, leading to increased stress, decreased sleep quality, and ultimately, fatigue [7].

The Queensland Health Fatigue Risk Management Systems (FRMS) implementation guideline defines fatigue as “a state of impaired physical and/or mental performance and lowered alertness resulting from imbalances of the resources required to perform the activity” [14]. The guideline states that in most healthcare‐related cases, fatigue is a result of a lack of quality sleep and long work hours [14]. Although the guideline was developed for the management of fatigue for qualified healthcare practitioners, the effects of fatigue are equally applicable to students on placement. Fatigue leads to “increases in error and accident rates” and “a decrease in cognitive function and task performance” [14], leading affected students to ironically put lives at risk whilst learning how to save them. For instance, a recent Australian study showed that 16% of surveyed nursing students admitted to having fallen asleep whilst driving to or from placement—a clear example of one of the more detrimental effects of fatigue [15]. Fatigue may also precipitate unhealthy coping and drive attrition from allied health pathways at a time of workforce shortage [14].

Studies examining fatigue in qualified healthcare practitioners have contributed to the development of workplace frameworks and fatigue‐management policies. However, evidence relating specifically to students undertaking WIL placements remains limited. While placement‐related stress, burnout, financial hardship, and wellbeing have been explored across a range of health disciplines, little attention has been given to interventions that may prevent or manage fatigue among radiography students. Existing approaches described in the literature include individual coping strategies, wellbeing interventions, placement model innovations, and financial support mechanisms. To date, no narrative review has synthesised evidence relating to fatigue‐management interventions, placement alternatives, and support mechanisms relevant to radiography students within the Australian WIL context. Understanding these approaches is important for supporting student wellbeing while maintaining educational quality and patient safety.

The aim of this narrative review was to investigate the current and potential support systems, alternatives to placement, and healthy coping strategies available for radiography students to help manage and prevent placement‐based fatigue.

Method

A structured literature search of databases including PubMed, EMBASE, ScienceDirect, and CINAHL was conducted to acquire appropriate literature for this narrative review. Search terms relating to placement, fatigue, management, student, radiography, and allied health were combined using Boolean operators to find literature on current and potential support systems, alternatives to placement, and healthy coping strategies available for radiography and other allied health students to help manage and prevent placement‐based fatigue. The search covered literature from 2016 to 2026 to reflect the last decade of WIL practice. Further grey literature was identified through snowballing. Abstracts were read to determine the appropriateness of the studies resulting from the searches. Ethics approval is not required for this research project according to the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research [16].

Review Design

A narrative review methodology was selected because the available literature was heterogeneous with respect to study design, participant populations, interventions, and outcome measures. Preliminary searching indicated insufficient methodological consistency to support systematic review or meta‐analysis approaches. A narrative review therefore provided an appropriate method for exploring and synthesising evidence relating to fatigue‐management interventions, placement alternatives, and support mechanisms relevant to radiography students and allied health learners.

Screening and Selection

Records identified through database searching were screened by the primary author through title and abstract review. Full‐text articles were subsequently assessed against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Duplicate records were removed prior to screening. Given the limited radiography‐specific evidence base, relevant allied health literature was included where findings were considered transferable to placement‐based fatigue experiences.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

Data extracted from each study included study design, participant characteristics, intervention type, outcome measures, and key findings. Findings were synthesised narratively and organised into thematic categories according to intervention type.

Quality Appraisal

Consistent with narrative review methodology, a formal risk‐of‐bias assessment was not undertaken. Instead, studies were critically discussed according to design, sample size, applicability to radiography students, and methodological limitations.

Results and Discussion

After screening for appropriateness and relevance, the search results yielded eight studies in total. The studies consisted of five randomised controlled trials (RCTs), one mixed‐methods pilot, one pre/post pilot, and one scoping review. Four themes structured the synthesis: (i) mindfulness and resilience workshops; (ii) exercise and therapeutic touch; (iii) integrated simulated placement; and (iv) financial aid. It is important to note that most identified studies measured fatigue‐adjacent outcomes, including stress, emotional exhaustion, burnout, and daytime sleepiness, rather than fatigue directly. While this represents a limitation of the evidence base, these outcomes are widely recognised as antecedents, manifestations, or correlates of fatigue. Consequently, interventions that reduce these factors may reasonably contribute to fatigue management even when fatigue itself is not directly measured. This distinction should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review.

Mindfulness and Resilience Workshops

Mindfulness is a form of self‐regulation that involves being aware of how one feels in the present moment without judgement [17]. It is often practised by focusing on a stimulus, such as breathing patterns, guided imagery, or even slow, intentional movement [18]. Theoretically, the ability to be mindful is thought to improve resilience as it reduces anxious overthinking and can allow one to recover from stressful events faster, therefore decreasing the chances of developing chronic stress, fatigue, and burnout [18]. Considering the role that placement‐based anxiety and stressful situations play in contributing to fatigue in students, knowing how to practice mindfulness can be a key tool in preventing placement‐based fatigue.

A mixed‐methods pilot study conducted by Kinser et al. on (n = 27) healthcare professionals and students explored the relationship between mindfulness and mindful movement on perceived stress, emotional exhaustion, and burnout [18]. All participants took part in workshops which consisted of mindfulness‐based discussions, worksheets, use of online resources and mobile apps, and yoga [18]. The stress levels of the participants, measured by the Perceived Stress Scale‐10, out of a range 0–40, decreased from a mean (and standard deviation) of 23.0 (5.5) to 20.8 (3.8) over the eight‐week intervention [18]. Similarly, using the Maslach Burnout Inventory‐Human Services (MBI‐HS), the exhaustion level decreased from 22.5 (11.9) to 16.9 (9.7) out of a range of 0–54. The MBI‐HS scale was also used to measure burnout levels of participants, which decreased from 6.3 (5.3) to 4.3 (4.2) out of a range of 0–30 (Table 2) [18]. These differences were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05) in terms of stress (p = 0.0372), emotional exhaustion (p = 0.0023), and burnout (p = 0.0106) [18]. The qualitative data collected in this study revealed that participants “expressed great appreciation for having learned practical tools” such as the breathing and yoga exercises and “reported plans to use specific techniques in the future” [18]. These results do not differentiate between qualified (n = 15) and student (n = 12) perceptions. As previously established, qualified healthcare practitioners face different sources and levels of stress and fatigue than students. Therefore, collective results of the two groups may not be representative of the effects of the intervention on students. Despite this, the study does overall suggest that mindfulness practices can be of significant value in reducing allied health students' stress, emotional exhaustion, and burnout, and therefore an effective form of preventing placement‐based fatigue.

Another study conducted by Clarkson et al. compared several psychological measures on (n = 14) radiation therapy students completing a modified five‐week version of the Living Mindfully Mindfulness‐Based Stress Reduction programme [17]. This renowned programme was developed by professor of medicine emeritus Jon Kabat‐Zinn in 1979 and consists of a structured course that combines mindfulness practices and neuroscience to help individuals cultivate emotional, psychological, and physical resilience [20]. Relevant to this narrative review was the participants' emotional exhaustion scores, which were measured by the MBI‐ Student Survey scale at baseline, 5 weeks, 3 months, and 12 months [17]. The control group's baseline mean emotional exhaustion score was 15.1 (7.99) out of a possible 30, which measured 14.3 (9.07) at 5 weeks, 15.1 (8.10) at 3 months, and then dropped to 10.0 (6.40) at 12 months [17]. The intervention group's baseline mean was 9.8 (4.52), then measured 10.37 (3.99), 8.25 (4.70), 6.4 (2.61) at each respective collection point [17]. Furthermore, the percentage of participants with high exhaustion in the intervention group began at 12.5% and remained consistent at 5 weeks and 3 months, until dropping to 0% at 12 months [17]. Interestingly, the control group started much higher at 50%, dropped to 33.3% at 5 weeks, returned to 50% at 3 months, then dropped again to 40% [17]. Possible explanations for these differences could be differing periods of clinical placement and assessment between the 2 year groups included in the study, as well as the lower quality of the study in terms of its pre/post pilot study design, the small sample size (n = 14), and the use of convenience sampling. Unlike Kinser et al. [18], despite the overall decrease in emotional exhaustion in the intervention group, the sample size of the study meant that statistical significance could not be established.

Finally, a RCT conducted by Lanz [19] explored the impacts of a 5‐week resilience intervention on several outcomes including exhaustion in (n = 27) first year nursing students. Resilience is the ability to endure and bounce back from difficult or negative experiences—an important trait to possess for allied health students who must deal with many placement‐based stressors [19]. Although students may have varying levels of resilience initially, it is a trait that can be built upon through proper education, training and exposure [19]. The intervention consisted of workshops that included a variety of modules—the most popular being on burnout, compassion fatigue, and time management [19]. Exhaustion levels, measured out of 25, by the MBI‐General Survey scale, decreased in the intervention group from a mean of 21.09 (4.85) to 17.27 (5.78) and in the control group from 23.55 (7.80) to 21.36 (6.77) (Table 2) [19]. The study found a significant decrease in exhaustion for the intervention group (p = 0.0500), but not for the control group (p = 0.1700) [19]. This suggests that the resilience intervention did have a positive impact on participants' exhaustion levels.

Across these studies, mindfulness and resilience were associated with reductions in stress, exhaustion, and burnout‐ factors that all contribute to fatigue. These studies have been conducted on first‐ and second‐year students, potentially due to first‐year students spending more time on campus and being less likely to have scheduling conflicts with the workshops and other activities, as noted by Lanz [19]. However, it is important to note that the heaviest placement demands occur in the third and fourth years, where higher workload and shift variations occur. Therefore, first‐ and second‐year students may not be the most indicative of true stress, exhaustion, or burnout levels.

Exercise and Therapeutic Touch

Physical exercise has a variety of benefits on physical and mental health and is a well‐known method of stress relief [21]. Exercising increases blood flow and therefore oxygen to the brain, promoting energy production that lowers stress and fatigue [21]. Progressive Muscle Relaxation Exercise (PMRE) is a form of exercise that involves guided voluntary tensing and relaxing of muscle groups, which decreases sympathetic nervous activity that arises when stressed [21]. Therefore, PMRE is thought to reduce stress levels and consequently reduce burnout.

An RCT conducted by Pelit‐Aksu et al. [21] explored how PMRE affected both stress and burnout levels in (n = 145) final‐year student nurse interns. Participants were provided with a 30‐min audio with guided instructions on how to conduct the PMRE exercises, to complete at least four times per week for 3 weeks. The intervention group's burnout scores, measured by the Burnout Measure Short version scale where high burnout scores were to a maximum of 7, decreased from a mean (and standard deviation) of 3.64 (0.97) to 2.51 (0.93), and the control group's scores also decreased from 3.32 (5.3) to 3.02 (1.17) (Table 3) [21]. Interestingly, both the intervention and control groups were found to have statistically significant differences between their pretest and post‐test scores (p = 0.0010). There was also a statistical difference between the post‐test scores of the intervention and control groups (p = 0.010), despite there being no statistical difference between the pre‐test scores [21]. This suggests that the intervention did have an effect on the burnout levels of participants, and that, even in the short‐term, PMRE and other forms of exercise would be an effective method of fatigue reduction for allied health students.

Unlike exercise, therapeutic touch is a less known method of reducing symptoms such as stress, fatigue, anxiety, and pain [22]. Therapeutic touch is a holistic, alternative medicine practice that involves assessing and rebalancing any disruptions or clogging of the body's energy fields through rhythmic and intentional movement of a therapist's hands over the receiver's body [22]. A RCT conducted by Vural Dogru et al. [22] explored the effect of 20‐min therapeutic touch sessions twice a week for 4 weeks on (n = 96) nursing and midwifery students. Additional to the intervention and control groups, this study also included a ‘sham‐intervention’ group, in which the therapist's hands moved across the participants' bodies in no specific sequence.

Fatigue, measured by the Visual Analogue Scale for Fatigue on a scale of 0–10, significantly decreased (p < 0.001) from pre to post‐test in the intervention group from a median of 6.68 (4.00 ± 10.00) to 3.78 (2.00 ± 7.00). Conversely, the fatigue scores of both the sham intervention and control groups increased from 6.81 (4.00 ± 10.00) to 6.93 (4.00 ± 10.00) and 6.12 (3.00 ± 10.00) to 7.06 (5.00 ± 10.00) respectively (Table 3) [22]. Additionally, the differences in post‐test scores between the intervention and sham intervention groups (p = 0.0010) and the intervention and control groups (p = 0.0010) were found to be significant [22]. Measured by the Perceived Stress Scale, stress levels had a significant decrease (p < 0.001) from a mean (standard deviation) of 22.00 (2.59) from a possible 40, to 18.81 (3.46). Once again, the stress level of the sham intervention and control groups increased from 22.59 (3.56) to 22.62 (2.75) and 22.96 (3.30) to 24.09 (4.21) respectively [22]. Finally, the median daytime sleepiness levels, measured by the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (range 0–24) decreased in all three experimental groups. The difference of the intervention group was statistically significant (p < 0.001) from 14.21 (11.00 ± 20.00) to 7.18 (2.00 ± 15.00). In the sham intervention and control groups, daytime sleepiness levels decreased from 14.06 (11.00 ± 19.00) to 12.68 (4.00 ± 24.00) and 13.93 (11.00 ± 22.00) to 12.53 (4.00 ± 24.00) respectively (Table 3). Neither the sham intervention nor control group had a statistically significant decrease (p = 0.270 and p = 0.253 respectively) [22].

With study powers of over 80%, Pelit‐Aksu et al. and Dogru et al. both suggest that their respective interventions could be effective in reducing fatigue or its associated symptoms such as burnout, perceived stress, and daytime sleepiness. However, as there is only one study on each intervention, it is difficult to say with much certainty whether such practices are truly effective.

Integrated Simulated Placement

MRPBA accreditation standards require education providers to ensure that the “quality, quantity, duration and diversity” of placement experiences are able to meet graduate capabilities [3]. Given the high placement load required of Australian radiography students relative to other allied health degrees, the use of simulated placement (SP), which can include role play, virtual reality (VR), digital simulation, and interprofessional simulation, is one of the potential alternatives to traditional placement (TP) [23]. As SP can be completed at university or remotely in a student's own time, partially substituting SP from TP could ease travel, accommodation, and rostering burdens that contribute to fatigue.

This concept was explored by a RCT conducted by Ketterer et al. [23] on (n = 29) first year radiation therapy students who underwent 2 weeks of either SP or TP. The SP involved working through case studies, Virtual Environment for Radiotherapy Training (VERT), and role play scenarios [23]. The two groups then completed a formative assessment to assess their safe practice, technical, and communication skills. The SP group had a higher mean score for safe practice of 65 (16) compared to the TP group's mean score of 62 (14). Contrarily, for technical skills, the TP group's mean score was 74 (14), compared to the SP group's lower score of 68 (16). However, for both safe practice and technical skills scores, there was no statistical significance between the two groups (p = 0.2930 and p = 0.1680 respectively). The mean communication score of the SP group was 10% higher at 88 (18) than the 76 (12) score of the TP group (Table 4). Again, there was no statistical significance between the scores of the two groups, suggesting that SP could be a feasible partial replacement for TP.

A similar RCT by Imms et al. [24] on (n = 570) first‐ and second‐year occupational therapy students had comparable findings. The intervention period was 1 week (40 h) and for the SP group consisted of case studies and scenarios and role playing [24]. After the placement, students completed a written examination that consisted of a series of questions regarding a scenario‐based clinical report. The mean examination scores for the SP and TP group were 71.34 (10.30) and 69.38 (10.18) respectively, which were not found to have a statistically significant difference (p = 0.0870) (Table 4) [24]. Overall, these studies suggest that partial replacement of TP by SP with no significant effect on students' knowledge and understanding could be feasible. However, the intervention periods in both studies were short (1–2 weeks) in comparison to the total number of hours of clinical placement (approximately 2000) required in radiography programs. Although both studies suggest SP may be able to replace 1–2 weeks of TP, it is difficult to say whether longer periods of TP can be replaced with SP without detriment to students' knowledge and skills. Furthermore, both Ketterer et al. and Imms et al.'s studies were conducted on first‐ and second‐year students, for whom the aims and responsibilities of TP are much less than third‐ and fourth‐year students, and therefore the results would not be necessarily transferrable. Additionally, Ketterer et al. [23] did note that planning, organising, and funding of a diverse range of engaging and high‐quality SP activities equivalent to clinical placement was a main challenge of the study. This could deter educational institutions from initiating SP programs, especially as there is such little evidence on the effectiveness of SP for allied health students.

Interestingly, a more recent scoping review [25] of simulation‐based education for MI students found that while SP is an effective complementary learning tool for MI students, it may not be a replacement for TP. Although SP was deemed useful in that it provides students with a safe environment to make mistakes and learn, the review noted that there can be difficulties for educational staff with planning effective SP activities and for students with technical issues and confusing software [25]. Furthermore, the review noted that there was a lack of literature on the financial burden of implementing SP activities, such as for buying the VR software or hiring the actors for role‐play activities [25]. Ultimately, if integrated correctly, SP could partially replace TP, which would not only reduce the challenges of managing placement‐based fatigue for students but also lessen the burden on clinical departments. However, further research is required regarding this intervention to truly assess the extent to which SP may be able to replace TP.

The Role of Financial Aid

Financial hardship has emerged as an important contributor to placement‐related stress and fatigue among allied health students. Morley et al. [6] reported that 66% of social work students experienced financial strain associated with placement commitments and that 41% reduced or ceased paid employment during placement. While a variety of bursaries, scholarships, grants, and allowances are available, evidence evaluating the direct impact of financial support on fatigue outcomes remains limited. Nevertheless, the relationship between financial stress, wellbeing, sleep quality, and fatigue suggests that reducing economic burden may be an important component of fatigue prevention strategies.

The Australian Society of Medical Imaging and Radiation Therapy (ASMIRT), through the Rural Clinical Placement Scheme, offers a $1000 grant biannually to each university provider of undergraduate Medical Radiation Science programs in MI and radiation therapy (RT) [26]. Considering that most placements are around 7 weeks long, this would mean that the very few students who receive this grant would have less than $150 to live on each week, not nearly enough to cover food, rent, and transport expenses, particularly in a cost of living crisis. Further, this grant comes with obligations with recipients of this scholarship being required to provide a report suitable for publication to ASMIRT as well as participate in surveys and studies regarding their rural placement experience [26].

Recently, ASMIRT began offering 100 Student Placement Support Grants of $500 annually to final year MI, RT, and Nuclear Medicine students who are also student members of ASMIRT. For students, membership with ASMIRT is free, but they can only receive the grant once [27].

Moreover, the Australian Collaborative Education Network (ACEN) and the National Association of Field Experience Administrators (NAFEA) offer rural placement scholarships of up to $1500 [28, 29]. With both scholarships, students must be from universities that are institutional members of the respective organisation and must be completing full‐time placement. To be eligible to receive the NAFEA scholarship, students must be relocating to a location at least 100 km from their usual place of residence, cannot be a previous recipient of the scholarship, and similar to the ASMIRT scholarship, must agree to provide a report to NAFEA for publication on their rural placement experience and participate in surveys and studies [29]. With the ACEN scholarship, students must be relocating to a location at least 60 km from their usual place of residence; however, there is no requirement for participation in surveys, studies or written reports [28].

Additionally, Queensland Health also offers a few allowances and bursaries. Two of note are the Southern Queensland Rural Health bursary through the Rural Immersion Placement Program and the Nursing and Midwifery Regional, Rural and Remote Student Placement allowance. The latter is a $5000 one‐off allowance for final‐year nursing and midwifery students completing regional, rural or remote placement and the former provides $200 per week for up to 10 weeks during placement for allied health students [30, 31]. Like the ASMIRT grant, the Southern Queensland Rural Health bursary is not enough to cover all living expenses. Despite differences in monetary value, application criteria, and other requirements of these financial aid systems, their commonality is that they are only available for students attending rural placement except for the new $500 ASMIRT grant for final year students.

There are other Centrelink‐based government financial support systems, such as Austudy and Youth Allowance, which are not exclusive to allied health students but are more widely accessible. The current maximum fortnightly payment for these schemes is $677.20 for a student without children ($854.20 with children), better than the ASMIRT and QH payments, but still not ideal [32]. The Australian government recently announced the introduction of the Commonwealth Prac Payment of $338.60 per week specifically for students on placement from July 2025 [33]. However, radiography students have not been classed as eligible for receiving this payment, despite their required placement hours far exceeding those of other allied health degrees. The inadequacy of financial aid systems, exacerbated by the current cost of living crisis in Australia, forces radiography and other allied health students to complete paid work on top of full‐time placement with insufficient inter‐shift recovery time—a key exacerbator of fatigue [7].

Limitations and Future Research

The primary strength of this narrative review is its design, as a narrative review allows for a general overview of the topic and identification of opportunities for further research. The primary limitation of this narrative review is the lack of studies available for analysis, especially quantitative, high‐quality studies on Australian radiography students. Studies conducted on interventions for other allied health students and/or radiography students in other countries are useful but have limited applicability to Australian radiography students. Additionally, although all appraised articles were from within the last 9 years, a major exacerbator of stress and fatigue is the recent cost of living crisis, which has only been prevalent for about 2 years, meaning most of the analysed studies do not account for this. In future, it may be beneficial to conduct further studies on radiography students, particularly third‐ and fourth‐year students to assess their unique sources of placement‐based fatigue and to trial different interventions. Furthermore, most studies did not measure fatigue; rather, fatigue‐adjacent symptoms such as stress, exhaustion and burnout. Therefore, it was difficult to compare the effectiveness of different interventions against each other. An additional limitation of the available evidence is that most studies examined fatigue‐adjacent outcomes rather than fatigue itself. Consequently, conclusions regarding intervention effectiveness should be interpreted cautiously. Future research should incorporate validated fatigue measures alongside indicators such as burnout, stress, emotional exhaustion, and sleep quality to better understand intervention effects on fatigue directly. Ultimately, the management of the wellbeing of students on placement is a collaboration between the students themselves, the education providers, and the clinical partners. Education providers and clinical partners should create environments where students feel comfortable to voice their needs and concerns, and then take action to address them, particularly when it comes to preventing and managing placement‐based fatigue.

Conclusion

The prevention and management of placement‐based fatigue among allied health students remains an under‐researched area, particularly within radiography education. This narrative review examined evidence relating to mindfulness and resilience workshops, exercise‐based interventions, therapeutic touch, integrated simulated placement, and financial support mechanisms as strategies to reduce placement‐related fatigue and its associated consequences.

The available evidence suggests that mindfulness and resilience interventions, progressive muscle relaxation, and therapeutic touch may reduce fatigue‐related outcomes including stress, burnout, emotional exhaustion, and daytime sleepiness. Similarly, simulated placement appears capable of supporting learning outcomes while potentially reducing some demands associated with traditional placement. However, the evidence base remains limited, with relatively few studies identified and most conducted among first‐ and second‐year allied health students rather than the later‐year students who experience the greatest placement burden. Importantly, most studies measured fatigue‐adjacent outcomes rather than fatigue directly. While these findings provide useful insight into potential approaches for fatigue management, further research is required to establish the effectiveness of interventions using validated fatigue measures and radiography‐specific populations.

Financial hardship emerged as a recurring contributor to placement‐related stress and fatigue. Although direct evidence examining the impact of financial aid interventions on fatigue outcomes remains limited, reducing placement‐related financial burden may represent an important avenue for supporting student wellbeing. Future research should investigate whether emerging initiatives, including placement support grants and practicum payment schemes, improve fatigue, wellbeing, educational outcomes, and placement participation among radiography students.

Overall, the findings suggest that managing placement‐based fatigue requires a multifaceted approach involving students, education providers, clinical partners, professional organisations, and policy makers. Supporting students through placement is likely to improve wellbeing, educational engagement, and workforce sustainability, while helping to ensure that clinical placements remain safe, effective, and educationally valuable learning experiences.
