Fast Online Distributionally Robust Optimization via Data Compression
Irina Wang, Bartolomeo Stellato

Abstract
We propose an online data compression approach for efficiently solving Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization (DRO) problems with streaming data. Our method constructs adaptive ambiguity sets around a compressed distribution obtained by online clustering, so the problem size stays fixed as data accumulate. We construct a dynamic regret bound with respect to a one-step-ahead non-compressed DRO oracle, and establish online clustering conditions such that, with high probability, the regret converges sublinearly to a clustering-discrepancy-based performance gap. This gap is defined in terms of the discrepancies between the true and compressed distributions, so that by varying the number of clusters, our method trades off robustness against computational effort. We additionally provide fast subgradient-based updates that replace direct solutions of both the full and compressed problems, and extend the regret analysis to this setting. Numerical experiments on portfolio optimization and sparse support vector machine problems, including mixed-integer formulations, show over an order of magnitude reduction in cumulative computation time, even compared to non-robust methods, with minimal loss in solution quality.



1
Introduction

Many decision-making problems in engineering, operations research, and computer science involve solving optimization problems affected by uncertain parameters, and

distributionally robust optimization (DRO)
has emerged as a disciplined framework to robustify the decision-making process against distributional misspecification
(
Rahimian and Mehrotra, 2022
;
Kuhn et al., 2025
)
.
Instead of assuming a known distribution,
DRO
considers an
ambiguity set
of possible distributions and optimizes for the worst-case expected cost under all distributions within this set.
This approach offers out-of-sample performance guarantees with a finite number of data samples, overcoming the
optimizer’s curse
of
sample average approximation (SAA)
.

Since its appearance, significant effort has been devoted to constructing ambiguity sets primarily in static settings, using either structural assumptions on the distribution (
e.g.
, moments
(
Scarf, 1958
;
Grünwald and Dawid, 2004
)
) or training data directly
(
Kuhn et al., 2019
;
Gao and Kleywegt, 2023
)
.
However, many real-time settings, such as healthcare resource allocation, air traffic control, and energy trading, are dynamic and new data becomes available sequentially over time.
Despite its potential,
DRO
remains underexplored in such scenarios.
Most
DRO
approaches construct a static ambiguity set, and model time progression through
adjustable wait-and-see variables

(
Chen et al., 2007
;
Bertsimas et al., 2019
;
Bertsimas and den Hertog, 2022
)
.
There are limited works focusing on how to adapt the ambiguity sets dynamically over time, and each targets a specific problem class: distributions supported on a finite set of scenarios
(
Aigner et al., 2023
)
, continuous variables with locally strongly concave objectives
(
Li and Martínez, 2021
)
, or continuous variables with first-order updates tailored to Wasserstein-1 sets and losses with strictly sublinear growth in the uncertainty
(
Chen et al., 2026
)
.
In particular, streaming data settings can be challenging for the popular Wasserstein-based
DRO

(
Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn, 2018
;
Kuhn et al., 2019
;
Gao and Kleywegt, 2023
)
, where the ambiguity set is defined as a Wasserstein ball around the empirical distribution of a dataset.
As new datapoints arrive, the problem dimension grows, increasing computational complexity
(
Kuhn et al., 2019
)
.
While a larger dataset improves confidence in the empirical measure as an approximation of the true distribution, the reduction in the ambiguity set’s radius is not enough to offset this complexity; for mixed-integer problems, even
SAA
is difficult to solve with large amounts of data, without any added complexity from ambiguity. This leads to computational bottlenecks that are especially limiting for real-time applications.

1.1
Our contributions

We propose an online data compression approach for efficiently solving Wasserstein
DRO
problems with streaming data.
Our key contributions are:

•

Online
DRO
framework with data compression:

We develop a generic framework for solving Wasserstein
DRO
problems with streaming data, where
adaptive
ambiguity sets are formulated as gradually shrinking Wasserstein balls, of any order, around a clustered empirical distribution.
Since the compressed reference distribution is supported on at most a fixed number of clusters, the problem size does not grow with the data stream. At every time step, the decision-maker can either solve the compressed problem directly, or apply fast approximate subgradient updates.
We show that our method is compatible with general clustering procedures, and provide examples fast and memory-efficient online clustering algorithms.

•

Online regret analysis:

We construct a dynamic regret bound comparing our online solutions to a one-step-ahead nominal
DRO
oracle,
i.e.
, with access to the subsequent realization of the uncertainty.
We show that, with high probability, the regret converges sublinearly to a function of the discrepancy between the true and compressed distributions.
This quantifies the impact of data compression, and highlights the
trade-off between computational effort and optimality.

•

Fast subgradient subroutines:

For maximum-of-affine losses, we decompose the worst-case expectation into per-cluster oracles with closed-form or efficiently computable solutions, under various Wasserstein orders, ground norms, and supports, together with per-iteration complexity and tolerance guarantees.
These subroutines replace direct solves of the compressed and full
DRO
problems, and we extend the dynamic regret analysis to this setting.

•

Computational gains in numerical experiments:

We demonstrate the efficiency of our approach on portfolio optimization and sparse (cardinality-constrained) support vector machine problems with streaming data.
Our results show significant computational savings, even compared to
SAA
, with minimal loss in solution quality.

1.2
Related work

Distributionally robust optimization

DRO
has been extensively explored in recent years, with successful applications in machine learning
(
Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al., 2015
)
, among many other areas.
We focus on discrepancy-based ambiguity sets, defined as a ball around a data-driven nominal distribution, where the distance, expressed
e.g.
via the
\phi
-divergence
(
Bayraksan and Love, 2015
)
or the Wasserstein distance
(
Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn, 2018
;
Kannan et al., 2024
)
, signifies the trust in the data at hand.
Due to its expressivity and favorable statistical properties, we focus on Wasserstein
DRO
.

Online learning and
DRO

Online learning provides algorithms for solving repetitive problems over time, and has recently been applied to robust optimization
(
Ho-Nguyen and Kılınç-Karzan, 2018
;
Chen et al., 2017
)
and to
DRO
:
Namkoong and Duchi (2016)
and
Qi et al. (2021)
propose online stochastic methods for
\phi
-divergence ambiguity sets or KL-divergence regularization.
We focus here on a different problem, where the same
DRO
problem is solved repeatedly over time with growing knowledge of the uncertainty distribution; our work is thus closest to the few approaches that adapt the ambiguity set to the samples collected progressively.

Aigner et al. (2023)
update the ambiguity set through projected gradient descent, but consider distributions supported on a fixed and finite set of scenarios, a setting in which the problem size does not grow with the data stream.

Li and Martínez (2021)
incorporate streaming data through assimilation, but require continuous variables and strongly concave objectives.
Most recently,
Chen et al. (2026)
solve the online
DRO
problem through first-order updates, with efficient subroutines for losses with strictly sublinear growth in the uncertainty, for Wasserstein order one; this is similar in spirit to our subgradient approach, which instead covers max-of-affine losses for any order, so the two works are complementary.
Unlike these purely first-order approaches, our framework also supports direct solves, and in particular mixed-integer formulations, which are our main computational focus.

Scenario reduction and
DRO

Since the size and computational complexity of data-driven optimization problems generally increase with the number of samples, scenario reduction techniques have been developed to reduce the number of scenarios while retaining an accurate representation of the underlying uncertainty.
Common approaches minimize the distance between the reduced and the original distribution
(
Dupačová et al., 2003
)
, or aggregate scenarios via clustering
(
Hewitt et al., 2022
)
, moment matching
(
Mehrotra and Papp, 2013
)
, objective approximation
(
Zenios and Shtilman, 1993
)
, nested distances
(
Horejšová et al., 2020
)
, and, more recently, decision-focused metrics that involve the loss function itself
(
Bertsimas and Mundru, 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2023
)
.
Our previous work
(
Wang et al., 2025
)
uses clustering in static, finite-sample
DRO
problems as a tool to bridge robust and distributionally robust optimization.
Closely related,
Aigner et al. (2025)
embed scenario reduction into a
DRO
framework with suboptimality bounds, albeit limited to monotonically homogeneous uncertain objectives with strictly positive uncertainty; both works, however, assume data are available a priori, while we focus on
online
scenario reduction for
DRO
problems with streaming data.

Clustering with streaming data

Data stream clustering methods can be broadly classified into partition-based algorithms, which group data using distance-based similarity metrics, density-based algorithms such as DenStream
(
Cao et al., 2006
)
, and hierarchical algorithms
(
Murtagh and Contreras, 2012
)
; see the survey
(
Carnein and Trautmann, 2019
)
for details.
Among the first class, the most popular ones are incremental
k
-means
(
Aaron et al., 2014
)
and CluStream
(
Aggarwal et al., 2003
)
, which we adapt to fit our framework, although our approach can incorporate any online clustering algorithm, with various memory and run-time trade-offs.

2
The online stochastic problem

We consider a
stochastic optimization (SO)
problem of the form

H_{\star}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}[f(u,x)],

(1)

where
x\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{n}
is the decision variable,
\mathcal{X}
a compact set,
u\in S\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{d}
the vector of uncertain parameters that is governed by some probability distribution
\mathbf{P}
, and
H_{\star}
the optimal objective value.
We assume that the support
S
of
{\mathbf{P}}
lives within the domain of
f
for the variable
u
,
i.e.
,
S\subseteq\mathop{\bf dom}_{u}{f}
, and is closed and convex.
The function
f:\mathcal{X}\times S\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
is assumed to be of the form
f(u,x)=\max_{j\leq J}f_{j}(u,x)
, with each
f_{j}
being proper, concave, and upper-semicontinuous in
u
for all
x
, and convex in
x
.
We make the following two regularity assumptions on
f_{j}
, where the existence of global constants independent of
x
is implied by the compactness of
\mathcal{X}
.

Assumption 2.1
.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the functions
f_{j}
are Lipschitz continuous in
u
with constants
L_{j}
, i.e.,
\left|f_{j}(v,x)-f_{j}(u,x)\right|\leq L_{j}\|u-v\|,\;\forall u,v\in\mathop{\bf dom}_{u}f
. Let
L=\max_{j\leq J}L_{j}
.

Assumption 2.2
.

For all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the functions
f_{j}
are smooth (w.r.t. the
\ell_{2}
norm) in
u
with constants
M_{j}
, i.e.,
\left\|\nabla f_{j}(v,x)-\nabla f_{j}(u,x)\right\|_{2}\leq M_{j}\|u-v\|_{2},\;\forall u,v\in\mathop{\bf dom}_{u}f
. Let
M=\max_{j\leq J}M_{j}
.

Analogously to
(
Wang et al., 2025
)
, we assume the following on the domain of
f
.

Assumption 2.3
.

The domain
\mathop{\bf dom}_{u}{f}
is
{\bf R}^{d}
. Otherwise,
f
is either element-wise monotonically increasing in
u
and only has a (potentially) lower-bounded domain, or element-wise monotonically decreasing in
u
and only has a (potentially) upper-bounded domain. In all cases, the domain
\mathop{\bf dom}_{u}{f}
is independent of
x
.

Assumptions
2.1
and
2.3
hold throughout the paper, and we do not restate them in subsequent results.
The smoothness condition of Assumption
2.2
is instead invoked only where explicitly stated.

Streaming data

We assume the distribution
{\mathbf{P}}
to be unknown, so the value
H_{\star}
cannot be computed directly.
Instead, we have access to a
streaming dataset
of i.i.d. realizations of
u
: starting from an initial dataset
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\{\hat{u}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}}
with
n_{0}\geq 1
datapoints, one realization is disclosed (w.l.o.g.) at the end of each time step
t\geq 1
, so that
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{\hat{u}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{t}}
with
n_{t}=n_{0}+t
.
We define the corresponding empirical distribution
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t}=(1/{n_{t}})\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\delta_{\hat{u}^{i}}
.

2.1
DRO
over time

The
DRO
approach under the Wasserstein metric constructs an ambiguity set of order
p
around
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t}
of radius
\varepsilon_{t}\geq 0
,
i.e.
,

\mathcal{P}_{t}=\left\{\mathbf{Q}\in\Xi_{p}({S})\mid W_{p}(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t},\mathbf{Q})\leq\varepsilon_{t}\right\},

where the Wasserstein type-
p
distance between two probability distributions
\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2}\in\Xi_{p}(S)
is defined by

W_{p}(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2})}(\int_{S\times S}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{p}\mathrm{d}\pi(u_{1},u_{2}))^{1/{p}}
, with
\Pi(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2})
the set of all couplings,
i.e.
, probability distributions over
S\times S
with marginals
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{2}
.
We suppress the dependency of
\mathcal{P}_{t}
on
p
for conciseness, and note that unless otherwise specified,
p
is of an arbitrary order and
\|\cdot\|
is an arbitrary norm.
Intuitively, the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}_{t}
contains all probability distributions in
\Xi_{p}(S)
that can be obtained by transporting probability mass from
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t}
with a transportation budget of at most
\varepsilon_{t}
.
With this uncertainty modeling framework in place, we compute the
DRO
solution
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
at time step
t
by solving the optimization problem

H_{t}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,x)],

(2)

where we minimize the worst-case expected value of
f
.

As
t
increases, our confidence in
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t}
grows, allowing us to safely reduce the
nominal radius

\varepsilon_{t}
; suitable choices of
\varepsilon_{t}
yield
finite sample performance guarantees
of the form

{\mathbf{P}}^{t}\left(H_{\star}\leq{\mbox{\bf E}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}[f(u,x_{t})]\leq H_{t}\right)\geq 1-\beta_{t},

(3)

where
\beta_{t}>0
is a specified time-varying probability of constraint violation, and
{\mathbf{P}}^{t}
is the product distribution of the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
.
We choose the radius according to the classic literature
(
Fournier and Guillin, 2015
;
Kuhn et al., 2019
)
, which, for finite
p
, requires the following light-tailed assumption. Note that it is satisfied by sub-Gaussian and bounded distributions, and holds throughout the paper.

Assumption 2.4

(Light-tailed distribution)
.

There exists an exponent
\alpha>1
, such that

R={\mbox{\bf E}}_{\mathbf{P}}[\exp(\|u\|^{\alpha})]=\int_{S}\exp(\|u\|^{\alpha}){\mathbf{P}}(du)<\infty.

Then, measure concentration results for Wasserstein-
p
distances imply the following.

Theorem 2.1

(Guarantees using Wasserstein measure concentration)
.

Under Assumption
2.4
for the case
p\geq 1
, and under analogous assumptions for
p=\infty

(
Trillos and Slepčev, 2014
)
, for
n_{t}\rightarrow\infty
we can choose confidence levels
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
and corresponding radii
\varepsilon_{t}=O((\log(\beta^{-1}_{t})/{n_{t}})^{\min(p/d,1/2)})
such that
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\beta_{t}<\infty
,

{\mathbf{P}}^{t}(W_{p}({\mathbf{P}},\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t})\geq\varepsilon_{t})\leq\beta_{t},
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon_{t}=0.
The
DRO
problem (
2
) solved with these radii gives the finite-sample guarantee (
3
).

Larger datasets thus reduce the distributional ambiguity: Theorem
2.1
gives
\mathbf{P}^{\infty}\{\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}W_{p}(\mathbf{P},\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t})=0\}=1
, which in turn implies
\mathbf{P}^{\infty}\{\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}H_{t}\downarrow H_{\star}\}=1

(
Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn, 2018
, Theorem 3.7)
.
This statistical benefit, however, comes at a steep computational price: the radius decays only as
O(n_{t}^{-p/d})
, suffering from the curse of dimensionality, while the reformulation of the
DRO
problem (
2
) adds a constraint for each datapoint in
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t}
, so the problem size grows without bound as
t
increases.
We are thus motivated to introduce a new tradeoff, where some performance is sacrificed for faster computations.

2.2
Our online algorithm

We propose a data-compression framework for fast online
DRO
.
Our goal is to solve an adjusted
DRO
problem over time, where
K
is the chosen number of data clusters, and

\displaystyle x^{K}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t}}\mathbf{E}_{u\sim\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,x)],\quad t=0,1,\dots,

(4)

where

observed so far. From here onward, we refer to the solution

Unlike the standard

Each cluster

This reformulation is for

2.2.2
Dynamic regret

In Section
4
, we analyze the
dynamic regret

R(T,K)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}_{t+1}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,x^{K}_{t})]-\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}_{t+1}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,x)]\right),

DRO

;
Besbes et al., 2015
)

We give deterministic finite-sample and asymptotic bounds, and additionally show sublinear convergence to the asymptotic bound.
In Section
5
, we extend the analysis to subgradient solves.

3
Online clustering requirements

The key to our approach is the adaptive ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t}
, which is constructed using a
compressed
representation of the available data,
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}
.
Below, we formalize the requirements on generating
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}
, and note that the decision-maker has the flexibility to use
any
method to generate
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}
, provided it satisfies the given assumptions.
These requirements ensure the validity of the asymptotic regret bounds established later in this paper, and can be satisfied by any partitioning of the support set
S
.

The number of clusters
K
reflects the available computational budget, as it caps the number of atoms in
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}
, and hence the size of the compressed problem (
5
).
For all methods and time periods
t
, we maintain a set of
K_{t}\leq K
clusters
\{C_{t}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
and a corresponding set of supports
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\{S_{t}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
, defined as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Cluster support)
.

For each cluster
k
and time
t
, the cluster support
S_{t}^{k}\subset S
is defined as a region in
S
such that any datapoint
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{D}_{t}
that falls within this region is contained in cluster
C_{t}^{k}
.
We say that the clusters
\{C_{t}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
are induced by the supports
\mathcal{S}_{t}
.

Optimal clustering

If the distribution
{\mathbf{P}}
is known, then for any
K
, the optimal set of supports can be determined using
k
-centers clustering
(
Graf and Luschgy, 2000
, Chapter 1)
.

Definition 3.2

(
k
-centers)
.

The
k
-centers problems, with respect to a distribution
\mathbf{Q}
with support
Z
, and orders
p\geq 1
(left),
p=\infty
with compact
Z
(right), are

\inf_{A\subset{\bf R}^{d},|A|\leq K}{\mbox{\bf E}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\left[\min_{a\in A}\|u-a\|^{p}\right],~\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{A\subset{\bf R}^{d},|A|\leq K}\sup_{u\in Z}\min_{a\in A}\|u-a\|,

(7)

where
A^{\star}
is a set that achieves the infimum.

Given optimal centers
A^{\star}
, each cluster support is a cell of the Voronoi diagram of
A^{\star}
,

S^{k}=\left\{u\in S\,\middle|\,\|u-a^{k}\|=\min_{a\in A^{\star}}\|u-a\|\right\},

where each point
u
is assigned to the region of its closest center
a^{k}
.
This clustering attains the
minimum quantization error

w_{K}(\mathbf{Q})=\left({\mbox{\bf E}}_{\mathbf{Q}}\left[\min_{a\in A^{\star}}\|u-a\|^{p}\right]\right)^{1/{p}}~~(p\geq 1),\qquad w_{K}(\mathbf{Q})=\sup_{u\in Z}\min_{a\in A^{\star}}\|u-a\|~~(p=\infty),

which is the smallest Wasserstein-
p
distance between
\mathbf{Q}
and any distribution supported on at most
K
atoms
(
Graf and Luschgy, 2000
, Chapter 1)
.

Without knowledge of
{\mathbf{P}}
, the optimal clustering with respect to the empirical distribution
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t}
can be found in the same way.
However, the
k
-centers problem is NP-hard
(
Hochbaum and Shmoys, 1985
)
, and its size grows with
n_{t}
, conflicting with our goal of decreasing computational effort.
The goal of an online clustering algorithm is to cheaply approximate the
k
-centers solution.
Below, we give the assumptions the cluster supports must satisfy; Section
6
gives two algorithms.

Assumption 3.1
.

The following holds for any time step
t
: the elements in the set
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\{S_{t}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{t}}
have pairwise disjoint interiors, i.e.,
\mathrm{int}(S^{k}_{t})\cap\mathrm{int}(S^{k^{\prime}}_{t})=\emptyset
for
k\neq k^{\prime}
; the cluster supports cover
S
, i.e.,
S=\cup_{k\leq K_{t}}S_{t}^{k}
; the cluster supports have nonzero measure, i.e.,
{\mathbf{P}}(S^{k}_{t})>0
for
k=1,\dots,K_{t}
; and the cluster boundaries have measure 0.
Furthermore, there is some finite time
\tau<\infty
such that
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\mathcal{S}_{\tau}
for all
t\geq\tau
.

Assumption
3.1
holds throughout the remainder of the paper.
By this assumption,
\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{t}
represents an approximation of the empirical distribution
\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t}
, and converges to some distribution
{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star}
as
t\to\infty
.

Lemma 3.1

(Clustering consistency)
.

The sequence of clustered empirical distributions
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}
converges
{\mathbf{P}}^{\infty}
-almost surely to a distribution
{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star}
. Furthermore, the distributions achieve almost sure convergence with respect to the Wasserstein metric, that is, for any
p
such that
{\mathbf{P}}
has finite
p
-th moments,

{\mathbf{P}}^{\infty}\left\{\lim_{t\to\infty}W_{p}({\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star},\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t})=0\right\}=1.

Remark on
\tau

We enforce the support-fixing time
\tau
to ensure the convergence of the clustered distribution, which is required by our subsequent asymptotic results.
Without a fixing time, convergence can only be ensured for specific algorithms:
So et al. (2022)
adjust the
k
-means center update rate to obtain asymptotic convergence, while
Tang and Monteleoni (2017)
impose a uniform and deterministic update rate to further obtain a
O(1/t)
convergence rate.
Both approaches fit in our framework, with the cluster supports set as the Voronoi cells of the obtained centers.
However, they require specific and difficult-to-compute constants, which motivates our more general support-fixing condition.

4
Online performance analysis

We are now ready for our regret analysis.
In online settings, we want to robustify the solution against the
subsequent realization
of the uncertainty; the dynamic regret (
6
) therefore compares our online solution, with information up to time
t
, against the nominal
DRO
oracle with information up to time
t+1
.
In order to obtain a bound on (
6
), we first introduce a number of prerequisite lemmas.
We begin by formalizing the performance difference between two general Wasserstein
DRO
problems with potentially different centers and/or radii.

Lemma 4.1

(Radius and/or center discrepancy)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
be two Wasserstein balls of order
p
, centered at arbitrary distributions
\mathbf{P}_{1}
and
\mathbf{P}_{2}
, and with radii
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
. For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,x)]-\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,x)]\leq L(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})_{+}+F_{p}W_{p}(\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}),

where
F_{p}=L
if
p=1
and
F_{p}=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}(\int\|\nabla f(u,x)\|_{*}^{q}d\mathbf{Q})^{1/q}
if
p>1
.

For
p=1
this outperforms the classic Kantorovich–Rubinstein bound
(
Kantorovich and Rubinstein, 1958
)
, since then
F_{p}=L
and
(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})_{+}\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
; for
p>1
the two are not directly comparable, as
F_{p}\leq L
while
W_{p}\geq W_{1}
.
This bound then motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.1

(Worst-case expectation gap)
.

For two Wasserstein balls
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
of order
p
, with centers
\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2}\in\Xi_{p}(S)
and radii
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\geq 0
, define the (ordered) expectation gap

\mathop{\bf gap}(\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2})=\min\left\{L(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+W_{1}(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2})),~L(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})_{+}+F_{p}W_{p}(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\mathbf{Q}_{2})\right\},

where a distribution
\mathbf{Q}
in place of an ambiguity set denotes the degenerate set
\{\mathbf{Q}\}
of radius
0
.

The two clauses of
\mathop{\bf gap}
come from Kantorovich duality and Lemma
4.1
, respectively.
Next, we also quantify the discrepancies induced by the clustering itself.

Definition 4.2

(Clustering discrepancies)
.

For any clustering of the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, with clusters
C^{k}_{t}
, cluster means
\bar{u}^{k}_{t}
, and clustered empirical distribution
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}
, define the clustering cost
c_{t,p}
and the piece-switching error
e_{t}
,

c_{t,p}=\left(\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{t}}\sum_{\hat{u}\in C^{k}_{t}}\left\|\hat{u}-\bar{u}^{k}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{p}\right)^{1/{p}},\quad e_{t}=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{t}}\sum_{\hat{u}\in C^{k}_{t}}\max_{j\leq J}\,(z_{t}^{{jk}})^{T}(\bar{u}^{k}_{t}-\hat{u}),

where
z^{jk}_{t}
are dual variables from (
5
), interpretable as supergradients of the pieces
f_{j}
in
u
at the worst-case scenarios (for maximum-of-affine
f
, exactly the slopes
a_{j}(x^{K}_{t})
).
The error
e_{t}
thus averages, at no extra computational cost, a first-order estimate of the worst per-piece change incurred by replacing each datapoint with its cluster mean; when
J=1
, deviations from the mean cancel within each cluster and
e_{t}=0
, while the maximum prevents this cancellation for
J\geq 2
.

We are then ready to present a static (same time step) difference between the online and nominal
DRO
performance, under the following general assumptions.

Assumption 4.1
.

Fix an order
p
and shrinking radius schedule
\varepsilon_{t}
. For all
t\geq 0
:

1.

The distribution
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}
is constructed using cluster supports
\mathcal{S}_{t}
that satisfy Assumption
3.1
.

2.

The adaptive ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t}
has radius
\varepsilon^{K}_{t}=\varepsilon_{t}
.

Assumption
4.1
holds throughout the remainder of the paper.

Lemma 4.2

(Static bounds)
.

The value
H^{K}_{t}
of the compressed
DRO
problem (
4
) satisfies

\begin{gathered}-\underline{\psi}_{t}\leq H_{t}^{K}-H_{t}\leq\bar{\psi}_{t}+\delta,\\
\underline{\psi}_{t}=\min\left\{e_{t},\mathop{\bf gap}(\mathcal{P}_{t},\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t})\right\},\qquad\bar{\psi}_{t}=\min\left\{L\varepsilon_{t}+{\frac{M}{2}}(c_{t,2})^{2},\mathop{\bf gap}(\mathcal{P}_{t},\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t})\right\},\end{gathered}

where
H_{t}
is the nominal
DRO
value and
\delta
is the tolerance. The bounds
\bar{\psi}_{t}
and
\underline{\psi}_{t}
have inner terms given in Definitions
4.1
and
4.2
.
If smoothness (Assumption
2.2
) does not hold,
M=\infty
.

The guarantees of nominal
DRO
are thus inherited by our online solutions, with certificates adjusted by the clustering-based discrepancies
\underline{\psi}_{t}
and
\bar{\psi}_{t}
, whose best clause can be chosen depending on the function
f
.
The clause involving
W_{1}(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t},\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t})
, a Wasserstein-1 distance between two empirical distributions, is always computable via a linear program, and under smoothness, the
c_{t,2}
term is applicable and easily computed from the given empirical distributions.
Furthermore, in some cases these bounds simplify: when
J=1
,
i.e.
,
f
consists of a single concave function, we have
\underline{\psi}_{t}=e_{t}=0
. When instead
f
is maximum-of-affine,
M=0
, so
\bar{\psi}_{t}=\min\{L\varepsilon_{t},\mathop{\bf gap}(\mathcal{P}_{t},\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t})\}
.

We use this result to formulate a deterministic bound
\tilde{R}(T,K)
for the dynamic regret (
6
).

Theorem 4.1

(Deterministic regret)
.

We have

\displaystyle R(T,K)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\underline{\psi}_{t+1}+\bar{\psi}_{t}+\mathop{\bf gap}(\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t+1},\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t})\right)+\frac{1}{T}\mathop{\bf gap}(\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{T})+\delta=\tilde{R}(T,K),

(8)

with
\underline{\psi}_{t}
and
\bar{\psi}_{t}
as defined in Lemma
4.2
, and
\mathop{\bf gap}(\cdot)
as defined in Definition
4.1
.

The bound has three sources of discrepancy: clustering, captured by
\underline{\psi}_{t+1}+\bar{\psi}_{t}
; the difference in time step, captured by the
\mathop{\bf gap}
terms and the index offset in
\underline{\psi}_{t+1}
; and the optimality tolerance
\delta
.
Under clustering consistency (Lemma
3.1
), the clustering discrepancy converges to a fixed value and the time discrepancy to
0
, which we analyze next.
Specifically, the clustering-based values we previously defined have the following asymptotic limits.

Lemma 4.3

(Asymptotic Wasserstein limit)
.

With
{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star}
as in Lemma
3.1
, we have

\mathbf{P}^{\infty}\{w_{K}({\mathbf{P}})\leq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}W_{p}(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t},\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t})=W_{p}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star})\}=1
,
where
w_{K}({\mathbf{P}})
is the minimum quantization error of
{\mathbf{P}}
.
Furthermore,
\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}W_{p}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star})=0
.

Lemma 4.4

(Asymptotic clustering-discrepancy limits)
.

Let
\kappa(u)
denote the index of the fixed cluster support
S^{\kappa(u)}_{\tau}
containing
u
, which is
{\mathbf{P}}
-almost surely unique by Assumption
3.1
.
With
{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star}
as in Lemma
3.1
, we have

{\mathbf{P}}^{\infty}\{\lim_{t\to\infty}c_{t,p}=c_{\star,p}=(\mathbf{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}}[\|u-\bar{u}^{\kappa(u)}_{\star}\|^{p}_{2}])^{1/{p}}\}=1
, with
\bar{u}^{k}_{\star}
the limits of the cluster means (and are therefore atoms of
{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star}
).
Furthermore, if there exist accumulation points of
z_{t}^{jk}
for all
j\leq J,k\leq K
, then

{\mathbf{P}}^{\infty}\{\limsup_{t\to\infty}e_{t}=e_{\star}=\mathbf{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}}[\max_{j\leq J}(z^{j\kappa(u)}_{\star})^{T}(\bar{u}^{\kappa(u)}_{\star}-u)]\}=1
,
where
z^{jk}_{\star}
are the accumulation points that achieve the limit superior.

We are now ready to present our asymptotic regret, which shows that as
T\rightarrow\infty
, the time-discrepancy disappears, and the clustering discrepancy is bounded by the above limit terms.

Theorem 4.2

(Asymptotic regret)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
be chosen according to Theorem
2.1
.
With probability 1,

\lim_{T\to\infty}R(T,K)\leq O(\max\{\underline{\psi}_{\star},\bar{\psi}_{\star}\})+\delta,

where

\underline{\psi}_{\star}=\min\left\{\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}{e_{t}},\mathop{\bf gap}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star})\right\},~\bar{\psi}_{\star}=\min\left\{\frac{M}{2}(c_{\star,2})^{2},\mathop{\bf gap}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star})\right\},~\mathop{\bf gap}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star})=\min\left\{LW_{1}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star}),F_{p}W_{p}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star})\right\}
.
Additionally, as
K\to\infty
,
\lim_{K\to\infty}\lim_{T\to\infty}R(T,K)=0.

For maximum-of-affine
f
, the following corollary additionally simplifies some of the clauses.

Corollary 4.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.2
, if the function
f(u,x)
is maximum-of-affine in both
u
and
x
, with
S={\bf R}^{d}
, we have
\limsup_{t\to\infty}e_{t}=e_{\star}

where the latter is defined in Lemma
4.4
, and
\bar{\psi}_{\star}=0
.
Furthermore, if the clustering-induced coupling is optimal with respect to
p=1
, i.e.,

c_{t,1}=W_{1}(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{t},\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}),

then almost surely with respect to
{\mathbf{P}}^{\infty}
,

\underline{\psi}_{\star}\leq{e_{\star}}\leq LW_{1}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star}).

Overall, the converged values always depend on the number of clusters
K
and the clustering algorithm, which control the quality of the clustered distribution and its limit
{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star}
, and hence the trade-off between efficiency and optimality.

So far, we have obtained deterministic regret bounds. We can, however, also establish probabilistic bounds, which gives the
rate
at which the regret bound converges to the asymptotic limit above. This requires an additional lemma on the clustering convergence rate.

Lemma 4.5

(Clustering convergence rate)
.

Suppose the sequence
\varepsilon_{t}
is computed using Theorem
2.1
such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon_{t}=0
and
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
satisfies
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\beta_{t}<\infty}
. Then, for
t\geq\tau
, the distance
W_{p}(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star})
converges to
0
with rate
O(\varepsilon_{t}+(\log(\beta_{t}^{-1})/t)^{1/(2p)})
. For
p=1
, this rate is
O(\varepsilon_{t})
.

Theorem 4.3

(Dynamic regret bound)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
be chosen according to Theorem
2.1
. For finite
T\gg\tau
, with probability
1-\bar{\beta}
, the dynamic regret is bounded as

R(T,K)\leq O\left(\left(\log(\beta_{T}^{-1})/T\right)^{\min(p/d,1/2)}\right)+O\left(W_{1}({\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{P}}^{K}_{\star})\right)+\delta,

where
\beta_{T}
decays at least sublinearly in
T
, and

1-\bar{\beta}\geq\Pi_{t=1}^{T}(1-\beta_{t})^{4}
.

This provides a sublinear convergence rate for the dynamic regret, matching the convergence rate of the underlying empirical distributions, for which no sharper rates exist in the literature.

5
Subgradient method

When the
DRO
problem is too expensive to solve in full, we can instead obtain an approximate solution.
In this section, for general
p
, we derive fast decompositions of the worst-case expectation,
\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}^{K}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,x^{K}_{t-1})]
, under various supports
S
and ground norms
\|\cdot\|
of the dynamic ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}^{K}
.
The results differ depending on the finiteness of
p
, so for
p=\infty
, we instead denote the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}^{K}_{\infty}
.
We focus on
f
that is maximum-of-affine in
u
, given as

f(u,x)=\max_{j\leq J}(a_{j}(x)^{T}u+b_{j}(x)),

where
a_{j}(x)\in{\bf R}^{d}
and
b_{j}(x)\in{\bf R}
are convex functions of
x
. For simplicity, throughout this section we denote
\bar{x}=x^{K}_{t-1}
, drop subscripts
t
, and let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon^{K}
.

5.1
Time complexity

We reduce the worst-case expectation by decomposing it into per-centroid and per-piece oracles, and construct the subgradient from the individual solutions. We begin with the following definitions, which hold by strong duality
(
Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn, 2018
;
Wang et al., 2025
)
.

Definition 5.1
.

Fix
\bar{x}
. For a centroid
u\in S
and piece
j\leq J
, denote the per-piece oracles:

\varphi_{\lambda}^{j}(u)=\sup_{\zeta\in S}\big(a_{j}(\bar{x})^{T}\zeta+b_{j}(\bar{x})-\lambda\|\zeta-u\|^{p}\big),\ \ \lambda\geq 0,\qquad\varphi_{\infty}^{j}(u)=\sup_{\zeta\in S,~\|\zeta-u\|\leq\varepsilon}\big(a_{j}(\bar{x})^{T}\zeta+b_{j}(\bar{x})\big),

with
p\geq 1
(left) and
p=\infty
(right). The respective per-centroid oracles are
\varphi_{\lambda}(u)=\max_{j\leq J}\varphi_{\lambda}^{j}(u)
and
\varphi_{\infty}(u)=\max_{j\leq J}\varphi_{\infty}^{j}(u)
, and the worst-case expectations are given by

\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}^{K}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,\bar{x})]=\inf_{\lambda\geq 0}h(\bar{x},\lambda),\qquad\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}^{K}_{\infty}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,\bar{x})]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta^{k}\varphi_{\infty}(\bar{u}^{k}),

where
h(\bar{x},\lambda)=\lambda{\varepsilon}^{p}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta^{k}\varphi_{\lambda}(\bar{u}^{k})
.

At optimality, the subgradient takes the following form.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\lambda^{\star}(\bar{x})\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\lambda\geq 0}h(\bar{x},\lambda)
, and let
\zeta^{k}
be a maximizer of cluster
k
’s oracle at
\bar{u}^{k}
(
\varphi_{\lambda^{\star}}(\bar{u}^{k})
or
\varphi_{\infty}(\bar{u}^{k})
).
Except when
p=1
with
S={\bf R}^{d}
, where Corollary
5.1
gives an explicit subgradient, a subgradient of the worst-case expectation at
\bar{x}
is

g\in\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta^{k}\,\partial_{x}f(\zeta^{k},\bar{x}).

(9)

As presented, this subgradient may not be unique, but is exact. However, in order to speed up the computation, for general bounded support
S
, we may instead solve the worst-case expectation to a value-tolerance
\delta_{\text{approx}}
, which also subjects the subgradient to a tolerance
\delta_{\text{grad}}
, defined below.

Lemma 5.1

(Subgradient tolerance)
.

Suppose
S
is bounded, with
R_{S}=\sup_{\zeta,\zeta^{\prime}\in S}\|\zeta-\zeta^{\prime}\|<\infty
. For finite
p\geq 1
, suppose
\hat{\lambda}\geq 0
satisfies
|\hat{\lambda}-\lambda^{\star}(\bar{x})|\leq\sigma
, and that for each
k\leq K
, the inner problem at
\hat{\lambda}
is solved to value-tolerance
\Gamma
, at solution
\hat{\zeta}^{k}\in S
. Then, the worst-case expectation is solved to value-tolerance
\delta_{\text{approx}}=\max\{\Gamma,R_{S}^{p}\sigma\}
.
Furthermore, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, any subgradient

\hat{g}\in\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta^{k}\,\partial_{x}f(\hat{\zeta}^{k},\bar{x})
satisfies

\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}^{K}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,x)]\geq\max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{P}^{K}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}[f(u,\bar{x})]+\hat{g}^{T}(x-\bar{x})-\delta_{\text{grad}},

(10)

where
\delta_{\text{grad}}=\Gamma+R_{S}^{p}\sigma+R_{S}^{p}\big|\lambda^{\star}(\bar{x})-\lambda^{\star}(x)\big|
.
If
p=\infty
,
\delta_{\text{approx}}=\delta_{\text{grad}}=\Gamma
.

The subgradient tolerance is required for quantifying the additional regret we incur with inexact subgradient solves. We now derive the computational complexities of our subroutines for obtaining
g
(when the solution is closed-form) or
\hat{g}
, which are notably faster than the cost
O((KJd)^{1.5})
(or
O((KJ(m+d))^{1.5})
for polyhedral
S
with
m
facets) of the original formulation.

Proposition 5.2

(Time complexity)
.

If
S={\bf R}^{d}
, the oracles admit closed form solutions at cost
T_{\rm inner}=O(1)
.
If instead
S\subsetneq{\bf R}^{d}
, the oracles follow the subroutines of Table
1
at cost
T_{\rm inner}(S,\|\cdot\|,p)
.
For finite
p
, except when
p=1
with
S={\bf R}^{d}
,
\hat{\lambda}
is found through bisection in
T_{\rm outer}=O(\log(1/\sigma))
iterations, with tolerance
\sigma
; for
p=\infty
, no outer search is needed since no shared
\lambda
exist, and
T_{\rm outer}=1
.
The subgradient is computable in total time
O(T_{\rm outer}KJ\,T_{\rm inner}(S,\|\cdot\|,p))
, or
O(T_{\rm outer}\,T_{\rm inner}(S,\|\cdot\|,p))
if the
KJ
inner solves are parallelized.

Corollary 5.1

(Closed form at
p=1
, unbounded
S
)
.

At
p=1
and
S={\bf R}^{d}
, let
j^{k}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{j\leq J}\big(a_{j}(\bar{x})^{T}\bar{u}^{k}+b_{j}(\bar{x})\big)
, and
j^{\star}\in\mathop{\rm argmax}_{j\leq J}\|a_{j}(\bar{x})\|_{*}
.
The subgradient is

5.2
Adjustments to the dynamic regret

Throughout Section
4
,

is the minimizer of the compressed problem, solved to tolerance

subgradient
solutions

, with the worst-case expectation solved via the above subroutines.
For this, we require that

\ell_{2}

Assumption 5.1

(Lipschitzness in
x
)
.

For every
u\in S
and
j\leq J
, the function
f_{j}
is
L_{x,j}
-Lipschitz on
\mathcal{X}
w.r.t. the
\ell_{2}
-norm. Let
L_{x}=\max_{j\leq J}L_{x,j}
.

Let

denote the dynamic regret (
6
) evaluated at the subgradient iterates

.
Compared to the regret of Theorem
4.1
, we incur three additional discrepancies: (i) the standard convergence rate of the subgradient method on a fixed objective; (ii) the drift of the objective itself, as
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}^{K}_{t}
shifts and the radius
\varepsilon^{K}_{t}
shrinks; and (iii) the subgradient tolerance from the subroutines of Section
5.1
.

Theorem 5.1

(Subgradient dynamic regret)
.

Suppose Assumption
5.1
holds. Let
\mathcal{X}
be convex, and
R_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathrm{diam}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
.
Suppose
x^{K}_{t}
is obtained through the subgradient procedure of Algorithm
1
, with step size
\eta_{t}=R_{\mathcal{X}}/(L_{x}\sqrt{t})
.
Then, with
\tilde{R}(T,K)
the full-solve regret bound (
8
),

R_{\text{approx}}(T,K)\leq\tilde{R}(T,K)+\frac{3R_{\mathcal{X}}L_{x}}{2\sqrt{T}}+\frac{1}{T}\Big(\mathop{\bf gap}(\mathcal{P}^{K}_{T},\mathcal{P}^{K}_{0})+2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathop{\bf gap}(\mathcal{P}^{K}_{0},\mathcal{P}^{K}_{t})\Big)+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta_{\text{grad},t},

(11)

where each
\mathop{\bf gap}
term (Definition
4.1
) measures the drift of the worst-case expectation between the ambiguity sets at two timesteps,
and
\delta_{\text{grad},t}
is the subgradient tolerance of Lemma
5.1
, evaluated at

and

, a minimizer of the compressed problem at time

The first additional term, the convergence rate of the subgradient method, vanishes as

However, if the subgradient is exact, the last term disappears.
Furthermore, if the problem is periodically re-solved in full, each drift term

Then, as the clustered distribution and radius converge, the resolve-relative drifts vanish, and the entire second additional term also converges to 0. We would then recover the direct-solve regret.

6
Online algorithms for data-compression

In Section
3
, we gave clustering requirements, and in this section, we provide two online clustering algorithms satisfying Assumption
3.1
.
These algorithms maintain a reasonably accurate approximation of the optimal discretization while minimizing computational effort.

6.1
Approximate
k
-centers clustering with warm starts (Reclustering)

To maintain a consistently good approximation of the data, we recompute an approximate
k
-centers clustering at each time step
t
, until the stopping threshold
\tau
, warm-starting each clustering with the previous centers
A_{t-1}
.
The support of cluster
k
is the Voronoi region around its center
a_{t}^{k}\in A_{t}
.
After time
\tau
, the centers are fixed at
A_{\tau}
: the datapoint
\hat{u}
observed at time
t
joins the cluster
k^{\prime}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{k\leq K}\|\hat{u}-a^{k}_{\tau}\|
, the cluster mean is updated to
\bar{u}^{k^{\prime}}_{t}=(n^{k^{\prime}}_{t-1}\bar{u}^{k^{\prime}}_{t-1}+\hat{u})/n^{k^{\prime}}_{t}
, and the weights rescale to
\theta^{k}_{t}=(n^{k}_{t-1}+\mathbf{1}\{k=k^{\prime}\})/n_{t}
for
k=1,\dots,K
.
We approximate the
k
-centers problem with
k
-means
(
Hartigan and Wong, 1979
)
, whose iterations cost
O(n_{t}Kd)
time each; in practice, warm-starting greatly reduces the number of iterations.
This method is not memory efficient, however, as storing the datapoints to recompute the clusters requires
O((K+n_{t})d)
memory.

6.2
Online clustering (OnlineClustering)

For an algorithm that is also memory conservative, we propose an online updating procedure that takes inspiration from CluStream
(
Aggarwal et al., 2003
)
.
We maintain two sets of clusters:
Q\geq K

microclusters
, and
K

macroclusters
.
Each arriving datapoint either joins the microcluster whose support contains it, if within a distance threshold of its center, or starts a new microcluster; in the latter case, the two closest microclusters are merged to maintain the budget
Q
.
Every 50 timesteps,
K
macroclusters are obtained by a warm-started approximate
k
-centers clustering of the microcluster centers.
Appendix
D
details the procedure and shows that it satisfies the clustering requirements of Section
3
.
Note that the clustering iterations cost
O(QKd)
, ordinary microcluster updates cost only
O(Qd)
, and the memory requirement is
O((K+Q)d)
, so, unlike Reclustering, neither time nor memory grows with
n_{t}
.

Portfolio optimization

We consider a market that forbids short-selling and has

The uncertain parameters are the daily returns, given by

The distribution

The objective can be written as the expectation of the maximum-of-affine functions

,
{\mbox{\bf E}}_{{\mathbf{P}}}\left[\gamma+(1/\omega)\max\{-u^{T}x-\gamma,0\}\right]

\|\cdot\|

For each stock, we set the lower and upper bounds

We let

and

n_{0}=5
, and use
Q=500

The out-of-sample values are computed on a test set of 1000 datapoints.
The selected radii decay as

We consider a cardinality-constrained (best-subset) support vector machine for binary classification.
The uncertain parameter is a labeled example

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced an online data-compression framework for solving Wasserstein
DRO
problems with streaming data.
By clustering the data online, our method keeps the problem size bounded as data accumulates, while inheriting the out-of-sample guarantees of nominal
DRO
with certificates adjusted by the compression discrepancies.
We quantified the price of compression through static performance bounds and dynamic regret bounds that converge sublinearly to a clustering-dependent gap, and extended the analysis to fast subgradient updates built on specialized worst-case-expectation subroutines.
Numerical experiments on portfolio optimization and sparse
SVM
problems, including mixed-integer formulations, show over an order of magnitude reduction in cumulative computation time with minimal loss in solution quality.

Inner-problem derivations

In this section, we derive the entries of Table

\varphi_{\lambda}^{j}(u)=\sup_{\zeta\in S}(a_{j}(\bar{x})^{T}\zeta+b_{j}(\bar{x})-\lambda\|\zeta-u\|^{p})

\varphi_{\infty}^{j}(u)=\sup_{\zeta\in S,\,\|\zeta-u\|\leq\varepsilon}(a_{j}(\bar{x})^{T}\zeta+b_{j}(\bar{x}))

S
and ground norm

.
For simplicity, we write

and drop the additive constant

Unbounded support.

See the proof of Proposition
5.2
.

Box,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.

Let
S=[l,h]
, and
v=\zeta-u
.
Let
\delta_{i}^{+}=h_{i}-u_{i}
and
\delta_{i}^{-}=u_{i}-l_{i}
denote the box slacks.

Case
p=1

The penalty
\lambda\|v\|_{1}=\lambda\sum_{i}|v_{i}|
separates additively by coordinate, so we can solve individually
\max_{v_{i}\in[-\delta_{i}^{-},\delta_{i}^{+}]}(\beta_{i}v_{i}-\lambda|v_{i}|)

v_{i}^{\star}=\delta_{i}^{+}
if
\beta_{i}>\lambda
,
v_{i}^{\star}=-\delta_{i}^{-}
if
\beta_{i}<-\lambda
, and
v_{i}^{\star}=0
otherwise. Cost:
O(d)
.

Case
p>1

Denote

, and split the problem in two stages: fix

and solve the linear problem
F(r)=\max\{\beta^{T}v:\|v\|_{1}\leq r,\ -\delta_{i}^{-}\leq v_{i}\leq\delta_{i}^{+}~\forall i\}

over

The inner problem is the continuous knapsack problem

, which can be solved via sorting.
We sort the coordinates by

in decreasing order,
\pi(1),\dots,\pi(d)
, and let
\rho_{0}=0

\rho_{k}=\sum_{l\leq k}\delta_{\pi(l)}

\delta_{i}^{-}
if
\beta_{i}<0
) be the cumulative slack of the

, a piecewise-linear function of

. Maximizing

is then a segment-by-segment search in
O(d)
, where on each segment the optimal

has closed form
(|\beta_{\pi(k)}|/(\lambda p))^{1/(p-1)}
. The total cost is
O(d\log d)
.

Case
p=\infty
.
Same as above, but with the budget fixed at

. Cost:
O(d\log d)
.

Box,
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.

Let
\delta_{i}=\delta_{i}^{+}
if
\beta_{i}>0
and
\delta_{i}=\delta_{i}^{-}
if
\beta_{i}<0

Case
p=1
.
At transport radius

The same reduction applies with

Appendix D
Online clustering algorithm (Section
6.2
)

We give details for the online clustering algorithm, introduced in Section
6.2
.
We keep track of two sets of clusters: a set of
Q\geq K

microclusters
, and a set of
K

macroclusters
.

Initialization

We initialize by solving approximately the
k
-centers problem (
7
) with respect to
\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{0}
, with up to
Q
clusters. This set of centers is denoted
A^{Q}_{0}
. We assign points to their closest center, and define the support of the
q
-th cluster
S^{q}_{0}
as the Voronoi region
V(a_{0}^{q})
around its center
a_{0}^{q}\in A^{Q}_{0}
.
Each microcluster stores its center, mean, root-mean-squared error (RMSE)
\eta^{q}_{t}
, and weight; for singleton clusters, the RMSE is heuristically initialized as twice the minimum stored RMSE.

To create
K

macroclusters
, we solve approximately the
k
-centers problem with respect to
A^{Q}_{0}
, and obtain a set of centers
A^{K}_{0}
. Each microcluster is assigned to the macrocluster with the closest center; a macrocluster then contains the points of its constituent microclusters, with their combined weight, and the weighted average of their means. The support of the
k
-th macrocluster is the Voronoi region around the
k
-th center
a_{0}^{k}\in A^{K}_{0}
, plus the datapoints assigned to it, and minus the other datapoints,
i.e.
,

S^{k}_{t}=\big(V(a_{t}^{k})\cup C^{k}_{t}\big)\setminus\textstyle\bigcup_{k^{\prime}\neq k}C^{k^{\prime}}_{t}.

With this, the supports satisfy Assumption
3.1
. The adjustment is needed due to the two-layer structure: a datapoint clustered into microcluster
q
and assigned to macrocluster
k
may be closer to the center of another macrocluster
k^{\prime}\neq k
. Note that for continuous supports, such adjustments are negligible, so datapoints can be discarded.

Online updating procedure

For
t\geq 1
, when we observe a new datapoint
\hat{u}
, we compute its distance to the center of the microcluster whose support contains it,
i.e.
, if
\hat{u}\in S^{q}_{t-1}
,

d_{t}=\|\hat{u}-a^{q}_{t-1}\|.

If
d_{t}\leq\rho\,\eta^{q}_{t-1}
, where
\eta^{q}_{t-1}
is the cluster’s RMSE and
\rho\geq 1
is a fixed multiplier (
\rho=1.25
in our experiments), we assign
\hat{u}
to this cluster.
We then update all microcluster weights, as well as the mean and RMSE of the assigned microcluster. The macroclusters are adjusted accordingly, based on their constituent microclusters.
Otherwise, we create a new microcluster with this datapoint as its center, and assign it the weight
1/n_{t}
. The weights of the other microclusters are decreased accordingly, as in the Reclustering weight update of Section
6.1
. If the number of microclusters then exceeds
Q
, we merge the two with the closest centers: the merged center is the weighted average of the two, with mean, RMSE, and weight combined accordingly, and the supports reassigned as above.
Every 50 steps, we perform approximate
k
-centers on the
Q
microclusters, warm-starting at the previous centers, and refresh the
K
macroclusters.
After time
\tau<\infty
, we only cluster points based on the latest supports
\mathcal{S}^{K}_{\tau}
.
