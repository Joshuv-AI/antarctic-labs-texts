Transcriptomic profiling across stages of non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer identifies fibroblast activation protein-alpha as a stromal biomarker associated with progression
Authors: Murat Akand, J. Alberto Nakauma-González, Tatjana Jatsenko, Thomas Gevaert, Loïc Baekelandt, Marcella Baldewijns, Joris Robert Vermeesch, Frank Van der Aa, Steven Joniau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundT1 non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) represents a biologically aggressive subgroup with substantial heterogeneity in recurrence and progression risk. Current clinicopathological risk stratification tools lack sufficient precision to identify patients at the highest risk of progression to muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC).ObjectiveTo characterize transcriptomic differences between T1 and < T1 (Ta/Tis) NMIBC and to explore the association of fibroblast activation protein-α (FAP) gene expression with disease progression.MethodsTranscriptomic profiling was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue from 66 patients with primary, treatment-naïve NMIBC and 5 patients with T2 disease (included for exploratory comparisons). Analyses included differential gene expression, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), molecular subtyping, immune cell deconvolution, and evaluation of FAP expression in relation to recurrence and progression. External validation of FAP was conducted in three independent NMIBC cohorts.ResultsT1 tumors demonstrated a distinct transcriptomic profile compared with < T1 tumors, characterized by enrichment of cell cycle-related and metabolic pathways and a higher prevalence of aggressive molecular subtypes. Despite these molecular differences, no statistically significant differences in recurrence-free, progression-free, cancer-specific, and overall survival were observed, likely reflecting limited event numbers. Among recurrent tumors, early recurrences (≤ 24 months) were associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition signatures. FAP expression increased with tumor stage (p = 0.0005) and was associated with progression (p = 0.002) and mortality (p = 0.01). Patients with tumors in the highest quartile of FAP expression had worse progression-free survival. This association was consistently observed in three external NMIBC cohorts.ConclusionsT1 NMIBC exhibits distinct transcriptomic features suggestive of increased biological aggressiveness. Elevated FAP expression is reproducibly associated with progression risk across multiple cohorts, supporting its potential role as a biomarker of aggressive disease. Given the limited number of progression events, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrant prospective validation before clinical implementation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10020-026-01497-2.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) is the ninth most common malignancy worldwide and the most frequent cancer of the urinary tract (Siegel et al. 2023; Bray et al. 2024). In Europe and North America, over 90% of cases are urothelial carcinoma. Approximately 75% of cases present as non-muscle-invasive BC (NMIBC) and 25% as muscle-invasive BC (MIBC) (Gontero et al. 2024). NMIBC is heterogeneous, with varying risks of recurrence and progression. Its high prevalence and recurrence rates complicate its management, and efforts to identify prognostic factors are currently underway. The European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) and the Club Urológico Español de Tratamiento Oncológico (CUETO) risk calculators incorporate tumor- and patient-related variables (e.g., tumor size, number, stage and grade, presence of concomitant carcinoma in situ [CIS], recurrence rate, age, and sex) (Sylvester et al. 2006; Fernandez-Gomez et al. 2009). Based on these criteria, the European Association of Urology (EAU) Guidelines categorize NMIBC into low-, intermediate-, high-, and very high-risk groups, with corresponding treatment recommendations (Gontero et al. 2024). However, significant heterogeneity in terms of oncological outcomes persists within the same risk groups, largely reflecting the underlying molecular diversity of BC.

Molecular classification efforts have further characterized this heterogeneity. Initial transcriptomic studies identified five distinct subtypes across MIBC and NMIBC (Sjödahl et al. 2012), followed by the distinction between basal and luminal subtypes in MIBC (Biton et al. 2014; Damrauer et al. 2014). Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have established a consensus of six molecular subtypes of MIBC (Kamaoun et al. 2020). Likewise, transcriptomic profiling of NMIBC has revealed distinct subclasses, highlighting its molecular heterogeneity (Lindskrog et al. 2021; Jong et al. 2023; Cotillas et al. 2024), although these subclassifications have yet to be clinically applied.

Among NMIBC, T1 tumors represent a particularly high-risk subset. They are often high grade (HG), with elevated recurrence and progression rates compared to Ta and Tis tumors (Jong et al. 2023). Roughly one-third of patients with T1HG disease progress to MIBC despite intravesical bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) (Robertson et al. 2020). Such BCG unresponsiveness is clinically significant, as it increases the risk of progression and may delay timely radical cystectomy, worsening outcomes (Gontero et al. 2024).

In our previous work, we demonstrated that fibroblast activation protein-alpha (FAP) protein expression correlated with tumor progression in T1 tumors (Muilwijk et al. 2024). In this study, we employed comprehensive transcriptomic profiling to compare T1 tumors with less advanced stages (Ta and Tis) and to elucidate the distinct molecular signatures underlying the increased aggressiveness of the T1 stage. We also aimed to validate the correlation between FAP expression and progression.

Patients and methods

Patients and samples

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee Research UZ/KU Leuven (approval date: 25/07/2022, approval number: S66536) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. Written informed consent had been obtained preoperatively from all patients for the scientific use of their data and leftover tissue samples.

Patients diagnosed with BC who underwent transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) at the University Hospitals Leuven between 2015 and 2019 were identified from the Vlaams Ziekenhuisnetwerk – KU Leuven (VZNKUL) registry for the Quality Control Indicators Program (NCT03973671) (Akand et al. 2019; Akand et al. 2024). Inclusion criteria were: i) primary, treatment-naïve BC; ii) macroscopically complete resection at TURBT; iii) available tissue blocks with adequate residual material; iv) pathological verification of stage and grade by a dedicated uro-pathologist; and v) adequate tumor load.

Information on repeat TURBT (reTURBT) was extracted from the VZNKUL registry, where reTURBT is prospectively recorded as a quality indicator (Akand et al. 2024; Muilwijk et al. 2023). In accordance with the EAU Guidelines, reTURBT was planned for all high-risk patients. For the present cohort, reTURBT performance (yes/no) was retrieved along with the other demographic, operative, and follow-up data; however, the underlying reasons for omission were not collected. ReTURBT was not included as a covariate in downstream transcriptomic or outcome analyses due to incomplete data and potential selection bias.

Tumors were staged and graded according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) system (8th edition) and the World Health Organization (WHO) classification (2004/2016). NMIBC cases were stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups (with a highest-risk subgroup) according to the 2019 EAU Guidelines (Babjuk et al. 2019). Recurrence was defined as the presence of a new tumor of any stage and grade, while progression was defined as ≥ T2 disease or metastasis.

After patient selection for screening, hematoxylin–eosin (HE)-stained diagnostic slides were re-evaluated by the same uro-pathologist to verify stage, grade, and adequacy of the remaining tumor. Discordance between the original pathology report and central re-review was recorded, including changes in tumor stage and/or grade. Cases with discordant diagnoses were reclassified according to central pathology review and included in the appropriate subgroups if sufficient tumor material was available. Tumors with insufficient tumor load were excluded from further analysis regardless of concordance status. Five 10-µm formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections per patient were cut for RNA extraction. The same uro-pathologist annotated two flanking 5-µm HE-stained sections to confirm tumor regions and semi-quantitatively grade tumor load (from + to + + +; + + or + + + were considered adequate).

Library preparation and sequencing

RNA was extracted using the Maxwell® RSC RNA FFPE kit with the Maxwell® RSC RNA 48 Instrument (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions, and then quantified using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer (ThermoScientific, Paisley, UK). RNA quality was assessed with the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), providing RNA integrity number (RIN) and DV200 metrics (dinucleotide value; percentage of RNA fragments > 200 nucleotides in size).

Strand-specific, poly(A) + RNA-seq libraries were prepared using 5 µl RNA with the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Prep kit (Lexogen GmbH, Vienna, Austria). Briefly, poly(A) + mRNA was extracted from total RNA, fragmented, and converted to cDNA, which was end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with Illumina dual barcode adapters. Libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq™ 6000 instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using the S4 reagent kit (300 cycles) to generate 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads.

Analysis

The quality of raw sequencing data was analyzed with FastQC. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were removed from the raw data using Trimmomatic, and rRNA contamination was filtered using the SortMeRNA tool. Filtered reads were aligned to the reference genome (hg38) using HISAT2. The output SAM files were converted to the BAM format using SAMtools, followed by sorting and indexing for downstream analyses. The number of reads mapped to each gene was counted using FeatureCounts, and differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 in R.

For the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), the Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) were used to compare sample groups (Liberzon et al. 2015; Subramanian et al. 2005). In addition, single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) was applied to estimate the activity of Hallmark pathways and phenotypic scores for each sample. The phenotypic gene sets for luminal, basal, squamous, neuroendocrine, and stroma were previously defined (Nakauma-González et al. 2022). Immune cell fraction estimation was performed using quanTIseq (Finotello and Trajanoski 2018; Finotello et al. 2019).

Transcripts were normalized using a variance-stabilizing transformation, and these normalized values were used for FAP expression comparison between groups and gene ranking.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and percentages. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess associations between continuous and categorical variables. Associations between progression and FAP expression (continuous variable) were analyzed using logistic regression (Wald test). For multiple testing, p-values were adjusted (q-values) using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6 (https://cran.r-project.org/).

Results

Patient cohort and clinicopathological characteristics associated with tumor progression

Of 5180 TURBTs registered between 2013 and 2020, 1352 TURBTs performed for 684 unique patients with urothelial carcinoma at our center between 2015 and 2019 were identified. Primary TURBT was performed for 422 patients, of whom 264 were selected for screening. Through re-evaluation of newly HE-stained first and last slides of each patient's section series by a dedicated uro-pathologist, patients with an incorrect diagnosis, minimal or no residual FFPE material, or low tumor burden were excluded. During central pathology re-review, 90 cases (34%) demonstrated discordance with the original diagnosis, primarily involving stage and/or grade reassignment (Supplementary Table 1). Discordant cases with adequate tumor material were reclassified and included in the corresponding subgroups, whereas those with insufficient tumor load were excluded. RNA was ultimately extracted from 94 patients; 3 samples were excluded due to low RNA quality and 20 samples were excluded due to low read numbers, leaving 71 treatment-naïve patients with a primary diagnosis of BC for further analysis. A summary of the patient selection process is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The median age was 73 years; 15.5% of patients were female, and 73.2% were former or current smokers. Among the 71 patients, 12 had Ta low grade (LG), 16 TaHG, 5 Tis, 33 T1HG, and 5 T2 tumors, the latter included for exploratory comparisons between NMIBC and MIBC. Among high-risk patients, reTURBT was performed more frequently in T1HG tumors than in TaHG tumors (75.8% vs. 37.5%, respectively).

Of 54 BCG-indicated high-risk patients, 75.9% received adequate BCG induction, while 60.8% received adequate BCG maintenance therapy according to the definition of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (Food and Drug Administration Guidance Document: BCG-unresponsive nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer: Developing drugs and biologics for treatment (Guidance for industry) (2018). Among patients in whom BCG induction was indicated but not initiated (n = 7), reasons included comorbidity and/or advanced age (n = 4), BCG shortage (n = 1), surgeon’s decision (n = 1), and patient preference (n = 1). Among those who initiated induction therapy, only one patient discontinued treatment prematurely due to severe adverse effects. Maintenance BCG therapy was not initiated in an additional 13 patients due to BCG-unresponsive disease (n = 5), treatment-related toxicity influencing patient and/or surgeon decision (n = 4), comorbidity or general condition (n = 2), or unreported reasons (n = 2). Early discontinuation of maintenance therapy due to toxicity occurred in one patient. Detailed treatment-related information is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

During a median follow-up of 55 months, 28 patients (39.4%) experienced recurrence and 6 patients (8.6%) experienced progression. Molecular characteristics of T1 tumors in comparison to < T1 tumors

The GSEA revealed a distinct transcriptional profile in T1 tumors compared to lower-stage (< T1) tumors. T1 tumors exhibited upregulation of proliferation- and cell cycle-related pathways, including E2F targets, the G2M checkpoint, the mitotic spindle, and mTORC1 signaling, while the p53 pathway and TGF-β signaling were downregulated (Fig. 1A). Transcriptional subtyping demonstrated significantly more aggressive phenotypes in T1 tumors, with higher frequencies of UROMOL class 2a, Lund Taxonomy (LundTax) genomically unstable (GU), and luminal unstable (LumU) consensus MIBC subtypes compared to < T1 tumors (Fig. 1B). Despite these molecular differences, survival analyses showed no significant differences in overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), cancer-specific survival (CSS), or progression-free survival (PFS) between T1 and < T1 groups (Fig. 1C).

Molecular associations with tumor progression and recurrence

The molecular landscape revealed associations between the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway and both tumor progression and recurrence (Supplementary Fig. 2). Transcriptomic molecular subtypes differed between progressors and non-progressors, whereas tumors with and without recurrences showed similar subtype distributions. None of the evaluated phenotypes was associated with recurrences; however, tumors with progression had a significantly higher stromal phenotypic score (q = 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 3). No differences in immune cell infiltration were observed between progressors and non-progressors, or between recurrent and non-recurrent cases (Supplementary Fig. 3). Notably, progression and recurrence did not significantly impact OS; however, patients with early recurrence (≤ 24 months) had significantly worse OS (Supplementary Fig. 2). These tumors with early recurrence also exhibited higher EMT pathway enrichment compared to all recurrences or progressors.

An overview of the transcriptomics landscape highlights the distinct characteristics of tumors that subsequently recurred and the impact of recurrence timing (Fig. 2). Tumors with early recurrences, compared to those with late recurrences (> 24 months), exhibited a pathway rewiring that resembles T1 tumors, characterized by upregulation of cell cycle-related pathways, including E2F targets and the G2M checkpoint (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, early recurrences showed increased EMT pathway activity, whereas late recurrences were characterized by elevated expression of extracellular matrix and stromal genes, such as FAP, COL1A1, COL1A2, PDGFRA, DCN, and CCDC80, as well as immune-modulating genes like CD274 (PD-L1) and MMP2. Late recurrences were predominantly Ta tumors with low- to intermediate-risk EORTC recurrence scores.

Prognostic role of FAP expression

In our early work (Muilwijk et al. 2024), we identified FAP protein expression as a promising biomarker for progression in T1HG tumors. In the present study, we assessed FAP at the mRNA level and observed that FAP expression increased with tumor stage (p = 0.0005, Kruskal–Wallis test). Notably, T2 tumors showed a substantial increase compared to NMIBC samples (p = 0.0007, two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test; Supplementary Fig. 5A). The association suggests that FAP expression may serve as a marker of tumor progression (Supplementary Fig. 5B).

Logistic regression analysis (Wald test) demonstrated a significant association between higher FAP expression and progression (p = 0.002) as well as death (p = 0.01) (Fig. 3A). Progression events were concentrated in the highest quartile of FAP expression; therefore, tumors in this quartile were defined as FAP-high, and these patients exhibited generally worse outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 6). Similarly, among high-risk NMIBC patients receiving adequate BCG, FAP-high tumors were associated with worse OS (p = 0.018), CSS (p = 0.0009), and PFS (p = 0.0009), but not RFS (p = 0.26) (Fig. 3B).

The prognostic value of FAP expression was further validated in three independent NMIBC cohorts: the Rotterdam (n = 283), the Chicago (n = 73), and the UROMOL (n = 530) cohorts (Lindskrog et al. 2021; Jong et al. 2023; Robertson et al. 2020). Given the limited use of BCG in the UROMOL cohort, these analyses should be interpreted as reflecting prognostic tumor biology rather than BCG-specific treatment response. Across all cohorts, higher FAP expression was associated with worse PFS with consistent directionality of effect, although no association with RFS was observed (Fig. 4). In the Chicago cohort, however, these associations did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited sample size and number of events as reflected by wide confidence intervals (CIs) in hazard ratio (HR) estimates, and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Overall, these results support FAP as a potential biomarker of tumor progression in NMIBC, although they should be interpreted with caution given the heterogeneity across external cohorts. Effect estimates with corresponding 95% CIs are provided to reflect uncertainty. In Cox regression analyses, higher FAP expression was associated with increased risk of progression across cohorts (HR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.02–2.46 for Rotterdam; HR = 2.92, 95% CI: 0.78–10.9 for Chicago; HR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.1–3.59 for UROMOL), although statistical significance was not reached in the Chicago cohort.

Discussion

In this study, we identified clear transcriptomic differences between T1 and < T1 NMIBC, underscoring the biological heterogeneity that exists beyond conventional clinicopathological staging. T1 tumors were characterized by enrichment of cell cycle- and proliferation-related pathways, including E2F targets and the G2M checkpoint, alongside downregulation of tumor-suppressive signaling such as p53 and TGF-β. TGF-β signaling exhibits a well-established context-dependent role in cancer, including bladder cancer. In early-stage disease, canonical (SMAD-dependent) TGF-β signaling exerts tumor-suppressive effects by inhibiting epithelial proliferation and promoting apoptosis. However, during tumor progression, TGF-β signaling frequently shifts toward a pro-tumorigenic role through both canonical and non-canonical pathways, promoting EMT, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune evasion (Derynck et al. 2021; Katsuno and Derynck 2021). In this context, the apparent downregulation observed in our bulk transcriptomic analysis likely reflects tumor cell–intrinsic signaling, whereas stromal TGF-β activity may not be fully captured, highlighting compartment-specific differences in pathway activity.

These molecular features were accompanied by a higher prevalence of aggressive transcriptomic subtypes across multiple classification frameworks, suggesting that biological aggressiveness is established early in disease evolution. Although we did not perform survival analyses stratified by molecular subtype due to the limited number of events in our cohort, prior studies have demonstrated that molecular subtypes such as UROMOL class 2a, LumU, and GU tumors are associated with more aggressive disease behavior and differential response to intravesical therapy, including BCG (Lindskrog et al. 2021; Jong et al. 2023). These observations support the biological relevance of our findings and suggest that integration of molecular subtyping into clinical risk stratification may improve prognostic accuracy.

Importantly, despite these pronounced molecular differences, no statistically significant differences in RFS, PFS, CSS, and OS were observed between T1 and < T1 tumors. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent clinical behavior; rather, it most likely reflects limited statistical power due to the relatively small cohort size and number of progression events. The absence of survival differences therefore emphasizes the need for molecular biomarkers that may capture aggressive biology before it becomes clinically apparent.

Beyond stage-based comparisons, our analyses revealed that disease course was influenced by distinct biological programs associated with recurrence timing. Early recurrences (≤ 24 months) were characterized by enrichment of EMT and proliferative signaling, closely resembling the molecular landscape of primary T1 tumors. In contrast, late recurrences (> 24 months) were predominantly Ta-stage tumors with low to intermediate EORTC recurrence risk and exhibited transcriptional features related to stromal remodeling and extracellular matrix organization. These observations suggest a “temporal rewiring” of tumor biology, whereby early recurrences may reflect intrinsic tumor aggressiveness, while late recurrences may arise from microenvironmental and stromal dynamics.

Within the stromal context, FAP emerged as the most consistent molecular correlate of disease progression. In our earlier work, we demonstrated that immunohistochemical expression of FAP was the only prognostic parameter predicting progression to MIBC in a retrospective nested case–control study involving 40 T1HG NMIBC patients who progressed and were pair-matched with 80 T1HG patients who did not progress (Muilwijk et al. 2024). Here, we extend these findings by demonstrating that FAP expression at the mRNA level increased with advancing tumor stage and was significantly associated with progression and mortality in our cohort. Patients with tumors in the highest quartile of FAP expression experienced worse PFS. Although the quartile-based definition of “FAP-high” is data-driven and exploratory, the robustness of this association was supported by consistent findings across three independent, external NMIBC cohorts. Notably, FAP was associated with progression but not with recurrence, reinforcing its relevance as a marker of aggressive disease rather than tumor multiplicity.

The biological plausibility of FAP as a progression-associated biomarker is well supported. FAP is a key marker of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which play a central role in tumor invasion, EMT, angiogenesis, immune suppression, and therapeutic resistance (Sahai et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2023). Experimental studies have demonstrated that FAP-positive stromal cells can directly suppress antitumor immune responses and promote tumor progression by attenuating TNF-α and IFN-γ responses, as well as impairing CD8 + T-cell function (Huang et al. 2023; Kraman et al. 2010). Beyond fibroblasts, FAP is expressed in subsets of macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), expanding its immunoregulatory role. Recent single-cell studies highlight the heterogeneity of CAFs, with multiple functional subsets contributing to tumor progression (Caramelo et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2025). In the context of NMIBC, where intravesical BCG relies heavily on immune-mediated tumor control, stromal-driven immune modulation may be particularly relevant. Our findings therefore align with emerging evidence that the tumor microenvironment, rather than tumor cells alone, critically shapes disease trajectory.

In addition to its biological role, FAP has recently emerged as a target for molecular imaging. Fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI)-based positron emission tomography (PET) imaging has shown promising results across multiple solid tumors, including urothelial carcinoma, enabling visualization of tumor-associated stroma with high tumor-to-background contrast (Novruzov et al. 2026; Unterrainer et al. 2025; Albers Acosta et al. 2025). This approach may provide a non-invasive means to identify aggressive, stromal-rich tumors and could complement tissue-based biomarkers in risk stratification and treatment planning.

Current NMIBC risk stratification tools, including the EORTC and CUETO models, are limited in their ability to predict progression at the individual patient level. Integration of molecular subtyping and stromal biomarkers such as FAP expression may further refine risk stratification beyond conventional clinicopathological models. For example, patients with high-risk molecular subtypes or elevated FAP expression may represent a subgroup with increased likelihood of progression despite standard intravesical therapy, potentially warranting intensified surveillance, early consideration of radical cystectomy, or enrollment in clinical trials. Conversely, patients with less aggressive molecular profiles may be suitable for more conservative management. Moreover, given the role of CAFs in modulating immune responses, FAP expression may also influence sensitivity to intravesical therapies and emerging immunotherapeutic strategies. However, the present data do not support immediate clinical implementation. The prognostic value of FAP should be validated prospectively, ideally alongside standardized assessment of protein expression and integration into multivariable risk models.

This study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective design and modest number of progression events limit statistical power and preclude extensive multivariable modeling. As a result, associations between FAP expression and outcomes should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Second, RNA sequencing was performed on FFPE tissues, which may have affected RNA quality despite stringent quality control. Third, the cohort was heterogenous with respect to stage, grade, and BCG exposure. Although analyses were restricted to treatment-naïve patients, variability in BCG adequacy and early response assessment may have influenced outcomes. Some of the high-risk patients were classified as BCG-unresponsive before completing adequate BCG treatment, as our departmental protocol includes a control cystoscopy following the BCG induction phase. Incomplete BCG exposure, including non-initiation and early discontinuation of induction or maintenance therapy, reflects real-world clinical practice but may have introduced bias in outcome analyses. As BCG adequacy is a key determinant of oncological outcomes in NMIBC, variability in treatment delivery may have influenced progression risk estimates and should be considered when interpreting treatment-related findings. Fourth, reTURBT, a recognized quality indicator in NMIBC management, was not uniformly performed across the cohort. Although prospectively recorded within the VZNKUL registry, reTURBT was omitted in a subset of patients due to clinical judgment, patient-related factors, or logistical constraints, and was performed more frequently in T1HG compared to TaHG tumors. Given its potential impact on staging accuracy and oncological outcomes, the absence of systematic reTURBT represents a source of heterogeneity and possible residual confounding. To partially mitigate this limitation, we restricted the cohort to patients with a macroscopically complete initial resection; however, this approach may also have reduced the clinical indication for reTURBT in selected cases. Fifth, our patients were stratified for recurrence and progression risk according to the EAU Guidelines version 2019, which may have influenced the results. Sixth, immune cell deconvolution using bulk transcriptomic data may lack the resolution to detect subtle but biologically relevant immune differences. Lastly, correlation between FAP mRNA expression and quantitative immunohistochemical measures was not assessed in the present cohort due to lack of matched data, and is currently being investigated in an ongoing study, representing a limitation. However, prior work from our group demonstrated a strong association between FAP protein expression and progression (Muilwijk et al. 2024).

Despite these limitations, several strengths enhance the credibility of our findings. All patients were treatment-naïve, minimizing confounding by prior therapy. Rigorous pathological re-review ensured accurate staging and adequate tumor content. Finally, external validation across three independent cohorts substantially strengthens the generalizability and reproducibility of the observed association between FAP expression and progression.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that T1 NMIBC harbors distinct transcriptomic features indicative of aggressive biology and identify FAP as a reproducible marker associated with progression risk. These findings highlight the importance of stromal biology in NMIBC progression and support further prospective evaluation of FAP as part of integrated molecular risk stratification strategies.
