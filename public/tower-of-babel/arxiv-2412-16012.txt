Discovery of an intrinsic non-Hermitian phase transition in a bulk condensed-matter system
Jingwen Li, Michael Turaev, Masakazu Matsubara, Kristin Kliemt, Cornelius Krellner, Shovon Pal, Manfred Fiebig, Johann Kroha, ∗ Corresponding author. Email: manfred.fiebig@mat.ethz.ch, jkroha@uni-bonn.de

Abstract
Across regular phase transitions, systems remain in thermal equilibrium. However, when a system is driven far from equilibrium, non-Hermitian phase transitions may arise where the dynamical behavior rather than steady properties undergo a qualitative change at a critical, so-called exceptional point. We experimentally realize a non-Hermitian phase transition in a bulk condensed-matter system. Optical excitation creates charge carriers in ferromagnetic EuO. In a temperature-dependent interplay with the Hermitian transition to ferromagnetic order, a non-Hermitian change of the relaxation dynamics occurs, manifesting in our time-resolved reflection data as the transition from biexponential real to single-exponential complex decay. Our theory models this behavior and suggests that non-Hermitian phase transitions may generically emerge in bulk condensed matter.



Systems that undergo a phase transition typically remain in thermodynamic equilibrium at all times, leading to a lasting change in ground state properties. A well-known example is the emergence of spontaneous magnetization at the transition between the paramagnetic and ferromagnetic phases. To a good approximation, this holds even when new states are transiently stabilized on short timescales, for example, when pulsed lasers are used to control magnetically ordered states in the field of ultrafast magnetization dynamics
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Driving a system truly away from thermodynamic equilibrium could therefore create qualitatively new states of matter that are characterized by their dynamics rather than their ground-state properties and cannot be realized otherwise. This leads us to the subject of non-Hermitian phase transitions (NHPTs)
[
7
]
. In contrast to equilibrium systems, which are invariant with respect to the displacement in or, more specifically, reversal of time as a fundamental law of nature, excitation and dissipation break time-reversal symmetry. This usually manifests itself in an asymmetry between the drive and loss terms. The matrix connecting the response of a system’s properties to the driving fields becomes non-Hermitian, its eigenvalues in general complex, and its eigenvectors non-orthogonal. Whereas in an equilibrium phase transition the free energies of two static phases (or, at zero temperature, the energies of two quantum states) become equal, a NHPT occurs when two real eigenvalues of a nonequilibrium response matrix
\chi
become equal and change into a single complex-conjugate pair. Formally, this can be expressed by eigenvalues
\gamma_{1,2}
of the form
\gamma_{1,2}=x\pm\sqrt{y}
, where
y
changes its sign from “
+
” to “
-
” at the exceptional point (EP)
[
8
]
. In consequence, the corresponding eigenvectors coalesce, which means physically that the two modes of the system dynamics collapse into a single one. This is accompanied by a qualitative change in dynamical behavior with enhanced sensitivity to the variation of external parameters near the EP. Therefore, a NHPT generally represents a transition in the dynamics of a system rather than in its equilibrium properties, where the latter would be described by a Hermitian Hamiltonian on the microscopic level
[
9
]
. It is this dynamical behavior that has generated considerable interest in NHPTs. Most of its studies are theoretical
[
10
,
11
]
, and experimental realizations have focused on single-spin or nanostructured magnetic
[
12
,
13
]
, optical
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
, ultracold-atomic-gas
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, or photon-condensate systems
[
22
]
, all of which are engineered. The possibility of an intrinsic NHPT in a homogeneous bulk solid-state system has not even been discussed because it was considered infeasible to establish the required extreme off-equilibrium conditions in the bulk.

Here, we report on the observation of an intrinsic NHPT in the bulk ferromagnetic semiconductor europium monoxide (EuO) after optical excitation of electrons from the
4f
valence to the
5d
conduction band of the Eu ions. Time-resolved pump-probe measurements of the linear reflectivity reveal the characteristic change of the ensuing relaxation dynamics from two real to a single complex-conjugate decay rate. The EP of this transition is observed at
\approx 85
K and therefore distinctly higher than the Curie temperature
T_{\rm C}
of the Hermitian phase transition to ferromagnetic order at 69 K. Our theory models this behavior and demonstrates that, as a unique aspect of many-body systems, a Hermitian and a NHPT may be linked to each other as originating from the same microscopic coupling mechanism, although representing very different physical phenomena occurring at different critical temperatures. We finally propose, based on the common ingredients of our theoretical model, that NHPTs should be allowed for a large class of condensed-matter systems, where they can be exploited to sensitively control bulk dynamic properties.

Signatures of a non-Hermitian phase transition in bulk condensed-matter

EuO exhibits a rock-salt structure and has a semiconductor gap of
1.2
eV. The ferromagnetic order of the Eu
4f^{7}
ions is mediated by virtual magnetic polarons involving charge-carrier fluctuations in the spatially extended Eu
5d(t_{2g})
orbitals that interconnect the tightly bound Eu
4f^{7}
magnetic moments
[
23
]
. Doping charge carriers into the
5d(t_{2g})
conduction orbitals by chemically substituting Eu with Gd atoms further enhances
T_{\rm C}

[
24
,
25
]
through the Ruderman-Kittel-Kasuya-Yosida interaction
[
26
]
mediated by the conduction electrons. Alternatively,
4f^{7}\to 5d(t_{2g})
photodoping leads to a transient strengthening of the magnetic order
[
27
]
. Both effects underpin the importance of the magnetic coupling
J_{df}
between the
4f
local moments and the
5d
conduction spins in EuO. The ferromagnetic transition is accompanied by a pronounced Stoner band splitting, which shifts the spectral weight below the Fermi energy and, thus, makes the material metallic with nearly completely spin-polarized electrons at low temperature
[
29
,
25
]
.

We use EuO single crystals grown by the gradient cooling technique with a Bridgman setup
[
31
]
. The crystals are cut to yield a [100]-oriented surface, lapped with SiC films to a thickness of 280
\mu
m, and polished with Al
2
O
3
films in ethanol. In a pump-probe experiment sketched in Fig.
1
A and further detailed in
[
28
]
, a 120-fs pump pulse of 1.55 eV, generated by an amplified Ti:sapphire laser system, promotes electrons from the Eu
4f
valence to the
5d(t_{2g})
conduction band. Note that the total spin
S=7/2
is conserved in this transition (Fig.
1
E). We then record
\Delta R(t)/R
, the change in reflectivity with respect to the non-pumped system after a delay
t
. For this we use 120-fs laser pulses at 1.31 eV, which are emitted by a downstream optical parametric amplifier and incident under an angle of 45
∘
. These pulses probe the dipole-allowed, spin-conserving electronic Eu
4f\to 5d(t_{2g})
transition at 1.29 eV resonantly (Fig.
1
E). Figure
1
B shows the corresponding reflectivity time traces after optical pumping between 10 K and 296 K.

Across this temperature range, the relaxation behavior exhibits a drastic qualitative change. At all temperatures, we observe an initial increase in the reflectivity that peaks within the duration of the laser pulse. At the lowest temperatures, subsequent relaxation exhibits a rapid and continuous decay of the reflectivity change on a time scale of about 1 ps, followed by a much slower second relaxation (Fig.
1
C). In the ferromagnetic phase, the normalized reflectivity change
\Delta R/R
settles to a positive value, indicating that the optically induced conduction-band population is energetically stabilized by the aforementioned Stoner shift of the majority conduction band below the Fermi energy
[
29
,
25
]
at least throughout our observed time range of 20 ps. Toward the highest temperatures, however, the initial reflectivity peak is followed by a single rapid decay until
\Delta R
becomes negative before approaching the original value at
\Delta R=0
(Fig.
1
D). For the pump fluence of 100
\mu
J/cm
2
used in the experiment, the transition from the all-positive to the partly negative evolution of
\Delta R/R
occurs in the paramagnetic phase, at a threshold temperature
T^{*}=(84.4\pm 2.6)
K or
(87.8\pm 1.6)
K in two independent fits that are discussed in
[
28
]
. The values are consistent and lie clearly above
T_{\rm C}
. In the following, we use
T^{*}\approx 85
K, as the threshold temperature.

We point out that common phenomena known to possibly cause a negative dynamic response cannot explain the behavior in Fig.
1
D. Among the multitude of effects that might come to mind are oscillations induced by coupling to coherent lattice vibrations
[
32
]
, Auger recombination
[
33
,
34
]
, band-filling effects in semiconductors
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
temperature or strain-dependent band shifts
[
39
]
, three-temperature-model relaxation
[
40
]
, lattice heating, excitation of optical phonons, or paramagnetic excitations
[
41
]
. Those and other effects are discussed and ruled out in
[
28
]
. In addition, none of these effects can explain (i) the very specific temperature and pump-fluence dependencies of the relaxation rates shown below and (ii) the presence of a critical temperature
T^{*}
for the onset of these dependencies.

Understanding the non-Hermitian phase transition in EuO

Having thus excluded known physical origins for the measured pump-induced transient reflectivity decrease, we now show that our observed response exhibits, in fact, the unique signature of a NHPT. For further analysis, we extract the relaxation rates
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
at
T<T^{*}
by a biexponential fit to the experimental data according to

\frac{\Delta R(t)}{R}=C_{1}{\rm e}^{-\gamma_{1}t}+C_{2}{\rm e}^{-\gamma_{2}t}+\Delta_{0},

(1)

with real
\gamma_{1,2}
,
C_{1,2}
and
C_{1,2}>0
.
\Delta_{0}
is the long-term-stable Stoner-shift-induced offset, which approaches zero toward
T^{*}
. To describe the negative response at
T>T^{*}
, there are two options. First, we can assume that either
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
becomes negative. As we show in
[
28
]
, the associated fits do not converge well, especially in the critical temperature range, where the negative contributions emerge, irrespective of the chosen pump fluence. In addition, as discussed above, we have excluded contributions from processes that involve negative exponential behavior for physical reasons. Second, we allow for complex relaxation rates and set
\gamma_{1,2}=\gamma^{\prime}\pm{\rm i}\gamma^{\prime\prime}
and
C_{1,2}=C_{0}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\varphi_{0}}
, with real values
\gamma^{\prime}
,
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
,
C_{0}
, and
\varphi_{0}
, so that real values for
\Delta R(t)/R
are retained. This fit converges well at all temperatures above
T^{*}
and for different laser fluences, and the results for the relaxation time(s)
\tau={\rm Re}(\gamma)^{-1}
and the relaxation parameter
\gamma^{\prime\prime}={\rm Im}(\gamma)
are shown in Fig.
2
. As a detailed fit error analysis (Fig.
S3
) in
[
28
]
shows, we observe biexponential relaxation with two relaxation times for
T<T^{*}
, whereas for
T>T^{*}
the dynamical behavior collapses to a single relaxation mode with regions of positive and negative measured data caused by the complex nature of the relaxation rate. Degeneracy occurs at
T^{*}
where a single exponential (with an additional powerlaw factor) describes the relaxation according to the inset of Fig.
2
A. This collapse at a specific temperature
T^{*}
is most striking in its resemblance to a system undergoing a NHPT. In particular, whereas several physical processes could in principle account for a negative signal like in Fig.
1
D and have been evaluated (and ruled out) as stated above, none of these processes except for the NHPT can explain the very distinctive square-root dependence of the relaxation parameter on temperature and pump fluence
P
shown in Figs.
2
, B and C, respectively.

In the following, we present a theoretical model that explains the occurrence of a NHPT in EuO in a natural way and describes all the temperature- and fluence-dependent features on the same footing, including the cusp visible in Fig.
2
A at the Curie temperature
T_{\rm C}
. To do this, we first identify the dynamical variables active in our material. The change in reflectivity after optical excitation is proportional to the density of photoexcited charge carriers in the conduction band, which recombine with holes in the valence band by the emission of a photon. Excitations of this type, Wannier excitons, have long been studied in EuO
[
42
]
, and an associated absorption spectrum is shown in Fig.
S2
. The biexponential relaxation after optical pumping at low temperature is in good agreement with the fact that there are two types of electronic excitations. These are “bright” (optically excitable) and “dark” (non-radiatively decaying) excitons, both formed as a bound state of a
5d(t_{2g})
electron and a
4f
hole in the valence band
[
43
]
. Furthermore, it is known that the conduction-electron spin is strongly coupled to the Eu
4f^{7}
local moments via a Heisenberg exchange coupling
J_{df}
(see the introduction on EuO above).

Putting this together, an ultrashort optical laser pulse excites electrons across the semiconductor gap of 1.2 eV without spin flip (Fig.
1
E), thus creating bright excitons which can recombine by the emission of a photon and induce the measured reflectivity change (Fig.
3
, A and B, D and E). Alternatively, a conduction electron can flip its spin with a local Eu
4f
moment through the coupling
J_{df}
, thus transforming a bright spin-0 exciton into a dark spin-1 exciton and vice versa (Fig.
3
, C and F). The spin-1 exciton cannot recombine by emitting a photon via the spin-conserving dipole decay, thus reducing the reflectivity below its equilibrium value (
\Delta R/R<0
). During the bright-dark exciton transformation, the orientation of the randomly oriented Eu
4f
moments is also altered. Because of the different energies of spin-0 and spin-1 excitons (see Fig.
1
E), the bright-dark transformation processes are dissipative and thus energy-non-conserving. The energy difference is absorbed or provided by a thermal bath of phonons at cryostat temperature
T
. This induces an asymmetry between the bright-dark and dark-bright transformation amplitudes,
\Gamma^{+}
,
\Gamma^{-}
, which breaks the time-reversal symmetry and makes these amplitudes temperature dependent, as formally derived by the Lindblad formalism
[
44
]
.

In the paramagnetic phase, this leads to a set of two coupled rate equations for the densities of bright and dark excitons,
n_{\rm b,d}(t)
. The equations are parameterized by a real-valued, asymmetric
2\times 2
matrix
\chi(T)
, see Eq.
S3
in
[
28
]
. Its eigenvalues are the relaxation rates
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
of the bright-dark–exciton-coupled system, which are the solutions of a quadratic equation of the form

\gamma_{1,2}=\left[\gamma_{\rm b}+\gamma_{\rm d}\pm\sqrt{(\gamma_{\rm b}-\gamma_{\rm d})^{2}-4\omega_{0}^{2}}\penalty\ \right]/2.

(2)

Here,
\gamma_{\rm b}
,
\gamma_{\rm d}
are the diagonal elements of
\chi
, that is, the bright- and dark-exciton decay rates without mutual coupling, and
\omega_{0}^{2}
is the modulus of the product of the off-diagonal elements of
\chi
. If there was no dissipation,
\omega_{0}
would be the natural frequency of the bright-dark–exciton-coupled system. Figures
4
, A and B, show the relaxation times
\tau_{\rm fast}
,
\tau_{\rm slow}
and the relaxation parameter
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
as the inverse real parts and as the imaginary part of these eigenvalues, respectively, whose temperature dependence is inherited from the transition amplitudes
\Gamma^{\pm}(T)
. The EP is reached when the argument of the square root in Eq.
2
, crosses zero at the critical temperature
T^{*}
of the NHPT. As the possibly most striking and unique feature associated with the NHPT, Eq.
2
predicts that the imaginary part of the relaxation rate behaves as
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\sim\sqrt{T-T^{*}}
for
T\geq T^{*}
, see Fig.
4
B. This is because the argument of the square root crosses zero linearly at the critical values. The fact that the experiment shows precisely this behavior (see Fig.
2
B), which is not expected from other physical phenomena, is an unambiguous confirmation that the optically pumped EuO exhibits a NHPT with the sample temperature as the control parameter.

In the ferromagnetic phase, the
4f
spin orientation acquires, in addition to the time-dependent variation, a non-zero, static equilibrium value, the magnetization, which has the temperature dependence
M(T)=M_{0}\sqrt{1-T/T_{\rm C}}
. This temperature dependence manifests itself in the relaxation times as a cusp visible in Fig.
4
A near the Curie temperature
T_{\rm C}
. A similar feature appears in the experimental data, Fig.
2
A, at
T_{\rm C}=69
K, which lends additional support to the correctness of our model and, hence, for the existence of the NHPT. The ferromagnetic transition and the NHPT are closely related because they are caused by the same microscopic interaction, namely the spin coupling
J_{df}
between Eu
4f
moments and electrons in the
5d(t_{2g})
orbitals. However, they represent fundamentally different physical phenomena. These are the phase transitions in the ground-state properties (ferromagnetic
\leftrightarrow
paramagnetic) and in the relaxation dynamics (biexponential
\leftrightarrow
complex-conjugate) that generally occur at different critical temperatures,
T_{\rm C}
and
T^{*}
, respectively.

We further note that the relaxation parameter
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
is not a fixed intrinsic material parameter but is of dynamic origin, determined by the interplay of bright-dark exciton transformation and dissipation. The pump fluence
P
controls how far the system is driven into nonequilibrium and determines the initial photoexcited exciton densities
\overline{n_{\rm b}}
,
\overline{n_{\rm d}}
. Our model thus predicts that with increasing pump fluence
T^{*}
shifts towards lower values (Fig.
4
C). At a given temperature, the imaginary part of the decay rate, therefore, scales as
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\sim\sqrt{P-P^{*}}
across the NHPT at the critical pump fluence
P^{*}
(Fig.
4
D). Again, this prediction is supported by our experiments; see Fig.
2
C. We then use Figs.
4
C and D to construct the schematic 3D phase diagram (Fig.
5
) of the dynamical space of the NHPT in EuO.

Discussion and outlook

To conclude, we have discovered a new dynamical state of matter in the exciton gas of a bulk solid-state material, europium oxide, which manifests itself in complex relaxation behavior detected by our optical pump-probe experiments. The nonequilibrium phase diagram of EuO encompasses both a Hermitian phase transition to ferromagnetic order and, as reported here, a NHPT towards the dynamical phase.
Both transitions are mediated by the magnetic coupling of Eu
4f^{7}
moments and
5d(t_{2g})
electrons, but represent separate phenomena — a phase transition between ground-state properties and a phase transition in the relaxation behavior — which occur at different critical temperatures,
T_{\rm C}
and
T^{*}
, respectively. We suggest that, based on Fig.
5
, both critical temperatures in EuO may be tuned via the pump fluence to coincide with characteristically altered critical fluctuations and enhanced sensitivity to parameter changes as a consequence
[
11
]
. This may be verified once we have found a way to realize the necessary fluence. Most importantly, none of the requirements of our theoretical model for the intrinsic bulk NHPT are specific to EuO. The necessary ingredients — two coupled excited states or modes subject to dissipative dynamics — are widely realized in condensed-matter systems. The general nature of our model therefore suggests that similar non-Hermitian phase transitions could occur across a wide range of materials.
