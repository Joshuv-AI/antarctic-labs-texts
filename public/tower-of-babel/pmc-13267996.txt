The Course of Scar Formation and Predictors of Scar Quality in 490 Paediatric Burn Patients During the First Year Post‐Burn
Authors: Frederique M. Kemme, Inge M. L. Brantjes, Esther Middelkoop, Paul P. M. van Zuijlen, Annebeth Meij‐de Vries, Anouk Pijpe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study analysed the course of scar formation and predictors of scar quality in children during the first year post‐burn. Scar quality was assessed using the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale (POSAS; patient scale completed by caregiver or child) and Dermaspectrometer during routine follow‐ups at the Burn Centre of the Red Cross Hospital, Beverwijk, the Netherlands between 2004 and 2022. Descriptive analyses examined scar characteristics over time. Multivariable regression analyses identified predictors of scar quality (POSAS overall opinion, thickness, pruritus) at 12 months. A total of 490 children (median age 2 years; 66% male) were included. Patient and observer overall opinion significantly improved between 6 and 12 months (p < 0.01). Most POSAS items improved over time, but observer thickness and relief scores initially slightly worsened before stabilising. Erythema and melanin differences with normal skin decreased significantly (p < 0.01). Patients (i.e., caregivers or children) consistently rated scars 1.5 points worse than observers. Observer‐reported scar quality (overall opinion and thickness) was independently associated with burn severity (length of hospital stay, %TBSA full‐thickness), burn cause, scar location and early scar characteristics (p < 0.10). Persistent pruritus (60%) was more prevalent than persistent pain (12%) and independently associated with age at burn and scar location (p < 0.05). Paediatric burn scars generally improve within the first year post‐burn, most notably after 6 months, although pruritus remains common. Early scar characteristics appear informative for predicting scar outcomes. Our findings highlight the complexity of (predicting) scar formation and support early risk stratification for more personalised follow‐up.

Introduction

Burn injuries affect millions of people globally each year, with children being particularly at risk [1]. In the Netherlands, approximately 40% of the patients in the three burn centres are under 18, and of those, two‐thirds are under 3 years old [2, 3]. Burns often lead to scars, impacting the physical, social and psychological well‐being of children [4]. Children with burn scars often have difficulty reintegrating into school; they may face discrimination and can endure lifelong reminders of the traumatic event [5]. Persistent physical complaints such as pruritus, affecting sleep and daily activities, can negatively impact the long‐term quality of life [6, 7, 8].

Scar formation is a dynamic process influenced by various patient‐, burn‐ and treatment‐related factors. Research on burn scar formation and its predictors has predominantly focused on adults or mixed populations [9, 10, 11, 12]. Factors such as wound depth, % total body surface area (TBSA), early POSAS scores, length of stay (LOS) and number of surgeries have been found to predict scar outcomes. However, findings from adult studies are not directly applicable to children because of key pathophysiological differences, such as thinner skin leading to deeper defects from comparable burns, and the need for continuing skin growth [13, 14].

Fortunately, some studies focused on paediatric burn scars specifically [15, 16, 17, 18]. Wallace et al. found that a higher %TBSA, healing time > 14 days, and multiple surgeries were independently associated with thicker scars, as assessed by clinicians using the Vancouver Scar Scale. They also highlighted the scarcity of rigorous research on predictors of scar outcome in children [15]. A longer healing time was associated with thicker scars in multiple other studies [17, 18, 19], and Gee Kee et al. found that burn depth was significantly associated with scar thickness [19]. More recently, Spronk et al. showed in a cross‐sectional study that even 5–7 years post‐burn, most paediatric scars differed from normal skin, particularly in colour and that the number of surgeries predicted scar outcome [16].

Knowledge about the course and predictors of scar quality is important to improve our understanding of scar formation, to guide treatment, personalise follow‐up, for shared decision making and to manage expectations of children and caregivers. Therefore, this study aimed to describe the course of scar formation in a large cohort of paediatric burn patients between 3 and 12 months after their burn injury. Second, we aimed to identify independent, clinically relevant predictors of scar outcomes.

Methods

Design

Data for this observational descriptive study were collected between August 2004 and October 2022 at the Burn Centre of the Red Cross Hospital in Beverwijk, the Netherlands, during routine outpatient follow‐ups. All burn patients are followed up by a multidisciplinary aftercare team at 3 months post‐burn or ‐discharge, with additional visits approximately every 6 to 12 months. The Medical Ethics Review Committee of the Amsterdam University Medical Centres ruled that the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) was not applicable to the current study (2022.068). Furthermore, the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Red Cross Hospital (22.024).

Population

Children with burn scars, aged < 18 years at the time of the burn injury, with at least one scar quality assessment within a year (at 3, 6 and/or 12 months) post‐burn, were included in this analysis. Consent for the use of the routine clinical care data in this study was implicit via the medical consult and based on the opt‐out protocol of the Red Cross Hospital, which is in accordance with national guidelines [20].

Data Collection

The scar that was subjectively most bothersome to the patient—due to appearance or symptoms—was assessed by a healthcare professional (clinical researcher, research nurse or skin therapist), and the same scar was reassessed during subsequent visits. Scar quality was assessed using the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale (POSAS, Dutch version) and the Dermaspectrometer (DSM Colorimeter, Cortex Technology).

The POSAS consists of two scales: the Observer scale which was completed by trained physicians or research nurses, and the Patient scale completed by the caregiver or by the child when deemed capable by the observer. Throughout this manuscript, patient‐reported scores refer to scores provided by the caregiver or by the child. During the study period, POSAS version 1.0 was used until 2011, after which POSAS version 2.0 was implemented. Version 2.0 added one additional Observer item (surface area) [21]. Because all other items in both versions are identical in content, wording and scoring, data were combined for all analyses. Only analyses involving the surface area item were limited to assessments performed with POSAS 2.0.

For the Dermaspectrometer, one measurement (erythema and melanin) was performed on the scar, and one on unaffected skin. Skin type was visually assessed by the observer, based on physical features such as ephelides, hair colour and eye colour. Scar assessments were not performed if children participated in clinical trials focused on improving scar quality, if they visited outside predefined time points, or if they were unable to complete the Dutch POSAS patient scale due to language or cognitive barriers.

Data on patient‐ and burn characteristics were derived from the clinical registry (Dutch Burn Repository R3 (DBR R3), Beverwijk). The following variables were extracted from the DBR R3 database and used as potential predictors: age at burn, sex, %TBSA, %TBSA full‐thickness, cause of the burn, number of surgeries, length of hospital stay (LOS) and the presence of a positive wound culture and antibiotic use upon admission. Flash flame burns were categorised under flame burns.

Analyses

IBM SPSS version 25.0 was used for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Non‐normally distributed continuous variables were analysed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as median (IQR). Differences in patient and burn characteristics between visits were evaluated using the independent samples median test and chi‐square test.

To analyse the representability of the sample, a comparison with the total paediatric burn population treated in the burn centre was conducted. The course of scar formation was analysed using the POSAS observer‐ and patient scale and the Dermaspectrometer. Erythema and melanin values are presented as absolute differences between scar and unaffected skin. Differences across visits (POSAS and Dermaspectrometer) were analysed using the independent samples median test and Mann–Whitney U test. Observed differences in POSAS scores over time are compared to the established minimal important change (MIC) and minimal clinically important difference (MCID) [22].

Linear regression was conducted to predict scar quality, using the POSAS observer overall opinion as primary outcome. All potential predictors were prechecked for multicollinearity (separately for every prediction model) using the Pearson's correlation test; a correlation coefficient > 0.7 was considered highly correlated. Therefore, %TBSA and %TBSA full‐thickness were not included in the multivariable regression analysis of the POSAS overall opinion prediction model. The backward elimination procedure was performed, including all significant univariable predictors (p < 0.10) to avoid premature exclusion of potentially relevant predictors. Variables were subsequently removed if p > 0.10 and remained in the multivariable model if p < 0.10. A similar analysis for POSAS patient overall opinion was performed. A separate regression model was performed to predict scar thickness in children with scar assessments at three visits (3, 6 and 12 months), using the POSAS observer thickness score at 12 months as primary outcome.

POSAS patient pain and pruritus scores were further analysed in a subgroup of patients with complete 3, 6 and 12 months follow‐up data. Patients were classified into no pain/pruritus (at all three visits), transient pain/pruritus (present at one or two visits but resolved at the final visit), or persistent pain/pruritus (present at the final visit). Overall differences in patient and burn characteristics between these groups were compared using the independent samples median test, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare symptom intensity. Pruritus and its predictors were further analysed by logistic regression analysis to identify predictors of persistent pruritus (vs. transient), using the same backward procedure and predictors as in linear regression. p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant for all analyses, except for the prediction models (p < 0.10).

A total of 946 scar assessments were performed: 348 scars at 3 months (range 1.3–4.4 months), 355 at 6 months (range 4.5–8.9 months) and 243 at 12 months (range 9.0–15.0 months) post‐burn. Out of 490 patients, 131 (27%) had their scar assessed at all three visits. Patient‐ and burn characteristics did not significantly differ between visits, except for %TBSA full thickness (p = 0.006) (Table 1).

Patient‐ and burn characteristics of the study sample were also compared to those of the total paediatric burn population treated in the burn centre (i.e., those admitted for at least 2 h, thus including outpatient day admissions and inpatients, regardless follow‐up visits) during the study period (n = 1948). No significant difference was observed for sex between groups. However, the study sample was older (median age at burn 2.0 (IQR 1–8) vs. 1.0 (IQR 1–5), p = 0.003), had a higher %TBSA (median 6% (IQR 3–10) vs. 4% (IQR 1.5–6), p < 0.001) and a higher proportion of flame burns (24% vs. 13%, p < 0.001).

POSAS Observer Scale

Median POSAS observer scores for all visits are shown in Figure 2. The overall opinion did not change between 3 and 6 months but improved between 6 and 12 months (4 → 3, p < 0.01). Vascularisation differed from normal skin at 3, 6 and 12 months in respectively 340/348 (98%), 337/352 (96%) and 211/241 (88%) children, but significantly improved between 6 and 12 months (4 → 3, p < 0.01). Pigmentation differed from normal skin in approximately 90% of children at all visits but significantly improved between 3 and 12 months (p < 0.01). Thickness and relief worsened significantly between 3 and 6 months but did not change between 6 and 12 months. At 12 months, scars differed the most from normal skin for pliability.

Predictors of Scar Quality

Observer Overall Opinion at 12 Months

Age at burn, scar location, %TBSA (total and FT), cause of the burn, undergoing surgery for wound closure, LOS, antibiotic use and the observer overall opinion score at 3 months were significant univariable predictors of scar quality at 12 months (Table 2). In the multivariable model, facial scars and foot scars (vs. thorax) were independently associated with better scar quality (−1.270, p < 0.05; −1.769, p < 0.01), while lower extremity scars (0.517, p < 0.10), oil/fat (grease) burns (vs. hot liquid) (1.312, p = 0.001) and a longer LOS (0.031, p < 0.001) were independently associated with worse scar quality. Moreover, a higher 3‐month overall opinion score predicted worse scar quality: each additional point at 3 months predicted a 0.353‐point increase in the 12‐month score (0.353, p = 0.001).

Patient‐Reported Scar Quality at 12 Months

The only significant predictor of patient reported overall opinion at 12 months was the patient's 3 months overall opinion: each additional point at 3 months predicted a 0.519‐point higher score at 12 months (p < 0.01).

Discussion

This study provides detailed insights into the course of scar formation and predictors of scar quality (including scar thickness and pruritus) in paediatric burn patients during the first year post‐burn. To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe this to such extent in a large cohort of children. Overall scar quality improves during the first year post‐burn, particularly after 6 months. From the patient's perspective, all scores improved over time, with the most improvement for pruritus and colour. From the observer's perspective, vascularisation and overall opinion significantly improved, whereas thickness and relief initially slightly worsened. Pliability showed the most prominent difference from normal skin at 12 months. Our findings align with prior research showing that scar quality significantly improves within the first year post‐burn [9, 23, 24]. Although some of these changes in scar quality over time seem small, they likely represent clinically meaningful improvements [22].

Besides describing scar formation over time, we identified several predictors associated with scar outcomes, such as burn cause, burn severity, scar location, age at burn and early scar characteristics. Burn cause was independently associated with overall scar quality and thickness, with specifically oil/fat (grease) burns predicting worse scar outcomes. Scar location was consistently associated with scar outcomes. Notably, locations associated with better scar outcomes—such as the face and foot—are areas where it is not implausible that the treating clinician paid specific attention, for example by using a less widely meshed graft in case of surgery. Indicators of burn severity—such as %TBSA FT and LOS—were independently associated with worse scar quality, consistent with previous research [9, 10, 11, 15]. A higher %TBSA likely impacts wound healing time, which has been identified as a predictor of scar outcomes in previous studies [15, 17, 18, 19]. The observed association between LOS and worse scar outcomes likely reflects burn severity, which is only partly captured by %TBSA. Although %TBSA was a significant univariable predictor in our study, it was not included in the multivariable analysis of the POSAS overall opinion model due to its high correlation with LOS. For this model, LOS was considered a more comprehensive indicator of burn severity, reflecting not only burn size and depth, but also factors such as the need for surgical intervention, inhalation injury and complications requiring prolonged hospitalisation. This underscores the challenge of translating prediction models to clinical practice, where prognostic information must be interpreted in the context of individual patient characteristics and treatment decisions.

Persistent pruritus remained prevalent in our population (63%), consistent with findings that it persists in a substantial proportion of burn patients [25, 26]. The probability of persistent pruritus was independently associated with a higher age at burn and scar location, specifically lower extremity scars. Although less common than pruritus, pain was still reported by approximately one in five children who visited the outpatient clinic at 12 months post‐burn. Since different types of pain may play a role at different time points, this suggests that pain should not be overlooked in long‐term follow‐up, and that it might be valuable to clarify its underlying mechanisms [7].

Lastly, early scar characteristics—the 3 month observer overall opinion and thickness score—were predictive for 12 month scores, supporting the clinical relevance of early scar assessment during routine follow‐up (both objectively and subjectively), which has been demonstrated previously [23, 27].

Patient and Observer Perspectives

We identified several predictors of observer‐reported scar quality, while predictors for patient‐reported scar quality were absent in our study. Patient POSAS scores (i.e., caregivers or children) were consistently 1.5 points higher (indicating worse scars) than observer scores. Similar discrepancies have been reported in other studies [16, 23] and likely reflect differences in perspective. Clinicians rely on their clinical experience and compare scars with other cases, while patients tend to compare their scar with peers who often have no or little visible scars. In addition, patients are more influenced by personal, emotional and social factors—such as worry about long‐term appearance or guilt related to the burn injury—that are not typically included in clinical prediction models [9, 16, 19, 28]. As a result, patient‐reported scores may be more variable and difficult to predict accurately. Importantly, caregivers may rate scars different from children themselves, often influenced by parental concerns, feelings of guilt, or expectations about cosmetic and social impact of the scar [29]. Nonetheless, both perspectives provide valuable information, and therefore the use of both clinician‐reported outcome measures (CROMs) and patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs)—eventually supported by objective tools—is essential for a comprehensive evaluation of scar quality [30].

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the large sample size with data collected over an 18‐year period (2004–2022) allowed us to examine the course of scar quality in a substantial paediatric cohort. Another strength is the use of both subjective (POSAS) and objective (Dermaspectrometer) outcome measurements. Finally, the extensive statistical analysis that provides insights into multiple scar characteristics, including prediction models for thickness and pruritus, is a strength of this study.

Several limitations must also be acknowledged. First, the use of routine clinical care data introduces potential selection bias. The study sample (n = 490) represents a relatively more severely burned subgroup compared with the total paediatric burn population treated at the burn centre during the study period (n = 1948), based on a comparison of age, %TBSA, and burn cause [3]. Changes in healthcare over the years—shifting from routinely scheduled in‐person follow‐up visits towards more individualised and on‐demand (remote) follow up—may have influenced follow‐up patterns. Children with more severe burns or more symptomatic scars are generally followed for longer periods, whereas children with less severe burns or uncomplicated scars may be followed on demand. This may have resulted in a relative overrepresentation of children with more severe burns at later follow‐up visits, reflected, for example, by the higher percentage of full‐thickness TBSA in the 12‐month group. Nevertheless, the internal validity within the study sample is likely robust, as patient‐ and burn characteristics did not significantly differ between follow‐up visits, except for %TBSA FT. The use of routine clinical care data could also be considered a strength, as it reflects actual clinical practice and includes a broader and more representative spectrum of patients than would typically be captured in a controlled research setting [31]. Second, reliance on caregiver‐reported outcomes introduced information bias. Other limitations include the lack of detailed information on scar treatment (e.g., pressure garments, silicone therapy, laser treatment). It is likely that a substantial proportion of children received scar treatment during follow‐up. However, this information was not recorded in the registry and could therefore not be analysed. As these treatments may influence scar quality, systematic registration of treatment information in future registries may support future research on scar outcomes. Another limitation is the assumption that, in case of surgery for acute wound closure, the scar assessed in the outpatient clinic was the one that had been operated on, based on the rationale that the most severe or symptomatic scar would have been assessed. However, our registry did not include this specific information. Lastly, a limitation was the inability to study potential predictors, such as detailed information on burn wound depth and skin type, which are expected to influence scar quality [10, 15, 32]. It would be valuable for future studies to study (surgical) treatment in relation to scar location, to better understand location‐specific differences in scar outcomes. In addition, integrating more comprehensive data sources and using advanced modelling approaches, such as machine learning, may support more personalised and accurate predictions [33, 34].

Conclusion

Scar quality in children generally improves within the first year, most notably after six months—not previously shown in a large paediatric burn cohort. Age at burn, burn severity, burn cause and scar location were independently associated with scar outcomes. Early scar assessment provides valuable prognostic information for long‐term scar outcomes. Our findings can help refine early risk stratification, personalised follow‐up and future research.
