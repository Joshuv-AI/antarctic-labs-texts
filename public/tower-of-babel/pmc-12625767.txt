Evaluating AI guidelines in leading family medicine journals: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Cameron O’Brien, Zohaib Thayani, Tim Smith, Andrew V. Tran, Patrick Crotty, Alec Young, Alicia Ito Ford, Matt Vassar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly integrated into family medicine research and practice, enhancing diagnostics, data analysis, and care delivery. Yet, its rapid adoption has outpaced the development of standardized editorial policies, raising concerns about transparency, ethics, and reproducibility. Clear guidance from journals is urgently needed to ensure responsible use of AI in research and publishing.ObjectiveTo evaluate editorial policies and reporting guideline endorsements related to AI across leading FM journals.MethodsUsing the SCImago Journal Rank database, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis of FM journals. From November 2024 to January 2025, we reviewed publicly available Instructions for Authors for AI-related policies, including authorship, manuscript writing, content/image generation, and disclosure. We also assessed whether journals endorsed AI-specific RGs (e.g., CONSORT-AI, SPIRIT-AI). Data were extracted in duplicate using a standardized form. Reproducibility was supported through protocol registration on Open Science Framework.ResultsOf 57 FM journals identified, 40 met inclusion criteria. Among these, 82.5% (33/40) referenced AI in their policies. Most (77.5%) prohibited AI authorship and required disclosure of AI use, while 72.5% permitted AI-assisted manuscript writing. Policies on AI-generated content and images varied, with 47.5% and 50.0% of journals allowing their use, respectively. Only 5.0% (2/40) endorsed AI-specific RGs. No correlation was observed between journal characteristics and AI policy adoption.ConclusionsMost family medicine journals now address AI use, but notable gaps remain, particularly in endorsing AI-specific reporting guidelines. Without broader adoption of structured guidance, AI-integrated research risks inconsistency, limited reproducibility, and ethical challenges. Strengthening journal policies and endorsing standardized reporting frameworks is essential to ensure high-quality, trustworthy AI research in family medicine.

Introduction

In the field of family medicine (FM), Artificial Intelligence (AI) has become an increasingly valuable resource, driving advancements in both clinical practice and research. Clinically, AI has been used to improve diagnostic accuracy, predict patient outcomes, and personalize treatment plans [1]. Additionally, AI tools are increasingly used in chronic disease management, telemedicine, and preventive care, improving efficiency and outcomes [2]. In FM research, AI aids large dataset analysis, automates data extraction for reviews, and supports predictive modeling of disease risk and treatment outcomes [3, 4]. However, as AI becomes embedded in research, it raises challenges around transparency, ethics, and reproducibility [5, 6]. These challenges include, but are not limited to, inadequate disclosure of AI use in study design or writing, hallucinated or fabricated outputs that risk undermining scientific integrity, algorithmic bias that can perpetuate inequities in research and publishing, and barriers to reproducibility stemming from limited access to code, data, and model parameters [7, 8]. Additionally, ethical tensions remain in balancing accuracy, fairness, explainability, and privacy when implementing AI tools in research workflows [5, 6, 9].

Our study’s primary aim was to examine the Instructions for Authors in leading FM journals to determine how they address key policy areas. Specifically, we assessed whether journals allow AI to be credited as an author, what limitations are placed on AI-assisted writing, content, and image generation, and whether disclosure of AI use is required. This work examines policies addressing the use of generative AI tools (e.g., text, content, and image generation) within research and publishing. While we recognize that ‘AI’ encompasses a broader range of applications, including predictive modeling and decision-support algorithms, our analysis is limited to editorial policies and reporting guidelines relevant to generative AI and AI-integrated research methods.

The second focus of our study extends beyond editorial policies to the methodological rigor of AI-integrated studies. Guidelines such as CONSORT-AI (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials involving Artificial Intelligence) and SPIRIT-AI (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials involving Artificial Intelligence) promote transparency, reproducibility, and ethical standards by offering structured frameworks for reporting AI methods [10]. However, it is unclear to what extent FM journals reference or require these reporting guidelines.

Accordingly, this study was designed to answer two questions: [1] Do FM journals have explicit editorial policies regarding the use of generative AI tools in authorship, manuscript writing, content and image generation, and disclosure? and [2] Do they endorse AI-specific reporting guidelines, such as CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI, that support the methodological rigor and transparency of AI-integrated studies?

Several recent meta-research studies have assessed the extent to which medical journals are adapting their policies to address the rise of artificial intelligence. For example, Inam et al. reviewed top cardiology journals and found that most prohibited AI authorship but permitted its use in manuscript generation, while few endorsed AI-specific reporting guidelines [11]. Almobayed et al. similarly reported that approximately two-thirds of ophthalmology journals had some policy on AI use, though more than one-third offered no clear guidance [12]. In radiology, Zhong et al. found that although general reporting guidelines were moderately endorsed, AI-specific guidelines such as CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI were rarely mentioned [13]. Collectively, these findings highlight variability across specialties and underscore a broader lack of standardized guidance for AI in medical publishing. However, to our knowledge, no prior study has examined AI-related editorial policies and reporting guideline endorsements in the field of FM. Our study addresses this gap.

By evaluating both AI-related editorial policies and endorsement of AI-specific RGs, we provide a comprehensive view of how FM journals are adapting to AI. This dual analysis highlights existing policies and uncovers gaps that, if addressed, could improve transparency, research quality, and ethical standards.

Methods

Study design and reproducibility

We followed STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines when writing the manuscript [14]. Our methods were based on a similar study by Inam et al. [11], which evaluated AI-related editorial policies in leading cardiovascular journals. The study was approved by the Oklahoma State University Center for Health Sciences IRB (Study #2024145).

Search strategy

With input from a medical librarian, we identified all clinical journals under the “Family Practice” subcategory in the SCImago Journal and Country Rank (SJR) database [15]. The SCImago platform provides open-access citation analysis data, ranking journals based on both citation quality and quantity [15–17]. We applied the “Journal” filter to exclude book series, conferences, proceedings, and trade journals.

Eligibility & exclusion criteria

To be included in the study, journal websites were required to meet the following criteria: they must provide an accessible Instructions for Authors webpage in English and be actively publishing research. Journals that were discontinued were excluded to ensure the assessment focused solely on actively publishing journals relevant to clinical research. Additionally, journals without a publicly accessible Instructions for Authors or a means of contacting the editorial office were excluded, as their policies could not be fairly evaluated.

Investigator training

Two investigators (CO, TS) were trained by AY prior to initiation and piloted data extraction using five journals not included in the study sample. Extraction was performed in masked, duplicate fashion to ensure consistency. After reaching consensus, they proceeded with the full dataset.

Data extraction process

Between November 5, 2024 and January 8, 2025, investigators independently extracted data from journal websites and relevant linked sources. Data were collected in masked duplicate using a standardized Google Form developed by AVT, then reconciled. No third-party input was needed. We used the Wayback Machine to confirm that no policy updates occurred during the study period [18]. The Wayback Machine archives all webpages and their updates over time, allowing researchers to confirm that the policies reviewed during extraction remained unchanged throughout the study period.

Data items

Extracted data included journal title, region, SJR rank/quartile, 2023 impact factor, publisher, and acknowledgment of International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE), Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), and World Association of Medical Editors (WAME) [19, 20]. Publisher names were standardized (e.g., grouping “Elsevier B.V.” and “Elsevier Ltd.” under “Elsevier”). To guide our assessment and contextualize journal policies, we analyzed and compared the official AI guidance from ICMJE, COPE, and WAME. Journal characteristics

For journals referencing generative AI, we recorded policy details on authorship, manuscript writing, content and image generation, and disclosure. We also noted endorsement of AI-specific reporting guidelines, including: [1] CONSORT-AI [2], SPIRIT-AI [3], FUTURE-AI (Fairness Universality Traceability Usability Robustness Explainability Artificial Intelligence solutions) [4], MI-CLAIM (Minimum Information about Clinical Artificial Intelligence Modeling) [5], MINIMAR (Minimum Information for Medical AI Reporting), and [6] CHEERS-AI (Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards for Interventions That Use Artificial Intelligence). Any additional AI-specific RGs mentioned that were not included above were also documented.

Editorial outreach

To ensure a fair assessment, we contacted journals without existing policies on generative AI tools to inquire whether they were in the process of updating their policies. Standardized emails were sent to either the editorial office or to the Editor-in-Chief. If no response was received within a week, a reminder email was sent, as this has been shown to improve response rates [21]. If an email response was not received a week after the second attempt, it was assumed that journal contents were up-to-date and that editors were not actively updating their policies.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the presence of AI-related editorial policies on authorship, manuscript writing, content, image generation, and disclosure. The secondary outcome was endorsement of AI-specific reporting guidelines. We aimed to identify policy gaps and assess readiness for AI in medical publishing.

Data synthesis

Descriptive statistical summaries of journal AI policies were compiled, which focused on five key areas: [1] AI-generated content [2], AI-generated images [3], AI authorship inclusion [4], AI-assisted manuscript writing, and [5] AI acknowledgment. A bias analysis was deemed unnecessary, as data collection involved a direct evaluation of the Instructions for Authors rather than the appraisal of individual studies. We used biserial correlation tests to assess relationships between binary AI policy variables (e.g., authorship allowed vs. not allowed) and continuous journal characteristics (SJR, impact factor). Correlation coefficients (r), corresponding two-tailed p-values, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the psych package in R (v4.2.1). All 40 journals were included in our analysis. Journals without a reported 2023 impact factor (n = 13) were retained in the dataset and included in analyses involving SJR, which was available for all journals. Correlations involving impact factor were restricted to the subset of journals with available values. To comply with STROBE reporting, sensitivity analyses were not applicable to this study, as all data were derived from journal websites and assessed in their entirety without sampling assumptions.

Reproducibility

This protocol was uploaded to the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform to encourage transparency and reproducibility. Any amendments to the original protocol, as well as raw data, analysis scripts, and extraction forms were also uploaded to OSF [22].

Discussion

Our study addressed two aspects of AI in FM journals. First, we examined the Instructions for Authors and editorial policies to determine whether they addressed ethical considerations of AI use, such as eligibility for authorship, its role in manuscript writing, content and image generation, and whether disclosure of AI use is required upon submission. Second, we assessed the AI-specific RGs endorsed by these journals. The development and implementation of these RGs were key focuses, as they are designed to enhance transparency and reproducibility in studies incorporating AI.

Our findings highlight notable trends in the adoption of AI-related editorial policies within leading FM journals. Most journals explicitly addressed AI use, with the majority having policies on authorship, manuscript writing, and disclosure. However, gaps remain: only half allow AI in content generation, and nearly one-third have no policy. Similar trends were found for AI image generation. Despite general alignment with ICMJE guidelines, variability among FM journals underscores significant gaps in editorial practices [23]. These findings highlight the lack of uniformity in how FM journals regulate AI-related content, emphasizing the need for clearer, standardized policies to ensure ethical and consistent AI integration. This variability is also seen in other specialties. A study by Inam et al. found nearly all of the top 25 cardiovascular journals endorsed AI-generated content but explicitly prohibited AI-generated images [11]. Almobayed et al. found nearly two-thirds of ophthalmology journals had AI policies for manuscript generation, though over one-third lacked clear guidance [12]. This variation reflects broader concerns about the ethical and practical implications of AI in publishing, with some journals taking a more cautious approach. For example, Science explicitly bans AI-generated content and images due to concerns about research integrity [24]. These restrictions highlight an ongoing debate; while AI improves efficiency and accessibility in research, it also raises concerns about data integrity, originality, and bias. The lack of standardized AI policies in FM journals mirrors broader inconsistencies in medical publishing. While organizations such as ICMJE, COPE, and WAME provide general ethical frameworks for manuscript preparation and authorship, they do not offer detailed guidance on AI-generated content or images, leaving room for ambiguity [25–27]. As AI tools continue to shape scientific writing, clearer definitions and structured guidelines are needed to ensure consistent policy implementation.

Despite widespread discussion of AI policies, explicit endorsement of AI-specific RGs remains rare among FM journals. We found only two FM journals explicitly endorsed AI-specific RGs—one recommending CONSORT-AI and the other SPIRIT-AI. Similarly, Zhong et al. evaluated 117 radiology journals and found that while general RGs were moderately adopted, AI-specific guidelines were rarely mentioned [13]. Studies show that failure to adopt such guidelines can lead to poor reporting, bias, and reproducibility issues. Nagendran et al. found most AI-driven imaging studies had a high risk of bias, poor RG adherence, and limited transparency [28]. A similar study by Salybekov et al. reported that the absence of standardized AI reporting in nephrology impedes reproducibility, reliability, and ethical application, ultimately hindering clinical translation [29]. Without adherence to established frameworks, these fields risk publishing studies with inconsistent methods, biased claims, and limited real-world applicability, compromising patient care and decision-making. The limited endorsement of AI-specific RGs is concerning, especially given that multiple RGs have been established to ensure methodological rigor and transparency in AI-related research across disciplines [30]. Given the demonstrated benefits, FM journals should consider proactively adopting and implementing these guidelines to improve rigor, transparency, and ultimately, patient outcomes [31, 32].

To address these gaps, we recommend FM journals take a more proactive approach by implementing clear, comprehensive guidelines. Journals should define whether AI-generated content and images are permitted. Additionally, mandating AI disclosure—in line with ICMJE recommendations—would enhance transparency by requiring authors to specify how AI tools were used [23]. Incorporating AI-specific RGs, such as CONSORT-AI, SPIRIT-AI, and CLAIM, would also improve reproducibility and reduce bias in AI-assisted research [33–35]. By adopting structured AI policies and endorsing AI-specific RGs, FM journals can set a new standard for ethical AI integration, promoting transparency, research integrity, and accountability across disciplines.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, data extraction was conducted in a masked, duplicate manner, minimizing bias and improving accuracy. Second, we used a structured, a priori protocol, enhancing reproducibility, and ensured consistency through independent, trained data extraction. Third, our study has a broad international scope, examining AI policies across multiple regions. Lastly, all data will be publicly available on OSF to ensure transparency and reproducibility. However, the study has limitations. Our reliance on publicly available policies means unpublished or evolving guidelines may have been missed. We also acknowledge that our use of the SJR database, rather than Scopus or Web of Science, may have excluded journals indexed exclusively in those databases; however, SJR was selected as a freely accessible, transparent, and reproducible alternative. Additionally, AI-related policies are rapidly changing, making our findings a snapshot in time. To mitigate this, we used the Wayback Machine and contacted journal editors to verify policy accuracy, ensuring a reliable and fair assessment. We also acknowledge our correlation analyses were restricted to impact factor and SJR, as these continuous citation analysis measures were suitable for biserial correlation testing. Other journal characteristics, such as continent and publisher, were categorical and heterogeneous, limiting their suitability for this analytic approachFinally, our focus on FM journals limits generalizability. Future research should examine AI policy adoption across other specialties to better understand publishing trends.

Conclusion

Although leading FM journals frequently endorse statements supporting the use of AI-generative tools and adhere to ICMJE standards, a notable gap remains in the adoption and endorsement of AI-specific RGs. Bridging this gap is essential to ensuring that research involving AI is conducted, reported, and interpreted with the highest standards of scientific rigor and ethical accountability. By adopting standardized policies and endorsing comprehensive RGs, journals can effectively navigate the challenges posed by AI and contribute to the advancement of high-quality, trustworthy research in the field of FM.
