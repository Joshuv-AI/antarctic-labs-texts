Druggable targets for Parkinson’s disease: transcriptomics based Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Qiong Lyu, Rong Chen, Zhengang Qiu, Chuhuai Wang, Rongrong Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder. Currently available drugs for PD, can only relieve the symptoms of PD, but cannot prevent the progression of the disease and have serious side effects. Other new druggable therapeutic targets for PD are needed. First, six GEO datasets with transcriptomic data from the substantia nigra (SN) region of the brain were downloaded to find dysregulated druggable genes in PD. Then, Mendelian randomization (MR) and summary statistics-based MR (SMR) analysis were conducted using eQTL data from both brain tissue and blood to investigate the relationship between gene expression and PD. Next, the association between the expression of candidate druggable targets and disease stage was validated in an additional dataset GSE49036. Finally, a phenome-wide MR analysis was carried out to investigate the potential impact of candidate druggable genes on several other complex diseases or traits. Our study revealed 313 differentially expressed genes that may be directly targetable and have an impact on PD (FDR-p < 0.1). Through MR and SMR analysis, P2RX7 and RNASET2 were identified as feasible PD therapeutic targets, which were highly expressed in PD tissues and increased as the Braak stages increased. Phenome-wide MR analysis revealed other effects of targeting RNASET2. This study presents genetic support for the potential therapeutic properties of targeting P2RX7 and RNASET2, which will be useful for developing druggable therapeutic targets for PD.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a pervasive neurodegenerative ailment distinguished by the gradual depletion of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra region of the cerebral cortex1. With the global population aging, the prevalence of PD is expected to rise in the coming years, highlighting the urgent need for effective interventions2,3.

The diverse pathways involved in PD, including dopaminergic, noradrenergic, glutamatergic, serotonergic, and adenosine, offer new perspectives on its clinical phenomenology and suggest other alternative therapeutic approaches beyond traditional dopamine replacement medical treatments4. However, existing drugs primarily focus on dopamine replacement therapy, and some new ester prodrugs for intranasal delivery have reached the pharmaceutical market, with undesirable side effects and fail to provide a cure or delay the progression of the disease5–7. In the last decade, serious attempts have been made to investigate PD disease-modifying therapies8–10. However, progress in this area has been hindered by the complex pathophysiology of the disease11,12. Fortunately, advancements in large-scale genetic studies have opened up new avenues for drug development8,13.

Large-scale human genetic research enabled novel drug development for complicated illnesses14,15. Specifically, “druggable” genes, characterized by their protein-encoding or gene expression properties, can serve as valuable indicators for identifying potential drug targets16,17. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have uncovered multiple single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with PD risk18. However, most of the discovered SNPs are located in non-coding or intergenic areas, lacking direct information on causative genes and possible therapeutic targets. Mendelian randomization (MR) is an efficient strategy for determining the causal links between changeable risk factors and clinical results19,20. MR analysis has gained momentum in repurposing approved drugs21 and identifying new treatment targets by integrating summary data from disease GWASs and expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) studies22.

Although common genetic variants contribute nearly 22% to the genetic heritability of PD, the genetic loci identified so far account for only a small portion of this estimate23, and further researches for PD are needed. Determining the functional impact of risk variants at the transcriptome or proteome level may help identify/prioritize therapeutic targets and potential biomarkers for the disease24, and a recent study suggested that 9 proteins may play an important role in PD25. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in the etiology of PD11, and Billingsley et al. have found 14 new genes related to mitochondrial function and causally associated with PD risk using MR analysis methods, which suggested that mitochondrial quality control and homeostasis contribute to PD risk26. To date, there is no systematic study of the druggable genome to explore therapeutic targets for PD.

Transcriptome sequencing can help explain the genes that are dysregulated in the disease, and transcriptome-wide MR analysis can discover the casual genes of the disease. Therefore, in the study, we analyzed transcriptome sequencing data of PD, and then used the summary-level data from GWAS and eQTL studies to estimate the causal effects of druggable genes on PD using a systematic druggable transcriptome-wide MR method, which can prioritize therapeutic targets for PD.

Method

Druggable gene list

The Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb; http://www.dgidb.org) is an archive that compiles data on drug-gene interactions and druggable genes from the literature, databases, and other publicly accessible resource. We obtained the latest released categories data from the DGIdb database, which includes the largest druggable genome and several other categories (February 2022)27. Besides, we also gathered a compilation of druggable candidates from an earlier study that offered a targetable gene list by investigating the possibility of genetic correlations with complicated illnesses and characteristics16.

Data collection and differential expression analysis

We obtained 6 human substantia nigra (SN) transcriptome array datasets from GEO to find dysregulated genes in PD, including GSE20186, GSE7621, GSE20292, GSE20141, GSE24378, and GSE8397. The ages of patients included in each data set were comparable to the ages of healthy individuals in the control group. The anatomical location of the SN was documented in GSE8397, which was included as a covariate, including lateral and medial SN. Then, we performed differential expression analysis of individual datasets using linear regression analysis followed by meta-analysis to identify genes that were dysregulated in brain tissue of PD patients28. Later, the random-effects model with a constrained maximum likelihood estimator was utilized in the meta-analysis29.

To identify targetable dysregulated genes, we focused on differential analysis of the druggable genome, and all p-values were adjusted using the classic Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure30. When the absolute value of beta in the meta-analysis was greater than 0.1, genes with FDR-corrected P values (FDR-p) < 0.1 were defined as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), while those with p values < 0.05 were defined as nominally significant DEGs. FDR-p < 0.1 was a less stringent threshold that can control false positives while maintaining power, thus providing a list of candidate genes for subsequent research.

Gene set enrichment analysis

For significant DEGs across the transcriptome, clusterProfiler was used for gene set enrichment analysis31. Gene sets for KEGG and gene ontology (GO) were from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/)32 and the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB)33,34. GO includes molecular function (MF), cell component (CC), and biological process (BF) categories. In the analysis, we got the significantly enriched KEGG or GO categories with FDR control (FDR < 0.05).

Expression quantitative trait loci datasets

For the expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) of the brain, we acquired the corresponding data from the PsychENCODE consortia (http://resource.psychencode.org), and the datasets primarily comprised of Europeans and were from the prefrontal cortex (PFC) involving 1387 individuals with genotypes and corresponding RNA-seq sampling. For each gene that has an expression of no less than 0.1 fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM) in at least 10 samples, we retrieved corresponding eQTLs with FDR < 0.05, with GRCh37/hg19 used as the reference genomes, and we also collected all detailed SNP information from the website35.

In addition, we obtained the blood eQTLs data from the eQTLGen Consortium (https://eqtlgen.org/), in which 37 datasets with 31,684 European individuals’ blood samples were included and 19,960 genes were studied36. In the blood eQTLs analysis, the cis-eQTLs of 16,989 genes were discovered. The goal of the Genotype-Tissue Expression project (GTEx) is to help study how inherited alterations in genes lead to common medical conditions, which collected different tissues from numerous individuals37. In this study, we downloaded the multi-tissue eQTLs from GTEx to validate our findings.

PD GWAS datasets

The summary data from the largest GWAS study of PD were used as outcome data for this study. Overall, this GWAS study comprising multiple published studies, analyzed 7.8 million SNPs in 37.7 thousand clinically diagnosed PD cases, 18.6k UK Biobank proxy-case and 1.4 million controls38. more detailed information is available in the original publication. With ieu-b-7, we extracted the variable genetic information of PD from the IEU OpenGWAS project39, which is a publicly available GWAS summary database and can be fast programmatic queried in R environments. Therefore, it was not needed to obtain permission from an ethical commission.

MR and summary statistics-based MR (SMR)

MR analyses were carried out with an R package called TwoSampleMR40. As the exposure data, the cis-eQTLs from the druggable DEGs were chosen, and the corresponding SNPs were obtained from PD GWAS data as outcome data, without using surrogate SNPs. To construct brain instrumental variables (IVs) of druggable DEGs, we picked SNPs with FDR < 0.05 and within ± 100 kb from the gene’s transcriptional start site, and then removed SNPs that were significantly associated with outcome (p < 1 × 10−5). For eQTLs from blood, we extracted SNPs of druggable DEGs (p < 5 × 10−8) and excluded those that were directly significantly related to outcomes (p < 1 × 10−5). Next, SNPs with mismatched alleles or palindromic sequences were also excluded. Finally, the Wald ratio was used to get the estimated effect size and its confidence interval for each SNP, while the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) was employed once there were no less than two SNPs for one gene. The SMR, which integrates summary-level data from GWAS as well as data from eQTL studies to pinpoint genes whose expression levels contribute to complex traits due to pleiotropy, and the heterogeneity in dependent instruments test (HEIDI) that uses all SNPs in the cis-regulatory regions region to sets apart pleiotropy from linkage effect, were both used in this study to verify the conclusion of MR41. Subsequently development multi-SNP-based SMR method using multiple cis-xQTL SNPs appeared to be more powerful than the single-SNP-based test, and was used to find genes whose expression levels are related to a complicated trait42.

Validation of the expression of candidate genes

First, we visualized the results of the differential expression analysis of genes in each GEO dataset and displayed the results of the meta-analysis using forest plots. Then, another public GSE49036 contains information on the Braak stage for 28 samples, including Stage 0 (the control group), Stage 1–2, Stage 3–4, and Stage 5–6 with 8, 5, 7, and 8 patients respectively. We explore the relationship between the expression of these druggable genes and Braak stages.

Phenome-wide MR

To perform phenome-wide MR examining the potential side effects of these previously identified druggable genes, eQTLs of candidate gene expression were used as exposure and summary-level data of systematically classified diseases from the UK Biobank were used as outcomes (https://www.leelabsg.org/resources). The Scalable and Accurate Implementation of Generalised Mixed Model (SAIGE) method was used to generate the summary data of diseases-associated SNPs from a large sample cohort while accounting for sample relatedness and unbalanced case–control ratios43. Only diseases with at least 500 individuals were considered in subsequent phenome-wide MR due to statistical power, and 783 traits were chosen in this study. We utilized identical parameters to conduct MR analysis using the preceding eQTLs of candidate genes from the brain tissue and blood. The causal relationship was deemed statistically significant when p < 6.386 × 10−5 (0.05/783).

Results

A brief overview of this study is provided in Fig. 1. To find out which genes may be the key driver genes involved in the development of PD, six substantia nigra transcriptome array datasets were included to compare the differences in RNA expression between PD and normal patients. Genes found dysregulated in individual datasets, were analyzed by meta-analysis. In total, 1722 genes were up-regulated and 1518 genes were down-regulated in PD based on meta-analyses (p < 0.05), which were nominally significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, these DEGs were significantly enriched in the proteasome, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 2A). Based on the results of GO enrichment analysis, the DEGs were significantly enriched in synapse organization and regulation of synapse organization. Druggable DEGs

In order to find several DEGs with more clinical importance, we searched for genes that can be directly targeted. After careful review and de-duplication, 5588 genes that could be targeted were found using the DGIdb database. Besides, we collected 4463 genes that could be targeted from an earlier study conducted by Finan et al. Finally, 5700 distinct genes with gene symbols were gathered for subsequent research (Supplementary Table 2).

Based on the differential analysis results of druggable genes, there were 134 upregulated and 179 downregulated druggable genes (FDR-p < 0.1) (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 3). In order to control false positives as much as possible, druggable genes with FDR-p < 0.1 were selected for subsequent analysis.

Estimated causal effects of targets on PD by MR analysis

We extracted genes with eQTLs data from the brain tissue and blood to intersect with druggable DEGs to nominate primary candidates. For the eQTLs of 66 genes from the brain tissue, 7 genes were identified to be related to PD. Among them, increased expression of 3 genes was associated with an increased risk of PD, while increased expression of the other 4 genes was associated with a decreased risk of PD. When we compared with DEGs, we found that the dysregulation of a total of 3 genes was consistent with the predicted risk of PD, including 2 genes that were highly expressed in PD (RNASET2 and P2RX7), whose expression positively correlated with the risk of PD. The expression levels of candidate genes with external datasets

Through the previous analysis, the MR and SMR results of brain tissue eQTLs collected from PsychENCODE and GTEx consortia supported P2RX7 and RNASET2 as feasible PD therapeutic targets. The feasibility of TIE1 as a therapeutic target remains to be discussed. Next, we investigated the association between these patterns of gene expression and diseases. Beneficial and adverse effects of prioritized targets on other complicated diseases

Because most drugs for the treatment of PD reach the brain tissue through the blood circulation to exert their effects, we evaluated whether two PD-related genes in blood and brain tissue have beneficial or harmful effects in other indications. In phenome-wide MR screen, we found that higher levels of RNASET2 may be a protective factor for hypothyroidism (blood, b = − 0.129, p = 2.897 × 10−17; brain, b = − 0.987, p = 2.686 × 10−20) (Fig. 5A,B). In addition, in brain tissue, higher levels of RNASET2 may be protective factors of thyrotoxicosis with or without goiter (brain, b = − 1.234, p = 4.620 × 10−5) and prurigo and Lichen (brain, b = − 1.810, p = 4.620 × 10−5), while higher levels of RNASET2 may increase the risk of skin cancer (brain, b = 0.642, p = 1.821 × 10−5). Discussion

In this study, we based on a transcriptome-based MR study to identify therapeutic targets for PD, our findings revealed 313 differentially expressed genes that were directly targetable and may have an impact on PD outcomes. Through MR and SMR analysis, we identified P2RX7 and RNASET2 as feasible PD therapeutic targets, which were highly expressed in PD tissues and increased as the Braak stages increased. Phenome-wide MR analysis highlighted additional effects of targeting RNASET2, with beneficial indications including hypothyroidism, prurigo, and lichenosis, and side effects such as skin cancer. Our study contributes to understanding the pathophysiology of PD and identifying novel therapeutic targets for PD treatment.

Currently available drugs for PD can only relieve the symptoms of PD, but cannot halt the disease from progressing. Dopamine agonists and catechol oxygen methyltransferase (COMT)/monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors are also employed in PD44. However, most of these drug classes have moderate to severe side effects, and new treatments still need to be found. Several natural and synthetic small molecules have been developed to target aberrant aggregation of α-Syn and its associated toxicity45–47, and the majority of this research has been done in vitro and animal models, with only a few substances being explored in human trials. PD is also a neuroinflammatory disease, and activated microglia in lesions not only promotes inflammation, but also promotes angiogenesis48, which makes some treatments that reduce inflammation show protective effects, against PD. Several clinical trials have been conducted testing anti-inflammatory drugs for the treatment of PD, but unfortunately, none have shown any significant neuroprotective effects49.

The results of existing studies support RNASET2 as a therapeutic target for PD. RNASET2, also known as Ribonuclease T2, plays a crucial role in protein synthesis as a genetic blueprint. Moreover, RNASET2 performs a variety of other tasks and is critical for pathophysiology, such as cancer and inflammation50. RNASET2 has been reported to promote inflammatory responses, which may be consistent with the inflammatory response in PD. Longer foreign RNA molecules are made into ligands for the TLR8 pattern recognition receptor by RNaseT2 activity, which has been revealed to stimulate type I interferon signaling51,52. While there have been no earlier studies linking RNASET2 and PD directly, our study provides convincing genetic data confirming the consistent function of RNASET2 in the risk of PD pathogenesis using eQTLs from brain tissue and blood. Our phenome-wide MR study revealed that the drug’s relationship with blood RNASET2 had a protective effect for hypothyroidism, whereas the association with brain RNASET2 produced a protective effect for thyrotoxicosis with or without goiter, prurigo, and Lichen. However, the phenome-wide MR study revealed that the higher expression of brain RNASET2 may raise the risk of skin cancer. Therefore, drug development targeting RNASET2 is promising while focusing on skin cancer risk, and additional investigation is required to elucidate the underlying mechanism by which RNASET2 influences the risk of PD.

P2X7 receptor (P2X7R), which is widely distributed throughout the human system53, primarily microglia and oligodendrocytes54,55, is another intriguing therapeutic target for PD. Neuroinflammation assumes a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of PD56. The manifestation and efficacy of the P2X7R, a ligand-dependent cationic channel abundantly found in microglial cells residing in the cerebral region, undergo substantial augmentation in the posthumous cerebral tissue of patients afflicted with PD, along with diverse animal models simulating neurodegenerative conditions57. While the exact mechanism remains elusive, an imbalance in the signaling transduction pathway within microglial cells could potentially amplify inflammatory responses, culminating in synaptic impairments and eventual neuronal demise9. The excessive activation of P2X7 receptors is intricately linked to inflammatory cascades, as they are triggered by the release of ATP from damaged cells and initiate the activation of inflammasomes58. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in the etiology of PD, and P2X7R was reported to mediate mitophagy and mitochondrial dynamics through reactive oxygen species (ROS) and CaMKKII pathways59,60. Notably, a recent study demonstrated the mitochondrial localization of the P2X7R ionotropic purinergic receptor and P2X7R plays a crucial role in regulating mitochondrial energy metabolism61. These findings substantiate the implication of the P2X7R pathway in the progression of neurodegenerative processes. In accordance with these discoveries, we present compelling genetic evidence that upholds the consistent contribution of P2X7R in the etiology of PD within cerebral tissue. Augmented expression of the P2X7R gene in cerebral tissue was observed to be linked to an escalated susceptibility to PD, as evidenced by the SMR and HEIDI analyses. Furthermore, by utilizing eQTLs derived from regional brain tissues of the GTEx project, we discovered that high expression of P2X7R in three specific brain regions was correlated with an increased risk of PD. The MR and SMR outcomes acquired from cerebral tissue eQTLs accumulated from the PsychENCODE and GTEx associations likewise corroborate P2X7R as a promising target for therapeutic interventions in PD. Significantly, the findings from phenome-wide MR analysis revealed no significant adverse effects associated with P2X7R (Supplementary Table 5).

In our study, we focused on identifying PD risk genes using blood and brain tissue. However, existing evidence points to systemic molecular changes in CNS neurodegeneration. Compared with brain tissue, skin and blood are easier to obtain, providing more opportunities for the study of PD mechanisms. Kurvits and Planken et al. found that the skin showed more PD-specific changes than the blood, which may reflect the existence of "skin-brain" crosstalk in Parkinson’s disease62,63. In addition, a longitudinal study using intronic RNA sequencing of blood revealed changes in the de novo transcriptional signature, the characterization of which may help to understand the molecular pathology underlying this disease64. In future studies, exploration of multiple tissues and systems such as the brain, blood, and skin may help to further understand the mechanisms of PD and prioritize therapeutic targets.

There were some limitations in our study. First of all, there were a limited number of eQTL as IVs in MR analysis, and the majority of them were fewer than three SNPs, which reduces the degree of confidence. However, when we performed SMR analysis, we included all cis-eqtls of the corresponding gene for analysis, which avoided the impact of a very small number of SNPs on the estimation. Second, we did not conduct co-localization analysis of gene expression and PD because this method is more about deriving the pleiotropy of genes rather than causality. To enhance the quality of our investigation, we conducted the HEIDI assessment, a mechanism employed to discern spurious positive outcomes stemming from LD. Third, we failed to find a GWAS study with a large enough number of PDs to verify our conclusions. To verify our conclusion, we selected eQTLs from GTEx-derived blood and brain tissue to repeat the analysis. In addition, we selected an external GSE49036 to verify the correlation between gene expression and PD, which also helped to verify the conclusions we found. Fourth, when selecting SNPs corresponding to the protein level based on targetable DEGs, no suitable eQTLs were obtained, which prevented us from conducting MR and SMR analysis at the protein level. Fifth, we only selected GWAS data that consisted mostly of Europeans, so our conclusions cannot be generalized across ethnic groups. Finally, even though MR and SMR analyses can yield interesting treatment targets, more investigation through further basic and clinical trials for PD is necessary to achieve a more thorough understanding.

In conclusion, our study utilizing a comprehensive druggable genome-wide MR approach has successfully identified novel therapeutic targets for PD. These discoveries substantiate the therapeutic advantages of targeting P2RX7 and RNASET2, which will be useful for developing druggable therapeutic targets for PD. Further research and clinical trials are warranted to validate and translate these findings into tangible benefits for individuals with PD.
