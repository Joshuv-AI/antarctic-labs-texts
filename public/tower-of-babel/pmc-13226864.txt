Efficacy and Adherence of Combined Fluticasone Propionate and Azelastine Nasal Spray Versus Fluticasone Propionate Alone in Pediatric Allergic Rhinitis
Authors: Figen Çelebi Çelik, Özlem Sancaklı, Özgen Soyöz, İdil Akay Hacı, Mehmet Şirin Kaya, Ayça Demir, Canan Ş. Karkıner, Demet Can
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIntranasal corticosteroids (INCS) and their combination with intranasal antihistamines (INCS/INAH) are treatment options for patients with allergic rhinitis (AR). Therefore, we aimed to prospectively compare these two different intranasal therapies regarding symptom scores, quality of life (QoL), and treatment adherence in adolescents.MethodsThe study included pediatric patients aged 12–18 years, diagnosed with moderate-to-severe AR. Patients’ symptom severity at baseline and at the one- and two-month follow-ups was assessed using the Total Nasal Symptoms Score (TNSS); additionally, QoL and treatment adherence were measured via the Pediatric Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (PRQLQ) and Medication Adherence Reporting Scale (MARS), respectively. These scores were compared between treatment groups.ResultsThe study included 79 patients with moderate-to-severe AR. Thirty-five patients (44.3%) had concomitant allergic diseases, and 28 (35.4%) had asthma. INCS (fluticasone propionate) therapy was started in 41 patients (51.9%), and combination therapy (azelastine hydrochloride + fluticasone propionate [AzeFlu]) was started in 38 patients (48.1%). Treatment adherence (MARS) was higher in the AzeFlu group (P = 0.002; odds ratio, 5.111; 95% confidence interval, 1.776–14.711). A positive correlation was found between treatment adherence and parental education levels (P = 0.002, r = 0.373 for mothers; P = 0.004, r = 0.351 for fathers). TNSS and PRQLQ scores were improved in treatment adherence patients (P = 0.001, P = 0.047). There were no significantly differences in TNSS and PRQLQ scores between the treatment groups.ConclusionsAzeFlu therapy may be preferred in patients with persistent symptoms and unresponsive to other treatments. Especially in adolescent children with persistent AR, AzeFlu should be considered as first-line treatment to increase treatment adherence.

INTRODUCTION

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is an inflammatory disease that causes symptoms such as runny nose, stuffiness, itching, and sneezing. It is the most common chronic allergic disease in children.1 Although not life-threatening, it can have a negative impact on quality of life (QoL), academic performance, social interactions, and sleep, and can exacerbate a number of common comorbidities such as asthma and sinusitis.2 AR treatment includes allergen avoidance, pharmacotherapy, allergen-specific immunotherapy, or a combination of the 3.3 The daily use of either intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) or oral antihistamines (OAH) has proved to be efficacious in the treatment of AR. The Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma Guidelines (ARIA) and the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) recommend INCS alone or in combination with intranasal antihistamines (INAH) in patients with moderate/severe symptoms.4 Although preparations combining INCS with INAH have been included in treatment in patients with severe symptoms, there is insufficient experience in children.

Intranasal treatments are generally safe and well tolerated, in contrast to the systemic side effects of antihistamines, such as sedation, dry mouth, dizziness, hypotension, and tachycardia. Such side effects can often lead to nonadherence or a preference for one formulation over another. We know that one of the key factors contributing to the success of AR treatment is adherence to treatment. Particularly in adolescents, treatment adherence poses a more significant problem. Although the effectiveness of intranasal treatments has been proven, there are limited studies comparing the superiority and treatment adherence of single steroid or fixed combination preparations in children. Previous pediatric studies have demonstrated the efficacy of intranasal fluticasone propionate (FP) and azelastine hydrochloride + fluticasone propionate (AzeFlu) combination therapy in improving symptom control and disease-specific QoL compared with placebo and intranasal monotherapy.56 However, most of the available evidence is derived from randomized controlled trials, and data focusing on real-life treatment adherence and patient-reported outcomes in adolescents remain limited. In particular, there is a lack of prospective studies evaluating how adherence influences symptom severity and QoL in routine clinical practice settings. Therefore, a clear knowledge gap exists regarding the real-life comparative effectiveness and adherence patterns of intranasal corticosteroid monotherapy versus fixed-dose combination therapy in adolescents with AR. To address this gap, we aimed to evaluate adherence to intranasal therapies in adolescents and the effects of different intranasal therapies on symptoms and QoL.

The primary objective of this prospective study was to compare symptom severity and quality-of-life outcomes between intranasal FP monotherapy and AzeFlu combination therapy in adolescents with AR under real-life clinical conditions. The secondary objective was to evaluate treatment adherence and its association with patient-reported outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

The study was designed as a prospective, non-randomized comparative study conducted under routine clinical practice conditions across all seasons, and it was carried out between September 2022 and September 2023 at the pediatric allergy outpatient clinic of Izmir Dr. Behcet Uz Children’s Hospital.

Patient selection

Male and female patients aged 12–18 years who were diagnosed with moderate-to-severe persistent or intermittent AR according to ARIA criteria had a disease history of at least one year and were symptomatic at admission were included in the study.1 Accordingly, moderate-to-severe AR is defined as reporting at least one of the symptoms, such as runny nose, nasal congestion, nasal itching, or sneezing, as a bothersome problem affecting daily activities and sleep. Intermittent AR is characterized by a frequency of symptoms < 4 days per week or < 4 weeks per year, whereas persistent AR is characterized by persistent symptoms (> 4 days per week and > 4 weeks per year).1 Intermittent AR patients with seasonal symptoms were evaluated in the season in which they were symptomatic. Persistent AR patients were similarly assessed during symptomatic periods, reflecting routine clinical follow-up under active disease conditions. Skin prick test and/or specific immunoglobulin E positivity or symptom history with allergen exposure were required.

We excluded the following patients from the study: those who were asymptomatic, those with mild AR, individuals receiving treatment or immunotherapy for rhinitis, and those with nasal polyps, structural nasal anomalies, or a history of adenotonsillectomy within the last year. Current AR history, physical and clinical examination, nose and eye examination were performed.

Patients were included from outpatient clinic visits managed by different physicians, each applying their usual clinical approach to AR management. Treatment decisions (AzeFlu combination or FP alone) were made prior to study enrollment based on individual physician judgment and guideline-based recommendations, and patients were subsequently grouped according to the intranasal therapy they were receiving. After initial evaluation, patients who were treated with FP (Flixonase® 50 mcg, 2 sprays per nostril once daily, in the morning) were assigned to group 1 and those who were treated with AzeFlu combination nasal spray (Dymista® 50/137 mcg, 2 sprays per nostril once daily, in the morning) were assigned to group 2.

Data collection

Clinical characteristics, demographic data, concomitant allergic disorders, laboratory tests and allergen sensitization of patients who met the inclusion criteria were recorded. The patients’ rhinitis symptoms, QoL and adherence to treatment evaluated with Likert type scales validated in Turkish were also recorded.

Efficacy variables were determined as Total Nasal Symptom Score (TNSS) scale for symptom severity, Pediatric Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (PRQLQ) for QoL, and Medication Adherence Reporting Scale (MARS) for treatment compliance.789 As all participants were adolescents aged 12–18 years, all validated questionnaires were self-administered by the patients, and caregiver proxy reporting was not used.

TNSS

Symptom severity was evaluated using the TNSS at baseline, 1st and 2nd month visits. The TNSS consists of runny nose, sneezing, nasal itching, and nasal congestion, each scored on a severity scale from 0 to 3 (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe), yielding a maximum total score of 12.

PRQLQ

QoL was assessed at baseline, 1st, and 2nd month visits using PRQLQ, a disease-specific QoL questionnaire that measures physical, emotional, and social problems in patients with allergy. In this scale, patients rate their experiences with activities, sleep, extranasal or extraocular symptoms, practical problems, nasal symptoms, and eye symptoms from 0 to 6. The total QoL score is obtained by dividing the score of items by 23. A decrease in the score indicates a good QoL, and an increase indicates a low QoL.

MARS

Medication adherence was assessed using the MARS at the 1st and 2nd month visits. The scale is evaluated as 5 = never, 4 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 2 = often and 1 = very often, and the scores vary between 5 and 25. An increase in the obtained scores indicates compliance, a decrease indicates non-compliance, MARS < 20 is considered non-adherence to treatment and MARS ≥ 20 is considered adherence. All patients’ 1st and 2nd month scores were compared, and all scores were also compared between treatment groups.

As a safety variable, patients were questioned for possible development of side effects for safety. Vital signs such as blood pressure and peak heart rate were evaluated at each visit. Patients who could not be reached and who had a history of medication use or disease that would affect the evaluation, and who were receiving OAH were excluded from the study.

Statistical analysis

Data entered in Microsoft Excel sheet (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and data analysis was done using statistical software SPSS version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The sample size was calculated using the OpenEpi program based on the TNSS results of a previous study, in accordance with the primary outcome of our study.10 Categorical variables were expressed using frequency and percentage. Numerical variables were presented using mean and standard deviation/median (interquartile range). In the comparison of continuous clinical parameters between 2 groups, paired t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used for parametric and nonparametric analysis, respectively. Chi-square test was used for categoric parameters. Additionally, Wilcoxon’s signed rank test was used in the significance of pre and post change of symptom scores. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

Ethics committee approval (No. 648/Dec2021) was obtained from the Local Research Ethics Committee of University of Health Sciences Türkiye, Dr. Behçet Uz Pediatric Diseases and Surgery Training and Research Hospital, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients and their parents were informed about the study and their consent was obtained.

RESULTS

A total of 79 patients with moderate-to-severe AR who met the study criteria were included in the study. Thirty-eight (48.1%) of the patients were female and 41 (51.9%) were male. The mean age was 14.9 ± 1.5 (12–18) years, and the duration of symptoms was 5.5 ± 3.6 (1–16) years. Fifty (63.3%) patients had persistent and 29 patients (36.7%) had intermittent AR. Thirty-five patients (44.3%) had concomitant allergic diseases and 28 patients (35.4%) had asthma. Allergen sensitization was detected by tests in 59 patients (74.6%), and most patients (58.2%) had indoor aeroallergen sensitization. Demographic data and laboratory findings of the patients are available in Table 1. Of the 79 patients enrolled in the study, 76 who continued their treatment were evaluated at the one-month follow-up, and 65 were evaluated at the two-month follow-up. Fourteen patients who could not be reached or did not continue their treatment were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1). Forty-one patients (51.9%) received intranasal FP treatment, and 38 patients (48.1%) received AzeFlu combination treatment. While there was no difference between the intranasal treatment groups in terms of demographic data, age, gender, duration of symptoms, allergen sensitization, concomitant allergic disease and asthma coexistence, patients with persistent AR were the majority in AzeFlu group (P = 0.005) (Table 1). Baseline TNSS and PRQLQ scores were similar in the groups (P > 0.05). Significant improvements in symptom scores and QoL were observed in all patients at the end of the first and second months of treatment compared to baseline (P < 0.001) (Table 2). When we evaluate the symptom and QoL scores according to the treatments, numerically better scores were observed in the AzeFlu group at the 2nd month; however, no statistical difference was detected between the groups (P > 0.05) (Table 2, Fig. 2). No statistical significance was found for nasal symptoms separately as runny nose, nasal congestion, sneezing; and for emotional, symptomatic, and activity assessments for QoL (P > 0.05). Additionally, no significant difference in improvement scores was also found in the subgroup population of persistent and intermittent AR.

As the second endpoint of our study, we evaluated treatment adherence in all children and observed that MARS scores were higher at the 1st month but decreased at the 2nd month (Fig. 3, Table 2). A correlation was found between MARS and improvement of TNSS and PRQLQ scores (TNSS P = 0.001, r = −0.406) (PRQLQ P = 0.015, r = −0.301). It was observed that as treatment adherence decreased, symptom scores increased and QoL decreased. A positive correlation was found between MARS and parental education levels (mother P = 0.002, r = 0.373; father P = 0.004, r = 0.351). No relationship was found between the presence of concomitant allergic disease, allergen sensitivity and type of sensitization (indoor/outdoor) and treatment compliance; MARS scores were higher in patients with persistent AR than in those with intermittent AR (21.6 ± 4.2 vs. 19 ± 5.6, P = 0.026). When examined according to treatment groups, treatment adherence scores at 1st and 2nd months were higher in AzeFlu group (1st month MARS 22.6 ± 2.4 (18–25) vs. 20.8 ± 4.6 (8–25); P = 0.045) (2nd month MARS 18.8 ± 6.4 (5–25) vs. 14.6 ± 6.1 (5–25); P = 0.008). When treatment adherence was categorized using a cut-off value of 20 (MARS ≥ 20 indicating adherence and MARS < 20 indicating non-adherence), adherence rates among all patients were 76.3% in the first month and 47.7% in the second month. A higher rate of treatment adherence patients was observed in AzeFlu group (P = 0.002; odds ratio, 5.111; 95% confidence interval, 1.776–14.711) (Table 2, Fig. 2). Additionally, in the subgroup population of persistent AR, only 2nd month MARS was significantly higher in the AzeFlu group compared to the FP group (19 ± 6.4 vs. 13.8 ± 5.8, P = 0.008), but this significance was not found in patients with intermittent AR.

Patients with a MARS score < 20 most frequently (32.7%) reported ‘not wanting to use medication for a long time’ as the reason for low treatment adherence. The other reason was reported as side effects. They reported side effects such as bad taste (n = 5), bad odor (n = 4), nausea (n = 3) and nosebleed (n = 3). Although side effects were reported numerically more frequently in AzeFlu group, statistical significance was not determined (P > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

In our study, higher treatment adherence was found in combination therapy (AzeFlu) compared to steroid (FP) alone, while no difference was found between the 2 drugs in symptom scores and QoL in adolescents with moderate-to-severe AR. It was observed that treatment adherence in adolescents decreased significantly over time and that the level of education of parents was effective in adherence. It was determined that symptom and QoL scores were better in patients with high treatment adherence.

Previous studies have demonstrated the superiority of intranasal FP and AzeFlu over placebo in the treatment of AR in children. In a study evaluating the efficacy of AzeFlu in children diagnosed with seasonal AR, in which a total of 348 children between the ages of 4–11 were included; superiority was found over placebo in terms of TNSS and PRQLQ.5 There are also studies in children showing that the AzeFlu combination therapy is superior not only to placebo but also to FP alone and Azelastine alone. In a study of children with AR aged 6–12 years, children treated with AzeFlu had significantly shorter and greater symptom relief compared to children treated with FP.6 Another study conducted on adolescents and adults diagnosed with chronic sinusitis, AzeFlu was found to be effective in long-term treatment, the number of symptom-free days was higher compared to the FP group, and side effects were similar.11 In a phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group study that included 779 patients over the age of 12 with seasonal AR, patients received AzeFlu, FP, azelastine, or placebo in separate arms. Based on evaluations using TNSS, total ocular symptom score, and PRQLQ, AzeFlu was found to be superior to all other treatments across all outcome measures. No difference was detected between the other treatment arms in terms of one-year safety data.12 In a recent systematic review and network meta-analysis comparing the efficacy and safety of intranasal medications for AR, INAH + INCS were the highest ranked improvement, followed by INCS.13 In our study, a significant improvement in symptom scores and QoL was found in both groups, and no superiority of the intranasal treatments was found over each other.

Intranasal treatments may cause less frequent and milder side effects than systemic treatments in AR. In a 1-year randomized, open-label, active-controlled, parallel-group study in 612 patients with chronic rhinitis, the incidence of treatment-related adverse events was low for AzeFlu and FP (9.4% vs. 11.1%). The most common adverse events were headache for FP and dysgeusia for AzeFlu, with no apparent decrease in serum cortisol from baseline.14 In a real-life study by Klimek and colleagues,15 < 1% of adverse events were reported with MP-AzeFlu. These were dysgeusia, nausea, sneezing, and nasal discomfort, respectively. In our study, bad taste in the mouth, bad smell, feeling of nausea and nosebleed were observed as side effects, respectively, but there was no significant difference in terms of side effects between the treatment groups. Furthermore, bad taste did not result in reduced treatment adherence in our study. We speculate that perceived rapid symptom relief, and a higher proportion of patients with persistent AR may have outweighed mild sensory side effects, leading to improved adherence in the AzeFlu group.

In a meta-analysis of 60,000 patients, AzeFlu was reported as an ideal treatment associated with better symptom relief and improved QoL compared to placebo and first-line treatments.16 Debbaneh et al.,17 in another meta-analysis of 8 randomized controlled trials, showed that AzeFlu alone was superior to either drug alone. A rapid onset and more effective relief of multiple symptoms than intranasal monotherapy were demonstrated. However, some authors suggest that combination therapy be used as a second-line treatment due to its higher cost.17 In the UK, the AR pharmacological management consensus statement from an expert panel recommends the use of a combination preparation as a second-line treatment in primary care in patients unresponsive to INCS, to avoid unnecessary investigations and unnecessary costly referrals.18 In ARIA guideline, INCS/INAH (single device) is recommended as first-line for patients with Visual Analogue Scale scores > 5 cm.19 Rapid onset of activity and use of a single spray may improve treatment adherence and treatment outcomes.20

Low treatment adherence in respiratory allergies is a common problem in clinical practice. In addition to educating patients and caregivers, the use of new technologies is also effective in improving adherence. There may be many reasons for non-adherence related to the patient, physician, disease, medication, and healthcare system.21 There are limited studies published in the literature regarding intranasal medication adherence in children. A review of the few available studies reported adherence rates of 83% for antihistamines and 65% for INCS.22 In a multicenter cross-sectional study, only 40% of 394 children with symptomatic AR were adherent to their prescribed treatment.23 In a questionnaire study of 103 patients, 29.1% were found to have discontinued their intranasal steroids. Patients with poor adherence cited a lack of symptom improvement or side effects, such as nasal irritation and epistaxis, as reasons for their nonadherence.24 In a cross-sectional survey study involving 250 children with AR, most children preferred OAH to INCS.25 In a recent study that evaluated AR treatment adherence during the European pollen season using the MASK-air app, adherence rate for OAH, INCS and AzeFlu were found to be similar. Moreover, it was observed that treatment adherence increased during periods of heightened symptoms.26 Bollinger et al. 27 reported that adherence to treatment in AR is approximately 50% and noted that multiple medication regimens further reduce adherence. Fast-acting combination therapies in a single delivery device may mitigate such nonadherence, paralleling the success of combination therapy in asthma. In our study, treatment compliance, which was 76.3% at the end of the first month, decreased to 47.7% at the second month, and the education level of the parents was found to be effective in treatment compliance. In the study by Ocak et al. 28 from our country in pediatric patients with AR, having more than 2 children was associated with low adherence. In our study, adherence with combination therapy was found to be 5-fold higher than with single steroid therapy. This situation can be explained by the rapid symptom relief superiority of the combined preparation. In addition, the higher proportion of patients with persistent AR in the AzeFlu group may also have contributed to this finding. In contrast to our study, in the study by Agrawal et al.,29 single FP spray showed better patient adherence than AzeFlu, due to the associated bitter taste. In our study, although AzeFlu was reported to have a bad taste, it did not affect treatment adherence.

Although our study is not randomized controlled and does not have a placebo group, it is an original study that provides prospective real-life data and investigates the relatively long-term efficacy, safety, and treatment compliance of 2 different groups of intranasal drugs used in the treatment of AR in children. In addition, the sample size was calculated based on symptom scores as the primary outcome; although treatment adherence (MARS) showed adequate power in post hoc analysis, the sample size was not specifically designed for this outcome, which represents a limitation of the study.

AzeFlu intranasal combination therapy had no significant effect on symptoms or QoL compared to FP alone in adolescents with moderate to severe AR. INCS/INAH combination therapy may be preferred in patients with persistent symptoms and unresponsive to other treatments. Treatment adherence plays a key role in treatment effectiveness, and especially pediatric patients and their caregivers need to be supported and monitored for treatment continuity. Collectively, our findings suggest that AzeFlu combination therapy may serve as an effective first-line option for adolescents with persistent AR. This approach appears to offer potential benefits in enhancing treatment adherence, which is often challenging in this age group.
