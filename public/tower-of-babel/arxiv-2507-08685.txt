Beer Path Problems in Temporal Graphs
Andrea D’Ascenzo *, Giuseppe F. Italiano *, Sotiris Kanellopoulos †, Anna Mpanti ‡, Aris Pagourtzis †, Christos Pergaminelis †

Abstract
Computing paths in graph structures is a fundamental operation in a wide range of applications, from transportation networks to data analysis. The beer path problem, which captures the option of visiting points of interest, such as gas stations or convenience stops, prior to reaching the final destination, has been recently introduced and extensively studied in static graphs. However, existing approaches do not account for temporal information, which is often crucial in real-world scenarios. For instance, transit services may follow fixed schedules, and shops may only be accessible during certain hours. In this work, we introduce the notion of beer paths in temporal graphs, where edges are time-dependent and certain vertices ( beer vertices ) are active only at specific time instances. We formally define the problems of computing earliest-arrival, latest-departure, fastest, and shortest temporal beer paths and propose efficient algorithms for these problems under both edge stream and adjacency list representations. The time complexity of each of our algorithms is aligned with that of corresponding temporal pathfinding algorithms, thus preserving efficiency. Additionally, we present preprocessing techniques that enable efficient query answering under dynamic conditions, for example new openings or closings of shops. We achieve this through appropriate precomputation of selected paths or by transforming a temporal graph into an equivalent static graph.

Introduction

Answering path queries is a fundamental operation on graphs, with numerous optimization and information retrieval tasks relying on an efficient response to such queries.
Bacic et al.
[
1
]
recently introduced the concepts of
beer vertices
and
beer paths
to model the option of taking a detour towards a set of
points of interest
when travelling from a source point to a destination point.
In fact, choosing detours is an essential part of daily life and modern computing systems. When travelling from point A to point B, we often need to adjust our route, perhaps to refuel at a gas station or pick up a beer.
Given a weighted graph
G=(V,E,w)
and a subset
B\subseteq V
of beer vertices, a beer path
between two vertices
u
and
v
is a path that starts from
u
, ends at
v
, and visits at least one beer vertex
in
B
. The
beer distance
between two vertices is the shortest length of any beer path connecting them.

Since their introduction, beer paths and distances have been extensively studied in different graph classes and frameworks
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
All existing techniques, however, are applicable to
static
graphs, which limits the uses of beer paths. In fact, because of the inherent nature of beer path applications, temporal information is a fundamental feature to consider in such scenarios. For example, bus routes may operate only at specific times, while beer shops may be subject to opening and closing hours.
Temporal graphs
[
9
,
10
,
13
,
21
]
extend the concept of static graphs to include such temporal information, meaning that each edge is associated with a starting time and a travelling time, and two vertices are adjacent only in the time instants in which an edge incident to them exists.
In this work we introduce and study beer path problems in temporal graphs.

1.1
Related work

Temporal graphs have been the focus of research in several fields due to their ability to model a wide range of real-world scenarios. Comprehensive overviews on temporal graphs are provided in
[
8
,
19
]
. The concepts of earliest arrival, latest departure, fastest, and shortest paths in temporal graphs were introduced in
[
20
]
. In the same paper, it is shown that all corresponding problems can be solved in time polynomial in the size of the temporal graph.

The problem of computing
shortest beer paths
in static graphs was recently introduced by Bacic et al.
[
1
]
.
Since the problem is polynomially solvable, researchers have focused on specific graph classes to design data structures that allow answering beer path queries faster than the baseline method. Bacic et al. developed an index for
outerplanar graphs

[
1
]
; Das et al.
[
5
]
studied the beer path problem on
interval graphs
; Hanaka et al.
[
7
]
achieved optimal query time on
series-parallel graphs
and linear preprocessing time on graphs having
bounded-size triconnected components
; Gudmundsson and Sha
[
6
]
focused on bounded treewidth graphs.
Additionally, Bilò et al.
[
2
]
address the problem of
constructing graph spanners w.r.t. the
group Steiner metric
, which generalizes the beer distance metric.
The shortest beer path problem is a special case of the
Generalized Shortest Paths
problem and the NP-hard
Generalized Travelling Salesperson Problem
, both of which asking, for a given pair of vertices, to determine a path of minimum total cost that visits at least one location from each of a set of specified location categories (e.g., gas stations, grocery stores)
[
11
,
15
,

1.2
Our contribution

In this paper, we introduce the concept of beer paths in temporal graphs and several related optimization problems, namely
earliest-arrival
,
latest-departure
,
fastest
, and
shortest temporal beer paths
(in short EABP, LDBP, FBP, SBP). In order to further incorporate temporal information that more accurately reflects real-life applications, our framework also considers timestamped beer vertices, i.e., the case where each beer vertex is augmented with the time instants
1
1

1

We consider time instants and not intervals. With slight modifications, our algorithms can work for intervals as well, with logarithmic dependence on the number of intervals.
in which it is active as a point of interest.
This feature naturally models many scenarios, e.g., opening and closing times of bars or gas stations; active motorway toll booths; instants in which airports may serve for emergency landing, and so on.

In Section
3
, we propose efficient algorithms for the aforementioned objectives, with each one differing both in methods and in time complexity. We define multiple-source earliest-arrival paths (MSEAP) and multiple-target latest-departure paths (MTLDP) and propose algorithms for calculating them, as an intermediate step for EABP and LDBP.
2
2

2

MSEAPs and MTLDPs have interesting applications in their own right; for example, a MSEAP is the optimal path for sending an ambulance to some target destination, assuming an ambulance can start from multiple sources (hospitals), at a different time for each source.
Using MSEAP subroutines, we propose two algorithms for EABP, one for the edge stream model and one for the adjacency list model, with the second one being significantly faster for graphs with no dominated edges. Similarly, we propose two algorithms for LDBP based on MTLDP subroutines. For FBP and SBP we rely on the computation of certain non-dominated paths via various domination criteria. For SBP in particular, we define a new path domination criterion and propose an algorithm for computing respective paths. The time complexity of our algorithms generally aligns with that of respective temporal path algorithms.

In Section
4
, we propose preprocessing methods for answering beer path queries faster. For EABP and LDBP, this is achieved through the precomputation of selected non-dominated paths in the graph. For FBP and SBP, we utilize the graph transformation proposed by
[
20
]
. For FBP, this offers a speedup compared to the algorithm of Section
3
. For SBP, no speedup is achieved, but we obtain a one-to-all SBP algorithm, which is significant because the respective algorithm of Section
3
only runs for a specific source and target.

2.2
The temporal beer path problem

We assume a temporal graph with a set
B=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\}\subseteq V
of
k

beer vertices
.
For each beer vertex
b\in B
, we are given a set
T_{b}

is

We assume that beer vertices can still be traversed in time units in which they are inactive, functioning as regular vertices.
We define

Given a temporal graph
G

x,y\in V
and a time interval
[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}]

and

starting after

and ending before

P_{B}(x,y,[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}])=

\{P=\langle x=v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{\ell}=y\rangle:P
is a temporal path from
x
to
y
such that
start(P)\geq t_{\alpha},\ end(P)\leq t_{\omega},\ \exists\ (i\in[\ell],t\in T_{v_{i}}):(v_{i}\in B,\ t\in[t_{i}+\lambda_{i},t_{i+1}])\}
. Note that a beer path must traverse at least one beer vertex at a time in which it is active.

Definition 3

(
EABP
)
.

A temporal
x
-
y
path
P\in{P_{B}}(x,y,[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}])
is an earliest-arrival beer path if

.

Definition 4

(
LDBP
)
.

A temporal
x
-
y
path
P\in{P_{B}}(x,y,[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}])
is a latest-departure beer path if
start(P)=\max\{start(P^{\prime}):P^{\prime}\in P_{B}(x,y,[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}])\}
.

Definition 5

(
FBP
)
.

A temporal
x
-
y
path
P\in{P_{B}}(x,y,[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}])
is a fastest beer path if
dura(P)=\min\{dura(P^{\prime}):P^{\prime}\in P_{B}(x,y,[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}])\}
.

Definition 6

(
SBP
)
.

A temporal
x
-
y
path
P\in{P_{B}}(x,y,[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}])
is a shortest beer path if
dist(P)=\min\{dist(P^{\prime}):P^{\prime}\in P_{B}(x,y,[t_{\alpha},t_{\omega}])\}

3
Algorithms for temporal beer path problems

In all our algorithms, we focus on the computation of the objective function (e.g., the earliest arrival time) instead of reporting the corresponding optimal path. This path can be easily recovered by storing parent information.

3.1
Algorithms for earliest-arrival beer path

In this subsection we propose an EABP algorithm (from source
x
to all nodes), based on the earliest-arrival time algorithm of
[
20
]
with a modification for calculating
multiple-source
earliest-arrival paths (MSEAP - Def.
7
). Our algorithm makes two sequential passes of the graph’s edge stream, calculating the earliest arrival times from
x
to each beer vertex in the first pass. For the second pass, all arrival times are initialized to
\infty
, except those of the beer vertices, which are initialized to the minimum active time greater than or equal to their respective earliest arrival time. We will later prove that this correctly calculates EABP times.

We also present a MSEAP subroutine for graphs containing no dominated edges.
4
4

4

This is a reasonable assumption for temporal graphs, since dominated edges are not useful for any of the commonly considered objectives.
This algorithm is similar-in-spirit to Dijkstra’s algorithm and uses binary search for parallel edges, achieving a running time independent of
M
. Although faster than the previous one, it requires random access to the edges of the graph (through e.g. an adjacency list with temporal logs
[
3
]
), instead of scanning an edge stream in a specific order.

3.1.1
An earliest-arrival beer path algorithm in edge stream representation

Definition 7

(MSEAP)
.

Given a temporal graph
G=(V,E)
and initial times
init[v]\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\{\infty\}

to

of

Intuitively, a MSEAP is a path that can reach a target node in the earliest possible time unit, starting from any node of the graph, with restrictions for the starting time of each node. If some

We first show that a generalization of the earliest-arrival path algorithm of

4
Beer path queries with preprocessing

In this section we present preprocessing techniques that help improve the asymptotic complexity of answering beer path queries. This is useful for applications where multiple queries need to be answered, each with different source and target vertices, different time intervals and even different active beer shops. A natural application, for example, would be to quickly recompute a beer path assuming some beer shops just closed (or some new ones opened), without computing everything from scratch.

We assume that each query contains as input a source node

For simplicity, we assume that each beer vertex

is either active or inactive for the entire duration of a query. We also assume that the set

4.1

We rely on the observation that Lemma

also holds for EABPs and LDBPs, with the same proof (although not for SBPs). This allows us to run Algorithm

To compute all non-dominated paths to each beer vertex (starting from any vertex) and all non-dominated paths from each beer vertex (to any vertex), it suffices to run Algorithm

Appendix C
The graph transformation of Wu et al.

In this section we describe in detail the graph transformation of
[
20
]
, which converts a temporal graph
G=(V,E)
into an equivalent static, weighted, directed acyclic graph
\tilde{G}=(\tilde{V},\tilde{E})
.

For each vertex
v\in V
, the transformation creates two sets of time-stamped nodes:
\tilde{V}_{in}(v)
, which includes a node
(v,t)
for every unique time
t
at which an edge arrives at
v
, and
\tilde{V}_{out}(v)
, which includes a node
(v,t)
for every time
t
at which an edge departs from
v
. These sets are sorted in descending time order. Edges are then created in three categories. First, for each node in
\tilde{V}_{\text{in}}(u)
, a zero-weight edge is created to the earliest node
(v,t_{out})
in
\tilde{V}_{out}(v)
that has a time greater than or equal to it, if no edge from any other
(u,t^{\prime}_{\text{in}})\in\tilde{V}_{in}(u)
to
(v,t_{out})
has been created.
Second, zero-weight edges are added between consecutive time-stamped nodes within
\tilde{V}_{\text{in}}(v)
and within
\tilde{V}_{\text{out}}(v)
to allow temporal “waiting” at the same vertex. Finally, for each original temporal edge
(u,v,t,\lambda)\in E
, a directed edge with weight
\lambda
is added from
(u,t)\in\tilde{V}_{\text{out}}(u)
to
(v,t+\lambda)\in\tilde{V}_{\text{in}}(v)
. Therefore, the graph
\tilde{G}
has a size complexity of
\mathcal{O}(M)
in both the number of vertices and edges.

The complexity of the transformation from the temporal graph
G=(V,E)
to the static weighted graph
\tilde{G}
primarily depends on two steps: vertex creation and edge creation. During vertex creation, for each vertex
v\in V
, time-stamped copies are created corresponding to distinct arrival and departure times. Sorting these time instances for all vertices takes
\mathcal{O}(M\log(d_{\max}))
time, where
d_{\max}
is the maximum degree (either in-degree or out-degree) of any vertex in
G
. For edge creation, zero-weight edges are constructed between consecutive time-stamped nodes and from arrival to departure nodes, which requires
\mathcal{O}(M)
time. Additionally, each original temporal edge induces a weighted edge in
\tilde{G}
. Placing these edges in
\tilde{G}
also requires
\mathcal{O}(M\log(d_{max}))
time. Therefore, the overall time complexity of the graph transformation is
\mathcal{O}(M\log(d_{\max}))
.
