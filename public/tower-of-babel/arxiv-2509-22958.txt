Interfacing of an Optical Nanofiber with Tunably Spaced Atoms in an Optical Lattice
Hyok Sang Han, Ahreum Lee, Sarthak Subhankar, Fredrik K. Fatemi, S. L. Rolston

Abstract
We experimentally demonstrate efficient interfacing of a large number of atoms to an optical nanofiber using an optical lattice with tunable spacing ( 0.88 − 1.5 ​ μ 0.88-1.5~\mu m) Han et al. 2025 projected onto the nanofiber. The lattice beam and reflections from the nanofiber yield trap potentials that provide tight confinement in all motional degrees of freedom ≈ \approx 220 nm above the nanofiber surface, enabling efficient atom-photon coupling. We achieved trapping of ≈ 1300 \approx 1300 atoms in periodic trap sites with a trap lifetime of ≈ 15 \approx 15 ms. We also observe the effect of varied lattice period on the atomic motional frequencies. Our new scheme is adaptable to other nanophotonic cold-atom systems and provides a versatile and scalable platform for studying photon-mediated long-range collective interactions. Our tunable lattice-nanofiber system could be seamlessly integrated with optical tweezers, allowing both the scalable atom-photon interface and individual single-atom access, and its intrinsic connectivity to conventional fiber provides a significant advantage towards quantum networks.



I
Introduction

Efficient coupling between quantum emitters and an electromagnetic (EM) mode
Hammerer et al. 2010
;
Chang et al. 2014
is an essential element for photon-based quantum information processing. A one-dimensional (1D) periodic array of atoms interfaced with an optical waveguide
Sheremet et al. 2023
;
Arcari et al. 2014
;
Goban et al. 2014
;
Lodahl et al. 2015
has been of particular interest
Chang et al. 2012
;
Cardenas-Lopez et al. 2023
, due to the collective enhancement of atom-photon coupling. One promising platform to achieve this is optical nanofibers
Tong et al. 2003
;
Leon-Saval et al. 2004
;
Vetsch et al. 2010
;
Nayak et al. 2018
;
Solano et al. 2017a
for their lossless photon guiding, which enables long-range collective interactions between a large number of atoms
Solano et al. 2017b
;
Le Kien et al. 2005
;
Kien and Hakuta 2008
;
Liedl et al. 2023
. Moreover, the suspended structure allows for easy integration with cold-atom systems, and its inherent connectivity to conventional fibers is advantageous for distributed quantum systems
Cirac et al. 1997
;
Kimble 2008
;
Reiserer and Rempe 2015
.

A widely used method for creating a periodic array along the nanofiber is to utilize the evanescent field modes of the trapping light propagating through the fiber
Le Kien et al. 2004
;
Balykin et al. 2004
;
Vetsch et al. 2010
;
Goban et al. 2012
;
Kato and Aoki 2015
;
Kestler et al. 2023
. In this setup, the lattice period (
d_{\mathrm{lat}}
) is determined by the wavelength of the counter-propagating trapping beams, which is constrained by the conditions for stable trapping. Our focus lies on cases where
d_{\mathrm{lat}}
satisfies the one-dimensional Bragg condition:

d_{\mathrm{lat}}=\frac{q\,\lambda_{0}}{2\,n_{\mathrm{eff}}},

(1)

where the integer
q
is the diffraction order,
\lambda_{0}
is the free-space wavelength corresponding to the atomic resonance, and
n_{\mathrm{eff}}
is the effective refractive index of the propagating mode. However, to meet this along the nanofiber, the trap laser wavelength
\lambda_{\mathrm{laser}}\approx q\cdot\lambda_{0}
has to be either near-resonant (
q=1
)
Corzo et al. 2016
;
Sørensen et al. 2016
, which is prone to decoherence due to a large photon scattering, or extremely far off-resonant (
q\geq 2
)
Vera and Solano 2024
, requiring large laser power. An alternative approach is to use optical tweezers
Kaufman and Ni 2021
to interface the atoms with the nanofiber
Nayak et al. 2019
;
Sunami et al. 2025
, controlling the array period independent of the wavelength of the trapping laser. However, interfacing a large number of atoms (
\sim 10^{3}
) with a nanofiber remains a challenge.

In this work, we use a side-illuminated optical lattice, featuring a wide range of quasi-continuously adjustable lattice period (0.88 - 1.5
\mu
m), to couple a large number of atoms (
1270\pm 35
) to an optical nanofiber. Our tunable lattice period is set by the interference between two converging beams, similar to an optical accordion
Li et al. 2008
;
Huckans et al. 2009
;
Ville et al. 2017
;
Wili et al. 2023
. Instead of opto-mechanically shifting the optical paths or actively steering the beams, we employ a stationary
4f
imaging system that passively converts the phase pattern of our custom grating into a sinusoidal intensity modulation at the nanofiber
Han et al. 2025
. The straightforward
4f
imaging scheme enables the generation of an optical lattice within a highly elongated envelope profile, allowing an efficient and stable interface with the nanofiber. Our method leverages the advantages of both an evanescent-mode optical lattice (encompassing a large number of sites and high periodicity) and optical tweezers (offering high tunability), with modest experimental complexity. Our system provides a versatile tool for controlling the positions of a large number of atoms coupled to an optical mode to study various photon-mediated collective dynamics
Dicke 1954
;
Gross and Haroche 1982
;
Yan et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Lodahl et al. 2017
. The technique is applicable to more general nanophotonic waveguide systems
Goban et al. 2014
;
Samutpraphoot et al. 2020
;
Kaufman 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2024
, and with its compatibility with optical tweezers and conventional fiber systems, our system has potential application to quantum networking
Kimble 2008
;
Sunami et al. 2025
.

Setup

A schematic diagram of the experiment is shown in Fig.

x

x

\mu

w_{x}

The lattice laser is detuned by -130 GHz (0.26 nm) from the
85
Rb D
2
transition, creating an attractive trapping potential for the atoms in the ground state at the local intensity maxima. For our typical trap depth of
U_{0}=k_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot 0.5
mK, where
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant, the off-resonant photon scattering rate is
4.9\times 10^{2}
/s, which is negligible on the time scales of interest (
\sim
100 ns). The corresponding recoil heating rate 0.2 mK/s is also a negligible rate. Background atomic collisions and a hot nanofiber surface limit the lifetime of the trap on a much shorter time scale (
\sim
10 ms)
Hümmer et al. 2019
. We note that our choice of the lattice detuning was due to the laser available at the time, and we plan to use a larger detuning with higher power in future experiments.

We fabricate the optical nanofiber by heating and stretching a standard single-mode fiber (Thorlabs SM800) using a hydrogen-oxygen flame and a computer-controlled motor stage

. The tapered nanofiber has a radius
R=240
nm over a length of 7 mm. To prevent background atoms from adhering to the dielectric surface, we deliver a constant 100
\mu

1

2

2
cycling transition between
|F=3\rangle
and
|F=4\rangle
, co-propagates with the heating laser through the nanofiber and is quasi-linearly polarized
Kien et al. 2004
in the
z

y

III
experiment

Our experimental sequence begins with a 2.5-second loading of the magneto-optical trap (MOT) surrounding the nanofiber, followed by a 30-ms sub-Doppler cooling phase. The lattice beams remain on throughout the experiment. Once we turn off the MOT lasers, we apply a “push-out” beam driving both the (
\left|5^{2}S_{1/2}\,F=2\right>\rightarrow\left|5^{2}P_{1/2}\,F=3\right>
) and (
\left|5^{2}S_{1/2}\,F=3\right>\rightarrow\left|5^{2}P_{3/2}\,F=3\right>
) transitions for 4 ms along the
y
direction, removing the ambient atoms from the MOT, and leaving only the atoms trapped in the lattice. During the lattice holding time, the lattice beam power provides a trap depth of
U_{0}\approx k_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot 0.5
mK. After a holding time of 5 to 60 ms, we gradually reduce the trap depth to
U_{0}\approx k_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot 0.2
mK over a duration of 1 ms, prior to interrogation. This reduces the Stark-induced light shift and inhomogeneous spectral broadening during our optical depth measurement. We verified that reducing the trap depth for less than 2 ms does not cause a significant loss of trapped atoms. We turn on a free-space repumping beam driving the
\mathrm{D}_{1}
transition 0.2 ms before the interrogation stage, so the atoms are optically active for the probe beam. Following this, we send a 1-ms probe pulse through the fiber, driving the
|F=3\rangle\rightarrow|F=4\rangle
atomic D
2
cycling transition.

We measure the transmitted probe beam power
P_{\mathrm{T}}
in the presence of atoms, the incident probe power
P_{\mathrm{in}}
without atoms, and the background power
P_{\mathrm{bg}}
without probe beam sequentially measured within the same experimental shot to calculate the transmission
\mathcal{T}
:

\mathcal{T}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{T}}-P_{\mathrm{bg}}}{P_{\mathrm{in}}-P_{\mathrm{bg}}}.

Finally, to investigate the effect of the variation in the lattice period on the atomic motional degree of freedom, we perform parametric heating-induced atomic loss spectroscopy
Savard et al. 1997
. We modulate the lattice beam power, targeting a frequency twice the lattice’s axial trap frequency,
f_{\mathrm{ax}}
. The relationship between
f_{\mathrm{ax}}
and the lattice period
d_{\mathrm{lat}}
is given by:

f_{\mathrm{ax}}=\frac{1}{d_{\mathrm{lat}}}\sqrt{\frac{U_{0}}{2M}},

(6)

where
U_{0}
is the lattice trap depth and
M
is the mass of the
85
Rb atom.
To mitigate the axial inhomogeneity of trap depth due to Gaussian beam profile, we restrict the spatial extent of the lattice to approximately 1 mm around its center by placing a slit on the grating plate to mask the beam intensity outside the targeted extent. Following the 4-ms push-out stage, we modulate the lattice beam power with an amplitude of
\approx 3\%
of the total power for 20 ms. For these trap-frequency measurements, we reduce the time-averaged trap depth to
k_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot 0.4
mK (80% of the maximum depth) to stay in the linear regime of the acousto-optic modulator (AOM) that controls the lattice beam power. We ramp down the lattice depth to
U_{0}\approx k_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot 0.1
mK for 1 ms before the interrogation to remove the parametrically heated atoms more effectively. We fix the probe laser frequency at the light-shifted resonance of the D
2
cycling transition, and keep the power below 1 pW to avoid atomic saturation.

From the FEM simulations (see Fig.
2
), applying a harmonic approximation around the intensity maximum, the trapping frequencies for radial and azimuthal degrees of freedom are estimated to be
\approx
300 kHz and
\approx
70 kHz, respectively. However, despite our extended scan beyond the presented modulation frequency range, we were unable to identify those trapping frequencies through this measurement. We hypothesize that this may be due to reduced visibility caused by the anharmonicity of the trapping potentials in those degrees of freedom and the inseparability between them. Additionally, the signal corresponding to the azimuthal trap frequency may have been dominated by the axial frequencies, hindering clear identification.

With the fixed lattice extent (
\approx 1
mm), the measured number of atoms remained nearly constant (
N\approx 1000
) throughout the range of lattice periods. This results in the atomic filling factor of approximately 0.9 to 1.5 per site, which is significantly higher than half-filling due to photon-assisted collisional blockade
Schlosser et al. 2002
;
Vetsch et al. 2012
. We attribute this difference to the larger trap volume and shorter lattice holding time (5 ms) employed in our experiment. We anticipate that employing near-ground-state cooling of the atomic motion and a larger trap depth would bring the atoms into the blockade regime and realize a half-filled sub-Poissonian number distribution. It is worth noting that the Poissonian distribution of the atom numbers in the individual sites does not pose a significant hindrance to the application to study collective radiation. The 1D nature of the infinite-range interaction
Solano et al. 2017b
effectively overlooks the specific atomic number distribution.

Our ongoing work aims to enhance the trap depth and achieve near ground-state cooling
Meng et al. 2018
to provide better axial localization. To establish a highly reflective atomic Bragg mirror, we need to increase the Debye-Waller factor
f_{\mathrm{DW}}=e^{-4k^{2}\sigma_{\mathrm{ax}}^{2}}
, where
k
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{ax}}
denote the wave number of the probe light and the atomic spread in the axial direction, respectively
Ashcroft and Mermin 1976
;
Birkl et al. 1995
;
Corzo et al. 2016
. The axial atomic spread
\sigma_{\mathrm{ax}}=d_{\mathrm{lat}}\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{B}}T/({2U_{0})}}/\pi
depends both on the temperature
T
and the trap depth
U_{0}
. Our current temperature
T\approx 30\,\mu
K, trap depth
U_{0}=k_{\mathrm{B}}\cdot 0.44
mK, and
d_{\mathrm{lat}}=1.0\,\mu
m yields
f_{\mathrm{DW}}\approx 0.3
. With a higher power laser system and enhanced cooling to 10
\mu
K, we can achieve
f_{\mathrm{DW}}
= 0.9.

IV
Summary and outlook

In summary, we have demonstrated a new method for interfacing a one-dimensional ordered array of over a thousand atoms, with variable lattice periods, using a simple optical imaging technique. We have verified the maximum number of trapped atoms to be
1270\pm 35
through a saturation measurement and estimated the single-atom optical depth to be
\approx 0.86\%
. To confirm control of the lattice period, we conducted parametric heating loss spectroscopy and found a good agreement between the experimental results and the model. We highlight that our method holds potential for application in various cold-atom nanophotonic systems
Goban et al. 2014
;
Samutpraphoot et al. 2020
;
Kaufman 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2024
beyond the nanofiber platforms.

Our approach provides lattice constant tunability irrespective of the trap beam frequency, suitable for exploring collective radiative behavior of the atomic array coupled to nanophotonic waveguides. This method allows for the exact first-order Bragg condition (
q=1
) to create an atomic mirror
Chang et al. 2012
;
Corzo et al. 2016
;
Sørensen et al. 2016
;
Sinha et al. 2025
, without dissipation from the trapping potential. The wide range of lattice tunability allows study of externally driven collective radiative behavior, providing both symmetric (even
q
) and antisymmetric (odd
q
) coupling conditions. These options enable us to explore super- and sub-radiant behavior, respectively
Dicke 1954
;
Gross and Haroche 1982
;
Yan et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2025
. The single-sided trap geometry provides a convenient platform to study chiral quantum optics, which typically requires additional experimental complexity to trap atoms only on one side of the waveguide
Lodahl et al. 2017
;
Petersen et al. 2014
;
Suárez-Forero et al. 2025
. The combination of chirality and precisely ordered collective coupling may offer a promising opportunity to advance the quantum optics field.

Our work is an important stepping stone towards realizing quantum networking architectures
Sunami et al. 2025
, providing ability to interface thousands of atoms to the waveguide with great flexibility. Our platform can be combined with optical tweezers to interface free space tweezer arrays for quantum computation with nanophotonic waveguides for quantum networking. While the static lattice can host thousands of atoms near the waveguide, facilitating their collective interaction, the dynamic tweezers could dedicate their finite resources to individually transporting
Barredo et al. 2016
;
Bluvstein et al. 2022
;
Nayak et al. 2019
or addressing
Zhang et al. 2006
;
Weitenberg et al. 2011
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2016
single atoms. The nanofiber’s inherent connectivity to conventional fibers further promotes this interface as a promising building block for a functional quantum network, providing seamless optical linkage between the local quantum processors
Kimble 2008
;
Sunami et al. 2025
.
