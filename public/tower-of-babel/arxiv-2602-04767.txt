Descent-restricted subsequences via RSK and evacuation
Krishna Menon, Anurag Singh

Abstract
The length 𝗂𝗌 ⁡ ( π ) \mathsf{is}(\pi) of a longest increasing subsequence in a permutation π \pi has been extensively studied. An increasing subsequence is one that has no descents. We study generalizations of this statistic by finding longest subsequences with other descent restrictions. We first consider the statistic which encodes the longest length of a subsequence with a given number of descents. We then generalize this to restrict the descent set of the subsequence. Extending the classical result for 𝗂𝗌 ⁡ ( π ) \mathsf{is}(\pi) , we show how these statistics can be obtained using the RSK correspondence and the Schützenberger involution. In particular, these statistics only depend on the recording tableau of the permutation.

Introduction

The study of longest increasing subsequences (LIS) in permutations is a classical and central topic in combinatorics, with strong connections to representation theory, probability, and theoretical computer science.
A foundational result of Schensted shows that the length of a longest increasing subsequence of a permutation can be read off from the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth (RSK) correspondence, namely as the length of the first row of the associated Young diagram
[
12
]
.
This discovery initiated a vast body of work on variants, distributions, and refinements of LIS (see, for instance,
[
11
,
14
]
, and the references therein).

A popular generalization is Greene’s theorem
[
7
]
, which extends Schensted’s result to give interpretations for other row lengths of the Young diagrams obtained using the RSK correspondence.
This involves the statistic
\mathsf{is}_{k}(\pi)
, which is the longest length of a subsequence that can be expressed as a union of
k
increasing subsequences.
Variants replacing monotonicity with prescribed up-down patterns have also been explored.
A notable example is longest alternating subsequences, introduced and studied by Stanley
[
15
]
.
There are several other statistics whose motivation comes from LIS
[
2
,
4
,
17
]
.

For a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and a set
D\subseteq[n-1]
, we define

\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)\coloneqq\max\{\#I\mid\operatorname{Des}(\pi_{I})=D\},

the length of a longest subsequence of
\pi
whose descent set is precisely
D
.
This statistic simultaneously generalizes longest increasing subsequences (
D=\varnothing
), alternating subsequences, and subsequences with a fixed number of descents.

For special cases of these statistics, we describe bounds on them and also characterize and count the permutations that achieve these bounds.
For the general statistic
\mathsf{ls}_{D}
, we describe a method (based on a decomposition of
D
into intervals) to determine when such subsequences exist and how their maximum lengths can be computed.
This relies on the use of growth diagrams and the Schützenberger involution
[
6
,
13
]
, and leads to a complete tableau-theoretic description of these descent-restricted subsequence statistics.
In particular, for fixed
D
, the value of
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
depends only on the recording tableau
Q(\pi)
, extending the classical result for longest increasing subsequences.

The paper is organized as follows.
In
Section

2
, we review the necessary background on the RSK correspondence and Schützenberger involution.

Section

3
studies longest subsequences with a fixed number of descents.
We first obtain bounds for these statistics and characterize when they are met.
We then describe a method to extract these statistics from the evacuation growth diagram.
In
Section

4
, we extend these results to the statistic
\mathsf{ls}_{D}
.
We also characterize when two permutations attain the same value for all these statistics.
We conclude with several open problems and directions for future work.

\mathsf{ls}_{D}

Permutations are written in one-line notation.

For any permutation

•

\pi
.

•

For

2.1
The RSK correspondence

We now recall some basic facts about the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth (RSK) correspondence defined in
[
12
]
.
The definitions and results we state can be found in
[
16
]
.

A
partition

\lambda
of
n
, denoted
\lambda\vdash n
, is a weakly decreasing sequence of positive numbers that sums to
n
.
The
Young diagram
of a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
consists of rows of boxes where the
i
-th row (from the top) has
\lambda_{i}
boxes.
A
standard Young tableau (SYT)
of shape
\lambda
is a filling of the boxes in the Young diagram of a partition of
n
with the numbers from
[n]
(each used once) such that the rows and columns are increasing.

Given a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the RSK correspondence associates to
\pi
a pair
(P(\pi),Q(\pi))
of SYTs of the same shape
\lambda\vdash n
.
The
insertion tableau

P(\pi)
is obtained by performing row-insertion on the entries
\pi(1),\pi(2),\ldots,\pi(n)
in order, while the
recording tableau

Q(\pi)
records the positions at which new boxes are added in the insertion process.
We will not go through the details of this well-known correspondence but only mention the results we will use.

If
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
is the shape of
P(\pi)
, which is the same as that of
Q(\pi)
, then
\mathsf{is}_{k}(\pi)=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}
.
If the first row of
Q(\pi)
is
u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{\mathsf{is}(\pi)}
, then
u_{i}
is the leftmost (smallest) index where an increasing subsequence of
\pi
of length
i
can end, i.e.,
\mathsf{is}(\pi(1)\pi(2)\cdots\pi(u_{i}))=i
and
\mathsf{is}(\pi(1)\pi(2)\cdots\pi(k))<i
for all
k<u_{i}
.
Note that while performing the RSK algorithm,
u_{i}
is the index at which the length of the first row becomes
i
.

For an SYT
Q
, we define
\operatorname{Des}(Q)
to be the set of entries
i
in
Q
such that
i+1
is in a strictly lower row than
i
and set
\operatorname{des}(Q)=\#\operatorname{Des}(Q)
.
For any permutation
\pi
, we have
\operatorname{Des}(\pi)=\operatorname{Des}(Q(\pi))
.

2.2
The Schützenberger involution

An important operation on SYTs that we will use is the Schützenberger involution
[
13
]
.
This in a shape-preserving involution on SYTs and the image of an SYT
Q
under this map is called its
evacuation tableau
and denoted by
\operatorname{evac}(Q)
.
We use Fomin’s
growth diagrams
to define this involution
[
6
]
(other equivalent definitions can be found in
[
16
]
).

For two partitions
\lambda
and
\mu
, we say that
\lambda
contains
\mu
if the Young diagram of
\lambda
contains that of
\mu
.
To start, we note that any SYT can be encoded using a chain of partitions.
These partitions indicate the shapes formed by boxes labeled with numbers having value at most
i
for each
i\geq 0
.
For example, the SYT
P(\pi)
from
Example

2.2
is encoded by the chain

\varnothing\subset 1\subset 2\subset 21\subset 211\subset 221\subset 222\subset 322.

The evacuation growth diagram associated to an SYT
Q
of size
n
is an triangle of partitions
\Lambda_{i,j}
for
0\leq i\leq j\leq n
.
The tableau
Q
is encoded using
(\Lambda_{0,j})_{j=0}^{n}
and we set
\Lambda_{i,0}=\varnothing
for all
i
.
We want the final diagram to be such that
\Lambda_{i,j}
is contained in
\Lambda_{i,j+1}
and contains
\Lambda_{i+1,j}
.
Note that if
\lambda\vdash n
and
\mu\vdash n+2
, then there are at most two partitions that contain
\lambda
and are contained in
\mu
.
If
\Lambda_{i-1,j-1}
,
\Lambda_{i,j-1}
, and
\Lambda_{i-1,j}
are defined, we set
\Lambda_{i,j}
to be different from
\Lambda_{i-1,j-1}
whenever possible.
These conditions uniquely define the triangle of partitions.
We define
\operatorname{evac}(Q)
to be the SYT that is encoded by the chain of partitions
(\Lambda_{n-i,n})_{i=0}^{n}
.

3
Restricting the number of descents

We first consider subsequences of a permutation with a prescribed number of descents.

Definition 3.1
.

For any permutation
\pi
and
d\geq 0
,

\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)\coloneqq\max\{\#I\mid\operatorname{des}(\pi_{I})=d\}.

Hence,
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)
is the maximum length of a subsequence of
\pi
that has exactly
d
descents.
When
d=0
, this reduces to the longest increasing subsequence statistic, so
\mathsf{ls}_{0}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
\pi=1573426
. Then the values of
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)
are as follows.

•

\mathsf{ls}_{0}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)=4
.
Consider the subsequence
1346
.

•

\mathsf{ls}_{1}(\pi)=6
. Consider
157346
.

•

\mathsf{ls}_{2}(\pi)=7
, since the entire permutation
\pi
has exactly two descents.

•

\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=0
for all
d>2
, because no subsequence of
\pi
can have more descents than
\pi
itself.

The following lemma records a few basic properties of these statistics which will be used repeatedly in the sequel.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any permutation
\pi
, we have
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)\geq d+1
for all

d\leq\operatorname{des}(\pi)
and
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=0
for all
d>\operatorname{des}(\pi)
. Moreover, for any
d_{1}<d_{2}\leq\operatorname{des}(\pi)
, we have
\mathsf{ls}_{d_{1}}(\pi)<\mathsf{ls}_{d_{2}}(\pi)
.

Proof.

Let
\operatorname{Des}(\pi)=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{\operatorname{des}(\pi)}\}
.
For any
d\leq\operatorname{des}(\pi)
, consider the subsequence
\pi(1)\pi(2)\cdots\pi(i_{d})\pi(i_{d}+1)
.
This subsequence has exactly
d
descents, since each descent of
\pi
occurring at a position in
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}
is preserved.
As
i_{d}\geq d
, this subsequence has length at least
d+1
, and hence

\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)\geq d+1
.

Any subsequence of
\pi
can have at most
\operatorname{des}(\pi)
descents, so

\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=0
for all
d>\operatorname{des}(\pi)
.
Finally, the strict inequality
\mathsf{ls}_{d_{1}}(\pi)<\mathsf{ls}_{d_{2}}(\pi)
for

d_{1}<d_{2}\leq\operatorname{des}(\pi)
follows from the fact that inserting one number into a sequence either does not change the number of descents or increases it by
1
.
∎

We next establish general bounds on the statistics
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)
in terms of classical subsequence parameters associated with
\pi
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any permutation
\pi
and
d\leq\operatorname{des}(\pi)
, we have

\mathsf{is}(\pi)+d\leq\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)\leq\operatorname{min}\{\mathsf{is}_{d+1}(\pi),\operatorname{asc}(\pi)+d+1\}.

Proof.

Any subsequence of
\pi
with
d
descents is the union of
d+1
increasing subsequences and hence
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)\leq\mathsf{is}_{d+1}(\pi)
.
To obtain the other upper bound, break up the permutation
\pi
into maximal decreasing factors.
If
\pi=741632985
, it breaks up into maximal decreasing factors as
741\,|\,632\,|\,985
.
Note that the number of such factors is
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)+1
.
By the pigeon-hole principle, any subsequence with more than
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)+d+1
terms has at least
d+1
descents.
This shows that
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)\leq\operatorname{asc}(\pi)+d+1
.

The lower bound follows from
Lemma

3.3
.
Since
d\leq\operatorname{des}(\pi)
, we have
\mathsf{is}(\pi)=\mathsf{ls}_{0}(\pi)<\mathsf{ls}_{1}(\pi)<\cdots<\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)
which gives us
\mathsf{is}(\pi)+d\leq\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)
.
∎

The bounds in
Lemma

3.4
are not sharp in general. We characterize precisely when the lower bound
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)+d
is attained.

Proposition 3.5
.

For any permutation
\pi
and
1\leq d\leq\operatorname{des}(\pi)
, we have
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)+d
if and only if
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)-1
.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)-1
, then
Lemma

3.4
immediately gives
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)+d
.
Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Note that if there exists
1\leq i<j\leq n
such that
\pi(i)<\pi(j)
, then there exists an ascent at some index in
[i,j-1]
.
Using an increasing subsequence of length
\mathsf{is}(\pi)
, this shows that
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)\geq\mathsf{is}(\pi)-1
.

Hence, by
Lemma

3.4
, we have to show that if
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)\geq\mathsf{is}(\pi)
, then
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)\geq\mathsf{is}(\pi)+d+1
.
We only have to prove this for the case
d=1
.
Since if
\mathsf{ls}_{1}(\pi)\geq\mathsf{is}(\pi)+2
, from
Lemma

3.3
, we get
\mathsf{ls}_{1}(\pi)<\mathsf{ls}_{2}(\pi)<\cdots<\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)
, which gives us
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)\geq\mathsf{is}(\pi)+d+1
, as required.

Set
k=\mathsf{is}(\pi)
and suppose that
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)\geq k
.
We will show that
\mathsf{ls}_{1}(\pi)\geq k+2
by constructing a subsequence of length
k+2
with exactly one descent.
Suppose that
I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
is such that
\pi_{I}
is an increasing subsequence of
\pi
.

If
\pi
has an ascent
j\in[1,i_{1}-1]\cup[i_{k},n]
, then
\pi_{I\cup\{j,j+1\}}
is a subsequence of length
k+2
with one descent.
Note that such a
j
can never be equal to
i_{1}-1
or
i_{k}
since this would contradict the fact that
\mathsf{is}(\pi)=k
.

If
\pi
has no ascent of the form mentioned above, then since
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)\geq k
, there must exist some
m\in[k-1]
such that
\pi
has at least two ascents in
[i_{m},i_{m+1}-1]
.
Note that
\mathsf{is}(\pi)=k
implies that for any
i\in[i_{m},i_{m+1}-1]
,
\pi(i)<\pi(i_{m})
or
\pi(i)>\pi(i_{m+1})
.
The fact that there are at least two ascents in
[i_{m},i_{m+1}-1]
means that there exist
i,j\in[i_{m},i_{m+1}-1]
where
i<j
such that

1.

\pi(i)>\pi(i_{m+1})
and
\pi(j)<\pi(i_{m})
, or

2.

\pi(i_{m+1})<\pi(i)<\pi(j)
, or

3.

\pi(i)<\pi(j)<\pi(i_{m})
.

In each case,
\pi_{I\cup\{i,j\}}
is a subsequence of length
k+2
with one descent.
∎

The above proposition, along with
Lemma

3.4
, gives us the following result.

Corollary 3.6
.

For any permutation
\pi
and
d\leq\operatorname{des}(\pi)
, if
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)
, then
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)+d+1

However, the converse of the above corollary is not true.
For example, for
\pi=563412
, we have
\mathsf{is}(\pi)=2
,
\mathsf{ls}_{1}(\pi)=4
, and
\operatorname{asc}(\pi)=3
.

We now describe a method to compute the statistic
\mathsf{ls}_{d}
on permutations.
For a permutation
\pi
of size

\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=\operatorname{max}\{\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[1,i_{1}]})+\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[i_{1}+1,i_{2}]})+\cdots+\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[i_{d}+1,n]})\mid 1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{d}\leq n\}.

Note that for any
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, the growth diagram for
\operatorname{evac}(Q(\pi_{[i,j]}))
is a sub-diagram of the one for
\operatorname{evac}(Q(\pi))
(see
Figure

This shows that we can obtain
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)
from the growth diagram of
\operatorname{evac}(Q(\pi))
.
We vary over all ‘break-ups’ of the diagram into
d+1
sub-diagrams as shown in
Figure

Proposition 3.8

For a non-identity permutation

, setting

4
Restricting the descent set

We now consider the finer statistic where we restrict the descent set rather than just the number of descents.

Definition 4.1
.

For a permutation
\pi
and set of numbers
D
,

\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)\coloneqq\max\{\#I\mid\operatorname{Des}(\pi_{I})=D\}.

Hence,
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
is the length of a longest subsequence of
\pi
whose descent set is
D
.

Example 4.2
.

For
\pi=42783561
, we have the following.

•

\mathsf{ls}_{\{3\}}(\pi)=6
. Consider the subsequence
478356
.

•

\mathsf{ls}_{\{1,2,3\}}(\pi)=0
since there is no subsequence with descent set
\{1,2,3\}
.

•

\mathsf{ls}_{\{1,3,4\}}(\pi)=5
.
Consider
42831
.

Note that the statistics on permutations we have seen so far can be obtained from this one.
For example,
\mathsf{is}(\pi)=\mathsf{ls}_{\varnothing}(\pi)
and
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=\operatorname{max}\{\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)\mid\#D=d\}
.

The statistics defined in
[
15
]
can also be obtained from the statistics mentioned above.
For a permutations
\pi
, set
\mathsf{as}(\pi)
to be the length of a longest
alternating
subsequence of
\pi
.
We say that a sequence
\sigma
of length
n
is alternating if
\operatorname{Des}(\sigma)=\{1,3,5,\ldots\}\cap[n-1]
.
Hence,
\mathsf{as}(\pi)=\max\{\#I\mid\pi_{I}\text{ is alternating}\}.

To obtain
\mathsf{as}(\pi)
, find the largest
k
such that
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)\neq 0
where
D=\{1,3,5,\ldots,2k-1\}
.
If
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)=2k
, then
\mathsf{as}(\pi)=2k
.
Otherwise,
\mathsf{as}(\pi)=2k+1
.

Another class of statistics defined in
[
15
, Section 5]
is via
descent words
.

Definition 4.3
.

For any sequence
\pi
of length
n
, its descent word
\operatorname{dword}(\pi)
is given by
w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n-1}
where
w_{i}=\mathtt{D}
if
i\in\operatorname{Des}(\pi)
and otherwise
w_{i}=\mathtt{U}
.

For example,
\operatorname{dword}(534612)=\mathtt{DUUDU}
.
Starting with any finite word
w
in the alphabet
\{\mathtt{U},\mathtt{D}\}
, set
\operatorname{len}_{w}(\pi)
to be the longest length of a subsequence of
\pi
whose descent word is a prefix of
w^{\infty}=www\cdots
, the infinite word formed by concatenating copies of
w
.
Note that
\mathsf{is}(\pi)=\operatorname{len}_{\mathtt{U}}(\pi)
and
\mathsf{as}(\pi)=\operatorname{len}_{\mathtt{DU}}(\pi)
.

Example 4.4
.

For
\pi=31452867
, we have
\operatorname{len}_{\mathtt{UUD}}(\pi)=6
.
Note that
(\mathtt{UUD})^{\infty}=\mathtt{UUDUUDUUD}\cdots
and
\pi

\sigma=345267

\operatorname{dword}(\sigma)=\mathtt{UUDUU}

Also,
\pi
has no subsequence with descent word

Determining whether
\pi

\mathsf{ls}_{D}
.
For example,
\pi

.

from statistics of the form

.

4.1
Exactly one descent

Before moving to the general case, we first consider the simplest case of when
\#D=1
.
We first determine when these statistics attain a non-zero value.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\pi
be a non-identity permutation of size
n
with last descent at
k
.
If
j=\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[k]})
, then
\mathsf{ls}_{\{i\}}(\pi)\geq i+n-k
for all
i\leq j
and
\mathsf{ls}_{\{i\}}(\pi)=0
for all
i>j
.

Proof.

Let
i\leq j
.
Choose an increasing subsequence of
\pi(1)\pi(2)\cdots\pi(k)
that is of length
i
(which is possible by the definition of
j
).
If the last term of this subsequence is
\pi(m)
and
\pi(m)>\pi(k)
, then appending
\pi(k+1)\pi(k+2)\cdots\pi(n)
to the end of this subsequence gives a subsequence of length
i+n-k
with descent set
\{i\}
.
If
\pi(m)\leq\pi(k)
, then deleting
\pi(m)
and appending
\pi(k)\pi(k+1)\cdots\pi(n)
gives us a subsequence of the required form.
Hence, we have
\mathsf{ls}_{\{i\}}(\pi)\geq i+n-k
.

If
i>j
, then any increasing subsequence of length
i
(if one exists) must have last entry
\pi(m)
for some
m>k
.
But no term after
\pi(m)
is smaller than it (by the definition of
k
).
Hence, there cannot exist a subsequence with descent set
\{i\}
.
∎

Just as we were able to extract
\mathsf{ls}_{1}(\pi)
from the first row of
Q(\pi)
and its evacuation tableau (see
Proposition

3.8
), we can also do so for
\mathsf{ls}_{\{i\}}(\pi)
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\pi
be a permutation of size
n
and set
Q=Q(\pi)
and
Q_{e}=\operatorname{evac}(Q)
.
For any
i\geq 1
, we have

\mathsf{ls}_{\{i\}}(\pi)=\operatorname{max}\{i+j\mid Q(1,i)+Q_{e}(1,j)\leq n\text{ and }Q_{e}(1,j+1)\neq Q_{e}(1,j)+1\}

where we set the maximum of the empty set to be
0
.

Proof.

For any
i,j\geq 1
, we use
u_{i}
to denote
Q(1,i)
and
v_{j}
to denote
n-Q_{e}(1,j)+1
.
We say that a pair
(i,j)
is a
good pair
if
u_{i}<v_{j}
and
v_{j+1}\neq v_{j}-1
.
Note that for any
j\geq 1
,
v_{j+1}\neq v_{j}-1
is equivalent to
v_{j}-1\in\operatorname{Des}(\pi)
.
Hence, the proposition asserts that for any
i\geq 1
,

\mathsf{ls}_{\{i\}}(\pi)=\operatorname{max}\{i+j\mid(i,j)\text{ is a good pair}\}.

Let
(i,j)
be a good pair.
We will construct a subsequence with descent set
\{i\}
of length
i+j
.
Consider an increasing subsequence of length
i
ending at
u_{i}
and combine it with an increasing subsequence of length
j
starting at
v_{j}
.
If
\pi(u_{i})>\pi(v_{j})
, we get a subsequence satisfying our requirements.
If
\pi(u_{i})<\pi(v_{j})
, replace
\pi(u_{i})
in this subsequence with
\pi(v_{j}-1)
.
This will give us a subsequence that satisfies our requirements.

Next suppose that we have a subsequence of size
i+k
with descent set
\{i\}
.
We will show that there exists a good pair
(i,j)
where
j\geq k
.
This will prove the proposition.

Suppose that there is no such good pair.
Since we already have
u_{i}<v_{k}
, this means that
u_{i}=v_{j}
for some
j>k
and
v_{j},v_{j-1},\ldots,v_{k+1},v_{k}
forms a sequence of consecutive numbers.
Hence, we have that

•

\pi(v_{j})\pi(v_{j-1})\cdots\pi(v_{k+1})\pi(v_{k})
is an increasing factor of
\pi
,

•

any increasing subsequence of length
i
in
\pi
must end at an index weakly to the right of
u_{i}=v_{j}
, and

•

any increasing subsequence of length
k
in
\pi
must begin at an index weakly to the left of
v_{k}
.

This tells us that it is impossible for
\pi
to have a subsequence of length
i+k
with descent set
\{i\}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

4.6

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\pi
be a permutation of size
n
and
i\geq 1
be such that
\mathsf{ls}_{\{i\}}(\pi)\neq 0

Suppose

\mathsf{ls}_{\{i\}}(\pi)=i+\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[k+1,n]}).

From definitions, it is clear that for any permutation

In the case

Corollary 4.9

\sum_{\lambda\vdash n-1}f^{\lambda+1}f^{\lambda}

4.2
The general case

We now move on to the general statistic
\mathsf{ls}_{D}
.
The proof of the following result is similar to that of
Proposition

4.5
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
\pi
be a non-identity permutation of size
n
.
Let
D=D^{\prime}\sqcup[i,i+l-1]
where
i
is the largest element of
D
such that
i-1\notin D
.
Let
k
be the largest index such that
\pi_{[k,n]}
has a decreasing subsequence of length
l
.
We have,
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)\neq 0
if and only if
i\leq\mathsf{ls}_{D^{\prime}}(\pi_{[k]})
.

The above proposition allows us to inductively determine if
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)\neq 0
by breaking up
D
into intervals.
We now describe a method to extract
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
using this break up of
D
.
We use the notation
\mathsf{ds}(\pi)
to denote the length of a longest decreasing subsequence of
\pi
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
D\subseteq[n-1]
such that
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)\neq 0
.
If
D=\varnothing
, we have
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)=\mathsf{is}(\pi)
.
Otherwise, we have

\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)=m+\mathsf{ls}_{D^{\prime}}(\pi_{[k,n]})

where
D^{\prime}=\{i-m\mid i\in D\setminus[m]\}
and

•

if
1\in D
, then
m
is the smallest number such that
m+1\notin D
and
k
is the smallest index such that
\mathsf{ds}(\pi_{[k]})=m+1
, and

•

if
1\notin D
, then
m=\min D-1
and
k
is the smallest index such that
\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[k]})=m+1
.

Proof.

We only consider the case when
1\in D
, the other case can be proved similarly.
By the definition of
m
and
k
, we must have that
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)\leq m+\mathsf{ls}_{D^{\prime}}(\pi_{[k,n]})
.
We prove the equality by constructing a subsequence of
\pi
with descent set
D
of the required length.

Let
\pi_{K}
be a decreasing subsequence of length
m+1
with
\max K=k
and
\pi_{J}
be a subsequence with
J\subseteq[k,n]
and
\operatorname{Des}(\pi_{J})=D^{\prime}
.
Let
j=\min J
.
If
\pi_{k}\leq\pi_{j}
, then consider
I=(K\setminus\{k\})\cup J
and note that
\operatorname{Des}(\pi_{I})=D
.
Similarly, if
\pi_{k}>\pi_{j}
, then consider
I=K\cup(J\setminus\{j\})
.
∎

We unravel the inductive method mentioned above to describe a procedure to extract
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
directly.
To do this, it is convenient to represent the set
D
as a composition.
To any composition
c=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k})
, we associate a set
D_{c}
as follows:
Set
d_{i}=c_{1}+c_{2}+\cdots+c_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
.
If
k
is odd, then

D_{c}=[d_{1}]\cup[d_{2}+1,d_{3}]\cup\cdots\cup[d_{k-1}+1,d_{k}].

Similarly, if
k
is even, then

D_{c}=[d_{1}+1,d_{2}]\cup[d_{3}+1,d_{4}]\cup\cdots\cup[d_{k-1}+1,d_{k}].

For example,
D_{(2,3,1)}=\{1,2,6\}
.

To extract
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
, we consider a triangle of pairs that record the longest increasing and decreasing subsequence lengths for factors of
\pi
.

Definition 4.12
.

For a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
,

a_{i,j}(\pi)\coloneqq(\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[i,j]}),\mathsf{ds}(\pi_{[i,j]})).

We draw the triangle of
a_{i,j}(\pi)
as shown in
Figure

3
.
We use blue entries for longest increasing subsequence lengths and red for decreasing.
If the triangle is rotated
45
degrees clockwise, then the labeling
i,j
is just like that of a matrix.
Note that the values
a_{i,j}(\pi)
are the lengths of the firsts rows and columns of the (nonempty) partitions in the evacuation growth diagram of
Q(\pi)
.

For
\pi=314526

We now describe an algorithm to extract
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
using the triangle of

\mathsf{ls}_{D_{c}}(\pi)=0

If the first entry of

From the algorithm described above, we can see that for any
D
, the value
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
only depends on the values
a_{i,j}(\pi)
as
i,j
varies over
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
We have mentioned that these values can be extracted from the evacuation growth diagram of
Q(\pi)
.
This gives us the following.

Corollary 4.17
.

For any permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
D\subseteq[n-1]
, the value
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
only depends on
Q(\pi)
.

However, the converse of the above result is not true. One can check that
\pi=2147635
and
\sigma=3247615
satisfy
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)=\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\sigma)
for all sets
D
, but
Q(\pi)\neq Q(\sigma)
.

Although we can not extract
Q(\pi)
from the values of
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
, we can extract the triangle of values
a_{i,j}(\pi)
, which gives us the following result.

Proposition 4.18
.

For any two permutations
\pi,\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the following are equivalent.

•

For all
D\subseteq[n-1]
, we have
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)=\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\sigma)
.

•

For all
1\leq i<j\leq n
, we have
a_{i,j}(\pi)=a_{i,j}(\sigma)
.

Proof.

We only have to prove that we can obtain the values
a_{i,j}(\pi)
from those of
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
.
For a given
1\leq i<j\leq n
, we show how one can obtain
\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[i,j]})
using the values of
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
.
The longest decreasing subsequence length in this factor can be found similarly.

To do this, it is more convenient to use the notation of descent words (see
Definition

4.3
).
Recall that we can use the values of
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
to determine if
\pi
has a subsequence with a given descent word.
Let
w=\operatorname{dword}(\pi_{[i-1]})
and
v=\operatorname{dword}(\pi_{[j+1,n]})
.
We find the largest value of
k
such that
\pi
has a subsequence whose descent word is of the form

w\quad x_{1}\quad\mathtt{U}^{k-1}\quad x_{2}\quad v

where
x_{1},x_{2}\in\{\mathtt{U},\mathtt{D}\}
and
x_{1}
is omitted if
i=1
and
x_{2}
is omitted if
j=n
.
It can be verified that this value of
k
is precisely
\mathsf{is}(\pi_{[i,j]})
.
∎

5
Future directions

We now gather some problems that could be of interest to explore.

1.

An obvious question would be to study the distributions of the statistics mentioned in this paper.
For example, are there reasonable ways to count permutations
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with
\mathsf{ls}_{d}(\pi)=k
for given values of
n,d,k
?
It might be easier to solve this problem for certain sub-classes of permutations.
Restricting to the class of permutations with at most
1
descent is done in
[
9
]
.

2.

The next direction is combining these statistics with pattern avoidance (basic definitions can be found in
[
1
, Chapter 4]
).
Several questions involving the distribution of the statistics
\mathsf{is}
and
\mathsf{as}
over classes of pattern-avoiding permutations have been explored
[
3
,
5
,
8
]
.
One could try to study the distribution of the statistics mentioned in this paper over pattern-avoiding permutations.

Another question, mentioned in
[
15
, Section 5.3]
, involves sets of patterns of the form
P_{k,D}\coloneqq\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}\mid\operatorname{Des}(\sigma)=D\}
.
Note that a permutation
\pi
avoids all the patterns in
P_{k,D}
if and only if
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)<k
.
Since
\mathsf{ls}_{D}(\pi)
only depends on
Q(\pi)
, it might be interesting to explore avoidance of these sets of patterns for involutions.

3.

Proposition

4.18
gives two descriptions of an equivalence on permutations.
What more can be said about this equivalence?
How many equivalences classes are there among permutations of size
n
?
Are there any general methods to obtain non-trivial equivalences among permutations?
