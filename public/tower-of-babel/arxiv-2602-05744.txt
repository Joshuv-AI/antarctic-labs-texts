Generalized Pinsker Inequality for Bregman Divergences of Negative Tsallis Entropies
Guglielmo Beretta, Tommaso Cesari, Roberto Colomboni

Abstract
The Pinsker inequality lower bounds the Kullback–Leibler divergence D KL D_{\mathrm{KL}} in terms of total variation and provides a canonical way to convert D KL D_{\mathrm{KL}} control into ‖ ⋅ ‖ 1 \left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1} -control. Motivated by applications to probabilistic prediction with Tsallis losses and online learning, we establish a generalized Pinsker inequality for the Bregman divergences D α D_{\alpha} generated by the negative α \alpha -Tsallis entropies—also known as β \beta -divergences. Specifically, for any p p , q q in the relative interior of the probability simplex Δ K \Delta^{K} , we prove the sharp bound D α ( p ∥ q ) ≥ C α , K 2 ⋅ ∥ p − q ∥ 1 2 , D_{\alpha}(p\|q)\geq\frac{C_{\alpha,K}}{2}\cdot\|p-q\|_{1}^{2}\;, and we determine the optimal constant C α , K C_{\alpha,K} explicitly for every choice of ( α , K ) (\alpha,K) .

Pinsker Inequality for Bregman Divergences of Tsallis Entropies

For the remainder of this work, we assume that
K
is any integer greater than or equal to 2.
For any
\alpha>0
and any
p\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, we denote by
\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\alpha}\coloneqq\bigl(\sum_{k\in[K]}\left\lvert p_{k}\right\rvert^{\alpha}\bigr)^{1/\alpha}
the usual Minkowski’s (quasi-) norm of the vector
p
.
We denote by

\Delta^{K}\coloneqq\bigl\{p\in[0,1]^{K}:\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{1}=1\bigr\}

the
(K-1)
-dimensional probability simplex
and its relative interior by

\relint\left(\Delta^{K}\right)\coloneqq\Delta^{K}\cap(0,1)^{K}\;.

We begin by recalling the entropies that appear as Bayes risks for the family of power/Tsallis losses; their negatives serve as convex generators of the Bregman divergences we study.

Definition 1

(Tsallis entropies)
.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, define
S_{\alpha}\colon[0,+\infty)^{K}\to[-\infty,+\infty)
by

S_{\alpha}(p)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\vskip 4.30554pt\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}^{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}\;,\qquad&\text{ if $\alpha\notin\left\{0,1\right\}$}\;,\\
\vskip 4.30554pt\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[K]}\ln p_{k}\;,\qquad&\text{ if $\alpha=0$}\;,\\
\displaystyle-\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}\ln p_{k}\;,\qquad&\text{ if $\alpha=1$}\;,\end{cases}

(2)

with the understanding that
0^{\alpha}=+\infty
for negative
\alpha
, and
\ln(0)=-\infty
but
0\cdot\ln(0)=0
.
For any
p\in\Delta^{K}
, we call the quantity
S_{\alpha}(p)
the
\alpha
-
Tsallis entropy

In the literature, definitions of the
\alpha
-Tsallis entropies may differ by a multiplicative factor and by the addition of an affine function of

(in particular, an additive constant).
For Bregman divergences, scaling the generator scales the divergence, while adding any affine function leaves it unchanged: for

,

, and

D_{c\cdot f+a+\langle u,\cdot\rangle}(p,q)=c\cdot D_{f}(p,q)\;,\qquad\text{for any $p,q\in(0,+\infty)^{K}$}\;.

Accordingly, changing the multiplicative factor in the definition of
S_{\alpha}
results in the same multiplicative scaling in the induced divergence—and hence scales the constant
C_{\alpha,K}
in
Theorem

8
by the same factor.

of

In the literature,
S_{0}
is also known as the Burg (or log) entropy, while

The reason to include

Remark 2

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

-S_{\alpha}
is strictly convex and of class

We are now ready to introduce our main objects of study: the Bregman divergences generated by

From the scores/losses perspective, they represent the excess risk of Tsallis scores/losses whose Bayes risk is

Definition 3

(Bregman divergences)
.

Let
f\colon(0,+\infty)^{K}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function.
The Bregman divergence of

D_{f}\colon(0,+\infty)^{K}\times(0,+\infty)^{K}\to\mathbb{R}\;,\qquad(p,q)\mapsto f(p)-f(q)-\langle\nabla f(q),p-q\rangle\;,

where
\nabla f(q)
is the gradient of
f
in
q
. In what follows, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and any

with the less cumbersome notation

Readers who are not familiar with Bregman divergences may find the next remark useful.

Remark 4
.

For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

-S_{\alpha}

D_{\alpha}(p\|q)\geq 0

From the losses perspective, Remark

The case

For all

3
Geometric Intuition Behind the Proof of
Theorem

We now give some insight into the technique we use to derive the explicit expression for the sharp constant
C_{\alpha,K}
.
First, we observe that
C_{\alpha,K}
admits a variational formulation involving the Hessian
of
S_{\alpha}
and the independent variables
(\gamma,v)
, where
\gamma
lies in
\relint{\left(\Delta^{K}\right)}
, whereas
v
is a
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
-unit vector (i.e.,
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{1}=1
) and it is tangent to
\relint{\left(\Delta^{K}\right)}
(i.e.,
\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{k}=0
).
Indeed,
C_{\alpha,K}
can be written as the infimum over
(\gamma,v)
of a parametric quadratic form in the variable
v
whose coefficients (i.e.,
\gamma_{k}^{\alpha-2}
) depend on
\gamma
and
\alpha
.
Of main importance is the qualitative behavior of these coefficients, which exhibits a phase transition at
\alpha=2
.
Specifically, in the case
\alpha\leq 2
the coefficients admit a positive lower bound (since
\gamma_{k}<1
and
\alpha-2\leq 0
), but in the case
\alpha>2
this is no longer true (since
\alpha-2>0
and the corresponding
\gamma_{k}
approaches
0
).

To handle the case
\alpha\leq 2
, we first fix
v
in the parametric quadratic form and take the infimum over all possible values of
\gamma
, which amounts (up to a multiplicative constant) to finding the minimal second directional derivative of
-S_{\alpha}
in the direction
v
over the relative interior of the simplex
\relint\left(\Delta^{K}\right)
.
It turns out that this infimum equals the square of
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\beta}
, with
\beta
being a positive number (specifically,
\beta=\tfrac{2}{3-\alpha}
) that depends on
\alpha
,
and so
C_{\alpha,K}
is related to minimizing
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\beta}
over the tangent
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
-unit vectors.
Interestingly, the expression for
C_{\alpha,K}
reflects the localization of
v
attaining such constrained minimum.
Indeed, the case
\alpha\in(-\infty,1)
yields
0<\beta<1
, and
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\beta}
is minimized by concentrating the mass of
v
over two coordinates, which causes
C_{\alpha,K}
to be independent of
K
.
If
\alpha=1
, then also
\beta=1
, and tautologically
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\beta}=1
, which results in
C_{1,K}=1
. This recovers the classical Pinsker inequality.
The case
\alpha\in(1,2]
yields
\beta>1
, and
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\beta}
is minimized by spreading the mass as evenly as possible across coordinates, producing an expression for
C_{\alpha,K}
that is decreasing in
K
and with a subtle role played by the parity of
K
.

For
\alpha>2
, we shall see that a substantial difference occurs depending on
K
.
In fact, if
K\geq 3
, then it is possible to show that
C_{\alpha,K}=0
thanks to the existence of some tangent vector
v
that has some zero components.
For
K=2
, a similar
v
does not exist, and indeed
C_{\alpha,2}>0
. This time, a phase transition occurs at
\alpha=3
, where the map
\gamma_{k}\mapsto\gamma_{k}^{\alpha-2}
transitions from concave to convex.

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\lambda_{k}\left(\sum_{i\in[K]}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{1}{\nu+1}}\right)^{\nu}\lambda_{k}^{-\frac{\nu}{\nu+1}}=\sum_{k\in[K]}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{\nu+1}}\left(\sum_{i\in[K]}\lambda_{i}^{\frac{1}{\nu+1}}\right)^{\nu}=\left(\sum_{k\in[K]}\lambda_{k}^{\frac{1}{\nu+1}}\right)^{\nu+1}\;.

(10)

\displaystyle=2^{-2}\left(\left(\frac{K}{2}\right)^{1-\beta}+\left(\frac{K}{2}\right)^{1-\beta}\right)^{2/\beta}=2^{-2}\left(2\cdot\frac{{K}^{1-\beta}}{2^{1-\beta}}\right)^{2/\beta}=\left({K}^{1-\beta}\right)^{2/\beta}={K}^{\frac{2}{\beta}-2}={K}^{1-\alpha}

whereas for

\displaystyle=2^{-2}\left(\left(\frac{K-1}{2}\right)^{1-\beta}+\left(\frac{K+1}{2}\right)^{1-\beta}\right)^{2/\beta}=\left(2^{-\beta}\cdot\frac{(K-1)^{1-\beta}+(K+1)^{1-\beta}}{2^{1-\beta}}\right)^{2/\beta}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{(K-1)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{3-\alpha}}+(K+1)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{3-\alpha}}}{2}\right)^{3-\alpha}={K}^{1-\alpha}\left(\frac{(1-\frac{1}{K})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{3-\alpha}}+(1+\frac{1}{K})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{3-\alpha}}}{2}\right)^{3-\alpha}\;.

Case
\alpha>2
,
K=2
:

Note that
\mathrm{B}^{2}=\bigl\{\pm(1/2,-1/2)\bigr\}
, and so

C_{\alpha,2}=\inf\left\{\sum_{k\in[2]}v_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}^{\alpha-2}:v\in\mathrm{B}^{2},\gamma\in\relint\left(\Delta^{2}\right)\right\}=\inf\left\{\sum_{k\in[2]}\frac{1}{4}\,\gamma_{k}^{\alpha-2}:\gamma\in\relint\left(\Delta^{2}\right)\right\}\;.

Consider the function
\gamma\mapsto f(\gamma)=\frac{1}{4}(\gamma_{1}^{\alpha-2}+\gamma_{2}^{\alpha-2})

which is continuous on
\Delta^{2}
. By density it follows that
C_{\alpha,2}=\min\left\{f(\gamma):\gamma\in\Delta^{2}\right\}
.
If
\alpha\in(2,3)
, then the function
f
is concave on
\Delta^{2}
, and so the minimum is attained on some extreme point of
\Delta^{2}
, leading to

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,2}=f\bigl((1,0)\bigr)=f\bigl((0,1)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(1^{\alpha-2}+0^{\alpha-2})=2^{-2}\;.

If
\alpha=3
, then
f(\gamma)=\frac{1}{4}
for every
\gamma\in\Delta^{2}
, thus
C_{\alpha,2}=\frac{1}{4}=2^{-2}
.
If
\alpha\in(3,+\infty)
, then the function
f
is convex on
\Delta^{2}
, and its symmetry about
\gamma=(1/2,1/2)
entails that

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,2}

\displaystyle=f\bigl((1/2,1/2)\bigr)=\frac{1}{4}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\alpha-2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\alpha-2}\right)=2^{1-\alpha}\;.

Case
\alpha>2
,
K\geq 3
:

By hypothesis on
K
, there exists some
\bar{v}\in\mathrm{B}^{K}
such that
\bar{v}_{1}=0
, and that we can use to define the function
\gamma\mapsto g(\gamma)\coloneq\sum_{k\in[K]}\bar{v}_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}^{\alpha-2}
.
In particular,

C_{\alpha,K}\leq g(\gamma)

for every
\gamma\in\relint{\bigl(\Delta^{K}\bigr)}
and, by continuity of

over
\Delta^{K}

\gamma\in\Delta^{K}
.
We readily see that
g(\bar{\gamma})=0
if
\bar{\gamma}\in\Delta^{K}

is the vertex of the simplex such that

\bar{\gamma}_{k}=1
if and only if

, and
this means that
C_{\alpha,K}=0
.
∎

We established a

Pinsker-type inequality for the Bregman divergences induced by negative Tsallis entropies, with an explicit optimal constant

for every
(\alpha,K)
and a geometric explanation of the phase transitions (including the sharp multiclass breakdown for
\alpha>2
and the precise dimension/parity effects for
\alpha\in(1,2]

Beyond its information-theoretic interest, this inequality has immediate learning-theoretic consequences.
First, it gives a tight way to convert control on the excess risk of Tsallis (power) losses into
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
(total-variation) control uniformly on predictive distributions over the simplex.
Second, via the standard plug-in argument, it yields a principled route from Tsallis surrogate performance to
multiclass
0

1
classification
regret bounds, clarifying exactly when the conversion is dimension-free and when it can degrade with

Third, in
online learning

\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
-strong convexity (curvature) of Tsallis regularizers, sharpening the constants that enter Follow-the-Regularized-Leader/Mirror-Descent analyses and making explicit how the choice of

controls the geometry behind the algorithm.

Appendix A
Pinsker Inequality: Total Variation vs.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}

Lemma 24

(Total variation vs.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
)
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
with
K\geq 2
and let
p,q\in\Delta^{K}
be probability distributions on
[K]
.
With

\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}\coloneqq\sup_{C\subset[K]}\left\lvert\sum_{k\in C}p(k)-\sum_{k\in C}q(k)\right\rvert,\qquad\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\lvert p(k)-q(k)\right\rvert\;,

it holds that

\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}\;.

Proof.

Let
d(k)\coloneqq p(k)-q(k)
for
k\in[K]
. Since
p,q\in\Delta^{K}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{K}d(k)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(k)-\sum_{k=1}^{K}q(k)=1-1=0\;.

Define the index sets

P\coloneqq\bigl\{k\in[K]:d(k)\geq 0\bigr\}\;,\qquad N\coloneqq\bigl\{k\in[K]:d(k)<0\bigr\}\;.

For any
C\subset[K]
we have

\sum_{k\in C}d(k)\leq\sum_{k\in C\cap P}d(k)\leq\sum_{k\in P}d(k)\;,

so
\sup_{C\subset[K]}\sum_{k\in C}d(k)\leq\sum_{k\in P}d(k)
, and equality is attained by
choosing
C=P
. Hence

\sup_{C\subset[K]}\sum_{k\in C}d(k)=\sum_{k\in P}d(k)\;.

Similarly, applying the same argument to
-d
yields

\sup_{C\subset[K]}\sum_{k\in C}\bigl(-d(k)\bigr)=\sum_{k\in N}\bigl(-d(k)\bigr)\;.

But since
\sum_{k=1}^{K}d(k)=0
, we have

\sum_{k\in P}d(k)=-\sum_{k\in N}d(k)=\sum_{k\in N}\bigl(-d(k)\bigr)\;.

Therefore,

\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}=\sup_{C\subset[K]}\left\lvert\sum_{k\in C}d(k)\right\rvert=\max\left(\sup_{C\subset[K]}\sum_{k\in C}d(k),\sup_{C\subset[K]}\sum_{k\in C}\left(-d(k)\right)\right)=\sum_{k\in P}d(k)\;.

On the other hand,

\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\lvert d(k)\right\rvert=\sum_{k\in P}d(k)+\sum_{k\in N}\bigl(-d(k)\bigr)=2\cdot\sum_{k\in P}d(k)\;.

Combining the last two displays gives
\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}
.
∎

Tsallis Losses and Their Bayes and Excess Risk

In this section we prove that for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
the Bayes and excess risk associated to Tsallis (or power) losses
\ell_{\alpha}
in (
1
) (with
\ell_{0}(q,k)\coloneqq q_{k}^{-1}-K+\sum_{i\in[K]}\ln q_{i}
, and
\ell_{1}(q,k)\coloneqq-\ln q_{k}
) are respectively the Tsallis entropy
S_{\alpha}
(Definition
1
) and their Bregman divergences
D_{\alpha}
(Definition
3
).
Specifically, if
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\left\{0,1\right\}
, for any
p,q\in\relint\left(\Delta^{K}\right)
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{\alpha}(q,Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\ell_{\alpha}(q,k)\cdot p_{k}=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{q^{\alpha-1}_{k}}{1-\alpha}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i\in[K]}q_{i}^{\alpha}\right)\cdot p_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{\alpha\cdot p_{k}\cdot q^{\alpha-1}_{k}+(1-\alpha)q_{k}^{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}

which, for the Bayes risk case
p=q
, becomes

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{\alpha}(p,Y)\right]=\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{\alpha\cdot p_{k}\cdot p^{\alpha-1}_{k}+(1-\alpha)p_{k}^{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}=\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{p_{k}^{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}=S_{\alpha}(p)\;,

while the excess risk becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{\alpha}(q,Y)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{\alpha}(p,Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{\alpha\cdot p_{k}\cdot q^{\alpha-1}_{k}+(1-\alpha)q_{k}^{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}-\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{p_{k}^{\alpha}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{p_{k}^{\alpha}+(\alpha-1)q_{k}^{\alpha}-\alpha p_{k}q_{k}^{\alpha-1}}{\alpha-1}=d_{\alpha}(p\|q)=D_{\alpha}(p\|q)\;,

where
d_{\alpha}
is the
\beta
-divergence for
\beta=\alpha
as defined in Definition
6
, and the last inequality follows from Remark
7
.

For the case
\alpha=0
, if
p,q\in\relint\left(\Delta^{K}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{0}(q,Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\ell_{0}(q,k)\cdot p_{k}=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(q_{k}^{-1}-K+\sum_{i\in[K]}\ln q_{i}\right)\cdot p_{k}=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}-1+\ln q_{k}\right)

which, for the Bayes risk case
p=q
, becomes

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{0}(p,Y)\right]=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p_{k}}-1+\ln p_{k}\right)=\sum_{k\in[K]}\ln p_{k}=S_{0}(p)

while the excess risk becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{0}(q,Y)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{0}(p,Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}-1+\ln q_{k}\right)-\sum_{k\in[K]}\ln p_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}-\ln\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}-1\right)=d_{0}(p\|q)=D_{0}(p\|q)\;,

where
d_{0}
is the
\beta
-divergence for
\beta=0
(or Itakura-Saito divergence) as defined in Definition
6
, and the last inequality follows from Remark
7
.

For the case
\alpha=1
, if
p,q\in\relint\left(\Delta^{K}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{1}(q,Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\ell_{1}(q,k)\cdot p_{k}=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(-\ln q_{k}\right)\cdot p_{k}=-\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}\cdot\ln q_{k}\;,

which, for the Bayes risk case
p=q
, becomes

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{1}(p,Y)\right]=-\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}\cdot\ln p_{k}=S_{1}(p)

while the excess risk becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{1}(q,Y)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim p}\left[\ell_{1}(p,Y)\right]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}\cdot\ln q_{k}+\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}\cdot\ln p_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}p_{k}\cdot\ln\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=D_{1}(p\|q)\;,

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, and the last inequality follows from Remark
5
.

.
Then

\sigma_{\alpha,K}\coloneq\left(\frac{(1-\frac{1}{K})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{3-\alpha}}+(1+\frac{1}{K})^{\frac{1-\alpha}{3-\alpha}}}{2}\right)^{3-\alpha}\;

satisfies

1+\frac{\alpha-1}{3-\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}\leq\sigma_{\alpha,K}\leq 1+\frac{7(\alpha-1)}{6(3-\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}\;.

(17)

Proof.

Define
\nu\coloneq(1-\alpha)/(3-\alpha)
and note that
\nu\in(-1,0)
.
Next, define

z(\alpha,K)\coloneq\frac{(1-\frac{1}{K})^{\nu}+(1+\frac{1}{K})^{\nu}}{2}-1\;,

which, by considering the binomial series for
(1+x)^{\nu}
and
x=\pm 1/K
, can be expanded as

z(\alpha,K)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\binom{\nu}{2n}\frac{1}{K^{2n}}\;,

(18)

where the coefficients are nonnegative, as shown by the recurrence

\binom{\nu}{2n+2}=\binom{\nu}{2n}\cdot\frac{(\nu-2n)(\nu-2n-1)}{(2n+1)(2n+2)}\;.

(19)

In particular, the function
K\mapsto z(\alpha,K)
is an increasing function of
1/K^{2}
, and

z(\alpha,K)\geq\binom{\nu}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}=\frac{\alpha-1}{(3-\alpha)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}\,.

This inequality, together with Bernoulli’s inequality, yields

1+\frac{\alpha-1}{(3-\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}\leq 1+(3-\alpha)\cdot z(\alpha,K)\leq(1+z(\alpha,K))^{3-\alpha}=\sigma_{\alpha,K}\;,

and this proves the lower bound in (
17
).
For the upper bound, first note that
K\geq 3
and that

\binom{\nu}{2n}
is decreasing in
n
by (
19
), hence

z(\alpha,K)\leq\binom{\nu}{2}\cdot\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{K^{2n}}\leq\binom{\nu}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{3^{2n}}=\frac{9(\alpha-1)}{8(3-\alpha)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}\,.

By Bernoulli’s inequality,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha,K}

\displaystyle=\left(1+z(\alpha,K)\right)\cdot(1+z(\alpha,K))^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq(1+z(\alpha,K))\cdot(1+(2-\alpha)\cdot z(\alpha,K))

\displaystyle=1+(3-\alpha)\cdot z(\alpha,K)\cdot\left(1+\frac{2-\alpha}{3-\alpha}\cdot z(\alpha,K)\right)\;,

hence

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha,K}

\displaystyle\leq 1+(3-\alpha)\cdot z(\alpha,K)\cdot\left(1+\frac{2-\alpha}{3-\alpha}\cdot z(\alpha,3)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{9(\alpha-1)}{8(3-\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}\cdot\left(1+(2-\alpha)\frac{9(\alpha-1)}{8(3-\alpha)^{3}}\frac{1}{3^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{9(\alpha-1)}{8(3-\alpha)}\cdot\left(1+\frac{(2-\alpha)(\alpha-1)}{8(3-\alpha)^{3}}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{9(\alpha-1)}{8(3-\alpha)}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{32}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{297(\alpha-1)}{256(3-\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1}{K^{2}}\;,

using that
1/32
is an upper bound of
(2-\alpha)(\alpha-1)/\bigl(8(3-\alpha)^{3}\bigr)
for
\alpha\in(1,2)
.
Noticing that we have
\frac{297}{256}\leq\frac{7}{6}
we obtain the conclusion.
∎

The inequality given in Remark
11
is based on Proposition
26
and on the observation that
0\leq\frac{\alpha-1}{3-\alpha}\leq 1
for
\alpha\in(1,2]
.

Appendix E
Nonexistence of Pinsker-Type Inequality for
\alpha>2
and
K\geq 3

It is possible to show directly that for
\alpha>2
and
K\geq 3
the divergence
D_{\alpha}
does not admit a Pinsker-type inequality, i.e., that it is not possible to find a
positive
constant
C
such that
D_{\alpha}(p\|q)\geq C\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}
for every
p
,
q\in\relint{\left(\Delta^{K}\right)}
.

Theorem 27
.

Let
K\geq 3
be an integer. For every
\alpha\in(2,+\infty)
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
p,q\in\relint\left(\Delta^{K}\right)
such that

D_{\alpha}(p\|q)<\varepsilon\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\;.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle p_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{cases}1-(K-1)\cdot t&\text{for $k=1$}\\
\frac{3}{4}t&\text{for $k=2$}\\
\frac{5}{4}t&\text{for $k=3$}\\
t&\text{for $3<k\leq K$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle q_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{cases}1-(K-1)\cdot t&\text{for $k=1$}\\
\frac{5}{4}t&\text{for $k=2$}\\
\frac{3}{4}t&\text{for $k=3$}\\
t&\text{for $3<k\leq K$.}\end{cases}

For
0<t<\frac{1}{K-1}
, both
p\coloneq p(t)
and
q\coloneqq q(t)
are in
\relint\left(\Delta^{K}\right)
.
By construction,
S_{\alpha}\bigl(p(t)\bigr)=S_{\alpha}\bigl(q(t)\bigr)
, hence

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla S_{\alpha}(q),p-q\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\cdot q_{k}^{\alpha-1}\left(p_{k}-q_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\left(\frac{5}{4}\cdot t\right)^{\alpha-1}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\cdot t\right)+\left(\frac{3}{4}\cdot t\right)^{\alpha-1}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=t^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{2(\alpha-1)}\cdot\frac{5^{\alpha-1}-{3}^{\alpha-1}}{4^{\alpha-1}}\;,

and note that
\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}=t
, thus

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\alpha}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)}{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle=t^{\alpha-2}\cdot\frac{1}{2(\alpha-1)}\cdot\frac{5^{\alpha-1}-{3}^{\alpha-1}}{4^{\alpha-1}}\;.

By hypothesis on
\alpha
we have
t^{\alpha-2}\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
, thus the thesis follows by considering, for every
\varepsilon>0
, a sufficiently small
t=t(\varepsilon)
.
∎

We underscore that the construction provided in the proof of Theorem
27
requires
K\geq 3
. In fact, the same argument fails for
K=2
.

Appendix F
Pinsker Inequality for Csiszár
f
-Divergences

In this section we recall key definitions and results on Csiszár
f
-divergences (in particular,
\alpha
-Tsallis relative entropies), and we compare the classical generalized Pinsker inequality for
f
-divergences with our generalized Pinsker inequality for the Bregman divergence induced by the negative
\alpha
-Tsallis entropies.

Definition 28
.

We say that
f
is an admissible generator if
f\colon[0,+\infty)\to(-\infty,+\infty]
is a convex function such that
f
is finite for every
x>0
,
f(1)=0
and
f(0)=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}f(x)
.
If
f
is an admissible generator, and
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
are probability measures both defined over some measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and such that
\mathbb{P}\ll\mathbb{Q}
(which means that
\mathbb{P}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{Q}
, i.e., that
\mathbb{Q}[A]=0
implies
\mathbb{P}[A]=0
, for every event
A\in\mathcal{F}
), the Csiszár
f
-divergence of
\mathbb{P}
from
\mathbb{Q}
is defined by

D_{f}^{\textrm{C}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}f\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}\;,

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mathbb{P}
with respect to
\mathbb{Q}
.

For any admissible generator
f
that is three times differentiable at
1
with
f^{\prime\prime}(1)>0
, and for any probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
on some measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that
\mathbb{P}\ll\mathbb{Q}
, it has been proven by
Gilardoni 2010
that a generalized Pinsker inequality holds (and is sharp) in the following form

D_{f}^{\textrm{C}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\geq 2f^{\prime\prime}(1)\cdot\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}\;,

(20)

where
\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}
is the total variation distance between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, formally defined as

\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}\coloneqq\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\bigl\lvert\mathbb{P}[A]-\mathbb{Q}[A]\bigr\rvert\;.

The KL-divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})
between two probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
on some measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
such that
\mathbb{P}\ll\mathbb{Q}
is indeed a Csiszár
f
-divergence for the admissible generator
f\colon[0,+\infty)\to(-\infty,+\infty]
given by
x\mapsto f(x)\coloneq x\ln x
(with the convention that
0\cdot\ln 0=0
), since, by a change of variables, we have

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\ln\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=\int_{\Omega}\ln\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}=\int_{\Omega}f\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}=D_{f}^{\textrm{C}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\;,

and, consequently, for
Equation

20
, we recover the (sharp) Pinsker inequality for probability measures

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})=D_{f}^{\textrm{C}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\geq 2f^{\prime\prime}(1)\cdot\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}=2\cdot\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}\;.

For
K\in\{2,3,\dots\}
, this last inequality implies the classic Pinsker inequality over the
(K-1)
-dimensional probability simplex
\Delta^{K}
.
Specifically, if
p,q\in\Delta^{K}
are such that, for any
k\in[K]
, whenever
q_{k}=0
it also holds that
p_{k}=0
, then
p
and
q
are the probability mass functions of two probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
on the discrete space
\Omega\coloneqq[K]
with
\mathbb{P}\ll\mathbb{Q}
, and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{A\in 2^{K}}\bigl\lvert\mathbb{P}[A]-\mathbb{Q}[A]\bigr\rvert=\sup_{A\in 2^{K}}\left\lvert\sum_{k\in A}p_{k}-\sum_{k\in A}q_{k}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{k\in[K]}|p_{k}-q_{k}|=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}=\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}\;,

(the last equality is proven in
Appendix

A
), which implies (with the convention
0/0\coloneqq 0
and
0\cdot\ln(0)\coloneqq 0
)

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\coloneqq\sum_{k\in[K]}\ln\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)p_{k}=\int_{\Omega}\ln\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})\geq 2\cdot\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}=2\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}\;.

An interesting class of admissible generators are those of the form

f_{\alpha}\colon[0,+\infty)\to(-\infty,+\infty]\;,\qquad x\mapsto\frac{x^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\;,

for
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
, whose corresponding Csiszár
f_{\alpha}
-divergences go under the name of
\alpha
-Tsallis relative entropies (up to perhaps different normalization constants), with
Equation

20
that becomes

D^{\textrm{TRE}}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})\coloneqq D^{\textrm{C}}_{f_{\alpha}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\geq 2f_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}(1)\cdot\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}=2\cdot\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}\;.

Again, if we specialize this last inequality in the discrete case, for any
p,q\in\relint\left(\Delta^{K}\right)
, if
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
are the probability measures of which
p
and
q
are the respective probability mass functions, we find that

\displaystyle D^{\textrm{TRE}}_{\alpha}(p\|q)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k\in[K]}f_{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)q_{k}=\int_{\Omega}f\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}=D^{\textrm{TRE}}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{Q})

(21)

\displaystyle\geq 2\cdot\left\lVert\mathbb{P}-\mathbb{Q}\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}=2\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{\textrm{TV}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\;.

It is interesting to investigate the relation between
\alpha
-Tsallis relative entropies and Bregman divergences of negative
\alpha
-Tsallis entropies, their respective generalized Pinsker inequalities, and whether they can be derived from each other.
Here, we show that for
\alpha\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,1)
, the generalized Pinsker inequality for
\alpha
-Tsallis relative entropies of
Gilardoni 2010
(Equation
21
) implies a
looser
version of our Pinsker inequality for Bregman divergences of negative
\alpha
-Tsallis entropies (
Theorem

8
), while the vice versa is true for
\alpha\in(1,+\infty)
.

Specifically, for any
\alpha\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
and any
p,q\in\relint(\Delta^{K})
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(p\|q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{p_{k}^{\alpha}+(\alpha-1)q_{k}^{\alpha}-\alpha p_{k}q_{k}^{\alpha-1}}{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)^{\alpha}+(\alpha-1)-\alpha\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\cdot q_{k}^{\alpha}\eqqcolon\sum_{k\in[K]}g_{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)\cdot q_{k}^{\alpha}\eqqcolon(\square)\;,

where

g_{\alpha}\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}\;,\qquad x\mapsto\frac{x^{\alpha}+\alpha-1-\alpha x}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\;.

Now, notice that
g_{\alpha}
is a non-negative function (its minimum is at
1
and its value is
0
) and hence

\displaystyle(\square)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\in[K]}g_{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)\cdot q_{k}\;,\qquad\textit{if $\alpha\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,1)$}\;,

\displaystyle(\square)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in[K]}g_{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)\cdot q_{k}\;,\qquad\textit{if $\alpha\in(1,\infty)$}\;.

Now, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in[K]}g_{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)\cdot q_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)^{\alpha}+(\alpha-1)-\alpha\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\cdot q_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\frac{\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\cdot q_{k}=\sum_{k\in[K]}f_{\alpha}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)\cdot q_{k}=D^{\textrm{TRE}}_{\alpha}(p\|q)\;,

and hence

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(p\|q)

\displaystyle\geq D^{\textrm{TRE}}_{\alpha}(p\|q)\;,\qquad\textit{if $\alpha\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,1)$\;,}

(22)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(p\|q)

\displaystyle\leq D^{\textrm{TRE}}_{\alpha}(p\|q)\;,\qquad\textit{if $\alpha\in(1,\infty)$\;.}

(23)

In particular, for
\alpha\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,1)
, by combining
Equation

22
and the generalized Pinsker inequality for
\alpha
-Tsallis relative entropies
Equation

21
, we get the following
looser
form (by a
2^{1-\alpha}
factor) of
Theorem

8
for
\alpha\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,1)
and
p,q\in\relint(\Delta^{K})

D_{\alpha}(p\|q)\geq D^{\textrm{TRE}}_{\alpha}(p\|q)\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\;,\qquad\textrm{if $\alpha\in(0,1)$}\;.

Since for
\alpha>1
and
p,q\in\relint(\Delta^{K})
the opposite inequality (Equation
23
) holds, from
Theorem

8
we obtain the following
looser
version of the generalized Pinsker inequality for
\alpha
-Tsallis relative entropies

D^{\textrm{TRE}}_{\alpha}(p\|q)\geq D_{\alpha}(p\|q)\geq\frac{C_{\alpha,K}}{2}\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\;,\qquad\textrm{if $\alpha\in(1,+\infty)$}\;.

The case
\alpha=0
is similar to the case
\alpha\in(-\infty,0)\cup(0,1)
, where the role of the
\alpha
-Tsallis relative entropy is played by the reverse KL,
(p,q)\mapsto D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)
.
Specifically, for any
p,q\in\relint(\Delta^{K})
, we can obtain a
looser
form (by a factor of
2
) of
Theorem

8
as follows

\displaystyle D_{0}(p\|q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\underbrace{\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}-\ln\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}-1\right)}_{\geq 0}\geq\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}-\ln\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}-1\right)\cdot q_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left(-q_{k}\cdot\ln\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\right)=\sum_{k\in[K]}q_{k}\cdot\ln\frac{q_{k}}{p_{k}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\;,

where the last inequality is the classic Pinsker inequality.

Appendix G
Weaker Inequalities for
\alpha>2
and
K\geq 3

As we have seen in Theorem
8
and in Appendix
E
, for
\alpha>2
and
K\geq 3
no positive constant
C
satisfies

D_{\alpha}(p\|q)\geq C\cdot\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}

for every

p
,
q\in\relint{\left(\Delta^{K}\right)}
.
However, we have seen in (
9
) that

2\cdot\frac{D_{\alpha}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)}{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}=\sum_{k\in[K]}v_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}^{\alpha-2}\;,

(24)

where
\gamma
is a convex combination of
p
and
q
and
v\coloneq\frac{p-q}{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}}
is an element of
\mathrm{B}^{K}
.
In some applications (notably sequential prediction under logarithmic loss and related proper scoring rules),
it is standard to restrict predictions to a proper subset of the relative interior of the simplex or to apply truncation/smoothing so that
the loss and KL-type divergences remain finite and well-behaved

[
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2006
,
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2001
,
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
,
van Ommen et al. 2016
]
.
For instance, we may require that some positive
\varepsilon
satisfying
0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{K}
is such that
p_{k}\geq\varepsilon
and
q_{k}\geq\varepsilon
for every
k
, a condition which bind both
p
,
q
to stay sufficiently away from the relative boundary of
\Delta^{K}
.
This condition entails that also
\gamma_{k}\geq\varepsilon
for every
k
, hence

\sum_{k\in[K]}v_{k}^{2}\gamma_{k}^{\alpha-2}\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha-2}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
Using that
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq C_{2,K}
we derive

D_{\alpha}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha-2}}{2}\cdot C_{2,K}\cdot{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}\;.

(25)

We may also derive a similar inequality in a less stringent setting, where only one among
p
and
q
is required to have its coordinates bounded below by
\varepsilon
. Indeed, the proof of Proposition
21
shows that (
24
) is a consequence of the first-order Taylor expansion of
S_{\alpha}
with Lagrange remainder. If instead we consider the integral remainder, which underlies the approach used, e.g., in
Reid and Williamson 2009b
,
Reid and Williamson 2011
to estimate Bregman divergences, what we get is

2\cdot\frac{D_{\alpha}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)}{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}=\sum_{k\in[K]}v_{k}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}2(1-x)\bigl((1-x)q_{k}+xp_{k}\bigr)^{\alpha-2}\,\mathrm{d}x\;,

If the constraint is valid only for
p
, then for every
k
we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}2(1-x)\bigl((1-x)q_{k}+xp_{k}\bigr)^{\alpha-2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}2(1-x)\bigl(x\varepsilon\bigr)^{\alpha-2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{\alpha-2}\int_{0}^{1}2(1-x)x^{\alpha-2}\,\mathrm{d}x={\varepsilon}^{\alpha-2}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\;.

This results in the inequality

D_{\alpha}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha-2}}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\cdot C_{2,K}\cdot{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}\;.

(26)

Similarly, if the constraint is valid only for
q
, then this time we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}2(1-x)\bigl((1-x)q_{k}+xp_{k}\bigr)^{\alpha-2}\,\mathrm{d}x\;

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}2(1-x)\bigl((1-x)\varepsilon\bigr)^{\alpha-2}\,\mathrm{d}x\;

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{\alpha-2}\int_{0}^{1}2\bigl(1-x\bigr)^{\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}x\;=\varepsilon^{\alpha-2}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha}\;,

hence

D_{\alpha}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha-2}}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha}\cdot C_{2,K}\cdot{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}\;.

(27)

We can rephrase what we have seen so far as follows:

Proposition 29
.

For
\alpha>2
and
K\geq 3
, consider the inequality

D_{\alpha}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)\geq\frac{C}{2}\cdot{\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}\;.

(28)

Then:

1.

For every
p
,
q\in\relint{\left(\Delta^{K}\right)}
, the inequality (
28
) is satisfied by

C=C(\alpha,K,p,q)=C_{2,K}\cdot\left(\min_{k\in[K]}\left\{p_{k},q_{k}\right\}\right)^{\alpha-2}\;;

2.

For every
p
,
q\in\relint{\left(\Delta^{K}\right)}
, the inequality (
28
) is satisfied by

C=C(\alpha,K,p)=C_{2,K}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\cdot\left(\min_{k\in[K]}\left\{p_{k}\right\}\right)^{\alpha-2}\;;

3.

For every
p
,
q\in\relint{\left(\Delta^{K}\right)}
, the inequality (
28
) is satisfied by

C=C(\alpha,K,q)=C_{2,K}\cdot\frac{2}{\alpha}\cdot\left(\min_{k\in[K]}\left\{q_{k}\right\}\right)^{\alpha-2}\;.
