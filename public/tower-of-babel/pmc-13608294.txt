Community-Based Prevention: Findings From Nearly 3 Decades of the Drug-Free Communities Support Program
Authors: Barbara K. O'Donnel, Brian G. Moss, Kelly A. Cooley, Vanessa Morales
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Community-based coalitions are a central strategy for preventing youth substance use, yet evidence describing how coalition-based prevention functions at scale and in contemporary practice contexts remains limited. The Drug-Free Communities (DFC) Support Program represents a long-standing national investment in locally driven, multisector prevention efforts.Objective:To examine recent trends in youth substance use in DFC-funded communities and describe coalition infrastructure and practices that provide context for these trends.Design:Descriptive trend analyses of coalition-reported data and youth substance use indicators. Long-term patterns were examined among coalitions with repeated core measures collections from all DFC funded since inception with the earliest data collected in 2002, and recent national trends were summarized using data collected from DFC coalitions implementing grants between 2021 and 2025.Setting:Community-based prevention coalitions operating across rural, suburban, urban, and tribal contexts in the United States.Participants:Drug-Free Communities–funded coalitions submitting annual reporting data and core measures data during the study period, including 1014 DFC-funded coalitions that received funding at some point between 2021 and 2025. Coalitions collect core measures data from youth in their service area using surveys reviewed by the National Evaluation team to ensure data harmonization.Intervention:Coalition-based environmental prevention model emphasizing multisector collaboration, evidence-informed strategies, and youth engagement tailored to local community conditions.Main Outcome MeasuresPast 30-day use of alcohol, marijuana, tobacco, and prescription drugs (misuse), reported by grade level (6 thru 12) through locally administered surveys conducted at least every 2 years.Results:Across all DFC implementing from inception through 2025 with at least 2 core measures collection time points (first and most recent), significant declines over time in past 30-day use of substances (alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, prescription drug misuse) across middle and high school grade levels were observed within DFC-supported communities. Focusing on data from DFC cohorts with repeated observations from 2021 to 2025, DFC funding was again generally associated with declines in past 30-day substance use, with larger reductions among high school youth and for substances with higher baseline prevalence. Among middle school youth, declines over time for prescription drug misuse was small and was nonsignificant in 2021; the same was true for middle school tobacco use in 2025. During the same period, coalitions demonstrated stable infrastructure, consistent multisector engagement, widespread school partnerships, and sustained youth involvement.Conclusions:Overall and recent declines in youth substance use occurred within the context of the DFC coalition model characterized by multisector engagement, sustained youth involvement, and implementation of comprehensive, evidence-based and evidence-informed strategies. While correlational in nature, these findings highlight the importance of maintaining long-term community-based prevention capacity focused on youth substance use prevention. Although factors outside of DFC may also contribute to youth substance use reductions, it is possible that DFC efforts may have contributed to national youth substance use declines.

Introduction

Youth substance use remains a persistent public health concern in the United States, with implications for health, development, and community well-being during adolescence and beyond.1 Alcohol remains the most commonly reported substance used by adolescents across a range of national surveys, even with declines over time.2–4 For example, national Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) data on high school youth past 30-day use of alcohol declined from 35% in 2013 to 22% in 2023. Adolescence is a developmental period during which connectedness to family, peers, schools, and communities plays a critical protective role in shaping behavioral health outcomes, including substance use.5 Community-based prevention approaches are widely used to address public health challenges such as youth substance use by targeting the social, environmental, and normative conditions that shape youth behavior, including supporting youth positive connections.6 Among these approaches, coalition-based models emphasize multisector collaboration, evidence-based strategies, local adaptation, and sustained engagement to influence various community contexts over time.

A range of studies have found evidence that the community coalition model is effective for prevention across a range of public health areas, including prevention of youth substance use.7,8 Although community coalition models are widely used, national evidence remains limited regarding how coalitions operate at scale, how they monitor and address community-level risk and protective factors related to substance use, and how youth substance trends unfold within coalition-supported communities.

A growing body of prevention research identifies social connectedness as a key protective factor against substance use and other co-occurring health risks. Social connectedness encompasses a sense of belonging, support, and care from peers, adults, schools, and communities. A recent meta-analytic review of 90 studies found that higher levels of school connectedness were consistently associated with lower rates of substance use, improved mental health, and reduced involvement in violence and other risky behaviors during adolescence, including substance use.9 This is consistent with broader evidence identifying social connection as foundational to mental and behavioral health.5 At the same time, social connection has gained increasing attention as a determinant for mental and behavioral health outcomes.10 Community coalitions by nature work to grow social connections, both within the coalition and within those they serve. This convergence underscores the importance of examining community-based coalitions as a pathway for influencing shared community conditions, including dimensions of social connectedness associated with substance use risk and protection.

The Drug-Free Communities (DFC) Support Program provides a unique opportunity to examine coalition-based prevention and youth substance use trends through a long-standing federal investment in locally driven efforts.11 DFC coalitions have been funded since 1998 including the most recent cohort of 741 coalitions funded in 2024. By supporting community coalitions that seek to influence local risk and protective conditions associated with youth substance use through implementing a comprehensive mix of prevention strategies, the DFC program offers a national platform for examining how coalition structures and practices align with evidence on social connectedness and other established protective mechanisms. This article examines overall changes in youth substance use in DFC-supported communities and, recent youth substance use trends, while also describing key characteristics of DFC coalitions, including their structure, multisector engagement, prevention practices, and community-level risk and protective factors that help contextualize these patterns.

National context and purpose of the DFC evaluation

Community-based prevention plays an important role in addressing youth substance use across the nation, particularly by shaping the social and environmental contexts in which young people develop.12,13 The environments surrounding young people shape their exposure to factors that may be contributing to youth substance use, including community-level risk and protective factors (or social determinants) present in places where youth live, learn, work, and play.14 Because these conditions vary across youth within and across communities, effective intervention approaches must be responsive to community-specific contexts while emphasizing implementing evidence-based prevention strategies.15 Prevention relies on local community engagement supported by multisector collaboration. Coalitions offer a mechanism for organizing these efforts by building capacity, aligning resources, and creating shared ownership of prevention priorities and actions.8 Community coalitions offer several potential benefits for prevention, including their emphasis on community engagement and involvement, their capacity to respond to local context, and their focus on addressing multiple risk and protective factors through coordinated strategies.16

The DFC Program is the nation's most substantial and sustained federal investments in community-based prevention. Since 1998, the program has supported at least 3734 grants awarded in 2376 communities, with coalitions across all 50 states, and many of the nation's territories and Tribal nations.17 A defining feature of the DFC model is its emphasis on multisector collaboration to identify local conditions associated with youth substance use and to implement strategies tailored to those conditions.11 Through its sustained duration, the DFC program has supported the development of a national prevention infrastructure spanning communities that vary widely in size, context, and need.17 As such, the program offers a rare opportunity to examine how coalition-based prevention operates across settings. Grants of up to $125,000 per year are awarded, with an initial 5-year award followed by the possibility to compete for an additional 5-year award (maximum of 10 years per coalition).

The Office of National Drug Control Policy (ONDCP), Executive Office of the President, provides oversight for the DFC grant. Office of National Drug Control Policy provides support to DFC coalitions through a range of partners. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention National Center for Injury Prevention and Control provides grant management services and project officer support and monitoring. Office of National Drug Control Policy funds a grant to support the National Coalition Institute (NCI) to provide training and technical assistance that support DFC coalitions in developing as a coalition. Through 2024, ONDCP awarded the NCI to CADCA, a national nonprofit whose mission is to build healthier, safer, and strong communities. The NCI consisted of several weeks of training spread out over a 3- to 6-month window, with year 1 participation a grant requirement. Finally, ONDCP awards a contract to the DFC National Cross-Site Evaluation Team to conduct the national evaluation and provide evaluation related technical assistance to coalitions. In addition to supporting program accountability, the national evaluation is designed to describe patterns in youth substance use and coalition structure and processes that can inform practice and learning across communities. The evaluation analyzes trends and patterns in current (past 30-day) youth substance use reported by DFC communities and systematically documents how coalitions function in varied contexts, including how they adapt to emerging substance use issues such as vaping and opioids, and to broader shifts in social and community norms. By synthesizing information across coalitions, the evaluation offers insights into contemporary coalition practice and provides a foundation for understanding recent youth substance use trends within DFC-supported communities.

Conceptual framework for community-level prevention

The DFC National Cross-Site Evaluation is grounded in an ecological logic model (Figure 1) that considers the complex, multiple factors that may contribute to youth decision making regarding substance use and how coalitions may work across levels to bring about community-level change, an effective model in prevention.18–20 The model is intentionally conceptual, emphasizing the comprehensive mechanisms through which coalition capacity building and prevention efforts influence youth behavior rather than outlining specific strategies that bring about change. At its core, the model suggests that youth development and behaviors are shaped by multiple, nested layers of influence, ranging from individual characteristics to relationships with family and peers and extending outward to community environments and broader societal norms and systems. These layers do not operate in isolation; instead, they interact dynamically in ways that can either enhance or reduce factors contributing to substance use over time.

Drug-Free Communities coalitions play a central role in turning this framework into action. Coalitions function as organizing structures, bringing together local stakeholders, coordinating cross-sector efforts, and building the capacity needed to influence local community conditions. Capacity in this context includes the ability to mobilize partners from a range of community sectors, use data to inform coalition decision making, develop and implement action plans, sustain engagement, and adapt to evolving community contexts. Through this process, coalitions are uniquely positioned to influence the shared community conditions that shape youth behavior, including norms, support, and access to substances and other risk and protective factors associated with substance use.

The logic model also helps explain how these mechanisms may contribute to change, in this case reduced or prevented youth substance use. When coalitions build capacity, they are better able to align efforts in ways that influence community conditions. Coalitions are designed to move sector work from being siloed to being collaborative and synergistic, working together on prevention in ways that would not have been possible or as powerful if done alone. Working together strengthens dimensions of social connectedness, including supportive youth-adult relationships, shared norms, and a sense of belonging within schools and communities, protective factors associated with lower substance use risk.9 The pathways in the logic model are shaped by context, including local, state, and even national contexts.

This model guides the national evaluation by showing not only whether change occurs, but how and why it occurs. By examining coalition capacity and contextual factors alongside youth substance use outcomes, the evaluation is positioned to generate insights into community-level prevention. The logic model also emphasizes a continual process of learning and adjustment. Coalitions assess local needs, examine their capacity, implement strategies, monitor change, and adjust structure and activities as needed. The conceptual framework anchors the evaluation in an understanding of how coalition structure, strategy implementation, and context interact to influence youth behavior over time. When coalitions successfully implement work at the local level, positive outcomes are more likely to be identified within the national evaluation.

Scope of the national evaluation

The national evaluation's scope and design are structured to accommodate the large scale of the coalitions awarded DFC grants each year, including 741 grants recipients with active funding in 2025. Evaluating coalitions operating in varied contexts requires data systems capable of capturing national-level patterns and community-specific insights. This approach allows the evaluation to characterize trends, monitor coalition progress, and generate meaningful feedback for both program oversight and field learning. Within this framework, this study centers on descriptive analyses of coalition activity and youth outcomes during the 2021–2025 evaluation period.

Over the program's history, the need to support a large-scale national evaluation accounting for natural variation in local contexts and grant implementation has driven the development of a strong data reporting infrastructure that balances consistency with flexibility. A central feature of this infrastructure is a set of standardized reporting tools used by all DFC coalitions. The required annual progress report provides essential information about coalition development, capacity building, and strategy implementation, supporting a descriptive understanding of how coalitions are structured and operated during each evaluation period.

Although progress reporting is standardized, it is also updated over time. One example of this flexibility is understanding coalition focus on risk and protective factors. In 2023, the national evaluation expanded its measurement framework to include a more comprehensive set of community-level risk and protective factor indicators reported by coalitions in their progress reports allowing for more systematic examination of contextual factors aligned with the program's logic model.21 Although the evaluation has consistently collected information on risk and protective factors, these newer indicators provide greater depth and breadth in capturing community conditions such as youth engagement in prosocial activities, perceptions of school and peer support, and community norms related to substance use. Although the current indicators were introduced in 2023 and, therefore, represent an emerging component of the evaluation infrastructure, they provide an opportunity to more directly examine how coalition activities align with established protective mechanisms linked to lower substance use risk.

Core measures play a particularly critical role in the national evaluation because they offer the most reliable insight into national trends in youth substance use within DFC communities. Although communities differ in sampling approaches, survey administration, and other contextual factors, the shared measurement structure ensures that shifts in outcomes can be interpreted on a national scale. The evaluation emphasizes descriptive patterns rather than causal conclusions, providing a meaningful characterization of collective progress across DFC supported communities. Together, these data systems provide the foundation for the analyses presented in subsequent sections, offering an integrated picture of youth substance use trends and coalition functioning across the DFC Support Program during the 2021–2025 evaluation period.

Methods

Methods overview

This study uses a within-coalition trend analyses to examine how youth substance use within DFC communities changed over time. Specifically, we conducted within-coalition repeated measures (paired t test) analyses of changes in past 30-day use measures across all DFC coalitions who have provided at least 2 data points (first report and most recent report). Means were weighted based on number of youth sampled by the coalition.17 Weighting was applied at the coalition level using reported sample sizes to ensure proportional representation in estimates.

For these long-term comparisons, the timing of data collection was not standardized across coalitions. Therefore, analyses focused on change between first and most recent observations rather than change between specific calendar years. To further examine recent trends in past 30-day use, separate descriptive and trend analyses were conducted using data from coalitions active in each year from 2021 to 2025. These analyses are presented here to understand national patterns in the direction and magnitude of change over time rather than year-to-year variation within individual coalitions.

Because the set of reporting coalitions varies slightly across years, observed trends reflect a combination of within-coalition change and differences in coalition participation over time. These findings are interpreted as descriptive indicators of national patterns in coalition-based prevention contexts that can inform community-based prevention practice, implementation, and policy rather than as estimates of causal program effects. This focus aligns with the goals of the JPHMP supplement to highlight contemporary, practice-relevant coalition approaches that can inform ongoing prevention efforts while also demonstrating long-term descriptive patterns observed over the nearly 30-year history of the DFC program.

Given the heterogeneity of coalition contexts, local implementation strategies, and data collection practices, the analytic approach prioritizes transparency and interpretability over causal attribution. Accordingly, descriptive and trend-focused analyses are used to identify consistent patterns across communities and over time. These analyses provide insights relevant to public health practitioners, coalition leaders, and funders interested in sustaining effective community-based prevention infrastructure. Community-level risk and protective factor indicators were summarized at the coalition level to characterize the perceived prevalence of protective and risk conditions across reporting communities. Because these measures represent coalition-reported assessments of local conditions and are available only for recent reporting years (2023–2025), analyses focused on cross-sectional descriptive patterns rather than longitudinal trend comparisons.

Program context

The DFC Support Program funds local community coalitions to prevent and reduce youth substance use through coordinated, multisector approaches emphasizing environmental prevention strategies. Coalitions serve as the organizing unit for prevention activities, bringing together representatives from multiple community sectors to assess local conditions, identify community-level risk and protective factors, plan and implement evidence-based strategies, and implement interventions tailored to community needs. Although coalitions operate within a shared program framework, implementation is intentionally locally driven. This natural variability in implementation is a defining feature of coalition-based prevention and is treated as an expected and informative aspect of the national evaluation rather than a limitation.

Data sources

Drug-Free Communities progress reports and core measure data submission serve as the data sources. Coalition characteristics, structure, and implementation practices were drawn from progress reports submitted by all DFC-funded coalitions as part of routine federal reporting requirements. Since 2023, progress reports are submitted annually in August; before that, they were submitted twice each year. For consistency, we focused on presenting August data from each year from 2021 to 2025 as available. Given the design of the DFC grant program, these data represent coalitions at various stages in their grants, and some consistency over time is associated with coalitions providing data in multiple years during the 5-year window (see Table 1). Among the 1014 coalitions funded during the 5-year time frame, 41% were in the first 5 years of funding, whereas the remaining were in the second 5 years of funding. Among analytic records included in the first-to-most-recent outcome analyses, the average interval between first and most recent outcome report was 5.4 years overall and differed substantially by funding stage, averaging 3.2 years for coalitions in years 1–5 of funding and 6.2 years for coalitions in years 6–10. In any given year from 2021 to 2025, approximately 19–20 percent of the US population lived in ZIP code reached by a DFC coalition.

Measures

Youth substance use outcomes

Coalitions collect youth survey data locally using instruments that meet program reporting standards. To support data harmonization, coalitions submit surveys used to collect core measures, which are reviewed for alignment with core measure wording, and only approved measures can be reported. Core measures include past 30-day use, perception of risk, perception of peer disapproval, and perception of parent disapproval. Each of these are collected for 4 substances, alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, and prescription drug (misuse). Coalitions are required to collect these data at least every 2 years from at least 3 grade levels; however, individual youth are not tracked longitudinally across survey administrations. These indicators are defined and reported in a consistent manner across communities. This shared measurement structure supports aggregation and comparison across communities, enabling the evaluation to examine national-level patterns, while acknowledging the underlying contextual differences. Coalitions are encouraged to survey a representative sample of youth and to report any challenges in meeting this expectation.

The analyses presented here focus on youth substance use outcomes derived from the DFC past 30-day use core measures, a standardized set of indicators aligned with the program goal of preventing and reducing youth substance use. Past 30-day use is defined as having 1–2 drinks of an alcoholic beverage, smoking part or all of a cigarette (tobacco), used marijuana or hashish (eg, smoked, vaped, edibles), and using prescription drugs not prescribed to you or not as prescribed (misuse). Alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana data have been collected from coalitions since 2002, whereas prescription drug misuse collection was added in 2012.

Coalitions report the year in which the data were collected, which can precede the year of submission by 1–3 years (this lag may occur because of delays in processing and returning survey data from the administering entity to the coalition). In addition, year 1 coalitions are encouraged to submit any available baseline data collected up to 3 years before the start of funding. For each substance, coalitions report the number of youth by grade (6th through 12th grade) who answered the survey question and the percent who reported use. Data from grades 6 through 8 are considered middle school, with the remaining grades considered high school.

Coalition characteristics and practices

Coalition characteristics and practices are measured using descriptive indicators drawn from coalition progress reporting. The progress report data presented here focus on coalition context (ie, geographic community type, school setting served), multisector engagement, youth engagement, risk and protective factors, and prevention implementation activities aggregated by strategy type. The measures are presented descriptively to characterize the scale, stability, and context of the national DFC coalitions over time. Geographic setting coalitions may select from include frontier, rural, suburban, urban, and inner city, with coalitions selecting all that apply based on their ZIP code(s) served. These indicators reflect the presence and scope of coalition practices rather than the quality or effectiveness of specific strategies and are used to contextualize coalition processes and support interpretation of youth substance use trends. As such, these measures are intended to describe coalition functioning rather than to assess program impact.

Community-level risk and protective factor indicators

Beginning in 2023, DFC coalitions reported on an expanded and standardized set of community-level risk and protective factor indicators aligned with the DFC logic model. These indicators build upon the broader risk and protective factor framework described previously and provided more detailed information on coalition perceptions of local community conditions. Coalitions reported extent to which each factor is present in their community and whether the coalition engaged in activities intended to address the factor or strengthen the corresponding protective condition.

These indicators are organized as complementary protective and risk statements reflecting coalition assessments of local conditions across ecological domains, including community, school, peers, family, faith, and individual factors that may contribute to youth decision making regarding substance use. The measures represent coalition-reported contextual conditions rather than individual-level survey responses, with coalitions encouraged to engage coalition members in completing the instrument. Consistent with prevention science, many of these constructs reflect dimensions of social connectedness, including relationships with adults, attachment to school, and involvement in faith- and community-based organizations. For the purposes of this article, analyses focus on coalition engagement in addressing risk factors and strengthening protective factors within their communities.

Results

The results are organized to distinguish between changes observed across the full history of DFC coalition reporting and more recent trends from 2021 to 2025. We first present national within-coalition changes in youth substance use based on comparisons of first and most recent core measures reports from all DFC coalitions funded since inception of the program with at least 2 data points (Table 2). Because the interval between first and most recent report varies across conditions, these analyses summarize change over time rather than a uniform observation period. We then examine recent trends using data from coalitions active between 2021 and 2025 to provide a more recent and policy-relevant snapshot of youth substance use patterns within DFC-supported communities. Finally, we describe coalition infrastructure and community context to support interpretation of observed outcome patterns.

Within-coalition changes in youth substance use

Table 2 presents changes in past 30-day substance use based on first and most recent reports among DFC coalitions with at least 2 data points for a given substance. The interval between first and most recent reports varied across coalitions and tended to be longer among coalitions in years 6–10 of funding than among those in years 1–5. Supplemental analyses indicated that, after accounting for differences in follow-up duration, coalitions in later funding stages did not consistently demonstrate larger reductions in youth substance use than coalitions in earlier funding stages (results not shown). Although at both first and most recent reports most youth did not report using a given substance, prevalence was consistently higher among high school youth compared with middle school youth. Alcohol was the most commonly used substance at both school levels. Across all substances and for both school levels, within coalition comparisons indicated statistically significant decreases in past 30-day use over time (pair t-tests, P < .001). Effect sizes were generally moderate and larger among high school youth than middle school youth, ranging from 0.47 (marijuana) to 0.74 (alcohol). Among middle school youth, effect sizes ranged from 0.26 (prescription drugs) to 0.54 (alcohol). Although several effect sizes fall within the conventional medium-to-large range, even smaller effects may represent meaningful public health changes when observed across large populations of youth. These patterns reflect consistent decreases in past 30-day substance use across DFC-supported communities, although the timing and duration of coalition reporting varied.

Cross-sectional trends shown in Figure 2 indicate that the magnitude and consistency of declines varied by substance and school level. For example, among middle school youth, past 30-day misuse of prescription drugs remained consistently low with minimal change across years, and past 30-day tobacco use showed little to no change in 2025. The following discusses trends within grade level in more detail.

Middle school youth

Among middle school youth, prevalence of past 30-day substance use was consistently low across all substances throughout the study period (see Figure 2). For example, alcohol was highest in 2021 at approximately 7%–8% and declined modestly through 2023, followed by smaller decreases through 2025. Marijuana use followed a similar trajectory, with gradual declines over time. In contrast, tobacco use remained at lower levels (less than 3%) and showed relatively little change across years, more closely resembling patterns observed for prescription drug misuse. Prescription drug misuse remained consistently low, generally below 2% across all reporting years. These patterns are consistent with the lower effect sizes observed among middle school youth and likely reflect the relatively low baseline prevalence of substance use at this grade level.

High school youth

Among high school youth, prevalence of past 30-day use was higher than among middle school youth across all substances, although most high school youth do not report substance use (see Figure 2). Alcohol and marijuana use were most common and exhibited the greatest reductions from 2021 to 2025, falling from about 20% to 14% and 15%–9%, respectively. Tobacco use and misuse of prescription drugs also showed consistent reductions over time, although the magnitude of change was smaller relative to alcohol and marijuana. Across substances, patterns of change were characterized by consistency in direction rather than volatility across reporting years.

Change over time analyses

Within DFC coalition change was assessed annually using paired t-tests comparing weighted mean prevalence at first and most recent report. Figure 3 presents the mean change estimates based on within-coalition comparisons during the 2021–2025 period. These estimates represent the difference between first and most recent reported prevalence within coalitions and quantify the magnitude of change over time. Across both school levels, within-coalition prevalence of use declined significantly from first to most recent report for all 4 substances (P < .001). Two exceptions to this occurred for middle school youth. In 2021, there was no change for middle school youth in past 30-day misuse of prescription drugs, with very few youth engaging in this use at each time point. In 2025, past 30-day use of tobacco among middle school youth was similarly low and unchanged within DFC coalitions.

The magnitude of change varied by both substance and school level. Among middle school youth, mean changes within any given year were modest across substances. Alcohol declined by approximately 1.8 percentage points on average, whereas marijuana and tobacco use declined by less than 1 percentage point. Prescription drug misuse showed the smallest change (approximately −0.5 percentage points), reflecting both low baseline prevalence and limited variability over time.

The mean reductions in prevalence of use for high school youth were larger and more consistent across substances. Alcohol use declined by approximately 7–8 percentage points on average, representing the largest observed change. Marijuana use declined by approximately 4.0 percentage points, whereas tobacco use declined by about 3–4 percentage points. Prescription drug misuse declined by approximately 2 percentage points, indicating smaller but still consistent reductions.

The larger magnitude of change among high school youth relative to middle school youth is consistent with higher baseline prevalence and greater potential for observed change. At the same time, the presence of declines across all substances suggests a broad pattern of reduction rather than changes limited to a specific substance.

Importantly, these within-coalition change estimates align with the directional patterns observed in Figure 2, suggesting that observed declines reflect not only differences in coalition composition across years but are also changes occurring within reporting communities over time.

Coalition infrastructure and prevention context (2021–2025)

During the 2021–2025 period, the DFC program maintained a large and stable national coalition network (see Table 1). Coalitions operated across varied geographic contexts and frequently reported serving more than one community type, underscoring the program's broad reach and adaptability. The number of active coalitions ranged from approximately 639 to 745 annually, reflecting sustained federal investment in community-based prevention across rural, suburban, urban, and tribal settings (see Table 3). In part, some aspects of continuity were related to coalitions being active across multiple years (see Table 1), although some coalitions left while new coalitions joined each cohort.

Multisector engagement remained a defining and stable feature of coalition processes. Within any given year, coalitions reported collectively mobilized thousands of community members across the 12 sectors to actively engage in youth substance use prevention efforts. Across reporting years, approximately 90% or more of coalitions included at least one member from each of the 12 required sectors, demonstrating strong adherence to program requirements. Maintaining a coalition of active, engaged members from all 12 sectors was more challenging, although most coalitions reported active participation from all sectors. Schools, youth-serving organizations, and organizations with substance use expertise were consistently among the most represented sectors. These patterns indicate broad and sustained cross-sector collaboration within participating communities.

Youth engagement serves as a core organizational element of coalition capacity. Nearly all coalitions reported partnerships with at least one school, and most collaborated with multiple schools within their communities. A majority of coalitions maintained direct youth representation within coalition membership, and approximately two-thirds reported sustaining a formal youth coalition or youth advisory structure during the study period. Hosting a youth coalition was key to youth engagement, with coalitions who hosted a youth coalition reporting significantly higher youth engagement than within coalitions not hosting a youth coalition each year. Combined, these indicators describe a prevention infrastructure grounded in multisector collaboration with predominately structured youth engagement during the 2021–2025 period.

From 2023 to 2025, coalitions reported engaging in activities to address a comprehensive set of community-level risk and protective factor indicators aligned with the program's logic model (see Table 4). Across ecological domains, coalitions reported substantial efforts to both reduce risk factors and strengthen protective factors. The highest levels of engagement were observed for factors related to community norms, family communication, peer norms, youth development, and social connectedness.

Although there was some variation across factors, generally when coalitions engaged in activities to address risk they also focused on enhancing protective factors in their communities. In 2025, among the protective factors that almost all coalitions were working to enhance were perceived community norms promoting nonuse of substance (97.3%), community activities for young people (91.2%), few youth perceiving peer acceptability of substance use (96.6%), families feeling confident about talking to youth about healthy behaviors and encouraging healthy behaviors (95.6% and 93.5), and delayed initiation of substance use (94.2%).

Consistent with the DFC logic model, coalitions reported substantial engagement in strengthening dimensions of social connectedness. This included strengthening youth connections to their community, including having a voice in the community through active engagement with community organizations (95.2%) and access to positive peers with whom to connect (87.2%), both of which were supported by coalition and youth coalition opportunities. Coalitions also engaged in efforts to enhance youth feeling connected to their families (86.1%) and schools (80.3%), connecting with meaningful adults in the youths' lives. These findings suggest that addressing community norms and youth connectedness are central in shaping coalition prevention priorities.

Discussion and Conclusion

This study examined both changes observed between first and most recent reports across all DFC coalitions funded who provided these data and recent patterns in youth substance use across DFC-supported communities. Across coalitions with at least 2 core measures collections (first and most recent), past 30-day substance use declined over time for all substances and both school levels, indicating reductions in youth substance use within participating coalitions overall. Focusing on the more recent 2021–2025 period, similar patterns were observed, with declines in substance use across all substances and more pronounced reductions among high school youth. These findings were supported by both cross-sectional prevalence estimates and within-coalition change analyses, suggesting that observed trends reflect not only differences in coalition participation over time but also changes occurring within communities. These findings are in line with recent national surveillance data from the YRBS that indicates declines in youth alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana past 30-day use among high school youth from 2013 to 2023. However, because timelines vary across the samples and because DFC communities are not nationally representative and coalition participation varies across years, direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. Given the correlational nature of the DFC national evaluation, it is unknown to what extent DFC coalitions may be influencing the broader trend or to what extent external factors may be contributing to change within DFC communities.

These patterns were observed within a large and stable national prevention infrastructure characterized by sustained multisector engagement, widespread school partnerships, and ongoing youth involvement, all of which are in line with research and theory. This consistency in coalition capacity provides important context for interpreting observed trends and highlights the importance of maintaining long-term community-based prevention systems, although these analyses do not assess the contribution of specific coalition characteristics to observed outcomes. At the same time, variation in the magnitude of change across substances and school levels, particularly the larger declines observed among high school youth and for substances with higher baseline prevalence, suggests that changes over time are influenced by both underlying prevalence patterns and local implementation contexts, and potential factors outside of the study that may contribute to change occurring more broadly.

Connectedness

Several of the most frequently reported risk and protective factors coalitions were engaged in events to address or enhance directly reflect dimensions of social connectedness emphasized in the program's logic model, including youth belonging, connections to family, schools and communities, and shared prosocial norms consistent with prior research identifying social connection as a protective factor associated with lower substance use and related health risks during adolescence.5,9 These patterns suggest that norms and connectedness remain central in shaping local prevention priorities within DFC-supported communities. Although coalitions are encouraged to identify local risk and protective factors as part of their needs assessment engagement with coalition members and the community, it is unknown to what extent coalitions do so. Coalitions who align engagement to address risk or enhance protective aligned to actual extent of risk and protective factors is likely to be more effective than those coalitions simply assuming the risk or protective factor is in place.

Implications for prevention practices

The findings also have implications for community-based prevention practice. The sustained scale and organizational stability of the DFC coalition network suggest that multisector prevention infrastructure can be maintained over time with at least a limited amount of funding ($125,000/year) across the full range of community settings. This finding aligns with prior research highlighting the potential of community coalitions to organize cross-sector resources and address shared behavioral health risks at scale.8 Consistent youth representation and school partnerships further illustrate how prevention systems can institutionalize youth engagement rather than treat it as episodic participation. In addition, the integration of standardized community-level risk and protective factor monitoring offers a practical framework for aligning coalition activities with established prevention science while generating nationally comparable descriptive data. Collectively, these features highlight the importance of sustained infrastructure, structured collaboration, and ongoing contextual assessment in supporting and guiding contemporary substance use prevention efforts.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analyses are descriptive and correlational, based on data from DFC-supported coalitions and, therefore, cannot establish causal relationships between coalition activities and changes in youth substance use. Although within-coalition comparisons provide insight into changes over time, the absence of a comparison group and the heterogeneity of coalition implementation limit the ability to attribute observed trends to specific prevention strategies. In addition, youth substance use outcomes are based on self-reported survey data collected locally by coalitions, which may be subject to reporting bias and variation in survey administration practices across communities although local bias is somewhat mitigated by the focus on within-coalition change over time. Although national trends in youth substance use also suggest declines, it is unknown what youth substance use would be like in the United States without the long-term contributions of DFC coalitions whose data contribute to these national trends.

Second, variation in data availability and reporting across coalitions introduces additional considerations. Coalitions differ in the timing and frequency of data collection, and the set of coalitions contributing data varies across years, particularly in analyses of recent trends. In years where there are high numbers of coalitions in years 1 and 2, relatively fewer may have outcomes. As a result, cross-sectional estimates may reflect changes in the composition of reporting coalitions in addition to changes in substance use patterns. Although weighting procedures were used to account for differences in sample size, these adjustments do not fully address differences in local data collection methods or population characteristics. Finally, measures of coalition characteristics and community-level risk and protective factors are based on coalition-reported data and reflect perceptions of local conditions rather than independently verified measures.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive, national-level examination of youth substance use trends within community-based prevention settings supported by the DFC program. Across both analyses of change between first and most recent reports across all DFC since inception and recent trends from 2021 to 2025, consistent declines in past 30-day substance use were observed across substances and school levels, with larger reductions among high school youth. These patterns were evident across both cross-sectional prevalence estimates and within-coalition change analyses, suggesting that observed declines reflect changes occurring within communities over time within DFC-supported communities.

The observed declines in youth substance use occurred within a large and stable national prevention infrastructure characterized by sustained multisector engagement, strong school partnerships, and ongoing youth involvement. Although causal inferences cannot be made, the consistency of these patterns highlights the potential value of maintaining long-term community-based prevention capacity and investing in systems that support coordinated, locally driven approaches. Findings also underscore the importance of community-level conditions, including social connectedness and norms, as central prevention priorities within DFC-supported communities.

Combined, these results demonstrate the utility of national descriptive evaluation systems for understanding real-world prevention efforts at scale and informing contemporary public health practice.
