Advances in Versatile Copper Sulfide-Based Nanoplatforms: From Synthesis and Properties to Multifaceted Biomedical Applications
Authors: Qiyong He, Haiyang Liu, Binghua Liu, Cunming Peng, Lulu Zhang, Haixia Ge, Jingmou Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCopper sulfide nanoparticles have become a versatile class of functional materials in the biomedical field. They exhibit responsiveness to near-infrared (NIR) light, catalytic activity, tunable electronic structures, and compatibility with integrated diagnostic and therapeutic designs. Controlled synthetic strategies have allowed copper sulfide nanomaterials to be precisely tailored with respect to their size, morphology, crystalline phase, and surface characteristics. Copper sulfide can serve as an imaging-active component, drug carrier, and bioresponsive modulator in composite biomedical nanoplatforms. This versatility has promoted the development of copper sulfide-based systems for tumor therapy, wound healing, biosensing, multimodal imaging, anti-inflammatory interventions and cardiovascular treatment. Copper sulfide has emerged as a promising platform for integrated therapeutic strategies. Copper sulfide-containing nanoplatforms can further support signal amplification and contrast enhancement in diagnostic applications. This review summarizes recent advances in the synthesis, properties and multifaceted biomedical applications of copper sulfide-bearing nanoplatforms. Special emphasis is placed on their contributions to composite fabrication, mechanism-driven design strategies, and application-oriented functional integration. We also discuss current challenges and future perspectives. Overall, copper sulfide-based nanoplatforms represent promising and adaptable systems for biomedicine.Keywords: copper sulfide, nanoparticles, controlled synthesis, tumor therapy, wound healing, biological detection

Introduction

Inorganic nanomaterials have attracted extensive attention for biomedical applications due to their nanoscale-tunable physicochemical properties.1 Their size-dependent behavior, surface chemistry and structural tunability have enabled diverse applications in tumor therapy, tissue repair and biodetection.2–4 Among them, semiconductor nanomaterials were particularly notable for their distinctive optical and electronic behaviors, which support photothermal conversion, catalytic reactions and bioimaging.5

As representative narrow-bandgap semiconductor nanomaterials, copper sulfide nanoparticles feature tunable stoichiometry and stable crystalline structures.6 The distinctive properties of copper sulfide arise from its unique crystal structure and electronic configuration. Its narrow bandgap allows efficient NIR absorption and energy conversion. The localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) enhances the optical response and endows the material with superior photothermal and electrochemical performance.7

The synthesis of copper sulfide nanomaterials has evolved from empirical preparation toward rational structural engineering, enabling coordinated control over size, morphology, crystalline phase and surface chemistry through the regulation of precursor chemistry, reaction kinetics, and interfacial growth processes.8 For example, the hydrothermal method is suitable for large-scale preparation due to its simplicity and high crystallinity,9 templating and elemental doping provide flexible pathways for functional customization.10 These advances in synthetic strategies have laid the foundation for expanding the biomedical utility of copper sulfide nanomaterials. They also enable precise alignment between material structure and performance.

CuS nanomaterials are generally relatively stable under physiological conditions, whereas acidic microenvironments, redox stimuli and external irradiation can accelerate surface degradation and promote copper-ion release.11 However, their degradation, pharmacokinetics and clearance highly depend on particle size, crystalline phase, surface coating, dose and route of administration. Excessive or sustained copper-ion release would induce oxidative stress and metabolic disturbance.12

Copper sulfide nanomaterials have shown broad utility in diagnostic and therapeutic settings.13 In tumor treatment, copper sulfide has been explored in photothermal therapy (PTT), photodynamic therapy (PDT), and chemodynamic therapy (CDT).14 Additionally, copper sulfide-containing systems have been developed for infection control and regenerative support through photothermal effects, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-related antibacterial activity, and copper-mediated tissue responses.15 In biodetection and imaging, copper sulfide has been used in multimodal contrast design and signal-amplifying biosensing systems.16 Its applications have also expanded to drug delivery, cardiovascular intervention, and inflammatory disease regulation.17

Notably, copper sulfide possesses distinctive physicochemical and biological properties that differentiate it from many other inorganic nanomaterials. Gold nanoparticles benefit from mature surface chemistry, high photostability, and strong photoacoustic or X-ray attenuation capabilities, yet their relatively high cost and prolonged retention in certain nondegradable formulations hinder broader application.18 Iron oxide nanoparticles have demonstrated advantages in magnetic responsiveness, MRI contrast enhancement and translational development, whereas their intrinsic NIR photothermal and redox-catalytic activities are comparatively limited.19 MXenes offer strong NIR absorption, high electrical conductivity and large surface areas. However, their susceptibility to oxidation and variability in surface terminations can affect material stability and reproducibility.20 Black phosphorus combines broadband optical absorption with the potential for degradation into phosphate-containing products, although rapid oxidation remains a major obstacle to storage and in vivo stability.21 Carbon-based nanomaterials exhibit excellent structural robustness and facile surface functionalization, with material heterogeneity and uncertain long-term elimination representing key challenges for some formulations.22 In this context, CuS stands out through its composition- and vacancy-dependent optical properties, copper-mediated redox activity, and compatibility with diverse drug-delivery and imaging platforms. These features provide a versatile basis for multifunctional nanomedicine, while issues related to copper release, phase heterogeneity, and the paucity of long-term pharmacokinetic data warrant further investigation.23

Recent studies indicate that copper sulfide-based nanomedicine is evolving beyond conventional photothermal ablation toward mechanism-driven and application-oriented designs.24,25 Representative advances include vacancy-engineered CuS nanomaterials with enhanced photodynamic activity and gene-regulatory functions,26 as well as confined or in situ synthesis strategies that improve formulation stability and biosafety.27 Emerging developments further encompass tumor-responsive multimodal therapeutic platforms,28 scalable second near-infrared (NIR-II) optoacoustic probes,29 and multifunctional CuS hydrogels for antibacterial treatment and tissue regeneration.30 Therefore, these advances have expanded the functional versatility and application scope of CuS nanomedicine, while simultaneously raising new challenges related to compositional control, mechanistic interpretation, manufacturing scalability and regulatory evaluation.

Previous reviews have primarily focused on CuS-based cancer theranostics, phototherapy, molecular imaging, or broader classes of copper-containing nanomaterials.31–34 Nevertheless, the relationships among controllable synthesis, stoichiometry- and defect-dependent properties, the functional contribution of CuS within multicomponent nanoplatforms, and application-specific biological outcomes have not been comprehensively integrated.

Herein, the present review provides a systematic overview of the synthetic strategies, physicochemical properties, and biomedical functions of versatile copper sulfide nanomaterials. Particular emphasis is placed on how synthetic parameters and compositional/defect engineering govern their performance in photothermal conversion, semiconductor catalysis, bioimaging, drug delivery and bioresponsive copper release. Their applications across tumor therapy, wound repair, biosensing, multimodal imaging, inflammatory disorders, cardiovascular intervention, and drug delivery are critically discussed. Finally, current challenges and future research directions are outlined to facilitate the rational design, optimization, and biomedical translation of CuS-based nanomaterials.

Hydrothermal/Solvothermal Method

The hydrothermal/solvothermal method is based on elevated temperature and pressure, which enhance precursor solubility and promote supersaturation in the reaction system. The reaction between copper and sulfur sources is accelerated, producing copper sulfide nanoparticles with well-defined morphologies and crystalline phases. Variation of the precursors and reaction conditions further allows the preparation of diverse copper sulfide nanomaterials, including nanorods,75 nanospheres,76 and nanoblossoms.77 As shown in Table 1, the structural and surface properties of copper sulfide nanomaterials are strongly governed by synthetic conditions. Variations in solvent system, reaction temperature and reaction duration influence crystallinity, morphology and surface characteristics.

Differences in solvent properties lead to distinct structural and morphological features in copper sulfide nanomaterials prepared by hydrothermal and solvothermal methods.78 Symmetrical and compact copper sulfide nanostructures are readily formed in aqueous media, whereas poor size uniformity is often observed.79 Singh et al reported a hydrothermal approach for the synthesis of CuS with diverse symmetric architectures, including spherical and flower-like morphologies, using different copper sources as precursors.39 Hollow spherical CuS showed particularly promising catalytic performance in the electrochemical reduction of CO2. In the solvothermal synthesis, non-aqueous organic solvents are commonly employed as reaction media. Their dielectric properties and coordination interactions with precursor species can promote the formation of anisotropic and low-dimensional structures, thereby enabling relatively precise control over product morphology.80 Nevertheless, the batch nature of solvothermal processing and associated elevated pressure can pose challenges for large-scale production. The use of organic solvents can further increase processing costs and complicate solvent and surfactant removal, as well as the subsequent transfer of the resulting nanomaterials into aqueous media.

Coprecipitation Method

Coprecipitation represents a classical wet-chemical method for synthesizing nanomaterials.81 In a typical process, soluble copper salts and sulfur-containing precursors are dissolved in solution, generating Cu2⁺ and sulfide species. When the ionic product exceeds the solubility product constant of copper sulfide, spontaneous precipitation occurs, leading to coprecipitation.82 The nucleation and growth of copper sulfide nanoparticles can be controlled by adjusting reaction conditions such as temperature, pH, and stirring rate, or by introducing additional components like surfactants. Precise control over complex morphologies presents greater challenges than hydrothermal and solvothermal methods.83 Despite these limitations, coprecipitation remains attractive because of its operational simplicity, low cost, and scalability. At the same time, relatively broad particle-size distributions, limited crystallinity, particle agglomeration and less precise morphological control can restrict its application when stringent structural uniformity is required.

It is noteworthy that the coprecipitation method demonstrates unique advantages in the preparation of doped and modified multicomponent materials, offering novel approaches for expanding the functionalities of copper sulfide nanoparticles. By introducing dopant precursors into the reaction system, the coprecipitation effect enables dopant ions to uniformly embed within the copper sulfide lattice or adsorb onto the particle surface, thereby forming multicomponent composite nanostructures.84

Microwave-Assisted Synthesis

The microwave-assisted approach offers a solution for the rapid synthesis of copper sulfide nanomaterials due to its unique energy transfer mechanism and highly efficient synthesis characteristics.51 As listed in Table 1, microwave-assisted synthesis enables the rapid preparation of copper sulfide nanomaterials with various morphologies. Hou et al reported a microwave-assisted hydrothermal synthesis of in situ carbon-coated two-dimensional CuS nanosheets using Cu-MOF (metal–organic framework) as the precursor.51 The product showed a thin-layered structure and uniform carbon coating, demonstrating the efficiency of the rapid preparation for well-defined CuS nanostructures.

Microwave-assisted synthesis employs microwave electromagnetic energy to regulate the reaction environment and facilitate the formation of copper sulfide nanomaterials. The rapid oscillation of polar molecules and migration of ions under microwave irradiation generate localized heating, which accelerates precursor decomposition and reaction kinetics by facilitating bond activation. This enhanced heating efficiency can promote the nucleation and directional growth of copper sulfide nanostructures.85 Copper sulfide nanomaterials with diverse morphologies can be prepared by tuning microwave parameters, precursor composition and additive selection.86 For example, Wei et al developed a microwave-assisted one-pot aqueous method for the synthesis of ultrasmall cysteine-capped CuS quantum dots. By integrating rapid microwave heating with in situ surface functionalization, this strategy enabled efficient preparation of CuS quantum dots with excellent water dispersibility, stability and optical properties, which were further applied as a rapid fluorescent platform for selective Ag⁺ detection in water samples and living cells.52 The ultrasmall dimensions of these CuS nanostructures are also of interest for biomedical applications. Such nanodots generally exhibit good colloidal stability and facilitate systemic clearance, although their high surface-to-volume ratio could also accelerate copper-ion release.87 A major challenge associated with microwave-assisted synthesis is achieving homogeneous energy distribution and maintaining batch-to-batch reproducibility during scale-up, particularly as reaction volumes increase.

Thermal Decomposition

Compared with high-temperature solid-state routes, thermal decomposition protocols can provide relatively accessible reaction conditions while enabling control over the phase and morphology of copper sulfide nanomaterials. This method relies on the use of single-source or composite precursors, such as copper-sulfur complexes containing Cu-S bonds.88 Thermally driven bond cleavage can be induced under specific temperatures and gaseous conditions. This process releases volatile by-products while facilitating the rearrangement of copper and sulfur ions, ultimately yielding copper sulfide nanomaterials with defined crystalline phases and morphologies.89 As shown in Table 1, thermal decomposition can generate copper sulfide materials with versatile structural features, such as core-shell microspheres and morphology-controlled assemblies, by regulating precursor composition and thermal conditions.

Sharma and Jeevanandam synthesized CuS nanomaterials with diverse morphologies by thermally decomposing copper–thioacetamide complexes under different solvent conditions.55 The colloidal dispersion of the products was further improved through the incorporation of capping agents or modulation of the polymer chain length in the precursors. Ravele et al employed oleylamine as a capping agent during the thermal decomposition of a copper dithiocarbamate single-source precursor.56 This approach merged ligand-assisted surface stabilization with temperature-controlled thermolysis, thereby suppressing nanoparticle agglomeration and enabling the controlled formation of CuS nanocrystals with tunable morphology and improved dispersion. Such ligand- and temperature-mediated control is beneficial for regulating crystalline phase and particle morphology. However, the relatively high reaction temperatures, hydrophobic surface ligands, and subsequent purification requirements can complicate the direct biomedical formulation of the resulting nanocrystals.

Polyol Method

The polyol method is widely used for the liquid-phase synthesis of nanomaterials. High-boiling polyols can simultaneously function as solvents, reducing agents, and coordinating ligands. Their high boiling points provide a broad liquid-phase temperature window, while their hydroxyl groups can coordinate with metal ions and modulate reaction kinetics.53 At suitable temperatures, polyols exhibit mild reducing ability, which facilitates the formation of metal-containing compounds with specific valence states.90 As displayed in Table 1, the polyol method has been employed to prepare copper sulfide nanoflower-like structures with high surface area under relatively high-temperature liquid-phase conditions. For example, nitrogen-doped carbon-coated Cu2S microspheres were synthesized in a conventional polyol process using tetraethylene glycol as the solvent and copper nitrate and thiourea as precursors. This material exhibited excellent reversibility during sodium-ion storage.91 In the context of precious metal recovery, Fe3O4@CuS magnetic nanohybrids were prepared via a polyol reflux route by using ethylene glycol as the solvent. The resulting nanohybrids enabled the rapid separation of Au ions from aqueous solutions, with the reflux temperature and duration serving as key parameters governing the complexation process.58

Thermal Injection Method

The hot-injection method is a rapid liquid-phase synthetic strategy in which a precursor solution is introduced into a preheated high-boiling reaction medium. As presented in Table 1, the hot-injection method yields copper sulfide nanocrystals. This process generates instantaneous supersaturation, separates burst nucleation from subsequent crystal growth, and thereby facilitates the preparation of nanocrystals with narrow size distribution and relatively high phase purity.92 Compared with gradual heat-up routes, the hot-injection method offers stronger kinetic control over nucleation and growth, which is particularly advantageous for regulating stoichiometry, morphology, and colloidal dispersibility of copper sulfide nanomaterials. Ligands are not merely stabilizing agents. They can coordinate with specific crystal facets, modulate precursor conversion, suppress agglomeration, and ultimately influence particle size, aspect ratio and surface chemistry.93

Li et al prepared monodisperse hexagonal covellite CuS nanocrystals through a hot-injection process using cupric oxide and elemental sulfur as precursors in an oleic acid/liquid paraffin system.60 The resulting nanocrystals exhibited broad visible and NIR absorption, indicating that hot injection is effective for preparing optically active CuS nanocrystals with controlled phase composition. Jia et al reported an atmospheric-pressure triethylene glycol-based hot-injection route, in which CuS graded microspheres, CuS nanosheets and Cu9S5 nanoparticles were obtained by adjusting the sulfur source, injection temperature, reflux temperature and triethylenetetramine content.61 These findings indicate that both phase composition and morphology play important roles in determining the LSPR characteristics of copper sulfides. In particular, anisotropic CuS architectures, such as nanorods and nanosheets, offer tunable LSPR responses and increased surface exposure. However, their shape-dependent cellular uptake and interactions with cell membranes should be carefully considered when evaluating their suitability for biomedical applications.94 Yepseu et al extended this strategy to the preparation of CuS-decorated CdS and ZnS nanocomposites using metal thiosemicarbazone complexes as single-source precursors.62 The obtained flower-like porous heterostructures exhibited enhanced photocatalytic activity. These results suggested that hot injection was suitable for constructing functional CuS-based composite nanomaterials. Although hot injection provides effective kinetic control over particle size, phase composition and dispersibility, the use of high-temperature organic media and surface ligands necessitates additional purification and aqueous-phase transfer in biomedical applications.

Electrodeposition Method

Electrodeposition is a reliable approach for efficient synthesis and offers simple operation, low cost and scalability.95 Wang et al developed an alginate-coordinated electrodeposition strategy for the green synthesis of CuS nanoparticles and CuS/alginate nanocomposites under mild conditions.64 CuS nanoparticles with an average diameter of approximately 6.0 nm were embedded within the deposited matrix. This finding suggests that electrodeposition can support the in situ generation and stabilization of ultrasmall CuS nanostructures.

In the electrodeposition process, redox reactions between copper and sulfur sources are driven on the surface of a conductive substrate through the regulation of electrode potential or current. Directional deposition of copper sulfide and related derivatives can therefore be realized.63 As displayed in Table 1, electrodeposition has been used to fabricate CuS-based nanorods and nanospheres in aqueous systems under relatively mild electrochemical conditions. A diverse range of fabrication processes from thin films to nano-heterostructures can be achieved by the mild regulation of the electrochemical system.96 Importantly, electrodeposition has been applied to the preparation of CuS-based heterostructured nanomaterials on preformed nanostructured substrates. For example, Ma et al assembled CuS nanoparticles onto ordered TiO2 nanotube arrays via galvanostatic electrodeposition, thereby yielding a CuS/TiO2 nanotube heterostructure with enhanced interfacial charge transfer.65 The resulting photoelectrode achieved a penicillin G removal efficiency of 99.1% within 150 min under visible-light-assisted photoelectrocatalytic conditions.

Electrodeposition can also be used to construct two-dimensional CuS nanostructures on flexible conductive scaffolds. Jin et al reported galvanostatic electrodeposition of CuS nanosheets on carbonized cotton fabric, producing a binder-free nanostructured electrode with a specific surface area of 450.76 m2 g−1. The material delivered an areal capacitance of 4676 mF cm−2 at 2 mA cm−2 and retained 89.8% of its initial capacitance after 10,000 cycles. This result suggested that electrodeposition was effective for the controllable growth of high-surface-area CuS nanosheet architectures.66 These characteristics make electrodeposition particularly attractive for fabricating CuS coatings and nanostructures integrated with conductive or implantable interfaces. However, the substrate-dependent nature of the process limits its applicability to freely injectable colloidal formulations.

Ion Exchange Method

Ion exchange is an efficient wet chemical approach for synthesizing copper sulfide and related composite architectures.97 This method is generally based on solubility differences or lattice matching of the ions. The in situ growth of copper sulfide can be prompted through targeted exchange between ions in the precursor and sulfur source ions. This process facilitates the synthesis of products with specific structures and crystal phases.98 As displayed in Table 1, ion exchange methods can yield copper sulfide nanotubes, microflower-like structures, heterostructures and core-shell composites, depending strongly on the nature of the precursor and exchange conditions.99

For metal oxide precursors, the difference in solubility equilibrium constants between CuO and CuS facilitates oxide-to-sulphide conversion, in which O2- ions are replaced by S2- ions to form a CuS coating. This creates a core-shell structure that enhances the material’s stability. Bai et al employed CuO nanowires as precursors, and immersion in Na2S solution was used for ion exchange to obtain CuO/CuS core-shell structures.100 Controlled adjustment of the sulfur source concentration helps reduce excessive agglomeration.101

When metal sulfides are used as precursors, cation exchange can be guided by cation-size and lattice-matching factors, enabling the construction of heterostructures and quantum-dot architectures. The specific precursor–reactant pair can strongly influence the exchange kinetics, while diffusion-limited exchange could result in prolonged reaction durations.102 For example, Jiao et al used ZnS nanoparticles as precursors, and CuS/ZnS type I heterostructures were obtained through ion exchange in Cu(NO3)2 solution. The reduction rate of Cr(VI) was higher than that of the single-component materials, which suggested potential utility in pollutant degradation.70 The ability to preserve or transform precursor architectures makes ion exchange particularly useful for the fabrication of CuS-based core–shell structures and heterostructured systems. Such multicomponent structures provide opportunities for integrating complementary imaging and therapeutic functionalities, although increased structural complexity also imposes high requirements for precise compositional regulation, degradation evaluation, and mechanistic understanding.103 Therefore, controlling incomplete exchange and diffusion-limited conversion remains critical for the rational design and reproducible preparation of CuS-based nanostructures.

Template-Assisted Method

Template-assisted strategies can be integrated with various synthetic methods to direct the morphological evolution of copper sulfide nanomaterials. By providing physical confinement or molecular-recognition sites, external templates facilitate the formation of complex and well-defined architectures.104 As presented in Table 1, hard/soft templates and biomolecular templates can be employed to improve structural uniformity and biocompatibility. For example, Yang et al synthesized hollow CuS nanoparticles with particle sizes below 100 nm through a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide micelle-assisted soft-template method. The resulting hollow nanostructures showed a photothermal conversion efficiency of 42.42%, indicating that soft templates were effective for controlling hollow architecture and nanoscale size.73 Biological templates have been used for the preparation of copper sulfide. Qi et al reported a melanin-instructed biomimetic route for the synthesis of folic-acid-modified CuS nanodots, in which melanin served as a biotemplate to guide copper-ion adsorption and subsequent CuS formation.74 Wang et al developed a DNA-templated strategy to prepare surface-vacancy-rich CuS nanoparticles. This approach supported the formation of CuS nanostructures and enabled defect engineering and further functional integration with aptamer and siRNA components.26 These studies indicate that template-assisted synthesis provides a useful strategy for controlling morphology and tuning structural defects, biocompatibility and functional versatility in CuS nanomaterials.

The evolution of CuS synthesis strategies has substantially broadened the accessible structural landscape, extending from conventional nanoparticles to ultrasmall nanodots, anisotropic nanostructures, hierarchical assemblies, hollow architectures and multicomponent heterostructures. Increasingly precise control over nucleation and crystal growth, precursor conversion, interfacial ion exchange, and template-directed assembly has enabled the coordinated regulation of particle size, morphology, crystal phase, defect chemistry and surface characteristics. These advances have transformed CuS from a structurally versatile nanomaterial into a highly tunable platform in which nanoscale structural features can be deliberately engineered to modulate optical, electronic, thermal and chemical properties. Such structure–property relationships provide an important foundation for tailoring CuS nanomaterials toward specific applications, particularly in energy conversion and storage, catalysis, sensing and biomedicine.

Properties of Copper Sulfide Nanomaterials

Copper sulfide nanomaterials exhibit a unique combination of physicochemical properties, including semiconducting characteristics, tunable optical responses, adjustable electronic structures and versatile surface chemistry.32 These intrinsic features provide a foundation for their broad functional applications.105

Semiconducting Properties

Copper sulfide nanomaterials are typical p-type semiconductors, with holes generated by copper ion vacancies in the lattice serving as the primary charge carriers.106 Their bandgap can be precisely tuned by adjusting the stoichiometric ratio, typically ranging from 1.0 to 2.6 eV.107 This narrow bandgap structure allows efficient absorption of NIR light and promotes energy conversion.33 Furthermore, the Moss-Burstein effect significantly influences their bandgap characteristics.108 Increased copper defect density leads to hole accumulation near the valence band, causing a shift in bandgap energy.32 This effect can be exploited to optimize the range of their optical response, thereby enabling them to be adapted for use in a variety of applications.

Optical Properties

The broad applications of copper sulfide nanomaterials are primarily attributed to their optical properties, especially their strong absorption in the 700–1100 nm biological window.109 Under NIR laser irradiation, valence band electrons absorb photon energy and migrate to the conduction band, subsequently converting this energy into heat through non-radiative relaxation and lattice collisions.110 This absorption originates from Cu2⁺ d–d transitions and copper defect-induced LSPR.111 The NIR absorption of copper sulfide nanomaterials is minimally affected by the dielectric constant of the medium, offering superior stability.112 Moreover, the optical properties of copper sulfide are closely linked to its morphology. Anisotropic copper sulfide nanostructures, including nanosheets and nanorods, display well-defined LSPR peaks with strong absorption intensity.113 Dendritic copper sulfide nanostructures demonstrate favorable photothermal performance under 980 nm irradiation.114

Electronic and Thermal Properties

The coexistence of short-range Cu-Cu and Cu-S bonds within the copper sulfide lattice confers semiconductor and metalloid characteristics.115 The high electrical conductivity and carrier mobility of copper sulfide at room temperature contribute to rapid signal transduction, thereby facilitating fast responses in biosensing applications.116 The carrier concentration can be modulated by adjusting the copper defect density, thereby regulating the LSPR frequency. Li et al demonstrated that active modulation of the LSPR properties of Cu2−xS nanosheets could be achieved by controlling the concentration of copper defects.117

Copper sulfide nanomaterials combine a low thermal conductivity with high photothermal conversion efficiency.118 They induce rapid localized temperature elevation while minimizing heat diffusion to surrounding healthy tissue.119 Furthermore, they exhibit excellent thermal stability, maintaining consistent photothermal performance after multiple laser irradiation cycles.120

Chemical Properties

Copper sulfide exhibits excellent chemical stability in physiological environments, with an exceptionally low solubility product constant (Ksp=7.9×10−37), thereby reducing nonspecific release of copper ions.121 Copper sulfide nanoparticles remain largely stable in simulated physiological fluids, but measurable copper leaching has been reported in simulated gastric or lung fluids. In addition, changes in the surface-ligand environment can facilitate partial surface degradation and Cu2+ re-coordination, indicating that the chemical behavior of copper sulfide is sensitive to local acidity and interfacial chemistry.122

Applications in Tumor Treatment

Copper sulfide-based nanomaterials have been extensively explored for tumor therapy owing to their unique optical and catalytic properties, which can be activated by NIR irradiation and tumor-associated microenvironmental stimuli. These intrinsic features enable multiple therapeutic functions, including localized photothermal conversion, photoinduced ROS generation, copper-mediated redox reactions, drug or nucleic acid delivery, and tumor microenvironment modulation.134 These capabilities support diverse therapeutic strategies, including PTT, PDT, CDT, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, gene therapy and immunotherapy, as mechanistically summarized in Figure 1.

The predominant therapeutic mechanism is determined by multiple factors, including composition, defect density, surface chemistry, tumor microenvironment, administered dose, and irradiation conditions.

PTT

PTT is a minimally invasive and controllable therapeutic strategy that employs photothermal agents to convert NIR light into localized heat.135 Copper sulfide emerges as an appealing photothermal material. Its defect-related NIR absorption endows it with excellent photothermal conversion performance in tumor therapy.136 CuS-based PTT platforms have evolved from basic heat production to rationally engineered systems with improved stability, targeting capability, biocompatibility and therapeutic precision. The obtained CuS@PLGA nanoparticles showed high stability, effective photothermal performance and good tumor-targeting ability. The major advantage of CuS lies not only in its efficient NIR-induced photothermal conversion, but also in its capacity to modulate optical absorption, heat generation and therapeutic outcomes through compositional engineering, defect regulation, morphology control and surface modification. PDT

PDT relies on light-activated photosensitizers to react with oxygen and generate cytotoxic ROS, thereby inducing tumor cell apoptosis and local immune activation.148,149 Copper sulfide can produce ROS under NIR irradiation. This phenomenon is closely associated with defect-related states, photoexcited charge carriers, and Cu ion-mediated reactions.150 Chen et al developed red blood cell (RBC) membrane-camouflaged AuNBP@CuS core-shell nanostructures,151 where AuNBP@CuS denotes a gold nanobipyramid core coated with a copper sulfide shell. The CuS shell served as the key semiconductor component for NIR-II-triggered singlet oxygen generation and intracellular ROS production under 1064 nm irradiation, thereby achieving effective photodynamic cytotoxicity against EMT-6 cells and retaining inherent photothermal activity. Such designs broaden the applicability of CuS-based PDT, particularly for deep-seated tumor treatment with photothermal and photodynamic effects. Li et al synthesized polyethylene glycol-modified copper sulfide nanoparticles (CuS-PEG NPs).150 Under excitation by a NIR laser at 808 nm, CuS exhibits significant photodynamic activity, capable of activating dissolved oxygen and inducing excessive production of mitochondrial ROS. This nanosystem demonstrated good antitumor efficacy in SPC-A-1 lung adenocarcinoma cells and tumor-bearing mouse models.

The efficacy of CuS-based PDT relies on efficient ROS generation and sufficient oxidative damage within tumors while minimizing injury to surrounding healthy tissues. The defect-mediated electronic structure of CuS facilitates NIR-responsive ROS generation, and NIR-II excitation further improves light penetration into deeper tissues. In addition, the intrinsic photothermal activity of CuS can enhance light-triggered therapeutic effects through thermal augmentation. ROS generation in CuS-based hybrid nanoplatforms involves multiple pathways, including photoexcited charge carriers from CuS, auxiliary photosensitizer activation, copper-mediated redox reactions, and photothermal-enhanced processes. Therefore, the overall PDT performance of CuS-based systems is often governed by the synergistic contribution of multiple mechanisms. Comprehensive evaluation of CuS-mediated PDT should distinguish the respective contributions of photoexcited charge carriers, singlet oxygen generation, copper-associated redox activity and heat-enhanced ROS production.

CDT

CDT uses the acidic tumor microenvironment to convert H2O2 into •OH through Fenton-like reactions, thereby inducing oxidative stress and tumor cell apoptosis.152 CuS-derived copper species exhibit Fenton-like catalytic activity across a broad pH range. The acidic and reductive tumor microenvironment promotes Cu ion release and redox cycling, enabling endogenous H2O2 to generate highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals for chemodynamic therapy.153 Importantly, copper sulfide can simultaneously consume intracellular glutathione (GSH) during the Cu2+/Cu+ conversion process, which weakens the antioxidant defense of tumor cells and enhances CDT efficacy.122 Xu et al developed an NIR-activatable CuS-based proenzyme system,154 in which ultrasmall CuS nanoparticles were not continuously active but instead released Cu2+ on demand after light activation at the tumor site. The released copper was reduced to Cu+ by intracellular GSH and drove the conversion of H2O2 into •OH, indicating that CuS can provide CDT with spatially controllable therapeutic activation. Singh et al engineered glucose oxidase (GOx)-modified CuS nanocomposites (GOx@CuS), in which the GOx enzyme catalyzed the conversion of glucose into hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid, thereby inducing tumor starvation and simultaneously supplying H2O2 to potentiate CuS-mediated CDT.155 This design was developed to address the dual challenges of hydrogen peroxide deficiency and tumor energy supply. Zhan et al developed CD47-overexpressing cancer-cell-membrane (CCM)-coated, β-lapachone-loaded CuS nanoparticles (CD47@CCM-Lap-CuS).156 The CCM coating provided biomimetic tumor-targeting capability and prolonged blood circulation through immune evasion. β-lapachone served as an in situ H2O2-generating agent. CuS-mediated synergistic photothermal and chemodynamic therapy. This combination enabled effective treatment of 4T1 tumors while maintaining good biocompatibility. Gao et al developed folic acid-modified hollow ZIF-8/Au/CuS nanocomposites (FACZ),157 in which CuS served as the major chemodynamic therapeutic component. After incubation at 80 μg mL−1 for 24 h, FACZ reduced the viability of MCF-7 cells to below 20%, whereas the viability of HCMEC/D3 cells remained above 40%. The results suggested that CuS-mediated ROS amplification led to relatively selective tumor killing. Qin et al developed a CuS@GOx@ES nanoplatform for photothermal-enhanced CDT and copper-induced tumor toxicity.14 Ultrafine CuS nanoparticles served as the Fenton-like catalytic component and the NIR-responsive photothermal agent, while GOx consumed glucose and generated H2O2 and gluconic acid to enhance CuS-mediated CDT. The acidified tumor microenvironment promoted CuS degradation and Cu2+ release, and elesclomol (ES) further facilitated intracellular copper delivery. Under 808 nm irradiation, CuS-mediated heating amplified CDT and copper-induced cytotoxicity, leading to effective tumor growth inhibition in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. Xia et al engineered iodine-doped CuS nanoparticles (CuS-I NPs) to enhance Fenton-like catalytic activity.5 Iodine doping increased sulfur vacancies, promoted electron transfer and increased the Cu+ proportion, thereby facilitating H2O2 decomposition and •OH generation. The CuS-I nanoparticles were further modified with a furin-responsive peptide to construct CuS-I@P1 NPs, enabling tumor-specific self-assembly through •OH-mediated tyrosine dimerization. This self-catalytic process enhanced photothermal activity and tumor-specific imaging, providing an integrated strategy combining CDT with responsive nanomaterial assembly (Figure 3).

The role of CuS in CDT originates from its dual function as a nanoplatform and a reservoir of catalytically active copper species. After tumor accumulation and cellular internalization, CuS nanoparticles are exposed to acidic and reductive intracellular environments, which can facilitate structural transformation, degradation, and copper ion release. The released copper species participate in Cu2⁺/Cu⁺ redox cycling, deplete intracellular GSH, and catalyze the conversion of H2O2 into highly reactive hydroxyl radicals through Fenton-like reactions. Consequently, the therapeutic efficacy of CuS-based CDT is governed by multiple factors, including nanoparticle internalization, degradation behavior, copper release kinetics, and the availability of endogenous H2O2 and GSH. Structural features and surface modifications further regulate these processes by affecting nanoparticle stability, intracellular responsiveness, and redox activity, thereby influencing ROS generation and antitumor performance. Therefore, CuS-based CDT can be regarded as a tumor microenvironment-responsive therapeutic strategy in which nanoparticle transformation and copper-mediated redox reactions collectively contribute to therapeutic outcomes.

Combination Therapy

CuS-based nanoplatforms have been increasingly investigated for combination therapy because their intrinsic photothermal, catalytic, and drug-delivery capabilities can be integrated with complementary therapeutic modalities. Through rational structural and surface engineering, CuS can simultaneously contribute photothermal conversion, photodynamic and chemodynamic activities, controlled drug delivery, and tumor microenvironment modulation, thereby enabling synergistic antitumor effects.154 Such multifunctional strategies are particularly relevant to heterogeneous tumors, metastatic disease, and therapy-resistant lesions, where single-modality treatment often shows limited efficacy. In addition to the intrinsic therapeutic activities of CuS, its integration with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, gene therapy or immunotherapy can further broaden the therapeutic repertoire and enable coordinated treatment responses.158

Importantly, CuS is not merely an inert carrier in these combination systems. Depending on the therapeutic modality, CuS can facilitate drug release, enhance cellular sensitivity to radiation, promote nucleic-acid delivery, regulate intracellular stress or modulate antitumor immune responses. These effects arise from the interplay between the intrinsic properties of CuS and the therapeutic mechanisms of the co-administered agents. Accordingly, the therapeutic performance of CuS-based combination platforms is determined not only by the activity of the accompanying treatment but also by the physicochemical characteristics, degradation behavior, and functional contributions of the CuS component.

Copper Sulfide Combined with Chemotherapy

Copper sulfide-based combination therapy is commonly designed around chemo-photothermal therapy and Cu-mediated catalytic strategies. Copper sulfide usually serves as a NIR-absorbing photothermal agent and a scaffold for drug loading, whereas chemotherapeutic agents provide direct cytotoxic effects.172 Upon NIR irradiation, copper sulfide converts light into localized heat, which can cooperate with chemotherapy and trigger drug release from responsive nanostructures. Meanwhile, Cu ions released in the tumor microenvironment can participate in catalytic reactions and contribute to CDT. This design links light-triggered activation, microenvironment-responsive drug release and copper-mediated catalysis.

Li et al developed a hyaluronic acid (HA)-modified copper sulfide-based nanoplatform for doxorubicin (DOX) delivery,159 in which hollow porous CuS served as the carrier for DOX loading and enabled synergistic, time-controlled drug release. In a 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model, the DOX/indocyanine green (ICG)-CuS group achieved a tumor suppression rate of 53.83%, markedly higher than the 34.54% observed for free-DOX group. These results indicated that CuS could effectively enhance the therapeutic efficacy of chemotherapy. Jia et al prepared a dual-lock responsive CuS@mesoporous silica nanoparticle/doxorubicin@manganese dioxide@cell membrane nanoplatform (CuS@MSN/DOX@MnO2@cell membrane, CMDMm).160 The coordination between manganese dioxide (MnO2) and copper sulfide enables DOX to be retained in the nanocarrier. Meanwhile, the acidic tumor microenvironment and intracellular GSH act as dual stimuli to facilitate drug release. Meanwhile, the homologous targeting capability provided by the cell membrane coating improves tumor-cell recognition, and the photothermal activity of CuS further accelerates DOX release. Gholami et al developed an oxaliplatin-copper sulfide@UiO-66-NH2 nanosystem (OXA-CuS@UiO-66-NH2), in which copper sulfide functioned as an auxiliary cytotoxic component to synergistically enhance the anticancer effect of oxaliplatin (OXA) against CT26 colon cancer cells.161 Notably, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was reduced from 37.58 ppm for oxaliplatin@UiO-66-NH2 (OXA@UiO-66-NH2) to 7.97 ppm after the introduction of CuS, demonstrating that CuS can effectively reinforce the therapeutic efficacy of the drug-loaded nanosystem.

Copper Sulfide Combined with Radiotherapy

Conventional radiotherapy uses high-energy radiation to induce direct DNA damage or trigger ROS generation in tumor cells.173 As an effective carrier for radionuclides, copper sulfide can be used to fabricate theranostic systems integrating internal irradiation and imaging functions. Copper sulfide and its composites can also promote ROS generation under X-ray irradiation. They can disrupt tumor redox homeostasis and relieve hypoxia. These effects contribute to improved radiosensitivity.162 CuS can act as a radionuclide carrier and a radiosensitizer, thereby enhancing the localization and therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy.163 CuS is particularly suitable for internal irradiation design because its lattice and surface features can support stable radionuclide incorporation. Zhang et al developed a 131I-labeled BSA-modified CuS nanoplatform (131I-BSA@CuS) for anaplastic thyroid carcinoma.164 The radiolabeled CuS-based carrier achieves targeted radionuclide delivery while preserving the inherent photothermal performance of CuS, which facilitates synergistic radio-photothermal therapy. Similarly, the CuS-131I-PEG system further demonstrated the advantages of CuS lattice defect sites for iodine ion loading. Cui et al proposed a “multiple mild-hyperthermia” strategy, in which a mild temperature increase to 39–40 °C was applied before each session of photothermal brachytherapy to sustainably alleviate tumor hypoxia, downregulate HIF-1α, and improve local oxygen supply.165 The mild hyperthermia also promoted uniform intratumoral distribution of the nanoparticles. Zhou et al designed platelet membrane-biomimetic hollow CuS nanoparticles that leverage the tumor tropism and immune evasion properties conferred by the cell membrane to enhance tumor accumulation.166 CuS releases Cu2⁺ within the acidic tumor microenvironment to consume GSH and alleviates hypoxic conditions through mild photothermal effects, which simultaneously impairs the tumor’s ability to repair radiation-induced chemical damage and reduces its hypoxia tolerance. These studies show that CuS-based radiotherapy systems can combine radionuclide delivery with redox modulation, hypoxia alleviation, and imaging guidance for synergistic radiosensitization.

Copper Sulfide Combined with Gene Therapy

Gene therapy is a therapeutic strategy that introduces functional genes into target cells to treat or prevent disease. It mainly aims to regulate the expression of specific genes by restoring tumor suppressor genes or inhibiting oncogenes.167 In gene-therapy-related systems, CuS has been developed as a multifunctional component for nucleic acid delivery, stimuli-responsive release, and phototherapy-assisted sensitization. The surface of CuS allows the loading of nucleic acid molecules such as pDNA and siRNA through electrostatic adsorption, coordination interactions or DNA-templated assembly. Its NIR responsiveness can further enhance gene-related efficacy by facilitating cellular uptake, endosomal escape, and local photothermal or oxidative stress.174 Moreover, strategies such as DNA self-assembly, aptamer modification and biomimetic coating have enhanced the stability, targeting capability and functional integration of CuS-based nanoplatform.175 Wang et al utilized a DNA template to construct CuxS nanoparticles with abundant surface defects, followed by terminal functionalization with NC3S aptamers and Nrf2 siRNA.26 CuS served as the core mediator of photothermal and photodynamic effects, with surface vacancies promoting electron–hole separation and enhancing ROS generation. Simultaneously, it functioned as a platform for siRNA loading and acid-responsive release, enabling Nrf2 silencing. This weakened the defense mechanisms of tumor cells against oxidative and thermal stress, ultimately amplifying the therapeutic efficacy of phototherapy. Zheng et al constructed a polycation-coated Au@CuS nanohybrid (termed ASC-PGEA) for p53 delivery.168 CuS primarily functions as a NIR-II-responsive photothermal shell and synergistic sensitization unit. Its coupling with the Au core improves photothermal conversion efficiency. Upon irradiation, the platform promotes cellular uptake, endosomal escape and gene release, thereby facilitating p53 transfection and enabling synergy between gene therapy and photothermal therapy. Guan et al developed a multifunctional hollow mesoporous Cu9S8-based nanoplatform (CAPSH) for siRNA-mediated regulation of copper homeostasis in breast cancer.134 Cu9S8 served as a copper reservoir, siRNA carrier and NIR-II-responsive photothermal agent. The delivered siATP7A suppressed ATP7A expression and copper efflux, thereby promoting intracellular copper accumulation and cuproptosis. Under 1064 nm irradiation, Cu9S8 further induced photothermal effects and AIPH-derived radical generation, ultimately enhancing antitumor immune responses and inhibiting tumor progression (Figure 4). Therefore, CuS contributes to gene therapy through cargo loading, stimulus-responsive release and phototherapy-assisted regulation of cellular responses.

Copper Sulfide Combined with Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy enhances antitumor effects through activation of immune effector mechanisms and regulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME).176 CuS-based immunotherapeutic designs have expanded from photothermal immunogenic cell death to strategies involving stimulator of interferon genes (STING) activation, adenosine blockade and cuproptosis-related regulation. Chen et al encapsulated CuS and ovalbumin (OVA) within PLGA nanoparticles to construct CuS@OVA-PLGA-NPs.169 CuS functioned as a photothermal core and an antigen-release-promoting unit. It generated localized thermal effects under NIR irradiation, thereby directly killing tumor cells. CuS-mediated heating accelerated OVA release, and enhanced antigen uptake and presentation efficiency. This process upregulated IL-6, IL-12 and TNF-α, promoted CD8⁺ T-cell activation, and ultimately suppressed primary tumor growth and inhibited distant tumor progression. Cui et al loaded OVA directly onto the CuS surface to construct a CuS/OVA system for osteosarcoma treatment.170 CuS provided photothermal therapy and improved the conditions for dendritic cell activation. Parthasarathy et al developed a hollow CuS nanovaccine modified with folic acid and loaded with the Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) agonist R837 (HCuS-FA@R837).171 Hollow CuS functioned as the NIR-responsive photothermal core and immunoadjuvant carrier, while folic acid enhanced recognition of folate receptor-expressing tumor cells. Upon NIR irradiation, CuS-mediated photothermal damage elicited antigen release, while R837 subsequently stimulated TLR7-dependent immune activation, thereby promoting dendritic cell maturation and augmenting the immune response against HeLa cells. Yalamandala et al developed membrane-disrupting polymer-wrapped CuS nanoflakes assembled into CuS nanoballs (CuS NBs) for glioblastoma immunotherapy.177 Under weakly acidic tumor conditions, the pH-responsive polymer promoted membrane disruption and enhanced penetration of CuS nanoflakes into deep tumor regions. Upon 1064 nm NIR-II irradiation, CuS-mediated photothermal ablation induced tumor-associated antigen release. The exposed amino groups of the polymer subsequently captured the released antigens and facilitated their presentation to dendritic cells, thereby activating CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell-mediated antitumor immune responses (Figure 5).

Zhang et al constructed an adenosine aptamer-modified CuS-PEG@Apt, in which CuS primarily served as an amplification unit for PTT-induced immunogenic cell death and ferroptosis.25 CuS also depleted GSH and downregulated GPX4 to induce ferroptosis. Meanwhile, the aptamer blocked the adenosine-mediated immunosuppressive pathway, thereby synergistically enhancing dendritic cell maturation and T-cell activation. Yang et al reported a CuS nanoparticle-based strategy combining cuproptosis induction, photothermal therapy and complement-targeted immunotherapy.28 C5aR blockade overcame CuS-induced cuproptosis resistance mediated by the C5a/C5aR–Wnt/β-catenin–ATP7B axis, accordingly enhancing antitumor efficacy under laser irradiation and improving the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings suggested that CuS could modulate antitumor immunity and exert direct cytotoxic effects.

The enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect has been widely recognized as a fundamental mechanism underlying the passive tumor targeting of nanomedicines. Nevertheless, its contribution to nanoparticle accumulation in human tumors remains highly variable. Therefore, the tumor accumulation of CuS-based nanomaterials should be interpreted with caution and cannot be solely attributed to the EPR effect. Unlike relatively homogeneous murine xenograft models, human tumors exhibit substantial heterogeneity in vascular permeability, blood perfusion, extracellular matrix density, interstitial fluid pressure, and macrophage-mediated sequestration, all of which can significantly influence nanoparticle transport and intratumoral distribution. Consequently, passive accumulation observed in preclinical models may not accurately predict delivery efficiency and therapeutic outcomes in patients.178 Active targeting strategies based on ligands or receptor recognition can further enhance cellular uptake after nanoplatforms reach the tumor site; however, they do not necessarily overcome the upstream barriers associated with systemic circulation, vascular extravasation, deep tumor penetration, and heterogeneous distribution. These biological complexities represent major challenges for the clinical translation of CuS-based nanomedicines, despite their promising multifunctional therapeutic capabilities. Future studies should therefore move beyond EPR-dependent evaluation and combine mechanistic analysis of tumor targeting with quantitative biodistribution assessment, advanced imaging-guided patient stratification, and clinically relevant tumor models to establish more predictive strategies for CuS nanoplatform translation.

Applications in Wound Healing

Wound healing is frequently impaired by bacterial colonization, biofilm formation, persistent inflammation and insufficient vascularization.179 In wound repair, CuS-based materials integrate NIR-responsive antibacterial activity with copper-mediated microenvironmental regulation.180 Under NIR laser irradiation, copper sulfide generates localized hyperthermia and enhances ROS-mediated antibacterial effects, while controlled copper release contributes to angiogenesis and tissue remodeling.181 Accordingly, current wound-related applications of copper sulfide increasingly focus on integrating infection control with regenerative support.

Antibacterial Activity

Wound infections remain a major challenge in clinical practice.182 Traditional antibiotic therapies can induce the emergence of drug resistance, while conventional wound dressings are incapable of eradicating biofilms, suppressing inflammation and facilitating tissue repair.183 In infected wound healing, CuS-based systems have been primarily explored for NIR-responsive photothermal activity, ROS-associated antibacterial action and copper-dependent microbial inhibition.184 Beyond this direct photothermal effect, structural and compositional engineering strategies, including defect modulation, heterostructure construction and integration with catalytic components, can promote ROS generation, bacterial membrane disruption and biofilm elimination.185 The composition, antibacterial and wound-healing effects of CuS-based nanoplatforms were summarized in Table 5. Qiao et al reported ultrasmall covellite CuS nanodots for the treatment of multidrug-resistant bacteria-infected chronic wounds.186 CuS nanodots functioned as laser-activatable photothermal agents and remotely controllable Cu2+ reservoirs. The nanodots effectively killed methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli. The released Cu2+ promoted fibroblast migration and endothelial angiogenesis, thereby accelerating repair in MRSA-infected diabetic wounds. Nain et al developed BSA-encapsulated CuS nanocrystal assemblies with surface-vacancy-associated enzyme-like and photodynamic activities.187 The BSA–CuS nano-assemblies combined photothermal and ROS-mediated antibacterial effects and eradicated more than 99% of bacteria in an MRSA-infected wound model within 1 min. Luo et al prepared quaternized chitosan-coated CuS nanozymes through a biomineralization strategy.188 The CuS-QCS nanozyme exhibited peroxidase-like catalytic activity, generated hydroxyl radicals from low concentrations of H2O2, and achieved approximately 99.9% antibacterial efficacy against E. coli and S. aureus, while also promoting the healing of S. aureus-infected wounds.

The antibacterial activity of CuS-based systems in wound treatment should be evaluated alongside their effects on surrounding host tissues. Photothermal heating, ROS generation and copper-mediated antibacterial activity can effectively inhibit bacterial proliferation and biofilm formation. However, excessive thermal or oxidative stress can compromise fibroblast function, endothelial responses and tissue regeneration. Therefore, the therapeutic outcome of CuS-based wound therapy depends on precisely controlling the intensity and duration of antibacterial activity while maintaining a favorable microenvironment for tissue repair. Successful CuS-based wound treatment requires an optimal balance between efficient microbial eradication and preservation of tissue viability.

Promoted Tissue Regeneration

Copper ions are essential trace elements in the human body and play important signaling roles in tissue repair. The regenerative processes regulated by copper ions include angiogenesis and tissue remodeling. These effects are associated with enhanced vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition, and modulation of macrophage polarization.221

Xiao et al reported ultrasmall BSA-stabilized CuS nanoparticles for skin regeneration.222 CuS@BSA acted as a biocompatible photothermal nanomodulator for mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). Under controlled 980 nm irradiation, CuS@BSA generated mild local heating at approximately 42 °C, promoted MSC proliferation, and induced differentiation toward a fibroblast-like phenotype. In a full-thickness skin wound model, CuS@BSA combined with MSCs and NIR irradiation accelerated wound closure and collagen deposition. Yang et al developed an NIR- and pH-responsive hollow CuS-based nanosystem incorporating epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) for infected wound repair. Hollow CuS nanoparticles provided photothermal and Cu ion-mediated catalytic activity, whereas EGCG helped regulate oxidative stress.30 This nanosystem supported ROS-mediated antibacterial action during infection and contributed to angiogenesis and tissue repair during wound regeneration. These findings indicate that CuS-based nanoplatforms can support wound healing by coordinating antibacterial activity, oxidative-stress regulation, angiogenesis, and collagen remodeling.

The regenerative role of CuS-based systems varies across different stages of wound healing. During the early inflammatory phase, CuS-mediated photothermal and antibacterial activities facilitate microbial reduction and infection control. In subsequent proliferative and remodeling phases, controlled photothermal stimulation and copper ion release promote fibroblast activity, angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and tissue reconstruction. Therefore, the therapeutic efficacy of CuS relies on the precise coordination of material-derived functions with the dynamic biological requirements of different healing stages. The ability of CuS to integrate infection control with tissue regeneration represents a key advantage for wound-healing applications.

Applications in Biological Detection

CuS-based nanomaterials have been explored for biological detection as electrocatalytic interfaces, signal-amplifying labels, plasmonic components and imaging-active nanostructures.223 CuS benefits from favorable electrical conductivity, high surface area, defect-tunable electronic structure and enzyme-like catalytic activity. These properties support sensitive electrochemical and electrochemiluminescent signal output. CuS is particularly attractive because it combines strong NIR absorption with the ability to incorporate radionuclides, paramagnetic ions or fluorescent moieties, thereby enabling multimodal imaging rather than a single signal alone.224 The analytical relevance of CuS lies in its compatibility with integrated sensing and imaging architectures.

Applications in Biosensing

Glucose Sensor

Reliable glucose detection is central to diabetes management. It supports diagnosis, treatment adjustment and routine clinical monitoring. Conventional enzyme-based sensors remain widely used, but their performance is easily affected by environmental conditions. Non-enzymatic glucose sensing has become a representative biosensing application of copper sulfide.225 CuS serves as the electrocatalytically active material and electron-transfer medium, with structural engineering modulating active-site accessibility, operating potential and long-term stability. Copper sulfide offers a robust inorganic electrocatalytic interface for glucose oxidation, supporting reliable performance under variable operating conditions.226

Zhou et al prepared ultrathin two-dimensional CuS nanowall arrays directly on fluorine-doped tin oxide substrates and used them as binder-free glucose-sensing electrodes. CuS acted as the low-potential electrocatalytic working interface. Its nanowall architecture increased the electroactive area, shortened the electron-transport pathway, and enabled glucose detection at a relatively low applied potential, thereby suppressing interference from competing electroactive species.227 Yang et al designed a triple-shelled CuS@Ni(OH)2@CuS heterogeneous nanocage,228 in which CuS functioned as the conductive protective shell and glucose-adsorption-promoting layer. The inner and outer CuS shells maintained the activity of the Ni(OH)2 core and facilitated charge transfer, endowing the sensor with excellent long-term stability for continuous monitoring. He et al synthesized vine-like copper sulfide nanowires and demonstrated that copper sulfide primarily forms a three-dimensional electrocatalytic network.229 This network exposes abundant active sites and speeds up electron transport. The platform achieved a low detection limit of 0.13 μM in glucose detection.

Immunosensor

Copper sulfide is frequently utilized as a catalytic signal tag, electrochemiluminescence (ECL) quencher or conductivity-enhancing heterostructure component. Compared with conventional labels, CuS can participate directly in signal amplification. It combines enzyme-like activity with semiconductor/plasmonic properties. This makes CuS particularly suitable for sandwich immunosensors and signal-off ECL platforms that require strong output modulation at low analyte concentrations.230

Li et al, adopted galvanic displacement to grow in situ Au nanoparticles on CuS nanoflowers, fabricating a CuS/Au heterostructure for carcinoembryonic antigen detection.231 CuS served as the peroxidase-like catalytic core. Au enhanced the conductivity and improved the current response. CuS/Au tag amplified the H2O2-based electrochemical signal and achieved a detection limit of 5.5 pg/mL. Ali et al constructed a dual-quenching ECL immunosensor for Aβ1-2 oligomers using Ag-decorated CuS@CoS2 double-shelled nanoboxes.232 CuS served as a critical ECL acceptor in the energy transfer system, which expanded the spectral overlap and enhanced ECL-RET-based signal quenching. Liang et al developed a luminol-LDH/CuS@Pt signal-off immunosensor for alpha-fetoprotein. CuS mainly functioned as a visible-light-absorbing ECL quencher scaffold.233 After Pt growth, the composite consumed the donor signal effectively and enabled ultrasensitive alpha-fetoprotein detection.

Nucleic Acid Detection

Copper sulfide is used as an electrochemiluminescent emitter, plasmonic enhancer, or field-amplifying nanointerface.234 In contrast to purely molecular probes, CuS-based systems can couple target recognition with structural amplification strategies such as hybridization chain reaction (HCR), anti-HCR or plasmonic cavity construction.235 Accordingly, copper sulfide participates directly in the signal-generation process rather than serving as a structural support. Li et al developed a CuS@Ag square-cavity heterostructure array coupled with an AIE-COF for the detection of glioma miRNA-124-3p. CuS was employed as a plasmonic semiconductor substrate that underwent surface plasmonic coupling with Ag, thereby markedly enhancing the ECL emission of the luminophore. The sensor exhibited a wide linear range from 1 fM to 10 nM and a low detection limit of 0.49 fM.236 Pan et al designed Al-doped CuS colloidal nanocrystals for ctDNA detection.237 CuS was applied as the ECL-emitting semiconductor core, while Al doping tuned the band structure and oxygen-vacancy-related electronic states to enhance light emission efficiency. In addition, Li et al constructed an Hf-nanoMOF/square-cavity Au nanostar@CuS nanocube array for miRNA-9 detection in glioma diagnosis.238 CuS was adopted as the plasmonic semiconductor of the square-cavity heterostructure and cooperated with Au nanostars to generate dense hot spots. It markedly enhanced the ECL emission of Hf-nanoMOF through the surface plasmonic coupling effect.

Applications in Biological Imaging

Copper sulfide has emerged as an important building block for biological imaging. Its effect is mainly associated with strong NIR absorption, which supports photoacoustic signal generation.239 A copper-based lattice allows chelator-free or intrinsic radiolabeling, rendering copper sulfide well-suited for positron emission tomography (PET)-related applications.240 Copper sulfide is compatible with Gd, Mn, organic dyes and high-Z elements, supporting its use in magnetic resonance (MR), fluorescence and computed tomography (CT) imaging. Therefore, copper sulfide is widely incorporated as an imaging-active moiety in multimodal imaging platforms.241

Photoacoustic Imaging (PAI)

PAI is a non-invasive imaging technique that combines the high optical contrast of light with the penetration depth of ultrasound.242 As a promising contrast agent, copper sulfide can significantly enhance PAI performance during deep-tissue imaging.243 Strong NIR/NIR-II absorption, high photothermal-acoustic conversion efficiency and vacancy-enabled plasmonic responses make the materials ideal for deep photoacoustic imaging.244 Gangwar et al developed dendritic CuS nanostructures. CuS functioned as the intrinsic photoacoustic contrast. It generated concentration-dependent PA signals under 850 nm excitation and supported photothermal therapy.114 Mutalik et al reported rhodamine B-modified, mucin-capped CuS nanoparticles. CuS served as the NIR-II photoacoustic and photothermal core, while the surface modification improved tumor targeting and intracellular localization.245 Tang et al, developed CuS-based nanoformulations for optoacoustic imaging in the NIR-II window.29 PEGylated CuS nanoparticles served as blood-pool contrast agents and produced strong absorption at 1064 nm. This enhanced deep cerebrovascular imaging and enabled dynamic visualization of vascular perfusion. Min et al prepared ultrasmall CuS nanoflakes (WK@CuS NFs) based on the conformational transition of wool keratin from an α-helix to a random coil structure.246 These nanoflakes showed strong absorption in the NIR-II region around 1100 nm, and the photoacoustic signal displayed a linear relationship with concentration. This feature supported high-resolution imaging of deep tumor tissue. It enabled real-time monitoring of probe accumulation and metabolism in the tumor. Signal reliability may decline in complex biological environments because of interference from endogenous components.247 Therefore, surface engineering remains an important direction, especially for improving anti-interference ability, targeting accuracy and in vivo stability.248

Positron Emission Tomography (PET)

PET provides non-invasive and quantitative molecular imaging by detecting positrons generated during radionuclide decay and paired γ-photons produced by electron annihilation in vivo.249 Its strong affinity for radionuclides such as 64Cu, 68Ga and 89Zr provides a useful basis for radiolabeling. CuS is attractive in PET-related design because it can support chelator-free radiolabeling. This reduces structural complexity and may improve radiochemical stability.250 CuS can be co-doped with other imaging elements to build PET-centered multimodal probes. Riedinger et al reported a room-temperature post-synthesis strategy for incorporating Cu and 64Cu into PEG-coated CuS nanocrystals.251 CuS acts as the intrinsic radiometal host lattice. It allows radionuclide incorporation without conventional chelators while preserving colloidal stability and the surface biotin-recognition function of nanocrystals. CuS can accommodate radionuclides through intrinsic or chelator-free labelling while retaining NIR-responsive imaging and photothermal properties. For example, Chakravarty et al prepared intrinsically radiolabeled 64CuS-PEG nanoparticles using neutron-activated 64Cu. CuS was applied as the host matrix for stable incorporation of the PET radionuclide 64Cu,252 which enabled reliable tracking of the nanoparticle in vivo. PEGylation improved blood circulation and tumor accumulation, progressively enhancing tumor-to-background contrast in B16-F10 melanoma tumors.

The imaging capability of CuS platforms can be further enhanced by rational functionalization. Zhou et al developed Mn/68Ga-codoped CuS nanodots using BSA as a template.253 The CuS matrix provided the basic nanostructure and intrinsic NIR/photoacoustic-photothermal properties. 68Ga endowed PET capability, and Mn doping introduced T1-weighted MR contrast, enabling PET/MR/photoacoustic trimodal imaging. In the SKOV-3 ovarian tumor model, this platform showed effective multimodal imaging performance and favorable renal clearance. Zhou et al conjugated folic acid onto 64Cu-labeled CuS nanoparticles.254 CuS simultaneously functioned as the radionuclide-bearing core and the photothermal transducer, whereas folic acid was responsible for active recognition of folate receptor-overexpressing tumors, resulting in higher tumor uptake and clearer PET visualization than that of non-targeted controls.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

MRI visualizes lesions through the relaxation behavior of magnetic nuclei in an external magnetic field.255 CuS is not a classical MR contrast agent, but it is highly useful as a carrier and functional framework for paramagnetic species such as Gd or Mn. CuS usually contributes structural support, NIR responsiveness and multimodal compatibility, while the doped or released magnetic ions generate the MR signals. Zhang et al developed Gd/CuS-loaded functional nanogels.256 CuS functioned as the NIR-responsive imaging and therapeutic core. Gd provided T1-weighted MR contrast enhancement. The nanogel system enabled tumor-targeted photothermal therapy under MR/PA dual-mode imaging guidance. Jin et al developed HA-CuS/MnO2 nanosheets for MR imaging and synergistic therapy. CuS mainly functioned as the structural and photothermal core, whereas MnO2 decomposition in the tumor microenvironment released Mn2⁺ to activate the MR signal.257 Zhang et al developed 3D hollow CuS nanoflowers assembled from 2D nanosheets.258 This work exploited the intrinsic paramagnetism of CuS itself. The 3D hollow nanoflower architecture, composed of 2D CuS nanoplates, increased the exposure of Cu(II) sites and their contact with water molecules, thereby promoting T1 relaxation and enabling positive-contrast T2-FLAIR MRI. The reported r1 of CuS HNs was 0.49 mM−1 s−1, while the mass concentration-related longitudinal relaxivity, mr1, reached 5.1 (mg mL−1)−1 s−1, approximately 1.6-fold that of Magnevist. These results indicated that the structural design of CuS could support the intrinsic MRI contrast capability.

Fluorescence Imaging (FI)

Fluorescence imaging achieves localization and quantitative analysis of biological targets.259 CuS can be used as a stabilizing nanoscaffold or multifunctional carrier for fluorophores. Its contribution lies in improving cellular uptake, colocalization and imaging-guided therapy integration.260 Zhao et al prepared CuS nanoclusters with dendrimer-based poly(amidoamine) as the template and rhodamine B as the fluorescent molecule.261 CuS mainly acted as the nanocarrier and photothermal agent, while rhodamine B provided real-time fluorescence tracking of tumor cells. Shi et al developed RGD-CuS-Cy5.5 nanoparticles for lymph node metastasis imaging.262 CuS was applied as the nanoparticle framework and photothermal component, while Cy5.5 provided NIR fluorescence. The platform enabled fluorescence/CT bimodal imaging and imaging-guided photothermal treatment of metastatic lymph nodes. These studies support the use of CuS-based constructs as multifunctional scaffolds for fluorescence-guided imaging and therapy.

Computed Tomography (CT)

CT imaging visualizes internal structures through differences in X-ray attenuation among biological tissues. Its imaging quality depends strongly on the attenuation capability of the contrast agent.263 CuS-based nanomaterials have attracted growing interest as CT contrast platforms. CuS rarely acts as the sole CT generator. CuS nanomaterials combined with high-atomic-number elements can markedly improve X-ray attenuation and enhance imaging performance.264

Dong et al developed Cu2-xS:Pt(0.3)/PVP nanoparticles for CT imaging-guided chemo-photothermal synergistic therapy.265 Cu2-xS served as the photothermal component and contributed to CT contrast because of its high X-ray attenuation coefficient. CT imaging showed that Cu2-xS:Pt(0.3)/PVP nanoparticles produced a tumor contrast effect comparable to that of the clinical contrast agent iobitridol at equivalent concentrations. After intravenous injection into 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, a significant CT enhancement signal was observed at the tumor site within 1 h and persisted for up to 24 h. Meng et al prepared Bi2O3@CuS nanoarchitectures, in which CuS-nanoplated Bi2O3 enhanced the CT imaging efficacy of Bi2O3, especially under an acidic tumor-mimicking environment.266 Compared with pristine Bi2O3, Bi2O3@CuS showed a higher Hounsfield unit slope at pH 5.5 (143.87 mL·mg−1), and the enhanced CT signals in tumors were observed in vivo after injection. These studies indicated that CuS was mainly valuable in CT-related systems as part of integrated imaging-guided therapeutic design.

CuS-based composite nanoprobes have been developed for CT-guided tumor therapy. Wang et al constructed CuS–Au heterostructured nanoprobes by in situ growth of Au nanocrystals on the surface of CuS nanosheets.143 This system exhibited stronger CT attenuation capability than Omnipaque in vivo, enabling clear delineation of tumor boundaries. Under 1064 nm laser irradiation, it achieved CT-guided photothermal ablation. CuS was adopted as the heterostructure platform that synergized with Au to enhance imaging and therapeutic performance.

Other Biological Applications

Inflammatory Diseases

Inflammatory diseases such as osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and periodontitis are not driven by a single pathological factor, but rather by the interplay of inflammatory-cell activation, extracellular matrix degradation, oxidative stress, biofilm persistence and tissue-destructive remodeling.267 In the treatment of periodontitis-related inflammation, CuS-based nanoplatforms have been developed to combine local photoregulation with drug delivery and microenvironment-responsive activation.268 Additionally, CuS-based platforms have improved intra-articular retention, drug-targeted delivery, and cartilage-protective efficacy for osteoarthritis therapy. Cai et al engineered mesenchymal stem cells using biomimetic CuS@PC nanoparticles loaded with plasmid DNA encoding TGF-β1.269 CuS primarily served as a gene-delivery nanointerface for MSC engineering rather than merely as a photothermal additive. The CuS-based platform enhanced plasmid DNA (pDNA) transfection, promoted MSC proliferation and migration, improved chondrogenic differentiation, and inhibited extracellular matrix degradation. Liu et al reported an MSCs@CuS@CDKN1A nanosystem,270 in which CuS was utilized as the carrier for CDKN1A plasmid delivery and fibroblast-phenotype regulation. The system improved chondrocyte proliferation and migration, reduced apoptosis, lowered osteophyte formation and disease severity, and showed no obvious toxicity in vivo.

Xue et al developed neutrophil–erythrocyte hybrid membrane-coated dexamethasone-loaded hollow CuS nanoparticles (D-CuS@NR NPs) for osteoarthritis treatment.271 Hollow CuS functioned simultaneously as a dexamethasone carrier and a photothermal functional unit. Upon NIR irradiation, CuS-mediated mild heating promoted drug release and enhanced anti-inflammatory efficacy, resulting in improved protection of articular cartilage and suppression of OA progression in vivo. Huang et al designed an octahedral yolk-shell AuNR@CuS nanosystem.272 It was further functionalized with vasoactive intestinal peptide and hyaluronic acid to enable targeted methotrexate delivery. CuS primarily served as the semiconductor shell that provided photothermal and photocatalytic/photodynamic contributions, while the yolk-shell architecture also allowed high-capacity drug loading. The coupling between the Au nanorod core and the CuS shell enhanced light absorption and promoted synergistic photothermal-photodynamic-chemotherapeutic effects, ultimately suppressing synovial hyperplasia and alleviating edema in the collagen-induced arthritis model. CuS can function as an active therapeutic module that integrates local physical ablation with targeted drug action against inflammatory synovial lesions in rheumatoid arthritis-related diseases.

Recently, CuS has been increasingly exploited for anti-inflammatory therapies targeting intricate pathological microenvironments with combinatorial local regulation strategies. Through the integration of drug delivery, stimuli-triggered drug release, biofilm disruption and local photomodulation, CuS-derived nanosystems are able to simultaneously inhibit inflammation, attenuate tissue damage and facilitate repair-associated signaling cascades.273–275 This synergistic integration of functions holds great promise for treating inflammatory conditions where simultaneous lesion suppression and tissue protection are essential.

Cardiovascular Disease

The requirements for spatial precision, vascular safety and therapeutic intensity are stringent in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases.276 CuS is promising for vascular applications owing to its NIR-responsive localized intervention and structurally tunable vascular targeting capability. Cao et al developed hyaluronic acid-modified CuS/TiO2 heterostructured nanosheets for macrophage-targeted treatment of early atherosclerotic plaques.277 CuS contributed the NIR-II-responsive photothermal function, while the CuS/TiO2 heterostructure improved sonodynamic ROS generation. Under combined low-intensity sonodynamic therapy and mild photothermal treatment, the platform induced macrophage apoptosis, reduced inflammatory responses and suppressed plaque progression (Figure 6). CuS has been explored for preventing arterial restenosis after endovascular injury. Wu et al showed that locally administered CuS nanoparticles could be used as NIR-absorbing agents to selectively eliminate infiltrating macrophages at injured arterial sites under 915 nm laser irradiation.278 CuS-mediated photothermal treatment alleviated negative arterial remodeling and reduced restenosis-related wall thickening. Therefore, CuS can be employed as a locally activatable material for macrophage-targeted vascular remodeling control.

Fu et al developed a CuS@mSiO2-PEG/uPA nanosystem for NIR-triggered thrombolytic therapy.279 The CuS core generated localized heat under NIR irradiation, which facilitated the release and thrombolytic action of urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) loaded within the mesoporous silica shell. Upon NIR irradiation, CuS-generated local heating accelerated uPA release and simultaneously enabled infrared thermal imaging, thereby achieving controlled thrombolysis with reduced risk of uncontrolled systemic drug leakage. This combined photothermal/thrombolytic strategy showed effective thrombus dissolution in vivo. Therefore, CuS-based cardiovascular strategies are increasingly directed toward targeted and image-guided local vascular intervention. These approaches integrate macrophage-targeted plaque regulation, photothermal prevention of restenosis, and triggered thrombolytic release within a single therapeutic framework. Collectively, these strategies suggest that CuS-based systems may serve as useful platforms for localized cardiovascular intervention.274

Biological Safety of Copper Sulfide Nanomaterials

Inorganic nanomaterials have attracted broad interest in imaging, drug delivery and tumor therapy because of their unique physicochemical properties.280 However, biosafety remains a major barrier to clinical translation. Their ultrasmall size, large surface area and distinct surface features can trigger complex biological interactions, leading to organ accumulation, oxidative stress, inflammation and metabolic disturbance.281

The toxicity of copper sulfide nanomaterials is strongly influenced by their physicochemical properties and the surrounding biological environment.282 Copper sulfide has a low solubility product constant and typically releases only a limited amount of copper ions under physiological conditions.283 Nevertheless, safety concerns remain associated with prolonged exposure, repeated administration, and impaired hepatobiliary or renal clearance. Slowly released copper ions from copper sulfide nanomaterials have been reported to interfere with hepatic metabolic pathways and contribute to organ injury.284 However, the biological behavior of CuS should not be evaluated solely based on the properties of intact nanoparticles. Acidic microenvironments, ligand exchange reactions, oxidative conditions and external irradiation alter surface stability and promote Cu2⁺ release. Moderate and transient copper availability contributes to antibacterial activity and tissue repair, whereas excessive or sustained copper exposure disrupts cellular redox homeostasis, mitochondrial function and metabolic processes. Under NIR irradiation and mildly acidic conditions, CuS-based materials enhance copper ion release and ROS generation through semiconductor-associated redox processes. Excessive ROS accumulation damages lipids, proteins and nucleic acids, thereby inducing apoptosis and oxidative injury.285 Therefore, the biological effects of CuS originate from the combined contributions of nanoparticle-associated interactions and released copper species.

Particle size represents an important determinant of the toxicological behavior of copper sulfide nanomaterials. Xia et al demonstrated that larger CuS particles preferentially accumulated in the liver and induced delayed tissue injury, whereas ultrasmall CuS nanoparticles underwent more rapid clearance and exhibited reversible hepatotoxic effects.286 Particle size also affects the relative contribution of renal and hepatobiliary elimination pathways. In addition, bare CuS nanoparticles are susceptible to aggregation in biological fluids, resulting in enhanced nonspecific cellular uptake and altered biodistribution. Surface ligands further regulate colloidal stability, protein corona formation, tissue retention and immune recognition.287 Therefore, the in vivo fate of CuS formulations is governed not only by primary particle size but also by aggregation behavior, surface chemistry, protein adsorption, administration route and treatment conditions. These factors collectively determine blood stability, cellular interactions, circulation persistence, organ distribution and subsequent clearance pathways. Their integrated effects help explain the substantial variations in biodistribution and toxicity profiles observed among different CuS formulations.

The design of CuS-based composite materials introduces additional complexity into toxicological evaluation. CuS is frequently integrated with metals and functional materials for improved imaging and therapy. However, the biological effects of these auxiliary components should also be taken into consideration. Wang et al reported that the long-term toxicity of copper sulfide/cadmium sulfide composites was mainly associated with the accumulation of Cd2⁺ in the liver and kidneys, rather than from the copper sulfide itself.288 Therefore, biosafety assessment of CuS-based composites requires comprehensive consideration of both the intrinsic properties of CuS and the potential toxicity of incorporated components.

The biological effects of copper sulfide vary considerably depending on material characteristics and application conditions. Feng et al reported that CuS nanoplates induced prominent toxicity in normal cells such as HUVECs and RAW 264.7 cells.289 In vivo biodistribution further showed that intravenously administered CuS nanoplates were mainly present in the spleen, liver and lung. Zhang et al found that CuS nanoclusters induced pericardial edema and reduced heart rate in zebrafish embryos at certain concentrations.290 These findings suggest that the toxicity of copper sulfide should be analyzed in the context of specific scenarios.

A comprehensive biosafety evaluation of CuS should integrate nanoparticle behavior, degradation characteristics and copper ion release profiles in vivo. Apart from conventional assessments, including cell viability, serum biochemical parameters and histopathological analysis, safety studies should examine hemocompatibility, inflammatory and immune responses, repeated-dose toxicity, and long-term organ retention. Differentiating the biological effects of intact CuS nanoparticles from those of released copper species is also essential, as they exhibit distinct distribution patterns, clearance pathways and toxicological mechanisms. Quantification of copper levels in blood, liver, spleen, kidneys, urine and feces provides valuable information regarding organ accumulation, degradation, redistribution, and renal or hepatobiliary elimination. For photoresponsive CuS systems, irradiation parameters including wavelength, power density and exposure duration, should also be considered because photothermal activation accelerates structural transformation and copper release. Collectively, these evaluations establish a mechanistic understanding of CuS biodistribution, persistence, clearance, and the contribution of released copper species to systemic toxicity.

Conclusion and Prospects

As versatile inorganic nanoplatforms, copper sulfide-based nanomaterials have attracted extensive attention for biomedical diagnosis and therapy. This review has summarized recent advances in CuS-based systems, covering synthesis strategies, physicochemical characteristics, and diverse biomedical applications. The functional versatility of CuS originates from its tunable structural features, including particle size, morphology, crystalline phase, defect configuration and surface chemistry, which collectively regulate its optical, thermal, catalytic and biological behaviors. In tumor therapy, CuS-based nanoplatforms have been explored for PTT, PDT and CDT, as well as combination therapies involving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, gene therapy and immunotherapy. In addition, CuS-based systems have demonstrated potential in antibacterial wound treatment, tissue regeneration, biosensing, multimodal imaging, inflammatory disease management, cardiovascular applications and drug delivery. These multifunctional capabilities are mainly derived from NIR-responsive photothermal conversion, ROS generation, copper-mediated redox activity, controlled cargo delivery and copper ion release.

Despite significant progress, several challenges remain before CuS-based nanoplatforms can advance from preclinical studies toward clinical applications. The reported therapeutic performance of CuS systems varies considerably due to differences in particle size, Cu/S stoichiometry, crystalline structure, surface modification, dosage, administration route and irradiation parameters, limiting meaningful comparison across studies. Moreover, the increasing complexity of multifunctional CuS-based composites introduces additional challenges in identifying the specific contributions of individual components and clarifying the underlying therapeutic mechanisms. In particular, tumor targeting strategies require reconsideration, as the EPR effect exhibits substantial heterogeneity among human tumors and cannot fully predict nanoparticle delivery efficiency in clinical settings. Furthermore, the long-term biological fate of CuS, including degradation behavior, copper speciation, organ retention, immune responses and clearance pathways, remains incompletely understood.

Future research should emphasize mechanism-driven design, standardized evaluation and translationally relevant validation. Establishing clear relationships among CuS composition, structure, degradation behavior and biological function will be essential for rational material optimization. For systemic applications, CuS formulations with predictable degradation, controlled copper release and efficient clearance should be prioritized, accompanied by comprehensive evaluations of repeated-dose toxicity, immunological effects and long-term organ accumulation. In addition, simplifying overly complex nanoplatforms while maintaining therapeutic advantages will facilitate mechanistic understanding and clinical manufacturing. Scalable and GMP-compatible preparation methods, together with strict control of batch consistency, sterility, endotoxin contamination, residual reagent, and colloidal stability, are critical requirements for future translation.

Emerging technologies, including advanced imaging approaches, artificial intelligence, machine learning and personalized nanomedicine, provide new opportunities to optimize CuS nanoplatform design by enabling real-time evaluation of biodistribution, rational selection of synthesis parameters, and prediction of structure–function relationships. However, the successful application of these approaches relies on the establishment of standardized and high-quality experimental datasets. Ultimately, the clinical value of CuS-based nanoplatforms will depend not only on achieving enhanced therapeutic efficacy but also on demonstrating volreproducible manufacturing, predictable biological behavior and acceptable safety profiles. Continued advances in material engineering and translational nanomedicine will facilitate the development of CuS-based systems as clinically relevant platforms for biomedical applications.
