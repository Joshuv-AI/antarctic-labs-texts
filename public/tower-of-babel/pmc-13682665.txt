Integrating machine learning and geospatial technologies for spatio-temporal analysis of urban expansion and land use transformation in Gambella Town, Ethiopia
Authors: Kanenus Fufa Dararo, Goanar Jal Dup, Weyesa Merga Chemeda, Shankar Karuppannan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid urbanization and the associated expansion of built-up areas are transforming land resources, ecosystem functions, and the spatial structure of cities. Distinguishing urban growth, urban expansion, and urban sprawl is essential for understanding the processes and patterns of urban transformation and supporting sustainable urban planning. This study integrates machine learning and geospatial technologies to analyze spatio-temporal land use and land cover (LULC) dynamics and urban expansion in Gambella Town, Ethiopia. Multi-temporal Landsat satellite images from 2005, 2015, and 2025 were processed using remote sensing techniques and a supervised machine learning approach based on the Random Forest (RF) classifier. Six major LULC classes were identified: Agricultural Land, Bush with Grass, Built-Up Area, Bare Land, Forest Land, and Water Bodies. The classification achieved overall accuracies of 90%, 90%, and 89% for 2005, 2015, and 2025, respectively. The results revealed substantial land transformation and rapid physical urban expansion, with the built-up area increasing from 93 ha (2.0%) in 2005–1,956 ha (41.3%) in 2025. This expansion was accompanied by major transformations of surrounding land resources, particularly the conversion of Bush with Grass and other open areas to built-up land. Agricultural land increased from 634 ha (13.4%) to 1,750 ha (36.9%), whereas Bush with Grass declined from 3,375 ha (71.1%) to 652 ha (13.7%). Socio-economic information collected from 277 respondents indicated that population growth, rural–urban migration, housing demand, employment opportunities, and infrastructure development were major drivers of urban expansion. The study distinguishes urban expansion, represented by the increase in built-up land, from urban sprawl, which refers to the spatial pattern and form of outward urban development. The integration of Random Forest classification and GIS-based spatial analysis provides valuable evidence of land transformation, vegetation loss, and increasing pressure on urban services, supporting more sustainable urban planning and land management in Gambella Town.

Introduction

Urbanization is one of the most significant demographic and socio-economic transformations of the twenty-first century. Currently, more than half of the global population resides in urban areas, and this proportion is expected to continue increasing in the coming decades. Although urban growth is a worldwide phenomenon, its pace is considerably faster in developing countries, where rapid expansion often exceeds the capacity of infrastructure, governance, and environmental management systems, placing substantial pressure on limited natural and economic resources [1–4].

Urban growth, expansion, and sprawl, characterized by the outward expansion of urban areas into surrounding rural and peri-urban landscapes, has emerged as a major consequence of rapid urbanization [5–9]. This process is primarily driven by population growth, economic development, and rural-to-urban migration. Examining the spatial and temporal characteristics of urban expansion provides valuable insights into the dynamics of urban growth and enables the prediction of future development patterns, thereby supporting sustainable urban planning and resource management. A comprehensive understanding of urban sprawl is therefore essential for developing effective land-use policies, improving infrastructure planning, and promoting environmentally sustainable urban development [10,11].

The continuous expansion of cities has resulted in increasing demand for land, housing, transportation, and public services. As urban areas grow, natural ecosystems and agricultural lands are frequently converted into built-up environments, leading to significant changes in land use and land cover (LULC) [10,12–15]. Monitoring these transformations has become an important component of global environmental change research because LULC changes directly influence ecosystem functions, biodiversity, climate, and sustainable resource management [10,16–18]. Previous studies have shown that uncontrolled urban expansion contributes to the loss of agricultural land, environmental degradation, deterioration of water quality, increased air pollution, fragmentation of green spaces, higher infrastructure and energy costs, and greater socio-spatial segregation [10,11,14,15,19–21].

Urban sprawl generally alters the spatial structure of cities by converting natural and agricultural landscapes into residential, commercial, and industrial developments. Depending on local geographical and socio-economic conditions, urban expansion may occur in concentric forms surrounding established urban centers or linearly along major transportation corridors. Identifying these growth patterns is fundamental for effective urban planning and sustainable land management. Advances in remote sensing (RS), Geographic Information Systems (GIS), and spatial analysis have significantly improved the ability to monitor, quantify, and evaluate urban expansion over time using satellite imagery integrated with ancillary spatial datasets such as topographic maps and field observations [7,8,10,11,22].

Urban sprawl has become a widespread challenge affecting both developed and developing countries. While large-scale suburban expansion was initially associated with the post-World War II development of industrialized nations, contemporary urban growth is increasingly concentrated in developing regions where rapid population increase and migration accelerate the expansion of urban boundaries [3,10]. Such uncontrolled growth often results in environmental degradation, loss of biodiversity, inefficient land utilization, increased demand for public services, and growing pressure on existing infrastructure.

Africa is currently experiencing one of the fastest rates of urbanization worldwide. The proportion of the continent’s urban population increased from approximately 15% in 1940 to about 40% in 2010 and is projected to reach nearly 60% by 2050. Nevertheless, substantial differences exist among African countries. Nations such as Burundi, Ethiopia, Malawi, Burkina Faso, and Uganda continue to have relatively low levels of urbanization, whereas South Africa has experienced much higher urban growth associated with industrialization and economic development [10,15,21,23–25]. The rapid expansion of African cities presents significant challenges for sustainable land management, infrastructure development, and environmental conservation Getu, K., & Bhat, H. G. [2].

Ethiopia is among the least urbanized countries in Sub-Saharan Africa; however, it is currently experiencing one of the region’s fastest urban growth rates Singh Chandel, A. [15]. Urban population expansion has been estimated at approximately 6%, exceeding the average rate for many other Sub-Saharan African countries. Although agriculture remains the dominant sector of the national economy [9–13,16,17,25], increasing rural-to-urban migration, population growth, and economic transformation are accelerating urban expansion. In many Ethiopian cities, residential developments have expanded horizontally through both planned and informal settlements, often extending onto agricultural land and other undeveloped areas Singh Chandel, A. [1,5,6,15]. This pattern of growth has contributed to inefficient land utilization and increasing pressure on urban infrastructure and environmental resources.

Population increase resulting from both natural growth and rural-to-urban migration has been identified as one of the principal drivers of urban sprawl in Ethiopia [2,10,19–21,24–26]. Understanding the spatial and temporal dynamics of urban expansion is therefore essential for supporting evidence-based urban planning and sustainable land management. The novelty of this study lies in integrating machine learning, multi-temporal Landsat observations (2005–2025), GIS-based urban expansion analysis, and socio-economic data to develop a comprehensive framework for assessing urban expansion dynamics in Gambella Town, an understudied and rapidly emerging urban center in Ethiopia. Unlike previous studies focused mainly on major cities or conventional LULC change assessment, this research provides new evidence on urban expansion patterns, land transformation processes, and socio-economic drivers, supporting sustainable urban planning and land management in rapidly expanding secondary cities.

To address this gap, this study integrates Random Forest machine learning, multi-temporal Landsat imagery, GIS-based spatial analysis, and socio-economic data to examine urban expansion dynamics in Gambella Town from 2005 to 2025. The study provides new insights into the magnitude, spatial patterns, and drivers of urban expansion, contributing scientific evidence for sustainable urban planning in understudied secondary cities of Ethiopia.

Therefore, this study aims to analyze the spatio-temporal dynamics of urban expansion in Gambella Town, Western Ethiopia, using an integrated machine learning and geospatial technology approach. Specifically, the objectives of this study are to: (i) classify and quantify land use and land cover changes between 2005, 2015, and 2025 using the Random Forest machine learning algorithm; (ii) evaluate the spatial pattern and magnitude of urban expansion and associated land conversion processes; (iii) identify the major socio-economic factors influencing urban sprawl through household surveys and stakeholder information; and (iv) assess the implications of urban expansion on agricultural land, vegetation resources, and sustainable urban development. The findings are expected to provide valuable scientific information for urban planners, policymakers, and environmental managers to develop sustainable land-use strategies for Gambella Town and other rapidly growing secondary cities in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

Gambella town, located in the Gambella Region of Ethiopia, has a unique historical background. The area has been inhabited by various indigenous ethnic groups, and others, Ethiopian highlanders who are lives in the region. The Gambella Region is situated in the western part of Ethiopia, bordering South Sudan to the west and south.In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the Gambella area became part of the Ethiopian Empire. During the Italian occupation of Ethiopia (1936–1941), Gambella experienced some changes in administration and infrastructure [27]

Gambella Peoples’ National Regional State (GPNRS) is located in the southwestern part of Ethiopia, at a distance of 777 km from Addis Ababa. Administratively speaking, the region has three zones and one Special Woreda. There are 226 Kebeles. Five of the kebeles are that of Gambella town. According to the regional government’s policy documents, GPNRS is one of the emerging regions of the country and a home for the five indigenous ethnic groups, the Nuer, Anywaa, Majang, Opo, and Komo. In addition, following the resettlement program of the 1980s, a good number of people from different parts of the country moved to the region. Recently, the refugees from South Sudan (estimated 350,000) are becoming the most dominant, imposing a threat to the environment, socioeconomic and cultural values. Gambella is located in Ethiopia with 8.12, 50 N- 8.16, 20N and 34.33, 00 E −34.37, 40 E coordinates (Fig 1). It shares a long border with South Sudan and two other Ethiopian regions: Oromia to the north and east and the Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples’ Regional State (SNNPRS) to the south.

Digital image processing

Digital image processing focuses on improving the usability of distorted or inferior image data for accurate interpretation and representation of scenes [25,28–31]. This involves preprocessing raw image data to eliminate noise, calibrate radiometric information, and correct geometric distortions. Techniques such as contrast stretching and histogram equalization enhance visual quality, making images clearer for human observers and more effective for automated analysis. Image classification, particularly through methods like maximum likelihood classification (MLC), sorts pixels into land cover categories based on their reflectance characteristics, facilitating detailed analysis of land use and environmental changes Getu, K., & Bhat, H. G. [2,32–36].

Supervised classification further refines this process by using training samples to categorize pixels into distinct land use/land cover (LULC) classes Getu, K., & Bhat, H. G. [2]. Ground truthing activities validate these classifications by collecting GPS data and verifying various terrain types in the study area. This comprehensive approach ensures the reliability of the data used for classification, ultimately enhancing the accuracy of information derived from remote sensing. By integrating these methodologies, digital image processing plays a crucial role in applications such as environmental monitoring, land-use planning, and resource management.

Random Forest classification and accuracy assessment

A supervised Random Forest (RF) classifier was used to classify the Landsat imagery into the identified LULC classes for 2005, 2015, and 2025. Training samples were selected based on field observations, GPS ground-truth data, Google Earth imagery, and visual interpretation of the satellite images. A total of 200 ground-truth points were collected and distributed across the identified LULC classes to represent the spatial and spectral variability of the study area. Care was taken to select representative and relatively homogeneous locations for each class.

The collected reference data were used to support the training and validation of the RF classification. The RF algorithm was applied to the preprocessed Landsat spectral bands to generate the LULC maps for each study year. The same LULC classification scheme was maintained across the three study periods to ensure consistency in the subsequent change-detection analysis.

The accuracy of the classified LULC maps was assessed using a confusion matrix, overall accuracy, and Kappa coefficient. The classified pixels were compared with the corresponding reference/ground-truth points to determine the number of correctly and incorrectly classified samples for each LULC class. Overall accuracy was calculated from the proportion of correctly classified reference samples to the total number of reference samples, while the Kappa coefficient was used to measure the agreement between the classified map and reference data beyond chance agreement. Accuracy assessment was conducted for each study year to evaluate the reliability of the resulting LULC maps.

Statistical analysis of socioeconomic drivers of urban expansion

A binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to statistically evaluate the socioeconomic factors associated with urban sprawl. The model included migration, population growth, infrastructure development, low land cost, and land sales as explanatory variables. The overall regression model was statistically significant (LR χ² = 44.524, p < 0.001), with a McFadden pseudo-R² of 0.120, indicating that the included socioeconomic factors provided explanatory power for the occurrence of urban expansion.

As shown in Table 6, migration was significantly and positively associated with urban sprawl (B = 1.191, p < 0.001; OR = 3.289). This indicates that respondents identifying migration as a contributing factor had higher odds of reporting urban-sprawl conditions. Population growth was also statistically significant (B = 0.736, p = 0.007; OR = 2.087), indicating a positive association with urban expansion. Similarly, low land cost showed a significant positive association (B = 0.825, p = 0.006; OR = 2.281), while land sales were also significantly associated with urban sprawl (B = 0.972, p = 0.003; OR = 2.643).

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis

The machine learning model achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) value of 0.852 (85.2%) based on the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, with 200 validation points used for evaluation (Fig 15). This result indicates a high discriminatory capability of the model in effectively distinguishing urban and non-urban areas in Gambella Town. AUC values between 0.80 and 0.90 suggest fair classification accuracy, whereas values above 0.90 show exceptional performance, according to well recognized performance metrics [37]. Typical of machine learning classifiers using finite validation datasets, the ROC curve has a distinctive step-like structure. There are discrete increases in the true positive rate (TPR) and corresponding changes in the false positive rate (FPR) at each step that corresponds to changes in the decision threshold, where additional pixels are classed as urban.

Map of the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Strong sensitivity during the early stages of classification is demonstrated by the sharp spike seen at low false positive rates, which shows that the model accurately recognizes a significant percentage of urban pixels while introducing comparatively few false alarms. Because of the spectral overlap between urban surfaces, bare land, and sparsely vegetated areas that frequently occur in the diverse peri-urban landscapes of Gambella, the extended plateau in the middle portion of the curve indicates that, over a range of thresholds, further increases in sensitivity require accepting additional false positives. The ROC curve constantly stays above the diagonal random-guess line in spite of these difficulties, demonstrating that the classifier outperforms chance at every decision threshold. Mixed pixels, seasonal land-cover variability, and the spectral similarity of some land-cover classes in medium-resolution Landsat imagery are the main causes of the remaining discrepancy between the observed AUC (0.852) and the ideal value of 1.0, which denotes a moderate degree of classification uncertainty. Previous ROC features and AUC values for machine learning-based urban mapping have been reported in previous studies. For instance, Chen et al. [38–41] obtained AUC values between 0.84 and 0.89 for urban expansion prediction using geospatial machine learning techniques, whereas Arabameri et al. [42–45] and Pham et al. [46] reported AUC values exceeding 0.85 for urban growth susceptibility modeling using Random Forest-based approaches.

Similarly, Liu et al. [47–50] showed that in quickly developing countries, AUC values of approximately 0.85 offer dependable discrimination for urban growth modeling. Consequently, the obtained AUC of 85.2% supports the application of machine learning and geospatial technologies in urban planning, land-use management, and sustainable development by providing a strong and dependable framework for capturing the spatiotemporal dynamics of urban expansion in Gambella Town.

Discussions

Gambella Town’s land-use and land-cover (LULC) changes are closely related to several socioeconomic variables, chief among them the migration of rural to urban residents looking for safety and opportunity in the host town. Due to the increased demand for land for residents and habitation purposes brought about by this demographic transition, there have been major environmental effects. Technology, environmental changes, economic development, and human population expansion are all identified by Getu, K., & Bhat, H. G. [2,11] as important factors that influence LULC variability. These elements are reshaping the environment and the socioeconomic structure of Gambella as they appear.

The rapid expansion of built-up areas observed in Gambella Town is consistent with urban land-cover changes documented in other Ethiopian cities. Studies in Addis Ababa, Bahir Dar, and Hawassa have documented substantial urban expansion accompanied by changes in agricultural and vegetated land [51–55]. Similar trends have been reported in rapidly growing African cities such as Nairobi, Dar es Salaam, and Accra, where urban expansion has increasingly transformed surrounding agricultural and natural landscapes [6,56,57]. These similarities indicate that the transformation observed in Gambella reflects a broader pattern of urbanization across Africa, although the simultaneous expansion of agricultural land in Gambella highlights the influence of local land availability, environmental conditions, migration, and agricultural investment on its LULC dynamics.

The settlement area increased dramatically from 93 hectares (2.0%) in 2005–1,956 hectares (41.3%) in 2025, highlighting the pressing need for infrastructure and housing to support the expanding population. This urban sprawl is indicative of a larger pattern seen in areas that are quickly urbanizing, where the growth of human settlements frequently comes at the price of natural ecosystems. The pressure on land resources can lead to habitat loss, increased pollution, and challenges in water management, as noted by studies on urbanization impacts [11,15,58,59].

The remarkable increase in agricultural land from 634 hectare (13.4%) in 2005–1,750 hectares (36.9%) in 2025 highlights, Gambella’s appeal as a fertile agricultural zone this expansion can be attributed to the area’s rich soil and favorable climate, attracting both local and foreign investors seeking to capitalize on agricultural opportunities. The shift towards commercial agriculture, particularly for crops like beans, grains, cotton, and sugarcane, is reflective of a global trend where fertile lands are increasingly utilized for large-scale agricultural production.

The decline of bush with grass from 3,375 hectares (71.1%) in 2005–652 hectares (13.7%) by 2025 signals a significant loss of biodiversity and natural habitat. This loss poses risks not only to local wildlife but also to ecosystem services that communities depend on, such as soil stability and water regulation. As noted [10,11] the conversion of natural landscapes bush with grass and others land use classes to urban use can lead to ecological imbalances, exacerbating environmental degradation and undermining resilience to climate change.

Additionally, the reduction in bare land from 466 hectares (9.8%) in 2005–234 hectares (4.9%) in 2025 reflects a broader trend of land reclamation for productive uses. While this may indicate positive land utilization, it also suggests a shift away from less productive or unmanaged land, further emphasizing the need for careful land-use planning and management. The fluctuations in forest cover, decreasing from 93 hectares (2.0%) in 2005–70 hectares (1.5%) in 2025, reveal the precarious balance between urban expansion, agriculture, and conservation efforts.

The stable area of water bodies, from 83 hectares (1.7%) in 2005–82 hectares (1.7%) in 2025, raises important questions about water resource management amidst growing demands. Population growth inevitably leads to increased water consumption, necessitating sustainable water management practices to ensure that both human and ecological needs are met. The consistent presence of water bodies highlights their critical role in the local ecosystem, but also the potential for conflicts over water access as urban and agricultural demands increase.

Overall, socio demographic data and the classified satellite images provide compelling evidence of LULC changes over the past two decades in Gambella Town. These transformations reflect complex interactions between human activity and environmental systems have both positives and negatives impacts on socio economic and environment, underscoring the need for integrated planning that considers ecological sustainability alongside socio-economic development.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study assessed land use/land cover (LULC) dynamics and the socioeconomic and environmental implications of urban expansion in Gambella Town between 2005 and 2025 using satellite imagery, GIS-based analysis, and socioeconomic data. The results demonstrate substantial landscape transformation during the study period, particularly a pronounced expansion of built-up areas, which increased from 93 ha (2.0%) in 2005–1,956 ha (41.3%) in 2025. Agricultural land also increased from 634 ha to 1,750 ha, while bush with grass declined substantially from 3,375 ha to 652 ha. Bare land and forest land also decreased, indicating continued transformation of natural and semi-natural land covers.

The findings indicate that the rapid expansion of built-up areas has altered the spatial structure of Gambella Town and increased pressure on agricultural and natural land resources. The conversion of bush with grass and other natural land covers may contribute to habitat loss and environmental degradation, while changes in agricultural land may affect local livelihoods and food-security conditions. The study therefore demonstrates the importance of managing urban growth in a manner that considers both development needs and the conservation of productive and ecologically important land.

To promote sustainable urban development, urban authorities should strengthen land-use zoning and development control, particularly around agricultural and environmentally sensitive areas. Future urban planning should be supported by regularly updated satellite imagery, GIS-based monitoring, and evidence-based land allocation. Integrating environmental protection, livelihood considerations, and infrastructure planning into future urban development strategies will help guide Gambella Town toward more sustainable and spatially balanced growth.
