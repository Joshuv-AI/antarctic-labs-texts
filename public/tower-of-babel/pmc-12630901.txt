Sensitive mechanical compression-patterned phosphorescence in the rubbery state of cross-linked polymer network
Authors: Zhen Li, Wenhuan Huang, Chuanzhen Zhang, Youshen Wu, Yang Bai, Zhiyuan He, Chenhui Cui, Kexiang Chen, Ting Xu, Haoqing Teng, Zhishen Ge, Guoqing Zhang, Yanfeng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe molecular motion of polymer chains inevitably quenches phosphorescence at temperatures higher than the glass transition temperature (Tg). Therefore, modulating phosphorescence at relatively high temperatures presents a significant challenge. Herein, we report a dye-doped, cross-linked epoxy resin with sensitive mechanical compression-patterned phosphorescence. The temperature range over which phosphorescence occurs can be modulated by the Tg values of the epoxy resins. The resulting doped material exhibits visible, long-lived phosphorescence at temperatures as high as 150 °C. Furthermore, we demonstrate sensitive, spontaneously reversible mechanical compression phosphorescence characteristics in the rubbery state of the cross-linked network. At compressive stresses as low as 11 kPa, the phosphorescence is significantly triggered and can spontaneously recover to its initial state within 13.7 sec, maintaining consistent thermal-mechanical coupling response properties over 12 cycles. Our study systematically explores the embedding of phosphorescent dyes in polymer matrices and comprehensively analyzes the effects of free volume, mechanical force, and temperature on phosphorescence properties, thus establishing a foundation for future practical applications in sensing and information storage across a wide temperature range, particularly in high-temperature environments.Subject terms: Conjugated polymers, Polymer synthesis, Information storage

Introduction

Organic room temperature phosphorescence (RTP) materials are gaining attention as attractive alternatives to conventional phosphorescence materials, such as luminescent complexes and inorganic compounds, due to their low toxicity and cost. These materials are being explored for a wide range of applications, including information encryption, medical imaging, and optical displays1–3. The main challenge in developing organic RTP materials lies in achieving a high inter-system crossing (ISC) rate while avoiding conditions, such as intense molecular vibrations and oxygen exposure, which lead to the quenching of the triplet excited state4,5. In recent years, various RTP enhancement strategies have been developed, including crystallization6,7, host-guest interactions8–10, H-aggregation11,12, heavy-atom effects13,14, and doped base systems15–17. Although long-lived RTP systems have been achieved in the crystal state, crystal-based chromophores face challenges, such as poor reproducibility, harsh growth conditions, and limited flexibility, which hinder their practical applications18,19. To develop long-lived RTP materials with high reproducibility and flexibility through simple methods, researchers have utilized polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), epoxy resins, and polyacrylate. These polymers create a rigid environment that mitigates non-radiative decay and shields triplet excitons from quenching by oxygen and moisture15,20–22. Polymer-based RTP materials have promising applications due to their high flexibility and large-area production capacity23. The rigid polymer environment can restrict the thermal motions of organic dyes, such as rotation and vibration, thereby suppressing the non-radiative decay of triplet excited states.

It is well known that the photoluminescence process is significantly influenced by temperature, and an increase in temperature tends to enhance the rotation and vibration of chromophores, which promotes thermal quenching24,25. However, temperatures exceeding room temperature are inevitable in many applications, such as optoelectronic devices, and consequently degrade luminescence performance. High-temperature phosphorescence (up to 170 °C) can currently be achieved in polymer systems containing metal ions26,27. Nonetheless, there are still very few reports of purely organic high-temperature phosphorescence materials, especially polymer-based organic materials with excellent high-temperature phosphorescence and mechanical force responsiveness above Tg23,28–30. Therefore, it is necessary to design and construct polymer-based RTP materials with high-temperature stability and to investigate the influence of temperature and polymer molecular motion on the phosphorescence of these materials.

Compared with common RTP materials, smart RTP materials with stimulus-responsive effects (including force, temperature, light, pH, etc.), especially those that do not depend on special crystal morphology, offer significant advantages for applications in anti-counterfeiting, sensing, and detection31–34. The responsiveness to mechanical stresses, such as compressive, shear, and tensile forces, provides an approach to studying the macroscopic and microscopic deformation, damage, and repair of polymeric materials35,36. Mechanochromic polymers (MCPs), which change their visible or fluorescent colors in response to mechanical forces (e.g., stretching or compression), have attracted extensive attention. Various MCPs have shown potential applications in damage detection and pressure sensing37. However, reports on mechanically RTP-stimulated responsive materials remain limited38–40. Conventional mechanically responsive RTP materials undergo deformation of their original crystalline structure when subjected to external forces, leading to a reduction in RTP emission. In contrast, the “turn-off” type responsive materials often have low reliability in practical applications, while the “turn-on” systems offer a different solution38. However, all these strategies depend on the specific conformation of the organic chromophore and its crystalline state, and mechanical forces are usually triggered by destructive means, such as grinding or sharpening. Therefore, developing an RTP material that does not depend on the conformation or crystalline state of the organic chromophore for repeated mechanical force stimulus-response, and can achieve the conversion of mechanical force to phosphorescent signals, presents a more challenging task.

In this work, we present a strategy to modulate phosphorescence intensity using both thermal and mechanical means, by limiting the non-radiative decay of organic dyes through a cross-linked network (Fig. 1). The intensity and afterglow of phosphorescence in the epoxy resins depend on the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the polymer matrices. Below the Tg of the polymer, the molecular chains are in a frozen state, effectively inhibiting the chromophores. The polymer chains begin to exhibit significant mobility as the polymer matrix reaches or exceeds its Tg, leading to a reduced phosphorescence afterglow. The resulting long-lived phosphorescence emission at high temperatures (up to 150 °C) is achieved by modulating the Tg of the cross-linked network. Interestingly, when the RTP material is in the rubbery state, low compressive stress (as low as 11 kPa) can significantly trigger the phosphorescence. This strategy for mechanical compression phosphorescence is sensitive, non-destructive, and can be triggered on a continuous cycle.

Results

To validate our strategy, organic dyes were doped into an epoxy resin with a rigid three-dimensional cross-linked network. The chemical structures of bisphenol A diglycidyl ether (DGEBA), hardeners (hexahydro-4-methylphthalic anhydride, PA; glutaric anhydride, GA; and sebacic acid, SA), and organic dyes (4,4′-bis(N-carbazolyl)-1,1′-biphenyl, CBP; N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylbenzidine, TMP; N,N′-bis-(1-naphthalenyl)-N,N′-bis-phenyl-(1,1′-biphenyl)-4,4′-diamine, NPB; and 1-pyrenylboronic acid, PyB) are shown in Fig. 1. To prepare the dye-doped epoxy resins (CBP-PAE, TMP-PAE, NPB-PAE, and PyB-PAE), the organic dyes (CBP, TMP, NPB, and PyB) were first added and dissolved in DGEBA with vigorous stirring, followed by the addition of the hardener (PA) with continued stirring to achieve phase miscibility. Finally, the mixture was cured in a mold at 80 °C for 8 h, 120 °C for 4 h, and 180 °C for 2 h and the diameter of the circular dye-doped epoxy resin films was 20 or 10 mm, and the thickness was 2 mm (Supplementary Fig. 1). As expected, the rigid cross-linked network effectively limits the thermal motion of the chromophores and reduces the non-radiative decay of the triplet excited state, enabling the construction of a purely organic RTP polymer system. At room temperature, after removing UV-365 nm excitation, these chromophore-doped epoxy resins exhibited intense full-color emission (from blue to red) visible to the naked eye, with an afterglow lasting up to 7 s (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Movie 1-4).

To evaluate the origin of RTP in epoxy resins, their prompt and delayed spectra were recorded. All the polymer films exhibited a double emission feature, as shown in Fig. 2. For example, the steady-state (prompt) photoluminescence spectrum of CBP-PAE displayed a main emission peak at 369 nm, while the time-resolved (delayed) spectrum showed a prominent blue-green emission peak at 526 nm. The emission peak at 369 nm was attributed to fluorescence emission based on its nanosecond lifetime (Supplementary Fig. 3). The emission band shift of over 150 nm in the delayed spectrum indicated the characteristic RTP emission. The lifetime monitored at 526 nm was up to 0.942 s, which is associated with long-lived phosphorescence (Supplementary Fig. 4). Phosphorescence spectra of dilute solutions of the chromophores (CBP, TMP, NPB, PyB) at a low temperature of 77 K further confirmed the origin of the double emission, showing the same vibronic structures as those of RTP epoxy resin films (Supplementary Fig. 5). Additionally, green, yellow, and red RTP were successfully obtained from TMP-PAE (τ = 0.952 s), NPB-PAE (τ = 0.413 s), and PyB-PAE (τ = 0.170 s), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4). Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE) coordinate diagrams of these RTP epoxy resin films were calculated from their phosphorescence spectra using spectrometer software, and the coordinates for the CBP, TMP, NPB, and PyB-doped PAE systems were (0.33, 0.55), (0.40, 0.55), (0.47, 0.51), and (0.64, 0.35), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6). It was observed that the CBP-PAE whereas NPB and PyB-doped films showed yellow and red afterglow. X-ray diffraction (XRD) results for epoxy resin films doped with chromophores demonstrated that the chromophores were in an amorphous rather than crystalline state within the epoxy resin (Supplementary Fig. 7)41. These findings collectively suggest that epoxy resin is an effective matrix for the fabrication of RTP polymer materials.

Subsequently, the optimal doping level of CBP in the epoxy resin was determined by recording changes in phosphorescence intensity and afterglow, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8. When the CBP concentration increased from 0.1 to 1.5% (wt/wt), the phosphorescence intensity and afterglow increased significantly. However, when the concentration exceeded 1% (wt/wt), further increases in concentration resulted in only marginal gains in phosphorescence intensity. Other chromophores (TMP, NPB, PyB) exhibited similar effects of doping concentration on phosphorescence properties. Consequently, a 1.0% (wt/wt) doping concentration was employed in subsequent experiments. Additionally, the effect of chromophore concentration on the thermal and mechanical performance of the cross-linked epoxy resin was investigated. The stiffness of the polymer matrices was assessed through tensile tests to evaluate the mechanical properties of chromophore-doped epoxy resins. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, the stress-strain curves of epoxy resins doped with different chromophores (CBP-PAE, TMP-PAE, NPB-PAE, and PyB-PAE) indicate that their Young’s modulus, elongation at break, and breaking strength (Supplementary Table 1) were generally consistent with the mechanical properties of undoped PAE. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was used to characterize the storage modulus (E’) and loss factor (tan δ) of the epoxy resin at different temperatures. The temperature-modulus spectra remained similar with comparable Tg values (Supplementary Fig. 10). The data presented above collectively demonstrate that the 1.0% (wt/wt) doping concentration did not compromise the mechanical performance of the cross-linked epoxy resin.

Due to the different rigidity of the different anhydride/carboxylic acid hardeners (SA, GA, and PA), the cured epoxy resins (SAE, GAE, and PAE) also had different moduli and Tg values (Supplementary Fig. 11). At room temperature, prompt and delayed phosphorescence spectra of CBP-doped SAE, GAE, and PAE films were firstly recorded, their main emission peaks were the same (Supplementary Fig. 12). At high temperatures (above Tg), the Young’s modulus and breaking strength of SAE, GAE, and PAE decreased substantially. The change in elongation at break was more pronounced for SAE, while the elongation at break of GAE and PAE was essentially maintained (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 3). The elevated temperatures result in an increase in molecular chain mobility; however, the covalent cross-linked network restricts molecular chain slippage. The combined effect of these factors was a small change in elongation at break, but a decrease in Young’s modulus. The higher elongation at break of SAE was attributed to its lower cross-link density, while GAE and PAE had a more tightly cross-linked network.

Further, the average distance r between the polymer cross-linking points could be obtained from v, with calculated r values of 2.19, 0.84, and 0.83 nm for SAE, GAE, and PAE, respectively44,45. The r values for GAE and PAE were smaller than the maximum three-dimensional sizes of the chromophores CBP, TMP, NPB, and PyB (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 14). Therefore, the data above further indicate that the three-dimensional cross-linked network of the epoxy resin can effectively reduce thermal quenching of the chromophore. Additionally, phosphorescence was affected by oxygen in the environment. To evaluate this, the oxygen barrier ability of the CBP-PAE film was measured using the pressure difference method, and its oxygen transmission rate was only 2.951 ± 0.177 cm³·m⁻² (24 h·0.1 MPa), which is much lower than that of PVA46, a common matrix for RTP polymer materials. Both the network size and the low oxygen transmission rate of the epoxy resin indicate that it provides a dense, three-dimensional rigid environment for intense RTP emission.

It is well known that temperature usually affects the luminescence of the organic dyes. In comparison to fluorescence, phosphorescence exhibits a longer lifetime, is time-resolved, and has a higher signal-to-noise ratio47. Therefore, utilizing the sensitivity of phosphorescence molecules to temperature can characterize the thermal physical parameters of a range of polymer materials, such as the characteristic temperature Tg, topology freezing transition temperature (Tv)48–50. In order to investigate the temperature dependence of phosphorescence behavior from dye-doped epoxy resins, phosphorescence photographs were recorded for CBP-SAE/GAE/PAE films at varying temperatures and the intensity of phosphorescence at different temperatures was plotted (Fig. 3a, b). In the case of CBP-PAE, for example, as the temperature increased, the phosphorescence intensity decreased in a nearly linear fashion. When the temperature exceeded 160 °C, the phosphorescence intensity dropped sharply to zero. Phosphorescence transition temperature (Tp) was obtained using the tangent midpoint method (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, we found that Tp values obtained for this system corresponds to the characteristic temperature Tg values of the epoxy resin (Fig. 3c). According to the glass transition theory51,52, when the cross-linked polymer was in the glassy state, the polymer chain and the intermolecular voids (free volume) were frozen, and the size and distribution of the free volume remain essentially fixed. Therefore, the dense three-dimensional network could effectively suppress the chromophore motions (i.e., rotation, vibration, torsion, and bending). The energy of the triplet excited state was annihilated through radiation decay, and thus phosphorescence was emitted efficiently53,54. Nonetheless, with increasing temperature, the space provided by the free volume began to be insufficient for conformational adjustment of the polymer chain. When the temperature surpasses the Tg of polymer and the polymer enters the rubber state, the free volume begins to expand, prompting the transition of polymer chain from a frozen state to a motion state. Consequently, the restraining effect of the cross-linked network on chromophore gradually diminished, thereby strengthening the non-radiative decay and leading to a reduction in phosphorescence intensity55.

To study the general applicability of the phosphorescence transition temperature Tp, the temperature dependence of phosphorescence behavior from CBP-doped epoxy resins (SAE, GAE, and PAE) with different Tg values (38 °C, 108 °C, 150 °C) were investigated. The CBP-doped SAE, GAE, and PAE films all exhibited distinct Tp values (42 °C, 105 °C, 143 °C) during heating (Supplementary Fig. 15). All these transition temperatures aligned well with the Tg values obtained via differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). To further demonstrate the generalizability of this strategy, the same epoxy resins (PAE) doped with different chromophores were all subjected to stepwise heating, and CBP-PAE, TMP-PAE, NPB-PAE, and PyB-PAE films all showed similar Tp values of 143 °C, 140 °C, 139 °C, and 138 °C, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 16). Hereby, the phosphorescence transition temperature Tp depended on Tg of the cross-linked polymer. This strategy exploited the temperature dependence of the Tg of the polymer matrices to modulate the phosphorescence of the chromophores, providing a wider range of application scenarios for polymer phosphorescence materials.

Notably, even at a high temperature of 150 °C, the CBP-PAE film exhibited an afterglow of 0.1 sec, visible to the naked eye (Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Movie 5). This capability enables the potential application of phosphorescent materials in high-temperature environments, such as fire rescue, heat detection, and other scenarios. Upon cooling to room temperature, the phosphorescence intensity of CBP-PAE returned to its initial level, demonstrating excellent reproducibility over multiple temperature fluctuation cycles (Supplementary Fig. 18). The CBP-PAE phosphorescent material also exhibited strong thermal resistance, maintaining stable room-temperature phosphorescence intensity even after prolonged exposure to high temperatures (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20). Thus, the phosphorescent emission of dye-doped epoxy resins shows strong resistance to high temperatures, confirming the feasibility of constructing high-temperature phosphorescent materials using high-Tg, rigid cross-linked polymers.

Furthermore, alterations in the free volume within cross-linked polymers are influenced not only by temperature but also by mechanical stress56,57. According to free volume theory, increasing compressive stress reduces the motion of polymer chains and decreases free volume. To assess whether mechanical stress positively affects phosphorescence emission at high temperatures, we monitored the phosphorescence behavior of CBP-doped epoxy resins (SAE, GAE, and PAE) after applying mechanical stress at temperatures above Tg. First, the circular pressing rod and the CBP-PAE film were preheated together on a thermostatic hot plate for 15 min (Tp + 20 °C). The pressing rod was then used to apply constant compressive stress to the sample surface for 20 s, after which it was rapidly unloaded. Phosphorescence emission was recorded at various time intervals (with 1 s UV-365 nm excitation every 2 s) after removing the pressing rod. As shown in Fig. 4a, phosphorescence emission was markedly triggered in the compressed region of the CBP-SAE, GAE, and PAE films, representing a “turn-on” phosphorescence response to mechanical compression. To avoid the effects of temperature, the temperature distribution of the CBP-PAE sample during the mechanical compression phosphorescence process was recorded using an infrared thermography camera (Supplementary Fig. 21 and Supplementary Movie 6). The infrared thermography images showed a uniform temperature distribution on the sample surface before the stress was applied and after the pressing rod was withdrawn, thus eliminating the effect of temperature on the phosphorescence during the mechanical compression phosphorescence process. The compression enhanced phosphorescence intensity of the SAE film was weaker than that of GAE and PAE at 275 kPa, with GAE and PAE showing similar intensities. To explain these observations, stress relaxation curves and creep experiments were conducted at the compression-enhanced phosphorescence temperatures. The characteristic relaxation times and deformation recovery rates supported these findings (Supplementary Fig. 22). Furthermore, the phosphorescence spectra under mechanical compression remained consistent with the initial room-temperature spectra, indicating that compressive stress did not alter the phosphorescent properties of the chromophore (Supplementary Fig. 23). Initial measurements were conducted to determine the stress response threshold for mechanical compression phosphorescence (Fig. 4b). Within the range of 11-275 kPa, a positive correlation was observed between phosphorescence intensity and compressive stress. A compressive stress of only 11 kPa was sufficient to increase the phosphorescence intensity fivefold, making this material significantly more sensitive to mechanical stimuli than typical fluorescence-force responsive molecules, such as rhodamine lactones (375 MPa)58.

At the same time, this mechanical compression phosphorescence did not disappear immediately by removing the compressive stress, the enhanced phosphorescence areas could be maintained for about 25.5 s (F = 175 kPa), during which the phosphorescence intensity gradually decreased (Fig. 4c). According to the Kelvin model, if a constant stress was applied at t = 0 s, a rate-varying deformation occurred, and after the stress was unloaded, the material did not rebound instantaneously, but the strain gradually returned with time56,59. Presumably, from the free-volume theory, the residual strain after compression led to a smaller lattice size between the cross-linked points, the free volume inside the cross-linked polymer was reduced, which increased the binding effect on the chromophores and inhibited their non-radiative decay60. The mechanical compression phosphorescence could be sustained for some time until the residual strain returns recover. To demonstrate the residual stresses and deformation mechanisms in the polymer system after the application of compressive stress, we further designed experiments of photoelasticity. When a material was deformed elastically under stress, the material exhibited optical anisotropy and macroscopic birefringence effects. The birefringence effect caused by the deformation was called photoelasticity (Fig. 4d)61. Photoelasticity was used to establish a standard experimental method to obtain the stress distribution in structures subjected to mechanical loads. This method was non-contact, non-destructive and could effectively monitor the stress distribution in structures with complex geometries and complex loading conditions, especially to measure the stress distribution in materials with residual stresses. By applying the stress in the vertical material direction and then removing the stress rapidly, it could be observed that the PAE stored residual stress after removal of the load and gradually recovered within 26 s (F = 175 kPa), and the residual stress release time was comparable to the duration scale of mechanical compression phosphorescence (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 24). When the applied stress on the sample was maintained for only a few seconds and then quickly released, no residual stress was observed. (Supplementary Movie 7). PAE films doped with different chromophores TMP, NPB and PyB all showed mechanical compression phosphorescence phenomenon, thus demonstrating the general applicability of the mechanical compression phosphorescence strategy (Supplementary Fig. 25).

Furthermore, the prevailing mechanical phosphorescence response strategies typically entail the induction of mechanical-chemical/physical transformations in luminescent molecules through the external force, which subsequently alters the phosphorescence intensity or phosphorescence emission wavelengths. However, most of these strategies are typically achieved through destructive techniques, such as grinding. As illustrated in Fig. 4f, this mechanical compression phosphorescence strategy was non-destructive, allowed for continuous cyclic enhancement of the phosphorescence, and did not necessitate complex recovery methods, such as heat treatment and fumigation. Following 12 cycles of mechanical compression phosphorescence, the intensity of mechanical compression phosphorescence remained constant. In addition, patterned mechanical compression phosphorescence could be readily attained by altering the shape of the pressing rod. Patterned phosphorescence offered discerning coverage, encompassing letters and Chinese characters (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 26, Movie 8-13). This mechanical compression phosphorescence was of great significance for the development of mechanically responsive phosphorescence materials, and provided an important strategy for the application of stimulus-responsive phosphorescence materials in the fields of anti-counterfeiting, sensing and detection.

Compared to molecular crystals, polymer systems, especially epoxy resin systems, were easy to manufacture with their excellent casting and molding capabilities and their solvent-free and environmentally friendly62,63. Leveraging the temperature-dependent luminescence and the distinctive phenomenon of mechanical compression phosphorescence at high temperatures, rendered them highly promising for advanced applications in data encryption and anti-counterfeiting measures. Consequently, the coupled temperature and mechanical response properties of epoxy resin-based RTP material could provide a variety of encoding procedures for data encryption. The numbers anti-counterfeiting information were consisting of CBP-PAE, TMP-PAE, NPB-PAE, and CBP-GAE. When UV-365 nm excitation was turned off, it presented the multi-colored information “8”. Over time, it only presented “3”. It was also possible to change the information “8” to “6” by increasing the temperature to obtain a second anti-counterfeiting information (Supplementary Fig. 27). By harnessing the temperature dependence, a plethora of diverse anti-counterfeiting applications were effortlessly achieved (Supplementary Fig. 28). In particular, since the presence of ester bonds and free hydroxyl groups in the epoxy resin, a dynamic transesterification reaction could be activated under the transesterification catalyst triazobicyclodecene (TBD), facilitating interfacial fusion to assemble epoxy resins with different Tg. Firstly, the TMP-PAE film was crushed to powder, then mixed into the NPB-GAE prepolymer solution, and poured in the heart-shaped mold to cure. During the curing process, high temperature and TBD catalysis activated the transesterification reaction, achieving the interfacial fusion of TMP-PAE and NPB-GAE, resulting in heart-shaped samples with TMP-PAE randomly distributed. Due to the different Tg of PAE and GAE, the temperature distributions of their phosphorescence intensities were different, and demonstrates the random phosphorescence emission (Supplementary Fig. 29).

Inspired by the mechanical compression phosphorescence feature, the “Mid-Autumn Moon Viewing” model was successfully constructed as an example of triple time-heat-force anti-counterfeiting demonstration (Fig. 5a). Firstly, prepolymer solution with different hardeners and organic chromophores: NPB-GAE, TMP-PAE, CBP-PAE and PyB-PAE were dripped into the molds, and the cured films could not be observed in different colors without UV-365 nm excitation (Supplementary Fig. 30). However, after UV-365 nm excitation, the “Mid-Autumn Moon Viewing” demo immediately changed into a colored pattern: the Jade Rabbit viewing a full moon against a red flower. Meanwhile, the pattern changed with time due to the difference of phosphorescence afterglows of NPB-GAE, TMP-PAE, CBP-PAE, and PyB-PAE, with the red flowers disappearing firstly, followed by the moon gradually becoming darker until finally only the pattern of the Jade Hare remains (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, due to the distinct Tg of GAE and PAE, their luminescence pattern changed with temperature increasing, thereby contributing an additional dimension to the demo (Fig. 5c). Importantly, with the mechanical compression phosphorescence characteristics, patterned phosphorescence could be obtained in localized areas. As shown in Fig. 5d, the phosphorescence in the moon region was extinguished at 130 °C. At this juncture, the pressing rod with crescent-shaped and circular were employed to compress the moon region of the sample, respectively. After taking off the pressing rod, crescent-shaped and full-moon shaped phosphorescence were enhanced on the originally darkened region, thereby demonstrating the third mechanical compression phosphorescence pattern (Supplementary Movie 14 and 15). A similar demonstration of triple time-heat-force anti-counterfeiting were shown in Supplementary Fig. 31. This patterned phosphorescence anti-counterfeiting strategy integrated on three scales of time, temperature, and mechanical force was distinctive, and suggested an important strategy for multiple anti-counterfeiting as well as sensing applications at high temperature environments.

Discussion

In summary, we had developed the dye-doped cross-linked epoxy resin materials with sensitive mechanical compression patterned phosphorescence. Impressively, the doped polymers showed both full-color room-temperature phosphorescence and high-temperature phosphorescence with ultralong lifetimes. It was found that the phosphorescence intensity and afterglow of the systems could be modulated by the glass transition temperature (Tg) of the cross-linked epoxy resin matrices. Additionally, in the rubbery state (Tp + 20 °C), even a low stress (11 kPa) could significantly obtain the mechanical compression phosphorescence, and the phosphorescence intensity showing a quantitative response to the compression stress. This mechanical compression phosphorescence method was non-destructive, reversible, and patternable, offering promise for the development of mechanically responsive smart phosphorescence materials. Finally, we successfully constructed a time-heat-force triple anti-counterfeiting demonstration of “Mid-Autumn Moon Viewing”, which holds potential applications in sensing, anti-counterfeiting, and imaging fields at high temperature environments.

Methods

Materials

The regents used in the experiments including bisphenol A diglycidyl ether resin (DGEBA, 85%), hexahydro-4-methylphthalic anhydride (Me-HHPA, PA, 98%), glutaric anhydride (GA, 98%), sebacic acid (SA, 99%), 1,5,7-triazabicyclo[4.4.0]dec-5-ene (TBD, 98%), 4,4'-bis(N-carbazolyl)-1,1'-biphenyl (CBP, 99%), N,N,N',N'-tetramethylbenzidine (TMP, 98%), N,N'-bis-(1-naphthalenyl)-N,N'-bis-phenyl-(1,1’-biphenyl)-4,4'-diamine (NPB, 98%), 1-pyrenylboronic acid (PyB, 98%) was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI Shanghai, China) chemicals. All chemicals were used as received.

General procedures for the preparation of RTP films

The epoxy resins (PAE, GAE, SAE) we used here are cross-linked polymers that are prepared by reacting DGEBA with hardeners (PA, GA, SA) in the presence of TBD as transesterification catalyst at 80 °C for curing 8 h, 120 °C for curing 4 h and 180 °C for curing 2 h. The epoxy resins-based RTP films are prepared as following procedures: 10 mg organic dyes, such as CBP, TMP, NPB and PyB, were added and dissolved in 1.00 g DGEBA with vigorous stirring, the mixtures were heated to 80 °C then the hardener (0.58 g SA, 0.34 g GA, 0.50 g PA) was added and kept stirring to obtain phase miscibility. The mixture was cured in a mold at 80 °C for 8 h, 120 °C for 4 h and 180 °C for 2 h. The diameter of circular samples was 20 or 10 mm, the thickness was 2 mm (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The tangent midpoint method

The procedure for determining the phosphorescence transition temperature using the tangent midpoint method is as follows:

Draw tangents: On the phosphorescence intensity curve, draw tangents at the points immediately before the transition begins and immediately after it completes. These two tangents represent the slopes of the baseline before and after the transition.

Determine the midpoint: Establish the phosphorescence transition temperature as the midpoint between the temperatures at which the two tangents intersect the curve.
