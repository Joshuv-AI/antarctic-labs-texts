Gut microbiota profiling in Lebanese ulcerative colitis patients and healthy controls from a pilot study
Authors: Fayez Yassine, Hassan Abbas, Abdallah Kurdi, Reem Mansour, Fadi H. Mourad, Ghassan Matar, Melhem Bilen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the colon, associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis. While global studies have explored this link, region-specific microbial profiles remain underreported. This pilot study aimed to characterize and compare, for the first time, the gut microbiota of Lebanese UC patients and healthy controls using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing (V3–V4 region). Fecal samples from 11 UC patients and 11 healthy individuals were analyzed. Alpha and beta diversity metrics were computed, and gut microbial composition was assessed across taxonomic levels. Statistical comparisons used Mann-Whitney and Fisher’s exact tests. UC patients showed significantly reduced microbial diversity based on Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity and Shannon index (p < 0.05), though evenness was unaffected. Beta diversity also revealed significant group-level dissimilarities (p < 0.05). At the phylum level, Bacteroidota was elevated in UC, while Bacillota and Actinomycetota were reduced. Genera such as Ruminococcus, Bacteroides, and Coprococcus were depleted in UC. Faecalibacterium, commonly reduced in UC, showed no significant difference. This first analysis of gut microbiota in Lebanese UC patients reveals a distinct microbial signature that partially diverges from global trends, supporting the need for region-specific microbiome studies and personalized microbiota-targeted therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31435-x.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is one of the main forms of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a chronic condition that has become a global health issue in recent decades1. Unlike Crohn’s disease, UC is an idiopathic inflammatory disorder confined to the colonic mucosa2. Both disorders are characterized by abdominal pain, diarrhea, rectal bleeding, weight loss, fatigue, and an overall significant impact on quality of life3. The pathogenesis of UC is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, environmental influences, immune dysregulation, and gut microbial dysbiosis3,4. In UC, an exaggerated immune response (often Th2-mediated) to commensal gut microbes drives chronic colonic inflammation5.

The gut microbiota is the community of microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, archaea) residing along the gastrointestinal tract, more specifically the colon6,7. A healthy gut microbiota plays a key role in host physiology: it modulates both the innate and adaptive immune responses within the gut8, protects against pathogenic colonization by competing for nutrients and adhesion sites9, and preserves epithelial barrier integrity10. Furthermore, it produces vital metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which serve as energy sources for colonocytes and have anti-inflammatory effects11. Imbalances in gut microbial composition, or dysbiosis, have been implicated not only in IBD but also in metabolic and neuropsychiatric disorders7.

In UC, dysbiosis often manifests as reduced diversity and stability of the gut microbiome, particularly with decreases in Firmicutes (Bacillota) and Bacteroidetes (Bacteroidota) and dysbiotic alterations in both Proteobacteria (Pseudomonadota) and Actinobacteria (Actinomycetota)12,13. Beneficial anaerobes (Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus) tend to decline, whereas facultative pathobionts (Escherichia and Enterococci) often expand under inflammatory conditions, taking advantage of the dysregulated intestinal microenvironment14. These taxa are not inherently pathogenic but can exhibit pro-inflammatory behavior when the gut barrier and immune regulation are compromised. Of particular interest is Faecalibacterium, a major SCFA producer, whose depletion is linked to UC pathogenesis through the loss of butyrate-mediated anti-inflammatory effects15. The reduction of SCFA-producing genera, such as Faecalibacterium and Roseburia, diminishes anti-inflammatory metabolites, compromising epithelial barrier integrity and promoting inflammation16.

While dysbiosis in UC has been well documented globally, regional and environmental factors, including diet, antibiotic use, sanitation, and smoking play a critical role in shaping the gut microbiota and its host interactions17. For instance, populations adhering to a Mediterranean diet rich in plant-based foods and fermented dairy products have been linked to greater gut microbial diversity and enrichment of SCFA-producing genera18.

This pilot study aims to characterize and compare the gut microbial composition of Lebanese patients with UC and healthy controls (HS). Lebanon’s population presents a unique opportunity for such research due its distinct Mediterranean dietary habits, diverse genetic background, and high exposure to both Western and traditional lifestyles, all of which may influence the gut microbiome. Our goals were to identify differences in alpha and beta diversity, and to determine which bacterial phyla, genera and species differ between UC and healthy cohort in Lebanon.

As the first study of its kind in Lebanon, it provides preliminary data (n = 22), for designing larger, longitudinal investigations. By generating a region-specific microbial profile for UC, we seek to advance understanding of disease- associated dysbiosis and inform future microbiome-based interventions.

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC) under approval number BIO-2024-0066 and Bio-2023-0244. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection, in accordance with ethical guidelines for human research. All methods were performed in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

Study cohort and sample collection

A total of 22 stool samples were collected, including 11 patients diagnosed with UC at AUBMC and 11 from healthy individuals. Samples were collected in sterile, airtight containers equipped with BD GasPak™ EZ Anaerobe Container System sachets (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) to maintain anaerobic conditions during transport, following standard anaerobic handling procedures described for stool microbiota preservation19,20. Approximately 200 mg of stool was collected per participant to standardize input for DNA extraction. Immediately after collection, samples were mixed with a cryopreservation solution composed of Columbia Broth (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK), glycerol (50% v/v; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), glutathione (Sigma-Aldrich), and uric acid (Sigma-Aldrich) to preserve microbial viability during freezing, following previously described protocols for anaerobic microbiota preservation19–21 The homogenized samples were aliquoted into sterile cryovials and stored at − 80 °C until downstream processing.

Exclusion and inclusion criteria

For the UC cohort, participants were eligible for inclusion if they were between 18 and 65 years of age, had an established diagnosis of UC confirmed by both endoscopic and histopathologic findings, were being followed at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC), and were residing in Lebanon. Exclusion criteria included a history of colonic cancer, ongoing chemotherapy for malignancy, use of antibiotics within the preceding 12 weeks, or a history of intestinal resection.

As for healthy individuals, adult volunteers aged 18 to 65 years, both male and female, who are healthy, with no known gastrointestinal disorders, chronic illnesses, or recent antibiotic use within the past six months were included. Healthy controls did not undergo endoscopic evaluation or UC activity scoring, as these measures are only relevant to patients with confirmed ulcerative colitis.

Eligibility was verified at enrollment by study staff using a standardized checklist derived from the IRB-approved protocol, including medical chart review for UC diagnosis (endoscopic and histopathologic confirmation at AUBMC) and recent medication/antibiotic history, and a brief health questionnaire for healthy controls. Participants not meeting any single criterion (including antibiotic exposure within the specified windows) were excluded.

Clinical and lifestyle data collection

Clinical and lifestyle data were collected through structured interviews and review of medical records. Immunosuppressants included biologic and systemic agents used for ulcerative colitis management, namely infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, tofacitinib, and prednisone. Psychiatric medications referred to antidepressant and anxiolytic agents, specifically cipralex (escitalopram), paroxetine, and sederal.

Smoking status was categorized based on self-reported daily cigarette use as follows: light smokers (< 3 cigarettes/day), moderate smokers (3–9 cigarettes/day), and heavy smokers (≥ 10 cigarettes/day). Alcohol consumption was classified according to weekly intake: light drinkers (1–2 drinks/week), moderate drinkers (3–5 drinks/week), and heavy drinkers (> 5 drinks/week).

Additionally, dietary habits were assessed using a short, self-reported lifestyle questionnaire. Participants were classified as following a Mediterranean diet if they reported regular consumption of fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and olive oil, moderate intake of fish and poultry, and limited consumption of red and processed meats. This self-reported measure was used to capture habitual dietary patterns rather than enforce a standardized diet. A restricted diet was defined as one that excluded one or more major food groups.

Metagenomic analysis

DNA extraction and quantification

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Qiagen PowerFecal DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was eluted in nuclease-free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored at − 20 °C until further analysis. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

16 S rRNA V3–V4 gene sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

We used amplicon sequencing of the 16 S rRNA V3–V4 region on fecal samples to profile microbial diversity and composition. 16 S rRNA V3–V4 libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions using the Quick-16 S™ Plus NGS Library Prep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) and Illumina 16 S Metagenomic Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Library concentrations were quantified using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), normalized, pooled, and sequenced on the MiSeq® platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The primers included Illumina adapter overhang sequences: forward (5’-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and reverse (5’-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC).

Bioinformatic analysis

Sequence Quality Control and Denoising Raw paired‑end reads were first processed to remove Illumina adapters using TrimGalore (a wrapper for Cutadapt) under default settings with automatic adapter detection. Trimmed reads were then imported into QIIME2 (version 2025.4) and subjected to denoising and error correction using the DADA2 plugin. Primer sequences were removed, forward and reverse reads were merged, and chimeric sequences were identified and removed. The resulting output consisted of a feature table of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and their representative sequences. Taxonomic Assignment was assigned to each ASV using the QIIME2 feature‑classifier plugin with a naïve Bayes classifier pre‑trained on the Greengenes 16 S rRNA gene database (updated 2024.09). Classification was performed with a confidence threshold of 0.7, resulting in taxonomic labels spanning from phylum to species (where possible) for each ASV. Diversity Analyses All diversity metrics were calculated within QIIME2 using a core‑metrics‑phylogenetic workflow. For measuring diversity, samples were rarefied to an even sequencing depth of 10,000 reads per sample to control for differences in sampling effort. Alpha and beta diversities were calculated using the “Alpha” and “Beta” plugins. Kruskal-Wallis statistical test was used and Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR), resulting in q-values that are the FDR-corrected p-values.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 and R (accessed via DataLab). Differences in demographic data between the UC and healthy cohorts were evaluated using the independent-samples t-test. Given the compositional and non-normal distribution of phyla, genus and species abundance data, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for group comparisons. All data was unpaired, and results were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Visualization of microbial composition was performed using R, including stacked bar graphs of genus- and phylum-level abundances and a heatmap of genus-level data. Categorical variables (sex, dietary adherence, smoking status, and alcohol consumption) were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, while the continuous variables age and BMI were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. These analyses were selected based on their suitability for small-sample, non-normally distributed microbiome data, ensuring appropriate statistical inference. A total of 22 participants were enrolled, including 11 ulcerative colitis (UC) patients and 11 healthy controls, all Lebanese origin. Demographic, clinical, and lifestyle characteristics, including sex, age, BMI, medication use, diet, smoking, and alcohol consumption, were recorded for all participants. A “restricted diet” was defined as the exclusion of one or more major food groups for medical or personal health reasons. Overall, no significant differences were observed between the UC and healthy cohorts, indicating comparable baseline characteristics across groups. Additional details are presented in Table 1.

Assessment of alpha and beta diversity in UC and healthy samples

Community structure and diversity were assessed through alpha diversity metrics, beginning with the Evenness index, which evaluates the uniformity of taxa distribution across samples (Fig. 1a). Although evenness appeared lower in the UC cohort compared to healthy samples, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.45), indicating that the proportional distribution of species was largely preserved. Phylogenetic richness was assessed using Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity index, which revealed a significantly lower diversity in the UC group (p = 0.000093), reflecting a notable reduction in the evolutionary breadth of microbial taxa (Fig. 1b). The Shannon Diversity Index further confirmed a significant decrease in microbial diversity among patients with UC relative to healthy samples (p = 0.0011), capturing both diminished richness and evenness (Fig. 1c). As well, ACE, Chao1 and Simpson indices demonstrated statistically significant differences (P < 0.05), indicating reduced richness and phylogenetic diversity in the UC group (Fig. 1d, e & f).

Beta diversity was assessed using unweighted UniFrac distances, which evaluate differences in gut microbial community composition based on the presence or absence of taxa (Fig. 1g & h). The analysis revealed significantly distinct microbial communities between the UC and healthy samples. This was supported by PERMANOVA testing (pseudo-F = 3.83, p = 0.002), indicating that the intergroup variation in microbial composition was greater than intragroup variation.

Differences in the gut microbiota composition among patients with UC compared to the healthy samples

At the phylum level, notable differences in gut microbial composition were observed between the UC and healthy samples, Bacillota and Bacteroidota were the most dominant phyla across both cohorts, though their relative abundances varied substantially between individuals, followed by Actinomycetota as the third most abundant phylum (Fig. 2). At the phylum level, the relative abundance profiles appeared broadly comparable across healthy individuals, whereas UC samples showed more heterogeneous patterns across participants (Fig. 2). However, these observations are descriptive and not based on diversity metrics. In particularly in the proportions of Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota, and Verrucomicrobiota. The phylum Bacillota was slightly reduced in the UC cohort compared to the healthy samples (78% vs. 80%), as was Actinomycetota (3.5% vs. 6.3%). In contrast, Bacteroidota was elevated in the UC group (16.6% vs. 12.4%). Accordingly, the Bacillota-to-Bacteroidota ratio was lower in the UC cohort (4.44) compared to the healthy samples (6.82). Among phyla entirely absent from one of the cohorts, Camplylobacterota was not detected in the healthy samples, while Methanobacteriota was absent from the UC cohort.

Regarding statistically significant differences, only two low-abundance phyla, Cyanobacteriota (p = 0.0481), and Methanobacteriota (p = 0.0351) differed significantly between cohorts, with the two showing higher abundance in the healthy samples. The rest of the phyla including, Actinomycetota, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Camplylobacterota, Desulfobacterota, Elusimicrobiota, Fusobacteriota, Pseudomonadota, and Verrucomicrobiota, did not exhibit any changes between the two cohorts.

At the genus level, analysis of the top 30 most abundant genera revealed five that were significantly different between the UC and healthy cohorts: Ruminococcus, Faecousia, Vescimonas, Bacteroides, and Coprococcus (Fig. 3a-e). Notably, all of these genera were significantly more abundant in the healthy samples. Among other key genera examined, Faecalibacterium, Blautia, and Prevotella did not exhibit statistically significant differences in abundance between the UC and healthy samples (Fig. 3f- h).

Among the top genera identified across both UC and healthy samples were Blautia, Faecalibacterium, Prevotella, Ruminococcus, and Agathobacter, which consistently appeared in high abundance across samples (Fig. 4) and were confirmed as dominant genera in the average relative abundance profiles (Fig. 5).

Several species showed significant differences in abundances between UC and healthy samples. Peptostreptococcaceae_256921 exceeded 5,000 reads in the UC cohort but was significantly lower in the healthy samples (Fig. 6b). In contrast, both unclassified Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcus_E sp003438075 were elevated in the healthy samples compared to the UC cohort (Fig. 6c&d). Other notable species of interest, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, were found at comparable levels in both cohorts (Fig. 6a). Species level read counts are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Discussion

This pilot study provides the first characterization of gut microbiota in Lebanese patients with UC compared to healthy samples. We observed significant reductions in gut microbial diversity (alpha), as well as distinct community composition (beta), confirming that UC is associated with gut dysbiosis in this population. Specifically, patients with UC exhibited lower phylogenetic richness (Faith’s PD) and lower Shannon diversity, while evenness was unchanged.

This pattern suggests that UC-related dysbiosis is driven by depletion of specific evolutionary distinct taxa, particularly SCFA producers, rather than a uniform decline across all microbial groups22. Beta diversity analysis further supported these findings, highlighting significant differences in overall gut microbial community composition between the UC and healthy samples. This indicates that UC pathogenesis is associated with a significant alteration in gut microbial composition, with the UC cohort harbouring a gut microbial profile that is distinct from that of the healthy individuals. These differences are thought to be driven by chronic mucosal inflammation23, epithelial barrier dysfunction24, and environmental factors such as diet or medication use25,26.

At the phyla level, Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Actinomycetota dominated both groups (~ 96% of reads). Interestingly, Proteobacteria remained consistently low across the entire UC cohort, a finding that contrasts the existing literature which frequently associates their increase with gut dysbiosis in UC and other morbidities27.

At the genus level, several taxa of biological relevance differed significantly between the UC and the healthy cohorts, while others remained unchanged. One of the key genera found to be significantly depleted in the UC cohort included Ruminococcus. Ruminococcus is a gram-positive anaerobic bacterium belonging to the phylum Bacillota28. They are known SCFA producers that play a critical role in the transformation of complex polysaccharides into nutrients available for the host29. This genus employs intricate enzyme assemblies known as cellulosomes and amylosomes capable of breaking down and digesting complex carbohydrate molecules to generate SCFAs29. Other key genera, such as Bacteroides and Coprococcus, were also depleted in the UC cohort, consistent with previous reports documenting the loss of SCFA-producing bacteria in ulcerative colitis30–32. SCFAs, such as acetate, butyrate and propionate, are essential biomolecules produced by bacterial fermentation22. SCFA are small organic monocarboxylic acids that improve gut health through a number of local effects, ranging from the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity, mucus production, and protection against inflammation33. SCFAs modulate inflammation by inhibiting histone deacetylases and upregulating heat shock protein expression, both of which contribute to the enhancement of intestinal barrier integrity34. Overall, the significant reduction of SCFAs or their microbial producers likely contributes to the pathogenesis of UC, as reflected in the findings of this study22. Faecousia and Vescimonas were also reduced, though their metabolic and immunological roles remain poorly defined given their recent identification in gut metagenomic datasets35.

Surprisingly, Faecalibacterium, a key biological genus, did not differ significantly between Lebanese patients with UC and the healthy samples36. Faecalibacterium is a strict anaerobe considered to be a ubiquitous member of the gastrointestinal tract and one of the key producers of butyrate36. Faecalibacterium displays a crucial role in producing energy for colonocytes as well as the production of anti-inflammatory metabolites that enhance intestinal health41. This genus performs its role through prebiotic fermentation generating SCFA41.

This finding diverges from global data, including cohorts from China37, Poland38, Egypt39, and Belgium40, which consistently report reduced Faecalibacterium abundance in UC41. For example, an Egyptian cohort (n = 48 UC; n = 48 controls) observed markedly reduced F. prausnitzii in UC, with partial restoration after treatment, consistent with loss of butyrate producers in active disease39. Similarly, an Iranian mucosa-associated study (n = 40 UC; n = 40 controls) reported significantly lower mucosal F. prausnitzii in UC patients compared to controls [48]. In contrast, our Lebanese fecal cohort maintained comparable Faecalibacterium levels between groups. These discrepancies may reflect differences in sampling compartment (fecal vs. mucosal), dietary patterns, disease activity, or genetic background, emphasizing that the relationship between F. prausnitzii and intestinal inflammation is likely context dependent. This finding reinforces the importance of population-specific microbiome profiling when designing microbiota-targeted interventions, particularly in regions like the Levant where data remain scarce.

The lack of Faecalibacterium depletion in this Lebanese cohort may reflect Mediterranean-diet exposure, disease- and treatment-related heterogeneity, and strain-level or functional variation not captured by 16 S profiling; confirmation in larger, shotgun-based studies is warranted.

At finer taxonomic resolution, certain unclassified species showed striking group-specific patterns. The notable enrichment of Unclassified Peptostreptococcaceae_256921 in the UC cohort suggests this taxon may have a potential role in disease progression or maintenance, potentially through its interaction with gut mucosa or modulation of inflammatory pathways. Its presence, though at lower abundance in the healthy cohort, indicates that a threshold effect might exist, where disease manifestation depends on exceeding a specific abundance.

Conversely, the predominance of Unclassified Lachnospiraceae in the healthy samples aligns with previous literature identifying Lachnospiraceae family members as beneficial microbes due to their butyrate-producing capabilities, a vital metabolite for maintaining gut epithelial integrity36. Similarly, Ruminococcus_E sp003438075 showed higher relative abundance in the healthy samples compared with UC. Members of the Ruminococcus genus are recognized as key degraders of resistant starches and contributors to short-chain fatty acid production, particularly butyrate, which supports mucosal integrity and exerts anti-inflammatory effects37,38. The depletion of this taxon in UC may therefore reflect disrupted carbohydrate fermentation and reduced butyrate availability, potentially contributing to epithelial barrier dysfunction and inflammation. Their reduced abundance in UC could reflect disrupted metabolic function associated with disease progression.

The similar abundance of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii in both UC and the healthy samples is intriguing, given its well-documented anti-inflammatory properties39. This observation might imply that mere presence or abundance is not solely indicative of functional activity or protective capability. Other factors, such as strain-specific differences or functional gene expression levels, might significantly influence the organism’s anti-inflammatory potential. This point is further underscored by the presence of three UC patients with a Mayo score of 3, indicative of severe endoscopic disease, who nonetheless maintained Faecalibacterium levels comparable to those of the healthy cohort40. This underscores the need to go beyond taxonomic profiling by incorporating functional assays and exploring additional bacterial taxa that may contribute to UC pathogenesis.

The absence of a significant change in Faecalibacterium abundance in the Lebanese cohort highlights the need for more personalized intervention for IBD management, including gut microbiota-based interventions such as bacteriotherapy41. Our findings raise the possibility that interventional studies might consider population specific variables such as diet, genetics, and the environment when designing gut microbiome-based therapies42. Given the known link between Mediterranean diet and SCFA producer abundance, future work should formally assess dietary patterns alongside gut microbiome profiling to test this hypothesis18. The lack of depletion in the Lebanese cohort differs with the literature, underscoring the importance of context-specific gut microbiome data guide therapeutic development.

Should bacteriotherapy approaches be pursued in this population, they may benefit from the integration of region-specific dietary patterns and gut microbiome profiles, pending validation in controlled studies.

Beyond sequencing-based techniques, culturing the fastidious gut microbiota remains critical, particularly for the unclassified or poorly characterized taxa. Many organisms detected by 16 s rRNA remain uncultured, limiting the ability to study their molecular characteristics, interactions, and potential immunomodulatory roles in UC43. Culturing these organisms provides access to viable isolates that can be characterized functionally through in vitro and in vivo studies44. This is essential for identifying candidate strains involved in disease pathogenesis and for the development of targeted bacteriotherapy45.

Given the small cohort size (11 UC and 11 controls), this study should be viewed as a pilot effort aimed at detecting broad gut microbial patterns rather than making population-level generalizations. Nevertheless, the significant alpha and beta diversity differences observed indicate that the sample size was adequate to identify major compositional shifts. These data provide essential groundwork for larger follow-up studies with greater statistical power.

Despite these findings, this study still has several limitations. The small sample size restricts the ability to generalize the findings on a larger scale, as this was designed as pilot study to establish baseline data within this population. The limited sample size (n = 22) inherently restricts the statistical power to detect subtle microbial differences or associations with clinical variables. This limitation is realistic for a pilot study, where the primary aim is to assess feasibility and generate preliminary data. As a pilot study constrained by facility availability, data was combined from two libraryprep- pipelines. Future work should employ a single standardized protocol to avoid potential batch effects. Moreover, 16 s rRNA sequencing offers limited resolution at the species level and does not capture gut microbial functional capacity or metabolite output46. Additionally, the study did not include mycobiome or virome profiling, which represents an important limitation as these microbial communities may also influence gut ecosystem dynamics. In addition, while the 16 S rRNA gene remains a robust and widely adopted marker for microbial community profiling, the use of short-read sequencing targeting the V3–V4 region inherently limits taxonomic resolution at the species level. Closely related taxa within genera often share nearly identical V3–V4 sequences, leading to potential misclassification. Therefore, the species-level identifications presented in this study should be regarded as putative rather than confirmative. These assignments were retained to provide preliminary insights into community structure, but all primary analyses and interpretations were performed at the genus level. Future investigations employing full-length 16 S rRNA sequencing or shotgun metagenomics will be required to confirm species-level identities and elucidate strain-specific functional differences. Additionally, as recruitment was limited to a single tertiary medical center (AUBMC) and relied on volunteer healthy controls, potential selection bias cannot be excluded. All inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied uniformly through medical record review and standardized health questionnaires, but future multi-center population-based cohorts will be needed to reduce referral and lifestyle-related bias.

In conclusion, our study conducted on the UC and healthy Lebanese population allowed us to reveal gut microbial dynamics, composition, and key alterations between the two cohorts. These findings indicate a significant reduction in gut microbial diversity within the UC cohort, accompanied by the selective depletion of certain SCFA–producing taxa, with other key ones relatively preserved contradicting some of the reported literature results. This pilot data suggests that bacteriotherapy approaches could benefit from incorporating regional gut microbiome profiles and dietary habits, but clinical efficacy remains to be established. While no firm therapeutic conclusions can be drawn from this pilot, the observed region-specific microbial trends, such as the relative preservation of certain SCFA-producing taxa, highlight the potential of personalized microbiota modulation strategies in Lebanese UC patients. Future research integrating metagenomic, metabolomic, and dietary data will be crucial to identify actionable microbial targets and design culturally and regionally adapted microbiota-based therapies.
