Accuracy of nurses’ Caprini risk assessment for venous thromboembolism: a simulation-based observational study in China
Authors: Yunting Luo, E Zheng, Yanling Chen, Mei Feng, Lingxiao He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk assessment using the Caprini Risk Score (CRS) is crucial for identifying high-risk patients and initiating preventive measures. While nurses are the primary assessors in many clinical settings, the complexity of the CRS poses significant challenges to implementation. Limited evidence exists regarding the actual precision of nurses’ scoring and the specific sources of error, which can directly compromise patient safety. This study aimed to evaluate nurses’ accuracy in applying the CRS and to identify factors and item domains associated with scoring errors.MethodsThis observational, single-center descriptive study was conducted in 2023 across eight inpatient wards at a tertiary hospital in Southwest China. A total of 213 nurses from internal medicine (including infectious diseases, rheumatology and immunology, communicable diseases, pain management, respiratory medicine, and comprehensive respiratory care) and surgical departments (thoracic surgery and ICU) performed CRS evaluations using standardized simulated cases.ResultsAmong the 213 participants, only 45 (21.13%) achieved full scoring accuracy. Chi-square tests revealed significant differences (P < 0.05) in accuracy based on experience, professional titles, and departments. Nurses with ≤ 5 years of experience showed the lowest accuracy (5.32%), while those with junior titles achieved 13.74%. Accuracy differed across departments, with rheumatology/immunology and the ICU showing lower accuracy than other wards (P < 0.05). Accuracy rates for key item domains - functional status, past medical history, and other high-risk factors were 43.66%, 40.85%, and 43.19%, respectively.ConclusionsNurses with ≤ 5 years of experience and junior titles require targeted VTE assessment training. Overall low accuracy indicates a risk of misclassification that may affect thromboprophylaxis decision-making. Enhanced training and clearer, culturally appropriate wording of the CRS translation, particularly for commonly confused items, are needed. Nursing managers should implement competency-focused training and quality monitoring to improve the reliability of CRS-based risk assessment.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04618-8.Keywords: Venous thromboembolism, Caprini risk score, Nursing assessment, Simulation training

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among hospitalized patients [1]. Hospital-associated VTE (HA‑VTE) is common and largely preventable; therefore, timely and accurate risk assessment is essential to guide prophylaxis and prevent adverse outcomes [2–7]. While diagnostic tools such as venography and Doppler ultrasound are important, routine prevention relies primarily on structured risk assessment because systematic screening with imaging is often impractical due to resource constraints [8–10].

With growing recognition of HA-VTE prevention’s clinical value and cost-effectiveness, clinical practice has seen the widespread adoption of risk assessment models. Among the more than 23 models developed, the Caprini Risk Score (CRS) stands out as one of the most widely adopted tools [11]. The CRS is a weighted scoring system based on individual risk factors and has been robustly validated in more than 250,000 patients across over 100 clinical trials globally. Extensive studies have demonstrated an exponential increase in VTE incidence corresponding to higher scores [12, 13]. Since its inception in the 1980s, the CRS has undergone multiple revisions [12, 14].These updates provide clarification but may also create implementation hurdles when older versions, local translations, or ambiguous item interpretations coexist in clinical use.

Research has affirmed the predictive efficacy of the CRS concerning VTE occurrence and recurrence among hospitalized patients in China [15–17]. Furthermore, studies have shown that implementing appropriate risk-stratified prophylaxis based on CRS results effectively reduces VTE incidence without increasing major bleeding risks, thereby minimizing preventable VTE complications [18–22]. Consequently, the CRS has been widely integrated into clinical practice across various healthcare institutions.

Despite its widespread use, accurate CRS application hinges on the correct interpretation of numerous, often dynamic, risk factors, and errors can lead to under- or over-prophylaxis [14, 23]. Current research, however, has mainly examined clinicians’ knowledge, attitudes, and self-efficacy, or reported inter-provider consistency in scoring, rather than directly assessing scoring accuracy in simulated clinical scenarios. Systematic reviews have identified knowledge gaps, insufficient understanding, and low self-efficacy among nurses regarding VTE risk assessment [24]. For instance, a survey of hospital nurses reported high confidence in educating patients and families about VTE symptoms, prevention, and treatment. However, their overall understanding of VTE risk assessment was moderate, and they reported the lowest confidence in performing the comprehensive risk assessment itself [25]. Similarly, a survey involving physicians, nurses, midwives, and physiotherapists found that only 49.8% of healthcare professionals were satisfied with their knowledge of VTE prevention. Moreover, nurses were less likely than physicians to hold positive attitudes toward VTE prevention in hospitalized patients [26].A recent retrospective study in China reported significant discrepancies between physicians and nurses in VTE risk assessments, which underscores the persistent challenge of ensuring consistent application of standardized assessment tools [27]. This highlights a critical gap: a lack of evidence regarding the true scoring accuracy of the CRS in practice and the specific factors that contribute to errors.

This gap is particularly relevant in China because VTE risk assessment in many hospitals is routinely performed by nurses as part of standardized inpatient workflows, making nurses’ scoring accuracy critical for evidence-based prophylaxis. Therefore, this study evaluates Chinese nurses’ accuracy in applying the CRS using standardized simulated cases, and identifies factors and item domains associated with errors to inform targeted training and potential tool/translation optimization, ultimately aiming to improve risk classification and patient outcomes.

Methods

Design

This cross-sectional, simulation-based observational assessment was conducted in 2023 across eight inpatient wards within a unit of a tertiary hospital in Southwest China. The wards included internal medicine departments (infectious diseases, rheumatology and immunology, communicable diseases, pain management, respiratory medicine, and comprehensive respiratory care) and surgical departments (thoracic surgery and intensive care unit [ICU]). The study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [28].

Data collection and participants

A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit participants because participation depended on staffing availability during on-site shift handovers and access to the study wards within the project period. Inclusion criteria included nurses who had worked in the selected wards for over a month, including staff nurse, standardized training nurse, short-term trainee nurse and intern nurses.

While all participating nurses receive standardized, hospital-wide training on the Caprini risk assessment model during onboarding and as part of annual competency reviews, the frequency of department-specific reinforcement and intensity of daily practical application may vary. Before entering clinical wards, intern nurses are required to complete standardized pre-clinical training and pass institutional qualification assessments covering basic nursing knowledge, clinical safety, and guideline-based risk assessment, which ensures a minimum level of clinical competency. Importantly, all nurses in this hospital undergo the same institutional onboarding training and follow uniform VTE risk assessment guidelines, and no department-specific VTE assessment training is implemented. Short-term trainees and interns were included because they participate in routine clinical care and are common targets for educational interventions; their perspectives are therefore integral to capturing real-world accuracy across departments.

The “≤5 years of experience” category intentionally included both early-career registered nurses and intern nurses completing their final year of clinical rotation. This inclusion was deliberate, as interns are actively involved in patient care under supervision, and their performance provides a critical baseline for the competency of individuals entering the profession. Assessing this group helps to identify gaps that may originate from academic training versus those that develop during early professional practice. Exclusion criteria included nurses who were unable to participate in on-site data collection.

Based on previous studies reporting a VTE assessment accuracy rate of 9.84% [29], we calculated the required sample size using the formula for cross-sectional surveys [30]. An absolute error (E) of 5% and a significance level (α) of 5% were set for the calculation. Our analysis determined that a minimum of 172 participants is necessary for this study to achieve sufficient statistical power while accounting for potential non-responses. This ensures the reliability of the results.

In November 2023, standardized simulated cases were distributed to participants during morning shift handovers (see Supplementary Material 1). Completion was required within 30 min, and responses were submitted electronically via an online questionnaire platform (Questionnaire Star). Demographic and professional characteristics were collected via the same online questionnaire (self-report). The assessment tool used was hospital-implemented 2013 Chinese translated version of the Caprini Risk Assessment Scale, which allows for multiple selections of risk factors and automatically calculates the weighted Caprini score (see Supplementary Material 2). We did not identify published validity/reliability metrics specific to this 2013 Chinese translated version; therefore, our analyses focus on scoring accuracy against a guideline-based reference standard rather than scale psychometrics.

The standard case was collaboratively designed by experienced clinical nursing experts with postgraduate education and over 20 years of surgical nursing experience, proficient in evidence-based practices. The case involved a 54-year-old male patient with complex medical conditions, including severe pneumonia, sepsis, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), type II respiratory failure, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and other comorbidities (See Supplementary Material 1). Participants were required to evaluate the patient using the CRS and determine the correct total score based on the provided clinical information. To ensure content validity, the expert panel reviewed the case for clinical realism and alignment with current Caprini guidelines, which also served as the reference standard for scoring [15]. The instrument was therefore designed as a performance-based assessment of scoring accuracy, not as a psychometric scale, and as such, traditional psychometric validation was not applicable.

To align with routine handover workflows, a 30-minute limit was chosen for Caprini score calculation. Although the actual per-patient scoring time is typically shorter, the 30-minute window was provided as a practical upper bound to accommodate variability in nurse workflow. This limit was determined through informal discussions with ward staff and a small pilot timing exercise conducted before data collection. The possibility of time-pressure bias is acknowledged.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the frequency and percentage of correct selections for each Caprini risk factor. The primary outcome was defined as full scoring accuracy (accurate vs. inaccurate). Although multivariable logistic regression was considered, it was not performed because only 45 participants achieved accurate scoring and several subgroup cells were sparse or zero, which would likely yield unstable estimates. Therefore, the analyses were limited to bivariate comparisons and should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory. A participant’s response was defined as “accurate” only if they simultaneously selected all applicable risk factors and excluded all non-applicable ones, resulting in a final calculated score of exactly 10. Any deviation, such as missing a correct item, selecting an incorrect item, or both, resulted in the response being categorized as “incorrect,” even if the final score coincidentally equaled 10 through offsetting errors. We also conducted secondary analyses on the distribution of total scores to assess underestimation/overestimation and performed item-level error analysis to identify specific areas of confusion. Overestimation and underestimation rates represented the proportions of participants whose assessed scores were higher or lower than the standardized score. Error rates were calculated as the percentage of participants who incorrectly selected each item.

Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were utilized, as appropriate, to compare evaluation results among nurses with different characteristics to determine statistically significant differences between groups. Fisher’s exact test was applied when the expected frequencies in the contingency tables were less than 5. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Rigor

To ensure rigor, the answers to the simulated case were strictly established according to the latest Caprini guidelines. During the assessment, participants were gathered in a designated area and instructed to complete the evaluation individually within 30 min without communicating with one another. They were assured that the results would not affect subsequent personal or departmental evaluations.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2023 − 1521) and conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. The anonymity of the nurses was maintained throughout the study, and all data were kept confidential. The simulated case referenced multiple clinical scenarios and involved no real patient privacy information. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection, and nurses who declined to provide consent were excluded from the study population. All participants volunteered to participate in this study.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 220 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding incomplete responses and duplicates, 213 nurses completed the survey (response rate: 96.81%). Among the 213 participants, only 45 (21.13%) achieved full scoring accuracy. While a score of ≥ 5 indicates “extremely high risk,” the majority of participants (108/213, 50.70%) underestimated the total score (< 10), suggesting that specific risk factors were frequently missed. Conversely, 60 (28.17%) participants overestimated the score. Notably, one participant scored below 5, failing to categorize the patient into the correct high-risk tier.

Table 1 presents the full scoring accuracy rates across different demographic characteristics. Comparisons were based on bivariate analyses, without adjustment for potential confounders such as clinical experience or department, and no effect sizes were calculated. Because no a priori hypothesis regarding linear or ordinal trends across experience level, professional title, or department was specified, formal tests for trend were not performed. The analyses were intended to be descriptive and exploratory. Significant disparities were observed based on clinical experience, professional titles, and departments. The relationship between years of experience and full scoring accuracy was found to be non-linear. Notably, participants with 6–9 years of experience demonstrated the highest accuracy rate (15/34, 44.12%), outperforming all other groups, including the most senior nurses. In contrast, the group with ≤ 5 years of experience had the lowest accuracy (5/94, 5.32%), while nurses with more than 15 years of experience achieved a high, but comparatively lower, accuracy rate (14/46, 30.43%) than the mid-career group. Similarly, nurses with Senior or Intermediate titles significantly outperformed those with Junior titles or no titles (P < 0.001). Departmental performance varied widely (P < 0.001): the Pain Management (8/16, 50.00%) and Infectious Disease (10/25, 40.00%) wards achieved the highest accuracy, whereas the Rheumatology/Immunology (0/28, 0.00%) and ICU (2/29, 6.90%) departments demonstrated the most significant gaps in assessment accuracy. Given the relatively large sample size, statistically significant p-values reflect group-level differences but should not be interpreted as indicators of effect magnitude. However, some subgroup sample sizes were small, and these results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Proportions of incorrect scoring by risk factor categories

In the functional status category, overestimation was common. Although the correct answers included “lower limb edema” and “restricted bed rest (> 72 hours)” with a risk score of 3 points, 27.23% of participants either selected additional incorrect risk factors or over-scored. For the medical history category, underestimation predominated; 33.8% of participants failed to select either “pulmonary dysfunction (e.g., COPD)” or “severe pulmonary disease (within 1 month).” Regarding other high-risk factors, the correct risk factor was “smoking,” which scored 1 point, yet 54.93% of participants overestimated this category.

Common causes of errors

Further analysis of the primary risk factors contributing to the low accuracy rate revealed specific areas where participants commonly made errors in Table 3. Regarding general information, 44.60% of participants incorrectly selected “bedridden medical patients” as a risk factor. Regarding medical history, 74.65% of participants mistakenly identified “congestive heart failure” as applicable to the case despite it not being present. Regarding other high-risk factors, 43.66% of participants erroneously classified a blood transfusion as a current risk factor from two years prior. Additionally, 28.64% of participants included diabetes mellitus as a risk factor based solely on the diagnosis, without verifying insulin usage.

Discussion

Key findings and comparison with existing literature

The results of this study reveal a concerning gap in clinical assessment competency. The participants’ overall full scoring accuracy was disappointingly low. Accuracy rates also varied across departments, including thoracic surgery, although these comparisons were descriptive and not adjusted for potential confounders. These findings align with previous studies in China, such as a survey of surgical nurses, which reported a full scoring accuracy of only 9.84% in simulated cases [29]. To our knowledge, published studies outside China have largely evaluated VTE risk assessment tools in terms of predictive performance or have examined clinicians’ knowledge, attitudes, and adherence [15, 31]. Reports explicitly focusing on the correctness of the VTE assessment process itself are scarce, which limits direct international comparisons. It should be noted that departments with higher clinical exposure to VTE risk, such as ICU or surgical wards, did not necessarily demonstrate higher scoring accuracy, suggesting that increased exposure alone does not guarantee better adherence to standardized assessment tools.

Our data reveals a complex, non-linear relationship between experience and assessment accuracy. On one hand, the finding that nurses with ≤ 5 years of work experience and junior professional titles exhibited the lowest accuracy aligns with established developmental models, such as Benner’ s Novice to Expert nursing theory [32]. According to Benner, novice nurses rely heavily on rigid rules and lack the situational context to make complex clinical judgments. The low accuracy rates suggest that many participants are still operating at a “novice” or “advanced beginner” level regarding VTE assessment, struggling to apply theoretical knowledge to dynamic clinical scenarios. It is crucial to acknowledge that this junior cohort includes both early-career registered nurses and intern nurses and the underlying reasons for their errors may differ. For intern nurses, low accuracy likely reflects gaps in foundational university education and a lack of clinical exposure. For early-career registered nurses, errors may be more attributable to the challenges of adapting to high-pressure environments, workplace orientation shortcomings, or inconsistent mentorship. Therefore, our findings highlight a dual challenge: the need to strengthen VTE assessment within academic curricula and the necessity for robust, standardized onboarding and continuous training programs within the hospital to support the transition from student to professional.

On the other hand, a particularly interesting finding was that peak performance was observed not in the most senior group, but in nurses with 6–9 years of experience, creating an “inverted U-shaped” trend. This group may represent an optimal balance between sufficient clinical exposure and sustained engagement with standardized assessment tools, as they are experienced enough to recognize key risk factors while still relying on formal scoring systems rather than habitual clinical judgment alone. This may indicate a “sweet spot” where nurses have accumulated sufficient clinical experience to grasp assessment nuances but have not yet become so reliant on heuristics or “clinical gestalt” that they deviate from standardized protocols. The slight decline in accuracy among the most experienced nurses (> 15 years) might reflect a reduced engagement in direct, hands-on assessment tasks or a greater reliance on habit over strict adherence to the scoring tool. This complex pattern suggests that continuous training and adherence checks are crucial for nurses at all career stages, not just for novices.

Sources of error and limitations of the scale

A detailed analysis of response errors highlights conceptual confusion rather than simple carelessness. A significant 44.60% of participants incorrectly selected “Medical patient currently at bed rest,” confusing it with “Confined to a bed for 72 hours or more.” While translation ambiguities in the Chinese version of the Caprini scale contribute to this issue [33], the persistence of this error suggests a deeper lack of pathophysiological understanding regarding immobility and thrombosis risk. The distinction relies on the duration of immobility, yet participants failed to differentiate between short-term restriction and prolonged confinement.

Similar patterns of error were observed in medical history assessment. The high error rates in identifying “congestive heart failure” (74.65%) and distinguishing between “Abnormal pulmonary function” and “Serious lung disease” (33.80%) indicate a difficulty in synthesizing patient history with specific scale criteria. Furthermore, errors regarding “blood transfusion” and “diabetes requiring insulin” (43.66% and 28.64%, respectively) reveal a critical misunderstanding of the time-bound nature of risk factors (e.g., current vs. past history). These findings suggest that nurses are memorizing terms rather than understanding the clinical criteria defined in the 2013 updated Caprini instructions [12].

Technological aids and their limitations

To mitigate the subjectivity of manual assessments, recent studies have advocated for automatic scoring systems via electronic medical record (EMR) extraction and machine learning algorithms [34, 35]. A recent quality improvement project demonstrated that integrating the clinical assessment scale into the EMR for patients undergoing non-oncologic gynecologic surgery significantly improved the appropriate use of VTE chemoprophylaxis [36]. Studies have shown that the Caprini score has good predictive performance for VTE, and using machine learning algorithms can further enhance predictive capabilities, achieving dynamic and precise risk prediction [37]. However, one study indicated that when using EMRs alone for clinical assessment scale, key VTE risk factors such as personal or family history of VTE or miscarriage history may be missed compared to face-to-face assessments, leading to underestimation of VTE risk [38]. Regardless of the accuracy and convenience achieved by VTE risk assessment systems based on EMR extraction, they still heavily rely on the records within the EMR system. Missing specific risk factors not explicitly mentioned in the records can affect the accuracy of VTE assessments.

Another potential solution is to incorporate patient self-reported Caprini assessments, which have shown promising validity across different cultures and languages [39–41]. This assessment method requires patients to have basic literacy and comprehension skills and a clear understanding of their medical conditions. Current research findings are inconsistent. Some studies suggest that patients take less time to complete the patient version than nurses, with good item-by-item consistency between patient and nurse completions [42]. However, another direct comparison between patient- and physician-completed Caprini scores indicated that patients could not reliably calculate their own 2005 Caprini scores, and relying solely on patient completion may lead to inappropriate prophylactic strategies for some patients [43]. Beyond limitations in basic literacy, cultural factors may also impact the accuracy of patient self-assessments. For example, in the Chinese healthcare environment, influenced by Confucianism, family consent often supersedes individual informed consent, meaning that families play a significant role in healthcare decisions, sometimes replacing patient decisions. This can make some patients unaware of their medical conditions, making accurate self-assessment difficult [44].

Therefore, technological tools should be viewed as adjuncts rather than replacements for professional nursing assessment. The integration of technology is most effective when combined with well-trained nurses who can critically appraise automated outputs and validate them against clinical judgment. From a workflow perspective, EMR-based prompts could be embedded into the routine admission or transfer assessment process and designed to require active nurse confirmation of each key risk factor rather than automatic acceptance of prepopulated results. Such tools may be most feasible when first piloted in departments with lower accuracy and then refined based on user feedback before wider implementation.

Implications for nursing education and practice

The findings of this study underscore an urgent need to shift educational strategies. Merely distributing updated scale versions is insufficient. Training programs must move beyond rote memorization and employ case-based learning and simulation training to bridge the gap between theory and practice. Drawing on Benner’ s framework, education should focus on helping nurses transition from rule-governed behavior to intuitive, experience-based judgment. In practice, such training could be incorporated into existing nurse onboarding programs and annual competency assessments, thereby minimizing disruption to routine clinical work. Given the particularly low accuracy observed among nurses with ≤ 5 years of experience, additional refresher sessions or supervised practice modules could be prioritized for early-career nurses and other lower-performing groups.

Additionally, although the primary deficit is competency, the scale itself requires refinement. Clarifying ambiguous items—specifically the distinction between “bed rest” and “confined to bed”—within the Chinese translation is a necessary structural improvement to reduce cognitive load on nurses. A feasible short-term approach would be to incorporate these clarifications into local hospital training materials, bedside quick-reference tools, or standardized scoring checklists while awaiting broader revision of the translated scale. This could help reinforce consistent interpretation during routine assessments without requiring major system-level changes.

Summary

In summary, this study demonstrates that nurses’ accuracy in applying the Caprini scale is suboptimal, reflecting broader challenges in clinical competency rather than isolated issues of scale design or translation. Enhancing nurses’ understanding of VTE risk factors through structured education, experiential learning, and competency-based training is essential. While technological innovations offer valuable support, the cornerstone of safe and effective VTE prevention remains the development of skilled, knowledgeable, and clinically competent nursing professionals. To improve feasibility in clinical settings, these strategies should be integrated into existing workflows, such as onboarding, annual competency review, routine EMR documentation, and targeted refresher training for departments or groups with lower scoring accuracy.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations due to funding and site constraints. First, a convenience sampling method was used, and the participating nurses were primarily from internal medicine departments in a tertiary hospital. This non-probability sampling structure limits the representativeness of the sample and may introduce selection bias. Consequently, our findings may not be generalizable to nurses in secondary hospitals or community health settings where resources and training protocols differ. Second, while simulation offers a controlled environment, it may not fully reflect real-time clinical reasoning under high-pressure conditions. In this study, nurses were assessed in a controlled, relatively stress-free setting; however, in clinical wards the CRS is often completed under time pressure, interruptions, and workload constraints, which may influence scoring accuracy and error patterns. The Hawthorne effect or social desirability bias might have influenced participants to perform better or differently than they would in daily practice simply because they knew they were being tested. Third, the use of a single standardized case restricts the assessment of the full spectrum of clinical scenarios; different cases might reveal distinct patterns of error. This study also used the 2013 Chinese translated version of the CRS, and this version is widely used in local practice, subtle differences in translation, wording, and item interpretation may affect scoring consistency and may partially explain some of the observed errors. Although the assessment relies on established clinical guidelines, the specific case vignette and questionnaire developed for this study did not undergo a formal content validation process, which may limit the instrument’s verified validity. Fourth, all assessment sessions were conducted on the same day during on-site shift handovers. Participants completed the assessment individually, and explicit instructions were given to prohibit discussion or collaboration during the session. This arrangement reduced the likelihood of information sharing between participants; however, informal communication outside the assessment setting cannot be entirely excluded. Fifth, although nurses across departments received standardized hospital-wide training, this study did not adjust for potential confounding factors such as differences in years of experience or department-specific training reinforcement, which may partly explain the observed departmental differences in accuracy. In addition, although logistic regression was considered, the current sample was not well suited for reliable multivariable modelling because of the limited number of accurate cases, sparse subgroup data, and the absence of several literature-based predictors. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Finally, we did not evaluate the correlation between assessment accuracy and actual patient outcomes (e.g., VTE incidence), which limits the direct clinical impact assessment of our findings.

Future directions

Based on the identified limitations and findings, future research should focus on several key areas. First, because nurses with ≤ 5 years of experience showed the lowest accuracy, longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the long-term retention of competency following simulation-based training, particularly when integrated into onboarding and early-career training programs. Second, because accuracy was relatively low in key item domains such as functional status, past medical history, and other high-risk factors, future studies should develop and validate item-level training modules and decision-support tools tailored to these commonly mis-scored items. Third, because lower accuracy was observed in certain departments, particularly rheumatology/immunology and the ICU, scenario-based simulations should be designed around the clinical situations most likely to generate errors in these settings. Finally, multi-center studies involving diverse hospital tiers are essential to validate these findings and support the development of a more universally applicable training curriculum.

Conclusions

This study delineates specific, modifiable deficiencies in the precision of nursing assessments, particularly among nurses with ≤ 5 years of experience. The overall accuracy of clinical nurses in applying the Caprini risk score was low, driven largely by conceptual misunderstandings of specific items rather than random errors. These findings underscore that effective VTE prevention relies on the nurse’s ability to correctly interpret clinical criteria. To bridge this gap, we advocate: (1) compulsory simulation-based CRS training for early-career nurses; (2) revision of the Chinese Caprini tool in accordance with the 2013 guidelines to improve clarity; and (3) incorporation of electronic medical record (EMR) prompts to mitigate documentation errors.
