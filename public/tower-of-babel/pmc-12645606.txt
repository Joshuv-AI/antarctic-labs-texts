Digital technology and teaching methods in nursing education: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Carolin Barthel, Fabian Montigel, Stefan Jobst, Vera Antonia Büchner, Christiane Kugler, Sandra Strube-Lahmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionIn a rapidly evolving world shaped by digital transformation, digital technologies are increasingly being used in healthcare. To use them effectively, nursing staff, patients and their caregivers need the appropriate skills. To date, digital literacy in general, digital skills and (digital) technologies have played a minor role in German nursing curricula. In addition, little is known about the use of technology in nursing education and about effective ways to enhance digital competence. This scoping review aims to identify current literature on innovative and experiential-based teaching and learning methods for developing competence in technology and digitalisation for nurses and informal caregivers, as well as digital technologies that are being used in this educational context.Methods and analysisThis scoping review, which will be designed and conceptualised using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for conducting scoping reviews, will take place from April 2025 to January 2026. The search will be conducted on the databases CINAHL, Emcare, Embase, Medline and ERIC. Screening and extraction will be peer reviewed and follow predefined criteria, as well as opportunities for free-text responses. The extracted data will be analysed using absolute and relative frequencies. Free-text responses will be analysed using qualitative content Analysis.Ethics and disseminationAs an ethics vote is not required to conduct a scoping review, this will not be obtained. The results will be disseminated via open access publication in a peer-reviewed journal and through presentations at conferences.Keywords: Digital Technology, Nursing research, MEDICAL EDUCATION & TRAINING, Review

Introduction

Technological innovations in healthcare

Digital technology and digital innovations in healthcare—for definitions, see table 1—are recognised as having great potential to support the care process, from acute inpatient care to home care and long-term care. Therefore, it is necessary to adapt teaching programmes in the field of nursing education to reflect the changes in nursing care and its tasks brought on by digitalisation.1 In the adult educational setting, digital media and technologies have become an integral part of learning, highlighting the importance of the promotion of digital literacy.2 Use-related training of digital knowledge and related skills should not be limited to the mere operation and use of specific technologies, but should also promote a broad understanding of digital technologies. It should aim for nurses to be able to navigate the dynamic and diverse digital world in a flexible and solution-oriented way, while meeting the resulting challenges, such as ethical requirements or fundamental changes in nursing-related work processes.1,3 The current framework curricula for nursing education in Germany provide some guidance on the development of technical skills (Specialist commission in accordance with § 53 of the Act on the Nursing Professions (Pflegeberufegesetz 2020)), but these remain largely unspecific and do not provide clear guidelines. As a result, the weighting given to digital skills is left to the sole discretion of the German educational institutions and is incorporated into the curricula in a correspondingly heterogeneous manner.4 However, those educational institutions are often insufficiently prepared for these much-needed changes, both in terms of their digital equipment and the digital skills of their faculty.5 6 To prepare nurses for the opportunities and challenges of technological innovation and digital media in the care process, (clinical) nurse educators must review existing teaching methods for their suitability for learning digital processes and may need to develop new ways of teaching. Digital media and technologies should be specifically integrated into programme design to promote a more effective and user-friendly teaching and learning process. At the same time, the needs and opportunities of an ever-changing nursing work environment need to be adequately addressed.7

Technological innovations in healthcare innovations

Nursing education has already incorporated digital transformation. The use of smartphones, educational videos, podcasts, apps, gamification and virtual reality (VR) is described in current research, as Szara and Klukow8 show in a recent literature review. They highlight the possibilities and potential of student-centred and self-directed learning by using digital innovations. In addition, many studies focus on VR because it allows for complete immersion in a realistic scenario, making it an effective and attractive option for the learning process.9 10 Hill11 emphasises the value of experiential learning for the clinical competence of nursing students. It is here that technological innovation, for example, can provide important support.12 The COVID-19 pandemic has significantly advanced research in the field of digital support and has an impact on students with geographical restrictions by offering flexibility, for example, the provision of materials and tools at individual times, and by opening up new possibilities, such as virtual simulations.9 13

Didactical framework

The following section outlines the didactic framework employed in this study. As there is no widely accepted definition of the didactic concepts employed, these are listed in table 1. The original definitions were translated from German using DeepL Translate14 and can be found in online supplemental file 1. Notably, all of the listed terms are rooted in specific cultural contexts, which emphasises the difficulty in translating terms used in teaching and learning into different languages.15 The scientific term ‘didactics’ comes from the tradition of theorising classroom learning and has its origins mainly in the German-speaking and Scandinavian countries, as well as in Russia. Based on this background, didactic models are understood as theoretical frameworks that comprehensively analyse and explain teaching and learning, based on scientific positions.16 In contrast, teaching methods offer practical guidance for didactic action by linking decisions about objectives, content and methods and by providing specific recommendations for the design of lessons.17 Rooted in recommendations by federal experts and curricular standards as well as framed by constructivist didactics, this study pursues an approach of action-orientation based on enabling didactics and experiential learning according to Kolb.18,20 Kolb’s concept of experiential learning has been established internationally, with its principles being embraced by educators and practitioners worldwide. It can be defined as ‘the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience’. (Kolb, p 38)20 However, next to Kolb’s experiential learning, Arnold’s activity-based teaching and learning approach is also widely recognised in German-speaking countries.19 21 As there are clear links between experiential learning and action-oriented teaching and learning,21 both concepts will be taken into account in this study. The terms are henceforth summarised under experiential learning.

Research gap

Despite several studies that explicitly address didactic approaches and experiential-based learning in relation to the use of digital innovations in nursing education,12 there is a lack of literature reviews that comprehensively compile these findings.22 Furthermore, there remains a critical knowledge gap regarding how digital teaching methods can be systematically aligned with experiential-based learning theories18 20 and which technologies fit best for an experiential-based teaching and learning in nursing education. Therefore, a literature review is needed to synthesise and systematise the most recent advanced and digital teaching methods from the perspective of an experiential-based teaching and learning approach. With this in mind, as well as extensive and diverse research conducted in recent years, a scoping review (ScR) was planned. The aim of this study is to identify current literature on innovative and experiential-based teaching and learning methods for developing competence in technology and digitalisation for nurses, patients and informal caregivers, as well as digital technologies that are being used in this educational context.

Research questions

The study will address the following research question: Which innovative and experiential-based teaching and learning methods are used in educational programmes for adults in the context of digitalisation in nursing? In order to provide a more detailed exploration of the research question, the study employed two distinct subquestions:

Methods and analysis

Study design

A ScR will be carried out to answer the research questions. This type of evidence synthesis is being used when existing literature is extensive and heterogeneous in order to provide an overview of the existing evidence.23 24 The development of the research questions and the search strategy was guided by the structure of the ‘Population, Concept and Context (PCC)’ mnemonic,25 26 as displayed in table 2.

Conduct of the ScR will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI) ScR methodology27 and will follow the best practice guidance and reporting items for the development of ScR protocols by Peters et al.28 The systematic review software Covidence29 will be used to manage the research process starting at the removal of duplicates through to data extraction.

The interdisciplinary review team consists of seven reviewers (CB, FM, KS, VAB, AU, SN, SS-L), of which five have a nursing degree and different additional training in nursing or healthcare education (CB, VAB, KS, SS-L), nursing science (CB, FM) and health economics (VAB, KS). FM, SJ and SS-L have previously been involved in designing and conducting a systematic literature review. The team members are affiliated with various institutions across different academic centres throughout Germany.

Eligibility criteria

This ScR will include records written in English or German and published since January 2015 that have undergone a publication process resulting in a persistent identifier (e. g. Digital Object Identifier (DOI)). This includes, among others, study reports, dissertations, conference papers and philosophical or opinion articles. Grey literature without a persistent identifier, as well as books or book chapters, are excluded from this ScR. Further criteria developed according to the PCC mnemonic25 26 are listed below.

Population

Records will be included in this ScR if they address teaching methods for adults, specifically registered nurses, nurses in training, (clinical) nurse educators, patients or their informal caregivers. Interprofessional studies that examine other healthcare professions in addition to nursing are also considered in this review.

Concept

Records will be included in this ScR if they cover teaching methods that are both experiential-based and innovative. If a teaching method is the subject of the record, at least one of the following three elements has to be described according to Oelke and Meyer’s17 definition of teaching methods: Aim, content and/or specific method of the educational offer. Furthermore, to ensure an experiential-based learning approach, at least one of the following five criteria has to be fulfilled: Self-paced, productive, activating, situated and/or social. These criteria are based on the ‘Fun-Dimensions of a Sustainable and Vibrant Learning’ by Arnold,18 which define eligible action-oriented teaching and learning methods. Whether a teaching method deemed innovative will be assessed in alignment with the operationalisation of innovation based on the explanations of Hauschild, Terstriep and Kaschny.30,32

Context

Records will be included in this ScR if they either (a) use innovative (digital) technology as the medium of the teaching method or if they (b) describe a teaching method used to enhance digital competency. The above-mentioned operationalisation of ‘innovative’ also applies here.

Search strategy

The overall search strategy consists of an individualised keyword and subject heading search in each of the above databases. The search strategy used for CINAHL can be found in online supplemental file 2. It was developed by four members of the research team and was guided by another colleague with pedagogical expertise to ensure correct subject headings, keywords and their synonyms of teaching methods. A librarian assisted in structuring the process of the development of the search string. To develop the search string, appropriate subject headings were first identified for the three concepts of the PCC-mnemonic (P: nursing, patients and their informal caregivers, C: experiential-based teaching and learning methods, C: digitalisation and technology; see table 2). Iterative CINAHL searches were then carried out and refined accordingly. The next step was to develop a keyword-based search string, based on the subject headings and their synonyms. Finally, based on the (CINAHL) subject heading search string, a MeSH-term search string (MEDLINE) and a thesaurus search string (ERIC, Embase) was developed, and the keyword search string was transferred to different databases using wildcards and truncations.

The search will be carried out in April 2025 in five databases: CINAHL, Emcare, Embase, Medline and ERIC. CINAHL and ERIC will be searched using the EBSCO search engine, while Medline, Embase and Emcare will be searched using the Ovid search engine. The following limiters will be applied in the search when applicable: English or German language, abstract available and publication between January 2015 and March 2025.

Selection process

Identified records will be imported into Covidence.29 First, duplicates will be identified and removed. A pilot test of the screening process was carried out using four core papers and the data extraction criteria were adjusted accordingly. Prior to screening, all reviewers will be briefed to ensure a common understanding of the eligibility criteria. The first step in the study selection process is to screen the title and abstract of all records and assess their eligibility as ‘Yes’—‘Maybe’—‘No’ by four reviewers. This step is to be conducted from April to May 2025. In the second step of the review, after all records have been screened, those marked ‘Yes’ and ‘Maybe’ are subjected to a full-text review. This step will be conducted by six reviewers and is to be conducted from June 2025 to August 2025. After the full-text review, the final records included in the review are identified. These are subjected to structured extraction using predefined criteria. Each record has to be examined by two reviewers in each step of the review (screening, full-text review, extraction). In case of disagreement, a third reviewer decides on the inclusion of a record.

Data extraction

Each full-text record included in this review will be subject to data extraction, which will be conducted by six reviewers. This step is to be conducted from September to November 2025. A critical appraisal of the included records will not be performed in line with the JBI recommendations.27 Data extraction will be carried out using a data extraction form. This form was developed by CB, FM and SJ and was pilot-tested by the review team using the identified core papers to ensure comprehensibility. The extraction form can be found in online supplemental file 3. Data to be extracted are depicted in the following.

Bibliographic information

The following bibliographic information will be extracted using free text fields: title, first author’s name, year of publication and country in which the study was conducted. In addition, the format of the publication, its methodology (if applicable) and the sample size (if applicable) are extracted using single choice response categories. Furthermore, the ethical considerations stated in the records will be extracted. At the end of the extraction protocol, there is an additional option for ‘further comments’.

Population

In terms of the population, both the recipients and the setting are extracted using single-choice response categories. In the case of an interprofessional study, each profession included should be named using a free-text option, for example, physicians or midwives.

Concept

With regard to the teaching method and/or the (digital) technology described, the aim of its use is extracted using multiple choice answer categories. In addition, the teaching method is extracted in more detail using the ‘Fun-Dimensions of a Sustainable and Vibrant Learning’ by Arnold,18 mentioned above, using multiple-choice answer categories.

Context

The (digital) technology described is extracted in more detail using the classification of digital educational media by Reich and Reich33 with multiple choice answer categories. If a technology found in the literature is not covered by the existing categories, an additional option of ‘further comments’ is provided.

Data analysis and presentation of results

Bibliographic data, as well as data regarding population, concept and context, will be analysed using absolute and relative frequencies. Free-text responses within population, concept and context are analysed using Hsieh and Shannon’s34 Directed Content Analysis to form deductive-inductive categories. This step is to be conducted from December 2025 to January 2026. The results will be presented by (a) graphical visualisation of the review results and (b) narrative description of the formed categories. It is planned to complete the analysis by January 2026.

Discussion

An estimated number of 500–600 records will be identified via the database search and an estimated number of about 100–150 records will be included in this ScR. By analysing those records, this review will identify the digital technologies currently used in nursing education and the teaching methods used to enhance digital literacy. It will also show the extent to which the teaching methods identified lend themselves to constructivist didactics and experiential-based teaching and learning. Furthermore, it will provide insight into which population is currently most prominently addressed by the identified teaching methods and which population is in need of more research activity and training in digital literacy. Thereby, this review contributes to answering the question of how digital teaching methods in nursing education can be systematically aligned with experiential-based learning theories.

The results of this ScR can be used by (clinical) nurse educators to provide an overview of existing teaching methods and (digital) technologies in nursing education as well as to assist them in the conceptualisation of their pedagogical activities.

The study has the following limitations: No critical appraisal of the included studies will be carried out following the ScR recommendations, and the appropriateness of the teaching methods will not be assessed. Therefore, no well-founded assessment can be made of the appropriateness and quality of the identified teaching methods. Furthermore, no conclusions can be drawn about specific interventions or effects. This should be the focus of further research.

Ethics and dissemination

The researchers have committed themselves to the code of ethics of the German Society of Nursing Science.35 No ethical review board approval is required for the ScR, but best practice for conducting reviews suggested by Vergnes36 will be followed. Therefore, the ethical considerations of the included studies are part of the extraction protocol. This research will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed open access journal and presented at national and/or international conferences. The results of the ScR can also be made accessible to nurse educators by compiling a pool of methods in German and English. In the further course of the project, teaching and training concepts are also to be developed based on the results of the ScR in order to promote the use of digital technologies in nursing education.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design of this ScR protocol, as this ScR does not apply to any patient group. However, people with expertise in various areas of nursing education (clinical nursing education, vocational training and university-based training) were involved in the design of the ScR. The results of the ScR should be made available to nursing educators in order to strengthen the use of technology in nursing education and the technical competence of nurses. This can be done, for example, by providing a collection of methods.
