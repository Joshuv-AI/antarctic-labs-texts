Nanopore-based haplotype-resolved X chromosome inactivation for severity assessment in X-linked disorders: An AIFM1 study with a mosaic PDHA1 carrier
Authors: Kohta Nakamura, Atsuko Okazaki, Daisuke Motooka, Naoyuki Matsumoto, Yuiko Hasegawa, Sayaka Fukuda, Manami Yabe, Ayumu Sugiura, Yukiko Yatsuka, Takuya Fushimi, Takanori Onuki, Yuko Aida, Akira Ohtake, Kei Murayama, Yasushi Okazaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryX chromosome inactivation (XCI) modifies disease severity in females with X-linked variants, but clinically applicable high-resolution assessment remains limited. We report a family with an apoptosis-inducing factor, mitochondria-associated 1 (AIFM1) variant that shows marked intrafamilial phenotypic variability and evaluated whether haplotype-resolved nanopore sequencing can inform clinical interpretation. Targeted long-read sequencing was performed in a severely affected hemizygous male, his asymptomatic heterozygous mother, and a severely affected heterozygous sibling. In the hemizygous male, the sample served as a technical control, with all reads mapping to a single haplotype, consistent with a hemizygous X chromosome. Among heterozygous carriers with the identical variant (c.506C>T [p.Pro169Leu]), XCI correlated with severity: the affected sibling showed 84% skew favoring activation of the pathogenic allele, whereas the mother showed preferential inactivation (20%). This family-based study shows that using nanopore sequencing for haplotype-resolved XCI analysis may provide a practical framework for selected X-linked disorders with variable expressivity.

Introduction

X chromosome (chrX) inactivation (XCI) is a fundamental epigenetic process in female mammals that ensures dosage compensation by randomly silencing one of the two chrXs in each cell. Skewed XCI patterns, favoring inactivation of one parental chrX, are a well-established determinant of clinical severity in X-linked disorders, where higher methylation skew amplifies phenotypic expression of pathogenic variants on the active chrX.1

Traditional PCR-based XCI assessment targeting 1–2 CpG sites (e.g., androgen receptor gene) often lacks resolution and yields inconclusive results. This limitation is particularly problematic for X-linked mitochondrial genes such as AIFM1 (apoptosis-inducing factor, mitochondria-associated 1), for which precise XCI quantification is essential for severity prediction in heterozygous female carriers.2,3

Recent nanopore sequencing advances have improved XCI analysis in several contexts. Cas9-targeted enrichment has been used for AR and RP2 XCI assessment.4,5 Adaptive sampling has been applied to short tandem repeat (STR) and methylation diagnostics in neurological disease (FMR1),6 and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) has been used for haplotype-based preimplantation testing in Duchenne muscular dystrophy and for detecting episignatures in developmental disorders. Most recently, adaptive nanopore sequencing has been used to measure XCI skew in X-linked diseases.7,8

However, these studies have not addressed mitochondrial X-linked disorders (AIFM1/pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 alpha 1 subunit [PDHA1]) using family trios with identical variants to directly relate X-linked dosage and XCI skew to clinical severity across a hemizygous male and heterozygous female carriers (asymptomatic mothers and severely affected siblings). Here, we apply nanopore adaptive sampling in a family with the AIFM1 variant (c.506C>T [GenBank: NM_004208.4] [p.Pro169Leu]), establishing proof of concept for mitochondrial disease risk assessment through haplotype-resolved XCI analysis.

Subjects and methods

Sample collection and clinical phenotyping

Five genomic DNA samples were analyzed from two families. The AIFM1 family included one severely affected male proband (hemizygous), one severely affected female sibling, and their asymptomatic mother. The PDHA1 family included one severely affected female sibling and her asymptomatic mother. All genomic DNA samples used in this study were derived from peripheral blood, and XCI and methylation analyses were performed exclusively on peripheral blood-derived genomic DNA.

Phenotypes were coded using Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms; detailed mappings are provided in Table S1.

Mitochondrial respiratory chain enzyme activity and oxygen consumption rate measurement

Mitochondrial respiratory chain enzyme activities and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurements were performed according to previously reported protocols.9,10

Enzyme assays were performed using tissue samples from the liver, skeletal muscle, and cardiac muscle of the AIFM1-variant patient. In addition, mitochondrial enzyme activities were assessed in cultured skin fibroblasts. For oxygen consumption analyses, skin fibroblasts were examined using a Seahorse XF96 Analyzer under both glucose- and galactose-containing media. Basal and maximum respiration rates (MRRs) were quantified after substrate challenge as indicators of mitochondrial oxidative capacity.

The same protocol was applied to fibroblasts from the PDHA1-variant patient.

Basal respiration (basal OCR) was also decreased, with the MRR falling below the established cutoff of 71.6%.11

Genetic analysis

Filtering criteria

Variant filtering was performed using population databases (gnomAD v.4.0, ToMMo 14KJPN, BBJ-WGS, ClinVar, and dbSNP) with a minor-allele frequency (MAF) threshold of <0.5% for candidate variants. We focused on genes known or suspected to be associated with mitochondrial disease and evaluated segregation pattern and clinical/biochemical concordance with the presumed mode of inheritance (including X-linked transmission). For each family, we carefully reviewed all rare variants and excluded those inconsistent with the phenotype or inheritance pattern.

AIFM1 male proband. Targeted sequencing was performed using a mitochondrial disease gene panel.

A previously unreported variant in AIFM1 (MIM: 300169) was identified: c.506C>T (p.Pro169Leu). According to ACMG/AMP guidelines, this variant was classified as likely pathogenic (PM2, PP3, PP4, and PS3_supporting; see Table S2).12 The variant was detected in a hemizygous state in the male proband. Sanger sequencing confirmed that the proband’s mother was heterozygous for the same variant. After panel-based filtering and multidisciplinary review, this was the only rare variant consistent with the clinical phenotype and X-linked segregation pattern in this family.

PDHA1 female sibling. In the PDHA1 (MIM: 312170) gene, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) revealed a known pathogenic variant, c.379C>T (GenBank: NM_001173456.1) (p.Arg127Trp), consistent with previous reports of PDHA1 deficiency.13,14 Its classification as pathogenic is supported by previous reports and our clinical/biochemical findings (PM2, PP3, and PP4; see Table S2). Sanger sequencing confirmed the presence of this variant in both the female sibling and her mother. After WGS-based filtering, no additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants explaining the phenotype were identified.

Western blotting

To assess the impact of the AIFM1 variant, SDS-PAGE followed by western blotting was performed using protein extracts from the patient’s liver, skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and cultured skin fibroblasts.15 The analysis revealed a marked reduction in AIFM1 protein expression across all examined tissues and cell types compared to controls. These findings support the pathogenicity of the identified AIFM1 variant by demonstrating a loss of protein expression. Representative results are provided in Figure S1.

Targeted nanopore sequencing workflow

Genomic DNA underwent Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing using the Native Barcoding Kit (EXP-NBD114, ONT), allowing for multiplexed library preparation. Four samples were individually barcoded and pooled into a single library. Sequencing was performed on a PromethION flowcell (FLO-PRO114M) using the P2 Solo platform with adaptive sampling enabled to target the entire chrX. Adaptive sampling configuration was applied using a BED file containing chrX coordinates based on the GRCh38 reference genome.

Predictive bioinformatics pipeline

Raw FASTQ files were processed using sample-specific read identifiers matched against merged POD5 files for accurate sample assignment. Methylation-aware base calling was performed using Dorado (ONT), followed by alignment to the T2T-CHM13v2.0 reference genome using minimap2. Variant calling utilized Clair3 with ONT-optimized models. Haplotype resolution was achieved through WhatsHap phasing, with BAM files tagged and split accordingly. Clinical prediction-focused methylation and allele-specific analyses were conducted on haplotype-separated data. All analysis steps relied on publicly available tools and standard ONT workflows, which makes them easy to implement in other clinical laboratories.

XCI-skew estimation and clinical interpretation

All XCI analyses were performed using peripheral blood genomic DNA. Reads were phased with WhatsHap, and methylation calls generated by Dorado were aggregated by haplotype. XCI skew was calculated as max(Hpath, Hnon)/(Hpath + Hnon) × 100, where Hpath and Hnon represent methylated reads assigned to the pathogenic and non-pathogenic haplotypes, respectively. Clinical severity was assessed by comparing XCI skew between affected individuals and carrier mothers harboring the same variant.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent for genetic analyses and publication was obtained from all participating individuals or their legal guardians.

Results

A hemizygous male individual with an AIFM1 variant

The male proband carrying a pathogenic AIFM1 variant was born as the seventh pregnancy to non-consanguineous parents. Family history included several affected siblings: one female with epilepsy, another suspected of having epilepsy, and one male who died in utero; other siblings were unaffected. A pedigree illustrating this family history is presented in Figure 1A. Fetal growth restriction (FGR) and ventriculomegaly were detected at 26 weeks’ gestation, and fetal MRI suggested prior intraventricular hemorrhage. The proband was delivered by emergency cesarean section at 37 weeks + 1 day due to worsening FGR and fetal heart rate abnormalities. Birth weight was 1,600 g. He presented with no spontaneous respiration, marked hypotonia, and severe lactic acidosis. A mitochondrial disorder was suspected, and metabolic support including dialysis was initiated. Despite intensive care, he developed persistent pulmonary hypertension and progressive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and died on day 50 of life. A female patient with PDHA1

The female patient with a pathogenic PDHA1 variant had a complex and progressive clinical course. Her older brother died on day 8 of life due to neonatal asphyxia. At 1 month of age, the patient was diagnosed with cerebral infarction and was hospitalized. At 6 months, she developed West syndrome. Subsequently, she was followed for cerebral palsy and symptomatic epilepsy.

Her activities of daily living (ADLs) required full assistance throughout her life. At age 22, a gastrostomy was performed to support nutrition. At age 23, she was admitted to the hospital with pneumonia, which necessitated artificial respiratory management. During this admission, she also developed acute kidney injury with tubular dysfunction. Even after improvement of the pneumonia, extubation was not possible, and a tracheostomy with laryngotracheal separation was performed. After discontinuation of sedative medications following extubation, it was noted that she no longer smiled and exhibited markedly reduced spontaneous movements. She also developed persistent anuria. Brain MRI revealed extensive high-intensity lesions on diffusion-weighted imaging, predominantly in the cerebral cortex, as well as bilateral symmetric lesions in the basal ganglia. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed elevated levels of lactate and pyruvate, raising suspicion for mitochondrial disease. As part of the diagnostic workup, mitochondrial respiratory chain enzyme activity was measured using skin fibroblasts, and WGS was performed.

Targeted coverage validation

Adaptive sampling achieved consistent enrichment across target loci supporting clinical-grade analysis. Mean coverage for PDHA1 was 42.7× in the proband and 35.7× in the mother (Figure S2). For AIFM1, coverage reached 52.3× in the proband and 37.8× in the mother, demonstrating workflow reliability for routine clinical application (Figure 1B).

Haplotype phasing and clinical correlation

Haplotype phasing successfully separated maternal and paternal alleles, enabling precise clinical prediction through allele-specific methylation pattern analysis (Figure 1C). IGV visualization revealed distinct methylation profiles between haplotypes, with methylated cytosines (5mC/5hmC) displayed in red and unmethylated sites in blue (Figures 1D and 1E).

XCI-skew patterns correlate with clinical severity in AIFM1 family

In the AIFM1 family, XCI-skew analysis revealed a clear correlation with clinical phenotype. The severely affected female sibling showed 84% skew, indicating preferential activation of the pathogenic haplotype. In contrast, her mildly symptomatic mother showed 20% skew, indicating preferential inactivation of the pathogenic haplotype. As expected for a hemizygous chrX, the male proband showed no detectable XCI skew and served as a technical control for haplotype-resolved methylation analysis.

Clinical prediction validation in AIFM1 family

The AIFM1 family demonstrated clear correlation between XCI-skew patterns and clinical severity. The affected sibling exhibited skew favoring activation of the pathogenic allele (84%), while the asymptomatic mother showed skew favoring inactivation of the pathogenic allele (20%). Combined with the technical validation provided by the hemizygous male control, in whom all reads mapped to a single haplotype, these findings support the clinical utility of nanopore-based XCI analysis for severity prediction in X-linked disorders. The AIFM1 family thus serves as a proof of concept demonstrating that haplotype-resolved XCI analysis can predict phenotypic severity in heterozygous carriers.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that adaptive nanopore sequencing enables high-resolution XCI analysis in X-linked disorders. By applying this workflow to an AIFM1 family trio, we established a robust correlation between XCI-skew patterns and clinical severity, demonstrating that affected siblings with pathogenic alleles predominantly on the active X show severe symptoms, while carriers with pathogenic alleles predominantly inactivated remain asymptomatic. Unlike RNA-based allele-specific expression analyses, DNA methylation directly reflects the stable epigenetic state of XCI, providing a more robust and technically stable measure of cellular mosaicism.

The accurate detection of 0% skew in the male proband supported the technical robustness of the approach.16,17,18

While our AIFM1 analysis demonstrated clear clinical utility, we encountered technical challenges when applying this approach to the PDHA1 locus. Haplotype phasing accuracy is dependent on sufficient heterozygous variant density within long-read spans, typically requiring multiple informative SNPs within 10–20 kb regions. In the PDHA1 locus, the density of heterozygous SNPs was extremely low, resulting in relatively few long reads spanning two or more informative SNPs, leading to fragmented phasing and unreliable methylation-based XCI-skew estimates (Table S3; Figures S3 and S4). Importantly, however, mosaicism at the PDHA1 variant locus was clearly supported by variant allele fractions in phased long reads, with the pathogenic allele present in only a fraction of maternal reads, consistent with somatic mosaicism and likely contributing to the attenuated maternal phenotype compared with her affected sibling (Figure 2). This experience highlights the importance of pre-screening target loci for adequate heterozygosity before applying nanopore-based XCI analysis and suggests that future improvements such as trio-based phasing, integration with population-based phasing algorithms, or targeted enrichment of flanking regions will be required to extend this approach to low-heterozygosity loci. Notably, western blot analysis in heterozygous females may not reliably reflect tissue-specific expression patterns that determine clinical phenotype, as the mosaic nature of X inactivation can result in apparently normal overall protein levels despite functional deficiency in affected cell populations (Figure 2). This limitation underscores the importance of XCI-skew analysis over conventional protein quantification methods in heterozygous carriers. This male proband had previously only been reported as one of the genetically diagnosed cases in a nationwide genome analysis study of critically ill neonates and infants. In contrast, the present work provides a detailed characterization of his family-specific XCI patterns and genotype-phenotype correlations.19

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our analysis was restricted to blood DNA, and while the observed consistency in AIFM1 supports the broader applicability of our findings, tissue-specific differences in XCI patterns may exist, particularly in disease-relevant tissues such as brain or muscle. Larger clinical cohorts and multi-tissue studies will be essential to establish standardized skew thresholds for severity prediction and to validate the clinical utility of this approach across diverse X-linked disorders. Future research should also explore integrating nanopore-based XCI analysis into routine diagnostic workflows and its potential to guide therapeutic decision-making in real-world clinical settings.

In summary, these limitations highlight both the technical and biological challenges of XCI analysis while underscoring the clinical value of high-resolution nanopore-based approaches for personalized prediction of X-linked disease severity. Our findings suggest that, when conventional XCI assays are inconclusive, incorporating haplotype-resolved XCI analysis using adaptive nanopore sequencing into existing diagnostic pipelines for X-linked disorders is a viable option.
