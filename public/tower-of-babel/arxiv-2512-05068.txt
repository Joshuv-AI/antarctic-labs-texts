The separating systole and the genus ratio of high genus triangulations
Baptiste Louf

Abstract
We show that the separating systole of high genus triangulations is of logarithmic order (in the size of the triangulation). Our methods also allow us to show an enumerative result, i.e. the convergence of the "genus ratio" for high genus triangulations. This complements the convergence of the "size ratio" that was proven in previous work with Budzinski.



Keywords:
Random maps, high genus, systole

Definitions and notations

2.1
Maps

A
map

m
is a finite graph (allowing loops and multiple edges) embedded on a compact, connected, oriented surface, considered up to homeomorphism. The connected components of the complement of the graph on the surface are called the
faces
of
m
. Equivalently, a map can be constructed as a surface obtained by gluing a collection of polygons along their sides. The
genus
of a map is the genus of its underlying surface.

We consider
rooted
maps, that is, maps with a distinguished oriented edge called the
root edge
. The face to the right of the root edge is the
root face
, and the vertex at the origin of the root edge is the
root vertex
.

The

of a face in a map is the number edge-sides that are incident to it. A
triangulation
is a rooted map in which all faces have degree
3
. For integers
n\geq 1
and
g\geq 0
, we denote by
\mathcal{T}(2n,g)
the set of triangulations of genus
g
with
2n
faces, and
\tau(n,g)
its cardinality. Finally, let
\mathbf{T}_{2n,g}
a random uniform element of
\mathcal{T}(2n,g)
.

A
triangulation with boundaries
is a map in which all faces have degree
3
except for
k
distinguished faces called the
boundaries
. The boundaries must be simple (i.e., having the same number of vertices as edges), and disjoint in the sense that there is no vertex that is incident to two or more boundaries. Finally, for each boundary an edge incident to it is marked and called a root. We denote by
\mathcal{T}_{p_{1},…,p_{k}}(m,g)
the set of triangulations of genus
g
with
m
faces and
k
boundaries, with the
i
-th boundary of size
p_{i}
.

2.2
Paths and cycles

In a map
m
, a

p
of length

is the data of a finite sequence of oriented edges
(\vec{e}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq\ell}
such that for each

is the starting point of
\vec{e}_{i+1}
. Given a path

, we call

the endpoint of
\vec{e}_{i}
, and

the starting point of
\vec{e}_{1}
. We will also say that

is the starting point of

A path

is called a

if

. A path

is

if all its

’s are distinct, and a cycle

is simple if all its
v_{i}
’s are distinct expect for
v_{0}=v_{\ell}
.

Given two paths

is the starting point of

2.3
Homotopy and homology

Any path (resp. cycle) of
t
can be seen as an oriented (resp. closed) curve on a surface
S
, and hence we can study their
homotopy
and
homology
. Let us recall some basic facts, we refer to
[
19
]
for more details. Two curves on a
S
are said to be
homotopic
if one can be continuously deformed into the other. A curve is said to be
contractible
(or null-homotopic) if it is homotopic to a single point. Homotopy classes of closed curves on a surface form a group (by concatenation), and the abelianization of this group is called the (first) homology group of the surface. This defines an equivalence relation of homology between closed curves. A curve is said to be
separating
(or null-homologous) if it is homologous to a contractible curve. The simplest example of a non-contractible but separating curve is a simple curve that, when cut, separates the surface into two subsurfaces both of positive genus, but non simple curves provide many more examples
2
2

2

for instance, if
\gamma
and
\mu
are two non homotopic non separating curves that intersect transversely at a single point, then
\gamma\mu\gamma^{-1}\mu^{-1}
provides such an example
.

Finally, the
separating systole
of a triangulation
t
is the length of its shortest non-contractible but separating cycle (abbreviated later as "SNCC"). We will write
\text{sepsys}(\mathbf{T}_{2n,g_{n}})
for the separating systole of
\mathbf{T}_{2n,g_{n}}
.

Preliminaries: enumeration

We start with an exact recurrence formula for triangulations, the

Theorem 3

(n+1)\tau(n,g)=4n(3n-2)(3n-4)\tau(n-2,g-1)+4(3n-1)\tau(n-1,g)\\

Lemma 4

Let

We now turn to enumeration results that are more specific to the high genus regime.
First, we have some exact results

As

\frac{\tau(n-1,g)}{\tau(n,g)}=\lambda(g/n)+o(1)

Preliminaries: geometry

In a triangulation
t
, we call
thin eight
any tuple
(c_{1},c_{2},p)
such that
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are two simple non-separating cycles which are not homotopic and do not intersect and
p
is a simple path whose starting point lies on
c_{1}
, whose endpoint lies on
c_{2}
but does not share any other vertex (and thus any edge) with
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.

Now, let us define the notion of
fat eight
. Take a tuple
(c,p)
such that
c
is a simple non-separating cycle and
p
is a simple path whose starting point is the starting point of
c
whose endpoint lies on
c
, but does not share any other vertex (and thus any edge) with
c
. In that case, we can write
c=qr^{-1}
, where
q
and
r
are two simple paths that share starting points and end points with
p
. Then
pr^{-1}
and
qp^{-1}
are two simple cycles, and we say that
(c,p)
is a fat eight if neither of these cycles is contractible.

Actually, we will add one special case to this definition: if

The goal of this section is to prove the following:

.

If

is a triangulation whose separating systole is

contains either:

•

a simple non-contractible separating cycle of length

•

a thin eight of total length
\leq L

•

a fat eight of total length
\leq L

With this theorem in hand, it will be much easier to prove the lower bound of

We need a few more definitions and technical lemmas before proving

A cycle

is a simple cycle, and

a (possibly empty) cycle.

Up to changing its starting point, any cycle

is reduced. In that case,

can be written in reduced form, that is, one can decompose
\tilde{\gamma}
as
\tilde{\gamma}=pr^{-1}
, where
p

are two simple paths whose starting and endpoints coincide, but do not share any other vertices or edges (with

\gamma=r^{-1}p
, one can write

where

is a path that starts with an edge that does not belong to
\gamma
(or the empty path).

Proof.

If
C
is the power of a simple cycle, the result is obvious, now assume this is not the case. Let
(\vec{e}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq\ell}
be its edges and
(v_{i})_{0\leq i\leq\ell}
its vertices. Since

is non simple, there exists

such that

. Taking

. Set

, the cycle

is now in reduced form. Since

for all

(note that

4.1
A nice cycle

In order to prove
Theorem

For any cycle

SL(C)=(SL_{i}(C))_{1\leq i\leq\ell}

This defines a weakly increasing sequence with increments

of cycles of same length

Proof of

We are now ready to prove

Assume

is not simple, otherwise we are done. In that case,

is non empty.
Write

As before, write

for the starting point of

and
v_{i}
for the endpoint of

Let also

be the length of
\gamma
and
0\leq r<m

Now, let
b>km+r
be minimal such that there exists

.
In other words,

starts with a path that will eventually hit

(the path hits itself but not where it started), set

If

Finally, if

Let
