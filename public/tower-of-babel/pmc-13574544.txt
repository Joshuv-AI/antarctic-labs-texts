A Review on Heat Stress in Broiler Chickens: Mechanisms, Effects and Mitigation Strategies
Authors: Maksuda Begum, Laura Musa, Guido Grilli, Dilşan Akçay, Md Golam Kibria, Mst Mayeeda Parvin, Sabiha Zarin Tasnim Bristi, Md Jisan Ahmed, Delower Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHeat stress (HS) is a major environmental challenge for broilers, particularly under rising global temperatures and high humidity. Broiler chickens are highly susceptible because of their rapid growth rate, high metabolic heat production, limited thermoregulatory capacity and genetic selection for fast growth.ObjectiveThis review aims to synthesise current evidence, evaluate the effectiveness of existing mitigation strategies, identify key knowledge gaps and provide future research directions to improve broiler resilience, welfare and productivity under increasingly HS conditions.MethodsThis review synthesised evidence published between 2010 and 2025 on the physiological, metabolic, intestinal, immunological and productive consequences of HS and evaluated mitigation strategies.ResultsThe reviewed studies demonstrate that HS reduces feed intake by approximately 10%–30%, suppresses body weight gain and feed efficiency and increases mortality, with severity depending on temperature, humidity and broiler genotype. HS disrupts carbohydrate, protein and lipid metabolism; induces acid–base imbalance and oxidative stress; compromises intestinal barrier integrity; alters gut microbiota; suppresses immune function; and reduces meat quality. Nutritional interventions, including dietary electrolyte balance, antioxidants, vitamins, selenium, zinc, phytogenic compounds, probiotics, betaine and optimised feeding strategies, environmental management and genetic approaches, including naked‐neck and frizzle genes, can partially alleviate these adverse effects. However, inconsistencies among studies persist because of differences in broiler strains, environmental conditions, dietary formulations and experimental protocols.ConclusionHS substantially compromises broiler health, welfare, productivity and meat quality. Nutritional, environmental and genetic approaches can partially mitigate its adverse effects; however, further research is needed to improve broiler resilience under increasingly HS conditions.Keywords: broiler, health, heat stress, mitigation, performance

Introduction

The main objective of the global broiler industry is to consistently supply meat in line with demand. There is a growing interest in contemporary broiler chicken production, emphasising the importance of nutrition and nutritional supplements to increase meat yield while ensuring safety and high quality for human consumption (Puvača et al. 2022). The amount of poultry meat consumed worldwide has doubled over the past decade and is expected to double again by 2050 (Wasti et al. 2020). The genetics of meat‐producing chickens have significantly improved to meet market demands, enhancing production performance and increasing broiler metabolic rates (Pawar et al. 2016). They generate more body heat due to their increased metabolic rate, which increases their vulnerability to heat stress (HS).

Broiler chickens are more susceptible to HS than other chicken breeds because of their advanced genetic selection for rapid growth, high metabolic rates and increased metabolic heat production, which make them dependent on tightly controlled environmental conditions (Sebho 2016). This intense selection has resulted in birds with high feed intake (FI) and elevated heat production that exceed their capacity for effective heat dissipation, rendering them particularly vulnerable to elevated temperatures. Therefore, increased environmental temperatures adversely affect livestock productivity, especially broiler chicken production. HS is one of the main climatic factors influencing the growth, reproduction and productivity of different livestock species and poultry (Alagawany et al. 2017). Under high temperatures, chickens develop physiological, behavioural and immune responses, such as panting, reduced FI, oxidative stress (OS) and immunosuppression, that further compromise performance, health and welfare, ultimately decreasing growth performance, feed efficiency and survival in broiler flocks (Wasti et al. 2020; Pontalti et al. 2025; Abioja and Abiona 2020).

The presence of dynamic ventilation also positively influences chicken performance parameters by improving air circulation, reducing the ambient heat load and helping to maintain a more favourable thermal environment. HS occurs when birds enter a physiological state in which the balance between body heat production and heat dissipation is disturbed (Vandana et al. 2021). Ambient temperature, humidity, radiation and air movement are among the factors contributing to HS. However, the most significant effects on HS in poultry are specifically caused by high ambient temperatures and humidity (Lara and Rostagno 2013).

Heat stress in poultry can be classified as acute HS, involving a rapid and short‑term rise in environmental temperature and humidity over a short period (27°C–38°C for 1–24 h) (Maibam et al. 2018), moderate HS occurs when similar temperatures are sustained for approximately 7 days and chronic HS, where high temperature and excessive humidity persist for a prolonged period (38°C–50°C for 7 days) (Yahav 2015; Zmrhal et al. 2018). The typical body temperature of a chicken is between 41°C and 42°C. The bird must stay in the thermoneutral (TN) zone between 18°C and 21°C to grow at its fastest possible rate (Naga Raja Kumari and Narendra Nath 2018).

Poultry health and performance are adversely affected by HS, a major industry issue. HS caused annual economic losses of $2.36 billion in the poultry industry in the United States in 2025 (Abbas et al. 2025). As global temperatures continue to rise, these costs are likely to increase further in the coming years. To improve the poultry industry, a summary of the extensive scientific knowledge on the adverse effects of HS on chicken health and performance, along with possible mitigating measures, is necessary (Wasti et al. 2020).

This review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of current knowledge on the effects of HS on broiler chickens, particularly with a focus on growth performance, nutrient metabolism, physiological and health responses and associated disease susceptibility (Figure 1). In addition, this review summarises existing mitigation strategies, including nutritional, genetic and management approaches and identifies key research gaps to guide future investigations for improving broiler resilience to HS. Unlike earlier reviews, this review integrates current evidence on gut microbiota, molecular mechanisms, OS, disease susceptibility and emerging mitigation approaches. Furthermore, previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the magnitude of growth depression, OS, immune dysfunction, intestinal barrier disruption and gut microbial alterations under HS (Figure 1). These discrepancies are largely attributable to differences in broiler genotype, age, acute versus chronic HS models, environmental temperature and humidity, dietary formulations, management practices and the biomarkers used to assess physiological responses, making direct comparisons among studies difficult (Ncho 2025, O. E. Oke et al.).

Mechanism of heat stress and its effects on broiler chickens.

Unlike previous reviews that primarily emphasised production performance or individual physiological responses, this review provides an integrated perspective by synthesising recent evidence on nutrient metabolism; molecular signalling pathways; OS; the gut microbiota; disease susceptibility and emerging nutritional, environmental and precision management strategies to mitigate HS. In addition, it critically evaluates the reasons for inconsistencies among published studies and highlights important knowledge gaps requiring further investigation (Ncho 2025, O. E. Oke et al.).

The remainder of this review is organised as follows. First, the effects of HS on broiler growth performance and nutrient metabolism are discussed, followed by its impacts on physiological functions, OS, intestinal health, immune responses and disease susceptibility. Subsequently, current nutritional, environmental and genetic mitigation strategies are critically evaluated. Finally, the review highlights existing research gaps and future perspectives to improve broiler resilience, welfare and sustainable poultry production under increasing global temperatures.

Literature Search Methodology

This narrative review was conducted through a comprehensive literature search of peer‐reviewed articles published between 2010 and 2025. Relevant studies were identified using the electronic databases PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar. The search employed combinations of keywords including ‘heat stress’, ‘broiler chickens’, ‘thermal stress’, ‘growth performance’, ‘nutrient metabolism’, ‘oxidative stress’, ‘intestinal health’, ‘immune response’, ‘meat quality’ and ‘heat stress mitigation'' using Boolean operators (AND/OR). Articles published in English that investigated the physiological, metabolic, intestinal, immunological, productive and meat quality effects of HS, as well as nutritional, environmental and genetic mitigation strategies in broiler chickens, were included. Review articles were also consulted to provide background information and identify additional relevant studies. Studies involving non‐broiler poultry species, non‐English publications, conference abstracts, duplicate records and articles lacking sufficient scientific data were excluded. Priority was given to recent, high‐quality studies, while seminal older publications were retained where necessary to provide essential background and context.

Performance of Broilers Under Heat Stress

Increasing environmental temperatures are a serious stressor for broilers and negatively affect their performance. As ambient temperature increases, FI and body weight gain (BWG) decrease, feed conversion ratio (FCR) increases, mortality increases and dressing percentage (DP) decreases in broilers (Figure 1, Tables 1 and 2).

Feed Intake

HS reduces FI, a physiological response that minimises internal heat production. Variation among studies may arise from broiler strain, age, humidity and the duration of heat exposure. This adaptive mechanism, however, compromises nutrient intake, which directly affects growth and overall performance. Under normal temperature conditions, Hubbard broiler chickens had an average FI of 3947.87 g, whereas those exposed to HS had a reduced FI of 3678.23 g (Jahejo et al. 2016). Broilers exposed to HS for 7 h per day for 22 days had an FI of 2832 g, whereas the FI of the control group not exposed to HS was 3226 g. This study revealed that chronic HS significantly reduced FI in broilers (Al‐Sagan et al. 2020).

Body Weight Gain

Another important consequence of HS is the reduction in BWG. Heat‐induced stress typically suppresses FI, thereby diminishing growth performance. Broilers exposed to HS for 5 days presented BWG values ranging from 1600 to 1922 g, whereas those maintained under TN conditions presented BWG values ranging from 1600 to 2086 g. These findings indicate that even short‐term exposure to HS can significantly impair broiler growth performance (Santos et al. 2019). However, the negative impact becomes more pronounced under chronic HS conditions. Studies have shown that broilers reared in heat‐free environments consistently demonstrate greater BWG than those subjected to prolonged heat exposure (Jahejo et al. 2016). Similarly, environmental management plays a crucial role in mitigating the effects of HS. By day 56, broilers housed in a poultry facility with dynamic ventilation had higher live weights than those reared under natural ventilation (1870 vs. 1682 g), highlighting the importance of effective thermal control strategies for maintaining optimal growth performance.

Feed Conversion Ratio

Studies have shown that environmental temperature negatively affects the FCR. The FCR increased by 10.8% in the group exposed to HS (Sahin et al. 2016). Compared with broilers at room temperature, there was no significant change in FCR on day 21 in broilers exposed to HS, whereas FCR was measured as 1.67 in broilers exposed to HS by day 42 (Sahin et al. 2016). This is confirmed by better feed conversion (1.98 vs. 2.66). In buildings with natural ventilation, examinations of 50 chickens revealed the presence of conjunctivitis in the animals (20%).

Dressing Percentages

Elevated temperatures impair fundamental physiological functions in broilers, leading to productivity losses, poor FI and suboptimal growth. Carcass weight is directly related to dressing percentage. Therefore, a decrease in the dressing percentage reduces meat quality. HS has been shown to reduce DP, likely through factors such as protein indigestibility and decreased FI (Attia et al. 2017). A previous study revealed that the dressing percentage decreased by 2.9%, and the amount of abdominal fat decreased by 35.9% in a group with chronic HS (Attia et al. 2017).

Mortality Rate

High environmental temperatures suppress the immune system of animals by disrupting their hormonal balance. Broilers exposed to a temperature of 21°C presented a mortality rate of 0%, whereas those exposed to 36°C had a mortality rate of 43.3% (Quinteiro‐Filho et al. 2010). Another factor that may contribute to the high mortality rate under HS conditions is a significant alteration in body mass gain and immunoglobulin levels, both of which are crucial for immune defence. Mortality in buildings with natural ventilation is twice as high as that in buildings with dynamic ventilation (10.5 vs. 4.5%) (Siddiqui et al. 2022).

Nutrient Metabolism of Broilers During Heat Stress

Carbohydrate Metabolism

To adapt to elevated temperatures, broilers should reduce their FI to minimise internal heat generation (Figure 1). However, reduced FI reduces energy intake, requiring broilers to rely on alternative energy sources. Under HS, glycogen stored in the liver is broken down via glycogenolysis to provide glucose for energy maintenance and growth. Additionally, energy is derived from gluconeogenesis, in which the liver produces glucose from non‐carbohydrate sources. Post‐slaughter, glycogen and glucose are rapidly converted to lactate (Wang et al. 2017). In HS broiler meat, glycogen levels were measured at 28.79 at 5 min post‐slaughter, decreasing to 15.98 within 2 h (Wang et al. 2017). The glucose concentration in the blood of HS broilers was 23 mmol/L, whereas it was 20.1 mmol/L in controls (Awad et al. 2019). Similarly, after 7 days of exposure, the plasma glucose level initially reached 12.3 mmol/L, but after 14 days, it decreased to 8.5 mmol/L, indicating a shift in energy metabolism over prolonged HS exposure (Lu et al. 2019).

The variability in glucose and glycogen responses reported across studies is likely attributable to differences in HS intensity (acute vs. chronic), broiler genotype, age, dietary composition and sampling time (Siddiqui et al. 2021; Onagbesan et al. 2023). Acute HS stimulates corticosterone (CORT) secretion and glycogenolysis, leading to transient hyperglycemia to meet immediate energy demands (Zheng et al. 2021). In contrast, prolonged heat exposure depletes hepatic glycogen reserves and increases glucose utilisation for maintenance metabolism, resulting in lower circulating glucose concentrations. Furthermore, differences in post‐mortem sampling intervals and muscle glycolytic activity may explain inconsistencies in glycogen depletion and lactate accumulation reported among studies (Oluwagbenga and Fraley 2023).

Protein Metabolism

Protein is the main energy source for the growth and performance of meat‐type poultry. At high ambient temperatures, broilers reduce FI, specifically protein‐based feed, to limit heat production, as protein metabolism generates more heat than carbohydrates or fat (Table 3). This may lead to decreased protein synthesis and deposition. During HS, the hormone CORT is released from the adrenal cortex and secreted into the bloodstream as an indicator and signal of energy availability. Ma et al. (2021) reported that increased plasma CORT levels indicate reduced protein synthesis and muscle growth. Heat stress suppresses mTOR signalling and activates AMPK pathways (Heberle et al. 2015). At the cellular level, HS reduces ATP availability and increases the AMP/ATP ratio, thereby activating AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), the primary cellular energy sensor (Xiang and Su 2026; Xia et al. 2019). Activated AMPK suppresses mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), a key regulator of protein translation, by inhibiting downstream targets such as ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K1) and eukaryotic initiation factor 4E‐binding protein 1 (4E‐BP1) (Chun and Kim 2021). Consequently, ribosomal activity and mRNA translation decline, reducing muscle protein synthesis (Millward et al. 1973). Simultaneously, elevated CORT promotes proteolysis by activating the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, thereby releasing amino acids for gluconeogenesis and maintaining cellular energy homeostasis (Wing and Goldberg 1993). Heat shock proteins, particularly HSP70, are selectively upregulated to protect cellular proteins from denaturation and maintain protein folding under thermal stress, although this occurs at the expense of growth‐related protein synthesis (Oluwagbenga and Fraley 2023; Montesinos‐Cruz et al. 2023). In response to HS, the liver undergoes gluconeogenesis to maintain energy, initiates protein breakdown and supplies amino acids (AAs) for energy (Ma et al. 2021). HS decreases protein synthesis except for the heat shock protein (HSP70) (Wang et al. 2020).

Lipid Metabolism

HS is an important stressor in broiler production, altering metabolic activities, feed consumption and growth. Heat stress promotes hepatic lipogenesis through activation of SREBP‐1 and FAS (Zhang et al. 2018). Under HS, broilers tend to reduce protein intake to minimise internal heat generation, often shifting towards lipid‐rich feed, which can lead to elevated fat deposition in the muscle and body. Acyl‐CoA carboxylase (AAC) and fatty acid synthase (FAS) are two essential enzymes for fat synthesis in the liver. A 7‐day heat‐exposure broiler showed elevated AAC and FAS levels, indicating an increase in lipid synthesis (Lu et al. 2019). This is evident in the higher fat content of the heat‐stressed broilers' breast muscle, which measured 0.98% fat compared with 0.80% in the controls (Zhang et al. 2012). Fatty acids are stored as triglycerides, which supports previous data showing that HS increases fatty acid synthesis (Jastrebski et al. 2017). During HS, fat deposition occurs, mainly in subcutaneous and intermuscular cells (De Antonio et al. 2017).

Heat stress not only increases lipogenesis but also impairs hepatic fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function (Song et al. 2026). Chronic heat exposure suppresses PPARα‐mediated β‐oxidation while upregulating lipogenic transcription factors, including sterol regulatory element‐binding protein‐1 (SREBP‐1), acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC) and fatty acid synthase (FAS), thereby promoting triglyceride accumulation in the liver (Wang et al. 2026). Heat stress also disrupts mitochondrial energy metabolism through altered AMPK signalling, leading to reduced ATP production, increased OS and excessive lipid deposition (Sun et al. 2021). These metabolic alterations contribute to poorer carcass quality and reduced feed efficiency. Activation of the AMPK–PPARα pathway by nutritional compounds such as genistein, rutin and melatonin can partially restore mitochondrial function, enhance fatty acid oxidation and reduce hepatic lipid accumulation in heat‐stressed broilers, highlighting these signalling pathways as promising therapeutic targets (Montesinos‐Cruz et al. 2023).

Acid–Base Equilibrium

The comfortable temperature for broilers is approximately 21°C–25°C. When environmental temperature exceeds 30°C, broilers attempt to adjust by losing body heat, reducing FI and increasing their respiratory rate, often leading to panting. However, increased CO2 loss exceeds cellular CO2 production, leading to concurrent loss of bicarbonate (HCO3 −) and potassium (K+) ions and a decrease in the blood hydrogen (H+) concentration, thereby elevating blood pH.

This shift disrupts the acid–base equilibrium in plasma, increasing the base concentration and causing an acid–base imbalance. Furthermore, this imbalance leads to respiratory alkalosis and poor performance. HS increased the pH of the blood (Van Goor et al. 2016). However, some studies have shown a decreased pH in carcasses, which may be the cause of lactic acid production after post‐mortem (Awad et al. 2019). Similarly, 7‐day post‐HS broilers presented a post‐mortem pH of 6.44 at 45 min and 5.89 at 1 h post‐mortem, highlighting the complex effects of HS on pH regulation in live and post‐mortem states (Lu et al. 2017).

Oxidative Stress

The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), which are known as the antioxidative enzyme system (AES), constitute the initial line of defence against free radicals, including mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Del Vesco et al. 2017). HS increases ROS production and activity, increasing the scavenging capacity of SOD, but does not activate GPx, which ultimately suppresses the protective ability of AES. HS impairs ROS generation and the scavenging equilibrium, leading to OS and tissue damage. OS further induces lipid peroxidation and nucleic acid damage [Miao et al. 2020].

Safety of Broilers During Heat Stress

Physiological Disorders

The negative effects of HS can be observed in physiological parameters (Table 4). HS reduces the haemoglobin concentration in the body. One of the most apparent indicators of HS in broiler chickens is the heterophil/lymphocyte (H/L) ratio. Recent studies have shown that the H/L ratio increases in broilers exposed to HS. For instance, a study reported an H/L ratio of 0.827 in broilers exposed to 40°C, whereas this ratio was reported to be 0.570 in broilers at room temperature (Jaiswal et al. 2017). Similarly, another study reported that the H/L ratio of 6‐week‐old broilers exposed to 35°C–40°C was 0.82 (Gharieb and Moursi 2013).

Immune Dysregulation

The immune system plays a vital role in protecting the body against pathogens. High temperatures increase hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activation and increase the release of CORT in the serum (Quinteiro‐Filho et al. 2010). Increased serum CORT suppresses the immune system. This imbalance in HPA axis activation may affect macrophage activity. The phagocytic macrophage rate was measured as 31% in chickens subjected to HS, whereas this rate was measured as 35.2% in chickens subjected to TN (Niu et al. 2009).

Endocrine Disorders

In broilers exposed to high ambient temperatures, physiological and immunological changes and some hormonal changes occur. One notable change is the increased transcription of the HSP70 gene (Xu et al. 2018). This gene protects the body from high temperatures by increasing the level of HSP70 protein. Some recent studies have shown that HS disrupts the balance of many hormones, leading to adverse effects on the body. It has been observed that the T3 hormone levels are decreased in broilers exposed to HS. A decrease in the T3 hormone may be the reason for the increase in body temperature of chickens exposed to HS (Naga Raja Kumari and Narendra Nath 2018).

Electrolyte Imbalance

Electrolytes are essential for the body to function properly. There are three important electrolytes, the dominant ones in poultry. These are sodium, potassium and chloride. These electrolytes significantly affect the growth performance of broilers (Mushtaq et al. 2013). While sodium helps maintain water and fluid balance, potassium supports proper nervous system function. High temperatures increase fluid loss in animals and cause electrolyte imbalance. Changes in sodium levels trigger fluid loss and affect muscle development. A previous study revealed that chronic HS reduces sodium and potassium concentrations in broilers (Azad et al. 2010). While the sodium rate is 123.5 (mmol/L) in 6‐week‐old broilers exposed to HS, it is 143.5 (mmol/L) in broilers growing at a normal temperature (Gharieb and Moursi 2013).

Cardiovascular System

One of the systems affected by HS is the cardiovascular system. Changes in the circulatory system of animals are vital for metabolism. Negative changes in cholesterol levels caused by high temperatures disrupt hormone balance and impair thermoregulation. In this context, recent research has shown that HS increases total cholesterol levels. At the end of the 42nd day, the total cholesterol level in chickens exposed to chronic HS was 2.81, compared with 2.71 in the normal‐temperature group. Additionally, an increase in the HDL‐C and LDL‐C levels of broilers in high‐temperature environments was observed (He et al. 2015).

Gastrointestinal Tract Microbiota

Bacteria in the digestive system of broiler chickens constitute the gastrointestinal microbiota. Studies have shown that rising temperatures alter the intestinal microbiota. The gastrointestinal tract microbiota contributes to immune system development (Ubeda and Pamer 2012). Disruption of the microbiota allows pathogens to invade the intestine (Burkholder et al. 2008).

Gastrointestinal Epithelium

The gastrointestinal epithelium is an essential structure for the digestive system. Epithelial tissue provides the density and integrity of the intestinal mucosa. The intestinal mucosa of chickens exposed to HS changes rapidly (Burkholder et al. 2008). The epithelial cell area ratio was dramatically decreased in chickens exposed to 39°C heat (Santos et al. 2015).

Intestinal Morphology

Intestinal morphology included villus height, villus width, villus surface area and crypt depth. Stress factors affect the physiological and morphological structures of the intestines. HS is one of the most severe stressors. In this context, it is inevitable that intestinal morphology changes in animals exposed to HS. The crypt depth increased by 20.0% in chickens exposed to chronic high temperatures (Song et al. 2018). Conversely, a decrease in the height of villi in the duodenum, jejunum and ileum was detected in broilers exposed to 39°C (Santos et al. 2015).

Effects of Heat Stress on Diseases

Viral Disease

On commercial poultry farms, one possible risk of a viral disease outbreak is HS. Chicken immunocompetence is reduced by HS, as evidenced by decreased immune tissue weights (Calefi et al. 2019); damage to the structure of bursal follicles and the thymic cortex, where T and B cells mature (Hirakawa et al. 2020); decreased antibody production against immunised antigens (Jahanian and Rasouli 2015); increased susceptibility to viral infection; altered levels of inflammatory cytokine expression in the spleen and caecal tonsils (Quinteiro‐Filho et al. 2017); failure of the vaccination protocol (Calefi et al. 2019) and increased mortality (Khattak et al. 2012), indicating that HS has a significant impact on the immune system. Chicks under HS had reduced antibody responses against viruses causing infectious bronchitis and Newcastle disease (Jahanian and Rasouli 2015). The number of helper (CD4+) T cells was significantly reduced, and the number of cytotoxic (CD8+) T lymphocytes was increased upon exposure to HS; as a result, the CD4+ to CD8+ T‐cell ratio in the peripheral blood circulation was significantly decreased.

Bacterial Diseases

It is anticipated that the frequency of HS incidents will increase across livestock species because of climate challenges (Rahimi et al. 2020). Key periods for HS include summertime, particularly in tropical/subtropical regions, as well as during farm‐to‐processing facilities (Kpomasse et al. 2021). HS has a negative effect on intestinal morphology by reducing the villus height‐to‐crypt depth ratio, the goblet cell number, the mucin‐2 mRNA level (Zhang et al. 2017) and the weight of the intestine (Abdelqader and Al‐Fataftah 2016); reducing the levels of immune cytokines (p‐IRF3, p‐TBK1 and IFN‐α) (Tang et al. 2021); impairing intestinal immune activities (Wu et al. 2018); causing OS (reducing diamine oxidase activity) (Zhang et al. 2017) and inflammation (increasing inflammatory cytokines: TNF‐α and IL‐2) (Alhenaky et al. 2017); increasing the pH (reducing serum D‐lactic acid); impairing the intestinal microflora by reducing beneficial bacteria (Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp.); and increasing the colonisation of pathogenic bacteria (Salmonella spp., E. coli and Clostridium spp.) (An et al. 2023; Tsiouris et al. 2018), which has detrimental effects on intestinal barrier integrity, causing the transport of endotoxins from the intestinal lumen to the bloodstream (Alhenaky et al. 2017); and encouraging bacterial translocation from the gut to other organs, causing multi‐organ dysfunction (Vicuña et al. 2015).

Parasitic Diseases

Coccidiosis

Eimeria spp. are the causal agents of coccidiosis, a digestive tract protozoan parasite disease (Table 5). Heat stress increases susceptibility to coccidiosis primarily by disrupting the intestinal barrier and suppressing both innate and adaptive immunity (Ghareeb et al. 2024). Increased CORT concentrations reduce lymphocyte proliferation, macrophage activity and T‐cell‐mediated immune responses, while OS damages intestinal epithelial cells and weakens tight junctions (Balakin et al. 2025). These changes facilitate Eimeria spp. invasion of the intestinal mucosa and impair the host's ability to eliminate intracellular parasites. Heat stress also alters cytokine expression and gut microbiota composition. It compromises mucosal immunity and increases the likelihood of clinical coccidiosis, particularly when birds are exposed to additional environmental stressors or high oocyst challenge (Nguyen et al. 2026; Ahmad et al. 2022). Cellular and humoral immunity is suppressed by HS and also impairs intestinal integrity, increasing susceptibility to Eimeria spp. infection (Wickramasuriya et al. 2022). This infection is contracted by susceptible chickens through ingestion of infectious (sporulated) oocysts in feed, litter, contaminated water and soil. Additionally, the CD4+:CD8+ ratio is greater in infected birds under HS conditions (Schneiders et al. 2020), which has a detrimental effect on broiler production. However, under HS conditions, changes in host cytokine mRNA expression have a negative impact on the pathogenesis of Eimeria spp., resulting in rare to minimal coccidial enteritis, decreased merozoites, scattered schizonts in the lamina propria and decreased oocyst shedding, all of which contribute to the reduced severity of the disease (Calefi et al. 2019; Schneiders et al. 2020). A greater percentage of broilers (20.86%) and young chicks (22.69%) were aged 2–6 weeks, August (52.94%) (Ali et al. 2014) and summer (42%), and 15–21 days (52%) and mud‐type floors (50%) were included (Iqbal and Begum 2010).

Intestinal Helminths

The greatest cause of mortality for livestock, including poultry and birds, in tropical and subtropical climates is helminth infections, particularly nematodes and cestodes (Baboolal et al. 2012). Ascaridia galli infection in birds induces some gross pathological lesions, haemorrhagic enteritis, reddish spots and necrotic patches with consolidation on the intestinal wall and content, dark red coloration on the liver, congestion of the lung and atrophy of the heart (Feroza et al. 2018). They identified four species of nematodes, namely, Ascaridia galli (5.8%), Heterakis gallinarum (0.9%), Capillaria spp. (0.3%) and Subulura brumpti (0.3%), and three species of cestodes, namely, Choanotaenia infundibulum (2.3%), Raillietina echinobothrida (2.3%) and Raillietina cesticillus (0.9%) (Baboolal et al. 2012).

Mycotoxicosis

Mycotoxin is a secondary metabolite produced by toxigenic fungi, particularly in hot, humid environments. A class of toxic compounds known as aflatoxins is generated by Aspergillus spp., specifically Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus. The consumption of aflatoxin‐contaminated feed results in significant morbidity and mortality as well as significant financial losses (Diaz et al. 2008). Aflatoxin‐affected broiler chickens exhibit specific clinical symptoms, including aberrant vocalisation, ataxia, pale shanks and combs, decreased FI, diarrhoea and 100% mortality. They have several notable necropsy features, such as haemorrhage, pale liver and enlarged gall bladder, as well as hydropericardium, enlarged kidneys, spleen and thymus and bursa atrophy. Aflatoxicosis (aflatoxin B1) is most common in autumn (13.29%) (Rashid et al. 2013).

Respiratory Alkalosis/Acid–Base Imbalance

The primary method of heat dissipation employed by chickens is latent heat loss through panting, or an increased respiratory rate resulting from short, rapid breathing (Mutaf et al. 2009); panting causes respiratory alkalosis because it increases CO2 exhaled relative to what is produced by cells. Carbonic acid levels decrease when carbon dioxide levels are reduced. In parallel, an increased quantity of bicarbonate ions causes the blood to become alkaline (Beckford et al. 2020; Park and Kim 2017). In birds, hydrogen ions are retained by the kidneys, and additional bicarbonate ions are excreted to restore blood pH to normal. An increase in hydrogen ions causes metabolic acidosis, which affects the acid–base balance and impairs production efficiency (Borges et al. 2007).

Footpad Dermatitis

Footpad dermatitis (FPD), also known as pododermatitis, is an important welfare and economic concern in broiler production. Although HS does not directly induce footpad lesions, it markedly increases the risk of FPD by altering bird behaviour and deteriorating litter quality (Forseth et al. 2025). Under HS, broilers increase water consumption and panting while reducing FI to facilitate evaporative cooling. Increased water intake results in wetter excreta, which elevates litter moisture, pH and ammonia production (Francesch and Brufau 2004). Prolonged exposure of the plantar surface of the footpad to moist, ammonia‐rich litter softens the keratinised epithelium, disrupts the skin barrier and promotes inflammation, erosions, ulceration and secondary bacterial colonisation, ultimately leading to FPD (Miao et al. 2021; Khalfi et al. 2026).

The severity of FPD is further influenced by environmental and management factors, including high stocking density, inadequate ventilation, poor litter management and prolonged exposure to humid conditions, all of which are commonly exacerbated during periods of HS (Fotouh et al. 2026; Ibrahim et al. 2017). Consequently, FPD should be considered an indirect consequence of HS, mediated by impaired litter quality, rather than a direct thermal lesion. Improving ventilation, maintaining dry litter, optimising drinker management and reducing ammonia accumulation are among the most effective strategies for preventing FPD (Fotouh et al. 2026). Furthermore, environmental enrichment and improved housing designs that enhance thermoregulation and litter conditions have been reported to reduce the prevalence of footpad lesions while improving bird welfare (Khalfi et al. 2026).

Bird Lameness

HS adversely affects broilers by suppressing body weight and skeletal health and inducing lameness. HS decreased plasma IL‐6 concentrations, SOD activity and latency to lie (LTL, a duration that measures a bird's lameness) on day 28, but it also decreased BW and IgY concentrations on day 42 (Hu et al. 2022). Heat‐stressed broilers presented a significantly greater incidence of subclinical tibial head necrosis (THN) at 28 and 35 days of age (Wideman and Pevzner 2012). Stress and immunosuppression must be considered key factors in the pathogenesis of tibial and femoral lesions associated with lameness in broilers.

Liver Cirrhosis

The liver is a crucial organ for metabolic activity and performs vital biological processes such as ammonia detoxification, vitamin and mineral production and energy metabolism balance (Schliess et al. 2014). The liver is extremely vulnerable to HS. After 14 days of heat exposure, HS increased liver relative weight, induced vacuolar degeneration and increased GRP78 protein expression. It also increased the mRNA expression of genes related to apoptosis and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress responses (Ma et al. 2022). HS elevates the levels of FAS and triglycerides in the liver of broiler chickens, potentially leading to hepatic steatosis and, ultimately, liver cirrhosis (Lu et al. 2019).

Strategies for Mitigating Heat Stress in Broiler Production

Management Factors

Housing Management

Numerous factors contribute to overall HS in chickens, as reflected in the internal temperatures of poultry houses (Table 6). Proper management of poultry housing is critical to reducing HS, a potential stressor that affects productivity. Previous research has highlighted the importance of ensuring adequate temperature regulation and ventilation control in environmentally controlled housing to prevent HS (Saeed et al. 2019). In nonenvironmentally controlled situations, such as open‐sided buildings, issues such as insufficient stocking density and ventilation can worsen HS (Oke et al. 2021). Addressing these issues is critical for maintaining a healthy environment for chickens and preventing HS‐related problems. The aforementioned elements include heat produced by avian species, ambient circumstances, roofs, the process of faecal matter biodegradation and exposed or shaded ground surfaces (Clark 2013). Under equal environmental conditions, dark‐coloured surfaces absorb more heat from direct sun radiation than light‐coloured surfaces do. Moreover, stagnant faeces within broiler houses have the potential to release heat and a range of detrimental gases, including NH3, CH4 and H2S, as they decompose (Apalowo et al. 2024). Effective HS management in poultry production requires meticulous examination of the diverse heat‐generating sources in the broiler house and the development of solutions to mitigate their detrimental effects on birds (e.g., cool cell pads, sprinklers, fans and tunnel ventilation). As a result, for optimal poultry welfare and productivity, appropriate ventilation is essential for broiler chicken health and productivity, as it removes harmful gases and ensures fresh air. Inadequate ventilation can lead to various health problems and reduced performance.

Welfare and Stocking Density

HS affects broiler chicken welfare. An increase in HS intensity, coupled with compromised stocking density due to broiler overcrowding, has serious consequences for bird health and well‐being (Ghosh et al. 2012). Despite the best intentions, overcrowding broiler chickens slows their growth, decreases their FI and efficiency and endangers their health and well‐being (Xiong et al. 2020). When broiler chicks are overcrowded in their house, their growth rate, feed consumption and FCR are affected (Heidari and Toghyani 2018). Overcrowding not only adversely affects broiler farmers’ bottom lines and overall income (Ghosh et al. 2012) but also puts bird health at risk. Research has shown that broilers are more likely to experience HS, including decreased digestion and absorption, mucosal injury and compromised intestinal processes, due to overcrowding (Li et al. 2019). Intestinal mucosal injury in broilers is closely linked to increased release of CORT, which is caused by stress associated with high stocking density. This stress includes factors such as competition for feed and water, elevated ambient temperature and increased litter moisture content and ammonia levels (Law et al. 2019). One of the most important parts of rearing broilers at a high stocking density is the effect of stocking density on carcass yield and meat quality (Beg et al. 2011). Highlighting the need to resolve welfare concerns in chicken production methods, the convergence of HS and high stocking density intensifies these issues.

Dietary Supplementation

Broilers decrease FI in response to HS. It reduces protein, minerals, vitamins and energy in the body. To maintain metabolic function and promote growth, broilers need these nutrients. Therefore, dietary mitigation strategies include supplementation with functional nutrients such as vitamin C (typically 200–500 mg/kg diet), vitamin E (100–250 mg/kg diet), selenium (commonly around 0.3 mg/kg diet) and betaine (1–2 g/kg diet), which have been reported to improve antioxidant status, immune response and heat tolerance in broiler chickens under HS conditions. Vitamin E reduces free radical generation and improves antioxidant activity (Dalólio et al. 2015). Vit A and E increase the growth rate of broilers (Bilal et al. 2021). Vitamin supplementation increases feed consumption, growth, FCR and heat tolerance (El‐Moniary et al. 2010). Furthermore, vitamin supplementation has been associated with birds carrying the F gene have been reported to exhibit the increased blood parameters (RBCs, lymphocytes) and improved immunity (Attia et al. 2017).

Baxter et al. (2020) reported increased CO2 and decreased plasma CORT in mineral‐supplemented broilers. He also demonstrated that a mineral‐rich diet has anti‐inflammatory activity and antioxidant capacity. Antioxidant‐provided broilers exhibit increased body weight and increased beneficial bacteria in the intestine (Wasti et al. 2021). Antioxidants help reduce ROS and minimise OS. The body weight (BW) of antioxidant‐supplemented broilers was greater than that of broilers fed a normal diet. Electrolyte‐supplemented broilers presented decreased mortality and acidosis and increased water intake (Bilal et al. 2021; Bhadauria et al. 2017).

Probiotics and prebiotics are very effective in mitigating the effects of HS in broilers. Probiotics are beneficial microbes, and prebiotics influence their growth. Probiotics enhance the intestinal microflora (Song et al. 2014). Broilers fed probiotics and prebiotics exhibit good growth performance and increased disease resistance (Sohail et al. 2012). Previous findings revealed increased final BW, improved intestinal microflora balance and reduced mortality (Al‐Fataftah and Abdelqader 2014). A high protein or amino acid supply in the diet is also effective against HS. Amino acid‐supplemented broilers exhibit reduced OS in muscle via increased antioxidant enzyme activities, protein supplies and energy supplies (Attia and Hassan 2017; Onagbesan et al. 2023).

Recent studies have demonstrated that targeted nutritional interventions can improve broiler resilience to HS by modulating antioxidant defences, intestinal integrity and cellular metabolism (Al Sulaiman et al. 2024; Mangan and Siwek 2024). Betaine has emerged as an effective osmolyte that maintains cellular hydration, improves intestinal morphology, enhances antioxidant enzyme activities and modulates gut microbiota composition, thereby improving growth performance and feed efficiency in heat‐stressed broilers (Buonaiuto et al. 2025; Abd El‐Ghany and Babazadeh 2022). Supplementation with 1–2 g/kg dietary betaine has been shown to increase average daily gain, improve villus morphology, reduce oxidative damage and increase beneficial intestinal bacteria under cyclic HS (Al Sulaiman et al. 2024; Buonaiuto et al. 2025). In addition, phytogenic feed additives, including curcumin, oregano, thyme, rosemary, resveratrol, quercetin and green tea polyphenols, exhibit antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties. These enhance thermotolerance by reducing ROS production, preserving intestinal barrier integrity and improving immune function (Oni et al. 2024). Furthermore, probiotics, postbiotics and synbiotics have gained considerable attention for restoring gut microbial balance, strengthening intestinal morphology, improving nutrient utilisation and reducing systemic inflammation (Hashemitabar and Hosseinian 2024). Recent studies have reported that Bacillus spp. and Lactobacillus spp. supplementation improves growth performance, antioxidant status, intestinal histomorphology and microbial composition in broilers exposed to chronic HS, while supplementation with probiotics combined with ascorbic acid further enhances thermoregulation and alleviates HS responses (Rakngam et al. 2024; Sumanu et al. 2024). Collectively, these findings indicate that combining antioxidant vitamins, osmolytes, phytogenic compounds and microbiome‐targeted feed additives provide a more comprehensive nutritional strategy than single‐nutrient supplementation for improving broiler welfare, productivity and resilience under HS.

Genetic Approach

HS is a major barrier in the production of meat‐type chickens. In tropical and subtropical regions, broilers suffer from HS and their growth is downregulated. At high ambient temperatures, broilers have difficulty dissipating heat. The entire body is covered with feathers, so the area for heat loss is small. In this case, adaptation of the naked neck (Na) gene could be an excellent method for broilers. In naked‐neck chickens, there are no feathers on the neck, which could increase the surface area available for heat dissipation. The Na gene improves immunity and lowers blood cholesterol levels (Rajkumar et al. 2010). The Na gene increases body weight and the FCR (Mahrous and Radwan 2011). The naked neck gene was associated with increased meat yield and improved dressing percentage (Rajkumar et al. 2010). The Na gene increases HSP70 expression, thereby improving heat resistance in poultry (Galal et al. 2019).

Another genetic way to alleviate HS is the frizzle (F) gene. It reduces feather size and curling, which helps broilers lose heat and maintain body temperature. The F gene increases the productivity and heat tolerance of meat‐type poultry (Kumar et al. 2021). Similarly, birds carrying the F gene have been reported to exhibit an elevated BW and area for heat loss (Abdelmoniem Hanafy et al. 2023). The dwarfism (dw) gene also helps minimise the effects of HS. The Dw gene reduces the body size of poultry and metabolic activities (Pawar et al. 2016). This gene adjusts metabolism to generate less heat in the body and helps maintain balance with environmental temperature. The dw gene decreases body size by 30%–40% and reduces metabolism (Fathi et al. 2022).

Feeding System

A major negative impact of HS is the reduction in FI, which leads to reduced BWG, decreased meat yield and poor performance. To diminish the adverse effects of HS, some feeding schedules and management strategies could be adapted. Feed withdrawal (FWD) is a process in which feed is provided to broilers at cooler times of day. Feeding at midday, when temperatures are high, may cause HS in poultry. However, feeding at a cool time of day (morning or night) helps reduce metabolic heat. The carcass weight and meat production of FWD broilers are greater than those of control broilers (El‐Moniary et al. 2010). It also decreases body heat and improves FI (Bhadauria et al. 2017).

Wet feeding enhances feed consumption, improves BWG and contributes to better growth performance by increasing palatability and reducing the adverse effects of HS (Dei and Bumbie 2011). During the HS period, the FI of the broilers decreased, and the broilers drank more water. Wet feeding maintains water intake and increases FI (dry) (Onagbesan et al. 2023). Early feed restriction (EFR) reduces metabolic heat generation and rice BW. EFR is another feeding system that involves restricting/preventing feeding for a short period at an early age in broilers. It increases heat tolerance and reduces mortality (El‐Moniary et al. 2010).

Other Management Practices

Broilers are forced to grow quickly and produce more meat in modern civilisation, resulting in a high susceptibility to disease and other stressors. HS is a common stressor in tropical countries where summer temperatures exceed 30°C. To mitigate the unfavourable effects of HS on adaptation in broilers in hot environments, proper management is needed. Early heat conditioning (EHC) is a management strategy in which young chicks are exposed to a short period of controlled, non‐lethal heat during early life to induce long‐term thermotolerance and improve their ability to withstand subsequent HS. Kang and Shim (2021) reported that EHC (40°C heat exposure for 24 h at 5 days of age) improved the FCR and BWG before marketing. EHC increases feed consumption and weight gain and decreases mortality (Günal 2013).

EHC has emerged as a promising preventive strategy for improving thermotolerance in broiler chickens. EHC involves exposing chicks to a controlled, non‐lethal period of elevated temperature during the first week of life, allowing the development of long‐lasting physiological adaptations to subsequent heat challenges (Ncho et al. 2021). EHC significantly improves BWG and FI while reducing body temperature during acute HS, indicating enhanced thermoregulatory capacity (Ncho et al. 2021). The beneficial effects of EHC are primarily attributed to developmental programming of the thermoregulatory system, increased expression of heat shock proteins (particularly HSP70), modulation of hypothalamic temperature set‐points and improved antioxidant and immune responses (Ravi et al. 2025; Jeyachandran et al. 2023). Furthermore, embryonic thermal manipulation during incubation (TMI) has been shown to induce epigenetic and metabolic adaptations that enhance heat tolerance after hatch by regulating genes involved in stress responses, energy metabolism, vascularisation and apoptosis (Loyau et al. 2016). Although the effectiveness of EHC depends on the timing, duration and intensity of heat exposure, current evidence suggests that it is a practical, low‐cost management strategy that can complement nutritional supplementation and environmental control to improve broiler resilience under hot climatic conditions (Onagbesan et al. 2023; Goel et al. 2023).

Meteyake et al. (2020) reported that TMI reduced the incubation time and increased the FCR. TMI is the process of incubating eggs at relatively high temperatures, which helps them perform better later in life. TMI reduces heat generation in the long term and increases heat generation in female broilers (Loyau et al. 2013). TMI increases thermotolerance in broilers in the long term (Morita et al. 2016). Furthermore, TMI has been associated with low heat generation and heat tolerance at a later age and increased growth of breast muscle in meat‐type poultry. In addition to this management, housing management should be maintained. Pawar et al. (2016) reported that sufficient ventilation reduces heat and increases productivity. Dark surfaces absorb heat inside the house, and the east–west orientation prevents direct sunlight (Oloyo 2018).

Post‐Mortem Indicators of Heat Stress in Broiler Chickens

Correlations Between On‐Farm Heat Stress and Slaughterhouse Findings

HS in broiler production is frequently associated with suboptimal on‐farm management conditions, including inadequate ventilation systems, high stocking densities, poor litter quality and insufficient environmental control. Under these circumstances, birds may experience chronic or acute thermal stress during the rearing phase, which can significantly compromise their physiological stability and increase their susceptibility to mortality during the preslaughter period (Panel et al. 2023). Several studies have demonstrated that on‐farm HS increases death‐on‐arrival (DOA) rates at slaughterhouses, even when transport conditions are considered adequate. Birds exposed to prolonged HS in the growing phase often exhibit impaired thermoregulation, dehydration and cardiovascular strain, making them less resilient to additional stressors such as catching, handling and transport (Chauvin et al. 2011). Consequently, HS originating in the farm environment should be considered a predisposing factor that increases mortality risk during the final stages of production.

From a pathological perspective, broilers exposed to thermal stress on farms may not exhibit lesions exclusively attributable to HS. During severe HS, broilers experience dehydration, reduced plasma volume, electrolyte imbalance and cardiovascular dysfunction resulting from prolonged panting and excessive heat (Olayiwola and Adedokun 2025). These physiological disturbances impair tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery, leading to hypoxia, endothelial injury and increased vascular permeability. Consequently, blood accumulates in major organs, particularly the lungs, liver and heart, resulting in pulmonary and generalised vascular congestion that becomes evident during post‐mortem examination (Xie et al. 2019). Simultaneously, HS accelerates glycogen depletion and alters muscle energy metabolism before slaughter (Mia et al. 2023). Following death, the remaining muscle glycogen is rapidly converted to lactic acid via anaerobic glycolysis, leading to an accelerated decline in muscle pH, protein denaturation and deterioration in meat quality (Scheffler and Gerrard 2007). Therefore, many post‐mortem lesions and carcass abnormalities observed at slaughter represent the cumulative consequences of physiological disturbances initiated in live birds during HS rather than pathological changes occurring only after death.

However, post‐mortem findings in birds found dead upon arrival commonly include pulmonary congestion, generalised vascular congestion and signs of acute circulatory failure (Kittelsen et al. 2015). These lesions are consistent with physiological collapse following severe stress but lack diagnostic specificity. Importantly, birds subjected to chronic HS during rearing may also develop subclinical metabolic and cardiovascular alterations, which remain undetected until an additional stressor triggers mortality. In addition to mortality, slaughterhouse indicators such as carcass quality traits may reflect prior exposure to HS. Thermal stress during the rearing phase can alter muscle metabolism, increasing susceptibility to abnormal post‐mortem glycolysis and subsequent meat quality defects, particularly under acute preslaughter stress conditions (Wang et al. 2017). These alterations may be exacerbated when birds already compromised by poor environmental conditions on the farm are exposed to further thermal challenges during transport. Inadequate airflow reduces heat dissipation and increases humidity, impairing evaporative cooling through panting. As a result, birds may experience respiratory alkalosis, electrolyte imbalance and OS, which contribute to reduced performance and increased mortality risk (Zaboli et al. 2019). When such conditions persist, the cumulative physiological burden can significantly affect the ability of broilers to cope with additional stressors encountered before slaughter. Current evidence suggests that slaughterhouse data provide valuable indirect information on HS conditions experienced on farms, particularly when interpreted in combination with environmental and management records. Increased DOA rates, seasonal mortality patterns and nonspecific post‐mortem findings, such as pulmonary congestion, should be considered warning signs of inadequate thermal management in broiler houses.

Research Gap and Future Perspectives

Despite extensive research on HS in broilers, several vital knowledge gaps remain. Most current studies primarily focus on growth performance and conventional physiological indicators, whereas the underlying molecular and microbiological mechanisms remain insufficiently explored. Future studies should investigate the role of gut microbiome and host–microbe interactions in mediating HS responses and maintaining intestinal health under elevated environmental temperatures. Advanced omics technologies, including transcriptomics and metabolomics, may provide deeper insights into gene regulation, metabolic adaptation, OS pathways and immune responses associated with HS. Integrating these approaches could improve understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying heat resilience in broiler chickens. In addition, future commercial poultry production systems may benefit from precision nutrition approaches that optimise dietary interventions based on environmental conditions and birds' physiological status. The application of artificial intelligence (AI)‐based monitoring systems and sensor technologies may also support early detection of HS and improve real‐time management decisions at the farm level. Further research should emphasise multidisciplinary approaches integrating nutrition, genetics, environmental management and precision livestock technologies to improve broiler chickens' resilience, welfare, productivity and sustainability under future climate change scenarios. Additionally, future research should incorporate economic evaluations to assess the cost‐effectiveness and practical feasibility of the proposed mitigation strategies across different production systems. These analyses will enable farmers and industry stakeholders to make informed decisions about adopting HS management interventions.

Conclusion

Heat stress has emerged as one of the most significant constraints to sustainable broiler production under a changing climate. The evidence reviewed demonstrates that its adverse effects extend beyond reduced growth performance, encompassing profound disturbances in metabolism, oxidative balance, intestinal integrity, immune competence and meat quality. These interconnected responses highlight that HS is a multifactorial challenge requiring integrated rather than single‐component solutions. Although nutritional supplementation, environmental modification and genetic approaches have shown promising results, their effectiveness depends on production systems, climatic conditions, broiler genotype and management practices. Therefore, future mitigation strategies should emphasise precision nutrition, climate‐smart housing, improved environmental control and the development of heat‐resilient broiler lines. In addition, greater attention should be given to understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying heat tolerance and validating mitigation strategies under commercial production conditions. Advancing these approaches will be essential to improving broiler welfare, maintaining productivity and ensuring the long‐term sustainability of the poultry industry in the face of increasing global temperatures.
