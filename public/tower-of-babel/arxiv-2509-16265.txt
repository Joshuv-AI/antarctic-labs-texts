Limitation of Stoquastic Quantum Annealing: A Structural Perspective https://www.vc-gladius.com
Vicky Choi

Abstract
We analyze the behavior of stoquastic transverse-field quantum annealing (TFQA) on a structured class of Maximum Independent Set (MIS) instances, using the same decomposition framework developed in our companion work on the Dic-Dac-Doa algorithm (Beyond Stoquasticity) [ 1 ] . For these instances, we provide a structural explanation for the anti-crossing arising from the competition between the energies associated with a set of degenerate local minima ( 𝙻𝙼 \mathtt{LM}{} ) and the global minimum ( 𝙶𝙼 \mathtt{GM}{} ), and analytically derive the associated exponentially small gap. Our analysis proceeds in two steps. First, we reduce the dynamics to an effective two-block Hamiltonian ℍ 𝖼𝗈𝗋𝖾 \mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}} , constructed from the bare (decoupled) subsystems associated with the 𝙻𝙼 \mathtt{LM}{} and 𝙶𝙼 \mathtt{GM}{} . This reduction is justified analytically using the structural decomposition. Second, we reformulate the eigenvalue problem as a generalized eigenvalue problem in a non-orthogonal basis constructed from the bare eigenstates of the subsystems. This transformation enables a clean perturbative treatment of the anti-crossing structure, independent of the transverse field—unlike standard perturbation theory approach, which requires treating the transverse field as a small parameter. This paper serves as a supplementary companion to our main work on the Dic-Dac-Doa algorithm [ 1 ] , where we demonstrate how appropriately designed non-stoquastic drivers can bypass this tunneling-induced bottleneck.

2.1
Same-Sign vs Opposite-Sign

The sign structure of quantum states plays a central role in our analysis.
Throughout this paper, all Hamiltonians are real and Hermitian, so we restrict attention to quantum states with real-valued amplitudes: each component has phase
0
(positive) or
\pi
(negative).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\psi(x)|x\rangle
be a quantum state with real amplitudes in a basis
\mathcal{B}
.

•

|\psi\rangle
is called a
same-sign state
if
\psi(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{B}
.
That is, all components are in phase (with relative phase
0
).

•

|\psi\rangle
is called an
opposite-sign state
if there exist
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\psi(x)>0
and
\psi(x^{\prime})<0
.
In this case, some components are out of phase, differing by a relative phase of
\pi
.

Unless stated otherwise, we take
\mathcal{B}
to be the computational basis when referring to same-sign or opposite-sign states.
More generally, the computational basis is assumed whenever the basis is unspecified.

Accordingly, we define the notions of same-sign and opposite-sign bases, sectors, and blocks:

Definition 2.2
.

An orthonormal basis consisting entirely of same-sign states is called a same-sign basis.
A basis that includes at least one opposite-sign state is called an opposite-sign basis.
A subspace spanned by a same-sign basis is called a same-sign sector; otherwise, it is called an opposite-sign sector.
A submatrix (or block) of a Hamiltonian is called a same-sign block if it is expressed in a same-sign basis.
Otherwise, it is referred to as an opposite-sign block.

Example.

The state
\ket{+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})
is a same-sign state,
while
\ket{-}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}-\ket{1})
is an opposite-sign state.
The computational basis
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
is a same-sign basis of
\mathbbm{C}^{2}
,
and the Hadamard basis
\{\ket{+},\ket{-}\}
is an opposite-sign basis.

These definitions connect directly to stoquasticity.
By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, the ground state of a stoquastic Hamiltonian (i.e., one with non-positive off-diagonal entries in a given basis) is a same-sign state in that basis
[
6
,
8
]
.
In particular, when expressed in the computational basis, the ground state of a stoquastic Hamiltonian is necessarily same-sign.
By contrast, a non-stoquastic Hamiltonian may have a ground state that is either same-sign (
Eventually Stoquastic
) or opposite-sign (
Proper Non-stoquastic
)
[
2
]
.
In this paper we focus on the stoquastic case, corresponding to
J_{\mathsf{xx}}=0
.

2.3
Anti-Crossing and the Two-Level Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{B}(w,x)

The term
anti-crossing
is sometimes used loosely, so we begin with a precise notion in the two-level case, then extend it to multi-level systems.
We also introduce a canonical two-level Hamiltonian whose eigensystem will be used throughout our analysis.

2.3.1
Level Repulsion vs. Anti-Crossing

We begin by distinguishing the concept of an
anti-crossing
(also called
avoided-crossing
) from level repulsion.

Consider a generic two-level Hamiltonian of the form

\mathbbm{H}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}(x)&v(x)\\
v(x)&e_{2}(x)\end{bmatrix},

where
e_{1}(x)
,
e_{2}(x)
, and
v(x)
are real-valued functions of a parameter
x
.

The eigenvalues of this Hamiltonian are

\lambda_{\pm}(x)=\tfrac{e_{1}(x)+e_{2}(x)}{2}\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(e_{1}(x)-e_{2}(x))^{2}+4v(x)^{2}},

and the energy gap between them is

\Delta(x):=\lambda_{+}(x)-\lambda_{-}(x)=\sqrt{(e_{1}(x)-e_{2}(x))^{2}+4v(x)^{2}}.

The off-diagonal term
v(x)
induces
level repulsion
: if
v(x)\neq 0
, then the eigenvalues never cross, and the gap
\Delta(x)
remains strictly positive.
Thus, assuming the off-diagonal coupling
v(x)
is nonzero, level repulsion is always present.

Definition 2.4
.

We say that an
anti-crossing
occurs when the two
unperturbed energy levels
e_{1}(x)
and
e_{2}(x)
cross, i.e.,

e_{1}(x_{*})=e_{2}(x_{*})
for some
x_{*}
, and the off-diagonal coupling

v(x_{*})\neq 0
.
In this case the eigenvalue curves form an anti-crossing with gap

\Delta_{\min}=2|v(x_{*})|.

The size of the anti-crossing gap depends on
|v(x_{*})|
: stronger coupling leads to a larger gap, while weaker coupling results in a narrower one.

By contrast, if the two diagonal entries
e_{1}(x)
and
e_{2}(x)
remain well separated for all
x
, then the system exhibits level repulsion but not an anti-crossing. Figure
3
illustrates an example of level repulsion without an anti-crossing.

The eigenvectors of the two-level Hamiltonian are given by

\ket{\lambda_{-}(x)}=\cos\theta(x)\ket{0}+\sin\theta(x)\ket{1},\quad\ket{\lambda_{+}(x)}=-\sin\theta(x)\ket{0}+\cos\theta(x)\ket{1},

where the mixing angle
\theta(x)
satisfies

\tan(2\theta(x))=\tfrac{2v(x)}{e_{1}(x)-e_{2}(x)}.

Thus, near the anti-crossing point
x=x_{*}
, the eigenstates interpolate between the unperturbed basis states.

Remark 2.5
.

The trigonometric expression for eigenvectors in terms of the mixing angle
\theta(x)
,
is equivalent to the rational-form representation

\ket{\lambda_{-}(x)}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma(x)^{2}}}\left(\gamma(x)\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right),\quad\ket{\lambda_{+}(x)}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma(x)^{2}}}\left(\ket{0}-\gamma(x)\ket{1}\right),

where the two parametrizations are related by

\gamma(x)=\tfrac{1}{\tan\theta(x)},\text{and}\quad\tan(2\theta(x))=\tfrac{2v(x)}{e_{1}(x)-e_{2}(x)}.

This rational-form expression is particularly useful for our analysis, as it aligns directly with the basic matrix form introduced below.

Our earlier work
[
2
,
14
]
, including the development of the original
Dic-Dac-Doa
algorithm, was motivated by investigating the structural characteristics of eigenstates around the anti-crossing.

2.3.2
Anti-crossing in a Multi-level System

In a multi-level system, the notion of an anti-crossing extends naturally
by restricting the Hamiltonian to the two-dimensional subspace spanned by
the pair of eigenstates whose unperturbed energies intersect.
This reduction yields a
2\times 2
effective Hamiltonian that captures the
essential structure of the anti-crossing, including both the energy gap and
the interpolating behavior of the eigenstates.
Thus, the same framework as in the two-level case applies.

With this perspective, we refine the definition of an
(L,R)
-anti-crossing
given in
[
2
]

E_{0}^{A}(t)

\mathbbm{H}_{A}(t)
projected to the subspace spanned by
the subsets of

In particular,
E_{0}^{\mathtt{LM}}(t)
and
E_{0}^{\mathtt{GM}}(t)
denote the bare energies
associated with
\mathtt{LM}
(where

\mathtt{GM}
, respectively.

Definition 2.6
.

We say an anti-crossing is an

-anti-crossing at

and

such that:

1.

and

approximate the unperturbed energy levels

2.

E_{0}^{L}(t)
and

3.1
Reduction to the Analysis of the Low-Energy Hamiltonian

Let
\mathcal{V}_{L}
be the Hilbert space of all vertices in the union of cliques in
L
.
This space decomposes into:

•

a
low-energy subspace
, spanned by all independent-set states within
L
;

•

a
high-energy subspace
, spanned by all dependent-set states within
L
.

We define

\mathcal{L}_{-}:=(\text{low-energy subspace of }\mathcal{V}_{L})\otimes\mathcal{V}_{R},\quad\mathcal{L}_{+}:=(\text{high-energy subspace of }\mathcal{V}_{L})\otimes\mathcal{V}_{R}.

Here,
\mathcal{L}_{+}
consists of states containing at least one intra-clique edge
and thus incurring the
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
penalty.

Let
\Pi_{-}
and
\Pi_{+}
be the orthogonal projectors onto

\mathcal{L}_{-}
and
\mathcal{L}_{+}
, respectively.
With respect to this decomposition, the system Hamiltonian can be written in block form:

\mathbbm{H}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}&\mathbbm{V}\\
\mathbbm{V}^{\dagger}&\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{high}}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}
and
\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{high}}
are the projections into

\mathcal{L}_{-}
and
\mathcal{L}_{+}
, respectively.

In the main paper, we showed that by the end of Stage 0, if

J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}
is chosen sufficiently large so that all states in

\mathcal{L}_{+}
lie well above those in
\mathcal{L}_{-}
in energy, the Stage 1
evolution is governed exactly by the restricted Hamiltonian

\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}=\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}
.
The subsequent analysis can therefore focus entirely on
\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}
.

In the present
J_{\mathsf{xx}}=0
setting, we have not identified the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver
graph explicitly, and we set
J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\mathsf{clique}}=J_{\mathsf{zz}}
, so we cannot
assume an energetic cut-off between
\mathcal{L}_{+}
and
\mathcal{L}_{-}
.
Nevertheless, removing higher-energy subspaces from a Hamiltonian cannot

decrease
the spectral gap, since doing so can only eliminate potential
low-lying excitations.
Consequently, if
\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}
already has a small gap, the full
Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}
can have a gap no larger.
Because the ground state lies in
\mathcal{L}_{-}
prior to the anti-crossing,
we have

\Delta(\mathbbm{H})\ \leq\ \Delta(\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}).

This allows us to use
\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}
to obtain an
upper bound

on the spectral gap of the full Hamiltonian.
Thus, in both cases—whether with or without the
\mathsf{XX}
-driver—the
subsequent analysis focuses on
\mathbbm{H}_{1}^{\mathsf{eff}}=\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}
.

Having reduced our focus to the low-energy subspace
\mathcal{L}_{-}
,
we next construct an angular momentum basis that reveals the block
structure of
\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}
and sets up the tunneling analysis.

3.2
Angular-Momentum Based Decomposition of the
\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}

Our construction starts from the local angular momentum structure of a single
clique, restricted to the low-energy subspace (see also
[
9
]
).
We then extend this to a global angular-momentum basis for
\mathcal{L}_{-}
,
in which
\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}
becomes block-diagonal, separating

same-sign
and
opposite-sign
blocks.

The construction proceeds hierarchically:

•

Single-clique decomposition.

We analyze the low-energy subspace of a single clique in detail,
introducing its total angular momentum basis, identifying the

).

•

Global block decomposition.

By tensoring the single-clique bases over all cliques in
L

\mathcal{V}_{R}

\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}}

3.2.1
Single-clique Decomposition

In this section, we focus on analyzing the Hilbert space associated with a single clique.
We begin with a single clique, since each clique in
L
exhibits the
same local angular momentum structure; this will serve as the building
block for the global block decomposition in Section
3.2.2
.

To fix notation, let
G_{c}=(V,E)
be a clique,
where
V=\{1,\dots,n_{c}\}
is the set of vertices, and

E=\{(i,j):i<j,\;i,j\in V\}
is the set of edges.
We assume all vertices in the clique have the same weight
w_{c}
,
i.e.,
w_{i}=w_{c}
for all
i\in V
.

The Hilbert space
\mathcal{V}_{c}
for a clique of size
n_{c}
consists of
2^{n_{c}}
computational basis states, each corresponding to a binary string of length
n_{c}
. Among these, only
n_{c}+1
basis states correspond to independent sets:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbf{b}_{0}=\texttt{00\ldots 0}\\
\mathbf{b}_{1}=\texttt{10\ldots 0}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}=\texttt{01\ldots 0}\\
\quad\vdots\\
\mathbf{b}_{n_{c}}=\texttt{00\ldots 1}\end{array}

with

\mathbb{N}_{\text{ind}}=\left\{\mathbf{b}_{0},\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{n_{c}}\right\},

where each
\mathbf{b}_{i}
is a binary string of length
n_{c}
with a single 1 in position
i
(and 0s elsewhere), and
\mathbf{b}_{0}
is the all-zero string.

The energy associated with each singleton state is
-w_{c}
, while the empty set has energy 0.
In contrast, the energy of all other bit strings—which correspond to dependent sets—is at least
(J_{\mathsf{zz}}^{\text{clique}}-2w_{c})
.
Hence, the Hilbert space admits a natural decomposition:

\mathcal{V}_{c}=\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\text{dep}},

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
is the low-energy subspace spanned by
\mathbb{N}_{\text{ind}}
,
and
\mathcal{H}^{\text{dep}}
is the high-energy subspace spanned by the dependent-set states.

In the following, we identify how
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
decomposes into distinct angular momentum sectors, describe the corresponding block structure of the restricted Hamiltonian,
and provide exact expressions for its eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Low-Energy Subspace in Total Angular Momentum Basis
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}

Lemma 3.1

(Lemma 6.1 in
[
1
]
)
.

The total angular momentum basis for
\mathcal{L}^{\text{ind}}
consists of the states:

\displaystyle({\mathcal{B}}_{a})\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\ket{s,-(s-1)},&\ket{1,(s-1),-(s-1)},&\ldots&\ket{n_{c}-1,(s-1),-(s-1)}\\
\ket{s,-s}&&&\end{array}\right.

where
s=\tfrac{n_{c}}{2}
is the total spin.

Explicitly:

•

\ket{s,-s}=\ket{\mathbf{b}_{0}}
, representing the empty set.

•

\ket{s,-(s-1)}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n_{c}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\ket{\mathbf{b}_{i}}
, a uniform superposition of all singletons with positive amplitudes.

•

\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}
, for
k=1,\ldots,n_{c}-1
, consists of a superposition of singleton states with both positive and negative amplitudes.

Thus,
\ket{s,-s}
and
\ket{s,-(s-1)}
are same-sign states, while
\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}
are opposite-sign states.

Remark (Basis Reordering).

For convenience, we reorder the basis states in
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}
as follows:

({\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime})\quad\ket{s,-(s-1)},\,\ket{s,-s},\,\ket{1,s-1,-(s-1)},\,\dots,\,\ket{n_{c}-1,s-1,-(s-1)}.

That is, we swap the order of the two same-sign basis states.
This ordering simplifies the representation of operators in the next steps.

Basis Transformation.

The transformation between the computational basis
\{\ket{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\}
and the angular momentum basis
({\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime})
can be derived either from the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients or directly from the relationships established in the proof. Specifically:

•

The state
\ket{s,-(s-1)}
is a uniform superposition over all singleton states
\{\ket{\mathbf{b}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n_{c}}
.

•

The remaining states
\ket{k,s-1,-(s-1)}
, for
k=1,\dots,n_{c}-1
, form an orthogonal complement to
\ket{s,-(s-1)}
within the subspace spanned by
\{\ket{\mathbf{b}_{1}},\dots,\ket{\mathbf{b}_{n_{c}}}\}
.

We denote the basis transformation matrix from the computational basis to the angular momentum basis by
{\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{clique}}}
.

Although the present analysis will later specialize to the

J_{\mathsf{xx}}=0
case, we retain the
J_{\mathsf{xx}}
term throughout the
single-clique derivation. This ensures that the resulting
expressions apply to the general setting and remain consistent
with the corresponding formulas in the companion paper.

Spin Operators in the
{\mathcal{B}}_{a}^{\prime}
Basis

Consider the spin operators on
\mathcal{V}_{c}
:

\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{Z}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\sigma_{i}^{z},\quad\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\tilde{\sigma}^{z}_{i},\quad\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{c}}\sigma_{i}^{x},\quad\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{XX}}=\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{ij\in{\mathsf{E}}(G_{\text{driver}})}\sigma_{i}^{x}\sigma_{j}^{x},

where

We project these operators onto the low-energy subspace

\overline{\mathrm{X}}={\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{clique}}}^{\dagger}{\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}\mathrm{X}{\Pi^{\tiny{\mathsf{ind}}}}{\mathbf{U}^{\mathsf{clique}}}.

4.0.1
Two Block Decompositions of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}

-Inner vs.

Physically, the same-sign block Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
is symmetric under interchange of the
L
and
R

Mathematically, this corresponds to a permutation similarity transformation: reordering the basis to place
L

R

Combinatorially, this symmetry allows the matrix to be organized into a two-layer block structure with either subsystem—
L
or
R

That is,
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
can be expressed either as a collection of
L

R
-blocks indexed by the states from
L
.

For illustration, we assume uniform clique size
n_{i}=n_{c}
for all

.
As in the main paper, we restrict the same-sign block Hamiltonian to the symmetric subspace:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathsf{sym}}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}\otimes\mathbbm{I}_{R}+\mathbbm{I}_{L}\otimes\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}+\mathbbm{H}_{LR},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{n_{c}}\,\mathtt{x}\,\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}(m)-w^{\text{\tiny eff}}\,\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}(m),

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}

\displaystyle=-\mathtt{x}(t)\,\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}(m_{r})-w\,\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}(m_{r}),

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{LR}

\displaystyle=J_{\mathsf{zz}}\,\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}(m)\cdot\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}(m_{r}).

Here

\displaystyle\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{\tilde{Z}}}(m)=\begin{bmatrix}m&0&\cdots&0\\
0&m-1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbbm{C}\mathrm{S}_{\mathsf{X}}(m)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\tfrac{\sqrt{m}}{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
\tfrac{\sqrt{m}}{2}&0&\tfrac{\sqrt{2(m-1)}}{2}&\cdots&0\\
0&\tfrac{\sqrt{2(m-1)}}{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\tfrac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\\
0&0&0&\tfrac{\sqrt{m}}{2}&0\end{bmatrix}.

5
Detailed Analysis of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}

In this section, we analyze the spectrum of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
using the exact eigensystems of the embedded bare subsystems
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{(0)}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{(0)}
, each of which is analytically solvable.
The analysis proceeds by reformulating the eigenvalue problem as a generalized eigenvalue problem, using a non-orthogonal basis constructed by extending the bare eigenstates of the subsystems to the full Hilbert space.

This transformation enables a clean perturbative treatment and forms the foundation for a structured spectral analysis. The approach proceeds in three main steps:

•

Describe the bare eigenstates of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
(Section
5.1
).

•

Reformulate the eigenvalue problem for
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
as a generalized eigenvalue problem in the non-orthogonal basis (Section
5.2
).

•

Develop the perturbative structure of the generalized eigenvalue system (Section
5.3
), including:

1.

Showing that the true energy values (away from the anti-crossing) are well-approximated by the bare energies (Section
5.3.1
).

2.

Analyzing the anti-crossing structure by approximating the lowest two energy levels, both away from and near the crossing,
and deriving a perturbative bound for the anti-crossing gap using an effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian (Section
5.3.3
).

Remark 5.1
.

The final step, constructing a reduced effective Hamiltonian for the generalized system, is structurally similar to the method of effective Hamiltonians for the non-orthogonal basis developed in
[
20
]
. However, in their setting, the non-orthogonal basis is derived from the physical structure of the original problem (e.g., localized atomic orbitals).
In contrast, our non-orthogonal basis is synthetically constructed from bare eigenstates and does not correspond to the physical structure of the full system.

5.1
Bare Eigenstates of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}

In this section, we describe the bare eigenstates of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
by relating the embedded bare operators
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{(0)}
,
\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{(0)}
to the bare subsystem Hamiltonians
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}
,
\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}
.
Recall that the effective Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
is

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}=\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{(0)}+\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{(0)},

where

\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{(0)}=\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}\otimes\ket{0}_{R}\bra{0},\quad\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{(0)}=\ket{0}_{L}\bra{0}\otimes\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}.

The eigenvalues and eigenstates of the the bare Hamiltonians
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}
,
\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}
are

\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}\ket{L_{i}}=e_{L_{i}}\ket{L_{i}},\quad\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{\mathsf{bare}}\ket{R_{j}}=e_{R_{j}}\ket{R_{j}},

where
\{\ket{L_{i}}\}
and
\{\ket{R_{j}}\}
are analytically known (Section 7.2 in
[
1
]
)
and form orthonormal eigenbases of the
L
- and
R
-subspaces.

The corresponding padded eigenstates in the full space are

\ket{\overline{L}_{i}}=\ket{L_{i}}\otimes\ket{\emptyset}_{R},\quad\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}=\ket{\emptyset}_{L}\otimes\ket{R_{j}},

and they satisfy

\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{(0)}\ket{\overline{L}_{i}}=e_{L_{i}}\ket{\overline{L}_{i}},\quad\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{(0)}\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}=e_{R_{j}}\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}.

Remark 5.2
.

We use a bar to distinguish padded eigenstates of the full Hilbert space from bare eigenstates of the subsystem Hamiltonians.
For instance,
\ket{L_{i}}
is an eigenstate of the bare Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{\mathsf{bare}}
acting on the
L
-subspace, while
\ket{\overline{L}_{i}}=\ket{L_{i}}\otimes\ket{\emptyset}_{R}
is the corresponding eigenstate of the embedded operator
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{(0)}
acting on the full Hilbert space. The same applies to
\ket{R_{j}}
and
\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}
.

Recall that the full eigensystem for both
\mathbbm{H}_{L}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{R}
(and thus for
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{(0)}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{(0)}
) is analytically known.
In the following, we first derive the results in terms of the bare eigenstates
\ket{\overline{L}_{i}}
and
\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}
, and then substitute their explicit forms in the anti-crossing structure section later.

5.2
Generalized Eigenvalue Reformulation

We express the Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
in the basis of padded bare eigenstates

\left\{\ket{\overline{L}_{i}}\right\}\cup\left\{\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}\right\}
, which leads to a generalized eigenvalue problem.
We define

\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{H}_{RR}&\mathbbm{H}_{RL}\\
\mathbbm{H}_{LR}&\mathbbm{H}_{LL}\end{bmatrix},\quad\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{S}_{RR}&\mathbbm{S}_{RL}\\
\mathbbm{S}_{LR}&\mathbbm{S}_{LL}\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathbbm{H}_{RR}
contains the entries
\bra{\overline{R}_{i}}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}
, and similarly for
\mathbbm{H}_{LL}
,
\mathbbm{H}_{LR}
, and
\mathbbm{H}_{RL}
.
The matrix
\mathbbm{S}_{RR}
contains the overlaps
\langle\overline{R}_{i}|\overline{R}_{j}\rangle
, and similarly for the remaining blocks of
\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}
.

We now consider the generalized eigenvalue equation:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}}=\widetilde{E}_{n}\,\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}},

(5.1)

where the pair
(\widetilde{E}_{n},\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}})
is referred to as a
generalized eigenpair
of the generalized eigenvalue system
(\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}},\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}})
.
We refer to
\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}
as the
generalized Hamiltonian
, and
\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}
as the corresponding
overlap matrix
.
In the numerical linear algebra literature, such pairs are also called
matrix pencils
.
4
4

4

The term “matrix pencil” refers to the parametrized family
\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}-\lambda\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}
, whose roots
\lambda=\widetilde{E}_{n}
yield the eigenvalues of the system.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
(E_{n},\ket{E_{n}})
be an eigenpair of the original Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
, and let
(\widetilde{E}_{n},\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}})
be a generalized eigenpair of
(\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}},\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}})
as in (
5.1
).
Then:

•

Every eigenvalue
E_{n}
of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
appears as a generalized eigenvalue
\widetilde{E}_{n}
.

•

Conversely, every generalized eigenvalue
\widetilde{E}_{n}
corresponds to an eigenvalue
E_{n}
of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
.

The eigenvalues coincide:
\widetilde{E}_{n}=E_{n}
for all
n
, while the eigenvectors
\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}}
and
\ket{E_{n}}
differ.

Proof.

We can write the eigenstate
\ket{E_{n}}
of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
in the padded bare basis as

\ket{E_{n}}=\sum_{k=0}^{2^{m_{R}}-1}c^{\mathsf{R}}_{k}\ket{\overline{R}_{k}}+\sum_{k=0}^{2^{m_{L}}-1}c^{\mathsf{L}}_{k}\ket{\overline{L}_{k}}.

Note that the coefficients
c^{\mathsf{R}}
and
c^{\mathsf{L}}
may not be unique, since the set
\{\ket{\overline{R}_{k}},\ket{\overline{L}_{k}}\}
spans a space of dimension
2^{m_{L}}+2^{m_{R}}
, whereas the true Hilbert space of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
has dimension
2^{m_{L}}+2^{m_{R}}-1
due to the identification
\ket{\overline{R}_{0}}=\ket{\overline{L}_{0}}
. Thus, the padded set is linearly dependent.

Now rewrite the eigenvalue equation:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}\left(\sum_{k}c^{\mathsf{R}}_{k}\ket{\overline{R}_{k}}+\sum_{k}c^{\mathsf{L}}_{k}\ket{\overline{L}_{k}}\right)=E_{n}\left(\sum_{k}c^{\mathsf{R}}_{k}\ket{\overline{R}_{k}}+\sum_{k}c^{\mathsf{L}}_{k}\ket{\overline{L}_{k}}\right).

Taking the inner product with
\bra{\overline{R}_{i}}
and
\bra{\overline{L}_{i}}
, we obtain the generalized eigenvalue equation in matrix form:

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{H}_{RR}&\mathbbm{H}_{RL}\\
\mathbbm{H}_{LR}&\mathbbm{H}_{LL}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\vec{c}_{\mathsf{R}}\\
\vec{c}_{\mathsf{L}}\end{bmatrix}=E_{n}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{S}_{RR}&\mathbbm{S}_{RL}\\
\mathbbm{S}_{LR}&\mathbbm{S}_{LL}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\vec{c}_{\mathsf{R}}\\
\vec{c}_{\mathsf{L}}\end{bmatrix}.

Then we have

\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}}=\widetilde{E}_{n}\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}},

with
\widetilde{E}_{n}=E_{n}
and
\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{c}_{\mathsf{R}}\\
\vec{c}_{\mathsf{L}}\end{bmatrix}
.

This shows that every eigenpair
(E_{n},\ket{E_{n}})
of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
corresponds to a generalized eigenpair
(\widetilde{E}_{n},\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}})
of
(\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}},\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}})
, though
\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}}
may not be unique.
Conversely, each generalized eigenpair
(\widetilde{E}_{n},\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}})
of
(\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}},\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}})
determines an eigenpair
(E_{n},\ket{E_{n}})
of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
with
\widetilde{E}_{n}=E_{n}
.
∎

Summary of the Reformulation.

We express the eigenvalue problem for
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
in a non-orthogonal (normalized) basis:

\big\{\ket{\overline{L}_{i}}\big\}\cup\big\{\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}\big\}.

Each subset is orthonormal, but the two sets are not mutually orthogonal.

This yields a generalized eigenvalue problem of the form

\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}}=\widetilde{E}_{n}\,\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}\ket{\widetilde{E}_{n}}.

This reformulation preserves the spectrum:

\boxed{\widetilde{E}_{n}=E_{n}\quad\text{for all }n,}

allowing us to analyze the dynamics using the generalized eigenvalue system
(\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}},\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}})
.
This forms the foundation for our perturbative analysis in the next subsection.

5.3
Perturbative Structure of the Generalized Eigenvalue System

In this section, we develop the perturbative structure of the generalized eigenvalue system
(\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}},\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}})
, and use it to analyze the spectrum of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
. Specifically:

1.

In Section
5.3.1
, we show that the true energy values of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
(away from the anti-crossing) are well-approximated by the bare energies.

2.

In Section
5.3.3
, we derive a
2\times 2
effective Hamiltonian that captures the tunneling-induced anti-crossing and provides a perturbative bound on the gap size.

We decompose the generalized Hamiltonian and overlap matrix as

\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}=\mathbbm{H}+{\Delta\mathbbm{H}},\quad\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}=\mathbbm{I}+{\Delta\mathbbm{I}},

where

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{H}_{RR}&0\\
0&\mathbbm{H}_{LL}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{(block-diagonal)},

\displaystyle{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbbm{H}_{RL}\\
\mathbbm{H}_{LR}&0\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{(off-diagonal)},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{I}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{I}_{RR}&0\\
0&\mathbbm{I}_{LL}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathbbm{S}_{RL}\\
\mathbbm{S}_{LR}&0\end{bmatrix}.

We now introduce a parameterized family of matrices:

\mathbbm{H}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbbm{H}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{H}},\quad\mathbbm{I}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}},\quad\lambda\in[0,1),

and consider the associated generalized eigenvalue problem:

\mathbbm{H}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}=e_{n}(\lambda)\mathbbm{I}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}.

At
\lambda=0
, the eigenvalues
e_{n}(0)
correspond to the bare subsystem energies.
At
\lambda=1
, the eigenvalues
e_{n}(1)=E_{n}
recover the true eigenvalues of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
.

We analyze how these eigenvalues deform as a function of
\lambda
.

Remark 5.4
.

There is one more eigenvalue in the unperturbed generalized eigenvalue system
(\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}(0),\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}(0))
than in the fully coupled system
(\widetilde{\mathbbm{H}}(1),\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}(1))
.
While perturbations typically split degenerate energy levels, here the effect is reversed: the perturbation merges two distinct eigenvalues into a single degenerate level.
In particular, the zero-energy levels from both subsystems appear to collapse into one.

Although
\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}=\mathbbm{I}+{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
is not invertible, the matrix
\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
is invertible for all
\lambda\in[0,1)
, as shown in the lemma below.

Lemma 5.5
.

The matrix
\mathbbm{I}+{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
is not invertible. However, for all
\lambda\in[0,1)
, the matrix
\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
is invertible.

Proof.

The overlap matrix
\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}=\mathbbm{I}+{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
is defined using the padded bare basis
\{\ket{\overline{L}_{k}},\ket{\overline{R}_{k}}\}
, where
\ket{\overline{L}_{0}}=\ket{\overline{R}_{0}}
.
This identification introduces a linear dependence in the set, so
\widetilde{\mathbbm{S}}
is singular and not invertible.

In contrast, for any
\lambda\in[0,1)
, the matrix
\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
is a Hermitian perturbation of the identity by an operator of strictly subunit norm.
All eigenvalues of
\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
lie strictly within
(-1,1)
, so the eigenvalues of
\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
are strictly positive.
Hence,
\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
is invertible.
∎

Using Lemma
5.5
, we can now convert the generalized eigenvalue
problem into a standard eigenvalue problem.
For
\lambda\in[0,1)
, we rewrite the generalized eigenvalue problem

\left(\mathbbm{H}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\right)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}=e_{n}(\lambda)\left(\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)},

in standard form using the transformation

\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left(\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)^{-1/2}.

This yields the equivalent standard eigenvalue equation:

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{QI}}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}=e_{n}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}.

where
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{QI}}(\lambda):=\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left(\mathbbm{H}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)
.
We refer to
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{QI}}(\lambda)
as the
quasi-interpolated Hamiltonian
.
At
\lambda=0
, the unperturbed equation becomes

\mathbbm{H}\ket{e_{n}}=e_{n}\ket{e_{n}},

where
e_{n}=e_{n}(0)
denotes the bare eigenvalue.

5.3.1
Perturbative Approximation Scheme for the True Energies

In this section, we develop a perturbative scheme for approximating the difference

e_{n}(1)-e_{n}
, where
e_{n}(1)=E_{n}
denotes the true eigenvalue of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
,
and
e_{n}=e_{n}(0)
is the corresponding bare eigenvalue.
We begin by showing in Theorem
5.6
that for any
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
the eigenvalue deformation
e_{n}(\lambda)-e_{n}
is exactly captured by a quadratic form involving the transformation matrix
\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)
and the coupling term
{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
.
Then, in Proposition
5.7
, we show that when the level
e_{n}
is non-degenerate
(i.e., well-separated from other bare levels), the eigenvalue deformation admits a second-order perturbative expansion in
\lambda
.
Finally, we approximate the true energy at
\lambda=1
using the same expansion, as given in Corollary
5.8
.

Theorem 5.6
.

For
0<\lambda<1
, let
e_{n}=e_{n}(0)
denote the unperturbed eigenvalue, and
\ket{e_{n}^{(0)}}
its corresponding eigenvector.
Then the perturbed eigenvalue
e_{n}(\lambda)
satisfies

e_{n}(\lambda)-e_{n}=\lambda\bra{e_{n}^{(0)}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}.

Proof.

First, add and subtract an auxiliary term
\lambda e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
to
\mathbbm{H}(\lambda)
:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}(\lambda)=\mathbbm{H}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}=\left(\mathbbm{H}+\lambda e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)+\lambda\left({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right).

We now express
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{QI}}(\lambda)
in terms of the unperturbed Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{QI}}(\lambda)=\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left(\mathbbm{H}+\lambda e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)+\lambda\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda).

(5.2)

This holds because

\displaystyle\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left(\mathbbm{H}+\lambda e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}}=e_{n}\ket{e_{n}},

(5.3)

which follows from adding the term
\lambda e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
to both sides of the unperturbed eigenvalue equation:

\left(\mathbbm{H}+\lambda e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\ket{e_{n}}=e_{n}(\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{n}}.

Next, consider the quantity
\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\mathbbm{H}(\lambda)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}
.
We compute it two ways—acting from the right and from the left.

From the right: by the generalized eigenvalue equation,

\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{QI}}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}=e_{n}(\lambda)\braket{e_{n}|e_{n}(\lambda)}.

From the left: using Eqs. (
5.2
) and (
5.3
),

\displaystyle\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{QI}}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left(\mathbbm{H}+\lambda e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=e_{n}\braket{e_{n}|e_{n}(\lambda)}+\lambda\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}.

Now expand
\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}=\ket{e_{n}}+\lambda\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}+\lambda^{2}\ket{e_{n}^{(2)}}+\cdots
as a power series in
\lambda
.
Assuming orthogonality
\braket{e_{n}|e_{n}^{(k)}}=0
for
k\geq 1
, we have
\braket{e_{n}|e_{n}(\lambda)}=1
.
Therefore,

e_{n}(\lambda)-e_{n}=\lambda\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}.

∎

Proposition 5.7
.

For
0<\lambda<1
, suppose the eigenvalue
e_{n}
is non-degenerate, i.e.,
|e_{k}^{(0)}-e_{n}|
is bounded away from zero for all
k\neq n
. Then

\displaystyle e_{n}(\lambda)-e_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda^{2}\left(\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}){\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\ket{e_{n}}-\sum_{k\neq n}\tfrac{\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{k}^{(0)}}\bra{e_{k}^{(0)}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\ket{e_{n}}}{e_{k}^{(0)}-e_{n}}\right).

Proof.

From Theorem
5.6
, we have

e_{n}(\lambda)-e_{n}=\lambda\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\left({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\right)\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}.

Up to first-order approximation, write
\ket{e_{n}(\lambda)}\approx\ket{e_{n}}+\lambda\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}
. Then

\displaystyle e_{n}(\lambda)-e_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}}+\lambda^{2}\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}.

(5.4)

We now approximate

\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)=(\mathbbm{I}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})^{-1/2}\approx\mathbbm{I}-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\approx(\mathbbm{I}-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})(\mathbbm{I}-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})

\displaystyle=({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})-\lambda({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}){\Delta\mathbbm{I}}+\tfrac{1}{4}\lambda^{2}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})-\lambda({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}){\Delta\mathbbm{I}},

where we used the identity
{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}={\Delta\mathbbm{H}}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
.

Now note that
\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{n}}=0
, since both
{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}
and
{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}
are zero on the diagonal blocks.

Therefore, the first term of Eq. (
5.4
) becomes

\lambda\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}}\approx\lambda^{2}\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}){\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\ket{e_{n}},

and the second term becomes

\lambda^{2}\bra{e_{n}}\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\mathbbm{T}(\lambda)\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}\approx\lambda^{2}\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}.

To compute
\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}
, note that

\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{QI}}(\lambda)\approx\mathbbm{H}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}+\lambda^{2}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}+\cdots,

so the first-order correction
\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}
agrees with standard non-degenerate perturbation theory for
\mathbbm{H}+\lambda{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}
.
That is,

\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}=-\sum_{k\neq n}\tfrac{\ket{e_{k}^{(0)}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}_{kn}}{e_{k}^{(0)}-e_{n}^{(0)}},\qquad\text{where }{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}_{kn}=\bra{e_{k}^{(0)}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\ket{e_{n}},\text{ and }e_{n}=e_{n}^{(0)}.

Then,

\displaystyle\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{n}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\neq n}\tfrac{\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{k}^{(0)}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}_{kn}}{e_{k}^{(0)}-e_{n}^{(0)}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\neq n}\tfrac{\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{k}^{(0)}}\bra{e_{k}^{(0)}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\ket{e_{n}}}{e_{k}^{(0)}-e_{n}^{(0)}}.

Thus, the full second-order correction is

e_{n}(\lambda)-e_{n}\approx\lambda^{2}\left(\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}){\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\ket{e_{n}}-\sum_{k\neq n}\tfrac{\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{k}^{(0)}}\bra{e_{k}^{(0)}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\ket{e_{n}}}{e_{k}^{(0)}-e_{n}}\right).

∎

Corollary 5.8
.

If
|e_{k}^{(0)}-e_{n}|
is bounded away from zero for all
k\neq n
(i.e.,
e_{n}
is non-degenerate), then

\displaystyle e_{n}(1)-e_{n}\approx\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}){\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\ket{e_{n}}-\sum_{k\neq n}\tfrac{\bra{e_{n}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{n}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{k}^{(0)}}\bra{e_{k}^{(0)}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\ket{e_{n}}}{e_{k}^{(0)}-e_{n}}.

(5.5)

Exact Bare Eigenvalues and Eigenstates and Some Combinatorial Facts

In this section, we recall the exact eigenvalues and eigenstates of the bare subsystems
\mathbbm{H}_{L}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{R}

(from Section 7 of
[
1
]
), and state several combinatorial identities that will be used in deriving the bounds in the next section.

For simplicity, we assume the unweighted case with
w_{r}=w_{l}=w(=1)
, and take
R
to be the unique global minimum with
n_{R}=1
. We also assume that all cliques in
L
have uniform size
n_{c}=n_{L}
, and that
m_{r}=:m_{R}>m_{L}:=m_{l}
but
m_{R}<m_{L}\sqrt{n_{c}}
, so that the bare (i.e., unperturbed) energies cross at some value
\mathtt{x}=x_{c}
.

Remark 5.9

(Notation)
.

In describing the bipartite graphs (
G_{\mathsf{dis}}
and
G_{\mathsf{share}}
),
we use
m_{l},m_{r}
and
n_{l},n_{r}
for the sizes of the left and right
vertex sets and cliques, respectively.
In the following analytical sections, we switch to
m_{L},m_{R}
and
n_{L},n_{R}
,
matching the convention
\chi_{A}
for
A\in\{L,R\}
used for other indexed quantities
(e.g.,
E_{A_{k}}
,
\ket{A_{k}}
).
These pairs of symbols refer to the same quantities.

Application of Exact Spectrum.

We apply the exact spectrum and eigenstates of the bare subsystem for
\mathbbm{H}_{L}
,
\mathbbm{H}_{R}
from Theorem 7.1 in
[
1
]

to the case where
n_{i}=n_{A}
for all
i\in A\in\{L,R\}
, and
w=1
. This yields the following exact form for the bare eigenvalues and eigenstates:

Theorem 5.10

(Bare Eigenvalues and Ground States for
\mathbbm{H}_{L}
,
\mathbbm{H}_{R}
)
.

For
A\in\{L,R\}
, the eigenvalues of
\mathbbm{H}_{A}
are given by:

\displaystyle e_{A_{k}}=-\left(\tfrac{m_{A}}{2}w+(\tfrac{m_{A}}{2}-k)\sqrt{w^{2}+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}\right),\quad k=0,\ldots,m_{A}.

(5.6)

(
k
is the number of one’s).

Each eigenstate is indexed by a bit string
z=z_{1}z_{2}\ldots z_{m_{A}}\in\{0,1\}^{m_{A}}
, with:

\displaystyle\ket{E_{z}^{(A)}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m_{A}}\ket{\beta_{z_{i}}^{(A)}},

(5.7)

where
\ket{\beta_{0}^{(A)}}
and
\ket{\beta_{1}^{(A)}}
are the eigenvectors of the local two-level system:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ket{\beta_{0}^{(A)}}&=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}}\left(\gamma_{A}\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right),\\[4.0pt]
\ket{\beta_{1}^{(A)}}&=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}}\left(\ket{0}-\gamma_{A}\ket{1}\right),\end{cases}

with mixing ratio

\gamma_{A}=\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{A}}\mathtt{x}}{w+\sqrt{w^{2}+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}}.

Ground State Energies and Vectors.

In particular, the ground states of
\mathbbm{H}_{L}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{R}
correspond to the all-zero string
z=0\ldots 0
, and take the form:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ket{R_{0}}&=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m_{R}}\ket{\beta_{0}^{(R)}},\\[4.0pt]
\ket{L_{0}}&=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m_{L}}\ket{\beta_{0}^{(L)}}.\end{cases}

The corresponding ground state energies are:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}e_{L_{0}}&=-\tfrac{m_{L}}{2}\left(w+\sqrt{w^{2}+n_{L}\mathtt{x}^{2}}\right),\\
e_{R_{0}}&=-\tfrac{m_{R}}{2}\left(w+\sqrt{w^{2}+n_{R}\mathtt{x}^{2}}\right).\end{cases}

We define the overlap of an eigenstate
\ket{A_{k}}
, where
k
denotes the number of 1’s in the bit string indexing the state, with the all-zero computational basis state as

{\mathsf{co}}(A_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\braket{A_{k}\mid 0_{A}},

where
\ket{0_{A}}\equiv\ket{0}^{\otimes m_{A}}
is the zero state on subsystem
A\in\{L,R\}
.

The following combinatorial identities will be used in the gap analysis:

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
A\in\{L,R\}
and let
\ket{A_{k}}
denote any eigenstate with Hamming weight
k
. Then the overlap of
\ket{A_{k}}
with the all-zero computational state is:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{co}}(A_{k})=\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}}\right)^{m_{A}}\gamma_{A}^{m_{A}-k}

(5.8)

In particular, for the ground state
\ket{A_{0}}
, corresponding to
k=0
, we have:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{co}}(A_{0})=\left(\tfrac{\gamma_{A}}{\sqrt{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}}\right)^{m_{A}}

(5.9)

One can verify the following non-obvious identity:

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
\gamma_{A}=\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{A}}\mathtt{x}}{1+\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}}
, we have

\tfrac{1-\gamma_{A}^{2}}{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}}.

Together with the two well-known binomial summation identities:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}a^{k}

\displaystyle=(1+a)^{m},\quad\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}ka^{k}=\tfrac{a}{1+a}\,m(1+a)^{m},

(5.10)

we have the folowing perhaps surprising identity.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
A\in\{L,R\}
, and let
e_{A_{k}}
denote the energy of the eigenstate
\ket{A_{k}}
with Hamming weight
k
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m_{A}}e_{A_{k}}\left(\braket{A_{k}\mid 0_{A}}\right)^{2}=0.

(5.11)

Proof.

We begin by expressing the energy as

e_{A_{k}}=e_{A_{0}}+k\cdot\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}.

The squared overlap of the eigenstate
\ket{A_{k}}
with the all-zero computational basis state is

\left(\braket{A_{k}\mid 0_{A}}\right)^{2}=\left(\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}\right)^{m_{A}}(\gamma_{A}^{2})^{m_{A}-k}.

There are
\binom{m_{A}}{k}
such states at each Hamming weight
k
, so the full sum becomes:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{m_{A}}\binom{m_{A}}{k}e_{A_{k}}\left(\braket{A_{k}\mid 0_{A}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}\right)^{m_{A}}\sum_{k=0}^{m_{A}}\binom{m_{A}}{k}\left(e_{A_{0}}+k\cdot\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}\right)(\gamma_{A}^{2})^{m_{A}-k}.

By the binomial summation identities in Eq. (
5.10
), we have

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}\right)^{m_{A}}\cdot\left[(1+\gamma_{A}^{2})^{m_{A}}\left(e_{A_{0}}+\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}m_{A}\cdot\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=e_{A_{0}}+\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}\cdot m_{A}\cdot\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\left(\tfrac{m_{A}}{2}+\tfrac{m_{A}}{2}\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}\right)+\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}\cdot m_{A}\cdot\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{m_{A}}{2}-\tfrac{m_{A}}{2}\sqrt{1+n_{A}\mathtt{x}^{2}}\tfrac{\gamma_{A}^{2}-1}{1+\gamma_{A}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{(by Lemma~\ref{lemma:surprising-id})}.

∎

5.3.3
Anti-Crossing Structure: Energy Approximation and Gap Bound

Ground State Energy Approximation

Let
e_{A_{0}}
denote the bare ground state energy, and let
E_{0}=e_{A_{0}}(1)
be the true ground state energy of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
.
We apply the correction formula in Eq. (
5.5
) from
Corollary
5.8
to the ground state energy before and after the anti-crossing to obtain an approximation
of the difference
E_{0}-e_{A_{0}}
.
Assume the bare energy ordering satisfies
e_{L_{0}}<e_{R_{0}}
before the anti-crossing, and reverses to
e_{R_{0}}<e_{L_{0}}
afterward.
Suppose further that the energy separation satisfies
|e_{L_{0}}-e_{R_{0}}|>1
(i.e. away from the anti-crossing), so that a first-order correction suffices.
Then the corrected ground state energies can be written explicitly as weighted sums over overlaps and energy gaps between the
L
and
R
blocks, as given in Proposition
5.14
.

Proposition 5.14
.

Suppose that before the anti-crossing,
e_{L_{0}}<e_{R_{0}}
, and that this ordering reverses afterward.
Assume further that
|e_{L_{0}}-e_{R_{0}}|>1
, so that a first-order correction suffices.
Let
E_{0}
denote the true ground state energy of
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
.

•

Before the anti-crossing:

E_{0}-e_{L_{0}}\approx-2e_{L_{0}}\sum_{j}\tfrac{e_{R_{j}}}{e_{R_{j}}-e_{L_{0}}}\left(\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{j}}\right)^{2}.

•

After the anti-crossing:

E_{0}-e_{R_{0}}\approx-2e_{R_{0}}\sum_{j}\tfrac{e_{L_{j}}}{e_{L_{j}}-e_{R_{0}}}\left(\braket{\overline{R}_{0}|\overline{L}_{j}}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

We apply the correction formula in Eq. (
5.5
) from
Corollary
5.8
to the ground state energy before and after the anti-crossing:

•

Before the anti-crossing:

\displaystyle E_{0}-e_{L_{0}}\approx\sum_{j}\bra{e_{L_{0}}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{L_{0}}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{R_{j}}}\left(\bra{e_{R_{j}}}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\ket{e_{L_{0}}}-\tfrac{\bra{e_{R_{j}}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\ket{e_{L_{0}}}}{e_{R_{j}}-e_{L_{0}}}\right)

(5.12)

•

After the anti-crossing:

\displaystyle E_{0}-e_{R_{0}}\approx\sum_{j}\bra{e_{R_{0}}}({\Delta\mathbbm{H}}-e_{R_{0}}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{e_{L_{j}}}\left(\bra{e_{L_{j}}}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\ket{e_{R_{0}}}-\tfrac{\bra{e_{L_{j}}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\ket{e_{R_{0}}}}{e_{L_{j}}-e_{R_{0}}}\right)

(5.13)

We now use the identities

\bra{e_{L_{i}}}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}\ket{e_{R_{j}}}=\braket{\overline{L}_{i}|\overline{R}_{j}},\quad\bra{e_{L_{i}}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}\ket{e_{R_{j}}}=(e_{L_{i}}+e_{R_{j}})\braket{\overline{L}_{i}|\overline{R}_{j}},

where
\ket{\overline{L}_{i}}
and
\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}
denote the normalized bare eigenstates in the
L
and
R
subsystems, respectively. Substituting into the above yields the result.
∎

We justify that the first-order correction to
e_{L_{0}}
is small by analyzing the weighted sum:

\sum_{j}\tfrac{e_{R_{j}}}{e_{R_{j}}-e_{L_{0}}}\left(\braket{\overline{R}_{j}\mid 0_{R}}\right)^{2}.

By Lemma
5.13
, the unweighted sum is exactly zero:

\sum_{j}e_{R_{j}}\left(\braket{\overline{R}_{j}\mid 0_{R}}\right)^{2}=0
.
The weighting factor
\tfrac{1}{e_{R_{j}}-e_{L_{0}}}
is smooth and strictly positive across the spectrum, since
e_{R_{j}}>e_{L_{0}}
for all
j
before the anti-crossing.
Thus, the weighted sum remains bounded in magnitude and does not grow with system size.
Moreover, the prefactor
\left(\braket{L_{0}\mid 0_{L}}\right)^{2}={\mathsf{co}}(L_{0})^{2}
is exponentially small in
m_{L}
, so the total correction is exponentially suppressed:

E_{0}-e_{L_{0}}\approx-2e_{L_{0}}\cdot{\mathsf{co}}(L_{0})^{2}\cdot\left[\text{bounded sum}\right].

Hence, the correction is negligible, and we conclude that

E_{0}\approx e_{L_{0}}.

This justifies the approximation
E_{0}\approx e_{L_{0}}
before the anti-crossing,
and similarly
E_{0}\approx e_{R_{0}}
after the anti-crossing,
completing the perturbative approximation scheme.

Effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian and Gap Bound

We now approximate the size of the anti-crossing gap between the degenerate bare states
\ket{e_{L_{0}}}
and
\ket{e_{R_{0}}}
, by reducing the full generalized eigenvalue problem to an effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian on the subspace they span.
Combining the structural reduction steps established above, we have the chain of approximations:

\framebox{$\Delta(\mathbbm{H})\ \leq\ \Delta(\mathbbm{H}^{\mathsf{low}})\ \approx\ \Delta(\mathbbm{H}_{\mathcal{C}})\ \approx\ \Delta(\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}})\ \approx\ \Delta(H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{(2\times 2)})$}

(5.14)

which shows that it suffices to approximate the gap of the final
2\times 2
Hamiltonian
H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{(2\times 2)}
.
We now derive
H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{(2\times 2)}
explicitly, starting from the projected generalized eigenvalue problem.
This approach is similar to
[
20
]
.
See also
[
9
]
.

We construct a two-level approximation by projecting the generalized eigenvalue problem onto the subspace

P=\text{span}\{\ket{\overline{L}_{0}},\ket{\overline{R}_{0}}\},

where
\ket{\overline{L}_{0}}
and
\ket{\overline{R}_{0}}
are the padded bare eigenstates of
\mathbbm{H}_{L}^{(0)}
and
\mathbbm{H}_{R}^{(0)}
, respectively.
Projecting the generalized eigenvalue problem onto
P
yields the intermediate effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{0}
in generalized form; converting it to standard form by left-multiplying with
\mathbbm{S}_{PP}^{-1}
produces the final
2\times 2
Hamiltonian
H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{(2\times 2)}
.

Lemma 5.15
.

The effective Hamiltonian for the subspace
P
is given by

H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{0}=\mathbbm{H}_{PP}+(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ})(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{QQ})^{-1}(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{QP}-\mathbbm{H}_{QP}),

where
e_{0}=e_{L_{0}}(0)=e_{R_{0}}(0)
,
Q=P^{\perp}
.
The eigenvalues of the pair
(H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{0},\mathbbm{S}_{PP})
approximate the ground and first excited energies near the anti-crossing.

Proof.

The perturbed generalized eigenvalue equation is:

\displaystyle(\mathbbm{H}+{\Delta\mathbbm{H}})\ket{\Phi}=\lambda(\mathbbm{I}+{\Delta\mathbbm{I}})\ket{\Phi}.

(5.15)

Write the eigenvector as

\ket{\Phi}=\sum_{p\in P}c_{p}\ket{p}+\sum_{q\in Q}c_{q}\ket{q},

and take inner products to obtain the generalized eigenvalue problem in matrix form:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{H}_{PP}&\mathbbm{H}_{PQ}\\
\mathbbm{H}_{QP}&\mathbbm{H}_{QQ}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{P}\\
c_{Q}\end{bmatrix}=\lambda\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{S}_{PP}&\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}\\
\mathbbm{S}_{QP}&\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{P}\\
c_{Q}\end{bmatrix},

(5.16)

where
\lambda=e_{0}+\Delta_{\lambda}
.

From Eq. (
5.16
), we obtain:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\mathbbm{H}_{PP}-\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PP})c_{P}=(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ})c_{Q},\\
(\mathbbm{H}_{QP}-\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QP})c_{P}=(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{QQ})c_{Q}.\end{array}\right.

We solve the second equation for
c_{Q}
:

c_{Q}=(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{QQ})^{-1}(\mathbbm{H}_{QP}-\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QP})c_{P}.

Substitute into the first equation:

\displaystyle(\mathbbm{H}_{PP}-\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PP})c_{P}=(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ})(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{QQ})^{-1}(\mathbbm{H}_{QP}-\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QP})c_{P}.

\Longrightarrow\left[\mathbbm{H}_{PP}+(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ})(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{QQ})^{-1}(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QP}-\mathbbm{H}_{QP})\right]c_{P}=\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PP}c_{P}.

Since
\lambda=e_{0}+\Delta_{\lambda}
and
|\Delta_{\lambda}|\ll|e_{0}|
,
we may approximate the terms
\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ}
,

\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{QQ}
, and
\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{QP}-\mathbbm{H}_{QP}

by their counterparts evaluated at
\lambda=e_{0}
, provided that the overlaps

\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{j}}
are small.

Concretely, the matrix element

\bra{\overline{L}_{0}}(\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ})\ket{\overline{R}_{j}}=\Delta_{\lambda}\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{j}}-e_{R_{j}}\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{j}},

differs from the
e_{0}
-version only by a term of order
\Delta_{\lambda}\cdot\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{j}}
,
which is negligible under the assumption that both
\Delta_{\lambda}\ll|e_{0}|
and
\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{j}}\ll 1
.
Thus, we may replace
\lambda
by
e_{0}
in the effective Hamiltonian to leading order.

Substituting
\lambda\approx e_{0}
, we obtain the effective Hamiltonian:

H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{0}c_{P}\approx\lambda\mathbbm{S}_{PP}c_{P},

where

H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{0}=\mathbbm{H}_{PP}+(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ})(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{QQ})^{-1}(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{QP}-\mathbbm{H}_{QP}).

∎

To understand the leading-order contribution to the anti-crossing gap, we examine the structure of the effective Hamiltonian in the
P
-subspace.
We expand the second-order approximation using

\mathbbm{D}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}e_{0}\mathbbm{I}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{E}_{QQ},\quad\mathbbm{V}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}{\Delta\mathbbm{H}}_{QQ}-e_{0}{\Delta\mathbbm{I}}_{QQ},

and approximate the inverse:

(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{QQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{QQ})^{-1}\approx\mathbbm{D}^{-1}+\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\mathbbm{V}\mathbbm{D}^{-1}.

Then we have

H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{0}\approx\mathbbm{H}_{PP}+\mathbbm{W}_{1}+\mathbbm{W}_{2},

where

\mathbbm{W}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ})\mathbbm{D}^{-1}(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{QP}-\mathbbm{H}_{QP}),\quad\mathbbm{W}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{PQ}-\mathbbm{H}_{PQ})\mathbbm{D}^{-1}\mathbbm{V}\mathbbm{D}^{-1}(e_{0}\mathbbm{S}_{QP}-\mathbbm{H}_{QP}).

The leading term

\mathbbm{H}_{PP}=\begin{bmatrix}e_{L_{0}}&(e_{L_{0}}+e_{R_{0}})g_{0}\\
(e_{L_{0}}+e_{R_{0}})g_{0}&e_{R_{0}}\end{bmatrix}=e_{0}\begin{bmatrix}1&2g_{0}\\
2g_{0}&1\end{bmatrix},

where
e_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}(e_{L_{0}}+e_{R_{0}})
and
g_{0}=\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{0}}
.

The first-order correction
\mathbbm{W}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&0\\
0&a_{1}\end{bmatrix}
is diagonal,
while the second-order correction
\mathbbm{W}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&c_{2}\\
c_{2}&0\end{bmatrix}
is off-diagonal, with
c_{2}\ll e_{0}g_{0}
.
Combining all terms yields:

H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{0}\approx\begin{bmatrix}e_{0}+a_{1}&2e_{0}g_{0}+c_{2}\\
2e_{0}g_{0}+c_{2}&e_{0}+a_{1}\end{bmatrix},

which shows that the off-diagonal coupling is governed primarily by the overlap
g_{0}
.

Finally, we convert the generalized eigenvalue problem to standard form using

H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{(2\times 2)}=\mathbbm{S}_{PP}^{-1}H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{0},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbbm{S}_{PP}=\begin{bmatrix}1&g_{0}\\
g_{0}&1\end{bmatrix},

which gives

H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{(2\times 2)}=\tfrac{1}{1-g_{0}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}e_{0}+a_{1}-g_{0}(2e_{0}g_{0}+c_{2})&2e_{0}g_{0}+c_{2}-g_{0}(e_{0}+a_{1})\\
2e_{0}g_{0}+c_{2}-g_{0}(e_{0}+a_{1})&e_{0}+a_{1}-g_{0}(2e_{0}g_{0}+c_{2})\end{bmatrix}.

We thus have the gap approximation based on the assumptions that
|a_{1}|\ll|e_{0}|
and
|c_{2}|\ll|e_{0}g_{0}|
:

Corollary 5.16

(Gap Approximation)
.

Under the effective two-level approximation,

\Delta(H_{\mathsf{eff}}^{(2\times 2)})\approx 2|e_{0}|g_{0},

where
e_{0}=e_{L_{0}}=e_{R_{0}}
is the degenerate bare energy, and
g_{0}=\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{0}}
is the overlap between two bare ground states.

Proposition 5.17
.

The overlap between the two bare ground states is given by

\displaystyle g_{0}:=\braket{\overline{L}_{0}|\overline{R}_{0}}={\mathsf{co}}(L_{0})\cdot{\mathsf{co}}(R_{0})=\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma_{L}^{2}}{1+\gamma_{L}^{2}}}\right)^{m_{L}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma_{R}^{2}}{1+\gamma_{R}^{2}}}\right)^{m_{R}},

where the amplitude ratios

\gamma_{A}=\tfrac{\sqrt{n_{A}}\mathtt{x}_{c}}{w+\sqrt{w^{2}+n_{A}\mathtt{x}_{c}^{2}}}\in(0,1),
for
A\in\{L,R\}
,
and
\mathtt{x}_{c}
is the location of the anti-crossing, defined implicitly by
e_{L_{0}}(\mathtt{x}_{c})=e_{R_{0}}(\mathtt{x}_{c})
.

Since the runtime scales inversely with the square of the minimum gap, by the chain of approximations in Eq. (
5.14
), we have

T\gtrsim\tfrac{1}{g_{0}^{2}}=\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{L}^{2}}\right)^{m_{L}}\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\gamma_{R}^{2}}\right)^{m_{R}}>2^{m_{L}+m_{R}}.

This completes the reduction from the full Hamiltonian
\mathbbm{H}
to the effective
2\times 2
model, providing the exponential runtime bound.

6
Conclusion

This work presents an analytical framework for identifying and quantifying tunneling-induced anti-crossing in stoquastic transverse-field quantum annealing (TFQA), based on a structured class of Maximum Independent Set instances. Our analysis reformulates the effective Hamiltonian in a non-orthogonal basis and derives an exponentially small gap using a generalized eigenvalue approach that remains valid beyond the small transverse field regime.
More broadly, our findings reveal the structural origin of tunneling-induced anti-crossings. Once the system localizes near critical local minima—as captured in the reduced form
\mathbbm{H}_{\mathsf{core}}
—a tunneling transition to the global minimum becomes unavoidable, and the resulting gap is exponentially small. This same localization and tunneling mechanism may also arise in less structured graphs.

It has been claimed that non-stoquasticity may not be essential for achieving quantum speedup
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. Indeed, simply introducing a non-stoquastic driver is not sufficient to bypass the tunneling bottleneck. For example, if
J_{\mathsf{zz}}
is set to be too large, the evolution
will fail the structural steering and encounter a tunneling-induced anti-crossing instead (as illustrated by an example in
Figure 16 of
[
1
]
).

In contrast to this work, a recent study
[
18
]
, which may be of theoretical interest on its own, investigated unstructured adiabatic quantum optimization, aiming to recover Grover-like quadratic speedup
[
15
,
16
]
. However, as we note in the main paper (Section 3.1 in
[
1
]
), a simple classical algorithm
[
17
]
can already solve the MIS problem in time
O(2^{n/3})
, outperforming the Grover-like
O(2^{n/2})
speedup.

This highlights the importance of algorithmic design and analysis, even for adiabatic quantum algorithms in order to achieve quantum advantage.
We note that the tunneling-induced anti-crossings analyzed here play a constructive role in the
Dic-Dac-Doa
algorithm:
a polynomial-time TFQA evolution through such an anti-crossing identifies configurations in the set of the degenerate local minima,
which are then used as seeds for constructing the non-stoquastic
\mathsf{XX}
-driver graph.

We hope that the structural insights developed here may guide future work in rigorously identifying similar bottlenecks in broader classes of problem instances, and in clarifying the fundamental limitations of stoquastic quantum annealing beyond heuristic or empirical claims.
