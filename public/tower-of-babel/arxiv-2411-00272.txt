Simulating incompressible flows over complex geometries using the shifted boundary method with incomplete adaptive octree meshes
Cheng-Hau Yang, Guglielmo Scovazzi, Adarsh Krishnamurthy, Baskar Ganapathysubramanian

Abstract
We extend the shifted boundary method (SBM) to the simulation of incompressible fluid flow using immersed octree meshes. Previous work on SBM for fluid flow primarily utilized two- or three-dimensional unstructured tetrahedral grids. Recently, octree grids have become an essential component of immersed CFD solvers, and this work addresses this gap and the associated computational challenges. We leverage an optimal (approximate) surrogate boundary constructed efficiently on incomplete and adaptive octree meshes. The resulting framework enables the simulation of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations in complex geometries without requiring boundary-fitted grids. Simulations of benchmark tests in two and three dimensions demonstrate that the Octree-SBM framework is a robust, accurate, and efficient approach to simulating fluid dynamics problems with complex geometries.

This process can be incredibly cumbersome when dealing with large-scale simulations, where the sheer size and complexity of the computational domain make manual mesh generation infeasible or prohibitively expensive. The overall cost and time associated with manual mesh generation not only slows down the simulation workflow but also limits the ability to perform extensive parametric studies, optimize designs efficiently, or train reduced-order models and machine-learning algorithms, for example. This poses a bottleneck when conducting large-scale simulations with traditional body-fitted meshes. Despite advances in automated mesh generation algorithms

One promising approach to address these difficulties is through the family of methods that started with the Immersed Boundary Method (IBM). First developed by Peskin

This method alleviates the need for complex mesh generation by allowing the use of simple, structured grids, such as Cartesian grids, irrespective of the geometry’s complexity. By simplifying the time-consuming mesh generation process, IBM significantly reduces manual effort and computational costs, making large-scale simulations more practical and efficient. The IBM and its variants have been extensively applied to simulate flows around complex geometries and moving boundaries. For a comprehensive review of the IBM, we refer the interested reader to

Building on the foundation laid by IBM, the Shifted Boundary Method (SBM) is an innovative numerical approach designed to address complex geometries and boundary conditions using non-boundary fitted meshes. Within the category of discrete forcing, SBM is a Finite Element Method (FEM) that employs Nitsche’s method
[
Nitsche, 1971

1

The central concept of the SBM approach – to ”shift” the application of the boundary condition from the true surface (which requires a body fitted mesh) to a surrogate surface (which could be a non-body fitted envelope around the geometry) – has been successfully applied to FEM
[
Main and Scovazzi, 2018a
,
Main and Scovazzi, 2018b
]
, VEM
[
Hou et al., 2024
,
Bertoluzza et al., 2024

[
Antonelli et al., 2024
]
formulations
. This technique offers significant advantages in fluid dynamics simulations, particularly its implementation and ability to address these computational challenges:

•

Ease of Implementation
: SBM is notably more straightforward to implement than other immersed methods, such as FCM and IMGA. These methods involve surface quadrature (usually Gauss points) over the actual boundary, requiring additional data structures into the existing code framework. Conversely, SBM simplifies the process by utilizing Gauss points along the surrogate boundary, which naturally aligns with the existing element edges (faces) in the original framework. Additionally, other discrete forcing approaches (e.g., IMGA and FCM) require a higher number of quadrature points in the cut elements for accurate volume integration, which increases their complexity of implementation and often creates a computational load imbalance across processors during parallel (distributed memory) simulations
Saurabh et al. [2021]
.

•

Resolving Sliver Cut-cell Issue
: A notable advantage of SBM is its capability to eliminate the poor conditioning of the stiffness matrix caused by sliver cut cells
[
Antonelli et al., 2024
]
. SBM addresses this by integrating over all volume Gauss points, thus avoiding the issues arising from these tiny cut cells.

2.1
Governing equations

Consider a fluid domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega

The non-dimensional form of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations can be stated as:

Momentum conservation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p-\frac{1}{Re}\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{f}=0\;,

(1a)

Mass conservation (incompressibility):

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0\;,

(1b)

where
\boldsymbol{u}
represents the non-dimensional (normalized) velocity vector,
p
the non-dimensional pressure, and
\boldsymbol{f}
the non-dimensional forcing vector.
Re=\frac{\rho_{r}u_{r}L_{r}}{\mu_{r}}
stands for the Reynolds number, where
\rho_{r}
is the reference density,
u_{r}
is the reference velocity,

is the reference length, and

Boundary conditions

The governing equations

We assume a non-overlapping partition of the boundary

\displaystyle=\;\boldsymbol{u}_{D}\;,\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{D}\;.

\|v\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}=\left(\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|l(\Omega)^{k}\boldsymbol{D}^{k}v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/2}\;,

(4)

To start the discussion, we introduce here a body-fitted FEM formulation of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, which will serve as a benchmark for the the Shifted Boundary Method, soon to be developed.
We will consider in particular a Variational Multiscale (VMS) formulation of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations

,
Hughes et al. [2004]
,
Bazilevs et al. [2007a]
, which can be interpreted as both a form of numerical stabilization (i.e., the SUPG and PSPG stabilization) or as a turbulence LES model.

Assuming that the domain
\Omega
is discretized using finite elements, we can introduce the family

of shape-regular discrete decompositions (meshes/grids) of

Here, with some abuse of notation, we have assumed that

whose vertices lie on

.
In what follows, for the sake of simplicity, we will omit this more precise, although complex, notation.

We introduce the discrete space of continuous, piecewise-linear, vector-valued test functions over the decompositions

\boldsymbol{V}^{h}(\Omega)=\left\{\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\mid\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\in(C^{0}(\Omega))^{d}\cap(\mathscr{P}^{1}(T))^{d}\,,\mbox{ with }T\in{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)\mbox{ and }\boldsymbol{w}^{h}_{|\Gamma_{D}}=\boldsymbol{0}\right\}

(5)

and the corresponding space of trial functions

\boldsymbol{S}^{h}(\Omega)=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}^{h}\mid\boldsymbol{v}^{h}\in(C^{0}(\Omega))^{d}\cap(\mathscr{P}^{1}(T))^{d}\,,\mbox{ with }T\in{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)\mbox{ and }\boldsymbol{v}^{h}_{|\Gamma_{D}}=\boldsymbol{u}_{D}\right\}\;.

(6)

More precisely, imposing the value
\boldsymbol{u}_{D}
on

for a

holds only if the boundary conditions
\boldsymbol{u}_{D}
is piece-wise linear along the edges/faces lying on
\Gamma_{D}
.
Also in this case, we simplify our notation, which is slightly abused.
We also introduce the discrete space of continuous, piecewise-linear, scalar-valued functions over
{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)

V^{h}(\Omega)=\left\{w^{h}\mid w^{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathscr{P}^{1}(T)\,,\,T\in{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)\right\}\;,

(7)

in which no boundary conditions are incorporated.
The VMS formulation of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations on body-fitted grids reads:

Find
\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}^{h}(\Omega)
and
p^{h}\in S^{h}(\Omega)
such that, for any
\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\in\boldsymbol{V}^{h}(\Omega)
and

,

{NS}[\Omega\,;{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)](\boldsymbol{w}^{h},q^{h};\boldsymbol{u}^{h},p^{h})\;=\;0

(8)

where

\displaystyle{NS}[\Omega\,;{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)](\boldsymbol{w}^{h},q^{h};\boldsymbol{u}^{h},p^{h})

\displaystyle=\;(\boldsymbol{w}^{h},\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h})_{\Omega}+\frac{1}{Re}(\nabla^{s}\boldsymbol{w}^{h},\nabla^{s}\boldsymbol{u}^{h})_{\Omega}-(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{w}^{h},p^{h})_{\Omega}+(q^{h},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{h})_{\Omega}-(\boldsymbol{w}^{h},\boldsymbol{f}^{h})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\;-\sum_{T\in{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)}(\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{w}^{h},\boldsymbol{u^{\prime}})_{T}+\sum_{T\in{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)}(\boldsymbol{w}^{h},\boldsymbol{u^{\prime}}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h})_{T}-\sum_{T\in{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)}(\nabla\boldsymbol{w}^{h},\boldsymbol{u^{\prime}}\otimes\boldsymbol{u^{\prime}})_{T}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\;-\sum_{T\in{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)}(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{w}^{h},p^{\prime})_{T}-\sum_{T\in{\mathscr{T}}_{h}(\Omega)}(\nabla q^{h},\boldsymbol{u^{\prime}})_{T}\;,

(9)

where
\nabla^{s}\boldsymbol{u}^{h}=1/2(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h})^{t})
is the symmetric part of the gradient of
\boldsymbol{u}^{h}
and
(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h})^{t}
is the transpose of the gradient of
\boldsymbol{u}^{h}
.
The terms in the first row of

represent the Galerkin formulation of the Navier-Stokes equations, while the other terms are associated with the VMS stabilization, as elaborated in

. From the VMS literature, the primed terms, also known as fine-scale variables, are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=-\tau_{M}\boldsymbol{r}_{M}(\boldsymbol{u}^{h},p^{h})\;,

(10a)

\displaystyle p^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\tau_{C}r_{C}(\boldsymbol{u}^{h})\;,

(10b)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{M}

\displaystyle=\;\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+\nabla p^{h}-\frac{1}{Re}\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{h}-\boldsymbol{f}\;,

(10c)

\displaystyle r_{C}

\displaystyle=\;\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\;,

(10d)

\displaystyle\tau_{M}

\displaystyle=\;\Big(\frac{4}{\Delta t^{2}}+\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+\frac{C_{M}}{Re^{2}}\boldsymbol{G}:\boldsymbol{G}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\;,

(10e)

\displaystyle\tau_{C}

\displaystyle=\;(\tau_{M}\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{g})^{-1}\;,

(10f)

\displaystyle G_{ij}

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial\xi_{k}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\xi_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}\;,

(10g)

\displaystyle g_{i}

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial\xi_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\;.

(10h)

In this context,
\boldsymbol{r}_{M}
and

are residuals of the momentum and mass conservation equations calculated using the coarse-scale (i.e., numerical) solutions

and

. The constant

is set to 36 and the quantities

\displaystyle-\langle\boldsymbol{w}^{h},\beta_{o}\min(0,\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\rangle_{\Gamma_{o}}.

2.6
A shifted boundary formulation of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations

We will develop next a shifted boundary formulation of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, in the specific case of Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Other types of boundary conditions can be treated with the SBM, but these are somewhat less interesting in the context of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations.
This is because the shapes that are immersed into a fluid domain typically require the no-slip condition, which is a Dirichlet-type condition.

Consider then a surrogate boundary
\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}
in proximity of a true Dirichlet boundary
\Gamma_{D}
.
Using the construction of the distance between the two boundaries, it is possible to introduce the Taylor expansion of the velocity vector:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})+(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{d})({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})+(\boldsymbol{R}_{D}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{d}))({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})

\displaystyle=\;\boldsymbol{u}_{D}(\boldsymbol{M}_{h}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}))\;,\quad\mbox{ on }\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}\;,

(15)

where the remainder
\boldsymbol{R}_{D}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{d})
satisfies
\|\boldsymbol{R}_{D}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{d})\|=o(\|\boldsymbol{d}\|^{2})
as
\|\boldsymbol{d}\,\|\to 0
.
We can then define, on
\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}
, the
extension
operator

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{u}_{D}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})

\displaystyle:=\;\boldsymbol{u}_{D}(\boldsymbol{M}_{h}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}))

(16)

and with
shift
operator

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{D,h}\,\boldsymbol{u}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})

\displaystyle:=\;\boldsymbol{u}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})\,\boldsymbol{d}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})\;.

(17)

Neglecting the higher-order residual term in
Eq.
15
, we obtain the final expression of the
shifted
boundary conditions

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}_{D,h}\,\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\;\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{u}_{D}\;,\qquad\mbox{ on }\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}\;.

(18)

In what follows - for the sake of simplicity and whenever it does not cause confusion - we will omit the symbol
\mathbb{E}
from
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{u}_{D}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})
and simply write
\boldsymbol{u}_{D}({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}})
.
Introducing now the scalar and vector discrete function spaces

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{h}(\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\;\left\{q^{h}\mid q^{h}\in C^{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathscr{P}^{1}(T)\,,\mbox{ with }T\in\tilde{{\mathscr{T}}}_{h}(\lambda)\right\}\;,

(19)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{h}(\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda))

\displaystyle=\;\left\{\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\mid\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\in(C^{0}(\Omega))^{d}\cap(\mathscr{P}^{1}(T))^{d}\,,\mbox{ with }T\in\tilde{{\mathscr{T}}}_{h}(\lambda)\right\}\;,

(20)

which does not incorporate any boundary conditions, we can present the shifted boundary variational formulation of the Navier-Stokes equations:

Find
\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\in\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{h}(\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda))
and
p^{h}\in V^{h}(\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda))
such that, for any
\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\in\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{h}(\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda))
and
q^{h}\in V^{h}(\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda))
,

\displaystyle 0\;=

\displaystyle\;{NS}[\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda)\,;\tilde{{\mathscr{T}}}_{h}(\lambda)](\boldsymbol{w}^{h},q^{h};\boldsymbol{u}^{h},p^{h})

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\langle\boldsymbol{w}^{h},(2\,Re^{-1}\nabla^{s}\boldsymbol{u}^{h}-p^{h}\boldsymbol{I})\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}}}_{Consistency\;term}-\underbrace{\langle(2\,Re^{-1}\nabla^{s}\boldsymbol{w}^{h}+q^{h}\boldsymbol{I})\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}},\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{u_{D}}\rangle_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}}}_{Adjoint\;consistency\;term}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\beta\,Re^{-1}\langle h^{-1}(\boldsymbol{w}^{h}+\nabla\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{d}),\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{u_{D}}\rangle_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}}}_{Penalty\;term}

(21)

where the term
{NS}[\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda)\,;\tilde{{\mathscr{T}}}_{h}(\lambda)](\boldsymbol{w}^{h},q^{h};\boldsymbol{u}^{h},p^{h})
is defined in analogy to
Eq.
9
.
This formulation is inspired by Nitsche’s method for the weak imposition of Dirichlet-type boundary conditions.
In particular, the term in the second row of
Eq.
21
is due to integration by parts and the fact that now
\boldsymbol{u}^{h},\,\boldsymbol{w}^{h}\in\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}^{h}(\tilde{\Omega}_{h}(\lambda))
.
The parameter
\beta
in the last term of
Eq.
21
is a penalty that, if sufficiently large, ensures the numerical stability of the overall formulation.

The proposed SBM formulation satisfies a principle of global conservation of mass and momentum, as already described in
Main and Scovazzi [2018]
. We briefly summarize the principle of conservation of mass (the momentum conservation can be proved in a similar way): taking
\boldsymbol{w}^{h}=0
and
q^{h}=1
in
Eq.
21
, we obtain, using the Gauss divergence theorem,

\displaystyle 0\;=

\displaystyle\;(1,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}^{h})_{\tilde{\Omega}_{h}}-\langle 1,(\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{u}_{D})\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}}

\displaystyle\;=

\displaystyle\;\langle 1,\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{h}}-\langle 1,(\boldsymbol{u}^{h}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{u}_{D})\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}}

\displaystyle\;=

\displaystyle\;\langle 1,\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{h}\setminus\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}}+\langle 1,(\boldsymbol{u}_{D}-\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{d})\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\rangle_{\tilde{\Gamma}_{D,h}}\;,

(22)

which corresponds to a statement of conservation of mass fluxes entering and exiting the surrogate domain.
Note that at surrogate Dirichlet boundaries the mass flux is given by
(\boldsymbol{u}_{D}-\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{h}\,\boldsymbol{d})\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}
, which includes the Taylor expansion correction.
This is expected, since the surrogate boundary is at a distance from the true boundary.

and
Interior

\lambda=0

Intercepted

FalseIntercepted
elements. Likely, the normal distance will not suffice to locate suitable extrapolation points accurately. While the geometry used here for demonstration purposes is merely a circle, in practical scenarios, the geometry could be far more complex and irregularly shaped, making the implementation of normal-based extrapolation point finding challenging. The alternative approach we adopt is simpler to implement and can be applied robustly across various cases and values of the parameter
\lambda
.

3.3
Numerical implementation

The numerical framework used in this study is built upon
Dendro-KT

Ishii et al. [2019]
,
Saurabh et al. [2021]
, a highly scalable, parallel octree-based library, and
PETSc
, a widely used toolkit for solving large-scale scientific problems.

Dendro-KT
is a massively parallel, adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) codebase that uses octree-based domain decomposition. We have previously used it to model a variety of multiphysics simulations, including two-phase flow
Khanwale et al. [2023]
, electrokinetic transport phenomena
Kim et al. [2024]
, and transmission risk assessments
Tan et al. [2023]
. Our framework employs in-out tests
Haines [1994]
,
Borazjani et al. [2008]
,
Saurabh et al. [2021]
to quickly determine whether a point is inside or outside a given geometry. This approach enables the creation of an incomplete octree
Saurabh et al. [2021]
, where regions that are not part of the solution domain are carved out. By avoiding unnecessary computations in these void areas, the incomplete octree significantly reduces computational overhead. Additionally,
Dendro-KT
supports local mesh refinement, allowing us to focus computational resources on regions of interest, such as wake regions and boundary layers. By using space-filling curves (SFC) to partition octants across processors,
Dendro-KT
ensures efficient load balancing in distributed memory environments. The library’s top-down and bottom-up traversal algorithms further streamline operations like matrix assembly, eliminating the need for traditional element mappings. Furthermore,
Dendro-KT
constructs 2:1-balanced
Sundar et al. [2008]
,
Fernando et al. [2017]
,
Ishii et al. [2019]
octrees, ensuring that neighboring octants differ in refinement level by at most one, which improves the stability and accuracy of the numerical solution. For more details on the
Dendro-KT
framework, readers are referred to
Saurabh et al. [2021]
,
Saurabh et al. [2021]
.

PETSc
(Portable, Extensible Toolkit for Scientific Computation) is the primary (non)linear solver (SNES and KSP constructs) used in this framework. It efficiently handles the parallel solution of the discretized systems resulting from the Navier-Stokes equations.
PETSc
’s flexible interface and optimized data structures allow for efficient management of the sparse matrices generated by
Dendro-KT
. Additionally,
PETSc
’s support for distributed memory systems ensures scalability, making it ideal for large-scale simulations. The integration with
Dendro-KT
enables seamless communication of nodal data between processes, facilitating the efficient assembly and solution of the linear systems arising from finite element discretization.

Beyond
Dendro-KT
’s role in finite element discretization and
PETSc
’s role in parallel system solving, the implementation of the Shifted Boundary Method (SBM) requires two key components: (a) optimal surrogate boundary generation, and (b) fast distance function calculation based on k-d tree using the
nanoflann
library
Blanco and Rai [2014]
. For more details on the implementation of these components, readers are referred to
Yang et al. [2024]
.

The no-slip boundary condition of the circular obstacle is applied using SBM. The non-dimensional timestep is set to 0.01. We conduct a mesh convergence study as detailed in

(24)

where

represents the boundary error in the

-direction,
\Gamma
denotes the true boundary, and

is the numerical velocity component in the
x
-direction.
Similarly, in the
y
-direction:

E_{y}=\sqrt{\int_{\Gamma}(u_{y}^{h})^{2}\,d\Gamma}=\|u_{y}\|_{0;\Gamma}\,,

(25)

where

denotes the boundary error in the
y
-direction, and

represents the numerical velocity component in the

Notably, even when utilizing the coarsest mesh (refine level equal to 12 and mesh size =
\frac{30}{2^{12}}
) with the SBM, the boundary error remains lower than that observed with the most refined mesh (refine level equal to 14 and mesh size =

) under a strong Dirichlet boundary condition applied to the surrogate boundary (

).
The results of
Figure

are very important, since it is often argued that a pixelated geometry representations are sufficient in some applications (e.g., simulation of Navier-Stokes flow in the porous space of porous media for numerical homogenization purposes).
The SBM offers significantly improved performance over a pixelated geometry, as seen by the fact that SBM errors are about 2 orders of magnitude smaller than the ones with the strong boundary condition on the pixelated geometry. Ultra-fine grids are required for the latter, to match the SBM results on relatively coarse grids. This situation is drastically exacerbated in three dimensions.

We conducted simulations of internal flow through a nozzle, as shown in

1-\frac{r^{2}}{r_{\text{inlet}}^{2}}
, where
r
represents the distance from a point on the inlet plane to the center of the inlet circle, and
r_{\text{inlet}}
is the radius of the circle at the inlet plane. The outlet boundary condition sets the pressure to zero, while the remaining boundary surfaces enforce a no-slip Dirichlet condition using the SBM.

The nozzle geometry was constructed using an incomplete octree framework. Simulations were performed at a Reynolds number of 10 with a non-dimensional time step of 1. Visualizations of the
x
-direction velocity (
\lambda=0.5
) at a refinement level of 8 are presented as volume rendering, cross-sectional slices, and streamlines in
Figure
28(a)
,
Figure
28(b)
, and
Figure
28(c)

Simulations were conducted on uniform octree meshes at refinement levels of 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8, resulting in a mesh size of
4/2^{\text{lvl}}
, where
lvl
denotes the refinement level. The effect of different
\lambda
values on the flow was analyzed. To verify mass conservation, we evaluated several cross-sectional slices (
Figure
28(b)
). The
x
-direction velocity was integrated over the area of each slice.

Figure
29
shows the error
1-\frac{\int udA}{\int_{\text{inlet}}udA}
, which represents the difference between the ideal and calculated flux ratios. The calculated flux ratio is the flux at a given cross-section divided by the inlet flux. A value closer to zero indicates better mass conservation. It is evident that
\lambda=0.5
achieves superior mass conservation across all refinement levels compared to non-optimal
\lambda
values. Notably,
\lambda=0
leads to an increase in flux, while
\lambda=1
results in a flux decrease through the nozzle.

30(a)
illustrates the convergence of the calculated flux at the outlet compared to the exact flux (
\frac{\pi r_{\text{inlet}}^{2}}{2}

\lambda=0.5
achieves nearly second-order accuracy.
Figure
30(b)
presents the Mean Squared Error (MSE), calculated as

\text{MSE}=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(1-\frac{\int_{i}u\,dA}{\int_{\text{inlet}}u\,dA}\right)^{2}}{n},

where
n
represents the number of cross-sectional slices (12 in this study). Here,
\int_{i}u\,dA
denotes the flux integrated over the area of the
i
-th cross-sectional plane, and
\int_{\text{inlet}}u\,dA
corresponds to the flux at the inlet. The MSE quantifies the average error across all cross-sections. The results clearly demonstrate that
\lambda=0.5
achieves the best convergence, with superior mass conservation compared to other values of
\lambda
.

Using
\lambda=0.5
and level of refinement equal to 8, the difference between the outlet flux and the inlet flux is approximately
1\times 10^{-4}

5
Conclusions and Future Work

In this work, we advance the field of computational fluid dynamics by applying the shifted boundary method in conjunction with a non-boundary-fitted octree mesh, leveraging an optimal surrogate boundary. This approach builds on previous successes in using SBM with an optimal surrogate boundary for solving Poisson and Linear Elasticity equations
[
Yang et al., 2024
]
, and extends its application to the Navier-Stokes equations – a novel venture that integrates the use of octree meshes with an optimal surrogate boundary for the first time. We utilize this sophisticated SBM framework to tackle benchmark fluid dynamics problems.

A selection of fluid dynamics case studies spanning two-dimensional and three-dimensional challenges governed by the Navier-Stokes equations underpins our rigorous evaluation of Octree-SBM’s performance. These include flow simulations around circular objects, lid-driven cavity flows with a circular obstacle, various aerodynamic problems, and flow passing through a complex gyroid topology. Our case studies show that the solutions produced by our Octree-SBM approach with optimal surrogate boundary closely align with established reference solutions. This alignment is notable in critical aerodynamic measures like drag coefficient and Strouhal number and effectively captures the pressure distribution near the geometric boundary. Our findings demonstrate that employing an optimal surrogate boundary ensures superior outcomes compared to alternative surrogate boundary choices. This improvement is evident in the reduction of boundary error, as defined in our paper, and in the enhanced accuracy of pressure profile representation near complex geometry boundaries.

Looking ahead, potential research directions could encompass several areas including: (a) Integrating the flow simulation framework with the convection-diffusion equation for thermal incompressible flow analyses. (b) Merging the flow simulation framework with shell formulations or other structural solvers to facilitate fluid-structure interaction studies. (c) Expanding the FEM basis functions within our framework to encompass higher-order basis functions.
