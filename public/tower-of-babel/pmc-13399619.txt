Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction Using a Hollow Ligament Advanced Reinforcement System Semitendinosus Autograft Combined With Anterolateral Complex Augmentation Using Gracilis
Authors: Tianhao Zhu, Changming Huang, Huaqiang Fan, Yangpan Fu, Zhiyong Gan, Zhenhuang Liu, Yaqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis technical note describes an alternative surgical technique for anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction combined with anterolateral complex (ALC) augmentation. The procedure uses a hollow ligament advanced reinforcement system (LARS), along with semitendinosus and gracilis tendons as grafts. Key surgical steps include arthroscopic exploration, graft preparation, tunnel drilling, and fixation. This technique reduces autologous tendon harvesting, reduces the risk of neurovascular injury, and facilitates immediate restoration of knee rotational stability with early postoperative mobilization. Nonetheless, potential limitations include the possibility of femoral positioning errors and a relatively steep learning curve. This method is particularly suitable for patients with thin autologous tendons, high athletic demands, or those undergoing revision ACL surgery, as it can augment smaller autologous grafts and confer immediate mechanical strength via the structural support of the hollow LARS ligament, thereby helping to maintain knee stability in the early postoperative period.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is among the most common knee injuries, primarily affecting young athletes participating in high‐intensity and high‐risk sports such as sumo wrestling and American football. 1 , 2 Single‐bundle ACL reconstruction remains the standard surgical approach; however, approximately 24% of patients continue to experience residual rotational instability after surgery, and 1.8% to 16.7% encounter surgical failure. 3

The anterolateral complex (ALC) consists of the anterolateral ligament (ALL), iliotibial band (ITB), and anterolateral capsule, with the ALL serving as the primary component responsible for limiting excessive internal rotation of the knee. 4 The reported incidence of concurrent ALL injury in patients with ACL tears ranges from 34.4% to 63%. 5 , 6 Combined ACL and ALL reconstruction has become a major focus in sports medicine research because of its superior rotational stability and reduced postoperative failure rate. 7 , 8 Studies indicate that combined ACL and ALL injuries lead to greater rotational instability, often requiring additional surgical intervention to restore normal knee function. 9 , 10

The ligament advanced reinforcement system (LARS) (LARS, Alk city, Dijon, France) is fabricated from polyethylene terephthalate and possesses properties that mimic native ligament tissue while allowing the ingrowth of surrounding tissues. In recent years, several studies show its advantages in improving joint stability, reducing recovery time, and enhancing the return‐to‐sport outcomes. 11 , 12 , 13

Previous techniques for combined ACL and ALL reconstruction primarily use grafts composed of gracilis and semitendinosus tendons. To address specific clinical requirements, specifically the option to augment smaller grafts and optimize graft strength at time zero, this technical note describes an alternative approach that integrates a hollow LARS ligament with semitendinosus and gracilis tendons for ACL reconstruction and ALC augmentation, offering a useful reference for clinical practice.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning and Preoperative Marking

Following spinal anesthesia, the patient is placed in a supine position. A pneumatic tourniquet is applied to the upper‐middle third of the affected thigh and inflated to 60 kPa. A positioning frame is placed laterally to the tourniquet to stabilize the affected limb. Anatomical landmarks are marked on the skin before disinfection of the limb (Figure 1). Under anesthesia, the Lachman test, anterior drawer test, and pivot shift test are performed on the affected knee to evaluate preoperative stability.

Anatomical landmarks marked on the skin before disinfection. (Left knee, Supine position) (LE, lateral femoral epicondyle.)

Arthroscopic Exploration and Exposure

A 4 mm 30° arthroscope (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) is inserted through the standard anteromedial, anterolateral, and midline portals for intra‐articular examination. The intercondylar fossa and the medial wall of the lateral femoral condyle are sequentially exposed, and any concomitant lesions are treated accordingly (Video 1).

Arthroscopic ACL reconstruction combined with ALC augmentation. The operation was performed on the left knee with the patient in supine position. The detailed procedure of surgery. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70099.

Graft Harvest and Preparation

A longitudinal incision of approximately 3 cm is made 2 cm medial to the tibial tuberosity. The pes anserinus is exposed layer by layer, and the gracilis and semitendinosus tendons are dissected and harvested. On the workstation, the semitendinosus tendon is folded, and the unfolded gracilis tendon of equal length is woven together with the folded semitendinosus using nonabsorbable surgical sutures (Delta Medical, Beijing, China). A 10 to 15 cm segment of the gracilis tendon is preserved for ALC augmentation. After weaving, the graft length and diameter are measured using a ruler (Figure 2). If the graft diameter is less than 8 mm, a hollow LARS ligament (LARS, Alk city, Dijon, France) is added for augmentation. Nonabsorbable surgical sutures (Delta Medical, Beijing, China) are then used to secure the LARS ligament 3 cm from the midpoint on each side, based on the length of the femoral tunnel (Figure 3). The graft diameter is remeasured to ensure it exceeds 8 mm.

Measurement of graft length using a ruler.

Use of a hollow LARS ligament for graft augmentation. (LARS, ligament advanced reinforcement system.)

Drilling ACL Tibial Tunnel

With the arthroscope placed through the anterolateral portal, the knee is flexed to approximately 90°. A tibial drill guide (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) is used to identify the tibial insertion site of the ACL stump intra‐articularly, located anterior to the tibial eminence. Extra‐articularly, the guide is positioned 2 cm medial to the tibial tuberosity and inferior to the sartorius muscle. A guide pin is then inserted through the drill guide by an assistant, and an 8 mm drill bit is used to create the tibial tunnel. The tunnel length is measured using a depth gauge (Figures 4 and 5).

The surgeon used a tibial drill guide to locate the tibial tunnel of the ACL. (Left knee, Supine position) (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Measurement of ACL tibial tunnel length using a depth gauge. (Left knee, Supine position) (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament.)

Drilling ACL and ALC Femoral Tunnels

A femoral drill guide (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) is positioned intra‐articularly on the medial wall of the lateral femoral condyle, inferior to the resident's ridge and posterior to the bifurcate ridge, near the apex of the intercondylar fossa. This corresponds to the 2 o'clock position in the left knee and the 10 o'clock position in the right knee. With the knee flexed to 120°, a guide pin is inserted, and an 8 mm drill bit is advanced 2 to 3 cm into the joint to avoid tunnel deviation. The femoral tunnel is then completed with a 4.5 mm drill bit, and its length is measured using a depth gauge (Figure 6). The proximal exit of the guide pin is palpated at the external aperture of the femoral tunnel, typically 1 cm proximal to the lateral femoral epicondyle. A 3 cm skin incision is made at this point, and the guide pin is reinserted through a sleeve and passed from the incision (Figure 7). Finally, an 8 mm drill bit is used to enlarge the femoral tunnel from the external aperture (Figure 8).

Guide pin reinserted through a sleeve and passed through the incision. (Left knee, Supine position) (AL, anterolateral.)

An 8‐mm drill bit is used to enlarge the femoral tunnel from the external aperture. (Left knee, Supine position) (AL, anterolateral; AM, anteromedial.)

Drilling ALC Tibial Tunnel

A 1 cm skin incision is made at the midpoint between the Gerdy tubercle and the fibular head, approximately 1 cm distal to the joint line. Using a tibial drill guide, a guide pin is inserted obliquely toward the medial side of the tibial tuberosity, about 1 cm lateral to the external aperture of the ACL tibial tunnel. The tunnel is then enlarged with a 6 mm drill bit, adjusted according to the graft diameter (Figure 9).

Tibial drill guide used to locate the ALC tibial tunnel. (Left knee, Supine position) (ACL, anterior cruciate ligament; AL, anterolateral; ALC, anterolateral complex; AM, anteromedial; MP, midpatellar portal.)

Passing of Graft and Femoral Fixation

The prepared graft is guided into position using a passing suture. After confirming proper graft placement, the excess LARS ligament is trimmed. The femoral end of the graft is then fixed using a cannulated ligament screw (LARS, Alk City, Dijon, France) (Figure 10).

The femoral end of the graft is fixed. (Left knee, Supine position) (AL, anterolateral; ALC, anterolateral complex; LARS, ligament advanced reinforcement system.)

Tibial Fixation

The preserved segment of the gracilis tendon is passed through the deep layer of the ITB into the ALC tibial tunnel. The knee is flexed and extended 20 times to pretension the graft and confirm isometry. After verification, the knee is flexed to 30°, and the tibial end of the ACL graft is fixed with a cannulated ligament screw (LARS, Alk city, Dijon, France). With the knee in full extension and neutral rotation, the proximal aperture of the ALC tibial tunnel is fixed using an appropriately sized PEEK anchor (Delta Medical, Beijing, China) (Figure 11). The distal ends of both grafts are additionally reinforced with a spiked ligament staple (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) (Figure 12). Arthroscopic re‐evaluation confirms graft isometry and absence of impingement in the intercondylar fossa (Figure 13). Postoperative Rehabilitation

The affected knee is dressed with sterile compressive gauze, and a hinged knee brace is applied and locked at 30° of flexion. After recovery from anesthesia, the patient begins gradual rehabilitation exercises. Ambulation with crutches is permitted without weight‐bearing on the affected limb. Continuous passive motion training is initiated 3 to 5 days postoperatively. Partial weight‐bearing with crutches is introduced at 3 to 4 weeks, and full weight‐bearing is allowed at 5 to 6 weeks. Light sports activities are permitted at 10 to 12 weeks, and, upon satisfactory muscle strength recovery, the patient gradually resumes normal sports activities at 4 to 6 months postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

This article describes a surgical technique for ACL reconstruction combined with ALC augmentation using a hollow LARS ligament to increase graft diameter. This approach provides a suitable alternative for patients with thin autologous hamstring tendons or high athletic demands. The technique offers 2 main advantages: first, it uses the hollow LARS ligament to augment the diameter of the graft; second, the gracilis tendon is used to reinforce the ALC. With this dual reinforcement, early postoperative rehabilitation and sports resumption are achievable. By maintaining ALC isometry, excessive joint constraint is prevented, thereby minimizing tension on the lateral compartment of the knee. Furthermore, the procedure requires only a small surgical incision and avoids harvesting additional autologous tendons, reducing overall surgical trauma. 3 , 14 , 15

Previous combined ACL and ALL reconstruction techniques predominantly rely on autologous tendons. In patients with small‐diameter autologous tendons, additional harvest of the peroneus longus tendon or hamstring tendons from the contralateral knee is often required, thereby increasing surgical trauma. Direct augmentation using a hollow LARS ligament minimizes the need for additional tendon harvest and reduces associated morbidity. 16 , 17 Moreover, early techniques frequently employ an outside‐in approach to locate the isometric point of the ALL on the femur, which can result in a thin posterior tunnel wall and an increased risk of iatrogenic fractures or neurovascular injury due to drill penetration into the posterior compartment. 18 , 19 In contrast, the present technique precisely positions the external aperture of the femoral tunnel approximately 1 cm proximal to the lateral femoral epicondyle, while the ALC tibial tunnel is positioned midway between the fibular head and Gerdy's tubercle, 1 cm distal to the joint line. Following graft passage, knee flexion and extension are performed to confirm isometry, and fixation is achieved with the knee in full extension and neutral rotation. 20 , 21 , 22

According to previous anatomical studies, the femoral insertion site of the ALL is variable among individuals and is generally located around the lateral femoral epicondyle. 22 The primary limitation of the present technique lies in the potential variability in the location of the external apertures of ACL and ALC femoral tunnels. The inside‐out drilling approach may occasionally cause slight positional deviations, requiring relocalization of the ALC femoral insertion site in certain patients. This issue can be mitigated by assessing graft motion during knee flexion and extension to identify signs of over‐constraint. If over‐constraint is detected, a Kirschner wire should be repositioned proximally and posteriorly relative to the lateral femoral epicondyle. The graft is then looped around the repositioned wire, and the movement pattern is re‐evaluated. Once normal movement without constraint is achieved, the graft can be refixed at the new site using an anchor.

The indications for this technique include ACL revision surgery, patients with pivot shift grades II to III, individuals participating in high‐intensity sports, those with generalized ligamentous laxity, bilateral ACL injuries, or concomitant Segond fractures or lateral femoral condyle notch signs.

This approach effectively restores knee rotational stability while offering the advantages of short operative time and reduced surgical trauma, making it a promising alternative for ACL reconstruction with ALC augmentation. Nonetheless, certain limitations exist and need to be considered carefully by surgeons (Tables 1 and 2).
