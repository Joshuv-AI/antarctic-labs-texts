Matchings: Source, Goal and Faithful Companion This article was prepared for the Centenary Issue of the Journal of the London Mathematical Society, it is submitted for publication in this journal.
András Sebő

Abstract
Matchings were among the earliest motivations for graph theory. They subsequently remained a central goal, inspiring the development of new tools that went well beyond problems directly concerning matchings. These tools proved widely applicable, accompanying the growth of graph theory over the past century. A legendary milestone in this trajectory is W. T. Tutte, The factorization of linear graphs , J. Lond. Math. Soc. (1), 22 , no. 2, (1947), 107–111., which firmly embedded graph theory—through matchings—into the body of classical mathematics, in particular, linear algebra and polynomials. In this note we revisit this article presenting its original content, sketching some aspects of its impact until some recent progress, and trace one of its subsequent lines of development finally leading to a new contribution answering an open challenge and extending known results. keywords : matchings, factors, path matchings, Tutte’s theorems, the method of variables, randomized polynomial algorithms, polynomials, algebraic complexity, T T -joins, conservative weightings, bidirected graphs, jump systems and their intersections



Introduction

After a few isolated eighteenth-century appearances — Euler’s bridges of Königsberg, Jacobi’s work on matrices, contributions of Kempe, Tait, and Heawood on map colouring and Petersen on matchings in cubic graphs — the ideas that shaped modern graph theory began to crystallize in the twentieth century through the work of Egerváry, Kőnig, Hall, Kuhn, Tutte, Edmonds, Lovász, and others.

After some first, foundational results, Tutte’s article
[
55
]
stands as a major milestone in the development of graph theory. His theorem on matchings became a celebrated result in discrete mathematics, and opened pathways towards classical and new areas: linear algebra, probabilistic algorithms, and emerging notions of complexity theory, including Edmonds’ definition of good characterization and polynomial-time solvability, heralding the dawn of complexity theory and further, leading to algebraic and randomized complexity.

Despite its influence, the concrete arguments of this legendary paper remain little known, and are often misunderstood. Section

Section

1.2
Determinants in Indeterminates

Tutte’s theorem itself received the attention it deserves. The algebraic machinery often associated with it, such as determinants and the Pfaffian, has also been credited for its power. This credit is well deserved, though not because such tools are required to prove Tutte’s theorem itself.

As we saw in the previous subsection, determinants are unnecessary, even for Tutte’s original proof. Paradoxically, this algebraic detour, superfluous for the theorem at hand, became a catalyst for major developments: it opened new directions in theoretical computer science, complexity theory, and even led to fresh combinatorial objects, such as the path matchings introduced by Cunningham and Geelen
[
8
]

A remarkable property of determinants explains their predictive power for algorithmic and complexity results as suggested by Lovász
[
33
]

Even though their expansion contains exponentially many terms, their value can be computed in polynomial time via simple algorithms such Gaussian elimination; moreover, if a nonzero multivariable polynomial
of degree

The latter result is the so called

4
4

4

Ore (1922), DeMillo and Lipton, Schwartz, Zippel, (1978-79). The assertion can be proved simply using elementary discrete probabilities, see for instance “Motwani, Raghavan, Probabilistic Algorithms, Cambridge University Press, 1995”. Repeated sampling drives the error probability exponentially to

Algebraic Complexity
:
The use of determinants in matching theory foreshadowed the notion of algebraic complexity. Determinants are polynomials with exponentially many monomials whose values can nevertheless be computed efficiently. This phenomenon is formalized via algebraic circuit complexity, with complexity classes such as VP and VNP, analogues of P and NP.

A recent breakthrough by Srinivasan
[
52
]
constructs explicit multilinear polynomials separating “monotone VP” from “monotone VNP” by demonstrating that a sequence of polynomials with nonnegative coefficients can require exponential-size monotone agebraic circuits.

Algebraic complexity theory, beyond formalizing the phenomenon that polynomials of enormous apparent complexity may admit efficient evaluation, also furnishes a conceptual laboratory in which theorems such as VP
\neq
VNP play a role analogous to the classical, legendary, mysterious, still open “Is P
=
NP ?” question.

Enumeration
: Lovász’s method extends from decision to counting problems. When a graph admits a Pfaffian orientation (see above), the number of perfect matchings can be computed exactly by evaluating a determinant. Determining when such orientations exist is a natural and central open question
[
35
, Section 8.3]
.

Counting perfect matchings arises in statistical physics and motivates several open problems surveyed in
[
35
, Chapter 8]
. One long-standing conjecture asserts that regular graphs in which every edge lies in a perfect matching have exponentially many perfect matchings; this has been proved for cubic graphs
[
13
]
.

This section has chosen to focus on one kind of application of Tutte’s original work on matchings. Entirely graph-theoretical consequences of Tutte’s theorem, such as those explored by Szigeti
[
54
]
, form a rich direction that lies beyond our scope, but can be found in the literature.

1.3
A short combinatorial proof

Despite the simplicity, beauty and novelty of Tutte’s theorem in 1947, the original proof, —whether algebraic or even in its combinatorial form—is not particularly intuitive.
The identity (
1
) as well as its combinatorial consequence in Lemma
1.2
may appear as coming out of a magician’s hat.

We present a version nourished by Lemma
1.4
specialized to an elementary combinatorial statement, and also influenced by Edmonds’ algorithm
[
10
]
as presented in
[
34
, Exercises 7.33–7.34]
.

Lemma 1.5

Let
G
be a graph, and
uv\in E(G)
. Then either

\nu(G-u)<\nu(G)
, or
\nu(G-v)<\nu(G)
, or else for any maximum
matching
M_{u}
of
G-u
, and
M_{v}
of
G-v
:
M_{u}\cup M_{v}

contains an
(u,v)
-path
P
alternating between
M_{u}
and

M_{v}
.

Proof
:
Suppose
\nu(G-u)=\nu(G)=\nu(G-v)
. Then
M_{u}
covers
v
, and
M_{v}
covers
u
, otherwise the edge
uv
could be added for a larger matching of
G
.

Since all degrees of
M_{u}\cup M_{v}
are
0
,
1
or
2
, this union is the disjoint union of alternating paths.
Moreover,
the other endpoint of the alternating path containing
u
is
v
: if it were some
x\neq v
, then exchanging on it, we get again a matching of maximum size missing both
u
and
v
. (Figure
3
).

\Box

Bipartite matchings are discussed in
[
4
]
. We only state Kőnig’s theorem
[
25
]
on the way:

Theorem 1.3

If
G=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph, then
\tau(G)=\nu(G)
.

Proof
:
The inequality
\tau(G)\geq\nu(G)
is straightforward. For the reverse inequality, we proceed by induction. Let
uv\in E
be arbitrary. Either deleting
u
or deleting

decreases
\nu
–say
\nu(G-u)<\nu(G)
– since otherwise there exists an even
(u,v)
path by Lemma
1.5
, contradicting bipartiteness. Then
\tau(G)\leq\tau(G-u)+1\leq\nu(G-u)+1\leq\nu(G)
, where we also used the theorem for

.
\Box

Short proof of Theorem
1.2
:
Let
uv\in E
be arbitrary. If
\nu(G-u)<\nu(G)
, we are done: a set
X\subseteq V\setminus\{u\}
satisfying the min-max equality in

extends to
X\cup\{u\}
for
G
.

Otherwise, if
\nu(G-u)=\nu(G)=\nu(G-v)
, Lemma
1.5
provides an alternating path
P
(which becomes a circuit if the edge
e
is added). In
G/P
the number of missed vertices by any matching

does not increase upon expanding

: whether or not the contracted vertex is matched by
M
, one can extend

by adding a perfect matching of the circuit

removing the vertex which is matched outside

, or an arbitrary vertex if unmatched.
\Box

The last reasoning of the proof also explains the correctness of Edmonds’ algorithm
[
10
,
34
]
[Exercises 7.33–7.34]: unshrinking an odd cycle, the number of missed vertices remains, and barriers remain barriers.

2
Classical factors

In an article following his seminal work on matchings, Tutte
[
57
]
derived necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of subgraphs with prescribed degrees not necessarily equal to
1
. His methods and results naturally extend to more general settings, allowing upper and lower degree bounds and parity conditions, and are extendible to orientations under degree constraints.

In this section we present these generalizations in a unified framework: first parity-restricted subgraphs, then degree-bounded subgraphs, and finally their simultaneous occurrence with possible extensions to orientations. Since the relevant definitions are most naturally introduced together, we adopt a broader perspective when we introduce the relevant notions in preparation for the abstract generalization of the next section, which builds on the results established here.

We consider subgraphs whose degree vectors are required to lie in a non-empty set
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{V}
. Such subgraphs are called
H
-factors
. If a
H
-factor does not exist, we are still interested in
F\subseteq E
minimizing the distance
\mu(d_{F},H)>0
. We simplify the notation:
\mu(F,H):=\mu(d_{F},H)
, that is also called the
deficiency
of
F
(with respect to
H
);
F\subseteq E
attaining this minimum is
(
H
-)optimal
. In order to prescribe the degree constraints independently in each vertex,
H
will always be defined as the Cartesian product of sets of one-dimensional vectors, that is, of sets of numbers.

We say that
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{V}
is a
sponge
, if it is the Cartesian product of nonempty subsets
H(v)\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
v\in V
,
and each
H(v)(v\in V)
is a one-dimensional
sponge
, i.e.

\hbox{ if }a,b\in H(v),\hbox{ and }a<i<b\hbox{ with }i\notin H(v),\hbox{ then }i-1,i+1\in H(v)\vskip-5.69046pt

holds, i.e. there are no “gaps” of at least two consecutive integers in
H(v)
.
We write
l
and
u
for the vector of entrywise minima and maxima of
H
.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
a sponge (called a “prescription” in
[
29
]
) is restricted to be
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{V}
, and then
0\leq l\leq u\leq d
, where
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{V}
is the degree function.

A
gap
of
K\subset\mathbb{Z}
is a closed interval
[i,j]
,
i,j\in\mathbb{Z},i<j
such that
K\cap[i,j]=\emptyset
, and
i-1,i+1\in K
; its length is
j-i-1
.

It is essential to restrict ourselves to sponges
[
35
, Section 10.2, paragraph after Exercise 10.2.2]
: the
H
-factor problem is already
NP
-hard in cubic graphs when
H(v)
is
\{0,3\}
or
\{1\}
for each
v\in V
. In contrast, when
H
is a sponge, a rich structural theory exists: degree-constrained factors with parity conditions and bounds were analysed in
[
28
]
, and extended to
H
-factors where
H
is a sponge
[
29
]
. However,
a polynomial algorithm was missing for a while for sponges, and explicitly raised in
[
29
]
and repeated in
[
35
]
.

A set of integers is an
interval
if it contains all integers between its minimum and maximum, and a
parity sponge
if it contains every second integer in this range. Intervals and parity sponges are referred to as
classical
sponges.
A sponge
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{V}
is called
interval sponge
,
parity sponge
or
classical sponge
if
H(v)
is of the corresponding type for each
v\in V
; the associated factors are named accordingly. Classical sponges may thus have both
parity-vertices
, and
interval-vertices
.

It is convenient to describe a classical sponge
H
with the triple
(l,u,\Pi)
, where
l,u:V\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{+}
are as before, and
\Pi\subseteq V
is the set of parity vertices. We assume without loss of generality that
l(p)\equiv u(p)\mod 2
for all
p\in\Pi
, and that
\{v\in V:l(v)=u(v)\}\subseteq\Pi
; the corresponding sponge is then denoted by
H_{(l,u,\Pi)}
, and
H_{(l,u,\Pi)}
-factors are also called
(l,u,\Pi)
-factors
.

The following theorem provides a good characterization for the existence of classical factors. It is essentially the same as the main result of
[
57
]
or of
[
27
]
for interval sponges; a polynomial algorithm follows from
[
12
]
. We present an algorithmic proof in Section
2.2
. For simplicity we state only the existence version but the minimization of the deficiency can be deduced with the same method. The ultimate generalization in Section
3
addresses deficiencies directly.

Theorem 2.1

No
(l,u,\Pi)
-factor exists if and only if there exist disjoint
L,U\subseteq V
such that

l(L)+d_{G}(L,U)+\omega(L,U)>u(U)+\sum_{x\in L}d_{G}(x),

where
\omega(L,U)
is the number of
(L,U)
-odd compoents
C
of
G-(L\cup U)
, i.e. components satisfying
C\subseteq\Pi
for which the parity of
l(C)\equiv u(C)\,\mod~2
differs from that of
d(C,L)
.

We first verify the so called “easy direction”. Suppose that an
(l,u,\Pi)
-factor
F
exists, and let
L,U\subseteq V
be disjoint. We first estimate from above the sum of the degrees in
F
on
U
together with the sum of the degrees in
E\setminus F
on
L
:

\sum_{y\in U}d_{F}(y)+\sum_{x\in L}(d_{G}(x)-d_{F}(x))\leq u(U)-l(L)+\sum_{x\in L}d_{G}(x).

The left hand side can be written as

2|E(U)\cap F|+d_{F}(U,V\setminus(U\cup L))+d_{F}(U,L)+2|E(L)\setminus F|+d_{E\setminus F}(L,V\setminus(U\cup L))+d_{E\setminus F}(L,U)\geq

\geq d_{F}(U,V\setminus(U\cup L))+d_{E\setminus F}(L,V\setminus(U\cup L))+d_{G}(U,L)\geq\omega(L,U)+d_{G}(U,L),

where in the first inequality
|E(U)\cap F|,\,|E(L)\setminus F|\geq 0
,
d_{F}(U,L)+d_{E\setminus F}(L,U)=d_{G}(L,U)
are used. Moreover, each
(L,U)
-odd component of
G-(L\cup U)
must contribute at least one edge either of
F
to
U
, or of
E\setminus F
to
L
, yielding the final inequality. We conclude by

\omega(L,U)+d_{G}(U,L)\leq u(U)-l(L)+\sum_{x\in L}d_{G}(x).

Thus the inequality of the theorem is violated whenever an
(l,u,\Pi)
-factor exists, completing the considered easy direction of the proof. In fact, the difference of the left and right hand sides is a lower bound for the deficiency
\mu(F,H_{(l,u,\Pi)})
, and in Section
2.2
we present
F\subseteq E
with equality here. We in fact outline a unified approach covering factorization and orientation problems under degree constraints, including algorithmic aspects. Theorem
2.1
will be proved as a representative example; this is yet another proof of Theorem
1.2
, by specialization, and contains min-max, structural and algorithmic consequences to classical factors outlined in Section
2.2
.

2.2
Adding bounds

A similar style of results to that of the previous section can be considered for classical sponges. However, we restrict attention here to Tutte-type
existence theorems
. Tutte
[
57
]
originally assumed
|H(v)|=1
for each
v\in V
. Lovász
[
27
,
35
, Section 10.2]
widely extended this framework by allowing arbitrary intervals in
H

H
-factors. Allowing parity constraints in intervals for an arbitrary subset of vertices does not substantially complicate the results, proofs, or algorithms. The corresponding classical factors then admit simple, parity-based good characterization, structural description and algorithms, which does not seem to be the case for further extensions. However, polynomial algorithms that reduce to the classical setting exist for some of them (Section
3
).

In this section we therefore restrict ourselves to feasibility questions for classical factors. These already cover Tutte’s factor theorem
[
57
]
and, more generally, Lovász’s min–max and structure theorems
[
27
]
, algorithmically, while also serving the abstract setting of the next Section. The related optimization problems fall within the linear programming framework of Edmonds and Johnson
[
12

Graph factors can be understood as intersections of degree vectors of subgraphs and sponges. Both are “jump systems”. This seems to be essential for their tractability.

Other examples of jump systems are independent sets or bases of matroids, feasible sets of delta-matroids, integer points of polymatroids or bisubmodular polyhedra. Lemma 3.1

Let

be a graph and let

Choose

and

so that

We claim that
F^{*}=F_{H}

Suppose otherwise. Then

is not optimal, so there exists

\mu\bigl(d_{F^{*}}(a),H(a)\bigr)<\mu\bigl(d_{F_{H}}(a),H(a)\bigr).\vskip-8.5359pt

(9)

Claim.

There exists an edge
e=ab\in F^{*}\Delta F_{H}
such that

(F_{H};a,b)
is an elementary change.

Case 1.

d_{F_{H}}(a)\geq u(a)
.

d_{F^{*}}(a)<d_{F_{H}}(a)
.
Choose any edge

Case 2.

.

.

We now state and prove the promised reduction of general factors to classical factors

Theorem 3.1

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
F\subseteq E
, and

a sponge. Then

is not optimal if and only if there exists an edge-set

with smaller distance than
\mu(F,H)
from
H_{F}
or from at least one of the
2n
escapes.

It is important to note a subtle point in this assertion: the goal is not to decrease the deficiency of
F
with respect to the environment or the escapes, but to decrease one of these below
\mu(F,H)
. In other words, the optimality of
F
with respect to
H
cannot be simplified to merely the optimality with respect to the concerned
2n+1
parity sponges:

Consider a graph on two vertices,
a
and
b
joined by
9
parallel edges. Let

H(a):=\{0,1,3,5,7,9\},\hskip 10.00002ptH(b):=\{2,4,6,8\},\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptF:=E.\vskip-7.11317pt

Then
F
is optimal, and
\mu(d_{F},H)=1
. But
F
is not optimal with respect to

H_{F,\underline{a}}
: since
l_{F}(a)-1=0
we have

H_{F,\underline{a}}(a)=\{0\}
,
H_{F,\underline{a}}(b)=\{2,4,6,8\}
, and hence

\mu(F,H_{F,\underline{a}})=10
, whereas for

consisting of a single edge,
\mu(F^{\prime},H_{F,\underline{a}})=1
. Thus, although

is not optimal with respect to

, Theorem
3.1
does establish optimality: the minimum deficiency of

with respect to
H_{F}
or to the escapes is not smaller than

.

Improving with respect to one of the escapes does not necessarily imply an improvement with respect to
H
.
(Improving with respect to the environment obviously does by Fact
3.2
(i).)

If parallel edges are to be avoided, one may subdivide each edge

into two edges with a new vertex

, and assign
H(v_{e}):=\{0,2\}
on the new vertices

(e\in E)
, obtaining an equivalent example without parallel edges.

Proof
:
The if part is easy: since

, for all

, we have for each

\displaystyle\mu(F^{\prime},H)\leq\min\big\{\mu(F^{\prime},H_{F}),\cup_{v\in V}\{\mu(F^{\prime},H_{F,\underline{v}}),\mu(F^{\prime},H_{F,\overline{v}})\}\big\},

and therefore, if the right hand side is smaller than

, then so is the left hand side, showing that

is not optimal.

To prove the only if part, suppose that
F
is not optimal, and apply Lemma
3.1
: let

F_{H}\subseteq E,\,F\rightarrow F_{H},\,\mu(F_{H},H)<\mu(F,H).\vskip-7.11317pt

(10)

Let

be the family of edge-sets occurring in the series of elementary changes starting with

and ending with

.

If for all

we have

, we are done,
for then

, and

\mu(F_{H},H_{F})=\mu(F_{H},H_{F_{H}})=\mu(F_{H},H)<\mu(F,H),\vskip-7.11317pt

where the first equality repeats

, the second is Fact

(i) applied to

, and the last, strict inequality is (

).

Otherwise, let

,

such that the next edge-set in the trajectory

is

, where

is the first set of the trajectory with

,
(e=ab\in E)
.

Suppose first that

. Since

, we have

. Applying Fact

(iii) to

i.e. the distance of

from the escape

. If

\Box

Corollary 3.1

An optimal general factor is found by solving
(2n+1)n
parity factor problems.

Proof
:
For given
F
, optimizing for

and the
2n

parity optimization problems. If none of these improves

, then by Theorem
3.1

is optimal. Otherwise, again by the theorem,
Y\subseteq E
,
\mu(Y,H)<\mu(F,H)
is found.

Starting with

minimizing the deficiency of the parity factor problem

\Box

The subtle point noted after Theorem
3.1
deserves attention when bounding the complexity. It is stated in
[
7

that “any improved solution relative to” [the environment and its escapes as we defined it] “is also an improved solution relative to

”. As the example above the proof shows, this is unfortunately not true in general, our task is more complex:

we have to improve the distance from the environment or escapes below
\mu(F,H)
.

Using the mentioned rough bound of

for finding a first

and that

is then at distance at most

), we see that finding improving paths in

and thereafter) for parity factors, and at most

.

In particular, the following curiosity follows:

Corollary 3.2

A classical deficiency problem for degree vectors can be solved by solving

\Box

3.2
Jump systems and sponges

A workshop held in Grenoble in 1995 on “Delta-matroids, jump systems and bisubmodular polyhedra” concluded with a lecture of Lovász presenting results developed during the meeting. These extended his theorems on classical graph factors to jump systems
[
36
]
.

As in the case of degree vectors
[
29
,
35
]
, however,
[
36
]
leaves open algorithmic questions about the deficiency problem of jump systems with respect to
H
, when
H
is a general sponge. This issue is explicitly raised in the closing paragraph of
[
37
]
.

The intersection of jump systems and sponges had in fact already been addressed in
[
42
]
, where a connection with a problem of Recski also became apparent. The tools offered by
[
36
]
were instrumental:
methods known for classical factors were translated into the language of jump systems.
For jump systems, elementary changes are replaced by
pushes
defined as follows, allowing one to proceed in the sequel by full analogy with Section
3.1
:

Let
J\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be a jump system, and
H\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
a sponge. A
push at

i\in[n]
of
x\in J
towards
H
consists of a step
x^{\prime}
at
i
towards
H
, followed by an arbitrary step
x^{\prime\prime}\in J
from
x^{\prime}
; as usual, we set
x^{\prime\prime}:=x^{\prime}
if
x^{\prime}\in J
. In contrast with the
2
-step-axiom, when
x^{\prime}\notin J
we do not require
\mu(x^{\prime\prime},H)<\mu(x^{\prime},H)
; only the first step must decrease the distance to
H
. We denote this push by
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
. If
x,y\in J
, a push from
x
towards
y
exists by the
2
-step-axiom.

Fact
3.1
and Fact
3.2
were directly stated in erms of vectors so that they could serve both factors in graphs and jump systems. However, Fact
3.3
was stated in terms of elementary changes in graphs, and we restate it for pushes because of minor differences. In jump systems both steps of a push
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
may occur in the same direction
i\in[n]
, (i.e.
x^{\prime}-x=x^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime}=e_{i}
), corresponding exactly to loops in graphs, but this is no more an “exception” here; additonally,
x^{\prime}=x^{\prime\prime}\in J
can occur, unlike in graphs. Despite these differences the assertions and arguments are mostly word to word the same, contain the graph special case, and are sometimes even simpler for vectors:

Fact 3.4

Let
H
be a sponge, and
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
a push at
i
, with second step at
j
,
i,j\in[n]
. Then

(i)

\mu(x^{\prime}(i),H(i))=\mu(x(i),H(i))-1,

(ii)

\mu(x^{\prime\prime},H)\leq\mu(x,H)
,

(iii)
If
H_{x^{\prime\prime}}\neq H_{x}
, then
x(j),x^{\prime\prime}(j)\in H(j)
,
\mu(x^{\prime\prime},H)<\mu(x,H)
, furthermore

– either
x^{\prime\prime}(j)=x(j)-1
,
x^{\prime\prime}(j)=l_{x}(j)-1\in H(j)
,  and
\mu(x^{\prime\prime},H_{x,\underline{j}})<\mu(x,H)
,

– or
x^{\prime}(j)=x(j)+1
,
x^{\prime\prime}(j)=u_{x}(j)+1\in H(j)
,  and
\mu(x^{\prime\prime},H_{x,\overline{j}})<\mu(x,H)
.

Proof
:
(i) can be readily checked from the definition of a push, and (ii) is a consequence of (i), because
|x^{\prime}(j)-x^{\prime\prime}(j)|\leq 1
.

For (iii), suppose for a contradiction that

x^{\prime\prime}(j)\notin H(j)
. Then, since
H
is a sponge, (
11
) and consequently (
12
) hold for
v=j
, and it can be seen from (i) that for
v=i
as well.

l_{x^{\prime\prime}}(v)=l_{x}(v),\,u_{x^{\prime\prime}}(v)=u_{x}(v),\vskip-5.69046pt

(11)

H_{x^{\prime\prime}}(v)=H_{x}(v).\vskip-2.84544pt

(12)

For
v\in V\setminus\{i,j\}
(
11
), (
12
)
are obvious. Thus
H_{x^{\prime\prime}}=H_{x}
,
contradicting the assumption, and proving

x^{\prime\prime}(j)\in H(j)
, moreover:

x^{\prime\prime}(j)\in H(j),\hskip 10.00002ptx(j)\in H(j),\hskip 10.00002ptl_{x}(j)=x(j),\vskip-5.69046pt

(13)

because
x(j)\notin H(j)
would imply (

) and then (

) would again hold for all

.

Let us check next that under the condition of (iii) we have:

\mu(x^{\prime\prime},H)<\mu(x,H).\vskip-5.69046pt

(14)

We have

\mu(x^{\prime\prime}(i),H(i))<\mu(x(i),H(i))

\mu(x^{\prime\prime}(j),H(j))\leq\mu(x(j),H(j))

2n
escapes from
H_{x}
. An optimal
x\in J
is found by solving
(2n+1)n
parity sponge problems.

Proof
:
The if part is easy: since
H_{x},H_{F,\underline{i}},H_{F,\overline{i}}\subseteq H
, for all
i\in[n]
, we have for each
x^{\prime}\in J
:

\displaystyle\mu(x^{\prime},H)\leq\min\big\{\mu(x^{\prime},H_{x}),\cup_{i=1}^{n}\{\mu(x^{\prime},H_{x,\underline{i}}),\mu(x^{\prime},H_{x,\overline{i}})\}\big\},

and therefore, if the right hand side is smaller than
\mu(x,H)
, then so is the left hand side, showing that
x
is not optimal.

To prove the only if part, suppose that
x
is not optimal, and apply Lemma
3.2
: let

x_{H}\in J,\,x\rightarrow x_{H},\,\mu(x_{H},H)<\mu(x,H).

(17)

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq J
be the set of those points of
J
that occur in the series of pushes starting with
x
and finishing with
x_{H}
.
If for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
we have
H_{f}=H_{x}
, we are done, for then
H_{x_{H}}=H_{x}
, and

\mu(x_{H},H_{x})=\mu(x_{H},H_{x_{H}})=\mu(x_{H},H)<\mu(x,H),

where the first equality repeats
H_{x_{H}}=H_{x}
, the second is Fact
3.2
(i), and the inequality is (
17
).

Otherwise, let
y\in\mathcal{F}
,
H_{y}=H_{x}
such that the next push in the trajectory
x\rightarrow x_{H}
is
(y,y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})
, where
y^{\prime\prime}
is the first point of the trajectory with
H_{y^{\prime\prime}}\neq H_{x}
. Let this be a push at
i
with second step at
j
. (Possibly
j=i
.) Then
y^{\prime}=y\pm e_{i}
.

Suppose first that
y^{\prime}(i)=y(i)-1
. Since
H_{x}=H_{y}
we have
H_{x,\underline{j}}=H_{y,\underline{j}}
. Applying Fact
3.4
(iii) to
y^{\prime\prime}
and the sponge
H_{y,\underline{j}}
and then Fact
3.4
(ii) successively along the elementary changes through each
f\in\mathcal{F}
in the order they occur in the trajectory
x\rightarrow y
, we obtain

\mu(y^{\prime\prime},H_{x,\underline{j}})=\mu(y^{\prime\prime},H_{y,\underline{j}})<\mu(y,H)\leq\ldots\leq\mu(f,H)\leq\ldots\leq\mu(x,H),

i.e. the distance of
y^{\prime\prime}
from the escape
H_{x,\underline{j}}
is strictly smaller than
\mu(x,H)
. If
y^{\prime}(i)=y(i)+1
, in an entirely symmetric way, the distance of
y^{\prime\prime}
from the escape
H_{x,\overline{j}}
is smaller than
\mu(x,H)
.

The final statement of the theorem follows exactly as in the proof of Corollary
3.1
.

\Box

Corollary
3.2
extends in the same manner, without the time bound.

The following related problem was recalled by Recski
[
42
]
, raised by applications in
[
41
,
43
]
.

Problem
: Given a number
q\in\mathbb{N}
, a finite set
S
with a partition and a sponge associated with each class of the partition, and a matroid on
S
, is there an independent set of the matroid of size
q
, so that the number of elements in each partition class is in the sponge of the partition.

This problem fits the sponge deficiency framework of jump systems, but Theorem
3.2
offered no immediate solution, as the classical case remained open.

Szabó
[
53
]
resolved the parity deficiency problem for these jump systems via matroid parity in polynomial time, and using this, Theorem
3.2
does indeed imply a solution of Recski’s problem in polynomial time. Furthermore, this result is then applied in
[
53
]
to rigidity theory.

Conclusion
:

We have revisited Tutte’s original proof of his celebrated theorem on matchings. It is not only a cornerstone result, but also a fertile source of combinatorial ideas, opening the door to far-reaching developments in algebraic methods and complexity theory.

We then traced a coherent line of development leading to increasingly general degree-constraints, and ultimately to a solution of the general factor problem in terms of jump systems. Along this path, new methods, notions, algorithms naturally occur.

This article reported on the journey of a theorem serving simultaneously as a source of motivation, a guiding goal, and a lasting companion, shaping methods, viewpoints, and problems.
