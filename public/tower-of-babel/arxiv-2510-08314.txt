To Ask or Not to Ask: Learning to Require Human Feedback
Andrea Pugnana, Giovanni De Toni ∗, Cesare Barbera ∗, Roberto Pellungrini, Bruno Lepri, Andrea Passerini

Abstract
Developing decision-support systems that complement human performance in classification tasks remains an open challenge. A popular approach, Learning to Defer (LtD), allows a Machine Learning (ML) model to pass difficult cases to a human expert. However, LtD treats humans and ML models as mutually exclusive decision-makers, restricting the expert contribution to mere predictions. To address this limitation, we propose Learning to Ask (LtA), a new framework that handles both when and how to incorporate expert input in an ML model. LtA is based on a two-part architecture: a standard ML model and an enriched model trained with additional expert human feedback, with a formally optimal strategy for selecting when to query the enriched model. We provide two practical implementations of LtA: a sequential approach, which trains the models in stages, and a joint approach, which optimises them simultaneously. For the latter, we design surrogate losses with realisable-consistency guarantees. Our experiments with synthetic and real expert data demonstrate that LtA provides a more flexible and powerful foundation for effective human–AI collaboration.

The concept of complementary performance has become an important focus in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI)
[
15
]
and, more recently, Hybrid Decision Making (HDM), motivated by the observation that fundamental differences between human reasoning and ML systems allow each to excel on distinct subsets of a task.
However, determining when and how humans and ML systems effectively complement each other remains an open challenge
[
36
]
.
This is particularly critical in high-stakes settings, where systems must be designed to reliably assess whether to rely on the human expert or the machine prediction to maximise overall performance
[
39
]
.
Among various decision-making frameworks, Learning to Defer (LtD)
[
19
]
has recently attracted significant attention.
LtD extends standard supervised learning by introducing a
deferring mechanism
, allowing an ML model to either predict or delegate the decision to a human expert
[
33
]
.
This framework naturally enables more adaptive and collaborative systems, especially in scenarios where human expertise complements algorithmic predictions.

If our goal is complementarity, Learning to Defer (LtD) approaches still have two major shortcomings.
First, LtD methods require human experts to provide task-specific predictions.
However, such a requirement overlooks other forms of expert feedback, which can be leveraged to improve the final task prediction,
e.g.
, concept annotations or partial feature descriptors.
Second, LtD frameworks treat the expert and the machine as
independent
decision-makers, failing to exploit existing synergies between them, and often resulting in systematic underfitting
[
18
]
, thereby discarding valuable information.

In this paper, we propose a novel framework,
Learning to Ask (LtA)
, which addresses key limitations of existing approaches while preserving the flexibility of the LtD paradigm.
Instead of optimising when to delegate the entire decision to either the machine or the human expert, LtA focuses on when to
ask for
expert feedback during the prediction process.
This is achieved through a two-component architecture consisting of a standard classifier and an enriched model that integrates expert input when needed. See Figure
1
for a visual representation.
This design not only captures the potential
complementarity
between the two actors but also enables synergistic collaborations, allowing both the machine and the expert to contribute simultaneously.

Our contributions.

First, we show that there exist data distributions (potentially many) for which Learning to Defer (LtD) is provably suboptimal
e.g.,
deferring to either the expert or the machine does not improve the expected accuracy (Section
3
).
Then, we introduce the formal framework for training ML models that can determine when to ask for expert feedback (Section
4
), under general additional inputs, and we derive the theoretically optimal
selection strategy
for Learning to Ask (LtA) (Theorem
1
).
Furthermore, we propose two different strategies to learn LtA methods:
(i)
a sequential model, which first trains the enriched model and then learns the standard model and the policy using well-known LtD losses;
(ii)
a joint model, which exploits novel realizable-consistent surrogate loss functions that enable joint optimization under LtA (Theorem
2
).
Lastly, we empirically validate our proposed strategy on synthetic and real-world classification tasks (Section
5
), showing that LtA outperforms LtD when richer feedback is provided.
We have released an open-source version of the source code used for the experiments and the raw results at the following link
https://github.com/andrepugni/LearningToAsk

Background

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a feature space, and let
{\mathcal{Y}}
be the target space.
In LtD, the goal is to learn a model
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
and a selector
s:{\mathcal{X}}\to\{0,1\}
, that can predict the target variable and when to defer to another (human) expert, respectively.
We define the hypothesis space of the predictors and selection strategies as
{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
. Further, in our paper, we denote the human expert as another
fixed
predictor
f^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
.
Given an unknown probability distribution
P^{\prime}
over
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
, the goal of LtD is to minimize the following objective
2
2

2

To reduce notational burden, we denote with
\mathbb{E}_{v}
the expectation computed with respect to a random variable
V
drawn from some distribution
P_{V}
,
e.g.,

\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y,f^{\prime})}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y,f^{\prime})\sim P^{\prime}}[\cdot]
.
:

\min_{(f,s)\in{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y,f^{\prime})}[L_{0-1}^{def}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),s({\mathbf{x}}),f^{\prime},y\right)]

(1)

where
L_{0-1}^{def}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),s({\mathbf{x}}),f^{\prime},y\right)=\mathbb{I}_{\{f({\mathbf{x}})\neq y\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{s({\mathbf{x}})=0\}}+(\alpha\mathbb{I}_{\{f^{\prime}\neq y\}}+\delta)\mathbb{I}_{\{s({\mathbf{x}})=1\}}
is the
deferral 0-1 loss function
, with a normalizing factor
\alpha\in[0,1]
and defer cost
\delta\in[0,1]
modeling the cost of querying a human expert.
Unless mentioned, we assume
\alpha=1
and
\delta=0
, as done in most LtD literature
[
26
,
27
,
22
]
.
The loss function in Eqn. (
1
) is generally intractable and hard to directly optimise, hence several works have considered
surrogate losses
.
Formally, let us denote by
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(q)
the generalization error of an hypothesis
q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
with respect to loss
L
,
i.e.,

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}},y,f^{\prime}}\left[L(q({\mathbf{x}}),y,f^{\prime})\right]
, and by
{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{*}
the best-in-class generalization error,
i.e.,

{\mathcal{E}}_{L}^{*}=\inf_{q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}},y,f^{\prime}}\left[L(q({\mathbf{x}}),y,f^{\prime})\right]
.
In the context of LtD, the hypothesis space
{\mathcal{Q}}
consists of all pairs of predictors
f
and selection strategies
s
.
One desirable property of surrogate losses is
Bayes-consistency
.
This property ensures that, in the asymptotic regime, minimising a surrogate loss
L_{surr}
over the entire hypothesis class
{\mathcal{Q}}
(
e.g.,

f
and
s
in LtD) guarantees the minimisation of the original intractable loss function
L_{orig}
over the same class
[
23
]
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{L_{surr}}(q_{n})-{\mathcal{E}}^{*}_{L_{surr}}=0\implies\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{L_{orig}}(q_{n})-{\mathcal{E}}^{*}_{L_{orig}}=0

(2)

where
q_{n}
is a sequence of hypotheses, each learned from
n
samples.

In general, the property in Eqn.
2
is defined over the family of all measurable functions, which is impractical in real-world settings where learning is restricted to a limited hypothesis class.
To address this, a key property for surrogate losses is the
realisable
{\mathcal{Q}}
-consistency
.
Formally, a surrogate loss is
relizable
{\mathcal{Q}}
-consistent
if, whenever there exists a hypothesis
q^{*}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{L_{orig}}(q^{*})=0
, we have that if
q^{\prime}=\argmin_{q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}{\mathcal{E}}_{L_{surr}}(q)
, then
{\mathcal{E}}_{L_{surr}}(q^{\prime})=0
.
In layman’s terms, it means that if the “perfect” hypothesis (
e.g.,

(f,s)^{*}
in LtD) exists in the given class, then minimising the surrogate loss will asymptotically recover it
[
23
]
.
An example of such a realizably consistent surrogate loss for LtD, proposed by
Madras et al. 2018
, replaces the discontinuous deferral loss with a combination of cross-entropy terms:

\displaystyle(1-s({\mathbf{x}}))\cdot\ell_{CE}(f({\mathbf{x}}),y)+s({\mathbf{x}})\cdot\ell_{CE}(f^{\prime},y)

(3)

where
\ell_{CE}
denotes the standard cross-entropy loss.

Further related work.

LtD is an instance of hybrid decision making where the automated predictor learns when to defer predictions to a human expert.
Due to the difficulties in directly optimizing Eqn. (
1
), consistent surrogate losses have been proposed to learn both the selection function and the predictor
[
26
,
29
,
5
,
40
,
27
,
4
,
18
,
43
]
.
Recent works extend the LtD problem to account for multiple human experts
[
41
,
20
]
; cases where the ML model is already given and not jointly trained
[
20
]
; and how to assess the causal effect of introducing LtD methods
[
30
]
.

Our work is closely related to both
Wilder et al. 2020
and
Charusaie et al. 2024
. Similarly, they consider the possibility of incorporating human expert feedback on the final task as additional input.
However, differently from us, they

Further, they lack any formal analysis of their learning approaches, while we provide new theoretical results for the optimal selection strategy (Theorem

Other human-AI frameworks.

Several works have focused on improving human-AI collaboration.
For instance, recent papers focus on how conformal prediction
[
34
]
can improve the joint decision-making of human-AI teams
[
37
,
10
]
.

Alur et al. 2023
develop a statistical framework to understand whether human experts can add value for a given prediction task, providing a data-driven procedure to test this hypothesis.
In

We differ from their work as our focus is to learn models that know when to ask for expert feedback, while

On the Suboptimality of Learning to Defer

In the previous section, we have shown how LtD admits practical and consistent surrogate loss functions to minimise the objective in Eqn.
1
with finite sample guarantees.
Nevertheless, there exist data distributions where the optimal deferring policy under Learning to Defer (LtD) can be suboptimal.
Consider a stylized classification task with a discrete random variable
{\mathbf{X}}=(x_{1},x_{2})
, where
x_{1},x_{2}\in\{0,1\}
, and a label variable
Y\in\{0,1,2,3\}
.
Each label
y\in Y
corresponds deterministically to a unique combination of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
.
Let
x_{1}
represent a feature used by an automated decision support system (
e.g.,
a complex function of vital signs), and
x_{2}
a costly feature accessible only by processing additional information from a human expert (
e.g.,
oral medical history).
Denote the machine’s prediction by
f(x_{1})
, based solely on
x_{1}
, and the expert’s prediction by
f^{\prime}(x_{2})
based solely on
x_{2}
.
Both agents are assumed to act as Bayes optimal predictors.
If the ML model can only observe
x_{1}
, we have
P\left(f(x_{1})\mid x_{1}=0\right)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and
P\left(f(x_{1})\mid x_{1}=1\right)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
for
f(x_{1})\in\{0,2\}
and
f(x_{1})\in\{1,3\}
, respectively.
Conversely,
P\left(f^{\prime}(x_{2})\mid x_{2}=0\right)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and
P\left(f^{\prime}(x_{2})\mid x_{2}=1\right)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
for
f^{\prime}(x_{2})\in\{0,1\}
and
f^{\prime}(x_{2})\in\{2,3\}
, respectively.
Given an instance
{\mathbf{x}}
, the expected accuracy of an LtD system with strategy
s(x_{1})
is a convex combination of the expert’s and the machine’s accuracy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[s(x_{1})\mathbb{I}_{\{f(x_{1})=y\}}+(1-s(x_{1}))\mathbb{I}_{\{f^{\prime}(x_{2})=y\}}\right]

(4)

In this setting, no selector
s(\cdot)
can achieve an expected accuracy greater than
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
The LtD system fails to exploit the complementary information available to the expert and the ML model,
i.e.,

x_{2}
in our example.
Now consider an alternative model that merges expert input into the classifier,
e.g.,
the expert provides the collected feature
x_{2}
to a unified model.
Let
g(x_{1},x_{2})
denote the prediction of this combined system.
With access to both
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, the model can now perfectly predict the true label
Y
, achieving optimal accuracy.
Then, for any
s(x_{1})>0
, the enriched system using
g(x_{1},x_{2})
achieves an accuracy strictly greater than
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
Figure
2
shows empirically the suboptimality of the oracle LtD, in three different scenarios, based on the base accuracy of the predictor and the human expert, given the features’ informativeness.
See Appendix
A.1
for the full derivation and a description of the setup.

Nevertheless, obtaining online expert feedback, in the form of a prediction or additional features for each example, might be costly, and not always possible
3
3

3

For example, consider a decision support system for the Emergency Department (ED) triage.
ED physicians face frequent interruptions and a high workload
[
7
]
.
Thus, the automated system must carefully optimise when to request additional expert feedback.

.
Thus, in the next section, we focus on characterising the optimal selection strategy under
budget
, balancing the need to elicit additional feedback from a human expert with maximising the system’s accuracy.

4
Learning to Ask

We aim to extend Eqn. (
1
) by explicitly modelling
f^{\prime}
as a different function
g:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
, where
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the space of expert feedback, with
d>0
.
Intuitively, one can think of
{\mathcal{H}}
as some other costly features to get beyond
{\mathcal{X}}
, including expert predictions for the task at hand (as in LtD), expert uncertainty predictions (
e.g.,
how certain is the doctor about its diagnosis), extra features (
e.g.,
some extra tests run by a physician), or some unstructured annotation (
e.g.,
the whole medical report).
We call
g
the
enriched predictor
, while we refer to
f
as the
standard predictor
. Notably, LtD is a specific setting of our approach,
i.e.,
whenever
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{Y}}
and
g({\mathbf{x}},h)=h
.
Let us define the
L_{0-1}^{ask}
loss as:

L_{0-1}^{ask}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),g({\mathbf{x}},h),s({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)=\mathbb{I}_{\{f({\mathbf{x}})\neq y\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{s({\mathbf{x}})=0\}}+\mathbb{I}_{\{g(x,h)\neq y\}}\mathbb{I}_{\{s({\mathbf{x}})=1\}}

(5)

where
s({\mathbf{x}}):{\mathcal{X}}\to\{0,1\}
is a selection function, which determines which model will provide the final prediction,
i.e.,
when
s({\mathbf{x}})=0
the standard model predicts, otherwise the enriched model predicts.
Since this loss is also intractable and hard to directly optimise, we consider a surrogate loss
L^{ask}
defined as follows:

L^{ask}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),g({\mathbf{x}},h),s({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)=\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)(1-s({\mathbf{x}}))+\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)s({\mathbf{x}}),

(6)

where
\ell^{f}
is a surrogate loss function for the standard predictor, and
\ell^{g}
is a (possibly distinct) surrogate loss function for model
g
. Given a distribution
P
over
{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{H}}
, the goal of LtA is therefore the following:

\min_{f,g,s\in{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y,h)}\left[L^{ask}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),g({\mathbf{x}},h),s({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]

(7)

To avoid over-reliance on the expert, we include a
budget constraint

\beta
to Eqn.
7
,
i.e.,
we require the standard model
f
to cover
(1-\beta)\%
of the instances, while querying the expert at most
\beta\%
of times
4
4

4

Modeling the cost of expert with a budget constraint
\beta
or with the parameters
\alpha
and
\delta
has a long story in the abstention literature. See,
e.g.,

Franc et al. 2023
,
Ruggieri and Pugnana 2025
for a more detailed discussion.
.
Thus, the minimization problem becomes:

\min_{f,g,s\in{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},h,y)}\big[L^{ask}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),g({\mathbf{x}},h),s({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\big]\qquad\text{s.t.}\,\,\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left[s({\mathbf{x}})\right]\leq\beta\quad\quad\quad

(8)

Similarly to
Okati et al. 2021
, given any predictor
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
and enriched predictor
g\in{\mathcal{G}}
, we can characterize the optimal selection function
s^{*}\in{\mathcal{S}}
as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
be a standard predictor, and let
g\in{\mathcal{G}}
be a fixed enriched predictor. Define
\Delta\mathbb{E}(\ell^{f},\ell^{g})=\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)]
as the difference between the expected conditional risk of
f
and
g
and assume that it is continuous.
Then the optimal selection function
s^{*}
for the minimization problem in Eqn. (
8
) can be written as:

s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if}\quad\Delta\mathbb{E}(\ell^{f},\ell^{g})>\tau_{\beta}^{*}\\
0\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(9)

where
\tau_{\beta}^{*}=\inf_{\lambda}\{\lambda\geq 0:P(\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]>\lambda)\leq\beta\}
.

Theorem
1
describes the optimal selection function under budget
\beta
: one queries the enriched predictor only if the difference between the standard predictor’s expected loss and the expected loss of the enriched model is greater than the
(1-\beta)
-th quantile of such a difference.
See Appendix
A.3
for the full proof.
In the next section, we will show how we can practically learn LtA methods via tailored surrogate functions, by also showing they are realizable and consistent.

4.1
How to Practically Learn LtA Methods

Deploying an LtA method requires learning three models: the predictor (
f
), the enriched predictor (
g
), and the selection strategy (
s
).
We propose two different approaches:
LtA-Seq
, where the models are trained sequentially, and
LtA-Joint
, where all the models are trained jointly.
For the former, we exploit existing theoretical guarantees from the LtD literature
[
26
,
23
]
, while for the latter, we propose a novel realizable consistent surrogate loss (
cf.
Theorem
2
).

Sequential Training

(
LtA-Seq
). We first propose to learn the
f
,
g
and
s
by dividing the learning into two steps:
(i)
learning the model
g
that requires additional human expert feedback, and then
(ii)
learning
f
and
s
afterwards.
This strategy is appealing because it leverages existing LtD theoretical guarantees to learn both the predictor
f
and the selector
s
as a single, unique model, as is commonly done in score-based LtD approaches
[
20
]
.
Indeed, once the model
g
is fixed, we can use it to provide the expert predictions required in the LtD paradigm to train the defer model.
In this way, one can benefit from existing theoretical guarantees from the LtD literature.

Joint Training

(
LtA-Joint
). Another option is to learn all three models jointly. However, existing LtD methods do not consider the case where the “expert” is jointly trained with the deferral model.
As done under the selector-predictor framework
[
20
]
, we exploit a surrogate loss
\ell^{s}
to learn the selector in Equation
6
. Hence, we obtain the following loss:

\tilde{L}^{ask}\left(\tilde{f}({\mathbf{x}}),\tilde{g}({\mathbf{x}},h),\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)=\ell^{f}\left(\tilde{f}_{y}({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\ell^{s}(-\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}}))+\ell^{g}\left(\tilde{g}_{y}({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\ell^{s}(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})),

(10)

where
\tilde{f}_{y}({\mathbf{x}}):{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
refers to the logit of the standard model
f
associated to class
y
,
\tilde{g}_{y}({\mathbf{x}},h):{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathbb{R}
is the logit of the enriched model
g
associated to class
y
and
\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}}):{\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a logit associated to a selection function, such that
s({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{I}_{\{\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})>0\}}
.
Under some assumptions on the surrogate losses
\ell^{f}
,
\ell^{g}
and
\ell^{s}
, we show that the
\tilde{L}^{ask}
surrogate loss (Equation
10
) is
{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
-realizable consistent, as formalized in the next theorem:

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{F}}
,
{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}
are closed under scaling, and consider the following surrogate losses such that

(a)

\ell^{f}=\ell^{g}=\ell^{surr}
is a comp-sum loss
[
21
]

\ell^{surr}:[0,1]\times{\mathcal{Y}}\to[0,1]
such that for a given
y
is non-increasing in the first argument,
\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},y)>0
and
\lim_{v\to 1}\ell^{surr}(v,y)=0
;

(b)

\ell^{s}
is a margin-based monotone loss such that
\lim_{v\to\infty}\ell^{s}(v)=1
,
\lim_{v\to-\infty}\ell^{s}(v)=0
;
\ell^{s}(-\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}}))+\ell^{s}(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}}))=1

then, the surrogate loss
\tilde{L}^{ask}

{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{S}}

Theorem

shows that considering surrogate losses that satisfy conditions

(b)

6
Conclusions

We introduced Learning to Ask (LtA), a novel framework for human–AI collaboration that learns when to query a human expert and integrates this feedback into prediction, rather than simply deferring as in Learning to Defer (LtD).
We first showed that there exist data distributions where LtD is suboptimal.
We then derived the optimal selection strategy, including the case of budgeted expert queries, and proposed two practical training approaches: a sequential regime and a joint regime with a novel realizable-consistent surrogate loss.
Lastly, we have empirically shown that LtA consistently matches or outperforms LtD, while offering greater flexibility in incorporating diverse forms of expert feedback.

Limitations and future work.

We conclude by discussing some limitations that open up interesting avenues for future work.
Some theoretical aspects remain open,
e.g.,
how to design surrogate losses that benefit from
{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
-consistency bounds.
In Theorem
2
, we show how to characterize a
{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
-realizable consistent surrogate loss for LtA.
However, although we show that by minimizing the surrogate loss, we can retrieve the optimal
(f,g,s)^{*}\in{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
, we still lack Bayes-consistency. This limitation highlights the need for more refined analyses, such as exploring
{\mathcal{Q}}
-consistency bounds
[
23
]
.
Another important open question is understanding which type of human feedback is most useful for a given instance. While our current formulation relies on a
fixed query structure
, real-world applications may require adapting the form of queries, ranging from simple predictions to richer annotations (
e.g.,
uncertainty estimates, structured explanations, etc.). Developing mechanisms that not only decide
when to defer
but also
what to ask
represents a promising research direction, enabling more effective use of human expertise.

Proof of Theorem 1

Proof.

We start by checking the unconstrained case, i.e., our goal is to minimize the following:

\min_{s\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y,h)}\left[\left(1-s({\mathbf{x}})\right)\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)+s({\mathbf{x}})\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]

(14)

.
The objective can be written as

\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},y,h)}\left[\left(1-s({\mathbf{x}})\right)\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)+s({\mathbf{x}})\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]=\\
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\left(1-s({\mathbf{x}})\right)\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]+s({\mathbf{x}})\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]\right]=\\
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]\right]+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left[s({\mathbf{x}})\left(\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]\right)\right]

Notice that since the first term of the problem does not depend on
s
, we are solving the following optimization problem:

\min_{s\in[0,1]\forall{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left[s({\mathbf{x}})\left(\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]\right)\right]

(15)

This is a linear problem, and it decouples with respect to
{\mathbf{x}}
. Hence, for every
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, it is immediate to check that the optimal selector is:

s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if}\quad\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]>0\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(16)

Now, let us consider the case where we have a budget constraint, i.e., the problem becomes:

\min_{s\in[0,1]\forall{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},h,y)}\left[\left(1-s({\mathbf{x}})\right)\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)+s({\mathbf{x}})\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{X}}}\left[s({\mathbf{x}})\right]\leq\beta

(17)

.

We can now consider the dual formulation, i.e.,

\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\min_{s\in[0,1]\forall{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\mathbb{E}_{({\mathbf{x}},h,y)}\left[\left(1-s({\mathbf{x}})\right)\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)+s({\mathbf{x}})\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]+\lambda\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left[s({\mathbf{x}})-\beta\right],

(18)

where
\lambda
is the Lagrangian multiplier for the budget constraint.
The inner argument can be solved similarly to the unconstrained case, leading us to the following optimal selector:

s^{*}_{\lambda}({\mathbf{x}})=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if}\quad\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]>\lambda\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(19)

Then, notice that since:

s^{*}_{\lambda}({\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{I}_{\{\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]>\lambda\}},

we can write that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[s^{*}_{\lambda}({\mathbf{x}})]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\mathbb{I}_{\{\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]>\lambda\}}]=\\
P_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]>\lambda\right)=\\
1-P_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]\leq\lambda);

then at the optimum level
\lambda^{*}
must satisfy the constraint
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[s({\mathbf{x}})]\leq\beta
with equality, i.e.,
\lambda^{*}
satisfies

P_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]\leq\lambda^{*})=1-\beta
.

Due to the continuity assumption,
\lambda^{*}
is the
(1-\beta)
-quantile for the expected difference
\mathbb{E}_{y\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{f}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{y,h\mid{\mathbf{x}}}\left[\ell^{g}\left(g({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\right]
.

Proof of Theorem 2

To show Theorem 2 holds, we follow a similar approach to

Let us denote (with a slight abuse of notation) as

the loss obtained when considering a logit whose softmax is

, i.e.,
\ell^{surr}(v)=\ell^{surr}(v,y)
.
We first show that for every
{\mathbf{x}},h,y
and every

it holds that:

\ L_{0-1}^{ask}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),g({\mathbf{x}},h),s({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)\leq\frac{\tilde{L}^{ask}\left(\tilde{f}({\mathbf{x}}),\tilde{g}({\mathbf{x}},h),\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})},

(20)

We consider six cases here:

Case 1:
both predictors are wrong, i.e.,

f({\mathbf{x}})\neq y
and

\left(1-\ell^{s}\left(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\right)\ell^{surr}\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},y\right)+\ell^{s}\left(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\ell^{surr}\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},y\right)

where the first qeuality holds by assumption
(b)

\sigma^{\tilde{f}}_{y}({\mathbf{x}})({\mathbf{x}})<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

\sigma^{\tilde{g}}_{y}({\mathbf{x}},h)({\mathbf{x}},h)<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. Then the second inequality holds since
\ell^{surr}
is non-increasing. Now let us divide both parts by the positive quantity

\frac{\tilde{L}^{ask}\left(\tilde{f}({\mathbf{x}}),\tilde{g}({\mathbf{x}},h),\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\geq\frac{\left(1-\ell^{s}\left(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\right)\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},y)}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}+\frac{\ell^{s}\left(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},y)}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\geq\\
\frac{\left(1-\ell^{s}\left(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\right)\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},y)}{\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}+\frac{\ell^{s}\left(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},y)}{\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}=1=L_{0-1}^{ask}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),g({\mathbf{x}},h),s({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)

(22)

where the second inequality holds as
\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\ell^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})
.

the selector

,
f({\mathbf{x}})\neq y
and

.

L_{0-1}^{ask}\left(f({\mathbf{x}}),g({\mathbf{x}},h),s({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)=1
.
Now, consider that

\tilde{L}^{ask}\left(\tilde{f}({\mathbf{x}}),\tilde{g}({\mathbf{x}},h),\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)=\left(1-\ell^{s}\left(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\right)\ell^{surr}\left(\sigma^{\tilde{f}}_{y}({\mathbf{x}}),y\right)+\ell^{s}\left(\tilde{s}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\ell^{surr}\left(\sigma^{\tilde{g}}_{y}({\mathbf{x}},h),y\right)\geq\\

The six cases show that
\frac{\tilde{L}^{ask}(\cdot)}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\geq L^{ask}_{0-1}(\cdot)
for any

and

Now consider a distribution and predictors such that there exists a zero error solution

with
{\mathcal{E}}_{L^{ask}_{0-1}}=0
. Consider
\hat{f},\hat{g},\hat{s}
as the minimizer of the surrogate loss
\tilde{L}^{ask}
. i.e.,
\hat{f},\hat{g},\hat{s}\in\argmin_{f,g,s\in{\mathcal{F}}\times{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\tilde{L}^{ask}}(f,g,s)
. Then the following inequality holds:

{\mathcal{E}}_{L^{ask}_{0-1}}(\hat{f},\hat{g},\hat{s})\leq\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\tilde{L}^{ask}}(\hat{f},\hat{g},\hat{s})}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\leq\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\tilde{L}^{ask}}(\alpha f^{*},\alpha^{\prime}{g^{*}},s^{*})}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}=\\
\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{L}^{ask}\left(\alpha\tilde{f}^{*}({\mathbf{x}}),\alpha^{\prime}\tilde{g}^{*}({\mathbf{x}},h),\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\mid s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=0\right]P\left(s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=0\right)+\\
\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{L}^{ask}\left(\alpha\tilde{f}^{*}({\mathbf{x}}),\alpha^{\prime}\tilde{g}^{*}({\mathbf{x}},h),\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\mid s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=1\right]P\left(s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=1\right)=\\

\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\mathbb{E}[\ell^{surr}\left(\sigma\left(\alpha\tilde{f}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right),y\right)\left(1-\ell^{s}\left(\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\right)+\ell^{surr}\left(\sigma\left(\alpha^{\prime}\tilde{g}^{*}({\mathbf{x}},h)\right),y\right)\ell^{s}\left(\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\mid s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=1]P(s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=1)\leq\\
\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\mathbb{E}[\ell^{surr}\left(\sigma\left(\alpha\tilde{f}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right),y\right)\left(1-\ell^{s}\left(\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\right)+\ell^{s}\left(\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\mid s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=0]P(s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=0)+\\
\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}{\ell}^{surr}(\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})}\mathbb{E}[\left(1-\ell^{s}\left(\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\right)+\ell^{surr}\left(\sigma\left(\alpha^{\prime}\tilde{g}^{*}({\mathbf{x}},h)\right),y\right)\ell^{s}\left(\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\mid s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=1]P(s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=1)

Where the first inequality holds by

, the second inequality holds because
\hat{f},\hat{g},\hat{s}
minimize
{\mathcal{E}}_{\tilde{L}^{ask}}(\cdot)
and both
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
are closed under scaling, the two equalities hold by definition and the last inequality hold as the loss

s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=0

\lim_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\gamma\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\ell^{surr}\left(\sigma\left(\alpha\tilde{f}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right),y\right)\left(1-\ell^{s}\left(\gamma\tilde{s}^{*}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\right)\mid s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=0]P(s^{*}({\mathbf{x}})=0)
