Entropy is all you need for Inter-Seed Cross-Play in Hanabi
Johannes Forkel, Jakob Foerster

Abstract
We find that in Hanabi ( 1 ) , one of the most complex and popular benchmarks for zero-shot coordination and ad-hoc teamplay, a standard implementation of independent PPO with a slightly higher entropy coefficient 0.05 0.05 instead of the typically used 0.01 0.01 , achieves a new state-of-the-art in cross-play between different seeds, beating by a significant margin all previous specialized algorithms ( 6 ; 5 ; 8 ; 3 ; 10 ; 2 ; 9 ) , which were specifically designed for this setting. We provide an intuition for why sufficiently high entropy regularization ensures that different random seed produce joint policies which are mutually compatible. We also empirically find that a high λ GAE \lambda_{\text{GAE}} around 0.9 0.9 , and using RNNs instead of just feed-forward layers in the actor-critic architecture, strongly increase inter-seed cross-play. While these results demonstrate the dramatic effect that hyperparameters can have not just on self-play scores but also on cross-play scores, we show that there are simple Dec-POMDPs though, in which standard policy gradient methods with increased entropy regularization are not able to achieve perfect inter-seed cross-play, thus demonstrating the continuing necessity for new algorithms for zero-shot coordination.



1
Introduction

When using self-play to train a joint policy in a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP) like Hanabi, this typically results in the joint policy adopting a coordination convention which breaks some symmetries of the Dec-POMDP. When agents from such a joint policy are then paired with agents from another joint policy which wasn’t seen during training, e.g. with a joint policy trained with the same algorithm but a different random seed, a wholly different implementation of the same algorithm, or with humans, this often results in coordination failures, since the coordination conventions of the different joint policies are incompatible with each other. For a simple example of such incompatible symmetry breaking in a Dec-POMDP, consider the one-round simultaneous action game with the pay-off matrix

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}2&-2&1\\
-2&2&1\\
1&1&1\end{bmatrix}

(1)

In this Dec-POMDP both optimal joint policies break the game symmetry in another way: either both players always choose the first action, or both always choose the second action, for a return of
2
. Those two joint policies are incompatible though, in that their
cross-play
(XP) score is very low: when the local policies from one of those joint policies are paired with the local policies of the other joint policy, then a return of
-2
is achieved. This is lower than return of the optimal symmetric joint policy, under which both agents always choose the third action, which gives a return of
1
.

In Hanabi, a very large and complex Dec-POMDP, standard MARL algorithms like independent PPO (IPPO) or independent DQN, typically produce joint policies which, while achieving high returns in self-play, do very poorly when paired in cross-play between independent seeds of the same algorithm
(
6
;
1
)
, and in cross-play with human proxy bots
(
4
)
. This is because they are unconstrained in which of the many different conventions they can develop and thus mostly converge to conventions which break symmetries of the game.

The goal of zero-shot coordination (ZSC) is to find algorithms, which produce compatible joint policies across different implementations, in particular across different random seeds of the same implementation. In Hanabi, ZSC algorithms have led to state-of-the-art scores in cross-play between independently trained joint policies from different random seeds of the same implementation
(
5
;
2
)
, and in cross-play with human-proxy bots
(
4
)
.

We find that when taking a standard IPPO implementation for Hanabi, and tune its hyperparameters to achieve not just high SP scores, but also high XP scores between different random seeds, then re-running IPPO with those hyperparameters gives a new state-of-the-art in XP between different random seeds, surpassing the previous SOTA achieved with Off-Belief Learning (OBL)
(
5
;
2
)
by a significant margin. The hyperparameters we found, which achieve those high XP scores, are fairly standard, with the main difference being a significantly higher entropy coefficient. We thus see that a higher entropy coefficient doesn’t just have the obvious benefit of better exploration and state coverage, but that it also prevents the forming of conventions which break game symmetries. We furthermore find that using RNNs in the actor-critic networks leads to much higher inter-seed XP scores than when using pure feedforward architectures, and that choosing
\lambda_{\text{GAE}}
close to
0.9
also significantly reduces the gap between SP scores and inter-seed XP scores.

While those results clearly show that some hyperparameters, in particular the entropy coefficient, can have a strong influence on inter-seed XP, we also give two simple examples of Dec-POMDPs, in which using standard policy gradient methods, even with an increased entropy coefficient, are not able to consistently find the optimal symmetric policy.

Background

2.1
Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (DecPOMDPs)

We formalize the cooperative multi-agent setting as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process (Dec-POMDP)
(
11
)
:

Definition 2.1
.

A Dec-POMDP is defined as a 9-tuple
(\mathcal{S},n,\{\mathcal{A}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\mathcal{O}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{R},\{\mathcal{U}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},T,\gamma)
, where:

•

\mathcal{S}
is the state space and
n
is the number of agents.

•

\mathcal{A}^{i}
and
\mathcal{O}^{i}
are the local action and observation spaces for agent
i
, and
\mathcal{A}=\prod_{i}^{n}\mathcal{A}^{i}
,
\mathcal{O}=\prod_{i}^{n}\mathcal{O}^{i}
are the joint action and observation spaces.

•

The state transition is governed by
s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{T}(s_{t+1}\ |\ s_{t},a_{t})
, and local observations are given by
o_{t+1}^{i}=\mathcal{U}^{i}(s_{t+1})
.

•

The rewards are given by
r_{t+1}=\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t})
, the horizon is
T
, i.e.
s_{T}
is always a terminal state, and
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor.

Given
t\in\{0,...,T\}
, the state-action history (SAH) is given by
\tau_{t}=(s_{0},a_{0},...,s_{t-1},a_{t-1},s_{t})
. Each agent
i
selects local actions based on his local action-observation history (AOH)
\tau_{t}^{i}=(a_{0}^{i},o_{1}^{i},\dots,a_{t-1}^{i},o_{t}^{i})
, following a local policy
a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{i}(a_{t}^{i}\ |\ \tau_{t}^{i})
. Given a SAH
\tau_{t}=(\tau_{t}^{1},\dots,\tau_{t}^{n})
, a joint policy
\pi=(\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{n})
chooses a joint action
a_{t}=(a_{t}^{1},...,a_{t}^{n})
in the space of legal actions
\mathcal{A}(\tau_{t})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}^{i}(\tau_{t}^{i})
, with probability
\pi(a_{t}|\tau_{t}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi^{i}(a_{t}^{i}|\tau_{t}^{i})
. We denote the set of joint policies by
\Pi
, and define the self-play (SP) objective
J_{\text{SP}}:\Pi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as the expected return:

\displaystyle J_{\text{SP}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{T}\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t}\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t})\right].

(2)

Furthermore, given a joint policy
\pi
, we define its greedified version
\hat{\pi}
by

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(a_{t}|\tau_{t}):=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}1/K&\text{if }a_{t}\in\text{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}(\tau_{t})}\pi(a|\tau_{t}),\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}

where here
K:=|\text{argmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}(\tau_{t})}\pi(a|\tau_{t})|
is the number of joint actions with the highest probability.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the fact that we are allowing the local observations
o_{t+1}^{i}
to only depend deterministically on
s_{t+1}
, rather than stochastically on
s_{t}
and
a_{t}
, i.e.
o_{t+1}^{i}\sim\mathcal{U}^{i}(s_{t},a_{t})
, is not a restriction on the Dec-POMDP. This is because any Dec-POMDP with a stochastic observation function of the latter form, can be transformed to an equivalent Dec-POMDP with the observation function
o_{t+1}^{i}=\mathcal{U}^{i}(\tilde{s}_{t+1})
, by just setting
\tilde{s}_{t+1}=(s_{t+1},o_{t+1}^{1},...,o_{t+1}^{n})
. Including the observations in the state eases notation, since then the local AOHs are just deterministic functions of the SAHs.

2.2
Zero-Shot Coordination (ZSC)

Given that there are typically many different Nash equilibria in a Dec-POMDP, as it is fully cooperative, and since those different Nash equilibria can be incompatible which each other, there is the problem of equilibrium selection. To measure the compatibility of different Nash equilibria in a Dec-POMDP, we define the cross-play score between different joint policies:

Definition 2.3

(Cross-Play (XP))
.

Given a Dec-POMDP with
n
players, we define the cross-play (XP) score
J_{\text{XP}}:\Pi^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, between
n
joint policies
\pi_{1}
, …,
\pi_{n}
by

\displaystyle J_{\text{XP}}(\pi_{1},...,\pi_{n}):=\mathbb{E}_{\phi\sim\text{Perm}(n)}\left[J((\pi_{\phi(1)}^{1},...,\pi_{\phi(n)}^{n}))\right],

where
\text{Perm}(n)
is the set of permutations of
\{1,...,n\}
.

The goal of zero-shot coordination is to find algorithms, which produce joint policies that are compatible with each other, in that they achieve a high XP score. A simplified setting is when the implementation is the same, but the random seeds are different. We refer to the XP score between different seeds of the same implementation as inter-seed XP.

2.3
Entropy Regularized Multi-Agent Policy Gradient Methods

Let
\theta\mapsto\pi_{\theta}
be a differentiable parameterization of the joint policy
\pi_{\theta}
. For an entropy coefficient
\alpha\geq 0
, we consider the entropy regularized policy gradient

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}^{\alpha}J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta})+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{T}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\text{Ent}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\tau_{t}))\right]

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{T}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}^{i}(a_{t}^{i}|\tau_{t}^{i})\Big(-\alpha\log\pi_{\theta}^{i}(a_{t}^{i}|\tau_{t}^{i})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}\mathcal{R}(s_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}})\Big)\Big].

Note that
\nabla_{\theta}^{\alpha}J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta})
is
not
the same as the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}J_{\text{SP}}^{\alpha}(\pi_{\theta})
of the maximum entropy reinforcement learning objective

\displaystyle J_{\text{SP}}^{\alpha}:=J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta})+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{T}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t}\text{Ent}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\tau_{t}))\right].

(4)

In practice one needs to estimate
\nabla_{\theta}^{\alpha}J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta})
from a batch of SAHs. Typically a clipped objective as in PPO
(
14
)
is used, and a loss term is added for the learning of either local or centralized value functions, whose parameters are possibly partly shared with
\pi_{\theta}
. The learned value functions, also referred to as critics, are used to reduce variance in the estimates of
\nabla_{\theta}^{\alpha}J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta})
. Those gradient estimates are then used with a variant of gradient ascent to train
\pi_{\theta}
and the critics.

In
15
it was found that IPPO, and multi-agent PPO (MAPPO), which uses a centralized critic instead of a local one like IPPO, are effective in learning joint policies with high SP scores in Hanabi and other Dec-POMDPs. In e.g.
9
however it was reported that IPPO achieves very low inter-seed XP scores.

3
An intuition why entropy regularization prevents symmetry breaking

In this section we assume a tabular softmax parametrization, and denote by
\theta^{i}(a^{i}|\tau^{i})
the logit corresponding to agent
i
taking local action
a^{i}
given local AOH
\tau^{i}
:

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}^{i}(a^{i}|\tau^{i})=\frac{\exp(\theta^{i}(a^{i}|\tau^{i}))}{\sum_{b^{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{i}(\tau^{i})}\exp(\theta^{i}(b^{i}|\tau^{i}))}.

(5)

We let
\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the initial parameters, and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
set
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}+k^{-1}\nabla_{\theta}^{\alpha}J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta_{k}})
.

When initializing
\theta_{1}
randomly and/or using noisy estimates of the gradients, then there are typically many different limiting policies
\pi_{\theta_{\infty}}
that one can converge to. Roughly speaking, increasing the entropy coefficient
\alpha
makes the optimization landscape “more concave,” since entropy is a strictly concave function on the probability simplex, having as its unique maximizer the uniform distribution. For
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, when assuming unbiased gradient estimates, the limiting policy
\pi_{\theta_{\infty}}
will always be the uniformly random policy. Thus one can expect that there is a threshold for
\alpha
, above which the limiting policy will always be the same. For a demonstration of this phenomenon in a simple Dec-POMDP, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Consider the one-round simultaneous action game with payoff matrix given in (
1
), and the parametrization
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\mapsto\text{softmax}(\theta_{1},-\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=:\pi^{1}_{\theta}(\cdot)=\pi^{2}_{\theta}(\cdot)
. Bottom: the function
\theta\mapsto J^{\alpha}_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta})
, with
\alpha=1.0
on the left and
\alpha=1.2
on the right. Top left:
\pi_{\theta_{\infty}}
and its return
J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta_{\infty}})
as a function of the entropy coefficient that was used during training of
\pi_{\theta}
. Top right: XP matrix between multiple greedified joint policies, which were trained with independent REINFORCE, with different entropy coefficients. 5 seeds per entropy coefficient.

A higher entropy coefficient typically results in a lower
J_{\text{SP}}(\pi_{\theta_{\infty}})
, since with a higher entropy coefficient
\pi_{\theta}
assigns higher probabilities to suboptimal actions. However, since during XP evaluation after training, the policies always take the action with the highest probability, all that matters is that the policies learn to assign the highest probability to the actions that are chosen by the optimal symmetric policies. Thus the way to use entropy regularization to learn symmetric policies is to increase
\alpha
to the lowest value where, when training multiple joint policies with that
\alpha
, the average SP score equals the average XP score.

We emphasize that while a sufficiently high entropy coefficient will ensure that
\pi_{\theta_{\infty}}
will always be the same, the greedification of that policy will not necessarily have the highest return among all symmetric policies. Consider for example the one-round simultaneous action game with payoff matrix

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}3&0&0\\
0&3&0\\
0&0&2\end{bmatrix}.

(6)

For high enough
\alpha
, the local policies of the unique
\pi_{\theta_{\infty}}
will assign the same probability to the first two actions, and a lower probability to the third one. The greedified version of this policy will have a return of
1.5
, while the optimal symmetric policy, under which both local policies always choose the third action, has a return of
2
. Roughly speaking this means that a high entropy coefficient will ensure that the learned policies are
respecting
the symmetries of the game, but they are not necessarily
exploiting
the symmetries of the game. To respect
and
exploit known symmetries in Dec-POMDPs for zero-shot coordination, one can use the Other-Play algorithm
(
6
)
.

4.1
An Illustrative Toy Example: The Cat/Dog Game

Consider the cat/dog toy game from
(
5
)
, shown in Figure
2
which is taken from that paper. In this game there are two SP optimal joint policies, with a return of
10
: one in which Alice always signals “on” when she sees a cat and “off” when she sees a dog, and one in which this pairing is opposite. The XP score of those two joint policies is
-10
though. Under the optimal symmetric joint policy Alice always reveals and Bob then guesses the correct pet, which has a return of
7
. There is also a suboptimal symmetric policy under which Alice always bails, for a reward of
1
.

We train
m=12
joint policies
\pi_{1},...,\pi_{m}
with independent REINFORCE with baseline, for different entropy coefficients, and then compute the XP matrix
\left(J_{\text{SP}}((\hat{\pi}_{j}^{1},\hat{\pi}_{k}^{2}))\right)_{j,k=1}^{m}
of the greedified joint policies
\hat{\pi}_{1},...,\hat{\pi}_{m}
. This matrix is shown on the left in Figure
3
. We see that when the entropy coefficient is
8.1
or above, both Alice and Bob learn to assign the highest probability to the optimal symmetric actions. However, we also see that for the entropy coefficients
7.5,7.7,7.9
, Alice assigns the highest probability to revealing, but that when Bob sees “on” or “off” he breaks the symmetry and guesses a pet instead of bailing, implying that Alice also breaks the symmetry among her suboptimal actions “on” and “off.” This means that we do not necessarily need to increase entropy so much that no symmetries at all are broken. It is enough to increase entropy to the first point where we achieve
\text{SP}=\text{XP}
during greedy evaluation, as then the policies have learned to assigned the highest probability to actions that belong to symmetric policies. We furthermore see that for an extremely high entropy coefficient, Alice assigns the highest probability to bailing, which results in the suboptimal symmetric policy. This is because Bob during training didn’t often enough guess the correct pet when he saw it.

Thus in this game we can find the optimal symmetric policy simply by increasing the entropy coefficient until the SP score of the learned policies equals the average XP score between multiple seeds. However, as we have mentioned already, this recipe doesn’t always work. Consider a version of the cat/dog game where the reward for revealing the pet is
-8
instead of
-3
. This gives the optimal symmetric policy a return of
2
. The cross-play matrix for policies trained with different entropy coefficient is shown on the right in Figure
3
. In this game, we see that above the threshold for the entropy coefficient where the policies stop breaking symmetries, Alice already prefers bailing over revealing. Thus in this game there is no entropy coefficient for which Alice learns to assign revealing the highest probability. We note that the ZSC algorithms Other-Play
(
6
)
and Off-Belief Learning
(
5
)
will learn the optimal symmetric policy in this game.

The cat/dog game provides the intuition that in games where there isn’t a lot of incentive to break symmetries, i.e. when the return of the optimal symmetric policy is almost as high as the return of the optimal asymmetric ones, then an increased entropy coefficient will result in policy gradient methods learning policies whose greedification is close to the optimal symmetric policy. In games where the optimal asymmetric policies have much higher returns than all symmetric policies, one can still learn symmetric policies through increasing the entropy coefficient, but those policies will not necessarily achieve the hightest return among the symmetric policies.

4.2.1
XP for different entropy coefficients

All Hanabi experiments are done in JaxMARL
(
13

We refer to this architecture as “LSTM.” The second architecture, which we refer to as “FF,” replaces the two LSTM layers in the first architecture by two feedforward layers. Third and fourth, the public-private LSTM architecture described in Figure
5
in the appendix, where the critic is either local or centralized. We refer to this architecture as “PP LSTM” and in the case of a centralized critic, the policy gradient algorithm becomes MAPPO
(
15

(
5
;
2
)
.

In 2-player Hanabi, we train four seeds of IPPO policies, for each of the first three architectures, and for each of the entropy coefficients
0.01,0.02,...,0.10

Related Work

In
12
it was found that in imperfect information two-play zero-sum games, generic PPO with an increased entropy regularization beats, in terms of exploitability of the trained policies, other popular algorithms which were specifially designed for this setting.

In the concurrent work
7
, a new multi-agent policy gradient method called
rational policy gradient
is developed for Dec-POMDPs, which aims to learn policies that are robust and diverse. They also briefly mention that in their experiments in the reduced 3- and 4-color versions of 2-player Hanabi, a higher entropy regularization lead to higher cross-play scores. They speculate that this is a feature of the smaller game or the fact that they use policies without history dependence, but do not expand on it further. Our results show though that higher entropy regularization almost perfectly closes the gap between SP and XP in full Hanabi with 2, 3, 4, 5 players, and that in fact, policies which lack history dependence, are
more
likely to break symmetries than policies which use RNNs.

6
Conclusion

We see that in terms of inter-seed XP in Hanabi, IPPO with properly tuned hyperparemeters, beats by a significant margin all previous methods which were specifially designed for this task. We see that a higher entropy coefficient, a higher
\lambda_{\text{GAE}}
, and using an RNN actor-critic network instead of a feed-forward one, play a significant role in preventing the emergence of asymmetric conventions. However, we have demonstrated through two counter examples, that tuning the entropy coefficient is not guaranteed to give the optimal symmetric policy in every Dec-POMDP. Thus, while we demonstrate the sometimes dramatic effect that hyperparameters can have not just on SP scores but also on XP scores, we emphasize that existing algorithms for ZSC are still needed, and the development of new ZSC algorithms is still necessary.‘
