Impact of Diagnosis-Intervention Packet Compliance Training on Inpatient Expenditures Under Case-Based Payment Reform: A Quasi-Experimental Study Based on Discharge Records
Authors: Zhangjie Huang, Hanli Lin, Jie Chen, Li Ai, Danlan Li, Yue Li, Shipei Zeng, Zhuodong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDespite the widespread implementation of case-based payment reforms, the role of hospital-internal compliance training remains underexplored. This study examines the impact of training on inpatient expenditures under Diagnosis-Intervention Packet (DIP) reform, and explores its role as an institutional mechanism for translating top-down policy incentives into frontline clinical practice.MethodsBased on 58,411 inpatient discharge records from a tertiary hospital in Shenzhen during 2022–2024, this study adopts the administratively assigned DIP training as a quasi-experimental shock, an approach well-aligned with our causal identification framework. The event-study design explicitly identifies the dynamic temporal trend of treatment effects post-training, and the staggered difference-in-differences estimation addresses potential endogeneity by controlling for department-specific fixed effects and time trends.ResultsDIP compliance training leads to sustained reductions in inpatient expenditures, with total costs decreasing by 14.5% at 12 months post-training. Significant declines are observed in drug (37.2%), laboratory (21%) and inspection (8.3%) expenditures. The effects are more obvious in coding-intensive surgical departments, accompanied by a structural shift toward service-oriented expenditure categories. Further robustness checks enhance the reliability of the empirical results.ConclusionBy improving coding competence and aligning clinical documentation with DIP grouping rules, DIP training serves as a key organizational mechanism for hospitals to internalize payment reform incentives. These findings highlight the importance of institutional capacity building for the sustainable implementation of local case-based payment reform and other health systems pursuing value-based care.Keywords: case‑based payment, hospital behavior, expenditure control, coding accuracy, diagnosis‑intervention packet, institutional adaptation

Introduction

Health systems worldwide continue to grapple with the persistent dual challenge of escalating medical expenditures and the imperative to preserve equitable access to care, a tension that has placed payment reform at the center of global policy debates.1 It is now widely recognized that provider payment mechanisms are not merely financial tools but structural determinants of hospital behavior. Evidence from diverse international settings demonstrates that payment rules shape clinical decision-making, resource allocation, and ultimately the delicate balance between care efficiency and quality.2 The traditional fee-for-service (FFS) model, in particular, has faced sustained criticism for incentivizing volume-driven care, leading to widespread overprescription, redundant diagnostic testing, and unsustainable cost growth without corresponding improvements in health outcomes.3–5 These challenges have driven the global adoption of case-based prospective payment systems, with the diagnosis-related group (DRG) framework emerging as the dominant international model.6,7

China’s payment reform mirrors this global shift but is distinguished by unique institutional complexities. Since the late 1990s, China has built a unified multi-tiered social health insurance system, culminating in the 2016 integration of urban and rural resident insurance schemes to standardize benefits and reimbursement rules.8,9 Following the landmark 2009 national healthcare reform, China piloted multiple prospective payment models to curb FFS-induced overutilization, formally launching national DRG pilots in 2019.10–12 Building on these experiences, the diagnosis-intervention packet (DIP) reform was rolled out in 71 pilot cities in 2020 as a localized alternative to DRG. Unlike DRG, which groups patients based on multidimensional clinical characteristics, DIP uses only primary diagnosis and procedure codes to create granular case groups, reducing administrative complexity and lowering the technical barrier for frontline implementation.13–16 By tying reimbursement directly to standardized case groups and shifting overspending risk to healthcare providers, DIP fundamentally reconfigures hospital incentives. In this new system, a hospital’s ability to adapt its internal workflows and coding practices becomes the key determinant of both financial sustainability and reform effectiveness.

Existing evaluations of China’s payment reforms have predominantly focused on system-level outcomes, including reductions in out-of-pocket spending, improved insurance fund sustainability, and more rational resource allocation.17–19 Studies specific to DIP have documented improvements in selected quality indicators, but findings regarding its cost-containment effectiveness remain inconsistent across different settings.14,15,20–26 A critical gap remains in understanding the micro-institutional mechanisms through which DIP reforms operate at the hospital level. Specifically, little is known about how hospitals internalize DIP rules, reorganize clinical workflows, and translate top-down policy incentives into day-to-day clinical and coding practices. This gap is particularly consequential because DIP’s grouping logic places heavy technical demands on frontline staff. Inadequate internal training can lead to widespread coding errors, claim disputes, and misalignment between clinical documentation and reimbursement requirements. Clinicians unfamiliar with DIP criteria may inadvertently assign suboptimal codes, undermining both hospital revenue and the accuracy of health system data, while administrators often struggle to align internal management systems with external payment incentives.

This study addresses this underexplored mechanism by examining the impact of hospital-internal DIP compliance training on inpatient expenditure patterns. We ask three interrelated questions: first, whether structured DIP compliance training leads to measurable reductions in inpatient expenditures; second, whether training effects vary across clinical departments with different coding complexities and service models; and third, whether training reshapes expenditure structures by shifting resources toward higher-value clinical services. As one of China’s earliest adopters of DIP payment reform, a core site for national and provincial medical insurance pilots since the establishment of the Pilot Demonstration Area in 2019, and the first city to issue the medical insurance settlement code guidelines, Shenzhen provides a policy‑dense and institutionally innovative environment where major insurance reforms are trialed and refined prior to broader provincial or national rollout, making it an especially informative setting for analyzing responses within the DIP context.27–29 Leveraging the staggered administrative rollout of DIP training across departments in a large tertiary hospital in Shenzhen as an exogenous quasi-experimental shock, we apply event-study analysis and the staggered difference-in-differences (DID) estimator to a sample of 58,411 inpatient discharge records from 2022 to 2024.30 By focusing on the micro-institutional foundations of payment reform implementation, this study provides new causal evidence on how case-based payment systems operate within hospitals. Our findings not only offer actionable guidance for Chinese hospitals seeking to optimize DIP implementation but also provide valuable lessons for other countries exploring case-based payment reforms in diverse institutional contexts.

Methods

Study Design

The experiment draws on a tertiary hospital in Shenzhen, a pioneer city in China’s healthcare payment reform and one of the earliest adopters of national policy pilots.31 In 2023, the hospital launched a hospital-wide DIP compliance training program to standardize clinical coding practices and align medical documentation with case-based payment requirements. Importantly for identification, the training rollout was determined exclusively by the hospital’s administrative scheduling system and was not triggered by departmental performance, expenditure trends, or clinical quality concerns. Instead, the hospital mandated a unified curriculum and assigned training dates through internal administrative scheduling. This institutional feature ensures that the timing of training is plausibly exogenous to pre-existing cost.

The training covered the operational logic of DIP grouping, coding specifications, and documentation requirements. Although the content was standardized, the timing varied across departments, creating a staggered adoption pattern that forms the basis of our quasi-experimental design. This staggered structure allows us to compare departments that have already received training with those that have not yet been exposed, while controlling for time-varying shocks common to all departments. Data and Sample Selection

We constructed a case‑level dataset using de-identified inpatient discharge records from January 1, 2022 to December 31, 2024. These records contain information on admission and discharge timestamps, diagnosis and procedure codes, expenditure breakdowns across multiple cost categories, and patient demographic and clinical characteristics. Because each inpatient case is linked to the department responsible for care, and each department has a clearly defined training date, we can precisely map each case to its relative position in the training timeline.

To ensure data quality and comparability, we applied several exclusion criteria. We removed cases from isolation wards due to their atypical cost structures, cases with full out-of-pocket payment (cost-sharing ratio = 1) because they are not subject to DIP reimbursement rules, cases reimbursed under per-diem payment schemes, and records with missing or incomplete information. After these exclusions, the analytic sample consists of 58,411 inpatient observations.

Variables

The definitions of key variables used in the analysis are presented in Table A2. To capture the multidimensional impact of DIP training, we organized outcome variables into three aspects:

Overall expenditure indicators. These include total inpatient expenditure, DIP-related expenditure (the sum of reimbursable and patient-paid amounts), and medical insurance billing. These measures allow us to assess whether training affects aggregate spending levels.

Expenditure components. We examine drug costs, inspection costs, and laboratory test costs per case. These categories represent the cost components most sensitive to coding accuracy and clinical decision-making, and thus provide insight into the mechanisms through which training may influence expenditure.

Expenditure structure indicators. We compute the shares of nursing, surgical operation, and bed costs in total expenditure. These indicators capture potential shifts in the composition of spending, which may reflect changes in clinical practice patterns or documentation behavior following training.

All continuous expenditure variables (except expenditure shares) were standardized (mean-centered and scaled by the standard deviation) to facilitate interpretation of coefficients because we can place heterogeneous cost components on a comparable scale and examine effect sizes across categories. Covariates include patient age, sex, insurance type, surgical status, length of stay, principal diagnosis fixed effects (first-two-digit level), department fixed effects, and time fixed effects. This specification controls for both observable case-mix differences and unobserved heterogeneity across departments and time.

Robustness Checks

We conduct several robustness exercises to assess the stability of our findings: (1) Extended event-time windows to evaluate long‑run effects; (2) Exclusion of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) departments, which operate under distinct clinical and coding norms; (3) Exclusion of stay length; (4) Removal of cases admitted before but discharged after training, eliminating mixed exposure; (5) Exclusion of cases with length of stay ≥30 days, reducing the influence of extreme outliers; (6) Placebo tests using artificially advanced training dates to detect spurious time patterns.

Statistical Analysis

Regression models cluster standard errors at the department-month level to account for serial correlation within departments and heteroscedasticity across observations. A two-sided P-value < 0.1 is considered statistically significant, with significance levels denoted by *****and * for the 1%, 5%, and 10% thresholds, respectively. We control for inpatient characteristics (sex, age, and insurance type), as well as fixed effects for diagnostic codes, departments, and calendar times. Further analyses are conducted across multiple temporal scales, including days, weeks, months, and quarters, to illustrate the evolution of expenditure outcomes. All specifications are performed using Python programming (version 3.11.7).

Discussion

Institutional Learning in Case-Based Payment Reform

This study identifies a key mechanism supporting the effectiveness of case-based payment reform: hospitals are supposed to internalize the technical logic of the payment system before financial incentives can meaningfully shape clinical and administrative behavior. Our analysis demonstrates that structured DIP compliance training leads to a 14.5% reduction in total inpatient expenditures over the 12-month follow-up period. This finding aligns with global evidence showing that bundled payment systems effectively curb unnecessary ancillary costs when providers clearly understand how grouping and reimbursement rules operate.34,35 Domestic experiences echo this pattern; Guangzhou’s DIP reform achieved reductions in drug spending.15

Our results extend prior research by explicitly demonstrating that the causal pathway from policy design to cost control runs through hospital-level institutional learning. Specifically, the ability of clinicians and coders to correctly interpret and operationalize DIP coding rules is the necessary precondition for reform success.36–38 This challenges the assumption that payment incentives alone will automatically drive behavioral change, highlighting the role of targeted capacity building in translating policy intentions into real-world outcomes.

Heterogeneous Effects Across Clinical Departments

The impact of DIP training varies across departments, reflecting differences in clinical workflows and policy contexts. Surgical departments experience remarkable cost reductions, with total expenditures declining around 30% within 6 months of training. In contrast, tuina departments show a nearly 10% reduction, likely due to explicit policy protections for TCM services and their distinct clinical coding norms.

This heterogeneous pattern reflects findings that case-based payment reforms are most effective when incentives align with existing clinical workflows.39,40 Surgical care relies heavily on standardized procedures and detailed documentation, making it particularly responsive to coding training. For example, accurate coding of surgical approaches and complication severity directly determines DIP group assignment and reimbursement levels. However, TCM services often involve individualized treatment plans that are less easily standardized into discrete codes, limiting the impact of generic training programs. These findings highlight the need for department-specific training curricula tailored to the unique characteristics of different clinical specialties.

Shifts from Volume-Driven to Value-Based Care

Analysis of expenditure components reveals that cost reductions are not achieved through indiscriminate service cuts but through a strategic reallocation of resources. Pharmaceutical expenditures decrease by 37.2%, inspection costs by 8.3%, and laboratory test costs by 21% following training. Concurrently, the shares of nursing expenditures, surgical expenditures, and bed expenditures increase significantly. When we re-estimate the models without controlling for length of stay (see Table A7), these expenditure patterns remain consistent: the reductions in drug, inspection, and laboratory expenditures per case become more pronounced, while the positive effects on service-related expenditure shares are attenuated but remain statistically significant. The stronger negative effects for drug, inspection, and laboratory costs reflect the fact that more efficient hospitalization processes reduce the need for repeated tests, prolonged medication use, and other duration linked expenditures. Meanwhile, the smaller positive coefficients for service-related expenditure shares arise because stay length related reductions lower both the numerator and denominator of these shares; although service-linked expenditures still decline less than total expenditures, allowing their shares to rise, the magnitude of this increase becomes smaller once the length of stay is allowed to vary. Importantly, the direction and significance of coefficients remain almost unchanged across specifications, suggesting that the observed structural upgrading toward service-intensive care is robust and not driven by model conditioning.

This compositional shift indicates a transition from volume-driven to value-based care delivery. Similar evidence has been reported elsewhere: Zhu et al41 showed that expenditure growth slowed as hospitals redirected resources toward core clinical strengths, producing targeted reductions rather than blanket cuts. Similarly, Guangzhou’s DIP reform reduced total health expenditures per case, as providers strategically shifted resource allocation away from volume‑driven pharmaceutical costs toward service‑focused care.15 Hong et al21 demonstrated that DIP alleviated the drug burden with sustained effects and optimized medication structures by reducing anti-infective and anti-tumor drugs, while recalibrating nutritional medication use. Zhao et al42 highlighted how medication and service fee reductions were accompanied by improved outcomes, with cure rates increasing and ICU transfers remaining stable.

Therefore, hospitals are not merely trimming expenditures, but recalibrating their revenue structures to emphasize procedural complexity and clinical expertise. This adaptive tendency is not unique in the context of China’s DIP. In England, the introduction of payment by results (a fixed tariff case‑mix system) led to reductions in unit costs without detrimental impact on care quality, reflecting how hospitals shifted toward more procedurally focused, efficient service models to align with payment incentives, rather than simply cutting costs.43 In this sense, rather than producing fleeting savings, the DIP embeds value into hospital finance and clinical practice, ensuring that cost control is achieved without compromising the quality.44,45

Coding Accuracy as the Operational Hinge

The mechanism through which DIP training reduces expenditures becomes clearer when examining the technical structure of the DIP grouping system (Figure 5). DIP groups are not simple one-to-one mappings from diagnosis to payment. Instead, they are constructed through a multi-layered process in which diagnosis and procedure codes are clustered into clinically coherent groups with similar resource consumption. In the example shown, multiple fine-grained disease codes (K31.701–K31.703) and procedure codes (43.4101–43.4108) jointly determine the final DIP group. Because reimbursement is tied to the group rather than the individual code, even small deviations in coding (eg, omitting EMR) can shift a case into a wrong group. It makes explicit what is often abstract in policy discussions: DIP is a coding-driven payment system, and coding accuracy is the operational hinge on which the reform turns.

Coding rules and technical requirements of DIP grouping.

This makes explicit what is often abstract in policy discussions: DIP is fundamentally a coding-driven payment system, and coding accuracy is the operational hinge on which the entire reform turns. Training equips clinicians with clarity on which clinical details must be documented and in what sequence, while enabling coding staff to translate unstructured clinical narratives into standardized codes that correctly map to DIP groups. This alignment reduces miscoding, prevents inappropriate downcoding, minimizes insurance disputes, and ensures that hospitals are fairly reimbursed for the services they provide. The sustained nature of the expenditure reductions further suggests that training functions as a catalyst for organizational learning, embedding DIP compliance into routine hospital operations rather than producing only temporary behavioral changes.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

Several limitations deserve consideration. First, the analysis draws on data from a single tertiary hospital in Shenzhen, which may limit generalizability to other settings with different case mixes or enforcement intensity. However, the mechanism linking frontline training to reform effectiveness is likely relevant across hospitals implementing case‑based payment. Second, although we find no evidence of indiscriminate cost cutting, our available quality measures are limited only to length of stay; future work should incorporate patient-reported outcomes and comprehensive clinical quality indicators. Third, our observation window covers only the first year after training; whether the effects persist, attenuate, or intensify over longer time horizons remains an open question for future research.

Future research should address these limitations by conducting analyses across multiple hospitals and diverse regional settings to improve external generalizability. Studies incorporating broader clinical quality indicators and patient-reported outcomes would provide a more holistic understanding of changes in care quality under DIP reform. In addition, longer follow-up data are warranted to examine the long-term dynamic evolution and sustainability of expenditure effects driven by DIP training.

Conclusion

This study examines the role of hospital DIP compliance training as a primary mechanism for embedding case-based payment reform into routine clinical practice. The findings indicate that DIP training effectively curbs inpatient expenditures and drives a structural shift toward high-value, patient-centered care. The long-term sustainability of such payment reforms depends on both policy incentive design and hospitals’ internal capacity to internalize regulatory rules and integrate reform requirements into practical operations.
