Perspectives on educating lifelong learning in postgraduate orthopaedic training: an interpretivist qualitative study
Authors: Megan O’Connor, Jason Bantjes, Jennifer Moriatis Wolf, Leonard Charles Marais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLifelong learning (LLL) is a core competency in medical education, reflecting the need for clinicians’ knowledge and skills to remain current. While postgraduate curricula emphasise LLL, a gap remains in understanding how it is interpreted and enacted within orthopaedic training. Little is known about how trainees and trainers conceptualise LLL and how these perspectives shape their views on its development and assessment in orthopaedic practice. Addressing this is necessary to align curricular expectations with the orthopaedic clinical context.MethodsAn interpretivist qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews and focus groups with 16 participants (eight consultant orthopaedic surgeons serving as training coordinators, and eight registrars) from all South African universities offering orthopaedic postgraduate training. Data were collected via Zoom™ (March 2023–May 2024), recorded, transcribed, and analysed inductively using thematic analysis. Trustworthiness was enhanced through triangulation, member checking, and reflexivity.ResultsParticipants conceptualised LLL in three ways: (1) as a natural predisposition linked to curiosity and pushing knowledge boundaries, (2) as a habit of mind that can be intentionally developed, and (3) as a value integral to professionalism and being a “good doctor”. Suggested strategies for LLL development included mentorship, fostering curiosity, and scholarly activities such as journal clubs and research.ConclusionLLL in postgraduate orthopaedics is perceived not only as a technical requirement but also as a personal characteristic, a habit of mind, and as a defining professional value. Recognising these dimensions informs strategies for cultivating LLL in trainees. Training programmes should support both engagement in and the effective practice of LLL by integrating approaches that promote curiosity, mentorship, and scholarly engagement with research and critical appraisal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09658-x.

Background

In medical curricula development, lifelong learning has been recognised as a core competency [1]. A competency is defined as “the combination of knowledge, skills, and attitudes that allows an individual to solve a job-related task or a problem at hand and act professionally” [2]. The significance of lifelong learning as a competency recognises that medical knowledge is rapidly evolving, as are innovations in medical technology and treatment [3]. Consequently, healthcare professionals need to keep abreast of current knowledge, adapt their practice, and master new skills, to remain competent and at the forefront of providing the best care for their patients [4]. Medical educators operate on the premise that to develop lifelong learning, its principles need to be taught during undergraduate medical education, reinforced in postgraduate training, and fortified in later practice [5]. Yet, while lifelong learning has been explored across healthcare professionals [6–9], relatively few empirical studies have explicitly examined how it is conceptualised and incorporated into practice [6, 9], with existing work demonstrating variation in how lifelong learning is understood across different healthcare professional groups [8, 10]. Furthermore, there has been little specific investigation within surgical disciplines such as orthopaedics, which medical education literature often frames as primarily focused on technical skill acquisition or apprenticeship-style learning [11, 12] and which may shape how lifelong learning is perceived and operationalised in orthopaedics.

Several orthopaedic training bodies have ostensibly adopted lifelong learning as an ideal [13, 14]. Consequently, monitored self-paced learning programmes and a heightened emphasis on annual meeting participation have been implemented [13–15]. However, these activities are representative of continuing medical education (CME) or continuing professional development (CPD), which is defined by formally structured activities, events, and mandated certifications [16]. Hojat et al. posit that these interpretations only partially satisfy the key features of the lifelong learning ethos proposed by Aspin et al., neglecting elements like personal motivation, self-direction, and recognition of learning needs that extend beyond that of the profession [5, 17]. Aspin et al. defined lifelong learning as “the development of human potential through a continuously supportive process which stimulates and empowers individuals to acquire all the knowledge, values, skills and understanding they will require throughout their lifetimes with confidence, creativity and enjoyment in all roles, circumstances and environments” [17]. This distinction suggests that participation in CME and CPD alone may not necessarily foster the deeper dispositions and habits associated with lifelong learning, which have been linked to broader individual and societal outcomes, including economic growth [18], individual adaptation and resilience [19], and social wellbeing [20].

LLL remains conceptually ambiguous, with no universally accepted definition, which may partly explain the gap between curricular intentions and practice [5, 17]. Additionally, while LLL is conceptualised as self‑directed and internally motivated, at the individual level, it is enacted within and can be shaped by broader educational and institutional contexts, creating further challenges in operationalising it in postgraduate training. The preceding discussion highlights potential disconnects between the broader ethos described by Aspin et al., the conceptualisation of lifelong learning adopted in this study, and the LLL ideal described in curricular competencies. In our experience within clinical and educational settings, these discrepancies become apparent as educators attempt to implement curricular proficiencies [6], an observation which served as the impetus for the present study [21]. Considering curricula competencies in orthopaedic training, there is also recognition that trainees and educators hold differing perspectives on key aspects of the educational process, and the value of learning [22]. These differences can influence how educational practices are experienced and enacted within the clinical environment. At the same time, evidence suggests that understanding both trainee and educator perspectives is essential for identifying barriers and facilitators to effective training and for aligning educational strategies with practice realities [23]. Considering both perspectives, therefore, provides a more comprehensive understanding of how lifelong learning is conceptualised and operationalised in practice.

This study was prompted by the observation that, despite growing recognition of the importance of lifelong learning in orthopaedics [4], there are limited dedicated teaching and training interventions to support it. This study aimed to explore how trainees and training coordinators conceptualise lifelong learning within postgraduate orthopaedic training and to consider the implications of these perspectives for its development in practice. Research of this kind could inform education policy and training reform in postgraduate orthopaedic education, thereby fostering the development of protean lifelong learning orthopaedic surgeons who value knowledge and continue to seek education as new technologies and evidence evolve.

Materials and methods

This interpretivist qualitative study was conducted using inductive thematic analysis of interviews with consultant orthopaedic surgeons serving as postgraduate training coordinators, and focus group discussions with orthopaedic registrar trainees. The study aimed to describe the current perspectives of lifelong learning in the South African postgraduate orthopaedic training landscape. Preparation of this article followed the COsolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) [24, 25].

Sampling of participants was conducted intentionally to obtain representation from each of the eight universities that conduct orthopaedic postgraduate training in South Africa, thereby enhancing generalisability to the local context and credibility of the findings [26]. Purposive sampling was used for coordinators, as it intentionally recruits participants with relevant experience or expertise. Invitations were sent electronically to department heads requesting the name(s) of the postgraduate training coordinator(s). Each university elects one or more consultant representatives to be responsible for matters related to postgraduate training, including programme development, mentorship, or assessment, with the role, to the authors’ knowledge, not subject to formal eligibility criteria at any university. Snowball sampling was utilised for registrars. Registrar trainee representatives from three universities were asked to refer potential participants, registrars in any year of training, within their networks from each of the eight universities [26]. Each individual was then personally contacted on email or WhatsApp, to gauge interest and coordinate the online interview or focus group.

Prior to data collection, all participants were provided with an electronic information document outlining the purpose and conduct of the study, along with a consent form for completion and return. At the commencement of the interview or focus group, verbal consent for participation was confirmed for voice and video recording, and subsequent use of deidentified quotes in a publication. No invited participants declined or withdrew. Data were collected between March 2023 and May 2024 through eight semi-structured interviews (training coordinators) and two focus groups (four registrar trainee participants each) conducted on Zoom™ (Version 6.1.11). All interviews and focus groups were conducted in English. Participants were given a small token of appreciation (an online shopping voucher) for their time at the conclusion of data collection, which they were unaware of before participation, ensuring it did not influence their decision to take part. A semi-structured interview guide was developed collaboratively by MO and JB to capture the breadth of understanding of lifelong learning, including its definition, how it should be educated, and how it might be assessed (electronic supplementary material). All interviews and focus groups were conducted by MO. Only the participants and the interviewer were present. Co-author JB, a registered psychologist with experience in medical research, reviewed the first three interviews to enhance reflexivity and reduce the influence of any single researcher’s perspective. Each session lasted approximately one hour. MO took handwritten notes on hard copies of the guide during and after interviews, capturing emerging ideas and connections between concepts. Recordings were stored on password-protected cloud storage and transcribed by an independent transcriber. Transcripts were reviewed for accuracy by MO. Member checking was conducted with three interview participants, whose data were prominently represented, who reviewed the full manuscript and confirmed its accuracy, with one minor clarification incorporated [27].

MO and JB conducted an initial inductive thematic analysis of the first three transcripts, through which preliminary overarching themes were identified. These themes were subsequently observed to recur across the remaining transcripts; however, later data collection contributed additional depth and nuance to the sub-themes. An interpretivist stance was adopted, acknowledging that participants’ accounts reflect socially and professionally situated constructions of meaning. Transcripts were coded iteratively, with codes grouped into themes that, through ongoing engagement between the researchers and the data, were discussed at consensus meetings to refine the thematic structure and develop a final codebook comprising three major themes, each with several sub-themes [28]. MM (an independent coder) then applied this codebook in a secondary deductive analysis using the final codebook in ATLAS.ti (©2024 ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH) to enhance consistency in the application of codes and to minimise the influence of individual researcher interpretation. MO and JB reviewed coded data for applicability to the respective themes and selected illustrative quotes. Trustworthiness was enhanced through iterative transcript review, triangulation among researchers, and member checking of the draft manuscript [27].

The primary researcher is a consultant orthopaedic surgeon involved in postgraduate orthopaedic training, with prior qualitative research experience and a scholarly interest in lifelong learning. This positionality informed the study’s design, which explored the perspectives of training coordinators and registrar trainees on how lifelong learning can be fostered within orthopaedic training. To support open dialogue, the researcher transparently disclosed a personal motivation to advance orthopaedic training and the view that lifelong learning may contribute to this, applied consistently across all interviews and focus groups. This may have encouraged participants to emphasise the positive attributes of lifelong learning. The researcher’s seniority relative to registrars may also have influenced responses, where the trainees may be sensitive to perceived expectations and reluctant to speak openly. To mitigate this, registrar data were collected through focus groups, intended to facilitate more open peer discussion, but this may have reduced data depth compared to individual interviews with training coordinators. Finally, to minimise prior professional influence, registrars from the researcher’s affiliated institution were recruited from a separate training site.

Results

Sixteen participants were included: eight consultant orthopaedic surgeons serving as orthopaedic postgraduate training programme coordinators and eight orthopaedic registrar trainees, representing all eight universities offering orthopaedic training in South Africa. The analysis of interviews and focus groups revealed three overarching ways participants conceptualised lifelong learning: (1) as a natural predisposition, (2) as a habit of mind that can be intentionally developed, and (3) as a value.

Lifelong learning as a natural predisposition

The first theme portrayed lifelong learning as an inherent predisposition, often described by participants as something that comes naturally rather than is shaped by external factors. This framing suggests that lifelong learning was sometimes understood as a relatively stable disposition reflected in behaviour patterns over time. Participants frequently described lifelong learners as inquisitive, constantly questioning assumptions, and open to challenge.

“I think people that are inquisitive in nature and don’t just accept things because someone said it are probably more naturally inclined to it [lifelong learning]… they’re curious, and they want to know why.” [Coordinator Interview 2]

“I think people that are inquisitive in nature and don’t just accept things because someone said it are probably more naturally inclined to it [lifelong learning]… they’re curious, and they want to know why.” [Coordinator Interview 2]

“You must be brave enough to put yourself out there and realise that you might be wrong… and in a year or two I might be proven completely wrong. If you’re scared of being wrong, that’s a problem.” [Coordinator Interview 1]

“You must be brave enough to put yourself out there and realise that you might be wrong… and in a year or two I might be proven completely wrong. If you’re scared of being wrong, that’s a problem.” [Coordinator Interview 1]

”If you don’t have an interest, then you don’t have that drive to want to learn…” [Focus Group 2, Registrar Participant 2]

”If you don’t have an interest, then you don’t have that drive to want to learn…” [Focus Group 2, Registrar Participant 2]

Lifelong learners were also described as people whose nature is to consistently push the boundaries of their knowledge and understanding to continue developing. They described lifelong learners as both “disruptors” who challenged the status quo, and as courageous people who were able to tolerate the unknown. An interviewee drew on a concept which they attributed to Jordan Peterson’s “12 Rules for Life” [29], to illustrate this idea:“…you have to have your one foot firmly in order… but your other foot has to be in chaos. Your foundation has to be in order, but your other foot has to be in the unknown, in the scary, in the risky, and it’s only if you can balance those two that you can fully participate and be the best that you can be.” [Coordinator Interview 4]

“…you have to have your one foot firmly in order… but your other foot has to be in chaos. Your foundation has to be in order, but your other foot has to be in the unknown, in the scary, in the risky, and it’s only if you can balance those two that you can fully participate and be the best that you can be.” [Coordinator Interview 4]

Importantly, these accounts of lifelong learning as an innate characteristic did not exist in isolation. Several participants expressed two seemingly contradictory perspectives simultaneously, describing lifelong learning as something that may come naturally to some individuals, whilst also acknowledging that elements of lifelong learning can be nurtured and developed. This co-existence of views was evident across both registrars and coordinators:

“Some people are born with more than others [lifelong learning predispositions], but it’s definitely something that can be improved in everyone.” [Coordinator Interview 1]

“Some people are born with more than others [lifelong learning predispositions], but it’s definitely something that can be improved in everyone.” [Coordinator Interview 1]

“I think it is personality based…” and later in the same interview, “but, …you can unlock that personality.” [Coordinator Interview 5]

“I think it is personality based…” and later in the same interview, “but, …you can unlock that personality.” [Coordinator Interview 5]

“I had no aspirations [for continued learning]… until I met Dr X… [who] stimulated… parts of me that lay dormant… rekindled what may perhaps have been a wish…” [Focus group 1, Registrar participant 1]

“I had no aspirations [for continued learning]… until I met Dr X… [who] stimulated… parts of me that lay dormant… rekindled what may perhaps have been a wish…” [Focus group 1, Registrar participant 1]

Lifelong learning as a habit of mind developed through training and practice

While often presented alongside the view that lifelong learning is an innate predisposition, some participants described lifelong learning as a capacity that can be developed independently of inherent traits. This second theme emphasised that lifelong learning can be intentionally cultivated through structured strategies. Participants described deliberate approaches, including developing critical thinking, modelling behaviours observed in respected mentors, and engaging in research, as mechanisms to foster sustained lifelong learning practices. These accounts suggest that lifelong learning was understood in multiple ways: for some, as both an inherent disposition and as a set of practices that can be consciously developed over time, and for others, as a capacity that can be actively and intentionally cultivated irrespective of predisposition. As one participant described it:“So you can encourage them [trainees] to think critically, and hopefully by the time that they are interested in… doing postgraduate studies, they will have that inquisitive mind, and chances are they will be lifelong learners.” [Coordinator Interview 7]

“So you can encourage them [trainees] to think critically, and hopefully by the time that they are interested in… doing postgraduate studies, they will have that inquisitive mind, and chances are they will be lifelong learners.” [Coordinator Interview 7]

On developing the inquisitive mind of a trainee, one participant related a practical way to do this:“…one of the things that I do with the medical students and registrars is ask them to ask ‘why’ three times. If you ask why three times, so you get it; you ask why, and you get an answer. Ask why again, you get the deeper answer … to always be curious about the reasons why things are the way they are.” [Coordinator Interview 1]

“…one of the things that I do with the medical students and registrars is ask them to ask ‘why’ three times. If you ask why three times, so you get it; you ask why, and you get an answer. Ask why again, you get the deeper answer … to always be curious about the reasons why things are the way they are.” [Coordinator Interview 1]

Participants asserted that the mental habit of lifelong learning can also be developed through mentorship or interactions with respected senior colleagues who model this way of thinking:

“Define key figures who have… moved along that path and use them as mentors to encourage or inspire younger doctors to perform likewise. So that mentorship plan has to start right from… the early years.” [Coordinator Interview 5]

“Define key figures who have… moved along that path and use them as mentors to encourage or inspire younger doctors to perform likewise. So that mentorship plan has to start right from… the early years.” [Coordinator Interview 5]

“you need that person… who’s going to tell you to just keep trying… and keep your love for it going” [Focus Group 2, Registrar Participant 4]

“you need that person… who’s going to tell you to just keep trying… and keep your love for it going” [Focus Group 2, Registrar Participant 4]

Participants also asserted that as a habit of mind, lifelong learning could be developed through scholarship, including understanding scientific research methods, conducting research, and through opportunities to critically appraise evidence:“… encouraging research… early on, especially in terms of the MMed [master of medicine research component], … also helps [lifelong learning] because if you are done with your MMed and we encourage the culture of research, it also helps you later on, you can tackle other things, not just your MMed. You can come out [of the training programme] with other papers before you finish your five years…“[Coordinator Interview 7]

“… encouraging research… early on, especially in terms of the MMed [master of medicine research component], … also helps [lifelong learning] because if you are done with your MMed and we encourage the culture of research, it also helps you later on, you can tackle other things, not just your MMed. You can come out [of the training programme] with other papers before you finish your five years…“[Coordinator Interview 7]

Participants said that academic scholarly practices such as regular journal clubs could foster a culture of research and encourage critical evaluation of evidence, which they believed was integral to the mental habit of lifelong learning:“But we also have journal clubs… for … critiquing the articles, they [lifelong learners] need to be able to determine what would change our practice, what we can actually use, what is useful information…” [Focus group 2, Registrar Participant 4]

“But we also have journal clubs… for … critiquing the articles, they [lifelong learners] need to be able to determine what would change our practice, what we can actually use, what is useful information…” [Focus group 2, Registrar Participant 4]

Lifelong learning as a value

The emphasis that the preceding participant placed on critically appraising evidence and determining what should inform clinical practice suggests that lifelong learning was not viewed as an end in itself but as a means of maintaining high standards of patient care. This framing also appeared to shape how participants evaluated themselves and others, with lifelong learning functioning as a marker of what constitutes a “good” or “committed” orthopaedic surgeon. This implicit orientation toward improvement and accountability points to a theme which underpinned several participants’ engagement with lifelong learning. Within this context, participants frequently framed lifelong learning as a deeply held value, as inseparable from being an ethical and competent orthopaedic surgeon, rather than merely an educational requirement. Across both training coordinators and registrars, accounts reflected an understanding of lifelong learning as something intrinsic to professional and personal identity and meaning:

“If what gives you satisfaction is providing good quality practice, being on top of your game, and being able to provide evidence-based service to your patients, then lifelong learning should be part and parcel of that process.” [Coordinator Interview 5]

“If what gives you satisfaction is providing good quality practice, being on top of your game, and being able to provide evidence-based service to your patients, then lifelong learning should be part and parcel of that process.” [Coordinator Interview 5]

“you can choose to… learn about something that you think is going to better you as a person…” [Focus Group 2, Registrar Participant 4]

“you can choose to… learn about something that you think is going to better you as a person…” [Focus Group 2, Registrar Participant 4]

The association between lifelong learning and professional identity was further reinforced through contrast, with participants describing differing clinician archetypes:

“you get the one that is an ongoing learner… and you get the one that’s your chronic, comfortable, complacent type consultant” [Focus Group 1, Registrar Participant 2]

“you get the one that is an ongoing learner… and you get the one that’s your chronic, comfortable, complacent type consultant” [Focus Group 1, Registrar Participant 2]

“they’ve got one way that they can treat it… and this is generally people… [who] have stopped engaging… and start deteriorating in terms of the care that they provide.” [Coordinator Interview 4]

“they’ve got one way that they can treat it… and this is generally people… [who] have stopped engaging… and start deteriorating in terms of the care that they provide.” [Coordinator Interview 4]

These contrasts highlight how lifelong learning operates not only as a personal value but also as a normative standard against which others are evaluated, with perceived deficiencies in lifelong learning framed as both professionally and clinically consequential, collectively positioning it as a moral imperative and a professional compass. Several participants extended this framing, describing lifelong learning as a way of life, a guiding principle for how to live and work:

“…in order for me to fulfil my purpose at my workplace, I have to do that to the best of my ability, and I can do it no other way than continuously evolving in all of those spheres, so both in terms of the practical and academic knowledge, but also in developing soft skills.” [Coordinator Interview 4]

“…in order for me to fulfil my purpose at my workplace, I have to do that to the best of my ability, and I can do it no other way than continuously evolving in all of those spheres, so both in terms of the practical and academic knowledge, but also in developing soft skills.” [Coordinator Interview 4]

“You’re learning every day, and I think the thing about a lifelong learner is that they’re more intentional about what they’re learning… you can choose to scroll through social media or learn something that’s going to better you as a person or in your career.” [Focus Group 2, Registrar Participant 4]

“You’re learning every day, and I think the thing about a lifelong learner is that they’re more intentional about what they’re learning… you can choose to scroll through social media or learn something that’s going to better you as a person or in your career.” [Focus Group 2, Registrar Participant 4]

Participants also highlighted that this value is shaped through social interaction and observation, and can be encultured or modelled in a learning community from respected senior colleagues. Observing senior surgeons who continued to read and adapt their practice was described as ‘stimulating’ and transformative:“…senior people at the top of their game, they were still reading up and looking at the latest thing and changing their practice, which I thought was very stimulating because before I thought surgery is like once you know how to do something, you know how to do something… and then I realised that’s not the case. It’s [orthopaedics] continually evolving.” [Coordinator Interview 2]

“…senior people at the top of their game, they were still reading up and looking at the latest thing and changing their practice, which I thought was very stimulating because before I thought surgery is like once you know how to do something, you know how to do something… and then I realised that’s not the case. It’s [orthopaedics] continually evolving.” [Coordinator Interview 2]

Such accounts illustrate how the value of lifelong learning is reinforced through role modelling, shaping expectations about what constitutes appropriate professional behaviour. Within this context, participants emphasised that lifelong learning, like other values, is learned through social observation, modelling, and interaction with others.

“You know this expression that says you are the average of two or three people you hang about with. I mean, I learn things from my children. I learn things from my wife.” [Coordinator Interview 1]

“You know this expression that says you are the average of two or three people you hang about with. I mean, I learn things from my children. I learn things from my wife.” [Coordinator Interview 1]

“You need to have the kind of aura around you that would make other junior doctors want to emulate your footsteps and your approach to your practice.” [Coordinator Interview 5]

Discussion

This investigation explored how postgraduate orthopaedic trainees and consultant orthopaedic surgeons serving as training coordinators conceptualise the implementation of lifelong learning within practice. From the earliest interviews, a tension was evident regarding whether lifelong learning can be meaningfully taught. A prominent view expressed by participants was that lifelong learning is, at least in part, an inherent characteristic, with some suggesting that individuals either possess this inclination or do not, reflecting a trait-like conceptualisation. Participants frequently described lifelong learners as demonstrating an innate commitment to continuous improvement, with natural curiosity attributes that align with literature describing highly successful orthopaedic surgeons and excellent physicians, in whom curiosity and continuous improvement are consistently identified as distinguishing characteristics [30, 31]. Such accounts are also consistent with psychological and educational perspectives that frame learning orientations as dispositional, characterised by relatively stable individual differences while remaining responsive to contextual influences [32].

While participants often described lifelong learning as a disposition, this view was not uniformly held. In parallel, participants emphasised that these attributes could be developed and shaped through training experiences and clinical environments. Journal clubs and the mandated research requirement for qualification as an orthopaedic specialist were suggested as mechanisms to garner lifelong learning habits of mind beyond formal training. Mi et al. explicitly explored the relationship between the orientation toward lifelong learning and skills in practising evidence-based medicine [33]. They determined that residents with prior research experience had a significantly higher orientation toward lifelong learning [33]. These findings align with the review of Pato et al., who suggested journal clubs as a model for lifelong learning in psychiatry residents, to train interrogation and critique of the medical literature [34].

This apparent tension within participants’ accounts, in which lifelong learning was described both as an inherent disposition and as something that can be cultivated, warrants further consideration. Rather than representing a contradiction, these perspectives may be understood as complementary. Psychological literature has considered that constructs such as curiosity and interest can be conceptualised as both relatively stable dispositions and contextually responsive states [35]. From this perspective, lifelong learning may be situated as a capacity that varies between individuals, yet remains amenable to development through appropriate environmental conditions and sustained engagement. This interpretation aligns with literature on self-directed learning in the health professions, which emphasises the interaction between individual motivation and supportive organisational and educational contexts in facilitating ongoing learning behaviours [36]. Importantly for educators, lifelong learning may therefore be understood as an inherent capacity that can be strengthened or attenuated over time through processes of socialisation and participation within clinical learning environments.

Similarly, the notion of lifelong learning as a core residency competency resonated with participants. They spoke of LLL not as a bureaucratic requirement, but as integral to professional identity, and a moral imperative tied to patient care. Participants described LLL as a guiding principle, a way of life. This aligns with Schwartz’s Theory of basic human values, which conceptualises values as enduring beliefs that motivate action and confer significance [37]. LLL reflects several of Schwartz’s motivational domains, including self-direction and openness to change [37]. Its status as a value is further supported by literature in medical education [38]. Lifelong learning is recognised as a core value in family medicine [38], and an Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education study ranked “growth” (encompassing curiosity and continuous improvement) as trainees’ second most important value [39]. Physicians who live by this value report greater needs satisfaction and professional fulfilment [40].

If LLL is understood as a value, its transmission cannot rely solely on teaching knowledge or skills; it requires enculturation, the internalisation of norms and behaviours within a learning community [41]. Participants emphasised the role modelling by senior surgeons as transformative. Previous investigations have determined that clinical work environments that foster a culture of critical thinking can enhance an individual’s motivation to engage in lifelong learning [42, 43]. Furthermore, professional identities may differ across clinical disciplines and have an impact on perceptions of and practices related to lifelong learning. Medical education authors suggest that surgical discipline training is skills-based and involves a different perceived stress level compared to nonsurgical disciplines, thereby influencing the learning modalities adopted [5, 44]. This perspective highlights the need for supportive environments that encourage LLL among trainers and trainees, reinforcing mentorship as a cultural driver [45, 46]. Medical education literature also identifies mentoring as a mechanism to support the ongoing development of trainees, especially as they transition from training to practice [46]. These strategies are particularly salient within orthopaedic training, where the strong emphasis on technical skill acquisition and apprenticeship-style learning may risk prioritising procedural competence over the explicit development of reflective and inquiry-driven learning practices.

Given the central role of mentorship and role modelling in shaping learning practices, it is notable that participants differed less in their understanding of lifelong learning and more in how they positioned themselves in relation to it. Registrar trainees, who predominantly appeared to occupy mentee roles, most frequently described lifelong learning in relation to themselves, reflecting an active process of identity formation during training. In contrast, training coordinators, who appeared more often to occupy mentor roles, variably referred to trainees as lifelong learners, but notably, several also positioned themselves as the lifelong learner. This suggests that, for these participants, lifelong learning is not understood as a competency achieved and concluded, but as an enduring professional stance characterised by ongoing openness to learning. Such positioning aligns with medical education literature that conceptualises lifelong learning as a continuous, developmental process extending across the professional lifespan, underpinned by self-awareness, and sustained engagement with evolving knowledge [6].

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study was conducted within a single clinical speciality and national context, which may limit the transferability of findings to other disciplines or healthcare systems. However, this focus was intentional and encompassed all institutions offering postgraduate orthopaedic training in the country, thereby providing comprehensive representation of perspectives in this specific context. Second, the positionality of the primary researcher, a consultant orthopaedic surgeon with a scholarly interest in lifelong learning, may have influenced both data collection and interpretation. The transparent disclosure of a personal interest in lifelong learning during rapport-building was intended to support reflexivity and open dialogue. However, it may have primed participants to frame lifelong learning in predominantly positive terms. As a result, critical or dissenting perspectives may have been underrepresented. Finally, differences in data collection methods between participant groups may have shaped the nature of the findings. Registrar trainees participated in focus groups, which facilitated interaction and the co-construction of shared perspectives, but may have limited the depth of individual articulation. In contrast, one-on-one interviews with training coordinators allowed for more detailed and nuanced individual accounts. While this methodological triangulation provided complementary insights, it may also have introduced variation in how concepts were expressed and elaborated.

The findings of this study highlight several important avenues for future research. The tension between lifelong learning as an innate predisposition and as a developable habit of mind warrants further exploration, with implications for trainee selection, educational design, and the support of learners across the training continuum. Future work should examine how educational strategies, including mentorship and critical appraisal of research, contribute to the development and sustainment of a lifelong learning orientation, including beyond specialist qualification. Further research is also needed to explore how lifelong learning as a value is encultured within clinical training environments, and how cultures across medical specialities shape its expression and prioritisation.

Conclusions

Lifelong learning in postgraduate orthopaedics is conceptualised as both a predisposition and a capacity that can be intentionally developed. Recognising this dual nature enabled participants to identify a range of strategies to support its development in trainees, including fostering a culture of curiosity, mentorship and positive role modelling, and engagement with research and critical appraisal. Lifelong learning also emerged as a value linked to notions of competence and responsibility, underscoring the importance of training environments that support not only learning behaviours but their sustained enactment in clinical practice.
