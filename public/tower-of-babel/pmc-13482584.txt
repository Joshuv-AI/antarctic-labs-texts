Assessment of healing after primer endodontic treatment and retreatment using fractal analysis and PAI score over a 24-month follow-up
Authors: Hilal Ekmen, Hüda Melike Bayram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to compare the healing after 24 months between initial endodontic treatment and retreatment using fractal analysis and the periapical index (PAI) scores. Eighty-three mandibular teeth with 5 mm lesions from 76 patients were selected, and then the teeth were divided into two groups: the primary endodontic treatment group and the retreatment group. Panoramic X-rays evaluated before and after 2 years of treatment were determined in both treatment groups. Differences between groups were evaluated by fractal analysis. Post-treatment fractal dimensions were not statistically significant in either group compared to pre-treatment values (p > 0.05). In the primary endodontic treatment group, the mean pre-treatment fractal dimension value was 1.48 ± 0.11 (mean ± SD), while it was determined as 1.52 ± 0.12(mean ± SD) at 24 months of follow-up. In the retreatment group, the mean pre-treatment fractal dimension value was 1.45 ± 0.16 (mean ± SD), while it was determined as 1.50 ± 0.15(mean ± SD) at 24 months of follow-up. There was no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of fractal dimension differences. Statistically significant decreases were observed in PAI values within the group (p < 0.001), and no significant difference was observed between the groups in terms of time-dependent decreases (p > 0.05). In the primary endodontic treatment group, the PAI score was 4.83 ± 0.38 (mean ± SD) before treatment, while it was determined as 1.71 ± 1.33(mean ± SD) at 24 months of follow-up. In the retreatment group, the pre-treatment significance level was 4.76 ± 0.43(mean ± SD), while at 24 months of follow-up it was determined to be 1.80 ± 1.21(mean ± SD). Fractal analysis may provide complementary quantitative information regarding periapical bone changes; however, FD measurements obtained from panoramic radiographs should be interpreted cautiously because of the inherent limitations of this imaging modality. Integrating fractal analysis with traditional scoring systems allows for a comprehensive assessment of long-term bone formation in both primary endodontic treatment and retreatment. Consequently, both primary endodontic treatment and retreatment showed similar patterns in periapical bone healing over 24 months, and no significant difference was found between the two groups in fractal dimension values or PAI scores. Introduction

The main objectives of root canal therapy are to ensure the continuity of long-term function and prevent re-infection in teeth with pulpal and periapical diseases caused by caries or trauma1. Retreatment has the same purpose as the primary endodontic treatment of infected root canals. Nonsurgical endodontic retreatment is often preferred due to its conservative nature and ability to preserve the natural tooth structure2. This is achieved by removing the initial root canal filling material, disinfecting the root canal system, and resealing the root canals3.

Apical periodontitis (AP) is a disease characterized by an inflammatory response and bone destruction in the periapical tissues caused by a microbial infection in the dental pulp4–6. Periapical lesions resulting from endodontic infections generally range from 5 to 8 mm in diameter7,8. The treatment of large lesions effectively eliminates infection9. As a result of correct treatment, the healing of a periapical lesion occurs with complex tissue repair. The radiolucent area is decreased in follow-up radiographs taken at regular intervals10,11. The evaluation of periapical lesion healing in endodontics relies on a combination of clinical, radiographic, and biomolecular methods. Clinically, healing is indicated by the resolution of symptoms, such as the absence of pain on palpation or percussion12. Radiographic assessment is a fundamental tool in monitoring periapical healing, allowing visualization of lesion size reductions and surrounding bone density increases13.

The Periapical Index (PAI) provides a standardized method for categorizing and tracking the radiographic appearance of lesions over time, offering a reliable measure of healing progression14,15. PAI can be classified from one to five according to the appearance of AP on the X-ray16. Advanced imaging techniques, such as fractal analysis, further enhance this evaluation by providing quantitative insights into trabecular bone structure changes during the healing process17.

The box-counting fractal analysis method is objective in the trabecular bone evaluation and provides quantitative data. In some studies, the follow-up of lesion healing in the relevant region was carried out by recording radiographs taken before and after root canal treatment and evaluating the trabecular bone structure with fractal analysis18,19. Many studies have evaluated periapical lesion healing in endodontics, especially using the PAI method20,21. Recent investigations have focused on fractal analysis to assess bone healing or changes in bone density in the periapical region following endodontic therapy19,22.

Yet, most of these studies predominantly concentrate on outcomes observed after the initial root canal procedure. In light of this, to the best of our knowledge, this present study is the first to assess the healing of lesions with a diameter of 5 mm and above using fractal analysis after endodontic treatment for the initial and retreatment cases. This study aims to evaluate the improvement of teeth with a large lesion size during the first endodontic treatment and retreatment at the end of 24 months using the box-counting fractal analysis method and the Periapical Index (PAI) method. According to the null hypothesis, the lesion healing is similar after the first treatment and retreatment.

Materials and methods

This single-center retrospective study uses recorded panoramic radiographs of patients taken from the Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University’s Faculty of Dentistry archives. All procedures performed in studies involving human biological material were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University’s Faculty of Medicine, project number 20- KAEK-248. A total of 76 patients were selected from those treated at the Department of Endodontics between March 2013 and February 2020; the inclusion criteria were patients aged 18–63 years, systemically healthy, with lesion diameters of 5 mm and above in the root apex, with 24 months of follow-up X-rays, with no broken instruments in the root, with no perforation, with no post core in the root, with no internal and external root resorption, with no ledge on the root canal wall and with no incomplete or overfilling.

Based on a previous fractal analysis study with a similar retrospective endodontic design21, a power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.2 (Franz Faul, Christian-Albrechts-Universität, Kiel, Germany) prior to data collection. The effect size represented the expected difference in FD values between the groups, and a medium effect size of 0.60 was assumed during study planning. The minimum sample size was determined to be 72 teeth, considering 80% statistical power and a significance level of 0.05. To prevent selection and measurement bias, all radiographic images were anonymized. Throughout the fractal analysis and PAI scoring processes, the researcher was kept unaware of each patient’s clinical data. To assess intra-observer reliability, an additional test-retest intra-observer reliability analysis was performed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) on 25% of the measurements (ICC = Preoperative FD measurements: 0.825, Postoperative FD measurements: 0.933).Repeated measurements were conducted by the same observer after a month interval using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement.

Eighty-three mandibular teeth with large periapical lesions were divided into the primary endodontic treatment group (n = 42) and the retreatment group (n = 41). Pre-op panoramic X-rays and 24-month follow-up panoramic X-rays were selected from both treatment groups. All panoramic X-rays taken from the patients were captured for 9 s at 70 kVp and 10 mA using Morita Veraviewepocs 2D (Kyoto, Japan). Lesion diameter measurements were made using the ImageJ 1.3 program (ImageJ; US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). Panoramic X-rays were transferred to the ImageJ 1.3 (ImageJ; US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, US) program. High-resolution panoramic radiographs with the same magnification ratio were used. A metal object with an actual length of 1 cm was remeasured in a panoramic radiograph imported into ImageJ. In the measured panoramic radiograph, the metal object was placed in the person’s buccal sulcus. These measurements were repeated in the anterior, premolar, and molar regions in panoramic radiographs, and magnification ratios were determined separately for each of the three regions. The distance between the two furthest points of the lesion was calculated by calibrating it with the magnification ratios obtained for the three regions. Teeth with a distance of 5 mm or more between the two furthest points of the periapical lesion were selected and recorded. In multi-rooted teeth, the roots of teeth with larger lesion sizes were included in the study. when determining lesion sizes. The study included mandibular anterior, premolar, and molar teeth.

Fractal analysis

Fractal analysis was conducted using the method developed by White & Rudolph (White et al. 1999)23. The process steps are as follows:

Panoramic radiography was transferred to the ImageJ program (Fig. 1).

Determination of the ROI in a panoramic radiograph.

The regions of interest (ROI) to be examined were selected with a pixel size of 60 × 60 square pixels (Fig. 2a). A rigorous anatomical registration process was followed to ensure the accuracy of ROI placement between pre-treatment and 24-month follow-up radiographs. Despite the inherent limitations of panoramic radiographs in terms of geometric reproducibility, ROI positioning was standardized using the root tip and the long axis of the tooth as fixed anatomical reference points. In the initial radiographs, the regions of interest (ROI) for each patient were selected 1 mm away from the root tip. In follow-up radiographs, the region of interest (ROI) was meticulously repositioned from the same location relative to this distance. For each tooth, the position and size of all ROIs selected in the two radiographs were the same (60 × 60 pixels). Thus, the selected ROI was standardized and made reproducible. This manual registration technique aims to minimize potential measurement errors resulting from variations in patient position during separate imaging sessions. The 60 × 60 pixel ROI value was determined based on a preliminary pilot study conducted on 30 radiographs randomly selected from our dataset. Various ROI sizes (ranging from 30 × 30 to 80 × 80 pixels) were tested to determine the optimal area that consistently captures the periapical trabecular structure and avoids interference with adjacent anatomical structures such as root surfaces, periodontal ligament spaces, and the mandibular canal. The 60 × 60 pixel size was deemed the most representative area for measuring bone complexity in all samples and minimized the inclusion of non-trabecular anatomical “noise” that could distort fractal analysis results. The selected ROI was cropped, saved, and replicated in JPEG format. The selected ROI areas did not contain anatomical formations such as tooth roots and mandibular canals. A ‘Gaussian Blur’ sigma 35-pixel filter was applied to blur the areas of high and medium brightness originating from soft tissue and different bone thicknesses (Fig. 2b). Using the ‘Subtract’ application, the blurred image was removed from the original image (Fig. 2c). Using the ‘Add’ application, 128 gray tones for each pixel were added and an image with an average of 128 was obtained (Fig. 2d). Using the ‘Threshold’ option, the image was converted into a two-color black and white image (Fig. 2e). Thus, the bone marrow and trabecular structures became distinguishable. The noise areas in the image were diminished using the ‘Erode’ option (Fig. 2f). The fields found were expanded using the ‘Dilate’ option (Fig. 2g). Using the ‘Invert’ option, the white areas were turned into black and the black areas were turned into white. The outline of the trabecular bone was then revealed (Fig. 2h). Using the ‘Skeletonization’ option, the skeletal lines of the trabecular bone were revealed (Fig. 2ı). The negative slope of the linear regression of the curve was calculated by dividing 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 32, and 64-pixel squares according to the box-counting method of fractal analysis. The slope obtained represents the fractal dimension of the selected area. After calculating the fractal dimensions, the periapical region of the teeth was evaluated according to the PAI classification.

Fractal analysis stages in bone.

PAI 1: Normal apical periodontium.

PAI 2: There are changes in the bone, but there is not enough bone loss to be observed on the radiography.

PAI 3: Structural changes are observed in the bone diagnosed with apical periodontitis.

PAI 4: There is bone loss with well-defined borders.

PAI 5: Advanced bone loss and large radiolucent areas are observed.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were carried out to provide information about the general characteristics of the study groups. The data on quantitative values were given as Mean ± Standard Deviation, while the data on qualitative values were presented as n(%). The differences between the groups regarding quantitative values were examined with the Independent Samples T-Test or the Mann-Whitney U Test, considering parametric properties. The differences between the groups regarding qualitative values were evaluated with the chi-squared test. When p values were calculated less than 0.05, it was considered statistically significant. A ready-made statistical software was used in the calculations (IBM SPSS Statistics 19, SPSS Inc., and IBM Co., Somers, NY, US). ICC values were interpreted as follows: values below 0.50 indicated poor reliability, 0.50–0.75 moderate reliability, 0.75–0.90 good reliability, and values above 0.90 excellent reliability.

Discussion

The success rates of endodontic treatment for teeth with AP are based on the analysis of clinical signs, findings, and radiographic evaluations24. The teeth and surrounding tissues are healed if there are no clinical and radiographic symptoms and radiolucent areas in the follow-up sessions after endodontic treatment25. Instead of using traditional indices such as PAI, we found it appropriate to evaluate periapical healing using fractal analysis in this study. Fractal analysis has been used in endodontic studies as a quantitative and non-invasive method for evaluating trabecular bone alterations26. The aim of this study; evaluates extensive periapical lesion healing after the first endodontic treatment and retreatment using fractal analysis after 24 months of follow-up. To the best of our knowledge, comparing the first and second root canal treatments of teeth with 5 mm lesions has not yet been conducted in the literature using fractal analysis. The null hypothesis of the study that trabecular bone healing is similar after the first endodontic treatment and retreatment was accepted.

Studies have shown that a high fractal dimension (FD) value indicates a more complex structure and smaller gaps between the bone trabeculae. In contrast, a low FD value means the bone structure is simple and has more space in the bone trabeculae. The ROI position is an important parameter that affects fractal analysis results27,28. In a previous study, the FD values of the ROI containing no anatomical structure or dental tissue were compared with the FD values of the ROI containing dental tissue or anatomical structure, and the FD values that contained dental tissue were found to be significantly different from each other. Dental structures should not be included while selecting the ROI29. In light of this information, in this study, a ROI in the exact location and size was generated using pre-op and 24-month follow-up radiographs of the same tooth to eliminate errors related to the location and size of the ROI.

Chen et al.18 reported increased FD values measured from the periapical area in intraoral radiographs taken 1 year after a successful root canal. In contrast, Yu et al.30 Observed decreased FD values in reactive bone sites after clinically successful endodontic treatments. According to one study, FD values increased significantly around the root tip after root canal treatment, and a significant decrease was detected in the adjacent region just above the apical lesion in cases that fully healed 1 year after root canal treatment31. Uğur Aydın et al.21 reported an increase in FD values after root canal treatment due to a 1 year follow-up of the diabetes mellitus(DM) and the control groups compared with the pre-treatment group. According to the findings of our study, in intra-group comparisons, the FD values before and after a 24-month follow-up in both treatment groups were numerically higher after root canal treatment. However, the differences were not statistically significant. Intergroup comparisons between the groups found no statistically significant difference in the mean FD values of the first endodontic treatment and retreatment. The differences between the studies may be due to the type of X-ray used, the size of the lesions, and the duration of follow-up.

The PAI scoring system used in clinical evaluations contains subjectivity depending on the observer and is mostly preferred in periapical imaging techniques. However, there are also studies that evaluate PAI scores in panoramic radiographs32. Pertek Hatipoğlu et al.33 reported that clinical decision-making processes and periapical status assessments can show significant differences among physicians, which can lead to variations in diagnosis and follow-up processes. This situation can become more pronounced, especially when using imaging methods such as panoramic radiography. Therefore, in our study, the preference for a mathematical and objective method such as Fractal Analysis in addition to visual indices such as PAI enabled the healing process to be quantitatively expressed with numerical data.

To determine whether the increase in FD values after root canal treatment was due to newly formed trabecular structures or decalcification, periapical conditions were also evaluated using the PAI index. In this study, no positive correlation was found between PAI values and FD. Therefore, although the increase in FD values after root canal treatment was not statistically significant, it may be due to increased trabeculation as a result of healing. Consequently, there may be inconsistencies between PAI and fractal analysis results. PAI is a clinician-based, qualitative scoring system focusing on the macroscopic decrease in radiolucency, which is often clearly visible as bone filling during healing. In contrast, fractal analysis is a mathematical, quantitative tool designed to detect microscopic changes in trabecular complexity. The absence of significant changes in FD despite improvement in PAI suggests the following possibilities: First, panoramic imaging may not have the necessary resolution to capture the fine-grained micro-architectural changes required by fractal analysis. Secondly, although the volume of the lesion has decreased (as reflected in PAI), the complexity of the newly formed trabecular bone may not yet have reached a mathematically different level of maturation compared to its baseline state within the 24-month period. Therefore, the lack of statistically significant FD changes may reflect either true biological stability or insufficient sensitivity of panoramic-based FD analysis to detect subtle trabecular alterations.

There is no consensus in the literature that gender is an effective factor in the success of endodontic treatment. Aydın et al.27 reported that the success of periapical surgery in diabetic patients was not different regardless of sex. A systematic review found that sex and age did not significantly affect lesion healing in the periapical region after the first and second root canal treatments. Similarly, in the present study, no statistically significant difference was found between males and females in the fractal analysis depending on the treatment options. Conversely, Marquis et al.34 reported that the success of endodontic treatment was higher in females than in males. Ling et al.35 showed that the fractal dimensions significantly differed by age and sex. These different results may be due to the population, age, sample size, or inclusion criteria in these studies.

A systematic review highlights the complexity and time-dependent nature of periapical healing, particularly when assessed with CBCT. While CBCT offers superior diagnostic capabilities and high sensitivity, its routine use compared to periapical radiology should be carefully evaluated. Future planned studies should optimize clinical use, minimize unnecessary radiation exposure, and establish standardized CBCT-based follow-up guidelines for accurate assessments36. Despite its advanced capabilities, CBCT imaging can miss small anatomical details due to resolution limitations37. Bollen et al.38 compared FD values measured on both panoramic and periapical radiographs and found lower in panoramic radiographs. Dosdogru and colleagues evaluated FD in patients with and without periodontitis using panoramic radiographs, examined the potential contribution of the FA method to radiographic diagnosis, and investigated a possible correlation between FD and clinical periodontal measurements. According to the findings, there was no statistically significant difference in FD levels between individuals with periodontitis and healthy individuals39. The inherent limitations of panoramic radiography, particularly its lower resolution and higher geometric distortion compared to periapical radiographs or CBCT, may have affected the sensitivity of fractal analysis in this study. Fractal dimension (FD) is largely dependent on the clarity of the trabecular microarchitecture. The relatively low resolution of panoramic images can lead to erasure of bone patterns, masking subtle differences in bone healing between primary and retreatment groups. Consequently, the lack of statistical significance in FD values can be partly attributed to the sensitivity threshold of panoramic imaging. In this retrospective study, panoramic X-rays were preferred because they allowed for 24-month follow-ups, all films were taken under equal conditions, and the radiation dose was minimal(ALARA). In this context, FA in 2D imaging stands out as a non-invasive method in dental radiology to evaluate trabecular bone40. In our study, archival records were limited to panoramic X-rays.

Panoramic radiographs inherently present unequal magnification, heterogeneous spatial resolution, and regional geometric distortion, particularly in posterior regions. These factors may have affected the sensitivity of FD measurements in the present study. Although test-retest results were observed to be between good and excellent, reproducibility alone does not constitute full biological validity of panoramic-based FD analysis.

Another limitation is that in this study, panoramic-based FD analysis could not be validated by comparison with CBCT, standardized periapical radiographs, or histological evaluation.

The chemical composition of root canal filling materials can affect both the interaction of the material with biological tissue and the difficulty of removing it from the canal during retreatment. C-Point is an innovative material for root canal filling that expands in humid conditions, adapting well to the canal structure while enhancing sealing. According to one study, no significant difference in inflammatory response was observed between C-Point and gutta-percha. C-Point’s biocompatibility level is the same as gutta-percha41.

Another limitation of this study is that, due to its retrospective nature, we do not have detailed records of specific disinfection protocols and root canal filling materials, as well as the technical methods used for disinfection and filling of root canals during retreatment. However, the study period preceded the routine clinical use of bioceramic-based sealers in our institution. The lack of statistically significant differences in fractal analysis results among the groups in our study suggests that different materials or procedural variations may not have a highly significant effect on periapical bone healing at 24-month follow-up. However, the findings can provide a basis for future scientific studies. Further studies with larger sample groups are needed in the future. While our retrospective approach naturally presents challenges in fully controlling for all variables in patient selection, it has allowed for the analysis of a relatively large sample size, a significant advantage for studying such a rare treatment.

Conclusion

In this study, no difference in success was found between initial root canal treatment and retreatment. Further studies with larger populations using standardized periapical radiographs or three-dimensional imaging techniques such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) are recommended.

Panoramic-based FD analysis may serve only as a complementary and preliminary quantitative method for evaluating periapical healing. Although the current study has methodological limitations, it is thought that it will provide a basis for future studies.
