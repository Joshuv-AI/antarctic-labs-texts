Early Postoperative Rehabilitation Behavior After Total Joint Arthroplasty: A Theory-Informed Qualitative Study
Authors: Zhonghua Xu, Zhenzhen Du, Ran Chen, Liangliang Wang, Jinhong Gong, Dan Su, Xindie Zhou, Jingjing Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly postoperative rehabilitation is important for recovery after total joint arthroplasty (TJA), but patients’ engagement may be influenced by multiple psychological, cognitive, and social factors. This study explored the facilitators and barriers to early postoperative rehabilitation behavior using the Health Belief Model (HBM) and Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) as interpretive frameworks.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study was conducted with 18 hospitalized patients who underwent total hip arthroplasty (THA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and were interviewed on postoperative days 2–7. Interviews were conducted by an orthopedic clinical pharmacist and a postgraduate student in clinical pharmacy. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis and interpreted through the HBM and SCT frameworks.ResultsTwo overarching categories were identified: facilitators of rehabilitation behavior and barriers to rehabilitation behavior. Facilitators included positive health beliefs, such as perceived functional impairment and recognition of rehabilitation benefits; high self-efficacy and positive outcome expectations; social and environmental support from family members and healthcare professionals, together with positive observational learning; and clear rehabilitation goals with internal behavioral regulation. Barriers included strong perceived barriers, such as postoperative pain, psychological concerns, and other physical discomforts; low self-efficacy and negative outcome expectations; insufficient social support or concerns about lack of support after discharge; and difficulties in behavioral regulation, including pain avoidance and fatigue-related interference.ConclusionIntegrating HBM and SCT provides a complementary framework for understanding early postoperative rehabilitation behavior after TJA by linking health-related perceptions and perceived barriers with self-efficacy, outcome expectations, social and environmental influences, and behavioral regulation. The findings may inform theory-informed support strategies during the early in-hospital rehabilitation phase.

Introduction

Total joint arthroplasty (TJA) is an important treatment for relieving pain and improving joint function and quality of life in patients with end-stage joint disease.1 Early postoperative rehabilitation is a key component in promoting functional recovery, preventing complications, and improving long-term quality of life, and its implementation is closely related to patients’ rehabilitation behavior.2–4 However, during rehabilitation, patients are often affected by multiple factors, including pain, fatigue, psychological burden, and insufficient self-management ability, which may lead to reduced adherence to rehabilitation training and consequently compromise postoperative recovery.5–7 Recent studies have suggested that rehabilitation behavior after TJA is influenced not only by disease- and surgery-related factors, but also by psychosocial factors such as patients’ health-related cognition, rehabilitation expectations, and social support.5 Although total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) may differ in pain trajectories, mobility restrictions, rehabilitation protocols, and recovery expectations, both involve an early transition from surgical treatment to active participation in rehabilitation.8–10 During this period, patients undergoing either procedure must respond to physical and psychological challenges and actively engage in rehabilitation within their clinical and social environments.11 Therefore, examining THA and TKA within a common TJA framework may help identify shared psychological and behavioral processes underlying early postoperative rehabilitation, while procedure-specific clinical differences should also be recognized.

The Health Belief Model (HBM) is mainly used to explain how individuals’ perceptions of disease threat, behavioral benefits, and behavioral barriers influence health behavior, whereas Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) emphasizes that behavior is shaped through the continuous and dynamic interaction among personal cognition (particularly self-efficacy), outcome expectations, and the social/physical environment.12,13 Although HBM and SCT each place emphasis on different aspects of health behavior, they are theoretically complementary. HBM helps explain the cognitive basis underlying patients’ engagement in rehabilitation behavior, whereas SCT is better suited to explaining behavioral engagement and self-regulation during the rehabilitation process. This complementarity has been demonstrated in a study of gynecologic cancer screening.14 Other studies have also shown that combining the two theories in behavioral interventions can simultaneously target individuals’ cognitive beliefs and social support systems, thereby promoting more sustained behavior change.15 Therefore, integrating HBM and SCT may facilitate a more comprehensive understanding of rehabilitation behavior after TJA from the perspectives of cognition, beliefs, social support, and behavioral regulation.

Previous studies have highlighted the importance of pain, psychosocial concerns, social support, self-efficacy, and rehabilitation expectations in recovery and rehabilitation after joint arthroplasty.16,17 However, these factors have often been examined individually or without an integrated theoretical framework, and there remains limited understanding of how multiple cognitive, psychological, and social-environmental factors jointly shape rehabilitation behavior during the early postoperative period.18,19 The HBM primarily helps explain patients’ perceptions of disease consequences, rehabilitation benefits, and perceived barriers, whereas SCT complements this perspective by emphasizing self-efficacy, outcome expectations, social and environmental influences, and behavioral regulation. Integrating these two theoretical perspectives may therefore provide a more comprehensive framework for understanding early postoperative rehabilitation behavior after TJA. Against this background, the present study adopted a qualitative descriptive approach to explore the facilitators and barriers to early postoperative rehabilitation behavior after TJA and to systematically interpret these experiences through an integrated HBM–SCT framework, with the aim of informing the development of theory-informed rehabilitation support strategies. Methods

Study Design

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design and used semi-structured, face-to-face interviews to describe patients’ experiences of rehabilitation behavior after TJA and its influencing factors. The reporting of this study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ)20 to enhance methodological rigor and reporting transparency; details are provided in Supplementary Material 1. This study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2100053191) and approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzhou Second People’s Hospital (approval number: [2022]KY008-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Audio recordings were deleted in accordance with regulations after transcription had been completed, and all study data were de-identified and stored securely.

Participants and Interviewers

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants from the joint surgery department of a tertiary hospital. Eligible participants were patients who had undergone THA or TKA. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) having undergone THA or TKA for a relevant underlying condition; (3) being 2–7 days postoperatively, representing the early in-hospital rehabilitation phase; (4) being conscious and able to understand the interview questions and communicate coherently; and (5) willingness to participate and provision of written informed consent. No predefined upper age limit was applied.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) cognitive impairment or mental illness that, based on clinical judgment, precluded understanding of the study information, valid informed consent, comprehension of the interview questions, or coherent communication; (2) severe medical or neuromuscular conditions that substantially affected postoperative rehabilitation; and (3) severe postoperative complications resulting in a rehabilitation course that deviated substantially from the usual early postoperative pathway. Patients with different surgical types and underlying conditions were purposively included to capture diverse early postoperative rehabilitation experiences rather than to assume clinical homogeneity across subgroups.

Interviews were conducted from December 2025 to January 2026 by two female researchers trained in qualitative research: an orthopedic clinical pharmacist and a postgraduate student in clinical pharmacy. Neither interviewer was directly responsible for the participants’ surgical treatment or routine clinical care. Before the interviews, the researchers reviewed the medical records and communicated with patients at the bedside to understand their general clinical condition. Participants were informed of the study purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, data confidentiality, and their right to withdraw without affecting their clinical care. Written informed consent was obtained before the interviews.

A total of 20 patients initially expressed willingness to participate. Two did not complete the interview because of communication difficulties, leaving 18 participants in the final sample. Interview Guide and Data Collection

During postoperative days 2–7, routine rehabilitation at the study center included early mobilization and rehabilitation exercises under the guidance of the clinical team. Rehabilitation activities were individualized according to surgical type, clinical condition, pain, and weight-bearing requirements, with analgesic treatment and rehabilitation guidance provided according to routine clinical practice.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted using an interview guide developed based on the study objectives, relevant literature, and clinical practice and informed by key constructs of the HBM and SCT. The guide covered patients’ perceptions and attitudes toward postoperative rehabilitation, experiences and difficulties during rehabilitation, individual and environmental factors influencing rehabilitation behavior, and perceived support from healthcare professionals and family members. The guide was pilot-tested and refined before the formal interviews, with additional probing based on participants’ responses. The interview guide is provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Interviews were conducted in a quiet and undisturbed environment and lasted approximately 20–30 minutes. All interviews were audio-recorded, with field notes documenting participants’ facial expressions, tone of voice, and relevant contextual information. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 hours, and transcripts were checked against the recordings for accuracy. Participants were re-contacted when clarification of ambiguous expressions was necessary. Data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently until data saturation was reached.

Data Processing and Analysis

NVivo 12 software was used to manage, code, and organize the interview data. The data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis approach,21 and the findings were further interpreted in light of the Health Belief Model (HBM) and Social Cognitive Theory (SCT). First, the researchers repeatedly read the verbatim transcripts to familiarize themselves with the data and generate initial codes. Second, the codes were compared and grouped to form preliminary themes. Subsequently, during the ongoing revision and refinement of the themes, key constructs of the HBM and SCT were used to further classify and interpret the thematic content and its relationship to rehabilitation behavior. Finally, the themes were reviewed, defined, and named, and the analytical report was completed.

To enhance the credibility of the analysis, two researchers independently coded the first five interview transcripts and developed a preliminary coding framework through comparison and discussion. The remaining transcripts were primarily coded by one researcher and reviewed by the other using the established framework. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with the broader research team. No numerical intercoder agreement coefficient was calculated; coding consistency was addressed through comparison, discussion, and consensus review. Audio recordings, verbatim transcripts, and field notes were triangulated to strengthen the trustworthiness of the interpretation.

Positionality and Reflexivity

Both interviewers had clinical pharmacy backgrounds, which may have increased their sensitivity to medication-related concerns, pain experiences, and rehabilitation difficulties. To enhance reflexivity and reduce the influence of a single professional perspective, the interview guide was applied consistently, probing was guided by participants’ responses, and coding and interpretation were reviewed through cross-checking and team discussion.

Results

Based on interview data from 18 patients after TJA, thematic analysis identified two overarching categories: facilitators of rehabilitation behavior and barriers to rehabilitation behavior. Further analysis informed by key constructs of the HBM and SCT showed that these themes mainly involved health beliefs, self-efficacy, outcome expectations, social support, observational learning, and behavioral regulation. Specifically, facilitators included positive health beliefs, high self-efficacy and positive outcome expectations, strong social and environmental support, as well as clear rehabilitation goals and behavioral regulation ability. Barriers included strong perceived barriers, low self-efficacy and negative outcome expectations, insufficient social support, and difficulties in behavioral regulation. Facilitators of Rehabilitation Behavior

Positive Health Beliefs

Most patients were aware that their preoperative joint disease had already had a substantial impact on their daily activities and quality of life, and they recognized the importance of postoperative rehabilitation training for functional recovery. Some participants reported that preoperative pain, activity limitations, and gait instability had continuously affected their normal lives. For example, one participant said, “If I want to go out and have fun, I can’t walk far, and I can’t go hiking” (P1, 66F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6), while another noted, “I tend to fall easily when walking” (P14, 73F, Primary Hip Arthropathy, THA, POD 4). At the same time, many patients believed that postoperative exercise would help improve joint mobility and physical recovery, for example by “promoting blood circulation” (P11, 60M, Knee OA, TKA, POD 5), “preventing joint stiffness” (P1, 66F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6), and making them feel that “moving helps me become more flexible” (P18, 75M, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 4). These findings suggest that patients’ firsthand perceptions of the consequences of the disease, together with their recognition of the benefits of rehabilitation, constitute an important cognitive foundation for engaging in rehabilitation training, mainly reflecting the HBM constructs of perceived severity and perceived benefits.

High Self-Efficacy and Positive Outcome Expectations

Some patients expressed strong confidence in their recovery during the interviews and believed that they could overcome difficulties and persist with training. For example, one participant stated, “With my own willpower, if I keep going, I’ll definitely recover faster” (P1, 66F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6). Another said, “I do have the confidence and the determination. I’ve spent so much money on this, so in the end I must recover well” (P3, 69F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 2). In addition, most patients held relatively positive expectations regarding the surgical outcome and postoperative recovery, believing that treatment and exercise would improve their function. As one participant noted, “I saw that other people could walk after having it done, so I thought surgery would definitely make things better” (P13, 54F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 5), while another commented, “The surgery has actually been quite good as long as it’s better than before the operation” (P10, 76F, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 3). These findings suggest that patients’ confidence in their own ability to recover and their positive expectations regarding recovery outcomes help strengthen their willingness to engage in rehabilitation and sustain their efforts, mainly corresponding to the SCT constructs of self-efficacy and outcome expectations.

Strong Social and Environmental Support

Patients’ engagement in early rehabilitation was also supported by family members and healthcare professionals. Family members’ encouragement, companionship, and practical assistance provided both emotional and behavioral support. For example, one participant said, “My son urges me to exercise. They all support me in doing the exercises” (P8, 75M, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 3). Another noted, “My grandson studies sports medicine, and since he’s back for the winter break, he can teach me a bit more” (P5, 65F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 5). At the same time, reminders, encouragement, and specific guidance from healthcare professionals also enhanced patients’ engagement in rehabilitation training. As one participant reported, “The doctor comes over and massages the sole of my foot, and also tells me to move my foot and do some exercises” (P12, 69F, Primary Hip Arthropathy, THA, POD 3). Others stated, “The doctors and the nursing assistants all come to remind me” (P16, 68M, Femoral Fracture Nonunion, THA, POD 3), and “The doctors are really good, and every nurse is good. They keep coming to encourage me, and I’m willing to cooperate” (P2, 64F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6). These reminders and encouragement from healthcare professionals also served as “cues to action” within the HBM framework, prompting patients to engage in rehabilitation activities. In addition, some patients gained confidence from the successful experiences of others. For instance, one participant said, “A fellow villager of mine also had surgery here, so I came to have it done too” (P4, 67F, Bilateral Knee OA, TKA, POD 5), while another commented, “I had seen someone who had undergone a similar surgery seven or eight years ago and had no major problems afterward, so that’s why I finally dared to do it now” (P13, 54F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 5). These findings suggest that family support, healthcare professional support, and others’ rehabilitation experiences were commonly described as facilitating early rehabilitation engagement, mainly reflecting the SCT constructs of social support, environmental support, and observational learning.

Clear Rehabilitation Goals and Internal Behavioral Regulation

Some patients were able to set relatively clear rehabilitation goals centered on functional recovery, independence in daily living, or returning to their previous social roles. For example, one participant said, “Of course I want to recover faster, and I still want to go out and have fun” (P1, 66F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6). Another focused on the time needed to return to work and physical labor: “I want to know how long it will take before I can work and exert myself again. How long will it take to recover well enough to go back to work?” (P6, 62M, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 7). Another participant stated, “My recovery goal is simply to be able to walk normally again” (P13, 54F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 5). Guided by these goals, some patients were also able to adjust the pace of training according to their physical condition. For example, one participant said, “Sometimes when it hurts, I just walk a little, rest for a while, and then keep going” (P1, 66F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6). Another noted, “For example, with squats, when I first started, doing 70 made me cry out in pain. But today I did 102. It’s getting better and better” (P13, 54F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 5). These findings suggest that clear recovery goals and a certain degree of self-regulation may support patients’ engagement in early rehabilitation, mainly reflecting the SCT constructs of goal setting and behavioral regulation.

Barriers to Rehabilitation Behavior

Strong Perceived Barriers

During early postoperative rehabilitation, participants commonly described pain, psychological concerns, and other physical discomforts as barriers to rehabilitation training. Among these, pain was the most frequently mentioned barrier. Some participants stated directly that “it really hurts too much” (P1, 66F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6), while others avoided activity because they were afraid of pain, for example: “Once I go home, I’ll be afraid of the pain” (P2, 64F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6), and “If it’s difficult and painful, then I just don’t want to do it” (P7, 75F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6). In addition to pain, some patients also expressed concerns about surgical safety, recovery outcomes, and functional prognosis. For example, one participant said, “Well, at my age, of course I’m a bit scared. I’m afraid the postoperative reaction will be severe” (P15, 77F, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 4). Another remarked, “I am worried. Surgery is risky. I’d almost rather not live than go through something dangerous like that” (P10, 76F, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 3). Others worried that “I’m afraid my legs won’t be the same length after surgery and I’m also afraid the surgery won’t be successful” (P17, 35M, Right Hip OA, THA, POD 3), or “I’m worried that if it hurts and I can’t walk, then what should I do? I’m also afraid the surgery won’t work” (P9, 62F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 4). In addition, symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, and fatigue also reduced patients’ willingness to participate in training. For example, one participant said, “I feel nauseous now, so I don’t want to do it” (P3, 69F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 2), while another said, “I vomit even after eating a little or drinking some water. I eat a little and then vomit a little” (P9, 62F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 4). Another noted, “I just feel uncomfortable when eating and some of the medicines taste awful. I don’t even want to take them” (P12, 69F, Primary Hip Arthropathy, THA, POD 3). These findings indicate that participants perceived postoperative pain, psychological concerns, and physical discomfort as important barriers to engagement in early rehabilitation, mainly reflecting the HBM construct of perceived barriers.

Low Self-Efficacy and Negative Outcome Expectations

Some patients lacked confidence in their ability to persist with rehabilitation training during the postoperative recovery process, showing a clear sense of helplessness or withdrawal. For example, one participant admitted, “I just don’t feel sure about it. Mainly because I haven’t exercised this leg for a long time” (P18, 75M, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 4), while another stated directly, “I just simply don’t want to do it” (P11, 60M, Knee OA, TKA, POD 5). At the same time, some patients held negative expectations regarding rehabilitation outcomes, worrying that the surgical effect might be unsatisfactory or that postoperative complications might occur, which in turn reduced their enthusiasm for rehabilitation. For example, one participant said, “I’m worried that after this operation, the result might not even be as good as the first one, and that would be troublesome” (P16, 68M, Femoral Fracture Nonunion, THA, POD 3). Another expressed concern that “I’m afraid it might cause inflammation, and then things would go badly, and the surgery would have been for nothing” (P9, 62F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 4). Such lack of self-confidence and pessimistic expectations regarding recovery outcomes may weaken patients’ willingness and persistence in actively participating in rehabilitation training, mainly corresponding to the SCT constructs of low self-efficacy and negative outcome expectations.

Insufficient Social Support or Concerns About Lack of Support

Some patients reported concerns, when anticipating their post-discharge rehabilitation, about the lack of ongoing companionship, supervision, and assistance. Such concerns were particularly evident among patients living alone or those with limited family caregiving resources. For example, one participant said, “Because there’s no one at home—I’m by myself” (P6, 62M, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 7). Another worried, “No one will remind me to do the exercises. I was saying that going home will actually be troublesome. Everyone will be at work, and I’ll be home alone” (P9, 62F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 4). Another stated, “Once I go home, I’ll still have to rely on myself. I’m worried that because of my own limitations, I won’t recover well” (P18, 75M, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 4). In addition, negative experiences reported by others could also weaken patients’ confidence in rehabilitation. For example, one participant said, “When the patient next to me said that, I got scared. I was just telling them both that if I had known earlier, I wouldn’t have done it” (P9, 62F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 4). Actual or anticipated lack of support, as well as negative external information, may interfere with patients’ engagement in early rehabilitation, mainly reflecting the effects of insufficient environmental support and negative observational learning in SCT.

Difficulties in Behavioral Regulation

Although some patients showed a certain willingness to engage in rehabilitation, they found it difficult to regulate and carry out their planned rehabilitation activities in actual practice. This was mainly manifested in reduced activity because of pain, fatigue, or concerns about wound healing. For example, one participant said, “I can bend my leg, but it hurts all the time when I do” (P7, 75F, Knee OA, TKA, POD 6). Another said, “I still don’t dare move too much. I just had surgery, so of course I’m afraid to move, because I’m worried it might affect the healing of the incision” (P12, 69F, Primary Hip Arthropathy, THA, POD 3). In addition, fatigue and lack of physical strength were also common problems. One participant said, “I don’t want to get down and walk because I’m tired from walking” (P10, 76F, Femoral Neck Fracture, THA, POD 3). Another noted, “I feel tired. I want to walk, but sometimes I just can’t keep going, so I come back and rest for a while” (P14, 73F, Primary Hip Arthropathy, THA, POD 4). These findings suggest that participants perceived pain and fatigue as factors that interfered with their ability to regulate and carry out planned rehabilitation activities during the early postoperative period.

Discussion

Using the HBM and SCT as interpretive frameworks, this study qualitatively examined the facilitators and barriers to rehabilitation behavior among patients after TJA. The findings showed that rehabilitation behavior was not determined by a single factor, but rather by the combined influence of health beliefs, self-efficacy, outcome expectations, social and environmental support, and behavioral regulation. Previous studies have also shown that recovery after TJA is a complex and individualized process, during which patients often face multiple challenges related to physical functioning, cognition, emotions, and social adaptation.22,23 The present findings further suggest that HBM and SCT are complementary in explaining rehabilitation behavior: the former helps clarify the cognitive basis underlying rehabilitation behavior, whereas the latter helps explain how patients engage in and regulate rehabilitation behavior during the early postoperative period.

This study found that patients’ expectations for functional improvement coexisted with the practical difficulties encountered during rehabilitation. In a qualitative study on recovery expectations among patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty, Mehta et al24 reported that patients generally hoped to regain mobility after surgery, but also required realistic and clearly defined rehabilitation goals in order to avoid psychological disappointment when recovery progressed more slowly than expected. In the present study, some patients actively set relatively clear rehabilitation goals, such as “wanting to go out and have fun” or “wanting to walk normally,” which reflects the potential role of goal setting in supporting early rehabilitation engagement.25 Theoretically, this process reflects the behavioral regulation mechanism proposed by SCT and is also related to perceived severity in the HBM, in that patients gradually formed concrete rehabilitation aspirations precisely because they had directly experienced the impact of preoperative functional limitations on daily life. At the same time, participants in this study frequently described postoperative pain, physical discomfort, and concerns about recovery outcomes as barriers to engagement in early rehabilitation. These findings should be interpreted as patient-reported experiences rather than evidence of a quantitative or causal association between these factors and rehabilitation behavior. Previous systematic reviews have shown that pain not only affects functional recovery after joint arthroplasty, but is also associated with an increased risk of chronic postoperative pain.26 Likewise, a meta-analysis of kinesiophobia after total knee arthroplasty suggested that pain, functional limitation, and insufficient support may exacerbate patients’ avoidance tendencies and thereby hinder rehabilitation participation.27 Theory-informed, systematic rehabilitation support, such as goal setting, observational learning, and behavioral feedback, has also been shown to improve postoperative self-efficacy, functional recovery, and adherence to rehabilitation exercise.23 These findings suggest that, in clinical practice, it is important not only to help patients develop appropriate rehabilitation-related understanding and realistic recovery goals, but also to prioritize pain control and symptom management in order to reduce the negative impact of perceived barriers on rehabilitation behavior.

In addition, social support from family members and healthcare professionals played an important role in patients’ rehabilitation process. Tornese et al28 reported that caregiving resources and postoperative support conditions influence the recovery trajectory, and that caregiver involvement is meaningful in shortening hospital stay. In a study on exercise adherence after total knee arthroplasty, Bakaa et al25 further suggested that family support, professional guidance, clear goals, and positive feedback can all promote persistence with training, whereas practical difficulties, pain, and lack of supervision are common barriers. In the present study, participants’ comments such as “When the doctor comes to remind me, I’m willing to cooperate” also suggest that reminders and support from healthcare professionals not only enhance patients’ motivation to train, but may also help translate rehabilitation intentions into early postoperative actions. From a theoretical perspective, this reflects both the social and environmental support emphasized in SCT and the “cues to action” construct in HBM. At the same time, this study also found that self-efficacy, social support, and positive outcome expectations were important factors promoting rehabilitation behavior. Evidence from TJA populations likewise supports this view. A systematic review by Sergooris et al18 showed that factors influencing activity and social participation after total hip arthroplasty are not limited to the surgery itself, but also include contextual factors and patients’ subjective experiences. Li et al29 further found a chain-mediating relationship among disease knowledge, social support, anxiety, and self-efficacy, with self-efficacy serving as an important psychological factor influencing early rehabilitation engagement. Conversely, when patients lack confidence, hold negative expectations regarding recovery outcomes, or worry about insufficient support after discharge, they are more likely to exhibit withdrawal and avoidance behaviors.30 These findings suggest that, in clinical practice, greater attention should be paid to enhancing patients’ self-efficacy and positive expectations regarding rehabilitation outcomes through individualized guidance, positive feedback, and mobilization of support systems.

Based on these findings, early postoperative rehabilitation support could be further tailored to address specific psychological and behavioral factors. For example, setting specific, achievable, and stage‑appropriate rehabilitation goals combined with timely positive feedback may help build patients’ confidence and self‑efficacy.31 Strengthening symptom management and providing targeted rehabilitation education regarding pain, physical discomfort, and concerns about wound or exercise safety may reduce patients’ perceived barriers. Meanwhile, involving family members in rehabilitation education and training—for instance, inviting family members or designated caregivers to participate in bedside physical therapy or rehabilitation sessions before discharge to provide them with behavioral guidance and supervision skills—as well as reinforcing reminders, encouragement, and feedback from healthcare professionals, could strengthen social support and cues to action.32 Additionally, structured goal tracking (eg, daily or weekly progress reviews) could help patients visualize their improvements and maintain motivation, addressing the behavioral regulation difficulties reported by participants. The medication-related concerns and symptom burden reported by participants, including nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, and unpleasant medication taste, also suggest a potential role for clinical pharmacists within multidisciplinary rehabilitation care. Clinical pharmacists may contribute through medication education, symptom assessment and management, and optimization of postoperative pharmacotherapy to support patients’ early rehabilitation engagement.33,34 These strategies hold promise for promoting early rehabilitation engagement, although their actual effectiveness requires further validation in future intervention studies.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was drawn from a single medical center; therefore, caution is needed when generalizing the findings to patients from different regions, cultural backgrounds, or healthcare settings. Second, the study included both THA and TKA patients with heterogeneous underlying conditions. The small qualitative sample was not designed for subgroup comparisons; therefore, potential procedure- or etiology-specific differences cannot be excluded and warrant further study in larger, more clinically homogeneous samples. Third, participants ranged from 35 to 77 years, and the sample was insufficient for formal age-stratified analysis. Potential age-related differences in rehabilitation beliefs and support needs therefore cannot be excluded. Fourth, the interviews were conducted during the early postoperative period (2–7 days after surgery) and therefore primarily reflected patients’ early in-hospital rehabilitation experiences. The findings cannot directly represent medium- or long-term rehabilitation behavior after discharge. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how rehabilitation beliefs and behaviors evolve over time. Fifth, socioeconomic status, living arrangements, standardized mobility measures, and preoperative quality of life were not systematically assessed, which may have limited the contextual interpretation of patients’ rehabilitation experiences. Future studies could expand sample sources, include patients with different disease types and family support backgrounds, adopt longitudinal designs, and incorporate standardized measures of socioeconomic status and preoperative quality of life. Such efforts would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamic evolution of rehabilitation behavior after TJA and further clarify the roles of these factors within the HBM and SCT frameworks.

Conclusion

Drawing on the HBM and SCT, this study identified multiple factors influencing patients’ early postoperative rehabilitation behavior after TJA. Facilitators included positive health beliefs, high self-efficacy, positive outcome expectations, social and environmental support, and effective behavioral regulation, whereas barriers included perceived rehabilitation barriers, low self-efficacy, negative outcome expectations, insufficient support, and difficulties in behavioral regulation. The integration of HBM and SCT provides a complementary framework for understanding early postoperative rehabilitation behavior after TJA. Given the single-center setting and the early postoperative interview window, future multicenter and longitudinal studies are needed to examine how rehabilitation behavior evolves over time and across different clinical settings.
