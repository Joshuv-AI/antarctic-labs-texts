Abstract
. This paper investigates the interplay between local and global equivalences on noncommutative polynomials, the elements of the free algebra. When the latter are viewed as functions in several matrix variables, a local equivalence of noncommutative polynomials refers to their values sharing a common feature point-wise on matrix tuples of all dimensions, such as rank-equivalence (values have the same ranks), isospectrality (values have the same spectrum), and pointwise similarity (values are similar). On the other hand, a global equivalence refers to a ring-theoretic relation within the free algebra, such as stable association or (elementary) intertwinedness. This paper identifies the most ubiquitous pairs of local and global equivalences. Namely, rank-equivalence coincides with stable association, isospectrality coincides with both intertwinedness and transitive closure of elementary intertwinedness, and pointwise similarity coincides with equality. Using these characterizations, further results on spectral radii and norms of values of noncommutative polynomials are derived.

Another natural area of applications of noncommutative functions is free probability. In fact, Voiculescu has independently discovered noncommutative functions

The goal of this paper is to study certain relations between polynomials described in terms of their values on

is algebraically closed of characteristic 0. We say that noncommutative polynomials

(1)

(2)

{\underline{X}}\in\mathcal{M}^{n}

(3)

{\underline{X}}\in\mathcal{M}^{n}

(4)

(
{\mathbbm{k}}=\mathbb{C}
)
pointwise the same norm

Note that the first condition can be thought of as the condition on classical analytic functions to have the same zeroes of the same order. The first condition is also related to the more general study of rank-stability of noncommutative polynomial equations
[
7
,
17
]
.
The second condition is, in a sense, generic similarity. The second condition first appeared in
[
48
]
, where a noncommutative version of Bertini’s theorem from algebraic geometry was obtained. The third condition is, of course, stronger than the second one. In fact, such questions arise from time to time in noncommutative analysis. Namely, given two elements of an operator algebra and a collection of representations that separate points, can we deduce that the two elements are similar or unitarily equivalent in the algebra, if their images under each one of our representations are similar or unitarily equivalent, respectively? Lastly, the last condition is metric. It is analogous to two analytic functions having the same modulus. It is well known that in this case, the two analytic functions differ by a multiplication by a unimodular scalar. Surprisingly, this is also true for noncommutative polynomials, as we will see.

As we have seen, Taylor introduced noncommutative functions to study a notion of joint spectrum and functional calculus for arbitrary tuples of operators on a Banach/Hilbert space. Taylor’s definition of the joint spectrum has some drawbacks, however. Firstly, it deals with categories of Fréchet modules over certain topological algebras, and Taylor builds a sophisticated cohomological machinery to deal with these categories
[
42
]
. Secondly, both in the case of commuting operators and the case of an arbitrary tuple of matrices, the spectrum and the functional calculus do not work as desired (see also
[
31
]
). A more naive approach is to study the singularity loci of pencils in order to understand the “joint spectrum”. Namely, one can think of the spectrum of an operator
T
as the singularities in the complex plane of the analytic operator-valued function
z\mapsto(zI-T)^{-1}
. Homogenizing we get the function
(zI-wT)^{-1}
. More generally, the projective spectrum of a tuple of operators was considered by Yang and his collaborators
[
13
,
50
]
. From the noncommutative analysis perspective, a natural notion of the joint spectrum of a tuple of matrices

is the singularity set of the pencil
I-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}A_{j}
or its homogenization
x_{0}I-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}A_{j}
. The latter relates to the noncommutative projective space that appeared first in the work of Voiculescu
[
46
]
and was recently used to solve a joint similarity problem for tuples of matrices by Derksen, Klep, Makam, and the second author
[
16
]
. The former relates to the domains of definition of noncommutative rational functions and was studied in
[
29
]
. More recently, realizations with operator coefficients were introduced by Augat, Martin, and the first author in
[
5

. Therefore, understanding the local behaviour of noncommutative polynomials and pencils on

is a natural first step in the study of joint spectra of arbitrary tuples of operators.

We say that two polynomials

(2)

3.3.
Stable association of powers

If
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are rank-equivalent, it is not necessarily true that their powers are likewise rank-equivalent (that is, while
f
and
g
may always have the same number of Jordan blocks at 0, these blocks might not have the same sizes).
For example, let
f=xyxy+xy+x
and
g=xy^{2}x+xy+x
. Then
f
and
g
are stably associated, and therefore rank-equivalent, by
f(yx+1)=(xy+1)g
and Theorem
2.1
.
On the other hand,
f^{2}
and
g^{2}
are not rank-equivalent, and thus also not stably associated by Theorem
3.4
. Indeed, denote

X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then
f(X,Y)^{2}\neq 0
and
g(X,Y)^{2}=0
.

On the other hand, the converse of the statement holds (and is useful in Section
5
below).

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
. If
f^{k}
and
g^{k}
are stably associated for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
f
and
g
are stably associated.

Proof.

Since
f^{k}
and
g^{k}
are stably associated, there is an isomorphism of left
R
-modules
\phi:R/Rf^{k}\to R/Rg^{k}
. Consider the chain of submodules

R/Rf^{k}\supset Rf/Rf^{k}\supset\cdots\supset Rf^{k-1}/Rf^{k}\supset\{0\},

where the quotient of any two consecutive terms is isomorphic to
R/Rf
. The image of this chain in
R/Rg^{k}
,

\phi(R/Rf^{k})\supset\phi(Rf/Rf^{k})\supset\cdots\supset\phi(Rf^{k-1}/Rf^{k})\supset\{0\},

corresponds to a factorization
g^{k}=f_{1}\cdots f_{k}
where each
f_{j}
is stably associated to
f
by
[
14
, Section 3.2]
. If two polynomials are stably associated, then their irreducible factors can be grouped into stably associated pairs. Thus,
g,f_{1},\dots,f_{k},f
all have the same irreducible factors up to stable association (and ordering in a complete factorization). Let
\ell
be the highest common left factor of
g
and
f_{1}
. If
g
and
f_{1}
are scalar multiples of
\ell
, then
g
and
f
are stably associated, as desired.

Suppose that
g=\ell g^{\prime}
and
f_{1}=\ell f_{1}^{\prime}
for nonconstant
g^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime}\in R
. Let
\ell^{\prime}
be an irreducible left factor of
g^{\prime}
, and write
g^{\prime}=\ell^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}
. Then
\ell^{\prime}
is stably associated to an irreducible factor
\ell^{\prime\prime}
of
f_{1}^{\prime}
, and write
f_{1}^{\prime}=a\ell^{\prime\prime}b
. The relation

\ell^{\prime}\cdot(g^{\prime\prime}g^{k-1})=\ell^{-1}g^{k}=\ell^{-1}f_{1}\cdots f_{k}=a\ell^{\prime\prime}\cdot(bf_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}\cdots f_{k})

shows that
\ell^{\prime}R\cap(a\ell^{\prime\prime})R\neq\{0\}
. Since
\ell^{\prime}
and
\ell^{\prime\prime}
are stably associated,
[
14
, Corollary 4.3.4 and Theorem 4.2.3(a)]
implies that
a\ell^{\prime\prime}\in\ell^{\prime}R
. Thus,
\ell\ell^{\prime}
is a common left factor of
g
and
f_{1}
, contradicting the choice of
\ell
.
∎

4.
Isospectrality

We say that
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are
intertwined
if there exists a nonzero

(called an intertwiner) such that
fa=ag
. By
[

, Corollary 4.4]
, two polynomials are isospectral if and only if they are intertwined. In this section, we revisit and strengthen this result, provide a new characterization of isospectrality in terms of elementary intertwinedness, and derive some consequences of isospectrality that are used in the next section.

4.1.
Elementary intertwinedness and isospectrality

Let
\lambda\in{\mathbbm{k}}
and
a,b\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
. We say that noncommutative polynomials
\lambda+ab
and
\lambda+ba
are
elementary intertwined
. Note that
(\lambda+ab)a=a(\lambda+ba)
, so elementary intertwinedness implies intertwinedness.

Theorem 4.1
.

The following are equivalent for
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
:

(1)

for infinitely many
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is a Zariski dense set
\mathcal{D}_{k}\subseteq\opm_{k}({\mathbbm{k}})^{n}
such that
f({\underline{X}})
and
g({\underline{X}})
share an eigenvalue for every
{\underline{X}}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
;

(2)

f
and
g
are isospectral;

(3)

f
and
g
are intertwined;

(4)

there exist
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell}\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
with
f_{1}=f
and
f_{\ell}=g
such that
f_{i}
and
f_{i+1}
are elementary intertwined for all
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
.

Proof.

The equivalence (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii) is
[
48
, Corollary 4.4]
.
The implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) is trivial, and the implication (iv)
\Rightarrow
(ii) follows by (iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) and transitivity of isospectrality.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv): Let nonzero
a\in R
be such that
fa=ag
.
We prove the statement by induction on the number of irreducible factors of
a
.

If
a
is irreducible, then
E(a)={\mathbbm{k}}
by Theorem
2.2
(b). Therefore
f=\lambda+ab
and
g=\lambda+ba
for some
\lambda\in{\mathbbm{k}}
and
b\in R
.

Now suppose that
a
is not irreducible. Note that
g
represents an element of
E(a)
. Since
E(a)
is finite-dimensional by Theorem
2.2
(a), there exists
\lambda\in{\mathbbm{k}}
such that
g-\lambda
is a zero divisor in
E(a)
. Thus there exists
c\in R\setminus Ra
such that
ac\in Ra
and
(g-\lambda)c\in Ra
.
If
1\in R(g-\lambda)+Ra
, then

c\in R(g-\lambda)c+Rac\subseteq Ra
, a contradiction. Therefore,
R(g-\lambda)+Ra
is a proper left ideal in
R
. Since
0\neq(f-\lambda)a=a(g-\lambda)\in R(g-\lambda)\cap Ra
, it follows by
[
14
, Theorem 2.3.7]
that
R(g-\lambda)+Ra=Ra_{1}
for some nonconstant
a_{1}\in R
. Therefore
g-\lambda=ba_{1}
and
a=\tilde{a}a_{1}
for some
b,\tilde{a}\in R
. Let
\tilde{g}=\lambda+a_{1}b
. Then
g
and
\tilde{g}
are elementary intertwined, and

f\tilde{a}a_{1}=fa=ag=\tilde{a}a_{1}(\lambda+ba_{1})=\tilde{a}(\lambda+a_{1}b)a_{1}=\tilde{a}\tilde{g}a_{1},

so
f\tilde{a}=\tilde{a}\tilde{g}
. Since
\tilde{a}
has less irreducible factors than
a
, there is a sequence of elementary intertwined polynomials between
f
and
\tilde{g}
by the induction hypothesis. Hence, there is a sequence of elementary intertwined polynomials between
f
and
g
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): The implication is trivial if either
f
or
g
is constant. Thus, assume
f,g\in R\setminus{\mathbbm{k}}
.
Let
\mathcal{O}_{k}
denote the set of
{\underline{X}}\in\opm_{k}({\mathbbm{k}})^{n}
such that
f({\underline{X}})
and
g({\underline{X}})
each have
n
pairwise distinct eigenvalues. For all large enough
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{O}_{k}
is a nonempty Zariski open subset of
\opm_{k}({\mathbbm{k}})^{n}
by
[
12
, Corollary 2.10]
. By the assumption,
\mathcal{D}_{k}\cap\mathcal{O}_{k}
is Zariski dense in
\opm_{k}({\mathbbm{k}})^{n}
for infinitely many
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For such
k
and
{\underline{X}}\in\mathcal{D}_{k}\cap\mathcal{O}_{k}
, the characteristic polynomials of
f({\underline{X}})
and
g({\underline{X}})
each have pairwise distinct roots, and share a root. Let
\underline{\Omega}
denote the
n
-tuple of
k\times k
generic matrices with independent indeterminate entries,
\operatorname{UD}_{k}
the universal division
{\mathbbm{k}}
-algebra generated by
\underline{\Omega}

[
36
, Section 3.2]
, and
\mathcal{Z}
the center of
\operatorname{UD}_{k}
. Consider monic polynomials
\chi_{f}=\det(tI-f(\underline{\Omega}))
and
\chi_{g}=\det(tI-g(\underline{\Omega}))
in
\mathcal{Z}[t]
. Since
\mathcal{O}_{k}
is dense in
\opm_{k}({\mathbbm{k}})^{n}
, the polynomials
\chi_{f}
and
\chi_{g}
each have pairwise distinct roots in
\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
, and are consequently minimal polynomials of
f(\underline{\Omega})
and
g(\underline{\Omega})
. Thus,
\chi_{f}
and
\chi_{g}
are irreducible over
\mathcal{Z}
since
\mathcal{Z}[t]/(\chi_{f})
and
\mathcal{Z}[t]/(\chi_{g})
are isomorphic to the
\mathcal{Z}
-subfields in
\operatorname{UD}_{k}
generated by
f(\underline{\Omega})
and
g(\underline{\Omega})
, respectively. Since
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is dense in
\opm_{k}({\mathbbm{k}})^{n}
, the polynomials
\chi_{f}
and
\chi_{g}
share a root in
\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
. By the irreducibility,

. Thus, the spectra of
f({\underline{X}})
and
g({\underline{X}})
coincide for all
{\underline{X}}\in\opm_{k}({\mathbbm{k}})^{n}

and

are isospectral.
∎

Theorem
4.1
in particular shows that intertwinedness coincides with the transitive closure of elementary intertwinedness (for an analogous phenomenon in symbolic dynamics, see elementary and strong shift equivalence of integer matrices
[
30
, Section 7.2]
).
Characterization of isospectrality in terms of elementary intertwinedness allows one (at least in principle) to construct the isospectrality equivalency class of a noncommutative polynomial (see Example
4.4
).
Item (i) of Theorem
4.1
is used later in Section
6
to investigate the pointwise norm equality.
For homogeneous polynomials, isospectrality implies elementary intertwinedness, as follows.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
be homogeneous. Then
f,g
are isospectral if and only if
f=ab
,
g=ba
for some homogeneous
a,b\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
.

Proof.

If
f,g
are isospectral, then
fa=ag
for some nonzero
a\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
by Theorem
4.1
. Since
f
and
g
are homogeneous, we may take
a
homogeneous (more precisely, we can replace the initial
a
with any of its nonzero homogeneous components). We distinguish two cases.
If
\deg a\leq\deg f
, then the uniqueness of factorization for homogeneous polynomials as in Proposition
2.3
applied to
fa=ag
shows that
f=ab
for some
b\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
, and then
g=ba
.
If
\deg a>\deg f
, then Proposition
2.3
implies
a=fa^{\prime}
for some
a^{\prime}\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
, and consequently
fa^{\prime}=a^{\prime}g
. Continuing in this fashion, we eventually arrive at the first case.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

An intertwiner of
f
and
g
(if it exists) of minimal degree is unique up to scaling, and all other intertwiners are its left multiples by polynomials that commute with
f
. Indeed, let
M=\{a\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}\colon fa=ag\}
, and let
\mathcal{C}
be the centralizer of
f
in
{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
. By Bergman’s centralizer theorem
[
8
]
, the
{\mathbbm{k}}
-algebra
\mathcal{C}
is isomorphic to the univariate polynomial ring over
{\mathbbm{k}}
; furthermore, the centralizer
\widetilde{C}
of
f
in the universal skew field of fractions of
f
equals the field of fractions of
\mathcal{C}
. Let us view
M
as a left
\mathcal{C}
-module. Clearly,
M
is torsion-free; since
\mathcal{C}
is a principal ideal domain,
M
is a free
\mathcal{C}
-module. If
0\neq a,b\in M
, then
fba^{-1}=bga^{-1}=ba^{-1}f
, and so
ba^{-1}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
. Thus, the vector space
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}\otimes_{\mathcal{C}}M
is one-dimensional, and so

is free of rank 1. Hence,
M=\mathcal{C}\cdot a
for an intertwiner

of minimal degree.

Example 4.4
.

Let
p,q,r\in{\mathbbm{k}}[x]
and
p,q\neq 0
. Consider

f=pyq+r,\qquad g=qyp+r

in
{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!x,y\!\mathop{>}
. Note that
qfp=pgq
. If
X
is such that
p(X),q(X)
are invertible, then

g(X,Y)=\left(p(X)q(X)^{-1}\right)^{-1}f(X,Y)\left(p(X)q(X)^{-1}\right).

In particular,
f(X,Y)
and
g(X,Y)
are similar for a generic pair
(X,Y)
, and thus
f
and
g
are isospectral by the continuity of eigenvalues.
Thus
f
and

are intertwined by Theorem

.
If
p\neq q
and

, then

and

The reader may observe that for general

such that

or

is not completely straightforward.
For a case study, fix a finite subset
S\subset{\mathbbm{k}}
. For every
A\subseteq S
denote

p_{A}=\prod_{\alpha\in A}(x-\alpha)\in{\mathbbm{k}}[x]
.
Then

\left\{p_{A}yp_{S\setminus A}+x\colon A\subseteq S\right\}

is an equivalence class (of size

) for the transitive extension of the elementary intertwinedness, and thus for isospectrality.
Indeed, observe that
p_{A}yp_{S\setminus A}+x-\lambda
for
\lambda\in{\mathbbm{k}}
is either irreducible, or has two irreducible factors:
x-\lambda
and a factor of degree
|S|
. This shows that
p_{A}yp_{S\setminus A}+x
is elementary intertwined only with
p_{B}yp_{S\setminus B}+x
for
|S\setminus(A\cap B)|\leq 1
.
More generally, one needs
|S\setminus(A\cap B))|
elementary intertwined pairs to pass from
p_{A}yp_{S\setminus A}+x
to
q_{B}yp_{S\setminus B}+x
.
In particular, to pass from
f=p_{S}y+x
to

, one requires
|S|
elementary intertwined pairs. Concretely, if
S=\{1,\dots,s\}
,

(8)

p_{\{1,\dots,s\}}y+x\rightsquigarrow p_{\{2,\dots,s\}}yp_{\{1\}}+x\rightsquigarrow p_{\{3,\dots,s\}}yp_{\{1,2\}}+x\rightsquigarrow\cdots\rightsquigarrow yp_{\{1,\dots,s\}}+x.

Note that
\deg f=\deg g=|S|+1
.
The minimal
a\neq 0
such that
fa=ag
has degree

; for example,
a=p_{S}
.
On the other hand, as the proof of Theorem

.

4.2.
Operator isospectrality

Let
V
be a vector space over
{\mathbbm{k}}
. The spectrum of a linear operator
Y:V\to V
is the set
\sigma(Y)=\{\lambda\in{\mathbbm{k}}\colon Y-\lambda I\text{ is not invertible}\}
.
We say that
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are
operator isospectral
if the spectra of
f({\underline{X}})
and
g({\underline{X}})
coincide, for all tuples of linear operators
V\to V
, and all vector spaces
V
.
2
2

2

It may be natural to restrict to
{\mathbbm{k}}=\mathbb{C}
, and evaluations on tuples of bounded operators on a separable Banach/Hilbert space. However, the conclusions of this subsection would remain the same.

Clearly, operator isospectrality implies isospectrality. However, the converse fails, as the following example shows.

Example 4.5
.

Let
f=xy
and
g=yx
. Then
f
and
g
are elementary intertwined, and thus isospectral.
Let
S\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}))
be the unilateral shift. Then,
f(S,S^{*})
is a non-trivial projection and
g(S,S^{*})=I
. Therefore,
f
and
g
are not operator isospectral.

While operator isospectrality is stronger than isospectrality, it is still weaker than pointwise similarity. More precisely, it does not imply rank equivalence, as demonstrated in Example (
4.7
) below.
First, we record a few straightforward observations regarding spectra of noncommutative polynomials on operators.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
, and let
{\underline{X}}
be a tuple of linear operators on a vector space over
{\mathbbm{k}}
.

(1)

If
f,g
are stably associated, then
f({\underline{X}})
is invertible if and only if
g({\underline{X}})
is invertible.

(2)

If
f,g
are elementary intertwined as
f=\lambda+ab
and
g=\lambda+ba
, then
\sigma(f({\underline{X}}))\setminus\{\lambda\}=\sigma(g({\underline{X}}))\setminus\{\lambda\}
.

(3)

If
f,g
are isospectral, there exists a finite set
S\subset{\mathbbm{k}}
, independent of
{\underline{X}}
, such that
\sigma(f({\underline{X}}))\setminus S=\sigma(g({\underline{X}}))\setminus S
.

Proof.

(a) Follows directly from the definition of stable association, using that
Y
is invertible if and only if
Y\oplus I
is invertible.

(b) If
\beta\neq\lambda
, then
f-\beta
and
g-\beta
are stably associated, via the comaximal relation
(f-\beta)a=a(g-\beta)
. Then, (b) follows from (a).

(c) If
f,g
are isospectral, one can pass from one to the other by a finite chain of elementary intertwined pairs by Theorem
4.1
. Then, (c) follows from (b).
∎

Example 4.7
.

Let
f=xy^{2}x
and
g=yx^{2}y
.
Let
X,Y
be linear operators on a vector space over
{\mathbbm{k}}
.
By Lemma
4.6
(b),
\sigma(f(X,Y))\setminus\{0\}=\sigma(g(X,Y))\setminus\{0\}
.
Thus, we only need to investigate the membership of
0
in the spectra, which is only possible if at least one of
X,Y
is singular (non-invertible).
If
X
is not injective, then
f(X,Y)
is not injective; if
X
is not surjective, then
f(X,Y)
is not surjective.
We conclude that if
X
is singular, then
0\in\sigma(f(X,Y))
.
Similarly, if
Y
is singular, then
0\in\sigma(g(X,Y))
. Therefore, if both
X
and
Y
are singular, then the spectra of
f(X,Y)
and
g(X,Y)
coincide. We are left with the option that
X
is singular and
Y
is not, or vice versa. If
X
is singular, then so is
f(X,Y)
. In order for the spectra to differ,
g(X,Y)
must be invertible. However, if both
g(X,Y)
and
Y
are invertible, then so is
X^{2}
, which is impossible. Thus, the spectra of
f(X,Y)
and
g(X,Y)
always coincide.

To see that
f
and
g
are not rank-equivalent, let

X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad Y=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

On the one hand,
X^{2}=0
implies
g(X,Y)=0
. On the other hand,
Y
is a projection, and
f(X,Y)=XYX=\frac{1}{2}X\neq 0
.

The above reasoning extends to the following family of non-trivial operator isospectral pairs:
if
a_{1},a_{2}\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are stably associated and
b_{1},b_{2}\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are stably associated, then

a_{1}b_{1}b_{2}a_{2}
and
b_{2}a_{2}a_{1}b_{1}
are operator isospectral polynomials.
Similarly, if
a,b\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are such that
ab
and
ba
are stably associated, then
ab
and
ba
are operator isospectral (see Example
5.2
below for an instance of noncommuting
a,b
with this feature). We leave the quest for characterization of operator isospectrality and related problems for future work.

4.3.
Isospectrality and composition

The following observations about isospectrality and composition are needed later in Section
5
below.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
be isospectral, and
p\in{\mathbbm{k}}[t]
. Then every irreducible factor of
p(f)
is stably associated to a factor of
p(g)
.

Proof.

Given
h\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
, its
free locus

\mathcal{Z}(h)

[
20
]
is the set of all matrix tuples
{\underline{X}}
such that
\det h({\underline{X}})=0
. Since
f
and
g
are isospectral, so are
p(f)
and
p(g)
, and in particular
\mathcal{Z}(p(f))=\mathcal{Z}(p(g))
. By
[
20
, Theorem 2.12]
it then follows that every irreducible factor of
p(f)
is stably associated to a factor of
p(g)
.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
f\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
, and let
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbbm{k}}
be distinct. Then no factor of
f-\alpha
is stably associated to a factor of
f-\beta
.

Proof.

Suppose
f-\alpha=ahb
and
f-\beta=a^{\prime}h^{\prime}b^{\prime}
, where
h
and
h^{\prime}
are stably associated. Then

ahb-a^{\prime}h^{\prime}b^{\prime}=\beta-\alpha.

But such a relation cannot hold in
{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
by
[
14
, Theorem 4.2.3(f)]
because
{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
possesses the strong distributive factor lattice property
[
14
, Corollary 4.3.4]
.
∎

We say that
f\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
is
composite
if
f=p(\tilde{f})
for some
\tilde{f}\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
and a nonlinear
p\in{\mathbbm{k}}[t]
, and
non-composite
otherwise.
Observe that detecting composition is straightforward by Bergman’s centralizer theorem
[
8
]
: namely,
f
is composite if and only if the linear space
\{h\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}\colon fh=hf\ \&\ \deg h<\deg f\}
is nonzero.
The following assertion appears implicitly in
[
48
]
.

Lemma 4.10
.

If
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are isospectral, there exist
p\in{\mathbbm{k}}[t]
and non-composite isospectral
\tilde{f},\tilde{g}\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
such that
f=p(\tilde{f})
and
g=p(\tilde{g})
.

Proof.

Write
f=p(\tilde{f})
and
g=q(\tilde{g})
for monic
p,q\in{\mathbbm{k}}[t]
and non-composite
\tilde{f},\tilde{g}\in R
. By
[
48
, Theorem 3.2]
, there is
A\subseteq{\mathbbm{k}}
such that
{\mathbbm{k}}\setminus A
is finite and
\tilde{f}-\alpha,\tilde{g}-\alpha
is irreducible for all
\alpha\in A
.
Since
{\mathbbm{k}}\setminus A
is finite, there exists
\Lambda\subset{\mathbbm{k}}
such that
{\mathbbm{k}}\setminus\Lambda
is finite and
p-\lambda,q-\lambda
both have pairwise distinct roots in
{\mathbbm{k}}\setminus A
, for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Let

p-\lambda=\prod_{j=1}^{r}(t-\beta_{j}),\quad q-\lambda=\prod_{j=1}^{s}(t-\gamma_{j}).

Since
\tilde{f}-\beta_{j},\tilde{g}-\gamma_{j}
are irreducible, Lemmas
4.9
and
4.8
imply that
r=s
, and there is a permutation
\pi
of
\{1,\dots,r\}
such that
f-\beta_{j}
is stably associated to
g-\gamma_{\pi(j)}
for
j=1,\dots,n
. In particular, after reordering we have
\gamma_{j}=\beta_{j}+g(0)-f(0)
for all
j=1,\dots,n
. Hence,
p=q
after an affine transformation, and
\tilde{f}-\beta_{j},\tilde{g}-\beta_{j}
are stably associated for
j=1,\dots,n
. Furthermore, as
\lambda\in\Lambda
varies, the roots
\beta_{j}
attain all but finitely many values in
{\mathbbm{k}}
. Thus,
\tilde{f}-\beta,\tilde{g}-\beta
are stably associated (in particular, rank-equivalent) for all but finitely many
\beta\in{\mathbbm{k}}
. Hence,
\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{g}
are isospectral by the continuity of eigenvalues.
∎

Remark 4.11
.

Lemma
4.10
shows that deciding isospectrality reduces to non-composite polynomials. By the proof of
[
48
, Corollary 4.4]
, non-composite
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are isospectral if and only if
(f-t)a=b(g-t)
for some nonzero
a,b\in{\mathbbm{k}}(t)\otimes{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
of degree less than
\deg f
, which turns deciding isospectrality into a linear problem over the rational field
{\mathbbm{k}}(t)
.

5.
Pointwise similarity

In this section, we show that two polynomials are pointwise similar if and only if they are equal (Theorem
5.6
).
Before embarking on the proof, let us briefly comment on the perceived complexity of this problem.
3
3

3

The authors admit that they expected a quicker resolution.

On the one hand, pointwise similarity is a much more restrictive local equivalence than isospectrality and rank equivalence, indicating a likelihood for a strong algebraic characterization. On the other hand, pointwise similarity lacks some desired features of isospectrality (continuity) and rank equivalence (clear interpretation of counting homomorphisms between free algebra modules).
The first main ingredient of the resolution of this problem comes from the Jordan canonical form:
f
and
g
are pointwise similar if and only if
(f-\lambda)^{k}
and
(g-\lambda)^{k}
are stably associated, for all
\lambda\in{\mathbbm{k}}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. However, as stable association of powers turns out to be inconvenient to work with, the intertwining emerging from isospectrality is the second main ingredient.

Proposition 5.1

Let

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

\Hom_{R}\big(R/Rp(f),R/Rq(f)\big)=\Ext^{1}_{R}\big(R/Rp(f),R/Rq(f)\big)=\{0\}.

In particular,
R/R(p(f)q(f))\cong R/Rp(f)\oplus R/Rq(f)

Proof.

Let us consider the free resolution of the left module

,

0\xrightarrow{\phantom{\ \cdot p(f)}}R\xrightarrow{\ \cdot p(f)}R\xrightarrow{\phantom{\ \cdot p(f)}}R/Rp(f)\xrightarrow{\phantom{\ \cdot p(f)}}0.

Applying the functor
\Hom_{R}(\rule{8.61108pt}{0.3014pt},R/Rq(f))
, we get the exact sequence

(12)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\phantom{\ \cdot p(f)}}

\displaystyle\Hom_{R}(R/Rp(f),R/Rq(f))

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\phantom{\ \cdot p(f)}}

\displaystyle R/q(f)R

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\ \cdot p(f)}

\displaystyle R/q(f)R

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\phantom{\ \cdot p(f)}}

\displaystyle\Ext^{1}_{R}(R/Rp(f),R/Rq(f))

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\phantom{\ \cdot p(f)}}

\displaystyle 0\,.

Here, we used the isomorphism
\Hom_{R}(R,R/Rq(f))\to R/Rq(f)
determined by
\varphi\mapsto\varphi(1)
.
Since

and
q
are coprime, there exist

, such that

. Therefore,

. So the induced map of right multiplication by

has an inverse given by right multiplication by

. Therefore, the exact sequence (
12

by

are trivial, the second part of the lemma follows.

The next lemma allows us to reduce the pointwise similarity characterization to the case of non-composite polynomials.

Lemma 5.5
.

Every pair of pointwise similar polynomials in
{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
is of the form

where

, and

Proof.

By Lemma

, pointwise similar polynomials are of the form

where

and

are non-composite and isospectral. Now fix

and note that there exists

\begin{split}R/R(f-\lambda)^{k}\oplus R/Rq(f)&\cong R/R(p(f)-p(\lambda))\\
&\cong R/R(p(g)-p(\lambda))\cong R/R(g-\lambda)^{k}\oplus R/Rq(g).\end{split}

Since

. By Lemma

,

.
Thus,
\rk(f({\underline{X}})-\lambda I)^{\ell}=\rk(g({\underline{X}})-\lambda I)^{\ell}
for all
\lambda\in{\mathbbm{k}}
,

and matrix tuples

and

We are now ready to show that pointwise similarity is equality.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
f,g\in{\mathbbm{k}}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
be pointwise similar. Then,
f=g
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.5
, we may assume that
f
and
g
are non-composite. By

48

\beta\in{\mathbbm{k}}\setminus\{0\}
such that

and
g-\beta
are irreducible.

denote an intertwinner of

and
g
of minimal degree; note that
(f-\beta)a=a(g-\beta)
is a coprime relation. Since

and

are pointwise similar,

is stably associated to

Throughout this section, let

In addition to
\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
, we consider its extension, the free
*

\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}^{*}}\!\mathop{>}
generated by
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{n}^{*}

x_{j}\leftrightarrow x_{j}^{*}

f^{*}({\underline{X}},{\underline{X}}^{*})=\left(f({\underline{X}},{\underline{X}}^{*})\right)^{*}

{\underline{X}}\in\opm_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{n}

The following is a well-known fact in complex analysis, which we record for convenience.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be an Euclidean open set, and let
\mathcal{D}
be an analytically dense subset of
\mathcal{O}
. If
\phi,\psi
are nonzero analytic functions on
\mathcal{O}
such that
|\phi(\underline{z})|=|\psi(\underline{z})|
, then there exists
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\zeta|=1
such that
\psi=\zeta\phi
.

Proof.

Let
V=\{\phi=0\}\subset\mathcal{O}
. Then
\frac{\psi}{\phi}
is analytic on the open set
\mathcal{O}\setminus V
, and
|\frac{\psi}{\phi}|=1
on
\mathcal{D}\cap(\mathcal{O}\setminus V)
. Since
\mathcal{D}\cap(\mathcal{O}\setminus V)
is analytically dense in
\mathcal{O}\setminus V
, it follows that
|\frac{\psi}{\phi}|=1
on
\mathcal{O}\setminus V
. Since the latter is open, the maximum modulus principle implies that
\frac{\psi}{\phi}
is constant, so
\psi=\zeta\phi
for some
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\zeta|=1
.
∎

The main message of the following proposition is that noncommutative polynomials with pointwise equal spectral radii are isospectral up to scaling by a constant of modulus 1. For a later use, we establish a slightly stronger statement, where
\oph_{k}(\mathbb{C})
denotes the real space of hermitian

Let

The statement is clear if either

Let

pairwise distinct eigenvalues. By
[
12
, Corollary 2.10]
, there is a cofinite
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\in K
, the set
\mathcal{O}_{k}
is nonempty and Zariski open in
\opm_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
.
For
k\in K
and
{\underline{X}}\in\mathcal{O}_{k}
, the eigenvalues of
f({\underline{X}}),g({\underline{X}})
can be viewed as algebraic functions
\mathcal{O}_{k}\to\mathbb{C}
.
Then there exists a Euclidean open subset
\mathcal{O}_{k}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{O}_{k}
and holomorphic functions
\lambda_{k},\mu_{k}
on
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{k}
such that
\lambda_{k}({\underline{X}})
(resp.
\mu_{k}({\underline{X}})
) is a maximal (by absolute value) eigenvalue of
f({\underline{X}})
(resp.
g({\underline{X}})
), for all
{\underline{X}}\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{k}
.
By assumption,
|\lambda_{k}|=|\mu_{k}|
on
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{k}\cap\oph_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
, which is analytically dense in
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{k}
.
By Lemma
6.1
, there exists
\zeta_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\zeta_{k}|=1
such that
\mu_{k}=\zeta_{k}\lambda_{k}
. That is,
\zeta_{k}f({\underline{X}})
and
g({\underline{X}})
share an eigenvalue for all
{\underline{X}}
in a Zariski dense subset of
\opm_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
.
As in the proof of Theorem
4.1
(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii), we see that
the spectra of
\zeta_{k}f({\underline{X}})
and
g({\underline{X}})
coincide for every
{\underline{X}}\in\opm_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
.
Now fix some
m\in K
and
{\underline{X}}\in\opm_{m}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
with
f({\underline{X}})\neq\{0\}
(e.g.,
{\underline{X}}\in\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{m}
).
Then, the spectrum of
f({\underline{X}})
satisfies

\zeta_{m}\cdot\sigma\big(f({\underline{X}})\big)=\sigma\big(g({\underline{X}})\big)=\sigma\big(g({\underline{X}}^{\oplus\ell})\big)=\zeta_{\ell m}\cdot\sigma\big(f({\underline{X}}^{\oplus\ell})\big)=\zeta_{\ell m}\cdot\sigma\big(f({\underline{X}})\big)

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, there is
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\zeta|=1
such that
\zeta=\zeta_{k}
for infinitely many
k\in K
. Hence,
\zeta f
and
g
are isospectral.
∎

The following theorem combines the results on isospectrality from Section
4
with the tracial Nullstellensatz for
\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}^{*}}\!\mathop{>}
from
[
11
]
.

Theorem 6.3
.

The following are equivalent for
f,g\in\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
:

(1)

f
and
g
have pointwise the same Frobenius norm;

(2)

f
and
g
have pointwise the same operator norm;

(3)

f
and
g
have pointwise the same singular values;

(4)

g=\zeta f
for some
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\zeta|=1
.

Proof.

The implications (iv)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii)&(i) are clear.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Let
{\underline{y}}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
and
{\underline{z}}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
be two tuples of freely noncommuting self-adjoint variables. Viewing
f,g\in\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
as elements in
\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}^{*}}\!\mathop{>}
, we consider
F=f({\underline{y}}+i{\underline{z}})f^{*}({\underline{y}}+i{\underline{z}})
and
G=g({\underline{y}}+i{\underline{z}})g^{*}({\underline{y}}+i{\underline{z}})
in
\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{y}},{\underline{z}}\!\mathop{>}
.
Since
f
and
g
have the same operator norm on
\opm_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
,
F
and
G
have the same spectral radius on
\oph_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{2n}
.
By Proposition
6.2
, there exists
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\xi|=1
such that
\zeta F
and
G
are isospectral. Furthermore,
\xi=1
since
F
and
G
are positive semidefinite on
\oph_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{2n}
. Thus,
f
and
g
have the same singular values on
\opm_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{n}
, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iv):
By assumption,
\tr(f({\underline{X}})f({\underline{X}})^{*})=\tr(g({\underline{X}})g({\underline{X}})^{*})
for all
{\underline{X}}
.
Consider
ff^{*},gg^{*}\in\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}^{*}}\!\mathop{>}
.
By
[
11
, Corollary 5.21]
,
ff^{*}
and
gg^{*}
are
cyclically equivalent
, in the sense that one obtains
gg^{*}
by cyclically rotating the words in the expansion of
ff^{*}

[
11
, Remark 5.5]
.
Note that all words in expansions of
ff^{*}
and
gg^{*}
are of the form
uv^{*}
for
u,v\in\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
. Observe that words
u_{1}v_{1}^{*}
and
u_{2}v_{2}^{*}
with
u_{j},v_{j}\in\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}\setminus\{1\}
are cyclically equivalent if and only if
u_{1}=v_{1}
and
u_{2}=v_{2}
. Thus, cyclic equivalence of
ff^{*}
and
gg^{*}
implies that
ff^{*}=gg^{*}
. Since
f,g\in\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
and
f^{*},g^{*}\in\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}^{*}\!\mathop{>}
, it follows that
g=\zeta f
for some
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
, and furthermore
\zeta\bar{\zeta}=1
because of the relation
ff^{*}=gg^{*}
.
∎

Looking at noncommutative polynomials in free variables and their formal adjoints naturally leads to further pointwise equivalences. For example, while pointwise (unitarily) similar polynomials in
\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}}\!\mathop{>}
are necessarily equal by Theorem
5.6
, there are non-trivial pairs of pointwise unitarily similar polynomials in
\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}^{*}}\!\mathop{>}
, such as
xx^{*}
and
x^{*}x
. Such pointwise equivalences on
\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}^{*}}\!\mathop{>}
have a distinct real flavor, and admit larger equivalence classes than their
*
-free analogs presented in earlier sections (for example, various nonconstant polynomials in
\mathbb{C}\!\mathop{<}\!{\underline{x}},{\underline{x}^{*}}\!\mathop{>}
are rank-equivalent to 1, cf.
[
27
]
). Their exploration calls for methods beyond the scope of this paper.
