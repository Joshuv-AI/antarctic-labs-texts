Real-Time QP Solvers: A Concise Review and Practical Guide Towards Legged Robots
Van-Nam Dinh

Abstract
Quadratic programming (QP) underpins real-time robotics by enabling efficient, constrained optimization in state estimation, motion planning, and control. In legged locomotion and manipulation, essential modules like inverse dynamics, Model Predictive Control (MPC), and Whole-Body Control (WBC) are inherently QP-based, demanding reliable solutions amid tight timing, energy, and computational limits on embedded platforms. This paper presents a comprehensive analysis and benchmarking study of cutting-edge QP solvers for legged robotics. We begin by formulating the standard convex QP and classify solvers into four principal algorithmic approaches: interior-point methods, active-set strategies, operator-splitting schemes, and augmented Lagrangian approaches. Each solver is examined in terms of algorithmic structure, computational characteristics, and its ability to exploit problem structure and warm-starting. Performance is evaluated using publicly available benchmarks, focusing on metrics such as computation time, constraint satisfaction, and robustness under perturbations. Feature tables and comparisons yield practical guidance for solver selection, underscoring trade-offs in speed, accuracy, and energy efficiency. Our findings emphasize the synergy between solver, task, and hardware—e.g., sparse IPMs for long-horizon MPC, and dense active-set for high-frequency WBC—to advance agile, autonomous legged systems, with emerging extensions to nonconvex and distributed QP.

2
Quadratic Programming Optimization Problem

In Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP)
[
13
]
, a nonlinear program (NLP) is solved iteratively by approximating it locally with a quadratic program (QP). At each iteration, the nonlinear objective function is replaced by a second-order Taylor expansion of the Lagrangian, while the equality and inequality constraints are linearized. Specifically, given the NLP

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad h(x)=0,\;g(x)\leq 0,

the SQP subproblem around the current iterate
x_{k}
takes the form:

\displaystyle\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d^{\top}B_{k}d+\nabla f(x_{k})^{\top}d~~~~~\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\nabla h(x_{k})^{\top}d+h(x_{k})=0,

\displaystyle\nabla g(x_{k})^{\top}d+g(x_{k})\leq 0,

where
B_{k}
approximates the Hessian of the Lagrangian. This subproblem is a convex QP when
B_{k}
is positive semi-definite and fits the standard convex QP form
[
6
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Hx+g^{\top}x~~~~~~~\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle Ax=b,

\displaystyle Cx\leq u,

by identifying
H=B_{k}
,
g=\nabla f(x_{k})
,
A=\nabla h(x_{k})^{\top}
,
b=-h(x_{k})
,
C=\nabla g(x_{k})^{\top}
, and
u=-g(x_{k})
. This connection allows SQP methods to leverage modern convex QP solvers for efficient inner-loop optimization.
For problems with only equality constraints (
Cx\leq u
absent), the solution can be found by solving the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) system:

\begin{bmatrix}H&A^{\top}\\
A&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x^{*}\\
\lambda^{*}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-g\\
b\end{bmatrix},

(1)

where
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}}
are the Lagrange multipliers for the equality constraints.

There are four major methodological families for solving QPs that are relevant to robotics applications:
active-set methods
,
interior-point methods
,
operator-splitting methods
(e.g., ADMM), and
augmented Lagrangian methods

[
6
]
. We provide a brief review of each, including how they work and examples of prominent solvers, before focusing on the augmented Lagrangian approach used by ProxQP.

Active-set methods

[
14
]
iteratively identify the active inequality constraints (
C_{\mathcal{A}}x=u_{\mathcal{A}}
) and solve equality-constrained subproblems:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Hx+g^{\top}x~~~\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle Ax=b,

\displaystyle C_{\mathcal{A}}x=u_{\mathcal{A}},

(2)

where
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n_{i}\}
is the active set of constraints.

These methods, implemented in solvers like qpOASES
[
14
]
and Eiquadprog
[
7
]
, are efficient for small to medium-sized problems where the active set changes slowly, enabling effective warm-starting. This is particularly valuable in robotics for iterative tasks, such as MPC in motion planning. However, their performance degrades for large-scale problems due to the need to solve multiple subproblems, and they lack support for early stopping or inexact solutions.

Interior-point methods (IPMs)

[
16
]
approach the feasible region from the interior by introducing a barrier function to handle inequality constraints. The resulting barrier-augmented problem is formulated as:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Hx+g^{\top}x-\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n_{i}}\log\left(u_{i}-(Cx)_{i}\right)~~\text{s.t.}~~~Ax=b,

(3)

where
\mu>0
is the barrier parameter. The logarithmic barrier ensures that the inequality constraints
Cx<u
are enforced strictly, as
u_{i}-(Cx)_{i}>0
must hold for all
i
. The barrier-augmented problem is solved iteratively using a primal-dual Newton method applied to the perturbed Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) system.
Solvers such as HPIPM
[
16
]
, Gurobi, MOSEK, and the recent qpSWIFT
[
17
]
offer high accuracy and are robust for large or sparse problems, making them suitable for precision-critical robotics tasks, such as trajectory optimization. qpSWIFT, designed for real-time robotics, uses Mehrotra predictor-corrector steps and Nesterov-Todd scaling, outperforming other solvers for small-scale problems. However, IPMs have high computational costs per iteration, and warm-starting is challenging because solutions often lie on the constraint boundaries.

Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)-Based Solvers

[
15
]
, reformulates the QP by introducing auxiliary variables to decompose the problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Hx+g^{\top}x+I_{\mathcal{K}}(z)~~\text{s.t.}~~

\displaystyle Ax=b,

\displaystyle Cx-z=0,

(4)

where
I_{\mathcal{K}}(z)
is the indicator function of the feasible set
\mathcal{K}
, typically the nonnegative orthant (
z\geq 0
) for inequality constraints. The ADMM iterations are given by:

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x}\left(\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Hx+g^{\top}x+\frac{\rho}{2}\|Cx-z^{k}+y^{k}/\rho\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(5)

\displaystyle z^{k+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}(Cx^{k+1}+y^{k}/\rho),~~y^{k+1}=y^{k}+\rho(Cx^{k+1}-z^{k+1}),

(6)

where
\rho>0
is the penalty parameter, and
\Pi_{\mathcal{K}}
denotes the projection onto the set
\mathcal{K}
.
ADMM-based solvers such as OSQP
[
15
]
are highly scalable and offer low per-iteration computational cost, making them well suited for large-scale robotics applications such as whole-body control and real-time model predictive control (MPC). These solvers support warm-starting and early termination, which are critical features for embedded systems. However, they may require a larger number of iterations to achieve high-accuracy solutions and typically rely on heuristics to detect primal or dual infeasibility.

Augmented Lagrangian methods
,
[
6
]
, minimize a penalized Lagrangian to handle both equality and inequality constraints:

\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,\lambda,\mu)=\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Hx+g^{\top}x+\lambda^{\top}(Ax-b)+\mu^{\top}(Cx-u)+\frac{\rho_{e}}{2}\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\rho_{i}}{2}\|\max(0,Cx-u)\|_{2}^{2},

(7)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{e}}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
are dual variables for equality and inequality constraints, respectively, and
\rho_{e},\rho_{i}>0
are penalty parameters. The dual updates are:

\displaystyle\lambda^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{k}+\rho_{e}(Ax^{k}-b),

(8)

\displaystyle\mu^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\max(0,\mu^{k}+\rho_{i}(Cx^{k}-u)),

(9)

where the
\max(0,\cdot)
ensures
\mu\geq 0
, as required for inequality constraints.
Solvers like ProxQP
[
6
]
and QPALM combine proximal regularization with Newton-type updates, offering robust convergence and the ability to handle infeasibility. These methods support warm-starting and early stopping, making them suitable for real-time, safety-critical robotics applications like closed-loop control. Their implementation complexity is higher than ADMM, but they perform well across various problem sizes.

Benchmarking and Practical Guide

The report
[
6
,
18
]
includes QPs from linear MPC tasks such as cart-pole balancing and humanoid walking.
Fig.
1
reports results on a representative MPC instance (
n\approx 216
,
m\approx 392
), showing solve times over a 100-step simulation at two solver tolerances.

ProxQP
consistently outperformed other solvers in both speed and accuracy, maintaining low constraint violation and runtime even under tight tolerances (
\epsilon=10^{-6}
). OSQP
[
15
]
, while fast at moderate accuracy, slowed significantly at high precision.
Active-set methods, such as
qpOASES
, were significantly slower and exhibited poor scaling.
MOSEK
offered high accuracy but with a significantly higher computational cost. These results highlight
ProxQP
’s favorable trade-off between speed, accuracy, and robustness for mid-size MPC QPs common in robotics applications.

Following the study in
[
1
]
, a detailed benchmarking of QP formulations and solvers was conducted to evaluate their suitability for real-time control in quadrupedal locomotion. The authors tested six widely used solvers—
HPIPM
,
PROXQP
,
OSQP
,
qpOASES
,
Eiquadprog
, and
qpSWIFT
—on two canonical control problems:
Model Predictive Control (MPC)
for trajectory optimization, and
Whole-Body Control (WBC)
for torque-level inverse dynamics under contact constraints. Both
dense and sparse
QP formulations were evaluated across three hardware platforms: a desktop x86 CPU, a LattePanda Alpha (x86 embedded), and an NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX (ARM).
In the
MPC benchmarks
, implemented using the
acados
framework
[
13
]
, the authors evaluated only
HPIPM
,
OSQP
, and
qpOASES
.
Among these,
HPIPM
consistently achieved the lowest solve times for sparse QP formulations due to its ability to exploit structured sparsity arising from the linearized dynamics.
Its performance scaled well with prediction horizon, making it a suitable candidate for embedded real-time MPC.

OSQP
, based on operator splitting, provided robust and consistent solve times and required minimal tuning, although it was generally slower than
HPIPM
.

qpOASES
performed best on small horizon problems due to its efficient active-set method, but became less competitive as problem size increased.
In the
WBC benchmarks
, which involves smaller, dense QPs for whole-body inverse dynamics, the solvers
PROXQP
,
Eiquadprog
, and
qpSWIFT
were evaluated using the
ARC-OPT
control stack.

Eiquadprog
, based on a classical active-set strategy, achieved sub-millisecond solve times across all platforms, making it well-suited for high-frequency control.
PROXQP
, which employs an augmented Lagrangian method, also performed reliably in the WBC tasks, showing consistent performance on both desktop and embedded platforms.
Although the study did not explicitly test robustness to ill-conditioning or infeasibility,
PROXQP
completed all trials successfully and with stable timing.
qpSWIFT
, although designed for embedded applications, showed weaker numerical robustness and generally underperformed relative to the other solvers.
The
Jetson Orin NX
achieved the highest SFPW overall, particularly when paired with
OSQP
for MPC and with
Eiquadprog
or
PROXQP
for WBC.
This demonstrates the potential of ARM-based embedded systems for onboard real-time optimization.
In summary, the study concludes that
sparse solvers like

HPIPM

are well suited for large-horizon MPC tasks
. In contrast,
dense solvers such as

Eiquadprog

and

are preferable for small, high-frequency control problems like WBC
. The benchmarking results, together with the proposed SFPW metric, provide valuable guidance for selecting solver–formulation–hardware combinations in energy-constrained legged robotic systems.
The
qpmad_benchmark

[
19
]
provides a targeted evaluation of dense QP solvers commonly used in robotics, particularly for applications such as condensed Model Predictive Control (MPC) and inverse dynamics.
It benchmarks
qpmad
, a modern and lightweight C++ solver that implements the Goldfarb-Idnani dual active-set method, against other C++ solvers, such as
qpOASES
and
EiQuadprog
.
All solvers are evaluated on dense, positive-definite QPs with fixed structure and problem size. Results show that
qpmad
consistently outperforms
Eiquadprog
in terms of solve time, achieving up to 2
\times
faster performance on small- to medium-sized problems.
This advantage is attributed to
qpmad
’s efficient memory access, low overhead, and minimal dependencies, making it especially suitable for embedded and high-frequency control applications
[
20
]
.
While
qpOASES
remains competitive, particularly when configured with its MPC-optimized settings
qpmad
demonstrates more consistent performance and better scalability under tight real-time constraints.
These findings suggest that
qpmad
is a strong drop-in replacement for legacy solvers like
Eiquadprog
in dense QP control pipelines.

4
Discussion and Conclusion

We reviewed QP solvers for legged robots, benchmarked their performance, and proposed practical selection guidelines.
For structured, long-horizon MPC problems, sparse solvers like
HPIPM
offer top-tier performance by exploiting problem sparsity, though their memory usage may hinder deployment on embedded platforms.
For dense QPs common in WBC, solvers like
qpmad
and
Eiquadprog
are highly efficient. Benchmarks using the SFPW (Solves per Watt) metric show
qpmad
achieves up to 2
\times
faster solves and better energy efficiency on platforms like the Jetson Orin NX.

PROXQP
, from the
ProxSuite
library, showed strong robustness and consistent performance across diverse problems and hardware. While not the fastest, its numerical stability and infeasibility handling make it suitable for contact-rich or safety-critical tasks.
Notably, for many WBC applications, solver choice is less critical sub-millisecond solve times were common. Here, effort is better directed toward problem formulation.
Finally, hardware-aware benchmarking highlights that ARM platforms like Orin NX offer up to 3
\times
energy efficiency over x86, reinforcing their suitability for onboard optimization.
Future work includes GPU-based solvers and distributed MPC integration.
