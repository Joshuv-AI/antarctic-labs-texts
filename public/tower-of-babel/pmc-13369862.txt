A global dataset of soil organic carbon accumulation rate in coastal ecosystems
Authors: Lihua Pang, Tianping Xu, Zhan Hu, Baoshan Cui, Na Zhang, Liming Xue, Shuai Liu, Shoufeng Chi, Zezheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoastal ecosystems are increasingly recognized as a crucial nature-based climate solution due to their remarkable capacity for carbon sequestration. However, the spatial distribution and quantification of soil organic carbon accumulation rate (OCAR) at the global scale remain limited. Here, we assemble an open global dataset of soil OCAR in coastal ecosystems from a systematic literature review. The dataset comprises 8,998 field observations from 247 peer-reviewed articles, encompassing four distinct ecosystem types (mangroves, salt marshes, seagrasses, and tidal flats). Furthermore, the dataset includes publication details of the source articles, geographical and ecological attributes of the sampling sites, and information for each observation. The dataset can be employed to more precisely quantify and map global coastal soil carbon sequestration capacity, enabling the development of global spatiotemporal prediction models. This dataset serves as a vital scientific foundation for informing management strategies of coastal ecosystems and guiding future carbon credit markets.

Background & Summary

Coastal ecosystems exhibit an exceptional carbon sequestration capacity as a nature-based climate solution1, contributing to more than 50% of the ocean’s carbon stock2 and over one-third of the earth’s soil carbon stock3. The soil organic carbon accumulation rate (OCAR), also known as the carbon sequestration rate or burial rate, is a critical metric for assessing the effectiveness of carbon sequestration in coastal wetlands4. This metric provides a precise reflection of carbon storage potential and sequestration capacity for carbon by quantifying the net accumulation of organic carbon in sediments per unit area over time5. In addition, this metric offers valuable insights into the carbon cycling process, thereby enhancing our understanding of the stability and sustainability of soil carbon pools6.

Despite its importance, there are few open data sources compiling soil OCAR value at global and regional scales. Previous datasets often focused on a single ecosystem, scattered sampling sites, or only extracted spatial/temporal averages. For instance, Wang et al. synthesized carbon accumulation rate in tidal wetlands of the conterminous United States and resulted in an average value of 161.8 g C m−2 yr−17, Fu et al. estimated sediment organic carbon stock and burial rate in Bahamian seagrass meadows8, and Breithaupt et al. compiled organic carbon burial rate in global mangrove systems and acquire a global estimate that mangrove systems buried 163 g OC m−2 yr−1 9. Consequently, the development of an open and accessible global dataset would allow direct policies related to carbon sequestration and guide the implementation of future markets for carbon credits, among other potential applications.

Here, we conducted a systematic search of the peer-reviewed literature to construct a global dataset of soil OCAR in coastal ecosystems. The dataset has 8,998 field observations from 247 peer-reviewed articles between 1981 and 2025, identified through Web of Science. Mangrove, salt marsh, seagrass, and tidal flat were classified as different ecosystem types. In addition, publication details of the source articles, geographical and ecological attributes of the sampling sites, and information for each observation were included in the dataset. This dataset aims to inform the estimation of the current status and potential of soil organic carbon sequestration in coastal ecosystems, revealing the spatial heterogeneity in its distribution. This study can help develop systematic forecast models and guide governments and institutions in establishing policies and measures for coastal ecosystem management.

Methods

Data collection

To compile the soil OCAR dataset, a literature search was conducted on 23 September 2024 using Web of Science with the following terms: TS = (marsh* OR mangrove* OR mangal* OR seagrass* OR kelp* OR coast* OR estuar*) AND TS = (accret* OR decomposit* OR accumulat* OR sequestrat* OR burial) AND TS = (carbon* OR SOC OR “organic matter*“ OR “organic content”). No restrictions were imposed on publication year or subject area to maximize the scope of results. This initial search yielded 13,053 published articles. Additionally, we added 165 other published articles associated with soil OCAR from several previous literature reviews7,10–14. After removing duplicates, a total of 13,142 articles were identified.

To minimize selection bias and ensure a standardized process, the following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) studies must be conducted within coastal ecosystems, such as salt marshes, mangroves, seagrasses, and tidal flats. Freshwater marshes, intermediate marshes, and brackish marshes were classified as salt marshes in this screening; (2) soil OCAR must be directly obtained from the studies or calculated using Eqs. 1–3 based on soil characteristics (e.g. bulk density, carbon content, and soil accretion rate); (3) soil OCAR must be derived from in situ field surveys, excluding modeling data. During the initial screening, articles were filtered mainly based on title, abstract, and keywords. Articles lacking information on soil organic carbon or soil accretion rate in coastal ecosystems were excluded, resulting in a selection of 1,817 articles. Subsequently, a full-text manual screening was performed to assess for eligibility. Finally, a total of 247 articles were retained (Fig. 1).

Data extraction

For each included article, we recorded the following information: author(s), title, published journal and year, study country, site name, latitude, longitude, sampled time, record period, ecosystem, marsh category, vegetation type, vegetation species, data source, value extraction source, soil accretion and elevation estimation method, organic carbon estimation method, soil OCAR, and standardized unit. When geographic coordinate information was absent in the original text, latitude and longitude data were obtained by locating the study site on Google Earth using the sampling map provided in the article. Notably, if the original text provided a single coordinate summarizing the area instead of multiple specific locations, this original information was preferred over manually derived coordinates. Sampled time refers to the date on which the soil sample was collected. The record period refers to the interval during which soil carbon accumulation rates were measured, either through direct measurement or radiometric dating methods. Ecosystems were classified into four types: mangrove, salt marsh, seagrass, and tidal flat. Freshwater marsh, intermediate marsh, and brackish marsh were classified as salt marsh in our compilation, with the marsh category recorded in a separate column. The vegetation type column indicates whether the study area was dominated by monospecies or multispecies, determined based on the vegetation species. Data source distinguishes whether the data were extracted from the original text or obtained from other compilations or repositories, and indicates whether the value could be directly extracted without additional computation. When it cannot be obtained directly, we performed calculations using three equations, which will be provided below. Value extraction source refers to its position in the original articles. Values were extracted from the main text, tables, figures, and supplementary materials of the articles. Values in figures were extracted with GetData Graph Digitizer (https://apps.automeris.io/wpd/). OCAR measurements typically involve two stages: soil accretion and elevation estimation method, and organic carbon estimation method. Common methods for soil accretion and elevation estimation include ²¹⁰Pb, ¹³⁷Cs, ¹⁴C dating, Marker Horizon, and Rod Surface Elevation Table method. Organic carbon content is generally determined using chemical oxidation, Loss-on-Ignition, or elemental analyzer methods.

By standardizing these diverse methodological approaches into a consistent unit, we ensured that data were directly comparable.

Overview of the dataset

The global dataset of soil OCAR in coastal ecosystems, compiled from 247 articles published between 1981 and 2025, encompassed a total of 8,998 observations. The full list of 247 data sources is available in the repository and also cited in the References8–10,16–259. This dataset spans 50 countries on six continents along the shorelines of the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, and Arctic Oceans. The majority of studies were in the USA (3,378 observations from 102 studies), Mexico (1,354 observations from 19 studies), Australia (1,141 observations from 24 studies), and China (835 observations from 23 studies). The most studied ecosystems were salt marshes (3,947 observations from 137 studies), mangroves (3,241 observations from 100 studies), and seagrasses (1,470 observations from 32 studies), while tidal flats accounted for only 313 observations (29 studies).

Technical Validation

Data quality was ensured through a systematic, multistep validation procedure, consistent with methodologies adopted in similar global synthesis studies. During the screening phase, data extraction was conducted by different authors with the same inclusion criteria to ensure data consistency. Before finalization of the dataset, a comprehensive review process was implemented to verify the overall quality of the data. This involved recalculating soil OCAR values and calibrating article information and experimental details, such as ID, site name, and methods. Finally, we performed the following visualization and statistical analysis to validate the spatial distribution and mean value of the dataset.

The global dataset exhibited significant spatial heterogeneity. Observations were concentrated in the mid-low latitude zones of the Americas, Europe, Asia, and Oceania, with minimal data available for the African coast and the high-latitude regions (Fig. 2). Mangrove observations clustered tightly in equatorial regions, particularly around the South China Sea and the Gulf of Mexico. Salt marsh observations showed mid-latitude dominance, with hotspots along the southeastern U.S., the UK, southern Brazil, and the southeastern Australian coast. Seagrass observations demonstrated a broader latitudinal distribution, concentrated on the east and west coasts of the Atlantic Ocean and the east coast of the Pacific Ocean. Tidal flat observations were predominantly documented in the mid-low latitude zone, notably the western U.S., eastern Brazil, southeastern China, and South Korea. The distribution of observations in this study was similar to that of global coastal wetlands, especially for mangrove and salt marsh data, which covered the majority of their respective distribution areas261,262. Although limited by sample size, observations of seagrass and tidal flat also encompassed major ecological hotspots263,264. Hence, the compiled dataset can be utilized to retrieve a reasonable map of the distribution of global coastal ecosystems’ soil OCAR.

Map showing soil OCAR observations, sampled by ecosystems as Mangrove (red pentagon), Salt marsh (blue triangle), Seagrass (green rhombus), and Tidal flat (yellow circle).

In the box plot, the median and mean values of soil OCAR for all four ecosystems were presented (Fig. 3a), with salt marsh exhibiting the highest mean value (1.54 Mg C ha−1 yr−1), followed by mangrove (1.42 Mg C ha−1 yr−1) and tidal flat (1.25 Mg C ha−1 yr−1), while seagrass showed the lowest rate (0.29 Mg C ha−1 yr−1). Notably, salt marsh soil OCAR was substantially lower than the compilation from 143 sites by Ouyang and Lee (2.447 Mg C ha−1 yr−1)201, yet comparable to the updated estimate of 1.94 Mg C ha−1 yr−1 by Wang et al.12. Other ecosystems showed stronger consistency with previous studies: mangrove paralleled the 1.63 Mg C ha−1 yr−1 measured by Breithaupt et al.9, seagrass corresponded to the 0.43 Mg C ha−1 yr−1 measured by Hiraishi et al.265, and tidal flat matched the 1.29 Mg C ha−1 yr−1 measured by Chen and Lee79. Furthermore, our comparative analysis of soil accretion and elevation estimation methods exhibits significant variability within each ecosystem, indicating that different soil measurements introduce systematic differences. However, the sample size for some methods is insufficient to conclusively reduce deviations arising from differences in sample size (Fig. 3b).

Usage Notes

The global coastal ecosystems soil OCAR dataset compiled from articles published from 1981 to 2025 provides detailed spatial information for carbon sequestration capacity across coastal ecosystems. Compared to previous datasets9,12,79,201,265, the advantages of this compilation lie in richer data, more comprehensive coverage of four coastal ecosystems, and more precise global spatial distribution due to the thorough extraction of coordinates. These improvements enable the dataset to support critical applications, such as improved estimation of the current status and potential of soil organic carbon sequestration, revealing spatial heterogeneity across multiple coastal ecosystem types, and informing large-scale predictive model development for soil OCAR. The dataset seeks to guide future research related to coastal restoration and protection, assessing the sustainability of this nature-based climate solution. Besides, it can serve as a scientific foundation for the formulation of management measures and policies by governments and institutions.

However, some limitations still need to be solved in further studies: (1) Plant species significantly influence soil carbon sequestration rates in coastal ecosystems. While species-level data are available for most sites, coverage and quantitative detail remain limited. The specific mechanisms by which different species influence soil carbon sequestration remain uncertain; (2) Some regions and ecosystem types have sparse observation stations, such as coastal areas of the Indian Ocean, southern regions of Africa and South America, tidal flats and seagrasses, which may limit the accuracy of future simulations of soil OCAR in these regions and ecosystems; (3) The dataset does not distinguish sources of soil organic carbon, such as plant biomass, microbial biomass, and terrestrial or marine inputs. Therefore, future efforts should be made to identify carbon sources at observational sites, as well as collect more observation stations from future articles and non-peer-reviewed grey literature (e.g., academic theses, technical reports, books, and relevant web pages), to improve the accuracy of assessments and predictions of soil carbon sequestration potential in coastal ecosystems.
