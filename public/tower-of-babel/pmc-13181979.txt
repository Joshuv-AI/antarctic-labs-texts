Unveiling genetic loci and natural alleles for seed iron accumulation in adzuki bean via genome-wide association study​
Authors: Yuxin Wen, Jianping Yu, Xiaocao Wu, Liwei Chu, Huizan Li, Xingen Li, Xuehan Zhang, Weiyi Li, Kun Wu, Yongqiang Li, Runzhi Li, Kai Yang, Ping Wan, Bo Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIron is an essential micronutrient for both plant growth and human health and iron deficiency affects over 30% of the global population. Adzuki bean (Vigna angularis), an iron-rich legume, serves as a strategic dietary iron source to combat global iron deficiency anemia. Despite its nutritional significance, the genetic basis of iron accumulation in legumes remains unexplored.ResultsThis study addresses this gap by performing the first genome-wide association study (GWAS) targeting seed iron content in a diverse panel of 320 adzuki accessions (220 cultivated, 100 wild/semi-wild), representing seven genetic subpopulations across East Asia. Multi-environment phenotyping (2014–2019) revealed extensive iron content variation, with wild adzuki bean exhibiting significantly higher concentrations than cultivated types. No significant difference was found between landraces and improved cultivars. GWAS identified 39 quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with iron content, distributed across chromosomes 1 to 11, with chromosomes 3 and 10 harboring the most loci. Twelve candidate genes were prioritized within QTL intervals, including genes involved in iron transport (e.g., Vigan01g178400, homologous to iron-regulated transporter IRT1), metabolism (e.g., Vigan07g168200, a nicotinamidase aminotransferase), and regulation (e.g., Vigan03g004100, a bZIP transcription factor). Haplotype analysis of key genes uncovered functional variations: for example, VaIRT1 haplotypes with a promoter SNP showed significant differences in iron content, likely due to altered cis-acting elements affecting gene expression.ConclusionsThis study provides the first genetic blueprint for iron biofortification in adzuki bean, identifying key haplotypes and regulatory variants that modulate iron homeostasis. These findings clarify the genetic architecture of iron accumulation in adzuki bean and provide valuable resources for breeding high-iron varieties to address global iron deficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08726-0.

Background

Adzuki bean (Vigna angularis L.) is an annual crop belonging to the Fabaceae family (Leguminosae) and the genus Vigna [1]. China, as the largest producer of adzuki bean, has consistently held the leading position globally in terms of both planting area and total yield. Adzuki bean is rich in various nutrients, with their iron content being particularly noteworthy: an average of 100 g of adzuki bean contains approximately 7.4 mg of iron, which is 1.45, 3.08 and 3.36 times that of wheat, rice, and corn, respectively [2]. Iron deficiency is the most common micronutrient deficiency, affecting over 30% of the world’s population and leading to anemia [3]. Adzuki bean is considered an important dietary source of iron [4].

Iron is an essential micronutrient for plant growth, playing a crucial role in chlorophyll synthesis, electron transport chains, and the regulation of various enzyme activities [5]. As an important food crop, the iron content of adzuki bean directly impacts their nutritional value and stress resistance. Iron deficiency leads to chlorosis (yellowing) of young leaves, resulting in significant reductions in yield and quality [6]. Plant iron uptake and metabolism are influenced by numerous factors, including soil iron content, soil pH, soil temperature and humidity, microbial metabolism, soil organic matter, oxygen levels, and the plant’s inherent ability to absorb and utilize iron [7, 8]. To cope with these challenges, plants have evolved two primary iron acquisition strategies: a reduction strategy, predominantly used by non-graminaceous plants, which involves the reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+ by root-secreted ferric reductases (FRO2) and subsequent uptake of Fe2+ into the roots via iron transporters [5]; and a chelation strategy, where plants secrete phytosiderophores (PS) to chelate Fe3+, forming soluble complexes that are transported into the plant [9]. It’s important to note that these strategies are not mutually exclusive. It’s interesting that some plants employ both mechanisms for iron uptake. Both iron uptake pathways were revealed in rice [10], a graminaceous plant, a finding later was confirmed [11]. Adzuki bean, as legumes, exhibits unique characteristics in their iron acquisition mechanism. They primarily utilize root-secreted PS and proton pumps (H+-ATPase) to mobilize iron in the soil and facilitate its absorption [12].

Long-distance transport of iron within plants relies on a series of transporters, such as those belonging to the YSL (Yellow Stripe-like) protein family. These proteins transport iron from the roots to the aboveground parts, distributing it to organs such as leaves and seeds [13]. However, for iron to exert its physiological functions, efficient transport at the subcellular level is crucial, particularly into key organelles like mitochondria and vacuoles. This process usually depends on vesicular transport mechanisms. In rice, the MIR gene encodes a mitochondrial iron transporter responsible for transporting iron ions from the cytoplasm into the mitochondria. Rice mutants with the MIR gene knocked out (mir) exhibit clear iron deficiency symptoms, yet accumulate more iron ions in their aboveground parts compared to wild-type plants. Studies have shown that the iron ions accumulated in the aboveground parts of mir mutants cannot be transported into the mitochondria, leading to mitochondrial iron deficiency and subsequently affecting mitochondrial function [14]. Vacuoles play a role in nutrient storage and maintaining osmotic pressure stability in plant cells. Arabidopsis thaliana’s vacuolar iron transporter VIT1, located on the vacuolar membrane, actively transports iron ions (Fe²⁺) from the cytoplasm into the vacuole for storage. In Arabidopsis mutants lacking VIT1, iron accumulation in cotyledons and apical meristems is reduced, while iron content in other areas such as the endosperm is increased [15]. Similar conclusions were drawn from studies on the rice vacuolar iron transporter OsVIT2. Under normal or excess iron conditions, the endosperm iron content is significantly increased in OsVIT2 knockout mutants (osvit2), while the embryo iron content is decreased. Under iron deficiency conditions, the iron accumulation levels of the mutant and wild-type (WT) are comparable. Furthermore, osvit2 plants grow more slowly than WT, but the overall iron content of the plants increases in the later growth stages [16]. This further confirms the role of vacuolar iron transporters in regulating iron storage distribution and plant growth and development.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have significantly advanced our understanding of the genetic basis underlying grain iron (Fe) concentration in rice, a critical target for addressing global micronutrient malnutrition [17–26]. Studies using diverse germplasm panels, including Indian landraces, 3 K rice collection subsets, and regional accessions from the Democratic Republic of Congo, have identified numerous marker-trait associations (MTAs) and quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with Fe accumulation in brown and milled rice. These loci are distributed across chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 11, and 12, with stable QTLs often co-localizing with genes involved in metal transport and homeostasis, such as nicotianamine synthases (OsNAS1, OsNAS2), mitochondrial iron transporter (OsMIT), metallothioneins (OsMT2D), and ubiquitin E3 ligase (OsATL31). Environmental factors, including soil Fe levels and milling processes, significantly influence Fe retention—with Fe showing low retention (≈ 24.9%) in milled rice compared to brown rice—highlighting the need for loci governing Fe localization in the endosperm [27]. Candidate genes identified, such as those encoding bZIP and MYB transcription factors and transporters (e.g., ZIP family), reveal complex regulatory networks involving uptake, translocation, and storage of iron [20]. These findings provide valuable markers and genetic resources for marker-assisted selection, facilitating the development of Fe-biofortified rice varieties to alleviate hidden hunger. While extensive GWAS have been conducted for agronomic traits in leguminous crops such as soybean (Glycine max) [28], Medicago truncatula [29], and adzuki bean [30, 31], genome-wide investigations specifically targeting iron accumulation in legumes remain unexplored. ​The absence of parallel Fe-focused GWAS in iron-rich legumes like adzuki bean—despite their nutritional significance—represents a critical knowledge gap limiting biofortification strategies across staple crops.​.

This study employed a diverse natural population of adzuki bean (Vigna angularis) germplasm to dissect the genetic architecture of seed iron (Fe) content through genome-wide association analysis. We identified significant quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and ​superior natural allelic variations​ associated with Fe accumulation, providing foundational insights​ into the molecular basis of Fe homeostasis in adzuki bean seeds and functional candidates for targeted gene cloning and validation.

Methods

Plant materials

This study utilized a diverse collection of 320 adzuki bean (Vigna angularis L.) accessions [31], encompassing 220 cultivated varieties (149 landraces and 71 improved cultivars) and 100 wild and semi-wild accessions (Table S1). These accessions originating from China, South Korea, Japan, Burma, and Nepal were collected and preserved in the Department of Agronomy, College of Plant Science and Technology, Beijing University of Agriculture, Beijing, China. The adzuki accessions were collected in accordance with local laws without any conflict of interest. All accessions were classified into seven genetic subpopulations [31]: Three cultivated subgroups: Cul_NC (Northern China), Cul_SC (Southern China), and Cul_JK (Japan and South Korea); Two wild subgroups: Wild_SC (Southern China) and Wild_N (Northeast Asia); One semi-wild subgroup: Semi_WildN; One mixed subgroup: Mix. This subgroup classification reflects patterns of genetic differentiation based on geographic origin and domestication status. The prefix “Cul” denotes cultivated types, “Wild” refers to wild types, and “Semi_WildN” designates semi-wild types. The Mix subgroup contains accessions with diverse or admixed genetic backgrounds. Detailed information for all accessions is provided in Table S1.

Field experiment details

​​The plant materials were planted at the experimental base of Beijing University of Agriculture, Beijing, China in 2014 (BUA_2014), 2015 (BUA_2015), 2017 (BUA_2017), 2018 (BUA_2018), and 2019 (BUA_2019); at the Tingzizhuang Experimental Station, Beijing, China in 2017 (TZZ_2017) and 2018 (TZZ_2018); and at the Ma’anshan Dangtu Experimental Station, Anhui Province, China in 2017 (MAS_2017), 2018 (MAS_2018), and 2019 (MAS_2019). However, data on wild adzuki bean (Vigna angularis var. nipponensis) were only obtained at BUA_2014, TZZ_2017, TZZ_2018, and BUA_2019 (Table S1).

Wild adzuki were sown for seedling raising on May each year, with 20 seeds per accession. They were transplanted at the three-leaf stage, with a row spacing of 80 cm and a plant spacing of 20 cm. Cultivated adzuki were sown in mid-June, with 3 rows planted per material and 35 seeds per row, using a row spacing of 50 cm and a plant spacing of 10 cm, arranged in sequence. To ensure seed harvest, materials from southern regions were subjected to shading treatment: from late June to late July, they received 8 h of light per day for a total of 28 days. All plants were harvested individually upon seed maturity, and seeds were preserved accordingly [31].

​Analysis of seed iron content

Iron (Fe) content in adzuki bean seeds was quantified using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) [25]. After threshing, mature adzuki plants were cleaned with distilled water to remove surface impurities and then dried in an oven to constant weight. The dried seeds were ground into a homogeneous powder using a universal grinder. A 2.0000 g sample of the powder was accurately weighed, placed in a pre-weighed crucible, and carefully heated on a hot plate or in a muffle furnace to complete carbonization under ventilation until no smoke was observed. Subsequently, the sample was ashed in a muffle furnace at 600 °C for 5–6 h. After cooling to below 200 °C, the crucible was transferred to a desiccator to cool to room temperature. 10 mL of 0.5 mol/L hydrochloric acid was added to the crucible, heated, and stirred with a glass rod until the ash was completely dissolved. The solution was filtered and diluted to 100 mL with deionized water. Iron concentration was measured using an atomic absorption spectrometer (AAS ZEEnit700P, Analytik Jena) equipped with an iron hollow cathode lamp (current 6 mA, wavelength 248.3 nm). A standard curve was constructed using Fe standard solutions (0, 1.00, 2.00, 3.00, 4.00, 5.00 mg/L), and the absorbance of the samples was measured and converted to iron concentration (mg/kg) using the standard curve. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the average value was reported.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to explore the genetic structure of the adzuki bean accessions. The analysis was conducted using the smartpca module within the EIGENSOFT 7.2.1 software package [32]. The analysis began by converting the genotype data from the vcf file format to the EIGENSTRAT format using the convertf utility provided by EIGENSOFT. Following this conversion, the smartpca command was employed to compute the first five principal components (eigenvectors). Finally, these results were visualized using the ggplot2 package in the R statistical environment, generating a scatter plot to represent the data’s principal components [33].

Genome-wide association analysis and haplotype analysis

A genome-wide association study was conducted to identify genomic regions associated with iron content in adzuki bean. A total of 3,136,004 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified by aligning the sequencing data against the adzuki bean reference genome “Jingnong 6”. PLINK v1.9 was used to filter these SNPs. SNPs with a missing rate exceeding 20% were excluded using the parameter “--geno 0.2”, resulting in the removal of 54,953 SNPs; SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05 were further filtered out using the parameter “--maf 0.05”. After filtering, a total of 1,022,988 high-quality SNPs were retained for subsequent genome-wide association study (GWAS) analysis. The analysis utilized a mixed linear model implemented in the GAPIT 3.4 software package [34], incorporating principal component analysis (PCA) and kinship matrix (K) to account for population structure and relatedness. A significance threshold of P = 1 × 10− 4 was applied to identify significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), determined via the 1000 permutation tests to visualize association signals. Manhattan and quantile-quantile (QQ) plots were generated using CMplot 4.5.1 [35] to visualize the GWAS results. Considering the average linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay distance across the adzuki bean population (r² = 0.35, corresponding to 78.4 kb), a quantitative trait locus (QTL) was defined as a genomic region extending 100 kb upstream and downstream of significant SNPs. To ensure robustness, a single QTL was required to contain at least two significant, adjacent SNPs separated by a distance of 100 kb or less, or a single highly significant SNP [36].

To identify candidate genes underlying the identified QTLs, a comparative genomics approach was employed. The protein sequences of genes located within each QTL interval were subjected to BLAST searches against protein databases of Arabidopsis thaliana (TAIR database: https://www.arabidopsis.org/) and rice (Oryza sativa; RAP-DB: https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/tools/blast) [37, 38]. Homologous genes in these model organisms were identified, and their annotations and functional descriptions were used to inform the selection of candidate genes for adzuki bean seed iron content. Haplotype analysis was performed using adzuki bean varieties from full population. Missing genotype data were imputed using the default parameters of Beagle 5.4 [39]. Haplotype analysis was then conducted, focusing on variations in the coding and promoter regions of the selected candidate genes to investigate potential functional variants associated with iron content [40]. Excel software was used to group adzuki bean varieties carrying different haplotypes. Haplotypes that contained at least five varieties were used for statistical testing. We calculated the differences in phenotypic value between haplotypes of each gene using one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-tests. Duncan multiple range tests were used for comparisons, when the results of one-way ANOVA were significant (P < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using a combination of Microsoft Excel 2021, GraphPad Prism 10, and IBM SPSS Statistics 25 [40]. Data preprocessing and mean calculations were conducted in Excel. GraphPad Prism 10 was used to create box plots visualizing the data and to perform t-tests and one-way ANOVAs to compare means between groups. Post-hoc tests were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 25 to determine statistical significance, and significant differences were indicated using letter-based annotations.

Results

Phenotype variation of iron content in adzuki germplasm from multiple years and ecological regions

Seed iron content was assessed in a collection of 220 cultivated and 100 wild adzuki accessions grown across three field environments over six years (2014–2019). A wide range of seed iron content was observed, varying from 20.95 to 195.33 mg/kg (Fig. 1 and Table S1). The highest iron concentration was found in 2018 at the Ma’anshan Dangtu experimental station in Anhui province (Fig. 1c), approximately nine times higher than the lowest value recorded in 2018 at the Beijing University of Agricultural experimental base. The majority of adzuki bean varieties showed seed iron content between 60 and 80 mg/kg. However, substantial variation in seed iron content was evident both across different locations in the same year and across different years in the same location, highlighting the significant impact of environmental factors. Furthermore, the range of seed iron content was greater among cultivated adzuki compared to wild adzuki under the same environmental conditions (Fig. 1d-f). These results underscore the importance of considering environmental influences when studying adzuki seed iron content.

A significant difference in average seed Fe content was observed between wild and cultivated adzuki germplasm (Table 1), with wild germplasm exhibiting substantially higher levels. This finding suggests that the domestication process may have resulted in a decrease in seed Fe content. The maximum seed Fe content observed in wild adzuki germplasm (191.28 mg/kg, a BLUP value) was far greater than that observed in cultivated adzuki germplasm (136.72 mg/kg), highlighting the potential for identifying high-iron genotypes within wild adzuki populations. While no significant difference in average Fe content was found between landraces and improved cultivars of cultivated adzuki subgroups (P > 0.05), the range of Fe content was significantly smaller among improved cultivars. Analysis of the coefficient of variation (CV) revealed greater variability in seed Fe content within wild adzuki germplasm compared to cultivated adzuki germplasm (Table 1). This high level of variation in wild adzuki germplasm underscores its potential as a valuable resource for improving Fe content in cultivated adzuki through breeding programs.

Significant differences in seed Fe content were observed between wild and cultivated adzuki accessions depending on the environment and year (Table S1). Wild accessions showed significantly higher iron content than cultivated accessions in the BUA_2014 environment. Conversely, in the TZZ_2017 and TZZ_2018 environments, cultivated varieties displayed slightly higher Fe content, although this difference was not statistically significant. These findings highlight the influence of environmental factors on seed Fe content. Analysis of best linear unbiased predictors (BLUPs) [41] based on multi-year, multi-location data demonstrated significantly greater long-term iron accumulation stability in wild adzuki (Fig. 2a). While improved cultivars generally exhibited slightly higher average Fe content than landraces across years and locations, this difference was not statistically significant. However, a significant difference was observed in the BUA_2014 environment, where landraces showed significantly lower Fe content than improved cultivars (P < 0.05). BLUP analysis across multiple years and locations revealed no significant difference in Fe content between landraces and improved cultivars, suggesting that Fe content has not been a major focus of selection in modern adzuki breeding programs (Fig. 2b).

Analysis of Fe content variation among different adzuki subpopulations

A principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to investigate the genetic relationships among different adzuki subgroups. PCA of the genotypes revealed that the first principal component (PCA1) accounted for 42.1% of the total variation, indicating its major role in differentiating the adzuki subgroups. In contrast, PCA2 contributed only 4.6% of the variation. The PCA plot (Fig. 2c) showed a clear separation of the Wild_SC group (wild adzuki from southern China) from other groups, positioned in the upper-middle region of the negative PCA1 axis. This suggests that this group possesses unique characteristics related to iron content or other correlated traits. While the Cul_SC and Cul_NC subgroups (cultivated adzuki from southern and northern China, respectively) were clustered closely, they remained distinguishable. However, the Cul_JK (cultivated adzuki from Japan and Korea), Wild_N (wild adzuki from northern China, Japan and Korea), and Semi_WildN (semi-wild adzuki from northern China, Japan and Korea) subgroups showed significant overlap. The mix group occupied an intermediate position between the Wild_SC and the cultivated subgroups. Analysis of seed Fe content revealed that differences among the three cultivated subgroups (Cul_SC, Cul_NC, Cul_JK) were not statistically significant (Fig. 2d). The Wild_SC group exhibited similar Fe content to the cultivated groups. In contrast, the Wild_N and mix groups showed significantly higher Fe content than both the cultivated groups and the Wild_SC group, with Wild_N displaying the highest average Fe content. The Semi_Wild_N group had a mean Fe content slightly lower than that of Wild_N (P > 0.05) and comparable to the cultivated groups, but its CV for Fe content was higher than that of the cultivated groups and Wild_N (Fig. 2d).

GWAS and candidate gene analysis

A genome-wide association study was conducted to identify genetic loci associated with Fe content in adzuki bean. The analysis utilized iron content phenotypes from 320 adzuki accessions and genotypic data consisting of 1,022,988 SNPs and the distribution and density of them across all chromosomes were shown in Fig S1. Two mixed linear models, BLINK and MLM, were applied to account for population structure and relatedness among accessions [42]. Each significant SNP, along with a 100 kb flanking region, was defined as a quantitative trait locus (QTL), considering the linkage disequilibrium specific to the adzuki genome (Table S2-8 and Methods). A total of 39 QTLs significantly associated with Fe content were detected, distributed across chromosomes 1 to 11, with chromosomes 3 and 10 showing the highest number of QTLs (7) (Fig. 3). Replicated GWAS analyses were performed across five environments (TZZ_2017, MAS_2018, BUA_2018, MAS_2019, and BUA_2019) from 2017 to 2019, revealing 5, 8, 2, 6, and 8 significant loci, respectively. The qFe6.1 locus showed consistent association across two environments (TZZ_2017 and MAS_2019) (Fig. 3a, d). Additionally, qFe1.3, qFe7.3, and qFe10.6 were also detected under both two models (Fig. 3 and Table S7, 8). Subsequent candidate genes analysis will be performed within these QTL regions.

Candidate gene analysis was performed within twelve QTL regions associated with Fe content (qFe1.3, qFe3.5, qFe3.7, qFe4.1, qFe5.1, qFe6.1, qFe7.2, qFe7.3, qFe8.1, qFe9.2, qFe10.6, and qFe10.7) (Fig. 3 and Table S7, 8). Protein sequences of genes located within these QTL intervals were aligned against those of model plants, Arabidopsis thaliana and rice, to identify homologous proteins. Based on the functional annotation of these homologs, and informed by previously published research on Fe metabolism and transport, twelve candidate genes were selected (Table S7, 8). These genes, previously reported to be involved in Fe uptake, transport, or metabolism in Arabidopsis or rice, include: Vigan01g178400 (iron-regulated transporter 1, IRT1), Vigan03g004100 (basic leucine zipper protein), Vigan03g112600 (ferredoxin 1), Vigan04g060900 (Fe-S cluster protein), Vigan05g154100 (Rieske FeS protein), Vigan06g159700 (ABC Transporter), Vigan07g046800 (Fe-S metabolism associated domain containing protein), Vigan08g146100 (FER-like regulator of iron uptake), Vigan09g155500 (zinc transporter ZIF2), Vigan07g168200 (nicotianamine aminotransferase), Vigan10g048300 (metal-nicotianamine transporter YSL7), and Vigan10g083800 (MATE transporter) (Table 2).

Important candidate genes and functional annotations identified in GWAS for Fe content across different environments in adzuki bean

Within the candidate interval for the QTL qFe1.3, we pinpointed Vigan01g178400 as a candidate gene influencing Fe content (Table 2 and S7, 8). This gene encodes a protein that shares homology with IRT1 (Iron-Regulated Transporter 1) from Arabidopsis thaliana. IRT1 functions as a primary transporter for high-affinity metal uptake when iron availability is limited. Arabidopsis knockout mutants lacking IRT1 display a chlorotic phenotype and exhibit significant growth retardation in soil, ultimately resulting in mortality [43]. These detrimental effects can be alleviated through the external administration of iron. Within the candidate region for the qFe3.5 locus, we identified two candidate genes for iron transport (Table 2 and S7). One is Vigan03g004100, which encodes adzuki VabZIP43. This protein is homologous to Arabidopsis thaliana AT1G75390 (bZIP44) and rice bZIP43. In Arabidopsis, bZIP44 interacts with MYB10 and MYB72 to activate the transcriptional activity of NAS2 and NAS4, promoting NA synthesis and improving iron transport, thereby enhancing plant tolerance to Fe deficiency stress [44]. The other potential candidate gene is Vigan03g004400, which encodes an unreported iron-regulated metal transporter.

We identified Vigan04g060900, a gene encoding an iron-sulfur cluster assembly protein, within the qFe4.1 region (Table 2 and S7). These proteins, like IscU in bacteria, are essential for building iron-sulfur clusters, which are then incorporated into other proteins. Reduced expression of the rice homolog (OsIsu) under iron deficiency [45] underscores the critical role of iron-sulfur cluster biosynthesis in plant processes such as photosynthesis and iron balance, ultimately impacting survival and development. Vigan07g168200, located at the qFe7.3 locus, encodes a nicotinamidase aminotransferase involved in the synthesis of iron carriers in adzuki bean (Table 2 and S7). In rice, OsNAAT1 encodes a nicotinamide aminotransferase that catalyzes the conversion of nicotianamine (NA) to deoxymugineic acid (DMA), a precursor to the iron carriers in graminaceous plants [46]. The qFe3.7 region contains Vigan03g112600, encoding mitochondrial ferredoxin 1 (MFDX1), a protein vital for Fe-S clusters in photosynthetic electron transport and chlorophyll processing (Table 2 and S8) [47]. Studies of the Arabidopsis pic1 mutant, displaying chloroplast iron deficiency and altered Fe-S cluster metabolism [48], highlight the importance of this pathway. Furthermore, Vigan10g083800 (qFe10.7) is highly similar to Arabidopsis ELS1, a MATE transporter linked to leaf senescence and iron balance (Table 2 and S8) [49].

Haplotype analyses of six QTL genes for Fe content

To further investigate the genetic variation of candidate genes and their distribution within the adzuki germplasms, we performed haplotype analysis using resequencing data, followed by an analysis of differences in Fe content among different haplotypes.

Haplotype analysis was conducted on the Vigan01g178400 (VaIRT1) gene to investigate its genetic variation and distribution among 320 adzuki accessions. Resequencing data revealed 12 SNPs within the VaIRT1 gene region, all located in the promoter region (Fig. 4a). No non-synonymous mutations were identified in the coding sequence, suggesting strong selective pressure maintaining the coding sequence. Based on these SNPs, three haplotypes were defined. Hap1 was the most prevalent, found in 276 accessions, including nearly all cultivated adzuki (Cul_SC, Cul_NC, Cul_JK), northern wild adzuki, semi-wild adzuki, and mix adzuki. The widespread presence of Hap1 might obscure small effects, indicating the involvement of multiple genes or environmental factors in iron content determination (Fig. 4a). Conversely, the Wild_SC group retained ancestral haplotypes (Hap2 and Hap3). Twenty Wild_SC samples exhibited Hap2, differing from Hap1 by a single SNP in the promoter region. This SNP’s appearance resulted in the gain of three new cis-acting elements (GT1CONSENSUS, IBOXCORE, and SREATMSD, sequentially) compared to Hap1. This SNP influence VaIRT1 gene expression, potentially explaining the higher Fe content observed in Hap2 and its maintenance in Wild_SC under natural selection (Fig. 4b and S3).

A total of 18 polymorphic sites were found within the Vigan07g168200 gene region. Analysis on the coding sequence revealed two SNPs, including one non-synonymous SNP (SNP_22250395) causing a valine to isoleucine substitution at amino acid position 9. The remaining 16 SNPs were located in the promoter and 3’-UTR regions (Fig. 4c). Four haplotypes were identified, with two being major and two rare. Hap1 was the most common, present in almost all Cul_NC and Cul_JK accessions, all Wild_N and Semi_WildN accessions, and approximately half of the Cul_SC accessions. In contrast, Hap2 was predominantly found in Wild_SC accessions and a subset of southern Chinese cultivars. A statistically significant difference in Fe content was observed between Hap1 and Hap2, with Hap2 showing significantly higher Fe levels (Fig. 4d). qPCR (Quantitative real-time PCR) analysis showed that the gene expression level of Vigan07g168200 in Hap2 was higher than that in Hap1(Fig. S3).

Analysis of the Vigan10g083800 gene region revealed five polymorphic variants, all located within the promoter region. No polymorphisms were detected in the coding sequence, indicating high conservation. The analysis identified four haplotypes (Fig. 4e), with two being major. All cultivated adzuki accessions belonged to Hap1. Hap2 consisted of only three Semi_WildN accessions. Hap3 included exclusively southern Chinese wild adzuki accessions, and Hap4 contained only Wild_N accessions. A significant difference in iron content was observed between Hap1 (51.37 mg/kg) and Hap3 (79.85 mg/kg) (Fig. 4f), suggesting that promoter region polymorphisms may affect gene expression and consequently Fe accumulation.

The Vigan03g004400 gene region contained eleven SNPs located within the promoter and 3’-UTR regions. Six haplotypes were identified from these SNPs. Hap2 was widely distributed across all populations, suggesting it may represent a core haplotype selected during domestication (Fig. 5a). Interestingly, Hap1 and Hap3 showed significantly higher Fe content than Hap2, potentially indicating positive selection for related agronomic traits. Hap3 was exclusively present in Wild_N accessions, possibly reflecting unique adaptive characteristics. To assess the differences in iron content among Hap1, Hap2, and Hap3, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. The ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences in iron content between Hap1 and Hap2 (P < 0.05) and between Hap3 and Hap2 (P < 0.01). However, no statistically significant difference was found between Hap1 and Hap3 (Fig. 5b). qPCR analysis showed that the gene expression level of Vigan03g004400 in Hap2 was lower than that in Hap1 and Hap3 (Fig. S3).

Ten haplotypes of the candidate gene Vigan03g112600 were identified based on ten SNPs located in its promoter and 3’-UTR regions (Fig. 5c). Hap1 through Hap5 represent the major haplotypes. The distribution of haplotypes shows a strong association with geographic origin and population type. The Wild_SC population is largely comprised of Hap1 and Hap3. Hap3 encompasses 93 adzuki accessions, with 72 belonging to the Cul_NC subgroup. Hap5 contains 81 accessions, 55 of which are cultivated adzuki varieties from Japan and Korea. Hap2 displays the highest average iron content and is predominantly found in Japanese and Korean adzuki varieties. Hap4 and Hap6 are specific to cultivated adzuki varieties from southern China. Hap9 and Hap10, both derived from the Semi_WildN subgroup, show exceptionally high Fe content (approximately 150 mg/kg), possibly representing high-iron genotypes within wild or weedy populations. A box plot and qPCR analysis illustrate that both the Fe content and gene expression level of Vigan03g112600 of Hap3 and Hap5 is significantly lower than that of Hap2 (Fig. 5d and S3).

In the Vigan04g060900 gene region, eleven polymorphic variants were identified, including nine SNPs in the promoter region, one in the 3’-UTR region, and one in the coding sequence. This coding SNP (SNP_7738673) results in a start codon mutation. This mutation alters protein translation, causing the ribosome to utilize a downstream start codon and producing a truncated protein. Six haplotypes were identified across the entire population (Fig. 5e). Hap1 is the most prevalent haplotype (81.9%), widely distributed among all cultivated adzuki varieties (Cul_NC, Cul_JK, Cul_SC). All 18 accessions of Hap2 are cultivated adzuki varieties from southern China, whereas Hap6 is predominantly found in wild adzuki accessions from the same region. Hap6 shows significantly higher iron content and gene expression level compared to Hap1 (Fig. 5f and S3). Hap3, Hap4, and Hap5 are rare haplotypes.

Discussion

Genome-wide association studies are a well-established approach for identifying quantitative trait loci associated with complex traits in crops such as rice, wheat, and maize [50, 51]. Although GWAS has been increasingly applied to various traits in legumes [28, 29], its application to adzuki bean remains scarce. Moreover, a significant knowledge gap exists concerning the genetic basis of iron content in adzuki bean and other legumes. This study addresses this gap by performing a GWAS on Fe content in adzuki seed, resulting in the identification of 39 significantly associated loci. Subsequent candidate genes and haplotype analyses revealed six candidate genes potentially involved in iron transport, metabolism, and regulation. These findings lay the groundwork for future studies aiming to elucidate the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying iron content regulation and iron biofortification improvement in adzuki.

Iron is an essential micronutrient for human health, and iron deficiency is globally recognized by the World Health Organization as the most prevalent nutritional disorder. Approximately 1.6 billion people worldwide suffer from iron deficiency anemia (IDA), with severe impacts on cognitive development, immune function, and economic productivity [27]. Staple crops such as rice inherently contain low levels of bioavailable iron. However, deep processing of cereals (e.g., rice polishing) further depletes micronutrient content. For instance, while brown rice of modern high-yielding varieties contains 6.3–24.4 mg/kg Fe and ​15.3–58.4 mg/kg Zn​ [52], polishing reduces these to ≤ 2 mg/kg Fe and ≤ 12 mg/kg Zn [53]—levels insufficient to meet daily requirements. In contrast, adzuki bean emerges as a potent dietary iron source. With an average iron content of ​7.95 mg/100 g​ across germplasm accessions in this study—surpassing polished rice by > 300% and aligning with HarvestPlus targets—adzuki bean offers a sustainable solution to combat IDA. Its high iron bioavailability, coupled with traditional consumption in Asia, positions it as a strategic crop for nutritional security. Consequently, deciphering the genetic architecture of iron accumulation in adzuki bean germplasm is imperative. This research not only advances legume biofortification but also provides molecular tools for developing iron-enriched varieties, ultimately bridging agricultural production and human nutritional resilience.

In natural, competitive settings, the maximization of nutrient density per seed is critical for ensuring seed viability and successful seedling establishment [54, 55]. Conversely, the selective pressures inherent in agricultural cultivation favor the maximization of total yield, encompassing both the number and size of seeds, often at the cost of per-seed nutrient concentration [56, 57]. Human intervention in the domestication process effectively prioritizes increased yield, even if this results in a decrease in the nutrient density of individual seeds [58, 59]. The observed inverse relationship between iron concentration and total iron output in cultivated adzuki bean serves as a compelling illustration of a common trend in plant domestication: the prioritization of yield and other desirable agronomic traits frequently comes at the expense of nutrient density. Although cultivated adzuki bean exhibits a lower iron content per unit mass compared to its wild progenitors, the significant increase in seed size leads to a substantial increase in the total iron yield per seed. This phenomenon underscores the shift in selective pressures from the maximization of nutrient density under competitive wild conditions to the maximization of overall yield within the cultivated environment.

The trend observed in adzuki bean is not isolated; many domesticated crops exhibit similar patterns [58]. Research on maize, for instance, reveals that modern high-yielding varieties possess lower protein and micronutrient levels per kernel compared to their ancestral forms. This is a consequence of selecting for larger kernel size and increased yield, resulting in a dilution of nutrient density [60, 61]. Similarly, the domestication of rice has led to decreased levels of certain vitamins and minerals in some cultivars [52, 62]. These observations collectively suggest that human selection for increased yield frequently compromises nutrient density—a trade-off driven by carbon allocation priorities [57]. It is crucial to acknowledge, however, that this is not a universally applicable trend. In some cases, domestication has led to enhanced nutrient concentrations in certain crops [63, 64]. Studies indicate that the domestication of some legumes, for example, has resulted in increased protein content [63]. The ultimate outcome of domestication on nutrient levels is contingent upon several factors, including the specific traits targeted during selection, the species’ genetic architecture, and the environmental conditions prevalent during the domestication process.

The adzuki bean example serves as a valuable illustration of how human intervention shapes the nutritional composition of crops, creating a trade-off between nutrient density and total yield. The increased total iron content per seed could prove beneficial to population health. Further research is necessary to unravel the underlying genetic mechanisms driving these changes in adzuki bean and other crops, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay between yield, nutrient concentration, and the domestication process.

Phenotypic data for iron content, collected across multiple years and locations from a natural population, were analyzed using Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) methodology. GWAS using these BLUP-estimated iron content values identified six QTL for iron content across the eleven chromosomes of adzuki bean. Among these, the major QTL, qFe1.3, contains 14 annotated genes within its candidate interval. One candidate gene, Vigan01g178400, encodes a Fe2+ transporter homologous to Arabidopsis thaliana AT1G31260 and Oryza sativa Os03g0667300. This gene is designated as VaIRT1, the adzuki bean iron-regulated transporter 1. Arabidopsis AtIRT1, a member of the ZIP (Zrt/Irt-like protein) family of transporters, was cloned by functional complementation in an iron-deficient yeast strain [65]. AtIRT1 is expressed in root epidermal cells and localized to the plasma membrane [43]. AtIRT1 exhibits broad specificity for divalent heavy metals, mediating most root iron uptake and also the transport of zinc, manganese, cobalt, and cadmium under iron-deficient conditions. irt1 mutants are characterized by severe chlorosis and specific developmental defects [66]. Overexpression of OsIRT1 in rice resulted in enhanced iron deficiency tolerance during the seedling stage. Transgenic plants exhibited increased sensitivity to excess zinc and cadmium, with elevated zinc and iron levels in shoots, roots, and mature seeds, suggesting that OsIRT1 could be used to improve micronutrient levels in rice [67]. In this study, haplotype analysis of VaIRT1 in adzuki bean germplasm revealed three haplotypes. Adzuki bean varieties within Hap2 showed significantly higher iron content than those in Hap1. Sequence analysis identified a single nucleotide polymorphism as the differentiating factor, located in the VaIRT1 promoter region. Comparative analysis revealed that the C-to-T mutation at SNP_24573037 introduced three novel cis-acting elements. These cis-acting elements may enhance VaIRT1 gene expression, leading to increased iron content. This suggests that VaIRT1 may be a target for improving Fe content in adzuki bean seeds.

Conclusion

This study presents the first genome-wide association analysis of seed iron content in adzuki bean, utilizing 320 accessions across multiple years and locations. We identified 39 QTLs distributed across chromosomes 1 to 11, with 12 candidate genes implicated in iron transport, metabolism, and regulation, including Vigan01g178400 (VaIRT1) and Vigan10g048300 (VaYSL7). Phenotypic analysis revealed wild accessions have significantly higher Fe content than cultivated ones. Haplotype analysis revealed functional variations, such as a promoter SNP in VaIRT1 that introduces novel cis-elements and correlates with elevated Fe content in wild adzuki germplasm. These findings clarify the genetic architecture of iron homeostasis in adzuki bean and provide valuable markers and candidate genes for breeding high-iron varieties, contributing to addressing global iron deficiency.
