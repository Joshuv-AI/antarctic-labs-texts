Integrated assessment of CI engine combustion and catalyst durability with graphene- infused Pongamia pinnata biodiesel
Authors: B. B. Ganesha, K. N. Arun Kumar, C. G. Ramachandra, Narasimha K Bailkeri, Haiter Lenin Allasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The need for sustainable fuels in compression ignition (CI) engines is highlighted by the escalating energy crisis and stringent emissions regulations. This study examined diesel- Pongamia methyl Ester (PME) blends (B10, B20, B30) and their graphene-infused counterparts (30 ppm; B10Gr, B20Gr, and B30Gr) at 1500 rpm under various loads in a single cylinder CI engine. In addition to the comparatively unexplored topic of catalytic converter durability, the investigation concentrated on combustion, performance, and emission behaviour. Because of the improved atomisation and catalytic effects of graphene, B20Gr produced the highest brake thermal efficiency (27.5%) among the tested fuels, outperforming both neat diesel (25.8%) and plain B20 (25.1%). Nox levels remained high even though biodiesel blends showed a decrease in CO and HC emissions. After 500 h of operation, energy- dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis showed accelerated carbon deposition on the catalytic converter, which produced a 33% reduction in service life with B20 and B20Gr compared to diesel. The results indicate that graphene-enriched Pongamia bio-diesel can greatly enhance engine performance, however for long term sustainability, strategies to prevent after-treatment degradation are necessary for its wide spread use.

Introduction

Economic stability and environmental health are seriously threatened by the un relating rise in the world’s energy demand, which is mostly supplied by fossil fuels. For developing nations like India, which imports over 80% of its crude oil requirements, this dependency represents a substantial strain on foreign exchange reserves and energy security1. The concurrent rise in petroleum prices and government rules aimed at reducing greenhouse gas emissions have strengthened the search for renewable and sustainable alternative fuels2. The transportation and power sectors, heavily reliant on CI engines due to their superior thermal efficiency and durability, are primary targets for this energy transitions3.

Biodiesel produced as monoalkyl esters of long chain fatty acids from vegetable oils or animal fats, has gained attention as a promising substitute for conventional diesel4. It is renewable, eco-friendly, non-toxic, and offers a favourable energy balance. Its application can notably lower emissions of UBHC, CO, and particulate matter5,6. India’s vast agro- climatic conditions supports the growth of over three hundred species of trees capable of producing oil-baring seeds, many of which are non-edible thus avoiding the food versus fuel debate7. Among these Pongamia pinnata (Karanja or Honge) is a major non edible oil source widely available in regions like Karnataka, showing higher potential as a feedstock for biofuel production8. The direct utilization of raw vegetable oils in CI engines presents serval challenges owing to their high viscosity, low volatility, and polyunsaturated nature. These properties result in inadequate atomization, incomplete combustion, carbon deposition and consequently shorten engine durability9. Transesterification is the most common process to convert these oils into biodiesel, reducing viscosity and improving their properties to be more akin to diesel10. Despite this improvement, biodiesel blends often suffer from lower calorific value, higher Nox emissions, and slightly inferior performance compared to pure diesel11,12.

The use of fuel additives, especially nano materials has proven to be successful way to address this issue13. They improve air-fuel mixing and vaporisation through catalytic action made possible by their surface to volume ratio. Cerium oxide14, alumina15, titanium dioxide16, carbon nanotubes17, and graphene18,19 are examples of metallic and carbon-based nanoparticles that have been shown to improve brake thermal efficiency, reduce fuel consumption, and lower emissions, however their effect on Nox is still unclear20. Graphene’s two-dimensional structure, superior thermal conductivity, and chemical stability make it an advanced additive for enhancing combustion and emission reduction are being investigated22–26.

A of research has been done on how biodiesel and nano-additives affect engine emissions and performance27, but there is still a big in our understanding of how these fuels will affect engine after treatment systems in the long term, especially catalytic converters28–32. Catalytic converters are necessary to meet modern emissions standards because they oxidise CO and HC and Lower Nox. Their effectiveness and durability are very important. Even though biodiesel burns cleaner in some ways, it can cause different deposition chemistries on the catalyst substrate. Adding nano-additives like graphene, which is basically carbon, could make carbon deposits, worse which could cause the catalytic system to clog up and stop working33. There is still a lot of research to be done how well systems hold up when exposed to biodiesel and nano biodiesel exhaust streams for long period of time.

This study aims to fill in the gaps in current research by using a combustion of experiments with three main goals. First, to assess the combustion and performance attributes of a CI engine utilising diesel, Pongamia biodiesel blends (B10, B20, B30) and their graphene nano particles improve the performance of Pongamia biodiesel. Third, to use SEM and EDS analyses to look at the composition and rate of deposit buildup and the durability of a catalytic converter under different fuel conditions. This research thoroughly examines the use of Pongamia-based biodiesel enhanced with graphene additives, emphasizing its effects on engine efficiency as well as the durability and consistent functioning of emission control systems over time. Novel contributions of the present study include 500-hour catalytic converter durability assessment under biodiesel and nano-biodiesel operation, SEM-EDS based deposit morphology and elemental characterization, catalytic converter lifespan estimation using deposit growth measurements and integrated evaluation of combustion, performance, emissions, and after-treatment durability.

Methodology

Fuel preparation and characterization

Pongamia pinnata methyl ester (PME, also referred to as B100) was obtained from the Biofuel Research, Information and Demonstration Centre (BRIDC) at PESCE, Mandya. Conventional diesel served as the base fuel for preparing the blends. Mixtures labelled B10, B20, and B30 were produced by combining diesel with 10%, 20%, and 30% PME by volume. To ensure proper mixing, each blend was thoroughly homogenized using a magnetic stirrer at 1000 rpm for a duration of one hour.

Graphene nanoparticles (Sigma Aldrich) with an average particle size of 22.5–26 nm, a surface area of 492 m²/gm, and 99.5% purity were used as the additive. A two-stage process was employed to create stable nano-fuel blends. First, 30 ppm of graphene nanoparticles were dispersed in the respective biodiesel blends (B10, B20, B30). An ionic surfactant, Sodium Dodecyl Sulphate (SDS), was used to ensure uniform dispersion and prevent agglomeration. Second, this mixture was subjected to ultrasonication for 45 min using a probe-type ultrasonicator to achieve a stable suspension, resulting in the nano-fuel blends B10Gr, B20Gr, and B30Gr. The graphene dosage of 30 ppm was selected based on preliminary stability tests and earlier literature34,35 reporting improved combustion enhancement without significant nanoparticle agglomeration at low concentrations.

Experimentation

IC Engine set up used in this research is Kirloskar make VCR Diesel Engine coupled with an eddy current type dynamometer having power 3.50 kW @ 1500 rpm which is 1 Cylinder, Four stroke, Constant Speed, Water Cooled, Diesel Engine, with Cylinder Bore 87.50(mm), Stroke Length 110.00(mm) and Compression Ratio 17.5:1. as shown in Fig. 1. The detailed specifications of the test engine are provided in Table 1. The engine operated at a constant speed of 1500 rpm while being subjected to varying load conditions (0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100%). Experiments were conducted at ambient conditions of 29 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 60 ± 5%. Lubricating oil and air filters were replaced after every 100 operating hours during durability testing. A fresh catalytic converter was used for each individual fuel durability experiment.

The experimental setup was fitted with measuring instruments to record key data related to engine performance, combustion behavior, and exhaust emissions. Performance measurements included brake power estimation using an eddy current dynamometer. Fuel consumption was determined volumetrically with a calibrated burette and stopwatch, while air intake was measured through an orifice meter coupled with a U-tube manometer. These measurements were subsequently used to compute brake thermal efficiency (BTE) and brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC).

For combustion analysis, a water- cooled, flush- mounted piezoelectric pressure transducer (PCB Piezotronics) recorded in- cylinder pressure, and a crank angle encoder with 10 resolution monitored crankshaft position. The sensor signals were acquired and processed using a high-speed NI USB-6210 data acquisition system, with data from 100 consecutive cycles averaged to calculate pressure rise rate, heat release rate (HRR), ignition delay, and combustion duration.

Catalytic converter durability analysis

A commercially available catalytic converter was installed in the engine exhaust line. The engine was operated continuously for 500 h for each test fuel: Diesel, B20, and B20Gr. post-testing, the catalytic converter was carefully dismantled. The monolithic substrate, made of cordierite ceramic, was extracted. To analyze deposit formation, small samples (10 mm x 10 mm x 10 mm) were cut from both the reduction and oxidation sections of the substrate. These samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to make them electrically conductive for SEM analysis. The morphology and thickness of the deposits were examined using a Scanning Electron Microscope (VEGA3 TESCAN) integrated with an Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) detector. EDS analysis provided the elemental composition of the deposits. The deposition thickness was measured at multiple points, and the average growth was calculated. This data was extrapolated to estimate the total operational hours required for the deposits to completely block the catalytic converter channels (assumed at 1200 μm thickness), providing a measure of its durability for each fuel.

Combustion and performance analysis

Combustion characteristics

Figures 3 and 4 present the variation of i- cylinder pressure with crank angle for all tested fuels under full- load conditions. Diesel exhibited a peak pressure of 65.56 bar at 3690 CA. In contrast, biodiesel blends (B10,B20,B30) Showed slightly lower peak pressure (64.71, 64.98, and 64.14 respectively compared to both diesel and their corresponding base bio blends. Among them, B20Gr recorded the highest peak pressure of 66.29 bar. This improvement is attributed to graphene nanoparticle’s large surface area and catalytic properties, which improves fuel atomization, encourage more effective air-fuel mixing, and speed up combustion, increasing heat release close to top dead centre14,18.

The heat release rate (HRR) profiles shown in Fig. 5 further reinforce these observations. In general, biodiesel blends exhibit lower peak HRR values compared to conventional diesel. However, in the case of the B20 Gr blend, the incorporation of graphene nanoparticles leads to a noticeable increase in peak HRR, bringing it close to or even exceeding that of diesel. This behavior indicates a more rapid combustion process, which can be attributed to the improved ignition characteristics resulting from the presence of graphene nanoparticles.

Figure 6 presents the variation in ignition delay (ID), which refers to the period between the start of fuel injection and the initiation of combustion. As engine load increases, the ID consistently reduces for all tested fuels due to the rise in in-cylinder temperature. Compared to conventional diesel, biodiesel fuels show relatively longer ignition delays at the same load conditions, with the B30 blend exhibiting the highest delay, mainly because of its greater viscosity and reduced volatility. However, incorporating graphene nanoparticles into the blends significantly decreases the ignition delay. This reduction can be linked to the superior thermal conductivity of graphene, which improves heat transfer to the fuel droplets, aids quicker evaporation, and facilitates earlier ignition19,20.

Figure 7 presents the combustion duration, defined as the time from the start of combustion 90% mass fraction burned. Biodiesel blends recorded a longer combustion duration (33–350 CA) compared to diesel (27–300 CA), indicating a 15–25% increase, mainly due to an extended diffusion combustion phase. The addition of graphene nanoparticles reduced the duration by approximately 3–50 CA across all blends, making it nearly comparable to diesel. This reduction is attributed to the enhanced oxidation kinetics and improved combustion completeness facilitated by the catalytic and thermal properties of graphene.

Performance characteristics

The variation of BTE with load is depicted in Fig. 8. The BTE for all fuels increased with load, reaching a maximum near full load. Due to their lower energy consent, the plain biodiesel blends B10, B20 and B30 showed lower BTE (24.3%, 25.1% and 23.1% respectively) compared to diesel (25.8%) at full load. Crucially, the addition of graphene nanoparticles improved the BTE for all nano-blends. The B20 Gr blend achieved the highest BTE of 27.5%, a significant improvement over both baseline and B20. This is a consequence of the enhanced combustion delay- driven by the catalytic effect of the nanoparticles, which components for the lower calorific value biodiesel.

Figure 9 illustrates the BSFC trends, which follows and inverse relationship with BTE. For all test fuels, BSFC decreased with increasing engine load. Plain biodiesel blends displayed higher BSFC compared to diesel, primarily due to their lower calorific value, necessitating greater fuel consumption to achieve the same power output with B30, recording the highest BSFC .In contrast, the nano-enhanced blends exhibited a noticeable reduction in BSFC, with B20 Gr achieving the lowest among all biodiesel and nano-biodiesel fuels. This improvement highlights the role of graphene nanoparticles in enchaining combustion efficiency and promoting better fuel utilization.

Durability analysis of catalytic converter

The durability of the catalytic converter is paramount for sustaining low emissions over the engine’s lifespan. SEM analysis provided clear visual evidence of deposit accumulation on the monolithic substrate’s channels after 500 h of operation with different fuels. The durability tests were conducted at full-load operation and 1500 rpm under ambient conditions of 27 ± 2 °C temperature and 60 ± 5% relative humidity. Fresh catalytic converters were used individually for Diesel, B20, and B20Gr fuels. Lubricating oil replacement and air filter cleaning were carried out after every 100 operating hours.

Figures 10 and 11 shows SEM images of the deposits in the reduction section for diesel, B20, and B20Gr fuels. The original channel dimensions were approximately 1100 μm x 1100 μm. For diesel, the average deposit thickness was measured at 208 μm (X-direction) and 124 μm (Y-direction). For B20 fuel, the deposition was markedly higher, at 331 μm and 421 μm, respectively. The B20Gr fuel exhibited the most severe deposition, with thicknesses of 393 μm and 333 μm.

A similar trend was observed in the oxidation section as shown in Figs. 12 and 13, though deposition was less severe than in the reduction section, as the exhaust gases pass through it after the reduction stage.

EDS analysis was conducted to determine the elemental composition of these deposits. The results are summarized in Table 2. The deposits were primarily composed of carbon. For diesel, the carbon content was 71.52% in the reduction section and 73.29% in the oxidation section. For B20 fuel, the carbon content increased to 78.38% and 74.45%, respectively. For B20Gr, the carbon content was the highest, reaching 81.40% in the reduction section. This confirms that combustion of biodiesel leads to exhaust streams with a higher tendency for carbonaceous deposit formation within the catalytic converter. The addition of graphene, pure carbon nanomaterial, further exacerbates this issue it can contribute directly to the particulate matter in exhaust or act as a nucleation site for deposit formation.

The deposition growth rate was calculated from the SEM measurements. By extra plating this linear growth rate, the total operational hours required for the deposits to reach a clogging thickness of 1200 m was estimated. For diesel, the estimated time to failure was approx. 8,000 h. Factoring in dynamic vibration, that is might dislodge some loose deposits the practical lifespan aligns with the manufacture’s claims of ~ 12,000 h. In stark contrast, the estimated lifespan for the catalytic converter using B20 and B20Gr fuels was only 3,200 and 3,00 h, respectively. After accounting for dynamic effects, the particle durability is reduced to 7,500 -8,000 h. This represents a 33% reduction in the operational life span of the catalytic converter when using biodiesel and nano-biodiesel blends compared to conventional diesel.

Conclusion

This work presents a detailed investigation of Pongamia biodiesel blends enhanced with graphene nano-additives in a compression ignition (CI) engine, with particular focus on the durability of the catalytic converter. The results show that the graphene-based B20 blend (B20Gr) delivers notable improvements in engine performance, including the highest recorded peak pressure (66.29 bar), increased brake thermal efficiency (27.5%), and reduced brake-specific fuel consumption, mainly due to finer atomization and more rapid combustion. However, SEM-EDS examination revealed increased carbon deposition, resulting in an approximate 33% decline in catalytic converter lifespan, which raises concerns regarding long-term applicability. Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of developing improved catalyst materials, incorporating effective fuel additives, and adopting suitable regeneration techniques. A comprehensive, system-level perspective that balances performance, emissions, and component longevity is essential for the sustainable implementation of nano-additive biodiesel fuels.
