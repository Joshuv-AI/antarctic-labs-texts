One chemistry, many pathways: Comparing carbon-negative cement, enhanced weathering, and ocean alkalinity enhancement
Authors: Chin-Hsien Cheng, Sasithorn Chornkrathok, Simon AT Redfern
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe compare carbon dioxide removal pathways involving carbon-negative cement and carbonate concrete (CNCm and CNCb), enhanced rock weathering (ERW), and ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE). All ultimately rely on alkalinity from enhanced weathering of alkaline silicate rocks, even when calcium and magnesium are extracted from seawater. We assess them using five practical criteria: carbonation efficiency, measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV), location flexibility, process optimization, and co-benefits. Bicarbonate-dominant carbon sequestration, including OAE, offers high carbonation efficiency, reasonable MRV, coastal or riverine deployment at moderate to alkaline pH, and lower energy use per unit alkalinity. However, co-production of carbon-negative cement or carbonate concrete may be economically most attractive by offsetting carbon storage costs, with further benefits from metal recovery and waste stabilization. We highlight the need for integrative design and systematic optimization.Keywords: decarbonization, enhanced weathering, carbonation, carbon dioxide removal, cement, ocean alkalinity enhancement

Introduction

Cement production accounts for approximately 6%–7% of global fossil CO2 emissions.1,2 Options to reduce these emissions range from incremental efficiency gains to more transformative approaches that store carbon in building materials and remove CO2 from the atmosphere.3,4,5,6,7 Since approximately 60%–65% of CO2 emissions from cement production result from carbonate decomposition, carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) is essential for achieving the sector’s net-zero emissions target by 2050.8 Recent estimates suggest that building materials could, in principle, store about one-third of global CO2 emissions if carbonate aggregates are produced by carbonating silicate minerals under complete transformation scenarios, although technical, economic, and logistical constraints will limit practical deployment.3 A central question is not simply whether building materials can store carbon, but where and how carbonation should occur to maximize removal efficiency and economic viability.

Carbon-negative cement and carbonate concrete (CNCm and CNCb) have not previously been systematically compared with other carbon dioxide removal (CDR) approaches, particularly enhanced rock weathering (ERW) and ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE). This perspective, as summarized in Figure 1, evaluates these interlinked pathways, demonstrating that their “carbon-negativity” ultimately relies on alkalinity generated from enhanced weathering of alkaline silicates. Subsequently, we provide an integrative comparison of these CDR approaches using five practical criteria: (1) carbonation efficiency—which offers a common carbon accounting framework across approaches associated with silicate weathering and carbonation; (2) ease of measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV), which determines whether the quantity of carbon sequestration can be credibly established for carbon markets; (3) location flexibility, including dependence on the availability of resources (e.g., water and concentrated CO2) and limiting factors in the downstream geochemical reactions; (4) ease of process optimization, influenced by process complexity, determining capital expenditure (CAPEX) and the energy-efficiency limits that shape operating expenses (OPEX); and (5) co-benefits beyond carbon sequestration for cost and profit optimization, which may drive technology development and deployment even if these pathways are less competitive on the first four criteria.

Comparison between different carbon dioxide removal approaches that rely on enhanced rock weathering.

We find that bicarbonate-dominant carbon sequestration (bCCS) approaches, in particular OAE, are generally more efficient for inorganic carbon (IC) sequestration, in terms of carbon per generated alkalinity from silicate weathering (ΔIC/ΔAlk), with simpler process setting and lower energy consumption. By contrast, production of CNCm and CNCb may be the most compelling once the commercial value of the products is considered.

Enhanced rock weathering drives the carbon negativity

All approaches considered here share a common foundation: ERW of silicate minerals as the source of alkalinity for driving carbon negativity. To avoid confusion, technologies that mitigate CO2 emission from cement industry that do not involve silicate carbonation are considered as other climate-mitigation solutions independent of ERW (see Note S1), thus they will not be compared and discussed in detail. In contrast, the carbon negativity of ERW, OAE, and CNCm/CNCb production all relies on enhanced weathering of alkaline silicates, followed by distinct carbonation pathways into carbonate or/and bicarbonate.

ERW typically refers to applying ground silicate minerals to terrestrial environments (e.g., agricultural lands). The increased surface areas enhance their weathering rate, dissolving the silicate and leaching out soluble metal cations (e.g., Mg2+ and Ca2+) to react with CO2, producing dissolved bicarbonate (reactions 1 and 2). As illustrated in Figure 1, the dissolving metal cations via ERW is the common first step for terrestrial ERW, bCCS (including OAE), and production of CNCm/CNCb. The different fates of the released metal cations and carbonation mark the distinction between these processes. The most important distinction between these CDR approaches is the different end products from the dissolved metal cations: bicarbonate (e.g., reactions 1 and 2), carbonate (e.g., reactions 1–3, moderated by pH and reaction 4), or other products without carbonation, such as stabilized by stronger non-carbonic acids (e.g., reaction 5). This is the key behind different carbonation efficiencies, and eventually other practicability concerns.

Carbonation efficiency

For OAE and other bCCS approaches, the optimal product is dominated by bicarbonate, maximizing the carbonation efficiency per generated alkalinity (ΔIC/ΔAlk). Here, additional alkalinity (ΔAlk) is defined by the increased difference between dissolved conservative cation and anion charges, i.e., Δ(∑ dissolved conservative cation charges−∑ dissolved conservative anion charges). If we ignore the dissolved silicic acids which are deemed to be polymerized into silica or/and secondary silicate minerals, the additional alkalinity can then be considered as the additional charges of soluble metal cations dissolved from silicates. Since each dissolved divalent cation from silicate can lock away up to two CO2 molecules into HCO3− (reactions 1 and 2), the ideal maximum of carbonation efficiency forming IC per additional alkalinity, ΔIC/ΔAlk, is 1, when all additional alkalinity is used to produce bicarbonate (e.g., NaHCO3). The additional alkalinity shifts the pH and equilibrium of reaction 4 forward. However, due to the very low solubility of calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg) carbonate, high pH drives the reprecipitation of bicarbonate into carbonate and releases CO2 (reaction 3). If only carbonate (e.g., CaCO3) is produced and stored, the efficiency, ΔIC/ΔAlk, is reduced to 0.5. Thus, reprecipitation is undesirable from the carbonation efficiency perspective. The practical maximal carbonation efficiency is determined by the carbonate-bicarbonate equilibrium, with bicarbonate dominance maximized around pH 8, which is also the pH of oceans. At such pH, most dissolved IC (DIC), including carbonic acid (H2CO3), bicarbonate (HCO3−), and carbonate ions (CO32−), is in the form of bicarbonate, and ΔDIC/ΔAlk is around 0.8 ± 0.05.9 If additional equilibrated particulate IC (PIC) carbonate is considered, the carbonation efficiency ΔIC/ΔAlk = (ΔDIC+ΔPIC)/ΔAlk, may be slightly higher, such as ΔIC/ΔAlk = 0.85 ± 0.1 for OAE (see Note S2).

Carbonate-bicarbonate equilibrium, however, is only one of the limiting factors controlling carbonation efficiency. Alkalinity can also be lost before carbon is captured and stored, thereby lowering the effective performance.9,10,11 This reduced carbonation efficiency is described by Equation 6, where ΔAlktemp is the temporarily generated alkalinity and ΔAlklost is the lost alkalinity.

For instance, for OAE that utilizes the vast ocean surface as a carbonation reactor, alkalinity can be lost via strong downwelling ocean currents into the deeper ocean, which will take thousands of years to resurface and capture atmospheric CO2.9 Such loss may be mitigated by air forcing and bubbling in circulated alkaline water tanks, to precapture CO2 with near-equilibrium DIC discharge, instead of leaving the ocean surface for carbonation.12 Furthermore, while OAE is apparently constrained to coastal areas, a similar engineering approach of CO2 precapture can be applied with fresh water from inland-water sources. It is possible for such reactors at the riverbank to achieve carbonation efficiencies close to that of coastal OAE.13 Hence, we classify OAE (regardless of whether CO2 is precaptured) and such fresh water system (with CO2 precapture) as bCCS.

For terrestrial ERW, the dissolved cations can be lost via multiple pathways, such as penetrating into underground water, stabilization by high cation exchange capacity soil or rising soil pH (e.g., reaction 5), or forming secondary minerals (reversed weathering) during riverine or other transport to the ocean, instead of ultimately removing atmospheric CO2.10,11 The accessibility to CO2 and the presence of non-carbonic acids are main concerns behind terrestrial ERW,14,15,16 resulting in a substantially lower carbonation efficiency of terrestrial ERW than that of OAE. Other than silicate weathering, terrestrially added carbonate, such as limestone (CaCO3) as an agricultural pH buffer, can either release CO2 via reaction with acid17 or remove CO2 via bicarbonate formation (reversed reaction 3), depending on initial soil pH and non-carbonic acid formation from fertilizer.18 For terrestrial carbonate weathering with an assumed zero carbonation efficiency, it corresponds to a maximal carbonation efficiency of 0.5, even if the agricultural CaCO3 (carbonate) is replaced by alkaline silicates.

The net CDR of terrestrial ERW can be even more complex since it may induce synergistic sequestration of soil inorganic and organic carbon (SIC and SOC),19 or lead to stronger SOC loss than SIC gain,20 though additional organic carbon input from plants due to fertilization effects may also offset such SOC loss.21 For land enriched with organic carbon at acidic condition, such as peatland, the impact of SOC loss may be dominant due to accelerated decomposition with neutralization of acid.22 A global meta-analysis suggested that the ERW of basalt on SOC tends to be positive in warm and humid regions but negative in cold and dry regions.23

The limiting factors behind the carbonation efficiency of terrestrial ERW also apply to freshwater-mediated bCCS. Re-emission of CO2 before it reaches the ocean remains a concern for inland sequestration. However, if the carbon accounting is based on sequestered CO2 in a bubbling reactor, and if the discharge water pH is above the downstream river pH, unutilized alkalinity could serve as buffer preventing re-emission, minimizing existing downstream emission via subsequent carbonation. Hence, such CO2 sequestration may be deemed rather reliable, unless downstream river waters are enriched with acidic organic carbon (e.g., in peatlands) where elevated pH could lead to substantial organic carbon loss.

For the production of CNCb or CNCm, the carbon negativity comes from the replacement of CaCO3 with silicate rocks as a source of Ca, via processes such as chemical extraction (e.g., Brimstone process,24 glutamic acid diacetate [GLDA] assisted carbonation25,26) and electrochemical extraction27 (including the claims of sourcing Ca and Mg from seawater or brines).28,29,30 As illustrated in Figure 1, these processes require acid-base separation, where acid accelerates silicate weathering, and an alkaline condition (typically pH > 10) is necessary for efficient precipitation of CaCO3, Ca(OH)2, or/and Mg(OH)2. For CNCm production, Ca(OH)2 and Mg(OH)2 are used as precursors to produce clinker, eliminating the CO2 emission from carbonate decomposition during conventional cement production. We term it as ΔCl, additional carbon on land, representing the “additional carbon by keeping CaCO3 on land instead of being decomposed for conventional clinker production”. The ΔCl per extracted Ca2+ or Mg2+ ion is then 1, corresponding to an efficiency ΔIC/ΔAlk = 0.5. Similarly, for CNCb production, CO2 removal can take place directly alongside the electrochemical process (e.g., by injecting CO2 and mixing with the alkaline products to precipitate CaCO3),29 with equal carbonation efficiency ΔIC/ΔAlk = 0.5. Such carbon negative CaCO3 can replace natural CaCO3 as feedstock in a conventional cement production plant, producing CNCm.27 However, in this case, there will still be CO2 emission during the clinker production, so that overall carbonation efficiency ΔIC/ΔAlk remains 0.5.

Note that the actual additional carbon on land for the CNCm will be higher if subsequent carbonation curing is also considered, but it is inappropriate to quantify that independent process under the comparison of silicate carbonation efficiency. Similarly, the sink from natural carbonation of the remained Ca or Mg hydroxides or oxides in cement during its lifespan—including its life after demolition—although it provides ∼0.84–0.93 GtCO2 yr−1 sink in 2023 (∼50% of emission from carbonate decomposition during cement production),31,32 is independent from our discussed silicate carbonation. Carbon sink by accelerated carbonation of recycled concrete aggregates (RCAs) for road-filling application,33 is also independent from silicate rock carbonation since the alkalinity is from remained hydroxides in RCA.

The necessary acid-neutralization after acid-base separation

We would like to emphasize that even for claims of seawater-sourced Ca and Mg for CNCm/CNCb production, seawater only serves as their “intermediate medium” instead of the source. Specifically, the strong alkaline condition for producing CNCb or hydroxides as precursors of CNCm, is basically the reaction 3 that leads to net CO2 emission. With Ca2+ and Mg2+ extracted from seawater, the alkalinity in the remained seawater is lowered, limiting the oceanic carbon sink. While claims of seawater-sourced Ca2+ for solid CaCO3 production also consider their process similar to the natural biocalcification by calcifiers (such as coral),29 biocalcification actually reduces the OAE efficiency in regions with prolific calcifiers.34

This not only highlights the silicate rocks as the alkalinity source for carbon negativity, but also the need of acid-neutralization for processes involving acid-base separation, including CNCm/CNCb production, as well as some bCCS designs (Figure 1). Any returned acid will weaken the oceanic carbon sink, lowering oceanic inorganic carbon uptake (ΔCo). With oceanic bicarbonate-carbonate equilibrium and ΔIC/ΔAlk ≈0.85 (Note S2), the ΔCo/ΔAlklost for each separated but ultimately returned acid equivalent (H+) is about −0.85. This means that solid carbonate production can become a net carbon source if acid is not durably neutralized: ΔIC/ΔAlktemp = ΔCl/ΔAlktemp + ΔCo/ΔAlklost ≤ 0.5−0.85 = −0.35. Such non-neutralization routes include resale of produced HCl or Cl2 for conventional market applications (see Figure S1 for various acid fates). In fact, acid produced with seawater-mediated cement or concrete must be neutralized by non-carbonate alkaline minerals (e.g., silicate minerals) because carbonate releases CO2 upon acid dissolution. Seawater-mediated cement with acid neutralized by CaCO3 is at best completely ineffective in net carbon storage (ΔIC/ΔAlktemp(Ca2+, Mg2+) < 0.5−0.5 = 0), and more likely a net carbon source. For OAE with acid neutralized by carbonate minerals, or enhanced coastal carbonate weathering, a net carbon sink is still achievable, with ΔCo/ΔAlktemp ≈ 0.85−0.5 = 0.35. Ease of MRV and location flexibility

Once we are clear about the distinction between products and involved processes, we will know how easily or challenging the MRV can be addressed, and how flexible about location selection for carbonation processes.

For MRV, production of CNCm or CNCb offers the highest certainty, since the sequestered carbon is also the avoided CO2 emission from carbonate decomposition for CNCm, and the produced carbonate for CNCb. MRV for the OAE with pre-captured CO2 is also rather certain, as the additional DIC and PIC in the reactor discharge can be readily measured, and the subsequent capture in the ocean surface and its associated uncertainty would be minimal. The approaches ranked next are ocean-surface mediated (without pre-captured CO2) and freshwater-mediated (with pre-captured CO2) bCCS, as they require more sophisticated modeling of downstream biogeochemical processes, to ensure the carbonation efficiency by minimizing the uncertainty of lost alkalinity, reemitted CO2, and impact on organic carbon storage. MRV for the terrestrial ERW in inland regions is the most challenging due to the complex biogeochemical processes, not only in the land where silicate powders are applied, but also the downstream transportation of alkalinity before reaching the ocean and capturing CO2.

For location flexibility, all these approaches prefer locations near to the source of alkaline silicates, minimizing the CO2 emissions and cost for rock transportation. Other than that, terrestrial ERW may allow greater flexibility, though different land types and conditions will affect the carbonation efficiency, MRV, and even the co-benefits. For instance, acidic farmlands may enjoy more agronomic co-benefits from rising pH with ERW, saving the cost of neutralizing CaCO3, but the carbonation efficiency may suffer as most dissolved metal cations can be stabilized by non-carbonic acids. bCCS requires access to natural water resources, ideally at coastlines, followed by riverbanks, probably with near neutral pH and without acidic downstream enriched with organic carbon. CNCm or CNCb production will likely require gas supply with CO2 concentrations higher than the atmosphere. Fortunately, this may not be very challenging as conventional cement production may produce highly concentrated CO2 from carbonate decomposition.

Ease of process optimization

Silicate weathering and carbonation are thermodynamically spontaneous but constrained by slow dissolution and imperfect carbonation efficiency. Recent atomistic work on diopside surface hydroxylation provides an example of how water adsorption and dissociation initiate mineral-surface hydroxylation, an early step in silicate dissolution and cation release.35 A cost-effective process should address the dissolution issue with minimal capital and operating expenditures, while ensuring the optimal pH for carbonation: ∼ pH 8 for bCCS approaches (e.g., OAE), and ∼pH 10 for CNCb where CaCO3 precipitation is required, and ∼pH 10.5 for CNCm if Mg(OH)2 precipitation as cement precursor is required. Due to the relatively high pH for CNCb/CNCm production process, acid-base separation (e.g., via electrochemical process) is difficult to be avoided – acid for dissolving silicate, and base for carbonation and/or hydroxide precipitation. On the other hand, the weaker alkaline condition for producing dissolved bicarbonate allows the possibility to concurrently run the weathering and carbonation processes, bypassing the acid-base separation (Figure 1). For instance, large-volume slow-flow (LVSF) reactor with ground silicate powders has been proposed as alkalinity factory for OAE with minimal energy consumption.36 Thermodynamically, to create each unit of pH difference, minimally 59.2 mV potential is required. For CNCb/CNCm production, the higher pH for carbonate and hydroxide precipitation is hence a thermodynamic limitation for the process optimization, as compared to bCCS approaches. Practically, this limitation is greater due to the need of electrolyte and/or solvent recycling, as well as separation of solid SiO2, leading to more complex process design (higher CAPEX) and higher energy consumption (OPEX) than bCCS. Such limitations are discussed in detail using wollastonite carbonation as a classic example.

Process for indirect wollastonite carbonation involving pH-difference

(A) Electrolysis of NaNO3 solution with concurrent NaOH and HNO3 production, modified from ref.27, with additional flow of recycling electrolyte (blue-dash, which is necessary but limits the efficiency).

(B) Non-electrochemical process with sodium salt of GLDA, modified from ref.25,26, with additional thermo-decomposition of NaHCO3 into Na2CO3 (brown-dash) for recycling sufficient Na+ to raise the pH for CaCO3 precipitation.

(C) Our proposed large-volume-slow-flow (LVSF) reactor, making use of CO2, DIC, pH, and mesh-size gradients, with optional natural or waste organic ligand that does not require recycling, to facilitate dissolution and carbonation. At the bottom reactor, solid CaCO3 is the target product so that most Na+ may stay in the solution, minimizing regeneration of NaOH.

For process (3a), the pH gradient between wollastonite dissolution (pH < 1) and carbonate precipitation (pH > 9, with added NaOH at pH > 13) is huge, and the concurrent water electrolysis for H2 and O2 production requires additional potential difference and energy consumption. This marks a low energy-efficiency, estimated at thermodynamic electric potential for water splitting over the actual operating potential, 1.23/3.2 ≈ 38%,27 even before electrolyte recycling is considered with expected more energy consumption. Note that in the published study, the cost of the process was underestimated due to the assumption of idealized electrolysis efficiency (100%).

For process (3b), GLDA chelant-assisted Ca-extraction facilitates release of Ca2+ under moderately alkaline conditions,25,26 thus minimizes the need of acid metal leaching. In addition, the moderate pH swing is driven by (1) thermodynamically favored dissolution of Ca2+ (raising pH above 8) and CO2 (decreasing pH from around 10 to 8), and (2) precipitation of NaHCO3 ∼ pH 8 followed by additional low-grade heat (80°C–100°C) input to recycle NaHCO3 to Na2CO3, which supports raising the pH to ∼10. This moderate pH swing between pH 8–10 also optimizes GLDA chelating capacity—binding to metal like Ca prefers deprotonated state—and GLDA is highly deprotonated at pH 8, dominated by HGLDA3− followed by GLDA4−.37

For process (3c), our proposed approach aims to coproduce a bicarbonate dominant aqueous discharge (as bCCS approach) with solid SiO2 in the multi-stage LVSF reactor and solid CaCO3 (as CNCb) in the bottom tray with higher Na+ concentration and pH. This setup demonstrates that the production of solid CaCO3 as an add-on process after dissolved Ca(HCO3)2 is produced. In the upper trays, we eliminate any energy-intensive acid-base separation and thermal treatment, while optimizing bCCS via these mechanisms—(1) having a reactor with large-volume (large reactor volume to contain large quantity of silicate powders) but slow-flow (LVSF), to ensure sufficient reaction surface and time for silicate dissolution; (2) keeping CO2 purging (e.g., via flue gas) that concurrently facilitates bicarbonate formation and assists dissolution; (3) adding waste or natural streams that contain chelating agents or ligands (e.g., liquid fraction of anaerobic digestate,38 peat-water from peatland;39 or other acidic wastewater40) that can be discharged together with other products, eliminating the need of chelating agent recovery; (4) multiple stages of LVSF reactor for optimizing rock weathering and carbonation—upper trays focus on rock weathering or metal leaching with slightly lower pH, while lower trays focus on carbonation with higher partial pressure of CO2 (PCO2), and completing the metal leaching with finer particle filtration mesh size. Note that SiO2 is selectively more stable than metals during the acid- or ligand-assisted leaching.41,42 Therefore, at the end of metal leaching/weathering process (i.e., at lower trays of multistage LVSF reactor), the solid CaSiO3-to-SiO2 ratio is lower and requires a higher liquid-to-solid ratio to assist complete metal leaching. In addition, the solubility of amorphous SiO2 is lower under weak acidic and neutral pH but rises with pH > 8,43 thus process (3c) separates solid SiO2 at pH around 8, while producing the bicarbonate-dominant aqueous product. Such an LVSF reactor can be classified as a specific fluidized bed reactor, with multiple stages optimized for different processes and conditions. The lower add-on reactor is with pre-added NaOH solution to raise the pH to 10 for CaCO3 precipitation and concurrent release of gaseous CO2 for upper LVSF reactor. The gap between the upper and lower reactors also prevents Na+ from entering bicarbonate production process, minimizing the demand of NaOH regeneration and associated energy consumption.

For CNCm production, the carbonate produced from wollastonite carbonation can be the raw material for cement production, utilizing existing cement-production facilities with an alternate feedstock (i.e., CNCb) replacing the CaCO3, to minimize capital expenditure.27 If the CNCm is produced with seawater-medium, involving the precipitation of Mg(OH)2, the process optimization will be more challenging due to the higher pH for precipitating Mg(OH)2 than CaCO3. In other words, with alkaline silicate rocks as the source of alkalinity, we do not consider any advantage for seawater-mediated CNCm production over freshwater-mediated approach.

Co-benefits beyond carbon sequestration

Basic comparison with carbon negative carbonate

We have discussed the advantages of CDR via dissolved bicarbonate as compared to CNCm and CNCb in terms of higher carbonation efficiency with comparable MRV and lower cost. Nevertheless, the commercial value of CNCm and CNCb can become a dominant benefit beyond carbon sequestration, especially when the silicate cost is sufficiently low, and/or the product value is sufficiently high (Figure 4).

Dependence of intrinsic minimal carbon price on intrinsic alkalinity cost (ΔCost/ΔAlk) and abatement efficiency (ΔCO2/ΔAlk)

ΔCost is defined as (silicate cost−product value). The minimal carbon price is to offset only this intrinsic alkalinity cost.

In Figure 4, we introduce the intrinsic alkalinity cost, defined as the difference between silicate cost and product value (co-benefit) per additional alkalinity introduced. This is the major cost to be offset by carbon credit, with “intrinsic minimal carbon price”, in addition to other process and transport related costs. For bicarbonate-dominant solution, it typically does not provide any economic benefits beyond CDR. Hence the intrinsic cost can be assumed as the raw silicate costs, with price ranges of expensive wollastonite and low-cost basalt shown on the x axis. On the other hand, CNCb, the direct product from silicate carbonation, has its intrinsic value, represented by the gray left-pointing arrows, lowering the intrinsic alkalinity costs. Financially, if the carbon price is high, the carbonation efficiency is more important. However, to improve the market viability with a low carbon price, the dominant deployment drivers are the economic value of products (e.g., CNCb) and the low silicate cost (e.g., basalt) that drive down the intrinsic alkalinity cost. As shown in Figure 4, for wollastonite, the higher carbonation efficiency of bCCS over CNCb provides only a marginal benefit to intrinsic minimal carbon price (∼165 vs. 173 USD/t CO2); but for basalt, the intrinsic minimal carbon price for CNCb is substantially lower than that for bCCS (∼14 vs. 65 USD/t CO2) (see Note S3).

Another major potential co-product from the reactor setting is the amorphous silica from the polymerization of dissolved silicic acid. While its price is much higher than that of CaCO3, the major limitation is its global demand, being < 10 Mt yr−1 for amorphous silica, or < 100 Mt yr−1 for the mixture of amorphous and crystalline silica (150–450 USD/t), much lower than the CaCO3 demand (5–6 Gt yr−1). In addition, co-production of silica could be feasible not only with CNCb/CNCm production, but also with the production of bicarbonate-dominant solution (Figure 3C, upper LVSF reactor), and thus it would not be a strong factor distinguishing the competitiveness between CNCb/CNCm production and bCCS.

On the other hand, terrestrial ERW eliminates most CAPEX and process-related energy cost, as compared to bCCS and CNCb reactors. Despite its drawback in MRV uncertainty and carbonation efficiency, its co-benefits on agriculture productivity, soil health, and replacement of agricultural CaCO3, especially with the low-cost basalt, may evolve into the strongest economic driver for its application.44,45,46,47 These examples showcase the importance of other economic benefits than CDR and the raw silicate cost for practical deployment.

Other co-benefits of carbonate precipitation and process optimization

Other niche co-benefits for CNCb are associated with waste stabilization and the recovery of valuable metals from suitable silicate feedstocks. The valuable co-products can potentially turn intrinsic alkalinity cost negative (further extending the gray left-pointing arrows in Figure 4), allowing the process to be profitable even in the absence of carbon credit. That is particularly relevant for materials such as olivine, laterite, iron-oxide-silicate tailings, municipal solid waste incineration ash, as well as low-cost basalt, where carbonation may be coupled either to immobilization of problematic metals or to recovery of economically valuable ones. For instance, the commercialized Brimstone process co-produces supplementary cementitious materials and alumina together with cement from low-cost silicate rocks.24 Cu and Ni extraction from silicate ores with concurrent carbonation has been tested previously, based on the different affinities between chelating agents and metals, resulting in selective leaching.48 GLDA has been highlighted as a promising chelating agent, since it is not only useful for extracting Ca and Mg,37 but also for extracting heavy metals from solid waste,49 and offering a promising route for extracting rare-earth elements.50 Similar to selective metal leaching, selective metal recovery can be achieved by treating the metal-containing GLDA with ion-exchange resins (IERs) with different affinities for the various metal ions of interest, followed by metal-desorption from IERs by acid.50 For immobilization, carbonation of waste or silicate helps immobilizing the contained problematic metals into solid carbonate,51,52,53,54 while microbial interaction could play an important role.54,55 Such a process could be combined with CNCb/CNCm production with purging of CO2 or combustion flue gas. The mineralogy of the precipitated carbonate is also relevant: first-principles calculations show that guest-ion incorporation differs among calcite, aragonite and vaterite because of their distinct coordination environments.56 Structural and experimental studies further show that carbonate formation can involve hydrous or metastable intermediates and polymorphic transformations that affect phase evolution and final material properties.57,58,59

With the goal to extract metals from cheaper but slower-weathering silicates (e.g., basalts), as well as potential extraction of valuable metals, processes (3b and c) would likely require further integrative optimization with other technologies, such as improved mechanical grinding,60,61 microwave,61,62,63 and chelating agents/ligands. These technologies appear to be complementary. Mechanical grinding is important for breaking coarse particles to increase surface area.60 Microwave-assistance is a useful pretreatment that minimizes subsequent grinding energy,61 as well as speeding up the dissolution via internal heating, in particular useful for the detachment of Si-rich layers from any unreacted mineral surface.63 Chelating agents (e.g., GLDA) or ligands bind to metal ions and extract them from silicate structure; and assist metal refining based on metal chelating affinity. We present this integrated route in Figure 5 as a testable hypothesis for improving CNCb production with metal refining rather than as a prescriptive design for immediate deployment.

Schematic process flow diagram for metal (M) ion extraction and refining from silicate rocks by integrating microwave, mechanical grinding, and chelating agents.

In this proposed process design, rocks are firstly pretreated with microwave, potentially creating a less stable crystalline structure (e.g., more crystalline defects), followed by mechanical grinding with a medium containing GLDA or other chelating agents, to minimize grinding energy while achieving smaller particle size with initial metal leaching. The particles and liquid effluent can be sent to a multi-stage LVSF reactor, with additional chelating agent at low concentration. Microwave treatment may be useful again in the lower tray of this multi-stage LVSF reactor to improve separation of SiO2 from unreacted metal silicates. If the chelating agent supply in this first LVSF reactor is far below the demand to dissolve all metals it can bind to, it will serve more as a catalyst for simultaneous rock weathering, especially for metals that can be readily dissolved (e.g., Na+, K+, and bicarbonates of Ca2+ and Mg2+). Figure 5 also shows second and third multi-stage LVSF reactors with increasing chelating agent concentration. We expect metals that are less readily soluble but with stronger chelating affinity to be dissolved in the subsequent LVSF reactors with higher chelating agent concentration. Certain extent of metal refining can then be achieved with this simple process concept.

Conclusions and next steps

We compare CNCm and CNCb with bCCS. CNCm or CNCb do not get their Ca and Mg originally from ocean, even if seawater is used as a medium. Instead, the “carbon negativity” comes from additional alkalinity from rocks, particularly enhanced weathering of silicate rocks. While bCCS, including OAE and riverbank reactor, provides higher carbonation efficiency with lower energy consumption and relatively simpler reactor design; production of carbon-negative concrete creates an additional revenue in addition to the sale of carbon credit.

Understanding such fundamental difference is important for planning research, development, and demonstration (RD&D) activities. Expensive silicate feedstock without additional value from metal recovery, e.g., wollastonite, results in less competitive CNCm and CNCb than bCCS processes. However, with lower alkalinity cost from silicate rocks, such as using basalt as the feedstock, the cost benefit for CNCm/CNCb production is more important than for bCCS, so that CNCm/CNCb production becomes a more profitable option. The priority for technology advancement for CNCm/CNCb production should hence focus on low-cost silicate feedstock, and ideally, on feedstock that produces additional valuable metals. For bCCS, it is important to explore co-benefits beyond carbon sequestration, especially those with financial incentives, such as diatom aquaculture that can be turned into feed or valuable biorefinery products.64,65 Diatom cultivation with enhanced silicate weathering can accelerate both the silicate dissolution and diatom growth, as well as increasing biogenic silica, bioavailable Si (biogenic silica + silicic acid [Si(OH)4]), and particulate organic carbon (POC), with reduced DIC due to diatom consumption.66 These are summarized in Table 1.

We have also proposed several process design ideas that integrate various technologies. The optimal design for extremely slow-dissolving silicate such as basalt will likely include some of the following: (1) microwave rock pretreatment, (2) ligand or chelating agent assisted mechanical grinding, (3) multi-stage, large volume, slow flow fluidized-bed reactor utilizing the gradients in PCO2, DIC, pH, liquid/solid ratio and gas/solid ratio for optimized separation, (4) microwave for late-stage SiO2 layer-removal, (5) natural or organic waste for supplying ligands or chelating agents, eliminating the need of chelating agent recovery, (6) constrained NaOH within the carbonate-precipitation reactor, and (7) a basic metal refining concept with varying chelating agent concentration and metal solubility. If basalt is chosen as the silicate feedstock, downstream integration as an agriculture additive in a terrestrial ERW setting should also be considered, for its benefits to agriculture sector.

In summary, these results have clear policy implications. Deployment decisions should be guided by co-benefits that largely determine the process profitability and market viability, as well as geographical context that affects the feedstock availability, costs, and potential downstream ecosystem influence, rather than focusing on the carbon sequestration alone.

Limitations of the study

This perspective compares carbon removal pathways using thermodynamic bounds and values drawn from the published literature rather than new experimental measurements, and the resulting rankings should be read as indicative rather than definitive. The adopted oceanic carbonation efficiency (ΔIC/ΔAlk = 0.85 ± 0.1) depends on local temperature, salinity, pCO2, and carbonate system conditions, and realized efficiencies will vary with deployment setting and with alkalinity loss processes that remain incompletely quantified. The intrinsic alkalinity cost analysis relies on indicative market prices for silicate feedstocks and mineral products, which vary regionally and over time, and it excludes transport, permitting, and other site-specific costs. Finally, the ecosystem effects of large-scale alkalinity addition and the long-term verification of carbon storage across all pathways carry uncertainties that will need to be resolved through field trials and standardized MRV protocols.
