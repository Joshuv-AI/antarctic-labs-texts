Quantifying motion blur by imaging shock front propagation with broadband and narrowband X-ray sources
Authors: Kathryn J Harke, Michael R Armstrong, David Martinez, Jonathan Lind, Mukul Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTime-integrated radiography using MeV Bremsstrahlung X-ray sources is the norm for imaging during system-level testing of components and structures under dynamic condition. One source of error in the analysis of the time-integrated radiography data sets stems from motion blur which smears out sharp interfaces to a greater degree with longer exposure times, which become necessary to provide sufficient signal-to-noise with low X-ray penetration of objects of interest. To quantify motion blur, a 1D shock wave through PMMA was investigated experimentally at The Dynamic Compression Sector at The Advanced Photon Source (DCS@APS) with tapered broadband and 25.46 ± 1.06 keV narrowband X-rays. Four cameras with different exposure times were used for each experiment to compare the effect that exposure time has on motion blur. In addition, our methodology to accurately simulate motion blur in terms of transmission and shape is presented and compared to our experimental results and quantified. There is a high level of agreement between the experimental and simulation results across the range of data sets investigated in this study with a percent difference range of 0.29–1.31% for the four shots. The methodology of this work serves as a steppingstone towards a physically validated model that could be used in conjunction with experimental results to deconvolve physical parameters, densities, and interfaces of interest in a way that would not be possible with experimental results alone.Subject terms: Physics, Imaging techniques, Scientific data

Introduction

Time-integrated (multiple pulse or longer-time continuous exposure) radiography using broadband MeV Bremsstrahlung X-ray sources is the standard diagnostic fielded during system-level testing of components and structures under dynamic loading conditions, as it allows an in-situ window into the internal structure of optically occluded materials1–3. This is in contrast to the radiographic investigations, utilizing synchrotron X-rays, carried out in small-scale experiments performed at DCS@APS located at Argonne National Laboratory4. Here the energy spectrum employed for imaging is typically narrowband - in the tens of keV energy range - allowing for the study of local physics phenomena. This narrowband X-ray spectrum, sometimes referred to as pink beam, is created by applying an energy filter on the undulator output, leaving only the lowest energy harmonics. The X-ray energy of narrowband sources can be tailored to optimize contrast in radiography images, but this is challenging for broadband sources where high energy photons are not significantly attenuated. Broadband X-ray sources, sometimes referred to as white beam sources, are unfocused and contain all harmonics. As such, broadband sources add unwanted background to radiography images, reducing the overall contrast and ultimately limiting the image quality. In both cases, the objective in the experiment is to document the moving interface between the regions of unshocked and shock compressed material, which allows us to construct a mechanical equation of state (EOS). Further analysis is necessary, however, to extract the time-evolved density fields that provide a further check on the EOS.

Motion blur, the apparent streaking or blurring of an object’s sharp edges due to relative motion between an object and detection system over a finite exposure time, while commonly associated with standard photography5,6, has additionally been an issue of concern for X-ray7,8 and neutron radiography9,10. In photography, motion blur can lead to smearing or streaking in the output photograph, degrading the overall quality of the photograph. Much like photography, in X-ray or neutron radiography, motion blur affects the output quality of the radiograph, decreasing the confidence in interface edge detection and density field determination, which are of importance in shock physics or dynamic loading experiments, for example.

While there have been several methods developed to deblur images, many utilize iterative algorithms which assume system constraints and prior information11,12. Other methods which apply deconvolution techniques to a motion blurred image rely on imaging of a static system and attempt to remove motion of the detection system13,14. While progress has been made in this area, these methods do not transfer to cases where the material flow velocity varies across the field of view, which includes all cases where dynamic X-ray radiography is employed. While motion blur has been acknowledged (or estimated) in a number of time-integrated experimental efforts, a rigorous methodology has not been established to quantify15–17 the effects.

In addition to motion blur, there are several factors that contribute to image quality in an X-ray radiograph, such as experimental geometry, X-ray source, scintillator, and detector properties18–20. The source blur, which derives from the non-zero, finite X-ray source spot size, is dependent on the focal spot size and shape, the magnification, the input energy spectrum, and the scattering of electrons and photons within the source18,21–26. The scintillator, which converts X-rays into visible light, produces an increasingly stronger signal with increasing thickness; however, thicker scintillators produce more artifacts in the form of spatially varying blur21,23,26. In high-energy X-ray radiography facilities where source energies approach the 20 MeV range, thick scintillators are required to capture sufficient X-ray dose per a given shot, which leads to edge blurring and results in a greater uncertainty in the density reconstruction23,26. Detector blur results from electron scatter within the detector electronics, the charge-coupled device (CCD) pixel size, and the quantum efficiency24.

Motion blur, which results from the movement of the measured object during the data acquisition window can be minimized by decreasing the exposure time; however, a shorter exposure time leads to decreased X-ray dose which means decreased signal-to-noise and a resulting poorer quality radiographic image22. Current practices in dynamic radiography lead to motion blur and loss of contrast as principal sources of error in the analysis of experimental data. However, some concerns, particularly related to pulse width (tens to hundreds of ns), interpulse spacing (hundreds of ns), illumination via a broadband spectrum, and flux remain.

In addition, time integrated images of moving interfaces exhibit motion blur in the μm to mm range depending on the velocity of the features of interest and the X-ray pulse width or gate width (in the case of a continuous beam). For example, shock compression of iron under a pressure of 100 GPa, can lead to a shock speed of 7 km/s through the material, which if probed with an X-ray pulse width of 100 ns, would lead to 700 μm of blur contribution from just motion blur27. While these effects have been known for decades, current radiography simulations do not account for motion blur combined with broadband illumination, relying instead on single “snapshot” calculations. This has resulted in incomplete quantification of the experimental radiography data, as for instance, density fields and interface locations with a high degree of precision.

In this work we present methodology to quantitatively simulate the motion blurring effects in a dynamic radiography experiment in terms of shape and X-ray transmission as well as quantify the experimental results in terms of motion blur effects. The experimental and simulation X-ray radiography results from a 1D shock through PMMA are compared and discussed. This methodology comprises two parts, hydrodynamic simulation calculations, which give the mechanical state of a material, giving stress and density location at every spatial location within a material undergoing deformation, as an input into our radiographic ray tracing simulation calculations. Conventionally, the radiographic simulation calculations can only be performed at one snapshot in time, which fails to capture effects like motion blur. Our approach involves taking the hydrodynamic calculation output at several time steps over an effective exposure time window, running a radiographic simulation at each time step with the correctly distributed X-ray flux, and then integrating all radiographic simulations together to give a simulated result with the correct X-ray transmission and motion blur contribution for a given exposure time window. This methodology is generalized and can be used on any material combination or geometry (including an asymmetric 3D event) and on any dynamic X-ray radiography platform, given the inputs like the EOS and the X-ray source spectrum are known. This simulation methodology additionally provides the capability to simulate a specific time step of interest in a dynamic experiment and optimize the input parameters like pulse width, interpulse spacing, and X-ray beam collimation ahead of time to produce the highest quality radiograph possible for each specific experimental configuration.

Methods and materials

For this study, A 1D shock wave experiment through a well characterized, homogeneous material was chosen. A shock front is a sharp discontinuity (where the rise time is considerably short compared to the time where the pressure, density, and energy are constant on either side of the interface) between the shocked and unshocked regions28. This discontinuity leads to a sharp interface between two regions of different densities which can be captured by X-rays due to the difference in X-ray attenuation. The sharp shock front travels at sufficiently high velocities through the material, as compared to the exposure time of the camera’s gate, to induce a motion blurring effect that is detectable in the X-ray radiograph.

Four cases, investigating a 1D shock wave propagating through polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)29 were studied experimentally and with simulations. The simulations were conducted with the hydrodynamic simulation code, ALE3D (Arbitrary Lagrangian Eulerian Three-Dimensional Analysis)30 and radiographic simulation code, HADES31. The experimental results were collected at DCS@APS using the single stage gas gun at beamline 35-ID-D (Fig. 1). The experimental configurations were used as the inputs for the simulations (Supplementary Table I).

Experimental

Dynamic, synchrotron X-ray imaging was performed in 324 bunch mode, which specifies an X-ray pulse spacing of 11.4 ns, a pulse duration of 22 ps (RMS), and a maximum current of 0.31 mA per X-ray bunch. At DCS@APS, the U27 and U17 undulators (with 27 and 17.2 mm period, respectively) were used for experiments. The U27 undulator was tapered to generate a broadband spectrum and the U17 undulator was used to generate a narrowband spectrum. The U17 spectrum is centered at 25.46 ± 1.06 keV with a maximum intensity at 8.8×1013 photons/sec/0.1%bw, seen in Fig. 2A (blue line). The U27 X-ray spectrum is broadband and includes energies from 5 to 100 keV, seen in Fig. 2A (orange line).

Four experiments were conducted, two with the tapered U27 broadband undulator (shots 22-2-130 and 22-2-132) and two with the narrowband U17 undulator (shots 22-2-133 and 22-2-138). A schematic of the experimental setup contained within the DCS@APS 35-ID-D hutch is shown in Fig. 1A with a more detailed schematic of the sample region shown in Fig. 1B, including the Ta backing (green), unshocked and shocked PMMA sample (dark blue and red), Al-6061 impactor (light blue) that impacts the sample from the right, the X-ray beam direction (perpendicular to the shock direction), and the scintillator (orange) and camera (black). A photograph of the experimental setup in the target chamber is seen in Fig. 1C, showing the sample in the sample holder, the direction of the impactor and the X-ray beam direction.

A single stage gas gun was used which includes a wraparound gas breech connected to a barrel, ending in a target chamber which includes the sample, stopping plates, a window for the X-ray beam path (which is perpendicular to the direction of motion of the projectile) and ports for diagnostics (Fig. 1C).

For X-ray radiography, a 100–150 μm thick single crystal LYSO:Ce scintillator was used with an expected 37.2 ns decay time (measured with PCI (phase-contrast imaging) detector delay sweep)32. A 5x magnification was used with a field of view of 2190 μm × 2580 μm. Four independent collection 13.1 mm × 13.1 mm PIMAX:1024i ICCDs (intensified charged-coupled device) with a resolution of 1024 pixels × 1024 pixels were used with exposure times of 200 ns, 100 ns, 50 ns, and 25 ns. The ICCD cameras were triggered with the target mounted optical beam break (TOBB) setup which consists of a source fiber, focusing lens, collection lens, and collection fiber mounted directly to the target plate and offset to be a few mm from the impact plane. The impactor velocity was measured with VOBB (velocity optical beam break), consisting of four free space laser beams pointing transverse to the direction of the barrel and bored out at the end of the muzzle.

As a calibration test, for shot 22-2-130 (U27), all four cameras had an exposure time of 50 ns with the same starting collection time. For the remaining three shots, 22-2-132 (U27), 22-2-133 (U17), and 22-2-138 (U17), the four cameras were set to begin collecting at the same time and the exposure times used were 25 ns, 50 ns, 100 ns, and 200 ns. Simulations

The shock propagation in the PMMA sample was simulated using LLNL’s hydrodynamic simulation code, ALE3D (Arbitrary Lagrangian Eularian Three-Dimensional Analysis)30, which specifies the mechanical state of the material, giving stress and density location at every spatial location within a material undergoing deformation, as an input into LLNL’s radiographic ray tracing simulation code, HADES31 (Supplementary Fig. 2). The inputs into ALE3D include descriptions of the PMMA sample and Al-6061 impactor materials’ equation of states, which describe the P-V-T relationship (pressure-volume-temperature) and evolving mechanical state model (i.e. strength model), derived from Steinberg33, LEOS34, and Marsh35 materials library databases; the materials’ geometries; and the impact velocity. ALE3D simulations were performed in 3D utilizing 14 symmetry with dimensions and starting impact conditions matching the exact target geometry and impact conditions delivered. Snapshots in time of the mesh (density and material composition) were produced at regular spacings of 1.25 ns time steps for the 25 ns exposure times, 2.5 ns time steps for the 50 ns exposure times, and 5 ns time steps for the 100 ns and 200 ns exposure times. The simulation time step is determined dynamically by ALE3D to ensure that the Courant condition is satisfied.

The ALE3D simulation outputs are then used as the inputs for the HADES radiographic simulation code. Radiographic images including motion blur were simulated by running HADES simulations to obtain radiographs for several time steps in the hydrodynamics simulation over a specified exposure time (or gate width), with each HADES simulation assuming the integrated photon flux (given by an assumed photon flux distribution) within a bin at the corresponding time of the hydrodynamics simulation. This series of simulated radiographs was summed to obtain a composite radiograph, to be compared quantitatively to experimental results.

HADES determines the transmission via ray tracing between the X-ray source (here specified as a parallel beam) and detector pixels. For finite dose, HADES then adds synthetic noise consistent with Poisson statistics for the detected dose per pixel. Summed HADES simulations have signal to noise consistent with the integrated flux from the X-ray time distribution and sample attenuation. For the HADES simulations, a total photon flux of 7.05×1015 photons/second was used, a DQE (detector quantum efficiency) of 0.5 is used and divided into the four ICCDs. For the calculation, we assume the scintillator response is f(t), where t is time and τ is the scintillator decay constant, which is 37.2 ns for the LYSO:Ce scintillator used in this experimental series (measured with PCI (phase-contrast imaging) detector delay sweep).

where Θ(t) is the Heaviside step function. The constant, 1τ is chosen to normalize the scintillator decay.

With this simulation methodology, the motion blur can be captured along with the other effects that contribute to the effective blur in an output radiograph. Since this simulation methodology is generalized, it can be applied to any sample and impactor material or geometry, as long as the equation of state is known for the material. It can also be applied to any dynamic X-ray radiography platform at any energy range, as long as the X-ray spectrum, scintillator and detector response, and exposure time or gate width are known.

Results

X-ray transmission differences due to source

The two input X-ray spectra used in this study, U17 and U27, are produced by a change in the undulator’s period from 27 mm to 17.2 mm and are shown in Fig. 2A with the narrowband spectrum, U17 shown in blue and the broadband spectrum, U27 shown in orange.

The U17 spectrum is centered at 25.46 ± 1.06 keV with a maximum intensity at 8.8x1013 photons/sec/0.1%bw. The simulated X-ray spectrum shown in Fig. 2A for the broadband U27 configuration has an added 250 μm of Al as a source filter and an additional 3 meters of air in the X-ray beam path. The difference in experimental X-ray transmission seen in Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table II illustrates the effect that a different input X-ray spectrum has on the output experimental radiograph, changing the average X-ray transmission through a nominally 14 mm thick PMMA sample from 0.6065 ± 0.0064 for a broadband, U27 spectrum to 0.5324 ± 0.0018 for a narrowband, U17 X-ray spectrum. This change in X-ray transmission stems from the distribution of photons, with a greater number of higher energy photons in the U27 spectrum, leading to a higher X-ray transmission as compared to the U17 transmission in the experimental radiograph.

Shot 22-2-130 (U27) was used as a calibration shot where all four cameras were collecting for the same 50 ns exposure time and the apparent motion blur for this shot looks consistent across all four cameras, as expected. This points to low camera-to-camera variation across the four ICCDs used in this experimental series. For the remaining three shots, 22-2-132 (U27), 22-2-133 (U17), and 22-2-138 (U17), the interface becomes more blurred as the exposure time is increased, seen as the widened sloped region connecting the flat shocked PMMA and Al-6061 regions. In Fig. 4B, the spikes in X-ray transmission in the transition region between the shocked and unshocked PMMA (around position = 1 mm), seen most prominently in the 25 and 25 ns exposure times, are likely due to PC (phase contrast) effects. Like the free space/PMMA and Ta/PMMA interfaces, the shock front is thin along the shock propagation direction and will exhibit PC effects for X-ray illumination parallel to the plane of the shock front, analogous to PC observed at other thin interfaces. Since the spikes are spaced in time by 11.4 ns, it is possible to measure the shock speed with an estimate of the spacing of the spikes. Estimates of the resulting shock speed are consistent with the shock speed from the Hugoniot. Generally, the relative amplitude of these spikes decreases with increasing integration time. This is because, for longer integration times, the fraction of signal from the shock interface at a given location becomes smaller compared to the fraction of signal from uniform shocked or unshocked material. During most of the integration time, the shock front was not located at any given point in the transition region (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion and conclusion

In a time-integrated X-ray radiography experiment, all components in the radiographic train from the input source spectrum to the detector configuration play a role in the quality of the output radiograph. While there have been many experiments conducted with time-integrated radiography, one of the lesser investigated sources of the effective blur is motion blur; and while it is widely acknowledged, it has not been previously rigorously quantified or incorporated into currently used simulation tools. Motion blur within a dynamic system, caused by a long exposure time relative to the speed of the event being imaged, negatively affects the quality of the radiograph by smearing out sharp interfaces, decreasing the confidence in interface location (i.e. shock front position). By using our time integrated approach with currently used hydrodynamic and radiographic simulation tools, the motion blur can be accurately captured in terms of shape and X-ray transmission, as shown in this work. It is important to be able to simulate correct motion blurring effects in a dynamic loading experiment to determine if the configurations with respect to pulse width and interpulse spacing will be sufficient to capture the interfaces of interest.

In terms of overall radiograph quality, the input parameters like the X-ray source spectrum, collimation, and filters can be optimized for each X-ray experimental configuration to produce the highest quality radiograph in terms of signal-to-noise ratio and contrast. Both Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4 show the ability of relatively small changes in the X-ray input spectrum to change the radiographic output in terms of transmission, which contributes to changes in the signal-to-noise ratio and contrast levels. It is shown in39 that the contrast-to-noise is optimized when the X-ray transmission in 13.5%, and while this can be easily tuned in a static radiograph, a dynamic radiograph is the result of a more intricate system with more variables to consider. Without proper simulation tools to obtain physically accurate dynamic radiography results, the necessary input parameters cannot be configured to optimize the output radiograph quality for each specific experimental configuration at the time steps of interest, where the physical characteristics of the system can drastically change on short time scales. In setting up dynamic radiography configurations, there is a direct trade off between the contrast-to-noise ratio and the integration time. As the integration time is increased, the contrast-to-noise ratio is also increased, leading to a higher quality image; however, with the increased integration time, motion blur is also increased, which leads to less confidence in the interface position. In large scale, system-level experiments which cannot be easily repeated, it is important to have a reliable simulation tool that can be used to tune input parameters ahead of the experiment to ensure physical parameters of interest can be reliably documented.

In the analysis of the results in this work, when comparing the experimental and simulation results to the 1D shock approximation shock speed values (Table 2), there are two main effects identified which contribute to the discrepancies seen in the quantified vs values; first, the effect of phase contrast (PC) which contributes to the large peaks at the inflection points that can be considered as artifacts in the moving interface region and second, the scintillator decay which smears out the results due to afterglow effects. These peaks from PC can be enhanced by adjusting the tilt alignment of the sample with respect to the X-ray beam path and are products of the use of a highly coherent synchrotron X-ray source40. This effect is seen most prominently in Fig. 5 (25 ns exposure time - experimental) where the intensity of the PC peaks in the four different samples vary based on sample alignment with respect to the X-ray beam path. This effect is most noticeable in the experimental 25 ns exposure time data and becomes less prominent as the exposure time is increased. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows the simulated effects of PC in both a continuous and pulsed X-ray case as well as a 25 ns and 200 ns exposure case, and supports the previous statement as well as the effects seen in the experimental data in the 25 ns and 50 ns exposure time cases. In Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 8, which have PC effects most notably in the 25 ns and 50 ns exposure time cases, by removing the PC effects at the inflection points before conducting the 1D response model fit, the fit is closer to the simulation (which was not setup to account for PC effects) and the fitted vs parameter seen in Table 2 aligns more closely to the US,PMMA value for each experimental case. It can be noted that with higher energies, the PC effects will not typically be seen unless there is a highly coherent beam source. For most MeV Bremsstrahlung experiments, PC effects will not be a concern. The second effect, the scintillator decay time, which is 37.2 ns for Cerium doped LYSO, will cause the results to be further smeared out due to the afterglow effects. This effect will be more prominent for an exposure time on the same order of the scintillator decay time, like the 25 ns case and will become less prominent for the 200 ns case. It should also be noted that the hydrodynamic mesh size plays a role in the fidelity of the simulation and that a mesh size that is larger than the detector pixel can produce results that include ’stair step’ features. While there is a trade off between the resolution of the hydrodynamic simulation and the run time of the simulation, in order to produce results that are representative of the physical system, the simulation mesh size should be chosen so it is on the order of the detector’s pixel pitch.

When comparing the shock speeds calculated from the experimental impact velocity against the vs parameters extracted from the experimental and simulation fits (Table 2), our 1D response model works especially well for the 100 ns exposure time case across all experiments. The 25 ns and 50 ns exposure times are more strongly influenced by PC effects, which affect the fit, appearing as the large peaks at the inflection points of the motion blurred region. The 200 ns exposure time data’s fit was influenced by the amount of unshocked PMMA region that was captured. For shot 22-2-132, the 200 ns exposure time data was removed from analysis as the shock had already reverberated back into the PMMA sample (Supplementary Fig. 7). For shot 22-2-133 (Fig. 5) and shot 22-2-138 (Supplementary Fig. 8), the section of unshocked PMMA was very small. We believe this 1D response model will work well in the case of a 1D shock wave propagating through a homogeneous sample, where both the shocked and unshocked regions are present, and the X-ray source is broadband or Bremsstrahlung.

A simulation methodology has been developed to accurately simulate motion blur in a dynamic radiography experiment in terms of shape and transmission levels. Radiographs including motion blur were simulated by running HADES simulations to obtain radiographs for several time steps in the hydrodynamics simulation, with each HADES simulation assuming the integrated photon flux (given by an assumed photon flux distribution) within a bin at the corresponding time of the hydrodynamics simulation. This series of simulated radiographs were summed to obtain the blurred simulated radiograph. A 1D response model was created to fit the data and there is a high level of agreement between the experimental and simulation results across the range of data sets investigated in this study with a percent difference range of 0.29 - 1.31% for the four shots. Our 1D response model will work well in the case of a 1D shock wave propagating through a homogeneous sample, where both the shocked and unshocked regions are present, and the X-ray source is broadband or Bremsstrahlung. With this simulation methodology, the motion blur can be captured along with the other effects that contribute to the effective blur in an output radiograph. Since this simulation methodology is generalized, it can be applied to any sample and impactor material or geometry, as long as the equation of state is known for the material. It can also be applied to any dynamic X-ray radiography platform at any energy range, as long as the X-ray spectrum, scintillator and detector response, and pulse train distribution are known.

The simulation methodology presented in this manuscript will provide the capability to design dynamic experiments ahead of time and optimize the X-ray inputs to produce the highest possible radiographic quality for a given experimental configuration and time of interest. This will lead to confidence in the choice of experimental configurations to produce results with sufficient resolution and contrast to extract the physical parameters sought, within the tolerance of the requirements. The results of this work serve as a steppingstone towards a physically validated model that could be used in conjunction with experimental results to deconvolve physical parameters, densities, and interfaces of interest in a way that would not be possible with experimental results alone.
