Relaxation approach to quantum-mechanical modeling of ferroelectric and antiferroelectric phase transitions
Nikhilesh Maity, Sergey Lisenkov, Arlies Valdespino, Milan Haddad, Lewys Jones, Amit Kumar, Nazanin Bassiri-Gharb, Inna Ponomareva

Abstract
Ferroelectrics and antiferroelectrics are the electric counterparts of ferromagnets and antiferromagnets. These materials undergo temperature- and electric-field–induced phase transitions that give rise to their characteristic hysteresis loops. Modeling such hysteresis loops and associated phase transitions enables both a deeper fundamental understanding and reliable property predictions for this important class of materials. To date, modeling has largely relied on classical approaches, often remaining qualitative and/or empirical. Traditional interpretation of these transitions rests on two assumptions: (i) they are activated Arrhenius-type processes and (ii) they occur well within the classical regime. Here, we demonstrate that a model can instead be built on two “orthogonal” assumptions: (i) the phase transitions are relaxational processes and (ii) they require a quantum mechanical treatment. Applying this model to both antiferroelectrics and ferroelectrics overcomes the limitations of traditional models and enables efficient first-principles simulations of phase transitions. Furthermore, the success of our unconventional approach highlights the significance of quantum mechanics in transitions long regarded as purely classical. We anticipate that this framework will be applicable to a broad range of phase transitions, including magnetic, elastic, multiferroic, and electronic, along with modeling of quantum tunneling, rates of chemical reactions, and others.

Secondly, we consider the phase transition process as a relaxation one instead of an Arrhenius-type, where a relaxation process describes the return of the perturbed system to equilibrium.

Ground-State Relaxation (GSR) Model.
Let us begin with describing the system being in a quantum state
\ket{\psi}
that propagates in time, that is
\ket{\psi(t)}
. For time-independent Hamiltonian
H=\frac{\mathcal{P}^{2}}{2M}+U(P)
, where
\mathcal{P}
is the momentum, this can be achieved as
\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{n}c_{n}(0)e^{-i\frac{E_{n}}{\hbar}t}\ket{\phi_{n}}
, where
c_{n}(0)=\braket{\phi_{n}|\psi(t=0)}
, while
\ket{\phi_{n}}
and
E_{n}
are the eigenstates and eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian, respectively. For a given Hamiltonian
\ket{\phi_{n}}
and
E_{n}
can be evaluated numerically. Suppose
\ket{\psi(t=0)}
corresponds to the metastable state of the system (black dot in Fig.
1
(a) and (d)). Then at time
t
, the probability of finding the system in the state
\ket{\phi_{n}}
is
\lvert\braket{\phi_{n}|\psi(t)}\rvert^{2}=|c_{n}(0)|^{2}
and independent of time. This means that the system will never settle into the ground state, which is the stable state associated with the green dot in Fig.
1
(a) and (d). However, any real system will relax from excited to the ground state (black and green dots in in Fig.
1
(a) and (d), respectively), owing to the interaction with the environment. This can be incorporated into the model by replacing
E_{n}
in
\ket{\psi(t)}
with
E_{n}^{\prime}=E_{n}-i\hbar\frac{\gamma_{n0}}{2}
, where
\gamma_{n0}=1/\tau_{n0}
is the relaxation rate, while
\tau_{n0}
is the associated relaxation time from the excited state
\ket{\phi_{n}}
into the ground state
\ket{\phi_{0}}

cohen2019quantum
. They can be treated as empirical parameters to model the actual relaxation process in the system. We take
\gamma_{n0}=(E_{n}-E_{0})/\hbar
for illustrative purposes. Now the probability of finding the system in the excited state
\ket{\phi_{n}}
is
|c_{n}(0)|^{2}e^{-\gamma_{n0}t}
and decays exponentially with time. Note, that such a decay is not norm-conserving and, therefore, we find it convenient to renormalize the state vector
\ket{\psi(t)}
. The polarization can be computed as the expectation value of the polarization operator
P(t)=\braket{\psi(t)|\hat{P}|\psi(t)}
.

Next we want to extend these ideas to a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H=\frac{\mathcal{P}^{2}}{2M}+U(P)-PE(t)V
, where
E(t)
is now the time dependent electric field. We can discretize the simulation time into intervals and keep the field constant during each interval, while updating it between intervals. After the field is updated we project the state
\ket{\psi(t)}
onto the new basis associated with the eigensatates of the
H
for the given field. We simulate 1.25 periods of AC field applied along polar direction of the rhombohedral phase of PbZrO
3
and show the results in Fig.
2
(b). The first quarter is removed for presentation purposes. The data correctly predicts antiferroelectric loops with the coercive field comparable to the experimental forward phase switching field (E

We find that the relaxation approach is capable of both reproducing an antiferroelectric hysteresis loop and predicting coercive fields in good agreement with experiment, unlike the models discussed in the introduction. However, the GSR approach is limited to zero Kelvin. To overcome this limitation we turn to the density matrix approach with the major advantage that it allows for natural incorporation of temperature.

Density Matrix Based (DMB) Model.
The equilibrium state of a system is now described by the density operator
\rho^{eq}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{n}e^{-\frac{E_{n}}{k_{B}T}}\ket{\phi_{n}}\bra{\phi_{n}}
, where
Z
is the canonical partition function. In such an approach the state of the system is described by the density operator
\rho(t)=\ket{\psi(t)}\bra{\psi(t)}
whose time evolution is given by the Liouville equation
alma99379890528406599

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho]
. The equation, however, does not include the relaxation term.
To recover the predictions of the GSR model we use the same expansion for
\ket{\psi(t)}
in the
\ket{\phi_{n}}
basis as before, and compute
d\rho/dt
. This approach contributes a relaxation term into the Liouville equation (written in
\ket{\phi_{n}}
basis) as follows

\frac{d\rho_{nm}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho]_{nm}-\frac{\gamma_{n0}+\gamma_{mo}}{2}\rho_{nm},

(1)

where the first term on the right hand side describes the intrinsic dynamics as given by the Liouville equation, while the last one models decay of densities (both populations and coherences) due to transitions into the ground state. Just as before, the equation does not capture the gain in densities for the ground state and, therefore, does not conserve the trace of the density matrix, which requires renormalization of the matrix by its trace. The polarization can be computed as the expectation value for the polarization operator
P=Tr\{\rho\hat{P}\}
. This approach reproduces precisely
P(t)
and
P(E)
dependencies computed with the GSR approach shown in Fig.
2
.

To incorporate temperature we recall that at finite temperature the system will be relaxing to
\rho^{eq}
rather than the ground state density implied by Eq.(
1
). This requirement can be captured by updating Eq.(
1
) as follows

\frac{d\rho_{nm}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho]_{nm}-\frac{\gamma_{n0}+\gamma_{mo}}{2}(\rho_{nm}-\delta_{nm}\rho_{nm}^{eq})

(2)

where
\delta_{nm}
is the Kronecker delta. The populations,
\rho_{nn}
, can both decrease and increase (due to first and second terms in parenthesis, respectively) to achieve their equilibrium values, while coherences can only decay. The analytical solution for the relaxation term is
\rho_{nm}^{rlx}(t)=\rho_{nm}^{eq}(1-e^{-\frac{\gamma_{n0}+\gamma_{mo}}{2}t})+\rho_{nm}(0)e^{-\frac{\gamma_{n0}+\gamma_{mo}}{2}t}
.
Note that the models considered so far do not preserve detailed balance. Rather, the balance is achieved globally through renormalization, which repopulates all the sates but not uniformly.

However, for temperatures higher than 10 K, there exists a discontinuity in the polarization at the field in between the two coercive fields, which is not observed experimentally. We speculate that such discontinuity could be due to the fact that population redistribution occurs only through the ground state. The other contributor could be

\rho^{eq}

Note, that for all the data presented so far we turn off intrinsic dynamics, as it is expected to average to zero. Later on we comment on its effects.

Lindblad Operators (LO) Models.
To eliminate the aforementioned issues potentially responsible for the discontinuity of polarization at the phase coexistence fields we state the master equations, that govern dynamics in the presence of relaxational processes, in terms of Lindblad operators
L_{jk}

nielsen2010quantum
, which represent interaction of the system with the environment:

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho]+\sum_{kj}[2L_{kj}\rho L_{kj}^{\dagger}-\{L_{kj}^{\dagger}L_{kj},\rho\}]

(3)

where curly brackets indicate anticommutator. The Lindblad operator,
L_{kj}=\sqrt{\gamma_{kj}}\ket{\phi_{k}}\bra{\phi_{j}}
, describes transition from state
\ket{\phi_{j}}
into state
\ket{\phi_{k}}
caused by the interaction with the environment, at a rate given by
\gamma_{kj}
. The summations go over all states
N
. Transition rate is
\gamma_{kj}=\gamma^{\downarrow}_{jk}
if
E_{k}<E_{j}
(down-transition) and
\gamma_{kj}=\gamma^{\uparrow}_{jk}
if
E_{k}>E_{j}
(up-transition).
We take
\gamma^{\downarrow}_{jk}=k_{scale}|E_{k}-E_{j}|/\hbar
and
\gamma^{\uparrow}_{jk}=\gamma^{\downarrow}_{jk}e^{-\frac{E_{j}-E_{k}}{k_{B}T}}
, where
k_{scale}
is a scaling factor that controls the overall decay rates.
The Lindblad approach preserves trace of the density matrix so we do not have to renormalize it.
In the basis of
\ket{\phi_{n}}
the master equation (
3
) becomes

\frac{d\rho_{nm}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,\rho]_{nm}+2\delta_{nm}\sum_{j}\gamma_{nj}\rho_{jj}-\rho_{nm}\sum_{k}(\gamma_{kn}+\gamma_{km})

(4)

The second term on the right hand side describes the density in-flow into populations
\rho_{nn}
due to transitions from all the other states, while the third term describes the density out-flow (from both populations and coherences,
\rho_{nm}
) due to transitions to all the other states.

We can limit the states participating in the transitions by limiting the number of terms in the sums of Eq.(
3
) and (
4
). For example, limiting summations to include only contributions from
L_{k0}
and
L_{0j}
limits transitions only to and from the ground state (just like the other models considered so far). We term such a reduced model as the Limited Lindblad Operators (LLO) Model, in contrast to a non-limited one indicated simply as LO. Note that LO and LLO models predict significantly faster overall relaxation rates as compared to the other models considered here. This difference is due to the fact that both models preserve detailed balance. So we choose to use
k_{sacale}=1/N
to slow down the overall relaxation. We take
N=
80, which corresponds to including the 80 lowest energy states in these simulations. 80 was chosen as a compromise between computational efficiency and accuracy. Taking too few of basis states may result in an incomplete basis. For reference, for the highest temperatures simulated here (1000 K) 99% of population “resides” in the 15 lowest energy states.

The loops are comparable in shape with the ones obtained from the LLO model, but slimmer due to more efficient relaxation contributed by all states and, notably, the discontinuity in polarization at the coexistence field is no longer present. The latter finding supports our hypothesis that the discontinuity originates from limiting transitions to/from the ground state only.

On the basis of our data, we conclude that all models developed here are capable of describing antiferroelectric behavior with coercive fields comparable to experimental ones. This result provides strong evidence that (anti)ferroelectric phase transitions can be well modeled as relaxational processes within quantum mechanical framework. Furthermore, DMB, LLO and LO models give access to finite-temperature predictions.
The critical methodological difference between the models is in the way they maintain the trace of the density matrix. The DMB approach along with GSR model accomplish that through renormalization, while LO-based models preserve detailed balance. We believe that these difference result in the differences in predictions of
P(t)
and
P(E)
evolutions given in Fig.

;

, while LO-based models predict exponential evolution. Consequently, DMB model predicts square hysteresis loops in contrast to slanted ones from LO-based models.

The role of mass.
Let us now address the role of mass in the predictions of the models. Smaller mass causes larger separation between the energy levels and more quantum-mechanical features, whereas larger mass does the opposite, tightening energy level separations. Figure
4
shows the dependence of the hysteresis loops on the mass for all three models. Larger mass slims the loops down while smaller mass broadens them. For all calculations presented here for PbZrO
3
we use the mass 10,000
m_{e}
as it results in localized states, which we believe are appropriate given that polar distortion is driven primarily by ionic motion. The ground state energy is 0.17 THz and the lowest energy states is 0.48 THz apart from the ground state. We notice that for LLO and LO models the polarization discontinuity at the coexistence field seems to emerge as the mass decreases, suggesting that mass may also play some role in such discontinuity.

Including intrinsic dynamics requires decreasing integration step by 100 times. This reduction is due to the fact that some of the Bohr frequencies can be as high as 81 THz for

m_{e}

Figure

\Delta E/\hbar
. However, these are also the values used for the relaxation rates. Consequently, decay occurs fast enough to mask the intrinsic dynamics in this case. For the LLO and LO models, decay rates are low enough for the intrinsic dynamics to be averaged out. However, the decay rates are not limited to the ones considered here and can be used as parameters that model the particular relaxation mechanism. Slower decay rates will allow for the intrinsic dynamics to contribute to time evolution, although, we still expect them to average out on the time scale associated with relaxation.

Based on our results, we conclude that treating (anti)ferroelectric phase transitions as relaxational processes within a quantum mechanical framework offers an unconventional yet powerful approach to their modeling and understanding. This framework overcomes the limitations of the traditional description of such transitions as Arrhenius-type processes within a classical regime and enables efficient, first-principles–based simulations of phase transitions.
We anticipate that this approach will be applicable to a broad spectrum of other phase transitions – including magnetic, ferroelastic, and multiferroic ones. Furthermore, the framework may provide a novel perspective for modeling chemical reaction rates, and applicable to modeling quantum tunneling phenomena, and electronic transitions.
Equally important, the model is directly applicable to phase transitions driven by electronic rather than ionic degrees of freedom
PhysRevLett.108.237601
;
doi:10.1021/acs.nanolett.4c00141
;
PhysRevB.104.195148
;
PhysRevResearch.3.L042028
, such as proton-ordering and hopping ferroelectrics
doi:10.1021/jacs.0c02924
. Beyond these specific cases, the framework provides a direct means to probe quantum-mechanical features underlying a wide variety of phase transitions.

Technical details of DFT simulations.

The DFT calculations to compute energy profiles were performed using Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)
kresse1996efficient
;
kresse1996efficiency
. The all-electron projector augmented wave (PAW) potentials
blochl1994projector
;
kresse1999ultrasoft
were used to represent the ion-electron interactions. For the calculations, the electronic exchange and correlation part of the potential was described by the local density approximation (LDA)

. The Kohn-Sham orbitals were expanded using the plane wave basis sets with an energy cutoff of value 600 eV. Structural relaxation (where needed) were carried out using the conjugate-gradient algorithm until the Hellmann-Feynman forces on every atom are less than 0.005 eVÅ
-1
. The
\Gamma
-centered k-point mesh with the spacing of 0.22 Å
-1
was used in our calculations. The polarization of the systems were calculated according to the modern theory of polarization
