Uterine leiomyosarcoma diagnostic challenges in a resource-limited setting: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Nigat Amsalu Addis, Kinfemicheal Tilahun Yigzaw, Bethlehem Aliye Asfaw, Girma Damtew Adisu, Melkam Feleke Mekonnen, Misganaw Abere Worku, Meseret Hussen Shibesh, Getachew Shiferaw Yigezaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionUterine leiomyosarcoma, constituting only 1–2% of all uterine malignancies, is a rare and aggressive cancer arising from the smooth muscles of the uterine wall. Owing to its rarity, identifying risk factors and clinical symptoms can be challenging, often leading to misdiagnosis as benign leiomyomas, particularly in settings with limited access to advanced imaging modalities. This case report presents a 40-year-old Ethiopian woman diagnosed with uterine leiomyosarcoma after presenting with nonspecific symptoms, underscoring the diagnostic difficulties associated with this malignancy in resource-limited environments.Case presentationA 40-year-old Nulliparous woman from North Western Ethiopia presented to the Gynecologic Outpatient Department with complaints of lower abdominal pain, intermittent vaginal bleeding, and abdominal swelling lasting 1 month. Initial imaging suggested a diagnosis of intramural myoma. She underwent total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, with histopathological examination confirming the diagnosis of uterine leiomyosarcoma. Unfortunately, she experienced rapid recurrence, presenting 77 days postoperation with a vaginal mass, foul-smelling discharge, and fever. Despite the suspicion of local metastasis based on magnetic resonance imaging findings, the patient was lost to follow-up before definitive treatment could be initiated. This case represents the first documented instance of uterine leiomyosarcoma in East Africa, underscoring significant diagnostic and management challenges faced in resource-limited settings.DiscussionThe case underscores critical diagnostic challenges, particularly the difficulty in distinguishing uterine leiomyosarcoma from benign tumors such as leiomyomas. Furthermore, it reveals the impact of limited access to advanced imaging techniques on timely diagnosis and treatment. Despite complete surgical resection, the patient’s follow-up revealed rapid disease recurrence, compounded by the loss to follow-up before definitive treatment could be initiated.ConclusionThis case emphasizes the aggressive nature of uterine leiomyosarcoma and the need for heightened clinical suspicion, particularly in low-resource environments. It calls for the development of a preoperative risk assessment tool tailored to leiomyosarcoma, enabling better differentiation from benign conditions and informing surgical management. The rapid progression of this patient’s disease highlights the urgent need for effective follow-up strategies and access to comprehensive oncological care in developing regions.

Introduction

Uterine leiomyosarcoma (ULMS) is an extremely scarce kind of cancer that develops from the smooth muscles of the uterine wall [1]. Although they can be identified in patients as young as 20 years old, uterine sarcomas often affect people over the age of 40. It is challenging to determine risk factors for uterine leiomyosarcoma due to its rarity. When a patient with ULMS presents clinically, their symptoms are frequently nonspecific, making it difficult to differentiate from leiomyomas on this basis, and its presentation result from the main tumors and its metastases displacement of specific anatomic areas rather than invasion [2, 3]. It is evident that many cases of uterine leiomyosarcoma (ULMS) in developing countries highlight significant challenges that arise due to limited medical resources and access to advanced diagnostic tools. For instance, imaging techniques such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) scans, which are crucial for differentiating between leiomyomas and sarcomas, are often unavailable or underutilized in these settings. As a result, patients frequently present with advanced disease, which complicates treatment options and outcomes [4, 5].

We report a case of a 40-year-old Ethiopian female who presented with lower abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding and was later diagnosed with leiomyosarcoma of the uterus, which is a rare case report. In addition, there is no report of ULMS in our country, Ethiopia, which triggers us to report this case to the rest of the world. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report to be reported in East Africa. This case exemplified clinical problems in the diagnosis of leiomyosarcoma and treatment.

Several factors influence the presentation and diagnosis of uterine leiomyosarcoma (ULMS) in the region. First, while the incidence of uterine cancers in Africa is lower than in Western countries, the rarity of ULMS leads to limited data, resulting in underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis as common conditions including leiomyomas. In Ethiopia, restricted access to advanced diagnostic tools heightens the likelihood of late-stage presentation or erroneous initial diagnoses. Second, cultural and socioeconomic factors may cause women to delay seeking medical attention due to stigma around symptoms, leading to advanced-stage presentations. Lastly, the healthcare infrastructure’s heterogeneity in East Africa impacts expertise in gynecologic oncology, influencing clinical decision-making and management of ULMS cases.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old Nulliparous woman from North Western Ethiopia presented to the Gynecologic Outpatient Department (OPD) on 22 January, 2024, with a 1-month history of lower abdominal pain radiating to the back, described as dull, aching, and boring in type, with no variability with changes in position. She also reported 2 weeks of associated vaginal bleeding, characterized as minimal in amount, dark clotted, intermittent, and irregular. In addition, the patient had a history of unquantified weight loss and decreased appetite, accompanied by a noticeable lower abdominal swelling that had remained unchanged in size over the past month.

Despite her attempts to conceive with two partners, she had never been able to bear children and had not undergone any investigations for infertility. She did not use contraceptive methods and reported no history of significant medical illnesses or surgeries.

Upon examination, she appeared chronically unwell. Vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 100/60 mmHg, pulse rate 96 beats per minute, respiratory rate 21 breaths/minute, and temperature 36.7 °C, with no signs of pallor. A 16-week sized, gravid-equivalent abdominopelvic mass was palpable, smooth, mobile, and associated with moderate tenderness upon deep palpation. A 3-cm fleshy tissue was also noted protruding from the cervical os with no active vaginal bleeding.

Owing to financial constraints and limited access to advanced imaging modalities, the patient was unable to afford a CT or MRI scan. Instead, an abdominopelvic ultrasound, the most accessible modality in our setting, was performed. The ultrasound revealed a 10 × 10 cm relatively hypo-echoic and well-circumscribed mass arising from the uterus, with no free fluid observed. The assessment suggested a probable diagnosis of an intramural myoma with degeneration. Given the presumptive diagnosis of a degenerating leiomyoma, the patient was planned for a total abdominal hysterectomy (TAH) and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO). All other necessary laboratory investigations were conducted and returned normal.

The patient underwent surgery on 29 January, 2024. Intraoperatively, findings included a uterus consistent with a 16-week sized gravid equivalent mass primarily located on the posterior wall. The mass was adherent to the left Fallopian tube and rectum, and no free fluid was noted. A peritoneal wash was taken for cytology analysis, and the entire uterus, along with bilateral ovaries and Fallopian tubes, was removed. No pelvic or para-aortic lymphadenopathy was palpable intraoperatively. The excised specimens were sent for histopathological examination.

Postoperatively, the patient had an uneventful recovery and was discharged in satisfactory condition after a 5-day hospital stay, with instructions to return after 1 month for pathology results.

In the first month of follow-up, the patient was investigated with chest radiography for possible metastasis, and were unremarkable. She was also imaged with abdominopelvic ultrasonography and was unremarkable at that time. She was appointed to come after 3 months for a follow-up.

On the 77th day postoperatively, she presented with protrusion of a vaginal mass of 3 days duration and foul-smelling vaginal discharge of 1-week duration. She also had high-grade fever and lower abdominal pain. Upon per vaginal examination, there was a 7 cm × 6 cm protruding mass per vagina which was fungated from the vaginal cuff, and there was foul-smelling vaginal discharge. She was investigated with a complete blood count, organ function test, and urinalysis, all of which were normal. The abdominopelvic ultrasonography revealed an 8 cm × 6 cm relatively hyperechoic central pelvic mass. We put her on ceftriaxone 1 g intravenously twice daily and metronidazole 500 mg intravenously three times daily while waiting for the MRI result.

Discussion

Uterine leiomyosarcoma, which makes up only 1–2% of all uterine malignancies, is a scarce kind of cancer that develops from the smooth muscles of the uterine wall [1]. It is part of the homologous type of uterine sarcoma, which only contains elements normally found in uterine tissues [6]. While leiomyosarcoma can occur in the uterus, as in our instance, it is usually seen elsewhere in the pelvis, such as the cervix or bladder [7]. The average age at diagnosis is almost 60 years old [8, 9]. In comparison with white individuals, Black patients have a roughly two-fold greater incidence of leiomyosarcomas [10, 11]. It is challenging to determine risk factors for uterine leiomyosarcoma due to its rarity. Previous radiation therapy to the pelvis (10–25%), long-term tamoxifen use (1–2%), and specific inherited genetic abnormalities, such as retinoblastoma and Li-Fraumeni syndrome, are among the possible risk factors [2, 12, 13]. With very few exceptions (atypical or cellular variants), leiomyomas do not seem to be the precursor to leiomyosarcomas [14]. We were unable to conclude the known risk factors for our patient.

When a patient with ULMS presents clinically, their symptoms are frequently nonspecific, making it difficult to differentiate from leiomyomas on this basis, and its presentation is a result of the main tumor and its metastases displacement of specific anatomic areas rather than invasion [2, 3]. Abnormal vaginal bleeding (56%), a palpable pelvic mass (54%), and/or pelvic discomfort (22%), are common presentations of uterine LMS [12, 15]. Our patient presented with all of the above cardinal symptoms. Further clinical manifestations in advanced disease may be the consequence of metastases and local disease dissemination, which are uncommon in benign leiomyomas.

Mostly, a fixed mass indicates the presence of a malignant tumor. A malignant tumor that has not yet spread to the uterine serosa, however, may be mobile; therefore, this is not pathognomonic. Unfortunately, a leiomyoma and a uterine sarcoma cannot be differentiated from one another by medical examination results.

Some affected people can be diagnosed with uterine leiomyosarcoma through endometrial sampling; however, there is limited information available regarding the test’s sensitivity [16, 17]. In a research, 13 out of 21 cases (62%) had the histology properly recognized as a sarcoma by a preoperative endometrial sample [18].

To image ULMS in resource-limited settings, ultrasonography often serves as the initial imaging modality owing to its accessibility and cost-effectiveness [19]. However, studies have shown that the diagnostic accuracy of ultrasound for ULMS can be quite low [19].

CT imaging, while more effective than ultrasound in certain situations, also presents challenges [20]. Though it can visualize the size and location of tumors and potentially reveal characteristics suggestive of malignancy, the specificity of CT for diagnosing ULMS is limited [19].

MRI has been recognized as the most sensitive modality for diagnosing ULMS, owing to its superior soft tissue resolution and ability to provide detailed anatomical information [19]. It can effectively distinguish malignant tumors based on specific imaging characteristics, including increased signal intensity and diffusion-weighted imaging parameters [19]. Research indicates that MRI can achieve diagnostic accuracy rates as high as 92.2%, making it a preferred method for evaluating suspected cases of ULMS [19].

It is evident that many cases of uterine leiomyosarcoma (ULMS) in developing countries highlight significant challenges that arise owing to limited medical resources and access to advanced diagnostic tools. For instance, imaging techniques such as MRI and CT scans, which are crucial for differentiating between leiomyomas and sarcomas, are often unavailable or underutilized in these settings. As a result, patients frequently present with advanced disease, which complicates treatment options and outcomes [4, 5].

Our case exemplifies this scenario. The patient presented with nonspecific symptoms such as lower abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding, which are common in many cases of ULMS but can easily be attributed to more prevalent conditions such as leiomyomas. Owing to the lack of adequate imaging facilities, the diagnosis was made postsurgically on the basis of histological examination, similar to other reports from low-resource regions where preoperative diagnostic capabilities are limited.

In addition, the treatment challenges include constraints on surgical facilities, availability of surgical expertise, and postoperative care, all of which can impact recovery and survival rates. In our case, the implementation of a comprehensive surgical approach was hindered by the lack of adequate preoperative risk scoring systems to justify more aggressive interventions, as highlighted in other case reports from similar settings.

After uterine tissue is removed (by myomectomy or hysterectomy), uterine leiomyosarcoma is diagnosed histologically on the basis of pathology assessment. Most frequently, uterine leiomyosarcomas are discovered after surgery for what are thought to be leiomyomas [21, 22].

It is frequently essential to examine the mass at several different areas [23].

Leiomyosarcomas are usually big (> 10 cm), solitary masses that are yellow or tan in color, and have soft, squishy cut surfaces with patches of necrosis and bleeding [24].

Leiomyosarcomas usually (but not always) show regions of coagulative necrosis, many mitoses (> 10 per 10 high-power fields), and significant cellular atypia (Stanford criteria). When two of the three characteristics were present, there was a greater than 10% chance of metastatic dissemination [25]. Our patient fulfills the above criteria, significant cellular atypia, and regions of coagulative necrosis. On the precise amount of mitotic activity needed for a sarcoma diagnosis, however, opinions differ [26]. Hypercellularity and infiltrative border are two more typical characteristics that are useful in borderline situations but are not diagnostic of themselves [26].

The majority of the time, uterine leiomyosarcomas are diagnosed after a myomectomy or hysterectomy for suspected benign uterine leiomyomas, as seen in our patient [16, 27].

Disease-free survival was substantially correlated with total cytoreduction with a complete hysterectomy for patients whose uterine LMS is contained within the uterus at the time of surgery [28]. When a total hysterectomy is performed, BSO is frequently carried out, especially for individuals who are menopausal or perimenopausal. It is unclear, nevertheless, if BSO affects survival in individuals with recently diagnosed LMS.

Despite the limited data, patients who have stage II or stage III are more likely to have improved overall survival rates following total hysterectomy with BSO and surgical cytoreduction of intra-abdominal and retroperitoneal disease [16, 29]. Surgery is not beneficial for people with diseases that cannot be treated with adequate cytoreduction or for those who have broad, extensive disease, such as in our patient [28].

When patients undergo a total hysterectomy and are later diagnosed with leiomyosarcoma (on the basis of a final pathologic examination), such as our patient, avoid doing a second surgery just for staging. To help direct postoperative care, instead, get a postoperative computed tomography of the pelvis, abdomen, and chest.

If there is evidence of extrauterine illness or if the pelvic nodes are palpably enlarged during surgery, a pelvic lymphadenectomy should be performed [30].

When the pathology confirms the diagnosis, all patients should have imaging to rule out metastatic disease [31, 32]. Chest radiographs, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET) scans are all utilized to assess in metastatic disease for soft tissue sarcomas, despite the lack of data regarding the best imaging evaluation for patients with LMS.

Staging of ULMS

Staging of ULMS is based on the TNM staging of AJCC-UICC and FIGO surgical staging [33].

The stage of our patient at the time of surgery was stage IB (limited to the uterus), but after surgery, while she presented with recurrence, it was stage IVB (distant metastasis).

The standard of care after resection of an intact, uterus-limited specimen is observation [34, 35]. Radiation therapy and combination chemotherapy were all assessed as adjuvant treatments for patients with early-stage illness, even though the results were not statistically significant [34–37]. Patients with intra-abdominal involvement (stage III) or distant metastases (stage IV) after complete resection of the disease may be offered postresection chemotherapy or combined-modality treatment of chemotherapy and radiation therapy; however, it has not been established whether treatment improves survival [35, 38]. Unluckily, our patient was not starting any modality of treatment after the first surgery.

In our case of uterine leiomyosarcoma (ULMS), the diagnostic pathway was severely hindered by the reliance on ultrasonography, which offered limited specificity in characterizing the mass, leading to a misdiagnosis of intramural myoma. The absence of MRI or CT scans, owing to financial constraints, prevented a thorough evaluation of tumor extent and complexity before surgery. Consequently, the definitive diagnosis was only made postoperatively through histopathology, causing significant delays in appropriate management. Furthermore, after surgery, the patient faced barriers to accessing adjuvant therapies, and the lack of effective follow-up protocols contributed to her loss to follow-up and exacerbated management challenges, highlighting systemic issues within the healthcare infrastructure in our resource-limited setting.

Clinical evaluations: schedule regular clinical assessments every 3–6 months for the first 2 years, then biannually for 3 years, and annually thereafter. Focus on symptoms including abdominal pain, changes in bowel or bladder habits, and unusual vaginal bleeding.

Imaging studies: conduct imaging studies, such as pelvic ultrasounds or CT scans, every 6–12 months for the first 2 years to monitor for recurrence. Adjust the frequency based on clinical findings and the patient’s risk factors.

Consideration of risk factors: in light of the patient’s location in Ethiopia, optimize surveillance according to available healthcare resources. Educate her on warning signs of recurrence and the importance of prompt reporting of new symptoms.

Incorporating psychological support: address the psychological impacts of cancer treatment by providing referrals for counseling or support groups for the patient and her family.

By implementing these strategies, clinicians can improve patient management and support, ultimately enhancing outcomes for those with uterine leiomyosarcoma [39].

Regardless of stage, patients with LMS have a devastating prognosis [40]. The 5-year disease-specific survival rate was 66% in a study of patients with LMS [31]. For stage I, II, III, and IV disease, the 5-year disease-specific overall survival was 76%, 60%, 45%, and 29%, respectively. Higher tumor grade, higher disease stage, and African American race were all linked to worse disease-specific survival.

Most frequently, ULMS spreads to the abdomen, pelvis, liver, and lungs [41]. Palliative care is provided for patients whose disease has spread and is not amenable to complete removal surgically [42].

Conclusion

This case highlights the critical importance of early suspicion and accurate diagnosis of uterine leiomyosarcoma (ULMS), particularly in resource-limited settings. The nonspecific symptoms can easily lead to misdiagnosis, often resulting in advanced disease at presentation. Our patient’s experience underscores the urgent need for a preoperative risk assessment tool tailored specifically for low-resource environments. Such a tool would facilitate better differentiation between malignant and benign conditions, guiding appropriate surgical interventions and potentially improving outcomes. Moreover, after surgery, regular follow-up imaging and clinical evaluations are essential for the early detection of recurrence. It is crucial to educate patients on warning signs and ensure access to psychological support, which can significantly enhance management outcomes for those diagnosed with ULMS. By strengthening our approach to diagnosis and management, we can create a cohesive narrative that emphasizes the need for increased awareness and vigilance to improve survival rates in this aggressive malignancy.
