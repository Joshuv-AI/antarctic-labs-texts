Cardiac MRI unveils the hidden storm: Myocarditis-induced ventricular tachycardia in a young patient with a structurally normal heart
Authors: Mohammed Alkhanafsa, Mohammed Aloqaily, Ra’ed Ababneh, Laith Rhabneh, Osayd Mosleh, Ibrahim Alfarrajin, Hanee S Valiyakath, Willington Francis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocarditis is an underrecognized cause of malignant ventricular arrhythmias, particularly in young adults with preserved cardiac structure on initial evaluation. We report a 37-year-old previously healthy man who presented with hemodynamically unstable ventricular tachycardia accompanied by significant troponin elevation but normal coronary arteries and preserved left ventricular systolic function on echocardiography. The arrhythmia initially mimicked idiopathic fascicular ventricular tachycardia. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging subsequently revealed subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement and myocardial edema, establishing the diagnosis of acute myocarditis as the arrhythmogenic substrate. Therapy was adjusted with good clinical stability and no recurrence of arrhythmia. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges and the value of cardiac magnetic resonance imaging in unexplained ventricular tachycardia and emphasizes the importance of thorough history followed by etiology-directed management in myocarditis-related arrhythmias, and that clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for myocarditis in similar presentations.Keywords: Myocarditis, Ventricular tachycardia, Cardiac MRI, Late gadolinium enhancement, Subepicardial edema, Idiopathic VT

Introduction

Myocarditis, an inflammatory disease of the heart muscle, is a diagnostically elusive cause of life-threatening arrhythmias, particularly in young adults whose initial evaluation may show a structurally normal heart [1]. While classical presentations include chest pain, heart failure, or elevated cardiac biomarkers, a subset of patients manifest with isolated ventricular tachycardia (VT) without overt hemodynamic compromise or echocardiographic abnormalities. This atypical presentation can mimic idiopathic VT or acute coronary syndrome (ACS), often delaying recognition of the underlying inflammatory substrate [2].

We present the case of a 37-year-old male with no prior cardiac history who developed hemodynamically unstable VT. The absence of regional wall motion abnormalities on echocardiography, angiographically normal coronary arteries, and the delayed reporting of a viral prodrome initially obscured the diagnosis. Advanced cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) ultimately revealed subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and myocardial edema, hallmarks of acute myocarditis, which served as the arrhythmogenic focus [1]. This case highlights the critical role of CMR in unmasking myocarditis in patients with unexplained VT and preserved cardiac function, while illustrating the importance of a broad differential diagnosis to avoid potentially inappropriate therapy [2].

Case presentation

Case history/examination

A 37-year-old male, non-smoker, with no known past medical history and no family history of sudden cardiac death at a young age, was brought by Emergency Medical Services (EMS) to the Heart Hospital Emergency Department with complaints of sudden-onset palpitations and chest discomfort described as a pressure-like sensation.

On initial EMS assessment, the patient was found to be in wide QRS complex tachycardia with a right bundle branch block (RBBB) pattern and left axis deviation, with a heart rate exceeding 220 beats per minute, features suggestive of ventricular tachycardia (VT) (Fig. 1). Initially hemodynamically stable, he soon deteriorated, with a drop in Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). The EMS team administered fentanyl and ketamine for sedation and performed synchronized electrical cardioversion. Sinus rhythm was achieved after three attempts (Fig. 2).

Following cardioversion, a 12-lead ECG showed normal sinus rhythm with subtle ST depression (0.5 mm) in the inferior leads and mild ST elevation (1 mm) in lead aVR (Fig. 3). The corrected QT interval (QTc) was 452 milliseconds. Due to concern for acute coronary syndrome (ACS), the patient was loaded with aspirin (300 mg) and clopidogrel (600 mg).

Post-cardioversion 12-lead ECG showing normal sinus rhythm with subtle ST depression (0.5 mm) in the inferior leads and mild ST elevation (1 mm) in lead aVR.

Upon arrival at the Heart Hospital ED, the patient was mildly drowsy but arousable, with a GCS of 14/15. He was not in respiratory or circulatory distress and maintained oxygen saturation of 97% on 2 L/min via nasal cannula. His blood pressure was 137/99 mmHg and heart rate was 87 bpm. He denied chest pain during evaluation. On physical examination, heart sounds were normal (S1, S2) with no murmurs or added sounds. Lungs were clear to auscultation, and the remainder of the examination was unremarkable.

A repeat ECG showed normal sinus rhythm without ST-segment deviations (Fig. 4). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal global systolic function with a biplane left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 52%, no regional wall motion abnormalities, normal diastolic function, and no pericardial effusion. The inferior vena cava (IVC) showed <50% inspiratory collapse.

Repeat 12-lead ECG demonstrating normal sinus rhythm with no ST-segment deviations.

The patient was admitted to the Coronary Care Unit (CCU) for monitoring. Serial ECGs and troponin levels were obtained. Three hours after admission, troponin-T increased from 88 to 489 ng/L, without any new ischemic changes on ECG. Coronary angiography was performed, revealing angiographically normal coronary arteries. As no obstructive coronary disease was found, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) was discontinued.

The electrophysiology (EP) team was consulted. Based on the ECG findings of wide QRS tachycardia with RBBB pattern and left axis deviation, the arrhythmia was presumed to be idiopathic left ventricular post-fascicular tachycardia, a form of idiopathic VT often responsive to calcium channel blockers. The EP team recommended initiating oral verapamil and obtaining cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) to evaluate for structural or inflammatory myocardial disease.

Conclusion and results (outcome and follow-up)

During his CCU stay, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, with a heart rate of 55-65 bpm and no recurrence of arrhythmias on continuous telemetry monitoring.

CMR revealed subepicardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) in the basal-to-mid anterolateral, inferolateral, and mid-inferior walls, along with corresponding myocardial edema, hyperemia, and regional hypokinesis (Fig. 5). These findings were consistent with recent myocarditis, likely serving as the arrhythmogenic substrate for his VT. Further history obtained after the CMR revealed that the patient had experienced a feverish sensation and sore throat several days prior to this presentation.

Given the cardiac MRI results confirming myocarditis, the electrophysiology team advised discontinuing verapamil and initiating bisoprolol 1.25 mg once daily, considering its preferable therapeutic profile for post-fascicular VT in the setting of inflammatory myocardial injury. The case was ultimately diagnosed as left ventricular post-fascicular ventricular tachycardia triggered by acute myocarditis. Following stabilization, the patient was discharged with arrangements for outpatient electrophysiology follow-up.

Discussion

This case presents a challenging presentation of ventricular tachycardia (VT) secondary to acute myocarditis. While the initial ECG showed a wide QRS tachycardia with right bundle branch block (RBBB) and left axis deviation—features classically associated with idiopathic left ventricular fascicular VT—the subsequent diagnosis of myocarditis as the underlying cause was unexpected. A 2022 cohort study found that a significant proportion of myocarditis-related VT cases can be initially misclassified as idiopathic VT, underscoring the importance of CMR in avoiding therapeutic missteps [3].

The differential diagnosis for a wide QRS tachycardia with hemodynamic instability is broad, primarily including VT, supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy, and pre-excited tachyarrhythmias. The RBBB morphology with left axis deviation in this case strongly favored VT of left ventricular origin [4]. After cardioversion, the significant troponin elevation and subtle ST-segment changes raised concern for ACS, but coronary angiography definitively ruled out obstructive epicardial disease. This left two leading possibilities: idiopathic fascicular VT or a primary myocardial process like myocarditis [5]. The echocardiogram, showing a preserved ejection fraction and no wall motion abnormalities, could not differentiate between these. A normal CRP, while not uncommon in viral myocarditis (as the inflammatory response can be localized to the myocardium without a robust systemic biomarker surge), did not rule out the diagnosis. Ultimately, CMR was pivotal in distinguishing between these entities by directly visualizing the myocardial tissue, revealing the subepicardial LGE and edema characteristic of inflammation [2]. This case demonstrates CMR's superiority over echocardiography and angiography in identifying subtle, non-ischemic myocardial injury [6].

The management of this patient evolved appropriately with the diagnostic findings. Initial emergency cardioversion and ruling out ACS were essential first steps. The electrophysiology team's provisional diagnosis of idiopathic fascicular VT and initiation of verapamil was a reasonable interpretation of the ECG findings. However, the CMR results prompted a critical etiological shift. In the context of active myocardial inflammation, beta-blockers like bisoprolol are generally preferred over verapamil due to their ability to reduce myocardial oxygen demand, suppress arrhythmias, and mitigate sympathetic overdrive, which can exacerbate inflammation [7]. This pivot to etiology-directed therapy was a key decision point.

Given the presentation with a hemodynamically unstable VT in the setting of acute inflammation, the question of arrhythmic risk stratification and potential need for defibrillator therapy is paramount. Current guidelines recommend against immediate implantation of a permanent ICD during the acute phase of myocarditis, as the arrhythmic risk is often transient and LV function can improve significantly with resolution of inflammation [8,9]. The decision for a permanent ICD is typically deferred for at least 3-6 months to allow for myocardial recovery on optimal medical therapy. In the interim, a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator (WCD) could be considered for patients deemed to be at high-risk for recurrent life-threatening arrhythmias [8]. In this case, while a WCD was not placed, the patient was monitored in the hospital and discharged with close outpatient follow-up. The burden of LGE on CMR, particularly its extent, is a known predictor of long-term arrhythmic risk [10]. A repeat CMR in 3-6 months is often useful to reassess LV function, the extent of residual LGE/scar, and guide further decisions regarding ICD implantation and activity restriction.

The clinical trajectory of this patient reinforces several key imperatives: (1) myocarditis must remain a differential in unexplained VT, even without classic inflammatory signs or heart failure [5]; (2) CMR is the preferred noninvasive modality for diagnosing myocardial inflammation when conventional testing is inconclusive [2]; and (3) etiology-driven management is crucial, as it can profoundly alter outcomes [7]. This single case cannot, however, establish broad clinical guidelines, and its findings should be interpreted within the context of its inherent limitations as a report of a single patient experience.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the complexities of diagnosing and managing myocarditis-induced VT in a young patient with a structurally normal heart on initial imaging. The discordance between troponin elevation and angiographically normal coronaries, alongside the absence of overt heart failure, highlights the need for a high index of suspicion for myocarditis in unexplained arrhythmias. CMR emerged as the cornerstone of diagnosis, revealing inflammatory changes that echocardiography missed. The transition from verapamil to bisoprolol underscores the importance of etiology-specific therapy to address both arrhythmia and myocardial inflammation. Clinicians should prioritize CMR in similar cases and maintain a low threshold for re-evaluating seemingly "idiopathic" VT when biomarkers or history suggest an inflammatory trigger. This approach, combined with careful arrhythmic risk stratification and follow-up imaging, not only refines diagnosis but also optimizes long-term outcomes by addressing the underlying substrate.
