A Study of the Extreme Points in the Unit Ball of J ​ T JT
Spiros A. Argyros

Abstract
. In this note, we investigate the extreme points of the unit ball of the James Tree space ( J ​ T JT ). We relate the geometric structure of J ​ T JT to the classical James space J J and provide partial characterizations of extremality based on the concept of separated vectors. We provide a complete characterization for positive vectors and establish the equal sums property for positive extreme points.



1.
Introduction

The motivation for this research stems from the characterization of the extreme points of the unit ball of the classical James space
J
, proved by S. Bellenot
[
2
]
and recently revisited by S. Argyros and M. González
[
1
]
. In that context, it was established that a vector in
J
is an extreme point if and only if it satisfies a geometric property termed “separated” (or equivalently, if its norm coincides with its
\ell_{2}
norm). This result naturally raises the question if the extreme points of the unit ball of
JT
satisfy similar properties, as
JT
is the extension of
J
on the dyadic tree.

In this note, we investigate the problem for the James Tree space
JT
. The space
JT
is defined as the completion of the space of finitely supported functions on the dyadic tree
\mathcal{T}
under the norm:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in S}x(\alpha)\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

where the supremum is taken over all families
\mathcal{P}
of pairwise disjoint segments in
\mathcal{T}
. Such families
\mathcal{P}
will be called partitions. A partition
\mathcal{P}
for which this supremum is attained is called an
x
-norming partition
.

Central to our study is the concept of a
separated vector
. A vector
x\in JT
is said to be separated if for every pair of distinct nodes
\alpha,\beta
in its range, there exists an
x
-norming partition that separates them (i.e.,
\alpha
and
\beta
belong to different segments).

The main problem we study here is whether the separation of a vector
x\in JT
implies that
x
is an extreme point of the unit ball.

We recall that for the classical James space
J
, the condition for extremality is stringent:
x
is an extreme point if and only if
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{J}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
. In
JT
, this equivalence holds for vectors supported on a single branch, but the tree structure allows for more complex extremal behaviors.

We provide an affirmative answer to the main problem for vectors with finite support. For the cone of positive vectors, we provide a complete answer: a positive vector is an extreme point if and only if it is separated. Unlike the case of the space
J
, where the only positive extreme points are the elements of the basis, in
JT
there are many positive extreme points. For example, any vector
x_{n}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq n}e_{\alpha}
is an extreme point of the ball of radius
\left\lVert x_{n}\right\rVert
. The analysis of positive vectors is significantly facilitated by the existence of a “Greedy Algorithm”, which we introduce to construct
x
-norming partitions effectively.

Finally, for these positive extreme points, we prove the validity of the
Equal Sums Property
. This property states that for any node in the support, the sum of the vector along any branch segment descending from that node is constant. This result highlights the rigid structure imposed by extremality on positive vectors.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we establish the notation and basic properties of the dyadic tree and define the space
JT
. We also introduce the geometric concepts of extreme points and separated vectors.

2.1.
The Dyadic Tree Structure

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the dyadic tree. The elements of
\mathcal{T}
are finite sequences of
0
’s and
1
’s, including the empty sequence
\emptyset
(the root). We equip
\mathcal{T}
with the natural partial order:
\alpha\leq\beta
if
\alpha
is an initial segment of
\beta
.

Definition 2.1

(Segments)
.

A subset
S\subset\mathcal{T}
is called a
segment
if it is convex with respect to the tree order and linearly ordered. That is, for any
\alpha,\beta\in S
with
\alpha\leq\beta
, if
\gamma\in\mathcal{T}
satisfies
\alpha\leq\gamma\leq\beta
, then
\gamma\in S
. A
branch
of
\mathcal{T}
is a maximal segment.

Definition 2.2

(Complete Subtree)
.

A subset
K\subseteq\mathcal{T}
is called a
complete subtree
if for any
\alpha,\beta\in K
with
\alpha\leq\beta
, the entire segment
[\alpha,\beta]=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{T}:\alpha\leq\gamma\leq\beta\}
is contained in
K
.

2.2.
The Space
JT

The James Tree space, denoted by
JT
, is constructed as a completion of finitely supported functions on
\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 2.3

(Support and Range)
.

Let
x:\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function.

(1)

The
support
of
x
, denoted
\operatorname{supp}(x)
, is the set of nodes where
x
is non-zero:

\operatorname{supp}(x)=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{T}:x(\alpha)\neq 0\}

(2)

The
range
of
x
, denoted
\operatorname{ran}(x)
, is defined as the smallest complete subtree of
\mathcal{T}
that contains
\operatorname{supp}(x)
.

Definition 2.4

(The Norm of
JT
)
.

Let
c_{00}(\mathcal{T})
be the space of functions
x:\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}
with finite support. For
x\in c_{00}(\mathcal{T})
, we define the norm:

(1)

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in S}x(\alpha)\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

where the supremum is taken over all families
\mathcal{P}
of pairwise disjoint segments in
\mathcal{T}
. The space
JT
is the completion of
(c_{00}(\mathcal{T}),\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{JT})
.

The following is well known.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a complete subtree of
\mathcal{T}
. Then for every
x\in JT
we have

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}\geq\left\lVert x|\mathcal{B}\right\rVert_{JT}

Proof.

Let
y=x|_{\mathcal{B}}
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be any partition of disjoint segments in
\mathcal{B}
. Since
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{P}
is also a valid family of disjoint segments in
\mathcal{T}
. Furthermore, for any
S\in\mathcal{P}
, since
S\subset\mathcal{B}
, the values of
y
and
x
coincide on
S
. Thus, the sum of squares calculated for
y
with partition
\mathcal{P}
is one of the sums considered in the supremum definition of
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}
. Therefore,
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}\geq\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}
. Taking the supremum over all partitions in
\mathcal{B}
, we obtain
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}\geq\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{JT}
.
∎

Throughout this note a partition
\mathcal{P}
is a collection of pairwise disjoint segments of
\mathcal{T}
. For a fixed partition
\mathcal{P}=\{S_{i}\}_{i}
, we denote the value of
x
with respect to this partition as:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}=\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in S}x(\alpha)\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

Proposition 2.6

(Existence of Norming Partitions)
.

For every
x\in JT
, there exists at least one partition
\mathcal{P}
such that the norm is attained:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}.

Such a partition is called an
x
-norming partition
.

2.3.
Extreme Points and Separated Vectors

We focus on the geometric structure of the unit sphere of
JT
.

Definition 2.7

(Extreme Point)
.

A non-zero vector
x\in JT
is called an
extreme point
of the ball of radius
\left\lVert x\right\rVert
if it cannot be written as a non-trivial convex combination of two distinct vectors with the same norm. Formally, if
x=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z
with
0<\lambda<1
and
\left\lVert y\right\rVert=\left\lVert z\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
, then
x=y=z
.

A crucial geometric property in
JT
is the concept of separation. As we will see next, separation within the support is insufficient for extremality; we must consider the entire range.

Definition 2.8

(Separated Vector)
.

A vector
x\in JT
is said to be
separated
if for every pair of distinct nodes
\alpha,\beta\in\operatorname{ran}(x)
, there exists an
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}
such that
\alpha
and
\beta
belong to different segments in
\mathcal{P}
.

In the sequel, by the term that
x
is an extreme point, we will mean an extreme point of the ball with radius
\left\lVert x\right\rVert
. The following proposition provides a specific characterization of non-extremality in
JT
, relating perturbations to the norming partitions.

Proposition 2.9

(Characterization of Extreme Points)
.

A vector
x\in JT
is
not
an extreme point if and only if there exists a non-zero vector
y\in JT
such that:

(1)

\left\lVert x\right\rVert=\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
, and

(2)

For every
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}=\{S_{i}\}
, we have
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}=0
.

Note that
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}=0
implies that for every segment
S\in\mathcal{P}
,
\sum_{\alpha\in S}y(\alpha)=0
.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) Suppose such a vector
y\neq 0
exists. We can write
x=\frac{1}{2}(x+y)+\frac{1}{2}(x-y)
. Since
\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
,
x
is the midpoint of a non-trivial segment on the sphere. Thus,
x
is not an extreme point.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose
x
is not an extreme point. Then there exist distinct vectors
u,v\in JT
with
\left\lVert u\right\rVert=\left\lVert v\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
such that
x=\frac{1}{2}(u+v)
. Let
y=\frac{1}{2}(u-v)\neq 0
. Then
u=x+y
and
v=x-y
, satisfying condition (1).

Now, let
\mathcal{P}=\{S_{i}\}
be any
x
-norming partition. For any segment
S\in\mathcal{P}
, let
x_{S}=\sum_{\alpha\in S}x(\alpha)
and
y_{S}=\sum_{\alpha\in S}y(\alpha)
. By definition,

\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}x_{S}^{2}.

Since
\left\lVert x\pm y\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert x\pm y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}
, we have:

\left\lVert x\pm y\right\rVert^{2}\geq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}(x_{S}\pm y_{S})^{2}.

Summing the inequalities for
x+y
and
x-y
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}\left((x_{S}+y_{S})^{2}+(x_{S}-y_{S})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}(2x_{S}^{2}+2y_{S}^{2})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}x_{S}^{2}+2\sum_{S\in\mathcal{P}}y_{S}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}+2\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}.

By assumption,
\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
, so the left-hand side is
2\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}
. This simplifies to:

2\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\geq 2\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}+2\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}\implies 0\geq 2\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}.

Since the norm is non-negative, we must have
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}=0
.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

The strength of the above result is indicated by the following proposition, which provides a concise proof for the characterization of extreme points in the classical James space
J
. This result was originally proved in
[
1
]
using a more complex argument.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
x\in J
. Then
x
is an extreme point if and only if it is separated.

Proof.

The “only if” direction follows from Proposition
3.1
stated below (non-separated implies non-extreme).

Assume now that
x
is separated but not extreme. Then there exists
y\in J
satisfying the conclusion of Proposition
2.9
. In particular,
y\neq 0
and

\sum_{k\in S}y(k)=0

for any
S\in\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is any
x
-norming partition.

Set
k_{0}=\min\operatorname{supp}(y)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\operatorname{supp}(y)\subset\operatorname{ran}(x)
. Since
x
is separated, there exists an
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}
separating
k_{0}
and
k_{0}+1
.

Let
S\in\mathcal{P}
be the segment such that
k_{0}\in S
. Since the partition separates
k_{0}
and
k_{0}+1
,
k_{0}
is the maximum element of
S
. Consequently,
y|_{S}
is different from zero only on
k_{0}
(since
k_{0}=\min\operatorname{supp}(y)
). Hence,

\sum_{k\in S}y(k)=y(k_{0})\neq 0.

This contradicts the property that the sum of
y
must vanish on all segments of an
x
-norming partition, completing the proof.
∎

3.
Necessary Conditions for Extremality

We first establish that the property of being “separated” is a necessary condition for extremality in
JT
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
x\in JT
. If
x
is not separated, then
x
is not an extreme point.

Proof.

Suppose
x
is not separated. By definition, there exists a pair of distinct nodes
u,v\in\operatorname{ran}(x)
such that for every
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}
,
u
and
v
belong to the same segment
S\in\mathcal{P}
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{\text{sep}}
denote the set of all admissible partitions
\mathcal{P}
in which
u
and
v
belong to different segments. Since the map
\mathcal{P}\mapsto\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}
is upper semi-continuous on the compact space of partitions, and no partition in
\mathbb{P}_{\text{sep}}
attains the norm
\left\lVert x\right\rVert
, there exists
\delta>0
such that:

\sup_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{P}_{\text{sep}}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}-\delta.

Let
\epsilon>0
be sufficiently small such that
2\epsilon\left\lVert x\right\rVert+\epsilon^{2}<\delta
. Define the perturbation
y=\epsilon(e_{u}-e_{v})
.

We claim that
\left\lVert x\pm y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
. Indeed, for any partition
\mathcal{P}
:

(1)

If
\mathcal{P}
puts
u,v
in the same segment
S
, then
\sum_{S}y=\epsilon-\epsilon=0
. Thus
\left\lVert x\pm y\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}
. Since the supremum of
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}
is attained on such partitions,
\left\lVert x\pm y\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert x\right\rVert
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{P}
separates
u,v
, then
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}-\delta
. The perturbation increases the norm by at most
O(\epsilon)
, which remains strictly below
\left\lVert x\right\rVert
for small
\epsilon
.

Thus,
\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
, and since
y\neq 0
,
x
is not an extreme point.
∎

Example 3.2

(Separated Support does not imply Separated Range)
.

Consider the vector
x\in JT
defined by:

x=e_{\emptyset}+e_{00}+e_{01}

1. Norm and Partitions:

The support is
\operatorname{supp}(x)=\{\emptyset,00,01\}
, and the range is
\operatorname{ran}(x)=\{\emptyset,0,00,01\}
.
To compute the norm, the root
\emptyset
must merge with one of its descendants to maximize the square sum. There are two symmetric optimal partitions:

•

\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{\{\emptyset,0,00\},\{01\}\}
.
Sum of first segment:
1+0+1=2
. Total square:
2^{2}+1^{2}=5
.

•

\mathcal{P}_{2}=\{\{\emptyset,0,01\},\{00\}\}
.
Sum of first segment:
1+0+1=2
. Total square:
2^{2}+1^{2}=5
.

Thus,
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\sqrt{5}
.

2. The Support is Separated:

We verify that every pair in
\operatorname{supp}(x)
can be separated:

•

Pair
(\emptyset,00)
: Separated by
\mathcal{P}_{2}
(where
\emptyset
merges with
01
).

•

Pair
(\emptyset,01)
: Separated by
\mathcal{P}_{1}
(where
\emptyset
merges with
00
).

•

Pair
(00,01)
: Separated in both partitions (they are incomparable).

3. The Range is NOT Separated:

Consider the pair
(\emptyset,0)\subset\operatorname{ran}(x)
.
In
\mathcal{P}_{1}
, they belong to the segment
\{\emptyset,0,00\}
.
In
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, they belong to the segment
\{\emptyset,0,01\}
.
Any partition that separates
\emptyset
from
0
would require isolating
\emptyset
(sum 1) and treating the subtrees at
00
and
01
separately (sums 1 and 1), yielding a total square sum of
1^{2}+1^{2}+1^{2}=3<5
.
Therefore,
\emptyset
and
0
belong to the same segment in
every

x
-norming partition.

Conclusion:

Although
x
is separated on its support, it is not separated on its range. Consequently,
x
is not an extreme point. Indeed, the perturbation
y=\epsilon(e_{\emptyset}-e_{0})
satisfies
\left\lVert x\pm y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
because the sum of
y
vanishes on any segment containing both
\emptyset
and
0
.

4.
Sufficient Conditions for Extremality

4.1.
\ell_{2}
-Behavior and Extremality

We begin by observing that vectors whose
JT
-norm coincides with their
\ell_{2}
-norm are always extreme points. This provides a fundamental class of extreme points.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
x\in JT
. If
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
, then
x
is an extreme point of the ball of radius
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}
.

Proof.

Let
y\in JT
be such that for every segment
s\in\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is an
x
-norming partition, it satisfies
\sum_{\alpha\in s}y(\alpha)=0
. From the assumption, the partition consisting of singletons is an
x
-norming partition. Applying the condition to singleton segments
\{\alpha\}
, we obtain
y(\alpha)=0
for all
\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}(x)
. Since we may assume
\operatorname{supp}(y)\subset\operatorname{ran}(x)
, we conclude that
y=0
. Proposition
2.9
yields that
x
is an extreme point.
∎

4.2.
Connection to the James Space
J

The local structure of
JT
along branches is isometric to the classical James space
J
. Recent results by Argyros and González provide a complete characterization of extremality in
J
which had proved by S. Bellenot
[
2
]
for a different norm of
J
.

Theorem 4.2

(Argyros & González, 2025
[
1
]
)
.

Let
x\in J
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

x
is an extreme point of the unit ball of
J
.

(2)

x
is separated and
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{J}=1
.

(3)

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{J}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}=1
.

This theorem suggests that for “branch-like” structures in
JT
, extremality is tightly coupled with the
\ell_{2}
-norm condition.

4.3.
Separated Vectors on Special Supports

We now examine vectors supported on specific substructures of the tree. We show that for these cases, the separated property forces the norm to be the
\ell_{2}
-norm, thereby implying extremality.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
x\in JT
.

(a)

If
x
is supported by a single branch and is separated, then
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
.

(b)

If
x
is supported by a family of pairwise incomparable segments and is separated, then
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
. Consequently,
x
is an extreme point.

Proof.

(a)
Suppose
\operatorname{supp}(x)\subset\mathbf{b}
for some branch
\mathbf{b}
of
\mathcal{T}
. The space of functions supported on
\mathbf{b}
equipped with the
JT
norm is isometric to the James space
J
. Since
x
is separated in
JT
, its restriction to
\mathbf{b}
is separated in
J
. By Theorem
4.2
(Argyros & González), this implies
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{J}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
. Since the norms coincide on the branch,
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
. By Proposition
4.1
,
x
is an extreme point.

(b)
Let
\operatorname{supp}(x)\subset\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}
, where
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a family of pairwise incomparable segments (i.e., no node in
S_{i}
is an ancestor or descendant of a node in
S_{j}
for
i\neq j
).

Any segment
S
in an admissible partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\mathcal{T}
is linearly ordered. Therefore,
S
can intersect at most one segment
S_{i}
from the incomparable family (or parts of it). If
S
intersects multiple
S_{i}
’s, there would be comparable nodes in distinct
S_{i}
’s, contradicting their incomparability.

Consequently, the norm calculation decouples:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=\sum_{i\in I}\left\lVert x|_{S_{i}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

Since
x
is separated, each restriction
x|_{S_{i}}
is separated. Note that a single segment
S_{i}
is a subset of a branch, so
x|_{S_{i}}
can be viewed as a vector in
J
. Applying Part (a) (or Theorem
4.2
) to each segment, we have
\left\lVert x|_{S_{i}}\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x|_{S_{i}}\right\rVert_{2}
.

Summing these contributions:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=\sum_{i\in I}\left\lVert x|_{S_{i}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Since
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
,
x
is an extreme point by Proposition
4.1
.
∎

Proposition 4.4

(Well-Founded Support)
.

Let
x\in JT
be a separated vector such that its range,
\operatorname{ran}(x)
, is a well-founded subtree of
\mathcal{T}
(i.e., it contains no infinite branches). Then
x
is an extreme point.

Proof.

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
x
is not an extreme point. By the characterization of non-extremality (Proposition
2.9
), there exists a non-zero vector
y\in JT
such that:

(1)

\left\lVert x\right\rVert=\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
.

(2)

For every
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}=\{S_{i}\}
,
\sum_{\gamma\in S_{i}}y(\gamma)=0
for all
i
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
\operatorname{supp}(y)\subset\operatorname{ran}(x)
. Since
\operatorname{ran}(x)
is well-founded, the subset
\operatorname{supp}(y)
is also well-founded.

Let
A=\operatorname{supp}(y)
. Since
A
is non-empty and well-founded, it possesses maximal elements. Let
\alpha\in A
be a maximal node (meaning no descendant of
\alpha
is in
A
). Thus
y(\alpha)\neq 0
, but
y(\beta)=0
for all
\beta>\alpha
.

Since
x
is separated, there exists an
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}
that separates
\alpha
from its parent (if
\alpha\neq\emptyset
). Consequently, the segment
S\in\mathcal{P}
containing
\alpha
must start at
\alpha
. Because
\alpha
is maximal in
\operatorname{supp}(y)
, the segment
S
contains no other nodes from
\operatorname{supp}(y)
. Therefore:

\sum_{\gamma\in S}y(\gamma)=y(\alpha).

By condition (2) of the non-extremality characterization, this sum must be 0. Thus
y(\alpha)=0
, which contradicts
\alpha\in\operatorname{supp}(y)
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

If
x\in JT
is separated and has finite support, then
x
is an extreme point.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
x\in JT
be a separated vector such that
\operatorname{supp}(x)\subset F\cup\sigma
, where
F
is a finite set of nodes and
\sigma
is a branch of
\mathcal{T}
. Then
x
is an extreme point.

Proof.

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
x
is not an extreme point. Then there exists a non-zero vector
y\in JT
satisfying the conditions of Proposition
2.9
(specifically, the sum of
y
vanishes on every segment of every
x
-norming partition). We assume
\operatorname{supp}(y)\subset\operatorname{ran}(x)
.

The set
K=\operatorname{ran}(x)\setminus\sigma
is finite. We first show that
y
must vanish on
K
by backward induction.
Let
C=\{a\in K:\text{for all }b>a,y(b)=0\text{ or }b\in\sigma\}
. Since
K
is finite, every element in
K
eventually leads to
\sigma
or terminates.
Let
a
be a maximal element of
\operatorname{supp}(y)\cap K
. Any descendant of
a
in
\operatorname{supp}(y)
must belong to
\sigma
(since
a
is maximal in the non-branch part). However, since
\sigma
is a single branch, if
a\notin\sigma
,
a
cannot be an ancestor of any node in
\sigma
. Thus,
a
has no descendants in
\operatorname{supp}(y)
.
Using the separation property, we can separate
a
from its parent. The resulting segment
S
starts at
a
and contains no other elements of
\operatorname{supp}(y)
. Thus
\sum_{S}y=y(a)=0
.
Repeating this argument (backward induction), we conclude that
y(a)=0
for all
a\in K
.

Thus,
\operatorname{supp}(y)\subset\sigma
. Since
y\neq 0
, let
u=\min\operatorname{supp}(y)
. Then
u\in\sigma
.
Let
v
be the child of
u
that lies on
\sigma
. If
v\notin\operatorname{ran}(x)
, then
u
is maximal in the range, and by isolating it, we find
y(u)=0
. Thus, assume
v\in\operatorname{ran}(x)
.
Since
x
is separated, there exists an
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}
separating
u
and
v
. Let
S\in\mathcal{P}
be the segment containing
u
. Since
S
does not contain
v
, and
\operatorname{supp}(y)\subset\sigma\cup K
(with
y|_{K}=0
), the segment
S
contains no other elements of
\operatorname{supp}(y)
except possibly
u
.
Specifically, if
S
continues to the other child
w
of
u
, then
w\notin\sigma
, so
w\in K
(or is outside the support). Since
y
vanishes on
K
, the sum on
S
is simply
y(u)
.

By Proposition
2.9
,
\sum_{S}y=0
, which implies
y(u)=0
. This contradicts
u\in\operatorname{supp}(y)
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

We are not able to extend the above result for finitely many branches instead of one. In particular, we are not able to prove that
x\in JT
separated is an extreme point if
\operatorname{supp}(x)\subset\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}
with
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
branches of
\mathcal{T}
.

4.4.
Local Isolation Implies
\ell_{2}
Norm

We prove that if every node in the support can be isolated in some optimal partition, the global norm collapses to the
\ell_{2}
norm. In the sequel for
\alpha\in\mathcal{T}
we denote by
W_{\alpha}
the wedge of
\mathcal{T}
rooted at
\alpha
defined by

W_{\alpha}=\{\beta\in\mathcal{T}:\alpha\leq\beta\}

For
x\in JT
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{T}
we denote by
x|_{W_{\alpha}}
the restriction of
x
onto

W_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
x\in JT
be a vector such that for every
a\in\operatorname{supp}(x)
, the singleton
\{a\}
belongs to some
x
-norming partition. Then:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}.

Proof.

Let
B=\operatorname{supp}(x)
. Since
x\in JT
,
B
is a countable set of nodes. We define the relative depth of a node
a\in B
, denoted
|a|_{B}
, as follows:

•

If
a
is minimal in
B
(i.e., no ancestor of
a
is in
B
), then
|a|_{B}=1
.

•

Generally,
|a|_{B}=k
if the longest chain of ancestors of
a
within
B
has length
k
.

We prove by induction that for every
n\in N
:

(2)

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=\sum_{\{a\in B:|a|_{B}\leq n\}}x(a)^{2}+\sum_{\{c\in B:|c|_{B}=n+1\}}\left\lVert x|_{W_{c}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

(Note: The second sum runs over nodes
c
that are “roots” of the remainder of the support at level
n+1
).

Base Case (
n=1
):

Let
M=\{a\in B:a\text{ is minimal in }B\}
. These are the nodes with
|a|_{B}=1
.
Since the subtrees rooted at distinct
a\in M
are disjoint, we have:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=\sum_{a\in M}\left\lVert x|_{W_{a}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

Fix
a\in M
. By hypothesis, there exists an
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}_{a}
such that
\{a\}\in\mathcal{P}_{a}
.
Since
\{a\}
is a segment in
\mathcal{P}_{a}
, the partition splits the subtree
W_{a}
into the singleton
\{a\}
and partitions of the subtrees rooted at the children of
a
(specifically, those children
c
which are in
B
or have descendants in
B
).
Thus, the norm squared decomposes:

\left\lVert x|_{W_{a}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=x(a)^{2}+\sum_{c\in\operatorname{children}(a)\cap\mathcal{T}}\left\lVert x|_{W_{c}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

Summing over all
a\in M
:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=\sum_{a\in M}\left(x(a)^{2}+\sum_{c\in\operatorname{children}(a)}\left\lVert x|_{W_{c}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}\right)=\sum_{\{a:|a|_{B}=1\}}x(a)^{2}+\sum_{\{c:|c|_{B}=2\}}\left\lVert x|_{W_{c}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

This establishes the formula for
n=1
.

Inductive Step:

Assume the formula holds for
n=k
.

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=\sum_{\{a:|a|_{B}\leq k\}}x(a)^{2}+\sum_{\{c:|c|_{B}=k+1\}}\left\lVert x|_{W_{c}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

Consider a specific node
c
with
|c|_{B}=k+1
. By the global hypothesis, there exists an
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}_{c}
(for the whole vector) containing
\{c\}
. The restriction of this partition to
W_{c}
is necessarily an
x|_{W_{c}}
-norming partition.
Therefore, just as in the base case, the existence of a norming partition isolating
c
implies:

\left\lVert x|_{W_{c}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=x(c)^{2}+\sum_{d\in\operatorname{children}(c)}\left\lVert x|_{W_{d}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

Substituting this equality for every term in the second sum of the inductive hypothesis:

\displaystyle\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{a:|a|_{B}\leq k\}}x(a)^{2}+\sum_{\{c:|c|_{B}=k+1\}}\left(x(c)^{2}+\sum_{d\in\operatorname{children}(c)}\left\lVert x|_{W_{d}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\{a:|a|_{B}\leq k\}}x(a)^{2}+\sum_{\{c:|c|_{B}=k+1\}}x(c)^{2}\right)+\sum_{\{d:|d|_{B}=k+2\}}\left\lVert x|_{W_{d}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{a:|a|_{B}\leq k+1\}}x(a)^{2}+\sum_{\{d:|d|_{B}=k+2\}}\left\lVert x|_{W_{d}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

This completes the inductive step.

Conclusion:

Since
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
is generally false but
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}\geq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}
is always true (using the partition of singletons), we check the reverse inequality.
The above equality derived in the limit is:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}-\sum_{\{a:|a|_{B}\leq N\}}x(a)^{2}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\{c:|c|_{B}=N+1\}}\left\lVert x|_{W_{c}}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

As
x\in JT
, the tail norms go to 0. Thus,

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}=\sum_{a\in B}x(a)^{2}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

∎

5.
Positive Extreme Points

In this section, we focus on positive vectors (
x\geq 0
). The absence of sign cancellations allows for a constructive approach to determining the norm using a greedy algorithm. We begin by establishing the necessary notation.

5.1.
Notation and Basic Properties

Definition 5.1

(Notation)
.

Let
x\in JT
and
a\in\mathcal{T}
.

(1)

Let
W_{a}=\{b\in\mathcal{T}:a\leq b\}
denote the wedge rooted at
a
.

(2)

Let
x_{a}
denote the restriction of
x
to
W_{a}
, i.e.,
x_{a}=x\cdot\chi_{W_{a}}
.

(3)

For a segment
s\subset\mathcal{T}
we define the sum of
x
along
s
as:

S_{s}(x)=\sum_{b\in s}x(b).

(4)

We define the maximal segment sum starting at
a
as:

S_{a}(x)=\sup\{S_{s}(x):s\text{ is a segment with }\min s=a\}.

Remark 5.2
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector. If
\mathcal{P}
is an
x
-norming partition, then every segment
s\in\mathcal{P}
satisfies
b\cap\operatorname{ran}(x)\subset s
where
b
is a final segment of a branch in
\mathcal{T}
. This implies that segments in an optimal partition extend maximally downwards within the support.

Remark 5.3
.

If
x
is a positive vector in
JT
with finite support, then for every
a\in\operatorname{supp}(x)
, the supremum
S_{a}(x)
is attained by some segment
s
. Even for infinite support, since
x\in JT\subset c_{0}(\mathcal{T})
, the values tend to 0 uniformly along branches, so the supremum is attained.

5.2.
The Greedy Algorithm and Norming Partitions

In the context of the Greedy Algorithm, when we refer to the "children" of a node
a\in\operatorname{supp}(x)
, we refer to the children within the subtree defined by the support of
x
.

The following lemma provides the recursive step for calculating the norm of a positive vector. It essentially states that if we know how to optimally partition the subtrees, the optimal strategy at the root
a
is to merge with the child offering the largest segment sum.

Lemma 5.4

(Recursive Norm Formula)
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector and
a\in\operatorname{supp}(x)
. Let
C(a)
denote the set of children of
a
. Assume that for every child
c\in C(a)
and for every segment
s_{c}\subset W_{c}
such that

S_{s_{c}}(x)=S_{c}(x)
there exists an
x_{c}
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}_{c}
that includes a segment
s_{c}
.

Then the norm of the restriction
x_{a}
satisfies:

(3)

\left\lVert x_{a}\right\rVert^{2}=x(a)^{2}+2x(a)S_{c_{0}}(x)+\sum_{c\in C(a)}\left\lVert x_{c}\right\rVert^{2}

where
c_{0}\in C(a)
is a child that maximizes the segment sum, i.e.,
S_{c_{0}}(x)=\max_{c\in C(a)}S_{c}(x)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be any partition of
\operatorname{supp}(x_{a})
. The node
a
must belong to some segment
s\in\mathcal{P}
. Since
a
is the root of
W_{a}
,
s
must start at
a
.

If
s=\{a\}
, then the partition splits
a
from all its descendants. The norm would be:

\left\lVert x_{\alpha}\right\rVert^{2}=x(a)^{2}+\sum_{c\in C(a)}\left\lVert x_{c}\right\rVert^{2}.

However, since
x\geq 0
, merging
a
with a segment from a child is always superior or equal to isolating
a
.

Suppose
s
continues into the wedge
W_{c^{\prime}}
for some child
c^{\prime}
. Then
s=\{a\}\cup s^{\prime}
, where
s^{\prime}
is a segment starting at
c^{\prime}
. The contribution of this segment is:

\left(x(a)+S_{s^{\prime}}(x)\right)^{2}=x(a)^{2}+2x(a)S_{s^{\prime}}(x)+S_{s^{\prime}}(x)^{2}.

To maximize the total norm
\left\lVert x_{a}\right\rVert^{2}
, we must choose the best partition for the remaining nodes.
The remaining nodes in
W_{c^{\prime}}
(those not in
s^{\prime}
) contribute
\left\lVert x_{c^{\prime}}\right\rVert^{2}-S_{s^{\prime}}(x)^{2}
(by the hypothesis that
s^{\prime}
is part of a norming partition for
c^{\prime}
).
The nodes in other wedges
W_{c}
(for
c\neq c^{\prime}
) contribute optimally
\left\lVert x_{c}\right\rVert^{2}
.

Thus, choosing to merge through child
c^{\prime}
with segment
s^{\prime}
yields a total square sum:

\displaystyle\left\lVert x_{\alpha}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(x(a)+S_{s^{\prime}}(x)\right)^{2}+(\left\lVert x_{c^{\prime}}\right\rVert^{2}-S_{s^{\prime}}(x)^{2})+\sum_{c\neq c^{\prime}}\left\lVert x_{c}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=(x(a)^{2}+2x(a)S_{s^{\prime}}(x)+S_{s^{\prime}}(x)^{2})+\left\lVert x_{c^{\prime}}\right\rVert^{2}-S_{s^{\prime}}(x)^{2}+\sum_{c\neq c^{\prime}}\left\lVert x_{c}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=x(a)^{2}+2x(a)S_{s^{\prime}}(x)+\sum_{c\in C(a)}\left\lVert x_{c}\right\rVert^{2}.

To maximize this quantity, we must maximize the middle term
2x(a)S_{s^{\prime}}(x)
. Since
x(a)>0
, this is equivalent to maximizing
S_{s^{\prime}}(x)
.
The maximum possible value for
S_{s^{\prime}}(x)
is
\max_{c}S_{c}(x)
, achieved by choosing
c_{0}
and the optimal segment
s_{c_{0}}
provided by the hypothesis.

Substituting
S_{c_{0}}(x)
into the expression gives the stated formula.
∎

Definition 5.5

(Greedy Algorithm)
.

For a positive vector
x\in JT
, the
Greedy Algorithm
constructs a partition
\mathcal{P}_{G}
as follows:

(1)

For every node
u
, compute the maximal segment sum
S_{u}(x)
.

(2)

Identify the "heaviest child"
c_{max}(u)=\operatorname{argmax}_{c\in C(u)}S_{c}(x)
. (Here,
C(u)
refers to children within the support).

(3)

Construct segments by linking each node
u
to
c_{max}(u)
if
S_{c_{max}(u)}(x)>0
. If multiple children achieve the maximum, any one may be chosen (ties).

(4)

This process defines a set of paths (segments) covering the support.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector with finite support. Then the Greedy Algorithm produces an
x
-norming partition.

Proof.

We proceed by backward induction on the height of the subtree defined by
supp(x)
. We inductively we show that for
\alpha\in supp(x)
and every outcome
\mathcal{P_{\alpha}}
of the Greedy Algorithm for the vector
x_{\alpha}
we have
\mathcal{P_{\alpha}}
is an
x_{\alpha}
- norming partition.

Base Case:
If
a
is a leaf of
\operatorname{supp}(x)
, the only possible segment is
\{a\}
. The greedy algorithm selects this (as there are no children), and
\left\lVert x_{a}\right\rVert^{2}=x(a)^{2}
, which is correct.

Inductive Step:
Assume the proposition holds for all children of
a
. That is, for every
c\in C(a)
, the greedy partition of
W_{c}
is norming. By the definition of the greedy algorithm on
W_{c}
, one of the segments in this partition starts at
c
and achieves the sum
S_{c}(x)
.

This satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
5.4
. Therefore, the norm of
x_{a}
is given by:

\left\lVert x_{a}\right\rVert^{2}=x(a)^{2}+2x(a)S_{c_{0}}(x)+\sum_{c\in C(a)}\left\lVert x_{c}\right\rVert^{2}

where
c_{0}
is the child chosen by the greedy algorithm (maximizing the sum).
The partition constructed by the greedy algorithm at
a
merges
a
with
c_{0}
, creating a segment with sum
x(a)+S_{c_{0}}(x)
, and uses the optimal greedy partitions for all other descendants. As shown in the calculation of Lemma
5.4
, this configuration achieves exactly the value
\left\lVert x_{a}\right\rVert^{2}
.

Thus, the greedy partition is
x
-norming.
∎

5.3.
The General Case: Infinite Support

We extend the validity of the Greedy Algorithm to all positive vectors
x\in JT
with infinite support. The strategy relies on approximating
x
by vectors supported on subtrees consisting of finitely many branches, where the algorithm is shown to be norming.

5.3.1.
Approximation by Finitely Branched Subtrees

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector. Let
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in F}
be the family of segments generated by the Greedy Algorithm applied to
x
, where
F\subset\mathcal{T}
is the set of starting nodes of these segments.

For each
n\in N
, we define the truncated set of starting nodes:

F_{n}=\{\alpha\in F:|\alpha|\leq n\}.

We define the approximating domain
Q_{n}
as:

Q_{n}=T_{n}\cup\bigcup_{\alpha\in F_{n}}s_{\alpha},

where
T_{n}=\{u\in\mathcal{T}:|u|\leq n\}
. Note that
Q_{n}
is a
complete subtree
of
\mathcal{T}
.
We define the vector
y_{n}
as the restriction of
x
to
Q_{n}
:

y_{n}=x\cdot\chi_{Q_{n}}.

Notice that
\operatorname{supp}(y_{n})\subset\bigcup_{\alpha\in F_{n}}s_{\alpha}
.

Lemma 5.7

(Approximation and Stability)
.

Let
x\in JT
be positive and
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{\alpha}:\alpha\in F\}
be a partition generated by the Greedy Algorithm on
x
. For
y_{n}
as above the following hold:

(1)

The family
\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{s_{\alpha}:\alpha\in F_{n}\}
is the outcome of the Greedy Algorithm applied to
y_{n}
.

(2)

The sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
converges in norm:
\left\lVert x-y_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}\to 0
.

Proof.

1. Stability of the Greedy Choice:

The Greedy Algorithm constructs segments based on the comparison of sums
S_{s}(x)
. We inductively show that the family
\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{s_{\alpha}:\alpha\in F_{n}\}
is an outcome of the Greedy Algorithm applied on
y_{n}
. The details are left to the reader.

Thus, the greedy partition for
y_{n}
coincides with the greedy partition for
x
on the segments starting in
F_{n}
.

2. Convergence:

Observe that the set
R_{n}=Q_{n}\setminus T_{n}
is a complete subtree of
\mathcal{T}
. Hence, setting

z_{n}=y_{n}|_{R_{n}}
and
x_{n}=x|_{\mathcal{T}\setminus T_{n}}
, Lemma
2.5
yields

\left\lVert z_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}\leq\left\lVert x_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}.

Therefore,
\left\lVert z_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}\to 0
and
\left\lVert x_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}\to 0
.
Since
x-y_{n}=x_{n}-z_{n}
, we conclude that

\left\lVert x-y_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}\to 0.

∎

5.3.2.
Verification for Finitely Many Branches

The vectors
y_{n}
constructed above have a specific property: their support is contained in
T_{n}
plus a finite collection of segments. Thus,
\operatorname{supp}(y_{n})
is contained in a finite union of branches.

Lemma 5.8

(Finite Branch Case)
.

Let
z\in JT
be a positive vector such that
\operatorname{supp}(z)
is contained in a union of finitely many branches. Then the Greedy Algorithm produces a
z
-norming partition.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{k}\}
be the finite set of branches containing
\operatorname{supp}(z)
.
There exists a level
N
sufficiently large such that for any node
u
with
|u|\geq N
, the wedge
W_{u}
intersects at most one branch from
\mathcal{B}
.

For any such node
u\in supp(z)
(with
|u|\geq N
), the restriction
z_{u}
is supported on a single path (a subset of some
b_{i}
). For a positive vector on a line, the Greedy Algorithm trivially selects the entire path (or the maximal positive sub-segment), which corresponds to the sum along that path. This is known to be the norming partition for the restriction of a positive vector to a branch (James space case).

Having established that the Greedy Algorithm yields norming partitions for all subtrees rooted at level
N
, we proceed by
backward induction
from level
N-1
down to the root
\emptyset
.

The inductive step is identical to the finite support case (Proposition
5.6
):
If the greedy partitions are optimal for all children
c\in C(u)
, then the optimal value at
u
is determined by:

\left\lVert z_{u}\right\rVert^{2}=\max_{c}\left(z(u)+S_{s_{c}}(z)\right)^{2}+\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\left\lVert z_{c^{\prime}}\right\rVert^{2}.

Since the Greedy Algorithm chooses exactly the child maximizing
S_{s_{c}}(z)
, it constructs the norming partition at
u
.

By induction, the partition generated by the Greedy Algorithm at the root is
z
-norming.
∎

5.3.3.
Main Result for Positive Vectors

We combine the approximation and the finite branch case to prove the result for general positive vectors.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector. The partition
\mathcal{S}
generated by the Greedy Algorithm is an
x
-norming partition.

Proof.

Let
y_{n}
be the sequence of approximations defined on
Q_{n}
.
By Lemma 4.2,
\operatorname{supp}(y_{n})
consists of the finite tree
T_{n}
and the segments
\{s_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in F_{n}}
. This support is covered by finitely many branches.
Therefore, by Lemma 4.2, the Greedy Algorithm is norming for
y_{n}
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the partition generated by the algorithm for
y_{n}
. We have:

\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}.

The partition
\mathcal{S}
generated by the algorithm for
x
contains all the segments in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
(possibly extended). Since
x\geq y_{n}\geq 0
, and the partition
\mathcal{S}
is consistent with
\mathcal{S}_{n}
on the support of
y_{n}
, we have:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}}\geq\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}}\geq\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}_{n}}=\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}.

Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}}\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}.

Since
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}
by definition, we conclude
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{S}}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}
.
Thus, the Greedy Algorithm defines an
x
-norming partition.
∎

The following is easily proved.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector and
\alpha\in\mathcal{T}
a minimal node in
supp(x)
. Then for every segment
s
of
\mathcal{T}
with
\alpha\in s
satisfying
S(x)=S_{\alpha}(x)
there exists an
x
- norming partition
\mathcal{P}
with
s\in\mathcal{P}
.

5.4.
Converse of the Greedy Algorithm

We have established that the Greedy Algorithm constructs a norming partition for positive vectors. We now prove the converse: every norming partition must be consistent with the decisions made by the Greedy Algorithm.

Definition 5.11

(Consistent with Greedy Algorithm)
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector. A partition
\mathcal{P}
is said to be
consistent with the Greedy Algorithm
if for every segment
s\in\mathcal{P}
and every node
u\in s\cap supp(x)
that is not the leaf of
s\cap supp(x)
, if
c
is the child of
u
contained in
s\cap supp(x)
, then:

S_{c}(x)=\max_{k\in\operatorname{children}(u)}S_{k}(x).

In other words, the segment always proceeds to a “heaviest” child.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector. Then every
x
-norming partition is consistent with the Greedy Algorithm.

Proof.

We proceed in three steps: first for finite support, then for support on finitely many branches, and finally for the general case.

Step 1: Finite Support

Let
x
have finite support. We use backward induction on the height of the subtree
supp(x)
. In particular we inductively show that for every
\alpha\in supp(x)
, every norming partition of
x_{\alpha}
is consistent
with the Greedy Algorithm.

Base Case:
If
u
is a leaf of
\operatorname{supp}(x)
, any segment containing
u
must end at
u
. The condition holds vacuously.

Inductive Step:
Assume the property holds for all children of
u
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be an
x_{u}
-norming partition. Let
s\in\mathcal{P}
be the segment containing
u
.
Since
x(u)>0
and
x
is positive, it is always optimal to merge
u
with a child rather than isolating it . Thus
s
must continue to some child
c
.

The contribution of the subtree
W_{u}
to the total norm square, given that
u
merges with child
c
, is:

\left\lVert x_{u}\right\rVert^{2}=x(u)^{2}+2x(u)S_{s}(x_{c})+\sum_{k\in\operatorname{children}(u)}\left\lVert x_{k}\right\rVert^{2}.

To maximize this quantity, the term
S_{s}(x_{c})
must be maximized.
Since
\mathcal{P}
is norming for
x_{u}
, its restriction to
W_{k}
must be norming for
x_{k}
for all
k\neq c
, and the continuation of
s
in
W_{c}
must be optimal.
Therefore,
S_{s}(x_{c})
must equal
S_{c}(x)
(the maximal segment sum starting at
c
), and we must choose
c
such that
S_{c}(x)
is maximal among all children.

If
\mathcal{P}
chose a child
c^{\prime}
with
S_{c^{\prime}}(x)<\max_{k}S_{k}(x)
, the total value would be strictly less than the greedy maximum, contradicting the assumption that
\mathcal{P}
is norming. Thus,
\mathcal{P}
must choose a child attaining the maximum.

Step 2: Finitely Many Branches

Let
\operatorname{supp}(x)
be contained in a finite union of branches. Let
\mathcal{P}
be an
x
-norming partition.
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\mathcal{P}
is not consistent with the Greedy Algorithm. Then there exists a node
u\in supp(x)
and a segment
s\in\mathcal{P}
passing through
u
to a child
c
, such that
S_{c}(x)<S_{c^{\prime}}(x)
for some other child
c^{\prime}
.

Since the support lies on finitely many branches, we can choose
N
large enough such that for all
v
with
|v|\geq N
, the tree
W_{v}
consists of single paths. On single paths, the partition is unique (full merge).
We can now apply the backward induction argument from Step 1, starting from level
N
down to the root. The strict inequality
S_{c}(x)<S_{c^{\prime}}(x)
at node
u
implies a strict loss in the total norm square compared to the optimal greedy choice. This contradicts the optimality of
\mathcal{P}
.

Step 3: General Case

Let
x
be an arbitrary positive vector and
\mathcal{P}
be an
x
-norming partition.
Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is not consistent. Then there exists a specific node
u\in supp(x)
and a segment
s\in\mathcal{P}
merging
u
with
c
, while another child
c^{\prime}
satisfies:

S_{c^{\prime}}(x)>S_{c}(x)+\delta

for some
\delta>0
.

Consider the approximations
y_{n}
supported on the finite subtrees
Q_{n}
( Lemma
5.7
). For sufficiently large
n
,
Q_{n}
contains
u
,
c
,
c^{\prime}
, and sufficiently long initial segments of the optimal paths starting at
c
and
c^{\prime}
to preserve the inequality
S_{c^{\prime}}(y_{n})>S_{c}(y_{n})+\delta/2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}\subset\mathcal{P}
defined by
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{s\in\mathcal{P}:|mins|\leq n\}
. It is easy to check that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a norming partition of
y_{n}
.If
\mathcal{P}
persists in choosing the “wrong” child
c
at node
u
, the value of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
on
y_{n}
will be strictly less than the value of the greedy partition for
y_{n}
(which chooses
c^{\prime}
).

Since
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}=\lim\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}_{n}}
and
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}=\lim\left\lVert y_{n}\right\rVert_{JT}
(by the convergence of norms), the gap
\delta
at node
u
would persist in the limit.
Specifically:

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}-2x(u)\delta<\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}.

This contradicts the assumption that
\mathcal{P}
is
x
-norming. Thus,
\mathcal{P}
must be consistent with the Greedy Algorithm.
∎

5.5.
Characterization of Positive Extreme Points

We are now ready to state and prove the complete characterization of extreme points for positive vectors in
JT
.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive vector (
x\geq 0
). Then
x
is an extreme point if and only if
x
is separated.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Direction:

We have already established in Proposition
3.1
that if a vector is not separated, it cannot be an extreme point. This holds for all vectors, including positive ones.

(
\Leftarrow
) Direction:

Assume that
x
is separated but, for the sake of contradiction, that
x
is
not
an extreme point.

By the characterization of non-extreme points (Proposition
2.9
), there exists a non-zero vector
y\in JT
such that:

(1)

\left\lVert x\right\rVert=\left\lVert x+y\right\rVert=\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
.

(2)

For every
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}
, and every segment
S\in\mathcal{P}
, the sum of the perturbation vanishes:
\sum_{\gamma\in S}y(\gamma)=0
.

Since
y\neq 0
, we can choose a node
a\in\operatorname{supp}(y)
. We may assume without loss of generality that
a\in\operatorname{ran}(x)
(since perturbations outside the range do not affect the norming partitions within the range).

Step 1: Isolate
a
from above.

Since
x
is separated, there exists an
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}_{1}
that separates
a
from its parent (if
a\neq\emptyset
). Let
s_{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
be the segment containing
a
. Because
a
is separated from its parent,
a
must be the minimal element (the starting node) of
s_{a}
.
By the property of
y
, we must have:

(4)

\sum_{\gamma\in s_{a}}y(\gamma)=0.

If
s_{a}=\{a\}
, then
y(a)=0
, which contradicts
a\in\operatorname{supp}(y)
. Thus,
s_{a}
must contain at least one descendant of
a
. Let
b
be the child of
a
contained in
s_{a}
. We can write
s_{a}=\{a\}\cup t_{b}
, where
t_{b}
is a segment starting at
b
.
Equation (
4
) becomes:

y(a)+\sum_{\gamma\in t_{b}}y(\gamma)=0\implies\sum_{\gamma\in t_{b}}y(\gamma)=-y(a)\neq 0.

Step 2: Isolate
a
from below.

Since
x
is separated, there exists another
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}_{2}
that separates
a
from
b
.
In this partition,
a
belongs to some segment
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
which does
not
contain
b
.
This implies that the restriction of
\mathcal{P}_{2}
to the subtree
W_{b}
(rooted at
b
) is an
x_{b}
-norming partition. Let us denote the value of the optimal partition on
W_{b}
as
\left\lVert x_{b}\right\rVert_{JT}^{2}
.

Step 3: The Contradiction.

We return to the segment
s_{a}=\{a\}\cup t_{b}
from Step 1.
Since
\mathcal{P}_{1}
is an
x
-norming partition, it must be consistent with the Greedy Algorithm. This implies that the tail
t_{b}
is an optimal path starting at
b
. Specifically,
t_{b}
must be part of some
x_{b}
-norming partition for the subtree
W_{b}
.(Proposition
5.6
)

Let
\mathcal{P}_{b}
be an
x_{b}
-norming partition that contains the segment
t_{b}
. We can construct a new partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
for the whole vector
x
by combining:

•

The partition
\mathcal{P}_{b}
for the subtree
W_{b}
(containing
t_{b}
).

•

All
s\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that
s\cap W_{b}=\emptyset
.

This partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is
x
-norming.

However, strictly speaking, we rely on the property of non-extreme points:
Any

x
-norming partition must have
y
-sum zero on its segments.

The segment
t_{b}
is a segment in the valid
x
-norming partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
.
Thus, we must have:

\sum_{\gamma\in t_{b}}y(\gamma)=0.

But from Step 1, we established that:

\sum_{\gamma\in t_{b}}y(\gamma)=-y(a)\neq 0.

This is a contradiction. Therefore, the assumption that
x
is not an extreme point must be false.
∎

5.6.
Equal Sums Property for Positive Extreme Points

We prove the Equal Sums Property to all positive, separated vectors in
JT
. Note that in this subsection, when we discuss children of a node, we refer to the children in the full dyadic tree
\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 5.14

(Equal Sums Property)
.

A vector
x\in JT
satisfies the
Equal Sums Property
if for any node
u\in\operatorname{supp}(x)
and any two final segments
S_{1},S_{2}
with
\min S_{i}=u
for
i=1,2
we have:

\sum_{\gamma\in S_{1}}x(\gamma)=\sum_{\delta\in S_{2}}x(\delta).

5.6.1.
Local Equality

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
x\in JT
be positive and separated. Let
a\in\operatorname{supp}(x)
. If
a
is not a maximal node of the
\operatorname{supp}(x)
, and
c_{0},c_{1}
are its children in
\mathcal{T}
, then
W_{c_{i}}\cap\operatorname{supp}(x)\neq\emptyset
for
i=0,1
, and:

S_{c_{0}}(x)=S_{c_{1}}(x),

where
S_{u}(x)
denotes the maximal segment sum starting at
u
.

Proof.

If
a
is not maximal, it has descendants in
\operatorname{supp}(x)
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
W_{c_{1}}\cap\operatorname{supp}(x)=\emptyset
. Then all descendants of
a
lie in
W_{c_{0}}
. In this case, the Greedy Algorithm at
a
would always choose
c_{0}
(since the sum at
c_{1}
is 0). This effectively implies
a
and
c_{0}
are never separated, contradicting the separated property of
x
. Thus, both subtrees must contain support.

Furthermore, if
S_{c_{0}}(x)\neq S_{c_{1}}(x)
, say
S_{c_{0}}>S_{c_{1}}
, the greedy choice would be unique and strictly prefer
c_{0}
. Again, this would imply
a
and
c_{0}
belong to the same segment in every optimal partition, violating the separated condition. Thus
S_{c_{0}}=S_{c_{1}}
.
∎

5.6.2.
Global Constancy via Density

We shall prove the result for
x\in JT
with a unique minimal element of the support of
x
. The general case is proved by applying the proof in each minimal element of the
supp(x)
. Let

\mathcal{B}
denote the set of branches of the tree
\mathcal{T}
intersecting
\operatorname{supp}(x)
. Note that every
s\in\mathcal{B}
contains the minimum of the
supp(x)
. We define the maximal branch sum functional:

\Sigma(x)=\max\{S_{s}(x):s\in\mathcal{B}\}.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
x\in JT
be a positive, separated vector. Then the sum of
x
along any infinite branch
s
which intersects the support is constant. That is, if
s\cap\operatorname{supp}(x)\neq\emptyset
, then
S_{s}(x)=\Sigma(x)
.

Proof.

We prove this by showing that the set of branches attaining the maximum sum is dense in
\mathcal{B}
.

We claim that for every node
a\in\operatorname{supp}(x)
, there exists a branch
s
containing
a
such that
S_{s}(x)=\Sigma(x)
.
We proceed by induction on the structure of the support (or implicitly, the depth relative to the support).

If
a
is a maximal node of
\operatorname{supp}(x)
, any branch passing through
a
collects the full sum up to
a
and 0 thereafter. Since
x
is separated, the maximal sums must be balanced throughout the tree (as shown in the Lemma
5.15
), so the sum along this path must be
\Sigma(x)
.

If
a
is not maximal, by the Lemma, both children
c_{0},c_{1}
are roots of subtrees with equal maximal sums. By the inductive hypothesis (or the recursive nature of the maximum), we can extend a path through
a
to one of its children, and eventually to an infinite branch
s
, such that the total sum is maximized.

Thus, the set of branches
\{s\in\mathcal{B}:S_{s}(x)=\Sigma(x)\}
is dense in the set of all branches intersecting
\operatorname{supp}(x)
. Since the mapping
s\mapsto S_{s}(x)
is continuous on the branch space
\mathcal{B}
(for
x\in JT
), and the function is constant on a dense subset, it must be constant on the whole set.
∎

As a consequence we have the following.

Theorem 5.17
.

Every
x\in JT
positive and separated satisfies the Equal Sums Property.

6.
Topological Properties of the Set of Extreme Points

We address the question of whether the set of extreme points is closed in the norm topology. We show that this is true for the set of separated points. Hence, if the equivalence between extremality and the separated property holds, then this set is indeed closed.

Proposition 6.1
.

The set of separated points of the unit ball of
JT
is closed in the norm topology.

Proof.

We show that the property of being separated is preserved under norm convergence.

Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence of separated vectors in the unit sphere such that
\left\lVert x_{n}-x\right\rVert\to 0
. We must show that
x
is separated.

Let
u,v\in\operatorname{ran}(x)
be distinct nodes. We claim that there exists an
x
-norming partition separating
u
and
v
.

Consider the “Gap Function” for the pair
(u,v)
defined on
JT
:

\Phi_{u,v}(z)=\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}-\sup_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{S}_{u,v}}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}

where
\mathbb{S}_{u,v}
is the set of admissible partitions where
u
and
v
belong to disjoint segments.

The function
z\mapsto\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}
is continuous. The function
g(z)=\sup_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{S}_{u,v}}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}
is also continuous (as the supremum of a family of convex, continuous functions). Therefore,
\Phi_{u,v}
is a continuous function.

Since each
x_{n}
is separated, for any pair
u,v\in\operatorname{ran}(x_{n})
, there exists an
x_{n}
-norming partition separating them. This implies
\Phi_{u,v}(x_{n})=0
.

For sufficiently large
n
,
u,v\in\operatorname{ran}(x_{n})
. Thus
\Phi_{u,v}(x_{n})=0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
By the continuity of
\Phi_{u,v}
, we have:

\Phi_{u,v}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{u,v}(x_{n})=0.

The condition
\Phi_{u,v}(x)=0
implies that
\sup_{\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{S}_{u,v}}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{\mathcal{P}}^{2}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}
. Since the set of partitions is compact and the map is upper semi-continuous, this supremum is attained by some partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}\in\mathbb{S}_{u,v}
.

Thus,
\mathcal{P}^{*}
is an
x
-norming partition that separates
u
and
v
. Since
u,v
were arbitrary,
x
is separated.
∎
