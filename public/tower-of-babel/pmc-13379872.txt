Performance-Enhancing Drug-Associated Myocarditis in a Young Athlete
Authors: Emily A. Parks, Jeremiah Essig, Serafim Pistiolis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent guidelines on activity restriction in myocarditis carry significant implications for athletes.Case SummaryWe present a case of fulminant myocarditis in a 21-year-old athlete after performance-enhancing drug use. He presented with chest pain and dyspnea with ST-segment elevations on electrocardiogram. Coronary angiography was normal, and cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed myocarditis. His course was complicated by cardiogenic shock requiring inotropic support but improved with diuresis and high-dose corticosteroids. A shared decision-making approach guided a stepwise return-to-exercise strategy based on symptoms, biomarkers, cardiac magnetic resonance, and exercise stress testing. The patient gradually resumed training and returned to unrestricted exercise 3 months after presentation.DiscussionThis case demonstrates the value of individualized, shared decision-making for management of myocarditis in athletes.Take-Home MessagesIndividualized, guideline-informed return-to-exercise strategies help balance safety with athlete goals after myocarditis. Screening for performance-enhancing drug exposure is essential because these agents may represent an underrecognized contributor to myocarditis.

History of Presentation

A 21-year-old male former college football player and novice bodybuilder developed fulminant myocarditis after first time use of performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs). He developed shortness of breath and chest tightness approximately 10 days after injecting testosterone. He thought the sample was pure testosterone, but he could not verify the contents. He noted mild nasal congestion but no other infectious symptoms. His chest pain and dyspnea progressively worsened, prompting him to call emergency medical services. On presentation, he was tachycardic (110-120 beats/min) and saturating 88% on 4 L of oxygen. He was pale and in moderate respiratory distress with rhonchorous breath sounds bilaterally. He had no peripheral edema. His chest pain was characterized as worse when supine and improved when leaning forward.Take-Home Messages•Athletes recovering from myocarditis benefit from individualized, guideline-informed return-to-exercise plans developed through shared decision-making and supported by data from CMR, continuous telemetry monitoring, biomarkers, and exercise stress testing to balance safety with the athlete's goals and motivation.•Thorough screening for performance-enhancing drug use is essential because these agents may contribute to cardiovascular disease and should be considered a potential etiology of myocarditis.

Past Medical History

The patient had limited past medical history aside from nicotine use with electronic vaping and occasional marijuana use. He had no family history of cardiac disease.

Differential Diagnosis

The description of the patient's chest pain was concerning for myocarditis and/or pericarditis. Differential included acute coronary syndrome; however, he had minimal risk factors for acute coronary syndrome as a young man with limited past medical history. Acute exacerbation of previously undiagnosed heart failure was also considered less likely without prior history of heart failure symptoms.

Investigations

His electrocardiogram (ECG) showed ST-segment elevations in lead I and V4 to V6, consistent with a lateral ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (Figure 1). Chest radiograph demonstrated pulmonary edema. Initial laboratory work-up was notable for troponin elevation (23,551 ng/L), elevated N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (1,408 pg/mL), leukocytosis (14.1 10 × 9/L), elevated creatinine (1.6 mg/dL), and elevated C-reactive protein (65.1 mg/L). A code ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction was activated and emergent cardiac catheterization showed right dominant coronary circulation without any coronary artery disease. He had an elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure at 32 mm Hg. His initial echocardiogram showed a moderately reduced left ventricular ejection fraction at 40% to 45%. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) confirmed myocarditis with late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) of subepicardial left ventricle lateral and inferior wall and mild myocardial edema (Figure 2A). Repeat echocardiogram was obtained 48 hours after admission due to respiratory decompensation and revealed a severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction at 30% to 35%. By discharge, the patient's ejection fraction improved to 50% to 55%, and high-sensitivity troponin down trended to 4,660 ng/L. Management

He was diagnosed with acute myopericarditis and started on high-dose aspirin 650 mg by mouth 3 times a day, colchicine 0.6 mg twice a day, in addition to intravenous methylprednisolone. Due to cardiogenic shock, he was started on dobutamine 2 μg/kg/min and transferred to an advanced heart failure center. His left ventricular function improved with diuresis and methylprednisolone. He was weaned off dobutamine in 48 hours and subsequently started on losartan, metoprolol, and spironolactone by discharge. He was discharged on guideline-directed therapy for heart failure, continuous telemetry monitoring, and a prednisone taper.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At his 2-week hospital follow-up appointment, troponin and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide had normalized. His chest pain and dyspnea had completely resolved, and he reported very mild ankle edema. ECG showed T-wave inversions in V5 and V6, I, and aVL (Figure 3). Two-week continuous telemetry monitoring showed 4 episodes of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT). The fastest run lasted 5 beats at 171 beats/min. The longest run was 13 seconds at 101 beats/min. One episode of NSVT was associated with palpitations. Metoprolol was increased from 12.5 to 25 mg daily in response to the NSVT. Repeat echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular function. He had engaged in light exercise including ambulating up to 3 miles without symptoms.Figure 3Electrocardiogram at 2-Week Hospital Follow-UpElectrocardiogram demonstrates T-wave inversions in V5 and V6, I, and aVL.

Given the resolution of his symptoms and biomarkers, in addition to his desire to resume light exercise, we used shared decision-making to determine a reasonable exercise plan. He was instructed to avoid heavy lifting and limit physical activity to a maximum of 6 metabolic equivalents with goal heart rate <110 to 120 beats/min (zone 2). He was advised to closely monitor for symptoms of chest pain, dyspnea, or palpitations during activity and to stop exercise immediately and contact our team if he developed any of these symptoms. He was instructed to follow this restricted activity plan for the first 3 months after discharge.

At 3 months, the patient reported adherence to the recommended exercise regimen and was tolerating activity without symptoms. Per contemporary return-to-play guidance, repeat ambulatory rhythm monitoring to reassess arrhythmic burden before clearance for unrestricted activity was discussed. However, given geographic and work-related barriers to ambulatory monitoring, we agreed to assess for ventricular ectopy solely with exercise testing. He underwent a maximal exercise treadmill test without inducible ventricular arrhythmia. Repeat CMR showed normal left ventricular function, evidence of resolving myocarditis with mild residual LGE in the subepicardial basal inferior wall, normalization of T1 mapping, and resolution of myocardial edema (Figure 2B). Collectively, the improved diagnostic and clinical findings led to the patient being stratified as low risk for exercise-induced arrhythmia and/or sudden cardiac death, supporting a return to exercise without restriction. His heart failure therapies were gradually weaned given the restoration of his left ventricular function.

Consistent with current recommendations for athletes with prior myocarditis, a structured longitudinal surveillance plan was established. He will return this spring for repeat ambulatory rhythm monitoring and transthoracic echocardiography, with focused reassessment of symptom burden including palpitations, exercise intolerance, and dyspnea. After this early recovery evaluation, he will transition to clinical follow-up every 6 months for the first 2 years, with additional rhythm monitoring or imaging performed if new symptoms develop or if increasing ventricular ectopy is detected. We continued to reinforce strict avoidance of performance-enhancing supplements.

Discussion

We describe a case of a 21-year-old athlete that presented with symptomatic myocarditis that evolved into cardiogenic shock. The etiology of myocarditis in this case may be attributed to a viral syndrome or cardiotoxic effects of PEDs, specifically testosterone, that may have triggered the inflammatory cascade resulting in severe myocarditis. The patient clinically improved and experienced full recovery of cardiac function; however, he had persistent ECG changes and residual LGE. He was closely monitored for exercise-induced arrhythmia and safely returned to unrestricted exercise at 3 months postdischarge.

Due to the potential for triggering arrhythmia and risk of sudden cardiac death, current guidelines recommend that patients with stage C and D myocarditis refrain from strenuous physical activity and competitive sports for 3 to 6 months, which has major implications for competitive athletes.1 COVID-19 brought attention to return-to-play guidelines and the lack of an individualized approach that accounts for age, sex, or initial severity of disease.2 Experience during COVID-19 emphasized the role of CMR in guiding return-to-play, with resolution of myocardial inflammation occurring as early as 4 to 6 weeks in some athletes.3 Although prospective athlete-specific data directly linking CMR findings to sudden cardiac death are limited, the presence of LGE has been consistently associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in myocarditis.4 Persistent LGE likely reflects myocardial fibrosis and may serve as a substrate for ventricular arrhythmias. Accordingly, CMR findings should be interpreted in combination with symptom trajectory, biomarker normalization, and rhythm surveillance rather than as isolated determinants of return-to-play eligibility.5,6

Although a conservative approach—restricting the patient to very low-intensity activity for the full 6-month period—would have been entirely reasonable, his strong motivation to resume training for both physical and psychological well-being prompted a more nuanced strategy. Our team developed a personalized return-to-exercise plan that balanced patient goals and safety through risk stratification and follow-up guided by recent updates on myocarditis.3 Repeat CMR demonstrated mild residual LGE in the subepicardial basal inferior wall, indicating possible myocardial fibrosis and risk of arrhythmia. Location of LGE does influence risk, with inferior wall LGE having a better prognosis than anteroseptal patterns.7 Thus, the combination of the location and overall decrease in LGE, resolution of edema, and absence of arrhythmia on exercise stress test provided reassurance to support return to activity. The risk of adverse events after myocarditis cannot be eliminated and underscores the importance of transparent discussions of uncertainties and a comprehensive plan for continued monitoring.

This case also illustrates the potential harms associated with PEDs. The use of anabolic-androgenic steroids extends across the athletic spectrum, from elite competitors to recreational lifters, with the 2021 American College of Sports Medicine consensus statement reporting a 15% to 25% prevalence rate among male gym-goers.8 Testosterone and other anabolic-androgenic steroids have been well documented to increase the risk of ventricular arrhythmias, accelerate atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, and promote myocardial inflammation.9,10 Although a causal relationship between this patient's testosterone use and myocarditis cannot be definitively established, we strongly advised complete avoidance of these agents moving forward. The case further emphasizes the importance of obtaining a detailed history of supplement and PED use in athletes, as such exposures may have significant implications for diagnosis, management, and counseling.

Conclusions

An individualized, shared decision-making approach is important to managing myocarditis in athletes, particularly when balancing recovery, safety, and strong motivation to return to exercise. A detailed assessment of PED exposure remains critical because such agents may play an underrecognized role in cardiovascular disease, including myocarditis.
