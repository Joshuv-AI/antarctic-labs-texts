Age at Menopause and Brain Atrophy Among Older Women
Authors: M. Pia Campagna, Julie A. Schneider, Lisa L. Barnes, Konstantinos Arfanakis, Galit Levi Dunietz, David A. Bennett, Riley M. Bove
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs menopause age associated with brain volume trajectories in older women?FindingsIn this cohort study of 2603 older women, earlier menopause age was associated with faster cognitive decline (global and episodic), earlier Alzheimer disease onset, and greater white matter hyperintensity accumulation, with the largest effect sizes for structural outcomes observed in spontaneous menopause. Associations with total brain volume loss were present but modest.MeaningThese findings suggest menopause age has lasting associations with structural and functional brain aging decades after the menopausal transition, positioning it as a midlife-identifiable marker for dementia risk stratification and early intervention.

Introduction

Women experience a distinct trajectory of brain and cognitive aging, with a higher lifetime risk of Alzheimer disease (AD) than men,1 after accounting for longer lifespan. The menopausal transition represents a major inflection point in biological aging, marked by the loss of cyclic ovarian hormone production (mainly estrogens) and accompanied by metabolic, vascular, and neuroendocrine changes. Indeed, estrogens exert widespread neuromodulatory actions on synaptic plasticity, myelination, mitochondrial function, and neuroimmune tone,2 and progesterone and testosterone have been implicated in neuroprotection.3,4 Together, these observations suggest that age at menopause (ie, estrogen deprivation) could be a factor in long-term brain resilience and susceptibility to neuropathological changes.

Epidemiologic evidence links earlier menopause and shorter reproductive lifespan (RLS, the number of years between menarche and menopause) with faster cognitive decline and increased AD risk.5,6,7 Yet, it remains unclear whether these functional and pathological associations correlate with measurable, long-term structural differences in the brain. Cross-sectional neuroimaging cohort studies focusing on women at midlife have reported heterogeneous region-specific gray-matter reductions and altered cerebral metabolism across the menopausal transition: one study reported greater hippocampal atrophy and hypometabolism after menopause,8 whereas a UK Biobank study reported small, region-specific associations between later menopause age and less subcortical atrophy.9 A recent systematic review of 14 studies (1 longitudinal, 13 cross-sectional) limited by midlife focus and limited sample sizes, concluded that menopause age-related associations with brain volume are generally modest.10,11 To our knowledge, no studies to date have systematically examined late-life brain atrophy rates in association with menopause timing.

To address this gap, we leveraged data from 2 deeply phenotyped harmonized aging cohorts, Religious Orders Study12 and the Rush Memory and Aging Project13 (ROS/MAP), to test whether menopause timing is associated with brain atrophy rate. We hypothesized that earlier menopause (and secondarily, shorter RLS) is associated with accelerated brain volume loss. We additionally validated associations across cognitive trajectories and clinical AD diagnosis to establish a coherent, multidomain framework linking reproductive aging to lasting neurobiological resilience decades after the menopausal transition.

Methods

Study Population

Participants were nuns (ROS) or community-dwelling (MAP) females without known dementia at enrollment who underwent annual clinical evaluations, neuropsychological testing, and brain donation at death. Both studies were approved by the Rush University Medical Center Institutional Review Board. All participants signed informed and repository consents and an Anatomic Gift Act. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.14 Participants were included if both age at menarche and menopause were recorded, enabling derivation of RLS, and if self-reported menopause type was spontaneous or surgical. Participants contributed to analyses based on data availability: longitudinal cognitive trajectories (n = 2603), neuropathologic outcomes at autopsy (n = 1253), and longitudinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans (n = 774). Data were obtained through Synapse (dataset syn23446022).

Reproductive Exposures

Reproductive history variables were obtained from harmonized ROS/MAP questionnaires and defined according to prior analyses in this cohort.5 The primary exposure variable, menopause age, was derived from self-report (“At what age did you stop menstruating?”), with extreme ages (below 30 [unless surgical] or above 60 years) excluded. The secondary exposure, RLS, was calculated as menopause age minus self-reported menarche age (“At what age did you begin menstruating?”) and served as a proxy for cumulative endogenous estrogen exposure across the lifespan. Given the strong correlation between menopause age and RLS (Spearman ρ = 0.96; P < .001), RLS is analyzed as an exploratory secondary exposure.

Menopause type was classified as spontaneous or surgical based on self-report (“Was your menopause natural or caused by surgery?”). Due to missing information on surgical menopause subtype, this variable may capture a mix of procedures, including bilateral oophorectomy (with or without concurrent hysterectomy) and hysterectomy with ovarian conservation. Data on premature ovarian insufficiency (chemotherapy or radiotherapy) was not collected; therefore, women who experienced treatment-induced menopause were classified based on their self-reported menopause type. Main analyses combined all participants, whereas sensitivity analyses stratified by type.

Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) use was defined as systemic estrogen initiated within 5 years before or after menopause, corresponding to the clinical window of opportunity hypothesis for neuroprotection.15 Women who used MHT outside this 5-year window were not classified as MHT users for this variable but were retained in all analyses. MHT use was coded as a binary covariate (yes or no) in primary models and as an effect modifier in sensitivity analyses.

Clinical Diagnosis and Cognitive Outcomes

Incident AD was diagnosed annually by clinician consensus using standard criteria.16 Participants completed annual neuropsychological testing using a uniform battery of 19 tests assessing episodic memory, semantic memory, working memory, perceptual speed, and visuospatial ability. Test scores were standardized and averaged to create global and domain-specific composite scores, with higher values indicating better performance.17

Neuropathological Outcomes

At autopsy, AD neuropathology was assessed using National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer’s Association criteria for pathologic diagnosis18 incorporating Braak stage,19 Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease neuritic plaque score,19 and Thal amyloid phase.18 Categories 1 to 4 (none, low, intermediate, high) were evaluated as an ordinal outcome. All assessments were performed by board-certified neuropathologists blinded to clinical data.

MRI Outcomes

Structural 3 Tesla MRI was acquired biennially using harmonized protocols. T1-weighted images were processed using standardized pipelines to derive gray matter volume (GMV), white matter volume (WMV), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) volume, intracranial volume, and total brain volume (TBV; gray plus white matter volume), as described previously.20 Volumes were normalized to intracranial volume. White matter hyperintensities were segmented based on T1-weighted and T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery images, normalized to intracranial volume, multiplied by 100, and log-transformed. All segmentations underwent manual quality control, and macrostructural volumes (TBV, GMV%, WMV%, white matter hyperintensity volume [WMHV] %, CSF %) were analyzed as global indicators of atrophy and vascular injury. Exploratory regional GMVs were derived using multi-atlas segmentation for regions implicated in menopause and AD, including the hippocampus,9 amygdala,8 thalamus,8 anterior cingulate cortex (rostral and anterior), frontal cortex (caudal middle frontal plus rostral middle frontal plus superior frontal).21

Covariates

All models adjusted for covariates selected a priori based on established associations with both menopause timing and brain aging. First, demographic characteristics including study cohort (ROS vs MAP), education (years), race, and age (baseline age for AD diagnosis and cognition, age at death for neuropathology, age at scan for MRI analyses). Second, clinical characteristics including body mass index (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by heigh in meters squared), smoking pack-years, and MHT use (yes or no). Third, genetic characteristics, including APOE ε4 allele presence (yes or no) determined per prior research22 based on its association with menopause age23,24 and AD risk and severity.25 Race was included as it is associated with age at menopause and outcomes of interest (eg, AD risk, cognitive decline rate, and neuropathy). Race was self-reported at baseline using the question what is your race, and options were American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, other, White, and unknown.

Statistical Analyses

Analyses tested whether menopause age was associated with clinical AD diagnosis, cognitive trajectories, pathological burden, and brain volume trajectories in older women. To examine associations between age at menopause and AD diagnosis, we used 2 complementary approaches: (1) adjusted logistic regression estimating the odds of AD diagnosis at last follow-up and (2) accelerated failure time models with age as the time scale, accounting for delayed entry (left truncation) and right censoring at death or last follow-up. Log-normal, Weibull, and log-logistic distributions were evaluated using Akaike information criterion and quantile-quantile diagnostic plots. The log-normal accelerated failure time model was retained. Significance by FDR-adjusted P < .05 and data were analyzed using R studio version 2025.051+513 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Because AD diagnosis represents a discrete clinical threshold influenced by timing of assessment and diagnostic adjudication, we evaluated whether results were robust when modeling longitudinal cognitive decline based on menopause age (continuous exposure). Polynomial testing (quadratic and cubic age terms) did not reveal statistically significant nonlinearity in the association between study follow-up and global cognition (eTable 1 in Supplement 1), justifying the use of linear mixed-effects models with linear age slopes. These models were adjusted for the same covariates as clinical diagnosis models, and random intercepts and slopes accounted for within-person variability, while fixed effects characterized population-average associations. Exploratory analyses examined domain-specific cognitive trajectories. Ordinal logistic regression evaluated associations with the 4-level neuropathologic burden score, testing whether menopause timing was associated with cumulative AD pathology independent of demographic and genetic factors.

Before evaluating the association between menopause age and volumetric trajectories, we needed to account for selection biases in the MRI cohort. Since this subsample was overall healthier with higher baseline cognition than the whole cohort, each participant’s probability of MRI participation was assessed using propensity score models that included baseline variables associated with either the exposure (menopause age, RLS) or the likelihood of MRI (metabolic health indicators, cognitive and functional measures, psychosocial characteristics, and sociodemographic factors).26 Propensity scores were derived using logistic regression, and stabilized inverse probability weights (IPW) were applied to subsequent MRI analyses. Despite stabilized IPW weighting, residual imbalance in study cohort (ROS vs MAP) participation was observed after weighting; however, study cohort was included as an a priori covariate in all MRI models, mitigating against this residual confounding.

Polynomial testing of TBV trajectories revealed nonlinear trajectories (eTable 1 in Supplement 1), with accelerated atrophy at older ages, consistent with prior work on late-life structural decline.27,28 Accordingly, longitudinal brain atrophy was modeled using generalized additive mixed-effects models (GAMMs) with age at scan as the time scale. Models included random intercepts and slopes to account for individual differences in baseline volume and rate of change. To separate cross-sectional age differences from within-person aging, age was decomposed into between-person (mean scan age) and within-person (deviation from mean) components.

Menopause age was included as a main factor and in interaction with within-person aging to test associations with TBV and rate of atrophy. Restricted cubic splines were applied to menopause age to allow for nonlinear exposure-response relationships. This approach enabled evaluation of whether menopause timing influenced late-life brain structure and longitudinal atrophy trajectories without imposing linearity assumptions. Exploratory regional analyses were conducted to localize TBV associations, focusing on brain regions implicated in menopause and memory, including the hippocampus,9 entorhinal cortex,10 amygdala,8 thalamus,8 anterior cingulate cortex, and frontal cortex.21

Exploratory analyses evaluated whether MHT use modified menopause-related brain atrophy rates, modeling MHT use as a binary effect modifier (use vs no use), rather than as an independent main factor, allowing menopause age and its interaction with within-person aging to vary by MHT group within the GAMM framework. Models retained the same covariates and MRI-participation weights and were fit in the full sample and stratified by menopause type.

Hypotheses were organized into 3 families defined prior to analysis. The primary family addressed whether reproductive exposures (menopause age, RLS) are associated with cognitive decline, total brain volume, and AD neuropathology in the full sample. Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction applied across all 8 tests simultaneously.

The secondary family addressed domain specificity, comprising cognitive subdomains (episodic memory, working memory, semantic memory, perceptual speed, and visuospatial ability), brain volume subregions (GMV, WMV, WMHV, and CSF volume), and non-AD neuropathologies (hippocampal sclerosis, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, cerebral atherosclerosis, chronic microinfarcts, gross chronic infarcts, arteriolar sclerosis, and TAR DNA-binding protein 43 stage) in the full sample. Within each of these 3 domain-specific subfamilies, BH correction was applied simultaneously across both exposures. This secondary family addresses domain specificity within the full cohort, which is a biologically distinct question from whether associations differ by menopause type (see subgroup analyses); therefore, pooling it with the 84-test subgroup family would have been inappropriately conservative.

Subgroup analyses in women with spontaneous and surgical menopause were assigned a separate correction family, as these analyses were prespecified to test a biologically distinct question from the primary family. Critically, spontaneous and surgical menopause subgroup tests were pooled into a single BH correction step rather than corrected independently, as both subgroups jointly address this single prespecified question. BH correction was therefore applied simultaneously across all tests in the subgroup family (outcome domains, exposures, and subgroups).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The analytic sample included 2603 women contributing longitudinal cognitive data (mean [SD] age at enrollment, 78.3 [7.9] years; 172 [6.6%] Black or African American and 2400 [92.2%] White; mean [SD] education, 16.1 [3.6] years; 475 [22.3%] APOE ε4 carriers; mean [SD] BMI, 27.4 [5.6]; 1753 [67.3%] with spontaneous menopause) (Table 1), 1287 contributing neuropathology at autopsy, and 774 contributing serial MRI data. Women with surgical menopause had earlier menopause (median [IQR], 43.0 [39.0-49.0] vs median [IQR], 50.0 [46.0-52.0] years; P < .001) and shorter RLS (mean [SD], 30.41 [7.40] vs mean [SD], 35.91 [5.19] years; P < .001) than those with spontaneous menopause. MHT use within 5 years of menopause was approximately twice as common in women with surgical menopause (cognitive cohort: 238 [28.0%] vs 252 [14.4%]; MRI cohort: 94 [18.5%] vs 79 [31.5%]; both P < .001). Baseline global cognition, APOE4 carriage, and global AD pathology burden did not differ significantly by menopause type in either cohort. A healthy volunteer effect was identified in the MRI subsample, characterized by higher baseline global cognition (0.27 vs 0.10 SD), older age at death (91.3 vs 90.3 years), and later age at AD diagnosis (89.7 vs 88.4 years) relative to the full cognitive cohort, consistent with selective participation of cognitively intact survivors in 3-Tesla neuroimaging which started in 2012 (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Age at Menopause and AD Onset

In adjusted logistic regression models, earlier menopause age was associated with higher odds of AD diagnosis at last visit in the full cohort (N = 2603), though this did not reach statistical significance (OR, 1.01 per year earlier menopause age; 95% CI, 1.00-1.03; P = .11) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). In stratified analyses, the association was significant among women with surgical menopause, where each additional year of earlier surgical menopause age was associated with 3% higher odds of AD (OR, 1.03 per year earlier menopause age; 95% CI, 1.00-1.05; P = .03), whereas no association was observed in spontaneous menopause (OR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.96-1.01; P = .40).

Log-normal accelerated failure time models found that earlier menopause age was associated with faster time to AD diagnosis in the full cohort (TR, 0.998 per year earlier menopause age; 95% CI, 0.997-0.999; P = .003; FDR-corrected P = .02) (Figure 1A). Subgroup analyses showed minimal separation of AD-free survival curves among women with spontaneous menopause (TR, 1.000; 95% CI, 0.998-1.000; P = .97) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). Surgical menopause demonstrated directionally consistent graded delays in AD onset (TR, 0.998; 95% CI, 0.996-0.999; P = .01) (Figure 1B), although neither survived correction within the pooled subgroup family (both FDR-corrected P = .14) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

Age at Menopause and Rate of Cognitive Decline

Linear mixed-effects models showed that earlier menopause age was associated with faster decline in global cognition (β = –0.0009 SD per year earlier menopause age; SE = 0.0004; FDR-corrected P = .04) and episodic memory (β = –0.0014; SD per year earlier menopause age, SE = 0.0005; FDR-corrected P = .03) in the full cohort (Figure 2). Associations with semantic memory (β = –0.0011 per year earlier menopause age; SE = 0.0005; FDR-corrected P = .08) and working memory (β = –0.0005 per year earlier menopause age; SE = 0.0003; FDR-corrected P = .10) were directionally consistent but did not survive FDR correction. No associations were observed for perceptual speed or visuospatial ability (eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Stratified analyses revealed a consistent pattern of attenuation in spontaneous menopause and directionally apparent, though nonsignificant, point estimates in surgical menopause. Among women with spontaneous menopause, all findings were directionally consistent but nonsignificant (global cognition: β = –0.0009 per year earlier menopause age; SE = 0.0007; P = .18) (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Among women with surgical menopause, nominally significant associations were observed for episodic memory (β = –0.0017 per year earlier menopause age; SE = 0.0008; P = .02), directionally consistent with prior findings.5 None survived correction within the pooled subgroup family, reflecting the more stringent multiple comparisons burden across 84 pooled subgroup tests. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as directionally consistent with prior reports rather than independently confirmatory. These analyses extended prior findings from this cohort,5 given additional longitudinal data.

Age at Menopause and AD Neuropathology at Autopsy

Among women with neuropathologic data at autopsy (n = 1287), ordinal AD neuropathologic change (ADNC) was not significantly associated with menopause timing in the full cohort after BH correction within the primary family (OR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.97-1.00; P = .14; FDR-corrected P = .15) (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). In subgroup analyses, earlier surgical menopause was associated with greater ADNC burden before FDR correction (ordinal ADNC: OR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95-1.00; P = .04; FDR-corrected P = 0.26), directionally consistent with prior findings from this cohort.5 Associations in spontaneous menopause were null (OR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.98-1.03; P = .94) (eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

Age at Menopause and Brain Volume Trajectories

Consistent with reduced cognitive variability among women who survived and remained well enough to undergo MRI, the association between menopause timing and cognitive trajectories was attenuated and nonsignificant in the unweighted MRI subsample (n = 758) (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). This was true across all subgroups and domains, confirming that the MRI subsample represents a healthier, more cognitively homogeneous subset. Stabilized IPW-balanced baseline characteristics, reducing standardized mean differences to less than 0.1 for 84% of variables (eFigure in Supplement 2), were applied to all MRI analyses.

In IPW-weighted GAMM models, TBV trajectories varied by menopause timing in the full cohort; the interaction between within-person aging and earlier menopause age was significant (change in R2 [ΔR2] = 0.0004; f2 = 0.013; P = .01; FDR-corrected P = .03); however, the effect size fell below conventional thresholds for a small association (f2 = 0.02) and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Spontaneous and surgical subgroup TBV analyses were directionally consistent but did not survive the pooled subgroup BH correction (eTable 7 in Supplement 1). Similarly, GMV, WMV, and CSF trajectories were directionally consistent with faster decline with earlier menopause but did not survive FDR correction in the full cohort or by subgroup (spontaneous or surgical) (eTable 7 in Supplement 1).

There was an association between menopause age and WMHV accumulation in women with spontaneous menopause (Figure 3B). Earlier menopause age was associated with substantially faster WMHV accumulation (ΔR2 = 0.0050; f2 = 0.30; P < .001; FDR-corrected P < .001), representing the largest effect sizes observed across all MRI outcomes (eTable 7 in Supplement 1). Earlier menopause age was associated with progressively faster WMHV accumulation at older within-person ages, suggesting that earlier estrogen deprivation is associated with faster late-life white matter injury accumulation in a dose-dependent manner that becomes more apparent with advancing age. In our cohort, the model-estimated rate of WMHV accumulation at age 70 years for a woman with median menopause age (50 years) was an additional 9.9% per year of baseline WMHV volume, consistent with the well-documented acceleration of white matter injury in older age.28 The association of menopause timing with this trajectory was not uniform across follow-up. The difference in annual WMHV growth rate between women in the earliest vs latest quartile of menopause age (47 vs 52 years) peaked at approximately 3.4% faster accumulation per year around the mean follow-up age, attenuating to 1.6% at later visits. Expressed over a clinically meaningful interval, a woman with menopause 5 years earlier would accumulate roughly 15% greater WMHV volume over a decade relative to an otherwise identical woman with later menopause, assuming the peak differential is sustained. These associations were absent in women with surgical menopause (ΔR2 = 00015; f2 = 0.006; P = .72; FDR-corrected P = .88) and in the full cohort (ΔR2 = 00014; f2 = 0.006; P = .30; FDR-corrected P = .36), indicating specificity to the gradual hormonal trajectory of spontaneous menopause (eTable 7 in Supplement 1).

Exploratory regional cortical thickness analyses did not survive FDR correction. MHT and Menopause-Associated Brain Aging Trajectories

Exploratory MHT-stratified GAMM analyses did not identify significant moderating associations of MHT use with brain volume trajectories. No MHT interactions reached significance in any volume outcome or subgroup (eTable 9 in Supplement 1). These patterns should be interpreted cautiously given the low proportion of women exposed to MHT according to modern guidelines.

Secondary Exposure: Reproductive Lifespan

RLS showed directionally consistent associations with the primary menopause age results across all outcome domains (eTables 3-10 in Supplement 1). Shorter RLS was associated with earlier AD onset in the full cohort (TR, 0.998; 95% CI, 0.997-0.999; FDR-corrected P = .02) but not surgical or spontaneous menopause (TR, 0.997; 95% CI, 0.996-0.999; FDR-corrected P = .14), but not spontaneous menopause. Moreover, shorter RLS was associated with faster decline across global cognition, episodic, semantic, and working memory in the full cohort and surgical menopause subgroup. The most substantial MRI finding was faster WMHV accumulation with earlier spontaneous menopause (f2 = 0.37; FDR-corrected P < .001). Taken altogether, these results suggest that menopause age is associated with lasting cognitive and structural brain aging outcomes, with earlier menopause corresponding to more vulnerable brain aging trajectories decades after the menopausal transition (Table 2).

Discussion

In this large, prospective cohort study of older women, earlier menopause age was associated with less favorable cognitive aging trajectories, earlier clinical AD onset, and faster late-life brain atrophy, with WMHV accumulation as the primary structural end point. These findings extend prior work focused primarily on perimenopausal or midlife changes,8,9,11,29 by demonstrating that age at ovarian hormone loss remains associated with the rate of structural brain aging decades after the menopausal transition. The convergence of cognitive, clinical, and imaging outcomes is consistent with a model in which prolonged endogenous hormone exposure contributes to sustained neurobiological resilience in late life.

The most striking structural finding was a robust inverse association between earlier menopause age and faster WMHV accumulation in women with spontaneous menopause. A woman whose menopause occurred 5 years earlier accumulated approximately 15% more WMHV volume over a decade, representing the largest effect size observed across all outcomes in this study. A TR of 0.998 per year of earlier menopause corresponds to approximately 2% faster AD onset per decade of earlier menopause age. At the cohort mean AD diagnosis age of 88.4 years, this translates to roughly 1.8 fewer AD-free years for a woman whose menopause occurred 10 years earlier. The association of earlier spontaneous menopause with faster late-life WMHV accumulation connects to a growing body of work implicating the menopausal transition in cerebrovascular aging.30 Specifically, the Rhineland Study found that postmenopausal women had greater WMHV burden than premenopausal women and age-matched men, with menopause rather than chronological age as the primary driver of this sex difference.31 Our findings extend this literature longitudinally, demonstrating that earlier menopause age is associated with faster WMHV accumulation decades later, and specifically in women whose hormonal decline was gradual (spontaneous) rather than abrupt (surgical).

The pattern of stronger cognitive associations in surgical menopause, alongside more apparent structural associations in spontaneous menopause, likely reflects a combination of biological and methodological factors. Immediate and profound hormonal withdrawal following bilateral oophorectomy may be associated with greater sensitivity to timing-related differences in clinical outcomes, consistent with a withdrawal vulnerability model.5,32 In contrast, the gradual hormonal decline of spontaneous menopause, during which residual ovarian signaling may persist,33 appears more strongly associated with structural trajectories. This distinction is important beyond estrogen too, as the ovaries continue to produce testosterone after menopause, and bilateral oophorectomy eliminates this source of postmenopausal androgen-derived estrogen, compounding the hormonal deficit in ways that spontaneous menopause does not. At the same time, the surgical MRI subgroup was substantially smaller, survivor selection into imaging may preferentially exclude women with the most severe surgical menopause–associated decline, and abrupt hormonal withdrawal may compress atrophy slope variability. These factors together likely explain the observed divergence.

Strengths of this work included longitudinal follow-up across deeply phenotyped cohorts, which enabled characterization of within-person brain aging trajectories across decades. Critically, these findings provide among the first evidence that menopause age has a lasting association with structural brain aging into late life, extending prior work that focused on short-term perimenopausal changes or cross-sectional snapshots at midlife. Integration of structural MRI with longitudinal cognition and clinical AD diagnosis provided convergent evidence across multiple domains, strengthening confidence that menopause age is a determinant of late-life brain aging trajectories rather than an artifact specific to any single outcome measure.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. Among them, self-reported age at menarche and menopause, while generally reliable at the population level,34 may introduce recall bias. Indeed, nondifferential misclassification (early menopause overestimated, late menopause underestimated) could bias results toward the null, potentially underestimating true associations. Additionally, data on parity, oral contraceptive use, pregnancy, and breastfeeding were not systematically collected and therefore could not be included as covariates or effect modifiers. However, the ROS cohort, consisting of Catholic nuns, presumably had fewer pregnancies and contraceptive exposure. Second, ROS/MAP does not distinguish subtypes of surgical menopause (oophorectomy with or without hysterectomy, or hysterectomy with ovarian preservation), or how early iatrogenic menopause is categorized, which has historically been considered a limitation of this cohort. However, emerging evidence suggests that hysterectomy with ovarian conservation is not endocrinologically neutral, but associated with premature ovarian failure, likely through compromise of the ovarian blood supply,35 and with cognitive risk comparable in direction with that of bilateral oophorectomy.36,37 Our broad surgical menopause classification therefore likely captures a population vulnerable to surgery-related hormonal disruption regardless of specific procedure type. Third, menopause timing is shaped by biopsychosocial factors and coincides with systemic changes and manifestation of other chronic medical conditions (eg, sleep disturbance, obstructive sleep apnea, mood changes, cardiometabolic health) that are also associated with structural and functional brain changes.38 Menopause timing should therefore be interpreted both as a biological exposure and as an integrated marker of broader aging processes. A critical interpretive challenge in observational studies of this kind is the possibility of reverse causation, where early menopause may reflect accelerated systemic aging rather than cause it. Disentangling these pathways is methodologically difficult without an exogenous perturbation to ovarian function. Bilateral oophorectomy provides such a test, as the timing of hormone withdrawal is iatrogenic rather than biologically determined. The directionally stronger clinical and cognitive associations observed in our surgical menopause subgroup are therefore consistent with a causal interpretation, as these analyses are less susceptible to the confound that biological aging simultaneously drives both exposures and outcomes. Fourth, the possibility of a nonlinear association between menopause age and brain outcomes warrants acknowledgment. Our quartile-based visualizations suggest a graded pattern rather than a U-shape, and restricted cubic splines were applied in MRI analyses to allow for nonlinear exposure-response associations without imposing linearity. The upper exclusion threshold of 60 years for menopause age in this cohort also limits exposure to very late menopause, a range where some prior reports have noted divergence.39 Polynomial testing did not reveal significant nonlinearity in cognitive associations, and our RLS findings are monotonic across quartiles, which together argue against a clinically meaningful U-shaped association in this sample.

Conclusions

In this cohort study of older women, convergent findings across cognitive, clinical, and structural imaging domains reinforced the concept of menopause age as a key determinant of late-life brain aging trajectories, providing new evidence that structural associations persist and intensify decades beyond the menopausal transition. Menopause therefore represents a midlife window for targeted prevention and personalized risk mitigation, with menopause age itself serving as an early marker for risk stratification well before structural or cognitive changes become apparent. These findings provide a basis for further investigating the mechanisms linking reproductive aging to brain health in future studies.
