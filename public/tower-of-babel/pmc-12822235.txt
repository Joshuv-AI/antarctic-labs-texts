The role of religion and spirituality in the adaptation to life and psychological resilience of young adults who survived the disaster of the century: a phenomenological study
Authors: Süreyya Yiğitalp Rençber, Nilgün Ulutaşdemir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacgroundThe aim of this study is to examine in depth the role of religion and spirituality in the re-adaptation processes of young adults who survived the earthquake, a large-scale natural disaster of the 20th century. The study focuses on understanding the role of spirituality as a coping resource in individuals’ post-traumatic psychological recovery and resilience processes. It offers a unique contribution to the literature by shedding light on the cultural dimension of spiritual coping mechanisms in the Turkish context. It is also valuable for providing important implications for considering spirituality-based approaches in psychosocial intervention programs.MethodsPhenomenological research design, which is a qualitative research design, was used in this study. This qualitative research was conducted with 22 volunteer earthquake survivor nursing students. Interviews were conducted via WhatsApp. The data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s phenomenological analysis method.ResultsIn the analysis of the data, two themes (the effects of the earthquake and religious and spiritual practices to cope with the earthquake) and five sub-themes (social, physical, spiritual, religious practices, spirituality and coping) were identified.ConclusionAs a result of this study, it was determined that individuals in the disaster of the century were affected psychosocially and had difficulty in coping with the earthquake they experienced. In addition, it was determined that individuals benefited from religious and spiritual practices to cope with these problems.Keywords: Disaster of the century, Earthquake, Religion, Spirituality, Qualitative study

Introductıon

Disasters are inevitable events that have always existed in human history. Among these events, earthquakes have a special importance because they have the potential to completely change human life and social structure in terms of their effects and consequences. In regions where earthquakes occur, the social structure as well as the physical structure is severely damaged. Deaths caused by earthquakes cause incurable spiritual wounds and the loss of trained human resources that are very difficult to compensate [1, 2].In a ddition, the reconstruction of destroyed houses, workplaces, workshops, factories, production centers, infrastructure and superstructure requires very high costs and a long time [3, 4].

Numerous studies conducted in Turkey following the 1999 Marmara Earthquake revealed that rates of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression, in particular, reached very high levels following the disaster [5, 6]. Başoğlu et al. reported a PTSD rate of approximately 43% and a major depression rate of 31% after the Marmara Earthquake. A subsequent study found a PTSD rate of 23% in the epicenter and 14% in Istanbul [6]. Similarly, in an assessment conducted two months after the earthquake in Bingöl, approximately one-quarter (25%) of participants were diagnosed with PTSD [7]. Similar rates have been reported in studies conducted in other regions of Turkey; for example, PTSD prevalence rates were found to be 17% in Bolu, 26% in Ankara, 22% in Gölcük, and 37% in Düzce [8]. All these findings demonstrate that earthquakes can cause not only physical destruction but also profound and long-lasting damage to individuals’ psychological well-being. Therefore, it is crucial that disaster preparedness plans include protective and supportive approaches to psychological trauma [5].

Disasters interrupt the normal flow of human life and bring along a series of negativities including fear, anxiety, restlessness and anxiety [9, 10]. The February 6, 2023 earthquake, which was extremely effective in eleven provinces in Turkey, has been described as the “Disaster of the Century” as it was an event that embodied all the negativities that a disaster could cause to humanity. According to the official authorities, more than 50 thousand people lost their lives and hundreds of thousands of people were injured and/or lost limbs [11]. It is also reported that the number of destroyed houses and workplaces reached very high figures [12]. Although earthquake is seen as a physical (natural) event related to nature, in a broader context, earthquake has a social and psychological impact dimension. The effects of earthquake can be evaluated in the axis of different social problems such as economy, religion, NGOs, social policy, psycho-social situations, disadvantaged groups [13]. Psychosocial interventions refer to meeting the social, physical and psychological needs of the society affected by the disaster at both individual and social levels and supporting them overcome this process [14]. In addition, factors such as trauma experienced after the earthquake, post-traumatic stress disorder, radical changes people’s beliefs, attitudes and behaviors, being more sensitive, fragile, anxious and panicked before the earthquake can also be expressed in terms of psychological dimension [15]. If we add migration to these, many problem areas emerge in terms of adaptation conditions such as moving to a different city, establishing a new environment there, the role of NGOs in the process, psychological support services, both at the level of psychological and social relations.

Especially after the earthquake disaster, which was a traumatic event, religion and spirituality may have functioned as a source of refuge and solace for people [16]. Religion and spirituality, which are thought to be functional in this direction, offer people a perspective, shape the way they approach events and organize these processes [13, 17]. This process is motivated by the human needs to explain and understand events, predict and control them, and protect their self-esteem [18]. The cultural structure of society in Turkey demonstrates that religious and spiritual elements play a significant role in individuals’ coping strategies during social crises and disasters. Particularly after major disasters, individuals face not only physical and economic harm but also psychosocial processes involving existential uncertainty and triggering a search for meaning. In this context, research in Turkey suggests that religious/spiritual mechanisms such as spiritual belief, worship, social solidarity, and the creation of meaning in the context of “post-disaster spiritual coping” can positively impact individuals’ psychological adjustment and resilience [19, 20]. In Turkish culture, religion and spirituality are considered not only as belief systems but also as psychosocial resources that serve to “find meaning in what has happened,” “regain a sense of control,” and “re-establish social bonds” in times of crisis.

In Turkey, the coordination of psychosocial support services in disasters and emergenciesis carried out under the responsibility of the Ministry of Family and Social Serviceswithin the framework of the Turkey Disaster Response Plan (TAMP). Psychosocial support in disaster areas is provided by multidisciplinary teams, primarily consisting of the ministry, AFAD, andthe Turkish Red Crescent. A review of the guidesshows that it is necessary to learn about local, cultural, religious, and spiritual support systemsand adapt this information to psychosocial interventions, as well as to support traditional coping mechanisms related to funerals andthe mourning period [21]. However, the guides emphasize that, rather than a specific, structured, and nationally standardized religious/spiritual intervention program for young adults, religious and spiritual resources are treated more as supportive elements that take cultural context into account Spiritual/religious intervention program for young adults, but rather that religious and spiritual resources are treated more assupportive elements that take cultural context into account [22]. The Presidency of Religious Affairs, particularly following the earthquakes of February 6, 2023, has implemented “spiritual counseling and guidance” services in disaster areas within an institutional framework through a cooperation protocol signed with AFAD; and tens of thousands of religious officials were assigned to the field to provide spiritual support and religious guidance to earthquake victims [23, 24]. These services aim to support earthquake victims in making sense of the disaster, coping with loss, experiencing the grieving process, and strengthening the sense of social solidarity. However, these spiritual counseling activities are primarily organized around religious services and are mostly flexible according to the needs on the ground; they are still far from being integrated programs with standardized protocols carried out by mental health professionals through clinical psychosocial interventions and systematically evaluated results [25]. A review of recent studies conducted in Turkey shows that religious/spiritual coping styles are closely related to psychosocial adjustment, post-traumatic stress symptoms, and well-being among both adolescents and earthquake survivors. A study conducted with Turkish adolescents during the COVID-19 pandemic reported that positive religious coping was associated with better psychosocial adjustment, while negative religious coping was associated with psychosocial risk [26]. A study conducted with adults affected by the February 6 earthquakes indicated that psychological well-being and spirituality play a protective role in alleviating post-traumatic symptoms [27]. However, it is emphasized that significant structural barriers persist in field studies related to spiritual counseling and guidance services in emergency situations, such as the lack of clear definition of roles and responsibilities, inter-institutional coordination problems, uncertainties regarding the boundaries between mental health and religious services, and the limited training of personnel providing spiritual support in psychosocial trauma [28]. (Proceedings of the Symposium on Spiritual Counseling and Guidance in Emergency Situations, 2020). Similarly, mental health professionals and civil society actors point out that the lack of coordination between psychiatric/psychological support and spiritual support services in the field after a disaster makes it difficult to provide comprehensive and evidence-based care [28].

Although Turkey’s official psychosocial support frameworks following disasters recognize religious and spiritual coping resources as culturally significant elements, it is evident that interdisciplinary and evidence-based spiritual/religious intervention programs for young adults have not yet been systematically designed and implemented. The aim of this study is to examine in depth the role of religion and spirituality in the reintegration processes of young adults who survived the February 6 earthquakes, referred to as the “disaster of the century.”

Methods

Type of research and study group

In this study, phenomenological research design, which is a qualitative research design, was used. Phenomenology is used for studies that aim to investigate the phenomena that we frequently encounter daily, are familiar to us, but whose meaning we cannot fully grasp, and it creates a suitable research ground. This design was chosen because it offers a high degree of freedom in describing a new phenomenon (event or experience) from the participants’ perspective, providing rich data and detailing their experiences [29]. In particular, a descriptive phenomenological approach was followed to deeply examine and understand the role of religion and spirituality in the adaptation to life and psychological resilience of earthquake survivors who survived the disaster of the century. This approach is about uncovering the essence of the phenomenon under investigation and presenting the experience ‘exactly as it presents itself, neither adding to nor subtracting from it’ [30, 31].

Criterion sampling method, which is one of the purposeful sampling methods, was used in the study. Criterion sampling is the creation of a sample from people, events, objects or situations with the qualities determined for the problem [32, 33]. The sample of the study consisted of nursing students who experienced the earthquake. Inclusion criteria for sampling are nursing students who were affected by the earthquake, do not have a clinical case, have post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria are defined as not being an undergraduate student, not experiencing the effects of the earthquake, not having PTSD, and not agreeing to participate in the study. When discussing ethical issues in medical ethics, there are ethical principles that guide the decision-making process and must be taken into consideration. Although these principles are expressed in different ways, Beauchamp and Childress’s four-principle approach is accepted worldwide [34]. When working with individuals who survived such an extraordinary disaster, the principles of “beneficence, non-maleficence, respect for autonomy, and justice” were followed, which Beauchamp and Childress argued encompass the entirety of medical ethics [35]. 22 earthquake survivor students living in 11 different provinces where the disaster of the century was experienced were interviewed and interviews were terminated when data saturation was reached. At the end of the interviews, they were referred to a psychologist for psychological support.

Data collection tools

In the study, questions including sociodemographic data of the participant (age, gender and damage to the house) and a semi-structured interview form were created as data collection tools. The semi-structured interview form was prepared by the researchers by scanning the literatüre [36]. Semi-structured interviews allow us to obtain in-depth information in the relevant field [37]. The researchers asked two open-ended questions, which they prepared by taking the opinions of two lecturers who are experts in the field of public health and psychiatry, to examine the problems experienced by earthquake victims. Questions in the semi-structured interview form;

What were the mental, physical and social effects of the earthquake on you? Explain….

Data collection

The data were collected by the researchers between 01.03.2023 and 15.03.2023. Semi-structured interviews were conducted individually via Whatsapp application. The purpose and process of the study were explained to each potential participant in advance by contacting over the phone. After obtaining permission from the participants who agreed to participate in the study and filling out the voluntary consent forms, interview forms were delivered to them via Whatsapp. The data obtained from the participants as written text were delivered to the researchers.

Data analysis

Phenomenological analysis was used to analyze the qualitative data. Phenomenological analysis is an approach primarily developed within existential psychology and attempts to understand how people evaluate events happening around them. Phenomenological analysis examines individuals’ cognitive, emotional, and physical states from a holistic perspective [38]. The data obtained from the interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis method. Data analysis was done by researchers. In the analysis of the qualitative data obtained from the interviews, the 7-step analysis method developed by Colaizzi (1978) for phenomenological studies was used [30, 39]. In this context, the interview texts were first read independently and repeatedly by three researchers. Thus, it was tried to understand what was explained in the data. The important statements in the interview texts were selected, reorganized and expressed in general terms. Then, the data that were tried to be explained in the statements were identified and analyzed. The researchers formulated and validated the meanings by discussing them until they reached a consensus. The researchers then identified and organized the themes into main and sub-themes. The themes and sub-themes of the study were developed through clear articulation. In addition, participants’ statements were included so that the reader could verify the interpretation and analysis of the data.

Ethical aspects of the research

Prior to commencing the study, ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Gümüşhane University on February 24, 2023, with reference number E-95674917-108.99.99.99–164436. Informed consent was obtained from the participants before starting the interview. The recordings and transcripts were stored on a password-protected device. Each step of the research was written using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), which was developed for use in reporting qualitative research [10]. All of our authors confirm that all methods have been carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations (Declaration of Helsinki).

Theme 1. effects of earthquake

Subtheme 1. spiritual

Individuals were negatively affected by the earthquake and reported experiencing many emotions such as fear, despair, hopelessness, astonishment, mental depression, sadness helplessness and confusion.

“The earthquake was a very bad situation for us, one feels very helpless…God forbid it to anyone.” (P2).

“For me, earthquake means fear and panic…” (P3).

“Staying in the earthquake rubble is like experiencing the apocalypse, in my opinion… (P18).

“Earthquake means helplessness, earthquake means fear, people experience these to the fullest, we have experienced this situation in the most painful way…. (P22)

Subtheme 2. physical

Individuals stated that they experienced many physical problems such as being trapped in the rubble, being homeless, having to stay on the streets, living in tents and experiencing health problems.

“Like many people, we stayed on the streets and lived in tents during the earthquake. The earthquake affects people negatively in every aspect. May God not give it to anyone…(P17).

“I went to wake up my mother and father. At that time, I was taking steps and falling because of the shaking. Finally, I found a safe place and stopped and waited for the shaking to stop, but I remember something falling on my head… (K6).

“Even though I did not stay in the rubble for a long time, it is very difficult to experience that fear…I stayed in the rubble for an hour and then the teams came and rescued me, but believe me, that fear is very different… Afterwards, being homeless, living in a container were all very difficult situations for us…. (P15).“I was actually crouching on the edge of the bed, but a pillar fell on me and I collapsed there. When I woke up, everywhere was dark… It was very cold and raining. I managed for a while by sucking the water flowing from the curtains, my mouth was getting dry. I was sucking the water flowing from the curtains… (P12).

Subtheme 3. social

Individuals stated that society provided them with a lot of social support during the earthquake, but they still experienced problems such as feeling lonely, knowing that we are helped as a community, being happy with incoming aid, and experiencing the pain of the loss of loved ones. “You feel lonely, even if you are happy with the aid…Thanks to everyone, everyone rushed to help, they sent everything, but it is very different to experience the earthquake… (P9).

“It feels good to know that we are being helped as a society, but it still makes you very sad. Losses of loved ones are not forgotten….(P16).

“Generally, our needs were met, even if it was difficult… (P29).

Theme 2. practices on religion and spirituality to cope with earthquake

Subtheme 1. religious practices

Earthquake victims stated that they tried to cope with the earthquake by performing religious duties such as praying, reciting salawat, submitting to God, reading the Quran, and saying the Rosary.

“I prayed a lot, I read the Qur’an, I took refuge in God, otherwise you can’t cope, you’ll go crazy. It was not easy for us to experience that pain, there was a really big drama for us, we saw the cold face of death until the end……(P1).

“Earthquakes are from God and they kill some and give health to others… I believe that Allah knows and plans everything. A believer and a Muslim must submit to God will and wish… (K11).

“I tried to perform my prayers and prayed a lot, we have no one to turn to except Allah…(P21).

Sub-theme 2. spirituality and coping

Individuals affected by the earthquake of the century stated that they embraced spirituality in order to cope with the effects of the earthquake by embracing and chatting with loved ones, not being sad about the days they were living in, getting psychological support, self-suggestion, believing in fate, and spending time with loved ones and friends.

“Spirituality is important, otherwise you couldn’t cope with the pain. When you see those wreckages and dead people, it is very bad. I took refuge in God, I believed in fate, we thought that everything comes from God and we tried to be patient… (P17).

“I passed out when I saw those dead, dismembered human corpses…I can’t tell you more, but we came from God and we will go to God, that’s what I think…(P10).

“While we were under the rubble, the love my mother and I had for each other motivated us and gave us strength. (P26)

Discussion

The aim of this study is to deeply examine the role of religion and spirituality in the adaptation to life and psychological resilience of earthquake survivor nursing students who survived the disaster of the century. The role of religion and spirituality in the adaptation to life and psychological resilience of the earthquake survivor nursing students who survived the disaster of the century in two themes.

Effects of earthquake

As a result of the study, it was determined that individuals were negatively affected by the earthquake psychologically, socially and physically. Although earthquakes are the third most common type of disaster in the world after floods and hurricanes, they cause the most losses [40]. Turkey, which is located in an active earthquake zone due to its geographical location, has faced great earthquake-related destruction in the historical process [2]. The earthquakes in Erzincan in 1939 and 1992, in Gölcük in 1999, in Van in 2011, in Elazığ in 2020 and in İzmir in 2020 are among the major disasters that caused great losses in Turkey’s history. On February 6, 2023, an earthquake with its epicenter in Pazarcık district of Kahramanmaraş province and a second earthquake centered in Elbistan about 9 h after this earthquake caused a very high number of casualties and heavy damage in 11 provinces including Kahramanmaraş, Hatay, Adıyaman, Gaziantep, Malatya, Kilis, Diyarbakır, Adana, Osmaniye, Şanlıurfa and Elazığ [2].

In national and international studies on earthquakes and post-earthquake processes, it is a common conclusion that earthquakes have short, medium and long-term psychosocial effects. There are analyzes that earthquakes worldwide have increased with the effect of climate changes as of the 21 st century. For this reason, it is very important to intensify studies and sensitivities on earthquakes [41].

Religious and spiritual practices to cope with earthquake

The study found that young adults affected by the “earthquake of the century” experienced great difficulty coping and attempted to cope by turning to both religion and spirituality during this process. Religion constitutes an important part of human life in that it offers a system of beliefs and values in people’s individual and social lives and ensures the concretization of this system through rituals, worship, and institutional structures. Religion provides comprehensive explanations about the causes and consequences of certain events, thus providing a framework for understanding and interpreting life events that cause pain and trauma [42–45]. In this context, it is known that one of the oldest ways people seek to understand and cope with disasters is through religious orientation and explanations [42].

The focus of our study is not solely on “religion,” but rather on the dimension of “spirituality,” which is closely related to religion but conceptually distinct from it. In the literature, religion is defined more as a set of institutionalized and organized beliefs, worship, and practices associated with a particular tradition; spirituality, on the other hand, refers to a more personal, internal, and flexible dimension, such as an individual’s search for meaning and purpose in life, transcendental experiences, and a deep connection with oneself, others, nature, or a transcendental being [46–48]. For some individuals, spirituality is experienced within a traditional religious framework, while for others, it is described as an experiential realm shaped around meaning, hope, commitment, and values, with weaker ties to institutional religiosity. In this study, participant narratives show that these two aspects of religion and spirituality are both theoretically distinguishable and often intertwined in practice. Young adults engage in more “religious” practices such as prayer, worship, reading sacred texts, and seeking support from religious communities, while also experiencing processes of questioning and reinterpretation that are more “spiritual” in nature, such as the meaning of life, the responsibility of survival, increased sensitivity towards others, hope, and new goals for the future.

Studies have shown that religious belief and spiritual orientation can have positive effects on individuals’ psychology [43–45]. A meta-analysis of 49 studies examining the relationship between religious coping and psychological adjustment to stress found a significant positive relationship between positive religious coping and psychological adjustment; a positive relationship was also reported between negative religious coping and negative psychological adjustment [49]. Similarly, religion and spirituality often play a central role in the treatment and recovery process of post-traumatic stress disorder; positive religious/spiritual coping style, religious/spiritual attachment, and internalized religiosity have been shown to be associated with post-traumatic growth [36, 45]. Similar to the literature, our study shows that participants’ practices of praying, surrendering, trusting, nurturing feelings of hope and gratitude, and placing the disaster within a broader spiritual meaning framework can support their post-traumatic resilience. Participants’ narratives indicate that not only their relationship with God, but also deeper connections with others and with life, the meaning of survival, value-oriented living, and spiritual hope for the future, point to a strong dimension of “spiritual coping.”

The findings of the study also reveal that religious and spiritual coping does not always yield positive results. Viewing the earthquake as God’s punishment will not only prevent people from taking the necessary precautions by avoiding responsibility, but will also make it difficult to adapt to developing situations and cope with the challenges they bring. This mindset and evaluation process will lead the individual to blame themselves or others, causing feelings of excessive guilt, helplessness, hopelessness, worthlessness, and sinfulness, which will increase psychological problems [42]. It has been shown that these situations, defined in the literature as “negative religious/spiritual coping” or “spiritual conflict,” can intensify stress symptoms and negatively affect mental health [36, 42, 46]. The study found that some disaster victims questioned their beliefs and experienced religious and spiritual breakdowns as a result of the event, which may be related to both the high impact of the event and its challenging nature in terms of seeking justice and meaning. From this perspective, this study is significant in two different ways: it highlights not only the protective aspect of religion but also the spiritual questioning, conflict, and breakdowns that emerge in young adults.

These findings show that the discussion should not be limited to the “religion” axis alone, but that religion and spirituality are two distinct dimensions that can be separated both theoretically and experientially, yet are intertwined in practice. Therefore, in psychosocial interventions conducted after an earthquake, it is important to address both religious and spiritual coping styles together and in a balanced manner; to support positive religious/spiritual coping, and to allow negative religious/spiritual assessments and spiritual conflicts to be reviewed in a safe environment [36, 42, 47, 48, 50]. Given that religion and spirituality are deeply embedded in the cultural fabric of Turkey, this phenomenological study provides context-specific qualitative evidence on how young adults experience religion and spirituality in two different dimensions in their processes of readjustment to life and psychological resilience. It provides important insights for the development of spirituality-based, culturally sensitive, and interdisciplinary intervention programs for young people.

Limitations of the study

The research is limited to earthquake victims who experienced the 2023 earthquake and lived in a specific region; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to all disaster victims in Turkey. The relatively small sample size and the use of purposive sampling strategies, consistent with a qualitative phenomenological design, constitute another limitation in terms of representativeness. The collection of data through self-reported, semi-structured interviews raises the possibility of recall bias and social desirability effects due to participants’ retrospective recounting of their experiences. Furthermore, the fact that the interviews were conducted after a specific time period following the disaster may not fully reflect the changes in psychosocial effects over time. Finally, due to the nature of the phenomenological approach, researcher subjectivity cannot be completely excluded in the analysis and interpretation of data; therefore, this limitation was mitigated as much as possible through methods such as bracketing, inter-researcher consensus, and the inclusion of direct participant quotes.
