Protocol for detecting protein S-glutathionylation in Arabidopsis thaliana under oxidative stress using a non-transgenic chemical toolkit
Authors: Shuai Zhao, Xiaoyuan Chen, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryProtein S-glutathionylation is a redox-sensitive post-translational modification that regulates proteostasis and signaling during oxidative stress. However, its in vivo detection is challenging due to labile disulfide bonds and ex vivo oxidation artifacts. Here, we present a protocol for detecting protein glutathionylation in Arabidopsis thaliana using a non-transgenic toolkit. We describe steps for using propargyl glycine (PG) metabolic labeling and click chemistry. We detail workflows for global quantification, target enrichment for western blotting or liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and in situ spatial visualization.For complete details on the development, use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Zhao et al.1Subject areas: Microscopy, Plant sciences, Molecular/Chemical Probes

Before you begin

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) act as pivotal signaling molecules in plants, executing their functions through reversible oxidative modifications of protein cysteines.2 Among these, S-glutathionylation (the formation of a mixed disulfide with glutathione) is essential for maintaining proteostasis under environmental challenges.3,4 Traditional detection methods, such as the biotin-switch technique (BST) or anti-glutathione antibodies, often suffer from incomplete thiol blocking and ex vivo oxidation artifacts during cell lysis.5 To provide a more accurate representation of the in vivo redox state, we developed the this protocol.1

Innovation

This chemical toolkit-based method introduces three significant innovations that distinguish it from traditional redox-proteomics methods. First, it utilizes a non-transgenic approach by leveraging the plant’s endogenous biosynthetic machinery to incorporate the alkyne-tagged precursor (PG) into the native glutathione pool. This strategy eliminates the requirement for genetic transformation, providing a versatile framework that is potentially adaptable to diverse plant species.1,6 Second, it ensures artifact-free redox preservation through a strictly non-reducing buffer system combined with rapid N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) alkylation to effectively “freeze” the native glutathionylation state and prevent post-extraction disulfide exchange. Finally, the workflow provides a uniquely versatile multidimensional detection toolkit, enabling researchers to seamlessly transition from global biochemical quantification to specific target identification via IP-WB or proteomics and high-resolution spatial localization through in situ imaging within a single experimental setup. Specifically for imaging, the integration of fluorogenic CalFluor dyes—which emit robust fluorescence only upon successful cycloaddition—drastically minimizes non-specific background, thereby maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio in complex plant tissues.7

Normalization and Loading Controls

Expected outcomes

Following the CuAAC click reaction and Western blotting with Streptavidin-HRP, a complex pattern of multiple distinct bands should be observed across a wide range of molecular weights (Figure 1). The overall signal intensity should increase substantially in samples treated with SA, NaAsO2, or H2O2 compared with the mock control. The “−PG” control lane may exhibit a basal background signal, which mainly reflects endogenous biotinylated carboxylases and nonspecific binding. However, the signal in the +PG samples should be substantially stronger than that in the −PG control, demonstrating specific incorporation of the metabolic precursor. The specific S-glutathionylation signal should be nearly abolished in the +reducing agent control lane, such as DTT- or β-Mercaptoethanol-treated samples, confirming that the captured biotinylation is linked through reversible disulfide bonds.

For a specific protein of interest, enrichment is validated by comparing the input, which represents the lysate before immunoprecipitation, with the Streptavidin eluate, which represents the enriched fraction. A distinct band at the expected molecular weight of the target protein should be detected by the target-specific primary antibody, such as an anti-GFP antibody, in the Streptavidin eluate lane of stress-treated +PG samples, as demonstrated for RBP47B in Figure 4F of Zhao et al.1 This band should be absent or substantially weaker in the mock +PG eluate and absent in the −PG control eluate, even when the protein is detectable in the corresponding input lanes. If the eluate is treated with a reducing agent, such as DTT or β-mercaptoethanol, before or during elution, the target protein signal should disappear from the biotin-captured fraction, confirming that the modification depends on a reversible disulfide bond.

In Figure 2, the primary comparison is between the Mock control (normal growth conditions) and various stress-treated groups, both performed in the presence of PG. In the basal state, the mock +PG samples should show minimal and diffuse CalFluor 488 or CalFluor 555 signal within the cytoplasm. This low baseline is consistent with low physiological S-glutathionylation levels in unstressed cells and the fluorogenic turn-on property of CalFluor dyes, which remain optically silent unless the azide group reacts with the alkyne-labeled PG-derived glutathione signal.

Calfluor 555 or Calfluor 488 labeled glutathionylated foci (indicated by white arrows) in the root cells of WT seedlings with 0.5 mM PG pre-treatment for 1 h. Upon oxidative stress induction, such as SA treatment, users should observe a robust increase in fluorescence intensity, mainly localized in extranuclear compartments. Depending on the stressor and cellular state, this signal may appear as distinct fluorescent puncta within the cytoplasm or as an enhanced diffuse cytosolic signal. The punctate signals likely reflect localized accumulation of S-glutathionylated proteins in stress-associated assemblies, such as stress granules or protein aggregates, whereas the enhanced cytosolic signal reflects broader glutathionylation of soluble proteins during the stress response. The use of fluorogenic probes provides a high signal-to-noise ratio, allowing clear discrimination between the weak basal cytoplasmic signal in the mock group and the stronger, localized signal in the stress-treated group. This contrast demonstrates the sensitivity and specificity of this protocol for spatial analysis of protein S-glutathionylation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

CRITICAL: For accurate densitometry and fluorescence quantification, it is imperative to use raw, uncompressed 12-bit or 16-bit images (e.g., TIFF format or native microscope files). Standard 8-bit images (such as JPEGs) possess a highly limited dynamic range (0–255) that easily leads to signal saturation, rendering it impossible to accurately quantify the differences between the highly intense stress-induced signals and the basal mock levels.

1.Western Blot Quantification: Scan the ECL films or export the digital blot images in a high-resolution, 16-bit TIFF format. Limitations

Although the CamLog method provides a robust toolkit for detecting protein S-glutathionylation, several limitations should be considered. First, while NEM alkylation is essential for freezing the thiol-disulfide status, it is an irreversible process. To avoid under-labeling or the formation of post-extraction artifacts caused by the non-physiological oxidation of free thiols, NEM must be prepared fresh and added immediately during the early stages of sample preparation. Second, despite the high efficiency of Streptavidin-based enrichment, researchers should be aware of potential interference from endogenous biotinylated proteins, such as various plant carboxylases, which may manifest as background bands in downstream assays. To mitigate this, the inclusion of a “-PG” control is indispensable for distinguishing true S-glutathionylated signals from these naturally occurring biotinylated species. Finally, a significant constraint of this in vivo labeling strategy is the difficulty in achieving precise absolute quantification of PG incorporation into the endogenous cellular glutathione pool. Because the uptake efficiency and metabolic incorporation rate of PG can fluctuate significantly depending on the plant species, tissue type, and developmental stage, results are primarily qualitative or relative. While Western blot and imaging provide strong confirmation of PG utilization, determining the absolute stoichiometric ratio of PG to unmodified GSH within the cell would require additional, more complex parallel LC-MS/MS profiling.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

No Western blot signal or very weak pulldown efficiency (related to Steps 17–20 and 29–31).

Potential solution

Reducing agents may have been inadvertently added to the loading or wash buffers. Strictly use non-reducing buffers. Exclude DTT or β-mercaptoethanol from all steps except the deliberate validation controls.

Problem 2

No target protein detected in the Pulldown Eluate, despite being present in the Input (related to Steps 21 and 22).

Potential solution

The Streptavidin beads might be saturated by unreacted free biotin. Ensure the Zeba Spin Desalting step (Step 21–22) is performed thoroughly prior to bead incubation to fully remove free Azide-Biotin.

Problem 3

High background smear observed in all lanes, including controls (related to Step 19).

Potential solution

Skim milk was likely used for membrane blocking. Exclusively use 5% BSA. Milk contains high levels of endogenous biotin and biotinylated proteins that strongly interact with Streptavidin-HRP.

Problem 4

High intensity of non-specific bands in the “-PG” control IP (related to Step 27).

Potential solution

Insufficient washing of the magnetic beads. Increase the number of washes with Wash Buffer A (up to 4 times) or increase the concentration of TWEEN-20 to 1% to increase wash stringency.

Problem 5

Weak or diffuse fluorescence signal during in situ confocal imaging (related to Step 6).

Potential solution

The CalFluor dye reaction may be sub-optimal. Ensure that the sodium ascorbate is prepared strictly fresh (within 30 minutes of use) and added last to the reaction mixture to effectively catalyze the CuAAC click reaction.
