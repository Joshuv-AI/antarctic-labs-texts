Challenges and coping practices of frontline health workers in newly created districts in Uganda: an exploratory qualitative study
Authors: Aloysius Mutebi, Moses Mukuru, Suzanne Namusoke Kiwanuka, Frederick Makumbi, Elizeus Rutebemberwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis paper examines the challenges frontline health workers face, as well as their coping practices following district splitting. It also has ramifications for the need to take into account the nexus between district splitting and subnational health system functioning.DesignA qualitative cross-sectional study employing an exploratory design. Data were collected using an in-depth interview guide for individual interviews. We used a deductive thematic analysis to identify and structure challenges faced and coping mechanisms by health workers in new districts. The data were analysed deductively using Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis.SettingFrontline health workers from four randomly selected regions with one parent district selected randomly from each region and a respective child district that had been split from it between 2005 and 2015. Interviews were conducted between June and November 2018.ParticipantsIn-depth interviews were conducted with 24 frontline health workers whose age ranged from 33 to 51 years and these had changed locations between districts after district splitting occurred.ResultsThe challenges frontline health workers faced included work-related role changes, social demands, team integration and health system inadequacies. Health workers switched roles across districts, adapting to leadership while balancing clinical and administrative duties. Overall, five themes emerged during analysis. Newly promoted staff faced knowledge gaps in facility management. Social demands included accommodation issues and family/community pressures. Team integration impacted daily work, requiring newcomers to navigate hierarchy and culture. Health system issues, such as understaffing, poor infrastructure, drug shortages and strained work relations, worsened working conditions. Coping practices included induction, leadership training, staff appraisals, duty rosters, supervision, team building, partner support and financial/community planning.ConclusionsDistrict splitting worsens challenges for frontline health workers. While they show resilience through coping strategies, systemic improvements remain essential. Addressing root causes like better resource distribution, expanded training and stronger administrative support is crucial to achieving the district splitting goal of improving healthcare delivery in newly formed districts in Uganda.Keywords: Human resource management, Health policy, Health Workforce

Introduction

The creation of local governments has been credited with improving service delivery in developing countries, although with concerns of quality.1 In the Ugandan context, local governments, among others, constitute districts.2 District splitting involves dividing existing districts through redrawing geographical boundaries. The district health system is integrated into local governance, with recruitment, discipline and promotions managed by district leadership.3 While district splitting follows physical boundaries, it significantly impacts the health workforces given that health system decentralisation is layered according to local government structures.4 Uganda’s administrative units grew from 33 districts in 1986 to over 135 by the early 2020s, reflecting a rapid proliferation of new districts intended to bring services closer to communities.

Decentralisation has mixed effects, often negatively impacting health workers through job instability, pay disparities and limited career growth.5 6 However, this has led to under-resourced and understaffed local governments, which undermine governance and service delivery goals.7 Many newly created districts operate with less than 50% of the authorised staffing, particularly in critical sectors like health and education.7 The government spends significant funds to establish and run new districts (eg, US$556 000 annually per district for salaries), but often fails to recruit sufficient staff. Even when staffing structures were approved (eg, FY 2023/24), many districts struggled to implement due to limited wage budgets (Team). The proliferation of districts has stretched health system management capacity. Newly created districts often lack experienced managers, while many district health managers have limited prior experience. This has resulted in diminished capacity to manage health services effectively.8 Approved staffing structures have remained outdated for over two decades. Although new guidelines were developed around 2022, their implementation in districts has been slow due to insufficient funding. Across public health facilities, staffing levels remain low, with around 60% of necessary positions filled, and some facilities as low as 35% staff presence. This staffing shortfall leads to workload burdens and discouragement among health workers.9 10 In low-resource settings, newly created districts face staffing shortages, inadequate medical supplies and poor infrastructure, worsening workloads and stress.11 12

While Uganda initially benefited from decentralisation, continuous district creation has stretched resources, threatening Universal Health Coverage.13 Although decentralisation policies should enhance service delivery, in the Ugandan case, they seem to have exacerbated health system challenges, given the rigid job structures, staffing shortages and biased recruitment,14,16 notwithstanding increased service demands with limited resources.17 18 Despite these challenges, district creation has continued, yet limited research has explored health workers’ experiences post-district splitting. Health restructuring aims to improve governance and access but also places pressure on frontline workers.19 These workers face logistical challenges, resource gaps and weak infrastructure in new districts. Understanding their coping strategies is essential for strengthening healthcare in these areas. This study employed a deductive approach, drawing on the theory of street-level bureaucracy (SLB) to examine challenges and coping practices of frontline health workers who switched workstations across or within districts. The theory provided a framework for developing research questions and guided the interpretation of findings.

Methods

Theory of street-level bureaucracy

Given the focus on the challenges government frontline workers face in their work context and how they respond, the theory of SLB was deemed suitable to guide the methodology of the study. Lipsky argued that frontline public workers have to perform their functions in an imperfect environment characterised by high demand for their services, with limited resources to meet the service expectations. Lipsky argued that frontline workers must balance professional expectations and citizen demands in challenging environments.19 20 In discharging their roles, street-level bureaucrats exercise reasonable discretion to determine how to respond to the challenges in their work context to continue performing their duties. This is what constitutes coping practices.20 Lipsky’s stance concerning coping practices is that the actions of frontline health workers are a selfless response to their challenging working conditions to save public services from total collapse. On the other hand, empirical studies have argued that some of the coping practices of frontline workers are sometimes intended to subvert policy intentions, resist change, control demand21 or simply ignore policies that conflict with their professional obligations, daily routines and challenges.22,24 Other scholars have also argued that the copying practices adopted by frontline implementers of public services impact service delivery by causing confusion, varied implementation and ineffective implementation, leading to poor service outcomes.25 26 This study applied the theory of SLB to explore the challenges in the work context (new directives) faced by frontline health workers and their coping practices. At the analytical level, the copying practices were interpretively analysed in the context of whether they were selfless responses to sustain public services, subversion, resistance to change or controlling demand. Since this was cross-sectional, we did not explore the consequences of the frontline workers’ coping practices, which have been proposed in empirical literature.

Study design

Between June and November 2018, a qualitative study employing an exploratory design to capture the lived experiences, coping mechanisms and adaptation practices of frontline health workers in newly created districts was conducted. This approach was chosen to provide in-depth insights into contextual challenges and strategies that may not be captured through quantitative methods. In-depth interviews were employed to collect data from health workers in eight districts purposively selected from the four geographical regions of Uganda. In-depth interviews were conducted with 24 frontline health workers who had changed locations between districts after district splitting occurred.

Study area

The study was conducted in four randomly selected regions with one parent district selected randomly from each region with a child district that has been split from it between 2005 and 2015, that is, a parent and child district; Northern region—Lira (Parent), Dokolo (Child); Western region—Mbarara (Parent), Ibanda (Child); Eastern region—Pallisa (Parent), Kibuku (Child); Central region—Mukono (Parent), Buikwe (Child). Of these, two parent districts were selected from the best performing districts while the other two were from the poor performing districts. Both categories had split within the mentioned period and due to the ongoing transition process in their healthcare systems and the likelihood that frontline health workers would have experienced unique challenges. Both parent and child districts in real terms were new districts due to the changes that affected both categories following the split. Main economic activity for each of the selected districts turned out to be predominantly agriculture with population sizes ranging between 183 000 and 596 000.

Sampling

The study targeted frontline health workers, which included nurses, midwives, clinical officers and doctors that were affected by the transfers and relocations. Participants were selected through purposive sampling, ensuring a mix of workers with varying levels of experience and roles within the healthcare system. In total, 24 (11 were male and 13 were female) health workers were interviewed until data saturation was reached in the study.

Participant selection and data collection

Information about frontline health workers who switched districts or workstations was obtained from personnel officers and district health offices by social scientists with at least a university degree and qualitative experience from previous studies. Lists were compiled and used as sampling frames for random selection. The researchers were not known to the participants prior to the data collection. Permission was sought from the chief administration officer before conducting any interviews by the principal investigator and a written consent was obtained from each of the respondents. Eight research assistants with at least a first degree were trained to conduct face-to-face interviews using a structured open-ended topic guide in English. The interview guide (in-depth interview (IDI) guide) featured open-ended questions along with adaptable prompts to explore their perceptions and experiences. The guide included questions on challenges and coping practices, allowing quick note-taking while recording interviews. The interview guide was designed by the author (AM) with support from supervisors and pre-tested in two non-participating health facilities with eight frontline health workers, to ensure questions were clear and that interviewers had understood the guide. The interviews were face to face at work places, conducted in English and lasted between 45 mins and 60 mins. No repeat interviews were done given the training and supervision that took place.

Data analysis

We used a deductive thematic analysis to identify and structure challenges faced by health workers in new districts. The process involved six steps27: (1) Familiarisation; transcribing, reading and noting key points. (2) Coding; highlighting text and assigning labels. (3) Generating themes; identifying patterns among codes. (4) Reviewing themes; ensuring accuracy. (5) Defining and naming themes; clarifying meanings. (6) Writing up; two coders (AM and AT) independently analysed transcripts using Atlas.ti V.7, resolving discrepancies by consensus. Themes were mapped onto the SLB theory’s concepts of challenges and coping practices. These were further sub-themed into individual and health system levels and presented with illustrative quotations included. We followed the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist for qualitative research reporting.28

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement was integral to this study on district splitting and the health workforce in Uganda. At the design stage, consultations with frontline health workers, district health officials and community representatives refined the research questions, ensuring attention to both workforce challenges and community experiences in new districts. During tool development, frontline health workers and local leaders were consulted while developing the interview guide for clarity and cultural relevance. Their input improved the accuracy and content of the final guide.

District stakeholders and community representatives were also consulted during a stakeholder’s workshop prior to the pre-testing of the tool. Preliminary findings were later shared with district leaders, health managers and community leaders through feedback workshops, whose perspectives informed interpretation and validated emerging themes. For dissemination, patients and the public have been engaged through policy briefs and stakeholder feedback meetings, ensuring the findings are accessible and meaningful to those most affected.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

In-depth interviews were conducted among 24 frontline health workers, 11 were male and 13 were female (table 1). The health workers’ age ranged from 33 to 51 years. Eight of them were senior nursing officers, two were clinical officers, six were senior nursing officers and four were midwives. Regarding the duration of stay, health workers from Ibanda, Mukono and Pallisa districts had longer durations in service, with a mean of 16 years while Mbarara district had the lowest mean of 12 years. Ibanda district had the highest health workers’ age mean of 41 years while Mbarara district still had the lowest age mean of 35 years.

Frontline health workers’ characteristics

Challenges faced by frontline health workers

Various challenges, including changing work roles, personal and social demands, integration into new teams and inadequacies within the health system, were reported to have influenced the delivery of services. Health workers reported three main challenges, that is, work-related changing roles, personal social demands, integration in the new team and health system inadequacies. These were observed to be relatively similar across old and newly created districts. Those who moved horizontally (between districts) faced similar challenges to those who moved vertically (within districts). There were, however, challenges which were cross-cutting. These are presented in detail under the three subthemes below.

Work-related changing roles

Increased responsibilities and workload

The study findings show that health workers horizontally and vertically switched workstations within and across districts. The health workers who were promoted or assigned new roles in the newly split districts had to adapt quickly to leadership roles, balancing clinical duties with administrative ones. Knowledge gaps in managing health facilities for staff recruited on promotion were reported. More pressure emanated from the fact that they were taking on leadership roles for the first time. Some health workers who switched districts reported they were assigned leadership roles (Health Facility In-Charge) in an acting capacity to cover up for understaffing. They reported increased workloads as they had to discharge clinical and leadership responsibilities. After district splitting occurred, all frontline health workers transferred between or within districts experienced increased responsibilities and workload. Many became in-charges, handling drug requisitions, preparing weekly duty rosters and quarterly reports. These new tasks were initially stressful, but they adapted over time.

When I reported for duty, I went to collect my appointment letter at the DHO’s office and he briefed me about my tasks. The list of responsibilities in my new role was overwhelming but that was it. Man, it was a very stressful moment but somehow, I have overcome it over time though it took me some good months. -- Health worker, Ibanda district.

Short notice for new roles

A few health workers were promoted from health facilities to the district health office (DHO) as part of the DHT, particularly in new districts where they took on roles like Assistant District Health Officer (ADHO) Maternal and Child Health and ADHO Environmental Health. They found that they had completely new roles and responsibilities at the DHO office, requiring them to learn tasks such as supervision and mentoring of health centre colleagues.

I had longed for promotion for a long time and finally it came but at short notice and with a lot of responsibilities. I had not done support supervision and I needed training or at least guidance from my bosses but they were also equally new -- Health worker, Buikwe.

Personal and social demands

Lack of accommodation

Health workers who switched workstations found accommodation a major challenge due to lack of staff housing at health facilities. They also faced increased rent as landlords perceived them as having more money. The search for housing was stressful as new staff often had to pay for temporary accommodation while their families stayed at the previous location, resulting in increased cost of maintaining two residences.

First of all, these up-country trading centres have very few rentals. You find shops in most of the trading centres that is if you are lucky. I waited for almost four months before I could get a fairly habitable house, meanwhile I was residing in a nearby town from where I paid an average of 10,000/= (~$3) daily. -- Heath worker, Dokolo district.

Let me tell you, wherever you go, you will find accommodation is the biggest challenge. The government has not built houses for very many years. People have worked until retirement without ever staying in a government house. That’s why staff arrive late and leave early because they commute from far places, and this can be stressing. --Health worker, Mukono district.

Challenges of finding good schools and arranging transport for their children

When health workers changed workstations and ended up joining their families, they had no accommodation issues but faced challenges of finding good schools and arranging transport for their children. Some had to pick up and drop off their children, which was inconvenient. Those who stayed at their workstations also faced high transportation costs and long commutes.

The reason I moved to this district was because it’s my home district and I have my own house. But I struggled to get a good primary school for my children and its in the neighbouring village. So, I have to transport them on daily basis. I am lucky that I was given an official motorbike for my workplace, but the fuel budget is covered by myself. The task of dropping and picking the kids is so stressful but I have to endure until that time when I get a trusted bodaboda rider to take over that role, --Health worker, Ibanda district.

Hooo …I think this problem of transport is everywhere, because I have been commuting from the nearest trading centre since I was deployed to this facility but the transport fares keeping rising due various issues like increase in fuel prices. --Health worker, Mukono district.

Pressures of family and community obligations

Health workers who moved to their personal homes had free accommodation and food but faced the challenge of relatives and friends expecting them to attend functions and make contributions. This was stressful, especially as they were trying to settle in and often had to borrow money to make ends meet.

Settling really takes time whether you like or not. There are many unforeseen expenditures that we incur on family or community or clan matters. I don’t know why we lost many people in the village during that time when I just come with family. I spent a lot on these funeral functions and ended up borrowing from money lenders. “(Health worker, Buikwe district)

Integration into the new team

Integration in the new team impacted on daily functioning more than formal rules and structures. New health workers had to fit and establish their place within a pre-existing hierarchy and culture to function effectively. Health workers who remained at their workstations had to get used to new workmates who were reporting at their new workstations. New staff observed that health workers formed cliques or ‘camps’ that often led to conflicts and stress. These camps were common in most districts as staff tried to recruit new members, causing trouble for newcomers who sought to avoid offending either group.

You can tell from the way people greet each other and the tension is very visible on their faces. I noticed that there was something between these guys and I convened a meeting to discuss the way forward. During the meeting there was a lot of tension between staff but I managed to make my point. I assured them that I was there to do work and I was willing to work with everyone. --Health worker, Buikwe district.

I went thinking of the type of colleagues that were waiting for me. When I arrived at the health facility, I found three health workers working and I introduced myself as a new member of staff, but they all seemed less concerned and went on with their work until they left. --Health worker, Buikwe.

Health system inadequacies

Understaffing

New staff landed amidst challenges of understaffing and regular stockouts of drugs and therefore started work with challenges that were not easy to handle due to their nature. Health workers who remained at the same workstations experienced similar challenges, and it was noted that these were to be handled by the central government through the DHO.

Some of these challenges like understaffing are still everywhere though in some districts they have reduced. Our district is old enough, but we still have big gaps especially with theatre and laboratory staff --Health worker, Pallisa district. lack of transport for supervision

Promoted staff in the District Health Teams (DHTs) faced challenges like lack of transport for supervision, even with reduced distances to health facilities. Those remaining at the same workstations also saw worsened transport problems after district splitting. Healthcare quality did not improve until equipment and supplies were available, and there was high staff absenteeism due to lack of transport.

Separating districts did not come with the expected improvements. At least not immediate because they never gave us any vehicles or equipment. It was business as usual. --Health worker, Dokolo district.

There are things that improved surprisingly without any additional support or inputs, like OPD improved and somehow coverage also improved. -- Health worker, Kibuku district.

Lack of adequate space

Almost all health workers who switched workstation mentioned how the infrastructure had not improved at all for very many years. The lack of adequate space was experienced everywhere, coupled with dilapidated buildings that are a danger to both the staff and patients. They also mentioned drug stocks and lack of other supplies.

I reported at a health centre III that had very old, dilapidated buildings. The labour room was terrible but luckily enough, a USAID came and renovated it with the maternity ward and even supplied new beds and mattresses. Otherwise, patients were risking their lives in such environment where I could do very little --Health worker, Buikwe district.

Absenteeism at the new workplaces

Newly posted health workers encountered absenteeism at their new workplaces. Many health workers were used to well-planned systematic absenteeism, whereby they did it in turns. Several new in-charges had to fight this practice using very diplomatic means so as to avoid any collision with their subordinates.

It’s not easy to fight a bad habit which you know is a way of survival for some of your colleagues. I really found hard time changing people’s attitude towards planned absenteeism, but with time they complied --Health worker, Ibanda.

Coping practices

In response to a series of challenges in their work contexts, health workers devised several coping practices. For each challenge, a set of practices was put in place to mitigate the challenges. These coping practices included induction and tightening of administrative controls, new staff orientation, leadership workshops, implementing staff appraisals, introducing duty rosters by in-charges, improving personal discipline through enhanced support supervision, team-building activities and staff support strategies from projects and partners, adjustments in personal discipline, basic financial and community planning. Details are explained in the following narrative.

Induction and tightening of administrative controls

Orientation meetings and workshops

New in-charges faced the challenge of increased workload and new leadership tasks like preparing duty rosters, requisitioning for drugs and other supplies and supervising colleagues. The only way they coped with these administrative challenges was through orientation workshops organised by the DHO’s office with the support of partner organisations.

Yaaah … orientation workshops were very useful especially for us the new in-charges because we were taught how to conduct business at the health facilities, and we also got a chance of knowing other in-charges. -- Health worker, Kibuku district.

I really liked the mentorship meetings with DHT members and other district leaders, because they were very useful to us the new staff in leadership positions --Male health worker, Ibanda district.

Interacting with new workmates and adjusting new working plans

In cases where they were reporting as in-charges, they had to adjust new working plans according to their way of planning. Even where the new member of staff was just a workmate, they still had to start understanding his/her ways of working so as to work well as a team.

Somehow, I had to do my work and prevail over the situation because that’s the reason why my superiors deployed me to this health facility. Of course, I had observed how my supervisor used to handle his assignments at my previous workstation and therefore I had some insights on how to handle my workmates. --Health worker, Kibuku district.

Teamwork

The challenge of absenteeism of staff was also addressed using the advice acquired during the mentorship workshops. In-charges were told to make weekly rosters for their staff, and they were encouraged to do effective supervision on a daily basis. This improved attendance of staff and the teamwork.

When we work as a team, everything can move on smoothly. There is no friction between workmates. You can even request your colleagues to stand in for you when you have some other commitments.--Health worker, Pallisa district.

I have seen many advantages of working as a team with good understanding amongst ourselves. Even the work becomes very easy despite other challenges like the lack of drugs and equipment. --Health worker, Dokolo district.

Punctuality

Several new in-charges held staff meetings on their second day to emphasise punctuality. Though it seemed like a warning, it reinforced teamwork.

You know arriving at the health facility on day one and you find only a nurse plus a nursing assistant managing a health centre III. I felt very disappointed, but I put my trust in God and the following day when I reported for work, I found five health workers on duty working. --Health worker, Lira.

Transport support

Health workers who were promoted vertically from health facility to the DHT mentioned that supervision had improved due to the reduced distance from the district headquarters to the health facilities. Many new districts did not have vehicles but were supported by partners through various projects.

In our district we did not receive any car in working condition, we were given those that were in garages. When partners came in and supported us with transport, it was really very timely support. --Health worker, Kibuku district.

Support supervision

With good staff support supervision, health workers and their services have been effectively monitored. The system of monthly assessment of workers and staff appraisals has been reactivated, and it has motivated the staff into being present at work and reduced absenteeism.

Let me tell you, where we have seen consistent supervision, health workers have always made sure they are at work and doing the right things. The problem is that we rarely see any supervisors on the ground probably due to lack of funds or busy schedules – Health worker, Mukono.

Adjustments in personal discipline

Use part of staff salary for housing

All health workers who switched workstations faced a common challenge: lack of staff accommodation at their new locations. Without official accommodation, they had to use part of their salary for housing, unlike those at health facilities who had no such costs and could even grow food to reduce expenses. Many appreciated the lower cost of living in rural areas compared with urban settings, where accommodation could be five times more expensive.

These landlords in the towns or big trading centres think health workers have a lot of money. They ask for higher rates because they know you must be accommodated’. -- Health worker, Ibanda district.

The truth is that accommodation is cheap in rural trading centres and the food fits well in my budget. I have no problem renting near the health facility. --Health worker, Dokolo district.

Family adjustments

The problems of finding good schools for the children and transporting them on a daily basis were a big problem to both those who switched districts and those who switched workstations within districts. Some health workers decided to take the children to boarding schools which helped them concentrate on work, while others negotiated with workmates and agreed on an early time of departure to enable them to pick the children.

We really need each other if we are to manage this world. We agreed to stand in for each other in times like this and it worked well. We all have different challenges but when we work as a team, then we overcome. -- Health worker, Kibuku district.

Our work needs to get done whether we find it stressful or not. In spite of the immediate deadlines, I still get my work done, and done on time. There is no choice. I just do it in stride. I become very alert and try to discipline myself. I skip lunch and try to work very fast. I try to understand what the situation requires and try to meet the requirements. -- Health worker, Buikwe district.

The problem of attending to family or relatives or communities was mentioned by all the health workers that switched workstations, but more common among those who went home. The only way they managed to handle it was by trying as much as possible to attend any meetings or at least delegate a family member and also make any financial contribution where need arose. The common meetings were: wedding meetings, last funeral rites, church fundraisings, etc.

These personal commitments can make work impossible if you do not handle them well. You have to find a way of addressing them so that you can concentrate on your work. -- Health worker, Ibanda district.

Generally, the health workers who switched to new workstations and stayed at their homes mentioned that financial and moral support from family helped a lot when it came to coping with personal challenges.

Basic financial and community planning

Establishment of income generation activities

Almost all health workers who switched workstations faced inadequate funds, using their own money for moving expenses and paying several months of rent, leading many to borrow at high interest rates. Common coping strategies included starting private clinics, working in others’ clinics for extra income, or opening drug shops.

When I arrived in this trading centre, there was no clinic or drug shop. This gave me the idea of starting a clinic/drug shop, which eventually gave me good income to supplement my salary earnings. -- Health worker, Mbarara district.

All health workers who switched work stations mentioned that financial hiccups were everywhere, due to the same low salaries earned. There were several strategies of coping with low earnings, like working in private clinics or drug shops after official working time. Some health workers had retail shops they managed with their spouses, while others supplemented through agriculture, poultry and animal husbandry.

Here in my trading centre I have started the only drug shop and it’s doing well. It’s giving me some extra income during my days off or in the evenings. I am still searching for drug shop assistant --Health worker, Dokolo district.

I am born in this place and I inherited land which I can now use for agriculture and other activities like animal rearing. I will manage it better now that I am at home. -- Health worker, Ibanda district.

However, in some districts where they had projects funded by international partners, health workers earned some allowances for the work they did on these projects. They mentioned HIV/AIDS, malaria and RBF for reproductive health as the most common projects.

I am a midwife in an HIV/AIDS project funded by USAID. I am very happy with my current employment, and I cannot move to another place because I know more projects are coming to the districts --Health worker, Mbarara district.

Shift to own home districts

Health workers who moved to new districts found a reduced workload due to new Health Center (HC) IIIs and HC IIs, which improved access and led to additional staff being recruited. In old districts, the patient load stayed the same. Those who moved to their home districts enjoyed the comfort of family and a better working environment, which also allowed them to work on personal projects.

I am actually very relaxed since I moved to my home district, because I now meet former schoolmates, relatives and friends, and we talk, chat and discuss plans for the future. I really love it here -- Health worker, Ibanda district.

Let me tell you, working while staying at home gives you a chance to plan better for your family and also plan well your retirement. It was always a challenge in my previous district because due to high costs of transport, I only came once in a month -- Health worker, Lira district.

Discussion

This study explored the challenges, adaptation and coping practices of health workers who switched workstations within or between districts. Key challenges included role adjustments, social demands, team integration and health system inadequacies, all affecting service delivery. Role changes, heavy workloads, conflicts and inadequate accommodations impacted both professional and personal lives. Coping strategies included induction programmes, administrative controls and enhanced supervision. Despite persistent issues like understaffing and resource shortages, teamwork and district leadership support helped alleviate some pressures. Improved infrastructure, resource allocation29 and policies to retain health workers, particularly in rural areas, are needed to maintain morale and performance.

Health workers promoted or assigned new roles in newly split districts had to adapt quickly to leadership responsibilities. Challenges such as absenteeism, late arrivals and early departures were common. Weekly meetings helped address these issues, but supervision remained detached.30 Orientation workshops for in-charges improved problem resolution, while weekly rosters managed absenteeism. However, poor conditions, lack of supplies, low salaries and demotivation persisted, reducing productivity.31 Strengthening supervisory systems30 could provide administrative and emotional support, reducing burnout and improving job satisfaction.

Conflicts among health workers affected both new and existing staff. Many remained neutral to avoid taking sides. Orientation workshops provided guidance on conflict management. Properly managed task conflict can improve team performance, depending on team composition.32 33 Addressing poor working relations among new and redeployed staff was crucial for team efficiency.

Heavy workloads were a major challenge across districts, involving multiple ministries: health, local government, public service and finance. Inter-ministerial cooperation is needed to allocate resources and fill vacancies. Some districts hired temporary contract staff with international partner support while awaiting government recruitment. Budget constraints limit universal health worker access, causing unemployment despite high demand.2 Strengthening human resource management at all levels is essential.15

Social challenges included accommodation issues and family/community obligations. Official accommodation was scarce, forcing health workers to rent in trading centres with utilities. Availability of housing is crucial for retention,34 35 while its absence deters retention.36 37 Rural postings had lower rent but often lacked utilities, complicating deployments.38 Finding quality schools for children was another challenge. Official motorbikes assisted some DHT members with transportation. Staff accommodation enhanced safety and reduced costs but also meant constant on-call duty, leading to stress.36 39 Workforce incentives like accommodation require careful planning to avoid unintended consequences.

Integration into new teams required navigating existing hierarchies and cultures. Many faced understaffing and excessive workloads,40 challenges only the central government can address. Despite this, teamwork helped distribute workloads, preventing staff from being overwhelmed. While workload frustrations were common, they did not always lead to job abandonment.41 Health workers seek recognition, better conditions and supervisory support.38 Addressing these challenges requires political commitment, funding and best practices.42 Besides workload and infrastructure issues, contextual factors like postings and living conditions also posed challenges.38 Health workers are expected to provide quality care despite resource limitations, often requiring improvisation and discretion.

Health system inadequacies such as understaffing, poor conditions and resource shortages worsened after district splitting. Infrastructure deteriorated, especially maternity spaces, and stockouts of essential drugs and supplies were frequent. Despite this, patient care continued. Previous studies noted that prescribing unavailable drugs provided minimal relief, and lack of medical equipment was a major concern.41 Some districts secured partnerships for transport, mentorship and resource negotiations.

Support supervision was hindered by inadequate transport for the DHT and in-charges due to unfair asset distribution post-splitting. New districts lacked official transport, making supervision difficult. Studies43 44 highlight that improved supervision, mentorship, supply availability and teamwork support health workers.41 New in-charges faced conflicts, with staff forming cliques to influence newcomers. Orientation and mentorship helped manage conflicts by redistributing tasks and adjusting duty rosters. Task shifting can improve performance, but success depends on team composition.45

Coping strategies included induction programmes and administrative controls. Orientation workshops helped health workers navigate new roles, improving effectiveness.46 Both formal and informal training played a key role in managing responsibilities. Continuous training programmes should be developed to equip staff with skills for emerging healthcare challenges.

Personal discipline was crucial. Many health workers maintained discipline47 to meet deadlines despite professional and personal challenges. This resilience mitigated workplace stress and sustained performance despite systemic constraints.

Financial and community planning was essential for coping. Many health workers took on extra income-generating activities to manage financial stress. Relocation led to increased financial obligations, causing some to borrow money or take additional jobs. Strategies to improve rural health worker distribution require integrated approaches, including increasing production and retention.8 Health workers often concentrate in urban areas,42 leaving rural areas underserved. National policies12 and adequate financing48 can address these disparities.

Policymakers should prioritise resource allocation for new districts to improve infrastructure, especially regarding accommodation, workspace and ensuring timely procurement of medical supplies and equipment. Addressing systemic challenges will improve health worker retention and service delivery in districts affected by administrative changes.

Conclusions

District splitting inadvertently creates challenges for frontline health workers, which makes the performance of their roles difficult. Coping practices adopted at individual and organisational levels conform to Lipsky’s stance of selfless response to sustain public services to prevent them from total collapse. Planners of district splitting should take into consideration the implications for the health workforce, specifically, and the district health system in general. Although a lot of effort was invested to ensure resilience, systemic improvements are essential. Addressing root causes like better resource distribution, expanded training and stronger administrative support will enhance healthcare delivery and reduce dissatisfaction. The government must anticipate workforce challenges and implement strategic plans before district splitting to ensure effective health service provision.
