Outcomes in patients with multidrug- or rifampin-resistant tuberculosis treated with bedaquiline and/or delamanid under the South Korean nationwide approval process
Authors: Hyeontaek Hwang, Jung Eun Shin, Soonryu Seo, Su Jin Hong, Mi Ri Jang, Hyungseok Kang, Jeongha Mok, Yong-Soo Kwon, Doosoo Jeon, Tae Sun Shim, Jae-Joon Yim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn South Korea, the National Tuberculosis Expert Review Committee (NTBERC) has authorized the use of bedaquiline and delamanid in patients with multidrug-resistant or rifampin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) since September 1, 2016. We aimed to evaluate the treatment outcomes in patients who used these drugs under NTBERC approval prior to the introduction of short-course all-oral regimens.MethodsThis nationwide cohort study included patients with pulmonary MDR/RR-TB whose NTBERC-approved treatment included bedaquiline and/or delamanid and was initiated between September 1, 2016, and December 31, 2022. Treatment outcomes were categorized according to 2013 WHO definitions, and the treatment success rate was defined as the proportion of patients in the “cured” and “treatment completed” categories.ResultsDuring the study period, 2,078 patients applied for NTBERC approval, and 1,985 (95.5%) were approved. Among 1,546 patients included patients (median age 54.0 years; 69.0% male), 63.3% had FQ-susceptible TB and 28.5% had FQ-resistant TB; FQ data were unavailable in 8.2%. The median total treatment duration was 19.3 months and was shorter in the FQ-susceptible group than in the FQ-resistant group (19.1 vs. 20.7 months, p < 0.0001). The median duration of bedaquiline and delamanid use was 5.6 months. Patients received a median of 5.0 drugs, and the number of drugs was lower in the FQ-susceptible group than in the FQ-resistant group (5.0 vs. 6.0, p < 0.0001). The overall treatment success rate was 80.0%, with no significant difference between FQ-susceptible and FQ-resistant groups (81.5% vs. 80.3%, p = 0.58). Younger age (<60 years) and higher BMI (≥18.5 kg/m2) were associated with higher odds of treatment success (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 4.19, 95% confidence interval (CI) [3.16, 5.55]; aOR 2.42, 95% CI [1.77, 3.31]). In contrast, chronic renal failure, bilateral lung involvement, and AFB smear positivity at treatment initiation were associated with lower odds of success (aOR 0.56, 95% CI [0.32, 0.98]; aOR 0.60, 95% CI [0.45, 0.79]; aOR 0.74, 95% CI [0.56, 0.99]).ConclusionsUnder South Korea’s nationwide approval process, longer individualized regimens containing bedaquiline and/or delamanid achieved acceptable treatment success rate in patients with MDR/RR-TB, including patients with FQ resistance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Trial registrationRetrospectively registeredSupplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13389-1.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Multidrug-resistant, Diarylquinolines, Nitroimidazoles, Oxazole

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to be a leading cause of death globally. To reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with TB, it is crucial to improve treatment outcomes in patients with multidrug- or rifampin-resistant TB (MDR/RR-TB) [1, 2]. The use of drugs with limited efficacy, prolonged treatment durations, frequent adverse events, and poor patient adherence have traditionally led to unsatisfactory treatment outcomes in such patients [3].

Efforts to enhance treatment outcomes in patients with MDR-TB are ongoing, and there have been significant advances in recent years, including the development and introduction of new anti-TB drugs, such as bedaquiline, delamanid, and pretomanid. Based on the findings of randomized controlled trials and cohort studies conducted with these drugs [4–6], the WHO released guidelines in 2019 recommending 18–20-month treatment regimens that incorporated these drugs for patients with MDR/RR-TB [7]. In 2022, the WHO released revised guidelines that integrated the findings of clinical trials in which shorter all-oral regimens (6–9 months) including newly developed and repurposed anti-TB drugs [8–11], and recommended the use of shorter all-oral regimens over longer regimens for the treatment of MDR-TB [2]. However, the recently published endTB-Q trial demonstrated that shorter regimens failed to show non-inferiority compared with longer regimens in patients with fluoroquinolone (FQ)-resistant MDR-TB [12]. Although the BPaL regimen has shown effectiveness as a shorter option, it is not applicable to all patients with FQ-resistant MDR-TB; therefore, longer individualized regimens continue to be necessary for this population. In line with this, both the WHO and the Korean Tuberculosis Guidelines recommend that when shorter regimens are not feasible, longer regimens incorporating bedaquiline and/or delamanid should be used [2, 13].

In South Korea, the use of bedaquiline, delamanid, and companion drugs has been approved by the National Tuberculosis Expert Review Committee (NTBERC) since September 1, 2016, and shorter regimens became available as of April 1, 2024 [13]. In this study, we aimed to analyze the treatment outcomes in patients with MDR/RR-TB who were treated with longer regimens that included bedaquiline and/or delamanid under the expert committee’s guidance before the introduction of short-course regimens in South Korea.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective nationwide cohort study was conducted in South Korea and included patients with pulmonary MDR/RR-TB in whom treatment with bedaquiline and/or delamanid was initiated between September 1, 2016, and December 31, 2022, following NTBERC approval. Data from 260 patients with MDR/RR-TB who used bedaquiline and/or delamanid with NTBERC approval between September 1, 2016, and February 28, 2018, were included in a previous analysis [14].

Process for obtaining approval for bedaquiline and/or delamanid use

The NTBERC was established on September 1, 2016, by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) to determine patient eligibility for treatment with bedaquiline and/or delamanid. It consists of 12 experts, and three members are selected to review each case and determine whether approval will be granted for the use of bedaquiline and/or delamanid. In addition, they evaluate the appropriateness of the proposed regimen and, when necessary, provide guidance to optimize the treatment regimen.

During the study period, the criteria for the use of bedaquiline and/or delamanid in patients with MDR/RR-TB differed depending on the time period. Before December 1, 2020, the NTBERC approved the use of these drugs only in selected patients with MDR-TB when the following criteria were met: (1) the construction of an adequate treatment regimen was not feasible with existing anti-TB drugs, (2) the inclusion of bedaquiline and/or delamanid enabled the development of an appropriate treatment regimen, and (3) the therapeutic benefits were deemed to outweigh the potential risks.

Beginning December 1, 2020, national policy was revised to recommend the routine inclusion of new drugs in all patients with MDR/RR-TB, regardless of whether a regimen could be constructed with conventional drugs. Under this revised policy, bedaquiline was recommended as the first-line new drug, while delamanid was reserved for patients with medical reasons that precluded the use of bedaquiline. This policy change also expanded eligibility to include patients with RR-TB without confirmed MDR-TB, who had previously been excluded from receiving new drugs.

Approval was not granted when an adequate treatment regimen could not be established, even with the addition of the new drug(s), or absolute contraindications to the use of the new drug(s) were present. The NTBERC evaluated the appropriateness of each treatment regimen with reference to the WHO guidelines released between 2016 and 2022, and the 2017 Korean TB guidelines [2, 7, 13, 15, 16].

Until November 30, 2020, bedaquiline and delamanid were approved for up to 24 weeks, and in most cases, these drugs were used within this timeframe. Prior to the approval of extended use, sequential use of bedaquiline and delamanid—substituting one for the other—was permitted when treatment beyond 24 weeks was required. Since the approval of extended use on December 1, 2020, sequential use has become uncommon. Concurrent use of bedaquiline and delamanid was approved for patients who exhibited TB resistant to fluoroquinolones (FQs) and second-line injectable drugs.

Definitions

MDR-TB was defined as TB caused by a Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex strain resistant to both rifampin and isoniazid. RR-TB was defined as either confirmed resistance to rifampin alone or rifampin resistance detected using the Xpert assay (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) when culture results were unavailable. Drug resistance was considered present when it was detected using either molecular techniques or phenotypic methods during drug susceptibility testing [7].

Longer regimens consistently referred to individualized or standardized multidrug treatment regimens lasting ≥ 18 months and constructed from WHO’s priority drug groups (Groups A, B, C, and previously D), although the specific drugs assigned to each group evolved over time in accordance with updates to WHO guidelines [2, 7, 15, 16]. These regimens were designed to include at least four likely effective second-line agents at treatment initiation, generally incorporating bedaquiline, linezolid, fluoroquinolones, clofazimine, cycloserine, ethambutol, delamanid, pyrazinamide, carbapenems, or amikacin—selected based on drug susceptibility testing and clinical judgment.

Treatment outcomes were classified into six categories using the 2013 WHO definition and reporting framework for TB: cured, treatment completed, treatment failed, died, lost to follow-up, and not evaluated. The treatment success rate was defined as the proportion of patients in the “cured” and “treatment completed” categories [17]. Culture conversion was defined as the occurrence of two consecutive negative culture results, collected at least 28 days apart, within the entire treatment period, in patients with an initially positive baseline culture. The time to culture conversion was the interval between the initiation of MDR/RR-TB treatment and the specimen receipt date of the first negative culture result in patients who achieved culture conversion.

Adherence support

To support medication adherence, a public–private mix approach was implemented, involving trained nurses and local public health centers. Patients diagnosed with MDR/RR-TB underwent a vulnerability assessment, and adherence monitoring was conducted according to the assessed risk level. Patients classified as low risk were monitored one to two times per month, those at moderate risk at least once per week, and those at high risk up to five times per week. Adherence was monitored using various methods, including telephone calls, mobile-based monitoring, directly observed therapy, and video-observed therapy. The monitoring method was primarily selected based on patient preference, while also considering adherence status and overall risk assessment [18].

Data collection and statistical analysis

Data on baseline characteristics were collected, including demographic factors, comorbidities, history of TB treatment, microbiological test results (including drug susceptibility test results), and radiologic findings. Data on the drugs used, duration of treatment, time to culture conversion, and treatment outcomes were also collected. The data used in this study were derived from a policy research project on pre-approval review of new drugs conducted by the KDCA and anonymized before being made available to the researchers.

The patients were divided into two groups based on the FQ resistance status of their disease: the FQ-resistant group and the FQ-susceptible group. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Pearson’s χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare categorical variables. For continuous variables, comparisons between the two groups were performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to assess the time to culture conversion, and differences between groups were analyzed using the log-rank test. Predictors of treatment success were identified from the patients’ demographic characteristics, comorbidities, radiologic findings, acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear test results, treatment modalities, and FQ resistance status.

Univariable logistic regression was conducted to assess the associations between potential predictors and treatment success. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in the univariable analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 14.2 software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

During the study period, 2,078 patients with pulmonary MDR/RR-TB applied to use bedaquiline and/or delamanid. The NTBERC approved 1,985 applications (95.5%). Among the 93 applications that were not approved, 43 patients (46.2%) were deemed treatable with existing regimens. Another 32 patients (34.4%) of RR-TB without confirmed MDR-TB were excluded under the eligibility criteria that applied before December 1, 2020. In addition, twelve applications (12.9%) were not approved due to insufficient documentation, and six (6.4%) due to infeasible or inadequate companion drug regimens.

Of the 1,985 patients approved to use the drugs, 439 were excluded from the analysis. Because some patients met more than one exclusion criterion, the numbers for individual reasons may overlap. In 65 patients, the drugs were not used despite approval, and in 349 patients, drug administration data were incomplete. Treatment outcomes were not available in 53 patients due to revised diagnoses after approval, in 32 patients because treatment had not yet been completed at the time of analysis, and in 93 patients due to missing treatment outcome data. Consequently, data from 1,546 patients were analyzed (Fig. 1). A comparison of baseline characteristics between patients included in the analysis and those excluded due to missing or incomplete data is presented in Additional file 1: Table S1. Excluded patients had a lower BMI and higher proportions of AFB smear positivity and bilateral lung involvement. In contrast, the proportions of prior tuberculosis treatment, malignancy, and fluoroquinolone resistance were lower in the excluded group.

In 979 patients (63.3%), FQ-susceptible TB was confirmed, and in 441 patients (28.5%), FQ-resistant TB was identified; the latter corresponds to pre-extensively drug-resistant TB (pre-XDR-TB). FQ susceptibility data were unavailable for 126 patients (8.2%). Among the 441 patients with FQ-resistant TB, five (1.1%) had extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB). Among these XDR-TB patients, one had bedaquiline resistance only, four had linezolid resistance only, and none had resistance to both agents. The median age of the patients with FQ-susceptible TB was higher than that of those with FQ-resistant TB (55.0 vs. 52.0 years, p = 0.0012). A history of TB treatment (79.6% vs. 68.6%, p < 0.0001), a history of MDR/RR-TB treatment (23.1% vs. 6.7%, p < 0.0001), AFB smear positivity (45.2% vs. 30.5%, p < 0.0001), cavitary lesions (52.0% vs. 40.1%, p < 0.0001), and bilateral involvement (46.8% vs. 36.2%, p < 0.0001) were more common in the patients with FQ-resistant TB. No additional differences in the baseline characteristics between the two groups were observed.

The median treatment duration was 19.3 months (IQR: 16.5–21.5), and patients received a median of 5.0 drugs during treatment. Bedaquiline was administered to 1,236 patients (80.0%) and delamanid to 537 patients (34.7%). Linezolid was used in 1,180 patients (76.3%), and FQs were administered to 1,190 patients (77.0%). Sequential and concurrent use of bedaquiline and delamanid were documented in 173 (11.2%) and 54 (3.5%) patients, respectively. The median time from the initiation of MDR/RR-TB treatment to the addition of bedaquiline and/or delamanid was 22.0 days (IQR: 9.0–76.0) (Table 2).

The FQ-resistant group showed a longer median treatment duration compared to the FQ-susceptible group (20.7 vs. 19.1 months, p < 0.0001). The median number of drugs was also significantly higher in the FQ-resistant group (6.0 vs. 5.0, p < 0.0001). Delamanid (54.0% vs. 28.7%, p < 0.0001) and linezolid (89.3% vs. 69.4%, p < 0.0001) were used more frequently in the FQ-resistant group, whereas bedaquiline usage was comparable between the two groups (81.6% vs. 77.7%, p = 0.095). Bedaquiline was used for a slightly longer duration in the FQ-resistant group than in the FQ-susceptible group (IQR 5.6–6.1 vs. 5.5–5.8 months; p = 0.0008). Similarly, delamanid was used for a slightly longer duration in the FQ-resistant group than in the FQ-susceptible group (IQR 5.5–6.0 vs. 5.4–5.8 months; p = 0.026). Both sequential (24.5% vs. 5.9%, p < 0.0001) and concurrent (11.1% vs. 0.5%, p < 0.0001) use of bedaquiline and delamanid were more common in patients with FQ-resistant TB. Additionally, surgical resection was performed in a larger proportion of patients in the FQ-resistant group than in the FQ-susceptible group (2.7% vs. 0.8%, p = 0.0060). The median time between the start of MDR/RR-TB treatment and the addition of bedaquiline and/or delamanid was also longer in the FQ-resistant group (35.0 vs. 21.0 days, p = 0.0018).

Among the 1,157 patients who had a positive sputum culture at the initiation of treatment with bedaquiline and/or delamanid, 1,127 (97.4%) achieved culture conversion. The median time to culture conversion from the start of treatment with bedaquiline and/or delamanid was 49.0 days (95% confidence interval [CI] [47.0, 54.0]) (Table 3). It was significantly longer in the FQ-resistant group compared to the FQ-susceptible group (65.0 vs. 44.0 days, log-rank p < 0.0001; Fig. 2).

Among the 1,546 patients included in the study, 791 (51.2%) were cured and 446 (28.9%) completed their treatment, resulting in an overall treatment success rate of 80.0%. The success rate did not significantly differ between the FQ-susceptible and FQ-resistant groups (81.5% vs. 80.3%, p = 0.58). Treatment failure occurred in 40 patients (2.6%), and 71 patients (4.6%) were lost to follow-up. A total of 198 patients (12.8%) died: 47 from TB-related causes and 151 from non–TB-related causes. The proportions of patients who experienced treatment failure, who were lost to follow-up, and who died did not significantly differ between the FQ-susceptible and FQ-resistant groups. No patients had a final outcome classified as “not evaluated” (Table 3).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, younger age (<60 years) was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of treatment success (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 4.19, 95% CI [3.16, 5.55]), as was a body mass index (BMI) ≥18.5 kg/m2 (aOR: 2.42, 95% CI [1.77, 3.31]). In contrast, chronic renal failure (aOR: 0.56, 95% CI [0.32, 0.98]), bilateral lung involvement (aOR: 0.60, 95% CI [0.45, 0.79]), and a positive AFB smear test result at the start of treatment (aOR: 0.74, 95% CI [0.56, 0.99]) were associated with lower odds of treatment success. The use of delamanid seemed to be associated with treatment success; however, the relationship was not statistically significant (p = 0.065) (Table 4).

Adverse events were reported in 227 patients (14.7%) during the treatment period, and 54 patients (3.4%) discontinued using either bedaquiline or delamanid due to adverse events. Prolonged Fridericia’s corrected QT (QTcF) intervals (11.8% vs. 7.7%, p = 0.017) and hearing disturbances (0.7% vs. 0%, p = 0.030) were observed more frequently in the FQ-resistant group. Electrocardiogram monitoring was generally performed before treatment initiation, at 2 weeks after treatment initiation, and subsequently every 4 weeks, although the monitoring interval could vary at the discretion of the treating physician. No significant differences in the frequencies of other adverse events were observed between the two groups. Likewise, there was no difference between the groups in the rate of bedaquiline or delamanid discontinuation due to adverse events (Table 5).

Discussion

In this nationwide cohort study conducted in South Korea, NTBERC-approved longer regimens that included bedaquiline and/or delamanid use led to acceptable treatment outcomes among patients with MDR/RR-TB, with an overall treatment success rate of 80.0%. Notably, the treatment success rate did not differ between the FQ-resistant and FQ-susceptible groups. Younger age and a BMI ≥ 18.5 kg/m2 were associated with higher treatment success rates, while chronic renal failure, bilateral lung involvement, and a positive AFB smear test at treatment initiation were significantly related to lower treatment success rates. This study highlights that individualized longer regimens can effectively fill the therapeutic gap in patients for whom shorter regimens are not feasible, offering important insights into how such outcomes can be achieved.

Our results show that in South Korea, the treatment success rate among patients with MDR/RR-TB increased when the nationwide approval process for treatment with bedaquiline and/or delamanid was introduced. According to national registry data, the average treatment success rate among patients with MDR/RR-TB was 65.7% in South Korea between 2011 and 2015 [19].

The treatment success rate observed in our study is comparable to the rates reported in other settings. For example, in the endTB Observational Study, an 80% treatment success rate was observed among patients with MDR/RR-TB who were treated with long all-oral regimens that included bedaquiline or delamanid [20]. Additionally, a prospective observational study conducted in Georgia that involved patients with MDR-TB who were treated with bedaquiline and delamanid reported a treatment success rate of 72% and a culture conversion rate of 90% [21]. It is well known that FQ resistance is associated with poor treatment outcomes in cases of MDR-TB, and this is the reason for its inclusion in the definition of pre-extensively drug-resistant TB [22]. In our study, the FQ-resistant group exhibited higher rates of bilateral lung involvement and AFB smear positivity compared to the FQ-susceptible group, and both characteristics were significantly associated with lower treatment success rates. Nonetheless, the treatment success rates did not differ significantly between the FQ-susceptible and FQ-resistant groups. These results are likely attributable to expert-guided individualized regimens characterized by more frequent sequential and concurrent use, and longer cumulative duration of bedaquiline and/or delamanid use, together with the inclusion of linezolid and surgical resection, which may have offset the adverse impact of FQ resistance.

A detailed analysis of the adverse events that occurred during the treatment period revealed that the FQ-resistant group had significantly higher rates of QTcF interval prolongation and hearing disturbances compared to the FQ-susceptible group. Among the drugs used to treat MDR/RR-TB, bedaquiline, delamanid, FQs, and clofazimine potentially cause QT interval prolongation [2]. In our study, the frequency of bedaquiline use did not differ significantly between the two groups; however, delamanid (54.0% vs. 28.7%, p < 0.0001) and clofazimine (53.1% vs. 25.1%, p < 0.0001) were used more frequently in the FQ-resistant group. Hence, the concurrent use of multiple drugs that prolong the QT interval may have increased the risk of this adverse event [23]. In addition, the extended use of these drugs may also have increased the risk of QT interval prolongation among patients with FQ-resistant MDR/RR-TB. Similarly, the higher frequency of hearing disturbances in the FQ-resistant group may have been due to the more frequent use of aminoglycosides in that group [24].

In this study, younger age (<60 years) and a BMI ≥ 18.5 kg/m2 were significantly associated with higher rates of treatment success. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, and they highlight the need to pay particularly close attention to the treatment of elderly and malnourished patients with MDR/RR-TB [25]. For example, improving the nutritional status of malnourished patients could enhance treatment outcomes in this cohort [26]. Conversely, bilateral lung involvement, AFB smear positivity, and chronic renal failure were significantly associated with lower treatment success, which is also in line with previous reports [27, 28]. Radiologic evidence of bilateral involvement and positive AFB smears suggest a higher burden of MDR-TB and thus indicate a need for early prognostic counseling and close monitoring during treatment. Additionally, in patients with chronic renal failure, impaired drug metabolism may increase the risk of adverse drug reactions, which could negatively impact treatment adherence [29].

The main strength of this study is that it encompassed all patients with MDR/RR-TB treated with bedaquiline and/or delamanid throughout South Korea. In addition, the drug use approval process and clinical data collection were performed in a systematic manner by national authorities. These factors ensured the accuracy of the analysis and minimized selection bias. However, this study also has limitations. First, many patients were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete drug administration records or missing treatment outcome data. Although baseline characteristics differed between included and excluded patients, these individuals were excluded because the available data were incomplete, which may have introduced potential selection bias. Second, adverse events were collected from hospitals during treatment rather than through a standardized system, which may have introduced reporting bias and underreporting. Third, we were unable to evaluate the duration of aminoglycoside use, which may be associated with hearing loss, because a substantial proportion of data on treatment duration was missing.

Conclusions

In conclusion, longer, individualized regimens that incorporated bedaquiline and/or delamanid and were approved via a nationwide process led to an acceptable treatment success rate in patients with MDR/RR-TB, including those with FQ resistance.
