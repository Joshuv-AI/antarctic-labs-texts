Exploring strategies and approaches to improve end-of-life care services for people experiencing homelessness: a systematic review and meta-synthesis of qualitative studies
Authors: Lijie Tian, Lili Guo, Zhongying Jiang, Xiaole Zhu, Mingmin Luo, Zhiguang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a socially disadvantaged group, people experiencing homelessness face numerous difficulties and challenges in accessing end-of-life (EOL) care. Existing strategies often fail to meet their needs, with evident deficiencies in workforce allocation, financial support, and service coverage, as well as a lack of consideration for their unique circumstances.ObjectiveThis study synthesizes published qualitative research to explore the expectations and needs of people experiencing homelessness in EOL care, as well as the perspectives of service providers. Its aim is to identify effective strategies and approaches to improve EOL care services for this population, ultimately promoting more targeted and higher-quality care.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CNKI from inception to November 2024 for primary qualitative studies addressing the EOL needs and service challenges of homeless populations. Using inductive thematic analysis in conjunction with Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, we examined systemic barriers and potential solutions across the microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem, and chronosystems, providing a critical framework for building an inclusive EOL care ecosystem for people experiencing homelessness.ResultsA total of 20 publications representing 16 independent studies were included. The synthesis yielded four major findings: People experiencing homelessness’ EOL Dilemmas and Attitudes, EOL care from the perspective of service providers, Challenges and Barriers to EOL Care, Strategies and Recommendations for Improving EOL Care Furthermore, by applying Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, these findings were integrated into a cross-system, multi-level analytical framework.ConclusionThis study systematically synthesized qualitative evidence on EOL care for people experiencing homelessness, revealing the multifaceted challenges and profound structural barriers they face at the EOL. Our synthesis indicates that the lack of trust and the need for dignity permeate all systemic levels, from the micro to the macro, in EOL care for this population. Reliance on traditional biomedical models alone may not fully address their complex needs. Future efforts should be further advanced through cultural sensitivity, cross-sectoral collaboration, and service evaluation, in order to build an inclusive, respectful, and diverse EOL care system that ensures equitable and dignified care for people experiencing homelessness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02004-4.

Background

Homelessness generally refers to individuals who lack a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence [1–3]. Its definition not only encompasses housing instability [4] but also extends to multiple dimensions such as the absence of social support networks [5], scarcity of economic resources [6], and limited access to healthcare [7]. The convergence of these structural vulnerabilities places people experiencing homelessness at particularly severe risk at the EOL. Among these, the absence of social support systems is especially critical [8]. Long-term disruptions in social relationships result in profound isolation during the terminal phase, leaving individuals without emotional comfort or consistent care and medical support [9]. In the absence of such networks, traditional care models often fail to provide individualized and continuous support. This not only exacerbates their vulnerability at the EOL but also highlights the structural deficiencies of traditional EOL care models in addressing complex social needs.

Recent studies have demonstrated that morbidity and mortality rates among people experiencing homelessness are significantly higher than those of the general population [10]. According to the U.S. government’s annual Point-in-Time (PIT) counts [11], the number of people experiencing homelessness steadily increased from 2015 to 2023. At the EOL, this population frequently faces heightened barriers, highlighting a pressing need for care strategies that are both inclusive and person-centered.

EOL care aims to alleviate symptoms and enhance quality of life, allowing patients to die peacefully and with dignity [12–14]. In this study, “EOL care” refers to supportive services provided during the terminal phase of life, consistent with widely adopted definitions that emphasize symptom relief, psychosocial support, and dignity [15]. Rather than applying a rigid timeframe (e.g., the last three or six months), we adopt a broader definition consistent with the included literature, which encompasses comprehensive supportive care for individuals facing advanced and incurable illnesses.

For people experiencing homelessness, the EOL stage is frequently complicated by chronic illnesses [16], multiple addictions [17], and psychological trauma. Their long-term marginalization often leads to pervasive distrust of the healthcare system, posing substantial barriers to effective communication and therapeutic relationships [18, 19]. Housing instability further hinders continuity of care, medication management, and follow-up [20], while financial hardship restricts access to essential medical services [21]. At the systemic level, inadequate policy support [22], under-resourced healthcare infrastructures [23], and insufficient training of professionals [24] collectively contribute to substantial gaps in the specificity, effectiveness, and coverage of current EOL services. Consequently, many people experiencing homelessness die in isolation and neglect.

Although existing studies have highlighted numerous issues in EOL care for people experiencing homelessness, including the fact that many are unable to access appropriate EOL services due to a range of structural and contextual barriers, exploration of systemic improvement strategies remains limited. Moreover, most research adopts only one single perspective—focusing either on the direct experiences of people experiencing homelessness or on the professional observations of service providers—without integrating both viewpoints to fully expose the structural roots of these challenges. To address this complexity, our study incorporates perspectives from both groups. People experiencing homelessness, as service users, provide direct insights into unmet needs and lived EOL experiences, while service providers illuminate systemic barriers within the care infrastructure. Integrating these perspectives allows for a more comprehensive analysis of the underlying mechanisms driving service gaps and informs the development of effective intervention strategies.

To further deconstruct the multilayered challenges of EOL care among homeless populations, this study adopts Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory as its core analytical framework [25]. The theory situates individual development within a nested model composed of the microsystem (direct interactional environment), mesosystem (connections among microsystems), exosystem (indirectly influential social structures), macrosystem (cultural values and policies), and chronosystem (life-course and historical changes). Guided by this approach, we systematically examine the barriers to EOL care for people experiencing homelessness and identify potential intervention pathways across multiple ecological levels.

Building on the ecological systems framework, we further adopt a health equity implementation lens [26, 27]. This perspective foregrounds how structural determinants—such as policy design and resource allocation—shape the equitable implementation of service models among structurally vulnerable populations. Integrating this lens with ecological systems theory enables a comprehensive interrogation of equity barriers spanning micro-level interactions and macro-level policy domains, and provides critical theoretical grounding for policy solutions to improve EOL care for people experiencing homelessness.

Aims

This study synthesizes published qualitative research to explore the expectations and needs of people experiencing homelessness in EOL care, as well as the perspectives of service providers. Its aim is to identify effective strategies and approaches to improve EOL care services for this population, ultimately promoting more targeted and higher-quality care.

Methods

Design

This study is a systematic review of qualitative research, employing a meta-synthesis approach to integrate the findings of the included studies. During the writing process, we strictly adhered to the methodological framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for qualitative evidence synthesis [28]. The reporting of this study followed the ENTREQ (Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research) statement [29] to ensure completeness and clarity across all stages, from study selection and data extraction to result synthesis. The completed ENTREQ checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 1. The study was registered on the PROSPERO platform (registration number: CRD42024612709).

Search strategy

Guided by the SPIDER framework—Sample (people experiencing homelessness), Phenomenon of Interest (EOL care), Design (interviews/focus groups/qualitative studies), Evaluation (quality of care), Research type (qualitative)—we systematically searched PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, EMBASE, CNKI, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to November 2024. Both MeSH terms and free-text terms were used to search Chinese and English articles, and the results were imported into EndNote for de-duplication. In addition to database searching, we conducted supplementary manual searches to ensure the completeness of the evidence base. We screened the reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews to identify additional eligible articles. Handling multiple reports from the same study

In cases where multiple publications originated from the same primary research project, we followed the methodological guidance for qualitative systematic reviews to ensure data integrity and avoid double counting. Reports were carefully examined for overlapping data, research aims, and participant samples. When multiple papers referred to the same underlying study, they were treated as a single study unit. In such cases, data were extracted from all relevant reports, with preference given to the most comprehensive or conceptually rich source.

Quality and risk of bias

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research [28]. Two reviewers independently evaluated each study, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

To provide an overall summary of study quality, an A–B–C grading system was applied based on the proportion of JBI criteria met: studies meeting ≥ 80% of items were classified as Grade A, those meeting 60–79% as Grade B, and those meeting < 60% as Grade C. This grading approach was used solely to describe the distribution of methodological quality and to inform interpretation of the robustness of the evidence base, rather than as a criterion for study exclusion [30]. All studies were retained for the final synthesis.

Data extraction and synthesis

The meta-synthesis followed the JBI guidance for qualitative evidence synthesis and the ENTREQ framework. Two reviewers (TLJ and JZY) independently conducted line-by-line coding of the extracted findings. Codes were then compared, merged, and organized into preliminary categories. Through an iterative process of constant comparison, categories were clustered into higher-order themes. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer (GLL).

We used a “Best-Fit” Framework approach to synthesize the data [31]. Through systematic searching and appraisal, we identified Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory as the “best-fit” framework most aligned with the phenomenon under our review, constructing an a priori analytical structure comprising five levels: micro-system, meso-system, exo-system, macro-system, and chrono-system. The specific steps included: (1) two researchers independently coding and mapping the text into the a priori framework; (2) applying open coding and inductive categorization for data that could not be incorporated into the framework, thereby generating new categories; (3) iteratively revising and expanding the framework based on these new categories; and (4) reaching consensus within the research team to finalize the thematic structure and hierarchical logic.

Results

This Results section presents the study selection process, characteristics and quality appraisal of included studies, followed by the thematic synthesis and ecological theory integration of key findings.

Search outcome

The initial systematic search across multiple databases identified a total of 1309 records, including PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CNKI. After removing duplicates, 1031 records were screened by title and abstract, with 964 subsequently excluded. 67 full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility, of which 20 articles met the inclusion criteria, representing 16 independent studies, and were subjected to further quality appraisal. Study characteristics

The 20 included articles represented 16 independent studies published between 2007 and 2024, comprising a total of 892 participants. Of these, 226 were individuals currently or previously experiencing homelessness. 528 were formal service providers, and 138 were informal caregivers, including family members or proxy respondents.

By country, the distribution of studies was as follows: Canada (n = 9), the United Kingdom (n = 6), the United States (n = 3), the Netherlands (n = 1), and Sweden (n = 1). All studies adopted qualitative methodologies, with semi-structured interviews being the most frequently used data collection method. Additionally, several studies employed complementary approaches, including focus groups (n = 5), observations (n = 3), and questionnaires (n = 1). Participants across studies included people experiencing homelessness, informal caregivers (proxies or family members), formal caregivers (shelter staff, outreach workers, health and social care professionals, community service providers, and academic researchers). People experiencing homelessness’ EOL dilemmas and attitudes

The needs of people experiencing homelessness at the EOL extend beyond a single dimension. Using the “total pain” framework [52], the synthesized findings were organized across four domains: physical, psychological, social, and spiritual (Table 5).

Physical dimension

People experiencing homelessness at the EOL face multidimensional dilemmas and complex needs. At the physical level, they long for effective pain management:

“About passing away? Sometimes if I am having a shitty day, if I’m having a bad day ya know, it makes me think if I have to struggle like this so bad and be in so much pain what is the point of still carrying on.” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 38].

“About passing away? Sometimes if I am having a shitty day, if I’m having a bad day ya know, it makes me think if I have to struggle like this so bad and be in so much pain what is the point of still carrying on.” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 38].

At the same time, some expressed concern about overly aggressive medical interventions that might prolong suffering at the expense of dignity:

“In ’73, I was actually declared brain dead…I regained consciousness…my only real fear about death is that the doctor tried too aggressively to keep me alive.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

“In ’73, I was actually declared brain dead…I regained consciousness…my only real fear about death is that the doctor tried too aggressively to keep me alive.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

Participants further reported living with multiple comorbid conditions and ongoing substance abuse, which complicated symptom management at the EOL.

Psychological dimension

Psychological distress among people experiencing homelessness at EOL was shaped by cumulative trauma and profound mistrust toward healthcare services. Some participants described strong resistance to seeking EOL care:

“He was very resistant to going to the hospital. If he could avoid that, he would at all costs.” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 42].

“He was very resistant to going to the hospital. If he could avoid that, he would at all costs.” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 42].

“Trying to facilitate him [care recipient] moving into a fairly stable structure [housing facility], but his perception of things would be “no, you’re trying to control me” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 39].

“Trying to facilitate him [care recipient] moving into a fairly stable structure [housing facility], but his perception of things would be “no, you’re trying to control me” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 39].

Among people experiencing homelessness, perceptions of death at the EOL were strongly shaped by cumulative traumatic experiences, including repeated exposure to deaths and bereavement:

“That hurt an awful lot. It was hard to regroup because it wasn’t just her. In a 12 months period, 13 of us were dead.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 33].

“That hurt an awful lot. It was hard to regroup because it wasn’t just her. In a 12 months period, 13 of us were dead.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 33].

“I’ve had a lot of tragedy. My girlfriend died in my arms with my baby. She was four months pregnant at the time…and she comes back in my dreams.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 33].

“I’ve had a lot of tragedy. My girlfriend died in my arms with my baby. She was four months pregnant at the time…and she comes back in my dreams.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 33].

Frequent exposure to the deaths of friends or loved ones further intensified psychological distress and anxiety among this population at the EOL.

Social dimension

At the EOL, social isolation shaped both fears of dying alone and a strong desire for companionship among people experiencing homelessness. Many People experiencing homelessness expressed deep concern about dying without dignity or being forgotten after death:

“I don’t know if the city will just take me to the furnace down there and burn me.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

“I don’t know if the city will just take me to the furnace down there and burn me.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

“…they’ll throw you in a pauper’s grave someplace and nobody’s going to mourn you.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

“…they’ll throw you in a pauper’s grave someplace and nobody’s going to mourn you.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

At the same time, participants consistently articulated a longing for companionship and for dying in a familiar, home-like environment:

“A bad death would be passing away by myself and not having my family with me.” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 38].

“A bad death would be passing away by myself and not having my family with me.” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 38].

Some further expressed the wish to avoid impersonal institutional settings and to die “at home” rather than in hospitals or unfamiliar facilities.

Spiritual dimension

Spiritual needs at the EOL centered on dignity, identity, and the desire to be recognized as a person rather than reduced to stereotypes. Participants described experiences of shame, trauma, and mistrust that shaped their ability to engage with care:

“There is a lot of shame. My experience has been that many of those I see in the shelter carry a lot of shame. Low self-esteem, horrific histories of trauma and abuse, mistrust of caregivers. They’ve lived very independent lives. At the end of life, when their needs increase, it’s distressing to them because they need to trust when they’ve never learned to trust. They bring with them experiences that are negative from healthcare providers. It’s a challenge.” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 35].

“There is a lot of shame. My experience has been that many of those I see in the shelter carry a lot of shame. Low self-esteem, horrific histories of trauma and abuse, mistrust of caregivers. They’ve lived very independent lives. At the end of life, when their needs increase, it’s distressing to them because they need to trust when they’ve never learned to trust. They bring with them experiences that are negative from healthcare providers. It’s a challenge.” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 35].

Long-standing experiences of exclusion contributed to a perception that their lives were undervalued:

“We are homeless. They say, ‘well this guy’s homeless…You ain’t got to worry about it.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

“We are homeless. They say, ‘well this guy’s homeless…You ain’t got to worry about it.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

At the EOL, people experiencing homelessness expressed a strong wish to be treated with respect and seen beyond stigmatizing labels:

“deal with us not as some sleaze bag out for trouble, but we are just homeless.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

“deal with us not as some sleaze bag out for trouble, but we are just homeless.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

EOL care from the perspective of service providers

To advance strategies for improving EOL care for homeless populations, it is essential to examine both the current state of service provision and the roles played by providers from their own perspectives.

Current status of EOL service provision

Analysis of the included studies identified several key barriers to effective EOL service provision, including financial constraints, shortages of staff and beds, communication difficulties, and limited coordination of care.

Financial barriers were frequently reported as preventing access to institutional EOL services:

“We kind of rightly or wrongly think palliative care and hospices are for the middle class. We never think about the poor. We are assuming the poor will automatically get in but because there is often a cost component, sometimes the homeless are left to die on the streets.” [Emergency Shelter Director, Canada; 49].

“We kind of rightly or wrongly think palliative care and hospices are for the middle class. We never think about the poor. We are assuming the poor will automatically get in but because there is often a cost component, sometimes the homeless are left to die on the streets.” [Emergency Shelter Director, Canada; 49].

Shortages of staff and bed capacity further constrained the quality of care delivery:

“At least three times a shift we check she’s okay. It’s hard … particularly on weekends and nights when we only have two staff … it’s a big hostel [60 residents] … you really can only do so much … this isn’t an appropriate environment, but it’s the best we have.” [Hostel staff, UK; 36].

“At least three times a shift we check she’s okay. It’s hard … particularly on weekends and nights when we only have two staff … it’s a big hostel [60 residents] … you really can only do so much … this isn’t an appropriate environment, but it’s the best we have.” [Hostel staff, UK; 36].

Communication barriers between providers and people experiencing homelessness were also widely described:

“oncologist who… talked very medical lingo and left [Lewis], like glazed. I was like, ‘He’s not understanding a word that you’re saying right now.’ It was the least accessible language possible!” [Healthcare worker, Canada; 42].

“oncologist who… talked very medical lingo and left [Lewis], like glazed. I was like, ‘He’s not understanding a word that you’re saying right now.’ It was the least accessible language possible!” [Healthcare worker, Canada; 42].

Roles of service providers

Across the included studies, service providers were described as fulfilling multiple roles in the delivery of EOL care for people experiencing homelessness, including direct caregiving, emotional support, care coordination, and advocacy.

As direct caregivers, providers managed complex physical and mental health needs under challenging care conditions:

“‘[I was] supporting a patient with complex physical and mental health needs, due to end stage renal failure other physical co- morbidities, drug use and living in a hostel environment’” [Inclusion health nurse, UK; 41].

“‘[I was] supporting a patient with complex physical and mental health needs, due to end stage renal failure other physical co- morbidities, drug use and living in a hostel environment’” [Inclusion health nurse, UK; 41].

Beyond medical care, providers frequently described serving as primary sources of emotional support and companionship:

“You become family for people… almost no one I work with has contact with their biological families… I’m the person in their life who’s only there for them.” [Healthcare provider, Canada; 45].

“You become family for people… almost no one I work with has contact with their biological families… I’m the person in their life who’s only there for them.” [Healthcare provider, Canada; 45].

Challenges and barriers to EOL care

To identify strategies for improving EOL care for people experiencing homelessness, it is essential to first understand the challenges and barriers that hinder its delivery.

Across the included studies, barriers to EOL care for people experiencing homelessness were reported at both individual and societal levels, particularly involving complex health needs, psychological distress and mistrust, stigma, and limited service accessibility.

Individual-level challenges

People experiencing homelessness were frequently described as living with multiple chronic illnesses, mental health conditions, and ongoing substance abuse, which complicated symptom management and limited access to conventional EOL services:

“The clients are still on drugs. They go out, pick up some crack cocaine and they’re using it. In main stream settings, you’re not going to get that. That’s not going to happen. Traditional hospices are very rigid with their rules. There’s no flexibility around behaviours. If someone gets angry or says something wrong, they’re asked to leave.” [Nurse,Canada; 35].

“The clients are still on drugs. They go out, pick up some crack cocaine and they’re using it. In main stream settings, you’re not going to get that. That’s not going to happen. Traditional hospices are very rigid with their rules. There’s no flexibility around behaviours. If someone gets angry or says something wrong, they’re asked to leave.” [Nurse,Canada; 35].

Prolonged experiences of homelessness were also associated with significant psychological distress and mistrust toward healthcare providers:

“We’re not even considered third-class citizens.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 33].

“We’re not even considered third-class citizens.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 33].

“All of a sudden, the medicine they were giving him—we figured it was medicine designed to take him out like that. He just went to sleep and didn’t wake up. He died. They don’t care.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 33].

“All of a sudden, the medicine they were giving him—we figured it was medicine designed to take him out like that. He just went to sleep and didn’t wake up. He died. They don’t care.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 33].

Societal-level challenges

At the societal level, stigma toward homelessness was widely reported as a barrier to equitable EOL care. Participants and providers described judgmental attitudes that sometimes prevented individuals from being recognized and treated as persons with unique needs:

“They meet attitudes and judgment. Healthcare professionals may not see them as individuals because they just see a poor person or someone who has a drinking problem or has been using drugs.” [Street Nurse, Canada; 35].

“They meet attitudes and judgment. Healthcare professionals may not see them as individuals because they just see a poor person or someone who has a drinking problem or has been using drugs.” [Street Nurse, Canada; 35].

Substance abuse and mental illness were also reported to further restrict access to appropriate care, as stereotyping sometimes replaced individualized clinical assessment:

“They don’t give you proper medical care because they know you are homeless.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

“They don’t give you proper medical care because they know you are homeless.” [People experiencing homelessness, USA; 32].

In addition, several studies described a lack of specialized training related to homelessness and EOL care among providers, which limited their ability to deliver tailored support:

“You have people that are not trained in palliative care. As a shelter environment, staff come with backgrounds but certainly not backgrounds that reflect this particular need.” [Physician, Canada; 35].

“You have people that are not trained in palliative care. As a shelter environment, staff come with backgrounds but certainly not backgrounds that reflect this particular need.” [Physician, Canada; 35].

Strategies and recommendations for improving EOL care

Across the 16 included studies, a range of strategies was proposed to improve EOL care for people experiencing homelessness. Recommendations clustered into four key domains: strengthening social support systems and policy safeguards; enhancing multidisciplinary collaboration and workforce training; building trust and improving care environments; and delivering personalized and comprehensive medical services.

Strengthening social support systems and policy safeguards

Several studies highlighted the need for stronger policy-level support and improved system capacity, particularly in response to ongoing resource limitations and discontinuities in discharge planning:

“There is no discharge planning for this population… They are pushed out of hospitals to make room for beds… people are dropped off at shelters by ambulance… there doesn’t seem to be a lot of continuity.” [Shelter director, Canada; 49].

“There is no discharge planning for this population… They are pushed out of hospitals to make room for beds… people are dropped off at shelters by ambulance… there doesn’t seem to be a lot of continuity.” [Shelter director, Canada; 49].

Studies commonly recommended increased investment in homelessness-specific EOL services and the development of dedicated models of care, such as socially oriented EOL care programs and designated “EOL shelters” [46]. In addition, enhanced legal and policy protections for the rights of people experiencing homelessness at the EOL were consistently emphasized.

“Referral to and support from specialist palliative care services for individual patients and service providers across the city. Co-ordination of personalised patient care and improved access to shared records to ensure continuity of care.” [Hospice nurse consultant, UK; 41].

“Referral to and support from specialist palliative care services for individual patients and service providers across the city. Co-ordination of personalised patient care and improved access to shared records to ensure continuity of care.” [Hospice nurse consultant, UK; 41].

Multidisciplinary collaboration and training

Across the included studies, multidisciplinary collaboration and specialized training were repeatedly described as essential components of EOL care for people experiencing homelessness.

Several studies reported that effective teams should include a mix of clinical and social care expertise—physicians, nurses, psychologists, social workers, and volunteers—to better identify EOL patients and arrange appropriate care settings:

“Building and maintaining links with local homeless GP practice and pall care team and have recently extended those to link in with hepatology specialist nurses to try to improve pathways” [Inclusion health nurse, UK; 41].

“Building and maintaining links with local homeless GP practice and pall care team and have recently extended those to link in with hepatology specialist nurses to try to improve pathways” [Inclusion health nurse, UK; 41].

Negative stereotypes and insufficient understanding were reported as key barriers to quality care:

“For some people, it is addictions or mental illness that prevents them from getting the best care. It’s the attitude of their health care provider not being particularly welcoming or understanding of their situation…I think they’re certainly stereotyped in a negative way and I think that people are kind of inclined to say “Oh the homeless, that homeless guy” and it just conjures up a whole set of connotations about how we expect them to look, how we expect him to act and how we can treat them.” [Physician, Canada; 49].

“For some people, it is addictions or mental illness that prevents them from getting the best care. It’s the attitude of their health care provider not being particularly welcoming or understanding of their situation…I think they’re certainly stereotyped in a negative way and I think that people are kind of inclined to say “Oh the homeless, that homeless guy” and it just conjures up a whole set of connotations about how we expect them to look, how we expect him to act and how we can treat them.” [Physician, Canada; 49].

Accordingly, several studies highlighted the need to equip providers with specialized knowledge and attitudes to address addiction, mental illness, and related stigma through targeted training programs covering the characteristics of homelessness, communication strategies, substance use management, and ethical aspects of EOL care, thereby improving provider competence and responsiveness.

Building trust and improving care environments

Across the included studies [32, 33, 36, 40–42], people experiencing homelessness were widely reported to harbor deep mistrust toward healthcare institutions, largely shaped by prior negative encounters and experiences of discrimination:“He was absolutely terrified of hospitals. He’d say, ‘If I go into hospital, I’m coming out in a box.’” [Day centre user, UK; 36]

“He was absolutely terrified of hospitals. He’d say, ‘If I go into hospital, I’m coming out in a box.’” [Day centre user, UK; 36]

“There was a lot of distrust in the hospital… He was very resistant to going to the hospital. If he could avoid that, he would at all costs.” [Support worker, Canada; 42].

“There was a lot of distrust in the hospital… He was very resistant to going to the hospital. If he could avoid that, he would at all costs.” [Support worker, Canada; 42].

The design and accessibility of care environments were also identified as influential. Study [42] emphasized that care settings need to be welcoming and easily accessible, with minimal administrative or geographic barriers:

“They built that new hospital way out in the boonies and it costs you 60 [taxi fare] to get in there and people in my stage of life and situation don’t have that kind of money… Why didn’t they build the fuckin’ hospital in town where it’s more accessible, that people could either walk or take the bus?” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 42].

“They built that new hospital way out in the boonies and it costs you 60 [taxi fare] to get in there and people in my stage of life and situation don’t have that kind of money… Why didn’t they build the fuckin’ hospital in town where it’s more accessible, that people could either walk or take the bus?” [People experiencing homelessness, Canada; 42].

Providing personalized and comprehensive care

Across the included studies, considerable heterogeneity was reported in people experiencing homelessness regarding attitudes toward medical intervention, perceptions of death, and preferences for EOL settings. Some people experiencing homelessness expressed a strong desire to avoid medical interventions altogether:

“He was very content… we allowed him to have his wish not to go into hospital… he wanted to carry on drinking… we respected his wish… it was what he wanted.” [Outreach worker, UK; 36].

“He was very content… we allowed him to have his wish not to go into hospital… he wanted to carry on drinking… we respected his wish… it was what he wanted.” [Outreach worker, UK; 36].

In contrast, others emphasized the importance of symptom relief, dignified care, and dying peacefully in familiar surroundings:

“The way I’m being sick now, I find bad enough, if it would get worse… rather not. Are you afraid of something? To suffer from pain and to go downhill. Actually, to become even more dependent on others.” [Patient with substance use disorder and multiple conditions, Netherlands; 48].

“The way I’m being sick now, I find bad enough, if it would get worse… rather not. Are you afraid of something? To suffer from pain and to go downhill. Actually, to become even more dependent on others.” [Patient with substance use disorder and multiple conditions, Netherlands; 48].

Studies consistently recommended that EOL care for people experiencing homelessness be individualized, person-centered, and flexible in order to accommodate diverse values, preferences, and care needs across settings.

This framework facilitated examination of how barriers to EOL care emerge and interact across ecological levels.

Further analysis of these dynamic inter-system relationships identified two cross-cutting core themes that permeate all ecological levels—dignity needs and lack of trust. Discussion

To our knowledge, previous research on EOL care for people experiencing homelessness has largely focused on care experiences and the specific challenges encountered during service access—such as fragmented care pathways, unstable care environments, mistrust, and structural barriers at institutional and policy levels. However, these findings have not yet been integrated into a coherent explanatory framework. This review is the first to apply Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory to systematically synthesize qualitative evidence, examining EOL inequities from the perspectives of both people experiencing homelessness and service providers, and exploring how interventions might be designed in response. While prior studies laid the conceptual foundation for our framework, our findings further expand the field.

Multiple studies [32–51] indicate that current EOL care for people experiencing homelessness is predominantly characterized by reactive, crisis-driven responses, with limited continuity and coordination, rather than a fully systematized or planned model of care. Across included studies, a similar trajectory of EOL service use emerged: EOL needs were often identified late; referrals to hospice or community-based EOL care teams were delayed or absent; after hospital discharge, individuals were frequently sent directly to shelters or returned to street settings, with little safety, continuity, or follow-up support. Strict EOL admission criteria and constrained care capacity within medical systems—combined with social stigma surrounding substance abuse and mental illness—resulted in care interactions marked by mistrust and ineffective communication. Consequently, many people experiencing homelessness spent their final days in environments lacking comfort, continuity, or dignity, relying primarily on overstretched shelter staff or outreach workers for basic support, without access to coordinated multidisciplinary EOL care.

Although the included studies were conducted in diverse high-income sociocultural contexts such as Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States, the core EOL challenges confronting people experiencing homelessness—dignity loss, fragile trust, and inequitable access to services and resources—were strikingly consistent across settings. This convergence suggests that EOL inequities faced by homeless populations are not isolated problems specific to a single region or institution, but rather reflect pervasive structural conditions within broader social systems. Within this context, Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory offers a powerful explanatory lens for unpacking the multi-level social determinants shaping EOL experiences and for informing the development of more inclusive models of care.

The ecological systems framework enables a system-level understanding of these challenges: how stigma at the macrosystem level permeates policy priorities and affects institutional safeguards; how imbalanced funding and service allocation at the exosystem level shape organizational capacity and the scope of care; how gaps in interagency coordination within the mesosystem lead to service discontinuity; and how care environments, communication practices, and interpersonal dynamics at the microsystem level directly influence EOL experiences. Meanwhile, the chronosystem highlights that trauma, displacement, and institutional exclusion experienced by people without stable housing are not discrete events but cumulative forces unfolding across the life course. Viewed together, the ecological model links these seemingly disparate barriers into a multilayered structural logic, demonstrating that failures in EOL care are not merely incidental lapses but frequently represent the concrete manifestations of deeper structural conditions within care settings.

When these multi-level structural forces converge at the end of life, they do not appear abstractly but are experienced in specific, repeated, and deeply personal ways—most notably through the erosion of dignity and the weakening of trust. In noisy wards, during repeated assessments across agencies, and in moments of being excluded from services, individuals repeatedly experience unequal treatment. Accumulated over years of trauma and social marginalization, these experiences solidify into profound systemic mistrust. Through the ecological lens, these events can be recognized as forming a “dignity–trust erosion pathway” that cuts across all ecological levels and continuously shapes the EOL experiences of people experiencing homelessness.

Based on ecological systems theory, we argue that improving EOL care for people experiencing homelessness cannot be achieved through isolated interventions at a single level, but rather requires reorienting care principles across the entire system. First, any effective model must place the restoration of dignity and trust at its core—whether for formal providers, informal caregivers, or policymakers—ensuring that these values are reflected in interpersonal interactions, care processes, and institutional design. Second, care systems should prioritize continuity and predictability, minimizing systemic disruptions, strengthening service linkages, and improving information sharing so that individuals do not repeatedly “fall through the cracks.” Third, ecological theory underscores the need to address both immediate clinical needs and long-term structural determinants, including housing security, resource allocation, and social support [53]. Finally, interventions must acknowledge the cumulative trauma within homeless life trajectories and promote trauma-informed, stigma-reducing, and relationally reparative practices across policy, organizational, and clinical levels [54]. Only when the care system simultaneously advances dignity, trust, continuity, and structural equity across multiple levels can meaningful improvements in EOL care for people experiencing homelessness be achieved [55].

Future research should assess the feasibility and adaptability of this ecological framework across different sociocultural contexts, particularly in resource-limited settings where housing and healthcare shortages are more pronounced. In multicultural societies, further exploration is needed to understand how cultural traditions and religious beliefs shape expectations and preferences for EOL care. In practice, sustained efforts are required to strengthen cross-sector collaborative mechanisms and build integrated service networks centered on the needs of homeless populations. Additionally, rigorous evaluation and monitoring mechanisms are needed to assess the effectiveness of multidisciplinary interventions, providing evidence to inform policy development and service design. Through these efforts, it may be possible to gradually build a more inclusive, coordinated, and dignity-oriented EOL care system—one that enables people experiencing homelessness to spend their final stage of life with respect and humanity.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively examine the multidimensional challenges faced by people experiencing homelessness at the EOL through the lens of Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, while also identifying strategies for improvement. While this approach offers notable strengths, it also carries certain limitations.

First, the vast majority of studies were conducted in five high-income countries (Canada, the UK, the US, the Netherlands, and Sweden), which are located in the Global North and feature relatively well-developed healthcare systems. This geographical and cultural concentration may limit the applicability and generalizability of our findings to low- and middle-income countries. Nevertheless, despite the existence of more advanced EOL care infrastructures in these high-income settings, our review still uncovered persistent barriers to EOL care access among the homeless population. This paradox underscores that structural exclusion and inequities remain deeply entrenched even in resource-rich environments. Future research should incorporate evidence from more diverse geographic and socioeconomic contexts to further validate and expand these conclusions.

Second, the review is constrained by the risk of selective representation inherent in qualitative evidence. Although we sought to preserve authentic voices by directly quoting participants’ narratives, most of the included studies lacked reflexivity regarding researchers’ positionality. For example, when shelter staff simultaneously served as researchers, “satisfaction with services” may have been presented from an institutional rather than a participant perspective. More critically, 78% of the included studies did not disclose the power dynamics between researchers and participants, reducing transparency and potentially obscuring structural oppression—such as framing “treatment refusal” as “non-compliance” rather than recognizing health policy exclusion as a root cause. To mitigate risks of “academic exploitation,” future research should consider mechanisms such as establishing advisory boards of homeless persons and engaging in co-authorship.

Third, although a comprehensive search strategy was applied, “palliative care” was not included as an independent search term. Instead, related keywords such as “hospice care,” “terminal care,” “end-of-life,” “dying,” and “death” were used to capture relevant literature. While this approach identified a substantial body of qualitative evidence, it is possible that some eligible studies indexed solely under “palliative care” terminology may not have been retrieved. Future reviews may benefit from expanding search terms to further enhance coverage.

Finally, an important limitation of this review relates to the substantial heterogeneity within the homeless population itself. Factors such as age, gender, substance abuse, and the presence or absence of social support networks may profoundly shape EOL experiences and care needs. However, due to the limitations of the primary qualitative studies included, this review was unable to systematically examine how these factors influence the diverse experiences of different subgroups. This limitation may risk oversimplification and obscure intra-group diversity. Caution is therefore warranted in interpreting and applying our findings. Future research should focus on specific subgroups to illuminate their differentiated needs and inform the development of more targeted and equitable EOL care strategies.

Conclusion

This meta-synthesis applies Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory to analyze, across the microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem, and chronosystem, the difficulties, barriers, and structural inequities encountered by people experiencing homelessness in both the provision of and access to EOL care, and to identify corresponding strategies and intervention pathways. Our findings emphasize the need to advance multi-level, cross-sector, and needs-oriented improvements across health service systems, social support networks, and policy environments, in order to promote more equitable, continuous, and person-centered EOL care for this population.
