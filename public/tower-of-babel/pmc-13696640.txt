Machine learning for early detection and mood shift prediction in bipolar disorder: a systematic review
Authors: Shihshuan Fang, Shenghan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBipolar Disorder (BD) is a serious mental illness characterized by recurrent episodes of mania and depression. Early detection and the prediction of shifts between mood states are critical for effective management and suicide prevention. Machine learning models analyzing data from clinical records, neuroimaging, and passive sensing (e.g., smartphones) are emerging as promising tools for this purpose.ObjectiveThis systematic review evaluates the use of machine learning for the early detection of BD and the prediction of mood shifts, synthesizing evidence on different data modalities, algorithms, and predictive performance.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, PsycINFO, and IEEE Xplore was conducted for studies published up to January 2026. We included original research articles that applied machine learning to data from individuals with BD for diagnosis/classification or prediction of mood episodes.ResultsA total of 37 studies met the inclusion criteria. For early detection (BD vs. HC or MDD), neuroimaging-based models achieved mean AUCs of 0.82–0.88. For mood shift prediction, digital phenotyping models using smartphone sensor data achieved AUCs of 0.75–0.85 for predicting manic or depressive episodes 1–2 weeks in advance.ConclusionMachine learning shows significant promise for both early detection and mood shift prediction in BD. Multimodal approaches combining neuroimaging, clinical, and digital phenotyping data represent the most promising future direction. However, larger, prospective studies with external validation are needed before clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40345-026-00436-8.Keywords: Bipolar Disorder, Machine Learning, Early Detection, Mood Prediction, Digital Phenotyping, Neuroimaging

Introduction

The clinical challenge of bipolar disorder

Bipolar Disorder (BD) is a chronic and severe psychiatric condition affecting approximately 1–2% of the global population (Merikangas et al. 2011). It is characterized by recurrent episodes of mania or hypomania (elevated mood, increased energy, decreased need for sleep, impulsivity) and depression (low mood, anhedonia, fatigue, suicidal ideation). BD is associated with significant functional impairment, high rates of comorbidity (particularly anxiety disorders and substance use), and a markedly elevated risk of suicide, with an estimated 15–20% of untreated individuals dying by suicide (Pompili et al. 2013).

The diagnosis of BD is challenging for several reasons. First, the disorder often presents initially with a depressive episode, leading to misdiagnosis as Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) in up to 40% of cases (Hirschfeld et al. 2003). This misdiagnosis can result in inappropriate treatment with antidepressants alone, which may be ineffective or even trigger manic episodes. Second, the average delay from symptom onset to correct diagnosis is approximately 5–10 years (Drancourt et al. 2013). This diagnostic delay is associated with poorer outcomes, including more frequent episodes, greater functional impairment, and higher suicide risk.

The importance of early detection and mood prediction

Early and accurate diagnosis of BD is crucial for initiating appropriate treatment (e.g., mood stabilizers like lithium) and improving long-term outcomes (Vieta et al. 2018, Grande et al. 2016). Beyond diagnosis, the ability to predict impending mood shifts—the transition from a euthymic (stable) state to a manic or depressive episode—would be transformative for clinical management (Phillips and Kupfer 2013). Such predictions could enable preemptive interventions, such as adjusting medication, increasing therapy sessions, or implementing lifestyle changes, potentially preventing full-blown episodes and their associated harms (e.g., hospitalization, job loss, relationship breakdown, suicide attempts).

Traditional approaches to monitoring mood rely on subjective self-report, which can be unreliable, particularly when patients have limited insight during manic episodes. There is a critical need for objective, data-driven tools that can aid in both early detection and real-time mood monitoring.

Machine learning in bipolar disorder research

Machine learning (ML) offers a powerful framework for analyzing complex, high-dimensional data to identify patterns that may not be apparent to human observers. In the context of BD, ML has been applied to a variety of data modalities. Clinical and demographic data models can analyze information from Electronic Health Records (EHRs), including diagnoses, medications, and clinical notes, to identify patterns associated with BD or predict future outcomes (Castro et al. 2015). Genetic and biomarker data models, leveraging genome-wide association studies (GWAS) that have identified numerous genetic variants associated with BD, can integrate these variants to create polygenic risk scores or identify gene-gene interactions (Mullins et al. 2021). Neuroimaging data models utilizing structural MRI (sMRI), functional MRI (fMRI), and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) have revealed brain abnormalities in BD, and ML models can learn to classify individuals based on these neuroimaging features (Nunes et al. 2020). Furthermore, digital phenotyping (passive sensing) using smartphones and wearable devices can passively collect data on behavior, including sleep patterns, physical activity, social communication (call/text logs), and mobility. These “digital biomarkers” can reflect underlying mood states and may predict mood shifts (Torous et al. 2016).

Objectives of this review

This systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the literature on ML applications for early detection and mood shift prediction in BD. The specific objectives are to identify and characterize studies that have applied ML to BD for diagnosis or mood prediction, to summarize the data modalities, ML algorithms, and features used, to quantitatively summarize the predictive performance for early detection (BD vs. HC or MDD) and mood shift prediction, to critically appraise the methodological quality of the studies, and to discuss the clinical implications and future directions.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Page et al. 2021).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, PsycINFO, and IEEE Xplore for studies published from database inception to January 31, 2026. The search strategy combined terms related to Bipolar Disorder (e.g., “bipolar disorder,” “manic depression,” “mania”), Machine Learning (e.g., “machine learning,” “deep learning,” “artificial intelligence,” “predictive model”), and Outcomes (e.g., “diagnosis,” “classification,” “prediction,” “mood,” “episode”). The full search string is provided in Supplementary File S1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria. First, they had to be original research articles. Second, the study population had to include individuals with a diagnosis of BD. Third, the study must have applied an ML model. Fourth, the outcome had to be either classification/diagnosis (BD vs. HC or BD vs. MDD) or prediction of future mood episodes. Finally, the study was required to report quantitative performance metrics. Studies were excluded if they were narrative review articles or conference abstracts without full-text availability. Additionally, studies focusing solely on treatment response prediction without addressing diagnosis or mood prediction were excluded, as were studies where the ML model was not clearly described. Systematic reviews or meta-analyses that provided pooled quantitative performance data (e.g., meta-analytic AUC estimates) relevant to the synthesis were retained and clearly marked in the supplementary tables, as they contributed aggregated evidence to the narrative synthesis.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data from the included studies. The information extracted included the first author, publication year, study design, sample size, data modality (clinical, genetic, neuroimaging, digital phenotyping), ML algorithm, features used, validation strategy, and performance metrics including Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. For Tables 1 and 2, the “Representative Mean AUC (95% Range)” values were derived by extracting the primary AUC value reported in each individual study for the respective classification task. These study-level AUC values were then aggregated to calculate a mean and the 95% range (from the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of the extracted values) within each data modality category. Study-level extraction of AUC values was possible for 33 of the 37 included studies (89.2%). For the remaining 4 studies (10.8%), which were systematic reviews or methodological papers providing qualitative synthesis rather than primary empirical AUC data, extractable AUC values were not available, and these were excluded from the summary AUC calculations.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST) (Wolff et al. 2019). This tool evaluates the risk of bias across four domains: participant selection, predictors, outcome, and analysis. Two independent reviewers performed the assessments, with disagreements resolved through consensus. Individual PROBAST ratings for each included study are provided in Supplementary File S3, and a summary of the risk of bias across domains is presented in the Results section.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis was conducted, structured by the primary outcome of interest, specifically distinguishing between early detection and mood prediction, as well as by the data modality utilized. Summary tables were created to compare the characteristics and performance of different approaches, including the frequency of each algorithm category.

Results

Study selection

The database search yielded 2,567 records. After screening titles, abstracts, and full texts against the eligibility criteria, 37 studies were ultimately included in the review. The detailed selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Of the included studies, 24 studies (64.9%) focused on early detection or classification, while 13 studies (35.1%) focused on mood shift prediction.

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the systematic literature search and study selection process

Early detection studies

The characteristics of early detection studies are summarized in Table 1. Neuroimaging was the most common data modality for early detection. Studies using structural MRI identified alterations in gray matter volume in the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and limbic structures (amygdala, hippocampus) as discriminative features (Hibar et al. 2018). Functional MRI studies identified aberrant connectivity in the default mode network and frontolimbic circuits (Vargas et al. 2013). Deep learning models, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) applied to raw MRI images, achieved some of the highest accuracies, reaching up to 92%. A key clinical challenge addressed by several studies is distinguishing BD from MDD, particularly during depressive episodes. Studies addressing this specific comparison achieved lower accuracies (mean AUC approximately 0.75) than BD versus healthy control comparisons, reflecting the inherent clinical difficulty of this distinction (Grotegerd et al. 2014).

Mood shift prediction studies

The characteristics of mood shift prediction studies are summarized in Table 2. The most rapidly growing area in this domain is digital phenotyping, specifically the use of smartphone sensor data for mood prediction. These studies analyzed various features, including changes in sleep duration and regularity inferred from screen-on/off times or accelerometer data, physical activity levels from accelerometer data, frequency and duration of social communication via calls and texts, mobility patterns derived from GPS data such as distance traveled and number of locations visited, and phone usage metrics including screen time and app usage patterns. Several studies demonstrated that ML models could predict the onset of manic or depressive episodes 1 to 2 weeks in advance with AUCs in the range of 0.75 to 0.88 (Faurholt-Jepsen et al. 2019, Jacobson et al. 2019). Decreased sleep, reduced social communication, and changes in mobility patterns were consistently identified among the most predictive features for both manic and depressive episodes.

Methodological quality and risk of bias

The methodological quality of the included studies, assessed using the PROBAST tool, revealed significant variability and several areas of concern. The distribution of risk ratings across the four PROBAST domains is illustrated in Fig. 2. A high risk of bias was most frequently observed in the analysis domain (73% of studies), primarily due to inadequate handling of missing data, small sample sizes leading to overfitting, and a lack of external validation. Only 16% of the included studies were rated as low overall risk of bias, and only 6 studies validated their models on a truly independent external dataset. The participant selection domain also showed a high risk of bias in 24% of studies, with an additional 16% rated as unclear, often due to the use of convenience samples or unclear inclusion/exclusion criteria. Furthermore, heterogeneous outcome definitions were a common limitation, as the definition of a “mood episode” varied significantly across studies, complicating cross-study comparisons. Detailed individual PROBAST ratings for all 37 studies are provided in Supplementary File S3.

Discussion

Summary of findings and the promise of digital phenotyping

This systematic review demonstrates that machine learning holds significant promise for addressing two critical challenges in bipolar disorder: early detection and mood shift prediction. Neuroimaging-based models have shown the capacity to distinguish BD from healthy controls with high accuracy, achieving mean AUCs around 0.85. Concurrently, digital phenotyping models can predict mood episodes 1 to 2 weeks in advance with AUCs approximating 0.80. These findings suggest that objective, data-driven tools for BD management are becoming increasingly viable (Bzdok and Meyer-Lindenberg 2018, Passos et al. 2016, Librenza-Garcia et al. 2017, Mwangi et al. 2016, Schnack and Kahn 2016, Woo et al. 2017, Varoquaux 2018). The emergence of digital phenotyping is particularly noteworthy for mood shift prediction. Because smartphones are ubiquitous, the passive collection of sensor data requires minimal effort from patients, overcoming the limitations of subjective self-report. The ability to predict mood episodes in advance could enable a paradigm shift from reactive to proactive care, allowing for timely interventions that prevent full-blown episodes and significantly reduce the associated morbidity (Saeb et al. 2015, Grünerbl et al. 2015, Abdullah et al. 2016, Beiwinkel et al. 2016, Palmius et al. 2017, Cho et al. 2019, Zulueta et al. 2018).

Towards multimodal integration

A key future direction for the field is the integration of multiple data modalities. Combining neuroimaging, which captures stable trait-like brain features, with clinical data that provides historical context, and digital phenotyping that captures real-time state changes, could lead to more accurate, robust, and clinically actionable models. Early studies exploring such multimodal fusion have already shown promising results, indicating that holistic approaches may outperform single-modality models (Koutsouleris et al. 2021, Barnett et al. 2018, Reinertsen and Clifford 2018, Insel 2017, Onnela and Rauch 2016, Torous et al. 2018, Mohr et al. 2017, Ebner-Priemer and Trull 2009).

Challenges and future directions

Despite the promising results, several methodological challenges must be addressed before these ML tools can be translated into routine clinical practice. The lack of external validation is a critical limitation across the current literature. Models must be rigorously tested on independent datasets from different clinical sites to demonstrate their generalizability and robustness against overfitting (Faurholt-Jepsen et al. 2015, Faurholt-Jepsen et al. 2013, Osmani 2015, Kessing et al. 2017, Stanislaus et al. 2020, Saunders et al. 2017, Luo et al. 2016). Furthermore, the majority of mood prediction studies to date have been retrospective. Prospective studies that test the clinical utility and safety of these predictions in real-time patient care are urgently needed (Collins et al. 2015, Dwyer et al. 2018, Passos et al. 2019).

Interpretability also remains a significant barrier; clinicians must be able to understand the rationale behind a model’s prediction to trust and act upon it. The development of inherently interpretable models or the application of Explainable AI (XAI) techniques is essential for clinical adoption. Additionally, digital phenotyping raises significant privacy and ethical concerns regarding the continuous monitoring of patient behavior. Robust data governance frameworks and transparent, informed consent processes are absolutely essential. Ultimately, for these ML tools to have a meaningful impact, they must be seamlessly integrated into existing clinical workflows and empirically demonstrated to improve patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Machine learning offers a powerful approach for addressing early detection and mood shift prediction in bipolar disorder. Neuroimaging-based models show promise for distinguishing BD from other conditions, while digital phenotyping enables real-time, passive monitoring of mood states. The integration of multiple data modalities represents the most promising future direction. However, the field must address significant methodological limitations, particularly small sample sizes and the pervasive lack of external validation, before these tools can be safely translated into clinical practice. With continued rigorous research, prospective validation, and careful attention to ethical considerations, ML has the potential to transform the management of bipolar disorder, enabling earlier intervention and more personalized, proactive care.
