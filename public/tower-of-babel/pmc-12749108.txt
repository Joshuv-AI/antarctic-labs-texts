Comparative proteomic analysis of Porphyromonas gingivalis challenged human dental pulp stem cells
Authors: Sze Wei Liu, Ching-Seng Ang, Rita Paolini, Rachael L. Moses, Alastair J. Sloan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) are increasingly recognised for their potential in regenerative medicine, particularly in oral and maxillofacial surgeries. However, the unique microbial environment of the oral cavity necessitates a better understanding of how bacterial challenges may affect DPSC function to optimise their reparative capabilities and potential clinical/therapeutic use. This study focused on identifying and understanding differentially expressed proteins in response to Porphyromonas gingivalis challenge and utilises comparative proteomic methodology of the secretome and whole cell lysates with deeper analysis of differentially expressed proteins using STRINGDB and REACTOME databases to elucidate key proteins and networks. This study identified significant proteomic changes in human DPSCs in response to Pg infection, particularly in growth factor signalling, immune response, and wound healing. These insights advance our understanding of DPSC-pathogen interactions, promoting their potential for regenerative and immunomodulatory therapies against infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29191-z.

Introduction

Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs) are a type of mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) found in the dental pulp, the soft connective tissue present at the inner centre of teeth. Secreted proteins released into the conditioned media from cell cultures are typically considered secretomes, which are defined as the set of molecules and biological factors secreted by cells into the extracellular space1. The use of DPSCs and their cell-free secretome as regenerative medicine-based therapies to treat tissue abnormalities caused by trauma, tumours, and infection is increasingly being advocated using in vivo and in vitro models2–4. DPSCs have shown great potential in promoting healing, particularly in oral and maxillofacial tissue engineering4. For example, a study involving the transplantation of human DPSCs into a swine model demonstrated significant improvement in periodontal bone regeneration and soft tissue healing5.

Periodontitis is an inflammatory disease that damages the soft tissue around the teeth, often leading to tooth loss6. Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), a gram-negative dental bacterium, is one of the key pathogens implicated in the chronic form of this condition7. It forms biofilms, evades the immune system, and releases trypsin-like proteases such as gingipains, triggering inflammation, tissue destruction, and bone loss7.

To explore the potential of using DPSCs or their cell-free secretome to treat periodontitis, we aimed to observe and understand DPSCs responses following Pg challenge, particularly what was secreted and the resultant cellular changes. Comparing the differetially expressed proteome to unchallenged controls can help identify key secreted proteins for further applications8,9. Here, we undertook unbiased comparative proteomic analysis of both secretome and whole cell lysate (WCL) of DPSCs challenged by an antagonising concentration of Pg that while significant, does not compromise DPSC viability. This analysis pinpointed the anti-inflammatory and wound-healing properties of DPSCs, similar to the use of mesenchymal stem cells10 for potential tissue repair treatment and future therapeutic applications. It is well established that there is a poor or moderate correlation of mRNA and protein abundances11. Reasons include variable half-lives of both mRNA and proteins, different translation rates across proteins and conditions, and post-translational modifications that can alter protein stability and degradation11–13. Additionally, mRNA levels may not reflect secreted protein levels and in vitro gene expression may not mirror in vivo expression14,15. To date, the proteomic studies involving DPSCs and MSCs have focused on knowing what has been expressed, secreted and comparing them with other cell types16–18 and different non-infectious conditions19. None have specifically analysed the effects of pathogen infection on DPSCs.

This study aimed to uncover growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines secreted in response to Pg, which may influence cell growth, differentiation, survival, and recovery20. Complementary analysis of the cellular responses to Pg was also performed by studying changes in the cytoplasmic proteins. Combining these pieces of information will provide a better understanding of how DPSCs respond to the presence of Pg, providing insights on cellular responses and pathways that could lead to potential tissue regeneration / wound healing therapeutic strategies for periodontal disease, particularly in the early stages of disease progression.

Result

DPSCs survive in the presence of invasive Pg

Two-way ANOVA was performed on DPSC counts at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h (Table 1), showing no significant difference between 0 Pg and Pg (p = 0.84). There was also no significant effect of time (p = 0.75), and the interaction between Pg challenge and time was not significant (p = 0.92). Post-hoc Tukey tests confirmed no significant differences between 0 Pg and Pg at any time point. Accordingly, no differences in DPSC viability or cell counts were observed across 24–96 h between Pg-challenged cultures and unchallenged controls. Live/dead staining revealed that Pg-challenged DPSCs (5 × 106 cells) appeared elongated and thinner at 48 h and 72 h but recovered by 96 h, resembling unchallenged controls (Fig. 1). These findings suggest that DPSCs develop survival strategies in response to invasive Pg, potentially through activation of specific pathways or secretion of adaptive factors.

After 72 h of co-culture with DPSCs, a total of 64 CFU was able to be recovered from the culture medium and 142 CFU intracellularly after lysis of the DPSCs with saponin. This demonstrates that Pg can survive under non-strict anaerobic conditions with 5% CO2 when cultured with a cell line. Given that viable bacteria were detected at 72 h, it can be assumed that Pg was also able to survive at earlier time points, such as 24 h.

The comparative proteomic analysis of co-cultures and control cultures

An area of great interest is in the early response of DPSCs and how they initiate survival strategies. To investigate this, proteomic analysis was performed on the secretome and whole-cell lysate (WCL) from Pg-challenged DPSCs and unchallenged control DPSCs collected at 24 h. The samples were categorised as follows: PS (Pg-challenged DPSC secretome), NS (unchallenged DPSC secretome), PL (Pg-challenged DPSC WCL), and NL (unchallenged DPSC WCL).

Initial data analysis identified one NS and one NL sample as outliers, which were subsequently removed. Heatmaps (Fig. 2A and B) and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plots (Fig. 2C and D) of the remaining samples - four PS and PL vs. three NS and NL demonstrated clear clustering, indicating significant biological differences between PS vs. NS and PL vs. NL samples.

The proteomic analysis, summarised in Table 2, detected 2,632 proteins in the secretome (Supplement Data 1 A) and 6,313 proteins in WCL samples (Supplement Data 2 A). Among them, 1,764 proteins were consistently identified in all three NS samples and four PS samples (Supplement Data 1B), while 3,918 proteins were found in all three NL samples and four PL samples (Supplement Data 2B). A total of 335 proteins in PS vs. NS (Supplement Data 1 C) and 532 proteins in PL vs. NL (Supplement Data 2 C) were significantly up- or down-regulated, with an FDR of 0.05 and S0 = 2. The S0 value of 2 was applied to balance fold-change with statistical significance in the t-test, reducing false positives and ensuring that the identified proteins represent robust and biologically meaningful changes21. The volcano plots (Fig. 3A and B) highlight proteins with significant differences, shown as red dots. These proteins exhibit –log₁₀(p) values ranging from just above 1 to 6, indicating high statistical confidence, and only those with a log₂ fold change ≥ 2 (S0 = 2) were considered significant. In PS vs. NS, 204 proteins were up-regulated and 109 down-regulated in PS, whereas in PL vs. NL, 211 proteins were up-regulated and 321 down-regulated. Interestingly, more proteins were up-regulated than down-regulated in PS vs. NS, while the opposite trend was observed in PL vs. NL. Additionally, 105 proteins were present in at least three PS samples but absent in all NS samples (Supplement Data 1D); however, a similar analysis was not conducted for PL/NL due to the complexity of WCL proteins.

The secretome proteomic analysis and identification of key regulatory proteins

Using the Human Protein Atlas database, 31% of the detected secretome proteins were identified as secreted, membrane-associated, or membrane isoforms (Table 3); membrane proteins that have at least one amino acid residue exposed to the extracellular space are also considered as secreted proteins23. In particular, 12% were classified as growth factors, 7% as cytokines, 1% as chemokines, and 1% as transcription factors. Among the 335 significantly up- or down-regulated proteins, an even higher proportion (43%) were secreted, membrane-associated, or membrane isoforms, and 20% were growth factors. These findings suggest that this dataset is highly reliable in identifying secretomes, and these significantly regulated secreted proteins and growth factors may play a crucial role in the cellular response to Pg.

To analyse protein-protein interactions within the secretome, functional profiling of 204 up-regulated PS proteins and 105 PS-present proteins was conducted using STRINGDB (https://string-db.org/) with a high-confidence interaction score of 0.700 and k-means clustering (no. of clusters = 9). The resulting network (Fig. 4A) revealed three well-defined clusters of proteins. PS Cluster 1 consists of 22 proteins (Supplement Data 1E) associated with insulin-like growth factor (IGF) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) binding. PS Cluster 2 contains 25 proteins (Supplement Data 1 F) involved in actin binding, while PS Cluster 3 includes 21 proteins (Supplement Data 1G) associated with complement activation, the regulation of insulin-like growth factor transport and uptake, and homeostasis. The volcano plot of PS vs. NS samples (Fig. 4B) highlights the cluster proteins through being coloured and labelled, indicating that they are highly up-regulated, significantly up-regulated, or both, suggesting their potential interesting role in the cellular response to Pg.

To further identify key regulatory proteins, analysis was extended to include both up- and down-regulated PS proteins, as well as PS-present proteins, within the STRINGDB network (Fig. 5) using the same confidence score of 0.700. This analysis identified additional key proteins, including Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR), which is 7.01-fold down-regulated in PS, PDGF Receptor A (PDGFRA), which is 4.08-fold down-regulated, PDGF Receptor B (PDGFRB), which is 4.65-fold down-regulated, and Neuropilin-1 (NRP1), which is 4.90-fold down-regulated. These proteins are centrally positioned in Fig. 5, suggesting that they play a crucial role in interacting with other secreted proteins in response to Pg.

The whole cell lysate proteomic and pathway analysis

To gain insight into the cellular processes occurring within the DPSCs, REACTOME database (https://reactome.org/) was used to identify pathways associated with differentially expressed WCL proteins, sorted by significance (S0 = 2, p-value < 0.05, Supplement Data 3). The top 10 pathways are listed in Table 4. The most significant pathway identified, with a p-value of 0.00014, was “response to elevated platelet cytosolic Ca2+,” followed closely by “Platelet activation, signalling, and aggregation,” which had a p-value of 0.0002. Both pathways are primarily involved in haemostasis. Most of the pathways in this list are associated with signal transduction and, protein metabolism, suggesting that these processes may concurrently occur as part of the DPSCs response to Pg infection.

Further analysis using BioGRID database (https://thebiogrid.org/) was conducted to explore further protein-protein interactions between differentially expressed secretome and WCL proteins. From this, several key proteins were identified, particularly those from the clusters of interest, that interact with at least 15 up- or down-regulated proteins from the PL dataset. Notable proteins include Myotrophin (MTPN), Drebrin (DBN1), and Cofilin-1 (CFL1) from PS cluster 2 (Supplement Data 2 F), along with EGFR, PDGFRA and NRP1 from the down-regulated PS protein list. Further STRING DB analysis, using a high confidence interaction score of 0.700, revealed that the PL proteins were interacting with PDGFRA (Supplement Data 4 A, Fig. 6A) and EGFR (Supplement Data 4B, Fig. 6B) and showed significant internal interactions, suggesting that these growth factors in the secreted medium influence various proteins inside the DPSCs. This, in turn, may help initiate survival strategies that allow the cells to survive Pg invasion.

Discussion

Although Pg is considered a strict anaerobe, previous studies have demonstrated that it can invade and remain viable within host cells under co-culture conditions24. In our study, we also confirmed bacterial viability beyond 24 h of co-culture with DPSCs, suggesting that Pg can persist under non-strict anaerobic conditions when cultured with mammalian cells. However, we note the potential influence of oxygen tension and host cell metabolism on Pg viability and gene expression, which may affect host-pathogen interactions. DPSC viability was determined through live/dead staining, which indicated that the cells appeared healthy, with minimal red-stained debris observed (Fig. 1). Furthermore, the enrichment of classical secretory proteins in the dataset supports the conclusion that the majority of detected proteins are likely secreted rather than released from lysed cells. However, we acknowledge that LDH release or other cytotoxicity assays could further confirm sample purity and the absence of intracellular contamination. While this system may not fully replicate the complex in vivo infection environment, it provides a tractable model to investigate host–pathogen interactions and the potential immunomodulatory responses of DPSCs.

The discussion of this secretome and WCL proteomics data highlights on key growth factors, cytokines, chemokines, and proteins that promote DPSC survival and contribute to wound healing, given the potential therapeutic use of these cells and their apparent resilience in the presence of invasive Pg, We focus on proteins significantly up- or down-regulated, including secretome proteins with extensive protein–protein interactions and WCL proteins linked to key functional pathways.

The most distinctive observation from our secretome proteomic analysis is the significant up-regulation of IGF and several insulin-like growth factor-binding proteins (IGFBPs) in response to exposure to Pg. Specifically, IGF-2 is up-regulated by 27.79-fold, while IGFBP-4, IGFBP-6, IGFBP-3, and IGFBP-5 are increased by 36.39-, 23.90-, 5.84-, and 5.61-fold, respectively. The volcano plot (Fig. 4B) further confirms that these proteins are highly up-regulated with strong statistical confidence.

The STRINGDB analysis and clustering of the up-regulated and PS present proteins (Fig. 4A) indicate that IGF-2, IGFBP-3, IGFBP-4, IGFBP-5 and IGFBP-6 belong to the same cluster with known protein-protein interactions. This suggests that these proteins were up-regulated to play a key role in counteracting Pg infection, along with promoting cell survival and wound healing.

IGFs are structurally similar to insulin and play critical roles in cell growth, proliferation, migration, differentiation, survival, and wound healing25. IGF-2, in particular, has been shown to promote cell proliferation and migration in both in vitro and in vivo models26. In a human keratinocyte scratch wound assay, treatment with 10–100 ng/mL recombinant IGF-2 enhanced wound healing, while in a mouse model, IGF-2 administration (5 µg per mouse) improved tissue repair26. Additionally, IGF-2 treatment in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated mice reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in both the hippocampus and serum, preventing their transformation to a pro-inflammatory phenotype and protecting them from hippocampal neurogenesis27.

To regulate IGF-2 activity, its complementary receptor, Insulin-like growth factor 2 receptor (IGF-2R) binds and directs IGF-2 to lysosomes for degradation when required. For instance, IGF-2R is highly expressed in dystrophic muscles from Duchenne muscular dystrophy patients and mouse models. Blocking IGF-2R with neutralising antibodies in these models enhanced muscle regeneration28. Another study under hypoxic conditions (3% O₂) demonstrated that suppressing IGF-2R expression even in hypoxic conditions significantly increased survival of a porcine intestinal epithelial cell line (IPEC-J2), indicating that IGF-2R suppression can prevent apoptosis29. In our study, IGF-2R expression was down-regulated 2.99-fold in PS and 2.89-fold in PL, suggesting that reduced IGF-2R levels in both secretomes and WCL may promote cell survival and recovery.

The biological activity and circulation of IGFs are regulated by a family of IGFBPs which can modulate IGF activity both positively and negatively25,30. The IGFBP family consists of seven members (IGFBP-1 to IGFBP-7), with IGFBP-1 to IGFBP-6 binding IGFs with high affinity31. In addition to their role in IGF regulation, IGFBP-3 and IGFBP-5 can interact with other extracellular, intracellular, and plasma membrane proteins to mediate IGF-independent processes30–33. For cell survival and recovery, IGFBP-3 has been shown to inhibit cancer cell growth and improve the survival of cancer patients34. Since EGFR is known to suppress IGFBP-3 expression35, the 7.01-fold down-regulation of EGRF in PS suggests that the IGFBP-3 expression is enhanced in response to Pg thereby supporting its role in cell survival.

IGFBP-5 is highly expressed in the PS secretome, reflecting its important role in promoting cell survival as it has been shown to inhibit cancer cell proliferation by modulating IGF signalling, both through IGF-dependent and independent pathways33,36. Specifically, IGFBP-5 can suppress IGF signalling by sequestering IGFs away from the IGF1R, thereby reducing vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression36. Conversely, during myoblast differentiation, secreted IGFBP-5 enhances IGF-2 signalling by binding to IGF-2 and promoting its interaction with IGF1R37. A similar IGFBP-5 and IGF-2 interaction may be occurring in the PS secretome, supporting cell survival.

Amongst the IGFBPs, IGFBP-4 and IGFBP-6 are the known inhibitors of IGF activity25,31. As IGFBP-4 blocks IGF-1 from binding to its receptor, IGF1R30,31,38, it is hypothesised that the significant increase in secreted IGFBP-4 in response to Pg may be inhibiting IGF-1, while promoting the wound healing and survival activities of IGF-2, given excess IGF-1 has been linked to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines39.

In contrast to IGFBP-4, IGFBP-6 binds to IGF-2 with higher affinity than IGF-1, and effectively inhibits IGF-2 activity31. However, IGFBP-6 also has IGF-independent roles, particularly in regulating cell proliferation and survival. It binds VEGF, thereby inhibiting angiogenesis40. The addition of human IGFBP-6 to cultured human vascular endothelial cells has demonstrated anti-tumour angiogenesis41. Consistent with our findings, the angiogenic factors VEGF-A and VEGF-B were absent in our secretome and WCL samples. Instead, the lymphangiogenic VEGF-C was detected exclusively in PS samples, with no presence in unchallenged controls.

IGFBP-6 is known to bind to Ubiquilin-4 (UBQLN-4)40, a regulator of protein degradation that plays a key role in DNA repair and maintaining genome stability. UBQLN-4 was up-regulated 3.76-fold in our PL samples and its function will be discussed further below.

When comparing Figs. 4A and 5, VEGF-C was observed to be present in PS but absent in NS, and clusters differently with the down-regulated proteins. It is not centrally located in Fig. 4A, as indicated by a red arrow, but appears more central in Fig. 5. This shift suggests that VEGF-C may interact more closely with PS down-regulated proteins. The exclusive presence of VEGF-C in PS samples is interesting, as VEGF-C is strongly associated with wound healing. Studies have shown that treating wounds in diabetic mice with VEGF-C fusion protein reduces immune cell density and inflammation, ultimately improving tissue regeneration42.

VEGF-C is highly expressed in macrophages challenged with gram negative bacteria, where it inhibits the production of proinflammatory cytokines and protects the challenged mice from septic shock43. Additionally, VEGF-C has been shown to suppress inflammatory cytokines and promote healing through myocardial lymphangiogenesis44.

Based on the observations in Fig. 5, which show the centralised position of VEGF-C in the STRING DB analysis of up- and down-regulated PS proteins, it is hypothesised that VEGF-C may interact with key down-regulated proteins in the PS secretome, including EGFR, PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and NRP1. This leads to an exploration of how these proteins contribute to cell survival and wound healing. However, most studies on these proteins focus on their roles in cancer cell proliferation rather than wound healing or tissue recovery45–47. Interestingly, a review on MSC-based wound healing suggests that combining MSCs with extracellular matrix proteins during the later stages of wound repair could help suppress angiogenic factors such as VEGF and EGFR48. This aligns with our findings, where DPSCs appear to respond to Pg by reducing secreted EGFR expression, and no angiogenic VEGF was detected in our secretome and WCL samples.

Independent studies on EGFR, PDGFRA, PDGFRB and NRP1 in bacterial or viral infection indicate that these proteins play crucial roles in cell survival49–54. For example, inhibiting EGFR has been shown to reduce bacterial-induced neuroinflammation and decrease pro-inflammatory cytokine production49. Similarly, blocking or down-regulating PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and NRP1 has been linked to reduced pathogen entry into human cell lines, thereby increasing the chances of cell survival50–54. Additionally, NRP1 knockout and overexpression in MSCs significantly reduce and promote Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus infection, respectively52; and pre-treatment of Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) with soluble NRP1 suppresses infection in nasopharyngeal epithelial cells by approximately 50%51.

Upregulation of NRP1 by overexpression or EGFR treatment enhances EBV infection. These findings suggest that the significant down-regulation of EGFR, PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and NRP1 in DPSC secretomes may serve as a defence mechanism to limit Pg entry.

Among these down-regulated proteins, EGFR stands out as a key growth factor involved in wound healing. While some studies demonstrate that EGFR is essential for cell migration and tissue repair, with evidence showing reduced wound healing in EGFR-null mice and mouse proximal tubular cell lines55,56, other research suggests excessive EGFR activity may impair healing. For instance, in chronic inflammatory skin disorders, expression of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) is reduced in EGFR null mice and when EGFR is inhibited57. The regulation of EGFR appears to be stage-dependent, as cells transition from the inflammatory phase to proliferative phase, with excessive inflammation impairing wound healing58.

Further BIOGRID analysis of EGFR with the up-regulated and down-regulated PL proteins identified 143 PL-associated proteins (Supplement Data 2), and further STRING DB analysis has revealed extensive interactions between secreted EGFR and these proteins (Fig. 6). Notably, two vesicle trafficking proteins stood out due to their significant down-regulation: Vacuolar protein sorting-associated protein 45 (VPS45), down-regulated 32.84-fold, and Rab11 family-interacting protein 5 (RAB11FIP5), down-regulated 31.10-fold. A previous study suggests that exosomes released from wounded cells may antagonise EGFR activation and, consequently, impair wound healing56. This raises the possibility that the down-regulation of vesicle trafficking proteins serves as part of a pro-healing strategy.

Stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF-1; CXCL12) is another interesting chemokine detected in the PS samples but absent in the unchallenged controls. It is known for its interactions with IGFBPs, as shown in Fig. 4A, and plays a crucial role in wound healing by promoting stem cell recruitment, migration, and differentiation into endothelial cells and fibroblasts during the tissue replacement stage of healing48,59,60. Beyond wound healing, CXCL12 is also secreted to facilitate immune cell migration and accumulation at infection sites61. Given its ability to accelerate wound healing59,60, CXCL12 is often proposed as a therapeutic agent for this purpose. Building on this, we aim in future studies to explore its potential in dental wound healing treatments, both alone and in combination with DPSCs.

Apart from understanding how the DPSCs respond to Pg challenge with its secretome, we were also interested in understanding the intracellular changes. Our WCL proteomic, combined with REACTOME analysis revealed that the expression of alpha-1-antitrypsin (SERPINA1, AAT) is up-regulated 18.05-fold, making it the second highest up-regulated PL proteins. Interestingly, SERPINA1 is involved in the first two most relevant pathways that are related to haemostasis (Table 4). This observation aligns with expectations as SERPINA1 is known to increase rapidly in response to infection and inflammation, exhibiting its protease inhibition, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory and antimicrobial properties62.

Often associated with wound healing, secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC) is found to be up-regulated 9.25-fold in PL samples. Similar to SERPINA1, our analysis indicates that SPARC is also involved in the first two most relevant pathways (Table 4). During wound healing, SPARC inhibits the activation of p53 pathway, which promotes collagen and extracellular matrix production, and facilitates fibroblast migration to the wound site63,64.

Consistent with the up-regulation of IGFBP protein family observed in the PS secretome, up-regulation of Ubiquilin proteins was detected in PL samples. Ubiquilin-1 (UBQLN-1), Ubiquilin-2 (UBQLN-2) and Ubiquilin-4 (UBQLN4) were up-regulated 4.39-, 4.91- and 3.76- fold, respectively and are known to be associated with IGFBP-6 based on BioGRID analysis. Ubiquilin is typically up-regulated in response to hypoxia, where it plays a protective role by suppressing hypoxia-induced neuronal cell death and promoting neuron survival65. Amongst the ubiquilins, UBQLN-1, UBQLN-2 and UBQLN-4 are highly homologous and participate in various aspects of protein quality control often discussed in the context of neurodegenerative diseases66–70. In addition, UBQLN4’s role in cell survival remains under investigation as it has been found to be up-regulated in hepatocellular carcinoma71 but down-regulated in gastric cancer where it appears to function as a tumour suppressor72. In this study, the up-regulation of these ubiquilins suggests their involvement in DPSC recovery from Pg infection.

Another up-regulated protein family observed in PL samples includes Superoxide dismutase [Cu-Zn] (SOD1) and Superoxide dismutase [Mn] (SOD2), which are up-regulated 3.60- and 6.42- fold respectively. Based on BioGRID analysis, both proteins are associated with EGFR. SOD1 and SOD2 function similarly in protecting against reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated injury; however, SOD1 is located in the cytoplasm and mitochondrial intermembrane space, while SOD2 resides in the mitochondrial matrix73. Many studies have explored strategies to mitigate excessive ROS production in wound healing, highlighting the role of SOD in this process74,75. The DPSC secretome has been shown to reduce intracellular and mitochondrial ROS activity, decrease pro-inflammatory markers, and inhibit the proliferation of human glioblastoma cells76. These findings suggest that DPSCs employ similar healing and anti-inflammatory strategies.

Regarding wound healing, significant down-regulation of a disintegrin and metalloproteinase domain-containing protein 9 (ADAM9), a transmembrane protein known to elevate inflammation in certain inflammatory diseases77, was observed as 30.50-fold down-regulated in PL samples. Furthermore, its associated protein, Sorting nexin-33 (SNX33), which is involved in cellular vesicular trafficking77 was also significantly down-regulated (50.65-fold). This substantial reduction in ADAM9 expression is particularly intriguing, as studies on ADAM9-knockout mice have demonstrated accelerated wound repair in its absence78, suggesting that ADAM9 down-regulation plays a significant role in promoting wound healing. Additionally, ADAM9 down-regulation is known to reduce EGFR signalling, thereby suppressing cancer cell metastasis79. This raises the possibility that exosomes from non-challenged DPSCs may not be suitable as a treatment for periodontitis, even though many studies advocate for the use of DPSC exosomes in tissue regeneration and healing80–83.

Our comparative proteomic analysis of the DPSC secretome in response to Pg infection revealed significant up-regulation of IGF-2 and IGFBPs, along with down-regulation of EGFR, PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and NRP1, and the exclusive emergence of VEGF-C and CXCL12. However, we acknowledge that proteomic detection does not necessarily imply bioactive secretion, so future functional studies, including endothelial migration84 and chemotaxis assays85,86, will clarify how VEGF-C and CXCL12 in Pg-challenged secretomes drive angiogenesis and cell recruitment, providing mechanistic insights into tissue regeneration under inflammatory conditions. These changes illustrate how DPSCs respond to infection and recovery. In parallel, the up-regulation of SERPINA1, SPARC, Ubiquilin, and Superoxide Dismutase, together with the down-regulation of vesicular trafficking proteins in the WCL proteome, provides further insight into their molecular responses, with pathway analysis highlighting key roles in signal transduction, haemostasis, and cell death. Collectively, these findings enhance our understanding of DPSC–pathogen interactions and underscore their potential for future regenerative and immunomodulatory therapies in infection.

Materials and methods

Table 5 summarises the primary reagents, instruments, and software used throughout the experimental procedures described below.

Human DPSC and Pg W50 culture condition

Human DPSCs were purchased from Lonza, which had been previously isolated from adult third molars collected during the extraction of a donor’s third molar teeth and cryopreserved at primary passage. The cells were maintained and expanded at 37 °C in 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere in DPSC media, comprised of DPSC Basal Medium (Lonza), supplemented with 10% v/v Dental Pulp Stem Cell Growth Supplement (DPSCGS) (Lonza), 2% v/v L-glutamine and 1% v/v ascorbic acid (Lonza). For subculturing and seeding the cells, HEPES Buffered Saline Solution (Lonza) was used to wash the cells to neutralise the complex proteins in growth medium first. 0.025% Trypsin / EDTA (Lonza) was then used to detach the cells from the culture vessel into suspension. To neutralise the Trypsin/EDTA, three times amount of Trypsin Neutralizing Solution (Lonza) was added before the cells were centrifuged at 220 x g for 5 min and resuspended in growth medium.

Pg W50 was cultured and maintained in an anaerobic chamber (MG500 TG anaerobic workstation; Don Whitley Scientific) at 37 °C. Pg was cultured on horse blood agar (Oxoid) plates supplemented with 10% v/v horse blood. Bacterial cells were cultured for 4–5 days before inoculating into 20 mL supplemented BHI medium containing 37 g/L brain heart infusion (BHI) medium (Bacto), 5.0 mg/mL cysteine hydrochloride and 5.0 µg/mL hemin. After overnight incubation in an anaerobic chamber at 37 °C, 200 uL of the culture was used to inoculate 20 mL of supplemented BHI medium followed by anaerobic incubation at 37 °C. Culture purity of the cells was routinely checked by Gram staining and colony morphology. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Co-culture of DPSC with Pg

DPSCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 2mL DPSC media; they were cultured for 72 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After 72 h, Pg was added to the DPSC cultures; the DPSC medium was removed, washed, and exchanged with growth supplement-free DPSC medium, comprised of DPSC Basal Medium, supplemented with 2% v/v L-glutamine and 1% v/v ascorbic acid. Growth of Pg was monitored by measuring absorbance at a wavelength of 650 nm (OD650) and were harvested at OD 0.500 by centrifugation at 8,000 x g for 5 min. Pg was washed and resuspended in 2 ml growth supplement-free DPSC medium, with 5 × 106 Pg cells added into each well containing DPSCs. Control DPSC cultures, without Pg, were set up simultaneously using 2 mL of DPSC medium without growth supplement and without exposure to Pg. Both co-cultures and control cultures were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 24 h. DPSC viability from 24 to 96 h was evaluated using the LIVE/DEAD Cell Imaging Kit (488/570; Thermo Fisher) following the manufacturer’s instructions, alongside viable cell counts with 0.2% Trypan Blue (Bio-Rad). Cells were stained with calcein AM and ethidium homodimer-1, and fluorescence images were captured with an EVOS imaging system to differentiate live (green) and dead (red) cells.

Pg viability after 72 h of co-culture was assessed using a colony-forming unit (CFU) assay. For extracellular Pg, the culture supernatant was collected, serially diluted in BHI, and plated on horse blood agar plates. To determine intracellular Pg counts, cells were lysed with saponin prior to serial dilution and plating. Plates were incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for up to 7 days, and colonies were counted to calculate CFU/mL.

Comparative secretome and whole cell lysate (WCL) liquid chromatography data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (LC DIA-MS) analysis

Conditioned media from the co-cultures and control cultures after 24 h were collected, centrifuged at 16,000 x g for 10 min, at 4 °C, to remove any cell debris and the supernatants were collected as secretome samples. To concentrate the proteins in lower volume, the secretome samples were freeze-dried and resuspended in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (Sigma-Aldrich) solubilisation buffer containing 5% SDS and 50mM Tetraethylammonium bromide (TEAB; pH 8.5; Sigma-Aldrich), acetone precipitated overnight and resuspended again in lower volume of SDS solubilisation buffer.

DPSCs from all conditions were lysed and collected as the whole cell lysate (WCL) samples, using SDS solubilisation buffer supplemented with EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). The WCL samples were sonicated on ice cold water (x 3; 30 s each), and the proteins in the samples were then solubilised further by adding 2 M urea (Sigma-Aldrich) in 10mM HEPES (Sigma-Aldrich) buffer at pH 8.

Protein concentration in both secretome and WCL samples was measured using the Micro Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assays Kit (Pierce) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein standards and samples (150uL each) were incubated with BCA working reagent (150uL) at 37 °C for 2 h, and absorbance was read at 562 nm. Protein concentrations were calculated from a standard curve. S-Trap micro spin column protocol (Protifi) was used to digest 50ug proteins from each sample.

For the WCL samples, overnight acetone precipitation was performed after BCA assay to concentrate and resuspend 50ug proteins in 25 uL SDS solubilisation buffer. All samples were then clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 x g for 8 min, at 4 °C. The proteins were reduced in 5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP; Thermo Fisher) at 55 °C for 15 min and alkylated in 20nM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 20 min. Proteins were acidified by adding 12% phosphoric acid (Sigma-Aldrich) to a final concentration 2.5% phosphoric acid), followed by 165uL S-Trap binding buffer (90% methanol 100 mM TEAB, pH 8.5) in each sample. The samples were loaded into S-Trap columns with a receiver tube for waste flow through. The proteins were trapped in the columns by centrifugation at 4,000 x g, 30 s and the columns were washed with S-Trap binding buffer by adding 150uL S-Trap binding buffer and centrifuging at 4,000 x g 30 s (x 3). After washing, the columns were centrifuged at 4,000 x g for 1 min to fully remove the binding buffer. 2ug trypsin (Thermo Fisher) in 25uL 50nM TEAB was added into each column to digest the proteins at 47 °C for 2 h. Peptides were eluted using 40uL 50nM TEAB, 40uL 0.2% formic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) and 40uL 50% acetonitrile, 0.2% formic acid. The eluted proteins were pooled and freeze-dried before being resuspended in 2% acetonitrile, 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid for mass spectrometry analysis.

LC-MS/MS was carried out using a Orbitrap Exploris mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a nanoflow reversed-phase-HPLC (Ultimate 3000 RSLC, Dionex) fitted with an Acclaim Pepmap nano-trap column (Dionex—C18, 100 Å, 75 μm× 2 cm) and an Acclaim Pepmap RSLC analytical column (Dionex—C18, 100 Å, 75 μm× 50 cm). The tryptic peptides were injected to the enrichment column at an isocratic flow of 5 µL/min of 2% v/v CH3CN containing 0.1% v/v formic acid for 5 min applied before the enrichment column was switched in-line with the analytical column. The eluents were 5% DMSO in 0.1% v/v formic acid (solvent A) and 5% DMSO in 100% v/v CH3CN and 0.1% v/v formic acid (solvent B). The flow gradient was (i) 0–6 min at 3% B, ii) 6–7 min, 3–4% B (ii) 7–82 min, 4–25% B (iii) 82–86 min 25–40% B (iv) 86–87 min, 40–80% B (v) 87–90 min, 80–80% B (vi) 90–91 min, 80 − 3% B and equilibrated at 3% B for 10 min before the next sample injection.

For data-independent acquisition (DIA) experiments full MS resolutions were set to 120,000 at m/z 200 and scanning from 350 to 1400 m/z in the profile mode. Full MS automated gain control (AGC) target was 250% with an IT of 50 ms. AGC target value for fragment spectra was set at 2000%. 50 windows of 13.7 Da were used with an overlap of 1 Da. Resolution was set to 30,000 and maximum IT to 55 ms. Normalised collision energy was set at 30%. All data were acquired in centroid mode using positive polarity.

Database search (Spectronaut) and statistical analysis

DIA data were analysed using the direct DIA analysis workflow with default settings on the Spectronaut software (v. 17.5.230413.55965) and against the Uniprot Homo Sapiens database (updated Sep 2023). Trypsin specificity was set to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethyl (Cys) was defined as fixed modification and acetylation (protein N-term) and oxidation (Met) as variable modifications. Results are filtered at a Protein and PSM false discovery rate of 1%. Precursor filtering using the Qvalue, quantification carried out on the MS2 level and cross-run normalisation strategy set to automatic. Quantitative data were exported for statistical analyses using Perseus version 2.0.7.0 (REF: PMID = 29344888). Differentially expressed secretome and WCL proteins in the co-culture and unchallenged control were identified using a two-sample Student’s t-test combined with a permutation-based correction for multiple hypothesis testing at a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05. This workflow, implemented in Perseus21, includes a randomisation procedure in which group labels are shuffled to generate a null distribution, enabling robust FDR control and minimising false positives. This approach ensures that detected protein changes reflect genuine biological differences and has been widely applied in high-impact comparative proteomics studies, becoming a standard in the field.

The secretome proteins with log2 fold change, S0 = 2 and present only in the Pg-challenged samples were analysed using The Human Protein Atlas https://www.proteinatlas.org/ identifying those that are secreted, membrane, membrane isoform, growth factors, cytokines, chemokines and transcription factors. The functional profiling of these secretome proteins was performed using STRINGDB (https://string-db.org/). The pathways that were associated with the differentially expressed WCL proteins with S0 = 2 were identified using REACTOME databases (https://reactome.org/). Further analysis using BioGRID databases (https://thebiogrid.org/) was performed to identify interactions between the differentially expressed secretome and WCL proteins of interest.
