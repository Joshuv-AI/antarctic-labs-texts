Four imprints of Belnap’s useful four-valued logic in computer science
Tomáš Jakl

Abstract
We review four areas of theoretical computer science which share technical or philosophical ideas with the work of Belnap on his useful four-valued logic. Perhaps surprisingly, the inspiration by Belnap–Dunn logic is acknowledged only in the study of d-frames. The connections of Belnap’s work and linear logic, Blame Calculus or the study of LVars are not openly admitted. The key to three of these connections with Belnap’s work go via the twist-product representation of bilattices. On the one hand, it allows us to view a large class of models of linear logic as based on Belnap–Dunn logic. On the other hand, d-frames admit two twist-product representation theorems and, also, the key theorem in Blame Calculus is essentially a twist-product representation theorem too, albeit with a strong proof-theoretic flavour.



keywords
Belnap–Dunn logic, twist-construction, twist-product representation, d-frames, Blame Calculus, LVars

In memory of Nuel Belnap.

Introduction

Belnap’s four-valued logic
Belnap (1976)
;
Belnap (1977)
, following Dunn
Dunn (1976)
, is an extension of the classical two-valued logic to encompass phenomena arising in computer-based reasoning systems. On top of the classical two values ‘True’ and ‘False’ it adds ‘None’ representing the lack of any information and ‘Both’ representing the inconsistency of claiming both ‘True’ and ‘False’.
One of the key ideas in Belnap’s approach is to distinguish between two orders of the four-valued lattice,
the usual ‘logical order’
\mathrel{\ThisStyle{\hbox{\hskip 3.8889pt\hskip-3.8889pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\SavedStyle\leq$}$}\hskip-3.8889pt\hskip-4.7222pt\raisebox{1.07777pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\SavedStyle\ \,\lx@scalerel@obj{\cdot}$}$}}\hskip-4.7222pt\hskip 3.8889pt}}}
and the ‘information/knowledge order’
\sqsubseteq
.
The latter order is inspired by domain theory, initiated by Scott
Scott (1970)
. Domains were introduced to give semantics to (abstract) programming languages such as the lambda calculus.

The tradition of using “Four Logical Alternatives,” known as catuṣkoṭi, goes back to the 6th century Indian logic.
These four alternatives precisely correspond to the aforementioned four values
Robinson (1957)
;
Jayatilleke (1967)
;
Garfield (2019)
.
However, undoubtedly, Belnap was the first to identify the two orders and to emphasise the usefulness of the four values for computer reasoning.

Later on Ginsberg
Ginsberg (1988)
introduced bilattices as an algebraic description of the connectives introduced by Belnap.
As his primary examples he used world-based bilattices, that is, structures consisting of pairs
(A_{+},A_{-})
of subsets of a chosen set of worlds.
Then, intuitively,
A_{+}
is a set of worlds where a given property holds and
A_{-}
are the worlds where it fails.
Under this interpretation four-valued connectives are calculated as simple set-theoretic operations. For example, the negation and logical conjunction are give by
\neg(A_{+},A_{-})=(A_{-},A_{+})
and
(A_{+},A_{-})\mathbin{\ThisStyle{\hbox{\hskip 3.33334pt\hskip-3.33334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\SavedStyle\wedge$}$}\hskip-3.33334pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-1.24445pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\SavedStyle\lx@scalerel@obj{\cdot}$}$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.33334pt}}}(B_{+},B_{-})=(A_{+}\cap B_{+},A_{-}\cup A_{-})
, respectively.

Then Fitting
Fitting (1991)
observed that the construction of Ginsberg holds more generally. That is, not only that the square products of a (powerset) lattice yields a bilattice but, in fact, this holds true for any lattice.
It is a remarkable result of Rivieccio, from his Ph.D. thesis
Rivieccio (2010)
, that every bilattice is obtained this way.
Philosophically speaking, this means that, even in this more general algebraic approach of Ginsberg
Ginsberg (1988)
, every bilattice-valued proposition can be decomposed into two parts, its positive and negative parts.

In this paper we discuss three different examples from theoretical computer science where this tension between positive and negative parts is crucial.
Firstly, in Section
3
, we observe that the famous Chu construction of models of linear logic is exactly the twist-construction of bilattices, with most connectives being our well-known four-valued connectives of Belnap and Ginsberg.
Secondly, in Section
4
, we discuss the author’s own work on d-frames, which came from considerations in denotation semantics of programming languages and domain theory. On the one hand, d-frames provide a relaxation which encodes properties of topological models of Belnap–Dunn logic. Our approach reopens some of Belnap’s original questions that have to do with computability in the information order.
After d-frames we turn to Blame Calculus (Section
5
). This is a type system for programming languages that allows to construct types from other types with set comprehension.

The attentive reader might notice that the title of this paper speaks of four imprints of Belnap’s work, and not merely three. Indeed, the final section (Section
6
) is about a very practical application of ideas similar to Belnap’s, in an implementation of special type of variables (called LVars) for programming purposes
Kuper and Newton (2013)
;
Kuper (2015)
. LVars were introduced to solve a particular problem in the theory distributed and parallel programs. Perhaps surprisingly, the core of the solution lies in adding the information order as part of the structure, which is very much in the spirit of Belnap who added the information order (and extra new values and connectives) to the usual two-valued logical lattice.

Linear logic

Girard’s
Girard (1987)
linear logic enjoys a fair deal of popularity within the computer science community. This is due to its its ability to track resource usage.
In fact, linear logic was the main inspiration behind Rust
Matsakis and Klock (2014)
, a relatively new and promising programming language whose strong points are safety guarantees of resource usage.

Linear logic is a substructural logic with connectives split into groups of multiplicatives
\otimes,\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={c}]{180.0}{$\&$}},\multimap
, additives
\oplus,\&
, exponentials
!,?
, and a linear negation
(\text{-})^{\bot}
.
It admits a very natural Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov (BHK) interpretation, akin to that of intuitionistic logic. Shulman
Shulman (2022)
defines this in terms of the dynamics between
proofs
(affirmations) and
refutations
. For example, the interpretation of additive conjunctions turns out to be very familiar to anyone who has seen twist-constructions:

•

a proof of
P\oplus Q
is a proof of
P
and a proof of
Q
, and

•

a refutation of
P\oplus Q
consists of a refutation of
P
or a refutation of
Q
.

Similarly, for negation we have that

•

a proof of
P^{\bot}
is a refutation of
P

•

a refutation of
P^{\bot}
is a proof of
P

Perhaps the most unusual are the multiplicative connectives, e.g. the multiplicative disjunction
\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={c}]{180.0}{$\&$}}
(called ‘par’) is interpreted as follows.

•

a proof of
P\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={c}]{180.0}{$\&$}}Q
is a method of converting a refutation of
P
into a proof of
Q
and method of converting a refutation of
Q
into a proof of
P
, and

•

a refutation of
P\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={c}]{180.0}{$\&$}}Q
is a refutation of
P
and a refutation of
Q
.

The main restriction, when compared to paraconsistent logics, is that we do not allow statements to be both provable and refutable.

Chu construction

The above BHK interpretation is justified by the so-called
Chu construction

Chu (1978)
;
Chu (1979)
, which is a standard method for building models of linear logic from models of intuitionistic logic. In the following we describe the
proof-irrelevant
version of this construction following the
proof-relevant
description of Shulman
Shulman (2022)
. This means that the same construction works not only for partially ordered structures but also for categories.

Given a Heyting algebra
H
, the universe of
\mathtt{Chu}(H,0)
consists of pairs
a=(a_{+},a_{-})\in H\times H
such that
a_{+}\land a_{-}=0
. In other words, we view propositions as pairs of intuitionistic statements (i.e. elements of
H
), representing the affirmation and refutation parts respectively. The latter condition forbids the statements to be both affirmable/provable and refutable.

The linear connectives are defined in
\mathtt{Chu}(H,0)
as follows:

\displaystyle a\oplus b

\displaystyle=(a_{+}\land b_{+},\,a_{-}\lor b_{-})

\displaystyle a\otimes b

\displaystyle=(a_{+}\land b_{+},\,(a_{+}\to b_{-})\land(b_{+}\to a_{-}))

\displaystyle a\mathbin{\&}b

\displaystyle=(a_{+}\lor b_{+},\,a_{-}\land b_{-})

\displaystyle a\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={c}]{180.0}{$\&$}}b

\displaystyle=((a_{-}\to b_{+})\land(b_{-}\to a_{+}),\,a_{-}\land b_{-})

\displaystyle a^{\bot}

\displaystyle=(a_{-},\,a_{+})

\displaystyle a\multimap b

\displaystyle=((a_{+}\to b_{+})\land(b_{-}\to a_{-}),\,a_{+}\land b_{-})

\displaystyle!a

\displaystyle=(a_{+},\,a_{+}\to 0)

\displaystyle?a

\displaystyle=(a_{-}\to 0,\,a_{-})

Note that
!a
is also sometimes defined as
(a_{+},a_{+}\to a_{-})
. This is equivalent thanks to the assumption that
a_{+}\land a_{-}=0
, since
a_{+}\to a_{-}\leq a_{+}\to 0
always holds and, by residuation,
a_{+}\land(a_{+}\to a_{-})=a_{+}\land a_{-}\leq 0
implies the converse
a_{+}\to 0\leq a_{+}\to a_{-}
. Dually,
?a

One of the original motivation behind linear logic in Girard

\displaystyle!a\multimap b

\displaystyle=(a_{+},a_{+}\to 0)\multimap b

\displaystyle=((a_{+}\to b_{+})\land(b_{-}\to(a_{+}\to 0)),\,a_{+}\land b_{-})

\displaystyle=((a_{+}\to b_{+})\land(a_{+}\to(b_{-}\to 0)),\,a_{+}\land b_{-})

\displaystyle=((a_{+}\to b_{+}),\,a_{+}\land b_{-})=a\supset b

where the penultimate equality holds because

Note that models of linear logic of the form

satisfy some extra theorems that are not typically true in linear logic, such as

,

,

As Shulman

uninteresting to linear logicians but, on the other hand, it provides a useful tool for studying constructive mathematics, by being a richer logic that allows for more precise distinctions in constructive statements when compared to intuitionistic logic. Given our observation above that the linear connectives of the Chu construction are term definable in Belnap–Dunn logic we might suggest that, in fact, Belnap–Dunn logic might be even better suited for studying constructive statements.

4
d-Frames

The second instance where four-valued logic occurs in theoretical computer science has the same origins as Belnap–Dunn logic, that is, in domain theory.

Continuous domains

Domains
are partially ordered structures
(D,\sqsubseteq)
with the extra requirement that its
directed subsets

A\mathrel{\subseteq\!{}^{\uparrow}}D
have suprema, denoted by
\dirsqcup A
.
Intuitively, domains encode the information obtained during some computation. The order
a\sqsubseteq b
encodes that, at state
a
, the computed information is less than or equal to that of
b
. Consequently, directed suprema
\dirsqcup
model computation, e.g. by means of recursion, loops and alike.

As argued already by Scott
Scott (1970)
, in order for a domain to correspond to a real computation, it has to be of a special type.
It has to have a countable (computer enumerable) base
B\subseteq D
, which we think of as ‘finite pieces’ of information from which the rest of the domain is computed.

To make this precise, for
k,l\in D
, we say that
k
approximates
l
, and write
k\ll l
, if for any
A\mathrel{\subseteq\!{}^{\uparrow}}D

l\sqsubseteq\dirsqcup A\kern 5.0pt\implies\kern 5.0ptk\sqsubseteq a\text{ for some $a\in A$}.

Then, the domain
(D,\sqsubseteq)
is
continuous
if it has a base
B
such that, for every
x\in D
,
x=\dirsqcup\{b\in B\mid b\ll x\}
.
This models the idea that every state in our domain can be computed from finite pieces of information that approximate it.
Note that the requirement that the base is countable is often dropped.

An important feature of domains is that they can be viewed as
topological spaces
. A domain
D
can be equipped with the topology of Scott-open sets, i.e. upwards closed sets
U\subseteq D
such that
\dirsqcup A\in U
implies
A\cap U\not=\emptyset
.
This opens up the door for connections between computation, domain theory and (non-Hausdorff) topology, which has been a fruitful field of study over the years, see e.g.
Mislove (1998)
;
Escardó (2004)
;
Goubault-Larrecq (2013)
.

Bitopological spaces and their duals

Domains were introduced by Scott
Scott (1970)

as an alternative to the untyped lambda calculus for uses in semantics. Famously, by coincidence, domains also played a key role in providing a concrete semantics to the untyped lambda calculus
Scott (1993)
, which was an important open problem at that time.
Since then they were used to model other programming phenomena such as non-determinism, exceptions, and so on.

However, it is still not known how to model probabilistic computation with continuous domains
2
2

2

Formally, it is not known if there is a cartesian closed subcategory of continuous domains which is closed under the probabilistic powerdomain construction.
, which remains a major challenge in the field.

The search for the solution accelerated the study of topological properties of domains (via their Scott topology) and, in particular, of the class of
stably compact spaces
which correspond to a well-behaved class of continuous distributive lattices, see e.g.
Lawson (2010)
;
Goubault-Larrecq (2013)
.

A distinct feature of stably compact spaces is that their open and compact subsets are dual to each other, in some sense.
In fact, compact subsets generate the so called
dual topology
, which plays an important role the theory.
This naturally led Jung and Moshier

to focus on bitopological spaces, as a convenient tool for describing properties of stably compact spaces in terms of their dual topology.
To this end, they introduced a new type of algebraic duals of bitopological spaces, called
d-frames
, and this is where our story turns back to Belnap.

Recall that any topological space
(X,\tau)
induces the lattice
L=(\tau,\subseteq)
of its open sets in the subset order. This lattice is a complete Heyting algebra or, equivalently, it is a complete lattice such that, for any
A\subseteq L
and
b\in L
, the equation

(\bigvee A)\wedge b=\bigvee\nolimits_{a\in A}(a\wedge b)

holds. Complete lattices satisfying this distributivity law are also often called
frames
or
locales

Picado and Pultr (2012)
.
For a large class of topological spaces, i.e. for the so-called
sober spaces
(which includes all Hausdorff spaces), the frame of opens
L
contains all information about the space
X
and, in fact, the set of points of
X
can be fully recovered from
L
.

In case we have a
bitopological space

(X,\tau_{+},\tau_{-})
, which constitutes of a set

and two topologies
\tau_{+},\tau_{-}
, we have two frames
L_{+}=(\tau_{+},\subseteq)
and
L_{-}=(\tau_{-},\subseteq)
.
In order to describe the interaction of the two topologies, Jung and Moshier

add two relations

which represent pairs of disjoint subsets and pairs of subsets that cover the whole space, respectively.
This is all is bundled into one structure as follows.

2.

\mathsf{con}

\mathsf{tot}
is upwards closed in the
\sqsubseteq

3.

\mathsf{con}

\mathsf{con}(a,b)

It is immediate that any bitopological space

The notation for

refers to ‘consistency’ from logic.
For example, similarly to Ginsberg

Then, a pair of opens

represents the set of models

where a given predicate holds and the set of models
U_{-}
where it fails.
Then,
\mathsf{con}(U_{+},U_{-})
represents that there is no model where the predicate would be both true and false, i.e. the predicate is consistent.
On the other hand, a predicate is ‘total’, formally written as
\mathsf{tot}(U_{+},U_{-})

, either
x
satisfies the predicate (i.e.
x\in U_{+}
) or

x\in U_{-}
).

A special type of predicates are those for which both

and
\mathsf{tot}_{X}(U_{+},U_{-})

and

are complements of each other.

Remark 4.3

(On completeness, continuity and computation)

We might be led to think of d-frames as of bilattices with additional relations.

Blame Calculus

We now leave the world of algebraic structures underpinning various logics and take a look at another example in theoretical computer science which shares some similarities with Belnap’s work.

To ensure (partial) correctness of computer programs many programming languages use the mechanism of ‘typing’ which allows the programmer to give a specification of output and input values of their programs. This specification is then checked by the compiler and programs that do not ‘typecheck’ are rejected and not compiled at all. On the other hand, compiled well-typed programs are guaranteed to preserve their typing specifications. For example, a program of type
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\{\mathsf{t},\mathsf{f}\}
, for a given input pair
(n,i)
where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, is guaranteed to output one of the boolean values (if the computation terminates).

In some situations it might be fairly useful to assume that either the input or output satisfies some further properties, rather than just merely be of a given type. This can be achieved by specifying new types by comprehension on previously defined types. For example,
T^{\prime}=\{x:T\mid Px\}
specifies a new type
T^{\prime}
, which is a subtype (subset) of
T
, consisting of only the elements
x
of
T
for which the program
P
on input
x
outputs value ‘True’.
However, adding comprehension to a programming language can make typing undecidable. For example, for a computer program on integers
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}
, it is not decidable if it restricts to the type
\mathbb{Z}\to T
where
T=\{x:\mathbb{Z}\mid x\geq 0\}
.

The typing mechanism of programming languages which happens during compile time (i.e. much before it is even executed) is called ‘static typing.’ To resolve the issue with comprehension, some kind of ‘dynamic typing’ (i.e. typechecking which happens during the execution of the program) is needed.

The
Blame Calculus

Wadler and Findler (2009)
of Wadler and Findler is a mixed ‘static’ and ‘dynamic’ typing system whose main property is that the statically typed part of a program is guaranteed to be always correct.

The underlying mechanism of the Blame Calculus gives the programmer the ability to
cast
between compatible types. Each such cast is marked with a label and if, during the execution, the cast fails then a blame of the given label is reported.
For example, if a program
A
is of type
S
, the program

\left<T\Leftarrow S\right>^{p}A

is of type
T
and
p
is the blame label for this cast. Then, during the run of this program,
A
calculates some value
x
of type
S
and then, as part of executing
\left<T\Leftarrow S\right>^{p}
, it is checked that
x
is also of type
T
. If this is the case then
x
is simply the returned value. On the other hand, if
x
fails to be of type
T
then
blame
is allocated to
p
, i.e. the program ends with the error code
p
.

For the purposes of casting between function types, for every blame label
p
we also introduce its negative version
\overline{p}
. However, double negations cancel out, i.e.
\overline{\overline{p}}=p
. This allows to distinguish blames of the input and output casts. For example, for a program
A
of type
S\to S^{\prime}
and a value
v
of type
T
, the cast of
A
to type
T\to T^{\prime}
and its application to the value

\big(\left<(T\to T^{\prime})\Leftarrow(S\to S^{\prime})\right>^{p}A\,\big)v

is the program that first casts

to type
S
, applies
A
to this and then casts the result back to
T^{\prime}
. Importantly, the first blame label is
\overline{p}
whereas the second blame label is
p
. In other words, the above is computed as:

\left<T^{\prime}\Leftarrow S^{\prime}\right>^{p}\big(A\,(\left<S\Leftarrow T\right>^{\overline{p}}v)\big)

We see that if the first cast
\left<S\Leftarrow T\right>^{\overline{p}}v
fails, negative blame
\overline{p}
is reported, which indicates on the issue of the outer environment of this program fragment. On the other hand, if the final cast
\left<T^{\prime}\Leftarrow S^{\prime}\right>^{p}
fails, it means that that the returned value of
A
does not follow the expected specification. This tension between ‘external’ and ‘internal’ failures (i.e. blaming
\overline{p}
and

The key result of
Wadler and Findler (2009)
is that casting from a ‘more precise’ type to a ‘less precise’ type can only give rise to negative blame and, conversely, casting from a ‘less precise’ to a ‘more precise’ type can only give positive blame.
To make this formal the Blame Calculus relies on various notions of ‘subtyping’. We do not want to go into the intricate details of the definitions of these notions here, the interested reader is advised to study
Wadler and Findler (2009)

For us, it is enough to know that there are two main subtyping relations:

\frac{S^{\prime}<:S\qquad T<:T^{\prime}}{S\to T<:S^{\prime}\to T^{\prime}}\qquad\qquad\frac{S<:_{n}S^{\prime}\qquad T<:_{n}T^{\prime}}{S\to T<:_{n}S^{\prime}\to T^{\prime}}

6
LVars

Let us finish with the last imprint of Belnap–Dunn logic in computer science which is of a very practical nature.
It concerns with
threads
which are the parts of a computer program that run in parallel to each other. They are used to speed up computation by running each of the different threads on a different core of the computer CPU.
Threads often need to communicate with each other and this can be done via shared
variables
(in sense of programming languages), i.e. locations in computer memory where some piece of data is stored.

However, variables shared among parallel threads pose many challenges.
In particular, it is not clear what the outcome should be if two threads try to write to the same variable at the same time. In case the two threads are attempting to write different values, how do we decide which value gets stored in the variable at the end?

There are many ways to deal with this problem. Typically, we restrict the access to the shared variables, e.g. by only enabling writes to some threads, temporarily locking variables (mutexes, atomic writes), or upon a failed write restarting the thread (STM).
The disadvantages of these approaches are either reduced efficiency or limited expressivity.

Kuper and Newton
Kuper and Newton (2013)
;
Kuper (2015)
propose a new type of variables, called
LVars
, to tackle some of these limitations. The main idea is to structure our data in a
bounded join-semilattice

(S,\sqcup,\bot,\top)
, which can be implemented in traditional data structures such as trees, arrays, etc.
Then, every write to a shared variable is computed as a join with its current value, which is guarantees to always increase the stored value.
Also, due to the associativity of
\sqcup
, it does not matter in which order we do the writes.

One way LVar types can be constructed is by taking an arbitrary algebra and freely adding the order. For example, starting from the structure
(\mathbb{N},+,\times,0,1)
we define the semilattice
S
on the set
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\bot,\top\}
by

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\ \bot\sqsubseteq n\sqsubseteq\top\quad\text{and}\quad(\forall n,m\in\mathbb{N})\ n\sqcup m=\top.

The value
\bot
represents the initial value of the variable. If the variable’s value is
x
and a thread writes to the variable the value
n+m
, the new value of the variable will be
x\sqcup(n+m)
. This means that unless
x=n+m
, the new value will be
\top
.

In case we freely add order to the two-variable boolean algebra
\mathbf{2}
, we obtain the lattice
\mathcal{FOUR}
(from Figure
1
) in the information order.
Admittedly, LVars are not as closed to the four-valued logic of Belnap as the other three topics we discussed. However, the underlying idea is very much in the spirit of Belnap, that is, an extra order (the information order) needs to be added in order to deal with computational phenomena.
In fact, Belnap
Belnap (1976)
;
Belnap (1977)
argues that his logic is suitable for scenarios where the computer obtains data from a variety of (potentially contradicting) sources. In this case, the different sources are the different threads of the computer program.
