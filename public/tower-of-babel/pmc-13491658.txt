Clinical characteristics and prognosis of focal cortical dysplasia with epilepsy in children
Authors: Jinfeng Wu, Lei Tang, Yu Deng, Jin Chen, Siqi Hong, Li Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo characterize the clinical features of epilepsy related to focal cortical dysplasia ( FCD) in children and to identify factors associated with favorable postoperative outcomes.MethodsClinical data from 97 pediatric patients with FCD-related epilepsy who underwent surgical treatment were retrospectively collected and analyzed. Patients were categorized into good outcome and poor outcome groups according to Engel grading. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify factors associated with favorable prognosis.ResultsA total of 97 patients were included. Focal seizures were the most common seizure type, occurring in 72 patients. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed positive findings in 82 patients. Ictal video-EEG (VEEG) demonstrated low-voltage fast activity in 46 patients, while interictal VEEG showed predominantly focal epileptiform discharges in 70 patients. Pathological examination revealed FCD type I in 17 patients, type II in 56, and type III in 24. At the most recent follow-up, 77 patients achieved Engel class I, 10 achieved class II, 5 achieved class III, and 5 achieved class IV. Univariate analysis identified focal interictal discharges on VEEG, epileptogenic focus resection, FCD type II, and temporal lobe lesion location as significant factors associated with good outcomes (P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis confirmed that epileptogenic focus resection, FCD type II, and temporal lobe location were independent predictors of favorable postoperative outcomes (P < 0.05).ConclusionMost children with FCD-related epilepsy in this cohort achieved favorable postoperative outcomes. Epileptogenic focus resection, FCD type II pathology, and temporal lobe lesion location were identified as independent factors associated with good prognosis.

Introduction

Focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) is a common cause of surgically treated drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) in children. Epilepsy surgery is an effective treatment option, with the aim of controlling seizures by resecting epileptogenic tissue while avoiding neurological deficits. Studies have shown that surgery can achieve seizure-free in 52.6–81.4% of patients with FCD [1–3]. Standardized epilepsy presurgical evaluation, surgical treatment, and postoperative management are crucial for improving the prognosis in children with epilepsy caused by FCD. However, there are currently few epilepsy centers in China that can perform mature pediatric epilepsy surgery. There is a lack of consensus on the selection and timing of surgery for patients with FCD and epilepsy, and research findings on factors influencing postoperative seizure control vary across centers. The aims of this study were to analyze clinical data from patients with FCD and epilepsy in our center, to explore factors associated with a favorable prognosis, and to provide a reference for selecting the timing of epilepsy surgery and evaluating postoperative efficacy.

Methods

A retrospective analysis of clinical data from children who underwent surgery for epilepsy caused by FCD at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University from January 2015 to December 2024 was conducted. Their clinical characteristics were summarized, and all enrolled patients were followed up for at least six months. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Chongqing Medical University and was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki(Ethics Approval No.:IIT2025159), and written informed consents were obtained from the parents of each individual.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) All patients were diagnosed with epilepsy and underwent epilepsy surgery(first time), clear postoperative pathological diagnosis and classification meeting the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) 2022 classification criteria [4]; (2) cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination; and (3) complete medical history and examination data, with at least interictal records in the video-electroencephalography (VEEG) data; (4) Age of onset was between 1 month and 18 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients without a confirmed postoperative pathological diagnosis of FCD ; (2) Patients who were not diagnosed with epilepsy; (3)Patients who had previously undergone epilepsy surgery; (4)patients whose data were incomplete or whose follow-up period was less than 6 months.

Epilepsy presurgical evaluation

All the pediatric patients underwent preoperative VEEG monitoring and 3.0T MRI. If there were no relevant contraindications, positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) was performed with the consent of the patients’ guardians, along with PET-CT and MRI fusion imaging, to help identify the epileptogenic focus. A multidisciplinary team consisting of members of the neurology, neurosurgery, radiology, pathology, and rehabilitation departments conducted an anatomical-electroclinical evaluation based on the pediatric patients’ clinical data to determine whether surgery was indicated, the surgical approach, and postoperative management strategies.For patients with non-drug-resistant epilepsy, if their cranial imaging lesions are clearly defined and consistent with the symptom localization of seizures, interictal EEG and/or ictal EEG localization, and their families consent to epilepsy surgery, these patients will also undergo epilepsy surgery after epilepsy presurgical evaluation.

Surgical methods

The specific surgical methods included the following: (1) Epileptogenic Focus Resection: This was applied to pediatric patients whose cranial MRI showed clear and relatively localized lesions, with typical clinical manifestations of epileptic seizures, and whose electrophysiological examinations confirmed that the epileptogenic zone was consistent with the seizure symptoms; (2) Hemispheric Disconnection: This was used for pediatric patients with widespread epileptogenic zones involving the entire cerebral hemisphere; (3) Multilobar Disconnection: This was employed for pediatric patients with extensive epileptogenic zones involving multiple cerebral lobes; (4) Stereoelectroencephalography-guided Radiofrequency Thermocoagulation (SEEG-RFTC): This was applied to pediatric patients whose epileptogenic focus was identified via depth electrode implantation.

Follow-up

Follow-up was conducted through regular outpatient reviews and/or telephone interviews, typically at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-operatively and annually thereafter. Follow-up data included seizure control outcomes, postoperative use of antiseizure medications, VEEG, and cranial imaging. Seizure control outcomes were evaluated at the endpoint of follow-up (at least 6 months) according to the Engel classification system. Engel Class I: Seizures completely disappeared or only auras persisted; Engel Class II: Rare seizures (≤ 3 times per year) or ≥ 90% reduction in seizure frequency; Engel Class III: Seizures > 3 times per year but with a ≥ 75% reduction in seizure frequency; Engel Class IV: <75% reduction in seizure frequency. Patients classified as Engel Class I were categorized into the favorable prognosis group, while those in Classes II–IV were categorized into the unfavorable prognosis group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 software. Normally distributed continuous data were described using mean ± standard deviation, and independent samples t-tests were used for comparisons between groups. Skewedly distributed continuous data were described using median and interquartile range, and Mann-Whitney U tests were used for comparisons between two groups. Categorical data were described using frequency and ratio (n, %), and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used for comparisons between two groups. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to analyze factors related to poor prognosis and case analysis; a two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 97 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 57 males and 40 females. The median age at first seizure onset was 53.16 ± 37.89 months, and the median age at surgery was 82.87 ± 47.32 months. Among the patients, 72 patients (74.23%) presented with focal seizures, 10 patients (10.31%) presented with epileptic spasms, 5 patients (5.15%) presented with generalized seizures, and 10 patients (10.31%) presented with seizures of unknown origin. Additionally, 48 patients (49.48%) combined with DRE before surgery.

All patients underwent cranial MRI before surgery. The neuroimaging findings were positive in 82 patients (84.54%) and negative in 15 patients(15.46%). Furthermore, 29 patients underwent interictal PET-CT, all of whom had positive findings. Among these findings, 28 patients presented hypometabolic lesions, while only 1 showed hypermetabolic lesions(Fig. 1). Notably, all the pediatric patients with negative neuroimaging results demonstrated hypometabolic lesions on PET-CT.

All patients underwent VEEG monitoring. Habitual seizures were captured in 63 patients, all of whom presented seizures originating from a fixed location. Among these, 46 patients (73.02%) presented low-amplitude fast activity, 12 patients (19.05%) presented rhythmic spike or sharp wave patterns, 10 patients (15.87%) demonstrated paroxysmal slow wave activity, 4 patients (6.35%) presented low-voltage patterns, and 9 patients (14.29%) presented two or more types of ictal patterns. Interictal VEEG findings indicated that 70 patients (72.16%) primarily had focal discharges, 13 patients (13.40%) had multifocal discharges, 5 patients (5.15%) showed generalized discharges, and 9 patients (9.28%) had no detectable epileptiform activity. For some patients whose seizure onset zone and extent could not be precisely determined through semiology, cranial imaging, or VEEG, further intracranial EEG monitoring was performed. This included electrocorticography (ECoG) in 6 patients and SEEG in 6 patients.

All patients underwent multidisciplinary and multimodal evaluations to determine the precise location, extent, and surgical approach. Among the 97 patients, 78 patients underwent Epileptogenic Focus Resection, 15 patients underwent Multilobar Disconnection, 3 patients underwent Hemispheric Disconnection, and 1 patient underwent SEEG-RFTC. Among the cases, 32 patients presented with temporal lobe lesions, 25 had frontal lobe lesions, 26 showed involvement of other lobes or clearly defined adjacent brain regions, and 14 were identified with multilobar or extensive lesions. Postoperative pathological results indicated that among the 97 patients:17 patients (17.53%) were classified as FCD Type I (including 1 patient of Type Ia, 9 patients of Type Ib, and 7 patients of Type Ic);56 patients (57.73%) were classified as FCD Type II (including 29 patients of Type IIa and 27 patients of Type IIb);24 patients (24.74%) were classified as FCD Type III (including 8 patients of Type IIIa, 13 patients of Type IIIb, 2 patients of Type IIIc, and 1 patient of Type IIId).

The most recent follow-up results showed that 77 patients (79.38%)achieved Engel Class I, 10 patients (10.31%) achieved Engel Class II, 5patients (5.15%) achieved Engel Class III, and 5patients (5.15%) were classified as Engel Class IV.

Discussion

The most common clinical manifestation of FCD is epileptic seizures. Recurrent seizures increase the risk of injury, impair cognitive function, and reduce patients’ quality of life. Early cessation of seizures is of great significance, and surgery is an effective treatment option. This study retrospectively analyzed the surgical outcomes and related influencing factors in 97 patients with FCD-related epilepsy who underwent surgical treatment following preoperative evaluation, aiming to provide a reference for the preoperative assessment and surgical treatment of epilepsy caused by FCD. The results of this study indicate that the most common type of epileptic seizure in patients with FCD-related epilepsy is focal seizures. In the majority of FCD cases, scalp EEG shows focal interictal discharges, and the ictal onset is characterized by low-voltage fast activity. Most patients with FCD have a favorable prognosis. Surgical procedure (epileptogenic focus resection), pathological type (type II), and temporal lobe lesions are independent factors associated with a good prognosis in children with FCD.

In this case series, the most common type of epileptic seizure observed in patients with FCD combined with epilepsy was focal seizure. The vast majority of patients with FCD demonstrated focal discharges during the interictal period on VEEG, with low-amplitude fast activity onset during the ictal period. These findings are consistent with those of most previous studies [3, 5, 6]. However, some studies have reported that the types of seizures associated with FCD combined with epilepsy may present as epileptic spasms [5, 7]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the predominance of FCD type II in our cohort, as research suggests that FCD type II lesions are relatively circumscribed and are more likely to exhibit focal discharge patterns during both the ictal and interictal periods [8, 9].

The pathological grade or type of FCD is associated with surgical outcomes; however, conclusions regarding the prognosis of different pathological subtypes vary across studies. Most studies indicate that patients with FCD type II have higher rates of postoperative seizure remission, which is consistent with the findings of this study [9–13]. Some studies suggest that FCD type III is associated with a higher rate of postoperative seizure freedom [9]. In contrast, FCD type I often indicates an unfavorable prognosis [14]. Nevertheless, Keene et al. [15] and Kloss et al. [16] reported no significant differences in postoperative outcomes among patients with different FCD subtypes. Similarly, Fauser et al. [17] reported no correlation between FCD type and postoperative seizure remission rates. Complete resection of the epileptogenic focus is fundamental to achieving seizure control [18, 19], whereas incomplete resection is a significant factor contributing to postoperative seizure recurrence [20]. FCD type I is often MRI negative and tends to involve the frontal lobe or multiple lobes, which increases the risk of incomplete resection and consequently increases the likelihood of persistent seizures [9, 21]. In contrast, FCD types II and III typically present with relatively well-defined lesions on cranial MRI, focal patterns on interictal and ictal EEG, and focal characteristics on interictal Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography. These features increase the likelihood of complete resection, thereby increasing the likelihood of seizure freedom [9],a representative case from this series is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The surgical resection approach and the location of the resected lesion may be associated with prognosis. In the present study, patients who underwent lesionectomy for well-defined lesions demonstrated a more favorable prognosis than those who received other surgical approaches, and patients with lesions confined to the temporal lobe also had better outcomes. Fauser et al. [17, 22] reported higher success rates with single-lobe resection than with multilobe resection, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. Research has indicated that postoperative seizure remission rates vary by lesion location, with significantly lower remission rates observed in visual, motor, and premotor areas than in other regions [23]. This may be attributed to the heightened caution exercised during neurosurgical resection of lesions near the functional cortex. Jayalakshmi et al. [8] reported that frontal lobe and multilobar FCD were associated with poorer seizure control, whereas temporal lobe FCD was linked to greater seizure remission. Multiple studies have indicated that temporal lesionectomy is associated with a favorable prognosis in children with FCD [18, 23–25], which may be related to the relatively small number of critical functional areas involved in the temporal lobe.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents an analysis of the clinical characteristics and the outcomes related to prognosis in pediatric patients with epilepsy caused by FCD at our center. The findings indicate that focal seizures were the most common seizure type, whereas focal discharges and low-amplitude fast activity were the most frequent interictal and ictal EEG patterns, respectively. Epileptogenic focus resection, FCD type II, and temporal lobe lesions were identified as independent factors associated with a favorable prognosis in children with FCD. However, it should be noted that the data in this cohort were derived from a single-center retrospective study, which inevitably introduces selection bias. Additionally, as the study population consisted of patients with FCD and epilepsy, some individuals in the early stages of the disease presented relatively few seizures, making it impossible to obtain ictal EEG recordings. Consequently, the predictive value of ictal EEG for prognosis could not be further evaluated. Our research team plans to expand the sample size in future studies and conduct multicenter, long-term follow-up research to more accurately evaluate the clinical characteristics of pediatric epilepsy caused by FCD and to analyze in depth the factors influencing postoperative seizure control outcomes.
