Preventing distal junctional kyphosis: Choosing a stable end for the lowest-instrumented vertebra is protective following adult cervical deformity surgery
Authors: Pawel P. Jankowski, Paritash Tahmasebpour, Oluwatobi O. Onafowokan, Max R. Fisher, Ethan Cottrill, Caroline M. Wu, Alexander Parsons, Khoi Than, Spencer Matthews, Louis Boissiere, John F. Burke, Peter G. Passias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurpose:This study investigates the role of a stable end vertebra (SEV) in surgical planning for adult cervical deformity (ACD). While the stable sagittal vertebra (SSV) is an established landmark in adolescent scoliosis, an equivalent concept in ACD is undefined. The objective was to assess whether selecting the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) at an SEV reduces the risk of distal junctional kyphosis (DJK) and related complications.Methods:A retrospective cohort study was conducted using a single-center, single-surgeon database of cervical deformity records. Patients with baseline and 2-year radiographic data were included. SEV was defined by three criteria: (1) LIV inclination angle ≥10°°, (2) LIV at or distal to the SSV, and (3) LIV Hounsfield unit >210. The primary outcome was DJK, defined as ≥≥10°° kyphosis between the superior endplate of the LIV and the inferior endplate of the vertebra two levels below. Multivariate regression adjusted for age and baseline deformity.Results:Among 120 patients (mean age, 58.5 ± 10 years; 60% female), 21 met the SEV criteria. DJK occurred in 20.6% overall but only 9.5% of SEV patients (vs. 30%, P = 0.009). SEV was associated with lower odds of DJK (odds ratio [OR], 0.28), distal junctional failure (OR, 0.03), and reoperation (OR, 0.016). SEV patients also had fewer radiographic and perioperative complications and were more likely to achieve neck disability index minimal clinically important difference (OR 2.8).Conclusion:Using SEV to guide LIV selection significantly reduces mechanical and clinical complications and improves outcomes in ACD surgery. It should be prioritized in preoperative planning.

INTRODUCTION

Adult cervical deformity (ACD) presents a unique surgical challenge due to its heterogeneous etiologies, complex biomechanical demands, and its association with profound functional impairment and reduced quality of life (QoL).[12] Unlike thoracolumbar deformity, cervical realignment must be achieved within a narrow corridor constrained by the spinal cord and nerve roots, limiting both the magnitude and direction of correction.[3] Despite advances in classification systems and surgical techniques – including three-column osteotomies, posterior-based realignment strategies, and combined anterior–posterior reconstructions – postoperative mechanical complications remain unacceptably common.[4]

Among these complications, distal junctional kyphosis (DJK) is among the most consequential, with reported rates as high as 35%. DJK disrupts postoperative alignment and is strongly associated with revision surgery, neurologic decline, and poorer patient-reported outcomes.[5] Historically, surgical planning in ACD has emphasized selecting an appropriate distal fixation level, as the proximal junction is rarely the site of failure in cervical constructs.[67]

In thoracolumbar deformity surgery, the stable sagittal vertebra (SSV) concept has proven effective in reducing junctional pathology by extending fusion to biomechanically neutral segments.[7] However, this principle has not been fully translated to ACD, where transitional biomechanics at the cervicothoracic junction (CTJ) generate concentrated stress between the mobile cervical spine and the rigid thoracic region.[8] Selecting a lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) at an unstable or kyphotic level may therefore exacerbate sagittal loading and increase the risk of mechanical failure.[9]

Emerging biomechanical and clinical work suggests that selecting a stable end vertebra (SEV) – defined as the most distal vertebra aligned with the central plumb line and without adjacent segment kyphosis – may reduce DJK, improve construct longevity, and enhance postoperative outcomes.[10] While global or regional sagittal malalignment is a well-established driver of junctional failure, abrupt changes in stiffness and load transfer at the CTJ may further heighten susceptibility when alignment is suboptimal.[1112] The SEV framework aims to systematize distal level selection using reproducible radiographic criteria rather than relying solely on surgical experience or convention.

Despite its conceptual appeal, SEV has not been formally defined or validated in the cervical spine. No prior study has established cervical-specific SEV criteria or examined their relationship with postoperative mechanical and functional outcomes. In this study, we aim to: (1) define radiographic markers of SEV at the CTJ, (2) determine the incidence of DJK when fusion terminates at an SEV versus a non-SEV level, and (3) assess associated radiographic and functional outcomes.

Although Yang et al. demonstrated that SSV-guided LIV selection reduced DJK in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis,[13] cervical and cervicothoracic biomechanics differ fundamentally from thoracolumbar models.[1415] Therefore, we propose a comprehensive SEV definition incorporating sagittal inclination, anatomic neutrality, and local bone quality as a cervical-specific stability framework. The goal of this study is to evaluate whether terminating fusion at an SEV reduces DJK, mechanical failure, and the need for reoperation in ACD surgery.

METHODS

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was derived from a single-center adult spinal deformity registry that has been maintained continuously and prospectively since 2012 and remains ongoing.

Setting

The registry aimed to record perioperative, radiographic, and functional outcomes in patients with complex spinal deformities, including ACD. Standardized radiographic and health-related quality of life (HRQL) assessments were performed at baseline, 6 weeks, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years after surgery.

Participants

Eligible patients were 18 years or older and had a preoperative plan for surgical correction of cervical deformity (CD). CD was defined using validated radiographic parameters, requiring at least one of the following:

Inclusion also required long-segment fusion with an upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) above C7 and complete baseline and ≥2-year radiographic data. HRQL data were collected when available, but were not required for inclusion in radiographic analyses. Deformity rigidity was assessed on dynamic flexion–extension cervical radiographs, with rigid deformity defined as <10° of angular change across the apex and flexible deformity as ≥10°. This classification was used to reflect heterogeneity in deformity and align with prior ACD literature.

To clarify cohort formation, all CD surgeries performed during the study period were reviewed. No additional cases were excluded for failing to meet radiographic deformity criteria, and no procedures with a UIV at C6 needed exclusion based on validated ACD definitions. The only exclusions were the 22 patients without a complete 2-year radiographic follow-up. Patients missing HRQL data were retained for radiographic analyses to minimize unnecessary exclusions and reduce selection bias. All 120 included patients met at least one validated ACD deformity criterion, and no isolated mild sagittal malalignments without clinical deformity were included.

Variables

The main exposure was SEV status, defined by three radiographic stability criteria:

Outcomes included DJK, distal junctional failure (DJF), reoperation, radiographic alignment parameters, and HRQL metrics. The HU threshold was based on prior work linking lower HU at the UIV/UIV + 1 with increased risk of junctional mechanical failure in long-segment constructs,[16] and we selected a conservative cutoff of >210 HU to define adequate local bone quality. Although cervical-specific HU thresholds are limited, this approach reflects established biomechanical correlations between HU and screw purchase stability. SEV status indicates whether the surgeon-selected LIV meets all three criteria. Constructs ending at levels that fail any criterion are classified as SEV−. Therefore, SEV is not a patient-specific anatomical classification but rather a designation of the distal fixation level.

LIV inclination angle was measured as the angle between the LIV superior endplate and the horizontal. Threshold selection (>−10°) was based on biomechanical evidence demonstrating increased junctional stress in highly kyphotic distal segments.

Because SEV requires all three criteria to be met simultaneously, only a minority of patients reach SEV thresholds, reflecting the strict biomechanical parameters needed to define a radiographically stable distal segment.

Data sources and measurements

Radiographic evaluation included full-length standing AP/lateral radiographs and dynamic flexion–extension films, supplemented by Établissement d’Imagerie Orthopédique Système (EOS) imaging when available. Measurements were performed using Surgimap software and included C2-7 Cobb angle, TS − CL, cSVA, CBVA, McGregor’s slope, C2 slope, and C2–T3 angle.

HRQL outcomes included SWAL-QoL, neck disability index (NDI), numerical rating scale-neck, Modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score, and euroqol 5-dimension questionnaire. Neurologic severity was measured with the mJOA score. Frailty was not considered as a study variable. All deformity definitions adhere to validated ACD radiographic criteria described in Jackson-Fowl et al., 2024, and Passias et al., 2018,[34] ensuring that the cohort accurately represents actual CD.

Bias

Including only patients with complete radiographic follow-up can introduce selection bias; however, 2-year radiographs are necessary for accurate DJK detection, as it often presents late. HRQL measures were not required for radiographic analysis to prevent unnecessary exclusion.

We acknowledge that excluding 22 patients due to incomplete 2-year imaging might introduce bias. However, demographic and baseline radiographic profiles did not differ significantly between excluded and included patients, reducing the likelihood that outcome associations were substantially affected.

Study size

The final study included 120 patients, all of whom met the deformity criteria and had complete imaging for at least 2 years. Of 142 eligible cases, 22 were excluded for missing final radiographic follow-up.

Quantitative variables

Continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations. Categorical outcomes were summarized as proportions.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics, radiographic measures, and outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were compared with independent two-tailed Student’s t-tests; categorical variables with Pearson’s Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Binary logistic regression identified predictors of mechanical failure and adverse outcomes, whereas linear regression assessed associations between SEV status and improvement in HRQL. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical considerations

This study received Institutional Review Board approval, and all participants provided written informed consent. Data supporting this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

RESULTS

Participant flow and cohort characteristics

Patients were classified as SEV+ if the surgeon-selected LIV met all three SEV criteria; those whose LIV did not meet all three criteria were classified as SEV−. Of the 142 patients screened, 22 were excluded due to incomplete 2-year radiographic follow-up, resulting in a final analytic cohort of 120 patients. Among these, 21 patients met SEV criteria (SEV+), and 99 did not (SEV−). No patients were lost to follow-up after enrollment, and no baseline or outcome data were missing.

This cohort spans 12 years of continuous enrollment in a prospectively maintained registry at a tertiary spine deformity center. Baseline deformity severity – including cSVA, TS − CL, and segmental kyphosis parameters – confirmed that most patients meet validated ACD definitions rather than having mild or incidental malalignment.

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was similar between groups (SEV+: 64.3 ± 8.5 years vs. SEV−: 62.1 ± 10.2 years, P = 0.40), as was sex distribution (48% vs. 50% female, P = 0.85). Compared with SEV− patients, the SEV+ cohort showed more severe baseline deformity and disability, including higher NDI (55.2 ± 17.8 vs. 42.7 ± 15.4, P = 0.008), larger cSVA (47.5 ± 14.0 mm vs. 30.3 ± 12.5 mm, P < 0.001), and greater loss of lordosis (C2-7 lordosis–8.4° ± 18.5° vs. + 4.1° ± 12.2°, P = 0.002). Comorbidity burden and ASA class (≥III: 24% vs. 20%, P = 0.68) were similar.

Adverse outcomes and complications

Adverse outcomes – defined as failure to meet composite clinical success criteria – occurred in 35 patients (29.2%). Detailed outcome comparisons by SEV status are presented in Table 2. SEV status was a significant predictor of outcome. SEV+ patients had substantially fewer adverse outcomes compared to SEV− patients (9.5% vs. 30.0%; absolute risk reduction [ARR] 20.5%, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 5%–36%; P = 0.009). In univariate analysis, SEV was linked to a threefold decrease in the odds of an adverse outcome (odds ratio [OR]: 0.30; 95% CI: 0.10–0.70; P < 0.01).

Functional outcomes

NDI scores improved significantly across the entire cohort, decreasing from 45.0 ± 14.0 at baseline to 20.5 ± 12.3 at the final follow-up (average improvement: 24.5 points; P < 0.001). SEV+ patients showed greater improvement compared to SEV− patients (28.0 ± 13.5 vs. 22.0 ± 11.0; between-group difference: 6.0 points; 95% CI: 0.2–11.8; P = 0.04).

A clinically meaningful improvement in NDI was observed in 75% of SEV+ patients compared to 50% of SEV− patients (ARR 25%; 95% CI: 3%–47%; OR: 3.00; 95% CI: 1.10–8.16; P = 0.030).

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis excluding all patients with flexible deformity (<10° angular change on dynamic films) resulted in a subset of 82 patients with rigid deformity (SEV+ =15; SEV− =67). DJK remained significantly less common among SEV+ patients (13.3% vs. 42.0%). SEV continued to show a protective effect (OR = 0.25; 95% CI: 0.07–0.84; P = 0.026), consistent with the primary analysis (OR = 0.28). DJF rates were similarly lower in SEV+ patients (6.7% vs. 19.4%; OR = 0.29; P = 0.041).

These results confirm that the SEV effect is not caused by flexible or mild deformities and that the association remains within a strictly rigid-deformity group.

Although the SEV+ subgroup was small, effect sizes across radiographic outcomes, perioperative complications, DJK, DJF, and HRQL consistently favored SEV, supporting the robustness and clinical relevance of the association despite the limited sample size.

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective analysis, we examined the incidence and predictors of DJK after ACD surgery, focusing on the effect of ending the construct at a radiographically stable LIV as defined by SEV criteria. Our results show that constructs ending at an unstable distal level were significantly more likely to develop DJK and mechanical failure. These findings support previous research indicating that upper thoracic LIVs (e.g., T1 or T2) provide better biomechanical support than lower cervical endpoints, such as C7, thereby reducing junctional stress at the CTJ.[5171819] SEV-based LIV selection is, therefore, a modifiable surgical factor with important implications for long-term outcomes.

The difference between our findings and earlier SSV-focused research might stem from the multidimensional nature of the SEV definition. While the SSV concept primarily relies on vertical alignment with a plumb line, SEV incorporates the vertebral inclination angle and local bone quality – two factors that directly influence construct stiffness and the transfer of mechanical loads. This broader perspective on stability more accurately represents biomechanical vulnerability at the CTJ and could explain the stronger predictive power observed in this study.

The overall DJK incidence in our cohort (19.3%) is consistent with previously reported rates of 19%–38% in ACD surgery.[20212223] Nearly half of the patients who experienced DJK required revision surgery, highlighting the significant morbidity associated with this complication and supporting the findings of Passias et al. and Ye et al.[1524] These data emphasize the clinical importance of preventing DJK as a key factor for surgical durability.

Consistent with previous studies that show inadequate construct planning and failure to restore physiologic alignment increase the risk of junctional pathology,[19252627] our results indicate that selecting a radiographically stable distal endpoint predicts both mechanical and clinical outcomes. SEV+ patients had significantly lower rates of DJK (12% vs. 40%) and mechanical failure (14.2% vs. 45.4%) than SEV− patients,[2829] highlighting the importance of stability-based LIV selection. Additionally, SEV+ patients showed greater improvements in functional outcomes, including a larger reduction in the NDI and a higher likelihood of achieving clinically meaningful benefits (75% vs. 50%).[30] These findings suggest that biomechanical stability at the distal endpoint leads to significant improvements in patient-reported health.

Reoperation rates further emphasize the impact of SEV-based planning: SEV+ patients were 98% less likely to require revision surgery within 2 years. Since reoperation rates of 20%–35% are typically reported in ACD surgery due to DJK and related failures,[2325] the notable reduction here is clinically significant. In addition, perioperative complications were lower in the SEV+ group (9.5% vs. 25.3%), likely because of improved construct biomechanics and more effective surgical planning.

While achieving proper global cervical and thoracic alignment remains a primary goal in deformity correction, our findings highlight the added advantage of stability-based LIV selection. SEV+ patients demonstrated superior correction of sagittal parameters – including cSVA and regional alignment[27] – and were more likely to meet established alignment standards, such as cSVA <37 mm and normalized thoracic kyphosis.[112930] These findings suggest that SEV-guided planning may help achieve age-adjusted alignment goals, thereby further reducing junctional stress.

Few previous studies have used a reproducible radiographic stability framework for LIV selection at the CTJ. Our research fills this gap by showing that SEV criteria are clinically relevant, easy to apply, and associated with significant improvements in radiographic, functional, and mechanical outcomes. The SEV model provides a practical, evidence-based method for LIV selection, reducing dependence on subjective judgment and potentially lowering inter-surgeon variability.

From a statistical perspective, although the SEV+ subgroup was relatively small, the consistent effect sizes across all measured endpoints – including DJK, DJF, reoperation, perioperative complications, and HRQL – strongly suggest a real underlying biomechanical signal rather than random variation.

The implications of this study extend beyond radiographic outcomes. SEV-guided LIV selection functions as a scalable surgical-planning tool that can be integrated into training programs, hospital workflows, and future clinical decision-support systems. By applying objective radiographic stability criteria in preoperative planning, surgeons can more effectively assess risk and customize treatment strategies. This model offers a consistent, measurable framework for LIV selection in complex ACD reconstructions, encouraging greater standardization and potentially improving outcomes across diverse practice environments.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It reflects the experience of a single surgeon at one institution and may limit generalizability. Its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and precludes causal inference. Although radiographic assessments were standardized, measurement variability and unmeasured confounders – including baseline pathology, osteotomy level, comorbidities, and individual biomechanics – remain potential sources of bias.

Excluding 22 patients without complete 2-year imaging may also introduce bias; however, their baseline demographic and radiographic characteristics were similar to those of the included patients, mitigating this concern. Despite spanning more than a decade of consecutive data collection, the cohort remains modest by deformity-surgery standards. Because the analysis focused on cervical and cervicothoracic alignment, global thoracolumbar parameters were not assessed; future work incorporating full-body alignment is needed to refine SEV criteria.

A key limitation is the small number of SEV+ patients (n = 21), reflecting the strictness – and relative rarity – of meeting all three SEV criteria. Although this reduces statistical power, the consistent direction and magnitude of effects across all analyses, including restricted sensitivity analyses, support the robustness of the SEV signal. Multicenter prospective validation will be essential to confirm these findings and optimize LIV selection guidelines.

CONCLUSION

Selecting a radiographically SEV as the LIV in ACD surgery is independently associated with markedly lower rates of DJK, mechanical failure, and revision surgery. Constructs ending at unstable levels were more than twice as likely to develop DJK, underscoring the biomechanical importance of distal stability. SEV-guided planning was also linked to greater functional improvement and higher rates of meaningful clinical recovery.

These findings support incorporating SEV criteria into routine preoperative planning as a practical, modifiable strategy to enhance the durability and clinical success of complex cervical reconstructions. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the SEV framework and establish standardized guidelines for LIV selection.
