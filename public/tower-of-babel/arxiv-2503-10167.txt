“Well, Keep Thinking” : Enhancing LLM Reasoning with Adaptive Injection Decoding
Hyunbin Jin, Je Won Yeom, Seunghyun Bae, Taesup Kim

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit strong reasoning abilities, often attributed to few-shot or zero-shot chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting. While effective, these methods require labor-intensive prompt engineering, raising the question of whether reasoning can be induced without reliance on explicit prompts. In this work, we unlock the reasoning capabilities of LLMs without explicit prompting. Inspired by zero-shot CoT and CoT-decoding, we propose a novel decoding strategy that systematically nudges LLMs to continue reasoning, thereby preventing immature reasoning processes. Specifically, we monitor the model’s generation and inject a designated phrase whenever it is likely to conclude its response prematurely, before completing the reasoning process. Our experimental evaluations on diverse reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that our proposed strategy substantially improves LLM reasoning capabilities, highlighting the potential of decoding-based interventions as an alternative to traditional prompting techniques.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing (NLP), demonstrating remarkable performance across a wide range of tasks, including question-answering, code generation, and machine translation.
Scaling up model size has led to improvements in fluency, generalization, and sample efficiency
Wei et al. (2022b)
.
However, despite these advancements, LLMs continue to struggle with complex reasoning tasks such as arithmetic, commonsense reasoning, and multi-step logical inference.
To address these challenges, researchers have primarily relied on prompt engineering techniques, such as few-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting and zero-shot CoT
(
Brown et al., 2020
;
Besta et al., 2023
;
Madaan et al., 2023
;
Wei et al., 2022a
;
Yao et al., 2023
)
.
These approaches guide LLMs by providing structured reasoning examples or explicit step-by-step instructions, significantly improving performance on various reasoning benchmarks.
However, they exhibit key limitations in that few-shot prompting requires human-crafted exemplars, while zero-shot prompting is highly sensitive to prompt phrasing.
More recently, some studies have explored CoT reasoning without prompts by modifying the decoding strategy
(
Wang and Zhou, 2023
;
Wang et al., 2023a
)
.
Additionally, a test-time scaling method has been introduced, which intervenes during inference to control test-time compute
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
)
.

Despite various efforts to enhance LLM reasoning, existing approaches have not directly addressed the fundamental causes of their reasoning failures. Through our analysis, we categorize these failures into three primary types, highlighting the need for a more targeted intervention.
First, models terminate generation without producing a response to given questions, returning only the <eos> token, a phenomenon we refer to as
silence
. Second, models often produce responses that lack actual logical reasoning, responding with uninformative outputs or irrelevant patterns, such as numeric sequences or question repetition, a failure type we call
no reasoning
. Finally, in some cases, models initiate reasoning but halt prematurely, leading to incomplete or logically inconsistent answers, which we refer to as
incomplete reasoning
.
These failure types suggest that LLMs do not inherently lack reasoning ability, rather their reasoning processes are disrupted or prematurely terminated during decoding.

To address these reasoning failures, we posed a fundamental question:
“How do humans think effectively when solving complex problems? What strategies help people avoid reasoning failures, and how do they enrich their thought process to reach accurate conclusions?”
.
When solving complex problems, humans rarely produce an immediate answer.
Instead, they pause, reflect, and often restart their thought process before reaching a final conclusion.
When encountering difficulties, people frequently engage in self-prompting mechanisms, such as asking themselves guiding questions, articulating partial thoughts, or seeking alternative perspectives to expand their reasoning.
These cognitive strategies ensure that reasoning continues rather than stopping prematurely, allowing for deeper and more thorough problem-solving.

Inspired by this cognitive process, we propose a novel test-time intervention technique based on an adaptive decoding strategy.
Rather than fully relying on a greedy decoding strategy, our method dynamically intervenes with a designated injection phrase during inference when the model’s reasoning process is on the verge of prematurely terminating or drifting away from logical reasoning.

Similar to how humans engage in self-reflection to sustain their reasoning process, our adaptive injection decoding strategy serves as an
implicit signal
that prevents premature termination and encourages adequate reasoning.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

We identify the
immature reasoning
as a major limitation in LLMs and systematically classify common reasoning failures.

•

We propose a novel test-time intervention that extends reasoning dynamically with an
adaptive injection decoding
strategy, ensuring logical continuity.

•

Our approach can significantly improve reasoning performance across reasoning benchmarks and is computationally efficient, making it suitable for practical real-world applications.

Observations: Immature Reasoning

We conducted an experiment using LLaMA 3.1-8B on the GSM8K dataset to assess the reasoning performance of LLMs and identify the underlying factors contributing to their suboptimal performance.
Our analysis highlights several challenges affecting the model’s reasoning abilities while also revealing insights into its latent potential.
The following sections summarize key observations, with specific examples provided in Appendix
D
.

We identify
Immature Reasoning
as a major factor behind the poor reasoning performance of LLMs.
Immature reasoning refers to instances where the model prematurely halts or drifting away from logical reasoning process, leading to incomplete, irrelevant, or absent responses.
Our analysis of the model’s responses revealed three distinct types of immature reasoning.

(Observation 1)
Silence

The model fails to generate any response despite receiving an input question.
This typically occurs when the model gives up on reasoning and refrains from attempting an answer.
In our evaluation of LLaMA 3.1-8B on GSM8k, 58.05% of incorrect responses fell into this category.
Refer to Appendix
D.1
for examples of this category.

(Observation 2)
No Reasoning

The model generates a response but does not engage in actual reasoning.
Instead, it follows learned response patterns, repeats the question, or outputs irrelevant content (e.g., numeric sequences or HTML) without attempting to solve the problem logically.
This issue accounted for 31.7% of incorrect responses.
Refer to Appendix
D.2
for examples of this category.

(Observation 3)
Incomplete Reasoning

The model initiates reasoning but stops short of completing the necessary steps to solve the problem.
It may prematurely terminate its reasoning process or deviate from the original problem-solving task, focusing on irrelevant examples or formats.
This type of failure represented 5.45% of incorrect responses.
Refer to Appendix
D.3
for examples of this category.

Despite these challenges, we observed that a substantial portion of responses that exhibited appropriate reasoning successfully arrived at correct solutions.
In our evaluation of LLaMA 3.1-8B on GSM8k, 32.22% of responses that engaged in proper CoT reasoning yielded correct answers.
This suggests that LLMs are not inherently deficient in problem-solving capabilities.
Instead, immature reasoning disrupts the reasoning process, preventing models from fully realizing their potential.

3
Adaptive Injection Decoding

To mitigate
immature reasoning
, we introduce a novel decoding strategy.
Our approach,
Adaptive Injection Decoding (AID)
, dynamically intervenes in such cases by injecting a predefined
injection phrase
(e.g.,
‘‘Well’’
) as the next token whenever the probability of generating the
<eos>
token meets a specified criterion.
This method is designed to prevent the model from prematurely concluding its reasoning process or deviating logical reasoning.

Given an input query
x
as an input prompt, the model
f
generates a sequence of response tokens
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{t}
through greedy decoding.
At each decoding step
t
, if the
<eos>
token ranks among the top-
k
next token predictions, it is replaced with injection phrase
p
.
The updated response
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{t},p
is then fed back into the model, ensuring continuity of reasoning.
The algorithmic details of our method are provided in Algorithm
1
.

To properly prevent premature termination and encourage reasoning, we rigorously evaluated various injection phrases, including single words, conjunctions, full sentences, and machine language expressions.
Among these,
‘‘Well’’
consistently outperformed other alternatives, as shown in Table
2
, and was selected for all subsequent experiments.
Further details on the experimental analysis are discussed later in Section
4.3
.

Additionally, our experiments revealed distinct model-specific tendencies in handling the
<eos>
token, particularly in how early and strongly it is considered during generation. LLaMA-3.1-8B tends to terminate responses earlier, whereas Mistral-7B-v0.3 and Gemma-7B generate longer outputs. However, despite their delayed termination, Mistral-7B-v0.3 and Gemma-7B consistently ranked
<eos>
among the top candidates from the early stages of generation. This suggests that while they had a higher potential for early termination, alternative tokens were ultimately selected.
Building on this observation, we explored replacing injection phrases when
<eos>
appeared in the top-
k
candidates and further investigated optimal top-
k
values for each model in subsequent experiments (see Section
4.3
for details).

Arithmetic Reasoning

In MultiArith, each model achieves at least a 150% increase in accuracy simply by injecting our proposed phrase
“Well”
during inference, even without using zero-shot-CoT prompting.

Commonsense and Logical Reasoning

Along with arithmetic reasoning, we also observe significant improvements in commonsense and logical reasoning tasks.
For instance, Mistral-7B-v0.3 achieves a +20.53% performance gain (16.30% → 36.83%) in StrategyQA and a +18.80% increase (13.20% → 32.00%) in DisambiguationQA under zero-shot-CoT with our method. These results highlight that even simple intervention during decoding can yield substantial performance gains across a wide range of reasoning tasks.

Enhancing Prompt-based Reasoning

Our method integrates seamlessly with prompting techniques, consistently improving performance compared to using prompting alone.
As shown in Table

Notably, this improvement is nearly twice as large as that achieved in the zero-shot setting.
For example, in MultiArith, where zero-shot prompting already provides some improvement, the application of our method results in additional performance gains.

Recovery from Ineffective Prompting

In certain cases, utilizing zero-shot-CoT prompts can reduce accuracy compared to not using any prompts, as demonstrated in Table

However, our method effectively mitigates these adverse effects, either restoring or even surpassing performance observed prior to the application of prompts.
For instance, in StrategyQA, applying a zero-shot-CoT prompt reduces Mistral-7B-v0.3’s accuracy from 23.58% to 16.30%.
In contrast, injecting our method boosts accuracy to accuracy to 36.83%, effectively counteracting the negative effects of prompting and surpassing the model’s previous best performance on this dataset.
A similar trend is observed in Logical Deduction, further validating the effectiveness of our approach.

Ablation Study and Analysis

Injection Phrase Analysis

Building on our findings in Section
2
, we now examine how different candidate phrases can help prevent abrupt termination and encourage more complete reasoning. The goal is to nudge the model toward continuing its chain of thought, rather than concluding too soon.

To compare how different injection words or phrases influence performance, we conducted a comprehensive ablation study, as shown in Table
2
.
Among all tested options, “
Well
” proved to be the most consistently effective and was selected for our experiments.

We interpret “
Well
” stands out because it provides a neutral yet effective nudge, encouraging the model to continue reasoning without introducing restrictive biases. Unlike phrases like “
I might be wrong.
” or “
Keep reasoning,
” which explicitly direct the model’s confidence, “
Well
” does not steer the reasoning in a particular direction. It also avoids forcing a specific reasoning trajectory, unlike conjunction words, and does not impose a rigid structured reasoning path, unlike step-indicating words. By sidestepping these constraints, “
Well
” serves as a subtle yet impactful cue, fostering deeper reasoning while preserving flexibility across various problem types.

Our finding aligns with prior work showing that large language models are highly sensitive to short prompts or cues, including those that subtly convey the need for additional reasoning steps
(
Kim et al., 2024
)
. Further details and examples of our injection-phrase experiments are provided in Appendix
A

Model Scales

On the AQUA dataset, we found that at smaller scales (LLaMA-3.1-8B), the performance gains were rather minimal. However, once the model size increased to around 10B, the improvements became more evident. This indicates that no matter how much we refine the inherent capabilities, if the model itself does not have sufficient internal knowledge to begin with, the impact remains limited. In contrast, larger models tend to benefit more on challenging tasks, suggesting that once a model’s internal capacity reaches a certain threshold, our method can further unlock its reasoning abilities.

For example, examining AQUA on smaller Llama models shows that the difference between Zero-shot (29.13%) and Zero-shot CoT (28.74%) is negligible. Yet, when moving to a 10B model, we see a clear boost in performance. This implies there is a point below which smaller models cannot substantially improve, even if we attempt to unlock their inherent reasoning capacity. Meanwhile, Falcon3-10B-Base
(
Team, 2024

Related Works

A variety of methods have been explored to enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). These techniques can be broadly categorized into
tuning-based
,
prompt-based
,
sampling-based
, and
test-time
approaches. Below, we discuss how our decoding-based strategy relates to these prior efforts, especially in its ability to improve reasoning without extensive additional resources.

Tuning-Based Approaches

Early research on boosting LLM reasoning focused on fine-tuning or distillation. For instance, instruction tuning
(
Wei et al., 2021
)
trains models on a broad set of tasks and instructions, improving zero-shot performance across multiple NLP benchmarks. System 2 distillation
(
Yu et al., 2023
)
further refines model reasoning by transferring multi-step solutions from a larger teacher model. Although these methods can substantially enhance performance, they typically require large datasets and significant computational resources. Additionally, recent exploration into symbolic or chain-of-thought distillation
(
Li et al., 2024
)
still relies heavily on the quality of the larger model’s outputs. Our work diverges from these in that we tackle reasoning failures purely at inference time, without additional model training or data curation.

Prompt Engineering

Prompt-based methods have garnered significant attention as a more resource-efficient alternative to fine-tuning. Chain-of-thought (CoT)
(
Wei et al., 2022a
)
structures responses into step-by-step explanations, while extensions such as Tree-of-Thoughts
(
Yao et al., 2023
)
and Graph-of-Thoughts
(
Besta et al., 2023
)
reorganize the reasoning flow into more complex hierarchies. Other lines of work employ iterative refinement, as in Self-Refine
(
Madaan et al., 2023
)
, where the model iteratively critiques and updates its solution. Although these approaches can boost LLM reasoning, they often involve substantial prompt engineering or multiple rounds of inference, which can be costly in practice. By contrast, our method obviates heavy prompt tuning and avoids repeated sampling, opting instead for a lightweight, decoding-centric solution.

Sampling-Based Methods

Approaches based on sampling seek to improve reliability of reasoning in LLMs by generating multiple candidate solutions. For example, self-consistency sampling
(
Wang et al., 2023a
)
aggregates multiple outputs to select a consensus or majority-vote answer, while universal self-consistency
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
generalizes this concept to tasks where multiple lines of reasoning may converge on the correct outcome. These methods benefit from exploring diverse reasoning paths but come at the cost of multiple forward passes through the model. In many settings, this extra computational burden can be prohibitive. Our approach, in contrast, requires only one pass; we adaptively intervene during decoding to extend the chain of reasoning rather than generating multiple completions.

Test-Time Strategies

More recent investigations have shifted attention to inference-time or post-processing methods that address reasoning shortcomings without altering the base model or training regimen.
Wang et al. (2023b)
introduces a penalty mechanism for abrupt shifts in reasoning flow, while
Snell et al. (2023)
proposes adaptive inference that strategically allocates resources for verification. Budget forcing
(
Muennighoff et al., 2025
)
constrains or terminates chain-of-thought decoding under a specified computational budget. Although these methods highlight the potential of inference-level interventions, many still rely on specialized heuristics or additional compute to manage extended reasoning steps.

By contrast, our work unlocks LLMs’ latent reasoning abilities
without explicit prompting
or additional overhead. Specifically, we reduce
immature reasoning
by inserting a brief injection phrase whenever the model risks early termination with the
<eos>
token. This simple yet powerful approach integrates seamlessly into a standard decoding pipeline and has proven effective in mitigating abrupt endings across diverse reasoning tasks.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the challenge of
immature reasoning
in large language models (LLMs), wherein the model’s reasoning process terminates prematurely, leading to
silence
,
no reasoning
, or
incomplete reasoning
. Our observations show that despite the substantial capacity of modern LLMs, this issue remains a major source of error in multi-step problem-solving.

To mitigate this, we proposed a novel decoding strategy. Rather than relying heavily on prompt engineering, our approach injects a designated “nudging” word whenever the model is likely to produce the
<eos>
token prematurely. This lightweight, test-time intervention encourages continued reasoning, acting as an implicit signal that mirrors human thinking process.

Extensive experiments across diverse reasoning tasks–including arithmetic, commonsense, and logical reasoning–demonstrate that our method substantially improves performance in both zero-shot and zero-shot-CoT settings. Moreover, it enhances reasoning stability, compensating for ineffective prompts and adapting to varying task complexities.

Overall, our proposed method, Adaptive Injection Decoding, represents a promising direction for unlocking the latent reasoning potential of LLMs while maintaining computational efficiency and ease of deployment. Future work could explore the dynamic selection of injected phrases based on task characteristics, the extension of our method to more complex generation tasks, or the integration with advanced sampling strategies (e.g., self-consistency, tree-of-thought decoding) to further enhance robustness and reliability.

Limitations

While our method significantly enhances LLM performance, several limitations warrant attention. First, we tested models up to only 10 billion parameters, so the generalizability to extremely small models or much larger systems remains to be explored. Second, our approach relies on a simple injection phrase (“
Well
”) identified to perform robustly in experiments. Although it was proven effective on multiple tasks, variations in domain or language may require additional tuning of this trigger. Third, our experiments focus on reasoning benchmarks in English; extending to multilingual scenarios might reveal different patterns of
immature reasoning
and alternative effective triggers.

We use

Disregard auxiliary examples or sub-problems introduced by the LLM as part of its reasoning, focusing strictly on the main query and final response.

Consider minor variations in phrasing valid, as long as the intended meaning remains correct.

5.

For True/False questions, infer correctness from the overall meaning of the response (e.g., a statement affirming or denying a proposition) when an explicit “true” or “false” label is not provided.
