The Role of Traditional Chinese Medicine in Acute Lung Injury: Targeting Programmed Cell Death
Authors: Guoxiong Hao, Guanwei Wang, Xiali Liang, Qian Xu, Bingge Han, Fu Li, Shuming Guo, Zhiming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute lung injury (ALI) is a critical clinical condition with high mortality and morbidity. It initially manifests as hypoxemia, pulmonary interstitial edema, and pulmonary inflammation, and may progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome, posing a serious threat to human health. Conventional treatments have certain limitations, underscoring the urgent need for new therapeutic strategies. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), with its advantages of low cost, low risk of drug resistance, and fewer adverse effects, has accumulated extensive experience in ALI treatment through its multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway regulatory effects. Research has shown that various TCM-derived active compounds from plants, animals, or microorganisms can effectively regulate programmed cell death (PCD), thereby reducing inflammatory responses, suppressing oxidative stress, and ameliorating ALI-induced lung tissue damage. Against this backdrop, this review aims to elucidate the mechanisms by which traditional Chinese medicine improves ALI by regulating individual and interacting PCD pathways, and to systematically evaluate the impact of existing evidence and barriers to translation on the clinical application potential of this therapeutic strategy. Based on a systematic literature search in databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Science Direct, and Google Scholar for studies published up to December 2025, this review comprehensively summarizes the pharmacological actions, mechanisms, efficacy, and safety of TCM with PCD-regulating functions, and proposes a novel TCM-PCD-ALI regulatory axis, aiming to fill the current gap in understanding the relationship between PCD and ALI, provide more treatment options for patients with ALI, and advance the development of therapeutic strategies in this field.

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a prevalent inflammatory respiratory disorder characterised by uncontrolled pulmonary inflammation, substantial neutrophil accumulation, damage to pulmonary endothelial and epithelial barriers, and diffuse alveolar injury, resulting in high mortality rates.1,2 Clinically, it primarily manifests as persistent hypoxaemia, progressive dyspnoea, and cyanosis. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a clinical syndrome characterized by acute hypoxic respiratory failure caused by non-cardiac pulmonary edema.3 When the condition worsens, approximately one-third of patients may ultimately progress to ARDS. ALI can be triggered by various factors including burns, shock, viral infections, and sepsis. Despite advances in treatment, the mortality rate for ALI/ARDS remains between 30% and 45%.4 Currently, Western medicine primarily targets underlying diseases, respiratory support, and pharmacological interventions.5,6 Treatments include protective mechanical ventilation, inhaled nitric oxide, and corticosteroid therapy. However, the complex aetiology, significant individual variability, and high prevalence result in mortality rates ranging from 30% to 50%.7 This issue imposes substantial socioeconomic burdens and severely compromises patients’ physical and mental wellbeing. Consequently, identifying safe and effective pharmacological interventions to mitigate ALI is a critical area of contemporary research.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) possesses a profound historical foundation in preventing and treating ALI. Through its holistic approach and syndrome differentiation principles, TCM demonstrates unique therapeutic advantages. Multiple studies confirm that TCM effectively alleviates clinical symptoms of ALI by suppressing inflammatory responses, modulating immune function, improving coagulation disorders, inducing autophagy resolution, and mitigating oxidative stress, thus enhancing quality of life.8,9 Defined as active, genetically regulated cell death mechanisms, programmed cell death (PCD) includes ferroptosis, autophagy, cuproptosis, and PANoptosis. PCD plays a critical role in maintaining physiological equilibrium, modulating inflammatory responses, and stabilizing immune functions.10–12 Prior studies have confirmed that characteristic PCD features are evident in alveolar epithelial cell death during ALI, underscoring a significant connection between PCD and ALI pathology.13 Contemporary research further indicates that aberrant activation or disrupted regulation of various PCD pathways, such as pyroptosis and PANoptosis, critically contributes to ALI development.14 PCD influences ALI pathogenesis by disrupting alveolar epithelial barrier function, promoting inflammatory cell infiltration, and exacerbating oxidative stress and cytokine storms. TCM can inhibit these mechanisms by regulating PCD, thus mitigating lung tissue damage. These findings offer novel potential targets for ALI intervention and treatment.

This article reviews the latest advances in research on PCD and ALI in recent years and summarizes the active ingredients in TCM (Flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, polyphenols, polysaccharides, etc), that intervene in this pathway, as well as TCM formulas (Clears heat and detoxifies; promotes lung function and relieves cough; tonifies the lungs and strengthens the spleen; nourishes yin and moistens the lungs; promotes bowel movement and expels heat; tonifies qi and invigorates blood circulation, etc), with the aim of helping researchers gain a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of ALI.

Materials and Methods

Literature Search Strategy

This review followed the reporting principles of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. A systematic literature search was performed across five major electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Science Direct, Google Scholar, and CNKI. The search period covered from database inception to December 2025 to ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant studies. A search strategy combining subject headings and free-text terms was adopted. The main keywords included “acute lung injury”, “acute respiratory distress syndrome”, “programmed cell death”, “natural products”, “active compounds”, “traditional Chinese medicine formulas”, and “traditional Chinese medicine”. Duplicate records were removed before subsequent screening. The PRISMA flow diagram of the literature retrieval process is presented in Figure 1. Inclusion Criteria

Studies meeting the following criteria were included in this review:

(1) Original studies focusing on interventions using TCM (including single herbal medicines, active components of Chinese herbal medicines, herbal extracts, and herbal formulae) for ALI;

(2) Studies involving the regulatory mechanisms of PCD, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, PANoptosis, Cuproptosis and NETosis;

(3) Preclinical studies (in vitro, ex vivo, or animal models) that provide clear evaluations of pharmacological effects, mechanisms, efficacy, or safety;

(4) Full-text articles published in English or Chinese.

Exclusion Criteria

The following types of literature were excluded:

(1) Secondary literature such as reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, conference abstracts, and case reports;

(2) Duplicate publications or studies with incomplete data;

(3) Studies that did not address the regulatory mechanisms of PCD or did not explicitly evaluate outcome measures for ALI;

(4) Studies for which the full text could not be obtained.

Data Screening and Selection

An initial database search yielded a series of literature records. After removing duplicates, a preliminary screening was conducted based on the relevance of titles and abstracts. Subsequently, the full texts of the documents that passed the preliminary screening were reviewed, and they were rigorously evaluated against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, a total of 163 articles met all requirements and were included in this review for comprehensive analysis.

Regulatory Mechanisms of PCD in ALI

Apoptosis and ALI

Apoptosis represents a highly conserved variant of PCD, integral to various physiological events including development, tissue regeneration, and immune responses.15 Apoptosis primarily proceeds via intrinsic and extrinsic pathways. Intrinsic pathways are regulated by B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) family proteins. Under stress, mitochondria release cytochrome c, thereby activating caspase-9 and effector caspases 3/7.16 Various TCM, such as Platycodin D and Huangqi Baihe Granule, exert anti-apoptotic effects through this pathway.17,18 Conversely, the extrinsic pathway involves death receptors binding ligands to recruit Fas-associated death domain (FADD) and caspase-8 precursor, forming a death-inducing signaling complex that subsequently activates caspase-3/7.19 Research indicates that Naringenin can mitigate Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced apoptosis in pulmonary epithelial cells by inhibiting caspase-8 activation through intervention in downstream signaling of tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1).20 In ALI, increased apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells and vascular endothelial cells (ECs) represents a critical pathological mechanism. Alveolar epithelial cells, essential for pulmonary gas exchange, are highly susceptible to apoptosis during ALI, particularly type II alveolar epithelial cells (AT2). Under oxidative stress, increased phosphorylation of p53 activates transcription of pro-apoptotic genes. p53 upregulates apoptosis regulators and Bcl-2-interacting protein 3 while suppressing anti-apoptotic Bcl-2, specifically inducing apoptosis in AT2 cells.21 The regulatory mechanisms controlling apoptosis in ALI are multifaceted, involving diverse intracellular signaling entities, notably the Bcl-2 family. Anti-apoptotic proteins, including Bcl-2 and B-cell lymphoma 2-like protein 1 (Bcl-xL), along with pro-apoptotic factors like Bax, dictate apoptosis initiation by influencing mitochondrial permeability.22 Additionally, DNA damage activates the tumor suppressor gene p53, promoting transcriptional regulation of apoptosis-associated genes, thus inducing cell death (Figure 2).23 Figure 2The Mechanism of Apoptosis in ALI (By Figdraw). In summary, inflammation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage in ALI can induce excessive apoptosis in alveolar epithelial and endothelial cells through pathways associated with p53 and the Bcl-2 family, thereby disrupting the alveolar-capillary barrier and exacerbating pulmonary edema and impaired oxygenation. Therefore, intervention efforts should focus on inhibiting pathological apoptosis while maintaining normal cellular turnover and damage clearance.

Pyroptosis and ALI

Pyroptosis, controlled by the gasdermin family proteins, involves cellular swelling, membrane perforation, and subsequent release of inflammatory factors.24–26 Two main pathways define pyroptosis: the classical pathway governed by caspase-1 and a non-classical route regulated by caspases-4/5/11.27 In the classical pathway, pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), leading to the assembly of inflammasomes such as NOD-like receptor family, pyrin domain containing protein 3 (NLRP3). This activates caspase-1, which cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD) to form an N-terminal (GSDMD-N) fragment. This fragment perforates the cell membrane, facilitating the release of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18. TCM such as Oridonin can inhibit caspase-1 activation, while Wogonin has been demonstrated to downregulate GSDMD cleavage levels.28,29 Alternatively, the non-classical pathway is activated directly by LPS, which triggers caspases-4/5/11 to cleave GSDMD, subsequently initiating pyroptosis.30 Alveolar macrophages (AMs), pivotal innate immune effector cells, activate inflammasomes following PAMP and DAMP recognition. Excessive inflammation from AM pyroptosis significantly contributes to ALI. In LPS-induced ALI, pyroptosis mediated by the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/NLRP3/caspase-1 pathway activates GSDMD, enhances immune cell cytokine production, elevates TLR4 expression, and increases Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) generation. These combined effects result in vascular leakage and neutrophil infiltration.31 Genetic ablation of TLR4 notably mitigates ALI/ARDS severity. Moreover, in NLRP3-associated ALI, secretion of high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) further activates caspase-1 and NLRP3, intensifying alveolar macrophage pyroptosis. Inhibiting the TLR4/myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88)/NF-κB axis significantly alleviates ALI manifestations.32 Additionally, Huang et al indicated that upregulation of caspase-11 in ECs induces EC pyroptosis via the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway, inhibiting EC proliferation and vascular repair (Figure 3).33 Figure 3The Mechanism of Pyroptosis in ALI(By Figdraw). In summary, pathogen- and injury-associated signals in ALI can sustainably activate the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway, inducing pyroptosis in alveolar macrophages, epithelial cells, and endothelial cells, which in turn promotes the release of inflammatory mediators, disrupts the alveolar-capillary barrier, and impairs oxygenation. Therefore, therapeutic interventions should focus on inhibiting excessive pyroptosis and the amplification of inflammation while maintaining essential host defense functions.

Necroptosis and ALI

Necroptosis, a caspase-independent regulated form of cell death, exhibits morphological characteristics resembling necrosis, including cellular swelling, compromised plasma membrane integrity, abnormal chromatin condensation, and the release of inflammatory cytokines and DAMPs.34,35 Mechanistically, necroptosis initiates with external stimuli like Tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) binding to TNFR1, recruiting TNFR-associated death domain protein (TRADD).11 TRADD protein assembles complex I alongside receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1), TNF receptor-associated factors, and cellular inhibitors of apoptosis proteins. Subsequent deubiquitination of RIPK1 facilitates recruitment of FADD and caspase-8 to form complex IIa, while RIPK1 phosphorylates RIPK3, establishing complex IIb. Phosphorylation of mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) then prompts its oligomerization and membrane translocation, creating pores that compromise membrane integrity, inducing cellular leakage and inflammation. TCM such as rhodiolol have been shown to downregulate RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL expression, while coumarin can inhibit the phosphorylation levels of RIPK3 and MLKL.36,37 Necroptosis also represents a significant form of cell death in ALI.38,39 Studies indicate that necroptosis in AMs is significantly activated in mouse models of ALI, with elevated RIPK1 phosphorylation and colocalisation of RIPK1 and RIPK3. Conversely, RIPK3 deficiency suppresses necroptosis and reduces inflammation.40 Elevated phosphorylated RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL expression, critical regulators of necroptosis, appears within pulmonary epithelial cells from ALI/ARDS patients.41 Additional pro-inflammatory mediators exacerbate this process. Activation of the TLR4/TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β (TRIF) pathway, an upstream trigger of necroptosis, strongly correlates with necroptotic cell death. Experimental studies confirm that TLR4/TRIF activation induces macrophage necroptosis, worsening ALI outcomes in LPS-exposed mouse models.42,43 Hence, blocking necroptosis may offer protective effects against ALI-induced pulmonary epithelial cell death (Figure 4). In summary, inflammatory stimuli and oxidative stress in ALI can activate the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway, leading to necroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and macrophages, which in turn exacerbates the inflammatory response and barrier damage through the release of DAMPs. Therefore, therapeutic interventions should focus on inhibiting abnormal necroptosis while regulating the balance between this process and other forms of cell death, such as apoptosis.

Autophagy and ALI

Autophagy constitutes a metabolic process that selectively eliminates defective proteins and impaired organelles through lysosomal degradation, initiated by cellular mechanisms that detect energy stress.44–46 Based on molecular features, autophagy-linked cell death is primarily classified into two forms: autophagy-dependent cell death, predominantly reliant on autophagic processes, and autophagy-mediated cell death, primarily influenced by alternative cell death pathways.47 Under energy-deprived conditions, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) inhibits the activity of the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 through phosphorylation, thereby activating the UNC-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1) complex to initiate autophagosome membrane synthesis. Subsequently, activation of the class III phosphoinositide 3-kinase complex promotes 3-phosphoinositide synthesis, driving autophagosome membrane extension and closure; simultaneously, the ATG5-ATG12-autophagy-related protein 16-like 1 (ATG16L1) complex synergizes with microtubule-associated protein light chain 3 (LC3) to convert LC3-I into lipidated LC3-II, promoting autophagosome maturation. TCM such as sophocarpine and apigenin F accelerate this conversion process.48,49 Finally, autophagosomes fuse with lysosomes to form autophagolysosomes, where their contents are degraded.50,51 Autophagy has dual roles in ALI, exerting protective or detrimental effects depending on the microenvironment. Inflammation is central to ALI, and autophagy modulates inflammatory responses in this condition. Research indicates that autophagy activation reduces cytokine production and oxidative stress, alleviating pulmonary injury in ALI.52 Autophagy restricts NLRP3 inflammasome activation, subsequently diminishing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β.53 Additionally, the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway represents a classical autophagy-associated signaling route. Rapamycin, a known mTOR inhibitor, induces autophagy through modulation of ULK1 and VPS34, subsequently suppressing IL-1β and IL-18 production and reducing pulmonary damage and barrier disruption.54 Conversely, excessive or uncontrolled autophagy can intensify ALI pathology. In summary, energy metabolism disorders, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial damage in ALI can significantly affect the autophagy process. Moderate autophagy helps clear damaged organelles and suppress inflammation, whereas excessive or impaired autophagy may exacerbate cellular damage. Therefore, interventions should focus on restoring an appropriate autophagy flux rather than unidirectionally activating or inhibiting autophagy.

Ferroptosis and ALI

Ferroptosis refers to an iron-dependent form of PCD characterized by iron metabolism disruption and the accumulation of lipid peroxides.56–58 In maintaining iron homeostasis, transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) mediates cellular iron uptake, after which iron is reduced to Fe2+ by six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 3 and transported into the labile iron pool via divalent metal transporter 1.59 During iron overload, Fe2+ drives the Fenton reaction, generating lipid ROS that oxidize polyunsaturated fatty acids such as arachidonic acid (AA) and adrenic acid (AdA). This process is catalyzed by enzymes including acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), leading to the formation of oxidized phospholipids.60 Ferroptosis is centrally regulated by the System Xc−/glutathione (GSH)/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis: GPX4 reduces toxic phospholipid hydroperoxides by oxidizing GSH to glutathione disulphide, thereby preserving membrane integrity; depletion of GSH compromises GPX4 activity, resulting in ROS accumulation and ferroptotic cell death.61 Ferroptosis is triggered by oxidative stress; under physiological conditions, low ROS levels facilitate signaling and metabolic processes. However, an imbalance between ROS production and antioxidant defenses causes oxidative stress, damaging pulmonary capillary endothelial and alveolar epithelial cells. PANoptosis and ALI

PANoptosis, a novel form of PCD, was first proposed by Malireddi et al in 2019. It integrates molecular mechanisms from apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis, exerting biological effects through their combined actions rather than a single pathway.68–70 PANoptosis is regulated at the molecular level by the panoptosome, integrating essential components from multiple PCD pathways.71 Its core regulatory mechanism involves three critical steps. First, PAMPs or DAMPs trigger initiation signals. Subsequently, PRRs such as Z-DNA-binding protein 1 (ZBP1) recruit downstream adaptor molecules (eg, FADD) through domain interactions to form multiprotein complexes. These complexes then activate effector molecules including MLKL, caspases, and gasdermin proteins, resulting in cell membrane rupture and inflammatory mediator release.72 During this process, the panoptosome integrates apoptotic (caspase-3/7), pyroptotic (GSDMD), and necroptotic (RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL) mechanisms, achieving crosstalk among these three PCD pathways. In sepsis-induced ALI, macrophages are activated through the TLR4/NF-κB signalling pathway, subsequently triggering PANoptosis.73 Macrophages stimulated by LPS exhibit significant upregulation of PANoptosis indicators, including caspase-8, GSDMD, and phosphorylated MLKL, signifying PANoptosis-mediated cell death. LPS also raises expression levels of Bax, GSDMD-N, NLRP3, and phosphorylated MLKL, markers intimately linked to PANoptosis. Simultaneously, PANoptosome complexes (ASC--caspase-8--RIPK3) form in pulmonary macrophages, facilitating PANoptosis.74 Research by Wang et al indicated that deficiency in Tumor Necrosis Factor-α-induced Protein 8-like 2 (TIPE2) elevates ZBP1 expression, a vital sensor activating the PANoptosome complex, thus intensifying PANoptosis in AMs.75 Moreover, Gong et al discovered that in septic ALI, lactate accumulation within macrophages promotes lactylation of CIRP, leading to extracellular CIRP (eCIRP) formation. eCIRP is internalised by pulmonary vascular endothelial cells (PVEC) via TLR4-mediated endocytosis, competitively binds ZBP1, preventing its ubiquitination and degradation. Other Forms of Cell Death and ALI

Cuproptosis, a recently recognized regulated cell death mode, results from excessive copper buildup within mitochondria coupled with proteotoxic stress. Copper accumulation leads to significant ROS production and disturbances in energy metabolism, ultimately triggering regulated cellular demise.77–79 This unique form of cell death is characterized by distinct metal specificity and mitochondrial targeting. Excess copper generates diverse stress conditions, including oxidative damage, endoplasmic reticulum stress, nucleolar dysfunction, and proteasome inhibition, collectively driving cellular destruction.80 Furthermore, cuproptosis intersects notably with drug resistance, metabolic pathways, and various intracellular signaling cascades.81 The principal molecular mechanism involves excessive copper ions selectively interacting with lipoylated mitochondrial proteins within the tricarboxylic acid cycle, resulting in aberrant protein oligomerization, compromised iron-sulfur cluster function, and subsequent proteotoxic stress-induced cell death.82 Presently, the precise role of cuproptosis in ALI pathogenesis remains unclear. Research indicates that in LPS-induced mouse ALI models, elevated copper levels in lung tissue are accompanied by molecular hallmarks of copper-mediated cell death, including increased Dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) oligomerization and destabilization of iron-sulfur clusters. Furthermore, pretreatment with the copper chelator dithiomolybdate reduces lung injury and inflammatory responses while suppressing copper-related death molecular signatures in vivo.83 Additionally, in alveolar macrophages, LPS stimulation elevates intracellular Cu2+ concentrations, promoting DLAT oligomerization and destabilizing ferritin. Both copper chelation and knockdown of upstream copper death regulators reduced DLAT oligomerization, restored ferritin levels, improved mitochondrial dysfunction, and decreased CD86+ macrophage polarization. Notably, altered copper transporter expression was observed, suggesting a reshaping of copper homeostasis during ALI.83 Another study demonstrated that disulfiram-induced copper death exacerbates the severity of malaria-associated-ALI/ARDS partly by promoting M1 macrophage polarization. Conversely, inhibiting copper death using the copper chelator tetrathiomolybdate reduced disease severity by promoting M2 macrophage polarization. Additionally, novel forms of PCD play a significant role in ALI. For instance, NETosis, a distinct mode of cell death, releases neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which play a central role in the pathogenesis of ALI.85 Through ERK Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signaling, neutrophils undergo hyperactivation and ROS bursts, which activate Peptidylarginine Deiminase 4 to drive histone citrullination and chromatin decondensation, ultimately releasing NETs composed of DNA-histones-granular proteins. These NETs contribute to lung injury through three primary mechanisms. First, proteases and histones within NETs directly disrupt the alveolar-capillary barrier, increasing vascular permeability. Second, NET components (such as S100A8/A9) serve as key drivers of the cytokine storm, amplifying the inflammatory cascade through interactions with macrophages. Third, NETs serve as procoagulant scaffolds that activate platelets and bind von Willebrand factor, inducing immune thrombosis that may progress to disseminated intravascular coagulation.86,87 Consequently, targeting key molecular nodes of NETosis has emerged as a critical therapeutic strategy for alleviating ALI.

In summary, Cuproptosis and NETosis contribute to the progression of ALI by disrupting mitochondrial protein homeostasis and promoting inflammatory–coagulation responses, respectively, thereby exacerbating immune cell dysfunction, damage to the alveolar–capillary barrier, and microcirculatory dysfunction. Therefore, relevant interventions should focus on the synergistic regulation of metal metabolism, mitochondrial homeostasis, and immune thrombosis; however, the specific mechanisms underlying these processes require further validation.

Interacting Mechanisms of Different Forms of PCD in ALI

Oxidative Stress and Related Signaling Pathways: Key Drivers Upstream of PCD

In the complex pathophysiological process of ALI, different forms of PCD do not act in isolation but are intertwined and synergistic, forming a precise and intricate regulatory network that influences the onset and progression of ALI. Emerging evidence indicates that these distinct PCD patterns converge on a shared set of upstream drivers and are synergistically regulated by them. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by excessive ROS/reactive nitrogen species (RNS) production, represent prominent core pathological hubs in ALI. These upstream stress signals exert central regulatory roles through key nodes such as NF-κB and the mTOR. NF-κB, as a classic sensor of oxidative stress, activates the transcription of proinflammatory factors (such as IL-1β and TNF-α) and NLRP3 inflammasomes, thereby setting the threshold for cells to undergo pyroptosis or apoptosis. Concurrently, the mTOR pathway integrates energy and stress signals. Under energy depletion induced by ALI, mTOR activity is suppressed, thereby releasing the inhibition on autophagy. However, prolonged stress can lead to impaired autophagic flux, subsequently triggering apoptosis or necroptosis. Characteristics of Different Forms of PCD

RIPK1: The Hub of Necroptosis and Pyroptosis

RIPK1 serves as a central regulatory hub linking necroptosis and pyroptosis. In ALI, phosphorylated RIPK1 binds to RIPK3 to form necrosomes, which subsequently activate MLKL to induce cell membrane rupture. This event not only executes necroptosis but also activates NLRP3 inflammasomes in neighboring macrophages by releasing endogenous danger signals such as ATP and HMGB1, thereby triggering pyroptosis.41 This “necroptosis initiation-pyroptosis amplification” cascade represents a key mechanism driving the sustained amplification of pulmonary inflammatory storms in ALI.

Mitochondria: A Common Effector in Multiple Forms of PCD

Mitochondria serve as a common effector in multiple PCD pathways, playing a central integrator role within the interactive network. In ferroptosis, the accumulation of mitochondrial membrane lipid peroxides directly mediates cell death. In pyroptosis, damaged mitochondria release mtDNA to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome. In apoptosis, mitochondria release cytochrome c to initiate the caspase cascade. The aforementioned pathways converge at the mitochondrial level. In an oleic acid-induced ALI model, elevated levels of lipid peroxidation, a hallmark of ferroptosis, were observed alongside increased ratios of the autophagy-related marker LC3-II/I, indicating autophagy activation. However, excessive autophagy activation can trigger “autophagic cell death”, which intertwines with ferroptosis, leading to significantly increased cell death and accelerated functional decline of lung tissue. This confirms the interaction between autophagy and ferroptosis in ALI.95

Ferroptosis and Pyroptosis: Bidirectional Regulatory Interactions

Ferroptosis and pyroptosis exhibit bidirectional regulatory interactions. In a mouse model of ALI induced by cecal ligation and puncture, administration of the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Fer-1 significantly reduced iron accumulation in lung tissue, restored GPX4 expression, and decreased levels of the lipid peroxidation-associated enzyme Alcohol Oxidase 2 and its product 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal. Concurrently, expression of pyroptosis effector molecules IL-1β and IL-18 markedly decreased, accompanied by increased Aldehyde Dehydrogenase 2 activity.96 This finding suggests that lipid peroxides generated during ferroptosis may serve as upstream signals that directly or indirectly activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby driving pyroptosis. Consequently, targeting ferroptosis not only blocks its intrinsic death pathway but also achieves a synergistic effect in inhibiting pyroptosis by attenuating inflammasome activation.

Beclin-1: Regulation of the Autophagy-Apoptosis Balance

Autophagy and apoptosis form a mutually restraining equilibrium mechanism through Beclin-1. The key autophagy-associated protein Beclin-1 determines a cell’s fate between apoptosis and autophagy by interacting with Bcl-2 and being cleaved by activated caspase-3.97 Persistent apoptotic stimuli induce Beclin-1 fragmentation, transporting the fragments to mitochondria where they inhibit autophagy and trigger apoptosis. Luo et al demonstrated that Bax-triggered apoptosis inhibits autophagy by inducing caspase-mediated cleavage of Beclin-1 at position D149.98 Further studies revealed that autophagy inhibition activates caspase-8, promoting apoptosis, while defects in autophagosome-lysosome fusion also induce apoptosis.99 Thus, autophagy and apoptosis form a dynamic equilibrium through the Beclin-1 node, jointly determining the survival or death decision of cells under stress conditions.

PANoptosis: A Multi-Pathway Integrated Cell Death Platform

PANoptosis, as an integrated death platform, achieves synergistic activation of multiple pathways based on the PCD interaction network. ZBP1, acting as an upstream sensor, assembles the PANoptosome complex (ASC-caspase-8-RIPK3) upon recognizing PAMPs or DAMPs, subsequently initiating caspase-3/7-mediated apoptosis, caspase-8 cleavage of GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, and RIPK3 phosphorylation of MLKL-mediated necroptosis, respectively. In ALI, PANoptosis may represent a core mechanism driving cytokine storms.68 Consequently, targeting the PANoptosome complex or its key components offers potential molecular targets for TCM to achieve multi-pathway synergistic regulation.

In summary, the PCD network in ALI centers on core hubs including RIPK1, mitochondria, Beclin-1, and ZBP1. It forms a complex regulatory system through mechanisms such as necroptosis-pyroptosis positive feedback, ferroptosis-pyroptosis cross-activation, and autophagy-apoptosis mutual inhibition. This network perspective suggests that a “control the network by targeting key nodes” strategy, which targets critical hubs or integrative platforms such as the PANoptosome, can overcome the therapeutic limitations of single-target interventions. This approach leverages the synergistic advantages of TCM acting on multiple targets, thereby providing a theoretical basis for developing ALI treatment strategies.

TCM Treatment for ALI

Regulation of Apoptosis by TCM in ALI Intervention

Bioactive constituents derived from TCM confer substantial protection against ALI through the multimodal modulation of apoptotic signaling networks. At the molecular level, polyphenols and saponins, including Resveratrol, Platycodin D, and Saikosaponin B2, restore mitochondrial integrity and suppress alveolar epithelial apoptosis by regulating the Bax/Bcl-2/caspase-3 axis and the AMPK/SIRT1 pathway.17,100,101 Flavonoids such as Quercetin, Naringenin, and Chrysosplenol D attenuate oxidative stress induced cell death by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades,20,102,103 whereas Andrographolide exerts dual cytoprotective effects via activation of the nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/Heme Oxygenase-1 (HO-1) axis coupled with concurrent suppression of Phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase (PI3K)/Protein Kinase B (AKT) mediated apoptotic signaling.104 Epigenetic regulation and noncoding RNA modulation further characterize these interventions, as evidenced by Hyperoside and Tanshinone, which downregulate miR-199a and DLX6-AS1 expression, respectively, thereby mitigating lipopolysaccharide induced epithelial injury.105,106

In parallel, TCM compound formulations achieve systemic therapeutic efficacy by integrating the suppression of apoptosis with the regulation of hemodynamic homeostasis and the gut-lung axis. Empirical and classical formulations, including modified Da Chai Hu Decoction, Xuan Fei Da Yu Decoction, and Da Cheng Qi Decoction, activate the PI3K/Akt and Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme 2 (ACE2)/Ang(1–7)/Mas pathways, thereby attenuating inflammatory infiltration, pulmonary edema, and apoptotic cell loss.107–109 Furthermore, formulations such as the Anti-O Compound and Gan Lu Qing Wen Formula exemplify the synergistic advantages of multiherb compatibility, simultaneously restraining NF-κB phosphorylation and p38 MAPK signaling to reduce fibrotic remodeling and restore alveolar architectural integrity.110,111

In summary, TCM can alleviate oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and barrier damage through pathways such as Bax/Bcl-2/caspase, AMPK/SIRT1, PI3K/AKT, and ACE2/Ang-(1-7)/Mas. Existing studies have covered models such as LPS, cecal ligation and perforation, paraquat, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. However, experimental designs vary significantly, and some evidence is derived solely from short-term cellular experiments. Their anti-apoptotic effects still need to be validated through studies involving delayed administration following injury.

TCM Modulation of Pyroptosis in ALI Intervention

TCM-derived bioactive molecules protect against ALI via selective modulation of pyroptotic signaling networks. Total glucosides of White Paeony, Oridonin, Farrerol, and Wogonin attenuate alveolar damage by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the downstream caspase-1/GSDMD axis, thereby limiting inflammatory cell death.28,29,112,113 Astragalus polysaccharides further reinforce this inhibitory effect by concurrently downregulating the upstream NF-κB signaling pathway, demonstrating a multi-tiered regulatory mechanism.114 Additional phytochemicals, including Forsythin, Curcumin, Acacetin, Ganoderic acid A, Amentoflavone, and Isorhamnetin, consistently impede pyroptosis by disrupting the assembly and activation of the NLRP3/ASC/caspase-1 complex, thereby preserving pulmonary architecture and mitigating inflammatory infiltration.115–120 These findings collectively establish the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD signaling axis as a central therapeutic target for TCM active components in the management of ALI.

TCM compound formulations achieve systemic attenuation of ALI by integrating pyroptosis inhibition with broader immunomodulatory and hemodynamic regulation. Classical and empirical formulas, including Ganlu Qingwen Formula, Pu Gong Ying Ke Li, Da Bu Fei Decoction, Xuanfei Baidu Formula, and Liangge San, consistently suppress the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD cascade, thereby reducing cytokine release and ameliorating pulmonary tissue injury.111,121–124 Notably, Da Bu Fei Decoction additionally modulates the Hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1α)/NLRP3 pathway, indicating crosstalk between hypoxic signaling and inflammasome activation.122 Further formulations, such as Ru Jin Jie Du San, Jin Yin Qing Re Oral Liquid, and modified Rhinoceros Horn and Rehmannia Decoction, reinforce these protective effects by targeting macrophage pyroptosis and downregulating pyroptosis-associated protein expression.52,125–127

In summary, TCM primarily alleviates pyroptosis and inflammatory damage by inhibiting the NF-κB/NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD axis. Since most studies have used LPS models, the consistency of the underlying mechanisms may be affected by excessive activation of TLR4/NLRP3 and may not necessarily apply to all types of acute lung injury. Future studies should employ multi-etiological models and evaluate the efficacy of therapeutic administration.

Regulation of Necroptosis by TCM in ALI Intervention

Various bioactive components in TCM can exert a protective effect against ALI by modulating necroptosis signaling pathways. Salidroside attenuates the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL axis, suppresses proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β), and downregulates HMGB1 expression.36 Aloperine inhibits the phosphorylation of RIPK3 and MLKL, activates Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defense, and curtails inflammatory mediator production.37 Amentoflavone alleviates ALI by disrupting the upstream TLR4/TRIF cascade, thereby limiting necroptosis-driven inflammation.119 The above studies indicate that regulating the core RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway and its upstream TLR4/TRIF signaling, while synergistically counteracting oxidative stress, represents a key mechanism by which TCM inhibit Necroptosis and alleviate ALI.

TCM formulas afford multifaceted protection against ALI by orchestrating the synergistic regulation of necroptosis and inflammatory cascades. The Ganlu Qingwen Formula reduces the expression of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL and lowers circulating and pulmonary levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, indicative of suppressed RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL-mediated necroptotic signaling.128 Kangyan Heji diminishes intestinal RIPK3 and MLKL expression and serum cytokine concentrations, alleviating cecal ligation and puncture-induced ALI through inhibition of RIPK3/MLKL-dependent necroptosis.129

In summary, TCM can alleviate necrotic apoptosis and inflammatory damage by regulating the TLR4/TRIF and RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathways; however, the number of existing studies is limited, and they are primarily based on LPS or cecal ligation and perforation models. Some studies involved single-dose or early administration, and there is a lack of validation regarding target specificity; therefore, the current evidence regarding the mechanism remains preliminary.

TCM Modulation of Autophagy in ALI Intervention

Active components derived from TCM predominantly activate autophagy by inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways or promoting mitophagy, thereby alleviating ALI. Enoxolone from Glycyrrhiza enhances autophagy via suppression of the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling axis,130 while Polydatin from Polygonum cuspidatum mitigates oxidative damage through the promotion of mitochondrial autophagy.131 Similarly, Sophoridine from Sophora japonica, Palmatine from Menispermum dauricum, and Isorhapontigenin from Gnetum cleistostachyum induce autophagic activity and concurrently inhibit NF-κB-mediated inflammatory cytokine release to attenuate ALI progression.48,132,133 Furthermore, Nervilifordin F upregulates the expression of ATG5 and Beclin-1 and elevates the LC3B-II/I ratio, directly facilitating autophagic flux to ameliorate LPS-induced lung pathology.49 Collectively, these monomeric compounds target the JAK2/STAT3 and NF-κB pathways as well as mitochondrial quality control mechanisms to restore autophagic balance in ALI.

In contrast to single components, TCM formulas exert protective effects against ALI primarily through the regulation of upstream nutrient-sensing and antioxidant pathways, such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR and Nrf2, to activate autophagy. For instance, the qi-tonifying and blood-invigorating combination of Astragali Radix-Salviae Miltiorrhizae Radix et Rhizoma downregulates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to enhance autophagy and reduce pulmonary edema.134 The Schizonepeta tenuifolia–Saposhnikovia divaricata herb pair activates the Nrf2 pathway while upregulating autophagy-related genes and proteins (ATG5, Beclin-1, LC3-II/I) to mitigate LPS-induced inflammation.135 Similarly, Guanning Decoction, Bai He Gu Jin Decoction, and Xuebijing Injection have been validated to alleviate pulmonary injury and reduce cytokine levels by modulating the Nrf2/Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)/autophagy axis and related pathways.136–138

In summary, TCM can regulate autophagy through the JAK2/STAT3, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Nrf2, and mitochondrial autophagy pathways. Most existing studies rely on static markers such as LC3, Beclin-1, and p62 to assess changes in autophagy, making it difficult to distinguish between enhanced autophagy and impaired degradation. Future research should incorporate measurements of autophagy flux and identify the optimal timing for intervention at different disease stages.

TCM Modulation of Ferroptosis in ALI Intervention

A range of naturally derived phytochemicals have been shown to attenuate ferroptosis and confer protection against ALI, predominantly through the activation of antioxidant signaling cascades. The Nrf2/HO-1 axis serves as a central mechanistic hub. Farrerol from Rhododendron species, Pachymic acid isolated from Poria cocos, Obacunone from Phellodendron, Astragaloside from Astragalus membranaceus, and Panaxydol from Panax ginseng all exert ferroptosis-inhibitory effects via this pathway.113,139–142 Beyond the Nrf2/HO-1 axis, alternative regulatory routes have been identified: Huperzine A suppresses ferroptosis through activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway,143 whereas Tilianin exerts protective effects via the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling cascade.144 The above studies reveal that activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant axis is the core mechanism by which TCM inhibit ferroptosis, while the PI3K/AKT and AMPK/Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) pathways also participate in its regulation, providing new intervention targets for ALI treatment. Mechanisms of Action by Which Active Components of TCM Modulate ALI

Notes: “↑” indicates a boost or increase; “↓” indicates a reduction or decrease.

Compound TCM formulations further illustrate multi-target regulatory capabilities in ferroptosis modulation. The Ganlu Qingwen Formula and Qingqi Liangying Formula both mitigate ferroptosis and associated inflammatory injury by potentiating the Nrf2/GPX4 pathway.111,146 In parallel, Qingfei Danya Keli exerts its protective action through activation of the SIRT3 pathway, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and pulmonary inflammation in experimental ALI models.147

In summary, TCM primarily improves iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation through Nrf2/HO-1, SLC7A11/GPX4, and SIRT-related pathways. Relevant studies have relied heavily on LPS models and have primarily used markers such as Fe2+, MDA, GSH, and GPX4, which may partially reflect general antioxidant effects. Further validation of their therapeutic effects is needed through iron death-specific rescue experiments and delayed-administration designs.

Regulation of Multiple Forms of PCD by TCM Formulas in ALI Intervention

Da Cheng Qi Decoction, an established Shang Han Lun formula, enhances vitamin D receptor (VDR), Zonula Occludens-1 (ZO-1), and Occludin expression in intestinal tissues of mouse models. It simultaneously inhibits NLRP3, caspase-1, and ASC expression in pulmonary tissues. Additionally, this formula elevates autophagosome formation and autophagy-related protein expression, including VDR, LC3, ATG16L1, and Beclin-1, within lung tissue. By regulating pyroptosis and autophagy, Da Cheng Qi Decoction fortifies intestinal barriers and mitigates inflammatory injury in septic ALI models.148 Qidong Huoxue Decoction, comprising Astragalus, Ophiopogon, Polygonum cuspidatum, Angelica sinensis, and Rheum officinale, significantly lowers serum ALT, AST, creatine kinase, and lactate dehydrogenase levels. Additionally, it reduces pulmonary TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, NLRP3, ASC, and IL-18 mRNA levels and decreases phosphorylation of RIPK1, RIPK3, MLKL, along with cleaved caspase-1 and GSDMD-N protein expression. Consequently, this decoction mitigates ALI by suppressing necroptosis and pyroptosis.149

Shenfu Injection, a representative TCM formula widely used clinically for sepsis and septic shock, improves tissue perfusion and inhibits inflammatory storms. It enhances MLE-12 cell viability, reduces levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and ROS, and increases protein expression of E-cadherin, Occludin, ZO-1, Beclin-1, and the LC3BII/LC3BI ratio. It simultaneously reduces p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT levels. Therefore, Shenfu Injection likely improves pulmonary epithelial cell function by inhibiting the PI3K-AKT pathway, activating autophagy, and suppressing apoptosis.150 Huanglian Jiedu Decoction, originating from Ge Hong’s Jin Dynasty text Emergency Formulas for the Elbow, possesses heat-clearing and toxin-resolving properties. Clinically used for sepsis, it reduces levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, elevates the LC3II/LC3I ratio, and downregulates p62 protein expression. It likely inhibits M1 macrophage polarisation and promotes M2 macrophage polarisation through enhanced autophagy, thus reducing apoptosis and alleviating ALI.151 The mechanisms by which TCM formulas regulate PCD in ALI are summarised in Table 3. Pharmacokinetic Characteristics of Key TCM for the Treatment of ALI

Although the pharmacological effects of TCM in regulating PCD pathways during ALI have been well established, their clinical translation still faces significant challenges. The core obstacle lies in the fact that the in vivo pharmacokinetic characteristics of their active components have yet to be systematically elucidated. Most TCM such as flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloids exhibit significant oral absorption barriers and rapid metabolism issues. For instance, although resveratrol demonstrates clear anti-apoptotic activity in the ALI model, it readily undergoes glucuronidation and sulfation modifications in the gut and liver, resulting in an oral bioavailability below 1%.152 Quercetin is similarly limited by poor water solubility and rapid metabolism by intestinal Phase II enzymes, but its methylated or glucuronidated derivatives can release active aglycones through deconjugation reactions in target tissues (such as the lungs), achieving local pharmacological effects.153 Andrographolide, as a diterpene lactone compound, possesses anti-inflammatory activity but exhibits a short plasma half-life and is prone to hydrolysis and oxidation. Nanoparticle or phospholipid complexes can significantly enhance its lung targeting capability.154

Certain TCM rely on active metabolites to exert their effects. For instance, the aglycone form of baicalin B2 exhibits poor absorption, but its deglycosylated product may serve as the true anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory agent. Baicalin is hydrolyzed by gut microbiota into baicalin, which is more liposoluble and thus absorbed into the bloodstream. It is subsequently re-converted into baicalin within the liver. This cyclic conversion process, involving a glycoside-to-aglycone reconversion, significantly prolongs the duration of its anti-pyroptotic effects. The pharmacokinetic behavior of TCM formulas further demonstrates the complexity of multi-component synergistic regulation. For instance, components like emodin and rhein in Da Cheng Qi Decoction mutually influence metabolic enzyme and transporter activity, synergistically enhancing overall bioavailability.155 In Gan Lu Qing Wen Formula, wisterin and baicalin extend half-life and reduce toxicity through multi-component interactions, achieving sustained inhibition of multiple death pathways.111

At the tissue distribution level, lung targeting capability is a key determinant of ALI treatment efficacy. Although curcumin potently inhibits pyroptosis in vitro, its poor tissue distribution in vivo can be significantly enhanced by liposomal encapsulation or combination with piperine, thereby increasing its concentration in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and strengthening its lung protective effects.156 Cynarin naturally exhibits lung tissue enrichment properties, achieving highly effective inhibition of Necroptosis and oxidative stress.37 To bridge the translational gap between preclinical efficacy and clinical application, future research should systematically undertake the following tasks: Structural modifications of active ingredients to enhance metabolic stability, development of novel delivery systems such as nanoparticles and liposomes to improve pulmonary targeting, establishment of PK/PD correlation models to elucidate dose-response relationships between drug exposure and inhibition of key pulmonary tissue biomarkers, and in-depth investigation of interactions among herbs within TCM formulas to reveal synergistic pharmacokinetic mechanisms-these efforts collectively provide scientific rationale for the clinical translation of TCM.

Toxicity and Adverse Reactions of TCM

This review comprehensively evaluates in vitro and in vivo research evidence demonstrating how TCM and their active components intervene in ALI by regulating PCD. Results indicate that most TCM exhibit significant intervention potential and favorable safety profiles under conventional experimental dosages and administration routes. However, their potential toxicity and adverse reactions should not be overlooked. Long-term or excessive exposure, along with individual variability, may induce adverse events such as hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and gastrointestinal discomfort. These toxic reactions are primarily associated with dosage, treatment duration, individual metabolic characteristics, and drug-drug interactions.

Tanshinone, a lipid-soluble component derived from Salvia miltiorrhiza, has been demonstrated to mitigate LPS-induced damage to alveolar epithelial cells. However, long-term or high-dose administration may lead to hepatotoxicity, gastrointestinal discomfort, rash, and local phlebitis, which may be closely related to its accumulation characteristics and metabolic properties in the body.157 Evodiamine, the primary active alkaloid in Evodia fruit, exhibits anti-apoptotic effects in sepsis-associated ALI models.145 However, its potential cardiac and renal toxicity risks warrant attention.158,159 This compound exerts its effects through potent regulation of multiple signaling pathways and may induce off-target effects due to interindividual metabolic variations, suggesting vigilance is warranted regarding its dose-dependent toxicity. Xuebijing Injection, a compound preparation widely used in clinical sepsis management, can significantly mitigate LPS-induced pathological changes in ALI. However, studies have suggested it may cause adverse reactions such as dyspnea and skin allergies.160

Notably, genetic polymorphisms in drug-metabolizing enzymes can lead to individual variations in pharmacokinetic behavior, thereby affecting drug efficacy and safety. Taking Sophoridine as an example, this compound has been demonstrated to alleviate ALI by regulating autophagy. However, it is primarily metabolized via the hepatic CYP450 enzyme system. Interindividual variations in enzyme activity may cause fluctuations in blood drug concentrations, thereby compromising therapeutic consistency and increasing the risk of neurotoxicity.161 Similarly, as an autophagy-regulating active ingredient, Palmatine metabolic pathway also involves multiple drug-metabolizing enzymes. Genetic polymorphisms may trigger drug accumulation, necessitating vigilance for potential liver damage during long-term use. The above evidence suggests that when evaluating the safety window for TCM interventions in ALI, beyond defining the boundary between effective and toxic doses, it is essential to systematically assess their applicability in specific populations by considering metabolic enzyme genetic polymorphisms.

In summary, TCM demonstrate comprehensive advantages through multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway regulation of PCD to intervene in ALI. However, their toxicity and safety issues warrant thorough consideration. Future research should prioritize strengthening systematic toxicological evaluations during early drug development, with a core focus on defining therapeutic windows and selectivity indices. It is recommended to integrate multidisciplinary approaches such as metabolomics and network toxicology to systematically elucidate dose-response relationships. Through structural modifications, the development of novel delivery systems (eg, nanoparticles, liposomes), and the establishment of pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic models, a more robust scientific foundation for clinical safety applications can be provided.

Conclusion and Future Directions

ALI is characterized by acute onset, rapid progression, high fatality rates, and a tendency to evolve into ARDS. Current therapeutic approaches are limited and frequently fail to provide satisfactory long-term outcomes. Central to ALI pathology is significant damage to alveolar epithelial cells and pulmonary capillary endothelial cells, disrupting the alveolar-capillary barrier, and resulting in pulmonary edema and severe hypoxemia. Inflammation is central to exacerbating lung injury and dysfunction. Advances in ALI pathophysiology have identified numerous therapeutic targets at cellular and molecular levels. PCD, a meticulously regulated cellular demise process, plays essential roles in maintaining tissue balance, defending against pathogens, and repairing damage. Emerging evidence confirms the significant contributions of apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy, PANoptosis, and cuproptosis to ALI initiation and advancement. These pathways intensify alveolar injury and hinder pulmonary repair through endothelial and epithelial barrier impairment, inflammatory cell infiltration, cytokine secretion, and oxidative stress, profoundly influencing ALI outcomes and prognosis.

In ALI, PCD critically contributes to alveolar epithelial and vascular endothelial cell destruction, central to pulmonary oedema formation and inflammatory cascade exacerbation. Apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells markedly increases during ALI, correlating strongly with pulmonary barrier dysfunction, representing an early pathological indicator. Necroptosis also participates significantly; activation of RIPK1 induces inflammatory cell death, aggravating lung injury. Pyroptosis, characterised by NLRP3 inflammasome activation and GSDMD-mediated pore formation, promotes pro-inflammatory factor release, amplifying pulmonary inflammation, pathologically closely related to sepsis-associated ALI. Emerging studies reveal PANoptosis as a critical inflammatory PCD integrating apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, notably contributing to cytokine storms in ALI. Formation of the PANoptosome drives concurrent activation of multiple cell death pathways. Ferroptosis, characterised by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, directly induces alveolar epithelial cell death, with regulatory molecules such as GPX4 closely correlating with ALI severity, may be initiated in the early stages of direct lung injury. Recent research further identifies cuproptosis as a novel PCD form. Preliminary evidence suggests disrupted copper metabolism in lung tissues may influence ALI progression, although exact mechanisms require further elucidation. Autophagy plays dual roles: moderate activation clears damaged organelles, maintaining cellular homeostasis, while excessive or inadequate autophagy exacerbates cell injury and ALI severity.

TCM demonstrates unique advantages in preventing and treating ALI through its multi-component, multi-target, and holistic regulatory mechanisms. Studies indicate that active constituents from TCM (resveratrol, andrographolide, emodin, rhodiola glycosides) and traditional formulas (Da Cheng Qi Decoction, Ganlu Qingwen Formula, Xuanfei Baidu Formula) alleviate pulmonary tissue injury by modulating PCD pathways. These interventions inhibit inflammatory responses, mitigate oxidative stress, and enhance alveolar-capillary barrier function. Key active constituents in TCM that regulate PCD include flavonoids, alkaloids, diterpenoids, triterpenoid saponins, and polysaccharides. Compound TCM formulations encompass proprietary Chinese medicines, classical formulas, renowned physicians’ empirical prescriptions, and clinically derived formulas. These formulations effectively regulate PCD pathways in ALI, aligning with TCM principles that emphasise “strengthening the body’s defences while expelling pathogens” and simultaneously addressing symptoms and underlying causes. Therapeutic strategies include clearing heat and detoxifying, promoting lung function, resolving phlegm, invigorating blood circulation, resolving stasis, tonifying qi, and nourishing yin.

It is worth emphasizing that the therapeutic advantage of TCM lies in their ability to act upon the interactive network of PCD rather than a single pathway. For example, Ganlu Qingwen Formula can simultaneously regulate apoptosis (Bcl-2/Bax/caspase-3 axis), pyroptosis (NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway), necroptosis (RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL cascade), and ferroptosis (Nrf2/GPX4 pathway), thereby achieving multi-target synergistic inhibition. Similarly, Da Cheng Qi Decoction modulates pyroptosis and autophagy through vitamin D receptor (VDR)-mediated signaling pathways, whereas Qi Dong Huo Xue Yin inhibits both necroptosis and pyroptosis by downregulating key molecules such as phosphorylated RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL and activated caspase-1/GSDMD. The above TCM demonstrate the most potent and broad-spectrum therapeutic effects across multiple pathways. This “network regulation” approach, which targets key hubs in PCD networks such as RIPK1, NLRP3, and the PANoptosome to block positive feedback loops that amplify inflammatory damage, represents the modern scientific embodiment of TCM holistic perspective and syndrome differentiation principles. TCM achieves comprehensive intervention in inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and pulmonary barrier homeostasis in ALI by integrally regulating multiple pathways, including NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD and Nrf2/Keap1.

Currently, the role of PCD in ALI pathogenesis is increasingly recognised; however, several critical issues remain unresolved: ① Most studies use an acute LPS injection model, which differs significantly from the pathological processes of chronic or viral ALI and thus fails to adequately simulate clinical heterogeneity such as viral infection, aspiration, mechanical ventilation, and the presence of underlying conditions; ② There are substantial differences among studies in terms of animal species, route of administration, dosage, and observation period. Furthermore, species-specific differences exist between animals and humans in immune responses, drug metabolism, and the regulation of PCD, which limit the clinical generalizability of the findings; ③ Most studies administer the treatment before or concurrently with model induction; the results primarily reflect preventive effects, and the therapeutic effects after lung injury has developed, the optimal intervention window, and the dose–response relationship remain unclear; ④ Although multiple forms of PCD have been implicated in ALI, their cell type-specific roles in pulmonary epithelial cells, vascular endothelial cells, and immune cells remain incompletely defined, and the mechanisms by which these pathways interact to drive alveolar structural disruption and impaired gas exchange have yet to be fully elucidated; ⑤The efficacy of most traditional Chinese medicine formulations has been validated only in models induced by LPS alone; therefore, their effectiveness in clinical settings more closely resembling those of ALI patients, as well as their efficacy when administered therapeutically rather than prophylactically, has not yet been fully demonstrated and requires further study.

Given the limitations of current research and the challenges in translating fundamental discoveries to clinical applications, future studies should advance systematically across multiple dimensions: First, it is essential to integrate single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and live-cell dynamic imaging techniques to precisely elucidate the spatiotemporal mechanisms by which different PCD pathways regulate alveolar structural disruption in organoids or humanized models. This will bridge the gap between omics data and actual pathophysiology and validate the genuine synergistic effects of TCM formulations across different biological dimensions—including kinetics, spatial distribution, and gene -protein-metabolic levels. Second, research should extend beyond single forms of cell death to focus on identifying key rate-limiting nodes (such as RIPK1 and ZBP1) within PCD interaction networks. This includes exploring “combination therapies” that pair pyroptosis and ferroptosis inhibitors to achieve efficient, synergistic blockade of the PCD network. For emerging pathways like PANoptosis and copper-mediated cell death, the expression and activation status of their key molecules must be validated in clinical samples to establish their clinical relevance as therapeutic targets. In TCM research, we must confront the bottlenecks in translation. Priority should be given to modified formulations such as nanoparticles or liposomes with well-defined pharmacokinetic data to enhance lung targeting. Additionally, fingerprint profiling and content determination should be employed to address the challenge of standardizing TCM compound formulations. Ultimately, biomarker-driven randomized controlled trials should be conducted, stratifying patients based on specific PCD biomarkers (eg, sRAGE, GSDMD fragments) in their bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. This approach will validate the clinical efficacy of targeted PCD therapies, propelling ALI treatment from “empirical medication” to “precision intervention”. Concurrently, subsequent studies should enhance the rigor of experimental design and conduct multicenter validation to improve the quality of evidence.

In summary, the role of PCD in ALI development and progression is increasingly evident, with new therapeutic targets continually emerging. This paper reviews and summarises the relevant mechanisms, focusing on key PCD pathways in ALI and the therapeutic potential of TCM. It aims to systematically clarify the core role of PCD, offering new perspectives and potential strategies for mechanistic research and clinical ALI management.
