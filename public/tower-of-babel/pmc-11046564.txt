Keeping abreast of change – learning from specialist practice
Authors: Anton Emmanuel
Published: 2024

In this edition of ClinMed we are delighted to publish the annual Harveian lecture, this year delivered by Sir Patrick Vallance.1 His theme was how clinicians integrate clinical information from history, examination and investigations to understand diseases from a holistic rather than single-discipline perspective. Covering the spectrum of clinical science from molecular medicine to the climate crisis, the lecture is a reflection of the approach of ClinMed. A thread running through the original manuscripts in this edition is the management of common clinical presentations, where advances in specialist care have led to opportunities for enhanced care offered by the non-specialist colleagues seeing these patients.

Aortic stenosis is an important and common clinical problem in clinical practice, especially among older individuals. Gonnah et al2 address patient experiences after two different treatment approaches, reviewing health-related quality of life and functional outcomes in patients taking part in the recent randomised trials of transcatheter aortic valve implantation and surgical aortic valve replacement. The time course of improved outcomes is compared between the two interventions, and the paper emphasises the importance of a multidisciplinary approach with a particular emphasis on holistic elderly care.

Chronic hepatitis B and C infection is a common problem throughout the world, presenting a serious threat to public health. There has been increasing recognition of the importance of social and economic determinants on health outcomes, exemplified by the disparities experienced by minority groups during the COVID-19 pandemic. There is a growing Nepali population in the UK, related to their contributions in healthcare and the armed forces. A paper by Ala and colleagues3 describes a mixed-methods study comprising qualitative assessment of understanding of chronic viral hepatitis among Nepali people living in the UK together with a second phase of wide-scale community testing for the disease. The paper stresses the role of peer researchers in promoting understanding and provides a model that can be utilised for other disease areas and minority groups.

A paper by Jalal et al4 draws attention to an under-appreciated clinical phenomenon. Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency complicates chronic pancreatitis and common clinical disorders. Early diagnosis can be challenging because symptoms are non-specific and structural changes associated with chronic pancreatitis can be subtle. The diagnosis of pancreatic exocrine is not straightforward and is one of the few areas of medicine where current diagnostic tests are less reliable than those available 30 years ago, albeit easier to undertake for the patient. The paper identifies at-risk individuals in whom faecal testing identifies individuals who would benefit from pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy.
