Pareto-Efficient Multi-Buyer Mechanisms: Characterization, Fairness and Welfare
Moshe Babaioff, Sijin Chen, Zhaohua Chen, Yiding Feng

Abstract
A truthful mechanism for a Bayesian single-item auction results with some ex-ante revenue for the seller, and some ex-ante total surplus for the buyers. We study the Pareto frontier of the set of seller-buyers ex-ante utilities, generated by all truthful mechanisms when buyers values are sampled independently and identically (i.i.d.). We first provide a complete structural characterization of the Pareto frontier under natural distributional assumptions. For example, when valuations are drawn i.i.d. from a distribution that is both regular and anti-MHR, every Pareto-optimal mechanism is a second-price auction with a reserve no larger than the monopoly reserve. Building on this, we interpret the problem of picking a mechanism as a two-sided bargaining game, and analyze two canonical Pareto-optimal solutions from cooperative bargaining theory: the Kalai-Smorodinsky (KS) solution , and the Nash solution . We prove that when values are drawn i.i.d. from a distribution that is both regular and anti-MHR, in large markets both solutions yield near-optimal welfare. In contrast, under worst-case MHR distributions, their performance diverges sharply: the KS solution guarantees one-half of the optimal welfare, while the Nash solution might only achieve an arbitrarily small fraction of it. These results highlight the sensitivity of fairness-efficiency tradeoffs to distributional structure, and affirm the KS solution as the more robust notion of fairness for asymmetric two-sided markets.

Introduction

The theory of Bayesian mechanism design has achieved remarkable success in the single-item multi-buyer setting,
when
optimizing a single objective: whether maximizing social welfare
(
Vickrey, 1961
)
, seller revenue
(
Myerson, 1981
)
, or buyers’
surplus
(
Hartline and Roughgarden, 2008
)
. These foundational results yield elegant—and often simple—mechanisms, such as the second-price auction, second-price auction with a monopoly reserve, and uniform lottery, which are
optimal under standard distributional assumptions like regularity or monotone hazard rate (MHR). For these objectives, the designer’s goal is unambiguous, and the resulting mechanisms are celebrated for their simplicity and robustness.

Yet many modern marketplaces are not zero-sum games between a monopolist and passive buyers. Instead, they are two-sided platforms—such as online advertising exchanges, cloud resource markets, ride-sharing services, or labor platforms—where both the seller and the collective of buyers are strategic stakeholders whose long-term participation hinges on perceived fairness. A mechanism that maximizes the seller’s revenue may leave buyers with negligible surplus, discouraging future engagement; conversely, a welfare-maximizing mechanism might allocate the item for free, yielding zero revenue and undermining platform sustainability. In such settings, optimizing a single objective is insufficient. What is needed is a balanced approach that respects the interests of both sides.

This has motivated the study of
bi-objective mechanism design
, where the goal is to characterize the Pareto frontier of achievable ex-ante payoff pairs—namely, (seller revenue, buyers’ surplus) pairs. While this frontier has been partially analyzed in the single-item, single-buyer setting
(
Chen et al., 2023
;
Babaioff et al., 2025
)
, the multi-buyer regime—where competition among buyers fundamentally reshapes the structure of optimal mechanisms—remains largely uncharted.
We thus ask:

(Question 1)

For the single-item, multi-buyer setting, characterize the structure of the Pareto frontier of achievable ex-ante payoff pairs: Which mechanism are Pareto-optimal? Are they simple? Do they need to randomize?

In a nutshell, we provide a complete structural characterization of this frontier under natural distributional assumptions.

Of course, characterizing the Pareto frontier does not tell us which mechanism to implement. Among the continuum of Pareto-optimal options, we seek one that balances the interests of both sides in a principled way. To this end, we interpret the selection problem as a cooperative bargaining game
(
Nash, 1950
)
between the seller and the buyers, and study two canonical solution concepts from bargaining theory—the
Kalai-Smorodinsky (KS) Solution

(
Kalai and Smorodinsky, 1975
)
and the
Nash Solution

(
Nash, 1950
)
—which formalize distinct notions of fairness. The social welfare highly depends on the chosen Pareto-optimal mechanism.
This leads to our second question:

(Question 2)

In the single-item, multi-buyer setting, what are the welfare guarantees of the
KS Solution
and the
Nash Solution
, particularly in large markets?

Our work shows that the welfare performance of
KS Solution
and
Nash Solution
depends critically on the underlying distribution, with
KS Solution
exhibiting greater robustness than
Nash Solution
.

Technique: revenue maximization subject to buyers’ surplus constraint.

From a technical standpoint, we develop a unified framework to characterize the Pareto frontier across all three distributional regimes. The key insight is to reduce the characterization problem to a constrained optimization problem: maximizing seller revenue subject to a lower bound constraint on buyers’ surplus. We prove that this reduction is exact for any target buyers’ surplus lying between the surplus achieved by the seller-revenue-optimal mechanism (i.e., the Myerson auction) and the maximum achievable buyers’ surplus. We believe that this buyer-surplus-constrained revenue maximization framework may be of independent interest.

To solve this constrained problem, we employ a Lagrangian duality approach. Specifically, for any non-negative dual variable
{\lambda}
associated with the surplus constraint, we construct an
indirect Vickrey auction

\mathcal{M}_{{\lambda}}^{*}
that maximizes the Lagrangian objective:

\displaystyle\text{Seller Revenue}+{\lambda}\cdot\text{Buyers' Surplus}.

Crucially, we show that as
{\lambda}
increases continuously from
0
to
\infty
, the resulting family of mechanisms
\{\mathcal{M}_{{\lambda}}^{*}\}_{{\lambda}\geq 0}
traces out all
extreme points
of the Pareto frontier, with buyers’ surplus varying
continuously
from that of the Myerson auction to the buyer-optimal benchmark.

For any buyers’ surplus lower bound constraint corresponding to an extreme point on the Pareto frontier, we establish the existence of a dual value
{\lambda}^{*}\geq 0
such that the mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{{\lambda}^{*}}^{*}
satisfies the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions—particularly, complementary slackness—for the original constrained problem. Consequently,
\mathcal{M}_{{\lambda}^{*}}^{*}
solves the constrained optimization and is therefore Pareto-optimal.

Moreover, the
continuity of buyers’ surplus
in the parameter
{\lambda}
enables us to derive monotonicity properties of Pareto-optimal mechanisms along the frontier. By analyzing how
\mathcal{M}_{{\lambda}}^{*}
evolves with
{\lambda}
—in terms of reserves and feasible bidding spaces—we obtain all the structural monotonicity results stated in
Section

3
.

1.2
Related Work

Pareto-efficient mechanism design.
A growing body of work studies mechanism design through the lens of Pareto efficiency, seeking mechanisms that balance competing objectives—typically seller revenue and buyer surplus—without leaving gains on the table. Most closely related is the work of
Babaioff et al., 2025
, who initiate the study of Pareto-optimal mechanisms in Bayesian bilateral trade and introduce the Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS) solution as a fairness-refined selection from the Pareto frontier. They establish tight welfare guarantees for KS-fair mechanisms under regularity and MHR assumptions, and contrast them with Nash welfare maximization. Extending this to multi-buyer settings, our work provides the first full characterization of the Pareto frontier and analyzes the robustness of KS fairness in competitive markets. In parallel,
Berzack et al., 2025
study dynamic rental games where agents are stagewise individually rational; they show that classical Myersonian tools break down, and develop alternative characterizations of Pareto-optimal allocations under various reward structures (e.g., mix between welfare, revenue, buyers’ surplus). Their work highlights how temporal constraints reshape Pareto efficiency, but focuses on a single-agent-per-stage model without competition. Finally,
Chen et al., 2023
propose an
(\alpha,\theta)
-CSR pricing scheme that interpolates between profit and buyers’ surplus. While their framework yields distribution-free bounds on welfare ratios, it only considers pricing mechanism, does not characterize the Pareto frontier or address multi-buyer strategic competition. In contrast, our paper bridges these threads by offering a structural characterization of the entire Pareto frontier among the entire mechanism space in multi-buyer auctions and evaluating canonical
KS Solution
and
Nash Solution
as principled selectors of fair and efficient mechanisms. Beyond two-sided fairness, there is also a line of research focused on fairness
among buyers
—for example, ensuring envy-freeness, proportionality, or equitable surplus allocation in multi-buyer settings (see, e.g.,
Deb and Pai, 2017
;
Azrieli and Jain, 2018
;
Mass et al., 2020
;
Reuter and Groh, 2020
;
Dworczak et al., 2021
;
Cohen et al., 2022
;
Akbarpour et al., 2024
;
Chen and Knyazev, 2025
;
Wang et al., 2025
;
Finster et al., 2025
;
Biggs et al., 2025
).

Cooperative bargaining with incomplete information.

The cooperative bargaining framework was introduced by
Nash, 1950
, who proposed the Nash solution as the unique outcome satisfying four axioms: Pareto optimality, symmetry, scale invariance, and independence of irrelevant alternatives. In the same complete-information setting,
Kalai and Smorodinsky, 1975
replaced the independence axiom with resource monotonicity to obtain the Kalai–Smorodinsky (KS) solution, while
Kalai, 1977
;
Myerson, 1977
introduced the egalitarian solution by substituting scale invariance with resource monotonicity. We refer the reader to
Aumann and Hart, 1992
for a comprehensive survey.
A separate strand of literature studies bargaining under private information
(
Harsanyi and Selten, 1972
;
Myerson, 1984
;
Samuelson, 1984
;
Kennan and Wilson, 1993
;
Ausubel et al., 2002
, e.g.,)
, where agents possess asymmetric knowledge about their own types. In this setting, researchers have proposed analogues of the classical axioms and corresponding mechanism-based solutions.
While these works focus on the axiomatization and existence of fair outcomes in abstract bargaining problems, our paper takes a concrete mechanism design perspective: we study the single-item, multi-buyer auction with i.i.d. private values, fully characterize the mechanisms that implement the Pareto frontier, and analyze the welfare guarantees of two canonical bargaining solutions—the
KS Solution
and the
Nash Solution
—which embody distinct notions of fairness between the seller and the buyers.

2
Preliminaries

In this work, we study the space of Bayesian single-seller, multi-buyer auctions, focusing on the Pareto frontier and the implications of fairness constraints on the welfare. We next present some background.

Environment.
There is a single seller and
n
unit-demand buyers, whom we collectively refer to as traders (or agents). The seller holds a single item with zero production cost. Buyers have quasi-linear utilities: given an allocation
{x}\in[0,1]
and a monetary transfer
{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
that she pays (i.e., her
payment
to the seller), a buyer with private value
{v}
obtains a utility of
{x}\cdot{v}-{p}
. Meanwhile, upon receiving a monetary transfer (buyers’ payments) of
{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the seller obtains revenue (and utility) equal to
{p}
.

For each buyer
i\in[n]
, her private value
{v}_{i}
is drawn independently and identically (i.i.d.) from a publicly-known common valuation distribution
F
. We assume that the distribution
F
has a continuous support
{\sf supp}(F)
,
so for some
{{v}}_{H}\geq{{v}}_{L}\geq 0
it holds that either
{{\sf supp}(F)}=[{{v}}_{L},{{v}}_{H}]
or
{{\sf supp}(F)}=[{{v}}_{L},\infty)
.
We assume that
F
has no atoms, except possibly at
{{v}}_{H}
.
Following the convention in auction design literature, we define the cumulative density function (CDF) of the buyer’s valuation distribution as
F(t)\triangleq\text{Pr}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{{v}<t}\right]
.
We assume that it is differentiable within the interior of the support, with measure 1, and denote the corresponding probability density function by
f
.
Consequently,
F
is left-continuous. Finally, we assume there exists a price
{p}\in{\sf supp}(F)
that maximizes the expected revenue
{p}\cdot(1-F({p}))
.

Mechanisms.
A benevolent social planner (i.e., a mechanism designer) aims to design mechanisms that facilitate trade between the seller and the buyers. Specifically, a mechanism
\mathcal{M}=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p})
first solicits bids from all buyers and then determines the allocation and payments according to an allocation rule
\boldsymbol{x}=({x}_{1},\dots,{x}_{n})
and a payment rule
\boldsymbol{p}=({p}_{1},\dots,{p}_{n})
. For each buyer
i\in[n]
, her allocation
{x}_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\to[0,1]
and payment
{p}_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
are functions mapping the bid profile of all
n
buyers to, respectively, the fraction of the item allocated to her and the monetary transfer she makes. Then, the social planner transfers all money received from buyers directly to the seller.

1

In the literature, such mechanisms are known as strong budget balanced (SBB) mechanisms, since the social planner does not keep any money. Such a restriction causes no loss on our goal of maximizing welfare, as we will discuss in
Appendix

A
.

Under the revelation principle, a standard argument in the literature
(
Myerson, 1981
)
, it is without loss of generality to restrict attention to ex-post feasible, Bayesian incentive compatible, and interim individually rational mechanisms. These properties are formally defined as follows.

•

Ex-post feasibility
: the mechanism never allocates more than its supply (of one unit), i.e., for any
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\sum_{i\in[n]}{x}_{i}(\boldsymbol{v})\leq 1
.

•

Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC)
: given any buyer
i
and her value
{v}_{i}
, when all other buyers bid truthfully, buyer
i
maximizes her interim utility by also bidding truthfully.

•

Interim individual rationality (interim IR)
: given any buyer
i
and her value
{v}_{i}
, her interim utility is non-negative when bidding truthfully.

Specifically, under BIC, we assume that buyers report their private values truthfully and thus interpret the allocation and payment rules
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p})
of a given mechanism as functions of the buyers’ true valuation profile
\boldsymbol{v}=({v}_{1},\dots,{v}_{n})
, mapping it to their allocations, and payments, respectively.

Finally, since all buyers are ex-ante symmetric (i.e., their values are drawn i.i.d. from the same distribution
F
), any mechanism can be symmetrized by randomly permuting the buyers’ identities.
Therefore, without loss of generality, we restrict attention to mechanisms that are symmetric across buyers.
In all, we use
\mathfrak{M}
to denote the family of all mechanisms satisfying the conditions stated above, i.e., ex-post feasibility, BIC, interim IR, and symmetry.

Welfare maximization.

An important consideration for the social planner is the economic efficiency of the mechanism, which we measure by its social welfare – the combined utility of the seller and buyers.
For a given mechanism
\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}
, the
ex-ante seller revenue
is
\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\!\left[{\sum_{i\in[n]}{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{v})}\right]
, and for each buyer
i\in[n]
, her
ex-ante utility

U_{i,F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\!\left[{{x}_{i}(\boldsymbol{v})\cdot{v}_{i}-{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{v})}\right]
is the expected utility she receives under
\mathcal{M}
, where the expectation is taken over the realization of all buyers’ valuations from prior
F
and any randomness in the mechanism (under truthful reporting).
In addition, we define the
ex-ante buyers’ surplus
(also called residual surplus) as the sum of all buyers’ ex-ante utilities, i.e.,
U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)=\sum_{i\in[n]}U_{i,F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)
.
Finally, the
(ex-ante) social welfare
(or gains-from-trade) is defined as the sum of the ex-ante seller revenue and the ex-ante buyers’ surplus, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)\triangleq\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)+U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right).

We denote the
optimum social welfare benchmark

\textsc{Opt}_{F}
by

\displaystyle\textsc{Opt}_{F}\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{v}\sim F^{n}}\!\left[{\max\{{v}_{1},\dots,{v}_{n}\}}\right].

Notably, this benchmark is achievable by the classic Vickrey auction
(
Vickrey, 1961
)
, i.e., the second-price auction.
We also define the optimal (ideal) seller revenue and the optimal (ideal) buyers’ surplus as

\Pi_{F}^{*}=\max_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}}\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right),\quad U_{F}^{*}=\max_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}}U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right).

Pareto optimality and Pareto frontier.

In addition to maximizing social welfare, another important aspect is to consider how to distribute it between the seller and all buyers.
In this sense, we define the Pareto-optimal mechanisms in
\mathfrak{M}
and the induced Pareto frontier as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Pareto domination, Pareto optimality, and Pareto frontier)
.

Fix a value distribution
F
. We say a mechanism
\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}
is
Pareto-dominated
by
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{M}
, if both
\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)\geq\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)
and
U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)\geq U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)
hold, and at least one of them holds with strict inequality.
\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}
is
Pareto-optimal
, if no mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{M}
Pareto-dominates
\mathcal{M}
. In this case, we say that the (seller revenue, buyers’ surplus) pair
(\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right),U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right))
realized by mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is on the
Pareto frontier
.

As a direct consequence of the definition, we remark that there is no point on the Pareto frontier with lower buyers’ surplus than that of the seller-revenue-optimal mechanism, i.e., the Myerson auction. In fact, the mechanism that realizes such a point is Pareto-dominated by the Myerson auction. Similarly, there is also no point on the Pareto frontier with lower seller revenue than that of the buyer-surplus-optimal mechanism. We will characterize the Pareto frontier in
Section

3
. Further, in
Sections

4
and
5
, we use that to derive implications on the welfare of two standard fairness solution concepts, i.e., Kalai-Somorodinsky fairness, Nash welfare maximization, that are on that Pareto frontier.

Additional Mechanism Design Notations and Concepts

Next, we introduce notations and concepts from the mechanism design literature that we use in our analysis.

Regularity, MHR, and anti-MHR.

We first present several prominent classes of distributions.

Definition 2.2

(Regularity, MHR, and anti-MHR)
.

For a valuation distribution
F
:

•

F
is called
regular
if its virtual value function
\psi({v})\triangleq{v}-\frac{1-F({v})}{f({v})}
is monotone non-decreasing in
{v}
.

•

F
satisfies the
monotone hazard rate (MHR) condition
if its hazard rate function
\phi({v})\triangleq\frac{f({v})}{1-F({v})}
is monotonically non-decreasing in
{v}
.

•

F
satisfies the
anti-MHR condition
if its hazard rate function is monotonically non-increasing in
{v}
.

The MHR condition is stronger than the regularity condition. Meanwhile, the only intersection between the anti-MHR and MHR distributions is the exponential distribution, whose hazard rate is constant. However, there are some natural distributions that are both regular and anti-MHR besides exponential distributions, e.g., equal revenue distributions, Pareto type I distributions with shape
\alpha\geq 1
, Pareto type II distributions (a.k.a. Lomax distributions) with shape
\alpha\geq 1
, etc.

The quantile
{q}
of a buyer with value
{v}
drawn from distribution

{q}=1-F({v})
. The value function

maps a buyer’s quantile to her value, i.e.,
v({q})\triangleq\inf\{{v}\,:\,F({v})>1-{q}\}
. Since
F(\cdot)
is left-continuous, so is
v(\cdot)
.
We further assume that
v(\cdot)
is also differentiable in
(0,1)
, and use

Indirect Vickrey auctions.

Finally, we introduce
indirect Vickrey auctions
, as originally defined in
Hartline and Roughgarden, 2008
. These auctions extend the Vickrey auction by restricting the bidding space and setting reserves.

Definition 2.4

(Indirect Vickrey auctions)
.

An
indirect Vickrey auction
is parameterized by a bidding space
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a uniform reserve
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Buyers are
required
to bid in
\mathcal{B}
, and the buyer
i^{*}
with the highest bid wins the item (with ties broken uniformly at random), provided that it is no smaller than the reserve
r
. The winner pays her
critical bid
, defined as the minimum bid she would need to submit to remain the winner.

We say the bidding space
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}
of an
indirect Vickrey auction

\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}
is a
refinement
of the bidding space
\mathcal{B}^{\ddagger}
of another
indirect Vickrey auction

\mathcal{M}^{\ddagger}
, if
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}
is a superset of
\mathcal{B}^{\ddagger}
. Generally speaking, if
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}
refines
\mathcal{M}^{\ddagger}
, it allows more abundant bidding strategies for each buyer than the latter.

We remark that although
indirect Vickrey auctions
are not direct-revelation mechanisms—and thus not BIC by definition—they induce dominant strategy equilibria. Specifically, it is a dominant strategy for each buyer to submit the supremum of the bids in the feasible bid space
\mathcal{B}
that are no larger than her true valuation. By the revelation principle, there exists an equivalent direct-revelation DSIC mechanism that yields the same interim allocation and payment rules. Therefore, with a slight abuse of notation, we treat
indirect Vickrey auctions
as belonging to the mechanism class
\mathfrak{M}
.

As shown in
Myerson, 1981
, a seller-revenue-optimal mechanism is an
indirect Vickrey auction
with the lowest monopoly reserve
r=v({q_{m}})
.
3
3

3

When the buyer distribution is regular, this mechanism uses the unconstrained bidding space and the lowest monopoly reserve. Otherwise, the feasible bid space
\mathcal{B}
corresponds to the set of values that remain fixed under Myerson’s ironing procedure.

For brevity, we denote this mechanism by
\sf Mye
, and we have
\Pi_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)=\Pi_{F}^{*}
.

3
Characterizing the Pareto Frontier

In this section, we present structural characterizations of the Pareto frontier of the set of (seller revenue, buyers’ surplus) pairs for mechanisms that are ex-post feasible, BIC, interim IR, and symmetric.

We begin with a warm-up by analyzing the single-buyer market in
Section

3.1
, then extend our results to the substantially more challenging setting of the multi-buyer market in
Section

3.2
. Subsequently, in
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
, we provide a unified framework that can yield all of our structural characterizations.

3.1
Warm-Up: Single-Buyer Market

We start with the single-buyer market as a warm-up. For several critical points on the Pareto frontier, we know that the seller-optimal, buyer-optimal, and welfare-optimal mechanisms all take the form of posting a single (take-it-or-leave-it) price
(
Myerson, 1981
)
. In fact, it is not hard to show that any point on the Pareto frontier can be realized by a mechanism that randomizes over at most two posted prices.

Proposition 3.1
.

In a single-item seller, single-buyer market, for any valuation distribution
F
, any point on the Pareto frontier can be realized by a mechanism that fixes a probability
{{\nu}}\in[0,1]
ex ante, and then posts a price
{p}^{\dagger}
to the buyer with probability
{{\nu}}
and posts a price
{p}^{\ddagger}
to the buyer with probability
1-{{\nu}}
. Both these prices are no larger than the lowest monopoly reserve to the buyer. If the buyer accepts, she receives the item and pays the corresponding price to the seller.

To obtain
Proposition

3.1
, we observe that in the single buyer case, any mechanism in
\mathfrak{M}
is equivalent to a randomization over some price-posting mechanisms. Therefore, consider the set of (seller revenue, buyers’ surplus) pairs that can be reached by some mechanism in
\mathfrak{M}
. We then know that all extreme points of this 2-dimensional set are achieved by some price-posting mechanisms. Thus, all points in this set can be realized by a randomization of at most two price-posting mechanisms.
We further have the following fact.

Fact 3.2
.

For a single buyer, her surplus increases as the posted price decreases.

Therefore, all price-posting mechanisms with a price larger than the lowest monopoly reserve are Pareto-dominated by the price-posting mechanism with the lowest monopoly reserve, i.e., the revenue-optimal mechanism. Subsequently, extreme points on the Pareto frontier are those with prices no larger than the lowest monopoly reserve. This proves
Proposition

3.1
.

In particular, when the value distribution is regular, we can show that price-posting mechanisms suffice to construct the entire Pareto frontier. We defer the proof of the following result to
Appendix

B
.

Proposition 3.3
.

In a single-item seller, single-buyer market, for any
regular
valuation distribution
F
, any point on the Pareto frontier can be realized by any point on the Pareto frontier can be realized by a single price-posting mechanism.

A natural subsequent question is whether, for general distributions, the Pareto frontier of randomized mechanisms strictly dominates the Pareto frontier of deterministic mechanisms.
In
Theorem

E.1
, we show that it is indeed the case.
In fact, under some valuation distribution
F
and some realizable buyers’ surplus
U
, the optimal seller revenue of single price-posting mechanisms can be arbitrarily small compared to that of a randomization over two price-posting mechanisms.
When restricting to the family of quasi-regular value distributions
(
Feng and Jin, 2024
)
, the gap between these two Pareto frontiers still exists, but for any buyers’ surplus
U
, the ratio of seller revenues is bounded by a constant. We defer a formal presentation and an analysis to
Appendix

E

3.2

We now consider the setting with multiple ex-ante symmetric buyers whose values are drawn i.i.d. from the same value distribution
F
. In contrast to the relatively simple form of Pareto-optimal mechanisms in the single-buyer market, Pareto-optimal mechanisms in the multi-buyer setting might be significantly more complex due to competition among buyers.
In this section, we additionally make the following assumption.
4
4

4

For multi-buyer markets, our characterizations of Pareto-optimal mechanisms rely on additional distributional assumptions. This is driven by both technical and conceptual considerations. As we illustrate in
Section

From a results perspective, they also enable a structurally simple and interpretable characterization of the frontier. 3.3
A Unified Framework for Characterizing Pareto Frontier

In this section, we present a unified analysis framework for characterizing the Pareto frontier, which can be applied to prove all the above results, both for the single-buyer market and the multi-buyer market. Our main idea is to transfer this problem to a constrained optimization problem that maximizes seller revenue subject to a lower bound constraint on buyers’ surplus. We then solve this problem via the Lagrangian dual method. This problem could be of independent interest itself.

We start with the following result.

Lemma 3.9
.

Any mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{M}
is Pareto-optimal, if and only if
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}
is an optimal solution to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}}\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right),\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\ U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)\geq U^{\dagger}.

(1)

In the above,
U^{\dagger}=U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.9
.

We prove the two sides in order.

“Only if” side.
Suppose that
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}
is not an optimal solution to (
1
). Then there exists a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
such that
\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)>\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)
and
U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)\geq U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)
, contradicting that
(\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right),U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right))
is on the Pareto frontier.

“If” side.
Suppose
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}
is an optimal solution to (
1
), then there is no mechanism such that
\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)>\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)
and
U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)\geq U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)
both hold.
We now show that it is also impossible that
\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)=\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)
and
U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)>U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)
both hold. Otherwise, consider the mechanism that randomizes between
\sf Mye
and
\mathcal{M}
such that the buyers’ surplus is
U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)
. Since
\sf Mye
maximizes seller revenue, we know that seller revenue under this randomized mechanism is at least
\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)
. By the optimality of
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}
, we know that
\Pi_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)=\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)=\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)
. Notice that
\sf Mye
is the mechanism that maximizes buyers’ surplus among all seller-utility-maximizing mechanisms; this implies a contradiction.
∎

Under
Lemma

3.9
, to characterize the full Pareto frontier, we only need to consider the solutions to the following program when
U\in[U_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right),U_{F}^{*}]
:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}}\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right),\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\ U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)\geq U.

This is to say, characterizing Pareto-optimal solutions is equivalent to studying mechanisms that maximize seller revenue under a buyers’ surplus constraint.
Now, for the above constrained optimization problem, we consider the dual problem with Lagrangian variable
{\lambda}
on the surplus constraint.

\displaystyle\min_{{\lambda}\geq 0}\max_{\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}}\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)+{\lambda}\cdot U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)-{\lambda}\cdot U.

Since for any given
{\lambda}\geq 0
, the term
-{\lambda}\cdot U
is a constant that does not depend on the mechanism, we aim to find a mechanism that maximizes the following expression:

\displaystyle\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)+{\lambda}\cdot U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)+{\lambda}\cdot\mathchoice{\left(\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)-U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)\right)}{(\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)-U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right))}{(\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)-U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right))}{(\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)-U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right))}

\displaystyle={\lambda}\cdot\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)+(1-{\lambda})\cdot\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right),

which is a mix of welfare and seller revenue.
En route to solving the original constrained optimization problem, our main idea is to take the following two steps:

Step 1.

For any Lagrangian variable
{\lambda}\geq 0
, we characterize the optimal mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{{\lambda}}^{*}
to maximize the mixed welfare-seller revenue objective
{\lambda}\cdot\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)+(1-{\lambda})\cdot\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)
. We call this the primal problem.

Step 2.

Prove strong duality of the problem, by establishing the existence of a dual variable
{\lambda}(U)\geq 0
and corresponding optimal primal mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{{\lambda}(U)}^{*}
that satisfy KKT conditions. This immediately induces dual optimality.

3.4
Proof of
Theorem

3.4

To demonstrate how the above framework can be applied, we prove
Theorem

3.4
in this section. Specifically, we show that when the valuation distribution is both regular and anti-MHR, every point on the Pareto frontier is realized by a second-price auction with a reserve no larger than the lowest monopoly reserve.

To analyze this multi-buyer setting, we consider the interim space. We use
{x}({q})
to denote any buyer’s interim allocation probability when her value is
v({q})
. We further use
{X}({q})
to denote the accumulated allocation, i.e.,
{X}({q})\triangleq\int_{0}^{{q}}{x}(t)\ \mathrm{d}t
. By Border’s condition
(
Border, 1991
)
, an interim allocation function
{x}({q})
on the quantile space can be realized ex post by a BIC and interim IR mechanism if and only if

\displaystyle{X}({q})\leq\frac{1-(1-{q})^{n}}{n},\quad\forall{q}\in[0,1];\qquad{x}({q})\leq\frac{1}{n},\quad\forall{q}\in[0,1];\qquad{x}({q})\text{ is decreasing. }

Specifically, in the single buyer case, the first constraint becomes
{X}({q})\leq{q}
, which is directly implied by the second constraint
{x}({q})\leq 1
and non-restrictive. But this is not the case for the multi-buyer case.

Back to our problem, according to the revenue equivalence theorem, the expected revenue under a mechanism with interim allocation
{x}({q})
is
n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{x}({q})\cdot R^{\prime}({q})}\right]
, which is derived by seeing
{x}({q})
as a randomization of price-posting mechanisms
(
Myerson, 1981
)
.
Thus, the constrained program (
1
) is formalized as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\max_{{x}}n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{x}({q})\cdot R^{\prime}({q})}\right],\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\ n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{x}({q})\cdot(v({q})-R^{\prime}({q}))}\right]\geq U,\\
{X}({q})\leq\frac{1-(1-{q})^{n}}{n},\quad\forall{q}\in[0,1];\qquad{x}({q})\leq\frac{1}{n},\quad\forall{q}\in[0,1];\qquad{x}({q})\text{ is decreasing. }\end{gathered}

For brevity, we let
\hat{X}({q})\triangleq\nicefrac{{1-(1-{q})^{n}}}{{n}}
and
\hat{x}({q})\triangleq\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}
.
As mentioned in our unified framework, we Lagrangify the surplus constraint to derive the following dual program:

\min_{{\lambda}\geq 0}\max_{{X}({q})\leq\hat{X}({q}),\;{x}({q})\leq\hat{x}({q}),\;{x}({q})\text{ is decreasing}}n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{x}({q})\cdot({\lambda}\cdot v({q})+(1-{\lambda})\cdot R^{\prime}({q}))}\right]-{\lambda}\cdot U.

In particular, we let

{R}_{{\lambda}}({q})\triangleq{\lambda}\cdot\int_{0}^{{q}}v(t)\ \mathrm{d}t+(1-{\lambda})\cdot{R}({q}).

We then notice that
{\lambda}\cdot v({q})+(1-{\lambda})\cdot R^{\prime}({q})={R}^{\prime}_{{\lambda}}({q})
. So the dual program is the following:

\min_{{\lambda}\geq 0}\max_{{X}({q})\leq\hat{X}({q}),\;{x}({q})\leq\hat{x}({q}),\;{x}({q})\text{ is decreasing}}n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{x}({q})\cdot{R}^{\prime}_{{\lambda}}({q})}\right]-{\lambda}\cdot U.

Our roadmap is as follows.
First, for each
{\lambda}\geq 0
, we derive the allocation
{x}({q})
that maximizes the primal objective.
Further, we establish the existence of a
\lambda^{*}\geq 0
such that the complementary slackness condition is met.
They in together solve the problem.

Here, one special property brought by the anti-MHR condition is that we can restrict ourselves to
{\lambda}\in[0,1]
. In other words, we can prove that it is without loss to suppose
\lambda^{*}\leq 1
in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.10
.

For anti-MHR value distribution
F
, there exists a
{\lambda}\in[0,1]
that minimizes

\max_{{X}({q})\leq\hat{X}({q}),\;{x}({q})\leq\hat{x}({q}),\;{x}({q})\text{ is decreasing}}n\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{x}({q})\cdot{R}^{\prime}_{{\lambda}}({q})}\right]-{\lambda}\cdot U

for all
{\lambda}\geq 0
.

Since the value distribution is regular, we know that
{R}({q})
is concave. Meanwhile, as
v(t)
is non-negative and non-increasing,
\int_{0}^{{q}}v(t)\ \mathrm{d}t
is also a concave function of
{q}
. Therefore, for
{\lambda}\in[0,1]
,
{R}_{{\lambda}}({q})

4
The Kalai-Smorodinsky Solution

Having characterized the Pareto frontier in
Section

3
, an important question remains: which point on this frontier should be implemented? A natural desideratum is to achieve fairness between the two sides—the seller and the buyers—while maintaining strong efficiency guarantees.

Guided by this principle, we interpret the selection of a Pareto-optimal mechanism as a cooperative bargaining problem
(
Nash, 1950
)
between the seller and the buyers.
5
5

5

We refer the reader to
Babaioff et al., 2025
for further discussion on interpreting mechanism design as a cooperative bargaining problem.
In this section, we study a canonical solution concept from bargaining theory: the
KS Solution

(

)
. (Another canonical solution—the
Nash Solution
—is analyzed in

Definition 4.1

(
KS Solution
)
.

Fix a valuation distribution
F
. A Pareto-optimal mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is a
Kalai-Smorodinsky (KS) Solution
if both the ex-ante seller revenue and ex-ante buyers’ surplus achieve the same fraction of each side’s own ideal benchmark, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)}{\Pi_{F}^{*}}=\frac{U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)}{U_{F}^{*}}.

(

)

Here, the benchmarks
\Pi_{F}^{*}
and
U_{F}^{*}
denote the optimal achievable ex-ante seller revenue and ex-ante buyers’ surplus, respectively, over all mechanisms in
\mathfrak{M}
.

We make the following remarks regarding the

.
First, due to the convexity of the mechanism space and the linearity of both ex-ante seller revenue and buyers’ surplus, the set of achievable payoff pairs (seller revenue, buyers’ surplus) is convex, and the Pareto frontier is continuous. Consequently, a
KS Solution
always exists
(
Kalai and Smorodinsky, 1975
)
.

Second, while multiple mechanisms may implement the

, they all correspond to the same payoff pair on the Pareto frontier and thus yield identical social welfare. Therefore, our structural characterization of Pareto-optimal mechanisms in
Section

3
can be used to construct an explicit implementation of the

.

Third,
Babaioff et al., 2025
study the single-seller, single-buyer bilateral-trade setting and refer to constraint (
KS fairness
) as
KS fairness
. By definition, the
KS Solution
maximizes social welfare among all KS-fair mechanisms.
6
6

6

Indeed, to establish positive welfare guarantees for the

, we sometimes construct a KS-fair mechanism and lower bound its welfare; by definition, this serves as a lower bound on the welfare of the

We now present the main result of this section: the welfare approximation guarantee for the

The Nash Solution

In this section, we consider another canonical solution concept from bargaining theory: the
Nash Solution

(

)
.

Definition 5.1

(

)
.

Fix a valuation distribution
F
. A Pareto-optimal mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is a

if it maximizes the product of all traders (seller and buyers)’s ex-ante utilities, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\in\argmax_{\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\in\mathfrak{M}}~\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right)\cdot\prod\nolimits_{i\in[n]}U_{i,F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}}\right).

maximizes the product of the seller’s utility and the utility of

F
is both regular and anti-MHR, the

performs well: like the

, it achieves asymptotically optimal social welfare as the market size grows.) All the missing proofs in this section can be found in

Appendix A
Strong Budget Balance vs. Weak Budget Balance

In this section, we introduce the concept of weak budget balance, by extending the definition of a mechanism. In general, a mechanism
\mathcal{M}=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p},\tilde{{p}})
includes an allocation rule
\boldsymbol{x}=({x}_{1},\dots,{x}_{n})
, a buyer payment rule
\boldsymbol{p}=({p}_{1},\dots,{p}_{n})
, and a seller payment rule
\tilde{{p}}
. As previously defined, for each buyer
i\in[n]
, her allocation
{x}_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\to[0,1]
and payment
{p}_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
map the bids of all
n
buyers to, respectively, the fraction of the item allocated to her and the monetary transfer she makes.
Further, we involve an additional seller payment rule
\tilde{{p}}\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which maps the bid profile to the monetary transfer received by the seller.

As standard in the literature
(
Myerson, 1981
)
, under this extended definition, it is without loss of generality to restrict attention to ex-post feasible, Bayesian incentive compatible (BIC), interim individually rational (IR), ex-ante (weak) budget balanced (WBB) mechanisms. Specifically, the last property is defined as follows.

•

Ex-ante weak budget balance
: The total ex-ante payment collected from all buyers is at least the ex-ante payment sent to the seller, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i\in[n]}{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{v})]\geq\mathbb{E}[\tilde{{p}}(\boldsymbol{v})]
. When
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i\in[n]}{p}_{i}(\boldsymbol{v})]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{{p}}(\boldsymbol{v})]
, the mechanism is said to satisfy
ex-ante strong budget balance (SBB)
.

One observes that in the main body, we focus on SBB mechanisms, since all buyers’ payments are directly transferred to the seller. We now show that this is without loss in characterizing the Pareto frontier, in the sense that every Pareto-optimal mechanism is SBB.

Lemma A.1
.

In the family of mechanisms that are ex-post feasible, BIC, interim IR, and
WBB
, all Pareto-optimal mechanisms are
SBB
.

Proof of
Lemma

A.1
.

Given any WBB mechanism that is not SBB, one can always transfer any money left to the mechanism to the seller in the ex-ante sense. This results in an SBB mechanism that Pareto-dominates the original mechanism, since seller revenue strictly increases while buyers’ surplus does not change. This finishes the proof.
∎

As a consequence, since all our results in the main body focus on Pareto-optimal mechanisms, they can also be extended to the family of mechanisms that satisfy WBB.

First Step: A General Lagrangian Analysis

To analyze the setting, we consider the interim space. With a little bias of notation, we use

Back to our problem, according to the revenue equivalence theorem, the expected revenue under a mechanism with interim allocation

Meanwhile, we give buyers’ surplus under

Proposition B.5

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{x}({q})\cdot(v({q})-R^{\prime}({q}))}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{x}({q})\cdot(-{q}v^{\prime}({q}))}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{q}}\!\left[{{X}({q})\cdot({q}v^{\prime}({q}))^{\prime}}\right]-{X}(1)\cdot v^{\prime}(1)

{{\nu}}

We now consider regular value distributions.

B.5

When the buyers’ value distribution is anti-MHR, we prove the following two results.

See

We first introduce the following result given by

B.13

For anti-MHR value distributions, under the process

C.1
Proof of

See

Our analysis relies on the following lemmas regarding the revenue and buyers’ surplus guarantee of the second-price auction.

Lemma C.1

(
Bulow and Klemperer, 1996
)
.

For any market size
n\geq 1
and any regular valuation distribution
F
, the revenue of the second-price auction (

) is at least

Lemma C.2

(
Hartline and Roughgarden, 2008
)
.

For any market size
n\geq 1
and any anti-MHR valuation distribution
F
, the buyers’ surplus of the second-price auction (
SPA
) is optimal, i.e.,
U_{F}\!\left({{\sf SPA}}\right)=U_{F}^{*}
.

Lemma C.3

(
Myerson, 1981
;
Hartline and Roughgarden, 2008
)
.

Fix a buyer with a value drawn from a valuation distribution
F
.
For every mechanism
\mathcal{M}\in\mathfrak{M}
,
the buyer’s ex ante payment and utility satisfy

\mathbb{E}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{{p}({v})}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{\psi({v})\cdot{x}({v})}\right]

and

U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{(1/\phi({v}))\cdot{x}({v})}\right]
,
where
{x}({v})
is the interim allocation of the buyer with value
{v}
,
\psi({v})
is her virtual value, and
\phi({v})
is her hazard rate.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

We now prove all the statements sequentially.

Given any valuation distribution
F
, we show that the social welfare of the

is at least
\frac{1}{2}
of the optimal social welfare. Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{1/\phi({v}_{\rm win})}\right]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle{x}_{l}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[{1/\phi({v}_{\rm win})\,\mid\,\text{winner exists and ${v}_{\rm win}\leq t$}}\right]+{x}_{h}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[{1/\phi({v}_{\rm win})\,\mid\,\text{winner exists and ${v}_{\rm win}>t$}}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}{}

\displaystyle{x}_{l}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{\phi({v})\,\mid\,{v}\leq t}\right]+{x}_{h}\cdot\phi(t)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}{}

\displaystyle{x}_{l}\cdot(1-o(1))\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}+{x}_{h}\cdot\frac{1}{2\ln\ln n}

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{=}{}

\displaystyle\left({x}_{l}\cdot(1+o(1))+{x}_{h}\cdot o(1)\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{{v}}\right],

where inequality (a) holds since buyers’ hazard rate function
\phi
is decreasing, and the buyer’s interim allocation rule is weakly increasing, equality (b) holds since hazard rate function
\phi({v})=2{v}
, and inequality (c) holds since
\mathbb{E}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{{v}}\right]=(1-o(1))\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}
by construction.

Putting both the pieces together, the social welfare of the
KS Solution

\mathcal{M}
can be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)+U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\min\left\{\frac{\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)}{\Pi_{F}^{*}},\frac{U_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)}{U_{F}^{*}}\right\}\cdot\left(\Pi_{F}^{*}+U_{F}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{}{\leq}\min\left\{\frac{\left({x}_{l}\cdot o(1)+{x}_{h}\right)\cdot K}{(1-o(1))\cdot K},\frac{\left({x}_{l}\cdot(1+o(1))+{x}_{h}\cdot o(1)\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{{v}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{{v}\sim F}\!\left[{{v}}\right]}\right\}\cdot(1-o(1))\cdot K

\displaystyle=\min\left\{(1+o(1))({x}_{l}\cdot o(1)+{x}_{h}),\left({x}_{l}\cdot(1+o(1))+{x}_{h}\cdot o(1)\right)\right\}\cdot(1-o(1))\cdot K

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)\cdot K=\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)\cdot\textsc{Opt}_{F},

where equality (a) holds by the welfare definition, equality (b) holds due to
KS fairness
in the definition of the
KS Solution
, inequality (c) holds due to the seller revenue and buyers’ surplus upper bounds derived above, and inequality (d) holds since
{x}_{l}+{x}_{h}\leq 1
by construction. This finishes the analysis of
Example

4.2
and concludes the proof of
Theorem

4.1
.
∎

Lemma C.5

(
Hartline and Roughgarden, 2008
)
.

Consider a market with
m
identical units of a good and

Lemma C.6
.

Consider a market with
m
identical units of a good and
n\geq m
unit-demand buyers whose valuations are drawn i.i.d. from a MHR distribution
F
. The social welfare of the
Buyer-Offer Mechanism
that maximizes the buyers’ surplus is at least an
\frac{1}{1+H_{n}-H_{m}}
-approximation to the optimal social welfare
\textsc{Opt}_{F}
.

Proof.

Invoking
Lemma

C.5
, the
Buyer-Offer Mechanism
(
BOM
) allocates all
m
units uniformly at random to
m
buyers at zero prices. Its social welfare is therefore

\displaystyle\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\sf BOM}\right)=m\cdot\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X}\right],

where
X\sim F
.
The optimal social welfare equals

\displaystyle\textsc{Opt}_{F}=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right],

where
X_{(i:n)}
denotes the
i
-th largest order statistic from
n
i.i.d. samples.

Thus it suffices to show that for every MHR distribution
F
and every
1\leq k\leq n
,

k\cdot\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X}\right]\;\geq\;\frac{1}{1+H_{n}-H_{k}}\sum\nolimits_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right].

(5)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum\nolimits_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right]}{k\cdot\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X}\right]}\;\leq\;1+H_{n}-H_{k}.

We prove (
5
) in two steps.

Step 1: Reduction to the exponential distribution.

Let
\xi_{T}(k,n)\triangleq 1+H_{n}-H_{k}
.
Recall the (quantile-)value function
v:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\displaystyle v({q})=F^{-1}(1-q),

and let
g_{i,n}({q})
denote the density of the
i
-th largest order statistic of
n
i.i.d.
U[0,1]
samples:

\displaystyle g_{i,n}({q})=\frac{n!}{(i-1)!(n-i)!}(1-q)^{i-1}q^{n-i}.

Using the quantile representation of expectations,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}g_{i,n}({q})\,v({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}q,\qquad\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}v({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}{q}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right]-\xi_{T}(k,n)\cdot k\cdot\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{X}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}w({q})\,v({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}{q},

where

\displaystyle w({q})\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{i\in[k]}g_{i,n}({q})-\xi_{T}(k,n)\cdot k.

Claim C.7
.

The function
w({q})
is weakly decreasing on
[0,1]
and has at most one zero.

Proof.

Each
g_{i,n}({q})
is a unimodal polynomial density whose derivative satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\ \mathrm{d}}{\ \mathrm{d}{q}}g_{i,n}({q})=g_{i,n}({q})\left(\frac{n-i}{{q}}-\frac{i-1}{1-{q}}\right),

which is negative whenever
{q}
exceeds its mode
\frac{n-i}{n-1}
. Since
i\leq k
, all modes lie weakly to the right of
\frac{n-k}{n-1}
. Therefore the sum
\sum\nolimits_{i\in[k]}g_{i,n}({q})
is decreasing on
[0,1]
, and subtracting a constant preserves monotonicity. A monotone function can cross zero at most once.
∎

Let
{q}^{*}
denote the (possibly nonexistent) zero of
w({q})
. Define
{v}^{*}=v({q}^{*})
.
Consider the exponential distribution
T^{\prime}
with CDF

\displaystyle F_{T^{\prime}}(v)=1-\exp\!\left(\ln({q}^{*})\,\frac{v}{{v}^{*}}\right),

whose quantile-value function satisfies

\displaystyle{v}_{T^{\prime}}({q})={v}^{*}\frac{\ln{q}}{\ln{q}^{*}}.

Because
F
is MHR, its quantile-value function
v({q})
is convex in
\ln{q}
. Hence
v
lies below the chord through
({q}^{*},{v}^{*})
, implying

\displaystyle{v}_{T^{\prime}}({q})\geq v({q})\quad\text{for }q\leq{q}^{*},\qquad{v}_{T^{\prime}}({q})\leq v({q})\quad\text{for }q\geq{q}^{*}.

Since
w({q})
is positive for
q<{q}^{*}
and negative for
q>{q}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}w({q})\,v({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}{q}\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{1}w({q})\,{v}_{T^{\prime}}({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}{q}.

Finally, expectations under exponential distributions scale linearly with the mean. Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}w({q})\,{v}_{T^{\prime}}({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}{q}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum\nolimits_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}_{T}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right]-\xi_{T}(k,n)\cdot k\cdot\mathbb{E}_{T}\!\left[{X}\right]\right)\cdot\frac{\mathbb{E}_{T^{\prime}}\!\left[{X}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{T}\!\left[{X}\right]}=0,

where
T
denotes the unit-mean exponential distribution and the equality follows by the definition of
\xi_{T}(k,n)
.
This completes the reduction.

Step 2: Evaluation under the exponential distribution.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
. The expected
i
-th largest order statistic satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right]=H_{n}-H_{i-1}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right]=k(H_{n}-H_{k})+k,

and since
\mathbb{E}\!\left[{X}\right]=1
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum\nolimits_{i\in[k]}\mathbb{E}\!\left[{X_{(i:n)}}\right]}{k}=1+H_{n}-H_{k}=\xi_{T}(k,n).

Combining the two steps proves (
5
) and completes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.8
.

Consider a market with
m
identical units of a good and
n\geq m
unit-demand buyers whose valuations are drawn i.i.d. from a MHR distribution
F
. The social welfare of the Myerson auction that maximizes the Bayesian revenue is at least an
\frac{1}{e-1}
-approximation to the optimal social welfare
\textsc{Opt}_{F}
.

Proof.

Consider
n\geq m
unit-demand buyers with i.i.d. values drawn from an MHR distribution
F
.
Let
\textsc{Opt}_{F}
denote the optimal social welfare, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textsc{Opt}_{F}=\sum\nolimits_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{{v}_{(i:n)}}\right].

where
{v}_{(i:n)}
is the
i
-th largest order statistics of
n
samples from distribution
F
.
The Bayesian revenue-optimal mechanism is the Myerson auction, which for MHR distribution is the
second-price auction with (unique) monopoly reserve
r^{*}
.
We denote this mechanism by
\sf Mye
.
We prove that

\displaystyle\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)\;\geq\;\frac{1}{e-1}\,\textsc{Opt}_{F}.

The proof proceeds in two steps.

Step 1: Reduction to a truncated exponential distribution.

Let
r^{*}
be the monopoly reserve of MHR distribution
F
.
Define the (quantile-)value function
v({q})=F^{-1}(1-{q})
.
For MHR distributions,
v({q})
is convex in
\ln{q}
.
Moreover, the Myerson allocation rule is monotone and allocates units only to buyers with
values at least
r^{*}
.

Similar to the proof of
Lemma

C.6
, both the welfare of
\sf Mye

and the optimal welfare can be written as linear functionals of
v
:

\displaystyle\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}a({q})\,v({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}{q},\qquad\textsc{Opt}_{F}=\int_{0}^{1}b({q})\,v({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}{q},

where
a({q})
and
b({q})
are deterministic weight functions induced by the allocation rules.
The function
b({q})
corresponds to allocating to the top
m
order statistics, while

a({q})
coincides with
b({q})
for
{q}\leq{q_{m}}
and vanishes for
{q}>{q_{m}}
, where

{q_{m}}=1-F(r^{*})
is the monopoly quantile.
Note that the difference

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\bigl((e-1)a({q})-b({q})\bigr)v({q})\,\ \mathrm{d}{q}

has a single-crossing structure: the coefficient
(e-1)a({q})-b({q})
is non-negative for

{q}\leq{q_{m}}
and non-positive for
{q}>{q_{m}}
.
By the same extremal argument as in
Lemma

C.6
, among all MHR distributions with monopoly reserve
r^{*}
,
the ratio
\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)/\textsc{Opt}_{F}
is minimized by the exponential distribution

F({v})=1-e^{-{v}}
, truncated below at
r^{*}=1
.
Hence, it suffices to prove the lemma for the truncated exponential distribution

\displaystyle F({v})=\begin{cases}1-e^{-{v}},&0\leq{v}\leq 1,\\
1,&{v}>1.\end{cases}

Step 2: Reduction to the case
n=m
under the truncated exponential.

Under the worst-case distribution
F
(i.e., truncated exponential distribution with truncation at monopoly reserve
r^{*}=1
) established in Step 1, each buyer’s value equals
1
with probability
e^{-1}
and lies in
[0,1)
otherwise. By construction, the welfare approximation ratio can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)}{\textsc{Opt}_{F}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum\nolimits_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{{v}_{(i:n)}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{{v}_{(i:n)}\geq r^{*}\right\}}\right]}{\sum\nolimits_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{{v}_{(i:n)}}\right]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sum\nolimits_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{{v}_{(i:m)}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{{v}_{(i:m)}\geq r^{*}\right\}}\right]}{\sum\nolimits_{i\in[m]}\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{{v}_{(i:m)}}\right]}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{{v}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\left\{{v}\geq r^{*}\right\}}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{F}\!\left[{{v}}\right]}=\frac{1/e}{1-1/e}=\frac{1}{e-1}

This establishes the desired bound and completes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4.2
.

Let
{\sf VCG}
denote the
VCG Auction
. Our analysis considers two cases, based on the relative performance of
\mathcal{M}
with respect to the seller’s ideal benchmark
\Pi_{F}^{*}
and buyers’ ideal benchmark
U_{F}^{*}
.

Case 1:
\displaystyle{\Pi_{F}\!\left({{\sf VCG}}\right)}/{\Pi_{F}^{*}}\geq{U_{F}\!\left({{\sf VCG}}\right)}/{U_{F}^{*}}
.

In this case,
consider an ex ante randomization between
VCG Auction
(
VCG
) and the
Buyer-Offer Mechanism
(
BOM) that maximizes the buyers’ surplus, and let
{{\nu}}
denote the probability of implementing the VCG Auction.

Since the valuation distribution is MHR, Buyer-Offer Mechanism allocates the
m
units uniformly at random to buyers for free, yielding zero seller revenue. To satisfy
KS fairness
(by case assumption and mixture construction, this can be done), the VCG Auction must be used with probability at least
1/2
. By
Lemma

C.6
, the social welfare of this mixture is lower bounded by

\displaystyle{{\nu}}\cdot\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({{\sf VCG}}\right)+(1-{{\nu}})\cdot\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\sf BOM}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle{{\nu}}\cdot\textsc{Opt}_{F}+(1-{{\nu}})\cdot\frac{1}{1+H_{n}-H_{m}}\cdot\textsc{Opt}_{F}

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot 1+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1+H_{n}-H_{m}}\right)\cdot\textsc{Opt}_{F}

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle\frac{2-\ln\!\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)}{2-2\ln\!\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)}\cdot\textsc{Opt}_{F},

where inequality (a) holds due to
Lemma

C.6
and the social welfare optimality of the VCG Auction, inequality (b) holds due to
{{\nu}}\geq 1/2
argued above, and inequality (c) holds due to the bound
H_{n}-H_{m}\leq\ln(n/m)
.

Case 2:
\displaystyle{\Pi_{F}\!\left({{\sf VCG}}\right)}/{\Pi_{F}^{*}}<{U_{F}\!\left({{\sf VCG}}\right)}/{U_{F}^{*}}
.

In this case, consider an ex ante randomization between the VCG Auction (VCG) and the Myerson auction (Mye) that maximizes the seller revenue, with
{{\nu}}
again denoting the probability of using
\mathcal{M}
. By
Lemma

C.4
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\Pi_{F}\!\left({\mathcal{M}}\right)}{\Pi^{*}}\geq\frac{n-m}{n}.

Moreover, since the distribution is MHR, the Myerson auction achieves at least a
1/e
fraction of the optimal social welfare
(
Hartline and Roughgarden, 2009
)
, implying that its buyers’ surplus satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{U_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)}{U_{F}^{*}}\leq 1-\frac{1}{e}.

To satisfy
KS fairness
(by case assumption and mixture construction, this can be done), the mixture must equalize the normalized seller revenue and buyers’ surplus:

\displaystyle{{\nu}}\cdot\frac{\Pi_{F}\!\left({{\sf VCG}}\right)}{\Pi_{F}^{*}}+(1-{{\nu}})\cdot\frac{\Pi_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)}{\Pi_{F}^{*}}\;=\;{{\nu}}\cdot\frac{U_{F}\!\left({{\sf VCG}}\right)}{U_{F}^{*}}+(1-{{\nu}})\cdot\frac{U_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)}{U_{F}^{*}}.

Using the bounds above and simplifying yields

\displaystyle{{\nu}}\geq\frac{1}{1+e\cdot\frac{m}{n}}.

Thus, the social welfare of the mixture is then lower bounded by

\displaystyle{{\nu}}\cdot\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({{\sf VCG}}\right)+(1-{{\nu}})\cdot\textsc{Wel}_{F}\!\left({\sf Mye}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle{{\nu}}\cdot\textsc{Opt}_{F}+(1-{{\nu}})\cdot\frac{1}{e-1}\cdot\textsc{Opt}_{F}

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}{}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e-1}{e}+\frac{1}{e+e^{2}\cdot\frac{m}{n}}\right)\cdot\textsc{Opt}_{F}

where inequality (a) holds due to
Lemma

C.8
and the social welfare optimality of the VCG Auction, and inequality (b) holds due to the lower bound of mixing probability
{{\nu}}
derived above.

Combining both cases, the welfare of the KS Solution is at least
\Psi(m/n)
times the optimum, completing the proof.
∎

Proof.

We analyze the welfare guarantee of the

in

, denoted by
\mathcal{M}
. Let random variable

Nash Solution

The revenue approximation ratio of a class of mechanisms
