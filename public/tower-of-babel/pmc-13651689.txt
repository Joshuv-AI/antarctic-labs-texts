Technical considerations for greater occipital nerve block in neuropathic scalp pruritus: A neurologic perspective
Authors: Josevânia Fulgêncio de Lima Arruda
Published: 2026

To the Editor: I read with interest the report by Kambala et al1 describing bedside bilateral greater occipital nerve block (GONB) with bupivacaine for recalcitrant scalp pruritus. Their work broadens the therapeutic perspective for neuropathic scalp itch, a challenging condition with limited effective options, and underscores the translational potential of peripheral nerve blocks in dermatologic practice. The proposed mechanism is biologically plausible given the shared C-fiber–mediated pathways between pain and itch.2

As neurologists, we routinely perform GONB for migraine, occipital neuralgia, cluster headache, and cervicogenic headache, with well-established efficacy and safety.3 I would like to offer a technical perspective that may help refine procedural standardization as this approach gains broader adoption.

In neurologic practice, the block is typically performed using a single skin insertion per nerve at the medial third of the line connecting the external occipital protuberance to the ipsilateral mastoid process.3 The needle is advanced to gently contact the periosteum and then withdrawn slightly before injection. Volumes of 2–3 mL per side are generally sufficient, given the superficial course of the nerve at the superior nuchal line.3,4 The technique is demonstrated in the Supplementary Video, available via Mendeley at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/p47hy8zh3r/1. A fanning technique is not routinely necessary, as adequate anesthetic diffusion is usually achieved with a single targeted injection. Ultrasound guidance at the C2 level may enhance precision in selected cases.4

Interdisciplinary collaboration may help standardize technique and clarify long-term outcomes as GONB emerges as a promising option for neuropathic scalp pruritus.
