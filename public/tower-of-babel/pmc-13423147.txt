Latent profiles of self-management behaviours in patients with type 2 diabetes and their associations with metabolic outcomes in China: a multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: Caihua Ye, Yan Lin, Kaining Chen, Zongcun Chen, Qiwei Zhou, Chengying Yu, Qihui Liu, Samuel Seidu, Xinjun Jiang, Vivien Xi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSelf-management behaviours are crucial for glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) but adherence is often poor. Most studies treat diabetes self-management (DSM) behaviours as a single construct, ignoring variations in behavioural patterns and their metabolic impacts.ObjectivesThis study aims to identify distinct DSM behaviour profiles in T2DM patients, explore associated factors, and compare their metabolic outcomes.DesignA multicentre cross-sectional study.SettingThe study was conducted from March to September 2024 at four tertiary care hospitals.ParticipantsA total of 304 patients diagnosed with T2DM were included.Primary outcomes and measuresThe primary outcomes were DSM behaviours, self-efficacy, habit strength and metabolic indicators (glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose, lipid profile, blood pressure and body mass index). Latent profile analysis was used to identify distinct DSM behaviour profiles. Univariate analyses (χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate) were performed to identify factors associated with profile membership, followed by multinomial logistic regression analysis. Analysis of variance with post hoc tests was used to compare metabolic indicators across profiles.ResultsThree distinct profiles were identified: low-engagement (14.0%), moderate-engagement (12.0%) and high-engagement behaviours (74.0%). These profiles were significantly associated with self-efficacy, personal monthly income, medication duration and diabetes-related complications (all p<0.05). Compared with the high-engagement profile, the moderate-engagement profile had significantly higher HbA1c, fasting blood glucose and total cholesterol, with the lowest high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, whereas the low-engagement profile showed higher fasting blood glucose, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol and systolic blood pressure (all p<0.05).ConclusionsDistinct DSM behaviour profiles exist among patients with T2DM and are influenced by psychosocial and clinical factors. The high-engagement behaviour profile was associated with more favourable glycaemic and lipid control, whereas the low- and moderate-engagement behaviour profiles were linked to poorer metabolic outcomes. These findings highlight the need for tailored interventions targeting behaviour-specific profiles to optimise metabolic management in T2DM.

Introduction

Diabetes is a major global public health concern. According to the International Diabetes Federation, an estimated 588.7 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes worldwide in 2024, of whom type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) accounted for more than 90% of all cases.1 In China, approximately 148.0 million adults were affected, representing the highest national prevalence globally.

Diabetes and its complications impose a substantial economic burden both globally and in China. The global diabetes-related health expenditure is estimated at around US$1 trillion, including approximately US$168.9 billion in China.1 Although lifestyle modification and pharmacological therapy can effectively delay disease progression and even induce remission,2 3 poor adherence to diabetes self-management (DSM) behaviours remains a major barrier to achieving optimal glycaemic control and preventing complications.4

DSM encompasses multiple domains, including dietary control, physical activity, self-monitoring, weight management, foot care and medication adherence.5 6 Previous studies have shown that DSM levels among patients with T2DM are generally suboptimal and influenced by a range of demographic, psychological and clinical factors.7 8 However, most research has relied on variable-centred approaches, treating DSM as a single construct and ignoring potential heterogeneity in behavioural patterns. Latent profile analysis (LPA) provides a person-centred approach that can identify distinct subgroups of patients with similar behavioural characteristics.9 While prior studies using LPA have revealed different DSM behaviour patterns, they often focused only on demographic and clinical factors,10 11 neglecting the role of psychological and social determinants emphasised in social cognitive theory, which posits that behaviour results from dynamic interactions among personal cognition, environment and behaviour practice.12

Moreover, although DSM behaviours are key predictors of metabolic outcomes, including glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), lipid levels, blood pressure and body mass index (BMI),13 14 existing studies have mainly examined single outcomes, such as HbA1c.10 Research exploring the associations between comprehensive DSM behaviour profiles and multiple metabolic indicators remains limited, especially among Chinese populations with T2DM.

Therefore, this study aimed to (1) identify latent profiles of DSM behaviours among patients with T2DM, (2) examine psychological, social and clinical factors associated with these profiles and (3) compare differences in key metabolic indicators across profiles. The findings are expected to provide evidence to inform individualised, profile-based interventions to improve diabetes management and metabolic outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a multicentre cross-sectional design and was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist to ensure methodological transparency and reporting quality.15

Study participants

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from the outpatient and inpatient departments of endocrinology in four tertiary hospitals in Hainan Province, China. As these institutions are major referral centres for diabetes care in the region and manage a large and diverse population of patients with T2DM, the cohort may broadly reflect the clinical characteristics of patients receiving tertiary diabetes care in Hainan Province. Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years old) diagnosed with T2DM according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes (2020 Edition).16 All participants were required to have normal communication ability and to be capable of completing the questionnaire independently or with assistance from trained researchers. Only those who provided written informed consent and voluntarily agreed to participate were included.

Patients were excluded if they had diagnosed psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment, or were unable to cooperate with the survey. Individuals experiencing severe diabetes-related complications or those diagnosed with cancer who had received radiotherapy or chemotherapy within the past 6 months were also excluded.

The sample size was determined based on the general rule that at least 5–10 participants should be included for each independent variable in multinomial analyses.17 Considering 22 independent variables, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 110–220. Allowing for an anticipated 20% rate of invalid responses, the final target sample size ranged from 132 to 264 participants.

Outcome measures

DSM behaviours

DSM behaviours were assessed using the Chinese version of the Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA) scale, originally developed by Toobert et al18 and translated and validated by Qiaoqin et al.19 The scale consists of six domains: general diet, special diet, physical exercise, blood glucose monitoring, foot care and medication adherence. Except for medication (one item), each domain includes two items, yielding a total of 11 items. Responses were rated on an 8-point Likert scale (0–7 points), reflecting the number of days per week the respondent performed each behaviour. The total possible score ranges from 0 to 77, with higher scores indicating better self-management. For each domain, the mean score of its constituent items was calculated and used for analysis, with higher scores indicating more frequent performance of the corresponding self-management behaviour. Based on established cutoffs, DSM behaviours were classified as poor (≤4.1), moderate (4.2–5.5) or good (≥5.6). The overall reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.83,19 with factor loadings exceeding 0.8 in prior validation, and the Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.788.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy was measured using the Chinese version of the Self-Efficacy for Diabetes Scale, developed and validated by Wei.20 This instrument includes four dimensions of diet, exercise, blood glucose control and disease management, that comprise a total of nine items. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1=no confidence to 5=complete confidence), producing a total score ranging from 9 to 45, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. The average item score was used to categorise self-efficacy as low (≤2), moderate (2-4) or high (≥4). The Cronbach’s α reported in previous studies was 0.890,20 while the Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.840.

Habit strength

Habit strength was evaluated using the Self-Report Habit Index (SRHI) developed by Verplanken and Orbell,21 an internationally recognised instrument.22 The SRHI uses a standardised item stem (eg, ‘[Behaviour] is something I do automatically’) and can be adapted to specific behaviours. In this study, five self-management behaviours—healthy diet, physical activity, foot care, self-monitoring and medication adherence—were assessed, yielding 20 items after contextual adaptation. Each item was scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1=completely non-compliant to 5=completely compliant). The total score ranged from 20 to 100, with higher scores reflecting stronger habitual performance. The Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.938, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Metabolic indicators

Metabolic data, including HbA1c, fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), were extracted from participants’ electronic medical records at four hospitals. Body weight and height were measured by trained researchers using a standardised, calibrated electronic scale. BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m²). Blood pressure was measured in a seated position after at least 5 min of rest, using a calibrated electronic sphygmomanometer.

Demographic and sociological data

These data were collected using a self-designed general information questionnaire developed based on prior literature. Variables included age, gender, educational level, marital status, personal monthly income, employment status, living arrangement, diabetes duration, medication duration and diabetes-related complications.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by five researchers who received standardised training prior to the study to ensure consistency and accuracy. Between March and September 2024, data were collected face-to-face from patients attending the outpatient and inpatient endocrinology departments of the four participating hospitals. Participants completed a self-designed electronic questionnaire on their mobile phones via the Nanhai Diabetes Management Application, a digital platform developed by the research team.

Researchers provided on-site assistance to participants as needed and performed real-time checks to ensure the completeness and accuracy of the responses, immediately addressing any missing or unclear items. Furthermore, two independent researchers carried out double-entry verification and cross-checking during data processing to minimise data entry errors and enhance data reliability. As a result, no missing data were identified in the final analytical dataset.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test, supplemented by visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean±SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables were reported as median (IQR). Categorical variables were summarised as frequency and percentage. Univariate analyses were performed to identify factors associated with latent profile membership. The χ² test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables, as appropriate. Variables with p<0.05 in the univariate analyses were subsequently entered into the multinomial logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with latent profile membership. Differences in metabolic indicators among profiles were analysed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Levene’s test was applied to assess variance homogeneity; when equal variances were assumed, LSD post hoc tests were used, otherwise Welch’s ANOVA and Games-Howell tests were performed. A two-tailed p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

LPA was performed using Mplus V.8.3 to identify subgroups of patients with similar self-management behaviour patterns. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), with lower values indicating better fit. Entropy values (>0.80) indicated good classification accuracy. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were used to compare adjacent models, with p<0.05 suggesting that the k-class model fit significantly better than the (k–1)-class model.9 23

Results

Participant characteristics and DSM behaviours

A total of 304 patients with T2DM were surveyed, all of whom provided valid responses, yielding an effective response rate of 100%. The participants had a mean age of 49.35±9.95 years, and the sample comprised 200 males (65.8%) and 104 females (34.2%). All participants were of Asian race, with 291 (95.7%) identifying as Han Chinese and the remaining 13 (4.3%) belonging to other ethnic minority groups. The mean total score for DSM behaviours was 34.40±13.46, with an average score of 3.13±1.22 per domain. Other details on participant characteristics can refer to table 1.

LPA of DSM behaviours

The six subscale scores of the SDSCA were used as observed variables in LPA. Five models (one- to five-class solutions) were fitted, and their fit indices are shown in table 2. With increasing class number, AIC, BIC and aBIC values gradually decreased. However, the LMR and BLRT tests were not significant for the five-class model (p=0.378 and p=0.368), whereas the three-class model showed significant LMR and BLRT results (p<0.001) and the highest entropy value (0.987), indicating excellent classification accuracy. The class probabilities in the three-class model were well balanced, supporting its interpretability. Therefore, the three-class solution was selected as the optimal model. The average posterior probabilities for all three classes exceeded 0.90 online supplemental table 1, confirming high classification reliability. In addition, the three-class model exhibited a clear elbow pattern in the AIC, BIC and aBIC plots (figure 1), together with optimal theoretical interpretability, further supporting its selection as the most parsimonious and conceptually meaningful solution.

Naming and characterisation of latent profiles

As shown in figure 2, the LPA classified patients with T2DM into three distinct DSM behaviour profiles. The values on the y-axis represent the mean domain scores derived from SDSCA items, with higher scores indicating more frequent performance of the corresponding self-management behaviour. Distinct patterns were observed across the six behavioural domains measured by the SDSCA. Category 1 (C1) had low scores across all domains (mean=16.42±11.54) and was labelled ‘low-engagement behaviours’ (n=43, 14.0%). Category 2 (C2) showed moderate scores in all domains (mean=32.86±11.06) and was defined as ‘moderate-engagement behaviours’ (n=35, 12.0%). Category 3 (C3) achieved relatively high scores in most domains except foot care (mean=38.06±11.18) and was termed ‘high-engagement behaviours’ (n=226, 74.0%).

Univariate analysis of latent profiles of DSM behaviours

Univariate analysis revealed significant differences among the three latent DSM behaviour profiles in patients with T2DM in terms of age (p=0.045), personal monthly income (p=0.003), diabetes-related complications (p<0.001), medication duration (p<0.001), diabetes duration (p=0.002), self-efficacy (p=0.002) and habit strength (p=0.038) (online supplemental table 2).

Multinomial logistic regression analysis of factors associated with DSM behaviour profile membership

A multinomial logistic regression was conducted with the C1 profile as the reference group. The model included variables that were significant in the univariate analyses (online supplemental table 2), and variable assignments are presented in online supplemental table 3. As shown in table 3, self-efficacy, personal monthly income, medication duration and diabetes-related complications were significantly associated with latent profile membership. Specifically, patients with a self-efficacy score of ≤24 had significantly lower odds of belonging to C2 than those with a score >24 (OR=0.257, 95% CI 0.084 to 0.790, p=0.018). Compared with patients with a personal monthly income of ¥>5000, those with a monthly income of ¥2000–¥5000 had higher odds of belonging to C3 (OR=3.165, 95% CI 1.233 to 8.121, p=0.017). Patients with a medication duration of ≤3 years had lower odds of belonging to C3 than those with a duration of >3 years (OR=0.197, 95% CI 0.044 to 0.883, p=0.034). Likewise, patients without diabetes-related complications had lower odds of belonging to C3 than those with complications (OR=0.284, 95% CI 0.101 to 0.794, p=0.016).

Discussion

This multicentre study identified three distinct DSM behaviour profiles among patients with T2DM, namely low-, moderate- and high-engagement profiles. Profile membership was associated with self-efficacy, personal monthly income, medication duration and diabetes-related complications. Furthermore, the high-engagement profile was associated with more favourable HbA1c, FBG, LDL-C and TC levels, whereas the moderate-engagement profile was associated with higher HDL-C levels. These findings suggest that different DSM behaviour profiles may be associated with distinct metabolic patterns rather than uniformly favourable psychosocial or clinical outcomes. This study not only confirmed substantial heterogeneity in DSM behaviours among patients with T2DM, but also revealed their differential associations with metabolic indicators. The findings provide empirical evidence to support more individualised and profile-oriented approaches to diabetes management.

Patients in the low-engagement behaviours profile exhibited the weakest DSM performance and relatively unfavourable cardiometabolic characteristics, particularly the highest SBP and LDL-C levels. However, HbA1c and FBG levels were highest in the moderate-engagement profile rather than in the low-engagement profile. Therefore, this profile should not be interpreted as having uniformly poorer metabolic outcomes. Instead, the coexistence of suboptimal self-management behaviours and multiple cardiovascular risk factors suggests that these individuals may benefit from more comprehensive cardiometabolic risk management. Lower self-efficacy, shorter medication duration, and absence of diabetes-related complications were key predictors, suggesting that insufficient disease awareness and weak confidence undermine sustained self-care. These findings highlight the importance of extending DSM education beyond glycaemic control to include blood pressure and lipid management, as cardiovascular risk factors may receive less attention in routine diabetes care. Consistent with prior evidence,24 structured nutrition and exercise education can improve HbA1c and blood pressure simultaneously,25 26 supporting comprehensive programmes that target diet and physical activity while integrating blood pressure and lipid control as core goals.

The moderate-engagement behaviours profile showed relatively balanced DSM behaviours, but paradoxically, the higher HbA1c, FBG and TC, along with worse HDL-C levels, suggested a threshold effect—partial or inconsistent behaviours may not achieve metabolic benefit. These individuals may engage sporadically in self-care without sufficient intensity or persistence. Evidence indicates that only regular physical activity sustained over ≥12 weeks meaningfully elevates HDL-C levels,26 27 underscoring the need to strengthen behavioural consistency, habit formation and reinforcement mechanisms within this subgroup.

By contrast, the high-engagement behaviours profile demonstrated the most favourable glycaemic and blood lipid levels, confirming the metabolic advantages of sustained DSM adherence.10 28 However, foot-care practices remained universally poor, echoing evidence that diabetic foot management is a persistent gap across populations.29 30 Notably, participants with lower income were more likely to fall within this profile, potentially reflecting compensatory self-care behaviours among those with limited medical access.31 Although this finding appears counterintuitive, it may reflect differences in health-related motivations across income groups. Further investigation is warranted. Additionally, multinomial logistic regression further showed that participants in this profile were more likely to have diabetes-related complications and longer medication duration. This finding suggests that accumulated disease experience and prolonged exposure to diabetes treatment may enhance awareness of disease management and encourage greater engagement in self-management behaviours. Therefore, the observed behavioural pattern may reflect not only profile-specific behavioural characteristics but also adaptive responses developed over the course of long-term disease management. These findings highlight the need for cost-effective, digitally supported DSM interventions that sustain engagement and equity. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, causal relationships cannot be established and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify how disease experience influences self-management trajectories over time.

Habit strength reflects the extent to which behaviours become automatic through repeated performance and is considered an important mechanism underlying the maintenance of health behaviours.32 33 Although habit strength was not significantly associated with profile membership in the present study, it remains a theoretically relevant factor for DSM.34 One possible explanation is the relatively young age and short disease duration of the study population, with approximately 68.1% of participants having a diabetes duration of ≤3 years. In the early stages of disease management, self-management behaviours may rely more heavily on cognitive processes, such as self-efficacy and conscious decision-making, rather than on well-established behavioural routines. As disease duration increases, repeated behavioural performance may facilitate habit formation, potentially strengthening the role of habit strength in differentiating self-management behaviour patterns. Future studies should examine this possibility in populations with longer disease duration and through longitudinal designs.

This multicentre cross-sectional study applied LPA to identify distinct patterns of DSM among patients with T2DM and their key predictors. By moving beyond single-dimensional analyses, it revealed marked behavioural heterogeneity across DSM domains and provided a data-driven foundation for stratified and precision interventions. Active and sustained DSM behaviours were closely associated with better glycaemic, lipid and blood pressure control.

Study limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not capture behavioural changes or long-term outcomes. Furthermore, the use of self-reported behavioural data may introduce recall or social desirability bias, and unmeasured confounders such as medication type or comorbidity burden could have influenced the observed associations. Future studies should incorporate additional objective behavioural measures and psychosocial factors to further validate and extend the findings across different DSM behaviour profiles. In addition, the study population was relatively young and predominantly consisted of patients with a diabetes duration of ≤3 years. Therefore, the findings may not be fully generalisable to older adults or individuals with long-standing diabetes. Future studies should recruit more diverse populations and conduct stratified analyses across different age groups and disease stages. Although convenience sampling facilitated recruitment across multiple sites, all participants were recruited from tertiary hospitals, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to community and primary care populations. Future studies should include patients from different healthcare settings to improve representativeness.

Conclusions

This study identified three distinct profiles of DSM behaviours among patients with T2DM, highlighting substantial behavioural heterogeneity within this population. Self-efficacy, personal monthly income, medication duration and diabetes-related complications were significant predictors of profile membership. Distinct DSM behaviour profiles were associated with different patterns of metabolic outcomes, suggesting that behavioural heterogeneity may have important implications for diabetes management. These findings indicate that a one-size-fits-all approach may be insufficient for supporting self-management among patients with T2DM. Future interventions should adopt profile-specific and precision-oriented strategies that address the unique behavioural characteristics and support needs of different patient subgroups, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of diabetes care.
