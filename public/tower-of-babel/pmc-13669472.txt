Targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-induced angiogenesis with Rosa hybrida leaf extract via VEGF receptor-2 signaling and matrix metalloproteinase-2 inhibition
Authors: Byungdoo Hwang, Soyeon Kim, Jun-Hui Song, Hoon Kim, Sanghyun Lee, Sung-Kwon Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESAngiogenesis is essential for tumor growth, tissue repair, and cell development. Rosa hybrida is a widely cultivated ornamental plant, and research on its petals has been conducted. However, despite the presence of flavonoids and terpenoids in the leaves, the biological activity of rose leaves remains unclear. This study investigated the anti-angiogenic potential of R. hybrida leaf extract (RLE) using a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-stimulated angiogenic model.MATERIALS/METHODSRLE was prepared, chemically profiled by high-performance liquid chromatography, and used in both in vitro and ex vivo assays. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were treated with RLE in the presence of VEGF. Assays for endothelial cell proliferation, tube formation, migration, invasion, and angiogenic signaling protein (VEGFR-2, protein kinase B [AKT], extracellular signal-regulated kinase [ERK], and endothelial nitric oxide synthase [eNOS]) phosphorylation were conducted. Molecular docking was performed to predict interactions between major RLE compounds and VEGFR-2. Matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) level was also evaluated by immunoblot and gelatin zymography. The mouse aortic ring assay was used to assess microvessel sprouting.RESULTSRLE inhibited VEGF-induced proliferation, tube formation, migration, and invasion in HUVECs. RLE treatment also reduced the phosphorylation of VEGFR-2 and downstream signaling molecules, including AKT, ERK, and eNOS. Molecular docking supported the potential interaction between major RLE compounds and VEGFR-2. Additionally, RLE inhibited MMP-2-mediated extracellular matrix remodeling and attenuated VEGF-stimulated microvessel sprouting in the aortic ring assay.CONCLUSIONRLE exerts potent anti-angiogenic effects by modulating the VEGF/VEGFR-2 signaling cascade and inhibiting MMP-2 activity. As a previously underutilized botanical resource, RLE may serve as a promising complementary therapeutic agent for diseases related to angiogenesis.

INTRODUCTION

Solid tumors tend to grow near blood vessels to secure essential oxygen and nutrients [1]. To sustain this supply, they either co-opt existing vasculature or initiate angiogenesis through complex molecular and cellular mechanisms [2]. Typically, vascular homeostasis is tightly maintained by a balance between pro- and anti-angiogenic factors. Disrupting this homeostatic balance activates the endothelium, promoting the formation of new vessels through endothelial cell proliferation [3]. Angiogenesis, a key driver of tumor growth and metastasis, is initiated by various pro-angiogenic stimuli, such as growth factors and cytokines, which are continuously secreted or upregulated by tumor cells. These factors activate endothelial cell sprouting [4], which enables tumors to escape dormancy, undergo rapid proliferation, and develop new blood vessels [5].

Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) regulates vascular permeability and is frequently secreted by tumors [6]. VEGF also binds to its receptor (VEGFR-2) on endothelial cells, initiating intracellular signaling cascades that stimulate cell growth, proliferation, and migration [6]. Through VEGFR-2 activation, VEGF orchestrates various angiogenic responses by engaging multiple downstream effectors [7]. The protein kinase B (AKT) and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) pathways play essential roles in promoting endothelial cell growth and survival [89]. In addition to promoting cellular proliferation, dysregulated VEGF signaling in the tumor microenvironment increases vascular endothelial activity by persistently stimulating endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), resulting in excessive nitric oxide (NO) production [10]. Although NO is essential for vasodilation and the maintenance of vascular homeostasis, its overproduction induces vascular leakage and destabilization, thereby facilitating tumor progression [11]. Furthermore, VEGF facilitates extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling by inducing matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) such as MMP-2, thereby supporting angiogenic sprouting and tumor cell invasion into adjacent tissues [12].

The floriculture industry generates extensive biomass waste, particularly from non-floral parts such as leaves and stems, and strategies for their effective utilization remain limited [13]. Among ornamental plants, roses are particularly significant owing to their extensive cultivation and waste production [14]. Although the pharmacological potential of rose, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial effects, has been extensively investigated [1516], the biological properties of Rosa hybrida leaves remain largely underexplored. R. hybrida leaves contain various bioactive compounds, including flavonoids and terpenoids [17]; however, their physiological availability remains an underutilized and understudied resource. Moreover, their potential in modulating VEGF-induced angiogenesis remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the anti-angiogenic potential of R. hybrida leaf extract (RLE) and elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms using a VEGF-stimulated angiogenesis model, including human endothelial cells and an ex vivo aortic ring assay.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of RLE

R. hybrida was obtained from Jincheon Agricultural Technology Center (Jincheon, Korea). The plant was separated into flowers, stems, roots, and leaves. A voucher specimen of the R. hybrida leaves (No. LEE 2017-04) has been deposited at the Department of Plant Science and Technology, Chung-Ang University. Extraction was carried out according to a previously reported method [17]. In brief, R. hybrida leaves were extracted with ethanol under reflux at 80°C for 3 h, and this procedure was repeated 7 times. The extract was dissolved in distilled water or culture medium to yield a working solution of 10 mg/mL.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis

RLE was dissolved in methanol, filtered (0.45 μm polytetrafluoroethylene), and analyzed using a personal digital assistant-equipped HPLC system with a YMC J’sphere ODS-H80 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 4 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.5% acetic acid in water (A) and methanol (B) under a programmed gradient. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, and detection was performed at 254 nm. Peaks were identified by comparison of retention times and ultraviolet spectra with authentic standards, and quantification was carried out using an external standard method.

Cell culture and treatment

Primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, c2517a) were obtained from Lonza (Basel, Switzerland) and cultivated in endothelial basal medium supplemented with an EGM-2 Bulletkit (Lonza). Cells were grown at 37°C with 5% CO2 and routinely passaged every 3–5 days under humidified conditions. All HUVEC experiments were conducted between passages 2 and 8. For VEGF stimulation, cells were serum-starved for 3 h, followed by treatment with 20 ng/mL recombinant human VEGF (293-VE; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) with or without the indicated RLE concentrations.

Cell viability assay

To measure cell viability, HUVECs were plated in 96-well plates and exposed to VEGF, with or without different RLE concentrations. After 24 h, cells were gently rinsed and cultured in fresh medium containing 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5- diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 4 h at 37°C. The medium was aspirated after incubation, 200 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide was added, and the absorbance at 540 nm was measured using a Thermo Scientific Multiskan GO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was coated onto 24-well plates and left to solidify at 37°C for 30 min. HUVECs were prepared in medium supplemented with VEGF, with or without RLE, and seeded onto Matrigel-coated plates. After a 24 h incubation period, capillary-like structures were visualized using an inverted microscope (Optika Microscopes, Bergamo, Italy). Tube formation was quantified using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin in ImageJ (https://imagej.net/ij/) by assessing the total tube length.

Wound-healing migration assay

Cells were cultured to approximately 90% confluence in 6-well plates after seeding. Linear wounds were generated by gently scratching with a sterile 200 μL pipette tip, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline to eliminate dislodged cells. To suppress endothelial cell proliferation, confluent monolayers were exposed to mitomycin C (10 μg/mL) for 1 h. The cells were subsequently exposed to VEGF with or without RLE. Images of the wounded area were captured at 0 and 24 h after scratching. Wound closure was quantified by measuring the decrease in wound width.

Invasion assay

The invasive potential of HUVECs was evaluated using a Transwell insert (SPL Life Sciences Co., Pocheon, Korea) coated with 0.1% gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were suspended in serum-free medium with various RLE concentrations and seeded into the upper chambers. VEGF-supplemented medium was placed in the lower wells. After 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were eliminated. Migrated cells on the underside of the membrane were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde. After 15 min, the membranes were washed and stained with a 0.5% crystal violet solution for 30 min. Excess dye was removed, rinsed with distilled water, and imaged using an inverted microscope (Optika Microscopes).

Immunoblot analysis

To extract total protein, HUVECs were lysed in a buffer containing 1% Triton X-100, 20 mM hydroxyethyl piperazine ethane sulfonic acid (pH 7.5), 2 mM ethylene glycol-bis(β-aminoethyl ether)-N, N, N', N'-tetraacetic acid (pH 8.0), 1 mM dithiothreitol, 50 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 400 μM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10% glycerol, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (one tablet per 50 mL; cOmplete, Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Protein levels were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each protein sample (20 μg) was denatured by heating with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer at 100°C for 10 min. samples were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Hybond; GE Healthcare Biosciences, Buckinghamshire, UK). After blocking for 1 h in 5% BSA, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with appropriate primary antibodies (Table 1). Membranes were washed 3 times with TBS-T and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using the Western Lightning Plus-ECL detection kit (#NEL105001EA; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Gelatin zymography

To assess MMP-2 activity, the conditioned medium was collected and subjected to 8% SDS-PAGE containing 0.5 mg/mL gelatin. Gels were washed twice with 2.5% Triton X-100 to remove SDS and incubated at 37°C overnight in activation buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM CaCl2, 0.1% sodium azide). After staining with 0.2% Coomassie Brilliant Blue, gels were destained and visualized. Proteolytic activity appeared as clear bands against a blue background.

Molecular docking analysis

Major compounds identified from RLE, including quercitrin (CID: 5280459) and afzelin (CID: 5316673), were selected (Fig. 1A). The 3-dimensional (3D) structures of these compounds were retrieved as Structure Data Files format from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The crystal structure of human VEGFR-2 (PDB ID: 4AG8) was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. All receptor and ligand structures were prepared with AutoDock Tools (v1.5.7), followed by docking analysis using AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.1) with default parameters. Among the generated docking poses, the complex with the lowest binding energy (i.e., strongest predicted interaction) was selected for further visualization. The best binding conformations were visualized in 3D using the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (version 3.1; Schrödinger, Mannheim, Germany), and 2D interaction diagrams were generated using Discovery Studio Visualizer (version 21.1; Dassault Systèmes BIOVIA, San Diego, CA, USA).

Major peaks in RLE were identified by comparing retention times and ultraviolet spectra with those of the corresponding reference standards.

Aortic ring assay

This research was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chung-Ang University (201700054). Thoracic aortas were isolated from the spines of male C3H/He mice, the surrounding fat and connective tissue were cleaned, and the aortas were sliced into rings approximately 1.0 mm thick. The rings were placed in Matrigel in 24-well plates and overlaid with medium containing VEGF with or without RLE. Microvessel sprouting was observed and photographed after 8–10 days of incubation using an inverted microscope (Optika Microscopes). The extent of sprouting was quantified by counting the number and length of microvessels.

Statistical analysis

Values represent means ± SDs obtained from a minimum of 3 independent biological replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 30; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Chemical characterization of RLE by HPLC

Our previous study structurally elucidated 4 major constituents of R. hybrida leaves—uncaric acid, oleanolic acid, quercitrin, and afzelin, using 1H- and 13C-NMR spectroscopy supported by mass spectrometric analysis [17]. Based on these findings, the same compounds were selected as target analytes for HPLC profiling in the present study Comparison of the RLE chromatogram with reference standards under identical conditions confirmed distinct peaks for quercitrin and afzelin. In contrast, uncaric acid and oleanolic acid were detected only at trace levels with weak signals (Fig. 1). Consequently, quercitrin and afzelin were identified as the representative marker compounds for RLE in this study.

RLE suppressed VEGF-mediated proliferation and tube formation.

Endothelial cell proliferation is a fundamental step in angiogenesis, as it contributes to the expansion of the vascular network in response to pro-angiogenic signals such as VEGF [18]. An MTT assay was performed to evaluate the inhibitory effect of RLE on VEGF-stimulated HUVEC proliferation. RLE treatment alone (2.5 to 40 μg/mL) had no significant effect on cell viability, indicating no cytotoxicity under basal conditions (Fig. 2A). In contrast, VEGF-induced proliferation was inhibited in RLE-treated HUVECs from 2.5 μg/mL (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Non-cytotoxic concentrations (2.5–10 μg/mL) were therefore selected for subsequent experiments. A tube formation assay was conducted using Matrigel to examine whether RLE affects the tube-forming ability of HUVECs. RLE markedly reduced VEGF-stimulated tube formation (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that RLE selectively impairs VEGF-triggered angiogenic activity while sparing basal endothelial cell viability.

RLE attenuates VEGF-induced HUVEC migration and invasion

Migration and invasion of vascular endothelial cells are essential processes for sprouting angiogenesis, enabling them to move through the ECM toward angiogenic stimuli [19]. Wound-healing and Transwell invasion assays were performed to investigate whether RLE modulates metastatic behaviors. RLE treatment significantly suppressed the migration and invasion of VEGF-stimulated HUVECs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 1). These results indicate that RLE can interfere with cellular behaviors critical for angiogenic sprouting.

RLE downregulates VEGF-mediated activation of angiogenic signaling pathways

VEGF-mediated activation of key angiogenic signaling molecules in HUVECs was investigated to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of RLE-mediated angiogenesis inhibition. VEGFR2, AKT, ERK, and eNOS phosphorylation in response to VEGF stimulation was attenuated by RLE (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). The inhibitory effect was more pronounced for AKT compared with ERK. Molecular docking analysis was performed using quercitrin and afzelin to further support the results at the molecular interaction level (Fig. 4B and C). All 4 compounds exhibited strong predicted binding affinities (< −8.6 kcal/mol) to VEGFR-2, suggesting their potential to directly interact with the receptor (Fig. 4C). MMP-2 expression and activity were also investigated because of its essential role in VEGF-driven cell migration and invasion [20]. RLE markedly suppressed VEGF-induced MMP-2 upregulation in HUVECs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that RLE disrupts multiple VEGF-triggered pro-angiogenic signaling pathways, including the regulation of endothelial cell proliferation, tube formation, migration, and invasion.

RLE treatment interferes with VEGF-stimulated neovessel sprouting in an ex vivo aortic ring model

The aortic ring assay was used to assess whether RLE suppresses VEGF-induced neovessel formation under physiologically relevant conditions. VEGF-stimulated microvessel sprouting from mouse aortic rings was significantly reduced in the presence of RLE (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5). This result reinforces the anti-angiogenic potential of RLE observed in vitro, highlighting its efficacy in a more physiologically relevant ex vivo model.

DISCUSSION

Angiogenesis, a biological process involving the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing vasculature, is essential for wound healing, organogenesis, and embryonic development [2122]. However, in pathological conditions like cancer, angiogenesis becomes dysregulated and contributes to disease progression by supporting an expanding tumor microenvironment [23]. VEGF is a principal regulator of angiogenesis, acting as a major endothelial cell-specific growth factor [24]. Notably, VEGF is frequently upregulated in various tumors and is widely recognized as a major contributor to cancer-associated neovascularization [25]. Therefore, in this study, a VEGF-stimulated HUVEC model was used to evaluate the anti-angiogenic effect of RLE.

The tumor microenvironment critically influences tumor growth, metastatic potential, and angiogenic activity [2627]. Efficient angiogenesis requires coordinated endothelial cell proliferation and motility to enable microvessel outgrowth [28]. VEGF triggers angiogenesis by activating the AKT, ERK, and eNOS signaling pathways, thereby facilitating endothelial cell proliferation, tube formation, migration, and invasion [8929]. In this study, RLE significantly reduced VEGF-induced proliferation and tube formation in HUVECs. It also attenuated VEGF-stimulated phosphorylation of VEGFR-2, the principal receptor mediating VEGF-driven angiogenic signaling. Molecular docking analysis provided supportive in silico evidence suggesting that several RLE-derived compounds may interact with VEGFR-2. However, these computational predictions do not constitute direct evidence of target engagement, and the predicted compounds may interact with either overlapping or distinct binding regions of the receptor. Consistently, the phosphorylation of AKT, ERK, and eNOS was also markedly reduced. Notably, the reduction in AKT was more pronounced than that of ERK, indicating a relatively stronger modulation of the AKT pathway. These results suggest that RLE disrupts VEGF-driven angiogenic responses by modulating the VEGF/VEGFR-2 signaling cascade at multiple levels. The overall anti-angiogenic activity of the extract may therefore reflect the combined actions of multiple constituents.

Activation of VEGFR-2 induces the expression and secretion of MMPs via downstream PI3K/AKT and ERK pathways, which upregulates matrix-remodeling enzymes required for early angiogenesis [293031]. MMP-2 plays a pivotal role in ECM degradation, enabling endothelial cell migration and invasion and facilitating the formation of new blood vessels [20]. Thus, suppression of VEGFR-2 signaling is expected to attenuate MMP-2 activity and impair ECM remodeling required for angiogenic progression. In this study, RLE treatment reduced the VEGF-induced migratory and invasive activity in HUVECs. Consistently, RLE inhibited MMP-2 expression and activity, which was associated with reduced HUVEC migration and invasion, suggesting that its anti-angiogenic effect involves the suppression of ECM remodeling. In addition, RLE significantly inhibited microvessel sprouting in an ex vivo aortic ring model, further confirming its anti-angiogenic potential. Although slightly higher concentrations were required in this model, this likely reflects the limited diffusion of the compound within the intact vascular tissue embedded in Matrigel, a well-known characteristic of aortic ring-based angiogenesis assays [32].

Inhibiting a single angiogenic factor can result in the compensatory upregulation of other growth factors, cytokines, and alternative pathways [33]. Although anti-angiogenic therapies targeting the VEGF/VEGFR axis are effective, resistance to anti-VEGF treatment frequently occurs, suggesting that angiogenesis can also be driven by VEGF-independent mechanisms and the induction of additional pro-angiogenic factors [34]. In particular, VEGF-independent angiogenesis involves interleukin (IL)-20 (20), IL-28A, fibroblast growth factors, platelet-derived growth factors, and stromal cell-derived factor-1, which contribute to angiogenesis through alternative signaling pathways [7353637]. Thus, targeting multiple angiogenic pathways may represent a promising strategy to overcome resistance to anti-VEGF therapies. These findings provide the foundation for ongoing efforts to further investigate such multi-targeted approaches. However, future studies are required to investigate the efficacy of RLE against VEGF-independent angiogenic responses.

This study had several limitations. Notably, the anti-angiogenic effect of RLE was not validated in vivo. Moreover, given that endothelial–tumor cell interactions are a critical component of the tumor microenvironment, further research is warranted to determine whether RLE modulates cancer-associated angiogenesis through direct effects on cellular crosstalk.

These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the anti-angiogenic activity of RLE. RLE inhibited VEGF-induced angiogenic responses, including endothelial cell proliferation, tube formation, migration, and invasion, by modulating VEGFR-2/ERK/AKT/eNOS signaling and MMP-2 expression. Furthermore, RLE strongly reduced VEGF-stimulated neovessel outgrowth in an ex vivo aortic ring model. Given its multi-pathway inhibition (Fig. 6), RLE represents a promising strategy for VEGF-targeted therapies and for broadening the therapeutic scope of angiogenesis-related diseases.
