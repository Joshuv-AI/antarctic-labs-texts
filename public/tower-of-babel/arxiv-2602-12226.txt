A resistance invariant of special alternating links
Michał Jabłonowski

Abstract
. We introduce a new numerical invariant for special, reduced, alternating diagrams of oriented knots and links, defined in terms of the Laplacian matrix of the associated Tait graph. For a special alternating diagram, the Laplacian encodes both the combinatorics of the checkerboard graph and the crossing signs. While its spectrum depends on the chosen diagram, we show that a specific quadratic trace expression involving the Laplacian and its Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse is invariant under flype moves. The invariant admits an interpretation in terms of total effective resistance of the associated weighted graph viewed as an electrical network. Explicit computations for pairs of flype-related diagrams demonstrate that, although the Laplacian characteristic polynomials differ, the invariant FP coincides. Values for several prime alternating knots are provided.

1.
Introduction

Alternating link diagrams occupy a central position in knot theory because their combinatorics is unusually rigid: diagrammatic data often controls classical invariants, and local moves can be globally constrained. In particular, the flyping theorem implies that reduced alternating diagrams of the same oriented link are related by a sequence of flypes, a fact that makes ”flype invariance” the natural target when defining new diagrammatic invariants.

Our construction lives in the special alternating setting. Briefly, a special diagram is one in which the Seifert circles from Seifert’s algorithm bound disks that are not nested; this hypothesis is standard when one wants a clean interface between an oriented diagram and a checkerboard spanning surface.

Given a checkerboard shading of a connected link diagram, one obtains associated checkerboard graphs (often called Tait graphs) by placing vertices in regions and edges at crossings. These graphs and their planar duality encode substantial information, and they are a familiar bridge between knot theory and graph polynomials.

Our invariant
FP
is not ”just spectral”. A flype changes the local placement of crossings inside a tangle; on the graph side, it alters Tait graphs by a local transformation that need not preserve the Laplacian up to conjugation. Consequently, the Laplacian characteristic polynomial (and hence the multiset of eigenvalues) can genuinely vary between flype-related diagrams.

2.
Definitions

A
diagram

D
of a (non-trivial) knot or link
KL
is a generic projection of the knot or link in the plane, a regular
4
-valent graph, with extra information at each vertex indicating which arc of the link passes over the other. Moreover, it is
alternating
when traveling around each link component on a diagram, we pass crossing in alternate type fashion (i.e., if one passes an over/under-crossing, the next crossing will be an under/over-crossing, see
[
10
]
for a recent survey).

A knot or link diagram is a
reduced diagram
if it is a diagram without
nugatory crossings
, i.e., a crossing whose neighborhood looks like the one in Figure
1
, that can be removed without changing the knot type, and it cannot be separated by any circle in the plane of projection (i.e. the diagram as projection has one connected component).

A nugatory crossing.

By a
region
of a reduced diagram
D
of a link
KL
we mean a face of the underlying projection of the link. The diagram can be checkerboard-shaded so that every edge separates a shaded region (black region) from an unshaded one (white region).

A standard operation of smoothing crossings of an oriented
D
, results in a collection of Seifert disks, possibly nested, in the plane that can be rejoined by half-twisted bands of
D
to produce a Seifert surface for a link
KL
. The diagram
D
is called
special
if the Seifert disks are not nested, see
[
14
]
. Any diagram
D
can be deformed to a special diagram
[
2
]
. From now on, we always assume this condition on a knot or link diagram and that the shaded regions correspond to the Seifert surface.

Figure 2.
The convention for positive and negative crossings.

We consider a graph
\Gamma(D)
associated with an oriented diagram
D
as the Tait graph for unshaded checkerboard regions of
D
. The graph
\Gamma(D)
has vertices corresponding to the unshaded regions of
D
, and an oriented edge between two vertices for every crossing at which the corresponding regions meet.

On each edge
e
of the graph
\Gamma(D)
, we assign its weight
wg(e)
as
-1
when the corresponding crossing is a positive, and
+1
if it is a negative crossing. See Figure
2

The Laplacian matrix
L(\Gamma)
of a directed graph
\Gamma
is the square matrix indexed by the vertex set of
\Gamma
with diagonal entries equal to the weighted out-degree of the vertex
v_{i}
, i.e., the sum of weights of edges with initial vertex
v_{i}
, and non-diagonal entries
L(\Gamma)_{i;j}
equal to
-1
times the sum of the weights of edges from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
.

The Alexander polynomial of a knot or link
KL
can be calculated from the Laplacian matrix (of size
n\times n
) corresponding to the special diagram of
KL
as follows. Deleting one row and column, corresponding to the same vertex, yields a matrix that serves as a Seifert matrix
S
for the link
KL

[
14
]
. Then the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{KL}(t)=det(S-tS^{T})
. If the link
KL
is alternating, then we know that the maximal degree of
\Delta_{KL}(t)
equals the canonical genus of
KL
, we always have
rank(L)=n-1
for the case of special alternating diagrams, see
[
13
]
.

Because two reduced alternating diagrams for a link
KL
have the same number of crossings,
n
(the number of unshaded regions) in a special alternating diagram is also an invariant of
KL
. We can conclude that
rank(L)=trace(LL^{+})
is a known invariant for special alternating knots and links.

Let us define the new ’resistance’ invariant.

Definition 2.1
.

For a special, reduced, alternating diagram
D
, define

FP(D)=trace(L^{T}L^{+}),

where
L=(L(\Gamma(D)))
and
L^{+}
is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of
L
.

We have the main theorem of this paper, as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

For special, reduced, alternating diagrams
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
of the same oriented knot or link
KL
, we have
FP(D_{1})=FP(D_{2})
.

Remark 2.3
.

The name ’resistance’ for the invariant
FP
comes from the fact that
FP(D)=\frac{\omega}{2}\sum_{e}r(e)
, where
\omega
is the weight of any edge in the graph
\Gamma(D)
, and
r(e)
is the resistance of the edge of the graph
\Gamma(D)
interpreted as a electric circuit, as will be explained in the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

If we take the Laplacian matrix
L
without transpose in the formula for
FP
then our invariant trivializes, as

trace(LL^{+})=rank(L)
.

4.
Proof

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

We use the theorem known as Tait’s Flype Conjecture, which states that if
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
are reduced alternating diagrams, then
D_{2}
can be obtained from
D_{1}
by a sequence of flype-moves (a local move on a knot diagram that involves flipping a part of the diagram, see Figure
5
) if and only if
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
represent the same link. This conjecture has been proven true by Menasco and Thistlethwaite
[
11
,
12
]
.

Figure 5.
A flype-move on the tangle
R
moves the crossing
c
from one side of
R
to the other, while rotating
R
by
180^{\circ}
around the horizontal axis.

Let us fix an alternating, reduced, special diagram
D
, its oriented Tait graph
\Gamma
, and a Laplacian matrix
L
from this graph.

Following
[
7
,
8
]
consider the general situation in Figure
6
. Let us name the reduced alternating diagram before the flype as
D
and after the flype as
D_{2}
.

Figure 6.
The general situation on graphs in flype-moves. Case I and II.

The diagrams can be checkerboard-colored, resulting in two types of regions (black and white). Notice that the black and white corresponding regions before and after the flype-move preserve their color.

We will show that the total resistance of edges in the graph treated as an electrical circuit does not change by applying a flype move.
Then we will show that
FP(D)=\frac{\omega}{2}\sum_{e}r(e)
, where
\omega
is the weight of any edge in the graph. For a special alternating diagram (one in which all crossings have the same sign), the induced edge weights on the directed Tait graph are all equal to a common constant
\omega\in\{-1,+1\}
, see
[
15
]
.

In
[
6
]
it is shown that the resistance
r(e)
does not change after knot isotopy (i.e., after performing the Reidemeister moves to the diagram) provided that the two regions that are the ends of the edge
e
do not change after the isotopy.

The whole sub-graph
\Gamma_{R}
containing the
R
can be replaced by a single equivalent resistance connected in parallel in Case I and in series in Case II from the Figure
5
.
That means the edges in the diagram
D
that do not intersect the
R
tangle region, or are fully inside the region in Figure
5
do not change their resistance. We observe that the flype acts as a dualizing transformation on the electrical network. Specifically, a flype on a special alternating diagram corresponds to a planar rotation or reflection of the subgraph
\Gamma_{R}
representing the tangle. In an electrical network, the total resistance of a planar network is preserved under such ”Whitney flips” or ”dual rotations” because the effective resistance between two points is a function of the network topology, and flype preserves the set of paths and cuts between the entry and exit points of the tangle. See also
[
5
]
.

Therefore, let us consider only the edges that intersect both the tangle region
R
and the crossing outside this region. Specifically such an edge have exactly one of its vertex in the set
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
, see Figure
5
and the other vertex inside the tangle
R
.

The vertices of your graph can be partitioned into
V_{ext}
(vertices outside the tangle and on its boundary) and
V_{int}
(vertices strictly inside the tangle
R
). The Laplacian
L
can be written in block form:

L=\begin{pmatrix}L_{ext}&L_{bridge}\\
L_{bridge}^{T}&L_{int}\end{pmatrix}

The effective resistances between any two boundary vertices are determined by the Schur complement (see
[
4
]
).

In a flype, the internal structure of the tangle
R
(represented by
L_{int}
) is rotated or flipped. For special alternating diagrams, this operation is a graph isomorphism for the internal sub-graph. The ”bridging” edges, those with one vertex in
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
and the other in
R
-simply get permuted or re-indexed during the flype.

Let us consider the edges that intersect the tangle region
R
. Specifically, let
V_{R}\subset V
be the set of vertices corresponding to regions inside the tangle, and
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
be the vertices corresponding to the regions adjacent to the flyping crossing
c
.
By applying a Schur complement to the Laplacian matrix, we can reduce the tangle
R
to an equivalent network consisting of edges between its boundary vertices. Since a flype on a special alternating diagram corresponds to a
180^{\circ}
rotation of the tangle, the equivalent resistance matrix of the tangle is preserved up to a permutation of the boundary indices. Because the flype essentially moves the crossing
c
from one side of this equivalent network to the other, the total resistance sum remains unchanged. This is a consequence of the Series-Parallel Law for electrical networks: in a special alternating diagram, the crossing
c
and the tangle
R
are arranged such that their equivalent total resistance is additive (in series) or reciprocal-additive (in parallel) relative to the rest of the link. Since the flype preserves the global connectivity and the weights
\omega
are constant, the sum
\sum r(e)
is invariant.

Let us define the resistance matrix
R=[r_{i,j}]
, where
r_{i,j}
is the resistance between vertices
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
in a graph
\Gamma
. The resistance of an edge is the resistance (distance) between its end vertices, see
[
9
]
. The Laplacian for a special alternating link is balanced directed graph (equal weighted in/out degree at each vertex), this follows from that all crossings have the same sign, and all regions of the same color have the same corner structure when traveling around the region boundary, see
[
10
]
. We have
r_{i,j}=l^{+}_{i,i}+l^{+}_{j,j}-2l^{+}_{i,j}
, see
[
1
]
, where
l^{+}_{i,j}
is a
{i,j}
-element of the matrix
L^{+}
being the pseudoinverse for the Laplacian
L(\Gamma)
.

We relate
\operatorname{tr}(L^{T}R)
to the sum of edge resistances.
Let
R=[r_{ij}]
be the resistance matrix, where

r_{ij}=(L^{+})_{ii}+(L^{+})_{jj}-2(L^{+})_{ij}.

Let
d=\operatorname{diag}(L^{+})
and
\mathbf{1}
be the all–ones vector. Then

R=d\mathbf{1}^{T}+\mathbf{1}d^{T}-2L^{+}.

Since the Laplacian satisfies
L\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
and
L^{T}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
,
cyclicity of the trace gives

\operatorname{tr}(L^{T}d\mathbf{1}^{T})=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{1}^{T}L^{T}d)=0,\qquad\operatorname{tr}(L^{T}\mathbf{1}d^{T})=\operatorname{tr}(d^{T}L^{T}\mathbf{1})=0.

Hence

\operatorname{tr}(L^{T}R)=-2\,\operatorname{tr}(L^{T}L^{+})=-2\,FP(D).

On the other hand, since
r_{ii}=0
,

\operatorname{tr}(L^{T}R)=\sum_{i,j}L_{ij}r_{ij}=\sum_{i\neq j}L_{ij}r_{ij}.

If all edge weights of
\Gamma(D)
are equal to a constant

\omega\in\{\pm 1\}
(which holds for special alternating diagrams),
and
m_{ij}
denotes the number of directed edges from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
,
then for
i\neq j
we have
L_{ij}=-\omega m_{ij}
. Therefore

\operatorname{tr}(L^{T}R)=-\omega\sum_{i\neq j}m_{ij}r_{ij}=-\omega\sum_{e}r(e),

where the sum is over all directed edges of
\Gamma(D)
.

Combining the two formulas yields

FP(D)=\frac{\omega}{2}\sum_{e}r(e).
