Morpho-phenological and pomological characteristics in differentiating and selecting superior quince (Cydonia oblonga mill.)
Authors: Soheila Khojand, Hamid Abdollahi, Ali Rezaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iranian plateau is the center of origin and diversity of quince (Cydonia oblonga Mill.) tree. This study aimed to evaluate morphological and phenological diversity among 52 quince genotypes and to identify the key characteristics contributing to genotype differentiation. Accordingly, morpho-phenological traits influencing genotype differentiation were evaluated across 52 quince genotypes from various Iranian provinces using 43 national descriptors. Analyses revealed complex variation, with flower- and fruit-related traits forming the main axes of differentiation. Phenological traits, such as leaf bud burst and flowering onset, were relatively stable (CV ≈ 18–19%), whereas growth habit and tree form showed high variability (CV > 40%). Leaf size traits exhibited moderate variation (CV ≈ 30–31%), while leaf morphology and fruit structural traits showed the highest fluctuations, with fruit neck thickness varying the most (CV ≈ 98.4%). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first two components explained 27.3% of the total variance among the studied genotypes. Strong correlations were observed among fruit and flower traits (e.g., FSi-FPS, r ≈ 0.99; FrWe-FrSi, r = 0.68), while negative correlations suggested trade-offs between tree architecture and fruit characteristics. NMDS and Envfit analyses identified fruit size and fruit neck as key explanatory traits (r² > 0.5). The studied genotypes exhibited considerable variation in fruit size, tree vigor, astringency, and fruit quality. Overall, fruit related traits showed the highest discriminative power, while phenological and leaf traits provided useful information on adaptability. These findings offer valuable insights for germplasm conservation, management, and breeding of superior quince cultivars.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08836-9.

Introduction

Quince (Cydonia oblonga Mill.) is a fruit tree with its center of origin located in the regions between the Caspian Sea and the Black Sea, extending to the Talysh region and parts of Gilan and Ardabil provinces in Iran. Over the 4,000 years since domestication, multiple centers of diversity have emerged around its origin, including the Iranian Plateau [1]. Iran harbors a rich and extensive quince germplasm, making it one of the major global centers of quince production. Due to its nutritional, medicinal, and industrial applications, as well as increasing consumer demand, the cultivated area and commercial varieties of quince in Iran have expanded considerably in recent years, contributing to rural development and local economies [2]. Indigenous Iranian germplasm exhibits substantial genetic diversity and wide phenotypic variation in fruit color, shape, and size. Quince, a member of the Rosaceae family, is valued both for direct consumption and as a rootstock for other fruit species. In Iran, quince ranks third among pome fruits after apple and pear, reflecting its economic and agricultural significance [1]. Key traits of quince include relative tolerance to diverse climatic conditions, longevity, and adaptability to a variety of soils and environments. Its fruits are commercially desirable due to high phenolic content, distinctive aroma and flavor, and favorable postharvest storage potential, particularly under cold storage. Fruit quality traits such as weight, size, firmness, and biochemical composition (sugars, organic acids, antioxidants), are crucial for selection in breeding programs [3, 4]. Extensive research has documented phenotypic diversity in Iran. Rahimi et al. [5] reported high morphological variation among local germplasm, while Azaddel and Ghorbani [6] evaluated 20 traits across 27 genotypes in Ardabil Province, revealing substantial differences. These studies confirm the potential of Iranian germplasm for selection and breeding. In terms of genetic diversity, Khoramdel-Azad et al. [7] assessed 40 genotypes using 15 SSR markers, detecting an average of 5.36 alleles per locus and high discriminatory power. Cluster analyses partially reflected geographic origins. Alipour et al. [8] used RAPD markers on 30 native genotypes and four foreign genotypes, identifying 105 polymorphic bands out of 164, demonstrating distinguishable genetic patterns among Iranian genotypes. Research on S-locus alleles has revealed new variants (e.g., S4 and S5) and the occurrence of both self-compatible and self-incompatible genotypes, providing valuable information for breeding and pollinizer selection [9]. Quince breeding objectives in Iran include improving fruit quality (weight, flavor, bioactive compounds), resistance to fire blight, drought tolerance, self-compatibility, and reduction of the juvenile phase. Over the past two decades, major targets have encompassed identification of self-compatible genotypes, selection of dwarfing rootstocks, and enhancement of fruit quality and secondary metabolites [1]. Morphological and phenological traits such as growth vigor, growth habit, trunk form, leaf dimensions, flower and fruit size and shape, fruit color, pubescence density, flowering time, and fruit ripening period are critical for parent selection, hybrid evaluation, and progeny assessment. For example, growth vigor and habit influence competitive ability, stress tolerance, and space utilization, while fruit traits directly affect market value, yield, and storability. Rahimi et al. [5] and Tatari et al. [10] confirmed wide variation in fruit and leaf morphology among Iranian genotypes, supporting their use as discriminative descriptors. Genetic analyses using SSR markers have further shown that Iranian quince genotypes cluster according to geographic origin [11], emphasizing the relationship between morphological traits and underlying genetic factors. Integrating phenotypic and molecular information can enable more targeted breeding, identification of superior parents, and efficient production of elite progenies. Global climate change, including rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, more frequent extreme weather events, shifts in suitable agricultural zones, and the expansion of pests and diseases, directly threatens crop yields and the stability of agricultural and fruit production, posing a significant risk to worldwide food security [12–14]. Under these circumstances, plant breeding represents a critical strategy for developing climate-resilient cultivars and plays a key role in maintaining and improving yield stability. Consequently, advanced breeding studies that explicitly consider future climate-related stresses are essential to ensure long-term food security and the sustainability of agricultural production. In perennial fruit trees such as quince, phenological traits including flowering time and fruit ripening period are particularly sensitive to climatic fluctuations and play a critical role in environmental adaptation. The evaluation of morpho-phenological traits in fruit trees provides fundamental phenotypic data that reflect underlying genetic diversity and constitutes a primary step in the identification, characterization, and differentiation of genotypes for plant breeding programs [15]. These evaluations, encompassing traits such as leaf and flower characteristics, fruit size, ripening time, and growth habit, facilitate the assessment of diversity and the identification of distinct genotype groups with specific environmental adaptability or market-preferred attributes. Multivariate analyses of these traits enable the classification of genotypes, the elucidation of relationships among traits, and the informed selection of suitable parents for hybridization and cultivar development. By identifying superior phenotypes with desirable agronomic and pomological characteristics, breeders can prioritize promising genotypes with enhanced yield potential, fruit quality, and adaptive capacity. Ultimately, integrating morpho-phenological diversity into fruit tree breeding strategies increases selection efficiency and accelerates the development of resilient, high-yielding cultivars suitable for both commercial production and conservation objectives.

This study provides the first comprehensive evaluation of morpho-phenological traits across a wide set of Iranian quince genotypes, identifying key descriptors for breeding and contributing to climate-resilient cultivar development. Building on these studies, the present research aims to comprehensively evaluate vegetative, fruit and reproductive traits of 52 Iranian quince genotypes collected from Ardabil, Isfahan, Khorasan, Astara, and Gilan provinces during the 2025 growing season. Using 43 descriptors based on UPOV guidelines, this study seeks to identify key traits for distinguishing and characterizing genotypes, providing a framework to guide selection in breeding programs and improve descriptor systems and DUS (Distinctness, Uniformity, and Stability) tests at the national level.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

This study was conducted at the Horticultural Research Station affiliated with the Agricultural Research, Education, and Extension Organization (AREEO) in the southern part of Alborz Province, Iran. The study area is located at 36°08′ N latitude and 50°03′ E longitude, characterized by a temperate climate with cold winters and hot summers. The hot season lasts for 3.6 months, from May 31 to September 17, with an average daily high temperature above 29 °C. The hottest month of the year in Karaj is July, with an average high of 34 °C and low of 22 °C. The cold season lasts for 3.5 months, from November 23 to March 8, with an average daily high temperature below 11 °C. The coldest month of the year in Karaj is January, with an average low of -2 °C and high of 6 °C. The wetter season lasts 6.9 months, from September 20 to April 15, with a greater than 13% chance of a given day being a wet day. The month with the most wet days in Karaj is November, with an average of 6.3 days with at least 1 mm of precipitation [16]. The 10-year climatological data for the period 2016–2026 are provided in Supplementary Tables and Figures a–d, with each dataset presented separately. A total of 52 genotypes were preliminarily selected during field survey from the living germplasm collection of the AREEO Horticultural Research Station based on primary phenotypic variation observed in the field, including differences in tree vigor, growth habit, flowering time, and fruit-related traits. Their names, abbreviations, origin, rootstock type, and year of grafting are presented in Table 1. All accessions were formally identified and described by Dr. Hamid Abdollahi, and voucher specimens are preserved in the institutional germplasm repository under their respective deposition numbers, which correspond to the accession codes provided in Table 1. All measured traits in the 52 quince genotypes were evaluated and scored based on the standard UPOV descriptors and according to the scales and criteria specified in the relevant guidelines, whereas the list of morphological traits used for characterization was compiled with reference to IBPGR descriptors (Table 2).

Trait assessment

Using the list of morphological traits provided by the International Board for Plant Genetic Resources (IBPGR), 43 qualitative traits were evaluated to characterize the genotypes (Table 3). These traits included growth vigor (Tvi), growth habit (Tha), form (Tform), length of internode (LoI), color (Co), position of the vegetative bud relative to the shoot (PVBRSJh), pubescence on the upper third of the leaf (PSc), leaf attitude (Latt), leaf length (LL), leaf width (LW), leaf shape (LSh), leaf base shape (LSoB), apex angle excluding the pointed tip (LAA), leaf tip length (LLt), leaf cross-sectional profile (LPCS), leaf margin undulation (LUM), leaf abscission (LA), fruit length (PoL), fruit size (PoS), time of leaf bud burst (FTLBB), sepal shape (FSSh), sepal size (FSSe), beginning of flowering (FTBF), sepal length (FSL), sepal width (FSW), flower size (Fsi), flower color (FCO), arrangement of petals (FAP), petal shape (FPSh), petal margin undulation (FPUM), position of the stigma relative to the pistil (FPSRS), petal size (FPS), fruit size (FSi), general fruit shape in longitudinal section (FGSL), symmetry in longitudinal section (FSLS), fruit neck (FNe), fruit neck length (FLNe), prominence of ribs at the stalk end (FPRSE), prominence of ribs at the calyx end (FPRCE), stalk cavity (FSC), fruit color (FCo), fruit weight (FWe), and time of fruit ripening onset (FTBFR) (Figs. 1, 2 and 3).

Data analysis

Phenotypic diversity of 43 qualitative traits was evaluated for 52 different tree genotypes using frequency distributions, and the Shannon-Wiener diversity index (H’) was calculated using the formula − ΣPi × LnPi [17, 18]. The mean of each quantitative trait was computed and used for subsequent statistical analyses. Parameters including minimum, maximum, mean, median, standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV, %) were calculated for all 43 quantitative traits using Prism software. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was employed to examine relationships among traits, and the normality of each trait was tested. Relationships among accessions were further explored using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) in R software, and the ggplot package was used for visual mapping. Additionally, for quantitative traits, statistical indices such as range (minimum and maximum values), Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (KS_Value and Sig_Value), skewness, kurtosis, and absolute, positive, and negative ranges (Abs_Range, Pos_Range, Neg_Range) were calculated. This comprehensive approach allowed a detailed assessment of trait distribution, data dispersion, normality, and overall phenotypic diversity [18]. In this study, NMDS coupled with Envfit was applied to identify significant qualitative traits and assess their contribution to the phenotypic distribution of genotypes. The analyses were conducted in R software (4.4.2) using the packages vegan, ggplot2, dplyr, and rgl. To examine patterns of similarity among treatments, a Euclidean distance matrix was computed based on quantitative trait data, followed by NMDS analysis using the `metaMDS` function in two dimensions (k = 2) and three dimensions (k = 3) with `trymax = 100` and a fixed random seed (`set.seed (123)`) to ensure reproducibility. The relationships between traits and sample ordination were assessed using the envfit test with 999 random permutations, and only significant vectors (p < 0.05) were interpreted. In addition, hierarchical clustering with the Ward. D2 method was performed on the same distance matrix to explore the grouping patterns among treatments. This approach enabled the detection of traits with high and low explanatory power in a multidimensional space.

Results

Based on the descriptive data presented in Table 4 for the evaluated morphological traits, the results indicate that considerable variation was observed among the different traits (Figs. 4, 5 and 6). For vegetative growth traits, growth vigor (Tvi) exhibited a mean of 4.67 with a range of 3 to 7 and a relatively low coefficient of variation (CV) of 28.54%, suggesting a relatively uniform distribution among the genotypes. In contrast, growth habit (Tha) and growth form (Tform) had lower mean values (1.57) but high CVs (exceeding 40%), indicating a wide dispersion and substantial diversity among the genotypes for these traits. Regarding leaf traits, leaf length (LL) and leaf width (LW) showed mean values of 4.74 and 3.82, respectively, with CVs of around 30–31%, reflecting a moderate level of variation. In contrast, traits such as leaf shape (LSh) and leaf base shape (LSoB) exhibited higher CVs (above 40%), suggesting greater genetic diversity. Similarly, leaf margin undulation (LUM) had a CV close to 49%, indicating pronounced variability among the genotypes. In phenological traits, the time of leaf bud burst (FTLBB) and the onset of flowering (FTBF) both had mean values above 5 with low CVs (approximately 18–19%), suggesting relatively stable expression among genotypes. Conversely, plant size (PoS) exhibited an extremely high CV of 90.88%, representing the highest variability across all evaluated traits.For floral traits, sepal size (FSSe) and sepal width (FSW) displayed CVs of approximately 36% and 53%, respectively, indicating high dispersion within the studied population. Additionally, petal shape (FPSh) with a CV exceeding 51% and flower color (FCO) with a CV around 37% revealed substantial phenotypic variation in floral traits. In terms of fruit characteristics, fruit size (FSi) recorded a mean of 5.40 with a lower CV (27.25%), suggesting greater stability compared with other fruit traits. However, traits such as fruit neck length (FLNe), prominence of ridges at the peduncle end (FPRSE), and depth of the peduncle cavity (FSC) exhibited very high CVs (ranging from 57% to 83%), highlighting strong variability among the genotypes. The highest degree of variability was recorded for fruit neck thickness (FNe), with a CV approaching 98.4%. Overall, the results revealed that phenological traits such as flowering time and leaf bud burst were relatively stable, whereas fruit morphological traits and certain leaf characteristics (such as shape and margin undulation) showed the highest levels of variability. This highlights the considerable potential of fruit traits and selected morphological leaf traits for use in selection and breeding programs aimed at improving quince genotypes.

Shannon’s diversity index

The results of Shannon’s diversity index for morphological traits revealed that the extent of genotypic variation differed across traits, with some traits exhibiting considerably wider ranges of variability than others (Fig. 7). Among the vegetative traits, Tvi (0.98), Tha (0.91), and Tform (0.94) fell within the moderate range, whereas LoI (1.04) and particularly Co (1.46) displayed higher levels of diversity compared to other traits in this group. Similarly, PVBRSJh (1.14) and PSc (1.06) showed relatively high levels of diversity, while Latt (0.89) exhibited the lowest diversity within this category. For leaf traits, LL (1.04) and LW (0.80) were classified as moderate and low, respectively. The index values for LSh (1.03) and LSoB (0.96) also indicated moderate diversity. LAA (0.94) and LLt (0.80) represented relatively low levels of variation. In contrast, LPCS (1.53) demonstrated the highest diversity among the leaf traits, while LUM (1.25) also showed a high level of variation. LA (1.06) and PoL (0.96) were moderate, whereas PoS (1.12) indicated higher diversity. Regarding phenological traits, FTLBB (0.75) and FTBF (0.73) showed the lowest levels of diversity across all traits.In floral traits, FSSh (0.92) and FSSe (0.81) exhibited relatively low variability; however, FSL (1.39) and FSW (1.42) showed the greatest diversity within this group. Moreover, Fsi (1.27) and FPSh (1.20) were also classified as highly diverse. By contrast, FCO (0.76) and FPSRS (0.35) represented the lowest values. FAP (1.02) and FPUM (0.94) fell into the moderate category, while FPS (1.26) exhibited a relatively high degree of diversity. In fruit traits, FSi (1.04) and FGSL (1.15) displayed relatively high variability, whereas FSLS (0.34) showed the lowest diversity in this category. FNe (0.65) and FLNe (1.12) differed markedly, indicating uniformity in FNe but greater variation in FLNe. FrPRSE (1.04) and FPRCE (1.00) were in the moderate range, while FSC (1.22) and FCo (0.95) showed relatively high diversity. FWe (1.25) was among the most important traits with a high level of diversity, whereas FTBFR (1.06) was placed in the moderate range. Overall, the greatest diversity was observed in LPCS (1.53), FSW (1.42), and FSL (1.39) among leaf and floral traits, as well as in FWe (1.25) and FSC (1.22) among fruit traits. In contrast, the lowest diversity was recorded for FSLS (0.34), FPSRS (0.35), and FTBF (0.73).

PCA biplot interpretation

The principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first two components (PC1 and PC2) explained 16.5% and 10.7% of the total variance in the dataset, respectively (Fig. 9). Although the proportion of explained variance was moderate, these two components successfully captured the main structure of genotype distribution and trait relationships. Traits with the highest positive loadings on PC1 were mostly floral and fruit-related traits, including, FSL, FSW, FSSh, FSSe, FPS, FSi, and FWe. This indicates that genotypes positioned on the right side of the biplot (e.g., AD7 and ChF) exhibited higher values for floral and fruit characteristics. Traits associated with PC2 were mainly vegetative and leaf-related traits such as Tvi, Tha, LoI, LSh, LSoB, PSc, LPCS, and LAA, which were located on the positive axis of PC2. These traits contributed to the differentiation of genotypes such as Behta, KVD1, and KVD3.Traits such as FTBF and FTLBB (flowering initiation and bud burst time) were positioned on the negative side of PC1, indicating that the genotypes located in this region (e.g., LA1, LA2, AD6) exhibited later developmental patterns. Trait vectors forming small angles with one another (e.g., FSi, FSL, and FSW) indicated a strong positive correlation among these traits, whereas traits oriented in opposite directions (e.g., FTLBB and FSi) showed a negative association. The higher density of genotypes clustered around the center of the biplot reflected greater phenotypic similarity among most genotypes. However, genotypes located on the periphery (such as CHI and AD7) were identified as distinct genotypes that could serve as valuable resources for the improvement of specific traits. Furthermore, genotypes such as CHI, AD1, SHAMS, GIV, AD7, KVD3, NB3, NAS1, BEHTA, and ASM1 exhibited strong associations with the traits contributing to the first two principal components and thus represent key examples of genotypes influencing the major axes of variation, as illustrated in Fig. 9. The biplot analysis demonstrated that floral and fruit-related traits (FSL, FSW, FSi, FWe) contributed most strongly to the differentiation of genotypes, while vegetative traits such as Tvi, PSc, and LoI played a greater role in separating genotypes within the central group. Furthermore, phenological traits (FTBF, FTLBB) served as key indicators of early- versus late-maturity patterns and exhibited a clear negative relationship with flowering and fruit-size traits. These findings provide direct implications for breeding programs, particularly in selecting genotypes with desirable traits such as early flowering or superior fruit quality.

Descriptive statistical results of morphological traits

The descriptive statistical analysis of the morphological traits evaluated in this study was conducted using various statistical indices, including Range, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test value (KS_Value), significance level (Sig_Value), skewness, kurtosis, absolute range (Abs_Range), positive range (Pos_Range), and negative range (Neg_Range) (Table 5).The observed ranges for the traits varied widely: for instance, Tvi ranged from 3 to 7, Tha from 1 to 3, and Co from 1 to 7. KS tests indicated that most traits significantly deviated from a normal distribution (Sig_Value < 0.05), although several traits could still be considered approximately normal due to absolute skewness and kurtosis values below 1. For all traits, the Abs_Range and Pos_Range matched the observed ranges, and none of the traits contained negative values.Traits related to vegetative growth, such as Tvi, Tha, Tform, LoI, and PSc, showed moderate skewness (0.19–0.92) and kurtosis (1.78–2.42), indicating relatively symmetric distributions without extreme outliers. Leaf-related traits, including LL, LW, LSh, LSoB, LAA, LLt, LPCS, and LUM, displayed more variation in skewness and kurtosis, reflecting diversity in leaf size, shape, and structure. Reproductive traits, such as FTLBB, FSSh, FSSe, FTBF, FSL, FSW, Fsi, FCO, FAP, FPSh, FPUM, FPSRS, and FPS, exhibited ranges from 1 to 9 or 3–9 and varied skewness (–0.33 to 3.5) and kurtosis (1.7327–15.29), indicating a mix of symmetric and highly skewed distributions. Fruit-related traits, including FSi, FGSL, FSLS, FNe, FLNe, FPRSE, FPRCE, FSC, FCo, FWe, and FTBFR, also displayed wide ranges and variable skewness and kurtosis, reflecting substantial phenotypic diversity among genotypes. Overall, the KS tests showed that although seven traitswere approximately normally distributed (Sig_Value > 0.05), several other traits deviated from normality, particularly those with absolute skewness or kurtosis values exceeding 1, such as FPSRS, FSSe, and FSSh. These results indicate considerable statistical variation in the measured traits, which provides a robust basis for subsequent analyses, including multivariate modeling, PCA, and clustering.

Results of NMDS and cluster analysis

Based on the results of the NMDS analysis and the subsequent clustering, the evaluated genotypes were organized into four distinct groups (Table 6; Fig. 11). In the first cluster, genotypes such as KVD3, ET1, PK2, KHO, NB4, EO, Behta, MV1, SIB, GAR, NAS1, NAS2, KVD2, LA1, LA2, NB3, SVS1, and SHA1 were grouped together. These genotypes were mainly distributed in the negative region of the NMDS1 axis and across various parts of the NMDS2 axis, indicating their tendency to form a dense cluster on the left side of the two-dimensional NMDS space. The second cluster included AD3, SBM, NB2, ASM1, AD5, DI, AD4, DAR, AD2, AMR, LA3, BRM, RJB, SHIR, KISH, and AD6. These genotypes were mostly scattered along the positive region of the NMDS1 axis and covered a broad range of positive values on the NMDS2 axis. Their position indicates a clear differentiation from the first cluster. In the third cluster, genotypes SVS2, AS1, KVD1, KVD4, ASP1, AS2, MV2, ASM2, ASM3, KM1, and ASP2 were included. These genotypes were mostly located around the central region and the positive portions of the NMDS2 axis, showing an intermediate distribution between the other clusters. This pattern suggests that these genotypes play an intermediary role in the overall phenotypic structure. Finally; the fourth cluster consisted of GIV, AD7, AD1, CHI, and SHMS. This group was primarily positioned in the highly positive and distant regions of the NMDS1 axis and in the negative parts of the NMDS2 axis, exhibiting a distinct and clearly separated distribution pattern compared to the other clusters. Overall, the results demonstrated that the evaluated genotypes were distinctly separated into four well-defined clusters in the two-dimensional NMDS space based on the measured traits. Genotypes of the first cluster were mainly concentrated in the negative range of the NMDS1 axis, whereas those of the second cluster were distributed in the positive range of this axis. The third cluster occupied an intermediate position, and the fourth cluster included genotypes with the greatest separation and dispersion along the NMDS1 axis. This pattern indicates the existence of substantial genetic and phenotypic differentiation among the various groups of genotypes.

Discussion

In this study, remarkable phenotypic variation was observed among the evaluated quince genotypes, providing a valuable opportunity for future breeding programs. In the vegetative growth section, the trait of vigor (Tvi) showed a relatively high mean with a low coefficient of variation (CV = 28.5%), indicating relative uniformity among genotypes. This suggests that the trait is under stronger genetic control or less influenced by environmental factors. In contrast, traits such as growth habit (Tha) and growth form (Tform) exhibited high variability (CV > 40%), implying a strong potential for genetic differentiation in these characteristics. Similarly, SVS1, SVS2, Viduja (KVD1), and KVD4 were weak, Isfahan, ET1, NB1, KVD2, and NB4 were moderate, and Torsh showed strong vigor [19]. Growth habit (Tha) was generally upright or semi-upright, whereas genotypes from central Iran mostly exhibited spur-type growth habits [19–21].

In the leaf trait section, leaf length (LL) and width (LW) had moderate variability (CV ≈ 30–31%), while leaf shape (LSh), leaf base shape (LSoB), and leaf margin undulation (LUM) displayed much higher variation, indicating strong genetic diversity in leaf morphology. This finding is consistent with reports in other fruit trees showing that qualitative leaf traits (such as shape and margin) are generally more variable than dimensional ones [19, 21].

Regarding phenological traits, time of leaf bud burst (FTLBB) and time of beginning of flowering (FTBF) showed relatively low variability (CV ≈ 18–19%), suggesting stability among genotypes. Such uniformity reflects natural selection favoring synchronized growth timing with suitable environmental conditions in perennial species. Conversely, the overall plant size (PoS) showed very high variability (~ 90.9%), indicating substantial differences among genotypes in growth potential and environmental adaptability. Flowering time (FTBF) also varied, with early flowering observed in SVS1, SVS2, KVD2, KVD4, Viduja (KVD1), and Torsh, and late flowering in Isfahan, Behta, and NB4 [19, 20].

Flower and fruit traits also exhibited high diversity. For example, calyx width (FSW) and petal shape (FPSh) showed high coefficients of variation and ranked among the most diverse traits according to the Shannon diversity index. Flower size was generally large, petal color was mostly light pink, while NB1 and Viduja (KVD1) had white flowers [19, 20]. Petal arrangement (FAP) varied from irregular (in most genotypes) to tangential (SVS2, Viduja (KVD1)) or overlapping (NB4) [19]. Petal margin undulation (FPUM) ranged from low (ET1, SVS genotypes) to high (KVD2, NB4, Behta), and stigma position (FPSRS) varied among genotypes, being at or below the anther level [4, 19].

Moreover, assessing trait stability across environments and years will be essential for ensuring long-term breeding success. In Cydonia oblonga (quince), flower size traits such as flower size index (FSi) and flower petal size (FPS), along with sepal dimensions including sepal length (FSL) and sepal width (FSW), play a crucial role in pollination success, fruit set, and final fruit quality. Selection for traits such as FSi and FPS can simultaneously enhance yield potential and improve both quantitative and qualitative fruit characteristics [22, 23].

Among fruit-related traits, while overall fruit size (FSi) showed relatively less variability, traits such as fruit neck length (FLNe), neck thickness (FNe), and stalk cavity depth (FSC) exhibited extremely high variation, suggesting a strong potential for selection and breeding based on fruit shape attributes. Fruit size ranged from small (Talash, Nalkot, Malam Jabba) to large (Isfahan, Torsh, Behta, NB4) [19, 20]. The general fruit shape varied from maliformis (apple-shaped) to pyriformis (pear-shaped) [20], while Iranian genotypes were mostly circular or elliptic [19]. Neck shape and symmetry (FNe, FSLS) also served as diagnostic traits, with most genotypes showing symmetrical fruits, except Isfahan and Torsh, which were asymmetric [19, 24]. Early ripening was reported for Viduja (KVD1) and KVD2, intermediate for Isfahan, ET1, NB1, Torsh, Behta, NB4, and KVD4, and late for SVS1 and SVS2 [19]. Fruits were generally harvested in October, indicating a commercially suitable harvesting period [25].

The Shannon diversity index provided a useful quantitative perspective on trait diversity. Notably, leaf pubescence (LPCS) exhibited the highest diversity (1,53), while among floral and fruit traits, FSW (1.42) and FSL (1.39) showed high diversity levels. For fruit traits, fruit weight (FWe) (~ 1.25) and stalk cavity depth (FSC) were also among the most variable. Overall, these findings emphasize that while phenological and dimensional traits tend to be more stable, structural and morphological traits particularly those influencing fruit shape, neck thickness, and surface characteristics exhibit greater genetic diversity and may serve as key targets for breeding and selection.

The NMDS analysis revealed that the phenotypic pattern of the evaluated quince (Cydonia oblonga Mill.) genotypes in the two-dimensional ordination space was clearly structured by a specific set of key traits. Among the 43 measured traits, only a subset including FSi, FSL, FSW, FSi, FWe, LPCS, LUM, PSc, Tvi, LoI, FTBF, FTLBB, and several other fruit-related characteristics showed significant associations. This indicates that phenotypic differentiation among genotypes was primarily driven by fruit-related traits, along with certain leaf indices. Such a dominance of fruit traits in genotype separation is consistent with previous reports on morphological diversity in quince, highlighting the central role of fruit characteristics in generating observable variation among genotypes [20].

Fruit-related traits such as FSL, FSW, FSi, and FWe had the highest explanatory loadings in genotype differentiation. Extremely strong correlations among these traits (e.g., r = 0.99 between FSi and FPS) suggest that they form a cohesive structural block directly linked to fruit quality and marketability. From a breeding perspective, these results imply that selection strategies focusing on improved fruit quality should be prioritized, as changes in one or two key attributes (e.g., FSi or FSW) typically lead to concurrent positive shifts in other related traits. Therefore, a composite selection index based on FSi, FSL, and FSW could be effectively used for breeding high-quality quince cultivars [26]. Traits with the highest r² values, such as FSi and FPS, indicate strong genetic determinism and can be used as primary selection criteria for choosing parental lines.

Leaf and physiological traits such as LPCS, LUM, PSc, Tvi, and LoI were also significant, reflecting structural and functional diversity in leaf morphology. These traits have two major breeding implications: leaf structural indices (LPCS and LUM) may serve as indicators of differences in light-use efficiency and gas exchange, making them valuable for improving performance under variable or stress-prone environments. Traits such as PSc, Tvi, and LoI, which represent vegetative growth vigor and leaf posture, could be utilized to optimize the balance between vegetative and reproductive growth particularly when the breeding objective is to increase fruit yield without compromising flowering stability [27]. Multiple studies on quince germplasm and vegetative trait evaluation have also confirmed the importance of leaf characteristics in adaptation and productivity [28].

Phenological traits such as FTBF and FTLBB, which exhibited low coefficients of variation (CV), indicated relative stability among genotypes and played key roles in determining earliness or lateness. The observed negative correlations between these traits and fruit size or quality traits indicated that early flowering is often associated with smaller fruits or reduced quality attributes, while late flowering tends to correspond with larger fruits and distinct quality profiles. From a breeding standpoint, this implies that achieving an optimal balance between flowering time and fruit quality is essential. In cases where early maturity is desired, parental genotypes with early phenology but minimal quality trade-offs should be prioritized. Previous studies on quince flowering and harvest time have also emphasized that managing phenology and its alignment with local climatic conditions plays a crucial role in the commercial success of new cultivars [20, 29].

In terms of vegetative growth, the vigor trait (Tvi) exhibited a relatively high mean and low coefficient of variation (~ 28–29%), suggesting uniformity among genotypes and potentially stronger genetic control or lower environmental influence. Conversely, traits such as growth habit (Tha) and growth form (Tform) showed CV values exceeding 40%, indicating notable genetic variability and potential for selection in these characteristics [28].

Among leaf traits, leaf length (LL) and leaf width (LW) showed moderate variation (CV ≈ 30–31%), whereas traits such as leaf shape (LSh), leaf base shape (LSoB), and leaf margin undulation (LUM) exhibited much higher variability, reflecting significant genetic diversity in leaf morphology. These findings are consistent with reports in other fruit trees indicating that qualitative leaf traits particularly shape and edge morphology tend to display greater variation than size-related traits [5].

Regarding floral and fruit traits, the results indicated that calyx width (FSW), petal shape (FPSh), fruit length, and fruit weight (FSi, FWe) had the highest loadings in the first principal component (PCA). Genotypes such as AD7 and CHI, characterized by outstanding floral and fruit attributes, were distinctly separated on the right side of the biplot. These findings reinforce the idea that floral and fruit traits possess greater discriminative power among quince genotypes, consistent with previous reports [20, 30]. Additionally, high variability in fruit traits such as weight, dimensions, and shape index has been previously documented [5, 25].

Cluster and heatmap analyses grouped the genotypes into four distinct clusters, with floral and fruit traits having the strongest influence on cluster differentiation. Genotypes located furthest from the center of the NMDS plot, such as CHI and AD7, represented the most divergent phenotypes and could serve as valuable genetic resources for introducing novel traits into breeding programs [31].

The Envfit analysis further confirmed that traits such as FSi, FSL, FSW, and FNe with r2 values exceeding 0.5 had the greatest explanatory power for genotype dispersion, whereas traits like Tha, PVBRSJh, PSc, and certain leaf or fruit traits contributed less strongly.

Cluster analysis revealed that the principal factors of genotype grouping were geographic origin, tree vigor, astringency presence/absence, and fruit end-use [1, 32]. Group 1 consisted of the genotypes Behta, KVD3, KVD2, ET1, and NB3, characterized by large fruit size, vigorous tall trees, upright growth habit, detectable astringency, high storage potential, and suitability for processing applications, with most members originating from the Isfahan region [33]. The phenotypic similarity of a resistant genotype from Khorasan to this group supports a hypothesis of possible historical genetic relatedness or parallel adaptive selection pressures across these regions [34, 35].

Group 2, mainly representing northwest Iran and Gilan, comprised the genotypes AD2, AD3, AD4, AD6, Shir, Darnak, and NB2, and was associated with moderate fruit size, apple-shaped form, soft texture, good flavor, absence of astringency, limited storage performance, suitability for fresh consumption, and low processing efficiency [36], and its non-astringent and favorable sensory profile confer breeding value as a parental source for improving tree stature and fresh-fruit quality [37–39]. This group predominantly exhibits inherent genetically dwarf characteristics in terms of genetic origin. This growth pattern suggests that many of the genotypes in this group likely trace their genetic background to the Caucasus region, where the diversity of foundational clonal rootstocks is also believed to have originated. In commercial propagation and the production of rootstocks for fruit-bearing trees-particularly for grafting pear and quince cultivars-clonal rootstocks developed by East Malling Research, including Quince A, Quince B, and Quince C, are widely used. The breeding origin and initial selections of some of dwarf rootstocks of this group is also associated with genetic material collected from the Caucasus region [19, 38]. Among these rootstocks, Quince C is recognized as the most dwarfing rootstock, exhibiting the strongest dwarfing effect. Considering that most genotypes in Group 2 are naturally dwarf, they demonstrate better compatibility with dwarfing clonal rootstocks, especially Quince C. This compatibility can be highly valuable in breeding programs aimed at maintaining a compact tree stature, improving the sensory quality of fresh, table-fruit varieties, and enabling easier canopy management in nurseries and orchards [40].

Group 3 included the Isfahan-origin genotypes KM1, KVD4, SVS2, and KVD1, displaying shorter trees, small fruit size, desirable fresh-eating quality, and absence of astringency, confirming notable intra-regional phenotypic diversity of non-industrial germplasm in Isfahan [41].

Group 4, represented by the northwest-origin genotypes AD7, GIV, and CHI, was separated primarily by larger fruit size, but showed inferior fresh-fruit and processing quality, reflecting localized selection for fruit size without concurrent improvement in other quality traits [23].

The present study provides a trait-based framework for understanding phenotypic structuring in quince germplasm and its direct implications for breeding. Our multivariate analyses demonstrated that fruit-related traits (e.g., FSi, FSL, FSW, FNe) and phenological descriptors (FTLBB, FTBF) contributed most strongly to genotype differentiation. In perennial fruit crops, flowering time and bud burst are key adaptive traits that determine synchronization with environmental cues and avoidance of abiotic stresses such as late spring frost or early heat waves [42, 43]. The relatively stable expression of phenological traits observed in our study (CV ≈ 18–19%) suggests potential genetic regulation, making these traits reliable targets for selection. Climate change is increasingly affecting temperate fruit production through shifts in chilling accumulation, irregular flowering, increased frequency of late frost events, and heat stress during fruit development [44]. In fruit trees, phenological shifts are among the most sensitive biological responses to climate variability, often leading to asynchrony between flowering and pollinator activity or increased exposure to extreme temperatures [45]. Such disruptions can significantly reduce fruit set, yield, and quality. Furthermore, the strong discriminatory power of fruit morphological traits aligns with previous findings indicating that fruit size and structural attributes are polygenic but heritable components suitable for parent selection in breeding programs [20, 46]. By identifying genotypes located at the extremes of the PCA and NMDS spaces, this study provides practical candidates for crossing programs aimed at improving fruit quality, yield stability, and adaptation. Given the perennial nature and long juvenile phase of quince, proactive identification of adaptive phenotypes is essential for long-term breeding success. Genotypes exhibiting variability in flowering time, fruit ripening period, and vegetative architecture may provide valuable resources for developing cultivars capable of escaping frost damage, tolerating heat stress, or adjusting to altered growing seasons. As breeding cycles in fruit trees require substantial time investment, early incorporation of climate-adaptive traits into selection programs is critical [47].

To accelerate breeding progress, it is recommended to develop a multi-trait composite index emphasizing fruit-related traits and to utilize distinct genotypes from peripheral clusters as genetic resources for rare or desirable attributes. Future studies should include heritability and genetic correlation analyses for key traits and integrate molecular data such as DNA markers to identify genomic regions associated with important morphological traits.

Conclusion

This study revealed substantial phenotypic variation among 54 quince (Cydonia oblonga Mill.) genotypes from different Iranian provinces, with flower- and fruit-related traits being the most informative for genotype differentiation. Traits such as growth habit (Tha), tree form (Tform), and leaf and fruit morphology exhibited the highest variability, whereas phenological traits like leaf bud burst (FTLBB) and flowering initiation (FTBF) were relatively stable. PCA and correlation analyses highlighted that flower- and fruit-size traits (FSi, FSL, FSW, FPS, FWe) largely explained the observed variation, while vegetative and leaf traits formed a secondary axis. NMDS and Envfit analyses identified key traits (FTLBB, FSL, FSW, FSi, FPS, FNe) with high explanatory power (r² > 0.5) driving genotype differentiation. Genotypes such as Behta, KVD1–KVD4, ET1, NB2, NB3, AD2, AD7, Shir, Darnak, GIV, CHI, and SVS2 demonstrated notable phenotypic distinctiveness, reflecting diversity in fruit size, tree vigor, astringency, and quality. Overall, flower- and fruit-related traits, particularly size and timing, are most valuable for selecting superior genotypes, while leaf and phenological traits complement assessments of adaptability and growth balance. Future breeding programs should focus on composite multi-trait indices based on key fruit traits (FSi, FSL, FSW) and consider heritability, genetic correlations, environmental stability, and molecular markers to enhance the identification and development of elite quince cultivars. These results indicate that the selection of superior genotypes for breeding programs should primarily focus on key floral and fruit traits in combination with phenological characteristics, while other traits may be used as complementary selection criteria. To accelerate breeding progress, the development of a multi-trait composite selection index with particular emphasis on fruit-related traits is recommended, and genetically distinct genotypes located in peripheral clusters should be exploited as valuable genetic resources for rare or desirable attributes. Future studies should incorporate estimates of heritability and genetic correlations of key traits and integrate molecular data, such as DNA-based markers, to identify genomic regions associated with important morphological characteristics.
