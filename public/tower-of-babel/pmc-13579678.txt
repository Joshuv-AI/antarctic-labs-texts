Building competence in emergency care: a proposed model for training Ghana’s next generation of medical doctors
Authors: Maame Akuamoah Yeboah, Nana Serwaa Agyeman Quao, Eugene Adomako
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile Emergency Medicine is increasingly being integrated into postgraduate medical training, its adoption into undergraduate education remains limited across Africa. This educational gap contributes to disparities in the quality of initial stabilisation and acute care delivery, particularly among newly qualified doctors.This paper proposes a model for integrating emergency medicine into undergraduate medical school curricula that is grounded in global standards and tailored to local realities. The model emphasises the development of core competencies in undergraduate emergency medicine. It advocates implementation in the first and final clinical years as a three- to six-week rotation led by emergency medicine consultants and specialists. The proposed core domains include trauma, medical and paediatric emergencies; procedural and ultrasound-guided skills; resource stewardship and disaster preparedness.Implementation of this model has the potential to standardise foundational emergency care knowledge, bridge the preparedness gap among junior clinicians and strengthen the resilience of Ghana’s emergency care response systems.Keywords: Emergency medicine, Undergraduate medical education, Curriculum development, Ghana, Emergency care training

Background

Emergency Medicine (EM) is a fundamental pillar in the development of robust emergency care systems [1,2]. While the speciality is increasingly recognised across many resource-limited countries in Africa, it remains either underdeveloped or in its formative stages compared to the substantial growth observed in high-resource settings [2,3].

The healthcare landscape in Ghana is structured such that lower-tier facilities serve as the initial point of contact for most medical emergencies. Yet these facilities often have limited access to EM specialists [4,5]. The current undergraduate medical curriculum centres on definitive patient care, not on emergency care or managing undifferentiated patients. As a result, students graduate with a gap in emergency care experience, which becomes apparent when they serve as house officers (newly qualified doctors) and medical officers — the first point of contact in emergency rooms [5]. Their limited emergency care training hinders effective patient assessment, contributing to suboptimal outcomes even after patients reach definitive care.

The impact of these systemic gaps is reflected in hospital mortality data. A 2020 one-year mortality review at the Tamale Teaching Hospital revealed that the majority (77%) of deaths occurred within the first 24 hours of admission [6]. This was more evident in the absence of an on-duty emergency physician. These findings suggest that the initial phase of acute management is a high-risk window in the Ghanaian health system.

Currently, the burden of this initial stabilisation falls heavily on house officers/ interns and medical officers, due to the limited number of emergency physicians. However, in the absence of a standardised undergraduate EM clerkship, these junior clinicians must rely on fragmented knowledge from traditional rotations to manage undifferentiated, life-threatening cases. A 2018 survey of junior doctors—those within their first five years of practice—revealed that while these clinicians are tasked with managing acute trauma and medical emergencies, they lack the formal training to do so confidently. This gap in preparedness highlights the urgent need for a dedicated undergraduate curriculum that transitions from disease-specific management to an acuity-based approach [7].

Over the past two decades, EM has emerged as a distinct medical speciality in Ghana, with postgraduate residency training formally introduced in 2009 [3,4]. The Ghana College of Physicians and Surgeons currently has three accredited centres for specialist EM training [8]. Although this development has contributed to improved management of acute conditions at tertiary and selected regional facilities, access to specialist-led emergency care remains limited across much of the country, particularly in district and primary-level health facilities [5].

As of 2020, Ghana had an estimated emergency physician-to-population ratio of approximately one per 600,000 people, with fewer than 100 trained emergency physicians serving a population exceeding 32 million [9,10]. Despite ongoing efforts to train emergency physicians, these numbers remain grossly inadequate to meet the emergency care needs of the population. Moreover, a recent retrospective cross-sectional study identified significant gaps in emergency preparedness across 460 health facilities, highlighting limited capacity to manage critical and life-threatening situations effectively [5].

Recent literature emphasises that early exposure to EM during undergraduate education is critical for preparing doctors to manage life-threatening emergencies. Consequently, there have been ongoing efforts focused on integrating EM modules into the early stages of medical education to improve both in-hospital and out-of-hospital outcomes [3,11,12].

In 2021, Dike and colleagues [3] proposed a model for integrating EM into the training pathway of Ghanaian doctors, encompassing undergraduate education, housemanship (intern) rotations and continuing professional development programmes. International experience further demonstrates that structured undergraduate EM curricula can improve student confidence, clinical competence and interest in the speciality [13,14]. For example, in Botswana, a prolonged undergraduate EM rotation incorporating problem-based learning and clinical exposure was associated with positive student feedback and increased engagement with emergency medicine [15].

Given the limited number of emergency physicians in the country, the most efficient way to bridge the gap in the national emergency response system is to embed the necessary skills at the undergraduate level, so that every doctor in Ghana, wherever they are, can manage emergency patients. Therefore, the primary aim of this paper is to propose a competency-based model for undergraduate EM training in Ghanaian medical schools, aligned with international standards and adapted to the specific needs of the local health system.

The Proposed Model

The authors propose a pragmatic, context-sensitive model for integrating EM into undergraduate curricula—one that is grounded in global standards yet tailored to local realities.

At its core, this model is anchored by clearly defined competencies. Rather than reinventing the wheel, medical schools can draw on established international benchmarks, such as the International Federation for Emergency Medicine (IFEM)'s undergraduate curriculum. This provides a solid foundation of essential knowledge and skills that every graduating doctor should possess, regardless of where they train.

However, adopting global standards without adaptation risks producing a curriculum that is either impractical or irrelevant. Therefore, the next critical step is contextualisation. EM training must reflect the local disease burden, which in many African settings includes a mix of trauma, infectious diseases, obstetric emergencies and increasingly, non-communicable diseases. It also considers the realities of available resources—both equipment and faculty expertise.

Equally essential is stakeholder engagement. Any proposed model needs to be shaped by those actively involved in training and service delivery. Senior emergency physicians, frontline clinicians and educators in major teaching hospitals bring invaluable insights into what skills are truly needed at the bedside. Their input ensures that the curriculum is not only academically sound but also practically relevant and aligned with national training goals.

This proposed model is about prioritising the skills that save lives in the first critical hours of illness and injury. By combining global best practices with local expertise and needs, medical schools can produce graduates who are better equipped, more confident and more responsive to emergencies from day one.

The model recommends a 3- to 6-week rotation in EM during the first and final clinical years (4th and 6th years) of medical school. It is proposed that the rotation be coordinated by an EM consultant, with support from EM specialists and residents, given the limited number of consultants. The goal of the model is to introduce students to the core principles of emergency care, adapted to the Ghanaian healthcare context. It emphasises early recognition, resuscitation, stabilisation, and the appropriate referral of critically ill and injured patients. It highlights the proper use of resources and bedside diagnostic tools, such as point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), which is an essential skill for Ghanaian clinicians. Under a competency-based model, the learning objectives are designed around patient care, medical knowledge, procedural skills, systems-based practice, professionalism, communication, and practice-based learning.

The course content centres on foundational emergency care; trauma, medical and paediatric emergencies; procedural and ultrasound-guided skills; resource stewardship; and disaster preparedness. Proposed teaching methods include bedside instruction, simulation training, problem- and team-based learning, and supervised emergency department shifts for real-world exposure.

We propose a student assessment model that combines formative tools (such as direct observation, simulation feedback and reflective logbooks) with summative evaluations, including objective structured clinical exams (OSCEs), written examinations and case presentations. This will give a better judgment of the students’ readiness for real-world scenarios. Continuous course evaluation relies on student feedback, faculty review and periodic updates to align with Ghanaian medical school standards.

Discussion

In Ghanaian medical schools, undergraduate students typically gain exposure to the emergency department during their clinical years (4th – 6th years). However, this exposure is fragmented across several major rotations, including medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynaecology, paediatrics, family medicine, and public health [16]. EM is often embedded within these rotations rather than delivered as a distinct discipline. In this regard, Dike and colleagues [3] further asserted that clinical clerkships in medical schools should formally include EM as a stand-alone module and rotation, with the curriculum taught through both didactic instruction and structured clinical exposure.

In alignment with this position, the proposed model emphasises increased bedside teaching and supervised clinical care. This includes structured, simulation-based learning and skills training delivered by competent emergency physicians and trained clinical preceptors. The model further emphasises simulation and team-based training, as well as resource stewardship, which are particularly relevant in resource-limited settings where opportunities for supervised clinical exposure may be inconsistent or limited [5,9].

Equipping graduating doctors with core EM competencies can reduce care variability at lower-level facilities and improve referral pathways across all three tiers of Ghana's healthcare system [9]. The introduction of EM training in the first clinical year provides a foundational knowledge base which is reinforced in the final year. This longitudinal exposure ensures that by the time students reach housemanship, they have developed the confidence to manage acutely ill and injured patients through timely, life-saving interventions.

Importantly, the proposed model is adapted to the curricula outlined by the African Federation of Emergency Medicine and the International Federation of Emergency Medicine, which together prioritise the effective management of common and recurring emergencies of both global and local relevance.

The primary hurdle in implementing the proposed model is the limited number of emergency physicians. A way to mitigate this is to allow senior residents in emergency medicine to serve as the primary bedside clinical trainers, with consultants providing supervision. This approach also builds residents' teaching competence. Additionally, simulation-based teaching provides the opportunity to develop necessary skills in a controlled environment with minimal human resources.

An integrated approach serves as a practical entry point, allowing medical schools to phase in the model incrementally rather than attempting an immediate, full-scale rollout. This may be offered within the established rotation blocks of other specialities (i.e., general surgery and internal medicine) in the beginning. As the number of faculty members increases, more parts of the model can be implemented. Launching at a flagship medical school also provides the opportunity to refine the model’s logistics and mentorship models before expansion into a standardised national programme.

The proposed model currently serves as a conceptual blueprint, with its effectiveness yet to be formally evaluated in Ghanaian medical schools. Validating this model through pilot implementation is the essential next step toward producing an evidence-based curriculum.

Other broad factors, such as resource constraints, including access to simulation facilities and ultrasound equipment, pose a challenge to nationwide implementation. However, through strategic interagency collaboration and a unified professional will, these barriers can be overcome, establishing a robust and sustainable undergraduate emergency medicine programme.

Conclusion

Overall, integrating EM into the undergraduate medical education curriculum represents a transformative step for medical education in Ghana. This model not only teaches skills but also fosters a culture of preparedness and paves the way for future growth in the speciality. Tailored to local needs and built on global standards, its implementation is essential to closing the gap in acute care and ensuring a stronger, more capable healthcare delivery system nationwide.
