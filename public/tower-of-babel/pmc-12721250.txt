Palliative care in advanced liver disease
Authors: Christopher Cussen, Amy Watret
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Patients with advanced liver disease have a high symptom burden compared to patients with other life limiting conditions.•Palliative care is an essential component of good quality care in chronic liver disease but is frequently not instigated until the last days of life.•Palliative care can reduce inpatient hospital admissions and associated healthcare costs for patients with CLD.•Discussions with patients and carers around palliative care should be instigated at an early stage and involve a multi-disciplinary approach.Keywords: Palliative care, Hepatology, Chronic liver disease, Ascites, Hepatic encephalopathy

Introduction

In contrast with other major disease groups, mortality from liver disease in the UK has been rising in recent decades, largely driven by increasing obesity and excessive alcohol consumption.1 Median age of death from liver disease is significantly lower than other major causes of mortality. Liver disease often presents at an advanced stage when patients may not be suitable for liver transplant (LT), with the majority of remaining therapeutic options being palliative. Even among those listed for transplant, annually 14% die or are removed from the list while waiting for an available organ.2

Decompensated chronic liver disease is a common presentation on the acute medical take. Decompensating events such as variceal haemorrhage, ascites and hepatic encephalopathy (HE) increase short, medium and long-term mortality. Despite recognition of this, factors such as immediate reversibility, patient age, perceived complexity of liver disease and uncertainty over disease trajectory mean that palliative care is often an overlooked aspect of the management of patients with decompensated liver disease.

Patients with advanced liver disease often die in hospital. This might be unavoidable and may be the patient’s preference, but there is evidence that care focused on morbidity and avoidance of hospital admissions is preferred by patients.3,4 It should, though, be recognised that high-quality care and comfort may be a greater priority than place of death. Involvement of palliative care teams and use of multidisciplinary teams (MDT) can reduce hospital admissions and increase the proportion of patients with end-stage liver disease dying at home.

This CME article offers an overview of the symptom burden in advanced liver disease and the role of palliative care in this patient group. After reading, one should be able to identify patients who would benefit from specialist palliative care input and describe interventions for common liver-related symptoms.

Symptom control

Patients with decompensated cirrhosis have a higher symptom burden than patients with other life-limiting conditions.5 Symptoms may be direct manifestations of liver disease, but patients also experience a high burden of other physical symptoms (Table 1).6 Additionally, the social, financial, spiritual and psychological impact of liver disease on patients and their families is high, requiring a holistic approach to patient care. Manifestations of decompensated liver disease include ascites, hepatic encephalopathy and variceal haemorrhage. These may have a clear and sometimes treatable cause such as constipation, infection, portal vein thrombus, hepatocellular carcinoma or ongoing alcohol use, but often there is no identifiable precipitant and the decompensation may represent progression of the underlying disease. The development of these complications is often an indicator of increased short- to medium-term mortality in itself and should prompt discussions with patients and carers around palliative care.

Factors such as nutrition are an integral part of care: sarcopenia is common in patients with chronic liver disease (CLD), increasing the risk of frailty and associated poor outcomes. Alongside management of HE and ascites, improved nutrition has the greatest positive impact on quality of life (QOL)7 and may reduce symptoms in covert HE. Involvement of dieticians at an early stage is an important part of care for these patients.

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE)

HE is a common a neurocognitive manifestation of CLD, associated with increased 12-month mortality and a significant impact on health-related quality of life (HRQOL). The leading mechanism is accumulation of neurotoxins, principally ammonia, due to liver dysfunction or portosystemic shunting. Treatment of HE with lactulose and/or the non-absorbed oral antibiotic rifaximin significantly improves HRQOL.8,9 These agents control the population of ammonia-producing bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract. Adjustments of lactulose dose made by patients or carers in response to symptoms might aid reduced hospital admissions and improve QOL. Symptoms of HE include mood and sleep disturbance and impaired cognition, which may be subtle and best identified by patients and carers. Despite this, patients and carers are often unaware of the potential for HE until it presents.10 Additionally, impaired cognition might further complicate already difficult discussions around palliative care, highlighting the importance of advance care planning in patients with CLD.

Ascites

Ascites is a common presentation of advancing liver disease, often associated with impaired renal or hepatic function. As liver disease advances, the ascites will become less sensitive to diuretics, at which point other treatment options are needed: consideration of LT, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPSS) or regular paracentesis. All treatments for refractory ascites short of LT are palliative. TIPSS may not be suitable due to heart failure, HE or anatomical limitations. Where regular paracentesis is used, it should be a planned procedure undertaken by a suitably trained healthcare professional. Regular, planned paracentesis in the last 12 months of life results in fewer days in hospital as an inpatient and a lower probability of death in hospital compared to unplanned care.11 Alternative methods have been explored to control ascites. The REDUCe feasibility study demonstrated patient acceptability and safety of indwelling peritoneal catheters for managing refractory ascites in a palliative setting and suggested cost savings vs regular large-volume paracentesis.12 A larger trial, REDUCe2, is currently being conducted to assess the effectiveness of indwelling drain vs large-volume paracentesis in improving HRQOL. The surgically implanted automated low-flow ascites (ALFA) pump diverts fluid from the peritoneal cavity to the bladder. It has likewise been reported as effective in controlling ascites and improving QOL,13 though its availability is limited to specialist centres.

Pain

Pain is frequently a troubling symptom, reported by 30–79% of patients with advanced liver disease.6 Concerns around metabolism of drugs and side effects including worsening of HE may lead to inadequate analgesia.14 Particular apprehension exists around the use of paracetamol and opiates. While lower doses are advisable in patients with advanced liver disease (eg oral paracetamol 2–3 g/24 h), both can be safely used in patients with Child–Pugh C cirrhosis. In the last days of life, a ‘start low’ approach for dosing (eg subcutaneous morphine 2.5 mg hourly) is appropriate for managing pain. Drug choice should take account of renal function, with fentanyl replacing morphine for patients with reduced eGFR. Guidance has been produced by the BASL Palliative Hepatology special interest group regarding use of medications to manage symptoms in the last days of life and common symptoms in advanced liver disease to support prescribing clinicians.15

Who should be referred for palliative care?

The principal aims of palliative care are symptom control and improvement in QOL. There is an increasing move toward identifying patients with liver disease who might benefit from early palliative care. The 2022 American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases guidance recommends that palliative care can be provided to patients with decompensated cirrhosis at any stage of their illness and without precluding delivery of disease-directed or curative treatments.6 End-stage liver disease presents significant challenges in palliative care, including identifying patients who would benefit most from intervention. In addition to specific manifestations of decompensated cirrhosis, patients with liver disease experience a higher burden of physical symptoms and greater deterioration in QOL scores compared to patients with other life-limiting conditions. Disease trajectory in CLD can be difficult to predict, but decompensating events are associated with decreased 12–24-month survival.16 Factors to consider include poor or declining performance status, unplanned admission with hepatic decompensation in the previous 12 months, high prognostic scores (eg UKELD), refractory ascites, refractory or recurrent encephalopathy, progressive renal impairment, progressive HCC, and continued alcohol consumption following an episode of alcohol-related hepatitis. Tools have been developed to aid identification of those most at risk of dying within the next 12 months (and consequently in greatest need of specialist palliative care), notably the Bristol Screening Tool17 (see Box 1) and the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool (SPICT)18 (see Table 2). Considering the question ‘would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?’ can be a useful prompt to begin palliative care discussions.

The multidisciplinary team

A multidisciplinary approach is often required to identify and manage a patient’s care needs in the terminal stage of liver disease. Where such formalised services exist, the make-up of the MDT and the approach taken in identification of patients and care planning may vary between sites. Generally, all include a core consisting of consultants in hepatology and palliative medicine and an alcohol support worker. Other roles represented may include social workers, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, dieticians, community hospice workers and community liver disease nurses. Patients identified as having advanced or high-risk disease are discussed and subsequently a holistic assessment of care needs and advanced care planning can take place. This affords patients the opportunity to express their priorities for care, such as place of death and management of decompensating events. Active identification and assessment of patients using these models have been shown to reduce in-hospital deaths.19

Palliative care for liver disease patients may range from symptom control in patients with advanced disease who are suitable for LT to those receiving care in the last days of life. Initiation of frank discussions with patients and carers around disease trajectory and advanced care planning at an early stage can help to avoid unnecessary hospital admissions.

The importance of palliative care as a key part of care is highlighted by the imbalance in the experience of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and those with decompensated cirrhosis alone. A 2018 study showed that those with HCC were more likely to receive palliative care despite similar symptomology. This was associated with fewer inpatient bed days, lower probability of in-hospital death and lower healthcare-associated costs in those with HCC.11

Conclusion

In contrast with other major disease groups, liver disease mortality continues to rise. This trend is set to continue, with rising rates of metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease and HCC. Liver disease is frequently silent until advanced stages, where complications develop and symptom burden is often very high. With the exception of LT, the treatments used to manage decompensated chronic liver disease are palliative. Early acknowledgement of this in discussions with patients and carers can enable advance care planning around the management of decompensating events. Involvement of the MDT in palliative care, both in hospital and in the community, can reduce symptom burden in the last months of life, reduce inpatient hospital stays and decrease the likelihood of death in hospital.
