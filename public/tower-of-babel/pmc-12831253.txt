From decline to rebound: a 25-year Joinpoint analysis of tuberculosis mortality in the United States, 1999–2023
Authors: Yuanyuan Xiao, Tao Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFollowing decades of consistent decline, tuberculosis (TB) mortality in the United States has exhibited indicators of stagnation since 2014. National-level summaries, however, conceal variability within demographic and geographic subgroups. Our objective was to quantify current trends and identify the demographics responsible for the deceleration.MethodsUtilizing the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) mortality data (1999–2023), we computed age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) for all fatalities with underlying ICD-10 codes A16–A19. Joinpoint regression was utilized to calculate annual percent changes (APC) and average annual percent changes (AAPC) overall and stratified by sex, age, race/ethnicity, census area, and urbanization.ResultsTuberculosis-related fatalities decreased from 911 in 1999 to 566 in 2023, resulting in an overall annual average percentage reduction of −3.36% (95% CI −4.55% to −2.15%). In 2014, the national trend flipped; the APC transitioned from −5.86% (1999–2014) to +1.64% (2014–2023). Males encountered a more pronounced deceleration (APC −0.75% post-2011) compared to females. Adults aged 45–54 years experienced a fall of 5.01% per annum until 2017, followed by an increase of 3.71% per annum until 2023. Comparable reversals transpired in the 65–74 age cohorts, while individuals aged 85 and above had a consistent linear fall (APC −3.91%). The South and Northeast demonstrated the most pronounced reversals post-2015, with annual percentage changes of +1.98% and +3.03%, respectively. Metropolitan counties had a resurgence post-2015, with an increase of 2.69%, while non-metropolitan regions stabilized. Non-Hispanic Black adults experienced the most significant early drop (APC −9.12%, 1999–2013) and Non-Hispanic Asian adults continued to represent the largest burden group in 2023.ConclusionsU.S. tuberculosis mortality has shifted from a rapid drop to stagnation or resurgence, particularly evident among middle-aged and older adults, males, urban inhabitants, and those in Southern and Northeastern states. Urgent, equity-centered actions are essential to avert further recurrence and expedite elimination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12342-y.

Introduction

TB is a persistent infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily spread through airborne droplets. It continues to be a major global public health concern and is among the foremost causes of mortality from infectious diseases globally [1]. In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated 10.6 million new cases of TB. Approximately 1.3 million fatalities were ascribed to tuberculosis, encompassing 167,000 instances of TB/HIV co-infection [2]. Recent statistics indicate a rise in tuberculosis incidence, with an additional 1.1 million cases documented between 2021 and 2023 [3]. The progression of socio-economic development and urbanization has significantly enhanced population mobility, which has affected tuberculosis transmission dynamics, alongside persistent risk factors such as HIV infection, diabetes, smoking, and malnutrition [4, 5]. These factors collectively enhance the complexity of tuberculosis prevention [6]. This is analogous in the United States. The United States, characterized by a substantial population, hosts numerous immigrants [7], diverse racial groups [8] and a significant homeless population [9]. Since 2020, the incidence and prevalence of tuberculosis have risen annually [10]. In 2023, a provisional total of 9615 tuberculosis cases was reported by the 50 U.S. states and the District of Columbia, indicating an increase of 1295 cases (16%) compared to 2022 [11].

Previous U.S. analyses have been constrained to brief durations, have not employed age-standardized metrics, or have lacked the detail necessary to investigate concurrent changes by sex, age, race/ethnicity, location, and urbanicity [12]. As a result, the demographic and geographic factors contributing to the observed deceleration are inadequately defined.

We performed a thorough evaluation of tuberculosis-related fatalities and AAMR utilizing nationally representative mortality data from 1999 to 2023 across all important population strata. We utilized segmented regression to discern inflection points and measure the extent and timing of alterations in mortality patterns.

Methods

This retrospective original research study utilized the CDC WONDER database [13]. Analyses cover 1999–2023, using CDC WONDER’s 1999–2020 (final) and 2018–2023 (provisional, single-race) datasets. Overlapping years 2018–2020 were drawn from the 1999–2020 dataset to maintain consistency. Data were obtained on August 13, 2025, from the CDC webpage (https://wonder.cdc.gov/ucd-icd10.html) on TB. The CDC WONDER database has been utilized to examine mortality across many disease conditions [14, 15]. Informed consent and institutional review board permission were unnecessary, as the CDC WONDER database comprises solely de-identified publicly accessible data [15]. This research employs an ecological analysis to analyze all patients who succumbed to tuberculosis from 1999 to 2023.

We primarily analyzed the trends in total death rates between urban and rural populations in the United States from 1999 to 2020. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code for tuberculosis, “A16–A19,” according to the ICD-10, was employed as the search phrase for data extraction from the CDC WONDER website. The data search was conducted independently for various categories, including year of death, gender, race, age group, and region. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). Urban-rural classification was based on the 2013 NCHS Urban-Rural Classification Scheme for Counties [16]. The term urban refers to metropolitan areas categorized into four types: (i) large central metropolitan, (ii) large fringe metropolitan, (iii) medium metropolitan, and (iv) small metropolitan. The term rural refers to non-metropolitan regions encompassing micropolitan and non-core areas throughout several counties in the United States. The four U.S. Census Bureau regions were defined according to standard federal classifications: Northeast (CT, ME, MA, NH, RI, VT, NJ, NY, PA), Midwest (IL, IN, IA, KS, MI, MN, MO, NE, ND, OH, SD, WI), South (AL, AR, DE, DC, FL, GA, KY, LA, MD, MS, NC, OK, SC, TN, TX, VA, WV), and West (AK, AZ, CA, CO, HI, ID, MT, NV, NM, OR, UT, WA, WY) [17]. Age categories were delineated as follows: 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85 years and older. In the analysis, the racial/ethnic groups considered were non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black/African American, NH Asian/Pacific Islander, and Hispanic/Latino. Data for the NH American Indian/Alaska Native group were deemed unreliable within the study’s parameters and thus excluded [18].

AAMR were calculated using annual U.S. Census Bureau population estimates as the denominator and standardized to the 2000 U.S. standard population [17]. All mortality rates are expressed as deaths per 100,000 population. We employed the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.4.0, National Cancer Institute) to construct segmented regression models, ascertain inflection points in annual variations, and calculate the APC along with its 95% confidence interval (CI); the overall AAPC for the period 1999–2023. For all analyses except age-stratified models, AAMR served as the dependent variable in Joinpoint regression. Age-stratified analyses used age-specific crude rates. State-level segmented trend analysis was limited to states with sufficient data quality (≥10 TB deaths annually and no data suppression) across the study period to ensure stable estimates. Only four states met these inclusion criteria: California, Florida, New York, and Texas. For these states, Joinpoint regression was performed to assess state-specific trends. Statistical significance was established at a two-sided α = 0.05.

Gender

In 1999, the AAMR for males was approximately double that of females: 0.79 per 100,000 population (95% CI 0.72–0.86) compared to 0.33 (0.29–0.37), which reduced to 0.29 (0.26–0.32) for males and 0.14 (0.12–0.16) for females in 2023. Deaths declined by 37.9% in men and 37.8% in women, with women having a marginally more rapid annual decline (AAPC −4.12% vs. −3.62%), though this difference was not statistically significant given overlapping confidence intervals.

Census region

All four Census regions demonstrated significant declines in TB mortality, with notable heterogeneity. The South, bearing the highest initial burden (AAMR 0.63 per 100,000 population), achieved the largest absolute and relative reduction (−50.0%; AAPC −3.86%). The Midwest exhibited the second steepest annual decline (AAPC −3.84%), while the West and Northeast showed more moderate decreases (AAPC −3.23% and −2.44%, respectively), though all trends remained statistically significant.

Race/Ethnicity

From 1999 to 2023, age-adjusted tuberculosis mortality rates declined across all analyzed racial/ethnic groups, albeit with marked heterogeneity. NH Black individuals experienced the steepest reduction, with AAMR decreasing from 1.44 to 0.29 per 100,000 (AAPC −6.42%; 95% CI: −7.57 to −5.27). Hispanic and NH White populations showed similarly substantial declines (AAMR: 1.20 to 0.33 and 0.28 to 0.08 per 100,000, respectively; AAPC −4.56% and −4.10%). NH Asian or Pacific Islander individuals, despite having the highest initial burden (AAMR 2.39 in 1999), demonstrated a more modest decline to 1.06 per 100,000 (AAPC −2.45%; 95% CI: −3.74 to −1.14). Consequently, while absolute mortality decreased universally, disparities persisted.

Urbanization

Both metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas achieved significant declines, though patterns differed slightly. Non-metropolitan areas demonstrated a more pronounced deaths reduction (43.6% vs. 36.1%) and faster annual decline (AAPC −4.07% vs. −3.74%). However, by 2023, AAMRs had converged (0.23 vs. 0.22 per 100,000) with substantially overlapping 95% confidence intervals (0.21–0.25 vs. 0.17–0.27), indicating no statistically significant difference.

Age groups

TB mortality demonstrated a clear age gradient, with rates escalating substantially with advancing age.The 85+ years cohort exhibited the highest absolute burden, decreasing from 4.16 to 1.34 per 100,000 (a 68% reduction), while the youngest group (35–44 years) experienced the most pronounced relative decline in deaths (−54.39%) Despite varying magnitudes, all age groups demonstrated consistent downward trends with statistically significant AAPCs ranging from −2.80% (55–64 years) to −3.95% (75–84 years).

Segmented trend analyses

Nationally, the AAMR for TB declined significantly from 1999 to 2014, exhibiting an APC of −5.86% (95% CI −6.73% to −4.98%). Beginning in 2014, the trend reversed; the APC increased to +1.64% (95% CI −0.68% to +4.01%) throughout the period of 2014–2023, signifying a shift from rapid decline to stagnation and possible recovery. At the state level (Supplementary Figure 1), California’s APC was −2.85% (95% CI −3.52% to −2.17%) and New York’s APC was −3.02% (95% CI −4.05% to −1.97%), reflecting similar magnitudes of decrease. Florida showed the steepest decline, with an APC of −4.92% (95% CI −6.12% to −3.71%), whereas Texas exhibited an APC of −4.64% (95% CI −5.32% to −4.00%).

Gender-stratified AAMR associated with TB

Joinpoint regression revealed distinct temporal patterns by sex (Fig. 1). For males, mortality declined substantially from 1999 to 2011 (APC −6.40%, 95% CI −7.62% to −5.17%), followed by a slower decline from 2011 to 2023 (APC −0.75%, 95% CI −2.20% to 0.73%). In contrast, females showed an earlier inflection point: mortality decreased steeply from 1999 to 2007 (APC −8.23%, 95% CI −11.32% to −5.04%), then plateaued with a more gradual decline from 2007 to 2023 (APC −2.00%, 95% CI −3.38% to −0.59%). From 1999 to 2017, mortality among individuals aged 45–54 years declined consistently at a rate of −5.01% per annum (95% CI −6.16% to −3.85%). This trend reversed from 2017 to 2023, resulting in an annual increase of +3.71% (95% CI −3.86% to +11.87%). The 55–64-year group experienced a sharp decline of −7.57% annually (95% CI −11.26% to −3.73%) until 2007, followed by a plateau (−0.32% per year, 95% CI −1.76% to +1.14%). TB-associated AAMR categorized by race/ethnicity

From 1999 to 2023, tuberculosis mortality trends varied substantially across racial/ethnic groups (Fig. 4). Non-Hispanic Black individuals demonstrated the steepest early reduction (APC −9.12% from 1999 to 2013), though this decelerated to a non-significant −2.52% (95% CI −5.06% to 0.09%) after 2013. Non-Hispanic Whites exhibited a two-stage deceleration, with APCs shifting from −7.05% (1999–2008) to −2.28% (2008–2023). Hispanic populations showed a sustained, significant decrease of −4.56% across the entire period. Notably, Asian or Pacific Islander populations displayed the most concerning reversal, with mortality falling at −4.31% annually (1999–2016) but increasing by a non-significant +2.23% (95% CI −1.62% to +6.08) since 2016. Discussion

Our 25-year research indicates that the United States has shifted from significant decreases in TB mortality to a general plateau or partial resurgence from around 2015. These findings align with previous worldwide data indicating a deceleration in TB control within low-incidence countries: the WHO stated that the annual reduction in TB fatalities in the WHO Region of the Americas diminished from 8% between 2000 and 2015 to merely 2% from 2015 to 2022 [2]. This study provides specificity by demonstrating that the stagnation is not uniform, but rather influenced by particular sex, age, racial/ethnic, and geographic sub-populations.

Demographic dynamics of sex and age

Throughout the study period (1999–2023), male AAMR remained approximately two-fold higher than female rates. Consistent with extensive literature identifying male sex as a risk factor for elevated TB incidence and mortality, our findings confirm the persistence of this disparity [18, 19]. Alcohol consumption and smoking—established risk factors for TB—are more prevalent among men than women [20]. Furthermore,.the crude mortality rate of TB increases with age, possibly because immune aging and increased complications among the elderly in the United States reduce the efficacy of conventional control strategies [21].

Geographic and urban-rural heterogeneity

Regional heterogeneity in TB-related mortality was observed, with the West and South exhibiting higher AAMRs—a pattern potentially attributable to socioeconomic disparities [22], a well-documented risk factor for TB burden [23]. Tuberculosis spreads via airborne droplets, with transmission risk amplified by population density and close contact [24]. Crowded living conditions substantially increase TB infection and disease risk, exemplified by high incidence rates among persons experiencing homelessness and incarceration [25, 26].

Racial and ethnic discrepancies

In the racial dimension, tuberculosis mortality exhibits significant heterogeneity yet an overall improving trend [27]. The Non-Hispanic Black population demonstrates the most pronounced decline, with deaths and AAMR decreasing by 63.64% and 79.9% respectively (from 1.44 in 1999 to 0.29 per 100,000 in 2023), and AAPC of −6.42%, far surpassing other racial groups—likely reflecting intensive public health interventions, and enhanced tuberculosis control programs [28]. However, this progress is nonlinear: the deceleration after 2013 signals the need for sustained investment to consolidate gains. Conversely, although Asian Americans experienced a 55.6% decline in AAMR, they represent the only racial group with increased deaths (56.7% increase) and showed an inflection point with a 2.23% annual increase from 2016 to 2023, potentially associated with shifting immigrant demographics, latent tuberculosis infection reactivation [29], or diagnostic and treatment delays due to cultural and language barriers; with a baseline AAMR of 2.39 in 1999 that was already the highest, their current absolute value of 1.06 remains higher than Whites (0.08) and Hispanics (0.33). Racial/ethnic minority populations exhibit higher prevalence of comorbidities [30]—including HIV, uncontrolled diabetes [31], and smoking [32]—that accelerate TB progression.This enduring imbalance reflects Asabor’s findings that institutional racism increased mortality rates among minority communities [8]. Hispanics, despite a significant decline (AAPC −4.56), continue to bear a considerable burden due to initially high mortality (1.20) and population growth. Accordingly, future tuberculosis elimination strategies should adopt race-differentiated approaches—to achieve health equity across all populations.

Advantages and disadvantages

Strengths comprise nationally representative mortality datasets, age standardization, and segmented regression for identifying inflection points. Limitations are threefold. First, this ecological study could not account for individual-level risk factors (e.g., US-born status, HIV co-infection, alcohol use, diabetes mellitus) [5, 33] that may have changed differentially over time and across urban/rural strata, potentially confounding observed trends. Consequently, individual-level causal inference is precluded; future cohort studies are needed to elucidate pathways. Second, CDC WONDER provides only aggregated ICD-10 codes A16–A19, excluding approximately 10% of deaths coded as A15 (respiratory TB confirmed by laboratory). This systematic underestimation may bias trends, particularly if coding practices changed differentially over time or across racial/ethnic groups. Third, death certificate misclassification further underestimates TB mortality burden [34–38]. A validation study found that TB was listed as an underlying or contributing cause on death certificates for only 34% of persons who died with the disease [39].

Conclusion

Following two decades of significant drop, U.S. tuberculosis mortality has reached a plateau-rebound phase since 2014, predominantly affecting males, middle-aged and older adults, inhabitants of the South and Northeast, and individuals in metropolitan counties. Ongoing, focused surveillance and enhanced prevention strategies in these high-risk groups are crucial to avert further resurgence and to progress towards the elimination of tuberculosis.
