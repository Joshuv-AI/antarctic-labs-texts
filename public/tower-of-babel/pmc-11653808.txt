Feedback: “overly nice” or a “roasting culture”? a student partnership approach in exploring a feedback literacy strategy in undergraduate pharmacy and medical education
Authors: Thomas Kearney, Anthony Maher, Anne McSherry, Judith Strawbridge, Muirne Spooner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Research emphasises the need for mutual understanding of feedback purpose, i.e. staff and student feedback literacy. Interprofessional practice is crucial to effective patient care. While undergraduates increasingly learn and are assessed together, less is known of their shared or differing experiences of feedback. This study aimed to explore how students and faculty in medicine and pharmacy perceive an evidence-informed draft feedback strategy.Methods A student partnership approach was taken to this cross-faculty study. Focus groups of faculty from the Schools of Medicine (SOM) and Pharmacy (SOP) were undertaken by a faculty researcher. A SOP student facilitated student focus groups across both schools. Data was thematically analysed using template analysis by staff and students from both schools.Results Three over-arching themes are described: feedback goals, environment and design. Heterogeneous conceptualisations make it difficult for faculty to signpost and students to recognise feedback. Feedback goals and operationalisation contrasted in junior and senior years. Junior students were frustrated by “overly nice”, generic feedback; senior students baulked at a “roasting culture”, more marked for medical students.Conclusion Despite an expanse of research-informed theory, processes in practice are unsupportive of feedback literacy. Even within programmes, context differs and must be considered when embedding a positive whole-programme feedback culture.Clinical Trial number Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06332-y.

Introduction

Effective feedback is a fundamental part of education to facilitate student learning and professional development [1]. While valued by faculty and students alike, it remains challenging to do sufficiently and to do well. Faculty can struggle to deliver feedback [2] and learners can struggle to use it [3, 4]. The effect of feedback on learning is not always positive, with one-third of feedback events having an adverse effect on student performance [5].

In recent years, feedback has been re-framed from rater-centred transmission to learner-centred collaborative conversations [6–8]. Given that evidence champions feedback as an “educational alliance” between learner and teacher, it is important that both have shared meanings of what feedback is and how it should be operationalised [7]. A BEME systematic review revealed heterogeneous interpretation of feedback between students and faculty [9]. Even when students and faculty agree that feedback should have a constructive focus, they differ on how this happens in practice [10].

Modern understanding of feedback indicates that learners’ experiences are highly contextualised [11]. Interprofessional learning is emphasised in modern curricula to support collaboration in the workplace [12]. Feedback is an intrinsic element of outcome-based curricula shared across health professions. While there is a large body of evidence in medical education on feedback literacy, there is less in pharmacy education [13]. Feedback literacy is defined by Carless and Boud [14] as ‘understandings, capacities, and dispositions needed to make sense of information and use it to enhance work or learning strategies’. Given these students will eventually work together, research to better understand how their undergraduate experiences of supervisory feedback has the potential to inform effective interprofessional feedback in the workplace. Our aim was to explore how students and faculty in medicine and pharmacy perceive an evidence-informed feedback strategy.

The objectives were to collect and analyse data from faculty and students of both schools, across all years of the programmes, using feedback literacy to inform the draft feedback strategy and to serve as the theoretical framework for analysing the data.

Our research questions were:

How do students and faculty in a School of Medicine and School of Pharmacy perceive a feedback strategy? How do differing experiences of feedback differ across these groups potentially influence their perceptions of the strategy?

Methodology

Study design and methodological orientation

Given that recent literature emphasises feedback as a social act, we approached this study with a relativist ontology and social constructivism, where participants create their meaning of feedback influenced by their circumstances and context. We employed qualitative methods to explore these questions via separate focus groups with medicine faculty, medicine students, pharmacy faculty and pharmacy students. A partnership approach was undertaken, whereby faculty from medicine and pharmacy collaborated with students in the design of the study, facilitating the focus groups, analysing the results, interpreting the findings and co-designing the recommendations. An exploratory qualitative strategy was used in applying template analysis to systematically interrogate the data. The study was reported in accordance with the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) (Appendix B)[15].

Theoretical framework

There are many existing feedback models, which the researchers have previously discussed in a systematic review (Ref available in non-anonymised version). Ramaprasad’s conceptualisation of “narrowing the gap” between actual and desired performance was valuable in emphasising the role for transparent performance standards, shared goals and vision. The RC2C model is a holistic model focussing on relationship-building and coaching adapted widely in postgraduate contexts that is used primarily in appraisal meetings, and more recently, in in-the-moment feedback [16]. As this study, concerned undergraduates exposed to a wide number of teachers rather than designated trainers, and encountering feedback in a broader range of formats (written, audio, group as well as individual face-to-face), we chose to apply a model with more universal application. We used the concept of feedback literacy as our theoretical framework Carless and Boud propose four main characteristics. Appreciating feedback means recognising it and its value: both feedback of knowing which informs how they are currently performing, and feedback for knowing which advises on what to do next [17]. Making judgements refers to the ability to evaluate their own and others’ work. Managing affect refers to acknowledging emotional reactions, which occur during feedback, and the learner’s strategies in responding. Taking action emphasises the learner’s role in engaging with and enacting feedback. These concepts relate to the learner’s capacity to make the most of feedback. The concept of teacher feedback literacy is fairly recently emergent[18], despite for some time, a conceptualisation of effective feedback requiring sharing of responsibility. The role of the feedback literate teacher has been proposed as focussing primarily on creating and managing assessment environments to help students practice and optimise their feedback literacy skills[18]. Therefore, this framework was considered most useful as it is learner-centred, but emphasises the shared work in achieving the conditions to support learners in appreciating feedback, making judgments, managing affect and taking action.

Participants and setting

The recruitment population included academic faculty and undergraduate students from each of the Schools of Medicine and Pharmacy in RCSI (Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland). The students in the medical programme in Ireland enter via direct or graduate entry. Feedback in the early years occurs at group activities such as tutorials (classroom-based) and in lectures, e.g. review of model answers, rubrics and discussion of common pitfalls/poorly answered questions with errors from submitted assignments. Each student is assigned a personal tutor with whom they can discuss their progress at two mandatory meetings per year, and at additional meetings on request. Feedback in the clinical environment (years 3–5 for Medicine, years 4–5 for Pharmacy) is primarily verbal. Mandatory written end-of-rotation appraisals are available to the student for their portfolio, but not provided to external bodies, e.g., intern or residency programmes. Students also have mandatory meetings with a staff member several times per year for progress reviews.

The students in the pharmacy programme enter via direct entry, the majority entering from second level and a minority of mature and graduate students. Feedback in the early years mirrors described for medical students, above. Individual written feedback is provided for some assignments and after the Year 2 placement. Individual verbal feedback occurs during practical classes and at formative Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE). Feedback in the senior years (years 4–5) is mainly verbal, with teaching facilitators providing a formal opportunity for bidirectional feedback (students both receive feedback on their performance and have the opportunity to provide feedback to their facilitators) at formative and summative competency assessment on placement. Each student is assigned a personal tutor at the beginning of the programme with whom they can discuss their progress.

Participant Information Leaflets (PILs) and subsequent recruitment were sent via email through a gatekeeper to faculty (JS) involved in teaching and feedback and to undergraduate students by the student researcher (AM). Faculty who were not involved in teaching and feedback were excluded from the initial email communication, students who were on leave of absence or supplemental programmes only were excluded from the initial communication. There were approximately 300 students in each of the medical classes and 50 students in each of the pharmacy classes contacted. Convenience sampling of those who responded and were available to participate within the desired timeframe occurred, while also ensuring an even gender balance. Focus groups were undertaken online in April 2022. A draft feedback strategy (Appendix 3) was provided to focus group members. This prompt was used to explore focus group members’ perceptions of feedback in terms of definition, the strategy and their experiences of feedback as faculty or students within the institution.

Recruitment and data collection

Participants were introduced to the study via a briefing session. The interview guide was created by MS, refined by AM and JS (Appendix 2). The faculty focus groups were facilitated by MS and the student focus groups by AM. Focus groups were recorded and transcribed verbatim using the transcription facility on Otter.ai [19]. Amendments were made to faculty focus groups (JS) and student focus groups (AM) by listening to the recordings and amending the generated transcript to ensure accuracy of transcribing and providing full anonymity for participants prior to data analysis.

Researchers and reflexivity

AM is a pharmacist who was an undergraduate pharmacy student at the time of the study. MS is a senior lecturer in medicine with a research interest in feedback. JS is an associate professor of pharmacy with a research interest in pharmacy education. TK and AMcS were third-year undergraduate medical students at the time of analysis. While all members of the same institution, we have diverse roles and intentionally came to the work with differing preconceptions. This was essential for the partnership approach. We used group discussion to challenge our views and refine our interpretations of the data. Two researchers independently read and coded the data (TK, AMcS) and then met regularly with MS and JS to discuss and negotiate the meanings made from it, focusing on our research questions. Member validity was undertaken by providing excerpts of the raw data and our interpretations to participants, which led to some minor adjustments but no significant alterations of dominant themes.

Ethics

The study was granted ethics approval by the RCSI (Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland) Research and Ethics committee (REC Approval Number 01557). The procedures used in this study adhere to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. All recruited participants were provided with a participant information leaflet and given the opportunity to ask questions, prior to agreeing to attend the focus group. They understood that their informed verbal and written consent was dynamic and could be withdrawn at any point.

Data Processing / Data Analysis

Data analysis employed template analysis so that all data were collated in a hierarchal structure in order to better identify key domains and subthemes. Manual thematic analysis was employed according to King’s Template Analysis [20]. A constructivist epistemology is not typically used with template analysis, but can be applied in as broader themes defining aspects of discourse [21], as was the case in this study. These a priori codes were based on socially constructed understandings form the literature, reflecting that knowledge on this topic has been constructed in previous contexts. In this case, they were derived from a previous scoping review[9]. There was careful consideration of how these a priori codes are applied. i.e. they were are used tentatively and treated as provisional and subject to change, which they did. This aligns with a constructivist approach that remains open to revising, refining and discarding codes as we moved through the data and grew in understanding of how our participants constructed meaning in their specific contexts. As a result, these codes did not impose a rigid structure, but evolved through our interaction with the data. The six-stage process of template analysis was followed as outlined in Table 1. TK, AMcS, JS and MS commenced with the ‘familiarisation’ stage, which involved multiple re-readings of each manuscript to develop an in-depth appreciation of the data. This allowed the initial identification of themes within the data. Each member of the research team undertook independent development of codes and clustering of these into themes. The team subsequently met and discussed each member’s outputs. MS reviewed all transcripts in developing her first template. TK and AMcS each reviewed half of the transcripts to develop their first iterations of templates. MS, TK, AMCS and JS then met and reviewed all three-draft templates. Discussion led to the removal of duplicates and clustering of new sub-themes with existing a priori themes and discarding of irrelevant a priori themes. This led to the development of the first template draft. The initial template was discussed until consensus reached (TK, AMcS, and JS, MS). Coding persisted until it was determined that no further information was being contributed that was relevant to the research objectives[22]. We repeated this process for further iterative cycles, finally resulting in three overarching themes. The final template is available in Table 2. Results

Eight focus groups (four faculty, n = 28; SOM faculty = 13, SOP faculty = 15. Four student focus groups, n = 30; SOM = 11 (six clinical, 5 pre-clinical), SOP = 19 (15 clinical, 4 pre-clinical)) were conducted. Three overarching themes related to implementation of a feedback strategy were identified- Feedback Goals, Environment and Design.

Feedback goals

Students expressed the view that feedback should acknowledge strengths and weaknesses and provide direction for future work:(feedback should) point you in the direction of what you’re good at. And then also, like, identify areas that you can improve on and can kind of guide you towards that for future assignments (Speaker P, Pharmacy, Student).

(feedback should) point you in the direction of what you’re good at. And then also, like, identify areas that you can improve on and can kind of guide you towards that for future assignments (Speaker P, Pharmacy, Student).

They felt it should make expected standards clear:So I do think that was probably the best example of good feedback. They would have probably been like, “this is the standard that we expect”. (Speaker C, Medicine, Student).

So I do think that was probably the best example of good feedback. They would have probably been like, “this is the standard that we expect”. (Speaker C, Medicine, Student).

Conceptualisation of feedback goals was considered complex by faculty. Most faculty, from across medicine and pharmacy, agreed with the principles presented to the focus groups, that the strategy should include defined feedback goals which allow students to recognise and value feedback in developing their learning.:Understanding of how we define feedback in different contexts in the curriculum (is important) because …we’re all here probably coming at it with very different definitions and very different understandings depending on our context. And I think if we don’t acknowledge that and unpack it a bit, then it’s really, really difficult to see how we can have a global strategy within the institution (Speaker J, Medicine, Faculty).

Understanding of how we define feedback in different contexts in the curriculum (is important) because …we’re all here probably coming at it with very different definitions and very different understandings depending on our context. And I think if we don’t acknowledge that and unpack it a bit, then it’s really, really difficult to see how we can have a global strategy within the institution (Speaker J, Medicine, Faculty).

Both medicine and pharmacy faculty highlighted challenges with defining goals as they depend on context. A feedback encounter directly after an observed performance will have differing goals to a written assessment:There’s so many different levels of feedback, and I think it’s really making note of that. What is the context? Are we talking something that is immediate (post-observation), which obviously is a different category, is a different kettle of fish, or are we talking about something a few months later, that’s supposed to be incorporating all of your learning? (Speaker H, Medicine, Faculty).

There’s so many different levels of feedback, and I think it’s really making note of that. What is the context? Are we talking something that is immediate (post-observation), which obviously is a different category, is a different kettle of fish, or are we talking about something a few months later, that’s supposed to be incorporating all of your learning? (Speaker H, Medicine, Faculty).

They also reported that the goals change depending on the level of the student and their year of training:So the feedback in first year may be more feedback on SBAS [Single best answer questions] or written assessments, you know, so I think we have to appreciate that it probably won’t be the same. And like, you won’t have those kinds of one-on-one relationships where you’ve seen a student do something, in first year (Speaker K, Medicine, Faculty)

Faculty expressed a need for definitions of feedback goals with signposting and examples for specific learning activities to ensure a consistent approach:…it would be worthwhile to have a clear definition of feedback in the strategy and to have it laid out, you know, the different examples of feedback that do occur so that everybody is kind of working on the same page (Speaker D, Pharmacy, Faculty).

…it would be worthwhile to have a clear definition of feedback in the strategy and to have it laid out, you know, the different examples of feedback that do occur so that everybody is kind of working on the same page (Speaker D, Pharmacy, Faculty).

Environment

Faculty agreed that creating a feedback environment supportive of feedback literacy is critical. Sub-themes included format and setting, language and dialogue and relationships and emotions.

Feedback environment

Setting and format

Both faculty and students expressed the importance of format and setting. Most students receive both written and verbal feedback, with the former more common in early years and the latter in senior years. Although the literature is reframing feedback in terms of bidirectional dialogue, some students did express satisfaction with one-way written feedback:Dare I say I’m happy with any method of feedback? … remember when they sent out the, the emails directly to us for the essays we did, I was happy with that (Speaker B, Pharmacy, Student).

Dare I say I’m happy with any method of feedback? … remember when they sent out the, the emails directly to us for the essays we did, I was happy with that (Speaker B, Pharmacy, Student).

Written feedback

They indicated that having the written record was helpful to review at later stage:It’s nice to have it written down, because you can like bank it away and read it again (Speaker A, Pharmacy, Student)

It’s nice to have it written down, because you can like bank it away and read it again (Speaker A, Pharmacy, Student)

Some faculty expressed the view that marking rubrics and model answers were provided for all written assessments and that this was adequate feedback:You know, a student might say why it was my score so low, and I could point them to the exact rubric or whatever it was that was used to assess the exam and I’d say it's quite blatant here, you know (Speaker C, Pharmacy, Faculty)

You know, a student might say why it was my score so low, and I could point them to the exact rubric or whatever it was that was used to assess the exam and I’d say it's quite blatant here, you know (Speaker C, Pharmacy, Faculty)

Group feedback

Students indicated that group feedback can be very useful and that it should be used more often. When a teacher engages in-group feedback in classroom settings, pharmacy students were empowered to give feedback both to the teacher and to each other. They identified this as a time and resource-efficient way to provide feedback:It also like sparked conversation as well, like, between us, like, even if it wasn’t individual, like we then went and spoke about it afterwards as well. So like, we’re all again, giving ourselves feedback, to each other (Speaker B, Pharmacy, Student).

It also like sparked conversation as well, like, between us, like, even if it wasn’t individual, like we then went and spoke about it afterwards as well. So like, we’re all again, giving ourselves feedback, to each other (Speaker B, Pharmacy, Student).

One-to-one feedback

There was agreement amongst senior students that individual feedback is valued for its potential to give personalised guidance, although they felt it was only sometimes executed well:I think in the later years you could kind of get more of an opportunity for more individualized (feedback). I feel sometimes, discouraged, like they'll give the general feedback and then say we're not giving individualised feedback whereas I think now is when we actually really really need it (Speaker K, Pharmacy, Student).

Language and dialogue

Both students and faculty gave examples of dialogical feedback. Pharmacy faculty highlighted some challenges in finding time for email “back-and-forth” and managing situations where students challenged or did not accept feedback:So my experience is that as soon as students engage in bidirectional dialogue, it becomes grade quibbling you know, ‘my friend said this’, ‘why don’t I get that? (Speaker H, Pharmacy, Faculty).

So my experience is that as soon as students engage in bidirectional dialogue, it becomes grade quibbling you know, ‘my friend said this’, ‘why don’t I get that? (Speaker H, Pharmacy, Faculty).

So face-to-face feedback, I'd maybe do that four or five times in the year. Everything else like I can deal with either through the (class-based) feedback sessions or through email correspondence” (Speaker I, Medicine, Faculty)

Discussion

We sought to explore how students and faculty in medicine and pharmacy perceive an evidence-informed feedback strategy. This work is novel in exploring both faculty and student perspectives in a partnership approach.

While defining clear feedback goals seems obvious for an effective strategy, this is not without complexity. Faculty highlighted specific professional and learning activity contexts requiring different feedback goals. They also highlighted challenges in aligning understanding across faculty. This adds to existing evidence that feedback literacy requires an understanding of the particular disciplinary context in which feedback opportunities occur [23]. The finding that faculty vary on their interpretations of what feedback is and what its goals are, supports the socio-constructivist stance that shared and individual feedback interpretations are developed, and co-constructed [24], underscoring the importance of our approach of collaborative strategy development.

Some faculty gave examples of feedback as model answers and marking rubrics, indicating outdated teacher-centred models of information transmission persist [25]. However, by creating learning activities, which use these feedback formats in exercises where learners evaluate them, propose changes, seek clarification on meaning and ultimately appraise their own work against them, is a practical way to change to a learner-centred activity that supports appreciating feedback (Table 3). A strategy that centrally positions feedback literacy is critical to ensure faculty and students alike develop research-informed feedback practices. How students in later years perceive feedback goals highlight concerns about their feedback experiences, where they felt the goal was to criticise rather than guide. A recurring theme was that feedback should serve as a roadmap to identify and support areas for growth. Learners felt faculty development and student partnership were imperative in the strategy to implement these goals. A practical implication for educators is to engage students to co-define what feedback means and the goals for any specific feedback activity in varying contexts within their programme, which is detailed further in the table of recommendations for implementing a feedback strategy (Table 3.)

Students, despite faculty having reservations that its generic nature limited its value, generally received group feedback positively. Students described it as a catalyst for communities of practice [26, 27] when done well, which meant proving exemplars of differing standards of performance, explaining them in context and facilitating dialogue. This allowed them to adapt the good practices of their colleagues and sparked conversation outside of class, which progressed to peer feedback. Feedback literate teachers are characterised as using available resources well, while creating authentic feedback-rich environments [28]. We propose that small group feedback sessions could be co-designed between faculty and students. These were piloted in Pharmacy with good success. Students appreciated getting feedback within a small group, where it further facilitated the creation of a safe space for bidirectional dialogue. There is the potential for strategically developing this model further across our institution, and wider. Other practical applications include harnessing audit and video technologies to make feedback faster, more timely, and relational, where tone and body language can support emotions.

Bidirectionality as a concept was generally welcomed, but few participants had experienced it so felt they could not get what they needed from feedback when they were unable to communicate to teachers where or in what ways it was lacking. Additionally, transmissive encounters were commonly described where there was no scope for dialogue, in keeping with more traditional rater-centred approaches [8, 29]. Students struggled to make sense of feedback in this context, in keeping with the literature [9]. It was acknowledged that written feedback did not lend itself well to engaging in dialogue, but was useful for reflection. As facilitated reflection is known to support feedback acceptance [30], considering how to incorporate this in written feedback could increase its learning value. Some practical suggestions include providing feedback glossaries and guides with strategies on how to use feedback can enhance literacy [31]. There is further scope for efficient and timely facilitated reflection with the advent of generative artificial intelligence [32].

Faculty diverged on their role in supporting students’ emotions in feedback encounters; with some feeling, this was a pastoral responsibility. Others took the view that respecting student vulnerability and cultivating supportive interactions was intrinsic to the feedback process. While the latter is in keeping with contemporary research that an educational alliance supports constructive feedback use [7], it is important to recognise that faculty are on a journey to develop the skillset to support student feedback literacy, and most recognise a need for formal training [33]. It is not practical to assume that all educators will adapt with ease to a contemporary understanding of feedback that is complex and requires considerable emotional intelligence. This underlines the critical role in faculty development to support assessor professionalism [18, 34].

While all students felt their relationships with faculty were imperative to positive feedback experiences, they differed on what constructive relationships should look like. Early-year medical students felt faculty were too nice. The concept of “hedging”, indirect correction in an attempt to mitigate upset [35], is commonly employed by teachers. Learners expressed it was a barrier to honest feedback, a phenomenon commonly reported [36, 37]. Both early-year medicine and pharmacy students acknowledged that they had minimal opportunities for individual interactions and described fewer emotions around feedback, factors that are potentially related. Their senior colleagues experienced hierarchical relationships, which discouraged bidirectionality and had the potential to cause embarrassment or humiliation. It is striking that students within the same university have such polarised experiences, but it not unexpected. Clinical medicine has a long reported hidden curriculum of hostility and humiliation [38]. Pharmacy students reported a mix of positive and negative feedback experiences.. Interestingly, while the final-year medical students attribute their negative experiences to the culture, senior pharmacy students consider feedback encounters to be teacher-specific. All of the examples of challenging relationships that evoked negative emotions were verbal feedback encounters. This is of note, as healthcare professions, particularly in senior years, will rely heavily on such face-to-face feedback, unlike many other academic disciplines. Each of these factors alone support how context and learning culture influence learner response to feedback [39]. We highlight to educators the careful consideration of situational factors in developing a strategy where learners feel equipped to manage their emotions and teachers are confident in their role and capability to support this.

Pharmacy students indicated that timeliness of feedback would significantly enhance their experience, while both pharmacy and pre-clinical medical faculty acknowledge resource-related delays [40]. Our faculty are not alone in finding the feedback-related workload burdensome [41]. Feedback timing is both a design and pedagogical issue, as embedding feedback in the day-to-day of learning activities (intrinsic feedback) rather than attaching to semester-end assessments, offers opportunities to implement improvement synchronously as learning progresses [42]. Timeliness did not feature as a dominant theme for senior medical students, suggesting there is scope for considering learning from alternative designs such as the in-the-moment feedback commonly occurring on the wards. Practically this could potentially be overcome with the growing potential in pharmacy for workplace-based assessments such as entrustable professional activities [43].

Curriculum design was surprisingly absent from the strategy development conversation. While faculty suggested signposting of feedback to help orientate students, and student curricular activities to upskill them in feedback literacy skills, neither faculty nor students suggested inherently embedding feedback processes in curricular design, though this is a frequent expert recommendation [18]. We have, however, enhanced the curricular design of Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) in pharmacy, by reducing the number of stations in mock OSCEs and extending the time available for the remainder of the stations to provide time for bidirectional dialogue. This helps to address design aspects such as timeliness and resources.

Conclusion

The results of this study focused on student and faculty perceptions of implementing an evidence-informed feedback strategy. The findings suggest that while an expanse of research-informed theory exists, processes and conceptualisation in practice may be outdated. Operationalisation challenges are considerable in upskilling both faculty and students to feedback literacy. Feedback experiences in the same programme at different stages contrast notably in students’ perception of their value. This represents a significant transitional challenge for students, having experienced feedback that was “overly nice” in their pre-clinical years, they encounter an environment describe as toxic, hostile and a “roasting culture” in clinical feedback interactions.

The recommendations in Table 3 require careful implementation, mindful of resources, and continuous evaluation to ensure their effectiveness across a range of educational contexts. Through cultivating a culture of open dialogue, acknowledging emotions, and equipping both students and faculty with the necessary skills, educational institutions can contribute to a transformative feedback landscape that enriches the learning journey.

Strengths and limitations

Utilising a partnership practice between students and faculty to perceive the difference in perspectives relating to feedback and particularly in the context of policy implementation is impactful [44]. The data generated a wide range of perspectives from students and faculty across medicine and pharmacy, which allowed for effective comparisons and contrasting points to develop. To optimise validity and reliability, two facilitators trained together in order to undertake the focus groups. MS undertook a pilot focus group with faculty, which JS and AM reviewed. This allowed refinement of interview schedule. AM undertook a pilot focus group with students, which MS and JS reviewed, allowing refinement of interview schedule. Having reviewed the pilot work together, we were satisfied that the two interviewers had a consistent approach. In analysing the data, our research group was diverse and brought a range of perspectives that assisted in challenging bias. There is always a risk that the faculty and students who participated have individual biases, which was mitigated by the number of focus groups conducted. Future research could employ a mixed-methods approach, triangulating qualitative insights with quantitative data to provide a comprehensive understanding, which would overcome any context specific nature of the findings. While our populations were formed from two separate health professions education schools, multi-site recruitment would be useful to improve the likelihood of transferability. Longitudinal studies that investigate the impact of interventions on feedback perceptions and learning outcomes would further contribute to the field.
