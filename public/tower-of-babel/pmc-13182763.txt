Macrophage‐Fibroblast Crosstalk Shapes Wound Repair Signaling In Vitro
Authors: Daniela Enriquez‐Ochoa, Raji R Nagalla, Yingzi Liu, Christian F Guerrero‐Juarez, Hamza Atcha, Gordon Chang, Nancy A Morales, Hannah J Hooper, Arian D Cano, Huy Eng Lim, Michael D Cahalan, Maksim V Plikus, Wendy F Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWound healing requires coordinated interactions between macrophages and fibroblasts, yet how contact‐dependent signaling integrates with paracrine pathways to regulate their reciprocal behavior is not well defined. Here, we investigated macrophage‐fibroblast communication using complementary 2D and 3D in vitro wound healing models combined with live‐cell calcium imaging and single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq). We show that bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) promote fibroblast scratch closure in a contact‐dependent manner independent of connexins, whereas fibroblasts reciprocally regulate macrophage cytokine secretion through distinct mechanisms. Direct cell–cell contact with fibroblasts enhanced macrophage IL10 production via connexin 43 (Cx43)‐dependent signaling, whereas TNFα secretion was suppressed through paracrine interactions. We further demonstrate that fibroblast‐macrophage contact induces connexin‐dependent intermittent calcium signals selectively in macrophages. ScRNA‐seq revealed that wounding reshapes macrophage and fibroblast populations, uncovering dynamic regulation of cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) and intercellular signaling pathways. Together, these findings reveal the integration of contact‐dependent calcium and connexin signaling with transcriptional remodeling to coordinate macrophage‐fibroblast behavior during healing.Keywords: calcium signaling, cell–cell contact, connexin 43, fibroblasts, macrophages, single‐cell RNA sequencing, wound healing

Introduction

Wound healing exemplifies the body's remarkable capacity for repair. In response to sudden environmental insults, such as tissue injury, a cascade of molecular and cellular processes is rapidly activated. Macrophages and fibroblasts are pivotal cell types that drive this process. Macrophages orchestrate the progression of all healing phases, from the initial inflammatory response at the wound site to the remodeling stage, by releasing inflammatory cytokines, clearing cell debris, and promoting tissue regeneration [1]. Fibroblasts also play multifaceted roles, contributing to inflammation, contracting the wound, and synthesizing and depositing new extracellular matrix (ECM) to restore damaged tissue [2]. It is well established that macrophages and fibroblasts do not act independently; rather, they influence each other's phenotype and functional behavior. Their dynamic interplay is essential for timely and effective wound repair, and disruptions in this coordination can result in aberrant healing outcomes such as chronic wounds or fibrosis [3]. However, how these cells coordinate their responses through different modes of communication is still not fully understood.

Macrophages and fibroblasts coordinate tissue repair through reciprocal paracrine signaling, with macrophage‐derived chemokines and cytokines influencing fibroblast activation, contraction, and recruitment, while fibroblast‐secreted factors regulate macrophage phenotype, migration, and proliferation [4, 5, 6, 7]. Most studies have focused on paracrine communication via soluble mediators, despite evidence showing that macrophages and fibroblasts frequently engage in direct physical contact within the wound site [7, 8, 9]. Identified cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) mediating macrophage‐fibroblast interactions include integrins and connexins. For instance, macrophage binding to fibroblasts through integrin αvβ3 promotes fibroblast activation in a contact‐dependent manner [10]. Connexin 43 (Cx43), in contrast, has primarily been implicated in paracrine macrophage‐mediated ATP release that drives fibroblast pro‐fibrotic responses, yet connexins also form gap junctions capable of supporting contact‐dependent signaling among macrophages themselves and other cell types, such as endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Nevertheless, how these contact‐dependent interactions translate into specific intracellular signals that coordinate reciprocal macrophage‐fibroblast responses remains unclear.

Calcium (Ca2+) plays a fundamental role in cell–cell communication, acting both as a key cofactor of CAMs and as a mediator of downstream signaling [16]. Ca2+ also regulates macrophage activation states and fibroblast responses such as contraction and collagen synthesis, processes that are critical during wound healing [17, 18, 19]. Recent studies have begun to uncover how Ca2+ signaling shapes communication between these cells: Ezzo et al. [10] demonstrated that IL4/IL13‐stimulated macrophages induce Ca2+ influx in fibroblasts via Piezo1 activation through integrin αvβ3, triggering Yes‐associated protein (YAP)/transcriptional coactivator with PDZ‐binding motif (TAZ) signaling and fibroblast activation. Similarly, Bhattacharyya et al. (2022) reported that macrophages elevate fibroblast Ca2+ levels through ATP efflux and activation of the P2rx4 receptor [11]. While Ca2+‐mediated signaling in these studies has been implicated in fibrotic processes, its role in macrophage‐fibroblast interactions during wound healing remains largely unexplored. Understanding how direct cell–cell contact shapes intracellular Ca2+ dynamics may reveal new mechanisms by which these cells coordinate their responses to promote tissue repair.

In this study, we investigated reciprocal communication between fibroblasts and macrophages using 2D and 3D wound healing culture models. We found that bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) promote 3T3 scratch closure in a contact‐dependent manner, independent of connexins. Conversely, 3T3 fibroblasts modulate macrophage cytokine secretion through distinct mechanisms: they enhance IL10 production via contact‐dependent signaling mediated by Cx43, while suppressing TNFα secretion through paracrine interactions. Using the genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator, Salsa6f, we further showed that direct contact between fibroblasts and macrophages induces Ca2+ signaling exclusively in macrophages, and that this response is blocked with the connexin uncoupler palmitoleic acid (PA). Finally, single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) revealed that wounding reshapes fibroblast and macrophage populations, uncovering changes in CAMs expression and intercellular signaling pathways that underlie these contact‐ and paracrine‐dependent interactions.

Methods

Animals

Mice expressing the Salsa6f Ca2+ reporter were generated through breeding of a Salsa6ffl/fl mouse (Jackson Laboratories strain no. 031968) and a myeloid cell‐specific Cre recombinase mouse (LysMCre, Jackson Laboratories strain# 00487) to generate progeny that were heterozygous for both genes. The generated heterozygotes were then bred with Salsa6ffl/fl mice to obtain Salsa6ffl/flLysMCre/+ mice for calcium imaging. TdTomatofl/+LysMcre/+ were obtained from Dr. Medha Pathak's laboratory at the University of California, Irvine. Wild type C57BL/J6 mice (Jackson Laboratories strain# 000664, RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) were used in experiments not involving genetic manipulation. Experiments were performed using animals of similar age. Mice were maintained at 12‐h light/dark cycles within (70°F–74°F) and humidity (30%–70%) controlled rooms. All experiments involving animal usage were performed in compliance with the University of California, Irvine.

Cell Isolation and Culture

To obtain BMDMs, bone marrow of 6–12‐week‐old mice was isolated by flushing femurs and tibias with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (Fisher Scientific) followed by centrifugation. Red blood cells were lysed with ammonium‐chloride‐potassium (ACK) lysis buffer (Gibco) and removed by centrifugation. The remaining cells were resuspended and cultured with Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (ThermoFisher), and 10% of conditioned media produced from CMG14‐12 cells (RRID:CVCL_A9A2) expressing recombinant macrophage colony stimulating factor (MCSF), which induces differentiation of bone marrow cells to macrophages. After 7 days, cells were harvested using an enzyme‐free dissociation buffer (Corning). NIH/3T3 (ATCC, Cat# CRL‐1658, RRID:CVCL_0594) fibroblasts were cultured below 85% confluence in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and used between passages 3–12.

For monocultures, 3T3s or BMDMs were seeded at a density of 79 000 cells/cm2 in fibronectin‐coated (10 μg/mL, Corning) polystyrene well plates. For cocultures, 3T3s were seeded at a density of 79 000 cells/cm2 in fibronectin‐coated (10 μg/mL, Corning) polystyrene well plates and allowed to adhere for 3 h. After that, BMDMs were seeded at varying densities (15 800; 39 500; and 79 000 cells/cm2) either on top of the 3T3s monolayer (juxtacrine) or into a 0.3 μm pore transwell insert (paracrine) and allowed to adhere for 4 h. Cultures were stimulated with LPS/IFNɣ (10 ng/mL, Fisher Scientific, R&D Systems) or IL4/IL13 (20 ng/mL, BioLegend) + LPS (10 ng/mL), with stimulation maintained throughout the overnight incubation period. Where applicable, palmitoleic acid (50 or 100 μM, Fisher Scientific) was added concurrently during stimulation prior to performing a scratch assay or collecting supernatant for ELISA studies. Fibroblast and macrophage monoculture conditions were treated under identical stimulation conditions as cocultures.

2D Scratch Assay

Prior to seeding, 3T3s were stained with DiO (green) and tdTomato (red) BMDMs to distinguish both cell types. 3T3 monocultures and 3T3‐BMDM cocultures were cultured as described previously, scratched once using a 10 μL pipette tip and imaged at 0 and 6 h at 4× using an Olympus inverted fluorescence microscope. Scratch area was quantified from paired single frames at both time points, with the total reduction designated as scratch area closed. This value was converted to average width, and the average distance traveled was calculated as half the reduction in width between time points.

3D Scratch Assay

Collagen gels (2 or 8 mg/mL, Corning) of 1 mm thickness were prepared following manufacturer's instructions using DMEM basal media as a solvent and with the addition of 3T3s stained with CellTracker Green CMFDA (Fisher Scientific) at a density of 1 000 000 cells/mL. For cocultures, BMDMs stained with calcein Red AM (Fisher Scientific) were added to gels at a density of 1 000 000 cells/mL. Gels were cast either in 6‐ or 12‐well polystyrene plates, gelled for 30 min at 37°C, hydrated with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and cultured for 48 h. After, gels were wounded using a 0.2 mm blunt needle and imaged at 4X using an Olympus FV3000 Confocal Spectral Inverted Microscope. After imaging, gels were washed and re‐hydrated with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and cultured for an additional 72 h. Gels were imaged once again to determine the percentage of wound closure designated as the difference in area between the initial time point and the final time point divided by the initial area and multiplied by 100.

ELISA

Following overnight stimulation, supernatants belonging to mono and cocultures were collected in the continued presence of stimuli and analyzed for the presence of TNFα and IL10 using ELISA kits (BioLegend). The assays were conducted following the manufacturer's instructions.

Magnetic Sorting

After being cocultured as previously described, 3T3s and BMDMs were separated using a QuadroMACS Separator (Miltenyi Biotec) with LS columns (Miltenyi Biotec) and mouse Anti‐F4/80 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer's instructions. The eluate enriched with BMDMs was collected, as well as the flowthrough enriched with 3T3s.

Flow Cytometry

To assess the purity of 3T3s and BMDMs after magnetic sorting, cells were resuspended in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Roche), stained using FITC mouse anti‐CD45 (BioLegend, Cat# 147709, RRID: AB_2563541) and APC mouse anti‐Cd11b (BioLegend, Cat# 101211, RRID: AB_312794), and analyzed using an Agilent NovoCyte 3000 flow cytometer.

RNA Isolation, cDNA Synthesis and qPCR

RNA was isolated using a Quick‐RNA Purification kit (Zymo Research). cDNA was synthesized using a High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Applied Biosystems) and qPCR was performed using GreenFast qPCR Blue Mix (AzuraView). All assays were performed in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The list of primers used in this study can be found in Table S1.

Calcium Imaging and Analysis

For monocultures, BMDMs harvested from Salsa6ffl/flLysMCre/+ mice or Salsa6f transfected 3T3s were seeded at 50 000 cells/cm2 in fibronectin‐coated (10 μg/mL) 35 mm MatTek dishes. For cocultures, the second cell type was seeded simultaneously at the same density. Cells were allowed to adhere for 6 h, stimulated with PA (100 μM) if indicated and incubated overnight. Following incubation, media was removed, cells were washed and replaced with Ringer's solution prior to imaging. Live‐cell imaging was performed using an Olympus FV3000 inverted confocal spectral microscope equipped with a high‐speed resonant scanner and an IX3‐ZDC2 Z‐drift compensator. Samples were excited with 488‐nm and 561‐nm lasers and imaged using a 40X silicone oil objective (NA 1.25) at 1 frame per second for 10 min per field of view. Ratiometric quantification of intracellular calcium flux was performed using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/, RRID:SCR_003070). Green calcium‐dependent fluorescence was normalized to the red cytoplasmic reference signal to control calcium‐independent changes in fluorescence. Subcellular regions exhibiting calcium activity were manually delineated, and calcium events were defined as transient increases in the green‐to‐red fluorescence ratio exceeding 2.5‐fold above baseline. This threshold was empirically selected to maximize specificity over background noise while remaining within the dynamic range of the Salsa6f probe, enabling reliable detection of transient calcium events [20]. For each condition, calcium event frequency (events per cell per minute) and the percentage of cells within each field of view exhibiting active calcium flux were quantified. A total of 50 cells were analyzed per biological replicate in each condition.

RNA Interference

For experiments involving Cx43 knockdowns, unstimulated BMDMs were transfected using INTERFERin (Sartorius) with either 50 nM of either non‐target or Cx43 siRNA (both designed by Integrated DNA Technologies) for 24 h. Following transfection, transfected BMDMs were seeded either in monoculture or coculture with 3T3s and stimulated as previously described.

Single Cell RNA Sequencing and Analysis

Collagen gels (2 mg/mL) were prepared as previously described with BMDMs and 3T3s cocultured at a 1:1 ratio (1 000 000 cells/mL) for 48 h. After, they were wounded using a 0.2 mm blunt needle and incubated for 72 h. To digest the gels, collagenase (10 mg/mL, Fisher Scientific) was added for 10 min, and a Dead Cell Removal kit (Miltenyi Biotec) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions to deplete dead cells. RNA was sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq X platform.

Sequencing reads were mapped to the mouse reference transcriptome (mm10‐3.0.0) using Cell Ranger version 3.1.0 (10× Genomics). Doublets were removed with Single‐Cell Remover of Doublets (Scrublet) version 0.2.1 as per developer's recommendations [21, 22]. After removal of putative doublets, low‐quality cell pruning was performed using a similar user‐defined pipeline as previously described [21]. Briefly, cells were kept if they met the following collective quality control criteria: (a) 350 < genes/cell < 5000; (b) cells contained no more than 15% of mitochondrial gene expression; (c) cells were not identified as outliers as defined in Pagoda (p < 0.001) [23]. After quality control, datasets were processed using Seurat (version 3.2.2) in RStudio (https://posit.co, RRID:SCR_000432). Data were normalized using SCTransform, and integration across wounded and unwounded samples was performed using the anchor‐based integration method with 30 dimensions [24]. The integrated dataset was scaled, and significant principal components (PCs) were identified for downstream analysis. Nearest‐neighbor graphs were constructed, and clusters were visualized using two‐dimensional space, t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE). Putative cell types were assigned based on differential gene expression of established lineage‐specific markers. A total of 7045 and 9267 cells were used for analysis of unwounded and wounded conditions, respectively.

Differential gene expression analysis was carried out for each Seurat‐defined cluster using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test implemented in the presto package, with cluster identities set as the active identities in the Seurat object. Genes were ranked by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and the top 50 marker genes per cluster were selected for KEGG enrichment pathway analysis. Gene symbols were converted to Entrez Gene identifiers using the org.Mm.eg.db annotation database. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was then performed on a per‐cluster basis using the clusterProfiler package, with the mouse genome (mmu) as the reference and a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Enriched pathways were used to inform functional annotation and biological interpretation of individual clusters. For clusters in which KEGG pathway enrichment did not reach statistical significance, cluster identities were manually assigned based on the functional annotation of differentially expressed genes and established macrophage marker expression. Cell–cell interaction networks between macrophages and fibroblasts were characterized using the CellChat package [25], and the contribution of identified pathways to receptor‐ligand interactions was subsequently assessed.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Data are presented as individual points corresponding to independent cultures and mean ± standard deviation (SD). The number of independent cultures per experiment are indicated in figure legends. Comparison between conditions were performed using either one‐way ANOVA, two‐way ANOVA or two‐tailed paired t‐test as indicated in figure legends. p Values are provided in figure legends with *p < 0.05 considered significant. All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 Software (http://www.graphpad.com, RRID:SCR_002798).

Results

Bidirectional Fibroblast‐Macrophage Interactions Regulate Scratch Closure and Cytokine Secretion

Fibroblasts are the primary cell type responsible for remodeling the wound environment and promoting closure. To evaluate whether macrophages influence fibroblast‐mediated wound closure, 3T3s were cultured alone or with BMDMs at varying ratios while maintaining constant fibroblast density to model a range of macrophage abundances, from low‐density to macrophage‐rich conditions. This approach reflects the dynamic shifts in stromal‐to‐immune cell composition that occur across distinct wound phases of wound healing in vivo [26, 27]. Cultures were then stimulated to mimic inflammatory or healing conditions and scratched to simulate a wound (Figure 1a). In the absence of stimulation, the addition of BMDMs increased scratch closure uniformly across all ratios (Figure 1b). In contrast, under inflammatory or mixed pro‐repair stimulation, enhanced closure was observed only at the highest 3T3s:BMDMs ratio (1:1). The latter models a mixed activation state generated by simultaneous exposure to pro‐repair (IL4/IL13) and inflammatory signals (LPS), mirroring the complex signaling environments cells encounter during wound healing in vivo [18]. Because 3 T3 density was maintained constant across all coculture conditions, we confirmed separately that fibroblast density alone does not influence scratch closure; doubling the number of 3T3s in monoculture had no effect on fibroblast migration (Figure S1).

Next, we tested whether the observed BMDM‐mediated enhancement of 3T3 scratch closure was dependent on paracrine or juxtacrine signaling. 3T3s were cultured alone, in direct contact with BMDMs, or separated from BMDMs using a transwell insert (Figure 1c). To maintain comparable cell densities across formats, 3T3s and BMDMs were seeded at a 1:0.2 ratio. In the absence of stimulation, scratch closure was enhanced only when BMDMs were in direct contact with 3T3s (Figure 1d). In contrast, cocultures stimulated with either LPS/IFNɣ or IL4/IL13/LPS showed no increase in scratch closure, consistent with the ratio‐dependent effects observed previously at the same 1:0.2 ratio for juxtacrine cultures (Figure 1b). Thus, BMDMs promote scratch closure in a contact and density‐dependent manner, with stimulation suppressing this effect at low BMDM ratios.

Macrophages orchestrate wound healing through multiple functions, with cytokine secretion playing a critical role in signaling to other cell types. To determine whether fibroblasts influenced macrophage cytokine secretion in response to stimulation, we cultured BMDMs alone, in direct contact with 3T3s, or separated from BMDMs using a transwell insert. We found that direct contact with fibroblasts increased IL10 secretion when cells were stimulated either with LPS/IFNɣ or IL4/IL13/LPS (Figure 1e). On the other hand, TNFα secretion decreased in the presence of fibroblasts both in juxtacrine and paracrine cocultures, exclusively under stimulated conditions, suggesting that soluble factors drive this effect. Together, these findings suggest that fibroblasts influence macrophage polarization by enhancing anti‐inflammatory signals while suppressing pro‐inflammatory responses.

Contact‐Dependent Connexin Signaling Modulates Macrophage Cytokine Secretion and Calcium Activity

Given that our previous findings suggested that fibroblasts regulate macrophage IL10 secretion in a contact‐dependent manner, we investigated whether specific adhesion molecules contributed to this effect. 3T3s and BMDMs were cocultured, magnetically separated and analyzed for mRNA expression of connexins and integrins relative to monoculture controls. Both have been implicated in coordinating macrophage‐fibroblast interactions during fibrosis, and in regulating their own cellular behavior [10, 11, 28, 29, 30]. Separation efficiency was confirmed by flow cytometry, which showed strong enrichment of macrophages and fibroblasts in their respective sorted fractions (Figure S2). Upon coculture, BMDMs exhibited an increase in Cx43 (Gja1) expression, followed by Connexin 31 (Cx31; Gjb3). In contrast, 3T3s displayed a more pronounced upregulation of integrin ß1 (Itgb1) and Connexin 30.3 (Cx30.3; Gjb4) (Figure 2a). Interestingly, Cx43 and Cx31 were the most abundant connexins expressed in both cell types before and after coculture (Figure S3a). Next, we evaluated the role of connexins on macrophage cytokine secretion using the broad connexin uncoupler PA under conditions that block gap junction communication but does not distinguish between specific connexin isoforms [31]. Cocultures stimulated with IL4/Il13/LPS and treated PA showed reduced IL10 secretion compared to untreated cocultures, while TNFα levels remain unchanged (Figure 2b,c). These results were consistent with our previous findings, in which TNFα secretion was not modulated by macrophage‐fibroblast contact.

Connexins are structural components of gap junctions and hemichannels, which mediate communication and exchange of small molecules and ions, such as Ca2+ between cells and the extracellular space [32]. To further assess their functional role in fibroblast‐macrophage communication, we evaluated Ca+2 dynamics in both cell types under monoculture, coculture and PA‐treated conditions. BMDMs derived from Salsa6ffl/flLysMCre/+ mice and 3T3s transiently transfected with Salsa6f were used. Salsa6f is a ratiometric genetically encoded Ca2+reporter consisting of GCAMP6f and tdTomato optimized for simultaneous tracking and Ca2+ monitoring [20]. Coculture with 3T3s increased both the frequency of intermittent Ca+2 events in BMDMs and the percentage of BMDMs exhibiting active Ca+2 events (Figure 2d,e). PA treatment abrogated this response. In contrast, 3T3s displayed fewer Ca+2 events when cocultured with BMDMs, and PA treatment did not alter this effect (Figure 2f,g). The percentage of 3T3s actively exhibiting Ca2+ signals remained unchanged across all conditions. Collectively, these findings indicate that coculture activates connexin‐dependent signaling that modulates calcium signals and cytokine secretion in macrophages, while fibroblast calcium dynamics remain largely unaffected.

Cx43 in Macrophages Mediates Contact‐Dependent Anti‐Inflammatory Signaling in Coculture

Because coculture modulated connexins and integrins expression in BMDMs and 3T3s, we next asked whether a scratch wound would further alter the expression of these adhesion structures. Cocultures were scratched, separated using magnetic sorting and assessed for mRNA expression relative to scratched monocultures. Scratching led to an overall downregulation of connexins and upregulation of integrins in BMDMs upon coculture, whereas cocultured 3T3s exhibited increased connexin and integrin ß2 (Itgb2) expression (Figure 3a). To test the functional role of connexins in scratch closure, we treated 3T3 monocultures and cocultures with PA, which had no effect on scratch closure (Figure 3b). Among the connexins evaluated, Cx43 has been extensively implicated in wound healing and was highly expressed in both BMDMs and 3T3s after coculture in both the presence and absence of a scratch (Figure S3) [28, 33, 34]. We therefore selectively knocked down Cx43 in BMDMs using siRNA to test its role in cytokine secretion. Cx43 knockdown abrogated the coculture‐induced increase in IL10 secretion, while TNFα secretion remained unaffected (Figure 3c,d), consistent with what was observed with PA treatment. Thus, these results suggest that Cx43 in macrophages contributes to the contact‐associated increase in IL10 secretion during macrophage‐fibroblasts interactions following scratch, without affecting TNFα secretion.

Fibroblast‐Macrophage Interactions in 3D Collagen Gels Enhance Wound Closure and Modulate Cytokine Secretion

During wound healing, fibroblasts and macrophages interact within a three‐dimensional environment that facilitates cell–cell communication, which cannot be fully recapitulated in 2D cultures. To better mimic this physiological context, we encapsulated 3T3s and BMDMs in a 3D collagen gel, performed a puncture, and assessed wound closure against 3T3 monocultures (Figure 4a). Overall, we observed a trend of greater closure of the punctured area in cocultures than in monocultures, supporting the notion that BMDMs enhance fibroblast responses to injury (Figures 1b and 4b). We also evaluated cytokine secretion and the impact of PA treatment in 3D. Similar to 2D cultures, cocultures stimulated with IL4/IL13/LPS showed increased IL10 secretion compared to BMDM monocultures, while fibroblasts decreased TNFα secretion in LPS/IFNɣ‐stimulated cells (Figure 4c,d). Notably, PA treatment reduced IL10 secretion in cocultures stimulated with IL4/IL13/LPS, consistent with 2D results, but decreased TNFα secretion in BMDM monocultures, an effect not observed in 2D cultures (Figure 2b,c). These findings suggest that 3D culture preserves fibroblast‐macrophage interactions while revealing context‐dependent modulation of macrophage cytokine responses to PA, possibly due to differences in gap junction formation within a 3D environment or the presence of distinct matrix proteins.

Wounding Tunes the Presence of Fibroblasts and Macrophages Subpopulations and Their Transcriptional Signatures

To investigate transcriptional changes associated with fibroblast‐macrophage communication, 3T3s and BMDMs were cocultured in 3D collagen gels, wounded, enzymatically dissociated, and processed for scRNA‐seq. By using unsupervised clustering of all cells from wounded and unwounded conditions, we identified 17 transcriptionally distinct clusters representing heterogeneous fibroblast and macrophage populations (Figure 5a–c). Cluster identities were assigned based on the expression of canonical macrophage and fibroblast markers (Figure 5a). Functional annotation of clusters was performed using KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of cluster‐specific differentially expressed genes (Figure 5b,c and Figures S4–S8). For clusters lacking statistically significant KEGG pathway enrichment, identities were manually annotated based on their differentially expressed gene profiles (Figure S9).

Clusters enriched after wounding included M5, F10, and F11, while clusters whose proportion decreased included M1, F4, and F9, highlighting a selective reshaping of the cellular landscape during wound repair (Figure 5b,c). The M5 macrophage cluster increased from 9.84% to 13.40%, with cells showing increased Ly6 expression upon wounding, consistent with a wound‐associated activation state (Figure S10a) [35]. In contrast, the M1 macrophage cluster declined from 21.40% to 18.64%, expressing complement and Fc receptors (i.e., C1qb, C1qc, Fcg2rb, Fcgr3, and Mrc1) and lysosomal/lipid‐handling genes (i.e., Lamp1, Laptm5, Abca1, Apoe, Pltp, and Npc2). Notably, Ifngr1 expression increased within the M1 cluster upon wounding, indicating gene‐specific modulation despite an overall reduction in cluster abundance (Figure S11a).

Among fibroblasts, the F10 cluster rose from 7.78% to 10.04%, exhibiting a tissue‐remodeling program with reduced expression of structural collagens and proteoglycans (i.e., Col1a1, Col1a2, and Dcn), while F11 doubled from 4.71% to 9.62%, maintaining strong ribosomal protein expression (i.e., Rpl39, Rpl9, Rpl6, Rpsa, and Rps7) and showing a wounding‐induced increase in Col6a2, suggesting targeted extracellular matrix repair (Figure S10b,c). In contrast, F4 fibroblasts decreased from 25.21% to 21.47%, retaining a matrix‐organizing signature (i.e., Col3a1, Fn1, Bgn, Col1a2, Col6a1, Dcn, Lum, and Tpm1) with minimal change post‐wounding, and F9 fibroblasts declined from 16.12% to 10.14%, expressing proliferative and ubiquitin‐related genes (i.e., Cdk1, Cdc20, Nedd4, and Ube2c) alongside ECM components (i.e., Vim, Bgn, Col1a2, Col6a1, and Dcn) without significant transcriptional modulation (Figure S11b,c).

Wounding Alters Contact‐Dependent Adhesion Pathway Expression in Fibroblasts and Macrophages

Next, we examined how the expression of CAMs varied across clusters identified in our scRNA‐seq dataset. We focused on genes from KEGG pathways for gap junctions, focal adhesions, adherens junctions, and tight junctions and determined which of these genes were expressed in our clusters. Only a subset of pathway genes showed detectable expression in our dataset; these expressed genes are summarized in heatmaps to illustrate cluster‐specific expression patterns (Figure 6a–d). Several clusters exhibited notably higher expression of adhesion‐related genes, indicating transcriptional programs enriched for cell adhesion and intercellular communication. Notably, F11 displayed broad upregulation across all four pathways. Based on these patterns, we selected clusters M0, M1, M7, M8, F9, F11, and M15 to evaluate adhesion‐specific transcriptional changes in response to wounding (Figure 6e). F11 showed the most pronounced wound‐induced changes, consistent with its elevated ribosomal activity. Consistent with our prior findings in 2D cocultures, macrophage clusters M7 and M8 primarily showed changes in the expression of focal adhesion‐associated genes, particularly integrins, whereas fibroblast clusters F9 and F11 revealed changes in connexin expression upon wounding (Figures 3a and 6e).

Fibroblasts and Macrophages Establish Bidirectional Signaling Networks Shaped by Wounding

To further investigate fibroblast‐macrophage communication, we analyzed our dataset using CellChat, a tool that quantifies intercellular signaling networks from scRNA‐seq data [25]. In both unwounded and wounded conditions, F11 emerged as a dominant sender cluster, exhibiting strong communication probabilities toward macrophage clusters M0, M1, M2, M7, M8, M12, and M16 (Figure 7a). Following wounding, the outgoing signaling strength of F11 decreased, but it remained as the main sender, while macrophage receiver clusters showed reduced incoming signaling strength. Information flow analysis, which quantifies the overall communication probability of specific pathways across all cell groups, revealed that wounding upregulated the vascular adhesion protein (VCAM), protein S (PROS), and CHEMERIN pathways (Figure 7b). Conversely, wounding downregulated the calcitonin receptor (CALCR), interleukin‐2 (IL2), and IL10 pathways, which were active in the unwounded condition but largely absent following wounding.

Considering the specific pathways that were modulated by wounding, we compared the receptor and ligand expression between F11, M0, M1, M2, M7, M8, and M16, as these clusters showed stronger signaling strength than others (Figure 7c). Fibroblasts in the F11 cluster acted as primary ligand expressors signaling to macrophages, with ligands including Vcam1, Rarres2, and Adm from the VCAM, CHEMERIN, and CALCR pathways. Notably, VCAM is a contact‐dependent pathway, whereas CHEMERIN and CALCR functions rely on secreted signals, collectively contributing to macrophage adhesion, recruitment, and activation [36, 37, 38, 39]. Conversely, macrophages signaled to fibroblasts via ligands Gas6 and IL10 from the PROS and IL10 pathways, respectively, with fibroblasts expressing the corresponding receptors, suggesting reciprocal regulation of fibroblast activity. We also observed autocrine signaling within the PROS, IL10, and VCAM pathways, as individual cell types expressed both ligands and their respective receptors. As expected, neither macrophages nor fibroblasts expressed the Il2 ligand, which is primarily produced by T cells; however, macrophages expressed the corresponding receptor, suggesting that wounding alone was sufficient to induce transcriptional activation of this pathway. Together, our findings indicate that wounding rewires the transcriptional landscapes of both macrophages and fibroblasts, thereby reshaping receptor‐ligand interactions across multiple pathways relevant to coordinated healing.

Discussion

Wound healing relies on tightly regulated communication between immune and stromal cells, driven by both soluble factors and direct cell–cell contact. In this study, we examined fibroblast‐macrophage communication in 2D and 3D culture systems. 3T3 fibroblasts were used as a standardized stromal model to minimize variability associated with primary fibroblasts, due to their intrinsic heterogeneity and culture‐induced activation [40, 41, 42]. While this model provides experimental consistency, we acknowledge that immortalized fibroblasts may not fully recapitulate primary fibroblast responses, including cytokine secretion and migratory responses. To better reflect the dynamic changes in cellular composition during wound healing, we tested a range of fibroblast‐to‐macrophage ratios. Our results showed that BMDMs enhance fibroblast‐driven scratch closure (Figure 1b). However, this effect was ratio‐dependent: low macrophage‐to‐fibroblast ratios did not influence closure under stimulation in either paracrine or juxtacrine cocultures, while higher ratios (1:1) enhanced closure under pro‐inflammatory (LPS/IFNɣ) or mixed pro‐repair (IL4/IL13/LPS) conditions (Figure 1d). Although macrophage‐derived soluble factors, such as TGFβ, PDGFs, and IL6, have been shown to enhance fibroblast migration, we did not observe this classical paracrine effect at lower macrophage densities [3, 43, 44]. This potentially reflects a threshold requirement for macrophage‐derived signals: low macrophage density may not generate sufficient ligand abundance to elicit a measurable response. Additionally, transwell systems may not fully capture short‐range paracrine signaling, particularly for factors with limited diffusion or stability [45, 46, 47, 48]. As such, the observed contact dependence may reflect both direct cell–cell interactions and locally restricted signaling that is not effectively recapitulated under transwell conditions. Collectively, our findings suggest that fibroblast migratory responses during wound closure require sufficient macrophage accumulation to generate effective pro‐migratory signaling. This observation aligns with the dynamic changes in macrophage abundance during wound healing, where increased immune cell infiltration precedes the transition from an inflammatory to a pro‐repair state [26].

Because macrophage‐fibroblast interactions are bidirectional during wound healing, we also examined how fibroblasts influence macrophage cytokine secretion. Coculture with 3T3s increased BMDM IL10 secretion and reduced TNFα levels under both pro‐inflammatory and mixed pro‐repair conditions secretion in a contact‐associated manner (Figure 1e,f). TNFα levels were similarly reduced when macrophages were separated from fibroblasts using a transwell, indicating that this suppression is driven primarily by soluble factors rather than direct contact. These findings align with prior work showing that fibroblast‐derived signals attenuate macrophage inflammatory activity and promote pro‐healing phenotypes; fibroblast conditioned media has been shown to reduce macrophage nitric oxide production after LPS/IFNγ stimulation and enhance arginase‐1 expression when macrophages are stimulated simultaneously with IL4 [49]. Another study also demonstrated that both juxtacrine and paracrine coculture with 3T3s suppress BMDM TNFα production, supporting our observations [6]. The elevated IL10 in our cocultures may contribute to this effect, as IL10 is known to inhibit TNFα expression through STAT3 activation [50, 51]. In summary, our findings suggest that fibroblasts engage multiple signaling modalities that are associated with differential regulation of anti‐inflammatory and pro‐inflammatory cytokines via contact and soluble pathways, respectively.

CAMs are increasingly being recognized as critical mediators of immune‐stromal communication during tissue repair [10, 11, 52]. Among them, connexins and integrins have been shown to facilitate macrophage‐fibroblast communication in the context of fibrosis [10, 11]. We found that coculture altered the expression of connexins and integrins in both BMDMs and 3T3s, suggesting that physical interactions reshape their communication (Figure 2a). Inhibition of connexins with PA selectively reduced IL10 secretion but did not alter TNFα secretion (Figure 2b,c). This aligns with our earlier observations: IL10 upregulation required direct contact, while TNFα suppression persisted in paracrine cocultures, highlighting distinct regulatory pathways for each cytokine. Together, these results suggest that connexin‐mediated signaling may contribute to the regulation of IL10 secretion, distinguishing this response from soluble inflammatory suppression.

Calcium is a critical regulator of macrophage inflammatory signaling and fibroblast function [16, 19, 53]. Using Salsa6f‐expressing cells, we found that direct contact increased Ca2+ events in BMDMs but decreased them in fibroblasts, and PA treatment abolished the increase in BMDMs without affecting 3T3s (Figure 2d–g). The absence of synchronized Ca2+ changes suggests that connexins may function as hemichannels rather than gap junctions in our system. These findings align with Ezzo et al. [10], who showed that IL4/IL13‐stimulated macrophages do not form functional gap junctions with fibroblasts as evidenced by negligible transfer of calcein‐AM between cell types. Bhattacharyya et al. [11] demonstrated that lung macrophages release ATP through Cx43 hemichannels, acting in a paracrine manner to increase Ca2+ events in fibroblasts. In our study, connexin‐dependent ATP release may similarly occur but act in an autocrine manner, driving Ca2+ signals within macrophages. Dosch et al. [29] established that macrophages release ATP via Cx43 hemichannels, which then binds to purinergic receptors on the same cell to trigger downstream signaling. Additional contact‐dependent mechanisms may also contribute; for example, macrophage integrins can activate Piezo1 in fibroblasts, modulating their calcium dynamics [10]. Consequently, our findings support a model in which physical contact activates macrophage connexin hemichannels, driving an autocrine calcium signaling loop rather than direct intercellular coupling. While our results also suggest that this connexin‐associated calcium signaling might be associated with IL10 secretion, direct causality remains to be established, and further studies are needed to clarify the precise molecular pathways involved.

In skin, wounding dynamically alters connexin and integrin expression [54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60]. For instance, Cx43 is rapidly downregulated in the epidermis over the first 24 h after injury, and subsequently upregulated by day 4, while connexin 26 (Cx26) expression increases as early as 6 h post‐injury and remains detectable in 6‐day‐old wounds [54, 56]. Integrin ß1 in keratinocytes is significantly upregulated by day 1 and persists in filipodia through day 3 post‐injury [60]. In line with these observations, we detected changes in the expression of these adhesion molecules after scratching (Figure 3a). However, the specific patterns we detected in vitro differed from those reported in vivo, possibly reflecting the limited capacity of our coculture system to recapitulate the full complexity of wound‐associated signals in vivo (Figure 3a). To assess whether connexins mediated scratch closure, we treated cultures with PA and observed no effect in either 3 T3 monocultures or cocultures (Figure 3b), unlike previous reports where Cx43 inhibition enhanced fibroblast migration [61, 62]. This discrepancy may arise from differences in the mode of connexin perturbation, as PA functions primarily as an acute channel uncoupler that closes the channel without removing connexins from the membrane [31, 63]. Notably, fibroblast migration has been shown to depend on non‐channel adhesive functions of Cx43, as Cx43 siRNA enhances migration whereas the channel‐blocking peptide Gap27 has no effect in dermal and neonatal fibroblasts [61]. Moreover, in cocultures, macrophage‐derived signals could further modulate closure, masking any effect of connexins. Consequently, our findings suggest a functional divergence in our system, where connexin channel activity may contribute to macrophage IL10 secretion, whereas fibroblast migration does not appear to be detectably influenced by channel function.

Cx43 has been extensively studied for its role in wound healing where its inhibition has been shown to reduce inflammation, accelerate wound closure and angiogenesis, modulate granulation tissue formation, enhance fibroblast activation, proliferation, and migration, increase keratinocyte proliferation, and reduce macrophage and neutrophil recruitment [33, 61, 64, 65, 66] In our experiments, Cx43 expression was upregulated in BMDMs during coculture and in 3T3s following coculture and scratch (Figures 2a and 3a). Cx43‐knockdown BMDMs cocultured with 3T3s showed reduced IL10 secretion under IL4/IL13/LPS stimulation, reinforcing our previous finding that PA dampens IL10 secretion and supporting a role for Cx43‐associated Ca2+ signaling in promoting IL10 production (Figure 3c). Although Cx43 deletion in peritoneal macrophages has been reported to reduce TNFα secretion after LPS stimulation, and a study using RAW264.7 macrophages suggested that Cx43 promotes M1 polarization, we did not observe changes in TNFα secretion in Cx43‐knockdown BMDMs cocultured with 3T3s (Figure 3d) [29, 30]. Therefore, these findings suggest that Cx43 may contribute to macrophage anti‐inflammatory signaling, with differential effects on IL10 and TNFα secretion during macrophage‐fibroblast interactions.

Consistent with our 2D experiments, 3D cultures showed that coculture with 3T3s enhances macrophage IL10 secretion in a connexin‐associated manner, while TNFα secretion is reduced through a contact‐independent mechanism (Figure 4). This indicates that fibroblasts can simultaneously promote anti‐inflammatory signaling while dampening pro‐inflammatory responses through distinct mechanisms. scRNA‐seq data analysis of 3D cultures revealed substantial heterogeneity within macrophages and fibroblasts populations, with clusters changing in abundance and transcriptional profile after wounding (Figure 5). Similar heterogeneity has been reported in scRNA‐seq studies of in vivo wounds, where multiple immune, stromal, and endothelial populations contribute to the dynamic wound environment [7, 27, 67]. The clusters showing the largest changes in relative abundance after wounding reflected a redistribution toward wound‐associated cell states, including macrophage populations with altered inflammatory and immune signaling gene expression and fibroblast subsets exhibiting distinct extracellular matrix expression programs. Taken together, these results indicate that in our 3D model, fibroblasts and macrophages exhibit transcriptional shifts into wound‐associated states, coinciding with the observed altered contact‐dependent cytokine secretion.

Further analysis of our scRNA‐seq data revealed that wounding induced changes in CAM expression across both macrophage and fibroblast clusters, with the most pronounced alterations occurring in fibroblast cluster F11 (Figure 6). Both F9 and F11 showed wounding‐associated modulation of connexins, including Gjb3, Gjb4, Gja1, and Gjc1, consistent with in vivo reports of dynamic connexin regulation during injury [28, 54, 56]. Macrophages exhibited broader changes in integrins (i.e., Itgb1, Itgb2, Itgb3, Itgad) reflecting their activation toward matrix remodeling and cell–cell interactions. Notably, integrin αvβ3 has been shown to mediate macrophage‐driven fibroblast activation during fibrosis, highlighting how these integrin changes align with macrophage‐fibroblast communication pathways activated during injury [10]. These transcriptional changes were also in line with our 2D coculture observations where connexin and integrin expression was altered in cocultures compared to monocultures (Figures 2a and 3a). The most substantial changes in adherens‐ and tight‐junction‐related genes occurred within F11. This cluster upregulated adherens junction components such as Cdh11, Jup, and Tjp1, as well as tight‐junction‐associated genes including Cldn12, Cldn19, Jam2, and Jam3. While tight and adherens junctions are traditionally associated with epithelial and endothelial cells, fibroblasts also express components of these pathways, where they are thought to contribute to mechanotransduction, and cell–cell interactions [68, 69]. Together, these findings indicate that wounding is associated with a reorganization of adhesion‐ and junction‐associated gene expression in distinct fibroblast and macrophage subsets, reflecting transcriptional programs characteristic of injury‐responsive states and potential changes in intercellular communication.

Finally, cell–cell communication analysis identified fibroblast cluster F11 as the dominant signal sender, with multiple macrophage clusters acting primarily as receivers, consistent with prior scRNA‐seq studies showing fibroblasts as highly dynamic regulators during wound healing [5, 7, 27] (Figure 7a). Wounding selectively modulated fibroblast‐to‐macrophage signaling, with predicted changes across several ligand‐receptor pairs, including Vcam1‐Itgb1, Rarres2‐Cmklr1 and Adm‐Calcrl, suggesting remodeling of immune cell recruitment and activation‐associated pathways (Figure 7b,c) [36, 37, 70, 71, 72]. Interestingly, IL10 also emerged as a selectively modulated pathway, representing a consistent signal across our scRNA‐seq analysis and 2D and 3D coculture experiments under both wounding and contact‐dependent conditions (Figures 2b, 3c, and 4c). These findings support a role for fibroblast‐macrophage interactions in regulating macrophage IL10 production. In parallel, the PROS pathway was predicted to be upregulated, with inferred Gas6‐Axl/Mertk interactions potentially associated with macrophage polarization, efferocytosis‐associated signaling, as well as fibroblast ECM regulation [73, 74]. Notably, Gas6‐Axl signaling has been reported to play a functional role in wound repair, with disruption of this pathway impairing tissue repair outcomes in vivo [75]. Thus, our analysis suggests that wounding reshapes bidirectional fibroblast‐macrophage communication through coordinated reprogramming of intercellular signaling pathways.

This study demonstrates that macrophage‐fibroblast interactions during wound repair are governed by both contact‐dependent and paracrine signaling mechanisms that differentially influence cellular behavior. Our findings suggest that cell–cell contact is associated with macrophage activation and may involve connexin‐mediated calcium signaling in cytokine secretion. While connexin inhibition in this study relied on a broad uncoupler in select functional assays, limiting isoform‐specific interpretation and precluding attribution to individual connexins, our findings support a broader role for connexin‐mediated signaling in macrophage‐fibroblast communication. Single‐cell transcriptomic analysis further indicates that wounding reshapes the transcriptional landscape of both fibroblasts and macrophages, with dynamic regulation of CAMs and intercellular communication pathways and identifies fibroblasts as dominant signal senders directing macrophage behavior. While our in vitro models cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of the wound environment in vivo, they allow precise dissection of fibroblast‐macrophage communication and the mechanisms by which contact and paracrine signals influence cellular behavior. An additional limitation is the use of 3 T3 cells as a model fibroblast system; future studies using primary dermal fibroblasts in more physiologically relevant systems, such as human skin equivalent organoids, will be important to validate these findings. Nonetheless, by defining the interactions underlying the immune‐stromal synapse in the context of wounding, we provide insight into the regulatory networks governing macrophage‐fibroblast crosstalk, suggesting that distinct contact‐dependent and paracrine checkpoints shape the outcome of tissue repair.

In contrast, M5 is defined by selective enrichment of Lpl and loss of homeostatic identity, accompanied by reduced Bcl2a1b expression relative to M14, consistent with diminished survival signaling and the onset of cellular turnover.
