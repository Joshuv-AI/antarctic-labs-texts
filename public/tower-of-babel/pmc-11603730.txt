Assessing the prevalence of unused medicines at home and associated factors: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Addisu Afrassa Tegegne, Yesuneh Tefera Mekasha, Million Girma, Liknaw Workie Limenh, Lamrot Yohannes, Gebremariam Genet, Abdulwase Mohammed Seid, Tekletsadik Tekleslassie Alemayehu, Habtamu Semagne Ayele, Wondim Ayenew, Wudneh Simegn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrescriptions come with extensive instructions on taking medicines but provide little information about the burden of unused medicines. This issue centers around the vast quantities of unused medications accumulated in households. The study aimed to assess the prevalence of unused medicines and associated factors in Gondar City.MethodThe study was conducted through a community-based cross-sectional survey in Gondar City from July 30 to August 30, 2023. The data was collected using the Kobo toolbox, exported into an Excel sheet, and then analyzed using SPSS version 27 software. Both multivariate and bivariate binary logistic regressions were performed in the data analysis. A p-value of 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval was used to determine statistical significance.ResultThe study interviewed 786 participants, with a 92.8% response rate. 55% of the 786 households surveyed kept unused medications at home, most commonly antibiotics (37%), antipyretics (17.6%), and analgesics (15%). Factors associated with having unused medicines at home included not receiving medication information (AOR = 2.327, 95% CI: 1.535, 3.527), having a chronic disease (AOR = 5.897, 95% CI: 3.667, 9.484), having health insurance coverage (AOR = 1.456, 95% CI: 1.036, 2.047), self-medicating with NSAIDs (AOR = 2.018, 95% CI: 1.396, 2.919), and poor disposal practices (AOR = 3.613, 95% CI: 2.546, 5.127).ConclusionThe study found a considerable high prevalence of unused medications in Gondar City due to factors like lack of medication education, chronic conditions, insurance coverage, self-medication, and poor disposal practices. To address this, public education programs and safe disposal guidelines should be implemented to promote safer practices.Keywords: Unsued medicines, Households, Medicine acquisition, Pevalence, Ethiopia

Introduction

Perpetually, the world faces a silent crisis that impacts the safety within our homes, the health of its populace, and the environment. This centered around the vast quantities of unused medications accumulated in every household [1]. The widespread availability of medicines has made it easier for consumers to obtain them. However, sometimes patients do not use up all of the medication they have procured [2]. As a result, these unused medicines often end up stored in their home that may be in drawers or simply thrown away with regular household trash [3]. The wrong hands can easily access this prevalence of unused medicines. Hence, accidental ingestion can result in poisoning, hospitalization, or even tragic outcomes [4].

The presence of unused medicines in the home can lead to inappropriate self-medication practices. Research conducted by Akici, A. et al., 2018 has identified an association between having unused medications at home and engaging in self-medication behaviors [5]. Hence, home acquisition of leftover medicines, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, opioids, and antimicrobials could lead to self-medication practices [5]. Leftover antibiotics are commonly found in household medicine cabinets, which poses a public health concern due to the risk of promoting antibiotic-resistant infections. Patients not finishing their full course of antibiotics, or stopping the medication prematurely, are major contributors to the development of antimicrobial resistance [6]. Additionally, people may take these unused antibiotics from past infections to self-treat new infections. This can delay seeking proper medical care and getting an accurate diagnosis. It also increases the chances of using inappropriate or suboptimal antibiotic treatments, rather than receiving the most suitable antibiotic for the current infection [7].

In many countries, the lack of effective public regulations for the proper disposal of unused medications results in the accumulation of these drugs in households [8–10]. This poses risks to the environment and has broader implications for “One Health,” which links human, animal, and environmental health [11]. For instance, Ethiopia currently lacks a national policy for effectively controlling the safe disposal of unused drugs, and the safe disposal of pharmaceutical waste is not enforced at the household level [12, 13]. In contrast, Kenya is considered a benchmark for pharmaceutical waste disposal, with comprehensive guidelines and procedures in place [14].

Household acquisition of unused medicines was presented by different literature as a considerably higher magnitude. A study was done in Malaysia, out of the participants, 84% reported having unused medicines [15]. Another study conducted in Pakistan corroborates the high rate of unused medicines which was reported as 87% of the respondents had unused medicines in their homes [16]. A similar study was conducted in Tanzania, 70.19% had medications kept in their houses. The incomplete course of treatment after feeling that they had recovered from illness was reported as the main reason for keeping unused medications at home [17]. A study conducted by Bekker, C. L., et al. reported that the expiration of medicines and medical conditions improvement, no longer using the full prescription, changing treatment plans, passing away, or hoarding the leftover drugs are among the reasons for their home acquisition [18, 19]. Moreover, non-adherence to the prescribed treatment regimen is another major factor leading to the accumulation of unused medicines in households [15]. This irrational use of medications represents a significant public health issue worldwide. In other words, having easy access to these unused medicines at home facilitates inappropriate usage patterns, which can then lead to negative health consequences for the patient, such as side effects and ineffective treatment [20].

Further research in different areas of the Ethiopian community has uncovered substantial rates of households accumulating unused medicines. For example, a survey conducted in the Awi zone found that 13.8% of sampled households had unused medications stored at home [21]. An even more significant issue was observed in Arba Minch, where nearly half (49.4%) of the households were discovered to have leftover medicines [22]. Studies exploring the medicine wastage problems were conducted in Gondar city; however. Most of the investigations were not conducted among the general public. Previous studies in different settings were conducted at Gondar Hospital and the Public Health Center in Gondar city reported the prevalence of unused medicines at 89.1% and 55.9% respectively [23, 24]. However, no more investigation was done to address what factors could lead to the prevalence of unused medicines. Thus, it is crucial to conduct research within the general community to understand how prevalent the issue of acquiring and accumulating unused medicines in their homes. Factors associated with investigation findings could help inform strategies to mitigate the issue. Consequently, this study aimed to assess the current prevalence and associated factors involved in the acquisition of unused medicines within households in Gondar City, located in northwestern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study settings, design, and period

A cross-sectional community-based study was conducted in Gondar City, located in the Amhara Region of northwest Ethiopia. Gondar City is situated 728 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. This city is among the most ancient and largely populated in the country. According to a report by the Central Statistical Agency (CSA) in 2021, the total population size of Gondar city was 443,156 [25]. The Gondar city is characterized by rapid population growth, driven by both natural increase and migration from rural to urban areas. During the study period, the city administration reported a population of 454,445 for the 2021/2022 fiscal year. The city has six sub-cities and 36 administrative Kebele units [26]. The study was conducted from July 30 to August 30, 2023. A total of 847 households were selected, which corresponds to the total number of participants included in the study.

Population

The source population for this study was all the residents of Gondar city while the study population consists of residents living in the selected households.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Individuals over the age of 18, of any gender, who were the primary persons responsible for the acquisition of unused medications within the selected households were included. Subjects who declined to allow observation of the unused medication packaging labels or the medications themselves were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size of 847 was calculated using a formula for a single population proportion, based on a prior study in Arba Minch town where 49.4% of households had unused medicines [22]. This calculation incorporated a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error, and an additional 10% for non-response rate. Additionally, a design effect of 2 was factored into the final sample size by multiplying the result. Owing to the homogeneity of the population, the researchers randomly selected nine Kebeles (Bilajig, Arebegnoch, Teda, Fasiledes, Kerkos, Ayra, Abajale, Angereb, and Hidase) out of the total 36 Kebeles to be included in the study, using a lottery method. The total sample size of 847 was proportionately allocated to each of the nine selected Kebeles using a stratified sampling technique. Within each Kebele, the samples were selected in proportion to the total population, with the number of households serving as the sampling frame. A systematic random sampling approach was utilized to select households from each of the nine Kebeles. The sampling interval for each Kebele was established by dividing the total number of households in that Kebele by its proportionally allocated sample size. Within each Kebele, a single street was randomly chosen, and the household with the lowest address number on that street was the starting point. Thereafter, every Kth household on the street was interviewed, following a consistent order (Fig. 1).

Data Collection Instrument and Data Collection Procedure

The data collection tool used in this study was adapted from previous research [15, 21, 22, 27]. The questionnaire comprised two parts - Part 1 covered socio-demographic information, while Part 2 assessed the presence of unused medications, household health status, and details about stored medicines. A single yes/no question was used to determine the prevalence of unused medications, similar to prior studies [21, 22]. The initial questionnaire was developed in English and then translated into Amharic, the local language. After adjusting, including deleting, rephrasing, and adding instructions, the final questionnaire was compiled. For electronic data collection, the questionnaire was structured within the KoboToolbox software (https://www.kobotoolbox.org/) for use on mobile devices and tablets during field data gathering. The data was recorded using structured data collection forms. In cases where the person responsible for the acquisition of unused medicines was unavailable during data collection, any household member aged above 18 and the senior member was selected to participate in the study. If no household member was accessible at the time of data collection, the next household in the sampling sequence was chosen instead. The unused medications kept in each household were observed, and the various types of unused medications were systematically recorded.

Data collectors and training

In this study, data collectors were selected based on their previous relevant knowledge of the topic of interest, which can better understand the nuance of the subject matter and lead to more insightful data collection. Four people with a Bachelor of Pharmacy degree were selected for the data collection. All had a mobile smartphone that supports to run an application tool for the data collection. Data collection training was provided to all personnel over two days, with the supervisor in attendance. The training covered the study’s objectives, an introduction to the data collection software (KoboToolbox), and guidance on recording respondents’ answers to each question. The training also includes information on how to troubleshoot and deal with technical difficulties that might arise with the collection software.

Variables of the study

Dependent variable

Prevalence of unused medicines.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors like gender, age, and educational background, as well as prior information, chronic disease history, self-medication, and medication usage within the past six months.

Measuring technique

The survey questions covered the participants’ age, gender, income, education level, and presence of children under 12 years old. The subsequent questions included: (1) Do you have any unused medicines at home? (2) Reasons for having unused medicines at home. (3) Diseases/symptoms in households with unused medicines. (4) How medicines were obtained. (5) Formulations of medicines kept in households. (6) Classes of medicines kept in households.

Participants were asked if they had any unused medicines in their home. Those who answered “yes” were considered to have unused medicines at home. The type of unused medicines was identified by examining the packaging or the medications themselves. The unused medicines were then categorized based on their pharmacological classification. The dosage forms of the unused medicines, such as tablets, capsules, liquids, injections, and semisolid formulations, were also identified and categorized.

Data Quality Control

The questionnaire was initially developed in English, then translated into the local language of Amharic, and finally back-translated into English to ensure consistency. The questionnaire was reviewed to verify the inclusion of meaningful sentences and the incorporation of important points. A pre-test was carried out on 5% of the households from a different Kebele in Gondar city, which is distinct from the randomly selected Kebeles. Necessary corrections were then made to address any potential issues, unanticipated interpretations, and cultural objections in the questionnaire.

Data collectors were provided with training on the study’s objective, the questionnaire, and the process of conducting interviews and recording responses. Finally, before data entry, the collected data was reviewed and checked by the investigators for completeness. The data was password-protected on the computer and only shared with the research team.

Data Analysis

The data was exported into SPSS version 27 for analysis after being cleaned in Excel. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and frequencies, were calculated to analyze the data. A binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify variables associated with the dependent variable. We performed a purposeful selection of covariates based on the Wald test from the bivariate logistic regression, using a p-value threshold of 0.25. The variables with a p-value less than 0.25 were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. Finally, adjusted odds ratio (AOR) at a 95% confidence level and a p value less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Result

Socio-demographic and economic information

In this study, 786 participants were interviewed, with a response rate of 92.8%. Of the total respondents, 228 (29.0%) were between the ages of 26 and 30 years. The mean age of the mothers was 35.77 (± 13.27) years, with ages ranging from 19 to 85 years. The majority of the respondents, 335 (42.6%), were employed, and 442 (53.7%) were married. More than half of the respondents, 432 (55.0%), were male. Regarding educational status, almost half of the respondents, 357 (45.4%), had a college degree or higher, while 103 (13.1%) could not read and write. The family sizes varied, with single-person households being the most common at 23.9%, followed by families with 5 or more members at 20.2%, 3-person families at 20.0%, 2-person households at 18.8%, and 4-person families being the least common at 17.1% (Table 1).

Prevalence of unused medicines

The survey asked households if they had any unused medicine at home. Out of the 786 total households surveyed, 432 (55%) said they still kept unused medicine at home (Fig. 2). Figure 3 shows the most common types of unused medicines found in these households. The most common were antibiotics, which were present in 37% of households. Antipyretics (fever reducers) and analgesics (pain relievers) were also commonly kept, found in 17.6% and 15% of households respectively. Medications for cardiovascular disease, such as antihypertensive agents, were still kept in around 7.4% of the households surveyed. In terms of dosage forms, solid dosage forms were the most common medication formulations, of which tablets and capsules account for 32.2% and 25.7%, respectively (Table 2).

Reasons for the presence of unused medicines

The reasons that influence people’s decisions to keep unused medicines at home are summarized in Table 3. The most prominent factor was that 34% of respondents indicated they stop taking a medication once they feel better, and keep the remaining medicine. A sizeable percentage, 29.9%, simply buy medicines and store them at home in case they get sick in the future. Additionally, 12.7% of respondents keep unused medicines past their expiration date. Another group, 12% of respondents, has unused medicines because their doctor changed their medication and told them to stop the previous one. Other factors (2.1%) that play a role, but to a lesser degree, include not wanting to use the medicines and difficulty following instructions. The most prevalent symptoms among household members in homes that kept unused medicines were (fever, body aches, and lack of appetite) at 24.8%, and upper respiratory infections (sore throat, cough) at 32.4% (Table 3).

Associated factors for the prevalence of unused medicines

The results of multiple logistic regression analysis showed that independent factors associated with the acquisition of unused medicines are: age, receiving any information on how to dispose of unused pharmaceuticals, having chronic illness, health insurance coverage, self-medication with NSAIDs, and practice toward proper disposal of unused medicines. The odds of having unused medicines at home is 2.327 (AOR = 2.327, 95% CI: 1.535, 3.527) higher in respondents who had not received any information, and 5.897 (AOR = 5.897, 95% CI (3.667, 9.484) in respondents having a chronic disease. In the same vein, possessing unused medicines were found to be 1.456 times higher AOR = 1.456, 95% CI: 1.036, 2.047) among respondents who had a health insurance coverage. It was 2.018 times more likely for participants to have unused medicines if they have self-medication with NSAIDs (AOR = 2.018; 95% CI: 1.396, 2.919) than their counterparts. Also, the odds of unused medications at home were 3.613 times (AOR = 3.613, 95% CI: 2.546, 5.127) higher among those with poor practice towards properly disposing of unused medicines (Table 4).

Discussion

Household acquisition of unused medicines was presented among more than half of the respondents in our study. This prevalence was found to be lower than as compared to similar studies conducted in Riyadh city, Saudi Arabia, at 89.3% [27]; 68% in Sunyani city, Ghana [28]; and 67.1% in Singur, West Bengal, India [29]. This might be attributed to the difference in access to pharmacies, where in Riyadh city and Singur city, people could have easy access to them. Compared to the report of Ghana, the pattern in self-medication practices may lead to a substantially higher prevalence, in which evidence showed that self-medication is indeed an unresolved menace in Sunyani city [30]. In contrast, the prevalence was found to be considerably higher than studies conducted in Turkey (28.0%) [5]; Maiduguri city, Nigeria(35.0%) [31], and Arba Minch, Ethiopia (49.4%) [22].

Our finding is also closer to the 52.4% rate reported in a previous study conducted in Adigrat city, Ethiopia [32]. This similarity can be attributed to Ethiopia’s lack of public education and awareness campaigns about the proper disposal of unused medicines at the national level. Additionally, the absence of an established drug take-back system in Ethiopia compounds this issue [33]. This results in their presence and could promote self-medication using leftover medicines for the treatment of familiar symptoms. Among the unused medicines found in households, the most common were antibiotics, present in 37% of respondents. This is comparable to similar studies that found antibiotics in 36.7% of households in Adigrat City, Ethiopia [32], 48.7% in Riyadh city [27], 46.5% in Sunyani city [28], 28.1% in Singur city [29], and 64% in Arba Minch [22]. The substantial high prevalence may be due to antibiotics being dispensed without prescription, as shown in an Ethiopian study where more than 80% of visits to community drug outlets resulted in obtaining non-prescribed antibiotics [34]. The widespread presence of unused antibiotics in households raises serious public health issues, including the menace of antimicrobial resistance [35]. This resistance can develop due to the easy availability of leftover antibiotics at home and the tendency to share them with family and friends for similar symptoms. Moreover, the acquisition of unused antibiotics at home can result in improper disposal, such as throwing them in the trash or flushing them down the toilet, which may lead to their presence in the environment. Addressing this issue requires improving health literacy and knowledge, which can enhance awareness of antibiotic resistance and positively impact the general public behaviors [36]. Unfortunately, there is no specially designed national guidelines have been in place for the proper disposal of antibiotics in Ethiopia. Thus, policies and guidelines need to be made at the national level to educate the general public about the proper disposal of unused antibiotics and the implications of antibiotic resistance.

Our findings also indicate that antipyretics (fever reducers) and analgesics (pain relievers) were commonly kept by one-third of respondents. Other studies have reported higher rates of analgesics at home, with 48.8% in Sunyani city [28], 41.5% in Adigrat city [32], and a lower rate of 31.25% in Singur city [29]. This might be related to the easy availability of over-the-counter antipyretics, which can result in improper buying, accumulation, and storage by individuals for common illness symptoms. Research has shown that self-medication with pain relievers in Ethiopia was reported at a rate of 46.1%, which supports the current findings [37]. The reasons for possessing unused medicines in this study were mostly to stop taking medication once they felt better and keep the remaining medicine. A sizeable percentage, one-third, simply buy medicines and store medicines at home in case they get sick in the future. Similar findings, with varying frequencies, have been reported in studies conducted in Adigrat city [32], Riyadh city [27], and Singur city [29] corroborating the current study findings. The main reasons cited were discontinuation of the drugs due to recovery from the illness and also keeping the medicine for future use.

It was found that respondents who had not received any information about proper medicine disposal were two times more likely to have unused medicines. This finding aligns with a previous study by Arba Minch [22], which showed respondents lacking information were 1.4 times more likely to have unused medicines at home. This reinforces that lack of awareness is a key driver of unused medicines accumulation at home. Additionally, the study found that respondents with chronic illnesses were 5.9 times more likely to possess unused medicines at home. In this study, the doctor changing medication and stopping previous treatment for chronic illness accounts for 12% of respondents’ acquisition. This is evidenced by the fact that frequent adjustments to medication regimens and patient non-compliance are the most commonly cited challenges in the treatment of chronic diseases [38]. Respondents who paid for out-of-pocket medicines were 1.46 times more likely to have unused medication than those whose health insurance covered their medical expenses. A study in the Awi zone corroborated this and showed that people who pay for medicines out of pocket by themselves were found 2.6 times more likely to have unused medicines [21]. Prior research found out-of-pocket medicine costs are 66% of total healthcare spending in Ethiopia [39]. The high out-of-pocket costs for medicines can push households into catastrophic healthcare payments, leading people to save unused medications at home.

It was 2.018 times more likely for participants to have unused medicines if they have self-medication with NSAIDs than their counterparts. Self-medication with NSAIDs often focuses on treating the immediate symptoms, rather than addressing the underlying condition. This could result in individuals continuing to acquire and use NSAIDs for recurring or persistent symptoms, leading to a surplus of unused medicines [40]. Respondents with poor unused medicines disposal practices were approximately four times higher in the acquisition of unused medicines at home. The lack of awareness of proper disposal methods and inaccessible take-back programs can discourage people from properly disposing of unused medicines, leading to their accumulation at home. Tackling the factors that contribute to poor disposal practices, such as by enhancing public awareness campaigns, expanding the availability of convenient return options, and encouraging responsible disposal habits, could help mitigate the accumulation of unused medicines in people’s homes [41].

Study limitation

The study’s findings may have limited applicability beyond the Gondar city community, as it was conducted solely within that local area. Additionally, the self-reported information from participants about their past medication use could be subject to recall bias. A larger-scale survey that encompasses remote areas of Ethiopia would be beneficial to gain more comprehensive insights.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study revealed that a worthwhile high prevalence of unused medications among the general public in Gondar City was observed. Respondents who had not been informed about proper medication use were more likely to have unused medicines. Those with a chronic disease were also more likely to have unused medicines. Participants who engaged in self-medication with NSAIDs were more likely to have unused medicines. Finally, respondents with poor practices towards properly disposing of unused medicines were more likely to have unused medicines at home. The study highlights critical areas for intervention and improvement, aiming to enhance public health and safety by reducing the accumulation of unused medications.
