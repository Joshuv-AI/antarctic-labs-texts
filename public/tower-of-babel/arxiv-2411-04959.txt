Abstract

1.
Introduction

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an analytic set of Hausdorff dimension
a
. For any
e\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
, let
\pi_{e}A
denote the orthogonal projection of
A
onto the line through the origin in the direction of
e
. In
[
6
]
, Marstrand proved that, for almost every
e\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
,

\dim_{H}(\pi_{e}A)=\min\{\dim_{H}(A),1\}.

Given the almost everywhere nature of Marstrand’s theorem, it is natural to investigate the size of the exceptional set of directions. That is, given
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
s\leq\min\{\dim_{H}(A),1\}
, to bound the dimension of the exceptional set of
A
,

E_{s}(A)=\{e\in\mathcal{S}^{1}\mid\dim_{H}(\pi_{e}A)<s\}.

Kaufman
[
4
]
gave the first non-trivial upper bound, showing that
\dim_{H}(E_{s}(A))\leq s
. Oberlin
[
8
]
conjectured that, for any analytic set
A
of Hausdorff dimension
a
and any
s\leq\min\{a,1\}
,
\dim_{H}(E_{s}(A))\leq\max\{2s-a,0\}
. In a recent breakthrough, Ren and Wang
[
11
]
proved Oberlin’s conjecture.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an analytic set,
a=\dim_{H}(A)
, and
0<s\leq\min\{a,1\}
. Then

\dim_{H}(\{e\in\mathcal{S}^{1}\mid\dim_{H}(\pi_{e}A)<s\})\leq\max\{2s-a,0\}.

In this paper, we consider the higher-dimensional analog of the exceptional set estimate. Let
G(n,k)
denote the set of all
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any
V\in G(n,k)
, let
\pi_{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow V
denote the orthogonal projection onto
V
. Mattila
[
7
]
generalized Marstrand’s theorem in this context, showing that for any analytic
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)=\min\{\dim_{H}(A),k\}
, for almost every
V\in G(n,k)
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
a,s\in\mathbb{R}
such that
k<n
,
a\leq n
and
0<s\leq\min\{a,k\}
. Define

T_{n,k}(a,s)=\sup_{A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\dim_{H}(A)=a}\dim_{H}(\{V\in G(n,k)\mid\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)<s\}),

where the supremum is taken over all analytic subsets.

Mattila
[
7
]
proved that

(1)

T_{n,k}(a,s)\leq k(n-k)+s-k.

Note that Mattila’s bound generalizes Kaufman’s bound to higher dimensions.

Mattila’s bound was subsequently improved by several authors. Falconer
[
1
]
, and later Peres and Schlag
[
9
]
, proved that

(2)

T_{n,k}(a,s)\leq k(n-k)+s-a

which improves Mattila’s bound when
a>k
. Ren
[
10
]
gave an
\varepsilon
-improvement of Mattila’s bound for the case where
k=n-1
. He
[
3
]
showed that if
k=1
or
k=n-1
then
T_{n,k}(a,\frac{k}{n}a)\leq k(n-k)-1
, which improves previous bounds for certain values of
a
. Finally, Gan
[
2
]
further improved these bounds and showed that his bounds are sharp for certain ranges of
a
and
s
.

Gan’s bounds are difficult to calculate due to their reliance on the Brascamp-Lieb inequality (which is expressed in terms of a
\sup
over an
\inf
) and his paper only provides more explicit bounds for certain values of
a
and
s
. Gan has shown that these explicit bounds are sharp when either
k\leq\frac{n}{2}
,
1<a\leq 2
and
s
falls in a certain range depending on
a
or
k\geq\frac{n}{2}
,
n-1<a\leq n
and
s
falls in a certain range depending on
a
. For more detail on Gan’s bounds and when they are sharp, it is best to consult Gan’s paper directly.
However, in general, sharp bounds for
T_{n,k}(a,s)
are unknown.

The main goal of this paper is to prove sharp upper bounds on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
in the cases where
k=1
and
k=n-1
, as well as to provide lower bounds on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
for all values of
n
and
k
, which we conjecture to be sharp. To do so, we introduce a function
S_{n,k}(a,s)
.

Definition 1.2
.

For any
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
a,s\in\mathbb{R}
, let
S_{n,k}(a,s)
denote

S_{n,k}(a,s)=k(n-k)-\left(\lfloor a-s\rfloor+1\right)\left(k-\lceil s\rceil+1\right)+\max\{0,\lfloor a-s\rfloor+2s-a-\lceil s\rceil+1\}.

Remark 1.3
.

Since it is somewhat difficult to understand the function
S_{n,k}(a,s)
simply by staring at the formula, we have included Figure

, which shows the values of
S_{n,k}(a,s)

We show that
S_{n,k}(a,s)
is always a lower bound on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
and that when
k=1
or
k=n-1
, they are equal (and thus in these cases we have found a sharp upper bound for
T_{n,k}(a,s)
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
0<a\leq n
,
1\leq k\leq n-1
, and
\max\{0,a-n+k\}<s\leq\min\{a,k\}
. Then
T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq S_{n,k}(a,s)
. Furthermore, if
k=1
or
k=n-1
, then
T_{n,k}(a,s)=S_{n,k}(a,s)
.

Although we cannot show that
T_{n,k}(a,s)
is equal to
S_{n,k}(a,s)
for all values of
n,k,a
and
s
, we conjecture that it is.

Conjecture 1.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
0<a\leq n
,
1\leq k\leq n-1
, and
\max\{0,a-n+k\}<s\leq\min\{a,k\}
. Then
T_{n,k}(a,s)=S_{n,k}(a,s)
.

The restrictions on
n,k,a
and
s
in the theorem and conjecture above are included in order to rule out trivial cases. In particular, note that for every
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
V\in G(n,k)
,
A\subseteq\pi_{V}A\times V^{\perp}
and therefore
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)\geq\dim_{H}(A)-(n-k)
. Since we always have
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)\geq 0
, it follows that if
s\leq\max\{0,a-n+k\}
, then
T_{n,k}(a,s)=0
.

Remark 1.6
.

Proposition 37 of Gan
[
2
]
gives a lower bound on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
. A careful comparison of our lower bounds with this proposition shows that the two lower bounds are equal in the regions that Gan calls Type I, II, and III while in Type IV our lower bounds are larger. In addition, we mentioned above that Gan finds sharp bounds for
T_{n,k}(a,s)
for certain ranges of
a
and
s
and it is not difficult to verify that these bounds agree with our lower bounds in this range.

2.
Proof of lower bound

In this section, we will prove the first part of Theorem
1.4
, namely that for any
n,k,a
and
s
which satisfy the assumptions of the theorem,
T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq S_{n,k}(a,s)
. We prove this by induction on
n
, with
n=2
as the base case.

When
n=2
, we have
k=1
and
\max\{0,a-1\}<s\leq\min\{a,1\}
. Therefore
\lceil s\rceil=1
,
\lfloor a-s\rfloor=0
and
S_{n,k}(a,s)=\max\{0,2s-a\}
. Kaufman
[
5
]
showed that if
2s-a\geq 0
, then indeed
T_{n,1}(a,s)\geq 2s-a
. On the other hand, if
2s-a<0
then the lower bound is trivial.

Now let
n\geq 3
be arbitrary. Our proof is based on the following, easy observations:

(i)

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
A^{\prime}=A\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
V\in G(n-1,k)
,
V^{\prime}\in G(n,k)
and
\pi_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}V^{\prime}=V
(this is projection onto the first

(n-1)
coordinates), then
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)=\dim_{H}(\pi_{V^{\prime}}A^{\prime})
. Consequently,
T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq T_{n-1,k}(a,s)+k
. Note that the added
k
is because there are
k
degrees of freedom when picking a
V^{\prime}
which projects onto a given
V
. Namely, to form
V^{\prime}
we can pick a basis for
V
(as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
) and then fill in whatever we want in the
n^{\text{th}}
coordinate of each vector in this basis.

(ii)

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
A^{\prime}=A\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
V\in G(n-1,k)
,
V^{\prime}=V\times\{0\}\in G(n,k)
, then
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)=\dim_{H}(\pi_{V^{\prime}}A^{\prime})
. Consequently,
T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq T_{n-1,k}(a-1,s)
.

(iii)

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
A^{\prime}=A\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
V\in G(n-1,k-1)
,
V^{\prime}=V\times\mathbb{R}\in G(n,k)
, then
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)=\dim_{H}(\pi_{V^{\prime}}A^{\prime})
. Consequently,
T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq T_{n-1,k-1}(a,s)
.

(iv)

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
A^{\prime}=A\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
V\in G(n-1,k-1)
,
V^{\prime}\in G(n,k)
,
V^{\prime}\supset V
, then
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)+1\geq\dim_{H}(\pi_{V^{\prime}}A^{\prime})
. Consequently,
T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq T_{n-1,k-1}(a-1,s-1)+n-k
. Note that the
(n-k)
term appears because there are
n-k
degrees of freedom when picking
V^{\prime}
which extends
V
. In particular, to extend
V^{\prime}
to
V
, we can pick one unit vector from the
n-(k-1)
-dimensional subspace
V^{\perp}
(where
V
is considered as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
rather than
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
).

Assume that
n,k,a,s
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.4
. We distinguish four cases: If
k,a,s>1
, then
n-1,k-1,a-1,s-1
also satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.4
, and hence, by (iv),

T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq T_{n-1,k-1}(a-1,s-1)+n-k\geq S_{n-1,k-1}(a-1,s-1)+n-k=S_{n,k}(a,s).

If
s\leq 1
,
a<1+s
, and
k\leq n-2
, then
n-1,k,a,s
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.4
, and hence, by (i),

T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq T_{n-1,k}(a,s)+k\geq S_{n-1,k}(a,s)+k=S_{n,k}(a,s).

If
s\leq 1
,
a\geq 1+s
, and
k\leq n-2
, then
n-1,k,a-1,s
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.4
, and hence, by (ii),

T_{n,k}(a,s)\geq T_{n-1,k}(a-1,s)\geq S_{n-1,k}(a-1,s)=S_{n,k}(a,s).

Finally, if
s\leq 1
and
k=n-1
, then
n-1,k-1,a,s
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1.4
, and hence, by (iii),

T_{n,n-1}(a,s)\geq T_{n-1,n-2}(a,s)\geq S_{n-1,n-2}(a,s)=S_{n,n-1}(a,s).\qed

3.
Proof of upper bounds

In this section, we will prove the second part of Theorem
1.4
, namely that when
k=1
or
k=n-1
,
T_{n,k}(a,s)\leq S_{n,k}(a,s)
. Our proof can be thought of as a ‘reverse’ of the proof of the lower bound discussed in the previous section. In order to explain what we mean by this, it helps to introduce the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
E\subseteq G(n,k)
are an
(n,k,a,s,t)
-pair if
A
is analytic, both
A
and
E
are nonempty,
\dim_{H}(A)=a
,
\dim_{H}(E)=t
, and
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}A)<s
for every
V\in E
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that
T_{n,k}(a,s)
is by definition the supremum of
t
over all
(n,k,a,s,t)
-pairs.

The key idea of our proof is that given an
(n+1,k,a,s,t)
-pair, we can use it to construct an
(n,k^{\prime},a^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
-pair for some appropriately chosen
k^{\prime},a^{\prime},s^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
. This allows us to lift upper bounds on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and thus prove our upper bound by induction on
n
(with Ren and Wang’s Theorem
1.1
providing the base case).

Our proof of the lower bound on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
from the previous section can be seen as dual to this proof. In particular, observations
(i)
through
(iv)
in that proof can all be seen as ways of taking an
(n,k,a,s,t)
-pair and using it to construct an
(n+1,k^{\prime},a^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
-pair for some appropriately chosen
k^{\prime},a^{\prime},s^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
. Moreover, these constructions closely mirror the constructions we will use in our proof of the upper bound—in a sense, the constructions in the proof of the upper bound can be seen as ways to “undo” the constructions from the proof of the lower bound.

The following four lemmas state precisely the different sorts of ways we have of turning an
(n+1,k,a,s,t)
-pair into an
(n,k^{\prime},a^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
-pair. The first pair of lemmas will be used to prove the upper bound on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
when
k=1
and are dual to observations
(i)
and
(ii)
, respectively, from the previous section.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Suppose that
0<a\leq n
,
a-n<s\leq\min\{a,1\}
,
s>0
, and
t>1
. If there is an
(n+1,1,a,s,t)
-pair, then there is an
(n,1,a,s,t^{\prime}-1)
-pair for every
1<t^{\prime}<t
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Suppose that
1<a\leq n+1
,
a-n<s\leq\min\{a-1,1\}
, and
s>0
. If there is an
(n+1,1,a,s,t)
-pair, then there is an
(n,1,a-1,s,t^{\prime})
-pair, where
t^{\prime}=\min\{t,n-1\}
.

The second pair of lemmas will be used to prove the upper bound on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
when
k=n-1
and are dual to observations
(iii)
and
(iv)
, respectively, from the previous section.

Lemma 3.5
.

If there is an
(n+1,n,a,s,t)
-pair, with
s\leq n-1
and
s>a-1
, then there is an
(n,n-1,a,s,t^{\prime})
-pair, where
t^{\prime}=\min\{t,n-1\}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

If there is an
(n+1,n,a,s,t)
-pair, with
a,s,t>1
, then there is an
(n,n-1,a-1,s-1,t^{\prime}-1)
-pair for all
1<t^{\prime}<t
.

Below, we will prove these four lemmas and then use them to complete the inductive arguments proving the upper bounds on
T_{n,k}(a,s)
. But first we will review some standard projection and slicing results that will be needed for the proofs of the lemmas.

Remark 3.7
.

In order to apply the projection and slicing results in the next subsection, we will often need to assume that for a given
(n,k,a,s,t)
-pair
(A,E)
, the set
E
of exceptional subspaces is analytic. We will now explain why it is always safe to make this assumption. Since
A
is analytic, it has an
F_{\sigma}
subset of the same dimension. Moreover, restricting to such a subset cannot shrink the set of exceptional subspaces and hence we may always assume that
A
is
F_{\sigma}
. It is then relatively straightforward to show that the set of exceptional subspaces of
A
, namely
\{V\in G(n,k)\mid\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}(A))<s\}
, is analytic. For the rest of this paper, we will assume without further comment that all
(n,k,a,s,t)
-pairs
(A,E)
have both
A
and
E
analytic.

3.1.
Projection and slicing results

Let
V\in G(n,n-1)
, and let
G(V,1)
be the set of one-dimensional linear subspaces of
V
. We denote the orthogonal projection of the lines in
G(n,1)\setminus\{V^{\perp}\}
onto
V
by

\pi_{V}:G(n,1)\setminus\{V^{\perp}\}\rightarrow G(V,1).

As discussed in the Introduction, Mattila
[
7
]
generalized Marstrand’s projection theorem to higher dimensions. We will need a simple corollary of his result in the context of orthogonal projections of subspaces.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
E\subseteq G(n,1)
. For almost every
V\in G(n,n-1)
,

\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}(E\setminus\{V^{\perp}\}))=\min\{\dim_{H}(E),n-2\}.

Proof.

Let
E^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the set of all points covered by the lines in
E
, i.e.,
E^{*}
is a union of lines through the origin. Note that
\dim_{H}(E^{*})=\dim_{H}(E)+1
. By Mattila’s projection theorem, for almost every
V
,
\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}E^{*})=\min\{\dim_{H}(E^{*}),n-1\}
. Therefore, for almost every
V\in G(n,n-1)
,

\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}(E\setminus\{V^{\perp}\}))=\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}(E\setminus\{V^{\perp}\})^{*})-1=\dim_{H}(\pi_{V}E^{*})-1=\min\{\dim_{H}(E),n-2\},

as claimed.
∎

Mattila
[
7
]
also proved the following slicing theorem.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
n-k\leq s\leq n
, and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be
\mathcal{H}^{s}
-measurable with
0<\mathcal{H}^{s}(A)<\infty
. Then, for almost all
V\in G(n,k)
,

\mathcal{H}^{n-k}(\{u\in V^{\perp}\mid\dim_{H}(A\cap(V+u))=s+k-n\})>0.

We will need the following corollary of Mattila’s slicing theorem.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
t>1
, and let
E\subset G(n,1)
be a compact set with
0<\mathcal{H}^{t}(E)<\infty
. Then, for positively many
V\in G(n,n-1)
,

(3)

\dim_{H}(\{W\in E\mid W\subset V\})=t-1.

Proof.

Write
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
. Without loss of generality we can assume that the dimension of the set of lines in
E
not contained in the ‘horizontal’ hyperplane
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is
t
. As before, let
E^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the set of points covered by the lines in
E
. Let
H
denote the horizontal affine hyperplane
H=\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\{1\}
. Let
A=E^{*}\cap H
,
i.e.,
A
is the set of points at which the lines in
E
intersect
H
(sometimes also called the gnomonic projection of
E
). Note that
\dim_{H}(A)=\dim_{H}(E)=t
.

By Theorem
3.9
, for almost every
V^{\prime\prime}\in G(n-1,n-2)
, there are positively many
(n-2)
-dimensional affine subspaces
V^{\prime}\subset H
parallel to
V^{\prime\prime}
