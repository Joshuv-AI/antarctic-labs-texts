Impact of Workplace Microaggressions on Surgeon Career Status and Trajectory: A Cross-Sectional Survey Study
Authors: Christine A. Heisler, Amy L. Godecker, Deborah Verran, Michael S. Sinha, Jerome Byam, Pringl Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Microaggressions in healthcare are associated with higher rates of part-time employment and early retirement, exacerbating the surgeon workforce shortage. However, the direct impact on surgeon career has not been investigated.Methods:This cross-sectional survey study evaluated career status and trajectory among American College of Surgeons (ACS) members who experienced workplace microaggressions. The survey was administered through the ACS Online Communities from November 2022 to January 2023. Primary outcomes were to characterize career impact and determine whether surgeons would choose a career in surgery again.Results:Of 377 ACS survey respondents, 249 (66.0%) experienced workplace microaggressions with 143 (of 249, 57.4%) taking action. When a supervisor was the perpetrator, career impact included microaggression escalation (P < 0.001) and changing jobs with a demotion (P = 0.025). Women surgeons who took action against microaggressions were less likely to report resolution of the microaggression than men surgeons (12.9% vs 39.1%, P = 0.005). Retaliation and no employer response were more common for women (P = 0.047), younger surgeons (P = 0.031) and those in specific surgical subspecialties (P = 0.035). Surgeons who experienced microaggressions in 2 or 3 jobs (out of 2 or 3 jobs) (n = 122) were more likely to say they would not choose a career in surgery again (P = 0.003).Conclusions:The professional role of the perpetrator directly impacted surgeon career trajectory, especially if the perpetrator was the supervisor. Addressing and mitigating workplace microaggressions could increase retention, and ensure access for patients who require surgical management.

INTRODUCTION

For nearly 2 decades, a shortage of surgeons in the United States has been forecasted.1 The Association of American Medical Colleges predicted a shortage of 10,000 to 19,900 surgeons by 2036,2 and early retirement attributed to an anticipated shortage of 3400 surgeons for the same time period.3 The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the attrition of healthcare professionals, with 36% of physicians surveyed in November 2020 intending to retire early or leave medicine altogether.4 The majority of surgical specialties are estimated to experience a workforce shortage that will increase clinical workload by 7 to 61% additional work relative value units by 2050.5

This workforce shortage exacerbates a serious public health crisis by creating inevitable delays in life-saving treatment and other quality of life interventions historically initiated and carried out by surgeons. This shortage has largely been ascribed to the increased clinical demands of an aging population coupled with aging out of the surgical workforce;6,7 therefore, consideration of factors that negatively impact surgeon retention are imperative. Career attrition occurs at steady rates across a number of surgical subspecialties, with higher rates of attrition for surgeons who identify as women,8 Black,9 and those with intersectional identities.10 Women in medicine are more likely than men to reduce their work hours within 6 years of completing training due to various types of workplace challenges, including exposure to microaggressions.11

Microaggressions are verbal, nonverbal or environmental insults or slights that originate from implicit bias and direct negative messaging toward a marginalized group or individual.12 Based on recent literature investigating the nature of microaggressions and those who are targeted, microaggressions occur within the surgical work environment regardless of practice type13,14 or surgical specialty.15–19 Research has begun to focus on understanding the professional impacts on surgeons who experience this form of discrimination.20 Surgeons who experience microaggressions endorse higher rates of professional burnout,20,21 negative emotional impact (selfdoubt, depression, and anxiety),22 increased rates of suicidal thoughts,23 and fewer workplace opportunities, especially if Underrepresented in Medicine (URiM).24

Concerns have been raised as to whether exposure to some types of microaggressions in the workplace contribute to the ongoing disparities in the representation of women and URiM within the surgical workforce.24,25 A surgeon faced with workplace microaggressions may consider leaving their workplace for another position; alternatively, they may remain in their current position if workplace microaggressions are mitigated. There are no prior studies evaluating the professional role of the microaggression perpetrator relative to the target, and how that relationship influences a surgeon’s career trajectory. The aim of this analysis was to explore the career trajectory of surgeons who experience workplace microaggressions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a planned secondary analysis of a cross-sectional survey study assessing surgeons’ experiences with microaggressions. We developed and internally validated a web-based survey and distributed the survey through nine American College of Surgeons (ACS) online Communities from November 2022 to January 2023. The ACS Communities platform offers an online platform for members to network, share ideas and promote professional development. ACS members choose to belong to online Communities based on their professional interest, though not all ACS members belong to or contribute to the Communities. All ACS Communities selected comprised members with experience in the surgical workforce. The specific Communities (and number of members at the time of initial survey invitation) included: advocacy (445), ACS wellness (136), diversity (295), international surgery (~3600), military surgery (927), senior surgeons (~3400), surgeon educators (990), women surgeons (~5100), and young fellows (~5300). The detailed study design has been previously reported26 and is included as Appendix A https://links.lww.com/AOSO/A623. Written approval to access the Communities was required and obtained from the ACS Communities administrator.

The survey adopted questions from the Racial and Ethnic Microaggression Scale,27 a validated instrument to measure racial microaggressions, and SexistMESS,28 a validated instrument to measure sexist microaggressions and impact on mental health. Microaggressions were rated on a Likert scale for frequency of 1 (never) to 5 (extremely often). Perpetrator roles included: supervisors, surgeon colleagues, support staff, students/trainees, and patients/families. Questions focused on the current or most recent job and the 2 jobs prior to that (if any), for a maximum total number of 3 jobs. Since no published data on surgeon career duration was available, we chose 3 jobs to create a longitudinal career platform from which a career trajectory could be based. Additional employment beyond 3 jobs was not included. Additional questions regarding academic rank (no rank, instructor, assistant professor, associate professor, full professor) at start and end of each job, as well as length of time in the job and at a specific rank, were included for those who indicated that their primary practice environment for that job was an academic medical center or a healthcare system with teaching responsibilities.

Details of perpetrator roles, types of microaggressions, and whether and how workplace microaggressions were addressed have previously been reported.29 Career impacts following microaggressions included staying in the same job and continuing to experience microaggressions, staying in the same job and microaggressions were reduced, job termination or contract not renewed because of formal reporting, and changed jobs due to microaggressions and procured a better, similar, or inferior job (yes/no for each impact). All responses included a free-text option, so respondents could include additional outcomes that were not categorized. Analyses included descriptive and chi-squared statistics, Fisher exact tests, t tests, and ANOVA. A threshold of P < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance. Analyses were conducted using Stata Release 18.30 The procedures, including obtaining informed consent, were done in accord with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. This study was granted exemption status by the University of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board.

RESULTS

A convenience sample of 377 ACS members completed the survey, with 249 respondents (66.0%) reporting they experienced workplace microaggressions in their 3 most recent workplaces. Perpetrator roles and the frequency of microaggressions experienced included surgeon colleagues (81.5%), supervisors (74.8%), nonsurgeon colleagues (ie, staff) (59.9%), patients/family members (58.5%), and trainees/learners (21.1%). A total of 143 (of 249, 57.4%) respondents took action after their microaggression experiences by reporting informally or formally, or through directly confronting the perpetrator. Women (P < 0.001), younger surgeons (P = 0.008), fellowship-trained surgeons (P = 0.005), surgeons in academic medicine, and government/veterans affairs (P = 0.001) and those unable to work or out of work (P = 0.019) were more likely to experience microaggressions and take action against them (Table 1). Additional details of perpetrator roles, whether surgeons took action and the specific types of action taken were previously reported.29 Demographic and occupational variables did not differ between surgeons who experienced microaggressions in one versus more than one job. There were no differences in the academic rank of surgeons who experienced microaggressions, nor in the rates of promotion to associate or full professor. Since the majority of microaggressions were experienced by surgeons who identify as women, we compared gender of the target to the perpetrator. Rates of microaggressions among gender concordant pairs (eg, woman target–woman perpetrator) did not differ from gender discordant pairs (eg, woman target–man perpetrator), regardless of whether action was taken.

There were differences in a surgeon’s career trajectory based on whether action was taken against the microaggression and the specific role of the perpetrator. Over two-thirds of surgeons who took action against their microaggressors stayed in their current position where microaggressions continued [action taken by perpetrator role: supervisor (65.9%, P = 0.025), support staff (63.9%, P < 0.001), trainees/learners (72.1%, P = 0.040), patients/families (71.1%, P < 0.001)]. When a supervisor was the perpetrator, career impact also included changing jobs with a demotion (P = 0.025). When the perpetrator was a patient/family member, surgeons who took action against the microaggression were less likely to lose a job due to informal reporting (9.4%, P = 0.048) or to change jobs with advancement or promotion (16.0%, P < 0.001). There was no difference in employment termination due to formal reporting, changing jobs for a lateral career move, or staying in jobs where microaggressions improved, regardless of whether surgeons took action against microaggressions.

Surgeons identifying as men who experienced microaggressions and took action against them were more likely to have the microaggression resolved than surgeons who identified as women (39.1% vs 12.9%, P = 0.005). There were also gender-based differences in reported employer response for surgeons who took action against microaggressions, with women having higher rates of retaliation, multiple responses, or no response (P = 0.047) (Table 2). There were differences in rates of microaggression resolution, retaliation, termination or lack of response by surgical specialty (P = 0.035) and for surgeons younger than 50 years of age (P = 0.031). When the perpetrator was a supervisor and the target took action against the microaggression, microaggressions were more likely to worsen (P < 0.001). There was no difference in microaggression outcomes within the other perpetrator roles.

There was a difference in the length of time surgeons worked at a specific job based on whether or not they experienced a microaggression and whether or not they took action. Surgeons who did not experience a microaggression had the longest duration of time at a job (14.4 years). Surgeons who experienced microaggressions had a shorter duration of time spent in a job, with time being slightly longer for those who took action than those who did not take action (11.9 years vs 11.1 years). There was a significant difference in length of time at a job between surgeons who did not experience microaggressions and those who experienced microaggressions and did not take action (P = 0.046).

Surgeons who experienced microaggressions in more than 1 job (n = 122) were more likely to say they would not choose surgery as a career again (P = 0.003) than those who experienced microaggression in only one job. DISCUSSION

This research adds to a growing body of literature detailing the impact of workplace microaggressions on a surgeon’s career trajectory. Our results show that microaggressions within our surveyed surgical community are common. Two-thirds of our respondents reported experiencing microaggressions in their current or most recent workplaces. This is consistent with other studies that report high rates of incivility within healthcare, particularly in surgical specialties.31,32

The primary focus of this study involved the specific role of the microaggression perpetrator and the associated career impact on surgeons. We identified that when the perpetrator was a supervisor, surgeons who took action were more likely to either stay in their current position while microaggressions escalated or change jobs with a demotion. An escalation of microaggressions may indicate retaliatory behavior.33 Supervisors have the power to provide recommendations for a surgeon seeking another job; negative input could reduce a surgeon’s opportunities for future employment, which may be considered tortious interference.34 Surgeons are thus confronted with a double-bind: remain in an increasingly hostile workplace or be reliant on their perpetrator for a favorable reference. In a survey of surgeons in academic medicine, the relationship with a supervisor was among the strongest predictors of a surgeon’s overall satisfaction with their department (P < 0.001), and lack of collegiality and collaboration was closely related to intent to leave within 1 to 2 years.35,36 These negative career impacts are novel findings within the literature on microaggressions and highlight the potential for power dynamics to disadvantage surgeons who experience microaggressions.

Microaggressions from patients and patient families were frequently reported, most often among surgical and perioperative trainees.36–40 We previously reported that patients/family members perpetrated 58.5% of microaggressions against surgeons, with 42.0% of surgeons taking action.29 In this sub-analysis, a surgeon was less likely to lose a job due to informal reporting or change jobs with a promotion/advancement when the perpetrator was a patient/family member. This was similar when a non-surgeon colleague (eg, staff) was the perpetrator (59.5% of microaggressions, 42.8% of surgeons took action).29 These findings may be due to the low frequency of microaggressions occurring with these perpetrator roles relative to the other perpetrator roles.

Interestingly, the majority of those who were impacted by microaggressions in their workplace opted to remain in their current position. There are many explanations for these findings, such as the culture and conditioning of surgical residency, which provides a breeding ground for tolerating incivility in the workplace. Targets of incivility may not feel sufficiently impacted at work because of parallel bias experienced outside of work. Practically, the cost of leaving a job might seem too great for economic and family reasons. We note that the surgeons most likely to be impacted were those early in their careers, women, surgical subspecialists, and those in academic medical centers or government/VA positions. These findings align with prior studies reporting that workplace incivility is attributed to a male-dominated hierarchy within surgery, respondent gender, and intergenerational differences.31,32,41

For surgeons who stayed in their current position, almost two-thirds reported that the microaggressions continued. Since microaggressions stem from implicit bias, a lack of awareness, and disrespect for persons, it is unsurprising that perpetrator behaviors continued in the absence of accountability or consequence. The persistence of workplace incivility impedes career development and satisfaction. A survey of surgical residents who reported exposures to mistreatment at least a few times per month were more likely to report symptoms of burnout than those who were not exposed to mistreatment.39 Among URiM surgical faculty and trainees, ongoing experiences with microaggressions were associated with impaired psychological safety, persistent burnout, and career dissatisfaction.41 In a survey of surgical residents, women trainees were 1.16 times more likely to experience attrition during residency than men, and Black/African American residents were 1.66 times more likely to experience attrition, more than any other racial or ethnic group.42 The cumulative injury that occurs and the impact on career trajectory for the practicing surgeon have not been previously quantified.

Surgeons who were unable to work or out of work were more likely to have taken action against experienced microaggressions. It is unknown whether being unable to or out of work was surgeon- or employer-initiated, or whether the lack of opportunity to work in a surgical field is directly related to being subjected to microaggressions or having then taken action. This unique and concerning association merits additional investigation.

There were differences in institutional response by respondent demographic. Among surgeons who took action against microaggressions, surgeon who were women were less likely to have microaggressions resolved than surgeon who were men. Higher rates of retaliation and no response were more common among surgeons who were women, younger, and those in specific surgical specialties. Retaliation is a feared consequence of reporting workplace abuses, including microaggressions, discrimination, and harassment, which leads to an underreporting of these behaviors in the surgical workforce.43–45 Academic faculty and trainees who reported workplace harassment faced a wide range of institutional responses, from support to minimization of the complaint, being labeled a troublemaker, or retaliation.46 Institutions must enforce their policies and pertinent laws that govern reporting of harassment or discrimination as protected activities.

Still, not everyone who was impacted by microaggressions took action against them or the perpetrator. This finding is consistent with other published work suggesting that many surgeons do not take action against experiencing incivility.14,30 As such, the scope and degree of impact from incivility in healthcare work environments may remain hidden from leadership explaining why perpetrators are not held accountable.

Job duration was shorter for surgeons who experienced workplace microaggressions, whether or not action was taken. These findings are novel and underscore an association between experiencing microaggressions and negative career impact. Surgeons who were men had longer careers (41 years vs 30 years, P < 0.001) and higher rates of retirement (32.6% vs 29.6%, P < 0.001), than surgeons who were women.47 The attrition of women from surgery in this study was more prominent in specific specialties, with higher odds of attrition among women in cardiac, general, orthopedic, urologic and plastic surgery. The decision to change jobs may be indirectly influenced by loss of clinical workplace opportunities. In a cross-sectional survey of academic surgical societies, respondents reported limited advancement opportunities due to gender disparities.48 Microaggressions were among the noted obstacles for women achieving leadership roles within academic medicine.49,50

The purpose of this work was to demonstrate the presence of incivility within surgical work environments and employment organizations and to detail the impact of incivility on surgeons. Surgeons who experienced microaggressions as a form of incivility in multiple professional settings were more likely to say they would not choose surgery as a career again. This finding correlates with recent evidence suggesting that physicians who experience microaggressions endorsed higher rates of burnout and decreased career satisfaction. Experiences of incivility in the workplace correlate with work withdrawal, otherwise coined “quiet quitting”.31 Creating excuses to avoid work, taking longer breaks and taking more time to complete tasks are indicators of work withdrawal and jeopardize workplace harmony, efficiency, and potentially the care being delivered to patients. These responses to workplace incivility also have the potential to contribute to workplace attrition, further impacting health care delivery.51

While individual efforts to identify and overcome implicit bias have proven beneficial with interpersonal interactions in the workplace,52 ultimately it is the responsibility of the organizational leadership to create and enforce a safe workplace culture. Departmental workshops using online learning modules and in-person role-playing demonstrated significant improvement in addressing unprofessional environments.53,54 The “Cup of Coffee” method promoted progressive action to address unprofessional behaviors.55 It recommended an informal conversation of a single event or raising awareness with patterns of behavior, and escalated to development of an action plan for unacceptable behavior and disciplinary processes for failed interventions. Though this approach outlines a systematic approach to enforce professionalism, it risks repetitive exposures to targeted individuals while protecting the perpetrator and organization. Strategies to ensure a safe workplace culture must enforce zero-tolerance policies of mistreatment (including harassment, bullying, hostility and microaggressions), and maintain transparent and safe reporting structures that aim to protect the target while holding the perpetrator accountable.54

Limitations

This study was designed as a cross-sectional survey study and has limitations. This was a convenience sample of ACS Communities forum participants, and it is unclear how representative this forum is of the larger community of surgeons. Surgeons who experienced microaggressions may have been more likely to respond to the survey, leading to selection bias. However, our findings are consistent with other investigations published in this area.31,32,56,57 While this study intended to illuminate how microaggressions influence the career trajectory, surgical trainees and early career surgeons who experienced microaggressions severe enough to resign from training or surgical practice may not be represented in this survey. In addition the number of respondents in nongeneral surgery subspecialties was small and this survey may not reflect the experiences of those surgeons. Therefore, the results may not be generalizable to all surgeons. Many of the results are focused on surgeons who experience microaggressions and then take action against them, which reduced the sample size; some comparisons may not be significant due to lack of power. The perpetrator roles listed were predefined and it is possible that other perpetrator roles had an impact on surgeons that was not represented in these findings. As respondents were reporting past experiences, there may be inaccuracies due to recall bias. Based on the most recent survey research literature, demographic information was collected at the end of the survey, which may have contributed to missing demographic data.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study addresses a gap in the literature regarding the career impact of workplace microaggressions on surgeons. Surgeons most at risk of experiencing workplace microaggressions are also those who are diversifying their fields and potentially expanding access of surgical services to vulnerable populations of patients otherwise under- or nontreated.58–60 By addressing and eliminating workplace microaggressions, healthcare systems and organization leadership improve the psychological safety for surgeons, leading to reduced burnout and surgeon attrition, improved quality of patient care and ensured breadth of management for patients who require surgical intervention.61
