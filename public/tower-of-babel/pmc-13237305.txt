Educational Frameworks for Diagnostic Decision-Making in AI-Enhanced Head and Neck Pathology
Authors: Tiffany Tavares, Linda Sangalli, Reshma S. Menon, Craig S. Miller, Guy Hochman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ContextThis paper examines how artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping diagnostic decision-making in academic and clinical head and neck pathology (HNP) and argues for the redefinition of post-doctoral education in the age of AI.PerspectiveWhile AI promises enhanced diagnostic accuracy, reproducibility, and efficiency, its integration also amplifies human cognitive biases and introduces new forms of error. Here we build on the 2019 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) pathology milestones to provide an educational framework that prepares clinicians to engage critically and responsibly with AI-assisted diagnostics. We emphasize the importance of integration of insights from decision science, cognitive psychology, and medical education with recent applications of AI in HNP, including histopathology, radiology, and multi-omics modeling. By mapping how human reasoning processes interact with algorithmic systems, we identify cognitive, ethical, and institutional vulnerabilities that arise in hybrid human–machine decision environments. Our analysis highlights that AI does not eliminate diagnostic uncertainty but redistributes it. Automation bias, overconfidence, and anchoring may distort reasoning when clinicians fail to question algorithmic outputs. To address these challenges, we outline a model of healthcare-optimization training, centered on three core skills: AI literacy, diagnostic skepticism, and ethical transparency. As such, reframing decision-making education in HNP requires moving beyond technical training toward reflective, ethically grounded practice.ReccomendationEmbedding AI literacy and critical reasoning within competency-based curricula and institutional governance can ensure that AI enhances rather than replaces human judgment—fostering safer, more equitable, and patient-centered diagnostic care.

Introduction

The rise of artificial intelligence (AI) in diagnostic medicine is rapidly reshaping clinical reasoning. AI tools promise enhanced accuracy, reproducibility, and scalability, particularly in histopathology, radiologic triage, and molecular profiling. Thus, they offer transformative potential for diagnostic and prognostic workflows [1, 2]. However, the potential benefits of AI also raise new challenges. AI does not eliminate diagnostic error; it reframes where and how such errors occur. Integration of algorithmic reasoning into clinical workflows also introduces new layers of decision-making that blends human judgment with probabilistic machine outputs [3, 4], which depend on technical sophistication as well as the clinician’s interpretive and critical reasoning skills [5]. As such, experts are recognizing the need for updated competencies in pathology and a new entrustable professional activity (EPA) framework that will help prepare pathologists for AI-supported diagnostics [6]. In addition, insights provided here into decision science and cognitive psychology support the need for a distinct framework, grounded not only in technical readiness but also in the psychology of human–machine interaction, diagnostic biases, and reflective clinical judgment.

In the following sections, we discuss how HNP providers and technological factors interact and sometimes conflict, supporting redefinition of decision-making education in the age of AI. As such, we propose an educational framework to guide AI integration into diagnostic education, emphasizing interpretive literacy, ethical reasoning, and reflective practice. This approach aims to align the promise of AI with the demands of safe, deliberate, and patient-centered care.

Applications and Promise of AI in HNP

The rapid integration of AI into HNP has generated substantial enthusiasm. AI applications are now being integrated across diverse domains, including histopathology, radiology, molecular profiling, clinical decision support, and electronic health record (EHR) data mining [7]. By combining computational precision with complex diagnostic data, AI promises to enhance accuracy, reproducibility, and efficiency across the diagnostic continuum. Its use, however, can also introduce notable cognitive challenges for clinicians.

In the hierarchical structure of technologies utilizing AI, large learning models may be further classified into generative and non-generative models, based on the task and output data. Some well-known applications of generative AI models include text generators for the purpose of report generation, image generators for clinical, research, and educational purposes, multi-modal models to integrate various data types and multi-agent frameworks utilizing dialogue between different AI models. [8]

High rates of success have been reported with both tabular and text data analysis, with the emergence of generative and non-generative AI models in pathology and medicine; although, their use-cases are still restricted, owing to limited datasets. Multimodal learning is particularly propitious, being able to extract data from diverse datasets, including clinical information, radiographic and histopathology images, and even “omics” data, when available, which can help physicians readily integrate these and expedite final comprehensive report generation. [9]

Non-generative AI models are more well-established and help with predictive tasks, relying on patterns discovered from prior input data and are classified into supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning. Supervised models require explicit labeling by expert annotators, whereas unsupervised models learn directly from raw data without such annotation. An example of a supervised model is a convolutional neural network that uses deep learning (DL) to classify histopathology grading of tumors through whole slide image (WSI) analysis. Digital pathology uses this technology, whereby glass slides can be scanned, then studied and analyzed repeatedly via AI models. On the other hand, unsupervised machine learning (ML) in pathology, has played a pivotal role in the discovery of novel findings. Vision transformer architectures trained via self-supervised learning approaches have gained traction as powerful pretrained models that learn transferable image representations, enabling unsupervised or weakly supervised analysis of pathology images without the need for exhaustive expert annotation. These models can be used for biomarker discovery and can be adopted in immunohistochemical analysis or applied to targeted therapeutics by learning patterns in unlabeled data. [10]

Recent data compared certified AI applications to detect metastases and was validated to be extremely capable across various cancer types (breast cancer, melanoma and head and neck cancer), supporting the broader application of AI-enabled digital pathology in routine practice for staging disease [11]. Similarly, a DL model based on vision transformer (a model that uses self-attention to establish context from image sequences) demonstrated excellent performance in classifying common salivary gland tumor subtypes with WSI; however these models can foster automation bias when analyzing rare and malignant subtypes due to underrepresentation in training data [12]. Likewise, AI based probabilistic outputs in thyroid nodule evaluation can support risk stratification and prognostication, but may also induce overconfidence. [13, 14]

Deep learning models have shown promising results in recognizing and quantifying intricate patterns within histopathologic images, overcoming the limitations of classical imaging analysis [9] For example, an AI-derived intraepithelial lymphocyte (IEL) score outperformed both binary and WHO grading systems in predicting malignant transformation reducing inter- and intra-observer variability; however, these models can create an anchoring bias, leading to overreliance on AI-generated scores. [15, 16]

Some highly sought-out applications of AI in the field of medical education include augmented and virtual reality tools to help train the next generation of healthcare teams. This momentum is further reflected in recent proposals to introduce EPA in medical education, particularly in diagnostic pathology, embracing what has worked well in image-based medical specialties such as radiology. [17]

Overall, these applications demonstrate that AI can serve as a powerful augmentative tool in HNP, extending interpretive, analytical, prognostic and integrative capabilities beyond traditional manual analysis while it simultaneously may bias clinical reasoning and decision-making, requiring careful implementation and oversight.

The Human Side of Diagnostic Reasoning

Clinical decision-making in HNP is inherently complex, [18, 19] with high inter-observer variability and frequent misclassification in oral and laryngeal lesions. [20–22] Thus, formal understanding of the cognitive mechanisms that underlie professional judgment is particularly important in HNP. It should be a central component of post-doctoral education, where pathologists are expected to transition from competent practitioners to independent experts who supervise, mentor, and critically evaluate diagnostic decisions. The integration of AI into pathology requires clinicians who can interpret, question, and contextualize machine outputs. To achieve this goal, it is essential to understand how humans make diagnostic decisions: what cognitive processes guide them, where they excel, and where they are susceptible to systematic error.

Traditionally, decision-making in medicine is taught under the dual-system account. According to this view, reasoning relies on two independent modes of thought: System 1, which is fast, intuitive, automatic, and associative, and System 2, which is slow, deliberate, and rule based. System 1 generates immediate impressions and heuristic judgments, often based on salient but limited cues, while System 2 monitors, verifies, or overrides these intuitive outputs through effortful analysis [23, 24]. This framework has been highly influential in medical and dental education, as it offers an accessible language for distinguishing between intuitive “pattern recognition” and reflective “analytic reasoning” in clinical contexts. In pathology, for example, intuitive processes may accelerate the recognition of familiar patterns, while deliberate processes enable careful evaluation of competing diagnostic possibilities. [22, 25]

Although the dual-system framework provides a useful general conceptualization, recent developments in decision science challenge its overly simplistic dichotomous nature. Clinical judgments rarely conform to a strict separation of “fast and biased” versus “slow and accurate.” Instead, they emerge from a dynamic interaction between processing modes, task features, and contextual constraints [26, 27]. For example, decision quality depends on the compatibility between the thinking style and the task requirements [26, 28]. Intuition can sometimes lead to more accurate and consistent outcomes than deliberation, while deliberate reasoning may introduce biases when it is misaligned with context. These findings underscore that both novice and experienced pathologists are susceptible to systematic error, regardless of whether they approach reasoning intuitively or analytically.

The complexity of diagnostic reasoning in HNP is further illustrated by the systematic biases that have been documented across clinical contexts [29]. These biases are not restricted to novices; indeed, research suggests that seasoned experts are often less aware of their own cognitive vulnerabilities and therefore more likely to perpetuate them. [30, 32] Collectively, these tendencies underscore the need for a more nuanced understanding of judgment processes, one that transcends the simplistic assumption that expertise or deliberate reasoning guarantees accuracy [27]. As diagnostic decisions reflect a dynamic interplay of cognitive systems, task demands, and contextual factors, clinicians are vulnerable to predictable judgmental errors and variability.

Importantly, the very same mechanisms that shape human judgment also determine how pathologists may engage with AI: whether they adopt its recommendations uncritically, resist them completely, or integrate them in a balanced and reflective manner [33–35]. Thus, appreciating the cognitive foundations of diagnostic reasoning is a prerequisite for critically assessing AI tools, deciding when and how to employ them, and avoiding technology-amplified biases. This understanding also underscores the educational imperative: post-doctoral training must prepare pathologists not only to master diagnostic content but also to recognize, monitor, and manage the cognitive processes that shape their decisions.

Limitations and Risks of AI in HNP

Despite its promise, the clinical implementation of AI in HNP remains constrained by significant technical, infrastructural, and governance challenges. Many models perform well in research settings, yet falter in real-world deployment where data heterogeneity, workflow complexity, and regulatory oversight play decisive roles. [36]

The reliability of AI depends heavily on the quality and representativeness of the training datasets. Numerous models are trained on small, single-center, or research-curated datasets that underrepresent rare cancers, minority populations, and atypical presentations, resulting in uneven performance—high accuracy for “textbook” cases but poor generalization to complex or marginalized populations. Data collected in tertiary centers and digital repositories may not translate effectively across scanners, institutions, or resource-limited settings [37–39]. Such gaps risk widening existing healthcare disparities: models developed to improve accessibility may inadvertently exacerbate inequity if training data are not inclusive. [37]

Further limitations stem from data quality, annotation bias, and lack of external validation, which compromise reproducibility and clinical robustness [40]. Other technical vulnerabilities include adversarial data manipulation (intentional modification of input data to negatively affect the performance of a model or alter its behavior), model drift (the decline of model performance or accuracy over time as new data differs from training data), and software decentralization, which can complicate accountability and interoperability when independent hospitals, vendors, and research algorithms are involved [3]. Without consistent oversight, AI may silently alter performance and blur responsibility when diagnostic errors occur.

Implementation also requires robust digital infrastructure, cybersecurity safeguards, and cross-disciplinary coordination. Adversarial data manipulation and malware vulnerabilities can corrupt diagnostic pipelines [3]. Moreover, AI development in pathology is highly decentralized: commercial vendors, research groups, and hospital teams often produce proprietary algorithms with minimal interoperability. This fragmentation complicates accountability, version tracking, and regulatory supervision. Without standardized validation and post-deployment monitoring, silent software updates can alter model behavior without the clinician's awareness, creating patient safety risks.

Finally, when diagnostic errors occur, responsibility is diffuse. It is often unclear whether fault lies with the clinician, the institution, or the algorithm’s developer. Effective integration, therefore, depends on clear governance frameworks that define liability, require independent auditing, and ensure algorithm retraining and performance reporting [3, 41]. Collaborative oversight involving data scientists, engineers, and clinicians is essential to maintain transparency, ensure equitable model performance, and sustain trust in AI-assisted diagnostics.

These limitations underscore that successful AI implementation in HNP is not solely a technical endeavor, but also an educational one. Clinicians must understand not only how AI systems operate but also how to interpret, question, and integrate their probabilistic outputs responsibly. Strengthening AI literacy and diagnostic skepticism at the post-doctoral level is therefore essential for safe translation of these technologies.

A Promise and a Challenge: When Human and Machine Errors Intersect

Beyond technical issues, AI interacts with and often amplifies human cognitive biases. For example, AI’s perceived objectivity and precision can create a “reliability halo,” encouraging clinicians, especially less experienced clinicians, to defer to its outputs instead of engaging in independent scrutiny [42, 43]. Similarly, automation bias refers to the tendency to over-trust algorithmic outputs at the expense of critical evaluation. [44, 45] In pathology, clinicians may accept AI-generated classifications without weighing the contradictory evidence from histopathological or clinical sources [42, 43]. Most AI outputs are probabilistic and opaque, generating predictions without clear explanations. Clinicians may misinterpret these predictions as facts, attributing certainty to statistical estimations, and continual use without scrutiny can reinforce misplaced confidence in those predictions [3]. These issues are particularly dangerous in HNP, where rare subtypes, borderline lesions, and complex anatomic relationships demand nuanced interpretation. Thus, the tendency to blindly accept AI recommendations risks perpetuating misdiagnosis.

Overconfidence is another common bias [46, 47], among medical professionals, including HNP providers [41]. Less experienced clinicians tend to accept AI predictions as precise and trustworthy, even if the underlying models are unstable or poorly calibrated [41]. This can erode epistemic humility, the professional recognition that all diagnoses carry a degree of uncertainty. In a domain as complex as HNP, such misplaced confidence can curtail dialogue, discourage second opinions, and suppress healthy skepticism.

Confirmation bias further contributes to the preferential seeking or interpretation of findings that support a favored diagnosis, while overlooking disconfirming evidence [45, 47]. AI outputs may reinforce this by framing uncertainty as certainty, inadvertently narrowing diagnostic reasoning. Indeed, initial AI suggestions can bias final grading or classification in H&N histopathology [42], highlighting the importance of maintaining critical appraisal skills in conjunction with AI-assisted interpretation.

In addition, anchoring occurs when clinicians place disproportionate weight on initial impressions, such as the first visible abnormality on a slide, and fail to adjust when new evidence emerges. [47–50] Initial AI-generated diagnoses can serve as an anchor that influences evaluators’ tendency to adhere to that frame and overlook discrepant histological or radiographic features. These anchors can exert a stronger influence than peer input because of their perceived objectivity. [42]

Finally, long-term reliance on AI may result in deskilling (diminishing human expertise), particularly at the postgraduate level. Medical education relies on repetition of judgment under uncertainty; it is through grappling with ambiguous slides, discrepant clinical histories, and imperfect evidence that future specialists acquire expertise. A recent multicenter, retrospective observational study investigated the risk of deskilling among endoscopists by assessing the rate at which they detected adenomas before and after using AI-assisted colonoscopy (AAC) technology. They reported an absolute 6% reduction in adenoma detection rate after three-month exposure to AAC, indicating potential deskilling of endoscopists [51]. Possible explanations for deskilling and overreliance in computer-aided detection (CAD) workflows have been suggested by evaluating eye-tracking when using these technologies in analyses of colorectal and breast imaging, where reduced time was spent reviewing the images and increased false positive rates were observed when CAD was used. [52, 53] As an imaging-based specialty, pathologists are at risk of similar phenomena. If trainees defer to AI for definitive answers, their ability to synthesize pathological, radiographic, and clinical data may weaken over time [48, 54] For example, while studies on oral epithelial dysplasia (OED) grading and multiomics modeling in HNP illustrate how automation can streamline routine assessments, they may also potentially limit experiential learning [15]. The same architecture that leads to cognitive biases also determines whether pathologists will trust, question, or dismiss AI-generated outputs.

Designing Educational Frameworks for AI-Augmented Decision-Making

The rapid adoption of AI technologies, including machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), computer vision (the processing and interpretation of visual data through machine learning techniques and AI), natural language processing (technology that enables computers to understand, interpret and manipulate human language), and large language models (e.g., GPT, Claude), is transforming healthcare education. These systems now support a wide range of activities, from admissions and tutoring to virtual simulation, adaptive learning, and clinical assessment [55]. Their integration into training programs offers unprecedented opportunities to personalize learning, expand exposure to rare cases, and enhance diagnostic reasoning.

Yet the educational promise of AI also introduces a profound pedagogical challenge (Fig. 1). Traditional healthcare curricula were designed around human expertise, emphasizing foundational knowledge, critical appraisal, ethical judgment, and professionalism. In the AI era, these competencies must evolve. Clinicians should learn not only how to use AI tools but how to think with them—critically, reflectively, and responsibly. Revised Core Competencies in the AI Era

Integrating AI into diagnostic education requires redefining core professional competencies rather than merely adding new technical skills. The American Medical Association (AMA) has issued policy recommendations that encourage education on the capabilities and limitations of AI [56]. However, current accreditation bodies, including the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME), Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME), and CanMEDS have not yet formally incorporated AI-specific competencies. Expanding on Forney and McBright’s work applied to radiology training, recently, Vos et al. proposed a new EPA entitled “using AI in diagnostic pathology practice.” [6, 57] They also suggest that certain traditional competencies such as grossing, autopsies and intraoperative histologic examination, as well as in multidisciplinary diagnostic and management settings will remain primarily the task of the pathologist. However, they speculate that microscopic evaluation and reporting are likely to be taken over by AI, including the detection, characterization, quantification and prediction of disease through automated image analysis. Furthermore, with the broader implementation of AI in pathology, they expect competencies to include more self-directed learning and quality control activities. These would shift the role of a pathologist from primarily a diagnostician to that of a data analyst, utilizing foundational biomedical knowledge and traditional pathology training to select, evaluate, interpret and analyze data inputs and outputs for quality and accuracy. Additionally, greater emphasis is expected in the understanding of foundational informatics in the development and implementation of AI models in diagnostic and operational workflows. Consequently, quality improvement-, quality assurance-, and self-assessment-related competencies will gain further importance for the identification and mitigation of biases and prevention of deskilling [6]. Rather than create separate AI competencies training pathologists in this new paradigm, we suggest that AI-assisted workflows are inevitable, contemporary expressions of the principles of pathology practice. Responsible integration of AI into traditional pathology educational competencies should be considered, for example, including AI validation for clinical and specimen data extraction, bias awareness during diagnostic reasoning, algorithmic performance monitoring during quality assurance tasks, and transparency and equity considerations with respect to AI use when considering professionalism standards. Thus, clinicians should learn not only how to use AI tools but how to think with them—critically, reflectively, and responsibly in every aspect of their practice. AI does not eliminate error but can influence where and how errors occur. This requires reframing medical education to address these new vulnerabilities.

Healthcare-Optimization Training: A framework for AI-augmented decision-making education

One approach to address these vulnerabilities is to implement healthcare-optimization training—a model that integrates AI literacy and diagnostic skepticism with the enduring principles of human accountability, ethical integrity, and patient-centered care. This training would incorporate a decision-making framework that facilitates the identification of important tasks of the practice of pathology where delegation to AI tools and workflows are indicated or appropriate, indicated with caution, or never appropriate. AI Literacy with Epistemic Humility

A foundational understanding of how algorithms are designed, trained, validated, and limited is fundamental to AI literacy [6, 57–59]. Clinicians must understand the strengths and limitations of model architectures, data representativeness, and bias sources to critically interpret outputs rather than accepting them as objective truths. AI literacy thus bridges technical awareness with epistemic humility, enabling clinicians to appreciate both the capabilities and fallibility of algorithmic reasoning, addressing both automation bias and overconfidence.

Diagnostic Skepticism

Diagnostic skepticism, the disciplined capacity to question AI-generated outputs before incorporating them into clinical reasoning, also is key to this training. Because algorithms can reproduce biases or generate errors and “hallucinations” (plausible but factually incorrect or fabricated AI output), learners must cultivate reflective reasoning to evaluate when an AI recommendation is consistent with evidence (i.e., clinical context, histopathological patterns, and molecular data) and when it demands scrutiny [48, 60, 61]. This skepticism protects against automation bias, confirmation bias and anchoring, and promotes independent judgment—skills essential for responsible collaboration between humans and machines.

Transparency

Additionally, transparency must be reframed as a professional and ethical obligation. Learners and clinicians should be able to articulate how AI tools have influenced their reasoning, justify their reliance on specific outputs, and clearly communicate uncertainty to colleagues and patients. Integrating these competencies shifts AI from a passive shortcut into an active partner in clinical learning, fostering accountability, interpretive depth, and patient-centered decision-making. [62]

Pedagogical Strategies and Implementation

Traditional lecture-based models have evolved into interactive, competency-driven learning models that emphasize problem-solving and reflection. [63] Such approaches, like flipped classrooms, small-group discussions, and case-based learning, form the foundation for AI-enhanced education in HNP. AI can strengthen these pedagogies by enabling simulation, adaptive feedback, and exposure to diverse and rare diagnostic scenarios. AI systems can provide real-time, individualized feedback, highlighting missed features, refining questioning, and prompting reevaluation—thereby promoting self-directed learning while complementing, not replacing, patient encounters [64]. Thus, we propose development of key cognitive skills that move the learner from basic self-awareness to human-Al reconciliation and simulation-based practice. This sequencing can foster metacognitively aware, bias-resistant, and diagnostically accurate pathologists equipped to work safely and effectively with AI.

Cognitive Bias Recognition

Recognition of these cognitive biases is the first step in establishing awareness of systematic thinking errors that may influence diagnostic decisions and are foundational to metacognitive skill development and safe AI implementation.

Non-AI Assessments

The constant and growing possibilities for incorporation of AI-driven processes in education and clinical practice can lead to deskilling (erosion of previous skills), “never-skilling” (lack of specific essential skill development), mis-skilling” (amplification of errors and bias) [65, 66]. Unaided diagnostic reasoning is an essential skill which allows identification of cognitive biases and knowledge gaps and help build and calibrate confidence [30]. Programs should incorporate workflows without the use of AI to ensure learners maintain independent clinical reasoning, diagnostic accuracy and metacognitive awareness, preserving the human expertise at the heart of clinical decision-making.

Confidence Calibration

When implementing AI tools, helping trainees distinguish their perceived diagnostic performance with their actual performance is a necessary calibration step, as both overconfidence and underconfidence can affect patient safety. Students in initial stages of training consistently exhibit overconfidence bias, often influenced by anchoring. Sample exercises to achieve confidence calibration consists of trainees reviewing slides independently, recording their confidence level, then comparing them with AI-generated outputs and the actual diagnoses. AI-generated outputs and confirmed final diagnoses act as benchmarks for calibration. [42, 48]

Decision Audits and Reflection

Decision audits further encourage metacognitive awareness. By comparing initial diagnostic judgments with those revised after consulting AI suggestions, learners can identify tendencies toward automation bias, algorithm aversion (loss of confidence, distrust, or negative bias towards AI outputs), or opacity of models [61, 67, 68]. Guided reflection has been shown to improve diagnostic decision-making, especially in complex cases and is most effective when immediate feedback is included and contrasting cases are also presented [68–70]. Trainees should first review slides and make their initial diagnosis using traditional pathology training methods (blinded to AI), review AI generated classification and diagnostic outputs, then determine the final diagnosis. This exercise should be followed by a formal reflective activity indicating whether the AI output changed their initial diagnosis and why, also elucidating additional datapoints that would increase confidence in the assessment. Concomitantly, trainees should identify potential influential cognitive biases. This structured reflection cultivates balanced collaboration between human reasoning and algorithmic input.

AI Discrepancy Assessment

Importantly, discrepancies among AI systems, arising from dataset imbalance, algorithmic variation, or differences in model architecture, should be reframed as teaching opportunities. Exposure to conflicting outputs helps learners appreciate the non-deterministic nature of AI and reinforces the need for evidence-based evaluations [71–73]. Through guided analysis, students can compare AI outputs against clinical guidelines, justify agreement or disagreement, and articulate their reasoning, strengthening accountability and critical reflection [73]. For example, trainees can review slides with outputs from three different AI models showing conflicting dysplasia grading, followed by an assessment of which model is trustworthy, justifying their decision by applying foundational medical knowledge that support or refute their conclusions, citing relevant evidence. The same process can be modeled for assessment of discrepancies between human and AI outputs. [42]

Simulation-Based Practice

Simulation exercises, once limited to manikins or physical models, now can incorporate AI-driven virtual environments that replicate complex diagnostic situations [74]. In pathology, AI-powered slide repositories and case-based modules allow learners to engage with both common and rare lesions previously encountered only in textbooks, promoting iterative diagnostic reasoning and deliberate practice [69, 74]. While AI can democratize access to high-quality educational resources, disparities in digital literacy, technology access, and faculty readiness risk widening the educational divide. [75–77]

Development of this pedagogical process requires access to high-quality WSI, publicly accessible databases integrating histological, genomic, and clinical data, and standardized annotation protocols. They should be integrated into a comprehensive educational framework that includes traditional microscopy, case-based learning, and expert consultation. Ethical, Legal, and Institutional Implications of AI in HNP

Integration of AI into HNP raises profound ethical, legal, and institutional challenges. The distinctive complexity of HNP makes it a domain particularly sensitive to questions of responsibility, trust, and governance [22, 26] While AI systems promise to enhance consistency and efficiency in diagnosis, implementation must be guided by frameworks that balance innovation with prudence, ensuring that AI functions as a decision-support tool rather than a replacement for human expertise.

Ethically, the adoption of AI in pathology intersects with the core principles of beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice [79]. AI tools may improve patient outcomes by reducing errors and expediting diagnoses, thereby serving beneficence. Yet risks of algorithmic bias, opaque decision-making, and over-reliance on technology can introduce potential harm [41]. Respecting patient autonomy also requires transparency. Clinicians must be able to explain AI-supported decisions in comprehensible terms to patients and colleagues. This aligns with calls for epistemic humility in medicine, emphasizing acknowledgment of uncertainty and limits of knowledge. [80] Without humility and understanding, there is a risk of overstating AI’s accuracy and underestimating its vulnerabilities.

From a legal perspective, AI raises open questions of liability. If an algorithm contributes to a diagnostic error, responsibility ultimately rests with the pathologist, as professional standards of care continue to place clinicians at the center of accountability [81]. This legal position underscores why AI should not be positioned as an autonomous decision-maker but rather as a supportive adjunct. In practice, this requires institutions to establish clear governance mechanisms for the validation, monitoring, and retraining of algorithms over time [41]. Pathology-specific evidence of diagnostic error further illustrates the stakes. Discrepancies in HNP remain a persistent problem, demanding institutional safeguards even in human-only practice. [22, 26] AI, if poorly governed, risks amplifying rather than mitigating these errors.

Institutional integration of AI must be accompanied by educational reform. Training should focus on equipping clinicians with the technical literacy and critical reasoning skills necessary to evaluate AI recommendations within the clinical context. This reflects broader arguments for embedding humility into professional systems, ensuring that both individuals and institutions retain the ability to challenge automated outputs. [42]

At an institutional level, governance should incorporate continuous algorithmic monitoring, clear delineation of responsibility, and processes for appeal and oversight. These structures parallel existing quality assurance mechanisms in pathology but require adaptation to account for novel risks such as dataset drift (changes to input data over time relative to model training data, adversarial vulnerabilities, and shifting regulatory standards. [82] Without these safeguards, the promise of AI may erode trust in technology and the institutions deploying it.

Conclusion

AI is transforming HNP, expanding diagnostic capacity while challenging long-held assumptions about expertise, reasoning, and accountability. Recent work [6] outlined infrastructural and regulatory competencies required for the responsible adoption of AI in pathology training. Our approach diverges by emphasizing the cognitive and decision-making dimensions of AI integration. Rather than focusing solely on formal EPAs or systemic implementation, we analyze how clinicians interpret, trust, or over-rely on algorithmic outputs, and how educational frameworks can mitigate such risks. This focus is especially critical in HNP, where diagnostic uncertainty can be high, and the interpretive demands are nuanced.

Our proposed framework complements existing competency-based initiatives by addressing the human factors that shape diagnostic decisions in AI-enhanced environments, aiming not only to integrate technology but to enhance clinical reasoning and ethical reflection in parallel. The intersection of human and machine reasoning introduces new vulnerabilities that demand deliberate educational reform. Reframing decision-making education for the AI era means teaching clinicians not only how to use intelligent systems, but how to think with them. AI literacy, diagnostic skepticism, and ethical transparency must therefore be treated as core professional skills, alongside reflective practice and continuous awareness of bias. Trainees must, therefore, be formally instructed to analyze AI-assisted outputs within pathology practice. Future educational development with relevant stakeholders can expand on the intersection of these AI-assisted workflows within the six core ACGME pathology competency domains and milestones to create AI-related themes for each subcompetency. By embedding these principles into post-doctoral education, competency-based assessments, and institutional governance, HNP can evolve toward an epistemically humble, technologically fluent, and ethically resilient model of clinical reasoning. In this model, AI does not replace the pathologist’s judgment; it refines it—turning human–machine collaboration into a catalyst for safer, fairer, and more patient-centered care.
