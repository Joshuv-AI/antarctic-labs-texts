Abstract
. Session-based recommendation (SBR) aims to predict the next item for an anonymous user in a timely manner. However, SBR suffers from data sparsity due to the short and anonymous nature of sessions. Recently, an emerging line of work has explored inferring the underlying user intents of a session using large language models (LLMs), with the generated intents serving as auxiliary training signals to enhance SBR models. Despite its promise, this approach faces three key challenges: validating intent quality, incorporating session-level multi-intents, and complementing inevitable LLM failure cases. In this paper, we propose \proposed , a two-stage framework that leverages V alidated and E nriched L LM-generated I ntents for SBR . In the first stage, we generate high-quality intents using a predict-and-correct loop that validates the informativeness of LLM-generated intents with a global intent pool to constrain the LLM’s output space and reduce hallucination. In the second stage, we enhance the SBR model using the generated intents through a lightweight multi-intent prediction and fusion mechanism. Furthermore, we introduce a training strategy that compensates for LLM failures by inferring intents from inter-session behavioral similarities. Extensive experiments show that \proposed outperforms state-of-the-art baselines while improving explainability.



1.
Introduction

With the explosive growth of e-commerce applications, there has been growing interest in session-based recommendation (SBR)
(
Liu et al. 2018
;
Kersbergen et al. 2022
;
Yuan et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
.
SBR is highly practical because it delivers timely suggestions without requiring user authentication, thereby preserving privacy and catering to short-term user interests
(
Wang et al. 2021a
;
Cho et al. 2021
)
.
Over the past decades, a wide range of neural architectures have been applied to SBR, including RNNs
(
Hidasi 2015
;
Li et al. 2017
)
, GNNs
(
Wu et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
, Transformers
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
Luo et al. 2020
)
and even large language models (LLMs)
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
.
However, SBR still faces the fundamental challenge of data sparsity.
Each session contains only a small number of interactions, making it difficult to learn reliable sequential patterns
(
Qiao et al. 2025
;
Choi et al. 2024
)
.
Moreover, the anonymous nature of sessions precludes the use of user profile information and prevents linking sessions from the same user, hindering the capture of long-term user preferences
(
Qiu et al. 2020
;
Ferrato 2023
)
.

To alleviate the sparsity problem, intent-aware recommendation has emerged as a promising approach.
An intent is typically defined as the underlying reason a user selects a particular item (e.g., buying running gear for health or gifting; visiting a café for its popularity, style, or taste
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
), offering contextual information not directly revealed from the sparse interaction data.
Because it is infeasible to obtain the ground-truth user intents, existing methods have inferred them in two general ways:
(1)
implicit modeling

(
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Qin et al. 2024
)
infers intents using unsupervised techniques such as clustering in the representation space.
(2)
explicit modeling

(
Li et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Qiao et al. 2025
)
mostly utilizes item metadata (e.g., genre, category) as observable proxies for user intent.
However, each approach has its own limitations.
Implicit methods lack explainability and rely on predefined factors such as the number of clusters, which can significantly affect their effectiveness.
On the other hand, explicit methods often suffer from noisy metadata and fail to capture the diversity and subtlety of user intents beyond what is available in the provided attributes.

Recently, there have been a few attempts
(
Sun et al. 2024
;
Qiao et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025a
)
to enhance the explicit modeling using LLMs.
Pretrained on massive web corpora, LLMs possess extensive world knowledge and reasoning capabilities
(
Wu et al. 2024
;
Lyu et al. 2024
;
Tsai et al. 2024
)
, enabling them to compensate for incomplete item metadata and to infer interpretable intents over item attributes.
To leverage these capabilities, one line of work
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
directly uses LLMs as SBR models by enriching the input prompts with inferred information and tailored instructions for sophisticated reasoning.
However, real-world applications operate under strict latency and resource constraints, making it difficult to deploy LLMs directly at inference time
(
Shao et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
.
A more resource-efficient approach
(
Qiao et al. 2025
;
Lian et al. 2025
)
is to use LLM-generated intents as auxiliary training signals for conventional SBR models, improving both accuracy and explainability—an approach our work follows.

Despite its promise, this direction presents three unique challenges that remain insufficiently addressed in prior work
(
Qiao et al. 2025
;
Lian et al. 2025
)
.

(C1) Validating the intent quality:

LLM outputs are not always reliable and can be hallucinated.
Moreover, even when a generated intent appears reasonable, it may not be informative for predicting actual user behavior.
For instance, we found that some inferred intents are overly broad (e.g., “general summer necessities” in the beauty domain), offering little meaningful guidance.
Prior methods
(
Qiao et al. 2025
;
Lian et al. 2025
)
accept LLM-generated intents as-is, risking the injection of spurious signals that can mislead the SBR model.
A systematic approach to validate intent quality is therefore critical.

(C2) Incorporating session-level multi-intents:

Users often have multiple underlying intents within a single session.
For example, a session involving outdoor swimming may reflect diverse intents such as sun protection, waterproof makeup, and salt-removing cleansers.
Accordingly, SBR models need to incorporate such multi-faceted intents based on their importance.
Previous methods
(
Lian et al. 2025
;
Qiao et al. 2025
)
have primarily focused on using one or two dominant intents per session, which may be insufficient to fully reflect user behavior.
A tailored strategy is required to incorporate multiple intents into SBR models with consideration of their relative importance.

(C3) Complementing LLM failures:

We found that once quality validation mechanisms are applied (as discussed in C1), LLMs fail to generate
any informative intents
for a substantial portion of sessions, with an average failure rate of 39.7% (see Appendix
C.1
).

Related Work

Session-Based Recommendation (SBR).

The goal of SBR is to predict the next item in an anonymous session by modeling short
-
term user behavior
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2018
)
.
SBR is widely adopted in e-commerce due to its practicality in delivering timely suggestions without user authentication
(
Liu et al. 2018
;
Kersbergen et al. 2022
;
Yuan et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2020
)
.
Early RNN
-
based methods encode temporal dynamics via gated recurrent units
(
Hidasi 2015
;
Li et al. 2017
)
.
Subsequent GNN-based methods model complex item transitions by linking items across sessions in a unified graph
(
Wu et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
,
and Transformer-based models leverage self
-
attention to capture relationships between distant items within a session
(
Xu et al. 2019
;
Luo et al. 2020
)
.
However, SBR models suffer from data
-
sparsity due to the short, anonymous nature of sessions
(
Wang et al. 2021a
;
Cho et al. 2021
)
.
With such limited context, conventional SBR models struggle to capture reliable sequential patterns, resulting in suboptimal recommendations.
To bridge this gap, LLM-based SBR models have recently emerged, leveraging their reasoning capabilities and world knowledge
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Sun et al. 2024
;
Qiao et al. 2025
)
.

Intent-aware Recommendation.

As a solution to the data sparsity, one notable research direction is to model user intent within a given session
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2021b
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2024
)
.
Intent reveals the underlying reasons behind the user choices, thereby providing contextual information that goes beyond mere interaction logs.
As intent has no ground-truth labels, existing intent modeling can be broadly categorized into two groups: (1) explicit intent modeling that utilizes side information (e.g., categories, titles) as direct intent labels
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Qiao et al. 2025
)
and (2) implicit intent modeling that infers latent intents via unsupervised techniques like clustering
(
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Qin et al. 2024
)
.
Despite these advances, explicit methods remain vulnerable to noisy or incomplete side information, while implicit methods offer little explainability and hinge on arbitrary clustering choices.
These limitations motivate the exploration of LLMs as a new paradigm in intent modeling, which leverages pretrained language knowledge to generate more robust and explainable intents
(
Sun et al. 2024
;
Qiao et al. 2025
;
Lian et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025a
)
.

LLM-Based SBR with Intent Modeling.

Recent LLM
-
based SBR methods with intent modeling follow two distinct paradigms:
(1) LLMs as SBR models
(
Sun et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025a
)
and (2) LLM-enhanced SBR
(
Qiao et al. 2025
;
Lian et al. 2025
)
.

In the first approach, LLMs serve as standalone SBR models that directly infer user intents and rank items based on the intents.
PO4ISR
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
optimizes prompts to extract user intents and then ranks candidates accordingly.
Re2LLM
(
Wang et al. 2025a
)
reinforces prompts by injecting hints to fix recommendation errors.
Though effective, directly using LLMs for recommendation inevitably incurs substantial computational costs at inference time, making them less preferred for real-time applications (See Table
3
for latency comparison).

In contrast, LLM
-
enhanced SBR methods leverage LLMs to generate user intents, which are then used to guide conventional SBR models.
EGRec
(
Lian et al. 2025
)
jointly trains an LLM for intent generation with a recommender, while LLM4SBR
(
Qiao et al. 2025
)
infers both long
-
and short
-
term intents from item titles to enrich session representations.
While effective, EGRec’s fine
-
tuning is computationally expensive and risks degrading the LLM’s pretrained world knowledge, while LLM4SBR treats intents as simple item titles, which constrains their expressiveness (see Figure
4
; Figures
6
and
7
in Appendix

).
Moreover, both methods directly consume LLM
-
generated outputs without validating their quality, risking spurious or incorrect patterns that can mislead the recommender and degrade recommendation accuracy.
Therefore, there is a pressing need for an LLM-enhanced SBR method that effectively validates the generated intents and compensates for failure cases in intent generation.

3.2.
Problem Definition

We follow the LLM-enhanced SBR paradigm.
Specifically, we aim to generate explicit intents using LLMs for training sessions, and leverage them to guide the training of SBR models, thereby improving both recommendation accuracy and explainability.
We focus on the three aforementioned challenges that remain insufficiently explored in prior work
(
Qiao et al. 2025

Lian et al. 2025

(C1) validating the intent quality, (C2) incorporating session-level multi-intents, and (C3) complementing LLM failures.

4.1.
Stage 1: LLM-based Intent Generation and Quality Validation

The goal of this stage is to generate a set of intents that best explain user behavior in each session, which will be used to enhance SBR models.
This stage is designed to
(C1) ensure high-quality intents
.
We first refine noisy item features to facilitate more accurate reasoning over them (
section

4.1.1
).
Then, we apply an iterative predict-and-correct loop to obtain reliable, informative intents (
section

4.1.2
).

4.1.1.
Item-level feature refinement

Raw item features are often noisy and inconsistent in expression styles and formats, as they are collected from various sources.
Directly feeding them into LLMs can hinder accurate reasoning of intents.
To address this, we generate refined textual features
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
by refining the raw features
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

(3)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\mathrm{LLM}(\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})),

where
\mathcal{P}_{1}
is the prompt for this process (see Appendix
B.2
).
This process allows the LLM to enrich the original features with its world knowledge, and to convert them into a more consistent, model
-
friendly format, facilitating subsequent intent prediction.
We then construct a session feature matrix as:
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{S}=[\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\scriptscriptstyle|S|}]
.

4.1.2.
Session-level multi-intent generation

A straightforward way to acquire intents is to simply instruct LLMs to infer them for each session
(
Lian et al. 2025
;
Qiao et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
.
However, this approach has several limitations.
First, it lacks control over the generation; there is always a risk of hallucination, and LLMs often use different terms to refer to the same concept (e.g., eco-friendly vs. environmentally friendly skincare), making it difficult to handle the outputs consistently.
Further, even when a generated intent appears reasonable, it often remains overly broad (e.g., general summer necessities), providing limited insight into user behavior.

As a solution, we propose a new approach to ensure the quality of generated intents.
We first construct the
Global Intent Pool
, then use LLMs to pinpoint the most relevant intents from the pool and expand it only when no appropriate candidates are available.
By restricting the output space to the predefined pool, we can reduce hallucination and improve consistency in expression.
Furthermore, the generated intents are validated to be high-quality and informative for user intent prediction through the
Predict-and-Correct Loop
.
An illustration of this process is provided in Figure
1
.

Global Intent Pool (GIP).

We maintain the GIP, denoted as
\mathcal{G}
, to constrain the LLM’s output space for session-level intent discovery.
We first initialize
\mathcal{G}
by using Prompt
\mathcal{P}_{2}
to ask the LLM, conditioned on the recommendation domain
D
(e.g., Beauty), to generate a set of basic intents (e.g., “anti-aging”, “fragrance-free products”).

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{LLM}(\mathcal{P}_{2}(D)).

After initialization, we sequentially generate intents for each session using the P&C loop (described in the next section), where the LLM attempts to identify one or more relevant intents from
\mathcal{G}
.
If no appropriate intent is found, the LLM generates a new intent
c^{\prime}
.
The new intent is then added to
\mathcal{G}
, only if it is validated to contribute to an accurate prediction of user behavior:

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\leftarrow\mathcal{G}\cup\{c^{\prime}\}.

By reusing intents in
\mathcal{G}
across sessions and accumulating validated ones on the fly, we improve output consistency, reduce hallucinations, and progressively incorporate novel intents.

We introduce the P&C loop, designed to validate the quality of the LLM-generated intents.

We obtain

\displaystyle V^{(0)}_{S}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{S},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{I_{S}},\mathcal{G}).

Remarks on the efficiency of the P&C loop.

We acknowledge that iteratively predicting and correcting intents inevitably incurs additional computations.
However, we highlight that the P&C loop is applied only once offline before training.
At test time, we use a lightweight intent predictor (
section

4.2.1
), eliminating the need for costly LLM inference.
Moreover, with the improved quality of intents,
\proposed
consistently yields large improvements, even when intents are generated for only a small fraction of sessions.
Please refer to the efficiency analysis in
section

5.3

Remarks on the novelty of our intent generation.

There have been recent attempts to iteratively correct LLM outputs, including both parametric updates
(
Meng et al. 2022b
;
Meng et al. 2022a
)
and verbal feedback
(
Shinn et al. 2023

Our work builds upon this line of research.
While a few efforts have been made for SBR
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Sun et al. 2024
)
, they solely focus on using LLMs as recommenders by modifying input prompts with tailored and detailed instructions.
In contrast, we propose a new strategy for LLM-enhanced SBR: a constrained yet expandable intent pool to ensure output consistency, along with an effective correction mechanism to validate quality based on informativeness.

4.2.
Stage 2: Intent-guided SBR Enhancement

This stage aims to enhance SBR models using LLM-generated intents.
A naive approach is to simply treat them as an input text feature;
for instance, one may obtain their textual embeddings and feed them into the models as inputs.
However, this approach cannot effectively handle multiple intents of varying importance.
It also requires additional LLM inference at test time to generate intents for new sessions, which is impractical for real-world deployment.
Moreover, it cannot address LLM failure cases, which exist for a substantial portion of sessions (Appendix
C.1
).

We first propose a multi-intent prediction and fusion mechanism to effectively
(C2) incorporate multi-intents for SBR
(
section

4.2.1

We then introduce dynamic intent enrichment to
(C3) complement deficits in the LLM-generated intents
(
section

4.2.2

Intent-guided SBR enhancement in Stage 2.

Predicting multiple intents from a session.

To model the session-intent relevance, we employ the query-key-value design
(
Vaswani 2017
)
, where the relevance is computed by matching a query and a key.
Specifically, we obtain a session-aspect query
\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
and an intent-aspect key
\mathbf{k}_{c}
and value
\mathbf{v}_{c}
as follows:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\mathbf{W}_{q}\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\mathbf{k}_{c}=\mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{e}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\mathbf{v}_{c}=\mathbf{W}_{v}\mathbf{e}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\mathbf{W}_{q},\mathbf{W}_{k},\mathbf{W}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are the projection matrices for queries, keys, and values, respectively.
We compute the session-intent relevance, denoted as
\mathbf{\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}=[\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}]_{c\in\mathcal{G}}
, as follows:

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}=\sigma(\mathbf{q}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{c})\in[0,1],

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.
Unlike the softmax function—which enforces
\sum_{c}\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}=1
and makes intents mutually exclusive—the sigmoid function allows more flexibility for a session to reflect multiple relevant intents.
The session-intent relevance is then learned by the following loss:

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{intent}}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{G}}\Big[y^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}\log\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}+(1-y^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc})\log(1-\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc})\Big],

where
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=[y^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}]_{c\in\mathcal{G}}
denotes the intent labels at training epoch
t
.
In the early stages of training, the labels are generated from the LLM-generated intents, excluding the failure cases.
As training progresses, we incorporate enriched intent labels that complement the LLM failure cases, which will be introduced in
section

4.2.2
.

Fusing multi-intents information.

We enhance the session representation with intents based on their varying relevance to the session.
We create the guidance representation, denoted as
\mathbf{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, as:

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\mathrm{Dropout}\Big(\mathrm{LayerNorm}\big(\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\sum_{c\in\mathcal{G}}\phi(\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc})\mathbf{v}_{c}\big)\Big)

where
\phi(x)=x\cdot\mathbb{I}[x>\tau]
is a filtering function that retains values
x
exceeds the threshold
\tau
.
This simple filtering function is used to better focus on intents above a certain degree of relevance, while reflecting their relative relevance.
In this work, we set
\tau=0.5
.
The final intent-enhanced representation is obtained as:

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\mathbf{g}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
.
Based on that, the typical next item prediction loss is adopted as:

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}=-\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{train}}}\Big[\log\sigma(\mathbf{e}_{i_{\text{pos}}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{S})+\!\;\sum_{i_{\text{neg}}}\log\big(1-\sigma(\mathbf{e}^{\top}_{i_{\text{neg}}}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{S})\big)\Big]

For each session, we compare the next item
i_{\text{pos}}
against two negative items:
one randomly sampled, and one hard negative selected from the items mispredicted in the P&C loop.
If no hard negatives are available, both negatives are sampled at random.

4.2.2.
Collaborative intent enrichment

As discussed earlier, LLMs often struggle to infer session intents due to limited context.
As a result, they may fail to pass the P&C loop, generating no intents for some sessions.
We aim to develop a cost-effective strategy to complement such LLM failures.
Our key idea is to leverage collaborative patterns captured by the SBR models;
during training, the model learns to predict intents for each session (Eq. (
9
)), and uses them for next-item prediction (Eq. (
11
)-(
12
)).
This enables the model to infer intents for unlabeled sessions based on behavioral similarities across sessions, providing valuable clues to fill in missing intent information.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LLM}}_{S}
be the set of intents generated by the LLM for session
S
, with
\mathcal{C}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LLM}}_{S}=\emptyset
if none are inferred.
At the beginning of the training, we generate the initial intent labels
\mathbf{y}^{(0)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=[y^{(0)}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}]_{c\in\mathcal{G}}
from
\mathcal{C}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LLM}}_{S}
only using the cases where the LLM successfully infers the intents:

(13)

\displaystyle y^{(0)}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}=\begin{cases}1,&c\in\mathcal{C}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{LLM}}_{S},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Intent label enrichment.

Our strategy is inspired by self-training
(
Nigam and Ghani 2000
;
Grandvalet and Bengio 2004
)
, a well-established semi-supervised learning technique where a model learns from its own predictions on unlabeled data.
During training, we enrich the incomplete intent labels using predictions from the intent prediction module.
Specifically, we utilize intent predictions of the top-
K
most similar training sessions. Using the session representation
\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, we first identify its top-
K
neighbor sessions
\mathcal{N}(S)
based on cosine similarity:

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(S)=\mathrm{TopK}(\{\text{sim}_{\scriptscriptstyle S^{\prime}}\}_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{train}},S^{\prime}\neq S}),\quad\text{sim}_{\scriptscriptstyle S^{\prime}}=\mathrm{cos}(\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle S},\mathbf{h}_{\scriptscriptstyle S^{\prime}}).

The intent distributions of these neighbors are aggregated using similarity-based weights
w_{\scriptscriptstyle S^{\prime}}\in[0,1]
, normalized via a Softmax
6
6

6

w_{\scriptscriptstyle S^{\prime}}={\mathrm{exp}(\text{sim}_{\scriptscriptstyle S^{\prime}})}/{\sum_{S^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(S)}\mathrm{exp}(\text{sim}_{\scriptscriptstyle S^{\prime\prime}})}
:

(15)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\text{nbr}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\!=\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(S)}\!w_{S^{\prime}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{S^{\prime}}.

At training step
t
, the final enriched intent labels
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
are obtained:

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\text{nbr}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}).

These enriched labels
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
are then used as targets for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{intent}}
(Eq. (
10
)).
As updating the labels every epoch is both inefficient and unnecessary, we update
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
every
\rho
epochs.
We set
\rho
to 5.

The enriched labels
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
provide richer supervision in two aspects.
First, for LLM-failure cases where the original labels are missing, the enriched labels provide pseudo-intents based on the predictions of most similar sessions.
Notably, compared to relying solely on a single session’s prediction
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, our neighborhood-based enrichment using
\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\text{nbr}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
can provide a more stable and reliable signal.
Second, unlike the initial binary labels
\mathbf{y}^{(0)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, the enriched labels
\mathbf{y}^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
reveal soft probabilities that reflect a session’s relevance to each intent, offering more nuanced supervision for learning.

4.3.
Optimization of
\proposed

The final learning objective of
\proposed
is as follows:

(17)

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\end{subarray}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Rec}}+\lambda_{\text{intent}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{intent}}+\lambda_{\text{decouple}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{decouple}},

where
\theta=\{\mathrm{SBR}(\cdot),\mathbf{W}_{q},\mathbf{W}_{k},\mathbf{W}_{v},\mathbf{E}_{c}\}
denotes the parameters of
\proposed
, and
\mathbf{E}_{c}
is the embeddings of all intents.
We additionally adopt an intent-decoupling loss, widely used in prior intent-aware methods
(
Yang et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024b
)
, to encourage each intent embedding to capture a distinct aspect:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{decouple}}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|(|\mathcal{G}|-1)}\sum^{|\mathcal{G}|}_{i=1}\sum^{|\mathcal{G}|}_{j=1,j\neq i}||\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{c_{i}}-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{c_{j}}||^{2}_{2}
with the L2-normalized intent embedding
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{c}
.
The overall training process is detailed in Appendix
A.1
.

Inference.

Test sessions do not have any LLM-generated intents.
As shown in Figure
2
,
\proposed
identifies the relevant intents and reflect them to prediction, without incurring LLM latency for intent extraction.
The next item is predicted as:
\operatorname{argmax}_{i}\,\sigma(\mathbf{e}^{\top}_{i}\;\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S_{\text{test}}})
.

5.2.
Performance Comparison

Table
2
presents the performance comparison of our two VELI4SBR variants against the baselines. Overall, both
\proposed
and
\proposed
+LE consistently outperform every baseline across all datasets and metrics.
This improvement stems from three key factors.

First, unlike implicit intent modeling (i.e., ELCRec, MiaSRec),
\proposed
explicitly infers intents via LLM reasoning, providing semantically meaningful guidance to the SBR model.
Second, compared to explicit intent modeling (i.e., FAPAT, C
a
F
e
) that rely on sparse and noisy item features,
\proposed
initially refines these features using the LLM’s world knowledge for more precise intent inference.
It then generates session
-
level multi
-
intents by considering the entire session context, making them more informative than a mere list of item
-
level features.
Third, in contrast to LLM-enhanced recommenders (i.e., LLM4SBR, LLM-ESR, RLMRec-Con, RLMRec-Gen) that directly use raw LLM outputs,
\proposed
validates the quality of LLM-generated intents within the P&C loop, thereby ensuring informative and reliable intents for the SBR model.

Interestingly,
\proposed
with LE outperforms
\proposed
only for BERT4Rec on the Beauty and Yelp datasets.
We speculate that LLM embeddings help mitigate data sparsity when BERT’s bidirectional modeling struggles with sparse interactions in Beauty or with very short sessions on Yelp (the average length
<4
)
(
Li et al. 2022
)
.

5.3.
Efficiency Analysis

As real-world systems process massive numbers of sessions, generating intents via LLMs for every session is often inefficient.
In this section, we analyze the training and inference efficiency of
\proposed
.
We use SASRec as a backbone SBR model.

Training efficiency.

We train
\proposed
using all training sessions, but provide LLM-generated intents for only a limited portion (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) of them.
Figure
3
shows that
\proposed
delivers competitive performance even when trained with only partial intent supervision, and significantly outperforms the 0% setup.
We attribute this to the synergy between validated intents from the P&C loop
and collaborative intent enrichment module, which enables the SBR model to propagate high-quality intent information effectively during training.
These results indicate the training efficiency of
\proposed
, showing that inferring intents for only a small subset of sessions can be sufficiently effective.

Inference efficiency.

\proposed
adopts an LLM-enhanced SBR approach that avoids costly LLM inference during the test phase.
To highlight its efficiency, we compare
\proposed
with PO4ISR
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
, a state-of-the-art LLM-as-SBR method that performs both intent prediction and recommendation using LLMs at inference time.
Following
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
, we use 20 candidate items, which include 19 negative items from the pretrained SASRec backbone and 1 ground-truth item.
We use a single RTX 3090 GPU and AMD EPYC 7413 CPU.

\proposed

Notably, even without LLM embeddings,
\proposed
shows highly competitive performance.
Given PO4ISR’s reliance on extensive LLM computations during inference time, this result is particularly impressive.
At test time,
\proposed
uses a lightweight intent predictor, requiring no LLM inference and enabling efficient deployment at scale.

6.
Conclusion

We introduce
\proposed
to address three unique challenges in existing LLM-enhanced SBR approaches.
Throughout a two-stage process,
\proposed
generates high-quality validated intents using LLM in the P&C loop equipped with a global intent pool, providing session-level multi-intent guidance to the SBR model.
We also propose a collaborative intent enrichment strategy to dynamically complement the deficits of LLM-generated intents.
Our experiments show that
\proposed
not only outperforms the baselines but also provides meaningful interpretability, offering insights into the multi-faceted nature of user intents.
We expect
\proposed
to alleviate data sparsity and enhance both recommendation quality and explainability, ultimately broadening the applicability of SBR.

A.2.
Derivation of
\mathcal{L}_{\text{intent}}

y^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}
denotes the relevance score of intent
c
for session
S
at epoch
t
, serving as a soft label.

\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}
is the model’s predicted probability of that intent.
For brevity, let
y:=y^{(t)}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}
and
\hat{y}:=\hat{y}_{\scriptscriptstyle Sc}
.
Since multiple intents can co-occur independently within a session, we treat each
y
as a Bernoulli random variable with parameter
\hat{y}
(i.e.,
y\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\hat{y})
).
The likelihood
L(\theta)
of all intents is
L(\theta)=\prod_{c\in\mathcal{G}}\hat{y}^{\,y}\,(1-\hat{y})^{1-y}.

Taking the negative log of
L(\theta)
and normalizing by
|\mathcal{G}|
yields

\frac{-1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\log L(\theta)=\frac{-1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{G}}\Big[y\log\hat{y}+(1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})\Big],

which is exactly the same as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{intent}}
(Eq. (
10
)).
