Monotonicity Testing of High-Dimensional Distributions with Subcube Conditioning
Deeparnab Chakrabarty, Xi Chen, Simeon Ristic, C. Seshadhri, Erik Waingarten

Abstract
We study monotonicity testing of high-dimensional distributions on { − 1 , 1 } n \{-1,1\}^{n} in the model of subcube conditioning, suggested and studied by Canonne, Ron, and Servedio [ CRS15 ] and Bhattacharyya and Chakraborty [ BC18 ] . Previous work shows that the sample complexity of monotonicity testing must be exponential in n n (Rubinfeld, Vasilian [ RV20 ] , and Aliakbarpour, Gouleakis, Peebles, Rubinfeld, Yodpinyanee [ AGP+19 ] ). We show that the subcube query complexity is Θ ~ ​ ( n / ε 2 ) \tilde{\Theta}(n/\varepsilon^{2}) , by proving nearly matching upper and lower bounds. Our work is the first to use directed isoperimetric inequalities (developed for function monotonicity testing) for analyzing a distribution testing algorithm. Along the way, we generalize an inequality of Khot, Minzer, and Safra [ KMS18 ] to real-valued functions on { − 1 , 1 } n \{-1,1\}^{n} . We also study uniformity testing of distributions that are promised to be monotone, a problem introduced by Rubinfeld, Servedio [ RS09 ] , using subcube conditioning. We show that the query complexity is Θ ~ ​ ( n / ε 2 ) \tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2}) . Our work proves the lower bound, which matches (up to poly-logarithmic factors) the uniformity testing upper bound for general distributions (Canonne, Chen, Kamath, Levi, Waingarten [ CCK+21 ] ). Hence, we show that monotonicity does not help, beyond logarithmic factors, in testing uniformity of distributions with subcube conditional queries.

1
Introduction

Distribution testing is the study of algorithms for testing properties of probability distributions
[
GR00
,
BFR+00
]
. A distribution testing problem is specified by a class of distributions
\mathcal{P}

supported on a domain
\Sigma
. The aim is to get low-complexity algorithms that distinguish an unknown probability distribution
p
having the property (i.e.,
p\in\mathcal{P}
)
from one that is “far” from having the property. One hallmark result is an algorithm and a matching lower bound, showing that
\Theta(\sqrt{|\Sigma|}/\varepsilon^{2})
independent samples are necessary and sufficient for testing whether
p
is the uniform distribution (see, the recent survey
[
Can22
]
and references therein for an overview of the area). For most problems of interest, such a polynomial dependence on the support size
\Sigma
is intrinsic. This makes classical distribution testing algorithms intractable for high-dimensional distributions, such as those supported on the hypercube
\Sigma=\{-1,1\}^{n}
, where the complexity becomes exponential in the dimension.

To circumvent this issue, Bhattacharrya-Chakraborty
[
BC18
]
and Canonne-Ron-Servedio
[
CRS15
]
introduced the
subcube conditional model

for distributions supported on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. An algorithm can query the distribution
p
with a subcube
\rho\in\{-1,1,*\}^{n}
, and receive an independent sample
\boldsymbol{x}\sim p
conditioned on
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\rho_{i}
for all
i
where
\rho_{i}\neq*
. We highlight some appealing aspects of this model.

•

For high-dimensional distributions, the subcube conditional model may provide an appropriate analogue to a “membership query” in learning theory, where distribution testing algorithms can overcome exponential dependencies in the dimension (e.g., see
[
CJLW21
,
BLMT23
]
).

•

There is a growing body of work seeking to make high-dimensional distribution testing practical (particularly in the context of testing software that produces samples), where one can often implement more powerful query oracles, and in particular, the subcube conditional sampling oracle (e.g., see
[
MPC20
,
PM22
,
BCP+24
]
).

•

Subcube conditioning lends itself to an elegant mathematical analysis, often leading to query complexities polynomial in the dimension (as opposed
to domain size). The key to an efficient testing result often involves (approximately) determining a global property of the distribution while only estimating marginals after conditioning on (random) subcubes of the domain (e.g., see
[
CCK+21
,
BCvV23
,
CM24
,
AFL24
]
).

In this paper, we focus on the classic property of monotonicity of distributions. We use
\preceq
to denote the coordinate-wise partial order on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
and use
p
to denote the probability mass function.
A distribution
p
supported on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
is monotone if, for any pair
x,y\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
with
x\preceq y
,

p(x)\leq p(y)
.
Monotonicity arises naturally in many scenarios and is a desirable property from an algorithmic perspective (e.g.,
[
BLMT23
]
).
For any distribution
p
, we define the
distance to monotonicity
as
\min_{q}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)
,
where the minimum is over all monotone distributions
q
and
d_{\mathrm{TV}}
is the total variation distance between distributions. We say that
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from monotone

if the distance to monotonicity is at least
\varepsilon
.
A
tester for monotonicity
gets access to a distribution
p
and a proximity parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
If
p
is monotone, the tester accepts with probability
>2/3
. If
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from monotone,
the tester rejects with probability
>2/3
.
Our main question is: what is the query complexity of monotonicity testing of distributions over the hypercube
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, in the subcube conditional model?

1.1
Our Contributions

Testing Monotonicity.

Our first results are a testing algorithm and a nearly-matching lower bound which shows the query complexity of testing monotonicity in the subcube conditional model is
linear
in the dimension.

Theorem 1

(Monotonicity Testing Upper Bound)
.

There is an algorithm for testing monotonicity of distributions over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
that uses
\tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
subcube conditioning queries. The algorithm works in the weaker
coordinate oracle
model
[
BCvV23
]
, where queries are only made on one-dimensional subcubes.

Theorem 2

(Monotonicity Testing Lower Bound)
.

Any monotonicity tester of distributions using subcube conditioning must use
\tilde{\Omega}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
queries.

We will overview the proofs of
Theorem
1
and
Theorem
2
shortly. Roughly speaking, the upper bound will follow from exploiting a connection between monotonicity testing and directed isoperimetric inequalities, and defining an “edge tester” for distribution testing. For the lower bound, we construct a pair of distributions over product distributions which will “hide” the negative biases.

Directed Isoperimetry.
The key tool enabling
Theorem
1
is a directed isoperimetric inequality for real-valued functions. As we expand on in Section
1.3
, isoperimetric inequalities relate the surface area of a geometric object to its volume. In the hypercube, the volume of a subset
A\subset\{-1,1\}^{n}
is its size and the surface area is a measure of edges “crossing” the set.
Directed
isoperimetry is the phenomenon where a non-monotone function exhibits evidence of its non-monotonicity via a directed edge boundary (points where the function value decreases). Directed isoperimetric inequalities relate the “non-monotone edge boundary” (the measure of surface area) to the distance to monotonicity (the measure of volume). These inequalities have played a major role in testing monotonicity of functions. In this work, we use a real-valued version of a (Boolean-valued) directed isoperimetric inequality of Khot-Minzer-Safra
[
KMS18
]
(also
[
PRW22
]
, who remove a logarithmic factor), which may be of independent interest.
1
1

1

As we discuss in Section
1.3
, real-valued versions of directed isoperimetric inequalities have been studied
[
BKR23
]
, although we will need one of a different form.
Namely, let
\mathrm{dist}_{1}(f)
denote the
\ell_{1}
-distance to monotonicity of any

f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, i.e., the minimum over monotone functions
g\colon\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbbm{R}
of
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[|f(\boldsymbol{x})-g(\boldsymbol{x})|]
.

Theorem 3
.

For any
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbbm{R}
, we have

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left[\left(\sum_{i:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=-1}\left(\big(f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})\big)^{+}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right]\geq\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}\right)\cdot\mathrm{dist}_{1}(f).

(We use
(z)^{+}
as shorthand for
\max(z,0)
, and for
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
,
x^{(i)}\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
refers to the point that agrees with
x
on all but the
i
-th coordinate.)
The proof of this theorem is obtained by a reduction to the Boolean case, using a thresholding technique of Berman-Raskhodnikova-Yaroslavstev
[
BRY14
]
.
We note that this theorem answers a open question in
[
Pin23
]

(the question mark in Table 1, which asks for an
(L^{1},\ell^{2})
-Poincáre theorem)).
It is an interesting open problem to determine whether the dependence on
n
in
Theorem
3
can be removed (no such dependence exists for the Boolean valued case).

Uniformity of Monotone Distributions.
We then turn our attention to a problem introduced by Rubinfeld-Servedio, where they seek distribution testing algorithms under the promise that the underlying distributions are monotone
[
RS09
]
. They study the classic problem of uniformity testing, and show that
\tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
independent samples suffice (a significant improvement over the exponential sample lower bounds). With subcube conditioning, one would hope for further significant improvements. In particular, uniformity testing (without any assumption on the distribution) can be done in
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2})
queries
[
CCK+21
]
; does the complexity decrease further when the input distribution is promised to be monotone?
We prove a lower bound, showing that uniformity testing of monotone distributions requires
\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2})
subcube conditioning queries, i.e., it is equally hard for general and monotone distributions.

Theorem 4

(Testing Uniformity of Monotone Distributions)
.

Testing uniformity of monotone distributions requires
\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2})
subcube conditioning queries.

1.2
Main Ideas

We give a short summary of the main ideas behind our results.

Monotonicity Testing Upper Bound.
The algorithm behind
Theorem
1
is an “edge tester” for distribution testing. We take a sample
\boldsymbol{x}\sim p
and choose a direction
\boldsymbol{i}\sim[n]
uniformly at random, and we consider the distribution
p
conditioned on
\smash{\{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{(\boldsymbol{i})}\}}
, or equivalently, on the 1-dimensional subcube
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
which is
\boldsymbol{x}
except for
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=*
. Notice that the conditional distribution is supported on one bit
\{-1,1\}
. For our algorithm, it suffices to condition on 1-dimensional subcubes, which have been studied under the name
coordinate oracles

[
BCvV23
]
. In a monotone distribution,
1
is more likely to appear than
-1
, so if the algorithm can detect (by few independent samples from the subcube) that the probability of
-1
is larger than that of
1
, it can safely output “reject” (meaning non-monotone).
The challenge is understanding, when
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from monotone, how likely it is that an “edge”
\smash{\{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\}}
is biased toward
-1
, and is this bias detectable from few samples.

The connection to directed isoperimetry then becomes clear:
considering the case when
p
is
\varepsilon
-far
from monotone, we apply
Theorem
3
on the probability mass function
p
, where
\mathrm{dist}_{1}(p)
can be shown to be
\Omega(\varepsilon)
(Corollary
2.4
). Then, the left-hand side of
Theorem
3
can be used to establish (via an averaging argument and an “importance sampling” trick) how large the bias toward
-1
will be on a random draw of
\boldsymbol{x}\sim p
and
\boldsymbol{i}\sim[n]
(in the proof of Lemma
2.3
). We emphasize that, even though we seek lower bounds on the biases of individual edges, which a (simpler) directed Poincaré inequality would seemingly handle (namely, Theorem 1.3 in
[
Pin23
]
), such an argument only gives a weaker
O(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
complexity. The reason is the following: directed Poincaré lower bounds the sum of
(p(x)-p(x^{(i)}))^{+}
across all edges
\{x,x^{(i)}\}
, and would imply an edge-wise bias of
\varepsilon/n
(since there are
n2^{n}
edges); however, the query complexity of detecting bias is inverse
quadratic
in the bias. On the other hand,
Theorem
3
rules out such situations: if all edges had negative bias
\varepsilon/n
,
p
would be
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon/\sqrt{n})
-close to monotone.

Monotonicity Testing Lower Bound.
Our starting point is to focus on proving sample-complexity lower bounds for testing the analogous problems (namely, monotonicity and uniformity of monotone distribution) in the restricted setting of
product distributions
. This is a significant restriction from general distributions
2
2

2

Product distributions over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
are fully specified by their mean vector, so in order to describe a product distribution, it suffices to specify the
n
values of
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim p}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}]
. General distributions over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, on the other hand, lie in the convex hull of
\{e_{i}:i\in[2^{n}]\}
and require
2^{n}-1
numbers to specify.
, and these immediately imply general query lower bounds for subcube conditioning (Lemma
3.1
). In particular, conditioning on subsets of coordinates (i.e., subcubes) do not change the distribution at all if coordinates were independent to begin with. The surprising aspect is that these lower bounds on product distributions will turn out to be nearly optimal (this was also the case in
[
CCK+21
,
CJLW21
]
).

Let us first focus on the monotonicity testing lower bound (
Theorem
2
), where we use Yao’s minimax
principle. Since a product distribution is fully specified by its mean vector, we construct a pair of distributions
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
supported on
[-1,1]
; and let the mean vector (and hence product distribution) have, for each
i\in[n]
, the
i
-th coordinate given by the
i
-th draw from
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{B}
(Lemma
3.2
).
\mathcal{A}
will always output non-negative numbers (so the corresponding product distribution will always be monotone), while
\mathcal{B}
will be
-\varepsilon/\sqrt{n}
with constant probability (so the corresponding product distribution will be
\Omega(\varepsilon)
-far from monotone). The key trick is to design
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
in such a way so as to have their first
\log n/\log\log n
moments being identical; in Section
3.4
we show how it implies that
\tilde{\Omega}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
samples are needed to distinguish product distributions they produce.
The matching moments technique for lower bounds has been used before
[
RRSS09
,
Val11
]
. Most recently, it was used to prove lower bounds for subcube conditioning for testing and learning
k
-juntas
[
CJLW21
]
.

Uniformity of Monotone Distributions.
The lower bound on testing uniformity of monotone distributions also proceeds by Yao’s minimax principle. We note that, the fact that testing uniformity of product distributions required
\Omega(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2})
samples was known
[
CDKS17
]
; however, the examples to obtain the lower bound were non-monotone (each coordinate behaves independently with bias set to
\pm\varepsilon/\sqrt{n}
). In our construction, each of the
n
coordinates is biased with probability
1/\sqrt{n}
, but its bias becomes
\varepsilon/n^{1/4}
(Section
4.1
). Note that all biases are positive, so the resulting distribution is monotone.
In order to prove indistinguishability, we reduce to the corresponding problem under Gaussian distributions (instead of bits). The calculation
boils down to the behavior of sum of exponentials of Gaussians, which can be determined by the (closed form) expression for the moment generating function (Section
4.3
).

Related Work

As mentioned above, directed isoperimetry theorems have been crucial to the development of testers for monotonicity of Boolean functions

The most relevant works to

[

Pin24
]

. In

-geometry and proves under a certain

]
, Pinto Jr. assumes

-smoothness and proves that the expected

-norm of the gradient is at least the square of the

In

, Black, Kalemaj and Raskhodnikova consider Boolean functions

In this setting,

Finally, in

Monotonicity Testing of Distributions.

Monotonicity of distributions has been studied extensively in the literature, in both low-dimensional and high-dimensional regimes

Testing Monotonicity

In this section, we show that using the directed and real-valued version of Talagrand’s inequality, we may design an “edge tester” for testing monotonicity of distributions using subcube conditioning. In particular, we give the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

There exists an algorithm that receives as input subcube conditioning access to an unknown distribution
p
supported on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, as well as an accuracy parameter
\varepsilon
. The algorithm makes
\tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
subcube conditioning queries and satisfies the following guarantees:

•

If
p
is monotone, the algorithm outputs “accept” with probability at least
0.9
.

•

If
p
is
\varepsilon
-far from monotone, the algorithm outputs “reject” with probability at least
0.9
.

The algorithm referred to in Theorem
5
is given in Figure
1
.
We break up the proof of
Theorem
5

into a few parts. First, we argue about the running time.

. We receive as input subcube conditioning access to an unknown distribution

which is supported on

. Furthermore, we receive the accuracy parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. We let

1.

For all integers

2.1
The Real-Valued Directed Talagrand Inequality

We will prove a “directed isoperimetric theorem” for real-valued functions. This is an important
tool used for the analysis of the monotonicity tester. We define notions of the
directed boundary for Boolean functions.

Let
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[0,1]
be a function defined on the
n

L_{1}
-distance
of

from monotonicity is defined as

\mathrm{dist}_{1}(f)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\min_{g~:~\text{monotone}}~~\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left[|f(\boldsymbol{x})-g(\boldsymbol{x})|\right]

where the expectation is over the uniform distribution over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
.
For a point

, define the directed derivative
\nabla^{-}f(x)
to be the
n
-dimensional vector defined as

\left(\nabla^{-}f(x)\right)_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\begin{cases}0&\textrm{if $x_{i}=1$}\\
\left(f(x)-f(x+2e_{i})\right)^{+}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(4)

where
(z)^{+}
is a shorthand for
\max(z,0)
. For Boolean-valued

, the distance
\mathrm{dist}_{1}(f)
corresponds to the “normal” Hamming distance notion,
\mathrm{dist}_{0}(f)
.
Based on isoperimetric theorems of Talagrand
[

]
, the quantity

can be thought
of as a “directed surface area” for the function

. A deep isoperimetric theorem of Khot, Minzer, and Safra
[
KMS18
]
(see, also
[
PRW22
]
, who showed how to remove the final logarithmic factor)
lower bounds this surface area by the distance to monotonicity.

Theorem 6

(
[
KMS18
,
PRW22
]
)
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for every

,

\hbox{\bf E}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left\lVert\nabla^{-}f(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert_{2}\geq C\cdot\mathrm{dist}_{0}(f)
.

Theorem
3
gives a real-valued generalization of the above theorem, with a

loss in the bound. The proof appears in Subsection
2.2
, but we state the following corollary used in the tester’s analysis.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let

Proof:
Let
\varepsilon(p)
be the distance of

So we need an extra calculation to apply

Let

Overall, we deduce that

With our lower bound for

So

We expand out the expression for

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left[\left\lVert\nabla^{-}p(\boldsymbol{x})\right\rVert\right]=2^{-n}\sum_{x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\left\lVert\nabla^{-}p(x)\right\rVert=2^{-n}\sum_{x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\sqrt{\sum_{i:x_{i}=-1}\left((p(x)-p(x+2e_{i}))^{+}\right)^{2}}

The

Lower Bound for Testing Monotonicity

In this section, we prove a query complexity lower bound on testing monotonicity of distributions using subcube conditioning queries.

Theorem 7

(Monotonicity Testing – Lower Bound)
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, an
\varepsilon
-test for monotonicity of distributions must make
\tilde{\Omega}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
queries.

3.1
Preliminaries

Our lower bound proofs proceed by Yao’s method. We consider a property of distribution
\mathcal{P}
that we want to test.
We describe two distributions,
\mathcal{D}_{\text{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
, supported on
product
distributions over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
which, in addition, satisfy the following properties:

•

Every distribution
p
in
\mathcal{D}_{\text{yes}}
is a distribution over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
lies in
\mathcal{P}
.

•

A distribution
\boldsymbol{p}
drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
is a distribution over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
which is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathcal{P}
with probability at least
0.99
(over the draw of
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
).

Consider any deterministic algorithm which can
\varepsilon
-test monotonicity which makes
q
queries. The algorithm is specified by a depth-
q
decision tree of subcube conditioning queries; each non-leaf node of the decision tree specifies a subcube
\rho\in\{-1,1,*\}^{n}
which is the query performed at that node, and has
2^{\mathrm{stars}(\rho)}
many children, corresponding to the possible completions of
\rho
that the algorithm would receive after such a query; each leaf is labeled “accept”, or “reject” corresponding to the output. In the case input distributions
p

product

\varepsilon
-testing algorithms.

Lemma 3.1

(Decision Tree to i.i.d Samples)
.

Let
p

\{-1,1\}^{n}
, and
\mathrm{Alg}
be a deterministic
q

\mathrm{Alg}^{\prime}\colon\{-1,1\}^{nq}\to\{\text{``accept''},\text{``reject''}\}
which exactly simulates the algorithm on independent samples, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}\left[\mathrm{Alg}(p)=\text{``accept''}\right]=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q}\sim p}\left[\mathrm{Alg}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})=\text{``accept''}\right].

We describe the function
\mathrm{Alg}^{\prime}\colon\{-1,1\}^{nq}\to\{\text{``accept''},\text{``reject''}\}
by specifying how it computes on independent samples
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q}

q

\mathrm{Alg}
. The algorithm maintains the current node

, initially set to the root of
\mathrm{Alg}
, and a counter

, initially set to

. If
u

which specifies a subcube conditioning query. We update

(\boldsymbol{x}_{c})_{|\mathrm{stars}(\rho)}

3.2
The one-dimensional mean distributions

A distribution supported on product distributions on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
may be equivalently specified by describing a distribution on vectors in
[-1,1]^{n}
, corresponding to the mean vectors of the distribution.
In this section, we show the existence of one-dimensional distributions with special properties. In the next subsection, we use these
distributions to generate mean vectors, from which the hypercube distributions are constructed. The properties of the next
lemma are crucial for the main lower bound.

Lemma 3.2

(One-Dimensional Bias Distributions)
.

Let
c_{0}
be some absolute positive constant.
Fix any natural number parameter
\ell
. There exist two discrete distributions
{\cal A}
and
\mathcal{B}
with the following properties.

•

{\cal A}
is supported on
\{0\}\cup[\ell^{3}]
and
\mathcal{B}
is supported on
\{-1,0\}\cup[\ell^{3}]
.

•

For every

,
\hbox{\bf E}_{i\sim{\cal A}}[i^{k}]=\hbox{\bf E}_{j\sim\mathcal{B}}[j^{k}]
.

•

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{j\sim\mathcal{B}}[j=-1]>c_{0}
.

The first step of the proof is to construct a solution to a linear system that captures the second point above (the equality of moments).
For convenience, let
\alpha_{j}=j^{3}
for each

Consider the following
(\ell+1)\times(\ell+1)

\displaystyle A:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}1&0&0&\dots&0\\
1&1&1&\dots&1\\

(-1)^{2}&\alpha_{1}^{2}&\alpha_{2}^{2}&\dots&\alpha_{\ell}^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
(-1)^{\ell-1}&\alpha_{1}^{\ell-1}&\alpha_{2}^{\ell-1}&\dots&\alpha_{\ell}^{\ell-1}\end{array}\right].

Claim 3.3
.

The matrix

is invertible and the vector

has

.

Proof:
It is convenient to index the rows/columns of

The proof of Claim

[

. Let

i<j\end{subarray}}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i})\neq 0,

as long as

are distinct, and this means that

is invertible.

We now compute

using Cramer’s rule:

and for each

is given by

\displaystyle z_{i}=\dfrac{\det(A_{i})}{\det(A)}=(\pm 1)\cdot\dfrac{\det(V(-1;\alpha_{-i}))}{\det(V)}.

The numerator

denotes the determinant of the matrix where the

is replaced by

\det(A_{i})
is the determinant of the Vandermonde matrix of the entries

\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}

\displaystyle\det\big(V(-1;\alpha_{-i})\big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}(\alpha_{j}+1)\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2}\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}\\
j_{1}<j_{2}\end{subarray}}(\alpha_{j_{2}}-\alpha_{j_{1}})\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\det(V)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j>i}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i})\prod_{j<i}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},j_{2}\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}\\
j_{1}<j_{2}\end{subarray}}(\alpha_{j_{2}}-\alpha_{j_{1}}),

and

\displaystyle|z_{i}|=\prod_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}\dfrac{\alpha_{j}+1}{|\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}|}=\prod_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{|\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}|}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}\right)=\prod_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}}\prod_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}\right),

and therefore,

\displaystyle|z_{i}|\leq\left|\prod_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}}\right|\cdot\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{j^{3}}\right)\leq O(1)\cdot\left|\prod_{j\in[\ell]\setminus\{i\}}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}}\right|.

Finally, the bound on the
\|z\|_{1}
follows from summing up the right-most expression above, which is
O(1)
, with the exact derivation in Claim 6.3 of
[
CJLW21
]
.

Proof:
(of Lemma
3.2
) Consider the vector
z\in\mathbbm{R}^{\ell+1}
from
Claim
3.3
.
For convenience we index
z
using

. Note that
z_{0}=1
. Let

be the
set of coordinates that are negative, and

be the set of positive coordinate. (We do

The distribution
{\cal A}
is supported on
\{i^{3}|i\in N\}

The distribution

The first bullet of the lemma holds by the above construction. For the second lemma, consider the row of the matrix

The distributions

Recall the distributions
{\cal A}

We now define

We applying a scaling to define the mean vector

Observe that all the coordinates means for

Lemma 3.4
.

Every distribution in the support of
\mathcal{D}_{\text{yes}}
is monotone.

Proof:
Consider

Lemma 3.5
.

A distribution
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}

Proof:

Let

Let

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{p}(x)-\boldsymbol{p}(y))=\Omega(\varepsilon).

Suppose

Then
g(x)>\boldsymbol{p}(x)-(\boldsymbol{p}(x)-\boldsymbol{p}(y))/2=(\boldsymbol{p}(x)+\boldsymbol{p}(y))/2

And
g(y)<\boldsymbol{p}(y)+(\boldsymbol{p}(x)-\boldsymbol{p}(y))/2=(\boldsymbol{p}(x)+\boldsymbol{p}(y))/2

g(x)>g(y)

contradicting the fact that

is monotone. Hence,
|\boldsymbol{p}(x)-g(x)|+|\boldsymbol{p}(y)-g(y)|\geq(\boldsymbol{p}(x)-\boldsymbol{p}(y))/2

Summing over all pairs

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\left\{(x,x_{\overline{\mathbf{N}}}\sigma(x_{\mathbf{N}}):x_{\mathbf{N}}\in\{-1,1\}^{m}\text{ contains at most $m/2$ entries set to $1$}\right\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}(y)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{p}(x)\cdot\prod_{j\in\mathbf{N}}\dfrac{1+y_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}}{1+x_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}}=\boldsymbol{p}(x)\left(1-\frac{c_{0}\cdot\varepsilon}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2r}\left(1+\frac{c_{0}\cdot\varepsilon}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{-2r}\leq\boldsymbol{p}(x)\left(1-\frac{c_{0}\cdot\varepsilon}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2r}.

\mathrm{Alg}\colon\{-1,1\}^{nq}\to\{\text{``accept''},\text{``reject''}\}
cannot output “accept” with probability at least
0.99
when samples are drawn from the uniform distribution, and output “reject” with probability at least
0.99
when samples are drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
.

4.1
The distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}

A draw of
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
is generated as follows:

•

First, we let
\mathcal{D}
denote the distribution over vectors
\boldsymbol{\mu}
where we
independently set
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
to be
\varepsilon/n^{1/4}
with probability
1/\sqrt{n}
and
0
otherwise.

•

Then, we let
\boldsymbol{p}
be the monotone product distribution on
\{-1,1\}^{n}
whose mean vector is
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

The fact that
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
is far from the uniform distribution follows from the subsequent lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

With probability at least
1-o_{n}(1)
,
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
is
\Omega(\varepsilon)
-far from the uniform distribution.

Proof:

We consider a draw of
\boldsymbol{\mu}\sim\mathcal{D}
, and note that with probability at least
1-o_{n}(1)
,
\boldsymbol{p}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
has a set
\mathbf{N}\subset[n]
of at least
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
coordinates
i
with
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}=\varepsilon/n^{1/4}
. Fix such a draw and let
\boldsymbol{p}
denote the corresponding distribution. The total variation distance from
\boldsymbol{p}
, generated with mean vector
\mu
, to the uniform distribution can be lower bounded by considering strings
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
which have fewer
1
’s than
-1
’s in coordinates of
\mathbf{N}
.
For each such string
x
, letting
t(x)
denote the number of coordinates in
\mathbf{N}
with
x_{i}=1
, the probability of
x
in
\boldsymbol{p}
is

\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{t(x)}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{|\mathbf{N}|-t(x)}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{t(x)}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{|\mathbf{N}|-2t(x)}<\frac{1}{2^{n}}.

As a result, we can bounded
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{U}_{n})
from below as follows:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{U}_{n})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}\\
t(x)\leq|\mathbf{N}|/2\end{subarray}}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{t(x)}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{|\mathbf{N}|-2t(x)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}\\
t(x)\leq|\mathbf{N}|/2\end{subarray}}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{|\mathbf{N}|-2t(x)}\right).

(7)

On the other hand, using
|\mathbf{N}|=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
, there is a constant probability over a uniform
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\{-1,1\}^{n}
that
t(\boldsymbol{x})
is bounded away from
|\mathbf{N}|/2
by
\Omega(n^{1/4})
.
For every such
\boldsymbol{x}
, we have

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{|\mathbf{N}|-2t(\boldsymbol{x})}\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{1/4}}\right)^{\Omega(n^{1/4})}\leq e^{-\Omega(\varepsilon)}\leq 1-\Omega(\varepsilon).

Combining everything we have from (
7
) that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\boldsymbol{p},\mathcal{U}_{n})\geq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\Omega(2^{n})\cdot\Omega(\varepsilon)=\Omega(\varepsilon).

This finishes the proof of the lemma.

4.2
Indistinguishability of
\mathcal{D}_{\text{no}}
from the uniform distribution

Consider the task of distinguishing via
q
independent samples,
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
, whether these samples were drawn from the standard
n
-dimensional Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, or an
n
-dimensional Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},I)
, where
\boldsymbol{\mu}\sim\mathcal{D}
. We consider the above problem because of the following simple claim, which shows how to generate a product distribution whose mean vector has
i
-th coordinate
\Omega(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i})
.

Claim 4.2
.

Let
\mathrm{sign}\colon\mathbbm{R}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}^{n}
denote the function which applies
\mathrm{sign}(\cdot)
coordinate-wise.

•

The uniform distribution over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
can be generated by sampling
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and outputting
\mathrm{sign}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

•

For a fixed
\mu\in[0,1/2]^{n}
, consider the product distribution over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
generated by sampling
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,I)
and outputting
\mathrm{sign}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Then, the mean vector of such a distribution has the
i
-th coordinate set to
\Omega(\mu_{i})
.

Proof:

The first condition is by symmetry of the Gaussian distribution, and the second condition is by standard Gaussian anti-concentration, whenever
\mu\in[0,1/2]
.

Hence, it suffices to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider an algorithm that takes
q
samples
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
and satisfies the following guarantees:

•

Standard Case
: If
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and the algorithm receives those samples, then the algorithm outputs “standard” with probability at least
0.99
.

•

Non-Standard Case
: We sample
\boldsymbol{\mu}\sim\mathcal{D}
,
3
3

3

The distribution
\mathcal{D}
is defined in the first bullet point of Subsection
4.1
.
then
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},I)
, and the algorithm receives those samples, the algorithm outputs “not standard” with probability at least
0.99
.

Then, the number of samples must satisfy
q=\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon^{2})
.

4.3
Proof of
Lemma
4.3

We prove by contradiction. We assume that
q\leq\sqrt{n}/(c\varepsilon^{2}\log^{4}n)
for some sufficiently large constant
c
and show below
that the algorithm cannot distinguish between the two cases.

We set up some notation for the proof. We use
i\in[q]
as an index over the set of queries,
while
j\in[n]
indexes the dimension/coordinate.
We write
f_{y},f_{n}\colon(\mathbbm{R}^{n})^{q}\to\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0}
to denote the probability density functions of a tuple of
q
independent samples from
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
(for
f_{y}
) or
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},I)
with
\boldsymbol{\mu}\sim\mathcal{D}
(for
f_{n}
) given by

\displaystyle f_{y}(x_{1},\dots,x_{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}x_{ij}^{2}\right)\qquad\text{and}\quad

\displaystyle f_{n}(x_{1},\dots,x_{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}x_{ij}^{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{1/4}}\sum_{i=1}^{q}x_{ij}-\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\right).

The definition of
f_{y}
comes from the product of
q
many
n
-dimensional Gaussian p.d.fs; the definition of
f_{n}
comes from the fact that each coordinate
j
behaves independently under the draw of
\boldsymbol{\mu}\sim\mathcal{D}
: with probability
1/\sqrt{n}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}=\varepsilon/n^{1/4}
and is otherwise 0.

The main lemma below shows that these pdfs are nearly the same with high probability over draws
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)
.
(Technically, we only need to lower bound
f_{n}
by
f_{y}
.)

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider
q
independent draws of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)
.
With probability at least
1-o_{n}(1)
,

\frac{f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}{f_{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}\geq 1-o_{n}(1).

Proof:

We set
\mathbf{X}_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{x}_{ij}
for each
j\in[n]
. Then the ratio can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}{f_{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mathbf{X}_{j}}{n^{1/4}}-\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)-1\right)\right)

(8)

and thus,

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}{f_{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\ln\left[1+\frac{\mathbf{W}_{j}}{\sqrt{n}}\right],\quad\text{with\ }\mathbf{W}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mathbf{X}_{j}}{n^{1/4}}-\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)-1.

(9)

At this point, we use the distributional information of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)
:

Claim 4.5
.

With probability at least
1-1/n
, we have

|\mathbf{W}_{j}|\leq 1/\log n
for all
j\in[n]
.

Proof:
Note that
\mathbf{X}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{x}_{ij}
where each
\boldsymbol{x}_{ij}\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
. Hence,
\mathbf{X}_{j}\sim{\cal N}(0,q)
.
With probability at least
1-1/n^{2}
, we have
|\mathbf{X}_{j}|\leq 4\sqrt{q}\log n
. By a union bound over all coordinates, with probability at least
1-1/n
, we have
|\mathbf{X}_{j}|\leq 4\sqrt{q}\log n
for all
j\in[n]
.

When this is the case, using
{q\leq\sqrt{n}/(c\varepsilon^{2}\log^{4}n)}

we have (when
c
is sufficiently large)

\frac{\varepsilon|\mathbf{X}_{j}|}{n^{1/4}}\leq\frac{4\varepsilon\sqrt{q}\log n}{n^{1/4}}\leq\frac{1}{4\log n}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{1}{4\log n}.

Using
1/(1-z)\geq\exp(z)\geq 1+z
for
|z|\leq 1
, we have

\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mathbf{X}_{j}}{n^{1/4}}-\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{2\log n}

and

\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mathbf{X}_{j}}{n^{1/4}}-\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{1-1/(2\log n)}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\log n}.

So
|\mathbf{W}_{j}|\leq 1/\log n
for all
j
and the claim follows.

We go back to
(
9
)
, and apply the inequality
\ln(1+z)\geq z-z^{2}
for
|z|\leq 1/2
.
With probability at least
1-1/n
over
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)
, we have
|\mathbf{W}_{j}|\leq 1/\log n
for all
j
and thus,

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}{f_{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{W}_{j}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{W}^{2}_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{W}_{j}-\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}\ \ \ ~~~~~~~\textrm{(by \hyperref@@ii[clm:wj]{Claim~\ref*{clm:wj}}, $\mathbf{W}^{2}_{j}\leq 1/\log^{2}n$)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mathbf{X}_{j}}{n^{1/4}}\right)-n\right]-\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}.

(10)

The heart of the matter is the
next claim on the distribution of sum of exponentials of Gaussians.

Claim 4.6
.

Let each
\mathbf{X}_{j}\sim{\cal N}(0,q)
be independent.
With probability at least
1-1/\sqrt{\log n}
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mathbf{X}_{j}}{n^{1/4}}\right)\geq n\cdot\exp\left(\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log^{0.25}n}.

Proof:
Denote
\mathbf{Y}_{j}=\varepsilon\mathbf{X}_{j}/n^{1/4}
and consider the random variable
\mathbf{Z}_{j}=\exp(\mathbf{Y}_{j})
.
Observe that
\mathbf{Y}_{j}

\sim{\cal N}(0,q\varepsilon^{2}/\sqrt{n})
. Using the formula for the moment generating function
of the Gaussian
[
gau
]
, we have
\hbox{\bf E}[\exp(t\mathbf{Y}_{j})]=\exp(q\varepsilon^{2}t^{2}/(2\sqrt{n}))
.
Hence,

\hbox{\bf E}[\mathbf{Z}_{j}]=\hbox{\bf E}[\exp(\mathbf{Y}_{j})]=\exp\left(\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\hbox{\bf E}[\mathbf{Z}^{2}_{j}]=\hbox{\bf E}[\exp(2\mathbf{Y}_{j})]=\exp\left(\frac{2q\varepsilon^{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

So
\textrm{var}[\mathbf{Z}_{j}]=\exp(2q\varepsilon^{2}/\sqrt{n})-\exp(q\varepsilon^{2}/\sqrt{n})\leq 1/\log n
using
q\varepsilon^{2}/\sqrt{n}=o(1/\log n)
.
Overall, we have

\hbox{\bf E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{Z}_{j}\right]=n\cdot\exp\left(\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{var}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{Z}_{j}\right]\leq\frac{n}{\log n}

since
all
\mathbf{Z}_{j}
’s are independent. By Chebyshev’s inequality, we have

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}\left[\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{Z}_{j}-n\cdot\exp\left(\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|>\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log^{0.25}n}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log n}}.

This finishes the proof of the claim.

In particular, with probability at least
1-1/n-1/\sqrt{\log n}=1-o_{n}(1)
, we get that

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mathbf{X}_{j}}{n^{1/4}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)\cdot\left(n\cdot\exp\left(\frac{q\varepsilon^{2}}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log^{0.25}n}\right)>n-\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log^{0.25}n}

where in the last inequality we used
\exp(-q\varepsilon^{2}/2\sqrt{n})<1
. Substituting in
(
10
)
, we get

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}{f_{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}\right)

\displaystyle>-\frac{1}{\log^{0.25}n}-\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}

(11)

Hence, with probability at least
1-o_{n}(1)
, we have

\frac{f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}{f_{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})}\geq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\log^{0.25}n}-\frac{1}{\log^{2}n}\right)=1-o_{n}(1).

This finishes the proof of the lemma.

We can now complete the proof of
Lemma
4.3
.
Consider the set
Y\subset(\mathbbm{R}^{n})^{q}
of tuples that lead the algorithm to output “standard.”
Then we must have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)}\big[(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})\in Y\big]\geq 0.99.

Let
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y

be the set of tuples that also satisfy the condition of
Lemma
4.4
.
By a union bound

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)}\big[(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})\in Y^{\prime}\big]\geq 0.99-o_{n}(1)\geq 0.98.

Thus,
\int_{Y^{\prime}}f_{y}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})d\boldsymbol{x}_{1}d\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\ldots d\boldsymbol{x}_{q}\geq 0.98
.
By the condition of
Lemma
4.4
, we have

\int_{Y^{\prime}}f_{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q})d\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\ldots d\boldsymbol{x}_{q}\geq(1-o_{n}(1))\cdot 0.98\geq 0.97.

This is exactly the probability that we see a tuple in
Y^{\prime}
,
when we generate the samples
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{x}_{q}
from the non-standard case. Thus,
with probability at least
0.97
, the algorithm outputs “standard” when the samples are generated
from the non-standard case. This completes the contradiction.
