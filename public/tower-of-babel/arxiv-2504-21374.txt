Topology of univoque sets in double-base expansions
Vilmos Komornik, Yichang Li, Yuru Zou

Abstract
. Given two real numbers q 0 , q 1 > 1 q_{0},q_{1}>1 satisfying q 0 + q 1 ≥ q 0 ​ q 1 q_{0}+q_{1}\geq q_{0}q_{1} and two real numbers d 0 ≠ d 1 d_{0}\neq d_{1} , by a double-base expansion of a real number x x we mean a sequence ( i k ) ∈ { 0 , 1 } ∞ (i_{k})\in\{0,1\}^{\infty} such that x = ∑ k = 1 ∞ d i k q i 1 q i 2 ⋯ q i k . x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{i_{k}}}{q_{{i_{1}}}q_{{i_{2}}}\cdots q_{{i_{k}}}}. We denote by U q 0 , q 1 \pazocal{U}_{{q_{0},q_{1}}} the set of numbers x x having a unique expansion. The topological properties of U q 0 , q 1 \pazocal{U}_{{q_{0},q_{1}}} have been investigated in the equal-base case q 0 = q 1 q_{0}=q_{1} for a long time. We extend this research to the case q 0 ≠ q 1 q_{0}\neq q_{1} . While many results remain valid, a great number of new phenomena appear due to the increased complexity of double-base expansions.

Introduction

The study of
non-integer base expansions
started with the pioneering papers of Rényi
[

[

. Since then hundreds of papers have been devoted to the study of expansions of real numbers of the form

(1.1)

x=\pi_{q,D}((d_{i})):=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{d_{i}}{q^{i}},

where
q>1
is a given real number, and
(d_{i})
is a sequence of
digits
, belonging to a finite
alphabet

D

Many remarkable results have been discovered, revealing deep connections to various fields of mathematics, including number theory
[

,

]
, topology
[
12
,

]
, ergodic theory
[

]
, Diophantine approximation and dynamical systems
[

.

Concerning the original alphabet
\{0,1\}
, Erdős et al.
[

,

discovered in the 1990’s that for each

there exist infinitely many bases

such that
x=1
has exactly
k
different expansions of the form (
1.1
).
Subsequently the unique expansions have been intensively studied, and a surprisingly rich theory has emerged
[

,
24
,
17
,

,
22
,
10
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

.
An essentially complete theory was presented in the papers
[

,

,
2
,

]
; it was also shown that the theory remains valid for the more general alphabets
\left\{0,1,\ldots,M\right\}
, where

is an arbitrary positive integer.
The paper
[

]
was devoted to the study of bases in which the number

has a unique expansion.
Based on these results, the papers

,

In the past few years the expansions (

and in

to

x=\pi_{S}((i_{k})):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{i_{k}}}{q_{{i_{1}}}q_{{i_{2}}}\cdots q_{{i_{k}}}},\quad(i_{k})\in\{0,1,\ldots,M\}^{\infty},

where

is a given finite

of pairs of real numbers satisfying

.
Although these generalized expansions have a much higher complexity
(see, e.g.,
[

), most theorems of
[

]
could be generalized in
[

]

to all

, i.e., to expansions of the form (

) with

.
A lot of new phenomena have appeared that do not occur in the equal-base case
q_{0}=\cdots=q_{M}
.
The purpose of this paper is to similarly extend many theorems of

]
to this more general framework.

Before stating the main results of this paper, let us recall the theorems of
[
13
]
that we are going to generalize.
We need some definitions and notations.
Unless stated otherwise, in this paper by a
sequence
we always mean an element of
\left\{0,1\right\}^{\infty}
, i.e., a sequence of zeros and ones.
We systematically use the notations of symbolic dynamics for sequences
(x_{i})
like
x_{1}x_{2}\cdots,

0^{\infty}
,
1^{\infty}
,
(10)^{\infty}
or
(10)^{k}1^{\infty}
.

We systematically use the lexicographical order between sequences: we write
(x_{i})\prec(y_{i})
or
(y_{i})\succ(x_{i})
if there exists an index
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i<n
, and
x_{n}<y_{n}
.
Furthermore, we write
(x_{i})\preceq(y_{i})
or
(y_{i})\succeq(x_{i})
if

(x_{i})\prec(y_{i})
or if
(x_{i})=(y_{i})
.
The
reflection
of a sequence
(x_{i})
is defined by the formula
\overline{(x_{i})}:=(1-x_{i})
, i.e., we exchange the digits
0
and
1
.
We denote by
\sigma
the
right shift
of sequences, so that

\sigma^{n}(x_{1}x_{2}\cdots)=x_{n+1}x_{n+2}\cdots\quad\text{for every integer}\quad n\geq 0.

We also consider the lexicographical order between finite words of digits of the same length, and the reflection of a word is defined similarly to the reflection of sequences.

A sequence
(x_{i})
is called

•

finite
if it ends with
10^{\infty}
, and
infinite
otherwise;

•

co-finite
if its reflection is finite, i.e., if it ends with
01^{\infty}
, and
co-infinite
otherwise;

•

doubly infinite
if it is both infinite and co-infinite, i.e., if it contains infinitely many zero digits and infinitely many one digits.

Remark 1.1
.

There are only countably many finite or co-finite sequences, so that “most” sequences are doubly infinite.

Now we consider the expansions of the form

(1.3)

x=\pi_{q}((d_{i})):=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{d_{i}}{q^{i}},\quad(d_{i})\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{\infty}

with a given base
q>1
on the alphabet
\{0,1\}
.
Observe that if
x
has an expansion, then
x\in J_{q}:=[0,\frac{1}{q-1}]
.
The converse is not true in general:

\left\{\pi_{q}((d_{i}))\ :\ (d_{i})\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{\infty}\right\}=J_{q}\Longleftrightarrow q\in(1,2].

Moreover, if
q\in(1,2]
, then every
x\in J_{q}:=[0,\frac{1}{q-1}]
has a lexicographically largest expansion
b(x,q)=(b_{i}(x,q))
, and a lexicographically largest infinite expansion
a(x,q)=(a_{i}(x,q))
, called the
greedy
and
quasi-greedy
expansions of
x
in base
q
, respectively.

Following
[
22
]
and
[
12
]
we introduce the sets

\displaystyle\pazocal{U}:=\left\{q\in(1,2]\ :\ 1\text{ has a unique expansion in base }q\right\},

\displaystyle\pazocal{V}:=\left\{q\in(1,2]\ :\ 1\text{ has a unique doubly infinite expansion in base }q\right\}.

Then the topological closure of
\pazocal{U}
has an analogous characterization:

\displaystyle\overline{\pazocal{U}}=\left\{q\in(1,2]\ :\ 1\text{ has a unique infinite expansion in base }q\right\}.

We recall that

\pazocal{U}\subsetneqq\overline{\pazocal{U}}\subsetneqq\pazocal{V}\quad\text{with}\quad\left\lvert\pazocal{V}\setminus\overline{\pazocal{U}}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\overline{\pazocal{U}}\setminus\pazocal{U}\right\rvert=\aleph_{0};

here and in the sequel
\left\lvert A\right\rvert
denotes the cardinality of a set
A
.
Furthermore,
\pazocal{V}
is compact and
\overline{\pazocal{U}}
is a
Cantor set
, i.e., a non-empty compact set having neither isolated, nor interior points.
Their smallest elements are the Golden ratio and the
Komornik–Loreti constant
, respectively, and their largest element is
2
, also belonging to
\pazocal{U}
.

As in
[
10
]
and
[
13
]
we introduce the following sets for each fixed base
q\in(1,2]
:

\displaystyle\pazocal{U}_{q}:=\left\{x\in J_{q}\ :\ x\text{ has a unique expansion in base }q\right\},

\displaystyle\overline{\pazocal{U}}_{q}\text{ is the topological closure of }\pazocal{U}_{q},

\displaystyle\pazocal{V}_{q}:=\left\{x\in J_{q}\ :\ x\text{ has at most one doubly infinite expansion in base }q\right\}.

Then
\pazocal{V}_{2}:=J_{2}=[0,1]
, and

\displaystyle\pazocal{V}_{q}:=\left\{x\in J_{q}\ :\ x\text{ has a unique doubly infinite expansion in base }q\right\}\text{ if }q\in(1,2).

We recall that

\pazocal{U}_{q}\subseteqq\overline{\pazocal{U}}_{q}\subseteqq\pazocal{V}_{q}\quad\text{with}\quad\left\lvert\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}\right\rvert\leq\aleph_{0},

and that
\pazocal{V}_{q}
is compact.
Finally, we introduce the following partition of
\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}
, where
\alpha(q)=a(1,q)
denotes the quasi-greedy expansion of
1
in base
q
:

(1.4)

\begin{split}&A_{q}:=\left\{x\in\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}:\sigma^{i}(a(x,q))=\alpha(q)\text{ for at least one digit }a_{i}(x,q)=0\right\},\\
&B_{q}:=\left\{x\in\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}:\sigma^{i}(a(x,q))\prec\alpha(q)\text{ for all $i$ with }a_{i}(x,q)=0\right\}.\\
\end{split}

Equivalently,
A_{q}
and
B_{q}
are the sets of numbers
x\in\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}
whose greedy expansions
b(x,q)
are finite and infinite, respectively.

In the following two theorems we recall the results of
[
13
, Theorems 1.2, 1.4, 1.5, 1.10 and 1.12]
in the case of the alphabet
\left\{0,1\right\}
.
(The case of the more general alphabets
\left\{0,1,\ldots,M\right\}
is completely analogous: we only have to define the reflection of a sequence by the formula
\overline{(x_{i})}:=(M-x_{i})
, and change
2

in Theorem

Theorem 1.2
.

(i)

If
q\in\pazocal{U}
, then every
x\in\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}
has exactly two expansions.

(ii)

If
q\in\pazocal{V}\setminus\pazocal{U}
, then every
x\in\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}
has exactly
\aleph_{0}
expansions.

Theorem 1.3
.

(i)

Let
q\in\overline{\pazocal{U}}.

(a)

|\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}|=\aleph_{0}
and
\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}
is dense in
\pazocal{V}_{q}
.

(b)

If
q=2
, then
\overline{\pazocal{U}_{q}}=\pazocal{V}_{q}=J_{q}=[0,1]
.

(c)

If
q\in\overline{\pazocal{U}}\setminus\{2\}
, then
\overline{\pazocal{U}_{q}}=\pazocal{V}_{q}
is a Cantor set.
Furthermore,
J_{q}\setminus\pazocal{V}_{q}
is the union of infinitely many disjoint open intervals
(x_{L},x_{R})
, where
x_{L}
and
x_{R}
run over
A_{q}
and
B_{q}
, respectively.
More precisely,

\text{if }b(x_{L},q)=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}0^{\infty}\text{ with }b_{n}=1,\text{ then }b(x_{R},q)=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}\overline{\alpha(q)}.

(ii)

Let
q\in\pazocal{V}\setminus\overline{\pazocal{U}}
.

(a)

The sets
\pazocal{U}_{q}
and
\pazocal{V}_{q}
are closed.

(b)

|\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}|=\aleph_{0}
, and
\pazocal{V}_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}
is a discrete set, dense in
\pazocal{V}_{q}
.

(c)

Each connected component
(x_{L},x_{R})
of
J_{q}\setminus\pazocal{U}_{q}
contains infinitely many elements of
\pazocal{V}_{q}
, forming an increasing sequence
(x_{k})^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}
satisfying

x_{k}\rightarrow x_{L}\text{ as }k\rightarrow-\infty,\text{ and }x_{k}\rightarrow x_{R}\text{ as }k\rightarrow\infty.

Moreover, each
x_{k}
has a finite greedy expansion

b(x_{k},q)=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}0^{\infty}\text{ with }b_{n}=1,

and then

a(x_{k+1},q)=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}\overline{\alpha(q)}.

(iii)

If
q\in(1,2]\setminus\pazocal{V}
, then
\pazocal{U}_{q}=\overline{\pazocal{U}_{q}}=\pazocal{V}_{q}.

Now we proceed to the formulation of our generalizations to double-base expansions.
Since every system
S=\left\{(d_{0},q_{0}),(d_{1},q_{1})\right\}
is isomorphic to

x=\pi_{Q}((i_{k})):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{i_{k}}{q_{{i_{1}}}q_{{i_{2}}}\cdots q_{{i_{k}}}},\quad(i_{k})\in\{0,1\}^{\infty},

where
Q:=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}
is a given
double-base
.
In the equal-base case
q_{0}=q_{1}
they reduce to the expansions (
1.3
).

We recall from
[
25
,
26
]
that

0=\pi_{Q}(0^{\infty})\leq\pi_{Q}((i_{k}))\leq\pi_{Q}(1^{\infty})=\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}

for every sequence
(i_{k})
; therefore we now define

J_{Q}:=[0,\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}]
.
The role of the interval
(1,2]
of bases

is taken by the set

\pazocal{A}:=\left\{Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in(1,\infty)^{2}\ :\ q_{0}+q_{1}\geq q_{0}q_{1}\right\}

(see Figure 1) because

\left\{\pi_{Q}((d_{i}))\ :\ (d_{i})\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{\infty}\right\}=J_{Q}\Longleftrightarrow Q\in\pazocal{A}.

Furthermore, if
Q\in\pazocal{A}
, then every
x\in J_{Q}
has a (lexicographically) largest expansion
b(x,Q)=(b_{i}(x,Q))
, a largest infinite expansion

, a smallest co-infinite expansion

, and a smallest expansion

.
They are called the

,

and

(in the double-base

Finally, a sequence is called

(

(

Remark 1.4
.

We often use the following observations in the sequel.

(i)

If
q_{0}+q_{1}<q_{0}q_{1}
, then
r_{Q}
and
\ell_{Q}
have no expansions because

r_{Q}=\frac{q_{0}}{q_{1}}>\frac{1}{q_{1}-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{Q}=\frac{q_{1}}{q_{0}(q_{1}-1)}-1<0

by a direct computation.

(ii)

If
q_{0}+q_{1}=q_{0}q_{1}
, i.e., if
Q\in\pazocal{C}
, then
r_{Q}=1/(q_{1}-1)
and
\ell_{Q}=0
.
They have the unique expansions
1^{\infty}
and
0^{\infty}
, respectively, so that

\alpha(Q)=1^{\infty}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(Q)=0^{\infty}.

(iii)

If
q_{0}+q_{1}>q_{0}q_{1}
, i.e., if
Q=(q_{0},q_{1})\in\pazocal{A}\setminus\pazocal{C}
, then
r_{Q}
and
\ell_{Q}
belong to the interior of the interval
J_{Q}
by a similar computation, and hence their expansions are different from
1^{\infty}
and
0^{\infty}
.

Furthermore,
r_{Q}>1/q_{1}
and
1/(q_{1}-1)>\ell_{Q}
by a direct computation; this implies by the definition of the quasi-greedy and quasi-lazy algorithms (we recall them at the beginning of Section
2
) that
\alpha(Q)
starts with 1, and
\mu(Q)
starts with 0.

Therefore we have

0^{\infty}\prec\mu(Q)\prec\alpha(Q)\prec 1^{\infty}.

(iv)

A direct computation shows that

\pi_{Q}\left(0\alpha(Q)\right)=\frac{1}{q_{1}}=\pi_{Q}(10^{\infty})\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{Q}\left(1\mu(Q)\right)=\frac{1}{q_{0}(q_{1}-1)}=\pi_{Q}(01^{\infty}).

(v)

We show in Remark
2.2
(i)–(ii) below that

\mu\preceq\sigma^{i}(\mu)\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{j}(\alpha)\preceq\alpha\quad\text{for all}\quad i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

In this paper,
\mathbb{N}
and

denote the sets of positive and nonnegative integers, respectively.

\displaystyle\pazocal{U}:=\left\{Q\in\pazocal{A}:\ell_{Q}\text{ and }r_{Q}\text{ have unique expansions}\right\},

\displaystyle\overline{\pazocal{U}}\text{ is the topological closure of }\pazocal{U},

\displaystyle\pazocal{V}:=\left\{Q\in\pazocal{A}:\sigma^{i}(\mu(Q))\preceq\alpha(Q)\text{ and }\sigma^{j}(\alpha(Q))\succeq\mu(Q)\text{ for all }i,j\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

It was also shown that
\pazocal{V}

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
Q\in\pazocal{A}
.

(i)

Q
belongs to
\pazocal{U}
if and only if

\sigma^{i}(\mu(Q))\prec\alpha(Q)\text{ and }\sigma^{j}(\alpha(Q))\succ\mu(Q)\text{ for all }i,j\in\mathbb{N}.

(ii)

Q
belongs to
\pazocal{V}
if and only if
\ell_{Q}
and

have unique doubly infinite expansions.

Now we extend the definition of the sets
\pazocal{U}_{q},\overline{\pazocal{U}}_{q}
and
\pazocal{V}_{q}
to all double-bases
Q\in\pazocal{A}
as follows:

We prove the implication

\begin{split}&b_{i+1}\cdots b_{n-1}1\preceq\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n-i}\quad\text{if}\quad 1\leq i<n\quad\text{and}\quad b_{i}=0,\\
&b_{i+1}\cdots b_{n-1}1\succ b_{i+1}\cdots b_{n-1}0\succeq\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{n-i}\quad\text{if}\quad 1\leq i<n\quad\text{and}\quad b_{i}=1.\end{split}

x>x_{k}
and

by the definition of quasi-lazy expansions.

.

It follows from (

for all

\displaystyle\sigma^{i}((c_{j}))

\displaystyle\preceq\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n-i}\mu\preceq\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n-i}\sigma^{n-i}(\alpha)=\alpha\quad\text{if}\quad 1\leq i<n\quad\text{and}\quad c_{i}=0,

\displaystyle\sigma^{i}((c_{j}))

\displaystyle\succeq\mu\quad\text{if}\quad 1\leq i<n\quad\text{and}\quad c_{i}=1.

Using (

is both quasi-greedy and quasi-lazy, as required.

Since
