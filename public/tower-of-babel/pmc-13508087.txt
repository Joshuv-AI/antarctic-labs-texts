Enhancing Enzyme Activity Via Noncovalent Association with DNA-Functionalized Nanoparticles
Authors: Seungheon Lee, Atri Bhattacharya, Regan Kuttler, Subrata Pandit, Busra Ozguney, Jackson Jacobi, Jeetain Mittal, Devleena Samanta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhancing enzyme performance without protein engineering remains a central challenge in biocatalysis. Here, we report the unusual discovery that nanoparticles densely functionalized with DNA, commonly known as spherical nucleic acids (SNAs), can bind enzymes without chemical conjugation, affinity tags, or engineered recognition motifs and substantially increase catalytic activity. Using cytochrome c as a model peroxidase, we show that noncovalent binding to SNAs increases the apparent turnover number by up to 15-fold. Mechanistic studies by varying DNA density, length, and nanoparticle core identity reveal that activity enhancement does not arise from free DNA or the nanoparticle core alone but from the three-dimensional, high-density presentation of DNA at the nanoparticle surface. Consequently, similar effects are observed when gold cores are replaced with glucose oxidase or antibody cores. Moreover, activity modulation also depends on DNA sequence, establishing sequence design as a programmable handle for tuning enzyme output. All-atom molecular dynamics simulations, binding studies, and circular dichroism further reveal that base-specific interactions reshape enzyme conformational flexibility and increase active-site accessibility. This effect generalizes beyond cytochrome c to catalase and carbonic anhydrase. Together, these findings establish SNAs as adaptable, sequence-programmable platforms for enhancing and coordinating biocatalysis and expand their functional scope into enzyme regulation.

Enzymes are nature’s most powerful biocatalysts, enabling complex chemical transformations with remarkable selectivity and efficiency. − Traditionally, improved enzyme activity has been achieved through protein engineering approaches such as site-directed mutagenesis and directed evolution. − At the same time, growing evidence suggests that catalytic behavior is not only dictated by an enzyme’s amino acid sequence, but also by the physicochemical properties of the enzyme’s microenvironment, i.e., local features that can influence structure, dynamics, and access to the active site. −

Nanomaterial-based platforms offer a powerful route to create engineered microenvironments because they can present high local ligand density, confined hydration, distinct pH, and tailored electrostatics that differ markedly from bulk solution. − However, in most cases, interfacing enzymes with nanomaterials leads to a substantial decrease in activity, in some cases, by >90%. − We and others have shown that appropriately designed nanomaterials can instead enhance enzyme activity by creating beneficial interfacial microenvironments. ,,, , − Together, these observations motivate the search for designer interfaces that reliably recruit enzymes while preserving, and ideally boosting, their catalytic output.

DNA-based nanostructures are particularly attractive in this context because DNA offers chemically precise, programmable interfaces. To date, most DNA-based strategies have used nanostructures comprised entirely of DNA to enhance enzyme activity. − However, these strategies largely rely on covalent attachment, affinity tags, or specific recognition motifs to immobilize enzymes on the DNA nanostructure.

Here, we report a distinct mechanism: nanoparticles densely functionalized with DNA, commonly known as spherical nucleic acids (SNAs), can noncovalently recruit enzymes and concurrently enhance turnover number up to 15-fold, without necessitating changes to the enzyme’s primary structure or requiring engineered binding handles (Figure ). We focused on SNAs considering they are one of the most widely used DNA-based nanomaterials in literature. − Rather than forming base-paired shapes, SNAs present DNA as a dense, radially oriented shell around a nanoparticle core, producing a three-dimensional, polyelectrolyte-rich interfacial layer. ,− This architecture endows SNAs with unique properties that have enabled applications in gene regulation, drug delivery, biosensing, and immunotherapy. − Our results, obtained by studying 18 different SNAs varying in core identity and DNA-shell properties (Figure , Table S1–S2, Figure S1–S2), demonstrate that SNAs also act as enzyme-regulating microenvironments.

We began with 5 nm gold nanoparticle (AuNP) cores functionalized with a 22-nucleotide (nt) thiolated DNA (M22: 5′-AACAATTATACTCAGCAATTTT-SH-3′), a sequence previously reported in the SNA literature. ,, To evaluate enzymatic activity, we initially used cytochrome C (Cyt C) as a model. During apoptosis, Cyt C binds to negatively charged phospholipids, called cardiolipins, which alter its structure and induces peroxidase activity. − Given Cyt C’s propensity to interact with anionic phosphate-containing molecules, we speculated that SNAs could bind Cyt C and enhance its peroxidase activity.

The peroxidase activity of Cyt C was evaluated using the substrate 2,2′-azino-bis­(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) which forms a green product that can be quantified spectrophotometrically at 420 nm (Figure A). In a typical experiment, 125 nM Cyt C was mixed with 2 mM H2O2 and 1.5 mM ABTS in 1X acetate buffer (pH 5.0), with or without 10 nM Au-SNAs. At this pH, Cyt C is expected to be positively charged (pI ∼ 10–10.5), facilitating its binding to negatively charged Au-SNAs. The ratio of the enzyme’s initial rate in the presence of a given SNA to its initial rate in the absence of SNA, after blank correction, was defined as the enhancement factor. A working concentration of 10 nM Au-SNAs was selected based on Cyt C activity assays performed with varying Au-SNA concentrations, which showed that the enhancement plateaued at approximately this concentration (Figure S3).

Our results showed a striking ∼ 9.4-fold increase in the initial rate of ABTS oxidation in the presence of Au-SNAs (Figure B). No enhancement was observed with free DNA (Figure B); bare AuNPs aggregated under similar conditions. While certain Au-SNA constructs have been reported to exhibit intrinsic peroxidase-like (“nanozyme”) activity, we detected no measurable activity from the Au-SNAs themselves under our experimental conditions (Figure S4). Together, these results indicate that the observed rate enhancement originates from Cyt C bound to the SNA surface rather than from the nanoparticle itself. Moreover, the lack of substantial effects from either free DNA or the AuNP core suggests that the enhancement of Cyt C activity is an emergent property of the SNA architecture, arising from the dense functionalization of the DNA ligands around a central core.

To test this hypothesis, we prepared SNAs using nonmetallic protein cores (Figures S5–S7), , such as glucose oxidase (GOx) and the human IgG antibody (Ab). Both GOx-SNAs and Ab-SNAs enhanced Cyt C’s peroxidase activity, whereas the corresponding free proteins (GOx or Ab) had no effect (Figure C–D and Figure S8). Collectively, these results support the idea that activity enhancement is primarily driven by the 3D, high-density presentation of DNA ligands, with the core functioning mainly as a scaffold.

We next asked whether enzymatic activity could be programmed by varying DNA-shell properties. We therefore systematically varied the number of DNA strands per Au-SNA, strand length, and sequence composition (Table S1–S2). Peroxidase activity increased progressively with the number of DNA strands, reaching a plateau at higher densities (Figure E), suggesting that surface charge density plays a central role in modulating catalytic efficiency. Au-SNAs modified with shorter T10 DNA strands exhibited higher peroxidase activity, likely due to increased charge density at the SNA periphery, whereas those functionalized with T20, T30, and T40 displayed comparable activity levels (Figure F).

Finally, we evaluated the impact of DNA sequence composition using Au-SNAs functionalized with B4T6 (B = A, T, G, C) (Figure G). Full homopolymeric sequences (i.e., G10), were not used to prevent secondary structure formation and avoid unintended interactions with the AuNP surface. Our results show that DNA bases at the outer surface of the Au-SNA enhance the peroxidase activity of Cyt C in the order A > T > G > C. To further investigate sequence-dependent effects, we evaluated two different Au-SNAs functionalized with distinct 10-nt mixed-sequence oligonucleotides (M10–1: 5′-AACAATTATA-SH-3′ and M10–2: 5′-CAGCAATTTT-SH-3′), as shown in Figure G. Interestingly, both exhibited higher activities compared to Au-SNAs functionalized with A4T6. Collectively, these results show that both the identity and the arrangement of nts in the DNA shell can be leveraged to tune enzyme activity. Notably, the M22 sequence showed the highest enhancement, despite being longer (22-nt vs 10-nt). This result suggests that DNA sequence plays a more important role than length.

To gain deeper mechanistic insight, we first focused on Au-SNAs bearing the M22 sequence, which produced the greatest enhancement in Cyt C activity. Michaelis–Menten kinetic analysis by varying H2O2 concentrations showed that association with M22 Au-SNAs increased the apparent turnover number, k cat , by 15-fold, while the apparent Michaelis-constant, K M , increased by nearly 2-fold (Table S3). This suggests that although substrate binding is modestly weakened, catalytic turnover is substantially enhanced. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy revealed conformational changes in Cyt C upon binding to M22 Au-SNAs (Figure A, Figure S9). Further deconvolution of this data using BeStSel demonstrated a decrease in the α-helical content of Cyt C upon binding to Au-SNAs (Figure S10). , This observation is consistent with previous reports showing that cardiolipin binding induces a similar structural opening in Cyt C, thereby making its heme pocket more substrate-accessible, and enhancing its peroxidase activity. ,, Consequently, enhanced activity is observed regardless of charge of the substrate, indicating local sequestration of oppositely charged substrates is likely not the dominant mechanism driving enzymatic enhancement (Figure S11).

Having established that Au-SNAs enhance Cyt C activity and alter its conformation, we next investigated the nature of the Cyt C-SNA interaction. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) showed that addition of Cyt C caused band retardation of the SNAs (Figure B, Figure S12A, Figure S13), indicating binding. Zeta-potential measurements further showed that increasing Cyt C concentrations progressively reduced the negative surface charge of the Au-SNAs (Figure C), consistent with adsorption of the positively charged protein. Quantitative binding analysis using Stern–Volmer measurements yielded an apparent dissociation constant of 14.7 ± 3.6 nM (Table S4), indicating strong Cyt C adsorption onto the SNA surface.

Given the opposite charges on SNAs and Cyt C, we hypothesized that interactions between the two are largely driven by electrostatics. Consistent with this model, charge-screening experiments with NaCl attenuated the enhancement (Figure S14). However, because all sequences have the same net negative charge, the persistence of pronounced sequence-specific differences in Cyt C’s activity enhancement under identical ionic conditions (Figure G) implies that additional, nonelectrostatic factors such as base-specific interactions or structural properties of the DNA shell also contribute to the observed effects.

To resolve these contributions at the molecular level, we performed all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Figure D−G, Figures S18–S25). − MD simulations show that Cyt C adsorbs strongly on the surface of all DNA sequences simulated (Figure D, Figure S21), consistent with experimental observations (Table S4). The simulations further revealed differences in binding modes. G4T6 and C4T6 promoted deeper enzyme penetration into the Au-SNA interface and formed more extensive contacts with Cyt C (Figure S23). These interactions involved electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged DNA phosphate backbone and positively charged lysine residues on Cyt C, as well as hydrogen bonding between DNA bases and lysine (Figure S24). In contrast, A4T6 and T4T6 sequences exhibited fewer base-specific contacts with lysine residues, allowing greater conformational flexibility of the enzyme (Figure S24). To probe how these interactions influence catalytic function, we monitored the Cα distance between G29 and G77, located on the Ω-loop B and Ω-loop D regions, as this separation serves as an indicator of heme accessibility and thus potential catalytic activity. Sequences such as A4T6 and T4T6 showed larger B-D loop separations (Figure E, Figure S25), consistent with higher activity, whereas stronger G/C-based interactions restricted Cyt C mobility and limited the conformational changes required for function (Figure S25). Further investigation of the highly active M10–1 and M22 sequences confirmed that optimal catalytic enhancement arises from a balance of interaction strength: sufficient for stable adsorption but not so strong as to hinder the dynamic motions essential for enzymatic activity.

Finally, to explore the versatility of SNAs in enzyme catalysis, we studied additional enzymes, including Cat (pI ∼ 5.4) and CA (pI ∼ 5.9), Figure S12C–D, S15–S17). , Cat peroxidase activity was evaluated using an assay analogous to Cyt C whereas the esterase activity of CA was monitored using the substrate 6-carboxyfluorescein diacetate in 1X PBS. SNAs enhanced the peroxidase activity of catalase by ∼ 3-fold (Figure A) and the esterase activity of CA by ∼ 1.6-fold (Figure B). These results show that SNAs can modulate the activities of distinct enzyme classes, underscoring their potential as tunable catalytic platforms.

Taken together, this work establishes DNA-functionalized nanoparticles as a fundamentally new and modular strategy for enhancing enzyme activity without protein modification. In contrast to prior DNA-origami approaches that typically immobilize enzymes through covalent or affinity-based attachment, − ,, densely packed DNA shells on nanoparticles recruit enzymes via a combination of electrostatic and base-specific interactions, with the nanoscale architecture itself acting as a functional recognition interface. This binding boosts catalytic output by up to an order of magnitude and generalizes across distinct enzyme classes. Looking ahead, several key questions remain, including whether DNA-functionalized nanoparticles can be engineered to selectively recognize and bind specific enzymes, whether particular DNA sequences are especially effective for specific enzymes and what the sequence-enzyme “matching” rules might be, how broadly general this platform is across enzyme families and reaction types, and the molecular-level mechanisms by which DNA sequence reshapes an enzyme’s local microenvironment. Nonetheless, this work positions DNA-functionalized nanoparticles as adaptable, sequence-encoded platforms for controlling biocatalysis and underscores their potential as programmable tools for synthesis, sensing, diagnostics, and therapeutic applications. We hope these findings motivate further exploration of this phenomenon and its underlying mechanisms.
