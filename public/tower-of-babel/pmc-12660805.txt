Thermophilic MoNxOy nanozyme for highly efficient degradation of phenolic pollutants
Authors: Xiaomin Zhang, Peiqiang Lin, Yali Wu, Minfei Fan, Chunqiu Xia, Liangqia Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn industry phenolic wastewaters are generated at high temperature and required for cooling before post-treatment. The utilization of thermal energy for the treatment of phenolic wastewaters is often overlooked. Herein, we utilize thermophilic MoNxOy nanozyme for highly efficient degradation of phenolic pollutants at high temperature. Both the laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities of MoNxOy nanozyme increase with the temperature up to 90 °C. The efficiency of phenol oxidation at 60 °C is about 3-fold of that at 25 °C. Phenol is mineralized into CO2 and H2O, the removal of total organic carbon by MoNxOy + H2O2 is 97.0 ± 0.2% at 60 °C within 20 min. Moreover, MoNxOy nanozyme can also degrade other phenolic pollutants such as 2,4-dichlorophenol, pyrocatechol, 4-aminophenol and o-nitrophenol. The catalytic mechanism and phenol degradation route are also analyzed. This work provides a promising strategy for highly efficient treatment of phenolic wastewaters in an energy-saving way.Subject terms: Nanoscale materials, Pollution remediation, Nanoscale materials

Introduction

Phenolic pollutants are primarily discharged from the wastewater of coking and oil refining plants1, chemical factories2 for the manufacturing of phenolic compounds3, plastics, phenolic resins4, explosives5, pesticides, etc. Phenolic compounds are high toxic even at low concentration6, which may cause the damage of nervous, urinary, respiratory, and digestive systems and even the risk of cancer7. Phenolic compounds have been listed as priority pollutants by the United States, the European Union, and China8. Therefore, it is imperative to develop an effective method for the degradation of phenolic pollutants before they are discharged to the environment.

Fenton reaction is the common chemical oxidation technology to degrade phenolic pollutants. However, catalyst non-renewability9, acidic pH condition (2~3), and sludge formation10 hinder its extensive practical applications. To overcome these limitations, various alternative technologies such as photo-Fenton11, thermal catalysis, and electrocatalysis12,13 have been developed for phenol degradation. However, the inputs of external energy such as light, heat and electricity are involved, which greatly increase the cost for the treatment of pollutants.

In industry, phenolic wastewaters are generated at 30~60 °C, and even 80~90 °C for coking plants, oil refining plants and phenolic resin factories, etc.14 and are normally required for cooling before post-treatment. The utilization of thermal energy for the treatment of phenolic wastewaters is often overlooked. From the view point of energy-saving, it is highly desired to utilize the thermal energy in phenolic wastewaters to degrade phenolic pollutants.

Nanozymes have attracted significant attention in the field of environmental remediation owing to the high stability, low cost, and superior catalytic activity compared to natural enzymes. Significant advances have been made in the development of oxidase-like nanozymes, including those based on Mn15,16, Mo17,18, and Fe19,20 and other metal elements21,22. Among these, laccase-like nanozymes have emerged as promising green catalysts owing to their high catalytic efficiency and substrate specificity, which make them suitable for detecting and degrading phenolic pollutants23. Inspired by the structure of laccase, numerous laccase-like nanozymes based on copper containing nanomaterials or complexes have been developed24–26. Nevertheless, the catalytic activities of these Cu-based laccase-like nanozymes often decrease when the temperature is higher than 60 °C27–30. Moreover, laccase-like nanozymes typically catalyze the incomplete degradation of phenolic pollutants, resulting in the accumulation of toxic intermediate products in the environment31,32. Although thermally adaptive nanozymes based on Au33,Cu34 and Mn35 have been proposed recently, their thermoresponsive catalytic properties have not yet been harnessed for practical pollutant degradation applications.

Herein, we utilize thermophilic nanozyme (molybdenum nitride oxide, MoNxOy) for highly efficient degradation of phenolic pollutants at high temperature (Fig. 1). Both the laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities of MoNxOy nanozyme increase with the temperature up to 90 °C, making it suitable to directly degrade phenolic pollutants in hot phenolic wastewaters without the need of pre-cooling. The investigation of degradation process indicates MoNxOy nanozyme can catalytically oxidize phenol into benzoquinone, and then carboxylic, alcohol, ketone intermediates, finally into carbon dioxide and water. The degradation process is highly efficient and the product is environmentally friendly, making it a promising alternative for the treatment of phenolic wastewaters in an energy-saving way.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of MoNxOy nanozyme

MoNxOy was prepared according to the reference36 with a slight modification. MoNxOy displays sheet-like and fragment shape and shows monodispersity in aqueous solution. The lateral size of MoNxOy is in the range of 68–255 nm with the most possible hydrodynamic size of about 106 nm (Supplementary Fig. S1). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns reveal MoNxOy possess Mo5N6 and MoO2 crystalline phases (Supplementary Fig. S2)37,38. The survey X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) shows MoNxOy is composed of Mo, N, O, and C elements (Supplementary Fig. S3a). The C element may come from the adventitious carbon in the instrument. The Mo 3d core-level XPS spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S3b) can be deconvolved into four components, the peaks with the binding energies (BEs) at 229.1 and 232.3 eV can be attributed to Mo3+ 3d5/2 and Mo3+ 3d3/2 of Mo3+-N39, while those at 229.6 and 232.8 eV correspond to Mo4+ 3d5/2 and Mo4+ 3d3/2 of Mo4+-N for Mo5N6, respectively40. The peaks with the BEs at 232.1 and 235.4 eV can be attributed to Mo4+ 3d5/2 and Mo4+ 3d3/2 of Mo4+-O for MoO238,41. While the peaks with the BEs at 232.6 and 236.0 eV can be ascribed to Mo6+ 3d5/2 and Mo6+ 3d3/2 of Mo6+-O42. The N 1s core-level XPS spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S3c) shows the peaks of N-Mo3+ and N-Mo4+ at 396.6 and 398.8 eV, respectively37. In addition, the peaks with the BEs at 394.5, 395.4, 396.7 and 397.9 eV are recognized as Mo3+ 3p3/2-N, Mo4+ 3p3/2-N, Mo4+ 3p3/2-O, and Mo6+ 3p3/2-O, respectively43. Additionally, the O 1s core-level XPS spectrum (Supplementary Fig. S3d) is deconvoluted into four peaks at 530.4, 531.2, 532.3 and 533.4 eV, corresponding to O-Mo4+, O-Mo6+, the adsorbed O2 and H2O, respectively36,44. The XPS spectra further verify MoNxOy is composed of Mo5N6 and MoO2. The morphology of MoNxOy was characterized by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). As shown in Fig. 2a, MoNxOy exhibits a two-dimensional (2D) sheet-like structure. HRTEM images demonstrate distinct lattice fringes with a lattice distance of 0.245 nm (Fig. 2b–d) and 0.345 nm (Fig. 2e–g) at different regions, corresponding to the (110) facet of Mo5N645 and the (011) facet of MoO246, respectively. These results align with XRD pattern, further confirming the existence of MoO2 and Mo5N6. The thermal stability of MoNxOy was studied by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The TGA curve (Supplementary Fig. S4) shows that the weight loss is less than 13.45% even up to 800 °C, indicating highly thermal stability of MoNxOy.

Laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities of MoNxOy nanozyme

The intrinsic laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities of MoNxOy nanozyme was first explored using 4-aminoantipyrine (4-AP) as the typical chromogenic substrate. As shown in Fig. 3a, the mixture of phenol and 4-AP shows no obvious absorption peak at around 510 nm even in the presence of H2O2 (Curve 1 and 2 in Fig. 3a). However, on addition of MoNxOy into the mixture of phenol and 4-AP, an obvious absorption with the peak at 510 nm (Curve 3 in Fig. 3a) appeares. This phenomenon indicates phenol is catalytically oxidized by MoNxOy into benzoquinone, which undergoes a condensation reaction with 4-AP to generate a red-colored anti-pyrilquinoneimine dye47, verifying the laccase-like activity of MoNxOy. When further addition of H2O2 into the reaction system, the absorbance at 510 nm is greatly enhanced (Curve 4 in Fig. 3a), indicating the peroxidase-like activity of MoNxOy to catalyze the oxidation of phenol by H2O2. The peroxidase-like activity of MoNxOy was further corroborated by using TMB as the chromogenic substrate. Under acidic condition, MoNxOy can also catalyze the oxidation of TMB by H₂O₂ to generate a blue oxidation product (oxTMB) with a characteristic absorption peat at 652 nm (Fig. 3b). These results indicate MoNxOy possesses both laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities which can catalyze the oxidation of phenol.

The effect of various experimental conditions on the catalytic activity of MoNxOy nanozyme was thoroughly investigated by performing the oxidation reaction of phenol in the presence of 4-AP at room temperature (25 °C). The catalytic activity is enhanced with increasing concentrations of MoNxOy nanozyme and H2O2 (Supplementary Fig. S5a, b). When H2O2 concentration increases 6.0 mmol L‒1, the catalytic activity reaches the plateau. The absorbance also increases with elevated 4-AP concentration and reaches the summit at 9.8 mmol L‒1 4-AP (Supplementary Fig. S5c). When the concentration of 4-AP further increases, the absorbance decreases instead due to the inhibition of condensation reaction or the generation of byproducts. The absorbance is also augmented with increasing reaction time and reaches the maximum value at about 100 min at 25 °C (Supplementary Fig. S5d). Effect of pH on the catalytic oxidation of phenol was investigated over pH 2.0 ~ 11.0. A shown in Fig. 3c, MoNxOy nanozyme shows the laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities over a broad pH range and achieves high catalytic activity at weak alkaline environment (pH 8.0 ~ 11.0), which matches well with the pH of industrial wastewaters such as coking plants48. Effect of the temperature on the catalytic activity of MoNxOy nanozyme was further examined. As shown in Fig. 3d, both the laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities are gradually enhanced with increasing temperature up to ~90 °C, demonstrating its remarkable thermal stability and thermophilic catalytic activities.

It is well known that nanozymes show better thermal stability than natural enzymes. However, the catalytic activities of most laccase-like and peroxidase-like nanozymes decrease over 60 °C due to their structure instability at high temperature28,49. For phenolic wastewaters produced from the factories are usually at high temperature, pre-cooling is involved before post-treatment14,50,51, the utilization of thermophilic MoNxOy nanozyme to degrade phenolic pollutants can avoid wasting the thermal energy in wastewaters and will greatly improve the efficiency and effectiveness of wastewater treatment.

The apparent steady-state kinetics of MoNxOy nanozyme were further investigated at 60 °C using phenol as the substrate. The laccase-like catalytic reaction (without H2O2) follows the classical Michaelis–Menten kinetic (Supplementary Fig. S6a, b). The values of maximum reaction velocity (Vmax) and Michaelis constant (Km) derived from the Lineweaver-Burk plot are determined to 29.56 μmol L⁻1 min⁻1 and 1.36 mmol L⁻1, respectively. For the peroxidase-like catalytic activity (with H2O2), the values of Vmax and Km are determined to 56.27 μmol L⁻1 min⁻1 and 0.18 mmol L⁻1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6c, d). Compared with previously reported nanozymes (Supplementary Table S1), MoNxOy nanozyme exhibits comparable or stronger affinity as well as a faster reaction rate for the catalytic oxidation of phenol.

Oxidation and degradation efficiencies of phenol catalyzed by MoNxOy nanozyme

The oxidation efficiency of phenol catalyzed by MoNxOy nanozyme was assessed by the generation of oxidation product (benzoquinone), which further condensed with 4-AP to generate a red-colored antipyrilquinoneimine dye and was quantified according to the calibration curve for phenol (Supplementary Fig. S7). At room temperature 25 °C (Supplementary Fig. S8a, b), without MoNxOy, the oxidation of phenol is negligible and less than 0.7% phenol is oxidized by H2O2 alone after 60 min. By contrast, when MoNxOy is applied for the oxidation of phenol, the oxidation of phenol is catalyzed by the laccase-like MoNxOy nanozyme and about 26.6% phenol is oxidized over the same time. Notably, when both MoNxOy and H2O2 are applied, the oxidation of phenol is synergistically catalyzed by both the laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities of MoNxOy nanozyme and most of phenol (92.5%) is oxidized within 60 min. The speeds of phenol oxidation increase at an elevated temperature (60 °C) (Supplementary Fig. S8c, d). About 1.9%, 24.0%, and 92.9% phenol are oxidized within 20 min in the presence of H2O2 alone, MoNxOy alone, and MoNxOy + H2O2, respectively. The efficiency of phenol oxidation at 60 °C is about 3-fold of that at 25 °C.

The kinetic of phenol oxidation indicates that the oxidation reaction follows pseudo-first-order reaction kinetics (Fig. 4a). At room temperature (25 °C), the rate constant (k) for the oxidation of phenol by MoNxOy + H2O2 is determined to (3.16 ± 0.03) ×10⁻2 min⁻1, which is 6.3-fold higher than that by MoNxOy alone [(4.34 ± 0.26) ×10⁻3 min⁻¹] and 195.3-fold higher than that by H2O2 only [(1.61 ± 0.01) ×10⁻4 min⁻¹]. At an elevated temperature (60 °C), the rate constant (k) for the oxidation of phenol by MoNxOy + H2O2 reaches (1.06 ± 0.08) ×10⁻1 min⁻1, 9.3-fold higher than that by MoNxOy alone [(9.88 ± 0.81) ×10⁻3 min⁻1], 211.4-fold higher than that by H2O2 alone [(4.99 ± 0.85) ×10⁻4 min⁻1], and 2.35-fold higher than the same condition at 25 °C. Therefore, the catalytic oxidation of phenol is more favorable at high temperature.

It is well known that laccase and laccase-like nanzymes catalyze the oxidization of phenol into the intermediate benzoquinone31. The evolution profile of the intermediate was monitored during the degradation of phenol in the presence MoNxOy + H2O2 at 60 °C. As shown in Fig. 4b, the concentration of benzoquinone rapidly increases in the first 3 min and then decreased to near zero after 70 min. This result indicates phenol is catalytically oxidized into the intermediate benzoquinone, and then further decomposed into smaller molecules during the degradation of phenol. Total organic carbon (TOC) analysis was applied to quantify the degradation efficiency (Fig. 4c). When phenol is degraded by H2O2 alone, TOC removal rate is only 0.3% and 2.7% for 20 min and 60 min, respectively. When MoNxOy alone is applied to catalyze the degradation of phenol, TOC removal rate is enhanced to 24.2% and 46.1% for 20 min and 60 min, respectively. When phenol is degraded by MoNxOy + H2O2, TOC removal rate reaches 92.4 % and 97.0% for 20 min and 60 min, respectively, indicating phenol is almost mineralized into CO2 and H2O by MoNxOy + H2O2. Compared with previously reported catalysts for the degradation of phenol (Supplementary Table S2), this system achieves faster degradation rate and higher TOC removal efficiency while employing less catalyst.

The consumption of H2O2 is also an important parameter for the catalytic degradation of phenol. The HRP-TMB-H2O2 colorimetric method was developed to quantify the consumption of H2O2 during phenol degradation (Fig. S9). The consumption of H2O2 is almost negligible when phenol is degraded by H2O2 alone at both 25 °C and 60 °C, indicating H2O2 alone hardly oxidizes and degrades phenol. When phenol is degraded in the presence of MoNxOy + H2O2, 0.0153 mmol H2O2 (per mg phenol) and 0.0275 mmol (per mg phenol) are consumed at 25 °C and 60 °C, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S10). The more consumption of H2O2 at an elevated temperature may be caused by the decomposition of H2O2. Compared with the conventional degradation systems (Supplementary Table S3), this system demonstrates a higher H2O2 utilization efficiency. The recyclability of MoNxOy nanozyme was also explored. After centrifuging and washing with water, MoNxOy nanozyme was applied for repeated degradation experiment. As shown in Fig. 4d, MoNxOy nanozyme retains 76% catalytic activity after 7 cycles. MoNxOy enzyme was also applied to degrade other phenolic pollutants (Supplementary Fig. S11). It is reported that phenolic compounds in petrochemical wastewater and coke oven process wastewater are about 2.8 mg L-1 ~ 1220 mg L-152 and 150 mg L-1 ~ 2000 mg L-153, respectively. The consumption of MoNxOy nanozyme for the treatment of phenol-containing wastewater is about for 9.6 g ~ 3.84 kg for petrochemical wastewater per tun and 0.51 kg ~ 6.86 kg for coke oven process wastewater per tun, respectively. The relatively low consumption of MoNxOy demonstrates its potential for practical application in industrial wastewater treatment. Additionally, in the presence of MoNxOy + H2O2, other phenolic pollutants such as 2,4-dichlorophenol, pyrocatechol, 4-aminophenol and o-nitrophenol are degraded for 60 min at 60 °C with TOC removal rate of 80.0%, 93.4%, 20.9%, and 92.4%, respectively, indicating that MoNxOy nanozyme possesses the versatility for the degradation of phenolic pollutants.

Catalytic mechanism and phenol degradation route

Laccase can reduce O2 to H2O and does not produce or require H2O2 during the catalytic oxidation of phenol54. To verify the laccase-like activity of MoNxOy nanozyme, we examined whether O2 was required in the catalytic oxidation of phenol using 4-AP as the chromogenic substrate. Compared to the normoxic condition, the absorbance of the product (antipyrilquinoneimine) decreases under hypoxic condition and increases in oxygen-enriched condition (Fig. 5a), indicating that O2 is involved in the catalytic oxidation of phenol. Then, we tested whether H2O2 was generated during the catalytic oxidation of phenol. As shown in Fig. 5b, no color change is observed upon adding TMB to the mixture solution of MoNxOy and phenol. However, further addition of H2O2 into the reaction mixture, the color immediately turns into dark purplet with a strong absorption peak at 550 nm. These phenomenon indicates the catalytic oxidation of phenol by MoNxOy nanozyme do not generate H2O2. These findings confirm the laccase-like activity of MoNxOy nanozyme.

To identify the reactive oxygen species (ROS) during the catalytic oxidation of phenol, ROS scavenging reagents were added to the reaction system. Thiourea is employed as a scavenger for hydroxyl radicals (•OH)55–57, superoxide dismutase (SOD) for superoxide radicals (•O2⁻)58,59, L-histidine for singlet oxygen (¹O₂)60, and catalase for H2O261. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S12, neither L-histidine nor catalase significantly inhibits the oxidation of phenol, suggesting that ¹O₂ and H₂O₂ do not participate in the oxidation process. In contrast, thiourea greatly inhibits phenol oxidation and SOD slightly inhibits phenol oxidation, indicating that •OH plays a primary role in phenol oxidation and •O2⁻ plays a secondary role in phenol oxidation. Furthermore, electron spin resonance (ESR) was used to verify the ROS in the reaction system. As shown in Fig. 5c, d, ESR signals corresponding to DMPO-•OH is captured in MoNxOy + H2O2 solution. While DMPO-•O2⁻ signal is observed in MoNxOy solution. These results verify MoNxOy nanozyme with the laccase-like activity to activate O2 to generate •O2⁻ and with the peroxidase-like activity to activate H2O2 to generate •OH, which are responsible for the oxidation and degradation of phenol.

To gain a deeper understanding of phenol degradation catalyzed by MoNxOy nanozyme, we analyzed the degraded phenol solution using LC-MS. The MS spectra and possible structure of the intermediate are showed in Supplementary Fig. S13 and Supplementary Table S4. Therefore, a degradation pathway of phenol is inferred and illustrated in Fig. 5e. This degradation pathway involves a series of reactions, including dehydrogenation, oxidation, ring-opening, and bond-breaking.

From above disscusion, the feasible mechanism for the cataytic oxidation and degradation of phenol by MoNxOy nanozyme is postulated and illustrated in Fig. 1. Phenol is deprotonated and phenoxide anion is adsorbed on the surface of MoNxOy nanozyme and donates one electron to MoNxOy nanozyme to form phenoxyl radical. O2 and H2O2 accept electron from MoNxOy nanozyme and are activated to generate •O2⁻ and •OH radicals, which oxidize phenoxyl radical into o-benzoquinone / p-benzoquinone. o-benzoquinone / p-benzoquinone is further oxidized by •O2⁻ and •OH radicals, and is decomposed into smaller molecules, and finally mineralized into CO2 and H2O via dehydrogenation, oxidation, ring-opening, and bond-breaking reaction. Discussion

In conclusion, thermophilic MoNxOy nanozyme was applied for highly efficient degradation of phenolic pollutants. MoNxOy nanozyme is composed of Mo5N6 and MoO2. Both the laccase-like and peroxidase-like activities of MoNxOy nanozyme increased with temperature up to 90 °C, which was utilized to oxidize and degrade phenolic pollutants at high-temperature. Compared with previously reported catalysts for the degradation of phenol, MoNxOy nanozyme demonstrates faster degradation rate, higher TOC removal efficiency and higher H2O2 utilization efficiency while employing less catalyst. In addition, MoNxOy nanozyme possesses the versatility for the degradation of phenolic pollutants. This work pioneers a new strategy for wastewater treatment in an energy-saving way by utilizing the thermal energy in the wastewater. Future research should focus on advancing sustainable and scalable separation technologies such as magnetic recovery, membrane filtration, or gravity sedimentation to promote practical implementation.

Methods

Preparation of MoNxOy nanozyme

MoNxOy was prepared according to the reference25 with a slight modification. Specifically, Mo powder was oxidized by H2O2 into Mo(OH)x. The mixture of Mo(OH)x (as the precursor), NaCl (as the template) and Ni(OCOCH3)2·4H2O (as the catalyst) was heated to 600 °C at 1 °C min−1 in a tube furnace under 5% NH3/Ar atmosphere, then annealed at 600 °C for 5 h. After washing with aqueous HCl to remove Nickel, the solid product was was ultrasonically exfoliated into nanosheets.

Monitoring of phenol oxidation

Typically, 500 μL MoNxOy dispersion solution (100 mg mL⁻¹), 500 μL H2O2 solution (20 mmol L⁻¹), 500 μL phenol solution (100 ppm), 500 μL 4-AP solution (98 mmol L⁻¹), and 3000 μL Tris-HCl buffer solution (200 mmol L⁻¹, pH 9.0) were mixed. After shaking slightly at 25 °C or 60 °C for predetermined intervals, the mixture (200 μL) was taken out and the absorption spectrum of the filtrate was recorded. Control experiment was also conducted with identical conditions but replacing phenol by H2O. The absorbance difference (∆Abs) at 510 nm was used to quantify the red product based on the calibration curve for phenol, where ∆Abs was the absorbance difference of the ample and the control. The residual phenol was obtained by subtracting the oxidized phenol from the initial phenol.

Monitoring of the intermediate benzoquinone and H2O2 consumption during phenol degradation

Typically, 500 μL MoNxOy dispersion solution (100 mg mL⁻¹), 500 μL H2O2 solution (20 mmol L⁻¹), 500 μL phenol solution (100 ppm), and 3500 μL Tris-HCl buffer solution (200 mmol L⁻¹, pH 9.0) were mixed. Then, the mixture was stirred at 500 rpm at 60 °C. For the detection of benzoquinone, the filtrate (150 μL) was mixed with 50 μL 4-AP (98 mmol L⁻¹) for 30 min to undergo condensation reaction prior to absorption spectroscopic analysis. For the detection of H2O2, the filtrate (50 μL) was mixed with 50 μL TMB (10 mmol L⁻¹), 50 μL HRP (250 U mL⁻1), and 350 μL Tris-HCl buffer (200 mmol L⁻¹, pH 9.0) at room temperature for 5 min, the absorption spectrum was recorded by a Tecan multifunctional microplate reader.

The corresponding control experiments were also conducted with identical conditions but replacing phenol by H2O. The concentrations of benzoquinone and H2O2 were quantified by laccase-phenol-4-AP and HRP-TMB-H2O2 colorimetric methods, respectively.

Detection of TOC after phenol degradation

Typically, MoNxOy (50 mg), 500 μL H2O2 solution (200 mmol L-1), 500 μL phenol (1000 ppm) were added to 49 mL NaOH solution (pH 9.0). Then, the mixture was stirred at 500 rpm at 60 °C. At a specified time interval, 15 mL solution was taken out and filtered by a 0.22 μm PES filter membrane. TOC in the solution was detected.
