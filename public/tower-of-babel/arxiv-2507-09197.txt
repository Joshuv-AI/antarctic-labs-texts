Polynomial skew products with small relative degree
Romain Dujardin, Charles Favre, Matteo Ruggiero

Abstract
. We investigate the local dynamics of a proper superattracting holomorphic germ f f in ( ℂ 2 , 0 ) (\mathbb{C}^{2},0) possessing a totally invariant line L L such that f ∗ ​ L = d ​ L f^{*}L=dL with d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , and such that f | L f|_{L} has a superattracting fixed point at 0 0 of order 2 ≤ c < d 2\leq c<d . We prove that any such map is formally conjugated to a skew product of the form ( z d , P ⁡ ( z , w ) ) (z^{d},P(z,w)) , where P ∈ ℂ ​ [ [ z ] ] ​ [ w ] P\in\mathbb{C}[[{z}]][w] is polynomial in w w of degree c c , hence it induces a natural dynamics on the Berkovich affine line over ℂ ⁡ ( ( z ) ) \mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right) . Such non-Archimedean skew products were recently studied by Birkett and Nie-Zhao. On the non-Archimedean side, we focus on the restriction of the dynamics on the Berkovich open unit ball (which naturally contains all irreducible analytic germs at the origin). We exhibit an invariant compact set 𝒦 \mathcal{K} outside of which all points tend to L L , and which supports a natural ergodic invariant measure. By a careful analysis of local intersection numbers, we prove that the growth of multiplicity of iterated curves is controlled by the recurrence properties of the critical set. In particular, when no critical branch of f f belongs to 𝒦 \mathcal{K} , any point in 𝒦 \mathcal{K} corresponds to a curve of uniformly bounded multiplicity at 0 0 . We then return to the complex picture and show the existence of an invariant pluripolar positive closed ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) -current T T , outside of which all orbits converge to 0 0 at super-exponential speed c c . Under the same assumption on the critical branches as above, we prove that T T admits a geometric representation as an average of currents of integration over the curves in 𝒦 \mathcal{K} , with respect to the natural invariant measure. In particular, T T is uniformly laminar outside the origin.



Introduction

0.1.
A class of superattracting germs

In this article we investigate the local dynamics of a class of
superattracting points in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
It has been known for a long time that, contrary to the
one-dimensional case, the local dynamics at superattracting points in higher dimension
gives rise to subtle and varied phenomena (see, e.g.,
[
HP94
,
Fav00
,
FJ07
,
Gig14
,
GR14
,
MZMS13
,
Uen19
,
BEK12
]
).

Our setting is
the following: assume that
f
is a germ of holomorphic map at

0\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
with
f(0)=0
, and that there are integers
2\leq c<d
satisfying:
(i) the line
{\left\{z=0\right\}}
is totally invariant and as divisors (or currents) we have

f^{*}{\left\{z=0\right\}}=d{\left\{z=0\right\}}
; and
(ii) the restriction of
f
to
{\left\{z=0\right\}}
admits a superattracting point
of order
c
. Then, in local holomorphic coordinates,
f
can be expressed as

(1)

f(z,w)=\big(z^{d},w^{c}+zh(z,w)\big),\text{ with }h(0,0)=0.

This class of mappings arises from our attempts at dealing with the missing case of
the dynamical Manin-Mumford conjecture for regular polynomial mappings
in
[
DFR23
]
.

When
p=(z,w)
is close to 0,
f^{n}(p)
converges super-exponentially fast to 0, and there are two distinct regimes: the majority of orbits converge tangentially to
{\left\{z=0\right\}}
, eventually landing in a region of the form
{\left\{{\left|{z}\right|}<C{\left|{w}\right|}^{c}\right\}}
. For such orbits, the
dynamics is dominated by that of
f\arrowvert_{{\left\{z=0\right\}}}
and the
asymptotic contraction rate

c_{\infty}(p)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{\big|\log{\left|{f^{n}(p)}\right|}\big|}

is equal to
c
.
There is however a thin subset
\mathcal{W}
of points converging faster to the origin, with
asymptotic rate
d
. The set
\mathcal{W}
may thus be viewed as a kind of “strong (super-)stable manifold” of the
origin. This is a closed pluripolar set with a rich and intriguing structure. More precisely, the
following result (which is already partly contained in
[
FJ07
]
)
is proven in Section
7
.

Theorem A
.

Let
f
be a germ of holomorphic mapping in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of the form (
1
) with
2\leq c<d
. Then for every
p=(z,w)
close to the origin, the asymptotic contraction rate exists and equals
c
or
d
.

The sequence
\left(\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{f^{n}(z,w)}\right|}\right)_{n\geq 0}
converges to a plurisubharmonic function
g
with values in
\mathbb{R}\cup{\left\{-\infty\right\}}
,
such that
e^{g}
is continuous,

g\circ f=cg
, and
g
is pluriharmonic in
{\left\{g>-\infty\right\}}
.

The closed pluripolar set
\mathcal{W}={\left\{g=-\infty\right\}}=\supp({\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}^{c}g)
is precisely the locus where
c_{\infty}=d
. It is not a subvariety unless
f
is conjugated to a product map.

Our main purpose in this paper is to demonstrate
that the structure of the set
\mathcal{W}
and of the current
\supp({\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}^{c}g)

are encoded by a natural dynamical system whose phase space is of non-Archimedean nature.
This space is defined as a suitable completion of the space of germs of irreducible curves
in
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, and corresponds to the valuative tree, which was extensively studied in
[
FJ04
,
FJ07
]
.
We found it more convenient to view this space as a ball in the Berkovich analytification of the affine line over the field of formal Laurent series
\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)
, which makes the connection with other relevant works more transparent
(these two points of view are compared in §
1.3.3
below).

0.2.
Polynomial skew products over a non-Archimedean field

To define our non-Archimedean model we work with mappings of the form

(2)

f(z,w)=\biggl(z^{d},w^{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}(z)w^{j}\biggr)

where
h_{j}\in\mathbb{C}[[{z}]]
satisfy
h_{j}(0)=0
, and
as in (
1
), we assume that
2\leq c<d
. Any map
f
as in (
2
) falls within the class of
polynomial skew products
.
It induces a self-map
f_{\diamond}

of the field of (formal) Puiseux series
\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle{z}\rangle\!\rangle
and of its completion
\mathbb{L}
for the
t
-adic norm, defined by the formula

(3)

f_{\diamond}\colon\phi(z)\longmapsto\left(\phi\big({z^{1/d}}\big)\right)^{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}\big({z^{1/d}}\big)\,\left(\phi\big({z^{1/d}}\big)\right)^{j}.

Recall that a Puiseux series (resp. an element of
\mathbb{L}
) is a series of the form
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{a_{n}}z^{\beta_{n}}

where
(a_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
(\beta_{n})
is an increasing sequence of rational numbers with a fixed
denominator (resp. an increasing sequence of rational numbers with
\beta_{n}\to\infty
).

The map
f_{\diamond}
admits a natural continuous extension to the Berkovich analytification of
the affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
over
\mathbb{L}
, enabling us to exploit analytic and potential theoretic
tools to analyze its dynamics. It is crucial to consider at the same time
the induced dynamics of
f_{\diamond}

on
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
. Indeed the latter space is more directly related to geometry,
as the classical points lying in the open unit ball correspond to irreducible formal germs in
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)

different from
\{z=0\}

(while classical points in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
should be thought of as
parameterizations of these curves). There is a canonical surjective map
\pi\colon\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}\to\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
, and
we say that a point (in either space) is of type 1 when it corresponds to a point in
\mathbb{L}
.

To make the discussion clearer, we focus on the dynamics of
f_{\diamond}
on

\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
.
We first observe (Theorem
2.6
) that the interesting dynamics takes place in the (Berkovich) unit ball
B^{\an}(0,1)
:
under iteration, a point
x\in B^{\an}(0,1)
either converges to the Gauss point
\zeta_{g}

or it remains in

B^{\an}(0,\rho)
for some
\rho<1
. We define

(4)

\mathcal{K}:=\{x\in B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)},\,f^{n}_{\diamond}(x)\text{ does not converge to }\zeta_{g}\}~.

The ball
B^{\an}(0,1)
is a
\mathbb{R}
-tree with one (non-compact) end, and the compact set
\mathcal{K}

consists of the set of points that do not reach a neighborhood of this end under iteration.
It thus plays the role of the filled-in Julia set in our context. It will also
turn out to be the non-Archimedean counterpart of
\mathcal{W}
. In Section
3

we prove the following:

Theorem B
.

Let
f
be a map of the form (
2
) with
2\leq c<d
. Then

\mathcal{K}
is either a Cantor set of type 1 points, or it is reduced to a single point corresponding to a smooth formal curve transverse to
{\left\{z=0\right\}}
, in which case
f
is conjugated to
a product map.

There exists a canonical
f_{\diamond}
-invariant ergodic probability measure
\mu_{\na}
, with
\mathrm{Supp}(\mu_{\na})=\mathcal{K}
and such that
for every
x\in B^{\an}(0,1)
, the sequence

\frac{d^{n}}{c^{n}}(f^{n}_{\diamond})^{*}\delta_{x}
converges to
\mu_{\na}
.

See below Section
1
for basics on Berkovich theory. Note that in Sections
1
to
6
, we
work in the slightly more general context of the field
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)
, with
k
an arbitrary algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic 0.

0.3.
History and related results

Polynomial and rational dynamics on Berkovich spaces in dimension 1
is now a well-developed subject; a valuable overview is provided in the books of Baker-Rumely
[
BR10
]
and Benedetto
[
Ben19
]
. Mappings of the form
f_{\diamond}
are not directly covered by these references. Their dynamical properties were recently investigated by Birkett
[
Bir23
]
and independently
by Nie and Zhao
[
NZ24
]
.

In
[
NZ24
]
, the authors consider
twisted rational maps
, on
\mathbb{P}^{1,\an}_{K}
, where
K
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
, that is non-trivially valued. Such maps are of the form
R_{\tau}=R\circ\tau
, where
R\in K(w)
is a rational map, and
\tau
is a continuous automorphism of
K
satisfying the property
{\left|{\tau(\cdot)}\right|}={\left|{\cdot}\right|}^{\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. The degree of
R
is called the
relative degree

of
R_{\tau}
(this terminology is from
[
Bir23
]
).
Under the assumption that
R_{\tau}
has

large relative degree

\lambda\deg(R)>1
, they obtain
equidistribution results (in the spirit of Favre and Rivera-Letelier
[
FRL10
]
) for backward orbits. When moreover
R
is a polynomial, the authors describe the action of
R_{\tau}
on the Julia set, as a one-sided shift on
\deg(R)
symbols.

For mappings of the form (
3
), if we let
\tau
be the automorphism of
\mathbb{L}

given by
\tau(z)=z^{1/d}
, we have
f_{\diamond}=\hat{f}\circ\tau
, where

\hat{f}
is the polynomial

\hat{f}(w)=w^{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}(\tau(z))w^{j}~.

It follows that in our situation
the relative degree equals
\deg\big(\hat{f}\big)=c
and
\lambda=1/d

so that we are in the opposite case
\lambda\deg\big(\hat{f}\big)=c/d<1

of
small relative degree
.

In the language of Birkett
[
Bir23
]
, the value
\lambda_{\tau}=1/d
is
the
scale factor
, which places us in the
superattracting
case of that paper.
Under this condition, it is shown in
[
Bir23
, Corollary 3.14]
that
f_{\diamond}
is a contraction on the hyperbolic space, given
the Berkovich projective line minus the rigid points, endowed with the hyperbolic distance.
This property was investigated in the context of valuation spaces in
[
GR21
]
.

It is worth mentioning that our set
\mathcal{K}
lies in the Fatou set of
f_{\diamond}
both in the sense of
[
Bir23
, Definition 5.1]
and
[
NZ24
, Definition 3.1]
.
However the set of iterates does not form a normal family near any point of
\mathcal{K}

in the sense of
[
FKT12
]
. This underlines some of the subtleties that may
arise in the Fatou-Julia
theory of general skew products.

0.4.
Curves of bounded multiplicity in
\mathcal{K}

Recall that a type 1 point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
is defined by a series
\phi
in
\mathbb{L}
.
When
\phi
is a Puiseux series, the point
x
corresponds to an irreducible formal germ
C(x)
, and
to comply with the terminology of non-Archimedean geometry, we say that it is
rigid
.
We define the
multiplicity

m(x)
to be the order of tangency of
C(x)
with

{\left\{z=0\right\}}
(this terminology is consistent with Berkovich theory but it is also a little unfortunate since
m(x)
is not the multiplicity of
C(x)
at 0).
When
x
is not defined by a Puiseux series, by definition it is
not rigid, and we set
m(x)=\infty
.
The geometric interpretation of non-rigid points
is less clear (see
[
FJ05
, Proposition 6.9]

for an attempt using currents).

In sections
4
and
5
we show that
the multiplicity of points in
\mathcal{K}
is controlled by the recurrence properties of the critical set.
More precisely, let
\mathcal{C}
be the (finite) set consisting of all rigid points associated with the
irreducible components of the critical locus of
f
different from
{\left\{z=0\right\}}
.
We also introduce the restricted critical set
\mathcal{C}^{+}\subset\mathcal{C}

corresponding to critical branches
c
such that
1+J(c)

does not divide
d
, where
J(c)=\mathrm{ord}_{c}\jac_{w}(f)
is the multiplicity of
c

as a critical branch.

Our second main theorem is the following. Here
\omega(x)
is the
\omega
-limit set, i.e.
the cluster set of the set of iterates of
x
.

Theorem C
.

Let
f
be a map of the form (
2
) with
2\leq c<d
. Then:

(1)

any point in
\mathcal{K}
such that
\omega(x)\cap\mathcal{C}^{+}=\emptyset
has finite multiplicity;

(2)

if
\mathcal{C}^{+}
is non-recurrent in the sense that
\omega(c)\cap\mathcal{C}^{+}=\emptyset

for any
c\in\mathcal{C}^{+}
, then the multiplicity is uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{K}
;

(3)

if
\mathcal{K}
is not reduced to a point and there is a periodic critical point in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
,
then
\mathcal{K}
contains non-rigid points and rigid points with arbitrary large
multiplicity.

In
[
Tru14
]
, Trucco studies
the dynamics of a polynomial mapping
P
with coefficients in the field of
complex Puiseux series. His Theorem F
asserts that the Julia set
J_{P}
contains only Puiseux series
if and only if no critical point in
J_{P}
is recurrent, and in that case the multiplicity is uniformly bounded.
Theorem
C
is thus
a generalization of his results to skew-products of small relative degree. Note that it
does not say anything in the presence of a non-periodic recurrent critical point in
\mathcal{C}^{+}
.
The very existence of such examples is a non-trivial fact and remains open.
We present explicit examples illustrating each case of the theorem in Section
6
.

The proof of Theorem
C
relies on the notion of

generic multiplicity
for type 2 points of
B^{\an}(0,1)

which we discuss in §
4.2
. The key step is to bound from above
the generic multiplicity at a point
x
in terms of the generic multiplicity along its orbit.
This delicate point is handled in Proposition
4.7
.

0.5.
Back to germs in
\mathbb{C}^{2}

Building on the previous results we come back to the problem of describing the structure of the super-stable set
\mathcal{W}

for a germ of the form (
1
).
After a formal conjugacy, we can bring
f
to the form (
2
) with
2\leq c<d
,
so that Theorem
A
applies. We work under the assumption that
no critical branch corresponds to a point in
\mathcal{K}
, or equivalently is contained in
\mathcal{W}
as a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
(see Proposition
8.1
). Then
by the second conclusion of
Theorem
C
, any point
x\in\mathcal{K}
is associated with a germ of (a priori) formal curve
C(x)
,
and the multiplicity
m(x)
is uniformly bounded by some integer
m
.

Recall from Theorem
B
, that
\mathcal{K}
supports a canonical ergodic measure
\mu_{\na}
.
Our main result is the following:

Theorem D
.

Assume that
f
is a holomorphic map of the form (
1
) with
2\leq c<d
, and that
no critical branch belongs to
\mathcal{K}
.

Then there exist an integer
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and a constant
r_{0}>0
, such that for any
x\in\mathcal{K}
there is a Puiseux series

\phi_{x}\in\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle{z}\rangle\!\rangle
which is convergent in the disk
\mathbb{D}(0,r_{0})
, and such that

C(x)
is parameterized by
t\mapsto(t^{m},\phi_{x}(t^{m}))
.

Moreover, we have the equality of currents

(5)

{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}^{c}g=\int_{\mathcal{K}}\frac{[C(x)]}{m(x)}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{\na}(x)~,

and
{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}^{c}g
is a uniformly laminar current outside the origin.

The proof of this result is given in Section
8
, and requires several steps.
We first make a suitable base change, that is, we take the pull-back by some branched cover
\beta(z,w)=(z^{k},w)

in order to reduce to the case
m=1
. Then we construct a suitable geometric model by exhibiting a sequence of point blow-ups over the origin
\kappa\colon X\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
,
and a finite set of points
p_{i}\in\kappa^{-1}(0)

such that a germ of curve
C
belongs to
\mathcal{K}
if and only if the strict transform of
f^{n}(C)
intersects

\kappa^{-1}(0)
at one of the points
p_{i}
for every
n\geq 0
.

At the non-Archimedean level, the set of points
p_{i}
corresponds to
a covering of
\mathcal{K}
by open balls.
More precisely, for any
i
,
the set of all germs of irreducible curves
C
whose strict transform contains
p_{i}

is an open ball
B_{i}
.
Furthermore, the open cover
{\left\{B_{i}\right\}}
can be chosen to form
a Markov partition for
f_{\diamond}
.
Introduce the oriented graph
\Gamma
whose vertices are
balls
B_{i}
and an (oriented) edge joins
B_{i}
to
B_{j}
if and only if

f_{\diamond}(B_{i})\supset B_{j}
.
We prove that the subshift of finite type induced by

\Gamma
is canonically conjugated to the dynamics of
f_{\diamond}
on
\mathcal{K}
.

Back to the Archimedean picture, let
\widehat{F}\colon X\dashrightarrow X
be the lift of
f
.
It is a meromorphic map such that all points
p_{i}
are indeterminate.
In the oriented graph
\Gamma
an edge joins
B_{i}
to
B_{j}
if and only if
\widehat{F}(p_{i})\ni p_{j}
.
Our analysis then proceeds as follows:
pulling-back curves by
\widehat{F}
gives rise to a natural
graph transform

\mathcal{L}_{e}

for every edge
e=(p_{i}p_{j})
of
\Gamma
. The superattracting nature of
f
implies that

\mathcal{L}_{e}
sends a curve passing through
p_{j}
of a definite size
r

to a curve through
p_{i}
of the same size.
Analyzing this graph transform in local coordinates shows that it is a contraction
on a suitable function space.
Since any point
x\in\mathcal{K}
is determined by its itinerary

(v_{j})_{j\geq 0}
in the cover
{\left\{B_{i}\right\}}
and, at the same time, the sequence

\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{L}_{e_{n}}(C)
converges, where
e_{j}=(v_{j}v_{j+1})
and
C
is any initial data, we conclude that
C(x)
coincides with (the projection under
\kappa
of)
its limiting curve.
The control of the size
r
guarantees the convergence of the Puiseux parameterizations over a fixed neighborhood of the origin.
Finally, the representation (
5
) follows from the equidistribution of pull-backs both at the Archimedean and non-Archimedean levels.

To complete the proof, we push this picture down by the branched cover
\beta
. A few non-trivial arguments are required to justify the uniform laminarity of the image current and the formula (
5
).
Note that the appearance of the multiplicities in (
5
) is a feature of our coordinate dependent approach.

0.6.
Related works

Theorem
D
implies that
\mathcal{W}
is a
bouquet of analytic curves, and a lamination with Cantor transversals outside the origin.
Such a structure first appeared in the work of Yamagishi
[
Yam01
]
(see
also
[
Shi10
]
) who exhibited a simple mechanism for a meromorphic map in
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)

to have an invariant Cantor bouquet of analytic curves at an indeterminacy point.
Gignac
[
Gig14
]
studied in detail
the map
(z^{4},w^{2}-z^{4})
(which is of the form (
2
))
to build a counter-example to a question of Arnol’d.
For this example, he proved that all curves in
\mathcal{K}
are smooth and intersect
{\left\{z=0\right\}}
transversally.
In all these works though, there was no explicit connection to non-Archimedean dynamics.
Note also that global variants of these constructions for polynomial mappings of
\mathbb{C}^{2}

were studied in
[
DDS05
,
Vig07
]
.

In a different direction, Kiwi
[
Kiw06
, Theorem 1.6]
proved that the
locus of non-renormalizable complex cubic polynomials with disconnected Julia set
is naturally foliated by analytic curves, corresponding to
non-renormalizable cubic polynomials defined over
\mathbb{L}
. It could be interesting to expand on his arguments
and see whether the bifurcation current defined in
[
DF08
]
can be described similarly
as in (
5
),
where the measure
\mu_{\na}
would be replaced by a suitable bifurcation measure in the non-Archimedean
cubic parameter space.

Finally, we note that although the laminarity of currents is a pervasive property in holomorphic dynamics, see e.g.
[
BLS93
,
BJ00
,
Duj06
,
Din05
,
Duj23
]
, a non-Archimedean
interpretation of the set of plaques defining the current is not generally possible.
The complex structure needs to degenerate to exhibit a limiting
non-Archimedean behaviour, a property which is guaranteed by the fact that we are working locally at a superattracting fixed point.
In the global situation which motivated this work,
where
f
is the germ of a regular polynomial mapping at some superattracting point on the line at infinity, the laminar structure of the current
{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}^{c}g
is reminiscent from that of the Green current obtained in
[
BJ00
]
, with the dynamics of
f_{\diamond}
playing the role of that of
f\arrowvert_{L_{\infty}}
.

, and

.

), we turn to complex dynamics. Theorem
A
follows from Theorem
7.1
, and Theorem
D
corresponds to Theorem
8.2
.

1.
Dynamics on the Berkovich affine line

In this section we gather the necessary material from Berkovich theory, in particular over
the field of Laurent series. The self maps induced by
our skew products will be introduced in the
next section.

1.1.
The Berkovich affine line

(See
[
Ben19
,
BR10
,
Jon15
]
for a more detailed presentation.)
Let
(K,|\cdot|)
be a complete non-Archimedean field.
The Berkovich affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
over
K
is
by definition the set of all multiplicative seminorms on
K[w]

whose restriction to
K
coincides with
|\cdot|
, endowed with the weakest
topology making all evaluation maps continuous.

Given a point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
and a polynomial
P\in K[w]
we write

|P(x)|\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
for the value of
P
at
x
, that is

x
is a seminorm
{\left|{\cdot}\right|}_{x}
and
{\left|{P(x)}\right|}={\left|{P}\right|}_{x}
.
Note that when
P=c
is a constant,
then
|c(x)|=|c|
. The space
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
is locally compact.

1.1.1.

Suppose that
K
is algebraically closed.
We denote by

\overline{B}(a,r)={\left\{b\in K,\ {\left|{b-a}\right|}\leq r\right\}}

(resp.

B(a,r)={\left\{b\in K,\ {\left|{b-a}\right|}<r\right\}}
) the closed (resp. open) ball of center
a
and radius
r
in
K
.
Then Berkovich classified points

x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
into four types:

Type 1:

there exists
a\in K
such that
|P(x)|=|P(a)|
;

Type 2:

there exists
a\in K
, and
r\in|K^{*}|
such that
|P(x)|=\sup_{|b-a|\leq r}|P(b)|
;

Type 3:

there exists
a\in K
, and
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\setminus|K^{*}|
such that
|P(x)|=\sup_{|b-a|\leq r}|P(b)|
;

Type 4:

there exists a decreasing sequence of balls
(B_{n})

such that
\bigcap_{n}B_{n}=\emptyset
and
|P(x)|=\inf_{n}\sup_{b\in B_{n}}|P(b)|
.

We denote by
\zeta(a,r)
the point (of type
1
,
2
or
3
) associated to the
ball of center
a\in K
and radius
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and by
\zeta_{g}=\zeta(0,1)
the Gauss point.
The norm
{\left|{\,\cdot\,}\right|}
being non-Archimedean, two (closed) balls

\overline{B}(a,r)
and
\overline{B}(b,r)
of same radius
r\geq 0
are either disjoint or equal.
It follows that
\zeta(a,r)
and
\zeta(b,r)
are distinct when
r<{\left|{b-a}\right|}
, and coincide when
r\geq{\left|{b-a}\right|}
.
This phenomenon hints at the
\mathbb{R}
-tree structure of
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
(see
[
FJ04
, Chapter 3]
), with branching points at points of type
2
.
More precisely, all points in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
are associated with nested collections of balls in
\mathbb{A}^{1}(K)
, i.e., filters for the poset of closed balls in
\mathbb{A}^{1}(K)
, ordered by inclusion, that are closed under the intersection of decreasing sequences (when it gives a ball).
The inclusion of nested collections of balls defines a partial order on
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
,
which gives to the latter the structure of a
\mathbb{R}
-tree (see
[
Jon15
, Section 3]
for further details).
More precisely, we say that
x \leq y\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
iff
|P(x)|\leq|P(y)|
for all
P\in K[w]
.
This order relation endows
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
with a tree structure, whose
minimal points consist of rigid and type 4 points.

We define the
diameter
of a point
x=\zeta(a,r)
by
\mathrm{diam}(x)=r
and observe that it admits a unique extension as a upper semi-continuous and nondecreasing function on
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
.
It follows that
\mathrm{diam}(x)=0
if and only if
x
is of type 1, and if
x_{n}
decreases to
x
, then

\mathrm{diam}(x_{n})\to\mathrm{diam}(x)
.

1.1.2.

When
K
is not algebraically closed, let

\widehat{K}
be the completion of the algebraic closure of
K
.
The absolute Galois group
G=\mathrm{Gal}(K^{\mathrm{alg}}/K)

(where
K^{\mathrm{alg}}
is an algebraic closure of
K
)
acts on
\widehat{K}
by isometries so it preserves the radii of balls and
acts naturally on
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\widehat{K}}
.
There exists a canonical
G
-equivariant
restriction map
\pi\colon\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\widehat{K}}\to\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
, which identifies

\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
with the quotient
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\widehat{K}}/G
.

The action of
G
preserves the type of points so that we may speak of
the type of a point in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
. Rigid points in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
are type 1 points
defined over a finite extension of
K

(in general the extension
\widehat{K}/K
is infinite).

We define the
capacity
of a point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
by

(6)

\log\capa(x)=\inf\left\{\frac{\log|P(x)|}{\deg P},\,P\in K[w]\text{ monic non-constant}\right\}\text{.}

When
K
is algebraically closed, the infimum above can be taken over polynomials of degree
1
and
in this case we deduce that
\capa(x)=\mathrm{diam}(x)
for every
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
.

For arbitrary
K
, we only have
\capa(\zeta(a,r))\leq r
. In particular the capacity is zero for a rigid point. The computation of the capacity is
detailed below in the case of formal Laurent series.

As it is customary, we use the following notation:
any
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
is a semi-norm
{\left|{\cdot}\right|}_{x}

on
K[w]
, and we denote
{\left|{P(x)}\right|}={\left|{P}\right|}_{x}
. Abusing slighlty, we write

{\left|{x}\right|}
for
{\left|{w}\right|}_{x}
.

1.2.
The field of formal Laurent series and its extensions

Let
k
be any algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
.
We endow the field
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)
of formal Laurent series
with the
z
-adic norm normalized by
|z|=e^{-1}

so that

{\left|{\phi}\right|}=e^{-\mathrm{ord}_{z}(\phi)}\text{, where }\mathrm{ord}_{z}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}z^{n}\right)=\min\{n\in\mathbb{Z},\,a_{n}\neq 0\}\text{.}

It is a complete metrized field with value group
e^{\mathbb{Z}}
, and ring of integers

k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)^{\circ}=\{x\in k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right),\,|x|\leq 1\}=k[[{z}]]\text{.}

The algebraic closure of
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)
is the field of Puiseux series

k\langle\!\langle{z}\rangle\!\rangle=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}k\big(\!\big(z^{1/q}\big)\!\big)

This field is not complete, and its completion

\mathbb{L}=\left\{ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}z^{\beta_{n}},\,a_{n}\in k,\text{ s.t. }(\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathbb{N}},\ \forall n\ \beta_{n}<\beta_{n+1},\,\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{n}=\infty\right\}

is algebraically closed.

The absolute Galois group of
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)
is isomorphic to the projective limit
\widehat{\mathbb{U}}

of the groups of
m
-th root of unity
\mathbb{U}_{m}
under the morphisms
\mathbb{U}_{nm}\to\mathbb{U}_{m}
defined by

z\mapsto z^{n}
. An element
u\in\widehat{\mathbb{U}}

is a collection of
n
-th root
u_{n}\in\mathbb{U}_{n}
such that

u_{nm}^{n}=u_{m}
for all
n,m
.
The action of an element
u\in\widehat{\mathbb{U}}
on
z^{\beta}

is given as follows: write
\beta=p/q
with
p
and
q
coprime,
and set
u\cdot z^{\beta}=u_{q}^{p}z^{\beta}
.

1.3.
The Berkovich affine line over
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)

Let us describe in more detail the structure of
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
,
insisting on its relation with
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
. This material is mostly taken from
[
FJ04
, Chapter 4]
(albeit with a different language).

1.3.1.
Representation by Puiseux series

Pick any element
\phi\in\mathbb{L}
, and any
r\in[0,+\infty)
.
Then we define the point
\zeta(\phi,r)\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}

by setting

(7)

{\left|{P\big(\zeta(\phi,r)\big)}\right|}:=\prod_{i}\max\left\{e^{-\mathrm{ord}_{z}P_{i}(\phi(z))},r\right\}\text{,}

where
P=\prod_{i}P_{i}\in\mathbb{L}[w]
and
\deg(P_{i})=1
(here, we are implicitly applying the Gauss
lemma on the multiplicativity of the norm
{\left|{P}\right|}_{B(\phi,r)}
).
Note that
\zeta(\phi,r)
is of type 1 if
r=0
;
of type 2 if
r\in e^{\mathbb{Q}}
; and of type 3 if
r\notin e^{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Moreover
\zeta(\phi,r)=\zeta(\phi^{\prime},r^{\prime})
iff

r=r^{\prime}
and
|\phi-\phi^{\prime}|\leq r
.
Conversely any point in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}

which is not of type 4 is of the form
\zeta(\phi,r)
for some

\phi\in\mathbb{L}
and some
r\geq 0
.
Observe that we have

\capa(\zeta(\phi,r))=\mathrm{diam}(\zeta(\phi,r))=r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\text{.}

A point
x
of type
4
can be obtained through series of the form

\phi=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}z^{\beta_{n}}
where
a_{n}\in k^{*}
,

\beta_{n}<\beta_{n+1}\in\mathbb{Q}
, and
\beta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{n}<\infty
,
by the analogue
of the formula (
7
)

{\left|{P\big(\zeta(\phi,r)\big)}\right|}:=e^{-\mathrm{ord}_{z}P(\phi(z))}.

Note that
(\beta_{n})
has unbounded denominators in this case, so that
P(\phi)
is never 0. In fact we have
\mathrm{diam}(x)=\beta
.

Pick now any point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
, and suppose that it is the image of a point of type 1, 2 or 3 under
\pi\colon\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}\to\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
.
Then
x
is represented by a point
\zeta(\phi,r)\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
, for some
\phi\in{\mathbb{L}}
and
r\geq 0
.
The representative is unique up to the action of the absolute Galois group
G
. When
\phi\in k\left(\!\left({z^{1/q}}\right)\!\right)
, the
G
-orbit of
\zeta(\phi,r)
is the same as its orbit under the action of
\mathbb{U}_{q}
.
If moreover
\mathrm{ord}_{z}(\phi)>0
, i.e.,
\phi\in k[[z^{1/q}]]
and
\phi(0)=0
,
then
\phi
is the Puiseux parametrization of a formal curve
C
in
\mathbb{A}^{2}
that intersects the line
\{z=0\}
with multiplicity dividing by
q
and all other representatives give other Puiseux parametrizations of
C
.
In plain words, a rigid point
x
is attached to a curve
C
while its preimages under
\pi

corresponds to its Puiseux parameterizations. We will say more about the correspondence between
germs of curves and points in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
in §
8.5
.

1.3.2.
Multiplicity and capacity in the closed unit ball
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}

Pick any point
x
in the Berkovich closed unit ball
\overline{B}^{\an}(0,1)=\{|x|\leq 1\}\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
.
We call the cardinality of
\pi^{-1}(x)\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}

the
multiplicity
of
x
and denote it by
m(x)
.
It is finite iff
x
is either of type
2
or
3
, or rigid.
Later on, we will define another related quantity, the

generic multiplicity

b(x)
.

Suppose
x\in\overline{B}^{\an}(0,1)
is a rigid point, so that it is represented by a Puiseux series

\phi(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n/q}\in k[[{z^{1/q}}]]
. Then, we have

(8)

m(x)=\operatorname{lcm}\left\{\frac{q}{\gcd\{n,q\}},\,a_{n}\neq 0\right\}

It can also be interpreted geometrically as follows.
Denote by
C
the formal curve parameterized by
t\mapsto(t^{q},\phi(t^{q}))
.
Then
m(x)=(C\cdot\{z=0\})
where the latter is the local intersection product
between
C
and the curve
z=0
viewed in the formal scheme
\spf k[[{z,w}]]
(beware that it is not
the multiplicity of
C
at
(0,0)
).

Pick any
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
, and choose any
y\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}

such that
\pi(y)=x
. Then we set
\mathrm{diam}(x)=\mathrm{diam}(y)
. This is independent
on the choice of
y
since the diameter function is Galois invariant.
The capacity of the point
x
in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
is defined as before by

\log\capa(x)=\inf\left\{\frac{\log{\left|{P(x)}\right|}}{\deg P},\,P\in k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)\![w]\text{ non-constant}\right\}\text{.}

Notice that the condition that
P
is monic is
superfluous in this setting, since the absolute value is trivial on
k
(i.e.,
{\left|{k^{*}}\right|}=1
).
It is customary to define

(9)

\alpha(x)=-\log\capa(x)

for any point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
.
Note that
\alpha(\zeta_{g})=0
, and
\alpha
is increasing on each segment
[\zeta_{g},x]
(resp. decreasing for the order
\leq
).

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
x\in\overline{B}^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
be a point of type
2
or
3
.
Pick any
\phi\in k\langle\!\langle{z}\rangle\!\rangle
such that

\zeta(\phi,0)<x
. Let
P_{\mathrm{min}}\in k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)\![w]
be the minimal polynomial of
\phi
. Then we have

\alpha(x)=\frac{-\log{\left|{P_{\mathrm{min}}(x)}\right|}}{\deg_{w}P_{\mathrm{min}}}\text{.}

Proof.

We may suppose
\phi\in k[[{z^{1/q}}]]
with
q=m(\phi)
.
Then the minimal polynomial is equal to
P_{\min}(z,w)=\prod_{u^{q}=1}(w-u\cdot\phi)
,
is monic of degree
q
, and belongs to
k[[z]][w]
.

The statement then follows from
[
FJ04
, Proposition 3.25]
.
The latter reference is written in the context of valuations instead of norm.
We refer to §
1.3.3
below for a quick discussion on how to compare these
point of views.
∎

Proposition 1.2
.

Suppose
x\in\overline{B}^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
.
Then there exists a decreasing sequence of type 2 points

x_{0}=\zeta_{g}
,
x_{n+1}<x_{n}
,
\lim_{n}x_{n}=x

such that:

(1)

the multiplicity is constant equal to
m_{n}
on the interval
I_{n}=[x_{n+1},x_{n})
;

(2)

-\log\frac{\mathrm{diam}(y)}{\mathrm{diam}(y^{\prime})}=m_{n}(\alpha(y)-\alpha(y^{\prime}))

for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\overline{I_{n}}
.

Moreover
m_{1}=1
, and
m_{n}
divides
m_{n+1}
for all
n
.

This sequence is finite if and only if
x
has finite multiplicity.

Proof.

We only give a sketch of proof, referring to
[
FJ04
, Chapter 5]
for more details.

Suppose
x=\pi(\zeta(\phi,r))
with

\displaystyle\phi=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}z^{\beta_{n}}\in\mathbb{L}^{\circ}
with
a_{n}\in k^{*}
,
0\leq\beta_{n}<\beta_{n+1}\in\mathbb{Q}

and
r\in[0,1]
.

Write
\beta_{n}=p_{n}/q_{n}
with
p_{n},q_{n}
coprime integers.
We define by induction a sequence of integers
n_{j}

such that
n_{0}=0
, and
n_{j}
is the smallest integer such that
q_{j}
is not
a multiple of
\operatorname{lcm}\{q_{l},l\leq n_{j}-1\}
. Then
we set
m_{j}=\operatorname{lcm}\{q_{l},l\leq n_{j}\}
so that
m_{j}
is a multiple of
m_{j-1}
.
One can check that if
\beta_{n_{j-1}}\leq-\log r<\beta_{n_{j}}
, then

m\big(\pi(\zeta(\phi,r))\big)=m\Big(\pi\Big(\zeta\Big(\sum_{n<n_{j}}a_{n}z^{\beta_{n}},r\Big)\Big)\Big)=m_{j-1}

which implies (1).
Moreover the supremum of
\log|P|/\deg(P)

on that segment is attained for the minimal polynomial of

\sum_{n<n_{j}}a_{n}z^{\beta_{n}}
. This implies (2).
∎

Since the multiplicity is increasing on
[\zeta_{g},x]
, we obtain:

Corollary 1.3
.

The function
-\log\mathrm{diam}|_{[x,\zeta_{g}]}
is a convex function of
\alpha
, or equivalently,
\alpha
is a concave function of
-\log\mathrm{diam}|_{[x,\zeta_{g}]}
.
In particular

-\log\mathrm{diam}\geq\alpha

and if
m=m(x)<\infty
, we have

-\log\mathrm{diam}\leq m(x)\alpha
on
[x,\zeta_{g}]
.

The second item of Proposition
1.2
implies that
the restriction of
\alpha
on any segment
[y,\zeta_{g}]
,
y>x

is continuous, and its concavity implies that it is continuous at
x
as well. From this
we get a canonical parametrization
[0,\alpha(x)]\to[\zeta_{g},x]
,

t\mapsto x(t)
such that
\alpha(x(t))=t
.

We shall also use the following

Lemma 1.4
.

For any
x\leq x^{\prime}\in\overline{B}^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
,
for any monic
P\in k[[{z}]][w]
we have

\frac{\alpha(x^{\prime})}{\alpha(x)}\leq 1\leq\frac{-\log|P(x)|}{-\log|P(x^{\prime})|}\leq\frac{\alpha(x)}{\alpha(x^{\prime})}

Proof.

The two inequalities on the left follow from the fact that both
\alpha
and
-\log|P|

are non-increasing (with respect to the order relation). Let us fix any
\phi\in k\langle\!\langle{z}\rangle\!\rangle
.
For any
t\in[1,+\infty)
, denote by
x(t)
the unique point in
[\zeta_{g},\phi]
such that

\alpha(x(t))=t
. Suppose that
x(t_{0})=x^{\prime}
and
x(t_{1})=x
for some
t_{0}<t_{1}
.
It follows from
[
FJ04
, Prop 3.25]
and a decomposition into irreducible components that
t\mapsto-\log|P(x(t))|
is concave. Since
P
is monic
and its coefficients are formal power series in
z
, it follows that

-\log|P(\zeta_{g})|=0
.
It follows that

-\log|P(x(t))|\leq-\frac{t}{t_{0}}\,\log|P(x(t_{0}))|

which implies the claim.
∎

1.3.3.
Relation with valuation spaces

In the literature
[
FJ07
,
GR14
,
GR21
]
, the local analysis of super-attracting analytic dynamical
maps in dimension
\geq 2
was mostly formulated in the language of valuations.
For the convenience of the reader, we indicate how to translate notions introduced above in the context of
the Berkovich affine line to valuation spaces, referring to
[
FJ04
, Chapters 3 & 4]
for further details.

Any seminorm
{\left|{\,\cdot\,}\right|}_{x}\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{K}
defines a semivaluation
\nu_{x}(\cdot)=-\log{\left|{\,\cdot\,}\right|}_{x}
over the ring
K[w]
.
When
K=\mathbb{L}
, we get a space of semivaluations over
\mathbb{L}[w]
which extend the
z
-adic valuation, defined by

\mathrm{ord}_{z}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}z^{\beta_{n}}\right)=\min\{\beta_{n},a_{n}\neq 0\}\text{.}

on
\mathbb{L}^{*}
and by
\mathrm{ord}_{z}(0)=+\infty
.
Any such valuation
\nu_{x}
extends uniquely to a semivaluation, again denoted by
\nu_{x}
, over
\mathbb{L}[[{w}]]
(and all semivaluations over
\mathbb{L}[[{w}]]
come from the ones in
\mathbb{L}[w]
).
Valuations associated to series
\phi\in{\mathbb{L}}
such that
\mathrm{ord}_{z}(\phi)>0

are called
centered
, and the corresponding subset
is denoted by
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{z}^{*}
. They correspond to the Berkovich open unit ball
B^{\an}(0,1)=\{|x|<1\}
in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
. By adding the non-centered valuation
\mathrm{ord}_{z}
, corresponding to the Gauss point
\zeta_{g}
, we get the valuation space
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{z}
.

The absolute Galois group
G
of
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)
acts on
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{z}^{*}
, and its quotient
\mathcal{V}_{z}^{*}=\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{z}^{*}/G
can be identified as the set of centered semivaluations over
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)[w]
extending the
z
-adic valuation on
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)[w]
, or equivalently with the set of centered semivaluations over
k[[{z,w}]]
that extend the trivial vauation on
k
, and normalized by the condition
\nu(z)=1
.
These valuations correspond to the Berkovich open unit ball
B^{\an}(0,1)
in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
. Again, the whole (normalized) valuation space is given by
\mathcal{V}_{z}=\mathcal{V}_{z}^{*}\sqcup\{\mathrm{ord}_{z}\}
. This space is also known as the
non-Archimedean link
of
(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{k},0)
, see
[
Fan18
]
.

The value
1-\log\capa(x)=1-\log\mathrm{diam}(x)
of
x=\zeta(\phi,r)\in B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
is often denoted by
A_{z}(\nu_{x})
, and known as the
relative thinness
, or
relative log-discrepancy
of the valuation
\nu_{x}\in\mathcal{V}_{z}
.

For the capacity
\capa(x)
of
x\in B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
, we have

\alpha_{z}(\nu_{x}):=-\log\capa(x)=\sup\left\{\frac{\nu_{x}(P)}{\deg P}\right\}\text{.}

Here
\deg P
is the degree with respect to the
w
coordinate, which coincides with the intersection multiplicity of

2.
Dynamics of polynomial skew products with small relative degree

2.1.
Polynomial skew products and their actions on the Berkovich line

In this section, we fix once for all an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
0

and consider a map of the form

(
2
)

f(z,w)=\biggl(z^{d},w^{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}(z)w^{j}\biggr)

where
d,c\geq 2
and
h_{j}\in k[[{z}]]
satisfy
h_{j}(0)=0
.
We will often work in the
small relative degree
regime, that is:
c<d
.

Such a map naturally induces a self-map
f_{\diamond}\colon\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}\to\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
as follows.
Observe first that thanks to the non-Archimedean property,
for any point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
, the assignment

P\mapsto|(P\circ f)(x)|^{1/d}
defines a multiplicative semi-norm on

k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)[w]
(where
P
is viewed as a function of
(z,w)
). In addition if
P
has degree 0,

|(P\circ f)(x)|=|z|^{d}
, so we conclude that
P\mapsto|(P\circ f)(x)|^{1/d}
belongs to
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
. Thus we may define a map

f_{\diamond}:\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}\to\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
by setting

(10)

|P(f_{\diamond}(x))|:=|(P\circ f)(x)|^{1/d}\text{, for any }P\in k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)[w].

It is also convenient to work over
\mathbb{L}
since the latter
is algebraically closed and complete. For this, it is enough to observe that (
10
)
also defines a map
f_{\diamond}\colon\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}\to\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}

such that
f_{\diamond}\circ\pi=\pi\circ f_{\diamond}
where
\pi\colon\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}\to\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}

denotes the canonical projection.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
\phi\in\mathbb{L}
, we have

(11)

f_{\diamond}(\phi)=\left(\phi\big({z^{1/d}}\big)\right)^{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}\big({z^{1/d}}\big)\,\left(\phi\big({z^{1/d}}\big)\right)^{j}.

Proof.

Indeed, since
\mathbb{L}
is algebraically closed
f_{\diamond}(\phi)
is determined by its value on
linear polynomials
w-\psi(z)
with
\psi\in\mathbb{L}
, and for such a polynomial
P
we have

\displaystyle|P(f_{\diamond}(\phi))|=|(P\circ f)(\phi)|^{1/d}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\phi^{c}(z)+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}(z)\phi^{j}(z)-\psi(z^{d})\bigg|^{1/d}_{\mathbb{L}}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\phi^{c}\big({z^{1/d}}\big)+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}(z)\phi^{j}\big({z^{1/d}}\big)-\psi(z)\bigg|_{\mathbb{L}},

thereby proving the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Theorem 2.6

w
. It follows that

\mathrm{diam}(f_{\diamond}(x))=\mathrm{diam}(\hat{f}(\tau(x)))=\mathrm{diam}(\tau(x))\times|\hat{f}^{\prime}(\tau(x))|=\mathrm{diam}(x)^{1/d}\times|\jac_{w}(f)(x)|^{1/d},

thereby proving (
15
).
∎

3.2.
The invariant Cantor set

The next theorem describes the topological dynamics of
f_{\diamond}
in
B^{\an}(0,1)
both in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}

and
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
. Let us introduce the sets

\mathcal{K}:=\{x\in B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)},\,f^{n}_{\diamond}(x)\text{ does not converge to }\zeta_{g}\}

and

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}:=\{x\in B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}},\,f^{n}_{\diamond}(x)\text{ does not converge to }\zeta_{g}\}.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
f
be a polynomial map of the form (
2.1
), with
2\leq c<d
. Then

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}=\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{K})
, and
both sets are compact totally invariant subsets included in
\overline{B}^{\an}(0,\rho_{0})

for some
\rho_{0}<1
. The set
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
(resp.
\mathcal{K}
)
consists of type 1 points (resp. type 1 points such that
\alpha=\infty
).

We have the following dichotomy:
either
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
are both Cantor sets, or they are both
reduced to a single point, that corresponds to
a totally invariant smooth (formal) curve
transverse to
(z=0)
. In the latter case,
f
is of the form
f(z,w)=(z^{d},(w+\phi(z))^{c}-\phi(z^{d}))
for some

\phi\in k[[{z}]]
.

Remark 3.3
.

Recall that a type
1
point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
is characterized by the condition

-\log\mathrm{diam}(x)=\infty.

If
\phi
is a Puiseux series, then
\alpha(\phi)=\infty
as well (see, e.g., Corollary
1.3
).
When
\phi\in\mathbb{L}\setminus k\langle\!\langle{z}\rangle\!\rangle
, letting
(x_{n})
be the sequence of points given by Proposition
1.2
, the condition
\alpha=\infty
is equivalent to

\sum_{n\geq 0} \frac{1}{m_{n}}\log\frac{\mathrm{diam}(x_{n})}{\mathrm{diam}(x_{n+1})}=\infty~.

Proof.

We first work in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
.
Set
\rho_{0}:=\max_{0\leq j\leq c-1}|h_{j}|^{1/(c-j)}<1
. Then, if
\phi\in\mathbb{L}
is
such that
\rho_{0}<|\phi|<1
, we get

|\phi|^{c}>\max_{0\leq j\leq c-1}\{|h_{j}||\phi|^{j}\}
, so that

(16)

|f_{\diamond}(\phi)|=\bigg|\phi^{c}\big({z^{1/d}}\big)+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}\big({z^{1/d}}\big)\,\phi^{j}\big({z^{1/d}}\big)\bigg|=|\phi|^{c/d}.

It follows that
U:=\{\rho_{0}<|x|<1\}\subset B^{\an}(0,1)

is a
f_{\diamond}
-invariant open set and

f_{\diamond}^{n}(x)\to\zeta_{g}
for any
x\in U
.
We thus define

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}:=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\Big(B^{\an}(0,1)\setminus f_{\diamond}^{-n}(U)\Big).

It is a compact totally invariant set, and

x\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
if and only if
f^{n}_{\diamond}
does not converge to
\zeta_{g}
.
Since
\pi^{-1}\{\rho_{0}<|x|<1\}=\{\rho_{0}<|x|<1\}
, we also get that

x\in\pi(\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}})
iff
|f_{\diamond}^{n}(x)|\leq\rho_{0}
for all
n
, and

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}=\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{K})
.

We now proceed to show that
\mathrm{diam}(x)=0
for any
x\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
.
In particular
x
must be of type 1.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
x\leq x^{\prime}\in B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
of
positive diameter, we have

(17)

\left|\log\frac{|\jac_{w}(f)(x^{\prime})|}{|\jac_{w}(f)(x)|}\right|\leq(c-1)\times\log\frac{ \mathrm{diam}(x^{\prime})}{ \mathrm{diam}(x)}~.

Proof.

By continuity, we may suppose that
x=\zeta(\phi,r)
and
x^{\prime}=\zeta(\phi,r^{\prime})

with
\phi\in\mathbb{L}
and
r\leq r^{\prime}<1
.
Write
\jac_{w}(f)=c\prod_{j=1}^{c-1}(w-c_{j}(z))
. Then

\log\frac{|\jac_{w}(f)(x^{\prime})|}{|\jac_{w}(f)(x^{\prime})|}=\log\frac{\prod_{j}\max\{|\phi-c_{j}|,r^{\prime}\}}{\prod_{j}\max\{|\phi-c_{j}|,r\}}\leq(c-1)\log\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}.

Indeed to justify the inequality, we split the products according to the position of
{\left|{\phi-c_{j}}\right|}
with respect to
r^{\prime}
: if
{\left|{\phi-c_{j}}\right|}\leq r^{\prime}
then
\frac{\max\{|\phi-c_{j}|,r^{\prime}\}}{\max\{|\phi-c_{j}|,r\}}\leq\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}
and otherwise this fraction equals 1. The desired inequality (
17
) follows.
∎

Now, let
x\in B^{\an}(0,1)
be of finite diameter, and observe that
[x,\zeta_{g})\cap U
is non-empty.
Pick any
x^{\prime}\in I\cap U
, so that
\mathrm{diam}(f^{n}_{\diamond}(x^{\prime}))\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
Then for every
n\geq 1
, Lemma
3.1
(applied to
f^{n}
) yields:

\displaystyle\left|\log\frac{\mathrm{diam}(f^{n}_{\diamond}(x^{\prime}))}{\mathrm{diam}(f^{n}_{\diamond}(x))}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{n}}\left|\log\frac{\mathrm{diam}(x^{\prime})}{\mathrm{diam}(x)}\right|+\frac{1}{d^{n}}\left|\log\frac{|\jac_{w}(f^{n})(x^{\prime})|}{|\jac_{w}(f^{n})(x)|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+(c^{n}-1)}{d^{n}}\left|\log\frac{\mathrm{diam}(x^{\prime})}{\mathrm{diam}(x)}\right|\longrightarrow 0

which implies
x^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
, as announced.

Note that
\mathrm{diam}(\pi(x))=\mathrm{diam}(x)
, hence
\mathcal{K}\subset\{\mathrm{diam}=0\}
as well.
We now work in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
and
prove
\alpha(x)=\infty
for every
x\in\mathcal{K}
. We rely on the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
x\leq x^{\prime}\in B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
, such that
\alpha(x)<\infty
and for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(18)

\frac{\alpha(x^{\prime})}{\alpha(x)}\leq\frac{\alpha(f^{n}_{\diamond}(x^{\prime}))}{\alpha(f^{n}_{\diamond}(x))}\leq\frac{\alpha(x)}{\alpha(x^{\prime})}~.

Proof.

By continuity, we may suppose that
x
and
x^{\prime}
are of type 2.
Since
f^{n}_{\diamond}
preserves the order, we have

f^{n}_{\diamond}(x^{\prime})\leq f^{n}_{\diamond}(x)
hence there exists

P\in k[[{z}]][w]
monic of degree
\ell
such that

\alpha(f^{n}_{\diamond}(x^{\prime}))=-\frac{1}{\ell}\log|P(f^{n}_{\diamond}(x^{\prime}))|=-\frac{1}{\ell d^{n}}\log|(P\circ f^{n})(x^{\prime}))|

and similarly

\alpha(f^{n}_{\diamond}(x))=-\frac{1}{\ell d^{n}}\log|(P\circ f^{n})(x))|
.
We conclude using Lemma
1.4
.
∎

Suppose now that
x\leq\zeta_{g}
, and
\alpha(x)<\infty
, and recall that
\alpha(\zeta_{g})=0
. Viewing
the relation (
18
) as an equicontinuity property, we infer that
the set of points in
[x,\zeta_{g}]
lying in the basin
of attraction of
\zeta_{g}
is both open and closed. This entails that

f^{n}_{\diamond}(x)\to\zeta_{g}
as required.

It remains to prove the dichotomy.
Note that, even if for convenience we drop the ‘an’ superscript, all the balls we consider in the proof are
balls in the affine Berkovich line.
Let us first work over
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
.
To do this, consider the closed ball
\overline{B}_{0,0}:=\overline{B}(0,\rho_{0})
.
It follows from the previous arguments that
\mathcal{K}=\bigcap_{n}f_{\diamond}^{-n}(\overline{B}_{0,0})
.
Since
f_{\diamond}
is finite, type preserving and order-preserving,

f_{\diamond}^{-n}(\overline{B}_{0,0})
is a finite union of disjoint closed balls
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{\overline{B}_{n,i}\}
such that
f^{n}_{\diamond}(\overline{B}_{n,i})=\overline{B}_{0,0}

for all
n
and
i
.

For any
\phi\in\mathcal{K}
, consider the sequence of balls
\overline{B}_{n}(\phi)\in\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}

containing
\phi
. It is decreasing hence its intersection
\bigcap_{n}\overline{B}_{n}(\phi)

is included in
\mathcal{K}
and
contains
\phi
. It is equal to
\{\phi\}
because
\mathcal{K}
contains only type
1
points.

Suppose now that there exists an integer
n_{0}
such that
\#\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n_{0}}\geq 2
.
We shall prove that
\mathcal{K}
is a Cantor set. To do so we need to show that

\mathcal{K}
is a compact, totally disconnected and perfect set.
The first property is clear. Pick any point
\phi\in\mathcal{K}
.
Then for any
n
the finite collection of balls
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{\overline{B}_{n,i}\}
covers
\mathcal{K}
. The function
\chi
taking value
1
on
\overline{B}_{n}(\phi)\cap\mathcal{K}

and
0
on all other pieces
\overline{B}_{n,i}\cap\mathcal{K}
is continuous since the closed balls are disjoint,
hence the connected component of
\phi
in
\mathcal{K}
is included in
\overline{B}_{n}(\phi)
,
so that
\mathcal{K}
is totally disconnected.
To see that
\mathcal{K}
is perfect, choose any open neighborhood
V
of some
\phi\in\mathcal{K}
.
For
n
sufficiently large
\overline{B}_{n}(\phi)\subset V
, and
f^{n}_{\diamond}\colon\overline{B}_{n}(\phi)\to\overline{B}_{0,0}
is surjective. It follows that
\overline{B}_{n}(\phi)
contains a preimage by
f^{n}_{\diamond}
of a point in each set
\overline{B}_{m,i}\cap\mathcal{K}
, and in particular at least
2
points.
Thus
\mathcal{K}
is perfect.

Suppose now on the contrary that for every
n
,
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n}=\{\overline{B}_{n,0}\}
consists of a single ball. Then
\mathcal{K}
is reduced to a singleton
\{x\}=\bigcap_{n}\overline{B}_{n,0}
. To conclude we need to argue that
x
is represented by a branch of the critical set, and that this branch is smooth and transversal to
(z=0)
.

Observe first that we can construct a sequence
\overline{\mathcal{B}}_{n,\mathbb{L}}
as above over
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
, and the same analysis
proves that either
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
is a Cantor set or a single point
\{\phi\}
with
\phi\in\mathbb{L}
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
is a singleton. Since
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
is Galois invariant,
x
must be a point of type
1
of (relative) multiplicity equal to
1
. We deduce that
x
is represented by a smooth branch
\phi
transversal to
(z=0)
.
We claim that
w-\phi(z)
is a branch of the critical set.
In fact, from the fact that
w-\phi(z)
is totally invariant we deduce that

f^{*}(w-\phi(z))=w^{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}(z)w^{j}-\phi(z^{d})

must be of the form
(w-\phi(z))^{e}u(z,w)
for a suitable
e\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

and a unit
u=\sum_{k}u_{k}(w)z^{k}
.
By computing at
z=0
we get
w^{e}u_{0}=w^{c}
, from which we deduce that
e=c
and
u_{0}\equiv 1
.
Assume that there exists
k\geq 1
so that
u_{k}\not\equiv 0
. Take
k
to be minimal, and let
h
be the order of
u_{k}
at
0
. Then the expression
(w-\phi(z))^{c}u(z,w)
has a non-trivial monomial
z^{k}w^{h+c}
, while
f^{*}(w-\phi(z))
does not, and we get a contradiction.
Thus we have showed that
u\equiv 1
and that
w\circ f(z,w)=(w-\phi(z))^{c}+\phi(z^{d})
.
Computing the derivative, we get

\jac_{w}(f)=c(w-\phi(z))^{c-1}\text{,}

from which we deduce that
w-\phi(z)
is a critical branch, as desired.

To conclude the proof of Theorem
3.2
, it remains to exclude the possibility that

\mathcal{K}=\{x\}
is a singleton and
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
is a Cantor set. For convenience, we postpone the
proof of this fact until Theorem
4.1
is established.
∎

3.3.
The non-Archimedean Green function

In this section, we can work indifferently over
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}

or
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
.

Recall that a subharmonic function
g\colon U\to[-\infty,\infty)

on an open set
U\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
(resp. in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
) is
upper-semicontinuous, not identically
-\infty
, and satisfies
the submean value inequalities, see, e.g.,
[
BR10
, Definition 8.16]
.
Suffice it to say that the set of subharmonic functions
is stable under taking maxima, sums, multiplication by positive constants
and decreasing limits. It contains all functions of the form

q\log|P|
for any
P\in k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)[w]
(resp. in
\mathbb{L}[w]
), and
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{+}
,
and the lattice generated by these functions is dense in the space
of all subharmonic functions, see, e.g.,
[
Ste19
]
(or
[
BR10
, Proposition 8.45]
).
Also, if
g
is subharmonic in
U\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
, then

g\circ\pi
is subharmonic in
\pi^{-1}(U)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
f
be a polynomial map of the form (
2.1
), with
2\leq c<d
.

Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
the function
g_{n}:x\mapsto\frac{d^{n}}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{f_{\diamond}^{n}(x)}\right|}
is subharmonic on
B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
.
The sequence
(g_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges
pointwise to a subharmonic function

g_{\na}\colon B^{\an}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{-}\text{ such that }g_{\na}\circ f_{\diamond}=\frac{c}{d}g_{\na}.

Furthermore
the convergence is locally uniform on compact subsets of
B^{\an}(0,1)\setminus\mathcal{K}
, in
particular
g_{\na}
is continuous there.

Furthermore

g_{\na}(x)=\log|x|
on an annulus of the form
\{\rho_{0}<|x|<1\}
, and

\mathcal{K}=\{g_{\na}=-\infty\}~.

The same results hold in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
and for the corresponding function

g_{\na,\mathbb{L}}
we have the relation
g_{\na,\mathbb{L}}=g_{\na}\circ\pi
.

The first assertion is a consequence of the following basic lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
g
is a subharmonic function on
B^{\an}(0,1)
then
g\circ f_{\diamond}
is
subharmonic.

Proof.

For any polynomial
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)[w]
, denote by
\log_{P}
the function
x\mapsto\log{\left|{P(x)}\right|}
on

\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
. It is enough to check that
\log_{P}\circ f_{\diamond}
is subharmonic for every
P
. Writing
f(z,w)=(z^{d},f_{2}(z,w))
, for every

\psi\in\mathbb{L}
we have

(19)

(\log_{P}\circ f_{\diamond})(\psi)=\frac{1}{d}\log{\left|{P\big(z^{d},f_{2}(z,\psi(z))\big)}\right|},

which shows that
\log_{P}\circ f_{\diamond}=\frac{1}{d}\log_{P\circ f}
is subharmonic.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

From (
16
), the density of type 1 points, and the continuity of

{\left|{\cdot}\right|}
, there exists
0<\rho_{0}<1
such that
denoting
U={\left\{\rho_{0}<{\left|{x}\right|}<1\right\}}
,
{\left|{f_{\diamond}(x)}\right|}={\left|{x}\right|}^{c/d}
for any
x\in U
.
Hence
U
is
f_{\diamond}
-invariant and we infer that for any
x\in U
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

g_{n}(x)=\log{\left|{x}\right|}
.

Pick any compact set
L\subset B^{\an}(0,1)\setminus\mathcal{K}
. There exists an integer

N
such that
f_{\diamond}^{N}(L)\subset U
. Then for
x\in L
and
n\geq N
we have

(20)

g_{n}(x)=\left(\frac{d}{c}\right)^{n-N+N}\log{\left|{f_{\diamond}^{n-N}\left(f_{\diamond}^{N}(x)\right)}\right|}=\left(\frac{d}{c}\right)^{N}\log{\left|{f_{\diamond}^{N}(x)}\right|},

thus the sequence is locally stationary, and the local uniform convergence statement follows. In addition, for
x\in\mathcal{K}
we have that

\log{\left|{f_{\diamond}^{n}(x)}\right|}\leq\log\rho_{0}
for every
n
, from which we infer that

g_{n}(x)\to-\infty
, hence the function
g_{\na}
is well defined, continuous outside

\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{K}={\left\{g_{\na}=-\infty\right\}}
.

It remains to check that
g_{\na}
is subharmonic. Outside
\mathcal{K}
this follows from the local uniform convergence. Next, fix a large integer
A\gg 1
. Then
\max\{g_{n},-A\}
forms a sequence of bounded subharmonic functions, hence by Hartog’s theorem
[
FRL10
, Proposition 2.18]
,
there exists a subsequence
\max\{g_{n_{k}},-A\}
converging pointwise
on the complement of type 1 points to a
subharmonic function
g_{A}
. When
A
increases, then
g_{A}
decreases toward
g_{\na}
which is hence subharmonic too.

Finally, the same proof works in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
, and the relation
g_{\na,\mathbb{L}}=g_{\na}\circ\pi
follows from the fact that
g_{n}(\pi(x))=g_{n,\mathbb{L}}(x)
, as follows from the definitions. ∎

3.4.
The invariant measure

We now turn to the invariant measure
\mu_{\na}
. By default we work in the unit ball in

\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
, but
for reasons that will appear necessary in Section
8
, we have to deal
at the same time with
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
.

Working in
B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
first,
recall that to any subharmonic function
g
on
B^{\an}(0,1)
one can associate
a positive measure
\Delta g
such that the identity

\Delta\log_{P}=\sum_{P(\phi)=0}\delta_{\phi}

holds for any
P\in\mathbb{L}[w]
, where as before we denote
\log_{P}:x\mapsto\log{\left|{P}\right|}_{x}
, and the roots are counted with multiplicity. Conversely, any probability measure
\mu
with compact support in
B^{\an}(0,1)
is of the form
\Delta g
, where
g
is subharmonic and
g(x)=\log{\left|{x}\right|}
in some neighborhood of

\zeta_{g}
: indeed we may take
g_{\mu}(x)=\int\log\langle x,y\rangle\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(y)
such that
g_{\mu}(\zeta_{g})=0
.
Here
\langle x,y\rangle=|x\wedge y|
where
x\wedge y
is the unique supremum of the segment
[x,y]
, see
the discussion in
[
Ben19
, §13.3]
, or
[
BR10
, Prop. 8.66]
. Observe that if
\mu_{n}\to\mu
weakly, then

g_{\mu_{n}}\to g_{\mu}
pointwise outside the set of Type 1 points.

Now, descending to
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
we can also associate
a positive measure
\Delta g
to any subharmonic function
g
on
B^{\an}(0,1)
, which
satisfies the fundamental identity

(21)

\pi_{*}\Delta(g\circ\pi)=\Delta g.

For instance, if
P\in k[[{z}]][w]
, we now have

(22)

\Delta\log_{P}=\sum_{P(\phi)=0}\pi_{*}\delta_{\phi},

where the roots
\phi\in k\langle\!\langle{z}\rangle\!\rangle
of
P
are counted with multiplicity.

In both situations, for any such positive measure
\mu=\Delta g
,
we put
f_{\diamond}^{*}\mu=\Delta(g\circ f_{\diamond})
, which is a positive measure
by Lemma
3.7
. The operator
f^{*}_{\diamond}
is continuous for the weak convergence of measures, and
Formula (
19
) entails that if
\mu
is a probability measure having compact support in
B^{\an}(0,1)
,
then
f_{\diamond}^{*}\mu
is a positive measure with compact support in the open unit ball and has mass
c/d
.

By duality, we infer the existence of a push-forward operator on the space of
continuous function with compact support on
B^{\an}(0,1)

so that

(23)

\sup{\left|{(f_{\diamond})_{*}h}\right|}\leq\frac{c}{d}\sup|h|\text{ , and }\int h\,{\mathrm{d}}(f^{*}_{\diamond}\mu)=\int\left((f_{\diamond})_{*}h\right){\mathrm{d}}\mu~,

for any continuous
h
. Namely,
(f_{\diamond})_{*}h(x)=\left\langle f_{\diamond}^{*}\delta_{x},h\right\rangle
.

Remark 3.8
.

The push-forward operator can be obtained explicitely as follows.
Over
\mathbb{L}
recall that
f_{\diamond}=\hat{f}\circ\tau

where
\hat{f}
is a polynomial map of degree
c
defined over
\mathbb{L}
, and

\tau(\phi(z))=\phi(z^{1/d})
.
Then we have

(f_{\diamond})_{*}h(x)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{f_{\diamond}(y)=x}e(y)h(y)

where
e(y)\in\{1,\cdots,c\}
is the local degree of
\hat{f}
at

\tau(y)
as defined in
[
FRL10
,
Jon15
,
Ben19
]
.
A similar description can be obtained over
k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)
.

Lemma 3.9
.

For any compactly supported measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
(resp.

\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
), we have

\displaystyle(f_{\diamond})_{*}(f_{\diamond}^{*}\mu)=\frac{c}{d}\mu
.

Proof.

By density and linearity, we only need to check the formula for Dirac masses at type 1 points, and may work over
\mathbb{L}
.
Then
\mu=\Delta\log|w-\phi|
for some
\phi\in\mathbb{L}
, and

f_{\diamond}^{*}\mu=\Delta\frac{1}{d}\log|(w-\phi)\circ f|
whose support equals
f_{\diamond}^{-1}\{\phi\}

so that
(f_{\diamond})_{*}(f_{\diamond}^{*}\mu)=C\mu

for some positive constant
C
.
Computing the mass on both sides leads to
C=c/d

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.10
.

Keeping the assumptions and notation
of Theorem
3.6
, define
\mu_{\na}:=\Delta g_{\na}
.
This is a
f_{\diamond}
-invariant probability measure
satisfying

We still denote by

We denote by

As usual, we denote by

Our main result can be stated as follows. Here we work in
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

be a polynomial map of the form (

, and recall that

(1)

If

Using Theorem

,
we can now complete the proof of a theorem from the previous section.

.

Suppose that

Since

4.1.
The Jacobian formula (II)

It is convenient to set
A(x):=1-\log\mathrm{diam}(x)
for any non rigid point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
, and work systematically over
\mathbb{L}
.
With this notation, (
15
) is equivalent to the formula:

(27)

A(f_{\diamond}(x))=\frac{1}{d}\left(A(x)+g_{\jac(f)}(x)\right)~,

where
g_{\jac(f)}(x):=-\log{\left|{\jac(f)(x)}\right|}
. Indeed,
\jac(f)=dz^{d-1}\jac_{w}(f)
so

\log{\left|{\jac(f)}\right|}=-(d-1)+\log{\left|{\jac_{w}(f)}\right|}

It can be checked that the

is locally constant outside the convex hull of
\mathcal{C}\cup\{\zeta_{g}\}

which we denote by
\mathcal{T}
, and call the critical tree.

Pick any rigid point
\phi
in the unit ball, and, for any positive real number

consider the point
\zeta(\phi,e^{-t})
.
Then we have

g_{\jac(f)}(\zeta(\phi,e^{-t}))=\sum_{i}-\max(\log|\phi-c_{i}|,-t)+(d-1)~.

Observing that
A(\zeta(\phi,e^{-t}))=1+t
, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\phi\in B(0,1)\subset\mathbb{L}
be any rigid point. For any
\tau\in[1,+\infty]
denote by

the unique point
in the segment
[\phi,\zeta_{g}]
such that
A(x_{\tau})=\tau
(so that
\phi=x_{\infty}
and
\zeta_{g}=x_{1}
).

The function

is a concave piecewise affine function with integer slope.
More precisely, if
x=x_{\tau_{0}}
, the left derivative

J(x):=\left.\frac{dg_{\jac(f)}(x_{\tau})}{d\tau}\right|_{\tau=\tau_{0}}

is an integer, equal to the number of critical curves

such that

.

We call

at

.
Notice that the notation

is consistent with the one used in (

If the critical slope is constant on a segment

with

,
then the function

is affine on
[x_{0},x_{1}]
with respect to the parametrization by

.
The following consequence of this discussion, together with (

), will be repeatedly used.

Corollary 4.4
.

If the critical slope is constant equal to
J
on an interval
(x_{0},x_{1})
with

\frac{A(f_{\diamond}(x_{1}))-A(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}{A(x_{1})-A(x_{0})}=\frac{J+1}{d}~.

4.3.
Estimates on the multiplicity

In a dynamical context, the key estimate on multiplicities is given by the following result.

Proposition 4.7
.

Pick
x_{1}\leq x_{0}\in B^{\an}(0,1)
, and suppose that the critical slope is constant equal to
J
on the
interval
(x_{1},x_{0})
. Set
\nu=q\big(\tfrac{d}{J+1}\big)

Then for any type
2

b(x)~\big|~\operatorname{lcm}\Big\{b(x_{0}),\nu b(f_{\diamond}(x)),\nu b(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))\Big\}

The proof relies on the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
x_{1}\leq x_{0}\in B^{\an}(0,1)
be such that the critical slope is constant equal to
J
on the interval

For any point

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x))-\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}{\alpha(x)-\alpha(x_{0})}=\frac{\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x))-\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}{A(f_{\diamond}(x))-A(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}\times\frac{A(f_{\diamond}(x))-A(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}{A(x)-A(x_{0})}\times\frac{A(x)-A(x_{0})}{\alpha(x)-\alpha(x_{0})}

By assumption,

\frac{\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x))-\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}{A(f_{\diamond}(x))-A(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}

is constant equal to the inverse of the multiplicity on
[f_{\diamond}(x_{1}),f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))

(see Proposition
1.2
).
Since
x_{0}
(hence the whole segment

) is outside the critical tree

4.4
we have

(28)

\frac{A(f_{\diamond}(x))-A(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}{A(x)-A(x_{0})}=\frac{J+1}{d}~.

By Corollary

is convex in
\alpha
hence the function

x\mapsto\frac{A(x)-A(x_{0})}{\alpha(x)-\alpha(x_{0})}

is non-decreasing. On the other hand the function

x\mapsto\frac{\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x))-\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}{\alpha(x)-\alpha(x_{0})}

is non-increasing, because we can write

\frac{\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x))-\alpha(f_{\diamond}(x_{0}))}{\alpha(x)-\alpha(x_{0})}=\frac{1}{\deg_{w}(P)}\frac{-\log|P\circ f(x)|+\log|P\circ f(x_{0})|}{\alpha(x)-\alpha(x_{0})},

for every

is concave in

are constant. Since the multiplicity on

is equal to the latter, we conclude that it is

∎

Proof of Proposition

.

Since

is strictly order preserving (see Equation (

))
and continuous, the segment

is mapped bijectively onto

Decompose the latter segment

so that the multiplicity is constant on

Denote by

\text{for every }y\leq x,\ q(A(y))~|~\operatorname{lcm}\{q(A(x)),\nu q(A(f_{\diamond}(x))),\nu q(A(f_{\diamond}(y)))\}\text{,}

\displaystyle b(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{lcm}\{m(x_{1}),q(A(x_{1}))\}

5.
Points of infinite multiplicity

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that

is not reduced to a singleton, and that there exists a periodic critical point

Under which dynamical circumstances can we ensure that

6.
Examples

In this section, we give explicit examples of skew products with uniformly bounded multiplicity on
\mathcal{K}
, as well as examples where there are both rigid points with unbounded multiplicity, and points with infinite multiplicity.

6.1.
Conjugacy to a map of the form (
2.1
)

The class of maps that we have studied so far arises quite naturally, as our next result shows.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(k,|\cdot|)
be any algebraically closed complete metrized field of characteristic
0
,
and pick two integers
d>c\geq 2
.
Suppose that
f\colon(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{k},0)\to(\mathbb{A}^{2}_{k},0)
is a finite analytic germ such that:

(1)

the curve
\{z=0\}
is (locally) totally invariant and
f^{*}[z=0]=d[z=0]
as divisors;

(2)

the restriction of
f
to
\{z=0\}
has a super-attracting point at
0
of order
c
.

Then
f
is formally conjugated to a (formal) map of the form

(32)

(z,w)\mapsto\Biggl(z^{d},w^{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}(z)w^{j}\Biggl)

where the
h_{j}
are power series vanishing at
0
.

Remark 6.2
.

The previous result applies to any polynomial map
f
of the complex affine plane
\mathbb{A}^{2}_{k}

that extends to a regular endomorphism to
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{k}
of degree
d\geq 2
,
and has a super-attracting fixed point on the line at infinity that is not totally invariant.

Remark 6.3
.

When the formal normal form is analytic, then one can argue that the conjugacy is indeed analytic.
Denote by
\varphi
the conjugacy between
f
and its normal form.
Up to a polynomial change of coordinates, we may assume that
f
and its normal form given by (
32
) coincide up to high order
so that
\varphi
is tangent to the identity up to any given order, say
\gg c
.
Up to a blow-up of the form
\pi(z,w)=(zw^{n},w)
with
n\geq c+1
, the map
f
lifts to a rigid germ of class
6
in the sense of
[
Fav00
]
.
Then
\varphi
lifts through
\pi
and conjugates
(zw^{n},w)
to the lift of the normal form (which is analytic). It implies the lift of
\varphi
to be analytic
which forces
\varphi
to be analytic.

The latter remark raises the following problem.

Question 6.4
.

Is it possible to find a holomorphic map

f(z,w)=\left(z^{d},w^{c}+zh(z,w)\right)

with
d>c\geq 2
and
h(0)=0

which is not analytically conjugated to a map of the form (
32
)?

Remark 6.5
.

Lemma
6.6
below shows that, under the assumptions of Theorem
6.1
,
we can at least make sure that the first component of
f
is
z^{d}
after a holomorphic change of coordinates.

The proof of the theorem proceeds in several steps.
First observe that by the Böttcher theorem, we can assume that the restriction of
f
to
\{z=0\}

is
w\mapsto w^{c}
, so that in suitable coordinates
any map satisfying the assumptions of the theorem can be written in the form

(33)

f(z,w)=\Big(z^{d}(1+\varepsilon(z,w)),w^{c}+zh(z,w)\Big),

where
\varepsilon
and

are two germs of analytic functions at
0
vanishing at that point.

Lemma 6.6
.

There exists a unique analytic germ
\Phi(z,w)=(z(1+\varphi(z,w)),w)
,
with
\varphi(0)=0
,
such that
\Phi\circ f\circ\Phi^{-1}(z,w)=(z^{d},\cdots)
.

Remark 6.7
.

Note that the assumption
c<d
is not used in this lemma.

Proof.

This is a consequence of
[
Fav00
]
, see also
[
Rug13
]
.
We include a proof here for convenience.
The conjugacy equation states:

z\circ\Phi\circ f=z^{d}(1+\varepsilon)(1+\varphi\circ f)=z^{d}(1+\varphi)^{d}.

Since
k
has characteristic
0
,
(1+z)^{1/\ell}
is well-defined and analytic in a neighborhood of
0
for any
\ell\geq 1
.
A solution of the above equation is then given by

1+\varphi=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1+\varepsilon\circ f^{n-1})^{d^{-n}}.

This expression converges as long as
\sum_{n\geq 1}d^{-n}{\left|{\varepsilon\circ f^{n-1}(z,w)}\right|}<+\infty
,
a property that holds in a neighborhood of the origin, thanks to the superattracting behavior of
f^{n}
.
More explicitly, for
\left\|(z,w)\right\|\ll 1
, we have
\left\|f^{n}(z,w)\right\|\leq\lambda^{n}
for some
\lambda<1
, while
{\left|{\varepsilon(z,w)}\right|}\leq M{\left|{(z,w)}\right|}
for some
M\gg 0
,
so
\left\|\varepsilon\circ f^{n}\right\|=O(\lambda^{n})
and we are done.

The uniqueness follows from the fact that since
f
is super-attracting,

(1+\varphi\circ f)=(1+\varphi)^{d}
implies
\varphi\equiv 0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

By the previous lemma we may assume that

f(z,w)=\Big(z^{d},w^{c}+\sum_{n\geq 1}z^{n}g_{n}(w)\Big),

where
g_{n}(0)=0
. Write
g_{n}=g_{n}^{-}+g_{n}^{+}

where
g_{n}^{-}
is a polynomial of degree at most
c-1
and

\mathrm{ord}_{w}(g_{n}^{+})\geq c
.

We will prove by induction on
m
that
f
can be analytically conjugated
so that
g_{1}^{+}=\cdots=g_{m-1}^{+}=0
. Indeed,
suppose that this condition is satisfied,
and for any analytic power series
\varphi
vanishing at
0
, let us
consider the analytic map:

\Phi(z,w)=(z,w+z^{m}\varphi(w))~.

We define
\tilde{f}
by
\tilde{f}=\Phi\circ{f}\circ\Phi^{-1}
, or

\Phi\circ{f}=\tilde{f}\circ\Phi
, and observe that
\tilde{f}
is expressed as

\tilde{f}(z,w)=\Big(z^{d},w^{c}+\sum_{n\geq 1}z^{n}\tilde{g}_{n}(w)\Big)
,
where the
\tilde{g}_{n}
are analytic and vanish at
0
.

By the inductive hypothesis, and using

we get

\displaystyle w\circ\Phi\circ f(z,w)

\displaystyle=w^{c}+\sum_{n\geq 1}z^{n}g_{n}(w)+z^{md}\varphi\left(w^{c}+\sum_{n\geq 1}z^{n}g_{n}(w)\right)

\displaystyle=w^{c}+\sum_{n\leq m}z^{n}g_{n}^{-}(w)+z^{m}g_{m}^{+}(w)~~\operatorname{mod}(z^{m+1})

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle w\circ\tilde{f}\circ\Phi(z,w)

\displaystyle=(w+z^{m}\varphi(w))^{c}+\sum_{n\geq 1}z^{n}\tilde{g}_{n}(w+z^{m}\varphi(w))

\displaystyle=w^{c}+cw^{c-1}z^{m}\varphi(w)+\sum_{n\leq m}z^{n}\tilde{g}_{n}(w)~~\operatorname{mod}(z^{m+1})

Identifying term by term, we infer that
\tilde{g}_{n}(w)=g_{n}^{-}(w)
for all

, and setting

\varphi(w):=\frac{g^{+}_{m}(w)}{cw^{c-1}}

we get
\tilde{g}_{m}=g_{m}^{-}
, thereby completing the induction step.

To conclude we observe that for any sequence of analytic maps
\varphi_{m}(w)
vanishing at
0

the sequence of analytic diffeomorphisms

\hat{\Phi}_{m}(z,w):=(z,w+z^{m}\varphi_{m}(w))\circ\cdots\circ(z,w+z^{2}\varphi_{2}(w))\circ(z,w+z\varphi_{1}(w))

satisfies

, hence
formally converges.
∎

6.2.2.
An example with unbounded multiplicity in

Consider now the map

7.
Dynamics in the complex basin of attraction

In this section, we suppose that

(
1
)

f(z,w)=\left(z^{d},w^{c}+zh(z,w)\right)

where

is a holomorphic map defined in

such that

.
Under this assumption, the point
(0,0)
is fixed and superattracting and we denote by

We write
{\left|{p}\right|}=|(z,w)|=\max\{|z|,|w|\}

Recall that the contraction rate

(34)

\log c_{\infty}(p)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\left|{\log{\left|{f^{n}(p)}\right|}}\right|},

when the limit exists.
The following is a more precise version of
Theorem
A
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let

be of the form (
7
) with

.
Then the sequence of psh functions

converges
in

to a psh function

such that

is continuous, and
satisfies

The positive closed
(1,1)
current
{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}^{c}g

is supported on

It gives no mass to curves unless

is conjugated to a product map, in which case

is concentrated on a smooth

{\left\{z=0\right\}}
.

Finally, the contraction rate exists at every

, and

if

, and

if

.

Remark 7.2
.

Note that the existence of the limit

follows from the general

[

, Thm B]
.
However, for a general superattracting germ,

may not be continuous, and the set
of possible attraction rates

[

]
.

Corollary 7.3
.

If

is of the form

P(z,w)=P_{z}(w)=w^{k}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{j}(z)w^{j},

with
\alpha_{j}(0)=0
for every

\frac{1}{kc^{n}}\log P\circ f^{n}
converges in

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

Writing
f^{n}(z,w)=(z_{n},w_{n})
, we have

\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{f^{n}(z,w)}\right|}=\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log\max({\left|{z_{n}}\right|},{\left|{w_{n}}\right|})=\max\left(\frac{d^{n}}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{z}\right|},\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}\right).

Since
\frac{d^{n}}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{z}\right|}
converges uniformly to
-\infty
, it is enough to focus on the convergence of

g_{n}\colon(z,w)\mapsto\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}
.

After appropriate scaling of the coordinates, we may assume that
r<1/2
and
\left\|h\right\|<1/2
.
Let
\Omega_{0}:={\left\{(z,w),\ {\left|{z}\right|}<{\left|{w}\right|}^{c}\right\}}
. We claim that

f(\Omega_{0})\subset\Omega_{0}
. Indeed, for
p=(z,w)\in\Omega_{0}
we have

{\left|{w_{1}}\right|}={\left|{w^{c}+zh(z,w)}\right|}\geq{\left|{w}\right|}^{c}-\left\|h\right\|\cdot{\left|{z}\right|}\geq\frac{1}{2}{\left|{w}\right|}^{c}

hence

{\left|{z_{1}}\right|}={\left|{z}\right|}^{d}<{\left|{w}\right|}^{cd}\leq{\left|{w}\right|}^{c(d-c)}2^{c}{\left|{w_{1}}\right|}^{c}<{\left|{w_{1}}\right|}^{c}.

Now, for
n\geq 1
, since
f^{n}(p)\in\Omega_{0}
, we have that

{\left|{f^{n}(p)}\right|}\sim{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}
and

w_{n+1}=w_{n}^{c}+z_{n}h(z_{n},w_{n})=w_{n}^{c}\left(1+\frac{z_{n}}{w_{n}^{c}}h(z_{n},w_{n})\right).

By definition of
\Omega_{0}
we have that
{\left|{{z_{n}}{w_{n}^{-c}}h(z_{n},w_{n})}\right|}\leq 1/2
,
hence

(35)

\frac{1}{c^{n+1}}\log{\left|{w_{n+1}}\right|}-\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}=\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{1+\frac{z_{n}}{w_{n}^{c}}h(z_{n},w_{n})}\right|}\leq\frac{\log 2}{c^{n}}.

Recall that
g_{n}(z,w)=\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}
.
It follows from the previous inequality that the sequence

(g_{n})
converges uniformly in
\Omega_{0}
to a pluriharmonic function
g

such that
g(p)=\log{\left|{p}\right|}+O(1)
at the origin.
Furthermore, since in
\Omega_{0}

f^{n}(p)\sim w_{n}
,
\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{f^{n}}\right|}
converges to

as well, so
g\circ f=cg
.

Let now
\Omega^{\prime}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}f^{-n}(\Omega_{0})\subset\Omega
and
\mathcal{W}=\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}
.
Then
g
extends by iteration to a pluriharmonic function in
\Omega^{\prime}
.
If
p=(z,w)\in\mathcal{W}
then for every
n\geq 0
we have
{\left|{z_{n}}\right|}\geq{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}^{c}
, and since
z_{n}=z^{d^{n}}
we infer that
c_{\infty}(p)=d
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{f^{n}(p)}\right|}=-\infty
.
Put
g(p)=-\infty
for
p\in\mathcal{W}
.

Let us show that
g(p)\to-\infty
when
p\in\Omega
tends to
\mathcal{W}
.
To establish this, for
N\geq 1
, let
\Omega_{N}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{N}f^{-n}(\Omega_{0})
, which is an increasing sequence of open sets, and fix
p\in\Omega_{N}\setminus\Omega_{N-1}
. For
0\leq j\leq N-1
we have
f^{j}(p)\notin\Omega_{0}
hence

{\left|{f^{N-1}(p)}\right|}\leq\max({{\left|{z_{N-1}}\right|},{\left|{w_{N-1}}\right|}})\leq{\left|{z_{N-1}}\right|}^{1/c}\leq{\left|{p}\right|}^{d^{N-1}/c}.

Being

superattracting, it follows that
{\left|{f^{N}(p)}\right|}\leq{\left|{p}\right|}^{d^{N-1}/c}
as well. Now, since
f^{N}(p)\in\Omega_{0}
,
when

we have
{\left|{f^{n}(p)}\right|}\sim|w_{n}|
, and

(36)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}&=\frac{1}{c^{N}}\log{\left|{w_{N}}\right|}+\sum_{q=N}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{c^{q+1}}\log{\left|{w_{q+1}}\right|}-\frac{1}{c^{q}}\log{\left|{w_{q}}\right|}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{c^{N}}\log{\left|{f^{N}(p)}\right|}+\sum_{q=N}^{n-1}\frac{\log 2}{c^{q}}\leq\frac{1}{dc}\frac{d^{N}}{c^{N}}\log{\left|{p}\right|}+\frac{c}{c-1}\log 2\text{,}\end{split}

where in the inequalities we use the estimate on

and (

for positive constants

and

, and the result follows by letting

.

We claim that
(g_{n})
converges to

pointwise, and even locally

, in the sense that
{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}^{1/c^{n}}

converges locally uniformly to

. Therefore, the convergence

is psh,

, and the remaining properties.
We already know that the sequence converges

to a continuous pluriharmonic function

and pointwise to

on

.
Fix a small

and a vertical line

depends only on

and

is a subset of the form

Therefore, by the Maximum Principle,

Suppose that
\mathcal{W}
is an analytic curve

. Then

is a current of integration, and

, it follows that

is totally invariant.
Let
{\left\{C_{i}\right\}}
be the set of branches of

, and denote

.
Then the finite set

to be reduced to a singleton by the convergence (

.
Changing coordinates, we may suppose that

. It then follows from Lemma

(with coordinates reversed, see Remark

)
that

is analytically conjugated to a product map.

Finally, suppose that
T
carries positive mass on an analytic curve. In this case we claim that
\mathcal{W}
is a subvariety, in which case the previous paragraph applies. Indeed write the Siu decomposition
T=\sum_{W\in\mathcal{A}}\alpha_{W}[W]+T_{\mathrm{diffuse}}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is an at most countable collection of analytic subvarieties and

gives no mass to curves.
Since

preserves both the analytic and diffuse part, the current

Suppose by contradiction that

The invariance relation then yields

Proof of Corollary

.

Put

\displaystyle h_{n}(z,w)

8.
Structure of the invariant current

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
D
(see also Theorem
8.2
below)
which describes the structure of the invariant current as an average
of integration currents over curves in
\mathcal{K}
.
We first need to
explain how a map of the form (

B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
.

8.1.
Action of a formal conjugacy on the Berkovich open unit ball

If
K
is any complete non-Archimedean field,
any power series
\phi=\sum_{n\geq 0} a_{n}T^{n}
such that

|a_{0}|<1
and
|a_{i}|\leq 1
for all
i\geq 1
induces a self map of the (classical)
unit ball
B(0,1)\subset K
.
If
K
is algebraically closed, it follows from Hensel’s lemma that
\phi
is invertible
iff
|a_{1}|=1
and
|a_{i}|<1
for all
i\neq 1
(see e.g.
[
Ben19
, §3.4]
).
This action extends to the Berkovich unit ball
B^{\an}(0,1)
(see
[
BR10
]
for a discussion of
\overline{B}^{\an}(0,1)
in these terms, which is readily adapted to this case
by using
the fact that
B^{\an}(0,1)=\bigcup_{r<1}\overline{B}^{\an}(0,r)
). In particular if
|a_{1}|=1
and
|a_{i}|<1
for
i>1
, then
\phi
induces an analytic isomorphism of

B^{\an}(0,1)
(whenever
K
is algebraically closed or not).

Back to
K=k\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)
, recall that a germ of irreducible formal curve
C
containing
0

and not included in
\{z=0\}
defines a
a type 1 point
x_{C}\in B^{\an}(0,1)
by setting

\displaystyle-\log|P(x_{C})|=\frac{C\cdot(P=0)}{m(C)}
for all
P\in\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)[w]
, where we recall that
m(C)=C\cdot(z=0)
.
The map
C\mapsto x_{C}
is a bijection between germs of curves as above and
type 1 points in
B^{\an}(0,1)
.

From now on we assume that
f
is a holomorphic map in
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)

of the form (
7
) with
2\leq c<d
.
It follows from Theorem
6.1
that
f
is conjugated by a
formal
map
of the form
\Phi(z,w)=\displaystyle\Big(z,w+\sum_{n\geq 1}z^{n}\varphi_{n}(w)\Big)
with
\varphi_{n}\in\mathbb{C}[[{w}]]

to

\tilde{f}(z,w):=\biggl(z^{d},w^{c}+\sum_{j=0}^{c-1}h_{j}(z)w^{j}\biggl)

so that
f=\Phi\circ\tilde{f}\circ\Phi^{-1}
.
From the above discussion,
the map
\Phi
induces an analytic isomorphism of the Berkovich open unit ball

B^{\an}(0,1)\subset\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{C}\left(\!\left({z}\right)\!\right)}
(resp.
\mathbb{A}^{1,\an}_{\mathbb{L}}
),
and for the corresponding actions on
B^{\an}(0,1)
, we have

f_{\diamond}=\Phi\circ\tilde{f}_{\diamond}\circ\Phi^{-1}
. Thanks to this conjugacy, all the definitions
and results from
Sections
2
to
5
apply to
f_{\diamond}
.
In particular we can define

\mathcal{K}:=\mathcal{K}(f)
and
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}:=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}(f)
, and the ergodic measure
\mu_{\na}
supported on
\mathcal{K}

(resp.
\mu_{\na,\mathbb{L}}
on
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbb{L}}
),
which are the images of the corresponding objects associated to
\tilde{f}
.

8.2.
The main statement

Let us first present a basic correspondence between the dynamics of

in
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
and that
of

in

, which may be viewed as an introduction to Theorem

.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
C
be any germ of analytic irreducible curve containing

and not included in
\{z=0\}
.

Then
x_{C}\in\mathcal{K}
if and only if

.

Proof.

Fix any parametrization
t\mapsto(t^{q},\phi(t))
where
q=m(C)
and
\phi
is analytic.
Note that
\phi(z^{1/q})
is then a Puiseux series determining
C
.

Suppose first that
x_{C}\notin\mathcal{K}
. Then for any given
\rho<1
, there exists an integer
n

such that

x_{f^{n}(C)}
belongs to the annulus
\{\rho<|x|<1\}\subset B^{\an}(0,1)

Choose any
c^{\prime}<1/c

\rho=e^{-c^{\prime}}
. Recall from the proof of Theorem
7.1
that since

{\left|{h}\right|}<1/2
, and
for any point

in

{\left|{w_{1}}\right|}={\left|{w^{c}+zh(z,w)}\right|}\geq{\left|{w}\right|}^{c}-\left\|h\right\|\cdot{\left|{z}\right|}>\frac{1}{2}{\left|{w}\right|}^{c}

holds, so that in particular
\Omega_{0}\subset\{g>-\infty\}
. Replacing

by its image under

, we may suppose that
\rho<|x_{C}|<1

which means that
\mathrm{ord}_{t}(\phi)<qc^{\prime}
.
Now one can find a constant

such that for any small enough

|\phi(t)|^{c}\geq A|t|^{c\times\mathrm{ord}_{t}(\phi)}\geq A(|t|^{q})^{c\times\mathrm{ord}_{t}(\phi)/q}>(|t|^{q})^{cc^{\prime}}>|t|^{q}~ .

Possibly reducing

, hence

is not included in
\mathcal{W}
.

Conversely suppose that

.
Define a sequence of negative subharmonic functions on a neighborhood of the unit disk by

Suppose by contradiction that

Then by the Hartogs lemma there exists a subsequence

Consider the Lelong number

This contradiction shows that

8.3.
The open ball partition and the coding map

The first step is to construct a sequence of coverings

This sequence differs from the one introduced in the proof of Theorem

We start with any open ball

.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from the definitions. The second one

8.6.
The graph transform

We work with the free models
X
and
X^{\prime}
constructed in the previous subsection.
For each
p^{\prime}_{i}
we fix coordinates
(z^{\prime}_{i},w^{\prime}_{i})
centered at
p^{\prime}_{i}
such that

(z^{\prime}_{i}=0)
is the exceptional divisor.

Pick any
p_{j}
, and let
p^{\prime}_{i}=\theta(p_{j})
.
Since
\theta
is a finite sequence of free blow-ups, then there exists a unique choice
of coordinates
(z_{j},w_{j})
centered at
p_{j}
and such that
(z_{j}=0)
is the exceptional divisor at
p_{j}
, and

(41)

(z^{\prime}_{i},w^{\prime}_{i})=\theta(z_{j},w_{j})=(z_{j},z_{j}^{n_{j}}w_{j}+P_{j}(z_{j}))

where
n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
P_{j}
is a polynomial of degree at most
n_{j}
vanishing at
0
.
Indeed, each free blow-up can be expressed in coordinates as

(z,w)\mapsto(z,zw+w_{0})
, by choosing the exceptional divisor to be
{\left\{z=0\right\}}
, from which (
41
) readily follows by composition.

On the other hand, if
p^{\prime}_{\ell}=F(p_{j})
, then there exists
other coordinates
(\hat{z}_{j},\hat{w}_{j})
centered at
p_{j}
such that
(\hat{z}_{j}=0)

is the exceptional divisor and

(42)

(z^{\prime}_{\ell},w^{\prime}_{\ell})=F(\hat{z}_{j},\hat{w}_{j})=(\hat{z}_{j}^{m_{j}},\hat{w}_{j})

with
m_{j}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Indeed, since
\mathcal{C}(f)\cap\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
, close to
p_{j}
, the critical set of
F
is reduced to
(z_{j}=0)
and
F^{*}(z^{\prime}_{i}=0)

is a multiple of
(z_{j}=0)
. Using these assumptions we get that
in the coordinates
(z_{j},w_{j})
,

F
is of the form
(z_{j}^{m_{j}}(c_{1}+g(z_{j},w_{j})),c_{2}w_{j}+h(z_{j},w_{j}))
, with
c_{1}c_{2}\neq 0
,

g(0,0)=h(0,0)=0
and
\frac{\partial h}{\partial w}(0,0)=0

and we choose

\hat{z}_{j}=z_{j}(c_{1}+g(z_{j},w_{j}))^{1/m_{j}}
and
\hat{w}_{j}=c_{2}w_{j}+h(z_{j},w_{j})
to arrive at the form (
42
).

The graph
\Gamma
can be re-interpreted as follows: the vertex set is in bijection with
\mathcal{B}

hence with the set of free points
p_{j}
in
X
, and an oriented vertex joins
p_{j}
to
p_{\ell}

iff
F(p_{j})=\theta(p_{\ell})
.

Fix some sufficiently small
r>0
, and
consider the space
\mathcal{H}_{r}
of holomorphic functions
z\mapsto h(z)

defined in the open disk of radius
r
, vanishing at 0, and
whose sup norm is bounded by
r
. In other words,
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
is the closed ball
of radius
r
in the Hardy space
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}(0,r))
.

For each vertex
p_{j}
and every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
, we define the
smooth curve
C_{j}(h)
parameterized by
(z_{j}(t),w_{j}(t))=(t,h(t))
in the
bidisk of radius
r
centered at
p_{j}
, in the coordinates
(z_{j},w_{j})
,
which thus intersects
(z_{j}=0)
transversally at
p_{j}
.

For any edge
e=(p_{j}\to p_{\ell})
,
we can define
a graph transform map
\mathcal{L}_{e}
on
\mathcal{H}_{r}

by the property
that
C_{j}(\mathcal{L}_{e}(h))
is the pull-back by
F
of the image by
\theta
of
C_{\ell}(h)
.
For this, let
p^{\prime}_{i}:=\theta(p_{j})
.
Then
\theta(C_{j}(h))
is parametrized by
(z^{\prime}_{i}(t),w^{\prime}_{i}(t))=(t,t^{n_{j}}h(t)+P_{j}(t))
, and
we obtain an equation for
C_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{e}(h))
of the form

\hat{w}_{j}=(\hat{z}_{j}^{m_{j}})^{n_{j}}h(\hat{z}_{j}^{m_{j}})+P_{j}(\hat{z}_{j}^{m_{j}})

To obtain the explicit form of
\mathcal{L}_{e}(h)
in terms of
h
, it is then necessary
to invert
(z_{j},w_{j})
in terms of
(\hat{z}_{j},\hat{w}_{j})
.
The formulas are not particularly convenient, but
we still can prove the following.

Proposition 8.9
.

For any
r>0
small enough,
\mathcal{L}_{e}
maps

\mathcal{H}_{r}
into itself, and it is
1/2
-Lipshitz for the sup norm.
Moreover, for any
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
we have

(43)

\mathrm{ord}_{t}(\mathcal{L}_{e}(h_{1})-\mathcal{L}_{e}(h_{2}))\geq 1+\mathrm{ord}_{t}(h_{1}-h_{2})

Proof.

The map
\mathcal{L}_{e}
is the composition of two maps
\mathcal{L}_{e}=T_{2}\circ T_{1}

where

T_{1}(h)=t^{m_{j}n_{j}}h(t^{m_{j}})+P_{j}(t^{m_{j}}),

and
T_{2}
is defined by the following condition:
the curve parameterized by
(t,T_{2}(\gamma)(t))
in the coordinates

(z_{j},w_{j})
is identical to the one defined by
(\tau,\gamma(\tau))
in the
coordinates

(\hat{z}_{j},\hat{w}_{j})
.
Write
\hat{z}_{j}=z_{j}A_{j}(z_{j},w_{j})
with
A_{j}(0)\neq 0
,
and
w_{j}=B_{j}(\hat{z}_{j},\hat{w}_{j})=b_{j}\hat{w}_{j}+O(\hat{z}_{j},\hat{w}_{j}^{2})
with
b_{j}\neq 0
. Then we have

\tau=tA_{j}(t,h(t))
and
T_{2}(h)(t)=B_{j}(\tau,h(\tau))
,
so that

T_{2}(h)(t)=B_{j}(tA_{j}(t,h(t)),h(tA_{j}(t,h(t))))~.

The map
T_{1}
is easy to analyze, and it is strictly contracting both for the complex and the non-Archimedean
norm because
m_{j}n_{j}\geq 1
. More precisely, on the disk of radius
r>0
, we have that

|T_{1}(h_{1})-T_{1}(h_{2})|_{\infty} \leq r|h_{1}-h_{2}|_{\infty}
, and

\mathrm{ord}_{t}(T_{1}(h_{1})-T_{1}(h_{2}))\geq m_{j}n_{j}+m_{j}\mathrm{ord}_{t}(h_{1}-h_{2}).

On the other hand,
T_{2}
is
C
-Lipschitz with
C
depending on the sup norm of the partial derivative
of
A_{j}
and
B_{j}
(details are left to the reader) so that the first statement follows
when
Cr<1/2
.
Observe also that
\mathrm{ord}_{t}(A_{j}(t,h_{1})-A_{j}(t,h_{2}))\geq\mathrm{ord}_{t}(h_{1}-h_{2})

which implies
\mathrm{ord}_{t}(T_{2}(h_{1})-T_{2}(h_{2}))\geq\mathrm{ord}_{t}(h_{1}-h_{2})
.
Combining this with
\mathrm{ord}_{t}(T_{1}(h_{1})-T_{1}(h_{2}))\geq 1+\mathrm{ord}_{t}(h_{1}-h_{2})
, we get the estimate (
43
), and
the proof is complete.
∎

8.8.
Uniform laminarity of the current
T(f)

To complete the proof of the theorem, we push forward the previous picture by
the base change
\beta_{k}
from §
8.4
, and need to
verify the assertions of the theorem. We resume the notation from §
8.4
.

Since
g(z,w)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{c^{n}}\log{\left|{w_{n}}\right|}
we see that

g(f)\circ\beta_{k}=g(f_{k})
, so

\beta_{k}^{*}{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}^{c}g(f)={\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}^{c}g(f_{k})
, hence

(\beta_{k})_{*}T(f_{k})=(\beta_{k})_{*}\beta_{k}^{*}T(f)=kT(f)
because
\beta_{k}
has topological degree
k
.

From this we see that the current
T(f)
is the image
of a uniformly laminar current outside the origin under
\beta_{k}
. While this still implies that
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{d}^{c}g
is laminar (because
\mathcal{W}
is pluripolar),
to get uniform laminarity we need to argue that the images of the curves
C(x)
do not intersect each other outside the origin also in this case.
Fix an open ball
U
such that
{\left\{z=0\right\}}\cap\overline{U}=\emptyset
. Since
\beta_{k}
is a covering outside
{\left\{z=0\right\}}
, the preimage of
U
is a union of
k
disjoint open sets, so that
T(f)
is actually the sum of
k
uniformly laminar currents
S_{i}
in
U
, and
\mathcal{W}(f)
is the union of the corresponding laminations.

Assume that two of these currents (say
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
) have non-trivially intersecting plaques, then by
[
BLS93
, Lemma 6.4]
some of these intersections are transverse, and by reducing
U
further we may assume that in
U
, the plaques are graphs of definite size over two different directions, and every plaque of
S_{1}
intersects every plaque of
S_{2}
.

We will argue that the fact (from Corollary
7.3
)
that
T(f)
is the limit of pull-backs of smooth curves makes such a local picture impossible.
For this, we first work with
f_{k}
. By construction of the model, there is a neighborhood of the points
p_{j}
in
X
that is disjoint from the (proper transform of) the critical set. By pushing under
\kappa
,
there is a

f_{k}^{-1}
-invariant “neighborhood” (not open at the origin)
\mathcal{N}(f_{k})

of
\mathcal{W}(f_{k})
disjoint from
\mathcal{C}(f_{k})
, and, since
\mathcal{C}(f_{k})=(\beta_{k,\diamond})^{-1}(\mathcal{C}(f))

we get a corresponding neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(f)=\beta_{k}(\mathcal{N}(f_{k}))
for
f
.

From our choice of
r
in §
8.6
,
a curve of the form
C_{j}=C_{j}(h)
passing by some
p_{j}
is disjoint from the critical set. Let

\tilde{\Gamma}=\kappa(C_{j}(h))
and
\Gamma=\beta_{k}(\tilde{\Gamma})
, so that
\Gamma
is contained in
\mathcal{N}(f)
. If necessary, we work
in a smaller neighborhood of 0 so that
\Gamma
is smooth outside 0, and we conclude that all
curves

are smooth outside the origin. Furthermore

is made up of plaques of uniformly bounded geometry (since they descend from

via

), so any transverse intersection between

and

is a lamination, which must have Cantor transverse structure as before, and that

is a uniformly laminar current.
∎

8.9.

It remains to justify the integral representation of

.
To simplify notation we write
\beta=\beta_{k}

Lemma 8.11

Let

Then we have

\beta_{*}\left(\int_{u\in B^{\an}(0,1)}\frac{[C(u)]}{m(u)}d\beta^{*}\mu(u)\right)=k\,\int_{v\in B^{\an}(0,1)}\frac{[C(v)]}{m(v)}d\mu(v)

Since

Recall that by construction we have that

\displaystyle T(f)=\frac{1}{k}\,\beta_{*}T(f_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\,\beta_{*}\left(\int_{u\in B^{\an}(0,1)}[C(u)]d\beta^{*}(\mu_{\na}(f))(u)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{v\in B^{\an}(0,1)}\frac{[C(v)]}{m(v)}d\mu_{\na}(f)(v)

Proof of Lemma (

We can assume that

is a Dirac mass at the point

In terms of currents, we may write

\beta_{*}\beta^{*}[C]=\sum_{i}k_{i}\beta_{*}[C_{i}]=\sum_{i}k_{i}e_{i}[C]=k[C]

Pick any equation of
