Pregnancy and lactation as window to maternal cardiovascular health☆
Authors: Corinne E. Chatham, Egle Bytautiene Prewit, Daniela Crousillat, Alex M. Parker, Adetola F. Louis-Jacques
Published: 2026

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of pregnancy-related death in the United States [1]. Pregnancy and parturition are the ultimate cardiometabolic “stress test,” revealing underlying susceptibility in maternal health [2]. Lactation, however, is proposed to be a metabolic reset for the postpartum person and is a strategy to reduce CVD risk [3], [4], [5]. The American Academy of Pediatrics and American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life as the “gold standard” for infant health [2]. However, the protective role of lactation for maternal CVD health is our focus. This editorial aims to present literature on cardiovascular disease in the perinatal period and convey the significance of lactation for maternal health.

Cardiovascular changes in the perinatal period

Pregnancy is accompanied by significant hemodynamic and metabolic changes. Plasma volume increases by 40% with a 45% rise in cardiac output to adequately perfuse both maternal and fetal tissues with subsequent rapid increase in SVR and preload immediately following delivery due to “autotransfusion” from the contracting uterus [3]. Pregnancy is a lipogenic state, in which the body increases serum triglycerides and LDL to transfer to the growing fetus, which may lead to endothelial damage and atherogenic predisposition [3], [4], [5]. Pregnancy is also a time of relative insulin resistance, increasing the body's ability to shunt glucose to the developing fetus [5], [6]. The need to handle complex physiologic changes places demands on the pregnant person's body. These demands are complicated by cardiometabolic risk factors, which are more prevalent among women of reproductive age of which only 40% demonstrate optimal cardiovascular health pre-pregnancy [2], [3], [7].

Cardiomyopathy

Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) is a major cause of maternal mortality worldwide [3], [8], [9], [10]. Despite high recovery rates, “7-13% of women experience major cardiac events, including need for left ventricle assist device and cardiac transplant, and death” [8]. Zughul et al. reported on two cases of cardiomyopathy in patients with a history of anthracycline use, recognizing radiation exposure and chemotherapy as possible causes of “cardiomyopathy in cancer survivors during pregnancy” [11]. For these patients, guideline-directed medical therapy is the current standard treatment, though appropriate considerations for pregnancy, birth, and lactation must be made [11].

Concerns regarding the safety of subsequent pregnancies and lactation with PPCM have the potential to impact decisions to breastfeed or become pregnant again [3], [9]. The concerns for pregnancy is due to the high risk of recurrence in future pregnancies, including in patients with complete LV recovery (LVEF >50%) [9] Ongoing research is assessing the safety of lactation versus lactation suppression using bromocriptine in PPCM through the REBIRTH trial (NCT05180773) [3]. Polsinelli et al. assessed the association between PPCM and cathepsin-D in a prospective cohort, a protease which cleaves prolactin to create a byproduct which is toxic to cardiac endothelial cells [8]. Cathepsin-D was higher in women with PPCM and was associated with worse symptoms and clinical outcomes. However, Cathepsin-D was not associated with breastfeeding status [8]. Subsequent pregnancies following diagnosis of PPCM remain high risk, and research is ongoing about safety of subsequent pregnancies, appropriate treatment strategies and surveillance, and safety of lactation [3], [8], [9].

Pre-existing cardiovascular disease

Structural and arrhythmogenic CVD

Pre-existing CVD can be a risk factor for the development of adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs). Nemov et al. presents that in 63 women with structural and arrhythmogenic forms of CVD from a cardio-obstetrics program, 1/3 had an APO; in those with an APO, “gestational age at delivery and infant birthweight” were lower [12]. Nemov et al. argues that since “pre-term birth is associated with future CVD morbidity and mortality in women independent of … cause” that optimization of maternal cardiovascular health should begin preconception, as these APOs may be preventable [12].

Pulmonary arterial hypertension

Pregnancy historically has been contraindicated in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), due to rates of maternal mortality up to 56%, neonatal mortality (7–13%), preterm birth (85–100%), and fetal growth restriction (4–33%) [13]. Pregnancy puts mothers with PAH at risk of thrombotic events, heart failure or disease progression [13]. However, Edgehill et al. presents the influence of “multidisciplinary… close, personalized ante- and postnatal management” on pregnancy outcomes in PAH, and in seven pregnancies among six participants they reported a “100% maternal and fetal survival rate” within one year postpartum, though one mother passed 14 months after delivery [13].

Racial disparities in CVD risk

The risk of developing CVD in pregnancy is not uniformly distributed across racial groups, leading to disparities in health outcomes. Even when controlling for socioeconomic factors, Black women have both “earlier onset” and “higher burden of coronary artery disease related mortality” in addition to a “3.2-fold increased risk of dying during pregnancy and to six weeks post-partum compared to White women” [2]. Black postpartum women have an “increased risk for hypertension related morbidity and mortality,” and “more commonly [develop] … severe hypertension, heart failure, stroke or readmission after delivery” [14]. Countouris and Magnani highlighted that patients with hypertensive disorder of pregnancy have a 50% chance of developing hypertension within “10 years of delivery” [14]. Therefore, a transition should be made from recognizing APOs as “isolated obstetric events” to determinants for CVD risk [2].

Lactation and cardiometabolic health

Lactation is a primary intervention to mitigate risks associated with pregnancy and is associated with improvements in cardiovascular health beyond the postpartum period. Lactation is associated with a decreased risk of cardiovascular disease, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular accident, type 2 diabetes and ovarian and breast cancer [2], [3], [5]. Lactation is also associated with lower maternal lipids, blood glucose, systolic blood pressure (BP), and heart rate [2], [3], [4]. De Assis et al. demonstrated a “positive association [between] lactation intensity and cardiometabolic health,” revealing that exclusive breastfeeding leads to an “improved lipid profile and lower BMI [likely through] fat mobilization and increased weight loss” [4]. These positive associations in human studies have been confirmed in mouse models [15], [16]. Though the mechanisms for lactation's association with maternal cardiovascular health is poorly understood, oxytocin is an important mediator, promoting uterine involution, acting as a vasodilator, improving lipid profiles, increasing insulin sensitivity, and reducing inflammation and fasting glucose [2], [3], [17]. Bytautiene Prewit et al. studies to understand the long-term effects of lactation in mouse models demonstrate that normal pregnancy followed by lactation had a protective role in a mouse model [18]. Primigravid mice who lactated had lower systolic BP, diastolic BP and fasting glucose levels and higher systemic oxytocin and GLUT-4 expression in visceral adipose tissue [18]. Though research deepening our understanding of the mechanisms underlying the relationship between lactation and cardiometabolic health is urgently needed, lactation has many positive associations and should be encouraged whenever possible as a strategy for reducing CVD risk.

Disparities in lactation

Sociodemographics

Disparities in lactation are multifactorial, affecting those across sociodemographic groups (i.e. insurance status, education), adverse social determinants of health (i.e. food insecurities), racial or ethnic groups, and various health conditions [3]. Native Hawaiian, Pacific Islander, Alaska Native, Black and Hispanic women have the lowest rates of breastfeeding initiation and duration, which compounds existing health disparities [2]. Lactation rates among Hispanic women are impacted by “nativity and acculturation,” as non-US born and Spanish-speaking women had higher rates of lactation [3].

Chronic disease

Women with HDP, “need for medical interventions [or] postpartum complications” in the perinatal period may face added barriers to lactation duration or exclusivity [3]. In Burgess et al., women with early onset pre-eclampsia's infants were often in the NICU, which impacted lactation initiation and milk supply due to separation of the mothers and infants [3], [19]. Obesity is associated with lower levels of oxytocin and prolactin [3]. Nunez Pellot cited that “patients with chronic diseases, including hypertension and heart disease, have twice the odds of early cessation of exclusive breastfeeding,” and those with more severe congenital heart defects have lower lactation rates [17]. As a result, providers should be educated on medication safety to better support their patients to breastfeed when possible [17], [20].

Next steps

Physician education

Nunez-Pellot et al. provides a framework to understand medication safety for breastfeeding through “general pharmacokinetic principles of drug secretion in lactation... to guide shared decision making” with patients [17]. Very few medications are absolutely contraindicated during lactation and patients should be counseled accordingly to optimize lactation [20]. Borgarelli et al. share an educational curriculum developed at a cardiology fellowship to foster a greater understanding of “sex-specific care;” the three greatest areas of intervention identified through surveys and literature review were “sex differences in CVD,” “cardio-obstetrics,” and “diagnostics and imaging” [1]. Additional training in these areas will equip providers to care for perinatal patients with CVD.

Public health and community intervention

Borgarelli et al. identifies the importance of “public health education and early detection of CVD in underserved populations,” to address health disparities [1]. Most maternal deaths occur in the postpartum period, which makes postpartum follow-up a key intervention to improve maternal health; Countouris and Magnani demonstrated the importance of telehealth to address racial gaps in attendance at a postpartum hypertension clinic [14]. Despite current challenges and disparities in breastfeeding rates, public health interventions, physician knowledge, and patient counseling can enable breastfeeding for a healthier population. As physicians, we can champion the knowledge that pregnancy and lactation are windows to future health to improve cardiometabolic health [21].
