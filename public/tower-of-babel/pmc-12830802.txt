Astragalus polysaccharide alleviates diabetic nephropathy via SIRT1-dependent activation of FOXO3a/BNIP3 pathway to enhance podocyte autophagy
Authors: Mingfei Guo, Yaji Dai, Xingxing Zhuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Astragalus polysaccharide (APS), a major bioactive component of Astragalus membranaceus, has received considerable attention for its potential in treating diabetes and its complications. However, whether its renoprotective effects in diabetic nephropathy (DN) involve autophagy modulation remains poorly understood. Here, Streptozotocin (STZ)-induced DN rats and high glucose (HG)-exposed mouse podocyte clone-5 (MPC5) cells were established as in vivo and in vitro models, respectively. APS effects were evaluated using renal function indicators, renal histopathology, and autophagy markers. Autophagic flux was assessed by MDC staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Furthermore, SIRT1 silencing or overexpression in vitro was used to confirm its role in regulating autophagy and its interaction with the FOXO3a/BNIP3 pathway. APS treatment significantly reduced fasting blood glucose proteinuria, and glomerular hypertrophy in DN rats. Mechanistically, APS upregulated SIRT1 deacetylase activity, promoted FOXO3a nuclear translocation and activated BNIP3-mediated podocyte autophagy. In vitro, APS restored autophagic flux in MPC5 cells, evidenced by increased Beclin 1 expression, elevated LC3 II/LC3 I ratio and reduced p62 accumulation. MDC staining and TEM further confirmed enhanced autophagosome formation in podocytes following APS treatment. These findings demonstrate APS alleviates DN by enhancing podocyte autophagy via the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 axis, thereby protecting renal function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33000-y.

Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is a severe microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and a leading cause of end-stage renal disease worldwide1,2. The pathogenesis of DN is multifactorial, involving hyperglycemia-induced metabolic and hemodynamic alterations, oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis3–5. Chronic hyperglycemia triggers the activation of various signaling pathways, such as the polyol pathway, protein kinase C (PKC) activation, and the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which collectively contribute to glomerular and tubular injury6,7. These pathological changes ultimately lead to proteinuria, glomerulosclerosis, and progressive loss of renal function. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, the management of DN remains challenging, necessitating the exploration of novel therapeutic targets and agents.

Podocytes, highly specialized epithelial cells lining the glomerular basement membrane, play a critical role in maintaining the glomerular filtration barrier8. In DN, podocyte injury and loss are pivotal events contributing to proteinuria and disease progression9. Autophagy, a lysosome-dependent degradation process, is essential for cellular homeostasis and survival, particularly in podocytes under stress conditions10,11. Emerging evidence suggests that impaired podocyte autophagy is a key mechanism underlying DN pathogenesis. Hyperglycemia disrupts autophagic flux in podocytes, leading to the accumulation of damaged organelles and proteins, which exacerbates cellular dysfunction and apoptosis12,13. Therefore, enhancing podocyte autophagy has been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy to mitigate DN progression.

Astragalus polysaccharides (APS), the primary bioactive components extracted from the traditional Chinese herb Astragalus membranaceus, have garnered attention for their renoprotective effects in DN14,15. APS exhibits a wide range of pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties16,17. Recent studies indicate APS ameliorates renal injury in DN by modulating pathways linked to autophagy regulation, such as lncRNA Gm41268/PRLR and Sirt1/FoxO118,19. APS also enhances podocyte autophagy, reducing injury and proteinuria in DN models20. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of APS in DN, particularly through its ability to restore podocyte autophagy and improve glomerular function. This study aims to further elucidate the mechanisms by which APS modulates podocyte autophagy and its therapeutic implications in DN.

Results

APS attenuates renal function injury in DN rats

After 6 weeks of APS intervention, body weight and FBG were assessed. FBG levels were significantly elevated in STZ-induced DN rats compared to normal controls (Fig. 1A). APS treatment dose-dependently attenuated this FBG elevation, though levels remained higher than normal (Fig. 1A). DN rats exhibited progressive body weight loss. APS treatment ameliorated weight loss in a dose-dependent manner, with the high-dose group showing near-stabilized body weight (Fig. 1B). Renal function markers (Scr, BUN, and urinary protein) were significantly increased in DN rats but reduced by APS treatment (Fig. 1C–E). Histological analysis (HE, Masson staining) revealed pronounced renal damage in DN rats, including basement membrane expansion, collagen deposition, and matrix hyperplasia. APS treatment significantly mitigated these pathological changes (Fig. 1F–G). APS enhances SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 axis activity

Compared with HG-induced MPC5 cells, APS significantly upregulated SIRT1, FOXO3a, and BNIP3 mRNA (Fig. 4A–C) and corresponding protein levels (Fig. 4D–G). This indicates APS enhances MPC5 cell autophagy via the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 axis, mitigating DN progression. Consistent with in vitro findings, renal tissues from DN rats showed downregulated SIRT1, FOXO3a, and BNIP3 mRNA versus controls (Fig. 4H–J). APS treatment restored these levels (Fig. Figure 4H–J), with parallel increases in protein expression confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. 4K–N). SIRT1 is necessary and sufficient for inducing autophagy in podocytes

To further investigated the effects of SIRT1-induced podocyte autophagy in MPC5 cells cultured under HG conditions. We examined the effect of SIRT1 lentiviral overexpression (OE-SIRT1) vectors and siRNA (Si-SIRT1) on podocyte autophagy, transfection efficiency validated via RT-qPCR (Fig. S1). As shown in Fig. 5, TEM results significantly reduced autophagosome formation in HG-mediated MPC5 cells versus control group. OE-SIRT1 markedly increased autophagosomes compared to the model group, whereas Si-SIRT1 further diminished autophagic vacuoles (Fig. 5A). Similarly, MDC staining revealed that the model group showed significantly fewer autophagosomes compared with the control group. Notably, OE-SIRT1 treatment significantly increased autophagosome number relative to the model group, while si-SIRT1 treatment further reduced autophagosome count compared to control group (Fig. 5B). Western blotting results showed that the expression of autophagy markers, including Beclin 1 and LC3 II/LC3 I ratio were significantly reduced (Fig. 5C, D), and p62 was obviously elevated in HG-cultured MPC5 cells (Fig. 5E). Meanwhile, Beclin 1 and LC3 II/LC3 I ratio were significantly increased, and p62 expression effectively decreased in OE-SIRT1 group (Fig. 5C–F). Above all, the HG environment enhanced the inhibition of the protein levels of Beclin 1, LC3 II/LC3 I ratio, and the number of autophagosomes, while the opposite trend was observed for p62. Discussion

DN is characterized by cardinal pathological hallmarks including mesangial expansion, glomerular basement membrane thickening, and progressive podocyte injury21,22. Substantial evidence indicate that early podocyte damage serves as a primary causal factor in DN progression23. Therefore, this study aims to explore the mechanism of podocyte loss and provide a basis for developing new therapeutic interventions.

Traditional Chinese medicines have garnered renewed interest for treating diabetic complications due to their proven clinical efficacy. As a bioactive component of Astragalus membranaceus, APS demonstrates significant renoprotective effects in DN24,25. Our study identifies APS as a potent activator of podocyte autophagy via the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 axis, providing a mechanistic basis for its renoprotective effects in DN. This work elucidates the novel role of APS in modulating podocyte autophagy during DN progression.

The combination of high-fat and intraperitoneal injection of STZ is currently a commonly used method for inducing DN, which is well-documented as being similar to its human counterpart26,27. The specific symptoms include elevated blood sugar, increased urinary protein, and elevated blood creatinine28,29. In our research, we found that after APS treatment, the above indicators all have a certain reducing effect, indicating that APS can improve renal function.

LC3, Beclin 1, and p62 serve as key marker proteins reflecting autophagic activity. Beclin 1, a core autophagy regulator essential for autophagosome formation and metabolic homeostasis, plays a significant role in DN pathogenesis30,31. Its activity in podocytes is suppressed under HG conditions, impairing autophagic initiation and flux. p62, an autophagy receptor protein, binds ubiquitinated substrates and links them to LC3 for degradation32. LC3 serves as a marker of autophagosome formation, where an increased LC3 II/LC3 I ratio indicates enhanced autophagic activity33. Critically, LC3 II/LC3 I and Beclin 1 levels correlate positively with autophagic flux, while p62 accumulation correlates negatively, reflecting impaired degradation.

Our results demonstrate that APS treatment significantly counteracts these autophagic deficits. Compared to the DN model group, renal tissues from APS-treated rats exhibited significantly elevated protein levels of LC3 II/LC3 I ratio and Beclin 1, accompanied by a significant reduction in p62 accumulation. This pattern indicates enhanced autophagic activity in renal tissue following APS intervention. Furthermore, in HG-exposed MPC5 podocytes, APS significantly restored autophagic flux, evidenced by increased Beclin 1 expression, an elevated LC3 II/LC3 I ratio, and reduced p62 levels. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that APS promotes autophagy in renal tissues and podocytes, contributing to podocyte protection and alleviation of glomerular filtration barrier damage in DN.

SIRT1 is an NAD+-dependent histone deacetylase, closely related to the occurrence and development of DN, and is a positive regulator of autophagy34. Sirt1 promotes autophagy by deacetylating autophagy related proteins, including autophagy related factors ATG5, ATG7, Beclin 1, LC3B and p6235,36. Current evidence establishes FOXO3a as a downstream target of SIRT1, mediating SIRT1-dependent signaling37. The SIRT1/FoxO3a pathway critically regulates cellular autophagy, influencing the expression of key autophagy proteins like p62 and Beclin 138. Experimental findings support this role: High glucose (HG)-induced podocyte injury in mice coincided with downregulated SIRT1 expression or was exacerbated by SIRT1 knockout, leading to podocyte damage and proteinuria39. Similarly, in DN model rats, renal SIRT1 and FOXO3a protein levels decreased alongside impaired podocyte autophagy and structural damage.

BNIP3, a key regulator of mitophagy, maintains cellular homeostasis by clearing damaged mitochondria and regulating mitochondrial apoptosis40. However, HG directly damages mitochondria and suppresses BNIP3 expression, disrupting mitophagy. Furthermore, HG inhibits SIRT1 activity, promoting acetylation and inactivation of the transcription factor FOXO3a. This inactivation further downregulates BNIP3 expression, exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction, podocyte injury and glomerular filtration barrier impairment.

In addition, our results showed significantly lower relative expression of SIRT1, FOXO3 and BNIP3 proteins in renal tissues of DN model rats compared to normal controls, confirming suppression of the SIRT1/FOXO3a pathway in DN. Conversely, APS treatment significantly increased the expression of SIRT1, FOXO3a and BNIP3 proteins compared to the model group. These findings demonstrate that APS activates the SIRT1/FOXO3a pathway, upregulates BNIP3 expression, promotes autophagy, and protects podocytes in DN.

Conclusions

In summary, APS ameliorates kidney damage and preserves glomerular filtration barrier function in DN rats. This renoprotective mechanism may be related to regulating the SIRT1/FOXO3a/BNIP3 axis to promote podocyte autophagy. These findings not only deepen our understanding of APS’s pharmacological actions but also identify potential therapeutic targets for DN intervention.

Materials and methods

Animal models and therapeutic interventions

Male Sprague Dawley rats, weighing approximately 220 ± 20 g, were acquired from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, China). All experimental procedures received ethical approval from the Animal Experiment Center Committee of Anhui Medical University (No. LLSC20241786). Additionally, this study was conducted and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Following one week acclimation period, 10 rats were randomly designated as control group and fed with basic feed. The remaining animals were administered HG/high-fat diet for 4 weeks. After a 12 h fast, diabetes was induced in experimental animals by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 35 mg/kg dissolved in 0.05 M citrate–phosphate buffer, pH 4.2). Control animals received the buffer vehicle alone. Venous blood samples obtained 72 h post-injection were analyzed for glucose concentration, with FBG ≥ 16.7 mmol/L serving as the diabetes confirmation criterion. Subsequent assessment of renal impairment was conducted through 24 h urinary protein quantification using metabolic cages, with albumin excretion rates greater than 30 mg confirming DN progression.

DN rats were stratified into six experimental groups: DN model group, low-dose APS (APS-L, 200 mg/kg), medium-dose APS (APS-M, 400 mg/kg), high-dose APS (APS-H, 800 mg/kg), reference treatment group (Metformin, 720 mg/kg), and control group. APS was purchased from Shanxi Junhe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (purity ≥ 95%, HQ20240310). Therapeutic agents were delivered via daily intragastric administration for 6 weeks, with control and model groups receiving matched volumes of saline solution. After 6 weeks, the rats were injected intraperitoneally with 2% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg), and blood and kidney tissue samples were collected for further experiment.

Cell culture and APS treatment

Conditionally immortalized mouse podocytes (MPC5) were procured from Shanghai Biological Technology Co., Ltd. enzyme research. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To simulate hyperglycemic conditions, MPC5 cells were incubated with 30 mM glucose for 24, 48, and 72 h. For therapeutic intervention, cells were co-treated with APS at specified concentrations. Gene modulation was achieved using SIRT1-targeting siRNA (Si-SIRT1) and a pCDNA3.1-SIRT1 expression plasmid (OE-SIRT1), synthesized by General Biol Co., Ltd. (Chuzhou, China), with scrambled siRNA (si-NC) and empty vector (OE-NC) serving as controls. Transfections were performed using Lipo8000MT DNA transfection reagent (C0533, Beyotime), with subsequent validation of transfection efficiency via RT-qPCR. The siRNA sequences are listed in Table S1.

Cellular proliferation assessment

The cytotoxic profile and temporal effects of APS were evaluated through cell counting kit-8 (GK100001, GLPBIO) analysis. MPC5 cells were plated in 96-well culture plates at 8,000 cells/well and subjected to serum-free synchronization for 12 h. Experimental groups were exposed to HG conditions (30 mM glucose) combined with APS gradients (10, 20, 50, or 100 mg/L) for 12, 24, or 48 h. Following interventions, CCK-8 reagent diluted in phenol red-free medium was administered to each well and maintained at 37 °C for 2 h. Optical density quantification was performed at 450 nm wavelength using a multimode microplate reader (RT6100, Rayto). Relative cellular viability was computed as: Viability (%) = (ODAPS-ODBlank)/(ODHG-ODBlank) × 100%.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (G3013-100ML, Life technogies) following established protocols. Complementary DNA synthesis was performed with All-in-One First-Strand Synthesis MasterMix (with dsDNase) (EG15133S, iScience) in 20 μL reactions containing 1 μg RNA template. Quantitative PCR amplification was conducted using Taq SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Universal) (EG20117M, iScience) on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (ABI, USA), with reaction conditions optimized as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression normalization was achieved through the comparative threshold cycle method using β-actin as an internal control. Western blot analysis

Cellular and renal tissue samples were prepared using RIPA buffer (BL504A, Biosharp) containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein quantification was performed via BCA protein assay kit (P0010S, Biosharp). Total protein was separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-Beclin 1 (AF5128, Affinity), anti-LC3 (AF4650, Affinity), anti-p62 (AF5384, Affinity), anti-SIRT1 (DF6033, Affinity), anti-FOXO3a (381,451, ZENBIO), anti-BNIP3 (381,756, ZENBIO) and anti-β-actin (TA-09, Zs-BIO). After TBST washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZB-2305, Zs-BIO) for 2 h at room temperature. The protein expression levels were visualized using an enhanced ECL chemiluminescence substrate kit, and protein intensity on the images was quantified using ImageJ software.

Histopathological analysis

Renal tissue samples were immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, and embedded in paraffin blocks using a tissue processor. Then, the samples were sectioned into 5 μm slices and mounted on adhesive-coated slides. Following deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration in graded alcohols, underwent Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson staining.

Biochemical parameter quantification

FBG levels were assessed using a portable glucometer (GA-3, Sinocare) following manufacturer protocols. Serum creatinine (Scr) concentration was determined via an creatinine assay kit (C011-2–1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), while urinary protein excretion was quantified using a coomassie brilliant blue assay (C035-2–1, Nanjing Jiancheng). Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were evaluated with a colorimetric assay kit (E-BC-K183-M, Elabscience).

Ultrastructural analysis

Renal tissue samples and MPC5 cells underwent primary fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C, followed by post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide. Specimens were subjected to ethanol gradient dehydration and embedded in epoxy resin. The samples were sectioned and stained, and subsequently examined using a transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Autophagic vacuole detection

The autophagic flux was monitored using monodansylcadaverine (MDC), a selective fluorescent tracer for autophagic compartments. The MPC5 cells were washed twice after experimental treatments. MDC solution (G0170, Solarbio) was administered to each well and incubated under 37 °C for 30 min. Following twice washes with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20. Fluorescent signals were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) with 355 nm excitation/525 nm emission filters.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were processed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from triplicate independent biological replicates. Intergroup comparisons were conducted through Student’s t-tests for pairwise analyses, while multigroup evaluations employed one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance thresholds were considered as p-value less than 0.05.
