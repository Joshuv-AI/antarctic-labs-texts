Challenges and coping strategies of family caregivers of older adults with dementia in Africa: a systematic review and meta-synthesis
Authors: Gebrezabher Niguse Hailu, Jonas Sami Albuquerque De Oliveira, Wanesca Caroline Pereira, Haftu Berehe Gebru, Yohannes Tesfay Abebe, Desta Siyoum Belay, Gustavo De Oliveira Tavares
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamily caregivers are central to the care of older adults living with dementia, particularly in African contexts where formal care services are scarce. Dementia caregiving is complex and unpredictable, often leading to emotional, physical, and economic strain. Despite these challenges, caregivers employ a range of coping strategies shaped by cultural values, religious beliefs, and family solidarity. Understanding these experiences is essential for developing appropriate support interventions, yet research in African settings remains limited compared to international contexts.AimThe study aimed to explore the challenges faced by family caregivers of older adults with dementia and to identify the coping strategies they adopt.MethodologyA systematic review of qualitative studies was carried out to examine the challenges and coping strategies of family caregivers of older adults with dementia in Africa. Comprehensive searches were conducted across major databases, including PubMed, CINAHL, MEDLINE, SCOPUS, ScienceDirect, African Journals Online (AJOL), PsycINFO, and EBSCOhost. The search initially yielded 3,742 records. After screening and eligibility assessment of 72 full-text articles, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final review. The included studies were conducted between 2010 and 2025. Data were synthesized thematically, and findings were reported narratively to capture recurrent patterns and variations across the studies.ResultsTwo overarching themes with several sub-themes emerged. 1.Challenges of Family Caregiving (emotional and psychological strain, physical demands and lifestyle disruptions, financial and economic burden, limited access to resources and services, and stigma and misconception. 2. Coping Strategies of Caregivers (emotion-focused coping, problem-focused coping, social coping, and resilience and adaptation.)ConclusionFamily caregivers of older adults with dementia in Africa face significant emotional, social, and economic challenges, often experiencing anxiety, exhaustion, and social isolation. Despite these burdens, caregivers demonstrate resilience through cultural values, family support, and adaptive coping strategies. However, the lack of formal support systems exacerbates their vulnerability, highlighting the urgent need for caregiver education, psychosocial interventions, and financial protection mechanisms.Keywords: Family caregivers, Older adults, Dementia caregiving, Africa, Systematic review, Home-based care

Introduction

Dementia encompasses a group of neurodegenerative disorders [1] affecting millions worldwide, with prevalence expected to rise substantially by 2050 [2, 3]. In 2021, an estimated 57 million people were living with dementia, more than 60% of whom were in low- and middle-income countries, with nearly 10 million new cases annually [4]. The Global Burden of Disease report identified 1.62 million dementia-related deaths in 2019, with the steepest projected increases in regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa, where prevalence is expected to grow by 70–90% [5]. Alzheimer’s disease accounts for 60–80% of cases [6–8]. As dementia progresses, affected individuals experience cognitive decline and increasing dependence on others for daily activities [9], making family members the primary caregivers, particularly in resource-limited settings [5–7, 9].

Caregiving involves managing behavioral symptoms, medications, nutrition, hygiene, and emotional support, often with significant personal, social, and economic consequences [10–15]. In Africa, these challenges are intensified by rapid demographic transitions [20–23] and health systems that remain largely oriented toward infectious diseases rather than chronic, degenerative conditions [20, 23–25]. Specialized dementia services, geriatric care, and long-term care facilities are limited [19, 20, 25–28], leaving family caregivers to shoulder most responsibilities with minimal training or support [29, 30].

Caregivers in African contexts also face culturally rooted stigma and misconceptions—such as attributing dementia to witchcraft or curses—which contribute to social isolation and discrimination [34–37]. Economic burdens are common, as many caregivers reduce or stop paid employment to provide care, often without health insurance coverage [29, 32, 33, 38, 39]. Psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and burnout, is widely reported due to high caregiving demands and limited respite services [29–31, 38, 40].

Despite these challenges, caregivers employ diverse coping strategies. Religious and spiritual practices, family cohesion, acceptance, and emotional support are frequently used [29, 30, 32, 33, 41], though these strategies vary across cultural and socioeconomic contexts. However, there is limited systematic synthesis of evidence on how African caregivers manage both the challenges and the coping processes involved.

Although studies from high-income countries demonstrate the benefits of structured caregiver education, respite care, and psychosocial interventions [42, 43], their transferability to African contexts is uncertain due to contextual differences in culture, resources, and health system structures. This highlights a critical gap: the need for region-specific evidence that reflects the lived experiences of African family caregivers.

Accordingly, this review synthesizes qualitative evidence on the challenges and coping strategies of family caregivers of older adults with dementia in Africa, aiming to illuminate the cultural, social, and economic factors shaping caregiving on the continent.

Methodology

Literature and search protocol

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify qualitative studies exploring the challenges and coping strategies of family caregivers of older adults with dementia in African settings. The review adhered to PRISMA guidelines to ensure methodological rigor. Searches were performed across major international and regional databases, including MEDLINE (PubMed), CINAHL, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science, African Index Medicus, and African Journals Online, supplemented by grey literature sources such as ProQuest Dissertations and Theses, WHO IRIS, and Google Scholar.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords related to family caregiving, dementia, coping, and the African context. Full details of the complete search strings, keyword combinations, and database-specific adaptations are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, with disagreements resolved through discussion or a third reviewer. Data were extracted using a standardized form capturing study characteristics and key findings related to caregiving challenges and coping strategies. Methodological quality was assessed using the CASP qualitative checklist. A thematic synthesis approach was used to integrate findings across studies, and the GRADE-CERQual framework was applied to determine confidence in the synthesized evidence.

Search results

The database search initially identified 3,742 records retrieved from eight major electronic databases: PubMed, CINAHL, MEDLINE, SCOPUS, ScienceDirect, African Journals Online (AJOL), PsycINFO, and EBSCOhost. After removing 526 duplicate entries, a total of 3,216 unique articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. At this stage, studies were assessed for their alignment with the review’s objectives, with particular emphasis on caregiving roles, challenges, and coping strategies within African contexts involving older adults with dementia aged 60 years and above.

Following this preliminary screening, 72 full-text articles were considered potentially relevant and subjected to eligibility assessment using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. To be included, studies needed to focus specifically on family or informal caregivers in Africa, with care recipients who were older adults with dementia, and be published in English between 2010 and 2025. Eligible designs included qualitative and mixed studies, while quantitative papers were excluded for this review. Studies focusing on professional caregivers, pediatric populations, or articles in the form of conference abstracts, commentaries, or editorials were also excluded.

Ultimately, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final synthesis. These studies represented diverse African countries and predominantly used qualitative methodologies, including in-depth interviews and focus group discussions. The findings provided rich insights into the caregiving experience, highlighting both the multidimensional challenges faced, such as emotional distress, financial strain, physical exhaustion, and social marginalization. The coping strategies employed, including religious faith, social support networks, and adaptive problem-solving.

The final pool of 15 studies formed the foundation for a thematic synthesis, from which key themes and subthemes were generated to address the research objectives. Article selection and review were conducted systematically in three stages by the first author.

Study characteristics

The review incorporated 15 studies from seven African countries: Ethiopia (n = 2) [32, 33], Nigeria (n = 2) [45, 50], Ghana (n = 2) [9, 49], South Africa (n = 5) [6, 15, 41, 44], Uganda (n = 2) [46, 47], Kenya (n = 1) [48], and Botswana (n = 1) [51]. The largest contribution came from South Africa with five studies, followed by Ethiopia, Nigeria, Ghana, and Uganda with two studies each, and single studies from Kenya and Botswana.

Most studies employed a qualitative design, primarily relying on in-depth interviews and focus group discussions to capture caregiving experiences. Only one study from South Africa used a mixed-methods approach [48].

Sample sizes ranged from 8 to 30 participants, with caregivers predominantly being family members providing informal, unpaid care to older adults diagnosed with dementia or related conditions. Female caregivers made up the majority across studies, although a few included male participants. In three studies [6, 45, 50], all or nearly all participants were women, while others reflected mixed-gender samples but still showed a female predominance.

Caregiver age ranged from 18 years to over 76 years, illustrating the diversity of caregiving roles across younger and older generations. Some caregivers were adult children balancing caregiving with other family duties, while others were older spouses caring for their partners.

Regionally, the Ethiopian studies [32, 33] each recruited 22 caregivers, all aged 18 years and above, caring for relatives with dementia. The Nigerian studies [45, 50] together contributed 30 caregivers, aged 22–76 years, providing care for older adults with dementia. The Ghanaian studies [9, 49] involved 51 caregivers in total, aged between 20 and the mid-70s, all informal family caregivers of older adults with dementia. South Africa contributed the most with five studies [6, 15, 41, 44], with sample sizes between 8 and 30 participants, focusing on family members caring for older adults with dementia in community settings. Uganda’s two studies [46, 47] included 37 caregivers, aged from 28 years and above. The single Kenyan study [48] involved 10 caregivers aged 50–69 years, while the study from Botswana [51] recruited 24 caregivers aged 23–74 years.

Across all included studies, women were more frequently represented than men, and caregivers were generally immediate family members—most often children, spouses, or close relatives—caring for older adults aged 60 years and above (Table 1).

Appraisal of included studies

The methodological quality and rigor of the included studies were appraised prior to synthesis, using established guidelines for qualitative systematic reviews [52]. This evaluation ensured that findings were interpreted within the context of how each study was designed and conducted. All included studies clearly articulated their aims, and the use of qualitative approaches was well aligned with the objective of exploring the experiences of family caregivers of older adults living with dementia in African settings.

Of the 15 included studies, 14 employed qualitative designs [6, 9, 15, 32, 33, 41, 44–47, 49–51], utilizing interviews or focus group discussions to capture caregivers’ lived experiences and coping strategies, while one study adopted a mixed-methods design [48]. The predominance of qualitative research reflects the need to understand the emotional, cultural, and social realities of dementia caregiving across diverse African contexts.

The qualitative studies provided detailed narratives on the burdens of dementia caregiving, including emotional strain, financial challenges, and social isolation, while also highlighting coping mechanisms such as faith, family cohesion, and resilience. The single mixed-methods study complemented these insights by combining narrative data with structured assessments, adding further depth to the understanding of caregiver experiences.

Nonetheless, limitations were observed across the body of evidence. These included small sample sizes, methodological inconsistencies, and a lack of longitudinal perspectives that could demonstrate how caregiving challenges and coping strategies evolve over the trajectory of dementia. Additionally, not all studies fully described caregiver demographics—such as socioeconomic status, caregiving duration, or household context—which limited opportunities for comparison across different African settings.

Despite these shortcomings, the included studies provide strong and contextually rich evidence on the challenges and coping strategies of family caregivers of older adults with dementia. Future research would benefit from larger, more diverse samples, longitudinal designs, and greater methodological rigor, which would strengthen the evidence base and inform the development of culturally relevant interventions to support dementia caregiving in Africa.

Method of data collection

The methods of data collection varied across the included studies, reflecting diverse qualitative approaches to understanding the caregiving experiences of families supporting older adults with dementia. Most of the studies employed semi-structured in-depth interviews as the primary tool, which provided both flexibility and focus in exploring caregivers’ lived experiences [6, 9, 15, 32, 33, 41, 44–47, 51].

One study employed both FGDs and in-depth interviews, thereby enriching the data through complementary methods [15]. In addition, a mixed-methods design was adopted in one study, where qualitative interviews were complemented with quantitative surveys; however, for the purpose of this review, only the qualitative component was considered [48].

Overall, while interviews dominated as the most frequently used technique, the inclusion of FGDs and mixed-methods approaches added methodological diversity, offering richer insights into the multi-faceted challenges and coping strategies of family caregivers for people living with dementia in Africa.

Sampling

The sampling strategies employed in the included studies were largely purposive, with participants selected based on their relevance to the research objectives. In most cases, data collection was guided by the principle of saturation, where recruitment continued until no new themes emerged. The sample sizes and participant characteristics varied, capturing diverse caregiving experiences across different contexts.

For example, one study included 22 family caregivers aged 18 years and above who were responsible for providing care to relatives with dementia [32]. Another study focused on 16 caregivers aged between 30 and 46 years [33]. Similarly, one study involved 31 caregivers aged between 21 and 76 years [41], while another included 22 caregivers over the age of 18 caring for relatives with dementia [45]. One study featured 18 caregivers aged 22 years and above [47], and another involved 6 caregivers aged between 21 and 79 years [48].

In addition, one study focused on 17 caregivers aged 26 to 60 years [49], while another examined 10 caregivers aged 28 to 64 years [50]. One study included 27 caregivers aged 31 years and above [51], and yet another featured 24 caregivers aged between 23 and 74 years [52]. Finally, one study employed a narrative reflection from a family caregiver (son) providing care to an older parent with dementia [44].

These varied sampling strategies enabled a comprehensive exploration of caregiving experiences, reflecting the perspectives of caregivers of different ages, genders, and family roles, thereby enriching understanding of dementia caregiving across African contexts.

Data analysis

In most of the included studies, interview data were transcribed verbatim to capture participants’ responses accurately [6, 15, 24, 32, 33, 41, 44, 45, 47, 51]. However, one study did not use verbatim transcription and instead relied on electronic transcription with field notes, focusing on informal conversations rather than recorded interviews [48].

Data analysis approaches varied across the studies. The majority of studies employed thematic or interpretative methods, often supported by qualitative software. For example, several studies used Atlas.ti 8 for coding and theme development [32, 33, 41, 44, 45, 51], while one study relied on manual analysis guided by the Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) framework [47]. In one study, Braun and Clarke’s five-stage thematic analysis was applied, with repeated reviews of transcripts to identify key topics and categorize findings under themes [49]. Another study used NVivo 12 to support coding, sub-coding, categorization, and theme identification, following an inductive-dominant approach and involving a team of experienced qualitative researchers [50].

To ensure trustworthiness and rigor, several studies adopted systematic strategies [6, 45]. Reflection was used throughout the research process, with researchers keeping journals to document self-reflection and minimize subjective bias [6]. Audit trails were employed to provide detailed accounts of the analytic process, including codebooks and analytical decisions [45]. Peer debriefing with experienced qualitative researchers was undertaken to validate emerging findings [45]. Reflexivity was also emphasized, enabling researchers to manage potential cultural and personal biases [45]. Finally, response validation was used in some cases to ensure that interpretations aligned with participants’ intended meanings [6].

Findings

Thematic synthesis and narrative reporting

Challenges of family caregiving

Emotional and psychological strain

Family caregivers of older adults with dementia across African contexts consistently reported high levels of emotional and psychological distress [6, 15, 41, 47, 51]. The unpredictable and progressive nature of dementia often led to feelings of worry, sadness, anxiety, and helplessness. Caregivers described being overwhelmed by the fluctuating behaviors of their relatives, which generated frustration and fear about the future.

The family caregivers of individuals with dementia experience intense emotional and psychological strain. They often face anxiety, hypervigilance, and constant worry about potential accidents or unpredictable behaviors, which leads to exhaustion. Alongside the caregiving role, the struggle to meet basic needs adds to their distress, frustration, and despair. The cumulative stress of daily responsibilities, emotional strain, and multiple household roles increases their risk of burnout. These challenges highlight the urgent need for psychosocial interventions and culturally sensitive support systems.

It’s anxiety more than anything, and it’s anxiety– to be very honest– it’s the anxiety around being able to control the environment. Ja, that’s my thing. Like when I feel it’s outside of my ability to handle, that’s when I get very anxious” …. Study2

Physical demands and lifestyle disruptions

Family caregivers of older adults with dementia reported that caregiving significantly disrupted their daily lives, personal goals, and physical well-being [3, 46, 47, 51]. Many caregivers described how their role brought about a shift in lifestyle, with responsibilities extending beyond caregiving to household management and family duties. These overlapping obligations often left them feeling overwhelmed and isolated, with little opportunity to engage in social life outside the home [46].

I normally get up at 4 am so that I can prepare breakfast and lunch for my husband, and also, I live with a grandchild who was abandoned here by my daughter. So, I have to prepare her for school as well. I also have a cow that I milk, and that also needs to be fed. Once that is done, I then go in search of some casual work …. Study7

Caregiving was also physically taxing, particularly when lifting or transferring older adults who had limited mobility. The lack of assistive devices meant that caregivers relied on manual effort, which resulted in fatigue and musculoskeletal pain [47].

When I am not at home, he stays indoors all the time, because no one else can carry him. I can’t remember how many times I have carried him for the last 3 years. I feel physically tired and my back is hurting too. The doctor gave me medicine that I am taking now …. Study8

In addition to the physical strain, many caregivers made profound sacrifices in their professional and personal lives. Some decided to leave their jobs, suspend their education, or set aside long-term ambitions in order to provide care. These sacrifices were often framed as necessary acts of responsibility and love, though they came at the cost of caregivers’ own independence and aspirations [6].

Well, I had to leave my job five years ago decide to stay with this woman to understand what is it that she’s going through before I can bring in someone you know who would be our help yes …. Study3

Financial and economic burden

Family caregivers of older adults with dementia frequently described severe financial strain resulting from their caregiving role [6, 41, 45, 51]. Many caregivers reported being unemployed or underemployed, which limited their ability to generate income and intensified their economic challenges. Daily struggles of “hand-to-mouth” living made it difficult to meet the basic needs of both their households and their family members with dementia [6].

What’s difficult is not having the finance to maintain that standard for her and basically leaving it up to people who don’t have those concerns and she’s now not in her full mind, has to sort of just take that, accept that. I mean, my mother was vegan, I think…so it’s hard watching her having to take what I know goes against her principles because of finance. It’s that, essentially …. Study3

Caregivers also emphasized the direct costs of dementia care, including spending on food, medications, clothing, and transportation to health facilities. These expenses were often disproportionate to their income and worsened by the fact that caregiving responsibilities limited opportunities for income-generating activities [32, 41].

…Another thing how it affects me in my life; is economically, I spend a lot. You find that my income is small yet the expenditure is very high. So, you find that sometimes I don’t have the money and yet am supposed to take care of her, buy for her a good dress, clothing, am supposed to give her shoes, am supposed to employ someone to stay with her…. Study4

In addition to household expenses, caregivers highlighted that the cost of medications, medical supplies, and healthcare services quickly accumulated, creating an overwhelming financial burden. Several reported having to take time off work or reduce their working hours to provide proper care, which further reduced household income and intensified economic pressures [32].

…. I found difficult to balance my work responsibilities and the financial costs of caregiving. I had to take time off or reduce my working hours to provide care, leading to a decrease in income. This posed a significant challenge, as I had to juggle between financial stability and ensuring the well-being of my aging parent …. Study11

Limited access to resources and services

Family caregivers highlighted significant gaps in access to essential resources and supportive services, which limited their ability to provide optimal care for older adults. Participants emphasized the importance of access to training programs, reliable health information, and professional support. Training workshops were seen as vital in equipping caregivers with the necessary skills for day-to-day care, while access to reliable and easily understandable information was perceived as crucial for informed decision-making. Furthermore, the availability of health professionals for consultation and periodic check-ups was identified as an unmet need, as it would offer both reassurance and guidance in managing complex health conditions [32].

Having access to information is vital for family caregivers like me. It is often challenging to navigate the complexities of caring for the elderly, especially when it comes to understanding their specific health conditions or finding appropriate resources. Assistance from health professionals is also much needed. While we try our best to care for our elderly relatives, there are often medical needs that go beyond our capabilities. Having health professionals available for consultation or periodic check-ups would be beneficial not only for the elderly but also for us as caregivers, as it would provide reassurance and guidance in providing the best care possible …Study11

In addition to these needs, caregivers described severe difficulties in accessing transportation services, especially in emergency situations. The problem was worsened by the war in Tigray, which led to a reduction in ambulance services and unreliable public transportation. Caregivers reported that taxi drivers often prioritized filling their vehicles before transporting passengers, which was particularly dangerous for elderly relatives requiring urgent medical attention. These challenges not only created delays in seeking care but also added stress, anxiety, and feelings of helplessness among caregivers [32].

Since the devastating war in Tigray, getting transportation service in Mekelle city has become even more difficult. The war has had far-reaching consequences, and one major impact is the lack of reliable transportation. The taxi drivers are hesitant to provide services, and even if they do, they prioritize filling up their taxis first before taking any passengers. This has made it extremely difficult for me to transport my elderly relative to the healthcare facilities when needed …Study11

Stigma and misconceptions

Evidence from a study in Nigeria [45] showed that dementia was widely misunderstood, with symptoms often attributed to normal aging, witchcraft, sorcery, or madness. As one caregiver said, “I call it age; I call it age because I don’t know what to call it.” Another noted, “It is an illness or behavior certain old or middle-aged people experience when they are getting old.”

Caregivers reported conflicting interpretations of dementia within their communities. For instance, one said, “In the village, they will think that somebody is bewitching that person. They don’t know it is a sickness.” Another explained, “Some people are saying somebody is trying to manipulate her. Others are saying it is what she did when she was young that is affecting her.” In many cases, dementia was equated with “madness,” as one caregiver recalled: “They called me asking and saying if it is madness that is happening to my grandma.”

These misconceptions affected the support caregivers received. One participant stated, “It is the reason people are not greeting her. My brother stopped coming home, even stopped giving her money.” Another explained, “My daughters do not help with caregiving because they do not want to be associated with their father’s condition.”

Attempts to seek help from churches or communities were also shaped by these beliefs. One caregiver described, “I sought assistance from my church members, but they only offered to take him to traditional healers to cure his mystical condition.” Others worried about leaving their relatives unsupervised, as one explained: “Locking her up, you don’t know what will happen.”

The stigma often led to family withdrawal and left caregiving responsibilities to only a few individuals. As one participant summarized, “This issue of the dementia patient being stigmatized also comes from the family members. When a family that has about five people, and three of them are not OK with it, you’re left with just two people to deal with what is going on. What five people should have been able to do breaks everybody down, especially the caregivers.”

Coping strategies of caregivers

Emotion-focused coping

Most caregivers relied on religious and spiritual practices as their primary emotion-focused coping strategies. Faith provided them with strength, acceptance, and resilience in managing the emotional challenges of caregiving [6, 9, 15, 32, 41, 46, 47, 49].

It is very difficult. I pray. If I feel, even if my emotions are just down, I just sit down and pray. God, please make understand my mother. Make me, you know because if I cannot, who is going to take care of her? Other people who will not understand her the way I do understand. So, I pray. Yes. …. Study2

Nobody is more powerful than God, and He has told us to ask Him for everything, so I think it is just a matter of time; God Himself will heal her in His own time. When the time comes for her to be healed, she will be healed. If she dies, we know it is also the doing of the Lord. So, I don’t get worked up over the problems I experience and the financial difficulties. I know that in time, everything will work out for my good. …. Study1

Caregivers also expressed acceptance, often grounded in their faith. Some viewed caregiving as a God-given responsibility, which brought them resilience and contentment [15, 32, 41].

So, it’s all about accepting. I accepted and once you go to religion, they will tell you that it is a gift which God gave to me such that maybe I take on caregiving. So, am contented per now, am contented to look after her until when she dies or I die…. Study4

Beyond religion, some caregivers sought emotional relief through short breaks and family visits.

On weekly basis, I visit my husband and children at a nearby village because I do not live with them; I am here with my mother. This is the way I use to reduce the pressure and difficulties I face. I am able to relax and have some time for myself when I go there. …Study10

Problem-focused coping

Caregivers adopted different strategies to manage the increasing demands and challenges of caregiving [15, 49]. These strategies reflected efforts to take concrete steps, either to secure resources, adjust lifestyles, or prepare for future care needs.

One major approach involved borrowing money from neighbors or friends to cover immediate care expenses. This was especially common in rural areas, where financial support systems were scarce. While borrowing provided short-term relief, it often created tension when repayment was difficult due to caregivers limited or nonexistent income.

I borrow money from a lady who lives in the next house when I do not have enough money so that I can take my mother to the herbalist. I go alone but sometimes the herbalist would ask that I bring her and the money would not be enough so I borrow to support that. …. Study10

In addition to borrowing, some caregivers coped by reducing personal expenditures to channel their limited income toward their relatives’ care. Many sacrificed basic needs such as clothing, shoes, or grooming to ensure their family member received adequate support.

Hmmm…my dear, I have not in the last seven months been able to buy common slippers for myself not to talk of going to see the hairdresser. You might not believe me but it is the truth. No one is supporting me so the little that I have I need to manage it so that it can last longer. …. Study10

Beyond financial adjustments, caregivers also faced the progressing needs of care recipients, which demanded constant adaptation. For some, this meant actively providing hands-on care despite limited support, while others began to recognize their limitations and the potential need for professional caregiving.

I’ve been executor of my parents’ estate…it’s just been overwhelming. I’m now at a point where I’m considering stopping working because leaving him by himself for extended periods is probably not a good idea for much longer. As a caregiver, you get tired. You’re juggling a lot of things mentally, physically, emotionally. And when you get tired, it’s very difficult to juggle and be kind and caring for him. So, we get into arguments then. …Study15

Social coping

Social Coping Unpaid family caregivers of persons living with dementia emphasized the importance of family and social networks as key resources that helped them cope with the challenges of caregiving [9, 49]. Strong family bonds ensured that caregiving responsibilities were shared, with members stepping in to assist whenever the primary caregiver was unavailable. This collective approach reduced the burden on individuals and fostered resilience within the family [9].

He is our father so when his relapse starts, any of us comes to help him. Even if we are asleep and another is awake, he that is awake calls for us that Baba’s condition has started. We all come together and help him. When I am away, my siblings are the ones who take care of him. No one speaks to him impolitely or harshly because we know it is a sickness and also that was not how he was at first. …Study1

Beyond family involvement, caregivers also pointed to the supportive role of friends and extended relatives. Small gifts—either cash or in-kind contributions—provided not only practical assistance but also encouragement to persevere despite difficulties [49].

Sometimes, his friends come here and other family members also pay us a visit. Through that, they give me some money since they are aware of the challenges I am facing. At least such good gestures make me feel okay to continue with the care. … Study10

Resilience and adaptation

Resilience and Adaptation Family caregivers of persons living with dementia demonstrated remarkable resilience in coping with the daily challenges of care. One recurring difficulty was ensuring treatment and medication adherence. To overcome this, caregivers often relied on coaxing, persuasion, and pampering strategies to encourage cooperation [9].

Sometimes, she would say she does not like medications made in Ghana so I would have to connive with someone that the medications are imported and say flattery words to her such as the medications will make her look young and pretty. …. Study1

Empathy and perspective-taking also emerged as common strategies. While the behaviors of persons living with dementia could trigger agitation, frustration, or anger, caregivers emphasized the importance of patience and understanding. They reframed such behaviors as consequences of the illness and ageing process, which helped them to respond with compassion.

I always tell her that even we who are young fall ill sometimes and it resolves with time. At your age, all your blood vessels are obstructed; they are not like ours. The way blood circulates freely in me is not the same way in her body so she should pray. She attends CAC [a Christian denomination] so she should continue to pray to God that ‘if it’s time for you to call me [to eternity], please do but if it’s not, give me the strength to continue’. So, when I come to her, all I do is to encourage her …Study1

Caregivers also expressed strong devotion, loyalty, and a sense of duty, believing that no one else could provide proper care. They often saw it as their personal responsibility to safeguard the well-being of their loved ones rather than abandon them [6].

Let me say that if I am in a situation of anger, normally I use to ask myself why this guy is doing that, but after a few minutes I will say to myself that it’s because of his situation that is why he is doing that. So, I take it like that. …Study3

Despite the hardships, many caregivers acknowledged positive personal changes as a result of their experiences. The caregiving role fostered patience, resilience, and deeper understanding of themselves and their circumstances [46].

Yes, there is a positive aspect (the person living with dementia mumbles). Even if this experience has hurt me so much, it has affected me so much. It also changed me in a way. I am very patient nowadays, in the beginning, I used to cry as I washed him, but these days I can even sing… you know…Studdy7

Discussion

Family caregivers of older adults with dementia in Africa consistently reported high levels of emotional and psychological distress [6, 15, 41, 47, 51]. The unpredictable and progressive nature of dementia often led to feelings of worry, sadness, anxiety, and helplessness, with caregivers frequently describing being overwhelmed by fluctuating behaviors and the uncertainty of the future. These findings mirror international evidence showing that caregivers of individuals with dementia, compared with caregivers of other chronic conditions, are at a significantly higher risk of stress, depression, and burnout [53–55]. In Europe and North America, for instance, the emotional impact of behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia, such as aggression, wandering, and agitation, has been strongly linked with caregiver anxiety and fatigue [56, 57]. Studies from Asia similarly highlight that hypervigilance and fear of accidents among caregivers contribute to chronic exhaustion [58]. However, unlike in high-income contexts where psychosocial interventions, counseling, and respite care are often available [59], African caregivers typically lack access to formal support services, which exacerbates their vulnerability to psychological distress.

The physical strain of caregiving was also a recurring theme, with caregivers reporting fatigue, sleep disruption, and musculoskeletal pain due to the manual effort of lifting, bathing, or supervising older adults with dementia [3, 46, 47, 51]. International studies confirm that caregiving has a substantial physical toll, particularly when the care recipient has mobility limitations [58–61]. In Japan and China, caregivers describe similar bodily strain, often compounded by limited use of assistive devices [58, 62]. In high-income countries, however, the burden of physical care is frequently alleviated by technological aids, home modifications, and community-based nursing services [63], resources that are rarely available in African settings. Consequently, African caregivers rely heavily on physical endurance, often at the expense of their own health.

Financial and economic burdens were among the most pronounced challenges in African contexts, as caregiving often led to reduced workforce participation and increased out-of-pocket spending [6, 41, 45, 51]. Caregivers reported borrowing money, cutting essential household expenditures, and experiencing worsening poverty as a result of caregiving responsibilities. This is consistent with global evidence that dementia caregiving is economically costly, with studies in the United States estimating the value of unpaid care in the hundreds of billions annually [64]. European caregivers also report reduced earning capacity and high indirect costs associated with long-term care [65]. However, the financial burden in African contexts is magnified by limited social protection systems, lack of health insurance coverage, and widespread poverty, making caregiving not only a personal responsibility but also a source of financial vulnerability for entire families [6–33, 41, 47].

Another key challenge was the limited access to dementia-specific health resources and services. Caregivers described a lack of information, training, and professional guidance in managing behavioral and psychological symptoms [32]. This is consistent with findings from other low- and middle-income countries in Asia and Latin America, where limited diagnostic facilities and lack of specialist care hinder timely management [66, 67]. In high-income countries, however, structured care pathways, caregiver training programs, and specialized clinics provide families with education, coping strategies, and emotional support [59]. The absence of such systems in Africa leaves caregivers to rely almost exclusively on their own judgment and informal networks.

Stigma and misconceptions surrounding dementia were also prominent, with many communities attributing the condition to witchcraft, curses, or spiritual punishment [45]. This often resulted in social isolation, discrimination, and reluctance of extended families to provide support. Internationally, stigma remains a significant barrier to dementia care, though its manifestations differ by culture. In Asian countries, for instance, dementia is often considered a normal part of aging, which delays diagnosis and care-seeking [34], while in Latin America, misconceptions about dementia contribute to underdiagnosis and social exclusion [68]. However, the strong association with supernatural explanations appears more distinct in African contexts, intensifying the marginalization of both caregivers and persons with dementia.

Despite these challenges, caregivers employed a range of coping strategies. Emotion-focused coping, particularly through faith and spirituality, was a dominant theme, with many caregivers drawing strength and patience from religious practices and beliefs [6, 9, 15, 32, 41, 46, 47, 49]. Caregivers described faith as offering resilience and a sense of divine timing, enabling them to endure the uncertainties of caregiving. This finding is echoed in international studies where spirituality and religiosity are shown to buffer psychological distress and promote caregiver well-being [69–71]. In Latin America, for example, religious coping is central to caregiver resilience [69, 70], whereas in Western contexts, caregivers are more likely to use peer-support groups, counseling, or professional services as coping mechanisms [71].

Problem-focused coping strategies were also evident, including borrowing money, cutting expenses, and relying on family cohesion to share responsibilities [15, 49]. These align with global findings that caregivers adapt through resource management and seeking social support [72, 73]. However, in high-income countries, such strategies are facilitated by access to social services and financial assistance programs [74], while in Africa, they depend heavily on fragile informal networks, which are often insufficient to meet sustained needs.

Resilience and adaptation further characterized caregiver experiences. Many caregivers reframed their role as a moral duty, an act of loyalty, or a religious calling, which fostered a sense of pride and meaning despite hardship [6, 9]. This resonates with international literature noting that caregivers frequently identify positive aspects of their role, such as personal growth, strengthened family bonds, and enhanced patience [75]. Yet, in African contexts, this reframing may function as both an adaptive strength and a survival mechanism in the absence of institutional support.

Conclusion

The synthesis of evidence demonstrates that caring for older adults living with dementia in African contexts places family caregivers under considerable strain, with challenges that are emotional, psychological, social, and economic in nature. Across the studies reviewed, caregivers reported pervasive anxiety, sadness, frustration, and hypervigilance, often fearing sudden health crises, accidents, or unpredictable behaviors of their relatives. These psychological burdens were compounded by persistent exhaustion from disrupted sleep and constant worry. The struggle to balance caregiving with other domestic roles, while simultaneously managing limited financial resources, further deepened caregivers’ sense of despair and increased their vulnerability to burnout.

The social dimension of caregiving emerged as both a strain and a source of resilience. While caregivers often experienced isolation and stigma associated with dementia, they also drew strength from family solidarity, reciprocal support, and community gestures such as food or financial donations. The narratives reflected how family bonds ensured continuity of care, with siblings or extended relatives stepping in when the primary caregiver was unavailable. This collective approach to caregiving reflects deeply rooted African cultural norms of interdependence and responsibility toward family members. However, reliance on informal social networks also underscores the absence of structured and institutionalized support systems, leaving caregivers vulnerable when such networks weaken.

At the same time, caregivers demonstrated remarkable adaptability and resilience in navigating these challenges. They employed strategies such as coaxing, pampering, and rational persuasion to promote medication adherence, while relying on empathy and perspective-taking to manage the behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia. By framing dementia as part of the aging process and approaching their relatives with patience and compassion, caregivers found ways to sustain their role despite immense challenges. Some caregivers even reported positive personal transformations, noting increased patience, understanding, and emotional growth as outcomes of their caregiving journey.

These findings align with international evidence showing that dementia caregiving is a universally taxing experience, often associated with high levels of psychological distress, social isolation, and economic strain. However, the African context presents unique intensifiers. Limited access to healthcare services, lack of caregiver training, scarce financial resources, and the absence of formal respite or psychosocial interventions exacerbate the burden. In contrast, in high-income settings, caregivers may have greater access to structured dementia care services, support groups, and state-funded financial assistance, which can buffer against burnout. The comparative gap highlights both the vulnerability of African caregivers and the need to adapt lessons from global best practices to local contexts.

The reliance on resilience mechanisms such as faith, spirituality, cultural norms of solidarity, and informal reciprocity systems illustrates how caregivers creatively adapt to adversity. Yet, these strategies alone are insufficient to mitigate the long-term risks of caregiver burnout, declining mental health, and compromised quality of care for older adults. Without formalized psychosocial support programs, caregiver education, and financial protection mechanisms, the burden of dementia care will continue to disproportionately fall on families, particularly women, thereby reproducing cycles of stress and economic hardship.

Recommendations

Based on the evidence synthesized, several recommendations emerge to strengthen the support systems for family caregivers of older adults living with dementia in African contexts and beyond.

Policy-level interventions

Governments should formally recognize unpaid family caregivers as a critical part of the healthcare system. This includes developing national dementia care policies that explicitly address caregiver needs and protections. Given the economic strain of caregiving, policies should introduce targeted social welfare programs such as caregiving stipends, subsidies for medications, and tax incentives for households providing long-term care. Policies should promote the integration of dementia management and caregiver support into primary healthcare services, ensuring that early diagnosis, treatment adherence, and psychosocial support are accessible at community level.

Practice-level interventions

Community-based psychosocial interventions, including support groups, peer mentoring, and counseling services, should be developed and scaled to provide emotional relief and reduce isolation among caregivers. Structured caregiver training programs should be designed to improve skills in managing behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia, medication adherence, and daily care routines. Training should be delivered in culturally appropriate formats and local languages. Pilot models of respite care, including community daycare centers or short-term institutional support, should be introduced to provide caregivers with temporary relief and prevent burnout. Faith communities and traditional community organizations should be engaged as platforms for education, stigma reduction, and support, building on caregivers’ reliance on spirituality and cultural solidarity.

Research recommendations

More empirical research is needed to explore the lived experiences of caregivers across diverse African countries, capturing regional, cultural, and socioeconomic differences. Rigorous evaluations of culturally adapted interventions, such as caregiver training programs, psychosocial support groups, and community-based respite models, are essential to build an evidence base for scalable solutions. Cross-regional research comparing caregiver burden and coping strategies between African and non-African contexts could provide insights into best practices and inform culturally sensitive policy design. Long-term research is needed to understand the cumulative psychological, economic, and health impacts of caregiving, as well as the intergenerational effects on caregivers.

Community and grassroots recommendations

Community awareness programs should be implemented to encourage shared responsibility, reducing stigma and ensuring that caregiving is not borne by one individual alone. Public education campaigns should be launched to demystify dementia, reduce stigma, and promote empathy toward persons living with dementia and their caregivers. Youth and volunteer engagement: Mobilizing youth groups and community volunteers can help share the burden of caregiving while promoting intergenerational solidarity.
