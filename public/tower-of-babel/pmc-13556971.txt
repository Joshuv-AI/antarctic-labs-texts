Candida albicans reprograms Staphylococcus aureus glycolytic metabolism to drive enhanced virulence
Authors: Xueting Wan, Yuan Wu, Qi Yang, Ruolan Wu, Ti Chen, Zhen Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe synergistic interaction between Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans exacerbates polymicrobial infection severity and mortality compared to monomicrobial infections. C. albicans metabolism is known to enhance S. aureus virulence, whereas the role of S. aureus metabolic reprogramming in this cross-species synergy remains poorly defined. In this study, we demonstrate that C. albicans remodels S. aureus glycolytic pathways, thereby increasing its virulence in glucose-rich environments. C. albicans triggers metabolic reprogramming in S. aureus, characterized by a reduction of lactate and accumulation of upstream glycolytic intermediates, including 3-phosphoglycerate, 2-phospho-D-glycerate, and phosphoenolpyruvate. The metabolic reprogramming phenotype is glycolysis-dependent, which is evidenced by 2-deoxy-D-glucose-mediated inhibition of virulence enhancement. Addition of exogenous lactate lowers extracellular pH and reduces the hemolytic activity of S. aureus-C. albicans co-cultures. Inhibition of lactate dehydrogenase by oxamate or extracellular alkalinization with sodium hydroxide significantly increases hemolysis in S. aureus mono-cultures. S. aureus-C. albicans co-infections promote inflammatory cell infiltration but do not affect the quantity of S. aureus, indicating that increased toxin levels, rather than bacterial quantity, drive the enhanced virulence. Collectively, these findings indicate that C. albicans enhances S. aureus virulence through metabolic reprogramming. Targeting lactate metabolism or glycolytic intermediates may disrupt this cooperative interaction, offering a novel therapeutic strategy against these polymicrobial infections.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans are widespread human pathogens that frequently co-colonize host niches, forming polymicrobial communities. This co-colonization exacerbates disease severity and complicates treatment, particularly in intensive care unit patients [1–3]. These two pathogens frequently co-exist in infections such as periodontitis, cystic fibrosis, burn wounds, and medical device-related infections, where their interplay increases morbidity and mortality [4]. Even at low concentrations, C. albicans can enhance the virulence of S. aureus [5–7], while S. aureus can also promote the proliferation and hyphal growth of C. albicans [8,9]. However, S. aureus virulence expression is impaired in mixed cultures with Lactococcus lactis, a lactic acid bacterium widely used in the dairy industry [10]. This asymmetry underscores the complexity of their interaction and highlights the urgent need to elucidate mechanisms by which C. albicans enhances the virulence of S. aureus.

Glucose, a key carbon source, plays a central role in the survival and pathogenicity of both S. aureus and C. albicans, driving their growth and the production of virulence factors [11,12]. In S. aureus, glucose metabolism through glycolysis not only produces adenosine triphosphate but also generates intermediates like pyruvate, which is converted to lactate under anaerobic conditions [13]. This metabolic adaptability allows S. aureus to colonize diverse host niches, ranging from oxygen-rich tissues to hypoxic abscesses [14]. Importantly, glycolysis is intricately connected to S. aureus virulence, as glucose availability regulates the expression of toxins and immune evasion factors [15]. A recent study demonstrated that lactylation, a post-translational modification of α-toxin by lactate, enhances its virulence in S. aureus infections [16]. Additionally, lactate also enhances Klebsiella pneumoniae virulence by promoting capsular polysaccharide biosynthesis [17]. These insights elevate glycolysis from a simple energy-producing pathway to a sophisticated regulator of S. aureus pathogenicity.

These two pathogens compete for glucose in overlapping niches, but their co-existence often results in mutual metabolic adaptations that enhance virulence [6]. The metabolic activity of C. albicans modulates S. aureus toxin production, leading to elevated toxin levels, including α-toxin and toxic shock syndrome toxin 1, thereby increasing S. aureus virulence [5,18,19]. These interactions are highly context-dependent, influenced by environmental factors such as pH and nutrient availability [5,20]. Under glucose-rich conditions, S. aureus allocates metabolic resources preferentially to biofilm formation rather than toxin production, a strategic adaptation that enhances immune evasion [21–23]. However, whether C. albicans manipulates S. aureus metabolism to enhance its virulence remains poorly understood.

C. albicans rapidly alkalinizes its extracellular environment via amino acid deamination, neutralizing the acidic microenvironment generated by S. aureus. This process enhances agr quorum-sensing activity and increases α-toxin production [5]. Accumulation of acidic metabolites, such as lactate, suppresses S. aureus virulence, while lactate depletion enhances bacterial cytotoxicity [24,25]. By reducing lactate levels, C. albicans may relieve this metabolic suppression, thereby enhancing S. aureus virulence. This metabolic interplay likely explains the glucose-dependent nature of their synergy. In this study, we investigate the hypothesis that C. albicans enhances S. aureus virulence by reprogramming its glycolysis, with a particular emphasis on the regulation of lactate metabolism.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, China (Approval No. S23660). Patient-informed consent was waived for the use of clinical S. aureus strain LZSA01 and C. albicans strains CA-W1, CA-W2, CA-W3, CA-W4, CA-W5, and CA-W6, as these clinical isolates were collected during routine clinical care procedures. All peripheral blood samples from healthy donors were obtained with written informed consent in accordance with ethical guidelines.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The S. aureus strain USA300 was generously provided by Abace Biotechnology (Beijing, China), while the S. aureus strain ATCC 25923 and C. albicans strain ATCC 90028 were kindly supplied by Tiandiren Biotechnology (Changsha, China). The clinical S. aureus strain LZSA01 was isolated from a diabetic foot ulcer patient, while the six C. albicans isolates (CA-W1 to CA-W6) were each obtained from sputum specimens of different patients. These isolates were identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Biotyper, Germany). For routine recovery, S. aureus strains were cultured on 5% sheep blood agar plates (Bio-caring, Guangdong, China), and C. albicans strains were grown on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar plates (SDA, Bio-caring, Guangdong, China), with both incubated at 35°C for 24 h. A single colony was cultured in Trypticase Soy Broth supplemented with 2.5 g/L glucose (TSB-g; Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China) at 35°C with shaking at 200 rpm.

For experimental cultures, overnight cultures of S. aureus and C. albicans were diluted into fresh TSB-g at predetermined ratios to establish three culture conditions: S. aureus mono-cultures (SA), C. albicans mono-cultures (CA), and S. aureus-C. albicans co-cultures (SA+CA). The S. aureus concentration was standardized to 1.5 × 106 CFUs/mL, while C. albicans was adjusted to 4.0 × 105 CFUs/mL. Cultures were incubated at 35°C for 24 h with shaking at 200 rpm. Post incubation, the supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone membrane (Millipore) to obtain cell-free conditioned medium (CFCM), which was either used immediately or stored at −70°C for further analysis.

Quantitative hemolysis assays

Quantitative hemolysis assays were conducted as previously described [26]. Briefly, whole blood from healthy donors was collected and centrifuged to separate plasma. Erythrocytes were washed twice with normal saline to remove residual plasma and resuspended in normal saline to a final concentration of 4% (v/v). Equal volumes of the 4% erythrocyte suspension were mixed with CFCM from SA mono-cultures, CA mono-cultures, or SA+CA co-cultures in a 96-well plate and incubated at 35°C for 90 min. After incubation, the supernatants were transferred to a new 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader. Positive controls consisted of erythrocyte suspensions mixed with 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 (BioFroxx, Germany), while culture medium alone served as the negative control.

To modulate the pH of cultures, sodium hydroxide (NaOH, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China) or exogenous lactate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added after 6 h of incubation to alkalinize or acidify the cultures, respectively. Glycolysis inhibitors 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, Solarbio Life Sciences, China), by targeting hexokinase, and oxamate (MedChem Express, USA), by targeting lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), were added at the initial stage of culture to inhibit glycolytic enzymes in SA mono-cultures or SA+CA co-cultures. Hemolysis assays were then repeated as described above.

Neutrophil lysis assays

Heparin-anticoagulated peripheral blood was combined with RPMI 1640 medium (Haoyang Huake Biotechnology, Tianjin, China) and layered onto Ficoll (MD Pacific Biotechnology, Tianjin, China). After centrifugation at 1000 × g for 12 min, the upper cell layer was discarded. Erythrocytes in the pellet were lysed using erythrocyte lysis buffer (Cowin Biotech, Beijing, China) at 35°C for 15 min, followed by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 min. The neutrophil pellet was washed with normal saline and resuspended in RPMI 1640 to a final concentration of 4.0 × 106 neutrophils/mL. CFCM from SA mono-cultures, CA mono-cultures, or SA+CA co-cultures was added to neutrophils in a 24-well plate and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. Then the supernatants were collected, and LDH activity was measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Skin abscess formation assays

This study followed the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Department of Laboratory Animals, Central South University, China (Protocol No. CSU-2024–0163). Female ICR mice (6–8 weeks old) weighing approximately 25 ± 3 g was obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal (Changsha, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle. To ensure the accuracy of the experimental results and comply with the 3 R principle, mice were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation (Ringpu, Tianjin, China). Mice (n = 4 per group) were randomly numbered from cage boxes, and each sample was allocated to one of four groups (TSB-g, SA, CA, or SA+CA) according to the group designation on the labels. Each mouse was considered the experimental unit in the studies. The dorsal fur was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol. A 100 μL inoculum was prepared for each infection group: S. aureus alone (2.0 × 107 CFU/mouse), C. albicans alone 2.0 × 106 CFU/mouse), or a co-infection with both S. aureus (2.0 × 107 CFU/mouse) and C. albicans (2.0 × 106 CFU/mouse). The inoculum was injected subcutaneously into the dorsal flank using a 27-gauge needle. Control mice received 100 μL of sterile TSB-g. Abscess formation was monitored for 24 h post-infection. Lesion size was measured using digital calipers and calculated as (length × width)/2. Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation. Abscesses and surrounding tissues were excised, homogenized in PBS, and serially diluted for CFUs enumeration on 5% sheep blood agar for S. aureus and SDA for C. albicans. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological analysis.

Colony-forming unit (CFU) assays

To evaluate microbial growth dynamics, overnight S. aureus cultures were standardized to 0.5 McFarland (McF) units in fresh medium using an electronic turbidimeter, DensiCHEK Plus (BioMerieux, France), ensuring identical starting inoculum densities. The standardized suspension was diluted 1:100 in fresh TSB-g medium to prepare SA mono-cultures and SA+CA co-cultures, while CA mono-cultures were established independently under the same conditions. All cultures were incubated at 35°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 24 h. Following incubation, these cultures were pre-treated with proteinase K (Sansure Biotech, China) at 35°C for 10 min. Then serial 10-fold dilutions of the SA mono-cultures, CA mono-cultures, and SA+CA co-cultures were prepared in sterile normal saline. For bacterial quantification, 5 μL aliquots of appropriate dilutions were plated onto 5% sheep blood agar plates, while fungal enumeration was performed by plating 5 μL aliquots onto SDA plates. After overnight incubation at 35°C, colonies were manually counted, and CFUs/mL values were calculated. To ensure precision, counts were performed in triplicate for each dilution, and results were averaged.

RNA isolation and real-time RT-PCR

SA mono-cultures and SA+CA co-cultures were grown in TSB-g medium for 12 h at 35°C with shaking at 200 rpm. After centrifugation, the bacterial pellets were washed and resuspended in normal saline containing 10 μg/mL lysostaphin (Aladdin, Shanghai, China). Homogenates of S. aureus-infected mouse skin tissues were lysed using Trizol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Total RNA was extracted and purified using the Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (paramagnetic particle method) (Sansure biotechnology, Hunan, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China), followed by qRT-PCR using ChamQ SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme, Jiangsu, China) on a CFX96TM Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA). The gyrB or GAPDH gene served as the internal control, and relative mRNA levels were calculated using a 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers were obtained from PrimerBank (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank) or derived from published sequences, as detailed in Table S1.

Toxin neutralization assay

CFCM from SA mono-cultures or SA+CA co-cultures was pretreated with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Tianhang Biotechnology, Zhejiang, China) and incubated with a 4% human erythrocyte suspension. Hemolysis was assessed after incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 90 min. For neutrophil toxicity testing, the FBS-pretreated CFCM was incubated with human neutrophils at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 2 h, and LDH activity in the supernatants was measured as described above.

Chemotaxis assays

Neutrophils were isolated from whole blood and quantified using a disposable cell counter. RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with CFCM from SA mono-cultures, CA mono-cultures, SA+CA co-cultures, unsupplemented medium (negative control), or a mixture of SA- and CA mono-cultures was added to the lower chamber of a Transwell insert (Corning Incorporated, USA). Subsequently, 100 μL of neutrophil suspension was placed in the upper chamber, and the system was incubated at 37°C under 5% CO2 for 5 h. Migrated neutrophils in the lower chamber were collected and counted using a disposable cell counter. The migration rate was calculated as the ratio of migrated cells to the initial neutrophil count, expressed as a percentage.

Intracellular killing assays

The intracellular bactericidal assay was conducted as previously described [27]. Briefly, neutrophils isolated from whole blood were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1.0 × 106 cells per well and pretreated with CFCM from SA mono-cultures, CA mono-cultures, SA+CA co-cultures, or a mixture of SA- and CA mono-cultures supplemented with 10% FBS for 3 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After incubation, 500 μL of neutrophil suspension was combined with the S. aureus USA300 strain (at an MOI of 50:1) and incubated for an additional 30 min. Extracellular bacteria were eliminated using 50 μg/mL gentamicin (MedChem Express, USA) for 1 h. Samples were then centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Serial dilutions of the lysates were prepared in sterile normal saline, and 5 μL aliquots were plated onto blood agar plates. After overnight incubation at 35°C, internalized bacterial colonies were enumerated, and CFUs were calculated.

Measurement of interleukin-6 (IL-6)

Neutrophils isolated from whole blood were seeded into 24-well plates and co-incubated with CFCM from SA mono-cultures, CA mono-cultures, SA+CA co-cultures, or a mixture of SA- and CA mono-cultures supplemented with 10% FBS. Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h. The supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 5 min, and IL-6 levels were quantified using an Elecsys® IL-6 kit (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/PI (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Neutrophils were treated under specified conditions for 90 min, harvested, and washed once with PBS via centrifugation (400 × g, 5 min). The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was retained. The cells were gently resuspended in 500 μL of binding buffer containing Annexin V (5 μL) and PI (5 μL). After incubation for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, the samples were analyzed by a flow cytometer (BD, USA).

Glucose and lactate measurement

SA, CA mono-cultures, and SA+CA co-cultures were incubated continuously at 35°C for 12 h. The supernatants were collected by centrifugation, and glucose/lactate levels were quantified using an automated biochemical analyzer Hitachi 7600, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

pH measurement

CA mono-cultures, SA mono-cultures, and SA+CA co-cultures were established by diluting overnight cultures into fresh TSB-g medium with an initial pH of 7.8 and incubating at 35°C for 12 h. The supernatants were collected at designated time points, and the pH was measured using a laboratory pH meter (SMART SENSOR, Guangdong, China).

Energy metabolomics analysis

Energy metabolomics analysis was performed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology (Hubei, China). Briefly, S. aureus from SA+CA co-cultures were isolated via 3 μm syringe filtration (Xingya Purifying Materials Factory, Shanghai, China), while SA mono-cultures were collected directly. CFUs were enumerated to quantify bacterial load. Bacterial pellets were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Biosharp, Anhui, China), inactivated with 75% ethanol, and stored at −70°C for further analysis. Metabolites were extracted using pre-cooled 80% methanol/water, subjected to freeze–thaw cycles, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g. Supernatants were analyzed by LC-MS/MS (SCIEX QTRAP 6500+), and data were processed using specialized software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.3 (GraphPad Software, USA). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired/paired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons involving three or more groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

C. albicans enhances S. aureus virulence in vitro

S. aureus strain USA300 was cultured alone or together with C. albicans ATCC 90028 in TSB-g medium. Hemolytic activity against human erythrocytes was assessed. As shown in Figure 1(A), C. albicans mono-cultures did not exhibit hemolytic activity. In contrast, co-culturing S. aureus USA300 with C. albicans significantly increased its hemolytic activity. Similar increases were observed in S. aureus strain ATCC 25923 (Figure 1(B)) and the clinical isolate LZSA01 (Figure 1(C)). Furthermore, co-culturing USA300 with clinical C. albicans isolates also significantly enhanced hemolytic activity (Supplement Figure S1). Human neutrophils exposed to CFCM from SA+CA co-cultures released more LDH than those treated with CFCM from SA mono-cultures (Figure 1(D-F)). These results indicate that S. aureus and C. albicans co-cultures are more cytotoxic to both human erythrocytes and neutrophils. C. albicans strains exhibit considerable diversity in hyphal growth, which can profoundly influence S. aureus growth. The C. albicans strains used in this study displayed weak, moderate, or strong filamentation capacity in TSB-g medium (Supplementary Figure S2(A-C)). Increased S. aureus biomass is associated with enhanced cytotoxicity. To determine whether co‑culturing with C. albicans affects S. aureus growth, we quantified the CFUs of both species in mono‑cultures and co‑cultures. CFU assays showed that the number of S. aureus strain USA300 cells was unchanged in SA+CA co-cultures compared to SA mono-cultures. In contrast, C. albicans CFUs were significantly reduced in SA+CA co-cultures relative to CA mono-cultures (Supplement Figure S3(A-B) and S4(A-D)). These findings indicate that co-culturing with C. albicans has no effect on S. aureus growth but significantly suppresses C. albicans proliferation.

Real-time RT-PCR analysis revealed significant alterations in the expression of several S. aureus toxin genes during co-culture with C. albicans. As shown in Figure 1(G), the expression of hla (encoding α-toxin) and RNAIII (encoding δ-toxin) in S. aureus strain USA300 increased approximately seven-fold and four-fold, respectively, in SA+CA co-cultures compared to SA mono-cultures. In contrast, only slight increases were observed for hlg, lukPVL, psmα, and psmβ expression under co-culture conditions. Moreover, co-culturing with C. albicans significantly upregulated psmα and psmβ, as well as hla and RNAIII, in both S. aureus strain ATCC 25923 (Figure 1(H)) and clinical isolate LZSA01 (Figure 1(I)). These results suggest that the C. albicans-induced enhancement of S. aureus virulence factor expression is not limited to the USA300 strain but also occurs in other S. aureus strains, indicating a broader phenomenon.

C. albicans enhances S. aureus virulence in vivo

We next evaluated the virulence of USA300 (SA) mono-infections, C. albicans ATCC90028 (CA) mono-infections, and S. aureus-C. albicans (SA+CA) co-infections using a murine skin abscess model. As shown in Figure 2(A,C), abscesses formed by SA+CA co-infections were significantly larger than those resulting from SA or CA mono-infections. Histopathological analysis revealed that SA+CA co-infections produced more extensive inflammatory cell infiltration and severe disruption of dermal tissue architecture than either mono-infection (Figure 2(B)). Despite these pathological differences, the bacterial and fungal loads in SA+CA co-infected tissues did not significantly differ from those in SA or CA mono-infections (Figure 2(D)). Furthermore, IL-6 expression in SA+CA co-infected tissues was similar to that in SA mono-infections, but significantly lower than in CA mono-infections (Figure 2(E)). Collectively, these findings indicate that SA+CA co-infections increase virulence without altering the microbial burden. Dual immunomodulatory effects of S. aureus-C. albicans on neutrophils

A previous study has demonstrated that serum inhibits neutrophil lysis induced by staphylococcal culture supernatants [28]. Consistent with this, our experiments showed that pre-treatment with 10% FBS markedly reduced the hemolytic activity of the S. aureus strain USA300 (Figure 3(A)). When neutrophils were exposed to CFCM from SA+CA co-cultures in the absence of serum, they underwent cellular swelling, membrane rupture, and subsequent cell death, accompanied by increased LDH release (Figure 3(B)) and a higher proportion of necrotic neutrophils (PI+ Annexin V- and PI+ Annexin V+ cells, Supplement Figure S5). Neutrophil-mediated immune responses are crucial for clearing S. aureus infections [29]. Given the chemoattractant properties of PSMs [30], the upregulation of psmα and psmβ in S. aureus from SA+CA co-cultures likely enhances neutrophil recruitment. Indeed, CFCM from SA+CA co-cultures induced higher neutrophil migration rates than those from SA mono-cultures or from a mixture of SA- and CA mono-cultures (Figure 3(C,D)), with random migration accounting for less than 5% of the total cells, highlighting the specific chemotactic activity. IL-6, a cytokine that enhances neutrophil bactericidal capacity, was significantly reduced in neutrophils exposed to CFCM from SA+CA co-cultures compared to SA mono-cultures but was significantly higher when exposed to CFCM from a mixture of SA- and CA mono-cultures (Figure 3(E)). However, no significant difference in neutrophil bactericidal activity was observed across these tested conditions (Figure 3(F)). These findings collectively indicate that SA+CA co-culture supernatants impair IL-6 production but do not affect neutrophil bactericidal capacity.

The enhanced S. aureus virulence by C. albicans requires glycolysis

Glucose, a primary carbon and energy source for S. aureus, is essential for the regulation of its virulence [31]. To determine whether glucose availability modulates the interaction between S. aureus and C. albicans, S. aureus strain USA300 was cultured in glucose-free TSB or LB medium, either alone or in co-culture with C. albicans. Hemolytic assays revealed no significant differences in hemolytic activity (Figure 4(A,D)) and S. aureus growth (Supplement Figure S3(C,E)) between SA mono-cultures and SA+CA co-cultures under glucose-free conditions. Comparable results were also observed for strain ATCC 25923 (Figure 4(B,E)) and clinical isolate LZSA01 (Figure 4(C,F)). These results indicate that glucose availability is required for C. albicans-mediated amplification of S. aureus hemolytic activity. C. albicans enhances S. aureus virulence by reducing lactate accumulation

S. aureus primarily relies on glycolysis for energy, generating pyruvate as a key intermediate, which is subsequently converted into acidic metabolites, mainly lactate [32]. To analyze the metabolic interaction between S. aureus and C. albicans, we quantified glucose and lactate levels in SA mono-cultures, CA mono-cultures, and SA+CA co-cultures in TSB-g medium. As shown in Figure 5(A), nearly all glucose was depleted within 8 h, with similar consumption rates in CA mono-cultures, SA mono-cultures, and SA+CA co-cultures. C. albicans alone produced negligible lactate, whereas SA mono-cultures exhibited significantly greater lactate accumulation than SA+CA co-cultures (Figure 5(B)), suggesting that C. albicans may reduce lactate accumulation by S. aureus. Although S. aureus-derived lactate in co-cultures could theoretically be utilized by C. albicans, CA mono-cultures showed that C. albicans did not consume exogenous lactate within the 4–6 h culture period (Supplement Figure S6). C. albicans reprograms S. aureus metabolism to promote virulence

Furthermore, concentrations of several amino acids and their derivatives, such as serine, ornithine, L-asparagine, L-cystine, and glutamine, were significantly reduced in S. aureus from co-cultures (Figure 6(C)). Discussion

Through comprehensive analyses of virulence factor expression, metabolic pathways, and host immune responses, we demonstrate that C. albicans reprograms S. aureus metabolism, specifically by altering glycolytic flux and suppressing lactate accumulation, thereby enhancing S. aureus virulence and disrupting neutrophil function. These findings not only advance our understanding of the dynamics of S. aureus-C. albicans co-infections but also highlight a promising therapeutic target for mitigating the severity of such polymicrobial infections.

To our knowledge, the hemolytic activity of S. aureus varies significantly among strains. Our previous study reported that S. aureus isolates from diabetic foot ulcers, non-diabetic sepsis, and wound infections exhibited different hemolytic activity against human erythrocytes [33]. Therefore, we selected three strains with different hemolytic activity in glucose-free medium for this study. However, the USA300 strain showed greater enhancement by C. albicans than the ATCC 25923 and LZSA01 strains in glucose-rich medium. Additionally, these three strains exhibited different lactate production abilities in glucose-rich medium (data not shown). Although the C. albicans strains used in this study displayed substantial heterogeneity in filamentation capacity, with weak, moderate, and strong phenotypes observed in TSB-g medium (Supplementary Figure S2). However, this diversity did not translate into differential effects on S. aureus virulence, as all isolates significantly increased the hemolytic activity of strain USA300, pointing to a relatively conserved cross-kingdom interaction mechanism. Overall, strain-specific variations in S. aureus virulence and glucose metabolism were observed, but the virulence of these three strains was all enhanced by C. albicans.

The increased cytotoxicity of S. aureus when co-cultured with C. albicans highlights the critical role of microbial cross-talk in modulating bacterial virulence. However, this enhancement is independent of S. aureus growth, pointing to C. albicans-driven metabolic or other signaling modifications in S. aureus. Unchanged hemolysis in co-culture with C. albicans in glucose-free medium or reduced hemolytic activity with the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG confirms that glycolysis is required for C. albicans-mediated enhancement of S. aureus virulence. These findings are consistent with earlier studies demonstrating that glucose availability regulates S. aureus toxin production through carbon catabolite repression [34–36]. Our data reveal complex metabolic reprogramming in S. aureus during co-culture with C. albicans. The accumulation of 3-phosphoglycerate, 2-phospho-D-glycerate, and PEP likely indicates carbon flux toward gluconeogenesis, as PEP serves as the direct precursor for glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) synthesis. Concurrent depletion of serine, ornithine, L-asparagine, L-cystine, and glutamine reflects their utilization as carbon skeletons for gluconeogenic precursors (e.g. conversion of serine to pyruvate). These coordinated metabolite changes strongly support gluconeogenic activation. These findings extend this framework by showing that C. albicans actively reprograms S. aureus metabolism to create a metabolic environment favorable for virulence factor expression.

C. albicans-mediated amino acid deamination contributes to environmental alkalinization [37,38]. However, we found that the reduction of acidic metabolites (notably lactate) in S. aureus intra- and extracellular compartments during co-culture was the primary driver of pH elevation. This increase in pH directly enhances toxin efficacy by counteracting acid suppression. For example, the stability and pore-forming efficiency of α-toxin are pH-sensitive, with neutral conditions being optimal for its activity [39]. Moreover, the elevated pH promotes activation of the agr quorum-sensing system in S. aureus, further enhancing toxin production and hemolytic activity [5]. During co-culture, S. aureus exhibited decreased levels of amino acids and derivatives (e.g. serine, glutamine; Figure 6(C)). This reduction likely reflects metabolic reprogramming in high-virulence strains that prioritize reallocating carbon/nitrogen fluxes and energy toward virulence factor synthesis [40] or gluconeogenic activation. Thus, C. albicans may act as a “pH modulator,” creating an environment conducive to S. aureus toxin efficacy.

S. aureus grows considerably well on lactate medium [41], whereas C. albicans cannot metabolize lactate (Supplement Figure S6). Exogenous lactate supplementation attenuated the hemolytic activity of S. aureus mono-cultures, while oxamate-mediated LDH inhibition suppressed lactate accumulation and enhanced cytotoxicity, mirroring the C. albicans effect. Lactate, a terminal glycolytic product, serves as a dual modulator of bacterial physiology and host immunity [42–44]. A recent study demonstrates that infection-induced lactate accumulation promotes α-toxin lactylation, thereby enhancing α-toxin-mediated virulence in S. aureus infections [16]. C. albicans significantly increases S. aureus α-toxin levels both in vitro and in vivo. However, neutralization of α-toxin only partially rescues co-infected mice from mortality, and co-administration of α-toxin with C. albicans fails to replicate the lethality observed during true co-infection [45]. Furthermore, α-toxin has been reported to possess limited lytic activity against human erythrocytes [39]. In line with this, we observed that the supernatants from SA+CA co-cultures exhibited pronounced hemolytic activity against human erythrocytes, suggesting that additional virulence factors contribute to this enhanced hemolysis. The upregulation of hla and RNAIII in co-cultured S. aureus aligns with prior evidence that C. albicans hijacks the S. aureus agr quorum-sensing system to amplify toxin production [5,45]. Except for RNAIII and hla, the expression of psm genes was also significantly elevated in the SA+CA co-cultures. Given that the agr system is greatly influenced by pH level [22], these findings suggest that lactate may act as a metabolic checkpoint for S. aureus virulence, likely through pH-dependent modulation of virulence factor expression.

Neutrophil migration from the blood to extravascular sites of sterile or infectious tissue damage is a hallmark of early innate immune responses [46]. Once outside the vessel, neutrophils are deprived of the protective effects provided by serum and become more susceptible to pathogen-induced damage. Components of serum, including complement, immunoglobulins, and high-density lipoprotein, neutralize S. aureus toxins, thereby safeguarding neutrophils against lytic destruction [28,47,48]. In serum-free environments, the virulence of S. aureus is markedly increased when cocultured with C. albicans, significantly enhancing the lysis of neutrophils in vitro and promoting the formation of abscesses in vivo. These findings highlight the protective role of serum in mitigating neutrophil lysis, underscoring the importance of humoral immunity in co-infections.

S. aureus produces several PSM molecules that stimulate and attract neutrophils at nanomolar concentrations through their interaction with the formyl-peptide receptor 2 [29,30,49]. There is an upregulation of psmα and psmβ in S. aureus during co-culturing with C. albicans, which may enhance neutrophil migration. Indeed, we observed that supernatants from SA+CA co-cultures increased neutrophil migration rates (Figure 3(C)), and S. aureus-C. albicans co-infections led to greater inflammatory cell infiltration at abscess sites (Figure 2(B)). However, a drawback is that we did not perform quantitative immunostaining to assess inflammatory cell infiltration. A recent study reported that C. albicans and S. aureus reciprocally enhance their virulence and pro-inflammatory effects in macrophages [50]. Interestingly, exposure to the supernatant of co-cultures significantly reduced IL-6 expression in human neutrophils, but this effect was not observed in the S. aureus-C. albicans co-infected tissues. Previous research has demonstrated that PSMs induce lower IL-6 and IL-8 expression in epithelial cells [51]. However, sublytic concentrations of synthetic or purified PSMα peptides have shown a different effect. When primary human keratinocytes are exposed to such PSMs, they show pronounced upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [52]. These studies show that toxins produced by S. aureus have distinct effects on the expression of inflammatory factors in different cell types. In the CA- and CA+SA-infected tissues, the CFUs of C. albicans showed no significant difference, but IL-6 expression was higher in the C. albicans-alone infections. Our previous study reported that S. aureus, but not S. epidermidis, significantly reduced lipopolysaccharide-induced IL-6 expression [33]. These findings suggest that the diminished IL-6 expression observed during co-infection may reflect PSM-mediated immunomodulation, a co-infection-specific adaptation in which fungal-enhanced S. aureus toxin expression paradoxically attenuates host inflammation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study elucidates how C. albicans metabolically reprograms S. aureus into a hypervirulent state. By suppressing lactate accumulation and alkalinizing the microenvironment, C. albicans enhances S. aureus cytotoxicity while impairing neutrophil IL-6 responses (Graphical Abstract). These findings redefine polymicrobial pathogenesis as a metabolically driven phenomenon. While our study establishes the central roles of extracellular alkalinization and lactate accumulation in the S. aureus-C. albicans synergism, future work should address whether specific C. albicans-derived metabolites directly potentiate S. aureus glycolytic flux. This mechanistic insight unveils a novel therapeutic target, suggesting that disrupting the metabolic crosstalk between S. aureus and C. albicans could mitigate the increasing threat of co-infections in clinical settings.
