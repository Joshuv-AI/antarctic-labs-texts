Health information delivery to patients at risk of having a small for gestational age/growth restricted baby in Aotearoa New Zealand: what can we learn from lived experience?
Authors: Joanna Louise James, Maria Juliet Ngauamo, Caitlin Baard, Mystie Jacobsen, Wendy Burgess, Alys Rachel Clark, Charlotte Oyston, Judith McCool
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe importance of access to reliable information during pregnancy is heightened in pregnancy complications. Impaired fetal growth (including small for gestational age (SGA) and growth restricted (FGR) fetuses) affects up to 10% of pregnancies and is associated with increased risk of morbidity and mortality. However, there is a paucity of accessible, evidence-based, information to support parents told their baby is not growing as expected. This study sought to explore patient experiences of information provision and seeking during pregnancies at risk of impaired fetal growth.MethodsFocus groups and individual in-depth interviews (totalling 24 participants from across Aotearoa New Zealand) were conducted and recorded via Zoom. Discussions were led using pre-defined prompts to explore the experiences, preferences, and suggested improvements for information delivery. Interviews were transcribed verbatim, and data underwent inductive thematic analysis.ResultsParticipants held expectations that their pregnancy would proceed normally; the majority experienced some level of distress at a diagnosis of complications. Information provided by the clinical team was highly valued, particularly when well curated and delivered with empathy. However, for many, this information needed to be supplemented. Additional, nuanced information was sought through online sources, and this included information on causes of impaired fetal growth, the meaning of medical terminology used, and lived experiences of pregnancy and birth. The timing of information provided by their clinical team impacted participant perceived capacity to comprehend information and their response to it. Participants preferred information to be offered at various times, in a range of forms, each adding to the layering of a more complete understanding of their situation. Preferences for information included being able to access a range of credible information in one place to promote greater coherence between the needs and lived experiences of patients and their healthcare providers.ConclusionsTimely, nuanced and salient information is extremely important to pregnant people and their families when navigating high-risk clinical pathways during pregnancy. Our results provide guidance on improving informational resources in the context of impaired fetal growth.

Background

Information is the basis for empowerment in healthcare settings. Access to information that is relevant, accessible, and salient, can have a profound impact on decision making [1–3]. The medicalisation of pregnancy, popularised since the 1900s has manifested an exponential growth in information about reproduction, pregnancy and fetal development [4]. By and large, this information is highly accessible, particularly via the internet and social media and increasingly via artificial intelligence tools such as ChatGPT [5–7], although barriers and inequities in access to information and misinformation about pregnancy exist [8]. However, for those facing complications during pregnancy, accessing reliable information pertinent to a specific medical condition, can present a challenge. Evidence suggests that the most trusted source of information is that provided by a health professional, followed by information provided by friends and family, and the internet [8, 9]. People tend to seek out information for various reasons, including to help make decisions [1, 10].

One of the most common pregnancy complications occurs when fetal growth is limited in utero, affecting ~ 6000 pregnancies in Aotearoa New Zealand each year [11]. In clinical terms, this includes pregnancies that are either ‘small for gestational age’ (SGA, where the estimated fetal weight is < 10th growth percentile on a customised chart), or exhibit ‘fetal growth restriction’ (FGR, <3rd growth percentile, and/or significant decline of the ultrasound-estimated fetal growth trajectory in utero) [12]. Both SGA and FGR are significant risk factors for perinatal mortality, short term neonatal morbidity, and long-term health complications including an increased risk of impaired neurocognitive development, diabetes and cardiovascular disease [13, 14]. In Aotearoa New Zealand, Māori, Pasifika, and Indian populations exhibit higher rates of perinatal and maternal morbidity and mortality (including SGA, FGR, preterm birth, and stillbirth) [11, 15].

Although access to trustworthy, credible, and reliable information is recognised as important, information overload (both during healthcare consultations, and via other non-clinical encounters) can act as a barrier to improving health literacy [16, 17]. Empowering parents with access to salient information that reflects their concerns can play a vital role in shaping the lived experience of pregnancy, particularly when concern regarding fetal development has been communicated [18]. Conversations that acknowledge and discuss potential adverse pregnancy outcomes are especially challenging for both patient and their health professional. Healthcare providers often carry responsibility to provide the right information, in the right amount, at the right time [18]. The message delivery is also important, with considerations of vocal tone, pauses in delivery, and setting coming into consideration.

The standard pathway of care for pregnancies with impaired fetal growth involves increased monitoring, with extra antenatal ultrasound scans and clnical visits, and planned early birth to reduce the risk of stillbirth [14]. The diagnosis of suspected FGR or SGA may trigger anxiety about the trajectory of the pregnancy and questions about why it occurs [19]. Information available via healthcare providers predominantly focuses on healthy pregnancies, for good reason. Most pregnancies in Aotearoa New Zealand are ‘normal’ with no adverse outcomes at birth. However, there is a lack of accessible evidence-based information on FGR and/or SGA to support parents during this period. This study explores experiences of information seeking during pregnancies in which patients were informed they were at risk of having a SGA or FGR baby.

Methods

Design and participants

The study was approved by the Auckland Health Research Ethics Committee (#22050) and conducted in accordance with the New Zealand National Ethics Standards. A series of focus groups and individual in-depth interviews were conducted via Zoom. Participants were recruited into the study by distribution of flyers at community venues across Auckland, and more widely across New Zealand via online advertising on social media and established relevant patient support networks. People were eligible to participate in the study if aged over 18 years, able to provide informed consent, could converse in English, had been advised that the baby they carried was born FGR or SGA, or was identified as being at risk of either of these conditions during pregnancy. To help ensure that participants recollections of their experience were accurate and currently relevant, only those that had given birth in the past 18 months were included in the study.

Data collection

Focus groups (six groups of 2–4 research participants) and five individual interviews were held over Zoom in October and November of 2021, each lasting 30 min–1.5 h depending on the number of participants. Focus group discussions were led by two female research assistants (CB and MN) using a pre-defined set of discussion prompts to explore the experiences, expectations, and preferences for salient and respectful information delivery to support understanding of impaired fetal growth. Māori and Indian participants were invited to participate in focus groups with others from the same cultural background to support cultural safety in sharing their viewpoints. The interview guide was pilot tested on a group of 7 recently pregnant participants with varying normal and pathological outcomes to obtain feedback on the prompts and interview style prior to use. Participants were invited to reflect on their expectations, needs during pregnancy and after birth, and contribute ideas on improving future information delivery. Interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim. The textual data were subject to an inductive thematic analysis process (conducted by CB, MN, MJ, JC and JJ) whereby data were coded according to higher level (dominant) themes and minor (sub themes). A process of intercoder reliability testing was conducted whereby a sample of the coded transcripts were recorded by the team and a consensus on the final explanatory description of the data was developed.

Results

In total, 24 people were interviewed in six focus group discussions and five individual interviews. Of these, 50% (12/24) identified as NZ European/Pākehā, 21% (5/24) identified as Indian, 17% (4/24) identified as Māori, 4% (1/24) identified as Pacific peoples, and 8% (2/24) identified as ‘Other’ (NZ American, NZ South African). Participants were geographically distributed across New Zealand, with 11 participants from the Auckland region, five from the Wellington region, two from Dunedin, two from the Christchurch region, and the remaining four from Hamilton, Nelson, Whakatāne, and Napier.

Six dominant themes were developed from the data: participants expectation of pregnancy, face-to-face information delivery, other sources of information, timing of information, the impact of information availability on parents and their families, and the ideal information package.

Participants expectations of pregnancy

For most participants, pregnancy was expected to be uncomplicated; few considered the possibility of major health complications for their baby, especially as they often had no known risk factors for pregnancy disorders. Participants described being surprised or shocked at being told their baby was at risk of being SGA or growth restricted.

Transcript 11 (T11), participant 2 (P2): “It’s a scary and emotional thing.” “It’s not expected. When you get pregnant, you’re not expecting to have a small baby and you’re not expecting to have to spend days and weeks and months in hospital.”

Transcript 11 (T11), participant 2 (P2): “It’s a scary and emotional thing.” “It’s not expected. When you get pregnant, you’re not expecting to have a small baby and you’re not expecting to have to spend days and weeks and months in hospital.”

T4 (P3): “I had no idea this was even a thing. I just assumed that everything would be fine. When she told me that it was an absolute shock.”

T4 (P3): “I had no idea this was even a thing. I just assumed that everything would be fine. When she told me that it was an absolute shock.”

Participants with a history of fertility/pregnancy problems (i.e. prior miscarriages, pregnancy through assisted reproductive technology) or a family history of pregnancy disorders were more aware that their pregnancy may not proceed “normally”, which made them more apprehensive from the start of the pregnancy. For participants of Indian ethnicity, there was an acceptance that their babies are often born smaller than those of other ethnicities, and that this is not necessarily a health risk.

T9: “Both my husband and I are Indian and we’re not that tall or we weren’t big babies, so we were always like our baby is going to be small.”

T9: “Both my husband and I are Indian and we’re not that tall or we weren’t big babies, so we were always like our baby is going to be small.”

For those who had expectations of having a normal pregnancy, being told that something was potentially ‘wrong’ with their pregnancy was very confronting, especially if they were alone at the appointment and without support. In particular, the uncertainty of the trajectory of the clinical situation and the speed at which things can change caused participants stress.

T10: “A really anxiety provoking time being pregnant with your first pregnancy and not knowing what’s going on.” “there was that time between appointments where I was like oh what if the blood flow to the baby tomorrow suddenly stops and I have to wait another week for a scan, then what happens in that time, like how would I know that there’s something wrong”.

T10: “A really anxiety provoking time being pregnant with your first pregnancy and not knowing what’s going on.” “there was that time between appointments where I was like oh what if the blood flow to the baby tomorrow suddenly stops and I have to wait another week for a scan, then what happens in that time, like how would I know that there’s something wrong”.

Participants responded to this anxiety differently, depending on their personality type. Many sought out information to help mitigate the stress and understand what this diagnosis may mean for their pregnancy going forward. However, this approach was not universal, and some found it easier to cope if they did not know as much.

T5: “when they say it should be fine… I mean that’s probably quite reassuring for some people but for me it’s very – like it’s the opposite because I’m not getting enough information”.

T5: “when they say it should be fine… I mean that’s probably quite reassuring for some people but for me it’s very – like it’s the opposite because I’m not getting enough information”.

T6: “I didn’t want to know that much, like I didn’t want to listen”.

T6: “I didn’t want to know that much, like I didn’t want to listen”.

Face to face information delivery – experiences and needs

Face-to face information delivery from their lead maternity carer or secondary care team was a primary source of information. Patient-health provider relationships and face-to-face time with clinical experts was highly valued among participants. During these interactions participants sought to both understand what was happening and be provided with reassurance. Information needs, in terms of depth and quantity varied; some valued detailed clinical explanations, whilst others sought more general reassurance. The balance of information sought and provided was inherently nuanced; some participants had the perception that clinicians were holding back information, providing just enough for them to be aware and concerned, but not enough to fully understand the risks or provide a rationale for the decisions being made.

Whilst dialogue was important, participants were not always able to formulate questions during the appointment, finding that they often surfaced afterwards. As such, participants valued their doctor providing ‘curated’ information:

T3, P2: “they answered questions also that I didn’t know I needed the answers to because unless you’re a paediatrician or an obstetrician or a midwife, you don’t know the questions you don’t know.”

T3, P2: “they answered questions also that I didn’t know I needed the answers to because unless you’re a paediatrician or an obstetrician or a midwife, you don’t know the questions you don’t know.”

In the Aotearoa New Zealand midwife-led healthcare system, participants often valued the relationship they had established with their community midwife, but some found this was lost when referred into secondary/obstetric-led care that involved multiple different doctors. The perception that in this secondary care setting was that patients had to advocate more for their own care, and were given different information by different doctors, eroding their trust in the information provided.

T4, P4: “it was always a different doctor, and they’d always say different things.”

T4, P4: “it was always a different doctor, and they’d always say different things.”

Many participants felt concerned that variable delivery of understandable information may have inequitable impacts on patients with different backgrounds, personality types, or levels of health literacy (i.e., those less likely to advocate for themselves or ask questions). A stretched health care system was also considered as a potential barrier to accessing timely, personalised information support.

T4, P1: “If you get that face-to-face time with the right type of specialist, it’s invaluable, but they’re so busy you can’t always do that.”

T4, P1: “If you get that face-to-face time with the right type of specialist, it’s invaluable, but they’re so busy you can’t always do that.”

T8: “That’s the system, overworked and burnt-out staff and therefore you don’t get information, you don’t get the right support.”

T8: “That’s the system, overworked and burnt-out staff and therefore you don’t get information, you don’t get the right support.”

In interactions with their health care provider, patients highlighted the importance of empathetic approaches to communication where they felt respected and heard. Together with the health care provider maintaining a calm tone, this had a significant impact on their emotional state. Transparency of communication was also valued, particularly as in FGR there is often a degree of uncertainty in potential pregnancy outcomes and management plans are often developed over time.

T10: “it’s really important that the people communicating the information to you communicate that information in a way that’s really not going to add extra stress or anxiety, because the thing is by that point it was out of my control”.

T10: “it’s really important that the people communicating the information to you communicate that information in a way that’s really not going to add extra stress or anxiety, because the thing is by that point it was out of my control”.

T6: “in terms of information, it was obviously something our obstetrician was very good at – but she’s kind and empathetic and she understood especially the journey we’ve been on and everything, but at the same time still very clinical”.

T6: “in terms of information, it was obviously something our obstetrician was very good at – but she’s kind and empathetic and she understood especially the journey we’ve been on and everything, but at the same time still very clinical”.

Other sources of information accessed – experiences and needs

Whilst some participants were provided with hard copy information (pamphlets) on pre-eclampsia, premature birth, or neonatal care, no participants received hard copy information about SGA or FGR. Participants sought to fill information voids with online searches to find information to better understand their own clinical scenario (e.g., terminology, scan types, metrics). However, without expert knowledge, filtering the information to determine what was both reliable and relevant to them was challenging.

T2, P1: “Those were things that I was Googling because they were words I’d never heard of and did I need to be concerned…”.

T2, P1: “Those were things that I was Googling because they were words I’d never heard of and did I need to be concerned…”.

T5, P3: “I’m going to try and gain as much information as possible, but it’s impossible to know whether it actually applies to you.”

T5, P3: “I’m going to try and gain as much information as possible, but it’s impossible to know whether it actually applies to you.”

T2, P3: “then you have to try and sift through to see what is a reliable source to read and what is not?”

T2, P3: “then you have to try and sift through to see what is a reliable source to read and what is not?”

Although participants could find information on other pregnancy disorders (e.g., pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes), many describe a lack of information specifically on impaired fetal growth. Reasonably, participants wanted to know how this may affect their baby’s outcomes in addition to any other potential co-morbidities (e.g. pre-eclampsia, preterm birth). Information they could find was often from overseas sources, and it was unclear how applicable this was to the Aotearoa New Zealand healthcare setting. Participants sought to access sites they perceived provided reliable, balanced, scientific and parental viewpoints. Common sources accessed included a range of websites (Mayo Clinic (US-based), Tommy’s (UK based), and Healthline (NZ-based)), apps (‘What to Expect’, ‘Babble’) and podcasts (‘Grow my Baby’). Patients also sought reassurance about potential outcomes, and to understand the path ahead, by reading about other stories and experiences. To this end, online forums and support groups (e.g., via Facebook, Reddit, or The Neonatal Trust), provided both emotional support and filled remaining information gaps:

T4, P2: “I found myself on these internet forums just looking at other people’s stories and just trying to make sense of what was going on”. “I think it was just more about trying to find information, and that was just the easiest way to find it was on these internet forums.”

T4, P2: “I found myself on these internet forums just looking at other people’s stories and just trying to make sense of what was going on”. “I think it was just more about trying to find information, and that was just the easiest way to find it was on these internet forums.”

T4, P1: “it would have put my mind more at ease knowing earlier on that there’s a huge amount of support for people who have premature or small babies.”

T4, P1: “it would have put my mind more at ease knowing earlier on that there’s a huge amount of support for people who have premature or small babies.”

Being told there were concerns about their baby’s growth caused significant ongoing stress, primarily driven by the uncertainty of what might happen. A key reason participants sought information was as a source of hope to help mitigate that stress. However, participants had mixed reactions to the information they found, finding it both reassuring and anxiety inducing.

T2, P2: “It gave me a little bit of confidence that okay, things do turn out well, it can work out, but it also told me about the hundred other things that can go wrong.” “I must have spent hours just up at nighttime in the middle of the night looking at them, which I don’t think was very good for my mental health.”

T2, P2: “It gave me a little bit of confidence that okay, things do turn out well, it can work out, but it also told me about the hundred other things that can go wrong.” “I must have spent hours just up at nighttime in the middle of the night looking at them, which I don’t think was very good for my mental health.”

As such, balance in the information provided was important. Patients wanted to obtain accurate and relevant answers to their questions from a reliable source, but also to understand the range of potential outcomes, not just worst case scenarios or risks, so they could hold some hope for their baby.

T6: “the official information, it was just all really negative so all you can do is feel fear.”

T6: “the official information, it was just all really negative so all you can do is feel fear.”

T1, P1: “with the information online you also run the risk of creating a lot of anxiety in people and so finding a way to balance that information.”

T1, P1: “with the information online you also run the risk of creating a lot of anxiety in people and so finding a way to balance that information.”

Timing of information delivery

The timing of delivery to patients impacted their ability to take on information, and their emotional response to the information. Different types of information (i.e. what is happening, why this is happening, what the next clinical steps are, what will happen after birth) are useful or required at different stages of each individual’s journey. There can be a tension between the urgency of medical timelines, and the ability of patients to take on information, which may lead to clinicians prioritising essential information.

T11, P1: “They’re like do you have any questions and I’m like bawling my eyes out here like no I don’t.”

T11, P1: “They’re like do you have any questions and I’m like bawling my eyes out here like no I don’t.”

As cases of growth restriction may be not recognised until after the baby is born, the timeframe of information delivery can be highly compressed. For some this meant a large amount of information to take on board at a time when they were not be physically or emotionally equipped.

The impact of information availability on family and support networks

Family members were a key support network for many participants, helping them seek information, or providing advice (particularly if they had any medical knowledge). However, some participants felt as if their partner or family did not understand what they were going through. This was exacerbated when for some participants when attending appointments alone, impacting their ability to absorb information and ask questions.

T4, P2: “My husband didn’t come with me to that appointment and so I was just alone, and I just cried all the way home. I was like what’s going on? When you just don’t expect that sort of information to be thrown at you and you’re all alone, it was quite confronting.”

T4, P2: “My husband didn’t come with me to that appointment and so I was just alone, and I just cried all the way home. I was like what’s going on? When you just don’t expect that sort of information to be thrown at you and you’re all alone, it was quite confronting.”

In line with this, participants highlighted the importance of their partner and/or family understanding what was happening in their pregnancy, and the value of having information available that partners and family could access to enable shared understanding. Enabling this understanding helped ensure their experience or anxiety was not diminished. Accessible and sharable information was also considered important in facilitating shared understanding when it was not the cultural norm to talk openly about pregnancy problems or losses.

T6: ““my mum was like oh what’s wrong, you were small? Like she just couldn’t get that this was an actual issue I was facing.”

T6: ““my mum was like oh what’s wrong, you were small? Like she just couldn’t get that this was an actual issue I was facing.”

T3, P2: “being able to say to them well actually mum, have a read of this…”.

T3, P2: “being able to say to them well actually mum, have a read of this…”.

The ideal information package

Participants were asked how the information they received in pregnancy could be improved in terms of timing, format, content, and mode of delivery. Several participants would have liked to receive information for themselves and their family earlier in the process. However, it was important to consider the balance of information ‘overload’ with a person’s capacity to take on information, which was often impaired due to feeling stressed, overwhelmed, or in a heightened emotional state.

T1, P3: “…at points in my journey I didn’t want to get into the detail because it was too scary, …at the time it was way too tough to be able to do that.”

T1, P3: “…at points in my journey I didn’t want to get into the detail because it was too scary, …at the time it was way too tough to be able to do that.”

The majority of participants reported wanting more information on impaired fetal growth and for this to be written with Aotearoa New Zealand context, reflecting the diversity of cultural experience and expectations. Participants identified the importance of being able to access reliable information in one place, that they could digest in their own time, and share with their family as an additional layer of support. They asked for information to span the pregnancy, from diagnosis through to delivery, the neonatal environment, and what the future might hold for their baby’s development postpartum. This information would reflect different cultural perspectives and norms, including diet. Participants valued being directed to a resource by their health care provider, not being relied on to find the information themselves. Traditional printed pamphlets that patients physically obtain from their healthcare provider remain useful, but opportunities were identified to connect these pamphlets to digital resources (website or app) allowing the potential to individualise information needs.

T3, P3: “when I was pregnant, I would have liked to go to one place where I can get reliable information…. instead of going on all these tangents and trying to figure it out for myself and making myself anxious through the process.”

T3, P3: “when I was pregnant, I would have liked to go to one place where I can get reliable information…. instead of going on all these tangents and trying to figure it out for myself and making myself anxious through the process.”

Participants emphasised the importance of hearing stories about other growth restricted or premature babies, to provide a positive balance when babies have done well, a more holistic understanding of what their journey may be like, and help patients prepare for hospital/NICU better. Indeed, many participants highlighted the need to provide practical information for navigating NICU/SCBU, including what to expect, and what parents are able to do in the care of their baby, where possible tailored to different geographic areas/hospitals. Finally, participants recommended including resources to support the emotional and mental health of parents during this time, and direction to further help if it was needed.

In addition to ‘one-way’ information delivery, participants ‘dream tool’ for information delivery in pregnancy would allow them to access their own clinical information and appointment summaries to help with continuity of care, familiarisation with terminology, and their ability to self-advocate. Interactive features were also suggested such the potential to plot baby’s growth on a chart, or understand how metrics from their pregnancy related to normal ranges. Finally, the importance of personal communication and connection to participants was reflected in their desire to have functionality that could both connect them with others in a similar situation, and ‘close the loop’ by enabling discussions with a healthcare provider so that their questions could be answered when they arose.

Discussion

This study highlights the intersection between the varying information needs of participants who have experienced issues with their baby’s growth, and the clinical (health care provided, in the moment) and online sources of information essential to help them navigate this challenging scenario. Although this study was undertaken within the context of Aotearoa New Zealand health system, the key messages and lessons about information delivery may be applicable across global healthcare settings.

Our study identified gaps in the provision of information about FGR and SGA, despite the efforts of healthcare providers to meet the informational needs of their patients. Access to trusted information was important to support face-to-face communication, give participants the confidence to ask questions, help them communicate with their family, and prevent self-blame. Suggestions for improvement included information that reflects the breadth of experiences, and needs of individuals, couples and families, throughout their journey. It is widely recognised that there is no ‘one size fits all’ health information package for impaired fetal growth. However, the initial verbal explanations provided by healthcare providers need to be supplemented with other sources of salient and ideally audience segmented information in a range of mediums [20].

Our findings support the importance of information ‘layering’ to patients in the context of pregnancy disorders, to augment information delivered face-to-face, help patients share information with their support networks (particularly for those who attended appointments alone). In this way, information can be customised to be relevant to different stages of their journey. Digital resources are an important component of modern information layering in healthcare. Indeed, brochures given to pregnant patients are often outdated, incorrectly referenced, poorly formatted, or printed [1]. For those with access, digital information has addressed this issue by providing multiple, engaging, updated and even personalised sources of information that can be accessed on demand [10, 21, 22]. However, due to the ease with which content can be produced, the reliability of the online information can vary, relying on consumers to determine integrity [10, 23]. Data in this study support the importance to patients of being able to identify and access relevant, reliable information that enabled them to understand their own clinical situation and potential outcomes. A balance of factual clinical information, practical/logistical guidance, and patient experiences was important in this context.

The effectiveness of information delivery in pregnancy has important impacts on shared decision making [24]. Indeed, the importance of trusted relationships, salient and timely face-to-face information delivery highlighted as important in the context of pregnancy disorders in our study are similarly important in facilitating shared decision making and supporting mental health (e.g. alleviating worry) during pregnancy [25–27]. To this end our findings may inform design of digital health tools targeted at patients and families information preferences, and that support shared decision making processes by improving provider-patient interactions. With the increased use of user generated content and generative AI tools, such as ChatGPT for health information, reliable, accessible digital content underpinned by clinical and empirical evidence is essential.

Whilst participants in our work were from diverse backgrounds, participant numbers in this work were not sufficient to allow us to draw conclusions about specific cultural or ethnicity-based needs. However, it is important to acknowledge that both cultural and health literacy considerations intersect with the effectiveness of information delivery across a range of healthcare settings [28–30]. In the context of pregnancy, the wide range of global cultural practices and beliefs around ‘normal’ and complicated pregnancies make this especially relevant [31, 32]. As such, it is important that tools designed to improve information delivery in pregnancy are developed in partnership with the communities they seek to serve and are regularly updated to ensure currency and salience, especially with younger people. Health information to have cultural integrity needs to move beyond linguistic translation to a more nuanced reflection of cultural understandings of pregnancy.

This study had several limitations. It is important to acknowledge that participants in this work had varying clinical scenarios, and their experiences of these were likely shaped by their intrinsic personality, culture, and circumstances, including social and economic backgrounds. These factors almost certainly impact their engagement with and expectations of information pertaining to their pregnancy. Furthermore, we appreciate that our participants were self-selected, therefore the sample likely reflected individuals who were more impacted by their experience of their at-risk pregnancy and the informational support they received, which may potentially bias the findings towards negative patient experiences. Finally, this study was undertaken before the widespread accessibility and use of artificial intelligence/large language models (such as ChatGPT) and so did not consider the information delivery in this context.

Conclusions

In summary, this work has highlighted the importance of reliable and salient information delivery in the support of patients and their families told their baby is at risk of impaired fetal growth. It identified a need for improved information delivery for couples with pregnancies complicated by FGR or SGA in Aotearoa New Zealand. Such information would have important uses to facilitate the flow of information between clinician and patient during shared decision making [27, 33]. This work highlights that design of information resources should be undertaken in consultation with, or ideally co-designed with both patients and clinical end users (in both community and secondary care settings). Indeed, a common thread in the ideal information tools proposed by participants was their integration with their in person clinical experience – be it via direct linking to reliable resources, or a portal for information sharing. A critical next step in this work is thus to engage healthcare providers and organisations to ensure that delivery of any prospective tool is fit for purpose within system for which it is designed.
