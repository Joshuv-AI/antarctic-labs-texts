Early‐Life Dietary Protein Source Reshapes Gut Microbiota but Fails to Curtail Diet‐Induced Metabolic Syndrome in Young Rats
Authors: Chunlong Mu, Micaela Hartslief, Jessica Chong, Shrushti Shah, Jennifer Thompson, Raylene A Reimer, David C Wright, Jane Shearer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDairy consumption plays a protective role in obesity that may be mediated through the gut microbiota. This study examined the role of dairy proteins, casein, whey, and complete dairy (skim milk powder), and a plant‐based alternative, soy, on gut microbiota composition, obesity susceptibility, and insulin sensitivity in early development.MethodsFollowing weaning, rat pups were randomized into four isocaloric diets (n = 8–11/sex/diet) for 4 weeks: (1) complete dairy; (2) casein; (3) whey; and (4) soy. Primary outcomes included food intake, growth, body composition, glucose tolerance, and fecal microbiota composition. Animals were then metabolically challenged with a high‐fat, high‐sucrose diet (HFHS, 40% kilocalories from fat) for 5 weeks.ResultsThe impacts on gut microbiota and metabolic outcomes were sex and protein dependent. Compared to casein, complete dairy consumption reduced diversity, while early exposure to complete dairy improved body fat percentage but not glucose tolerance or insulin sensitivity. Soy elicited differential effects in males and females. Despite preserved microbial patterns, diets were unable to overcome the detrimental metabolic impacts of HFHS diet exposure.ConclusionsResults show that complete dairy positively modulates gut microbial profiles and benefits body composition early in life, but it could not curtail the negative metabolic impacts of HFHS feeding.Keywords: dairy protein, growth, gut microbiota, insulin resistance, obesity

Introduction

Childhood obesity has tripled in the last 30 years, resulting in a global epidemic [1]. Obesity‐related comorbidities such as elevated blood pressure, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease are increasingly affecting younger age groups and persisting into adulthood [2]. Because excess weight gained early often persists, childhood is a key period for intervention that can help prevent long‐term physical, social, and psychological health consequences of obesity.

One promising avenue for early intervention is dietary modification of the gut microbiota, a community of microorganisms within the gastrointestinal tract that contributes to host homeostasis and plays a central role in immune, metabolic, and disease development [3]. In a commensal relationship with the host, it plays a pivotal role in nutrient absorption, vitamin production, immune system regulation, and toxin breakdown, providing a multitude of benefits [3]. Imbalances in the gut microbiota may contribute to the risk and development of obesity, with dietary influences being critical in shaping the composition and function of the microbiota [4, 5, 6].

Early life represents a critical period for gut microbiota development; perturbations during this window can increase long‐term disease risk [7]. Although the structure of the gut microbiota is complex and dynamic, early‐life dietary intervention appears to be particularly important in shaping a healthy gut microbial composition [8]. Early dietary exposures during key developmental windows can have lasting effects on metabolism and immune function. Accordingly, introducing evidence‐supported dietary patterns can mitigate disease outcomes by shifting the gut microbial community toward a healthier, more metabolically beneficial homeostasis. Among early‐life dietary exposures, dairy is of particular interest given its protein complexity, widespread consumption, and known effects on the gut microbiota.

Dairy contains an array of nutrients including vitamins, lipids, carbohydrates, proteins (comprising primarily 80% casein and 20% whey), bioactive peptides, and metabolites, all with the potential to influence the gut microbiota [9]. There is growing evidence supporting the beneficial impacts of dairy consumption on the gut microbiota, resulting in protective impacts on obesity [10]. Importantly, casein, whey, and complete dairy proteins all impact the gut microbiota [9, 11]; however, the results thus far are unclear and vary between studies, warranting further investigation. Further, the replacement of dairy with milk alternatives has become commonplace. Given the widespread replacement of dairy with plant‐based alternatives, this study aimed to investigate the impact of early‐life complete dairy (skim milk powder), dairy protein components (casein, whey), and a plant protein alternative (soy) on the gut microbiota, obesity susceptibility, and insulin resistance. Employing a pediatric rodent model, we hypothesized that early‐life dairy consumption would attenuate weight gain and improve metabolic outcomes in both sexes, and that a subsequent high‐fat diet challenge would reveal dairy‐associated microbiota features that protect against the development of obesity and insulin resistance.

Methods

Animals

All experimental protocols were approved by the Health Sciences Animal Care Committee at the University of Calgary (#AC21‐0184) and abided by the guidelines on ethical animal use determined by the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Rat cages were kept in temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled rooms maintained under a 12:12 h light/dark cycle. Adult male and female Sprague–Dawley rats (Charles River Laboratories) were bred until plugs were detected. At birth, large litters were culled to 10 pups to minimize litter‐size variability on growth and development [12, 13]. Rats were weaned on postnatal Day 21 (p21) and housed with one littermate of the same sex in standard cages with wood chip bedding, nesting material, and an enrichment object.

Experimental Diets

Following weaning, animals were randomized into one of four customized, isocaloric pelleted experimental diets (64%, 20%, and 16% kilocalories from carbohydrate, protein, and fat) (Dyets Inc.) for 4 weeks (p21–p49). Diets varied in protein source and included casein, whey, complete dairy (skim milk powder), and soy (n = 8–11/diet/sex), and they were fed ad libitum for the duration of the study. Body Composition

TD‐NMR imaging was used to assess body composition (Minispec Live Mice Analyzer LF110, Bruker Optics Inc.) at p49 and p84. Lean, fat, and body fluid percentages were calculated.

Oral Glucose (OGTT) and Insulin Tolerance Tests (ITT)

Prior to OGTT, rats were moved to clean cages with fresh bedding and water and fasted for 10 h. For OGTT, glucose was administered via oral gavage at 2.0 g/kg body weight at p42 and p70. Prior to ITT, rats were fasted for 6 h. For ITT, a 1.0 IU/kg body weight dose of insulin was administered via intraperitoneal injection on p49 and p75. For both tests, blood glucose was assessed by a tail nick using a portable glucose meter (One Touch Verio) at 0, 15, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min post injection. Total glucose area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the trapezoidal method [14]. For ITT data, the fall rate during the first 30 min following insulin administration was calculated.

16S rRNA Gene Sequencing

Fecal samples were collected at p49 and p84. DNA was extracted from fecal samples as previously described [15]. Briefly, total fecal genomic DNA was isolated using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen) from 200 to 300 mg of stool. 16S rRNA sequencing of the V3‐V4 region was targeted for bacterial sequencing and PCR amplification (International Microbiome Centre) [16]. Subsequently, gene library preparation was performed using a global microbial pooled library composition approach and sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq 16S rRNA gene sequencing platform. Taxonomic classification was completed at the amplicon sequence variant (ASV) level, using SILVA v138 as a reference database [17]. Sequencing depth was calculated, followed by quality control, threshold filtering, and rarefaction steps in R package DADA2 (v1.6). Rare ASVs, less than 20 sequencing reads, and those belonging to Cyanobacteriota phylum, mitochondria family, and chloroplast classes were removed. The rarefied and cleaned dataset was used for the analysis unless otherwise specified [15].

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 9 and are presented as mean ± SEM (n = 8–11 animals/sex/diet). For single time point comparisons, unpaired t‐tests or one‐way ANOVA was used, with Tukey's post hoc test as appropriate. Multiple comparisons were evaluated by two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc tests on males and females separately as significant sex effects were observed. Assessments with multiple time points including body weight, OGTT, and ITT were analyzed using repeated measure, two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc tests. The total AUC was calculated for each OGTT and ITT graph using the trapezoidal method. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient measures [18]. Multiple‐testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) where applicable. Outliers were excluded using ROUT (Q = 1%). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Microbiota Analysis

For microbiota analyses, MicrobiomeAnalyst 2.0 [19] was utilized for preliminary diversity analyses for visualization, interpretation, and integration of data. To adjust for even sequencing depth, the numbers of sequences were rarefied to the same amount as the minimum number of sequences detected. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis was performed to pick the significant and unique taxonomy in each group using a linear discriminant algorithm (LDA) effect size (i.e., LDA score, > 2) where intergroup differences at the phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species level or OTUs (operational taxonomic unit) in each cluster were analyzed. LEfSe was performed with default settings utilizing the Huttenhower Lab interface [20], performed by two‐tailed nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test to evaluate the significance of differences in OTUs in two groups [21]. Differences in alpha diversity across the study groups were estimated by the Shannon index [22]. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix, using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) [23].

Results

Following weaning (p21), animals were randomized to one of four isocaloric diets differing in protein source: casein, whey, complete dairy (skim milk powder), or soy for a 4‐week chow diet phase (p21–p49), followed by a 5‐week HFHS dietary challenge (p50–p84) (Figure 1A). Comparison of the effects of diet and sex on body weight gain (Figure S1A) and fat mass following the chow diet (Figure S1B) revealed significant main effects of both diet (p < 0.001) and sex (p < 0.001). Therefore, sex‐stratified analysis was conducted.

Dietary Protein Source Influences Body Weight Gain and Composition in a Sex‐Dependent Manner

All dietary groups gained weight progressively over both dietary phases. Significant effects of both time and diet were observed in males (Time p < 0.001; Diet p = 0.002) and females (Time p < 0.001; Diet p = 0.01) (Figure 1B,F). Despite consuming similar amounts of food during the chow diet phase (Figure S2), dairy‐fed males exhibited significantly greater cumulative body weight gain compared to both whey‐fed (p < 0.01) and soy‐fed (p < 0.01) animals (Figure 1C). In females, dairy‐fed animals gained significantly more than whey‐fed animals (p < 0.05) (Figure 1G). Despite these differences in cumulative weight gain, assessment of body composition at p49 revealed that dairy‐fed males had significantly lower fat mass percentage compared to whey‐fed animals (p < 0.05), with no significant differences in lean mass across dietary groups (Figure 1D,E). In females, dairy‐fed animals showed lower fat mass and greater lean mass relative to whey‐fed animals (p < 0.05) (Figure 1H,I). These findings indicate that, despite greater total weight gain, complete dairy consumption was associated with a more favorable body composition characterized by reduced adiposity early in development.

Dietary Protein Source Has Sex‐Specific Effects on Glucose Homeostasis and Insulin Sensitivity

To assess whether dietary protein source affected metabolic status, OGTT and ITT were conducted. Glucose excursion curves and AUC are shown in Figure 2. In males, fasting glucose at baseline was higher (p < 0.01) in both whey‐ and soy‐fed animals than in casein‐fed animals, while dairy‐fed animals had lower (p < 0.05) fasting glucose than whey‐fed animals and this tended to decrease (p = 0.086) compared to soy‐fed animals (Figure S3). No differences in fasting glucose were observed in females (Figure S3). During OGTT, no significant differences in blood glucose excursion or total AUC (relative to baseline) were detected across dietary groups in either males (Figure 2A,B) or females (Figure 2E,F), indicating that protein source did not substantially impair or enhance glucose tolerance under normal dietary conditions. In contrast, ITT revealed sex‐specific differences in insulin sensitivity. In males, the glucose fall rate during the first 30 min post insulin injection was significantly lower in dairy‐fed animals compared to all other groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 2D). No significant differences in the insulin‐stimulated glucose fall rate were observed among female dietary groups (Figure 2H). Collectively, results indicate that dairy protein consumption during early development is associated with mild, sex‐specific alterations in insulin sensitivity, most prominently observed in male animals.

Dietary Protein Source Shifts Gut Microbiota Composition and Diversity

Fecal microbial communities were characterized by 16S rRNA gene sequencing at p49, following 4 weeks of dietary protein exposure. Alpha diversity, as estimated by the Shannon index, differed significantly across protein groups in both sexes (Figure 3A,F). In males, dairy‐ and soy‐fed animals exhibited significantly lower alpha diversity compared to casein‐fed (p < 0.01) (Figure 3A). Similarly, in females, dairy‐fed animals showed significantly reduced Shannon diversity relative to casein‐fed (p < 0.01) and whey‐fed (p < 0.01) animals (Figure 3F). These findings suggest that complete dairy exposure during early life consistently attenuates gut microbial richness and evenness relative to casein, the standard reference protein.

Assessment of beta diversity using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and PCoA demonstrated significant separation in overall microbial community structure across dietary groups in both males (PERMANOVA p = 0.001; Figure 3B) and females (PERMANOVA p = 0.001; Figure 3G), confirming that protein source drove distinct microbiota configurations. At the phylum level, Bacillota and Bacteroidota were the dominant taxa across all groups in both sexes. Relative to casein‐ and whey‐fed animals, dairy‐fed animals showed proportionally greater Bacillota abundance, while whey‐fed animals in both sexes showed a more prominent Bacteroidota expansion (Figure 3C,H). Differences were also apparent in the Bacillota to Bacteroidota ratio (F:B) where dairy‐ and soy‐based diets resulted in a greater F:B ratio compared to casein and whey in both sexes (Figure 3D,I). Dairy‐fed animals had a higher F:B ratio than casein‐fed animals in both sexes (Figure 3D,I).

To better gain an understanding of the taxonomic differences in the gut microbiota, LEfSe analysis was utilized (Figure 3E,J). In males (Figure 3E), casein feeding was associated with enrichment of Romboutsia ilealis, Rothia (family Micrococcaceae), and Actinobacteriota taxa. Whey feeding enriched Alistipes finegoldii , Akkermansia muciniphila , and taxa within the orders Lachnospirales and Burkholderiales. Dairy feeding was associated with enrichment of Enterococcus durans and Lactobacillus intestinalis , consistent with the known presence of lactic acid bacteria in dairy‐based diets. Soy feeding was uniquely associated with high abundance of Lactobacillus johnsonii . In females (Figure 3J), casein and whey groups were enriched with Bacteroidota members including Eisenbergiella, Bacteroides intestinalis , Bacteroides acidifaciens , and Bacteroides vulgatus . Dairy feeding in females enriched Akkermansia muciniphila and taxa within the Erysipelotrichales and Verrucomicrobiales orders, while soy feeding was again marked by a strong enrichment of Lactobacillus johnsonii . While changes were largely sex specific, common changes included enrichment of the Verrucomicrobiota lineage under whey and dairy. Soy enriched Lactobacillus johnsonii in both sexes. These microbial signatures suggest that protein source programs distinct bacterial communities early in life, with dairy proteins particularly promoting lactic acid bacteria and mucin‐associated taxa ( Akkermansia muciniphila ).

HFHS Diet Challenge Attenuates Protein‐Driven Differences in Body Weight Gain

Following transition to the HFHS diet (p50–p84), all animals gained weight with a significant diet‐by‐time interaction across groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 4A,I). Cumulative body weight gain during the HFHS phase differed significantly by protein group in males wherein whey‐fed animals gained the least weight compared to all other groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 4B). Due to the crumbly nature of the HFHS pellets and significant cage spillage, an accurate measure of food consumption was not feasible. In females, HFHS‐whey animals also gained significantly less weight than all other groups (p < 0.05) (Figure 4J). Body composition at p84 showed that fat mass percentage did not differ across dietary groups in males following the HFHS challenge (Figure 4C), and female HFHS‐dairy animals had lower fat mass percentage compared to HFHS‐casein animals (p < 0.05) (Figure 4K). Likewise, lean mass was not different across groups in males (Figure 4D), while there was a trend toward higher lean mass in dairy‐fed animals in females (Figure 4L). Collectively, these results indicate that HFHS diet exposure largely abolishes protein‐source‐driven differences in body weight trajectory.

HFHS Diet Impairs Glucose Tolerance and Insulin Sensitivity Across Dietary Groups

Following the HFHS dietary challenge, glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity were reassessed. Fasting glucose was higher in HFHS‐whey‐fed animals than HFHS‐casein (p = 0.067) and HFHS‐dairy (p < 0.05) in males, while in females, HFHS‐soy feeding trended toward a higher fasting glucose relative to HFHS‐dairy and HFHS‐casein (Figure S3). During OGTT, HFHS‐whey‐fed males exhibited the best glucose tolerance, while HFHS‐soy‐fed males exhibited impaired glucose tolerance (Figure 4E,F). In females, HFHS‐soy exhibited better glucose tolerance than other diets (Figure 4M,N). For ITT, HFHS‐soy‐fed males had a higher fall rate relative to HFHS‐dairy (p < 0.05) (Figure 4G,H). A similar pattern was observed in females with HFHS‐soy having a higher insulin‐stimulated glucose fall rate compared to HFHS‐dairy and HFHS‐whey (Figure 4O,P). Under HFHS dietary conditions, soy protein appears to exert divergent metabolic effects in a sex‐dependent manner, impairing glucose tolerance in males compared to dairy while improving glucose and insulin sensitivity in females compared to dairy and whey.

HFHS Diet Reshapes Gut Microbiota With Partial Preservation of Protein‐Source Signatures

Gut microbial community structure was re‐evaluated following the 5‐week HFHS diet challenge. Alpha diversity remained significantly different across dietary groups in both males and females, with the lowest values seen in HFHS‐dairy groups (Figure 5A,F). Beta diversity analysis confirmed significant diet‐driven differences in microbial community composition in both males and females (PERMANOVA p = 0.001; Figure 5B,G). At the phylum level, HFHS‐dairy animals across both sexes showed substantial Bacillota dominance with relatively diminished Bacteroidota, consistent with patterns observed during the chow phase (p < 0.05) (Figure 5C,D,H,I).

LEfSe analysis revealed that HFHS‐casein was enriched for Romboutsia, Rothia, and Micrococcaceae in males and for Bifidobacteriaceae members and Bacteroides intestinalis in females (Figure 5E,J). HFHS‐whey was associated with Alistipes, Rikenellaceae, and Peptostreptococcaceae in males and Micrococcales and Streptococcaceae in females. Discussion

Protein sources influence metabolism and gut microbiota composition, yet whether early‐life exposure to specific dietary proteins, particularly dairy, shapes long‐term susceptibility to obesity and insulin resistance remains poorly understood. In the present study, we compared the metabolic and microbiota impacts of different protein sources. Although microbial signatures from prior protein exposure were preserved, they did not prevent HFHS‐induced glucose intolerance or insulin resistance, indicating that high dietary fat consumption exerted dominant metabolic effects. Microbiota signatures persisted without conferring metabolic protection, raising questions about their functional consequence. Protein type also induced sex‐dependent shifts in gut microbial composition and metabolic outcomes.

Complete dairy (skim milk powder) provided the most consistent benefits for body fat composition compared with other proteins under both chow and HFHS diets, with effects more pronounced in females. While dairy did not fully prevent metabolic damage caused by HFHS feeding, it enriched microbial species associated with favorable gut regulation.

With chow feeding, complete dairy reduced body fat percentage despite comparable or greater total weight gain in both males and females, consistent with clinical observations of fat loss in adults consuming dairy [24]. These effects diminished in males after HFHS feeding, while remaining in females. Complete dairy protein intake has been linked with increased hepatic lipid oxidation and reduced fat accumulation [25]. In line with previous studies [26], whey protein resulted in less body weight gain than casein under high‐fat diet exposure, potentially reflecting its pro‐satiety effects [27]. Another contributing factor may be differences in amino acid composition and release during digestion, as increased amino acid catabolism can promote fat oxidation [28]. Therefore, it is likely that the full fat mass‐lowering effect of dairy requires the combined casein–whey bioactive matrix, including branched‐chain amino acids, calcium, bioactive peptides, and fecal lipid excretion, since neither casein nor whey alone produced comparable reductions in fat mass.

Analysis of metabolic health showed that whey protein enhanced insulin‐stimulated glucose disposal in males on a chow diet. This effect is attributed to insulinotropic and glucose‐lowering properties of whey‐derived bioactive peptides, which inhibit dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 and increase incretin activity [29]. Whey is also enriched in branched‐chain amino acids that activate mTOR‐mediated β‐cell pathways to regulate insulin sensitivity [30]. Following HFHS diet exposure, whey continued to show improved glucose tolerance in males. Interestingly, after HFHS feeding, soy impaired glucose tolerance in males but improved both glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in females, underscoring sex as a critical biological variable. It is well documented that males are more susceptible to the metabolic effects of dietary soy compared to females. While soy protein with isoflavones has been shown to improve glycemic control in postmenopausal women with diabetes and improve insulin sensitivity in ovariectomized monkeys, the same dietary intervention in male monkeys resulted in dose‐dependent increases in insulin resistance and decreased plasma adiponectin [31]. This sex‐specific divergence is further supported by epidemiological findings showing that soy protein intake is positively associated with metabolic syndrome in men but negatively associated in women [32]. Another epidemiological study further supports the negative correlation between soy protein/isoflavone intake and type 2 diabetes risk in women but not men [33]. In spite of this, animals on the HFHS‐soy diet had the highest insulin sensitivity relative to other diets in males. A prior study using male mice showed that soy protein could upregulate PPARγ and AMPKα signaling, key pathways in improving insulin sensitivity relative to whey protein [34], which partially supports the observation in the present study. Another possibility is that the soy diet differentially impacted hepatic versus skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity: hepatic insulin resistance impaired suppression of hepatic glucose output during the OGTT, whereas skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity was preserved or enhanced during the ITT, when pharmacological insulin doses overcome hepatic resistance and directly stimulate peripheral glucose uptake.

Microbiota analysis revealed clear protein‐source‐specific effects. Dairy feeding resulted in reduced alpha diversity in both sexes, across both chow and HFHS diets. Lower alpha diversity in early life should not be reflexively interpreted as dysbiosis, as this relationship is context dependent. In a multicenter study of infants aged 3–16 months, increased alpha diversity was found in the gut microbiome of children with egg allergy compared to healthy controls showing that elevated richness can also reflect aberrant colonization [35]. Children who developed food allergy had lower abundances of Bifidobacterium and Clostridia, indicating that protective signals may reside in specific taxa and their metabolites rather than aggregate diversity. Reduced alpha diversity with dairy protein may therefore reflect selective enrichment of functionally beneficial commensals rather than community impoverishment. Supporting this hypothesis, dairy enriched microbial species positively associated with metabolic health, including Lactobacillus intestinalis in males [36] and Akkermansia muciniphila in females [37], indicating that alpha diversity alone is insufficient to define microbiota health. In contrast, soy consistently increased Lactobacillus johnsonii in both sexes; however, improved insulin sensitivity was observed only in males, not females with chow feeding. While Lactobacillus johnsonii has been linked to intestinal barrier integrity and metabolic health [38], this sex‐specific discrepancy suggests the involvement of mechanisms beyond microbial regulation.

Beta diversity differed significantly across all groups, confirming that protein source shapes distinct microbial community structures. A notable finding was that dairy feeding increased the Bacillota to Bacteroidota (F:B) ratio in both sexes and diets, which parallels the decreased fat mass on the chow diet. The F:B ratio has been debated as a potential biomarker of gut dysbiosis [39] and can vary depending on the type of dairy and its derivatives [9]. A study in children with obesity showed that the relationship between F:B ratio and BMI is age dependent [40], which may relate to differences in microbiome community maturation. In the present study, dairy and casein produced similar body weight outcomes during the HFHS challenge, suggesting that dairy may modulate microbial communities via mechanisms independent of body weight regulation [29].

Remarkably, protein‐driven microbiota signatures were largely preserved after 5 weeks of HFHS feeding, reflecting the resilience conferred by early dietary exposure. Examples include enrichment of Micrococcaceae and Rothia in casein‐fed males, Rikenellaceae in whey‐fed males, Micrococcaceae and Rothia in whey‐fed females, and Akkermansia muciniphila in dairy‐fed females. The role of Micrococcaceae and Rothia in metabolic health remains less clear. Akkermansia muciniphila has been well documented to demonstrate health‐promoting effects by improving lipid metabolism and anti‐inflammatory functions through the production of outer membrane proteins, extracellular vesicles, and/or short‐chain fatty acids (acetate and propionate) [41]. The enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila in HFHS‐dairy‐fed females, along with reduced fat mass, aligns with its known role in improving metabolic health by decreasing adiposity, metabolic endotoxemia, inflammation, and insulin resistance [42], supporting its mechanistic role in mediating dairy's metabolic benefits. Another taxon of interest is the enrichment of Alistipes after whey protein, particularly in males. Alistipes has been found to be enriched in lean individuals [43], suggesting a plausible relationship with metabolism regulation.

In the present study, microbiota signatures were retained without conferring protection against HFHS‐induced glucose intolerance or insulin resistance, raising questions about their functional consequences. Three explanations warrant consideration: (1) early‐life microbiota programming may confer benefits only under specific dietary conditions and fail under severe metabolic stress such as HFHS diet exposure; (2) beneficial microbes enriched by complete dairy may be insufficient (in numbers, metabolites, inflammatory response, etc.) to offset HFHS‐induced metabolic dysfunction; (3) observed microbiota changes may reflect stable compositional shifts without functional consequences.

The study also highlights pronounced sex differences in response to dietary protein that have been consistently reported in both preclinical and clinical populations [44]. For instance, in the present study, effects of dairy on body composition were more evident in females, while insulin sensitivity changes were more pronounced in males. Interestingly, dairy‐fed females exhibited a gut microbial profile resembling that of whey‐fed males, suggesting sex‐dependent microbiota–host interactions. These findings underscore the importance of analyzing males and females separately in nutritional and microbiota research. Limitations of this study include the lack of a true control diet and mechanistic exploration of the findings. While AIN‐93 (casein‐based) is the standard benchmark, all diets in this study altered body composition and gut microbiota, meaning none served as a neutral control. Additionally, the isocaloric diets differed in carbohydrate composition, as skim milk powder (used for the complete dairy group) contains 59% lactose. Therefore, observed changes in gut microbiota may reflect differences in dietary carbohydrates, not protein alone. In addition, the observed outcomes likely reflect multiple interacting pathways including energy balance, nutrient absorption, inflammation, and microbiota metabolism, making selection of specific targets challenging within the current study design.

In conclusion, findings from the present study show that early‐life protein sources are modifiable drivers of microbiota development and downstream metabolic resilience. The effects on metabolic health and microbiota vary depending on protein source and sex after exposure to a HFHS diet. Early‐life dairy feeding reduces fat mass and enriches microbes that are associated with improved metabolic health. These effects were more prominent in females when challenged with a HFHS diet, with less fat mass, increased Bacillota to Bacteroidota ratio, and enrichment of Bifidobacterium and Alistipes species. Overall, identifying the dairy‐associated microbial features may reveal diet‐microbiota targets for reducing obesity and insulin resistance risk across development.
