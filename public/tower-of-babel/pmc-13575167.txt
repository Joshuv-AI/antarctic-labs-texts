De-occluding broadband metalens
Authors: Seungwoo Yoon, Dohyun Kang, Eunsue Choi, Sohyun Lee, Seoyeon Kim, Minho Choi, Hyeonsu Heo, Dong-Ha Shin, Suha Kwak, Arka Majumdar, Junsuk Rho, Seung-Hwan Baek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obstructions such as raindrops, fences, or dust degrade images, especially when cleaning is infeasible. Conventional approaches use bulky compound-lens arrays or computational inpainting, compromising compactness or fidelity. Metalenses promise compact imaging, but achieving both broadband and obstruction-free imaging remains challenging, since a metalens cannot simultaneously focus distant scenes and defocus nearby occlusions across a broadband spectrum. We introduce a de-occluding broadband metalens that suppresses obstructions while enabling broadband imaging with a learned phase profile. Our approach divides the spectrum of each color channel into pass and stop bands with multi-band spectral filtering; the metalens focuses light from far objects through pass bands, then light from nearby obstructions falls in the stop bands and is rejected. A neural network further enhances imaging quality. Compared with a conventional hyperbolic metalens under obstructions, our system improves PSNR by 32.29% and raises performance on object detection and two semantic segmentation benchmarks by 13.54%, 48.45% and 20.35%, respectively. This promises robust obstruction-free sensing and vision for compact systems, including mobile robots, drones, and endoscopes.

Introduction

Obstructions such as fences, leaves, dirt, or raindrops frequently occlude distant scenes in everyday imaging1–5. These occlusions degrade the imaging quality and reliability of downstream vision applications, especially in autonomous or compact systems where manual removal of obstacles is infeasible–for instance, cameras on drones5,6, mobile robots3,7, or endoscopes8,9. Obstruction-free imaging aims to recover distant scenes covered by near-depth obstacles. Existing computational approaches tend to hallucinate the occluded content rather than capture the true scene10–13. Optical solutions using multiple compound lenses, or synthetic apertures, can physically remove near-depth obstructions by depth-of-field control14–17. However, their bulky and costly designs hinder integration into compact imaging systems (Supplementary Note 3).

Flat diffractive optics–particularly metalenses composed of subwavelength meta-atoms–offer a promising alternative to bulky compound lenses, as ultra-thin and lightweight imaging elements18–21. Yet, achieving both broadband imaging and obstruction-free imaging with a single metalens remains fundamentally constrained by depth-wavelength symmetry; the point spread function (PSF) change induced by a wavelength shift is similar to that of an appropriate depth shift of a point light source22–27. This depth-wavelength symmetry prevents simultaneous broadband imaging and defocusing for near-depth obstructions. While hybrid diffractive-compound refractive design28,29 or dispersion engineering30–35 mitigate this phenomenon, bulky refractive elements negate the advantage of compactness, and dispersion control is typically restricted to micrometer-scale apertures or extremely low numerical apertures, falling short of practical imaging needs (Supplementary Note 2).

This work introduces a de-occluding broadband metalens for simultaneous obstruction-free and broadband imaging (Fig. 1). We first derive an analytical model describing the depth-wavelength symmetry in diffractive lenses. Based on this understanding, we split the spectrum of each RGB channel into pass and stop bands using a multi-band spectral filter. This spectral splitting extends the metalens design space in a depth-wavelength decoupled manner. Our de-occluding broadband metalens achieves broadband obstruction-free imaging, yielding up to 23.90% PSNR improvement on unobstructed scenes compared to conventional hyperbolic metalenses, and 32.29 or 11.45% PSNR improvement on obstructed scenes compared to hyperbolic or broadband metalenses without a de-occluding approach (broadband baseline), respectively. We believe that the de-occluding broadband metalens promises robust obstruction-free imaging and perception in compact platforms such as mobile robots, drones, and endoscopes.

Depth-wavelength symmetry

Let Pλ,z denote the PSF of a diffractive lens for a point light source located at depth z and wavelength of λ. The depth-wavelength symmetry refers to the observation23–27,36 that the PSF remains invariant under a coupled shift in wavelength Δ λ and depth Δ z: 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{\lambda+\Delta \lambda,z}\approx {P}_{\lambda,z+\Delta z}.$$\end{document}Pλ+Δλ,z≈Pλ,z+Δz.

Here we present the first analytical model of depth-wavelength symmetry that explicitly relates the wavelength shift Δ λ to the corresponding depth shift Δ z. This model provides the theoretical foundation for the design of our de-occluding broadband metalens. We begin by considering the ideal phase profile that focuses a plane wave to a focal length f for all wavelengths λ. In practice, this condition is satisfied only at a design wavelength λd. For wavelengths λ > λd, the resulting mismatched phase can be interpreted, under the paraxial approximation, as the conjugate phase of the spherical phase of a point source located at the corresponding depth z. Consequently, if the incident light of wavelength λ originates from this depth z rather than infinity, its spherical wavefront counterbalances the chromatic phase mismatch, thereby yielding the sharp focus at f, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. Design of de-occluding broadband metalens

De-occluding broadband imaging requires a metalens that produces sharp PSFs for distant targets at far depth, zfar (typically zfar ≥ 0.5 m), while simultaneously inducing strong blur for obstructions located at near depth znear ≃ 0.045 m. The normally constant value of znear models the prevalent scenario where obstructions adhere directly onto the optical window, such as a cover glass. Significantly, the depth-wavelength symmetry fundamentally limits this simultaneous far-depth sharp imaging and near-depth defocusing in the visible. If a metalens forms a sharp far-depth PSF at wavelength λ, the corresponding near-depth PSF at the shifted wavelength λ + Δ λ, also becomes sharp, where the wavelength shift Δ λ is given by Eq. (3) with Δ z = znear−zfar. If this shifted wavelength λ + Δ λ remains within the sensor’s spectral response range, light from near-depth obstructions is also sharply captured, preventing de-occlusion.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce a split-spectrum design. Characterization of de-occluding broadband metalens

We employ geometric-phase anisotropic meta-atoms whose structural parameters were optimized via rigorous coupled-wave analysis to maximize the average conversion efficiency across the pass bands of the multi-band spectral filter, centered at 457, 530, and 628 nm. Silicon nitride (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{\rm{SiN}}}}}_{x}$$\end{document}SiNx) is chosen as the structural material owing to its sufficiently high refractive index and low extinction coefficient across the visible spectrum (Supplementary Note 6). The selected geometry–period p = 395 nm, height h = 700 nm, width w = 305 nm, and length l = 125 nm–yields average conversion efficiencies of 77.2, 73.0, and 54.4% across the respective pass bands (Supplementary Note 7). This high efficiency is uniformly maintained, with low standard deviations of the conversion efficiencies across wavelengths within each pass band (2.2, 0.2, and 4.9%, respectively), confirming their broadband characteristics (Supplementary Table S5)38. Beyond the achieved optical performance, the selected single-cell PB-phase design employs simple meta-atom structures that facilitate fabrication. Supplementary Note 15 provides a detailed discussion of spectral throughput, polarization throughput, sensitivity, robustness, and potential limitations. De-occluding broadband imaging

We evaluate the de-occluding and broadband imaging performance of the three metalenses. To recover high-quality images from the raw optical measurements, we further employ a reconstruction network39 trained individually for each metalens. The reconstruction network used throughout this work is trained separately from the metalens optimization so that the de-occluding behavior is enforced at the raw sensor-capture level, ensuring that obstruction suppression originates from the optical design rather than being compensated by the computational backend. The rationale for this decoupled strategy is discussed in Supplementary Note 4.

Figure 5 shows both the raw sensor measurements and the reconstructed images. The hyperbolic-phase metalens exhibits strong chromatic aberration and visible obstruction artifacts, demonstrating its limitation in achieving broadband and obstruction-free imaging. The broadband metalens without the de-occluding strategy improves broadband performance but fails to suppress near-depth obstructions that inevitably arise from the depth-wavelength symmetry. In contrast, the de-occluding broadband metalens leaves only faint obstruction traces in the raw measurements, allowing the neural network to reconstruct underlying structures reliably rather than hallucinating them. Quantitatively, the broadband metalens without a de-occluding strategy attains a PSNR of 18.79 dB, corresponding to an 18.7% improvement over the conventional hyperbolic design under obstruction. The de-occluding broadband metalens achieves a PSNR of 20.94 dB, equivalent to a 32.29% increase, confirming the advantage of our spectrally separated design. Downstream vision tasks

We further evaluate downstream vision tasks on images captured with each fabricated metalens to assess the practical utility of de-occluding broadband imaging. Off-the-shelf vision models are employed without fine-tuning on our metalens-captured data to demonstrate the universality of our imaging approach. We benchmark three representative scenarios: (1) object detection40 on drone-captured aerial imagery using VisDrone41, (2) semantic segmentation for medical endoscopy42 on Kvasir-SEG43, and (3) semantic segmentation for autonomous driving44 on Cityscapes45. For controlled quantitative evaluation with annotations, these benchmark tasks were performed by optically re-capturing printed validation images from the corresponding datasets through the fabricated metalenses, while complementary real-scene downstream-vision demonstrations are provided in Supplementary Note 17 as well as Supplementary Videos 4 and 5. As shown in Fig. 6, our de-occluding broadband metalens consistently achieves the best performance across all tasks: it attains an mAP of 0.1704 on VisDrone (vs. 0.0350/0.0292 for hyperbolic/broadband), an IoU of 0.8317 on Kvasir-SEG (vs. 0.3472/0.5950), and an mIoU of 0.6701 on Cityscapes (vs. 0.4666/0.4601) under task-specific obstruction scenarios. These results establish our de-occluding broadband metalens as a practical imaging solution for embedded and unmanned platforms such as drones, mobile robots, and endoscopic probes, where near-depth obstructions are prevalent and physical cleaning is often infeasible. Discussion

This work presents a single-shot obstruction-free imaging method using a de-occluding broadband metalens. We derive an analytical model for the depth-wavelength relationship of dispersive metalenses and harness it for broadband obstruction-free imaging through split-spectrum filtering of focused near-depth lights. We learn the metalens design via a differentiable framework based on the extended design space provided by the spectrum splitting, achieving PSNR of 20.94 dB under obstructed conditions. This corresponds to a 32.29 or 11.45% improvement compared to hyperbolic or broadband metalens without a de-occluding strategy, respectively. Importantly, this obstruction-suppression capability does not come at the cost of the imaging performance; our system maintains high-fidelity imaging even for unobstructed scenes, achieving a PSNR of 23.41 dB, which corresponds to a 23.90% improvement over the conventional hyperbolic metalens (refer to Supplementary Notes 10, 13, and 14). Furthermore, our de-occluding imaging directly translates to robust computer vision performance; our method achieves absolute gains of +13.54/+14.12% mAP, 48.45/23.67% IoU, and 20.35/21.00% mIoU compared to the two baseline metalenses.

The present implementation targets a finite near-depth range centered around (znear ≃ 0.045 m). This operating depth range can be extended by using a narrower pass-band design, with trade-offs including reduced spectral transmission, as analyzed in Supplementary Note 1. The current metalens was optimized using a normal-incidence PSF model and has a finite field of view, and spatially varying aberrations are mitigated by positional encoding in the reconstruction network. Further details on the field of view and the reconstruction network are discussed in the “Methods” section, Supplementary Notes 5 and 9.

Several avenues for future work remain. First, our analytical model for the depth-wavelength symmetry and split-spectrum strategy is also applicable to other diffractive optics applications, potentially providing a mathematical foundation to improve performance in color holography46,47, depth sensing48,49, and hyperspectral imaging27,50. Second, while the current implementation employs a commercial multi-band filter, jointly optimizing the metalens and spectral filter—or directly incorporating spectral filtering into meta-atom transmittance—could expand the design space, improve light efficiency, and enable monolithic implementations51. Third, while our current geometric-phase design prioritizes robust large-area manufacturing using simple meta-atom structures52,53, it requires additional polarization optics. However, our split-spectrum principle fundamentally relies on depth-wavelength symmetry rather than the geometric-phase mechanism itself; future implementations utilizing propagation-phase metalenses could potentially eliminate these optics and further reduce the overall optical-stack thickness (Supplementary Note 1). Finally, explicitly incorporating fabrication constraints into the inverse design of complex free-form geometries represents a promising pathway toward maximizing both optical performance and scalability. We anticipate that our obstruction-free broadband imaging is an important step towards robust sensing and vision, not only for unmanned systems like mobile robots, drones, and endoscopes, where manual obstruction removal is challenging, but also for applications with structure-induced occlusions, such as under-display cameras and shielded cameras.

Baseline metalens design

The hyperbolic metalens is designed at a wavelength of 532 nm. The broadband baseline is trained using the same optimization pipeline as the de-occluding metalens, but without the near-depth obstruction model and without the multi-band bandpass filter. Therefore, it targets sharp far-depth focusing across the broadband visible spectrum. Further details on the metalens training pipeline are described in Supplementary Note 4.

Imaging setup

For controlled quantitative evaluation, imaging experiments and vision benchmarks in the main paper were performed using a benchtop setup enabling repeatable lens exchange, ground-truth acquisition, and fixed obstruction geometry. Representative compact-prototype results are provided as an integration-feasibility demonstration in Supplementary Notes 14 and 17 as well as Supplementary Videos 4 and 5. Details on the benchtop imaging setup and compact camera system specifications—including optical-stack dimensions, estimated housing volume, and power consumption—are provided in Supplementary Note 9.

Image reconstruction specifications

We adopt LocalNet39 as the image reconstruction backend. High-resolution printed targets containing rich texture and color variations from the DIV2K dataset37 were used to train the neural network. Obstructed scenes were captured with the metalenses, while reference ground-truth scenes (without obstructions) were acquired with a compound lens. A focal length of f = 8 mm (twice that of the metalenses) was used for the ground truth captures to achieve better object space resolution54. The compound-lens captured ground-truth images serve as supervision to correct residual aberrations and remove remaining obstruction artifacts.
