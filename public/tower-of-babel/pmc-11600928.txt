Risk factors and prognostic analysis of microscopic positive esophageal margins after radical surgery for proximal gastric cancer
Authors: Zhenjiang Guo, Ning Wang, Guangyuan Zhao, Chaobo Cui, Fangzhen Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProximal gastric cancer has been on the rise worldwide in recent years. A positive surgical margin may result in incomplete tumor and affect the prognosis of patients. This study aims to analyse the risk factors for and prognosis associated with microscopic positive esophageal margins (R1 resection) after radical surgery for proximal gastric cancer patients.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of 316 patients with proximal gastric cancer who underwent surgical resection at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery of Hengshui People’s Hospital from January 2013 to June 2018. Patients were divided into the R1 group (n = 24) and R0 group (n = 292) according to the esophageal margin status. Differences in clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis between the two groups were compared.ResultsTumor location at the esophagogastric junction, Borrmann type 3/4, Lauren diffuse/mixed type, margin distance < 3 cm, pT4 stage, and vascular invasion were identified as independent risk factors for positive esophageal margins in proximal gastric cancer patients (all P < 0.05). The 5-year overall survival rate was significantly lower in the R1 group than in the R0 group (45.8% vs. 64.2%, P < 0.05). Subgroup analysis revealed that the 5-year overall survival rate was significantly lower in the R1 group in the pT2-3 and pN0 stages (P < 0.05), while there was no significant difference in the pT4 and pN(+) stages (P > 0.05). Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that Borrmann type, Lauren type, pT stage, pN stage, and lymphovascular invasion were independent risk factors affecting the prognosis of patients with proximal gastric cancer (all P < 0.05), while esophageal margin status was not an independent risk factor affecting prognosis (P > 0.05).ConclusionPositive esophageal margins in proximal gastric cancer are associated with various clinicopathological factors and lead to a worse prognosis in patients with pT2-3 and pN0 stages but do not affect the prognosis of patients with pT4 and pN(+) stages.

Introduction

Gastric cancer is a significant global health burden, ranking fifth in incidence and third in cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Although the incidence of distal gastric cancer has gradually declined, there has been a notable surge in proximal gastric cancer cases in recent decades [2–4]. The definition of proximal gastric cancer varies across studies, generally spanning tumors near the esophagogastric junction or involving the upper one-third of the stomach [5]. Despite advances in treatment strategies, the prognosis for patients with proximal gastric cancer remains relatively poor compared to that for patients with distal gastric cancer [6].

The critical treatment for proximal gastric cancer is radical surgical resection with the aim of achieving complete tumor resection. However, there is still a subset of patients with positive resection margins, indicating residual tumor cells at the surgical margin. Previous studies have explored the risk factors for positive margins in gastric cancer patients and their impact on prognosis. A retrospective study in the Netherlands showed that tumor location, cT stage, pN stage, histological type, and tumor differentiation were associated with positive margins in patients with gastric cancer [7]. Another study in Japan demonstrated that positive margins in gastric cancer patients are significantly associated with residual gastric cancer, a tumor size ≥ 80 mm, a histopathological diagnosis of undifferentiated type, Borrmann type 4, or pT4 stage. The incidence of positive margins increased as the number of risk factors increased [8]. Moiș E et al. [9] showed that tumor size, the presence of other organ invasions, pT staging, pN staging, vascular, lymphatic, and perineural invasion, Lauren type, and tumor grade were significantly associated with the occurrence of positive margins in gastric cancer. Nakanishi K et al. [10] applied propensity score matching to analyse the effect of margin status on the prognosis of gastric cancer patients, and the results showed that the recurrence-free survival of patients in the margin-positive group was significantly shorter than that of patients in the margin-negative group. In the subgroup analyses, the prognosis of patients with stage pI-III margin-positive gastric cancer was worse than that of patients with stage pIV margin-negative gastric cancer, whereas there was no significant difference in the prognosis of patients with stage pIV margin-positive gastric cancer. Overall recurrence was greater in the margin-positive group than in the margin-negative group (75% vs. 58%). Gaspar-Figueiredo S et al. [11] examined the impact of margin status on the prognosis of diffuse gastric cancer patients and showed that patients with microscopic margin positivity were susceptible to early recurrence, which led to poorer overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Hirata Y et al. [12] showed that compared with patients with negative margins, patients with R1 margins were more likely to have aggressive clinicopathologic features and worse 5-year OS. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that in patients with gastric cancer (excluding EGJ carcinoma), R1 margins identified by postoperative pathologic examination may be strongly associated with poor OS [13].However, there is a significant lack of studies on proximal gastric cancer. Patients undergoing transabdominal resection are more likely to have positive upper margins. Therefore, this study focused on the issue of positive esophageal margins, aiming to address this gap and provide insights to improve the prognosis of patients with proximal gastric cancer.

Materials and methods

Study Population

Clinicopathological data of patients with proximal gastric cancer who underwent radical surgery at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery of Hengshui People’s Hospital between January 2013 and June 2018 were retrospectively analysed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a tumor located within 1 cm above to 5 cm below the esophagogastric junction (EGJ), within the gastric fundus, or within the upper one-third of the gastric lesser curvature. (2) no visible tumor residue during surgery. (3) without preoperative neoadjuvant therapy. (4) definitive pathologic diagnosis of invasion of margins. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) missing important clinicopathologic information. (2) multifocal gastric cancer. (3) concurrent presence of other active tumors. (4) positive distal surgical margins. (5) lost to follow-up. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hengshui People’s Hospital (Ethics No. 2022-2-015).

Study methods

The surgery is performed by a fixed team, including laparoscopic or open approaches for proximal gastrectomy or total gastrectomy, combined with D2 lymphadenectomy. For proximal gastrectomy, an esophagogastric anastomosis was performed, whereas for total gastrectomy, esophago-jejunal Roux-en-Y anastomosis was performed.For patients with a tumor diameter of ≤ 4 cm, if imaging examinations do not indicate metastasis to lymph nodes No. 4d, 5, or 6, proximal gastrectomy may be considered. However, it is essential to ensure that at least half of the distal remnant stomach is preserved to maintain postoperative gastrointestinal function and the patient’s quality of life. The surgical plan should be adjusted based on the patient’s specific condition and preoperative assessment results. Patients with negative margins were treated with systemic chemotherapy after surgery, and the regimens included capecitabine or tiglio alone, capecitabine combined with oxaliplatin (CapeOX), tiglio combined with oxaliplatin (SOX), and fluorouracil combined with oxaliplatin (FOLFOX). Patients in the R1 group were treated with chemotherapy in combination with local radiotherapy. Age, sex, tumor size, tumor location, Borrmann type, Lauren type, margin distance, pT stage, pN stage, and vascular invasion data were collected from the patients. The staging criteria for proximal gastric cancer are based on the eighth edition of the AJCC staging system.

Diagnostic for positive margins

Surgical specimens were fixed in formalin and examined histopathologically by standard hematoxylin-eosin staining. The surgeon selectively performs intraoperative frozen pathology examinations based on clinical experience. For patients with positive margins, an additional resection is conducted, followed by postoperative pathological evaluation of the margins. Positive margins were defined as microscopic residual tumor cells within l mm of the margin. Negative margins were defined as no microscopic residual tumor cells found within l mm from the margin. The R0 group comprised patients with negative esophageal margins, whereas the R1 group included those with positive esophageal margin. Follow-up

All patients were followed up by outpatient review, telephone, or hospitalization. Follow-up visits were conducted every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for the next 3 years, and annually thereafter. The endpoint event was follow-up until death or the end of follow-up, with the last follow-up on June 30, 2023. Overall survival time was defined as the time from the pathologic diagnosis of gastric cancer to the patient’s death or the end of follow-up.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software (version 24, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Discrete variables are expressed as cases or rates (%), while continuous variables are expressed as M (P25, P75). Clinicopathologic factors that were significantly different between the two groups were analysed by multivariable logistic regression. The Kaplan‒Meier method was used to construct survival curves, the log-rank test was used to compare the differences in overall survival between different subgroups, the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was used to analyse the factors affecting the overall survival of patients, and differences were considered to be statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Discussion

The margin status of proximal gastric cancer directly determines the extent of radical surgery, and a positive margin will result in incomplete resection of the tumor, often requiring remedial treatment. The incidence rate of positive esophageal margins in this study was 7.59%, which is consistent with the positive margin rate of 1.8–8.4% for gastric cancer reported in previous studies [14], and the relatively high incidence may be related to the study population being limited to proximal gastric cancer. While intraoperative frozen pathology offers high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy in detecting margin invasion, its widespread implementation is impractical [15]. Limitations such as technical constraints in accommodating multiple extended resections in patients undergoing a transabdominal approach and technical challenges in performing intraoperative freezing in those undergoing total laparoscopic anastomosis, coupled with increased surgical duration and hospitalization costs, necessitate a selective approach to its use. Therefore, it should be reserved for patients with a high risk of margin infiltration and those for whom re-excision is technically feasible. Studying the high-risk factors for positive margins in proximal gastric cancer patients is helpful for screening patients who need intraoperative frozen pathology.

In this study, Borrmann type 3/4, pT4 stage, Lauren diffuse/mixed type, and vascular invasion were found to be independent risk factors for positive esophageal margins in proximal gastric cancer patients, and patients with these pathological features had a greater risk of gastric wall infiltration, which is especially likely to occur in the submucosal layer, and even jump metastasis [16]. In addition, patients in the esophagogastric junction are more likely to have positive esophageal margins, which may be related to the fact that tumors in this area are more likely to invade the esophagus, the difficulty of exposure during surgery, the narrow operating space, and the restriction of the length of esophageal freeing length upwards during the transabdominal transhiatal approach [17, 18]. There is no consensus on the optimal margin distance to ensure complete resection of the tumor and the safety of the anastomosis. In this study, a margin distance of < 3 cm was a risk factor for positive esophageal margins. Similarly, an Italian study concluded that a margin distance < 3 cm was an independent risk factor for positive margins in patients with pT2-T4 stage disease [15]. Mine S et al. [19]studied the feasibility of shorter margin distances in patients with Siewert type II/III AEG undergoing total gastrectomy and showed that a margin distance of > 2 cm was safe in patients with Siewert type II/III AEG. A Chinese expert consensus on endoscopic surgery for Siewert II adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction suggested that the esophageal margin should be ≥ 3 cm from the upper margin of the tumor in Siewert II AEG patients with stage cT2 or higher disease and suggested that the need for intraoperative frozen pathology examination should be assessed and judged based on the factors of tumor stage and the distance of the esophageal margin [20]. According to the results of this study, tumors located at the esophagogastric junction and those exhibiting aggressive histologic features, such as Lauren diffuse/mixed subtypes and Borrmann types 3/4, are at a greater risk of having positive esophageal margins, reflecting the technical challenges associated with surgical management. It is recommended that an intraoperative frozen pathology examination be performed if there are risk factors. It should be noted that with advances in imaging technology, the rate of compliance between preoperative cT staging and pathologic pT staging is very high [21–23]. Therefore, except for vascular invasion, which requires postoperative pathologic evaluation, all other factors can be evaluated preoperatively or intraoperatively.

We further investigated the effect of esophageal margin status on the prognosis of proximal gastric cancer patients, and the results showed that the 5-year OS of margin-positive patients was significantly worse than that of margin-negative patients (45.8% vs. 64.2%). However, further Cox proportional hazards model analysis revealed that a positive esophageal margin was not an independent risk factor for prognosis and that the poor prognosis of margin-positive patients may be because these patients were staged later than margin-negative patients. Therefore, it was necessary to stratify the analysis by pT and pN stage, and the results showed that the prognosis of patients with positive margins for the pT2-3 or pN0 stage was significantly worse than that of patients with negative margins, suggesting a survival benefit for these patients due to negative margins. In contrast, for patients with pT4 or pN(+) stage, there was no significant difference in survival between patients with negative and positive esophageal margins. In this group of patients, positiveesophageal margins were mostly associated with advanced tumor staging and high aggressiveness, which are pathologic factors that lead to a poorer prognosis for patients, thus masking the prognostic impact of margin esophageal status. Woo JW et al. [24] showed that positive margins in gastric cancer mainly affected the prognosis of patients with stage pN1-2 or pN0-1 disease. Sun et al.. reported that R1 resection only affected the prognosis of patients with T1-T2 gastric cancer, whereas Cho et al. [25] reported that only patients with pN0-1 stage gastric cancer had a poorer survival rate. All these studies suggest that the effect of positive margins on the prognosis of gastric cancer patients is stage dependent, which is consistent with our findings. Although the status of the esophageal margins is not an independent prognostic factor for overall survival, it significantly impacts the prognosis of patients with pT2-3 or pN0 stage, where tumors have not invaded the serosa or there is no lymph node metastasis. In these cases, patients with positive esophageal margins have a markedly worse prognosis compared to those with negative margins. Therefore, during surgical procedures, it is crucial to consider the possibility of extensive resection to achieve negative esophageal margins. Conversely, for patients with pT4 or pN + stages, the status of the esophageal margins does not exhibit a significant prognostic difference. It is essential to thoroughly evaluate the risks associated with extended resection against the potential survival benefits. For patients preoperatively staged as cT4 or cN(+), neoadjuvant therapy may be beneficial in downstaging the tumor, which could subsequently improve long-term outcomes for these patients.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with some selection bias in the included population. Second, preoperative staging was not included as a study variable because it was missing in some cases. Finally, recurrence and metastasis patterns were not further analysed due to incomplete follow-up information. Nevertheless, this study highlights the importance of accurate margin assessment and emphasizes the need for meticulous surgical techniques to achieve negative resection margins, particularly in patients with early-stage disease where margin status significantly influences prognosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients with proximal gastric cancer, particularly those with tumors at the esophagogastric junction, Borrmann type 3/4, Lauren diffuse/mixed subtype, margin distance < 3 cm, pT4 stage, and evidence of vascular invasion, face a higher risk of positive esophageal margins. Given these risk factors, the use of intraoperative frozen pathology is strongly recommended to ensure negative margins, especially for patients with pT2-3 or pN0 stage.
