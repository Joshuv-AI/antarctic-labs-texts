Influence of endodontic irrigants on hydraulic cements: solubility, color alteration and surface changes
Authors: Sıla Usta, Cangül Keskin, Ayşe Oktay, Emmanuel João Nogueira Leal Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study evaluated the effects of different endodontic irrigation solutions on the solubility, color alteration, and surface roughness alterations of PD MTA and Biodentine.Materials and MethodsCylindrical samples of PD MTA and Biodentine were prepared and subsequently immersed in distilled water, 17% EDTA, 5% NaOCl, 9% HEDP, and 0.2% chitosan nanoparticle solutions for 24 hours. Solubility was determined as the percentage mass loss at 24 h compared to initial values. Color alteration was analyzed using a spectrophotometer (CIE Lab system), and surface roughness (Ra and Sa) was measured with a confocal microscope. Data were statistically analyzed with Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U, and Wilcoxon tests at a 5% significance threshold.ResultsNo significant differences in solubility were observed between PD MTA and Biodentine, but EDTA and chitosan nanoparticles significantly increased solubility compared to other solutions (p<0.05). Biodentine exhibited greater color alteration than PD MTA for all solutions except distilled water (p<0.05), with NaOCl causing the most pronounced color alteration (p<0.05). PD MTA showed higher surface roughness before and after immersion (p<0.05), with HEDP inducing the greatest roughness alterations in both materials (p<0.05).ConclusionEndodontic irrigants affect the physical properties of hydraulic cements, with varying impacts on solubility, color alteration, and surface roughness. Selecting appropriate irrigation solutions is essential to maintaining material integrity and esthetic outcomes in clinical practice.Keywords: chitosan, endodontics, HEDP, sodium hypochlorite, solubility, surface roughness

Introduction

Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), primarily composed of Portland cement with the addition of bismuth oxide for radiopacity, was the first hydraulic cement introduced into dentistry in the early 1990s (1). Its biocompatibility, sealing ability, and ability to promote tissue regeneration revolutionized endodontic treatments. However, traditional MTA formulations presented limitations, including prolonged setting time, difficult handling, and a tendency to cause discoloration, leading to the development of modified hydraulic cements with enhanced clinical performance (2). One of these improved formulations is PD MTA (Produits Dentaires, Vevey, Switzerland), which contains tricalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, calcium oxide, and calcium tungstate as an opacifier. According to the manufacturer, PD MTA reduces the risk of discoloration associated with bismuth oxide while offering finer particle size distribution, allowing for easier handling and improved mixing with its hydrophilic powder (3). Another alternative is Biodentine (Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France), a material specifically designed as a dentin substitute. Its formulation also includes calcium carbonate, a water-soluble polymer, calcium chloride as a setting accelerator, and zirconium oxide as a radiopacifier. Biodentine has demonstrated favorable clinical performance in various endodontic applications and is often preferred for its improved handling properties and lower risk of inducing color alteration (4). Moreover, Biodentine could be both a temporary enamel restoration and a definitive dentine substitute (5).

During endodontic treatment, hydraulic cements frequently come into contact with chelating and antimicrobial irrigation solutions, which can significantly alter their physical and chemical properties (6, 7). Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), one of the most widely used antimicrobial irrigants, possesses strong oxidative potential and can contribute to material degradation over time (8). Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), a standard chelating agent for smear layer removal, has been shown to interfere with the setting reaction and compromise the mechanical strength of hydraulic cements (6). As an alternative to EDTA, 1-hydroxyethylidene 1,1-diphosphonate (HEDP) has been introduced due to its milder demineralizing effect, which may help preserve cement integrity while still ensuring effective smear layer removal (9). Another emerging irrigant is chitosan nanoparticles, which have gained attention for their biocompatibility, antimicrobial properties, and potential for dentin remineralization (10). However, their specific effects on hydraulic cements remain largely unexplored. These irrigation solutions can disrupt the hydration process of hydraulic cements, interfering with the formation of calcium silicate hydrates and portlandite—both critical for structural integrity and mechanical strength (11, 12), ultimately affecting their durability and sealing ability. Another frequently reported consequence is discoloration, which often results from oxidative reactions or the breakdown of cement components upon exposure to these solutions (6). Collectively, these interactions underscore the potential for irrigation solutions to compromise the clinical performance of hydraulic cements, reinforcing the need for a thorough understanding of their effects to optimize long-term outcomes.

The literature highlights the wide variety of hydraulic cements and irrigation solutions, each with distinct chemical compositions and properties. However, there is a clear and important gap concerning the interactions between alternative irrigants such as HEDP and chitosan nanoparticles, and hydraulic cements. Existing studies tend to focus on antimicrobial efficacy or basic cytotoxicity of these irrigants, but far fewer examine their downstream effects on the physical and chemical integrity of various hydraulic cements.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of different irrigation solutions—distilled water, NaOCl, EDTA, HEDP, and chitosan nanoparticles—on the color alteration, solubility, and surface topography of PD MTA and Biodentine. The null hypothesis tested was that there would be no statistically significant differences in the solubility, color alteration, and surface roughness of these cements following exposure to the different solutions.

Materials and Methods

Study design and sample size estimation

This laboratory study manuscript was prepared following the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology (PRILE) 2021 guidelines, as illustrated in Figure 1. The sample size for this study was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1, Heinrich Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) from a previously published study with a comparable methodology. The calculation was performed using a significance level (α) of 0.05, an effect size of 0.536, and a statistical power of 0.85 (6). These parameters indicated that a minimum of eight samples per group (n=8) was required to ensure sufficient power to detect statistically significant differences among the experimental groups.

PRILE Flowchart

Preparation and characterization of chitosan nanoparticles

Chitosan nanoparticles were synthesized using the ionic gelation technique (13). Low molecular weight chitosan (50–190 kDa, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), characterized by its solubility in aqueous acids and a viscosity range of 20–300 cP, was used. A total of 200 mg of chitosan was dissolved in 100 mL of a 1% (w/w) acetic acid aqueous solution under continuous magnetic stirring at 1000 rpm for approximately 2 hours, ensuring complete dissolution. The resulting homogeneous solution, with a pH of approximately 3.2, was then mixed with a sodium tripolyphosphate (TPP) solution (1 mg/mL, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), which was added dropwise under constant stirring. The chitosan-to-TPP weight ratio was maintained at 8:1. To achieve uniform nanoparticle formation, the mixture was further homogenized at 5000 rpm for 15 minutes using an UltraTurrax homogenizer (IKA, Staufen, Germany). The physicochemical properties of the synthesized chitosan nanoparticles, including particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential, were assessed in aqueous media using a Z3000-NICOMP Nano Optical Particle Size Analyzer. Measurements were performed in triplicate at a controlled temperature of 25.0 ± 0.1°C and a detection angle of 90°. The results were reported as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3) to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Sample preparation

Samples of PD MTA (Produits Dentaires, Vevey, Switzerland) and Biodentine (Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France) were prepared according to the manufacturers' instructions and carefully placed into cylindrical molds (7 mm in diameter × 1.5 mm in height) (n = 40 for each material). The samples were then incubated at 37°C and 100% relative humidity for 24 hours to ensure complete setting. After this period, each specimen was weighed three times using a high-precision analytical balance (accuracy: 10⁻⁴ g) (Mettler Toledo AT201, Greifensee, Switzerland), and the mean baseline value was recorded. Surface roughness (Ra, Sa (µm)) was measured using a confocal microscope (Smartproof 5, Zeiss, Germany), and color measurements were performed according to the CIE Lab system using a spectrophotometer (CM-26d, Konica Minolta, Osaka, Japan). Baseline values were recorded for all specimens. Subsequently, the samples were immersed in the designated solutions for 24 hours (n=8) as follows: distilled water, 17% EDTA, 5% NaOCl, 9% HEDP (Dual Rinse HEDP, Medcem, Weinfelden, Switzerland), and 0.2% chitosan nanoparticles.

Solubility assessment

All experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the ISO 6876/2012 standard. The molds were immersed in the designated irrigation solutions within glass plates and incubated in cabinets at a constant temperature of 37°C ± 1°C. After the immersion period of 24 hours according to the protocol, the samples were carefully removed, rinsed with 1 mL of distilled water to eliminate residual moisture, and placed in desiccators containing anhydrous calcium sulfate for 24 hours to ensure thorough drying and in a heating oven which capable of being maintained at a temperature of 110 ± 2°C. Each mold was then weighed three times using a high-precision analytical balance, and the mean values were recorded. The percentage mass loss relative to the initial weight was calculated to determine the solubility of the materials.

Spectrophotometric analysis

To ensure measurement consistency, all assessments were performed in a light-controlled cabinet designed especially for the study. This cabinet blocks external light and simulates natural illumination as D65, since CIE standard illuminant D65 is recommended for visual color measurements in dentistry (14). Before each measurement, the spectrophotometer was calibrated to maintain accuracy. A single-blinded operator conducted three independent measurements per sample to ensure reproducibility. Color alterations before and after immersion in the endodontic solutions were quantified using the Commission Internationale de l'Eclairage (CIE) system. The color change (ΔE) was calculated using the following formula ΔE={(ΔL)2+(Δa)2+(Δb)2}1/2 The parameter L* represents the lightness of the sample, ranging from black (0) to white (100), while a* and b* denote the chromaticity coordinates on the red-green and yellow-blue axes, respectively (15). To further document the visual changes, high-resolution images of the samples were captured before and after exposure to the solutions using a digital camera (EOS 250D, Canon, Tokyo, Japan).

Surface topography assessment

Surface roughness and topographical alterations were assessed through two key parameters: the arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) and the area-related mean arithmetic height (Sa). While Ra (two-dimensional) represents the arithmetic mean roughness value and is one of the most commonly utilized parameters for assessing surface roughness along a defined line, Sa shows the arithmetic mean deviation, calculated by determining the difference between the original surface height distribution and the least square datum plane (16). The analyses were conducted on images captured from a 0.5 × 0.5 mm area using a 10× 0.7 lens. Surface metrology software (ConfoMap ST 9.3.10494; Zeiss, Germany) was used for image processing and evaluation.

Statistical analysis

The data were processed and analyzed utilizing the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS 29, Chicago, IL, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene test were used to check the normal distribution and homogeneity of data, respectively. Since the data did not show a normal distribution for solubility, color alteration, and surface topography values, intergroup comparisons were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests. The analysis of repetitive measurements in the same group at different time intervals was evaluated with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The level of significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Chitosan nanoparticles were successfully obtained using the ionic gelation technique. Characterization studies revealed that the particle size was 344.6 ± 40.5 nm, with a particle size distribution of 0.387 ± 0.032. Additionally, the zeta potential value was determined to be 56.9 ± 9.9 mV (mean ± standard deviation, n = 3).

Discussion

The interaction between hydraulic cements and endodontic irrigation solutions remains a critical concern, as it can significantly influence their physicochemical and biological properties, ultimately impacting clinical performance. Among these irrigants, HEDP and chitosan nanoparticles have gained attention due to their biocompatibility, antimicrobial effects, and potential role in dentin remineralization (10, 17). However, limited data exist regarding their interaction with hydraulic cements, and further research is needed to elucidate their effects on material stability. This study evaluated the influence of different irrigation solutions on the color alteration, solubility, and surface topography of PD MTA and Biodentine. The findings demonstrated that irrigation solutions significantly affected these properties, with notable variations depending on the material and the specific solution tested. Given these results, the null hypothesis was rejected.

In the context of ideal chitosan nanoparticles synthesis, the zeta potential value was found to be positively charged. This value indicates that the prepared chitosan nanoparticles exhibit excellent stability.

To ensure optimal sealing ability and long-term durability, hydraulic cements should exhibit minimal solubility. According to the ISO 6876/2002 standard, solubility should be assessed after 24 hours which exceeds clinical contact time. Although the prolonged time of exposure to the irrigation solutions may not adequately mimic the clinical situations, extended evaluation periods could also provide further insights into long-term material stability (18). Although relatively lower solubility was observed in PD MTA, both hydraulic cements demonstrated statistically similar values below the 3% threshold recommended by ISO standards at the 24-hour assessment in this study. This could be explained by the composition of the radiopacifier, which plays a key role in the solubility of hydraulic cements. PD MTA contains calcium tungstate, while Biodentine is formulated with zirconium oxide. The incorporation of calcium tungstate has been associated with reduced solubility compared to zirconium oxide (19). Moreover, literature reports suggest that calcium tungstate reduces the material's water demand (20). Since a lower water ratio generally correlates with decreased solubility (19), the water-to-powder ratio could be another factor that explains the relatively lower solubility observed in PD MTA.

Among the tested irrigation solutions, distilled water resulted in the lowest solubility values, while EDTA and chitosan nanoparticles significantly increased solubility in both hydraulic cements. This increase may be attributed to their chelating properties, which promote calcium ion extraction and disrupt the formation of the calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) network—an essential component for material stability. Additionally, acidic environments can accelerate the degradation of hydration phases, increasing porosity and favoring the accumulation of crystalline structures, ultimately leading to structural alterations (21). Conversely, NaOCl and HEDP resulted in lower solubility values than EDTA, likely due to their milder interaction with the cements, attributed to their higher pH levels (6, 22).

The color alteration of PD MTA and Biodentine was assessed in this study due to its clinical relevance, particularly in esthetically critical regions. The results showed that both hydraulic cements exhibited minimal color alteration when immersed in distilled water, with ΔE values below the clinically perceptible threshold of 3.3. This stability may be attributed to the absence of bismuth oxide—known to cause dark discoloration—in both materials, as alternative radiopacifiers were used instead. However, when exposed to other irrigation solutions, Biodentine exhibited significantly greater color alterations than PD MTA. This increased discoloration may be linked to the presence of iron within its composition, as the oxidation of residual iron particles in the set material is considered a plausible mechanism for discoloration (23).

Among the tested irrigation solutions, NaOCl induced the most pronounced color alteration in both materials, consistent with findings in the literature, and exhibited similar effects to HEDP (6, 7). This is likely due to NaOCl's strong oxidative potential, which can alter cement constituents—particularly organic additives such as water-soluble polymers, setting modifiers or water-reducing or flow-enhancing agents and radiopacifiers—while also increasing the release of calcium ions (7). In addition, the similar color alteration values observed between these two solutions may be explained by the addition of NaOCl during the preparation of HEDP. Chitosan nanoparticles, on the other hand, caused less color alteration than NaOCl and had similar effects to EDTA. As this study represents the first investigation into the discoloration effects of chitosan nanoparticles on hydraulic cements, direct comparisons with previous studies are limited. However, Mehta et al. (24) reported that both EDTA and chitosan solutions induced minimal color alteration in white MTA, which aligns with the present findings. The lower pH of these solutions may contribute to reducing oxidative reactions involving metal ions, thereby minimizing color alterations. Additionally, EDTA's chelating ability facilitates the removal of metal ions that could lead to discoloration, while chitosan nanoparticles may exert antioxidant effects that prevent oxidation-induced color alterations (25).

Surface roughness was evaluated using an integrated widefield confocal microscope which enables unrivaled speed and accuracy for 3D data acquisition, providing raw data without pre-processing for results. In this sense, although 2D view provides a quick gauge of general roughness, it may not a complete picture of the sample surface (26). Besides, 3D metrology offers significant advantages by providing high-resolution quantitative and qualitative characterization of dentin surfaces (27). The initial similarity of the intra-group roughness values for both hydraulic cements supports that the materials were accurately and homogeneously mixed. However, significant changes in surface topography were observed in both PD MTA and Biodentine after 24 hours of immersion in different irrigation solutions. Since both materials have relatively short setting times (approximately 12–15 minutes), the presence of excess water may have influenced their dissolution kinetics, leading to increased porosity on the outer surface of the residual material (28). Additionally, PD MTA exhibited higher roughness values than Biodentine both before and after immersion, which may be attributed to its larger particle size and the presence of calcium tungstate as a radiopacifier, whereas Biodentine contains finer particles and zirconium oxide (28, 29). An increase in surface roughness can enhance adhesion by improving surface energy and wettability, which may be beneficial in certain clinical applications, such as improving the bond between the cement and dentin (30, 31). However, excessive surface roughness may also create a favorable environment for bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation, particularly when these materials are used in furcation repairs or as root-end filling materials (32). Therefore, while a certain degree of roughness may be advantageous for mechanical retention, its clinical implications should be carefully considered based on the intended application of the material.

Among the tested solutions, HEDP and NaOCl caused a significant increase in surface roughness for both hydraulic cements. This finding aligns with previous studies that reported enhanced adhesion of HCs to dentin when NaOCl and HEDP are used in combination (33), possibly due to their ability to modify the dentin surface and increase its wettability. However, NaOCl has also been reported to have a detrimental effect on the formation of MTA reaction products, potentially altering its surface microstructure and long-term stability (34). Additionally, chitosan nanoparticles induced greater changes in surface compared to EDTA. This effect can be attributed to the intrinsic chelating capacity of the chitosan polymer, which binds calcium ions on the cement surface, potentially altering hydration and leading to surface mineral depletion. However, this result contrasts with previous findings reporting higher roughness values associated with EDTA exposure (35, 36). These discrepancies may stem from variations in material composition, differences in irrigant concentrations, or distinct evaluation methodologies across studies. In particular, the use of three-dimensional imaging techniques for surface roughness assessment provides a more comprehensive analysis by capturing height variations relative to a known reference plane. This approach enhances the reproducibility of measurements and allows for a more detailed characterization of how different irrigants interact with hydraulic cements at the microstructural level.

While this study provides valuable insights into the interactions between hydraulic cements and irrigation solutions, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, which, while ensuring methodological standardization and reproducibility, may not fully replicate the complex intraoral environment. Factors such as salivary enzymes, mechanical stress, and variations in pH could influence material behavior differently in clinical scenarios. Additionally, the use of plastic molds instead of a dentin matrix, although aligned with ISO 6876 standards for solubility assessment, may not account for the potential effects of dentin permeability and bonding interactions between the cements and the root canal walls. Despite these limitations, the study offers several strengths, including a comprehensive evaluation of multiple physicochemical properties (solubility, color alteration, and surface roughness) under the influence of various irrigation solutions. Furthermore, the use of a structured experimental approach, with controlled exposure times and quantitative assessments, enhances the reliability and reproducibility of the findings. From a clinical perspective, the results underscore the importance of careful selection of irrigation solutions during endodontic procedures, as certain solutions, such as EDTA and NaOCl, may compromise the integrity or esthetics of hydraulic cements. The study highlights that chitosan nanoparticles and HEDP, although still requiring further investigation, may present viable alternatives with potentially lower adverse effects on cement degradation. Future research should aim to bridge the gap between in vitro and in vivo studies by evaluating these interactions under dynamic clinical conditions, including long-term exposure to irrigants, mechanical stress, and the influence of periapical tissue responses. Additionally, exploring the effects of alternative radiopacifiers and surface treatments could provide valuable insights into improving the long-term performance of hydraulic cements in endodontic applications.

Conclusion

EDTA and chitosan nanoparticles increased the solubility of both PD MTA and Biodentine. HEDP and chitosan nanoparticles significantly influenced surface roughness. PD MTA exhibited higher surface roughness values both before and after immersion, whereas Biodentine resulted in more pronounced roughness changes. NaOCl induced greater discoloration than HEDP, chitosan nanoparticles, or EDTA. Overall, Biodentine demonstrated higher discoloration compared to PD MTA.
