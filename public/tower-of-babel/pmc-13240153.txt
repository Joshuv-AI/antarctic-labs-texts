Newly Graduated Nurses' Professional Competency and Caring Perception: A Cross‐Sectional Correlational Study
Authors: Merve Yilmaz, Fatos Korkmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study explored how newly graduated nurses perceive their professional competencies and caring behaviours and examined the factors that shape these perceptions as they transition into clinical practice.MethodsThis study employed a descriptive relational cross‐sectional design adhering to STROBE guidelines for cross‐sectional research. Data were collected via an online survey, including a Data Sheet, Competency Inventory of Nursing Students (CINS) and Caring Behaviors Inventory‐30 (CBI‐30). Participants were newly graduated nurses from four state universities in Turkiye during the 2019–2020 academic year. Data were collected between May and November 2021. Univariate analyses were conducted to compare competency and caring perception scores across socio‐demographic characteristics. In addition, ordinal logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with caring perception.ResultsA total of 249 newly graduated nurses participated (90.8% female; mean age 23). In univariate analyses, no consistent significant differences were observed between total CINS or CBI‐30 scores and socio‐demographic characteristics (p > 0.05). Competency scores were highest in Caring and lowest in Clinical Biomedical Science and General Clinical Skills. Caring perception was highest in Professional Knowledge and Attitude. A moderate positive correlation was observed between competency and caring scores. In the ordinal logistic regression model, higher professional competency was independently associated with higher caring perception.Conclusions/Implication for PracticeNewly graduated nurses felt confident in addressing patients' psychological and emotional needs, yet they reported limitations in areas requiring clinical judgement, interpretation of clinical data and foundational technical skills. Strengthening clinical readiness through simulation‐based training, structured orientation programmes and supportive mentorship may help bridge this gap. Integrating competency‐based and caring‐focused educational strategies into nursing curricula can further support new graduates as they enter professional roles.Patient or Public ContributionParticipants provided data but were not involved in the study's design, outcome measures or conduct.Keywords: caring perception, cross‐sectional study, graduate nurses, nursing, nursing care, nursing education, professional competency

Introduction

Nurses, constituting most of the global health workforce, play a pivotal role in health promotion, disease prevention and community care. The World Health Organization (WHO) projects a significant nursing shortage, estimating that by 2030, one in every six nurses worldwide will retire, creating a demand for 9 million nurses (World Health Organization 2022). Newly graduated nurses are crucial to filling this gap and ensuring the continued supply of skilled nursing care, making their professional development essential in addressing this impending shortage.

The increasing complexity of healthcare and the growing demand for quality and safe nursing practices have prompted significant changes in nursing curricula worldwide. Advancing technology and the persistent gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice further underscore the need for nursing students to develop robust clinical competencies (Salem et al. 2018). Both the World Health Organization (WHO) and the American Institute of Medicine (IOM) emphasized clinical competencies as the gold standard in nursing, recognizing them as fundamental to the future of the profession (Davis et al. 2021; World Health Organization 2020). Consequently, the issue of nursing competency has become central to nursing education (Attallah and Hasan 2022).

The primary purpose of nursing education is to provide students with cognitive, affective and psychomotor competencies essential for delivering person‐centred and evidence‐based care. Nurses are responsible for managing the ‘care’ process, the most critical component of nursing practice, and are expected to provide care competently (Oldland et al. 2020). According to The World Health Organization (2015), competency encompasses the knowledge, skills and behaviours required to successfully perform jobs, roles or responsibilities. Professional competency is thus a prerequisite for achieving better nursing care outcomes (Han et al. 2020), minimizing medical errors (Vaziri et al. 2019) and making the most appropriate clinical decisions for individuals (Tyndall et al. 2018; Chang and Manojlovich 2023).

Given that nurses' working environments, educational preparation, and legal and regulatory frameworks vary across countries, the national nursing boards of some countries have developed and reviewed requirements for national competence qualifications for registered nurses (Black et al. 2008). In Turkey, the ‘National Qualifications Framework for Higher Education (NQF‐HETR)’ was established to align with the Bologna Process, classifying nursing education competencies such as ‘Competency to Work Independently and Take Responsibility, Learning Competency, Communication and Social Competency, and Field‐Specific Competency’ (National Qualificatıons Framework For Higher Education in Turkey 2010).

The concept of competency is widely recognized as a core element of professional development. However, there is no universal agreement on its definition or assessment, and competency remains an evolving construct in both theory and practice (Attallah and Hasan 2022; Jokiniemi et al. 2018). A systematic review indicates that competency development among newly graduated nurses is influenced by personal factors (e.g., academic background, clinical placement experiences, interpersonal relationships) and organizational factors (e.g., orientation programmes, workload and unit stability) (Charette et al. 2019).

Similarly, studies on perceptions of care suggest that these perceptions are influenced by factors such as empathy, communication skills and cultural sensitivity among students (Elyeli and Öztürk 2024; Amin et al. 2025). However, studies that evaluate professional competency and caring perceptions together remain limited. In particular, there is a need for research examining how these constructs are associated during the transition from education to professional practice, when contextual and organizational factors may interact with individual competencies.

According to the International Council of Nurses (ICN), a nurse is a professional educated in scientific knowledge, skills and nursing philosophy, and regulated to practice according to standards and ethical principles. Nurses support health, ensure continuity of care and coordinate healthcare systems through a combination of technical expertise, ethical conduct and therapeutic relationships. From a practical perspective, care quality is closely linked to competency; empathy or ethical sensitivity alone is insufficient without clinical knowledge, technical skills and sound judgement. Competency enhances the effectiveness of caring, and caring strengthens the ethical‐humanistic dimension of competency. As complementary constructs, caring and competency together uphold the integrity of professional nursing practice.

Understanding the relationship between professional competency and care perception is crucial for determining how newly graduated nurses can effectively perform care behaviours. Therefore, this study aimed to (a) investigate newly graduated nurses' professional competencies perceptions and caring perceptions, (b) explore the associations between socio‐demographic characteristics and these perceptions, and (c) examine the correlation between professional competencies' perceptions and caring perceptions.

Background

Previous studies have attempted to measure nursing students' (senior students) professional competency levels (Çelik et al. 2020; Grande et al. 2022) or care perception levels (Alquwez et al. 2021; Gül and Arslan 2021) using various measurement tools. These studies generally indicate that nursing students demonstrate reasonable competency (Çelik et al. 2020; Grande et al. 2022), and their care perceptions are positive (Gül and Arslan 2021; Alquwez et al. 2021). However, studies conducted with new graduate nurses concentrated on assessing clinical competencies, including components of direct clinical practice, professional development, ethical decision‐making, clinical leadership, cooperation and consulting, and critical thinking rather than broader professional competencies (Willman et al. 2020).

To contextualize these findings further, Benner's (1982) novice‐to‐expert model provides a valuable framework for understanding the developmental stages of newly graduated nurses, according to the model, which categorizes stages as novice and advanced beginner. They require substantial educational support and guidance to transition effectively into professional roles. As vital contributors to nursing teams and key players in maintaining a skilled healthcare workforce, it is essential to understand new graduates' competencies and care perceptions early in their careers.

Professionally competent nurses must understand all aspects of care and its impact on patients. Evaluating new graduates' professional competencies and caring perceptions immediately after graduation will help educational institutions decide which core competencies should be focused on during training to have competent nurses. This evaluation prepares new graduates who have not yet gained professional experience to be more skilled and instills the nursing standards necessary for providing safe and effective patient care. Moreover, distinguishing between clinical and professional competencies is crucial. While clinical competencies focus on direct patient care, professional competencies encompass many skills and knowledge for effective nursing practice.

Therefore, this study examined the association between professional competencies and caring perceptions among newly graduated nurses. It seeks to guide future research and professional practices, especially for graduate nurses. Additionally, the findings of this study may also provide valuable insights for nurse academicians and managers in designing effective nursing curricula to facilitate the transition of newly graduated nurses into competent professionals.

Methods

Design

This study used a descriptive, cross‐sectional design to evaluate the competencies and perceptions of care of newly qualified nurses in Turkey. To enhance methodological rigour and transparency, the study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE Statement guidelines. Specifically, key STROBE components, including participant selection, variable definition, bias control and statistical methods, were systematically addressed throughout the study design and reporting process (see Appendix S1).

Participants

The research was conducted with graduates from four state universities in Ankara, Turkey, during the 2019–2020 academic year (see Figure 1). All newly graduated nurses from these institutions were invited to participate. Eligibility criteria included: (a) graduation within the specified academic year, (b) willingness to participate, (c) completion of all data collection instruments.

Flow diagram of study.

The study population consisted of 702 newly graduated nurses from the selected institutions. The sample size was determined using an online sample size calculator (https://sampsize.sourceforge.net/iface/). The calculation indicated a required sample size of the minimum number 249 newly graduated nurses, with a margin of error of 5% and a confidence level of 95%.

Out of 702 potential participants, 258 online responses were received, corresponding to a response rate of 36.7%. Despite several reminders, some eligible participants declined to participate, primarily due to workload and time constraints during their transition into professional practice. Therefore, all reachable and consenting newly graduated nurses were included in the study.

Incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the analysis, and only data from participants who completed all items were included. Due to missing responses in nine questionnaires, the final analysis included 249 participants.

Measures

The data collection form comprised the Data Sheet, Competency Inventory of Nursing Students (CINS) and Caring Behaviors Inventory‐30 (CBI‐30).

Data Sheet

The data sheet comprised two sections. The first section contained nine closed‐ended questions and two open‐ended questions addressing socio‐demographic information, including gender, prior experience providing medical care, previous experience receiving medical care, willingness to choose the nursing profession, age and work experience. The second section included four closed‐ended and one open‐ended question that focused on caring and professional competency. This section covered issues such as difficulties with professional orientation or competency, challenges encountered while providing care (with an option to elaborate if applicable), evaluation of the nursing education received (categorized as Sufficient, Partially Sufficient or Not Sufficient) and recommendations for improving nursing education to enhance professional competency and caregiving skills.

Competency Inventory of Nursing Students (CINS)

The Competency Inventory of Nursing Students (CINS), developed by Hsu and Hsieh (2013), is designed to assess the competency of nursing students. The CINS has been adapted to Turkish, and its validity and reliability were established by Ülker and Korkmaz (2022). The Turkish version of the CINS includes 43 items distributed across six sub‐dimensions: Clinical Biomedical Science (5 items), General Clinical Skills (7 items), Critical Thinking and Reasoning (4 items), Caring (6 items), Ethics and Accountability (15 items) and Lifelong Learning (6 items). Each item is rated on a 7‐point Likert scale, from 1 (I do not feel competent) to 7 (I feel very competent). The total scale score ranges from 43 to 301 points, with higher scores indicating greater perceived competence. The original scale demonstrated Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficients ranging from 0.91 to 0.98. The Turkish version of the CINS showed Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranging from 0.79 to 0.97 across sub‐dimensions and 0.97 for the total score, indicating high reliability. In this study, Cronbach's alpha coefficients for the CINS ranged from 0.84 to 0.95 for the sub‐dimensions (Cronbach's alpha values for the subscales were as follows: Clinical Biomedical Science = 0.86, General Clinical Skills = 0.92, Critical Thinking and Reasoning = 0.85, Caring = 0.92, Ethics and Accountability = 0.95, and Lifelong Learning = 0.84).

Caring Behaviors Inventory‐30 (CBI‐30)

The Caring Behaviors Inventory (CBI) was initially developed by Wolf et al. (1994). The CBI was subsequently adapted to Turkish and Gul and Dinc (2020) evaluated its validity and reliability. The Turkish version, referred to as CBI‐30 consists of three sub‐dimensions: Respectful Deference to Others (10 items), Professional Knowledge and Attitude (10 items) and Assurance of Human Presence (10 items). The scale includes thirty 6‐point Likert‐type items, ranging from ‘never’ to ‘always’. Scores on the CBI‐30 range from 30 to 180, with higher scores indicating a greater perception of care. The original scale exhibited Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficients of 0.83 for nurses and 0.96 for patients. The Turkish version of the CBI‐30 demonstrated high reliability, with Cronbach's alpha coefficients of 0.97 for nurses and 0.99 for patients. In this study, Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficients ranged from 0.89 to 0.90 for the sub‐dimensions Cronbach's alpha values for the subscales were: Respectful Deference to Others = 0.89, Professional Knowledge and Attitude = 0.89, and Assurance of Human Presence = 0.90, with a total scale reliability of 0.95.

Ethics

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Hacettepe university (Approval Code: GO 21/476). Written consent was obtained from both the university administration and the developers of the scales used in the study. The purpose of the study was clearly explained to participants on the first page of the online questionnaire, emphasizing that participation was voluntary, personal data would not be used for purposes other than the study, and confidentiality would be strictly maintained. Legal and ethical standards were rigorously followed throughout the research process. All data were securely stored on password‐protected computers, with access restricted to the research team.

Data Collection Procedure

Data collection took place between May and November 2021. Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant Ethical Committee, and institutional permissions were secured before commencing the study. The scales were converted into an online survey using Google Forms, strictly adhering to ethical guidelines, including data privacy and security. The scales were converted into an online survey using Google Forms, ensuring user‐friendly design and easy access. The first author coordinated with faculty members responsible for fourth‐year students and new graduates to distribute the survey link via email. To minimize potential bias, clear instructions and information about the study were provided to both faculty members and participants. The ‘Limit to one response’ option was enabled in Google Forms to ensure data integrity. Informed consent was obtained digitally, with participants required to review a consent form and check a box indicating their agreement before proceeding to the survey. The survey was designed to take approximately 20–25 min to complete. Reminder emails were sent to institutions every 3 weeks to optimize participation. The frequency of reminders was adjusted based on response rates to balance encouraging participation with minimizing potential response fatigue.

Statistical Analyses

The statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were computed, including percentages, minimum and maximum values, and median and interquartile ranges. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the scale scores, and skewness, kurtosis values and variance coefficients were also calculated. The CBI‐30 and CINS scales have been validated for use with Turkish populations in previous studies, which established their psychometric properties, including construct validity. The internal consistency reliability of the Turkish versions of the CBI‐30 and CINS scales was assessed in the present sample using Cronbach's alpha coefficients.

Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to examine the associations between the socio‐demographic characteristics and the study variables because the data were not normally distributed and were ordinal. Correlation coefficients were interpreted according to Cohen's (1988) guidelines, where values below 0.30 indicate a low correlation, 0.30–0.49 a moderate correlation, 0.50–0.79 a strong correlation, and values of 0.80 and above a very strong correlation.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare two groups, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare more than two groups. Where statistically significant differences were identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni correction applied. Prior to analysis, category frequencies were examined to ensure adequate cell sizes; categories with fewer than five cases were merged where appropriate.

Responses to the open‐ended questions on the Demographic Data Form were analysed using qualitative content analysis. Two nursing experts (associate professors) coded the data independently and developed an initial set of 14 categories. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, particularly within three main domains: ‘Individual factors’, ‘Educational factors’ and ‘Uncertainty regarding the disease process’ (see Appendix S2).

Caring perception scores obtained from the CBI‐30 were considered as ordinal dependent variables due to their Likert‐type. Accordingly, ordinal logistic regression (proportional odds model) was conducted using the PLUM procedure in IBM SPSS Statistics to identify independent predictors of caring perception. Socio‐demographic, educational and professional competency variables (CINS total score) were entered as predictors. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model fit was evaluated using −2 Log Likelihood and Nagelkerke pseudo R 2 statistics. The coding of categorical variables included in the ordinal logistic regression model is presented in Table 5.

Results

Sample Description

A total of 249 participants were included in this study. The mean age of the newly graduated nurses was 23.44 ± 0.84 years, with the majority being female (90.8%), single (94%) and without children (97.6%). Most participants came from nuclear families (88.8%), and a significant proportion had prior experience in providing medical care (91.2%). However, more than half had no experience providing medical care (55.0%), and a substantial number chose the nursing profession willingly (70.3%). Among the newly graduated nurses, 74.3% were employed, with the majority working predominantly in ‘day and night rotating shifts’ (46.5%). The majority reported no issues with professional competency (60.8%) and the caregiving process (66.9%). However, 39.3% of the graduates identified challenges with their professional orientation, and 57.4% believed their education only partially met the knowledge and skills required in the working environment. The most challenging aspects of clinical orientation mentioned were acclimating to the clinic (44.8%) and caring for critically ill patients (53.8%). Suggestions for improving professional practice, refining the teaching process and better preparing for working life in nursing education are discussed (see Table 1).

The Competency Inventory of Nursing Students Scale Scores

The total median score for the Competency Inventory of Nursing Students (CINS) was 264.0 (70.0–301.0). Table 2 presents the median scores, interquartile ranges and bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals for the CINS total and subdimension scores. The highest median score was observed in the ‘Caring’ subdimension, with a median of 38.0 (range: 7.0–42.0). In contrast, the lowest median scores were recorded in the ‘Clinical Biomedical Science’ (28.0; range: 7.0–35.0) and ‘General Clinical Skills’ (42.0; range: 9.0–49.0) sub‐dimensions (see Table 2). A statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) was found in the total CINS scores of female newly graduated nurses who had prior experience in providing medical care, chose the profession willingly and believed their education was adequate. Additionally, statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed in the ‘Clinical Biomedical Science’, ‘General Clinical Skills’, ‘Caring’, ‘Ethics and Accountability’ and ‘Lifelong Learning’ subdimension scores among newly graduated nurses with prior caregiving experience, who willingly chose the nursing profession, and who perceived their education as sufficient (see Table 3).

Caring Behaviors Inventory‐30 (CBI‐30) Scale Scores

The total median score for the Caring Behaviors Inventory‐30 (CBI‐30) was 164.0 (103.0–180.0). The highest median score was recorded in the ‘Professional Knowledge and Attitude’ subdimension, with a median of 56.0 (range: 30.0–60.0) (see Table 2). A statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) was observed in the total CBI‐30 scores among newly graduated nurses who had prior experience in giving or receiving care, chose the nursing profession willingly, encountered no difficulties in professional orientation and believed their education was adequate. Additionally, significant differences (p < 0.05) were found in the ‘Respect for Others’, ‘Professional Knowledge and Attitude’ and ‘Assurance of Human Presence’ sub‐dimensions among nurses who willingly chose the nursing profession and perceived their education as sufficient (see Table 3).

Correlation Between Competency Levels and Care Perceptions

The Spearman correlation test revealed a direct and significant relationship between the total scores of the Competency Inventory for Nursing Students (CINS) and the Caring Behaviors Inventory‐30 (CBI‐30) (r = 0.653, p < 0.01), as well as across all sub‐dimensions (r = 0.364–0.572, p < 0.01). The strongest correlation was found between the CINS ‘Caring’ subdimension and the CBI‐30 ‘Respectful Deference to Others’ subdimension (r = 0.572, p < 0.01). Conversely, the weakest correlation was observed between the CINS ‘Clinical Biomedical Science’ subdimension and the CBI‐30 ‘Respectful Deference to Others’ subdimension (r = 0.364, p < 0.01) (see Table 4).

Determinants of the Caring Behavior Among Newly Graduated Nurses

Ordinal logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent factors associated with caring perception among newly graduated nurses. In the multivariable model, higher professional competency, as measured by the total Competency Inventory of Nursing Students (CINS) score, was independently associated with higher levels of caring perception. Willingness to choose the nursing profession and orientation‐related problems did not retain statistical significance after adjustment for other variables included in the model. Discussion

Professional Competencies in Newly Graduate Nurses

Nursing is a multidimensional profession that includes cognitive, affective and psychomotor areas. Nurses must be competent in professional knowledge and skills to provide care safely and effectively and transform their knowledge and skills into behaviour. The transition period following graduation is an excellent time to evaluate their preparedness, readiness and skillset. In this study, the newly graduated nurses assessed their professional competency levels as good, exceptionally more competent in ‘Caring’ and psychological, social and spiritual aspects of patient care. However, they reported limitations in clinical biomedical science, establishing a relationship between their professional and clinical knowledge and general clinical skills related to data collection, analysis and interventions. Similarly, the literature revealed that final‐year nursing students/newly graduated nurses perceived themselves as having good professional competency in areas like ‘Caring’ (Çelik et al. 2020), Value‐Based Nursing Care (Tanabe et al. 2025) while feeling limited in ‘Clinical Biomedical Science’ (Çelik et al. 2020). Unlike our results, Grande et al. (2022) reported a higher professional competency in ‘Critical Thinking and Reasoning’. Our findings revealed that new graduates struggle with applying theoretical knowledge to clinical practice, especially in interpreting data related to medications, diagnostic tests and physical examinations. Despite extensive clinical practice throughout their education, they reported feeling limited in their general clinical knowledge and skills. In Turkey, the emphasis during nursing education tends to be on practical skills such as medication administration, often at the expense of a deeper understanding of diagnostic tests and their implications. This focus on application over interpretation might contribute to the observed limitations in clinical competencies. On the other hand, newly graduated nurses demonstrated a strong commitment to addressing patients' psychological, social and spiritual needs. In Turkiye, nursing students learn the theoretical foundations of the nursing process early in their education and repeatedly apply it through classroom case studies. During clinical placements, they continue to use the nursing process when planning and implementing patient care, often under close supervision and with ongoing feedback. These structured learning experiences are designed to strengthen clinical reasoning and support students' confidence as they transition into professional roles. This may help explain why newly graduated nurses in this study demonstrated strong caring behaviours and holistic care practices.

International literature similarly emphasizes competency‐based education. For example, nursing curricula in Japan aim to develop scientific thinking, ethical judgement and evidence‐based care skills (Committee for Fostering Human Resources in Nursing Education 2017). National competency frameworks also highlight professional development and mentoring roles for nurses (Japan Nursing Association 2023). In Turkiye, the ‘Core Competencies in Nursing Guide’ outlines seven domains: Professionalism, Effective Communication, Evidence‐Based Practice, Care Management, Quality Improvement, Teamwork and Collaboration, and Professional Leadership which align with the competency areas assessed in this study. These shared expectations help contextualize the strong caring‐related competencies observed among newly graduated nurses.

The study also found that professional competencies, including ‘Clinical Biomedical Science’, ‘General Clinical Skills’, ‘Caring’, ‘Ethics and Accountability’ and ‘Lifelong Learning’, were positively associated with female nurses who had previous caregiving experience, chose the nursing profession willingly and found their educational content sufficient. This aligns with the understanding that individuals who voluntarily choose nursing and are motivated to help others tend to feel more competent and show more significant progress in professional development (Halperin and Mashiach‐Eizenberg 2014). Furthermore, newly graduated nurses with caregiving experience may better evaluate their competencies based on their practical experiences. However, it is essential to recognize that other factors, such as the type of nursing education programmes, extracurricular activities, country of residence and general point average (GPA), can also influence students' perceptions of their professional competency (Çelik et al. 2020; Hsieh and Hsu 2013; Grande et al. 2022). These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings and developing strategies for enhancing nursing education. Future research should explore the specific reasons behind the limitations observed in clinical competencies and develop targeted interventions to address these gaps. Additionally, longitudinal studies could provide insights into how competencies evolve and the impact of educational and professional experiences on competency development.

Caring Perceptions in Newly Graduated Nurses

Caring is the essence of nursing and central to nursing practice. Effective nursing care integrates professional knowledge and skills with care's affective and moral dimensions, making it a uniquely privileged aspect of the profession. Without this integration, care that relies solely on conscience, compassion and goodwill may fall short of meeting patients' needs and could be detrimental. Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate nursing care holistically, encompassing cognitive, affective and psychomotor domains such as ‘Respect for Others’, ‘Professional Knowledge and Attitude’ and ‘Assurance of Human Presence’.

In this study, the care perception levels of newly graduated nurses were assessed and found to be generally positive. New graduates exhibited a more favourable perception in the ‘Professional Knowledge and Attitude’ subdimension, where they demonstrated competency in using professional knowledge to provide expected medical and nursing care. In contrast, their perceptions were less positive in the ‘Respectful Deference to Others’ and ‘Assurance of Human Presence’ sub‐dimensions, where they struggled with supporting patients in expressing their physical and psychological needs. These findings align with previous research, highlighting the high value placed on ‘Professional Knowledge and Attitude’ among nursing students (Gül and Arslan 2021). Conversely, Labrague et al. (2017) reported a higher perception of care in the ‘Assurance of Human Presence’, suggesting variability in how different studies assess care perception. While several factors were associated with caring perception in the univariate analyses, only professional competency remained independently associated with caring perception in the multivariable ordinal logistic regression model.

The ‘Professional Knowledge and Attitude’ subdimension involves administering treatment promptly and skillfully, using medical equipment and emphasizing task‐oriented care. It appears that newly graduated nurses may prioritize fulfilling their responsibilities efficiently and responding to clinical needs rather than focusing on individualized patient care. This task‐oriented approach might lead to a neglect of personalized care, as new graduates may be more concerned with completing their duties quickly rather than addressing specific patient needs.

Despite being early in their professional careers, the new graduates demonstrated an ethical perspective, effective communication and a compassionate approach towards patients. They were prepared to deliver nursing care with a strong understanding of its importance. The perception of care, encompassing ‘Respect for Others’, ‘Professional Knowledge and Attitude’ and ‘Assurance of Human Presence’, was more optimistic among those with prior caregiving experience, a voluntary choice of the nursing profession, no professional orientation issues, and those who found their educational content sufficient.

Literature indicates that factors such as ‘university’ and ‘type of family’ significantly predict caring behaviour (Alquwez et al. 2021). Gender and professional knowledge level (year of study) have also been shown to influence care perception (Gül and Arslan 2021; Alquwez et al. 2021). Nursing is fundamentally based on care, and individuals who willingly choose the profession and have prior caregiving experience are often motivated to help others. Such experiences will likely enhance their care behaviours, leading to a more positive perception of care.

The perception of care including ‘Respect for Others’, ‘Professional Knowledge and Attitude’ and ‘Assurance of Human Presence’ was more positive among graduates with prior caregiving experience, those who voluntarily chose the nursing profession, those who reported no problems during orientation and those who viewed their education as adequate in the univariate analyses. Previous studies have similarly identified university attended, family structure, gender and academic year as influential factors in caring perception (Alquwez et al. 2021; Gül and Arslan 2021). Individuals who willingly choose nursing and who have caregiving experience may be more motivated to help others, which could contribute to stronger caring perceptions.

However, in this study, while voluntary choice of the nursing profession was associated with higher caring scores in the single‐variable analyses, this relationship became negative in the multivariate model. This suggests that the influence of voluntary choice may be confounded by other factors, particularly problems encountered during clinical orientation and perceived adequacy of education, which appear to play a more prominent role in shaping caring perception. Further qualitative research is needed to explore the underlying reasons for these patterns. The change in the direction and loss of statistical significance observed for voluntary choice of the profession in the multivariable model likely reflects shared variance and suppression effects among correlated predictors. Such coefficient instability is a recognized phenomenon in multivariable analyses and does not necessarily indicate a true negative relationship.

The Relationship Between Professional Competencies and Caring Perceptions in Newly Graduated Nurses

This study demonstrated a significant association between professional competency and caring perceptions among newly graduated nurses, indicating that stronger competencies are associated with more positive attitudes towards quality care. This finding aligns with existing literature showing that higher competency levels support more confident and effective caregiving behaviours (Çelik et al. 2020).

Further analysis of the sub‐dimensions suggests that competency, particularly within the ‘Caring’ domain, plays a central role in shaping favourable perceptions of care. Behaviours such as effectively informing patients, demonstrating patience and showing an understanding of patient needs may contribute to improved evaluations of care quality. Strengthening competencies related to interpersonal communication and caring behaviours may, therefore, enhance nurse–patient interactions and overall care outcomes.

Although the Caring subscale of the Competency Inventory of Nursing Students (CINS) and the Respectful Deference subscale of the Caring Behaviors Inventory‐30 (CBI‐30) originate from different instruments, the moderate correlation observed (r = 0.572) is likely due to their conceptual overlap. The CINS Caring subscale reflects emotional sensitivity, empathy and recognition of patient needs, whereas the Respectful Deference subscale emphasizes respectful communication, active listening and acknowledgment of patient preferences. Because both subscales assess relational and patient centred dimensions of practice, this correlation is theoretically expected and reflects shared conceptual foundations rather than measurement artefact.

International literature similarly underscores the close relationship between nursing competency and quality of care. For example, Tanabe et al. (2025) demonstrated that higher competency levels among nursing students were associated with stronger caring behaviours, while Katayama et al. (2022) reported that ethical care competency reinforces professional nursing practice. These studies support the present findings by highlighting that competencies, particularly those related to caring, communication and ethical practice, play a meaningful role in shaping nurses' perceptions and enactment of quality care.

Limitations and Recommendations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design restricts the ability to examine changes in competency and caring perceptions over time.

Secondly, participation was voluntary, which may have resulted in a self‐selection bias. Individuals with a heavier workload or lower motivation may have been less likely to participate.

Thirdly, the relatively small sample sizes of some socio‐demographic subgroups (e.g., males and graduates with no prior care experience) increase the likelihood of Type II error and limit the reliability of subgroup comparisons.

Fourthly, with a response rate of 36.7%, there is a possibility of non‐response bias. Additionally, difficulties in accessing graduates from certain departments may have impacted the representativeness of the sample and limited the generalizability of the findings (Polit and Beck 2010).

An a priori power analysis was not conducted for the multivariable ordinal logistic regression model, which may have limited the ability to detect smaller effects. Additionally, the large number of subgroup comparisons and predictor variables may have increased the risk of Type I error, meaning that the findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Finally, although the CBI‐30 and CINS scales have previously been validated in Turkish populations, their construct validity was not re‐examined in the present study. Therefore, the measurement properties were based on the results of previous validation studies. Additionally, self‐report measures may introduce common method bias.

Future research should employ longitudinal or mixed‐methods designs and incorporate qualitative inquiry to explore how competencies and caring perceptions develop over time. Replicating similar studies across institutions and cultural contexts may also enhance generalisability. The inconsistent findings regarding voluntary choice of the nursing profession across univariate and multivariate analyses further highlight the need for qualitative exploration to better understand contextual influences on caring perception. Such approaches may also help clarify the interplay between individual motivation, educational preparation and organizational factors during the transition to professional practice.

Conclusion

Newly graduated nurses demonstrated positive levels of professional competency and caring perception, though limitations were noted in clinical biomedical knowledge and general clinical skills. While several factors were related to caring perception in descriptive and univariate analyses, professional competency emerged as the only factor independently associated with caring perception in the ordinal logistic regression model. A significant association between competency and caring perception highlights the importance of strengthening both domains during undergraduate education. Practice‐based strategies that emphasize clinical reasoning, communication and interpretation of clinical data may better prepare graduates for professional roles. This study contributes to the limited empirical literature examining competency and caring together. Future longitudinal and qualitative research is needed to explore how these competencies develop over time and to identify educational approaches that effectively support new nurses' transition to practice.
