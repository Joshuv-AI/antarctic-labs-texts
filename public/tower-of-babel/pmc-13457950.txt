How barbule orientation drives iridescent coloration in Coeligena hummingbirds
Authors: Ana M. Valencia-Palacios, Germán Ricaurte, Juan L. Parra, Marco A. Giraldo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hummingbirds produce intense iridescent signals through multilayered melanosomes arranged within feather barbules. Because barbules are typically inclined relative to the feather surface, their orientation alters the direction of peak reflectance. Yet reflectance is commonly measured under normal incidence relative to the feather plane, implicitly assuming coplanar barbules. Here, we show that not accounting for this geometry in the design of reflectance measurements may influence the interpretation of the results, depending on the reference frame adopted. By integrating experimental angular measurements with multilayer optical modeling, we predict the angle of maximum reflectance and infer barbule inclination (δ) from nanostructural morphology. Reanalysis of published spectra across multiple hummingbird species suggests that a substantial proportion of reported measurements may be more consistently interpreted under geometries influenced by barbule inclination, rather than under conventional normal-incidence conditions defined at the feather plane. These estimates depend on the assumptions of the optical model and should be interpreted within the context of standard experimental conventions. Together, our results highlight barbule inclination as a key geometric parameter linking microstructure to signal directionality and establish a framework for standardizing optical measurements of iridescent coloration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54356-9.

Introduction

Hummingbird coloration is among the most striking in nature. Along with butterflies, they are arguably the most extensively studied organisms in terms of structural coloration1–3. The feathers of hummingbirds exhibit iridescence, defined as changes in hue and intensity as a function of the angle of observation, a phenomenon that likely plays a role in visual communication, particularly in mate choice and crypsis4,5. The phenotypic variation in feather coloration is determined by the precise organization of their nanostructures. In hummingbirds, the barbules are composed of stacks of melanosomes, complex nanostructures that incorporate melanin, air, and keratin layers, which interact to produce angle-dependent coloration2,3,6. Multiple barbules are arranged along both sides of a barb, and hundreds or thousands of barbs are symmetrically attached to a central keratinous rachis to form a complete feather. Optical analyses of feathers require the definition of two reference planes: (1) the feather plane, corresponding to the macroscopic plane formed by the rachis and barbs, and (2) the barbule plane, which describes the local orientation of barbules that may be inclined relative to the feather plane (cf. Figure 1b and c). Both planes coincide only when barbules are coplanar with what we can call the feather surface7. This distinction has direct implications for optical measurements: when measurements are performed assuming the feather as a horizontal surface (i.e., relative to the feather plane), variations in barbule orientation can lead to differences in the observed reflectance. Consequently, normal incidence defined at the macroscopic level does not necessarily correspond to the optical response at the microstructural level.

The physical basis of feather iridescence is now well established2,7,8. It arises from coherent light scattering within multilayered nanostructures composed of stacked melanosomes, typically comprising 6–15 layers. Species-specific variations in the organization of these nanostructures result in differences in the angle at which maximum reflectance is observed9. In hummingbirds, the upper laminae of feather barbules are arranged in a tilted configuration, often described as a Venetian-blind geometry, forming an angle relative to both the barb axis and the overall feather plane2. Using species of the genus Coeligena, we previously quantified how this hierarchical organization of feather elements varies across species. Reflectance measurements were conducted at both the level of individual barbules2 and across larger feather areas comprising multiple barbules10.

In previous studies, reflectance measurements were commonly referenced to the average feather plane defined by the feather barbs7,10. However, since barbule orientation can differ among species, the results may vary, which complicates comparative analyses between them. Using computational approaches, it is possible to predict reflectance and transmittance from the nanostructural morphology of multilayered systems. Methods such as the Transfer Matrix Method (TMM) have been widely applied in insects, birds, and plants3,11. These approaches are mathematically complex and depend on highly precise geometric descriptions2,12–14.

To study feather barbules, they are considered not to constitute ideal multilayers, but rather complex structures due to the composite nature of melanosomes. However, the use of modified TMM implementations incorporating an effective refractive index allows for improved modeling of these structures in hummingbirds2,8. The modified TMM proposed by Giraldo et al. (2018) does not rely solely on fixed or idealized geometries, but instead incorporates microstructural variations within the feather through the use of an effective refractive index of its components. In particular, melanin, air, and keratin are considered2. This integrated analysis, combining both optical modeling and in situ measurements, enables an explicit link to the angular reflectance behavior. Consequently, barbule orientation influences the intensity of maximum reflectance and constitutes a key parameter to consider when quantifying iridescence. In turn, this understanding will help clarify functional contexts such as signaling, sexual display, camouflage, and ecologically realistic illumination and viewing conditions, not only in hummingbirds but also across iridescent birds10,15.

Building on this framework, we developed a computational tool based on a modified TMM approach to reproduce published reflectance spectra for species of the genus Coeligena and other hummingbird taxa. Leveraging detailed anatomical knowledge of Coeligena feather microstructure, we propose that several reported spectra are better explained by illumination conditions defined by barbule orientation rather than by normal incidence relative to the feather plane. In addition, we introduce additional filling fraction indices beyond those proposed by Giraldo et al. (2018) to more accurately capture microstructural variation across species.

We hypothesize that barbule orientation determines the direction of maximum reflectance. To test this, we reanalyze previously published reflectance spectra to evaluate whether they correspond to normal-incidence conditions at the barbule plane or to geometries modulated by barbule inclination, estimating the corresponding angles where appropriate. We further validate this approach through the analysis of an intact specimen of Coeligena orina. This framework allows us to assess the extent to which conventional measurement methods capture the microstructural optical response and suggests that, to avoid ambiguity, maximum reflectance provides a more robust metric when a single value is reported.

Materials and methods

Hummingbird feathers

Gorget feathers of Coeligena helianthea, C. wilsoni, C. lutetiae, and C. phalerata (Fig. 2) were obtained from the ornithological collection at the Natural History Museum from the Institute of Natural Sciences (ICN) at the National University of Colombia (Bogotá), as well as from wild individuals captured and subsequently released in Colombia under appropriate collection permits (ANLA Resolution 0524, 27 may 2014). From each captured individual, up to three gorget feathers were carefully clipped and collected prior to release. All efforts were made to minimize handling time and stress to the animals. From the dataset, only intact gorget feathers were selected to ensure reliable and high-quality measurements. Also, a taxidermized specimen of Coeligena orina was provided on loan by the Museum of the University of Antioquia (MUA). For this species, measurements were conducted directly on the intact specimen without feather removal.

Electron microscopy and micrographs analysis

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to characterize the nanostructural morphology of individual feather barbules from four hummingbird species (Coeligena helianthea, C. wilsoni, C. lutetiae, and C. phalerata). For the remaining species, nanostructural parameters were obtained directly from previously published studies7. Feather samples were prepared following standard resin-embedding and ultrathin-sectioning protocols for biological tissues. Transverse sections were obtained using a diamond knife on an RMC MT-X ultramicrotome (Boeckeler Instruments, Tucson, AZ, USA). Sections were mounted on 200-mesh copper grids coated with formvar, post-stained with osmium tetroxide and lead citrate, and imaged using a Phillips EM301 transmission electron microscope (Veeco FEI Inc., Hillsboro, OR, USA) (Fig. 2).

Anatomical parameters were quantified in ImageJ from both newly acquired TEM micrographs and previously published images, and were used as input parameters for the optical model (see Methods: Reflectance calculations). Measured variables included the number of melanosomes (N), keratin cortex thickness (c), internal air layer thicknesses (b₁, b₂), keratin layer thickness between melanosomes (d), and air layer thickness of the upper melanosomes (a) (Tables 1 and 2).

Spectrophotometry

Reflectance spectra were acquired from the distal iridescent region of individual gorget feathers of Coeligena helianthea, C. wilsoni, C. lutetiae, and C. phalerata using a non-coincident illumination, detection probe configuration, coupled to a deuterium–halogen light source (AvaLight, Avantes) and a spectrophotometer (AvaSpec-2048, Avantes), calibrated against a white reflectance standard (WS-2, Avantes). Feathers were mounted on a three-axis rotatable stage, and spectra were collected using a bifurcated optical probe aligned to maximize the reflectance peak, thereby ensuring measurements at normal incidence relative to the barbule plane rather than to the barb or feather plane. For all additional species, reflectance spectra were obtained directly from previously published studies7,16.

Microspectrophotometry (MSP)

Reflectance spectra of individual gorget barbules from Coeligena helianthea, C. wilsoni, C. lutetiae, and C. phalerata were measured using an epi-illumination microspectrophotometry setup coupled to a fiber-optic spectrometer. Illumination was provided by a xenon light source, and spectra were acquired through a 20× objective. Due to ultraviolet absorption by the optical components, measurements were restricted to wavelengths above 360 nm. Individual barbs were mounted on glass micropipettes and positioned on a rotatable stage. Reflectance was recorded with barbules aligned horizontally, corresponding to normal incidence relative to the barbule plane (θi = 0°).

Spectrogoniometric validation on an intact specimen

To evaluate macroscopic iridescent effects, barbule inclination angles (δ) and corresponding angles of maximum reflectance (2δ) were calculated for 15 iridescent feather patches (gorget and/or crown) across multiple hummingbird species reported in the literature (see Methods: Reflectance calculations). Experimental validation was performed in situ on two representative patches (gorget and crown) from an intact specimen of Coeligena orina housed at the MUA. Angular reflectance measurements were conducted directly on intact feathers, without specimen disassembly, using a goniometric setup with fiber-optic illumination and detection coupled to a spectrophotometer (AvaSpec-2048) and a broadband light source (AvaLight-DH-D-BAL, Avantes) (Fig. 4a), allowing identification of the capture angle yielding maximum reflectance and subsequent comparison with predicted values.

Calculation of reflectance

This study integrates three data sources: (i) nanostructural measurements obtained using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to parameterize the optical model; (ii) experimental reflectance measurements for validation; and (iii) previously published spectra, reanalyzed to infer barbule inclination angles (δ). We explicitly distinguish between two geometric reference frames: the feather plane defined by the arrangement of barbs, and the barbule plane, defined by the local orientation of individual barbules, which are typically inclined relative to the feather surface (Fig. 1b). Reflectance spectra were simulated using a transfer-matrix framework for multilayer systems, in which feather barbules are modeled as stacks of optically thin layers characterized by complex refractive indices3,17,18. In this study, we extend these frameworks by explicitly incorporating barbule inclination (δ) as a geometric parameter that links nanostructural organization with angular reflectance. We predict the angle of maximum reflectance and provide a framework for interpreting reflectance spectra under non-coplanar conditions. Model predictions were compared with experimental and published spectra to infer barbule inclination angles (δ) using an iterative manual fitting procedure. All equations, numerical implementation details, and parameter definitions are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Simulator

The ANATOBIRD framework provides an accessible tool to simulate reflectance. Based on the reflectance and RGB calculations, we developed a computational simulator termed ANATOBIRD (Supplementary Material, S1–S3), designed to model angular reflectance and infer barbule inclination from spectral data (Supplementary Fig. S4). Structural parameters were derived from TEM measurements, and simulations explored various melanin filling fractions using the previously defined Type I, II, and III configurations (see Supplementary Information). ANATOBIRD is provided as an executable file (see Data Availability).

Statistical analyses

Given the descriptive and mechanistic focus of this study, mean and range values were calculated for each structural parameter (c, b₁, d, a, b₂). No inferential statistics were applied, as the analysis aimed to characterize nanostructural variation rather than to test differences between groups.

Results

The iridescent coloration of hummingbird feathers results from the interaction of light with the nanostructural organization of barbules and their orientation relative to the feather plane. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the geometric relationship between barbule inclination (δ) and the direction of reflected light. When barbules are coplanar with the feather plane (δ = 0), an incident beam perpendicular to the feather plane is reflected back along the same path. In contrast, when barbules are inclined (δ ≠ 0), the reflected beam is deflected, and the direction of maximum reflectance occurs at an angle approximately equal to 2δ relative to the feather plane. Under fixed illumination normal to the feather plane, this relationship allows the barbule inclination angle to be inferred directly from the observed angle of maximum reflectance, establishing a link between microstructural geometry and macroscopic optical response. We analyzed gorget feathers from Coeligena helianthea, C. lutetiae, C. phalerata, and C. wilsoni to evaluate reflectance under controlled illumination and detection geometries (Fig. 2a-d). For each species, we report three reflectance curves: a spectrum measured from the feather region, a spectrum measured at the level of individual barbules, and a simulated spectrum derived from the optical model. It was observed that the maximum reflectance tended to occur when the barbules were horizontally aligned, even if the feather plane itself was not horizontal (Fig. 2i–l, gray spectra), consistent with previously reported species-specific differences in barbule inclination9. To independently verify this condition, reflectance spectra were also obtained at the level of individual barbules using microspectrophotometry under epi-illumination, ensuring normal incidence relative to the barbule plane. Spectra acquired at the single-barbule level closely matched those obtained from intact feathers under the corresponding angular configuration (Fig. 2i–l, black spectra). Simulated reflectance spectra accurately reproduced the reflectance maxima observed in both single barbules and intact feathers. Experimental reflectance spectra obtained under barbule-normal incidence were compared with simulated spectra generated using the ANATOBIRD model (Fig. 2i–l, cyan curves). Simulations were parameterized using nanostructural measurements extracted from transmission electron micrographs of the corresponding species (Table 1). For all four Coeligena species analyzed, the simulated spectra closely matched the experimentally measured reflectance profiles in terms of spectral shape and peak position. This agreement was also reflected in the corresponding sRGB color representations derived from experimental and simulated spectra (Fig. 2i–l, color bars), supporting that the experimental measurements were obtained under barbule-normal incidence and validating the accuracy of the ANATOBIRD model. Together, this experimental–modeling approach allows precise control and verification of barbule inclination, ensuring that reflectance measurements are obtained under well-defined geometrical conditions.

Having validated the relationship between barbule orientation, capture geometry, and reflectance under controlled experimental conditions, we next extended this framework to assess whether similar geometric effects underlie previously reported reflectance spectra across a broader range of hummingbird species. Building on this correspondence, the ANATOBIRD model was applied to reanalyze reflectance spectra previously reported in the literature for 15 iridescent feather patches encompassing gorget and/or crown regions across multiple hummingbird species (Fig. 3; Table 2). Table 2 compiles nanostructural parameters and the inferred feather inclination angles (δ) for a total of 15 hummingbird specimens, including 12 gorget patches and 3 crown patches. The dataset is dominated by species of the genus Coeligena, with gorget feather samples from seven Coeligena species (C. helianthea, C. torquata, C. hemileucurus, C. orina, C. wilsoni, C. lutetiae and C. prunellei) and crown feather samples from two Coeligena taxa (C. orina and C. torquata inca). Additionally, crown and gorget patches from other hummingbird genera were analyzed, including Selasphorus sasin, Eugenes fulgens, Archilocus alexandri and Aglaiocercus coelestis with gorget, and Saucerottia cyanocephala with crown patch. Across the 15 analyzed iridescent feathers, the inferred feather inclination angles (δ) vary substantially, ranging from approximately 0 to 50 degrees (Fig. 3; Table 2). At the lower end of this range, Selasphorus sasin exhibited a δ of 0°, consistent with nearly horizontal feathers. Intermediate feather angles, δ = 15 to 30 degrees, were observed in several Coeligena species such as C. helianthea, C. lutetiae, and C. wilsoni. The largest inclination angles, δ ≈ 40 to 50 degrees, characterize species such as Eugenes fulgens and Archilocus alexandri (Fig. 3; Table 2).

Table 2 summarizes for each species and feather patch the inferred δ values, along with their corresponding nanomorphological parameters and filling-fraction type based on simulations. Additionally, Table 2 highlights three additional species: Coeligena helianthea (15°), C. wilsoni (29°), and C. prunellei (39°), for which the inferred feather inclination angles are consistent with those previously reported by Giraldo et al. (2021)9. Specifically, for C. helianthea, the estimation differs by only one degree from the previously reported, whereas for C. wilsoni and C. prunellei we recovered the same inclination angles.

In most cases, the experimentally reported reflectance spectra did not coincide with simulations performed under normal incidence relative to the feather plane (Fig. 3). Spectral overlap was achieved only after adjusting the capture angle. These results indicate non-zero feather inclination angles. Only one species, Selasphorus sasin, showed a perfect match between experimental spectrum and simulations at 0 degrees, consistent with nearly horizontal barbules (Fig. 3; Table 2). For all other species, the best-fit inclination angles required to reproduce the reported spectra corresponded to positive δ values, which were subsequently extracted from the fitting procedure. These results show that non-zero barbule inclination is a common feature among the iridescent hummingbird feathers analyzed in this study.

To directly validate the inferred barbule inclination angles under intact conditions, angular reflectance measurements were performed on a complete museum specimen of Coeligena orina (Fig. 4). The experimentally inferred inclination angles obtained under intact conditions are reported below. For the gorget, the maximum reflectance occurred at a capture angle corresponding to a barbule inclination of 26 degrees, which closely matches the value predicted by the ANATOBIRD model of 25 degrees. Similarly, for the crown patch, the experimentally determined barbule inclination angle was 14 degrees, consistent with the model prediction of 15°. In both cases, the simulated reflectance spectra were generated using the predicted inclination angles, matching the experimental spectra obtained for the intact specimen. These results demonstrate close agreement between theoretical predictions and experimental measurements of barbule inclination and angular reflectance in the intact hummingbird specimen.

Discussion

Geometric implications of barbule inclination

Iridescent coloration in hummingbirds emerges from a hierarchical organization spanning multiple spatial scales3,7, in which nanostructural multilayers determine spectral properties, while microstructural organizationgoverns the angular distribution of reflected light4. In this context, barbule inclination (δ), represents a key geometric parameter linking microstructure to macroscopic optical response.

Our results show that barbule inclination strongly controls the directionality of iridescent signals, small variations can alter the angle at which maximum reflectance (see Fig. 3; Table 2). Under a specular reflection model consistent with a Venetian-blind–like geometry2,9, and assuming illumination defined relative to the feather plane, the direction of maximum reflectance occurs at an angle close to 2δ (see Fig. 1c). This relationship establishes a direct and predictive link between microstructural geometry and the spatial distribution of reflected light2. Importantly, this geometric effect operates largely independently of nanostructural variation (e.g., Gruson et al., 2019). Building on this framework, we next examine how these geometric effects help reconcile discrepancies across previously reported reflectance datasets.

Reconciling previous reflectance datasets

Initially, simulations failed to reproduce the reflectance spectra reported in the literature, prompting us to investigate the origin of the discrepancy. We found that increasing the illumination angle led to close agreement with the measured spectra (Fig. 3). These differences can be explained by variation in microstructural orientation, which shifts the effective angle of maximum reflectance and alters the angular geometry of the reflected signal2,7,16,21. By accounting for barbule inclination δ, our framework enables more reliable comparisons of reflectance spectra across studies by relating the measured spectra to the effective angle of incidence determined by feather morphology (see Fig. 3).

In cases where multiple datasets were available for the same species (e.g., Eliason et al., 2020; Shawkey et al., 2009)7,16, differences in reported measurement angles further support the interpretation that variation among spectra is primarily driven by measurement geometry rather than by intrinsic nanostructural differences2. Despite careful control of illumination and observation geometry, “normal incidence” is typically defined relative to the feather plane rather than to individual barbules 19,20. This subtle but optically relevant distinction introduces a systematic offset between nominal and effective measurement geometry, without invalidating prior conclusions20. Instead, it offers a geometric reinterpretation that improves the comparability and interpretability of iridescence measurements across studies.

Consistent with this interpretation, and as illustrated in Fig. 2, the direction of maximum reflectance can be empirically identified as the angular condition at which the barbule plane is effectively aligned with the specular reflection geometry relative to the illumination direction9,20. Exploring reflectance over symmetric positive and negative angular deviations reveals a well-defined maximum, which serves as a robust proxy for this alignment. Framing measurements in terms of this maximum, rather than nominal normal incidence, reduces the confounding effect of barbule inclination and provides a more consistent basis for comparison across datasets.

Comparison with multilayer optical models

Reflectance calculations for complex biological systems using the transfer-matrix method have attracted considerable interest3,21,22. In this study, we not only consider nanostructural parameters, as is commonly done, but also integrate the macroscopic geometry of the system. In addition, we incorporate species-specific filling fractions (melanin, keratin, and air) derived from electron microscopy, extending previous approaches2,9, and improving the representation of melanosome composition across taxa (see Tables 1 and 2). While barbule inclination determines the angular position of maximum reflectance, species-specific nanostructural parameters control spectral shape and intensity. In situ validation, as shown in Fig. 4, revealed agreement within approximately 1° between inclination angles inferred from previously reported reflectance data for Coeligena orina and those measured directly, further supporting the robustness of the proposed geometric framework.

Estimated barbule inclination angles show remarkable agreement with previous reports9, and are further supported by the close correspondence between values inferred from reflectance spectra and those measured directly, with a deviation of ~ 1° in C. helianthea and identical values in C. wilsoni and C. prunellei (see Table 2).

Achieving accurate agreement between simulations and experimental data also requires accounting for interspecific variation in melanosome internal structure, as these differences modulate the effective optical response of the multilayer system (see Fig. 2e–h). In particular, the effective melanin concentration varies as a function of both melanosome packing density and layer thickness, directly influencing refractive index contrast and optical interference23,24. While a Type I filling fraction such as that described for Calypte anna in Giraldo et al. (2021), applies to several species, no single configuration applies universally; instead, some taxa are better represented by Type II or Type III arrangements, as proposed here (see Table 2).

Within this context, transfer-matrix approaches remain well suited for biological photonic systems that can be approximated as one-dimensional stratified media 2,3,25–28. For example, peacock feathers (Pavo cristatus) can still be reasonably described using effective multilayer models despite their two-dimensional organization27, whereas more complex three-dimensional architectures, such as the boomerang-shaped barbules of birds-of-paradise (Parotia lawesii), require full-wave approaches like finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations for accurate optical characterization29. While multilayer interference captures the primary mechanism underlying iridescent coloration in such models, biological photonic systems are inherently more complex, involving the combined effects of interference, scattering, and absorption. These interactions can enhance absorption and produce angle-dependent coloration beyond ideal multilayer predictions30, and structural heterogeneity and disorder may contribute functionally to optical behavior rather than acting solely as sources of noise31. In feather systems, features such as curvature, spatial variation in barbule orientation, and interactions between adjacent structures may further modulate reflectance, contributing to deviations from ideal multilayer behavior. Similarly, anisotropic porous multilayers in beetles (e.g., Hoplia coerulea) exhibit direction-dependent and nonlinear optical responses that fall beyond simplified one-dimensional models32, particularly in structurally disordered systems. Together, these observations highlight that the applicability of transfer-matrix approaches is ultimately constrained by the structural complexity of the underlying photonic architecture.

Biological and functional implications

Iridescent coloration plays a central role in hummingbird communication, contributing to courtship displays, territorial interactions, and species recognition 5,33,34. These signals are not defined solely by hue, but by the interaction between structural geometry and illumination/viewing directions, as the perceived color changes with the angle of illumination or observation 10,35.

Interspecific differences in melanin composition within barbules generate gradual variation in color properties36, as multilayered arrangements of melanosomes at the nanoscale determine the spectral potential of reflected light 2,7. While changes in feather orientation relative to solar illumination and to the position of the female can enhance contrast and signal visibility37, the spatial organization and orientation of barbules at the microscale modulate the directionality and angular distribution of the reflected signal (Fig. 1). This multiscale organization has been observed not only in extant birds but also in fossil species, highlighting the evolutionary conservation of the structural mechanisms underlying iridescent coloration38.

At the macroscopic level, feather curvature, patch geometry, and body posture further influence how these signals are ultimately presented to an observer5,33,39. While hummingbird structural coloration has been extensively studied, most investigations have primarily focused on nanostructural features of feathers, with less explicit attention to how microstructural organization and macroscopic optical properties jointly shape the expression of color under biologically relevant viewing conditions. Giraldo and colleagues (2021) demonstrated that barbules that are not coplanar with the feather plane exhibit fundamentally different optical behavior from those that are9.

In this context, variation in barbule inclination angle (δ) might itself be a consequence of selection pressures (e.g., dynamic camouflage) or might be coupled with variations in sexual display behavior (e.g., Simpson et al., 2019). During courtship and territorial displays, males can actively reorient or erect gorget and crown feathers, changing the macroscopic orientation of the feather plane relative to the sun and to the receiver. Importantly, such feather movements do not modify the intrinsic barbule inclination angle (δ). Instead, feather erection provides an additional behavioural degree of freedom that allows birds to align the feather plane such that the barbule-normal condition and therefore the direction of maximum reflectance predicted by δ intersects the receiver’s line of sight.

This decoupling between fixed microstructural geometry (δ) and dynamic feather positioning provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how hummingbirds actively control the directionality and visibility of iridescent signals. In this sense, barbule geometry constrains the angular “sweet spot” for iridescent signal transmission and may impose architectural constraints on the evolution of iridescent coloration40, while dynamic feather positioning enables individuals to exploit this constraint under biologically relevant viewing geometries. Together, these results suggest that barbule geometry provides a predictive framework linking microstructure to the spatial dynamics of iridescent signal transmission during biologically relevant interactions.

Methodological implications and standardization

There has been a longstanding concern regarding how iridescent coloration should be measured and reported. In particular, Gruson et al. (2019) highlighted that single reflectance measurements fail to capture the full variability of iridescent signals, as they do not account for their inherent angular dependence20. Instead, they proposed sampling across a range of illumination and detection angles to encompass the full variation in hue. Consistent with this perspective, our results indicate that a single reflectance value is insufficient to characterize iridescent coloration. By integrating optical modeling, however, we are able to evaluate reflectance responses arising from multiple geometric and structural configurations, thereby capturing a broader range of angular and spectral variation, even in cases where only a single reflectance value has been reported. In practice, we recommend performing angular sweeps and reporting both the angle of maximum reflectance and the corresponding spectral profile, which together provide a more robust and biologically relevant description of iridescent signals.

Despite the strong agreement between modeled predictions and experimental measurements, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, reflectance maxima are influenced not only by barbule inclination but also by additional factors such as surface roughness, feather curvature, and instrumental acceptance angles, which may introduce variability in measured spectra. These effects underscore the need for a system-specific approach, in which the optical geometry is first characterized, followed by angular sampling (e.g., using a spectrogoniometer) to identify the orientation at which maximum reflectance is achieved; this condition provides a robust and comparable metric for reporting iridescent coloration. Second, although the current framework captures key geometric effects, it does not explicitly incorporate dynamic behavioral adjustments or full three-dimensional feather architectures. Future work integrating these factors may further refine predictions of biologically relevant viewing geometries.

Overall, these considerations highlight the importance of explicitly defining measurement geometry and adopting standardized angular protocols when comparing iridescent signals across studies.
