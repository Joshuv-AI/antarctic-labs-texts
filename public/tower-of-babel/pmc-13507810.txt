Voluntary versus forced exercise modulates anxiety‑like behaviors and hippocampal inflammation gene expression following traumatic stress in rats
Authors: Nadia Abedi Oumali, Arvin Amiri, Mousa Kiani, Sakineh Shafia, Kataneh Abrari, Seyed Hamzeh Hosseini, Zahra Hosseini-khah, Hamideh Mohammadi-Berenjestanaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) arises after traumatic or life-threatening events and involves persistent anxiety and altered stress responses. Physical exercise is a proposed non-pharmacological intervention, yet evidence comparing voluntary and forced exercise regarding their behavioral and biochemical effects in experimental PTSD models is still limited.MethodsIn this experimental study, adult male rats were subjected to a single prolonged stress (SPS) protocol to induce a PTSD-like phenotype and were subsequently assigned to voluntary exercise, forced exercise, or control conditions. Anxiety-like behaviors were evaluated using behavioral tests, including the open field test, elevated plus maze, and light–dark box. The hippocampal mRNA expression levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL‑10, and TNF‑α, were quantified by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction.ResultsExposure to SPS induced a marked anxiety‑like behavioral phenotype, as evidenced by reduced exploration of anxiogenic zones across all behavioral paradigms, without significantly affecting general locomotor activity. SPS exposure was also associated with increased hippocampal expression of the pro‑inflammatory cytokines IL‑1β and TNF‑α, accompanied by reduced expression of the anti‑inflammatory cytokine IL‑10. Both voluntary and forced exercise attenuated SPS-induced anxiety-like behaviors and significantly improved hippocampal inflammatory gene expression. Voluntary exercise produced greater improvement across several behavioral measures. At the molecular level, both exercise modalities attenuated SPS-induced pro-inflammatory alterations, whereas voluntary exercise alone significantly restored the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10.ConclusionBoth voluntary and forced exercise effectively attenuated SPS-induced anxiety-like behavior and hippocampal inflammatory dysregulation. Voluntary exercise demonstrated greater behavioral improvement across anxiety-related paradigms. Although only the VolExc group reached statistical significance relative to the SPS group for IL-10, direct comparison between VolExc and ForExc did not reveal a significant difference, indicating that molecular outcomes were broadly comparable between the two exercise modalities. These findings suggest that voluntary exercise may provide behavioral advantages following traumatic stress, although the two modalities were not statistically distinguishable from one another at the molecular level.Keywords: Post‑traumatic stress disorders, Exercise, Physical activity, Anxiety behavior, Hippocampus, Neuroinflammation, Animal model

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a chronic stress-related condition characterized by persistent anxiety-like behaviors and long-lasting alterations in brain function (Ressler et al., 2022). Converging evidence indicates that these behavioral disturbances are accompanied by neuroinflammatory changes, which are increasingly recognized as integral features of the disorder (Ressler et al., 2022). Among brain regions implicated in PTSD, the hippocampus is particularly vulnerable due to its central role in emotional regulation and stress responsivity (Liberati and Perrotta, 2024). Clinical and preclinical studies consistently report structural and molecular abnormalities within the hippocampus following traumatic stress exposure, highlighting a close association between sustained anxiety and hippocampal inflammatory alterations in PTSD (Liberati and Perrotta, 2024, Li et al., 2023).

Given the complex behavioral and neurobiological features of PTSD, animal models are essential for systematically examining stress-induced changes under controlled experimental conditions (Sarapultsev et al., 2024). Among available paradigms, the single prolonged stress (SPS) model is widely regarded as a valid and translational approach for reproducing core PTSD-like phenotypes, particularly persistent anxiety-like behaviors (Ding et al., 2025). Importantly, SPS exposure has been reliably associated with hippocampal alterations at both structural and molecular levels, paralleling key findings reported in trauma-exposed populations (Ding et al., 2025, Andero, 2025). Accordingly, the SPS model provides a robust framework for investigating hippocampus-dependent behavioral and inflammatory outcomes relevant to PTSD.

In parallel with advances in experimental modeling, physical exercise has emerged as a promising non-pharmacological strategy for modulating stress-related psychopathology (Bisson et al., 2020). Within this context, a fundamental conceptual distinction exists between voluntary exercise (VolExc), which is self-initiated and reward-driven, and forced exercise (ForExc), which is externally imposed and may itself constitute a mild stressor (Hegberg et al., 2019, Greenwood and Fleshner, 2011). Although both modalities are commonly employed in preclinical research, they are not behaviorally or biologically equivalent, and their relevance to stress-based disorders may differ (Griesbach et al., 2012). This distinction is particularly important in PTSD, where baseline stress load and emotional reactivity are already altered (Speer et al., 2020). In animal models of PTSD, particularly the SPS paradigm, exercise interventions have been shown to improve cognition (Shafia et al., 2024) and anxiety-like behaviors and normalize stress-induced molecular alterations within limbic regions, including the hippocampus (Amiri et al., 2024). Notably, several of these studies have relied on ForExc protocols, such as treadmill running or forced running wheel, reporting reductions in behavioral indices of anxiety alongside suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways (Amiri et al., 2024, Patki et al., 2014, Ahmadi Veshki et al., 2022, Kim et al., 2026). While these findings underscore the therapeutic relevance of exercise in PTSD-like conditions, they provide limited insight into how the behavioral context of exercise itself may shape neurobehavioral and inflammatory markers.

In parallel, VolExc paradigms, most commonly voluntary wheel running, have been extensively investigated in models of chronic stress and affective disorders, where they are consistently associated with anxiolytic effects (Greenwood and Fleshner, 2011). Comparative studies in non-traumatized rodents further indicate that VolExc and ForExc elicit distinct neurobiological and emotional responses, including differences in stress hormone reactivity, central cytokine expression, and anxiety-like behavior (Nowacka-Chmielewska et al., 2022). These modality-dependent effects suggest that the degree of controllability and self-initiation inherent to VolExc may be a critical determinant of its impact on stress-sensitive neural circuits (Esch and Stefano, 2010, Gupta et al., 2024). However, despite this accumulating evidence, direct comparisons between VolExc and ForExc within PTSD models remain scarce, and few studies simultaneously assessed anxiety-like behavior and hippocampal inflammatory gene expression within the same experimental framework.

The present experimental study aimed to investigate the effects of VolExc and ForExc on anxiety‑like behavior and hippocampal inflammatory status in an animal model of PTSD. Behavioral outcomes were assessed using validated anxiety‑related paradigms, alongside molecular evaluation of hippocampal expression of the inflammatory markers interleukin−1 beta (IL‑1β), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‑α), and interleukin−10 (IL‑10).

Methods and Materials

Study design and experimental overview

This experimental study was conducted using adult male rats (200–220 g, n = 42) under controlled laboratory conditions. Animals were housed in standard polypropylene cages with free access to food and water and maintained under a 12 h light–dark cycle (lights on at 7:30 am) at a constant temperature (23 ± 2°C) and humidity (45%). After an acclimatization period, rats were randomly assigned to experimental groups based on stress exposure and exercise condition. The present study used the same animal cohort as our previous report on cognitive performance, thyroid function, and hippocampal integrity in this PTSD model (Oumali et al., 2026); the present analysis, however, focused on distinct behavioral (anxiety-like behavior across three paradigms) and molecular (hippocampal IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-10 expression) outcomes not addressed in that report.

The overall study followed a longitudinal design in which animals underwent stress or sham procedures, followed by an exercise intervention period. Behavioral assessments were subsequently performed, after which brain tissue was collected for molecular analyses.

All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences (ethical approval code: IR.MAZUMS.4.REC.1402.18297). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals used.

PTSD induction using the SPS model

Post‑traumatic stress disorder was induced using the SPS paradigm, a widely used and validated animal model of PTSD (Shafia et al., 2024). Briefly, rats were subjected to restraint stress for 2 h, immediately followed by a forced swimming session lasting 20 min in a cylindrical tank filled with water maintained at room temperature. After completion of the swimming stressor, animals were allowed a brief recovery period and were then exposed to ether anesthesia until loss of consciousness. Following the SPS procedure, rats were returned to their home cages and left undisturbed for a 7‑day incubation period to allow for the consolidation of PTSD‑like neurobehavioral alterations. Control animals were not exposed to the SPS procedure. All procedures were performed during the light phase of the cycle in a quiet room to minimize external stressors.

Experimental groups

Animals were randomly assigned to one of six experimental groups (n = 7 per group) based on exposure to SPS and exercise condition: Control (CTL), voluntary exercise (VolExc), forced exercise (ForExc), SPS, SPS + VolExc, and SPS + ForExc. Within each stress condition, sedentary animals (CTL and SPS groups) did not undergo exercise training, whereas exercised animals were subjected to either voluntary wheel running or forced treadmill running according to the assigned protocol.

VolExc consisted of moderate‑intensity wheel running, whereas forced exercise involved a treadmill running protocol matched for duration and overall workload. Control animals did not undergo any exercise intervention. This experimental arrangement resulted in six distinct groups and allowed for parallel evaluation of the effects of stress exposure and exercise modality within a single experimental framework.

Exercise intervention protocols

Two moderate‑intensity exercise paradigms were employed, consisting of voluntary wheel running and compulsory treadmill running. Exercise interventions were initiated seven days after SPS or sham procedures and continued for four consecutive weeks. ForExc sessions were conducted during the light phase of the light/dark cycle under standardized environmental conditions, while animals in the VolExc groups were housed in the exercise context with free wheel access for four weeks. Running activity in the VolExc groups was continuously monitored throughout the intervention using a magnetic revolution counter attached to each wheel, and the number of wheel revolutions was recorded daily for each animal.

VolExc was performed using a freely rotating running wheel installed in individual cages. Each rat assigned to the VolExc group was housed separately to allow unrestricted access to the wheel for 24 h per day throughout the intervention period. Wheel revolutions were continuously monitored using an external magnetic counter system, and running activity was recorded daily at a fixed time point. Voluntary running distance varied across animals, reflecting individual motivational and physiological differences (Oumali et al., 2026).

To enable comparison between the two exercise modalities, the treadmill protocol (10 m/min, 30 min/day, 5 days/week) was selected as a standardized moderate-intensity exercise paradigm based on established protocols. Because voluntary wheel running is inherently self-paced, individual running distances were expected to vary among animals. As previously described for this animal cohort (Oumali et al., 2026), only animals whose average daily running distance approximated the treadmill workload (approximately 300 m/day) were retained in the voluntary exercise groups, while rats exhibiting extremely low or excessive running activity were excluded from analysis. Beyond this distance-based selection criterion, equivalence between the two exercise modalities was further supported by comparable moderate-intensity exercise paradigms, identical intervention duration, and continuous monitoring of running activity throughout the study.

Compulsory exercise was conducted using a multi‑lane motorized treadmill designed for rodents. Prior to the initiation of the training protocol, rats underwent a habituation phase consisting of low‑speed treadmill exposure for 15 min per day over three consecutive days to reduce novelty‑related stress and improve task compliance. Following habituation, animals were subjected to a structured treadmill running protocol consisting of daily 30‑min sessions at a constant speed of 10 m/min, five days per week for four weeks. Treadmill sessions were consistently performed at the same time each morning.

To minimize variability and stress unrelated to the exercise intervention, animals that persistently resisted treadmill running or failed to complete at least 80 % of the scheduled sessions were excluded from subsequent analyses. The treadmill apparatus was cleaned and disinfected with 10 % ethanol after each session to prevent olfactory cues and cross-animal interference. No aversive stimuli, including electric shocks or air-puffs, were used to motivate treadmill running; instead, gentle manual guidance and gradual habituation were employed to encourage task compliance and minimize additional stress.

Control animals were handled daily but did not participate in exercise sessions. All exercise protocols were designed to induce moderate‑intensity physical activity without excessive fatigue or injury and were completed prior to behavioral assessments.

Assessment of anxiety-like behavior

Open Field Test

Locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior were initially assessed using the open field test (OFT). The apparatus consisted of a square polyvinyl chloride arena measuring 40 × 40 × 40 cm, with the floor divided into 16 equal squares defining central and peripheral zones. Each rat was placed individually in the center of the arena and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Animal behavior was recorded using a video camera positioned above the apparatus for subsequent analysis.

Locomotor activity was evaluated by measuring the total distance traveled, the number of crossings between squares, and indices of vertical activity, including rearing and grooming behaviors. Anxiety-like behavior was assessed by quantifying the time spent, distance traveled, and number of crossings within the central versus peripheral zones of the arena. The apparatus was cleaned thoroughly between trials to eliminate olfactory cues.

Elevated Plus Maze

Anxiety‑like behaviors were further evaluated using the elevated plus maze (EPM), a well‑validated paradigm that does not require prior learning or training. The maze was constructed of wood and consisted of two open arms measuring 50 × 10 cm and two closed arms measuring 40 × 50 × 10 cm, arranged opposite each other and elevated approximately 50 cm above the floor.

On the test day, each rat was placed individually at the center of the maze facing an open arm and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Behavioral parameters were recorded by direct observation and video tracking. The number of entries into the open arms and the total time spent in the open arms were used as primary indices of anxiety‑like behavior. An increase in open arm exploration, reflected by concordant changes in both measures and with at least one showing a statistically significant difference relative to controls, was interpreted as a reduction in anxiety‑like behavior.

Light–Dark Box Test

The light–dark box (LDB) test was employed as an additional measure of anxiety-like behavior based on the natural conflict between exploratory drive and aversion to brightly illuminated environments. The apparatus consisted of two compartments, a brightly lit chamber and a dark enclosed chamber, connected by a small opening. Rats were initially placed in the light compartment and allowed to move freely between compartments during the test session.

The latency to first entry into the dark compartment, defined as the placement of all four paws inside the chamber, was recorded. In addition, the total time spent in each compartment, as well as exploratory behaviors such as locomotion and rearing, was assessed. Rodents typically exhibit a preference for the dark compartment, and an increased proportion of time spent in the light compartment was interpreted as an anxiolytic-like response. The apparatus was cleaned between animals to prevent the influence of residual cues.

For all three behavioral tests (OFT, EPM, and LDB), video recordings were scored by an investigator who was blind to group allocation, in order to minimize potential observer bias in the assessment of anxiety‑like behavior.

Hippocampal tissue collection

Following completion of behavioral assessments, rats were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (2.5 mg/kg). After confirmation of adequate anesthesia, animals were euthanized by decapitation. The whole left hippocampus was rapidly dissected on ice, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −20 °C until further molecular analyses. No subregional dissection (e.g., CA1, CA3, or dentate gyrus) was performed prior to RNA extraction. All tissue handling procedures were performed promptly to preserve RNA integrity.

RNA extraction and quantitative real‑time PCR

The expression levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL‑1β, IL‑10, and TNF‑α, were assessed in hippocampal tissue using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. Brain tissues were homogenized, and total RNA was extracted using RNX‑Plus solution according to the chloroform–alcohol extraction protocol. RNA concentration and purity were evaluated by spectrophotometric analysis to ensure acceptable quality for downstream applications. Complementary DNA was synthesized from total RNA using a standard reverse transcription protocol. Gene‑specific amplification was subsequently performed using polymerase chain reaction. PCR conditions, including primer annealing temperature, cycle number, and reaction volumes, were optimized in preliminary experiments. Relative mRNA expression levels were determined for each target gene and used for subsequent statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 18 and GraphPad Prism. Data distribution was initially assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For normally distributed data, behavioral outcomes (open field test, elevated plus maze, and light–dark box test) were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with stress condition (control vs. SPS) and exercise modality (sedentary, voluntary, forced) as the two factors, whereas molecular outcomes were analyzed using one-way ANOVA to compare the six experimental groups. When a significant main effect or interaction was identified, Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was applied for multiple pairwise comparisons. For TNF-α, where the omnibus ANOVA approached but did not reach the conventional significance threshold (p = .050), the corresponding pairwise comparisons were treated as exploratory and are reported and interpreted accordingly. Behavioral assessments were performed in all animals (n = 7 per group). Based on predefined tissue availability and assay cost, molecular analyses were performed in a subset of animals per group (n = 5). All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes for the omnibus ANOVAs are reported as partial eta-squared (η²p) for the two-way behavioral analyses and as eta-squared (η²) for the one-way molecular analyses, calculated as (df_between × F) / (df_between × F + df_within).

Results

Open Field Test

Performance in the OFT was analyzed to evaluate locomotor activity and exploratory behavior across experimental groups. Total distance traveled differed significantly in the voluntary exercise groups. VolExc rats exhibited greater locomotor activity than CTL animals, and SPS + VolExc rats traveled significantly greater distances than SPS rats, whereas forced exercise did not significantly alter total locomotor activity (Fig. 1A). Analysis of anxiety-related parameters revealed significant group differences in center-related behaviors. Animals subjected to SPS exhibited a reduced time spent in the central zone (Fig. 1B) compared with control animals. Both VolExc and ForExc interventions increased center exploration relative to the stress group, with VolExc showing a more pronounced effect on time spent in the center than in the marginal zone (Fig. 1B-C). No significant differences were observed between exercise groups with respect to total locomotion, suggesting that changes in center-related measures reflected alterations in anxiety-like behavior rather than nonspecific effects on motor function. Two-way ANOVA confirmed significant main effects of SPS (F (Ressler et al., 2022, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 29.63, p < .001, η²p = .451) and exercise modality (F (Liberati and Perrotta, 2024, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 7.73, p = .002, η²p = .300) on time spent in the central zone, whereas the SPS × exercise interaction was not significant (F (Liberati and Perrotta, 2024, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 1.04, p = .363).

Elevated plus maze

Performance in the EPM was assessed to evaluate anxiety‑like behavior across experimental groups. Significant group differences were observed in anxiety‑related measures. SPS-exposed rats spent less time in the open arms (Fig. 2A) compared with control animals. Both VolExc and ForExc increased open arm exploration in SPS‑exposed rats relative to the SPS group. The effect appeared more pronounced in animals subjected to VolExc (Fig. 2B-C). Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of SPS (F (Ressler et al., 2022, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 59.60, p < .001, η²p = .623) and exercise modality (F (Liberati and Perrotta, 2024, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 14.22, p < .001, η²p = .441) on time spent in the open arms, with the SPS × exercise interaction approaching significance (F (Liberati and Perrotta, 2024, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 3.05, p = .060).

Light–dark box test

Anxiety-like behavior was further evaluated using the LDB test. Analysis of total transitions between compartments revealed no significant differences among experimental groups, indicating comparable general activity levels (Fig. 3A).

Significant group differences were observed in anxiety-related parameters. SPS-exposed rats spent less time in the light compartment and exhibited fewer entries into the light area compared with control animals. Both VolExc and ForExc increased exploration of the light compartment in SPS-exposed rats relative to the SPS group (Fig. 3B–C). Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of SPS (F (Ressler et al., 2022, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 10.79, p = .002, η²p = .231) and exercise modality (F (Liberati and Perrotta, 2024, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 4.71, p = .015, η²p = .208) on time spent in the light compartment, together with a significant SPS × exercise interaction (F (Liberati and Perrotta, 2024, Szeszko et al., 2018) = 28.72, p < .001, η²p = .615). Tukey post hoc comparisons confirmed that SPS rats spent significantly less time in the light compartment than controls (p = .002), and that both SPS + ForExc and SPS + VolExc rats spent significantly more time in the light compartment than the SPS group (both p < .001).

Notably, the direction of the exercise effect on light-compartment time differed according to stress condition, which accounts for the strong SPS × exercise interaction. Among non-stressed animals, both VolExc and ForExc reduced time spent in the light compartment relative to CTL, whereas among SPS-exposed animals, both exercise modalities markedly increased time spent in the light compartment relative to the SPS group. This stress-dependent, opposite-direction pattern indicates that the anxiolytic-like effect of exercise on this measure was specific to the stressed state rather than a uniform, stress-independent effect of physical activity.

No significant differences were detected between exercise groups in overall transition activity, supporting the interpretation that changes in L/DBOX performance reflected alterations in anxiety-like behavior rather than nonspecific effects on locomotion.

Hippocampal inflammatory gene expression

The effects of VolExc and ForExc on hippocampal inflammatory gene expression were evaluated using quantitative real-time PCR (Fig. 4). One-way ANOVA showed a significant overall difference in hippocampal IL-1β mRNA expression between groups (F (Ding et al., 2025, Sheikhzadeh Hesari et al., 2024) = 6.834, p < 0.001, η² = 0.59). For TNF-α, the omnibus ANOVA approached the conventional significance threshold (F (Ding et al., 2025, Sheikhzadeh Hesari et al., 2024) = 2.617, p = 0.050, η² = 0.35); given this borderline overall effect, the following pairwise comparisons for TNF-α should be interpreted with appropriate caution.SPS-exposed rats exhibited significantly increased hippocampal mRNA expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β compared with the CTL group (p < 0.001). Because the omnibus ANOVA for TNF-α approached but did not reach the conventional significance threshold, the corresponding post hoc comparisons should be interpreted with caution; these exploratory post hoc comparisons suggested a smaller increase in TNF-α expression relative to the CTL group (p < 0.01; Fig. 4A–B).

Both VolExc and ForExc significantly attenuated the SPS-induced elevation of IL-1β expression (p < 0.05). For TNF-α, given its borderline omnibus effect, post hoc comparisons suggested a similar attenuation pattern following both exercise interventions, which should be interpreted with caution. However, no statistically significant differences were detected between the VolExc and ForExc groups in SPS-exposed rats (Fig. 4A–B).

One-way ANOVA revealed a significant overall difference among the six experimental groups in hippocampal IL-10 mRNA expression (F (Ding et al., 2025, Sheikhzadeh Hesari et al., 2024) = 2.938, p = .033, η² = 0.38). SPS exposure was associated with a reduction in hippocampal IL-10 mRNA expression compared with the CTL group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Voluntary exercise significantly restored IL-10 mRNA expression in SPS-exposed rats compared with the SPS group (p < 0.05), whereas forced exercise increased IL-10 expression but did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4C).

No significant differences were observed among exercise groups in non-SPS animals for IL-1β and TNF-α, indicating that the SPS-associated changes in these pro-inflammatory cytokines primarily reflected modulation of stress-related inflammatory changes rather than baseline effects of exercise alone. For IL-10, however, non-SPS animals subjected to ForExc showed significantly higher expression than non-SPS sedentary controls (p < 0.05), suggesting a baseline stimulatory effect of forced exercise on this anti-inflammatory cytokine independent of stress exposure.

Discussion

The present study aimed to compare the effects of VolExc and ForExc on anxiety-like behaviors and hippocampal inflammatory gene expression in a rat model of PTSD induced by SPS. The results demonstrate that SPS exposure produced robust anxiety-like behavioral alterations and alterations in hippocampal inflammatory markers. Both exercise modalities attenuated these stress-induced behavioral and hippocampal inflammatory gene expression changes, indicating a general protective effect of physical activity. Our results indicate that both voluntary and forced exercise effectively attenuated SPS-induced anxiety-like behaviors and hippocampal inflammatory alterations. Behavioral improvements were generally more evident following voluntary exercise. In contrast, although voluntary exercise showed numerically greater improvement in inflammatory markers, no statistically significant molecular differences were detected between the two exercise modalities. Therefore, the present findings suggest a potential behavioral advantage of voluntary exercise while indicating comparable molecular responses under the current experimental conditions.

Based on the results of the present study, SPS-exposed rats exhibited a consistent anxiety-like behavioral pattern across the OFT, EPM, and L/DBOX tests, characterized by reduced time spent in the center of the OFT, decreased exploration of the open arms in the EPM, and diminished time spent in the light compartment of the LDB. Importantly, total distance traveled in the OF TEST and the number of transitions in the LDB were not significantly altered, indicating that these behavioral changes reflected anxiety-related alterations rather than nonspecific effects on locomotor activity. This pattern aligns with prior reports in rat models of PTSD employing SPS, where anxiety-related behavioral alterations are reliably detected across multiple paradigms without confounding impairments in motor function (Sarapultsev et al., 2024, Ferland-Beckham et al., 2021). Comparative studies in rodents have reported variable behavioral responses to ForExc, with some demonstrating anxiolytic-like effects (Duman et al., 2008) and others suggesting limited or context-dependent benefits (Shafia et al., 2023). In contrast, VolExc has been more consistently associated with reductions in anxiety-like behavior in rodent models of stress (Sheikhzadeh Hesari et al., 2024). These findings extend this behavioral literature to a PTSD-relevant framework, indicating that VolExc may confer superior anxiolytic-like effects under conditions of traumatic stress. It should be noted, however, that the anxiolytic-like effect of exercise was not uniform across all three behavioral paradigms. In the LDB test specifically, a significant SPS × exercise interaction indicated that the effect of exercise on time spent in the light compartment was stress-dependent: exercise increased light-compartment exploration only in SPS-exposed animals, whereas it reduced light-compartment exploration in non-stressed animals. This pattern suggests that, at least for this measure, exercise did not exert a generalized anxiolytic-like effect irrespective of stress history, but rather selectively normalized behavior in animals with a pre-existing anxiety-like phenotype, while potentially exerting a different, non-anxiolytic influence on exploratory behavior under non-stressed conditions. These findings indicate that both voluntary and forced exercise were associated with improvements in anxiety-like behavior and hippocampal inflammatory gene expression following SPS exposure. Although these parallel behavioral and molecular changes are consistent with previous evidence linking neuroinflammation to anxiety-related behavior, the present study was not designed to determine whether changes in hippocampal inflammatory cytokines directly mediated the behavioral improvements. Therefore, the behavioral and molecular outcomes should be interpreted as associated findings rather than evidence of a causal relationship. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the biological pathways underlying these associations.

Of note, the SPS × exercise interaction observed for time spent in the light compartment of the LDB (η²p = .615) represented the largest effect size detected in the present study, exceeding even the main effects of stress and exercise modality considered individually. This large interaction reflected a pattern in which the direction of the exercise effect was reversed depending on prior stress exposure: among non-stressed animals, both VolExc and ForExc modestly reduced time spent in the light compartment relative to CTL, whereas among SPS-exposed animals, both exercise modalities markedly increased light-compartment exploration relative to the SPS group. This crossover pattern suggests that exercise does not exert a uniform, stress-independent effect on light–dark exploratory behavior; rather, its anxiolytic-like influence appears to be specifically engaged under conditions of prior traumatic stress, possibly through normalization of stress-sensitive neurocircuitry that is not similarly recruited under non-stressed conditions. Given the magnitude of this interaction, it is arguably the most robust behavioral finding of the present study, and it underscores the state-dependent nature of exercise-related anxiolytic effects rather than a generalized behavioral consequence of physical activity.

The molecular findings of the present study indicate that exposure to SPS induces a distinct pro-inflammatory shift in hippocampal cytokine expression, as reflected by increased IL-1β and TNF-α mRNA levels alongside a concomitant reduction in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Both VolExc and ForExc attenuated SPS-induced elevations in the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α. For IL-10, although the overall group difference was significant, pairwise comparisons confirmed a significant restoration of IL-10 expression in the SPS + VolExc group relative to the SPS group, whereas the increase observed in the SPS + ForExc group did not reach statistical significance. However, direct comparison between the SPS + VolExc and SPS + ForExc groups did not reveal a statistically significant difference. Therefore, although only the VolExc group reached statistical significance relative to the SPS group, direct comparison between VolExc and ForExc did not reveal a significant difference, indicating that the two exercise modalities were not statistically distinguishable from one another at the molecular level.

Importantly, the absence of changes in IL-1β and TNF-α expression among exercised non-SPS groups suggests that the observed effects on these pro-inflammatory markers primarily reflect modulation of stress-related inflammatory dysregulation rather than baseline immunological effects of exercise alone. Notably, the significantly higher IL-10 expression observed in non-SPS ForExc animals relative to non-SPS sedentary controls indicates that forced exercise may exert some baseline immunomodulatory effect on this anti-inflammatory cytokine independent of stress. Collectively, these findings suggest that physical exercise is associated with attenuation of stress-related neuroinflammatory alterations. Importantly, the present data do not establish that modulation of hippocampal inflammatory signaling directly accounts for the observed behavioral improvements.

The present inflammatory findings are largely consistent with previous studies (Chen et al., 2023, Dib et al., 2021, Lee et al., 2018) employing SPS and related PTSD-like rat models, which have reported elevations in hippocampal IL-1β and TNF-α following traumatic stress exposure, accompanied by reductions in anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10 (Liu et al., 2016, Yang et al., 2022, Cotrone et al., 2021). Several investigations have demonstrated that treadmill-based ForExc can attenuate stress-induced pro-inflammatory signaling within limbic regions, supporting the notion that physical activity exerts anti-inflammatory effects even when externally imposed (Dong and Lin, 2025). Within this context, the current data extend prior work by directly comparing VolExc and ForExc within the same experimental framework. Both exercise modalities attenuated SPS-associated inflammatory alterations, indicating that engagement in physical activity per se is sufficient to counteract stress-related neuroinflammatory dysregulation, with no statistically significant molecular differences detected between VolExc and ForExc in SPS-exposed animals. Importantly, not all studies have reported uniform anti-inflammatory effects of ForExc under stress conditions. In some reports, ForExc has failed to normalize, or has even exacerbated, stress-related inflammatory responses, particularly under conditions of high workload or insufficient recovery (Svensson et al., 2016). Some reports have similarly linked VolExc with reductions in central pro-inflammatory cytokines across models of chronic stress and affective disturbance (Svensson et al., 2016). The current data are compatible with this broader literature and suggest that discrepancies across studies may be driven, at least in part, by differences in stress burden, exercise controllability, and experimental context rather than by exercise modality alone.

Traumatic stress is known to provoke hippocampal inflammation through multiple interacting neurobiological pathways (Karanikas, 2021). Dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis represents a central mechanism whereby prolonged or severe stress leads to impaired glucocorticoid feedback and the development of glucocorticoid resistance at the cellular level (Herman et al., 2016). Under such conditions, glucocorticoids lose their anti-inflammatory efficacy, permitting unchecked production of pro-inflammatory cytokines within stress-sensitive brain regions, including the hippocampus (EdA and Munhoz, 2016). This phenomenon has been well documented in both clinical PTSD populations (Szeszko et al., 2018) and preclinical SPS models (You et al., 2011). In parallel, stress exposure promotes microglial priming, rendering resident immune cells of the CNS hyper-responsive to subsequent challenges (Niraula et al., 2017). Primed microglia exhibit exaggerated release of IL-1β and TNF-α, contributing to a sustained pro-inflammatory milieu that disrupts synaptic signaling and cognition (Neher and Cunningham, 2019). The resulting imbalance between pro-- and anti-inflammatory cytokines within the hippocampus has been implicated in the persistence of anxiety-like behaviors and impaired stress adaptation (Hassamal, 2023). The inflammatory profile observed in SPS-exposed rats in the present study is consistent with these established mechanisms and reinforces the validity of the model for investigating stress-induced neuroimmune dysregulation. These mechanisms are discussed as potential explanations based on previous literature and were not directly examined in the present study.

Physical exercise exerts anti-inflammatory effects through a range of peripheral and central mechanisms, collectively referred to as exercise-induced immunomodulation (Krüger et al., 2016, Leal et al., 2018). Regular physical activity has been shown to suppress basal and stress-induced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines while enhancing anti-inflammatory signaling, including upregulation of IL-10 (Ropelle et al., 2010). Skeletal muscle-derived myokines released during exercise further contribute to this anti-inflammatory milieu by influencing immune cell function and cytokine production both systemically and within the brain (Leal et al., 2018). Moreover, VolExc, characterized by self-initiation and perceived controllability, is associated with reduced activation of stress pathways and more stable glucocorticoid signaling compared with ForExc (Svensson et al., 2016). In contrast, ForExc may partially engage stress-responsive circuits, particularly under demanding conditions, potentially limiting its anti-inflammatory efficacy (Hassamal, 2023). Although VolExc showed a numerically greater reduction in some inflammatory markers, this difference did not reach statistical significance compared with ForExc, suggesting that reduced stress load during physical activity may nonetheless contribute to its behavioral effects (Yau et al., 2011). Collectively, these findings suggest that both exercise modalities produced broadly comparable anti-inflammatory effects at the molecular level, although voluntary exercise was associated with additional behavioral advantages.

Overall, the present findings highlight the close association between traumatic stress, hippocampal neuroinflammation, and anxiety-like behavior. However, because this study did not directly assess endocrine pathways or other mechanistic mediators, the biological mechanisms linking exercise to neuroimmune regulation remain speculative. Future studies incorporating endocrine, neurochemical, and cellular measurements will be valuable for clarifying these mechanisms.

An additional limitation of the present study is that forced treadmill exercise may itself represent a mild stressor. Although animals underwent habituation before training, the externally imposed nature of treadmill running may partially activate stress-responsive pathways, potentially influencing both behavioral and inflammatory outcomes. Consequently, differences between voluntary and forced exercise should be interpreted with this consideration in mind.

A further limitation concerns sample size for the molecular analyses. Owing to the cost of the molecular assays, gene expression outcomes were assessed in a subset of five animals per group (n = 5), whereas behavioral testing was performed in the full cohort (n = 7 per group). This smaller molecular sample size may have reduced statistical power to detect modest between-group differences, particularly between the two exercise modalities, and should be considered when interpreting the molecular findings. In addition, because only mRNA expression was measured, gene expression changes may not directly reflect cytokine protein concentrations; protein-level assays such as ELISA or Western blotting would be needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that physical exercise effectively mitigates anxiety-like behavior and hippocampal inflammatory dysregulation induced by SPS in rats. Both VolExc and ForExc attenuated SPS-associated increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines. Although VolExc was associated with greater improvements in anxiety-like behavior, molecular responses were not statistically distinguishable between the two exercise modalities, although only voluntary exercise achieved significant restoration of IL-10 relative to the SPS group. The absence of significant inflammatory changes in exercised control animals underscores that these effects primarily reflect modulation of stress-related pathology rather than nonspecific baseline effects of physical activity. Taken together, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of moderate-intensity exercise in attenuating neuroimmune alterations associated with traumatic stress and suggest that voluntary exercise may provide additional behavioral benefits, although direct comparisons between the two exercise modalities were not statistically significant, and molecular responses were broadly comparable between the two exercise modalities under the present experimental conditions.
