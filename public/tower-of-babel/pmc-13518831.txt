Health-system barriers and missed opportunities in cardiovascular care in Sierra Leone: a scoping review
Authors: Mohamed B. Jalloh, Fatima Jalloh, Mamadu Baldeh, Thiess Lorenz, Foray M. Foray, Tahir Bockarie, Gari D. Clifford, Abdul Jalloh, Andrew Farlow, Bamba Gaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality is rising in Sierra Leone, but the health-system drivers of this trend are not well characterised. We mapped health-system barriers and facilitators for CVD care in Sierra Leone using a systems lens tied to universal health coverage (UHC).MethodsWe conducted a scoping review following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. We searched MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Global Health, and African Journals Online (1 Jan 2000 – 10 May 2025), Of 498 unique records, we included 40 sources reporting CVD-relevant data. Findings were mapped to WHO health-system building blocks, and synthesised narratively.ResultsOur findings show a health system shaped by path dependence: investments in infectious disease programmes have strengthened vertical delivery platforms with limited integration of non-communicable disease services. Facility readiness averaged 41% for HIV services versus 16.8% for cardiovascular care. An urban risk paradox was identified: urbanisation increased the odds of hypertension (OR 1.46) and diabetes (OR 1.84), while primary care infrastructure remained more oriented toward rural maternal health. Service delivery was undermined by diagnostic gaps; limited access to neuroimaging for stroke was associated with a threefold increase in mortality. High out-of-pocket costs narrowed effective coverage toward wealthier groups, and recurrent medicine stockouts reinforced distrust and disengagement from formal care. Scalable enablers included task-sharing, digital tools, pooled procurement, and community engagement.ConclusionStrengthening task-shared primary care, ring-fenced CVD budgets, pooling drug procurement, and improving digital infrastructure could accelerate UHC-effective coverage in Sierra Leone. Evidence on cost-effectiveness and socio-cultural determinants remains limited and should guide implementation research.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the world’s leading cause of death. In 2023, it caused 19.2 million deaths (95% UI 17.4–20.4), about one in three deaths worldwide1. The burden has shifted toward low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where more than three-quarters of global CVD deaths now occur1,2. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is the only region where both crude and age-standardised CVD mortality continue to rise3. In West Africa, about one in three adults has hypertension, and age-standardised mortality from ischaemic heart disease has doubled since 20004.

Sierra Leone is emblematic of these trends. The population aged 55 years and older is projected to nearly double by 2050, from about 0.8 million in 2020 to more than 1.5 million5. Much of this growth will occur in rural districts where health-service infrastructure is weakest6. In 2021, the country recorded a Universal Health Coverage (UHC) service-coverage index of 41/100, and an effective-coverage index (which accounts for quality of care) of 42/100, score that are approximately 13 points below the median for countries with comparable per-capita health expenditure7,8. Decomposition analyses attribute much of this shortfall to limited care for CVD and other non-communicable disease (NCD), whereas immunisation and family-planning services score well7. National mortality reports attribute about 14 % of deaths to CVD9, while risk factors and cardiometabolic multimorbidity are rising10. Specialist care, diagnostics and essential medicines remain scarce outside the capital city, Freetown9,11–14. Without new interventions, stroke- and heart-failure disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) are projected to rise by 75% by 203515,16.

To inform targeted policy action, we conducted a scoping review of cardiovascular care in Sierra Leone. We mapped barriers and facilitators across the six World Health Organization (WHO) health-system building blocks (financing, service delivery, workforce, information systems, essential medicines and governance) to identify evidence-based strategies that can strengthen equitable, high-quality cardiovascular services and accelerate national progress toward UHC.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a scoping review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses modified statement for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines17 to systematically map available evidence on health-system barriers and facilitators affecting CVD care in Sierra Leone. The completed PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided in Supplement Information.

Search strategy

Guided by a medical librarian, we developed a comprehensive search strategy using both controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords for “Sierra Leone,“ “cardiovascular diseases,“ and the six WHO health-system building blocks. We searched the following electronic databases from January 1, 2000, to May 10, 2025: MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Global Health, and African Journals Online. The search period was selected to capture health-system developments from the post-conflict reconstruction era through to the present.

To mitigate publication bias and capture policy-relevant data in this low-resource setting, we supplemented database searches with a targeted grey literature review. This included searching the repositories of the Sierra Leone Ministry of Health, WHO, and relevant institutional archives. Reference lists of included studies were hand-searched to identify additional sources. The full electronic search strategy is detailed in Box 1.

Selection process and charting

Search results were imported into screening software for deduplication. Three reviewers (M.B.J., F.J., and F.M.F.) independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Potentially relevant records underwent full-text review. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through consensus or consultation with a senior reviewer (B.G.). The selection process and reasons for exclusion are documented in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

After deduplication, three reviewers (M.B.J., F.J. and F.M.F.) independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. From 498 unique records, 40 met the inclusion criteria.

We developed a standardised data charting form, which was pilot-tested on the first ten included studies. Data extraction was conducted by three independent reviewers. To ensure accuracy and distinguish primary findings from secondary citations, data were extracted strictly from the “Results” sections of included studies.

Synthesis approach and conceptual framework

Given the methodological heterogeneity of the studies included (ranging from facility audits to qualitative interviews), a meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, we conducted a narrative synthesis. Findings were thematically mapped to the WHO health-system building blocks and interpreted through a complex-adaptive systems (CAS) lens to identify feedback loops and non-linear interactions between components. We used the WHO building blocks framework because it dominates global health policy dialogue and enables direct comparison with national NCD plans18. We map its six blocks onto the cardiovascular continuum, from risk prevention to long-term management. Gaps in one block can propagate across others, a pattern familiar in chronic disease research19. Alternative frameworks, such as Health System Dynamics, capture complexity but lack the policy familiarity needed for local uptake. To overcome these limits in chronic care, where system relations are iterative and nonlinear20, we incorporated adaptive systems thinking concepts, including feedback loops, path dependence, and emergence properties21. Cross-cutting enablers are grounded in this literature rather than treated as extra blocks, and can influence multiple blocks concurrently, consistent with evidence from low-income settings22. Each building block ultimately manifests as gain or loss in UHC-effective coverage, linking system functions to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.8.1 (Fig. 1).

Ethics statement

This review synthesised published and grey literature and did not involve human participants or identifiable data; institutional ethics approval was not required.

Results

Study characteristics

Our systematic search identified 40 studies meeting the inclusion criteria following screening. Full-texts were retrieved for 33 studies; seven studies where full texts were unavailable were included based on abstracts containing extractable quantitative data (Supplement Fig. 1). The included studies comprised cross-sectional studies (n = 15), mixed-methods approaches (n = 11), qualitative research (n = 13), observational studies (n = 7), retrospective analyses, and policy evaluations. Studies spanned 2000–2025, capturing post-conflict reconstruction through recent reforms. Geographic coverage included urban-only (n = 11), rural-only (n = 11), combined urban-rural (n = 12), and national-level analyses (n = 8). All studies addressed multiple WHO building blocks: service delivery (n = 38), governance (n = 36), health workforce (n = 33), financing (n = 33), health information systems (n = 29), and access to medicines (n = 25), with most examining four or more blocks simultaneously. Examples of study-level barriers by access dimension are now in Supplementary Table S1.

Health systems barriers by the WHO building block

We identified factors that facilitate or impede access to CVD care in Sierra Leone across all six WHO health-system blocks. UHC is achieved when all people receive the full continuum of quality services, from promotion and prevention to treatment, rehabilitation, and palliation, without financial hardship, as captured in SDG target 3.823. Equitable access, defined as the capacity of individuals and communities to obtain timely, appropriate, continuous, and affordable care, comprises four dimensions: availability, geographic reach, affordability, and acceptability, each mapped to the six WHO health-system building blocks20.

Cardiovascular health depends on an integrated optimisation of eight elements. These span behavioural factors (healthy diet, regular physical activity, nicotine avoidance, adequate sleep) through physiological markers (normal body-mass index, blood lipids, glucose, and blood pressure)24,25. Repeated stockouts of essential medicines, weak primary care, and the urban concentration and rural paucity of advanced diagnostics indicate how many LMICs fall short in UHC26,27. Key findings are mapped below, distinguishing between service delivery processes and physical infrastructure.

Service delivery

Service delivery for cardiovascular disease is characterised by a distinct geographical and structural mismatch. While general service availability in Sierra Leone is moderate, specific readiness for NCD management remains critically low. Facility assessments reveal a stark disparity where readiness scores for HIV services average 41% compared to only 16.8 % for cardiovascular services. This structural exclusion effectively creates a care desert for chronic conditions within the existing primary care network. This gap is further complicated by an urban risk paradox. Although the primary health unit network was historically optimised for rural maternal and child health, recent data indicate that urbanisation acts as a non-linear driver of disease risk. Urban residents show significantly higher odds of hypertension (OR 1.46) and diabetes (OR 1.84) compared to rural populations. However, a hidden accumulation of undiagnosed risk remains concentrated in urban informal settlements where accessible public care is absent, creating a disconnect between the epidemiological transition and the health system’s geographical footprint.

Regarding emergency care, capacity averages 66% across Freetown hospitals. However, referral processes are fragmented; the National Emergency Medical Services coverage is fragmented, and ambulances lack essential cardiac equipment, including electrocardiographic monitoring. An audit in five rural peripheral units in Tonkolili found one functioning sphygmomanometer and no laboratory transport links. Transfer delays are substantial, with median stroke transfer times reported at 25 h. National data on 14,266 transfers confirm that delays from district hospitals contribute to crowding at tertiary centres. This is exacerbated by community beliefs, gendered decision making, transport costs consuming one-third of rural weekly income for families.

Health workforce

The health workforce faces a dual crisis of absolute shortage and internal maldistribution. A significant internal brain drain is driven by vertical programming, where donor-funded initiatives for HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria recruit the most capable staff or absorb their time with disease-specific reporting. This leaves the general system responsible for cardiovascular care understaffed and undertrained. The national physician density is 1.34 per 10,000 population, falling below the regional mean of 4.0 per 10,00028. Specialist capacity is severely restricted; recent ministry reports and professional-society listings identify only three cardiologists in the public sector and no resident cardiac surgeon (Ministry of Health, unpublished 2025 data).

Task-shifting models have shown promise as a potential solution. In the Kono district, a nurse-led NCD clinic supported by physician mentorship and specific protocols achieved retention rates exceeding 50% and significant reductions in blood pressure. However, these successes largely remain isolated within donor-supported enclaves. In the broader public system, the lack of specialised training and the absence of standardised protocols for hypertension and stroke management at the primary care level result in variable quality of care and missed opportunities for secondary prevention.

Health information systems

Surveillance and information systems for cardiovascular disease exhibit broken feedback loops. While systems like the national District Health Information Software (DHIS2) tracks over 250 indicators but includes only four metrics relevant to NCD, none of which are specific to stroke or heart failure29. Reporting completeness for these NCD indicators was 41% in 2023, compared to 89% for malaria and immunisation14. While DHIS2 capture aggregate data, the quality is often poor with significant gaps in recording vital signs and risk factors. An evaluation of the hypertension surveillance system revealed that 57% of frontline health workers received no timely feedback on the data they collected. Consequently, data collection is viewed by staff as a bureaucratic ritual for upstream reporting rather than a clinical tool for local decision-making. This lack of reliable intelligence prevents the system from self-correcting or allocating resources based on actual disease burden. Furthermore, the fragmentation of information systems between vertical programmes and the general health system means that patient records are often disjointed, preventing the longitudinal tracking essential for chronic disease management.

Infrastructure and essential commodities

Physical infrastructure and supply chain reliability present major constraints. First-line antihypertensives are stocked in only 31% of facilities; no facility carries all 24 essential CVD medicines14. Shortages vary by region; in Kono and Tonkolili, supplies of atenolol and glucose strips are sporadic. The National Medical Supplies Agency prioritises commodities for infectious diseases and maternal health over NCDs. The Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) study links poor drug availability to a 32% higher risk of major CVD events at the community level30.

Diagnostic infrastructure is similarly limited: only 57% of facilities possess functional blood pressure equipment14. At the tertiary level, intermittent neuroimaging forces clinical diagnosis of stroke alone, which is associated with a threefold higher mortality risk. Reconditioned pacemaker initiatives show feasible workarounds, but remain small relative to system-wide gaps.

Health financing

Financial access acts as a rigorous selection pressure that effectively limits survival to the wealthiest quintiles. Unlike maternal and child health services covered by the Free Health Care Initiative, cardiovascular disease management requires total out-of-pocket expenditure for diagnostics and chronic medications. Data confirms that the need for care is statistically clustered among the wealthy. For the majority of the population, the cost of care is catastrophic. The expense of a single diagnostic investigation often exceeds the monthly household income. This financial toxicity forces patients to make impossible trade-offs between health and basic subsistence. While there is some willingness to pay for social health insurance among the informal sector, the current reliance on out-of-pocket payments drives patients into poverty or out of the biomedical system entirely.

Leadership and governance

Governance is characterised by high entropy and fragmentation. Although the 2020 National NCD policy aligns with WHO “best buy” recommendations, a significant policy-practice gap remains due to the lack of operational funding31. The governance landscape is fractured by the proliferation of partners and vertical funding streams, which create parallel command structures. These parallel systems erode the Ministry of Health’s central authority to direct resources toward cardiovascular priorities. Steering committees often operate without binding authority, and critical positions within the directorate have historically remained vacant or under-resourced, disrupting the continuity and accountability necessary for long-term system strengthening.

Emerging enablers

Several studies identified potential system enablers. Digital decision-support applications have been piloted to facilitate protocol-based care.

Task-sharing models, where nurses manage uncomplicated cases, have shown promise when supported by remote mentoring and electronic audits. Embedding these expanded roles in licensing regulations clarifies accountability and mitigates professional resistance, raising the probability that pilots will mature into policy.

Additionally, community engagement strategies, such as health clubs and participatory mapping, have been used to address literacy and geographic barriers in specific districts. However, these innovations require foundational strengthening in financing, infrastructure, and governance to achieve impact.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesises evidence from 40 studies to map the health-system barriers driving the high burden of CVD in Sierra Leone. We found that the health system is organised to respond to acute infectious illness but is not configured to provide the sustained, longitudinal care that cardiovascular disease requires. The constraints reflect interlocking weaknesses in service delivery, the health workforce, information systems, and financing. A CAS perspective suggests that these limitations are not discrete deficiencies but a product of entrenched path dependence (Fig. 1). Decades of prioritising infectious disease control have produced a system in which readiness for HIV services is substantially higher than readiness for cardiovascular care (41 vs. 16.8%). As a result, cardiovascular interventions are delivered in settings with limited infrastructure and support, where they must compete for scarce resources in a system designed for a different epidemiologic profile.

A major problem is that the health system has been shaped for years around separate, donor-funded programmes. HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria programmes often have strong supply chains and good data systems, but these do not usually support NCDs. These programmes can also pull the best staff into better-paid donor roles, leaving general outpatient clinics short-staffed.

Because programmes run in parallel, the Ministry of Health in Sierra Leone must manage multiple reporting lines and different funding rules. This makes coordination difficult. As a result, the system struggles to reuse existing resources, like GeneXpert machines or HIV counsellors, to help with hypertension and stroke. That leads to wasted effort and missed chances to integrate services.

Where people live strongly affects who gets care, and the gap is not simple. While the primary health unit network was designed to extend coverage to rural populations for maternal and child health, the CVD epidemic is accelerating in urban centres. We identified an urban risk paradox where urbanisation raises the chances of hypertension and diabetes, yet the public primary care infrastructure in these high-density areas is least equipped to manage them. This means many people, especially in informal settlements, build up risk without knowing it.

This is compounded by diagnostic blindness at the primary level, where the absence of functional sphygmomanometers and glucometers prevents early detection. At the tertiary level, the failure of neuroimaging technology forces clinicians to manage stroke via clinical diagnosis alone, a practice associated with a threefold increase in mortality risk32.

Failures in medicine supply and patient responses can create a reinforcing cycle that erodes health-system capacity. Recurrent stockouts of essential medicines in public facilities signal unreliability to patients and communities. In response, many patients seek alternatives such as private pharmacies, informal drug sellers, or traditional healers33. This behaviour is often a practical response to uncertainty and out-of-pocket constraints, but it fragments care and undermines long-term adherence.

As demand moves away from the public sector, facilities lose cost-recovery revenue, further constraining their ability to replenish medicines and provide consistent services. Distrust deepens, use of public care declines further, and the cycle intensifies. Over time, public hospitals are increasingly accessed as a last resort, late in the disease course, often after catastrophic events such as haemorrhagic stroke.

Financial barriers strongly shape who can access and remain in CVD care. Unlike the Free Health Care Initiative, which removes user fees for maternal and child health, most CVD services are financed through out-of-pocket (OOP) payments, which disproportionately burden poorer households. Our synthesis indicates that the need is concentrated in the highest wealth groups, while for many others, the long-term cost of chronic CVD management is catastrophic and can exceed total household income. These pressures force households to choose between healthcare and basic needs, driving high loss to follow-up.

At the system level, financial barriers remain among the most pervasive obstacles to timely care. OOP payments constitute about half of current health expenditure in Sierra Leone, a level associated with delayed presentation and high risk of catastrophic spending for emergencies requiring imaging, prolonged admission, or referral. Regional comparators illustrate how financing variations shape access. World Bank estimates for 2022 suggest OOP shares of about 25% in Ghana and about 76% in Nigeria34 Public financing remains constrained relative to the Abuja Declaration benchmark of allocating at least 15% of government expenditure to health, limiting risk pooling and subsidies for high-cost services. In practice, point-of-care payments for diagnostics and medicines can convert clinical urgency into administrative delay and worsen outcomes for time-sensitive disease.

Sierra Leone’s very low physician availability makes task-sharing and protocolised care necessary for chronic CVDs, particularly hypertension. In Bombali District, implementation work suggests that nurse-led hypertension management using standardised protocols can be feasible, supporting the case for expanding simplified care models aligned with local staffing realities35.

However, feasibility does not guarantee scale. Task-sharing initiatives remain fragile when supervision, reliable drug supply, protected scope of practice, and retention incentives are weak. This matters because cardiovascular control requires continuity: protocols only translate into outcome gains when they are embedded in stable teams, dependable commodities, and routine follow-up systems. In Sierra Leone, evidence from surveillance and service evaluations points to persistent weaknesses in routine data flow and follow-up infrastructure, which can undermine long-term hypertension control even when frontline delivery models are introduced36.

Comparative experience illustrates the governance requirements of scale. In Rwanda, task-sharing was expanded through centralised accountability and performance-based financing37,38. By contrast, Sierra Leone lacks binding governance structures to support these cadres at scale, raising the risk that gains achieved in pilot settings will not persist once external support concludes.

Digital health tools were identified as potential enablers for protocol adherence, but implementation is constrained by the digital divide. Successful models from Ghana and Rwanda link patient data to insurance claims39,40; however, transferring these approaches to Sierra Leone is challenged by infrastructure realities. With only 23% of facilities possessing electricity and just 4% having reliable internet access, reliance on cloud-based or real-time decision support is premature. Digital interventions, therefore, require “offline-first” capabilities to remain viable outside the capital, Freetown. More broadly, enabling technologies are unlikely to deliver sustained benefit unless they are matched to foundational capacity in infrastructure, financing, and workforce support, including reliable access to essential medicines that maintain patient trust.

Chronic CVD risk management needs to become a routine function of primary care in Sierra Leone, supported by simple, standardised workflows that enable continuity over time. Pragmatic building blocks include reliable blood pressure measurement, longitudinal registers, appointment and defaulter tracing, and protocol-guided treatment that can be delivered by non-physician clinicians with supervision. These elements align with regional evidence that hypertension outcomes improve when training is coupled with medicine availability, supportive supervision, and functioning information systems41. Task-sharing should therefore be understood not as a time-limited pilot, but as a health-system reform. Feasibility evidence is encouraging in defined settings, but sustainability depends on governance and financing that institutionalise new roles, protect time for chronic care, ensure supervision, and address retention and safety42,43. Without these supports, early gains may diminish as workloads shift, staff turnover rises, and chronic care responsibilities remain weakly embedded in routine service delivery.

Strengthening first-level referral capacity is also essential. Limited specialist availability and the absence of resident cardiac surgery indicate that referral alone cannot substitute for functional intermediate services. In this context, approaches such as PEN-Plus, which support structured decentralisation of severe chronic NCD services to first-level referral hospitals, may offer a pragmatic pathway for conditions that cannot be managed at the PHU level44,45. Such models are particularly relevant where tertiary services are concentrated, transport is costly, and delays in escalation contribute to avoidable morbidity and mortality.

Financing reform is central to any credible cardiovascular care strategy. The high reliance on out-of-pocket spending identified in the sources included in this review is likely to contribute to delayed presentation, treatment interruption, and poor continuity. The unimplemented 2017 Social Health Insurance Act is therefore not a peripheral policy detail, but a major unrealised mechanism for risk pooling and predictable chronic-care financing. In principle, the Act establishes a dedicated authority and a framework for mandatory and universal health insurance intended to reduce payment barriers at the point of care. The policy challenge is sequencing. Benefit design, provider payment systems, administrative capacity, and public trust need to be developed before major entitlement expansion, otherwise reform may increase expectations without reliably improving access.

Finally, “digital health” should be framed as a means to strengthen core functions rather than an end in itself. Low electricity and internet access outside urban centres limits feasibility of real-time, platform-heavy interventions. Designs that are offline-capable, built around routine clinical workflows, and matched to maintenance capacity are more likely to be sustainable46.

Despite growing attention to CVD in Sierra Leone, the evidence base remains limited and current strategies have produced only modest gains31. Implementation bottlenecks in financing, governance, and inter-sectoral coordination remain under-explored, leaving successive national plans underfunded and fragmented. These constraints suggest that the main gap is not only technical knowledge, but also the political and institutional capacity to convert plans into funded, accountable delivery systems.

Reliable CVD surveillance is a major priority. The only national STEPwise survey was conducted in 2009 and was incomplete, while routine reporting still aggregates ischaemic heart disease, stroke, and rheumatic heart disease into a single code. The absence of dedicated registries forces reliance on proxy diagnoses that may misclassify burden17 and weaken advocacy and resource allocation. Facility registries show selection bias, and generic quality-of-life tools lack local validation15. IHME models for Sierra Leone rely on sparse local inputs, so forecast accuracy declines in successive updates15. Strengthening the DHIS2 to capture validated cardiovascular indicators and outcomes could provide continuous, representative data.

Evidence on cost-effective cardiovascular care remains sparse. Beyond a handful of studies on hypertension and diabetes management, few studies evaluate acute coronary syndrome, decompensated heart failure, or palliative capacity, although facility audits suggest these services are rarely available47. Priority research should address: (1) care cascades and continuity; (2) referral and emergency pathways; (3) essential medicines and diagnostics; (4) task-sharing at scale and unintended effects;22–24 (5) high-value routine indicators and longitudinal tracking; and (6) political economy, accountability, and community experience.

This review synthesises disparate evidence on CVD-related health-system functions in Sierra Leone using a structured framework and a systems lens. This approach enables comparison across domains and across the care continuum, and helps identify interactions between service delivery, workforce, financing, information systems, governance, and access to medicines and technologies. Several limitations should be considered. Most included studies were cross-sectional or subnational, limiting national inference. Abstract-only records were included when they provided extractable quantitative data, which reduced exclusion in a data-sparse setting but constrained contextual interpretation. Formal risk-of-bias grading was not undertaken because the purpose was to map the evidence landscape rather than pool effect estimates. The findings should therefore be interpreted as an integrated description of constraints and system interactions, rather than as causal estimates of intervention effectiveness.

Conclusion

Cardiovascular disease prevention and care in Sierra Leone remain constrained by a system designed mainly for acute, episodic care. Progress toward universal health coverage will require a shift from vertical programmes to integrated chronic-care platforms. Existing HIV infrastructure, including supply chains, data systems, follow-up pathways, and community care, could be adapted to support NCDs. Financial barriers must also be addressed. Out-of-pocket payments make sustained cardiovascular care inaccessible for many households, especially the poorest. Expanding social insurance or free-care packages to include essential cardiovascular services and medicines would make coverage more meaningful. Future research should test scalable task-sharing models, reliable medicine delivery, and integrated primary-care networks. Achieving the 2030 SDGs will require health-system redesign aligned with Sierra Leone’s cardiovascular transition.
