Envelopes and evolutes
Ragni Piene

Abstract
. The study of evolutes of plane curves goes back at least to Huygens, and was continued and extended to space curves by Monge, Darboux, and others. Salmon studied projective curves and surfaces and their evolutes and gave many enumerative formulas for their degrees and number of singularities. We define envelopes of families of linear spaces in projective space. In order to define evolutes we impose a notion of perpendicularity, which allows us to consider the normal spaces to a projective variety. The evolute of a projective hypersurface is the envelope of the family of normal spaces. For a variety of dimension r r in n n -space, the evolute is defined as the ( n − r ) (n-r) th “iterated” cuspidal locus of the map from the total space of the normal spaces to projective space. Thus the envelope can be interpreted as a ( n − r ) (n-r) th order Thom–Boardman singularity. Further higher order Thom–Boardman singularities correspond, for a curve in the plane or in 3-space, to classical objects like the vertices of the curve; for a surface in 3-space, they give the cuspidal curve – and its cusps – on the evolute. Using known formulas for Thom polynomials we are able to verify and generalize many of Salmon’s formulas and find new ones.

1.
Introduction

The evolute of a curve in the real Euclidean plane is the locus of its centers of curvature (Huygens 1673, see
[
Huy
]
), i.e., the centers of its osculating circles. Alternatively, it can be defined as the envelope of the family of normal lines to the curve.
A hundred years later, Monge
[
Monge
]
considered space curves (“courbes à double courbure”). He replaced the centers of the osculating circles by the polar lines (“axes polaires”) – the lines through the center of an osculating circle perpendicular to the osculating plane. The union of these lines forms the “surface des pôles” (polar surface) of the curve (see
[
Del
]
*3.2, pp. 240–243). Monge observed that this surface is developable and equal to the envelope of the normal planes to the curve. He showed how to construct infinitely many curves – “développées” (evolutes) – on this surface, with the property that the tangents of these curves all intersect the given curve; indeed, the tangents are normals to the given curve. In particular, the “surface des pôles” is
foliated
by the “développées”.
These “développées” were also studied by Darboux
[
Darb
]
*p. 18.
Monge introduced the envelope of the polar lines, which he called the “arête de rebroussement” (cuspidal edge). Thus the tangents to the cuspidal edge of the polar surface are the polar lines of the given curve.
For more on the history of evolutes of space curves in the 18th and 19th century, see
[
Del
]
.

The natural generalization of the evolute of a plane curve to a space curve, in Euclidean space, is to take for the evolute
the locus of its centers of
spherical
curvature, i.e., the centers of its osculating spheres
[
P1
]
*p. 101.
Alternatively, one can define the evolute of a space curves as the cuspidal edge of the envelope of the family of normal planes to the curve. This evolute was called the “Evolute zweiter Art” (evolute of the second kind) by Blaschke and Leichtweiss
[
BL
]
*p. 56. They observed that the evolute is characterized by the fact that its osculating planes are the normal planes to the given curve. Hence the space evolute is a solution to the following interpolation problem: Given a space curve, find a space curve such that its osculating planes are the normal planes to the given curve.

Porteous
[
P1
]
treated real curves in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
from the point of view of differential geometry. There are also more recent works such as
[
Fuchs
]
,
[
FT
]
,
[
FIRST
]
.
The
generalized evolute
of a curve in higher dimensional Euclidean space was defined by Romero-Fuster and Sanabria-Codesal
[
Romero
]
and Uribe-Vargas
[
UV
]
as the locus of the centers of its osculating hyperspheres.
In
[
P1
]
, Porteous also considered the evolute (which he called the
focal surface
) of a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In particular he gave a detailed local description of its singularities.

Salmon treated the theory of polar surfaces (he called them polar developables) and evolutes of
complex projective
space curves. He observed that the cuspidal edge of the polar developable is the locus of the centers of spherical curvature
[
Salmon
]
*Art. 372, p. 339. In particular, he gave formulas for their degrees in terms of the numerical characters of the given curve. He also studied the evolute (which he called the “surface of centres”) of a surface in projective 3-space; in particular, he found formulas for the degree and class of the evolute and for the number of umbilics of the surface.

In this paper we consider projective complex varieties of any dimension and define the analogue of generalized evolutes of real curves. The evolutes are defined as “iterated cuspidal loci” of the envelope of the family of normal spaces to the given variety. These loci are Thom–Boardman singularities of the map from the total space of the family of normal spaces to the projective space, and hence their classes can be computed from known Thom polynomials.
In Section
2
we define envelopes of families of linear spaces and determine the cycle class of their singular (cuspidal) loci. In Section
3
we introduce a notion of perpendicularity which allows us to define normal spaces to a projective variety. We can therefore consider the family of normal spaces to the given variety and define evolutes. In Section
4
, we prove numerical formulas for the envelope of the normal spaces to curves in arbitrary dimensional projective space. We show that our formulas agrees with those of Salmon in the case of curves in 3-space. Section
5
computes various cycle classes associated to the evolute of a surface, and, in the case of a surface in 3-space, the degree of its evolute, the degree of the cuspidal curve of the evolute, and the number of cusps of the cuspidal curve. Section
6
treats osculating developables of curves in the context of envelopes.

We fix a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
V
of dimension
n+1
.
Let
X
be a projective nonsingular variety of dimension
r
, and
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}(V)
a morphism. Assume
\mathcal{F}
is a rank

locally free sheaf on

V_{X}\to\mathcal{F}

Then

gives rise to a family of linear

is the composition of the inclusion

The

\psi
.
From a singularities-of-mappings point of view, this means that the envelope is the image by

\Sigma^{1}:=\Sigma^{1}(\psi)=\{y\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{F})\,|\,\rk d\psi_{y}\leq n-1\}.

The classes of these loci are given by Thom polynomials in the Chern classes
\overline{c}_{i}:=c_{i}(\psi^{*}T_{\mathbb{P}(V)}-T_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{F})})

Recall that the Thom polynomial for

\overline{c}_{1}^{2}+\overline{c}_{2}

[

]
, and that for

]
.
(The Thom polynomial for

\overline{c}_{1}^{4}++6\overline{c}_{1}^{2}\overline{c}_{2}+9\overline{c}_{1}\overline{c}_{3}+2\overline{c}_{2}^{2}+6\overline{c}_{4},

and those for

Rim
]

Set

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

\displaystyle\overline{c}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi^{*}c_{1}^{\prime}-c_{1}

\displaystyle\overline{c}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi^{*}c_{2}^{\prime}-c_{2}-\psi^{*}c_{1}^{\prime}c_{1}+c_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\overline{c}_{3}

\displaystyle\psi^{*}c_{3}^{\prime}-c_{3}-\psi^{*}c_{2}^{\prime}c_{1}-\psi^{*}c_{1}^{\prime}c_{2}+\psi^{*}c_{1}^{\prime}c_{1}^{2}+2c_{1}c_{2}-c_{1}^{3}.

Proof.

This is straightforward, from the fact that

is the

∎

Lemma 2.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\psi^{*}c_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n+1}{i}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{F})}(1))^{i}

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\pi^{*}c_{1}(\Omega^{1}_{X})-\pi^{*}c_{1}(\mathcal{F})+(n-r+1)c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{F})}(1))

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=0}^{3}(-1)^{3-i}\binom{n-r+1-i}{3-i}\pi^{*}c_{i}(\mathcal{F})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{F})}(1))^{3-i}

\displaystyle-\pi^{*}c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})\sum_{i=0}^{2}(-1)^{2-i}\binom{n-r+1-i}{2-i}\pi^{*}c_{i}(\mathcal{F})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{F})}(1))^{2-i}

\displaystyle-\pi^{*}c_{3}(\Omega_{X}^{1}).

Proof.

The exact sequence

0\to\Omega_{\mathbb{P}(V)}^{1}(1)\to V_{\mathbb{P}(V)}\to\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(V)}^{1}(1)\to 0

gives

c(T_{\mathbb{P}(V)})=c(V^{\vee}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(V)}(1))=\bigl(1+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(V)}(1))\bigr)^{n+1}.

Since
\psi^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(V)}(1)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{F})}(1)

the first equality follows. For the other three, consider the two exact sequences

Together with the fact

we conclude that

∎

4.
Evolutes of curves

Let
f:X\to\mathbb{P}(V)
be a morphism from a nonsingular curve
X
. Assume
f(X)
is in general position with respect to
H_{\infty}
and
Q_{\infty}
.
Let
d=f^{*}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(V)}(1))\cap[X]
denote the degree of
X
,
g
its genus, and
k_{0}
the degree of the branch locus of
f
, i.e.,
k_{0}
is the (weighted) number of cusps of
f(X)
. Let
\mathcal{P}^{1}
denote the image of the first jet map
V_{X}\to\mathcal{P}^{1}_{X}(1)
, and set

).

Proposition 4.1
.

The class of the envelope

is

[E_{\mathcal{E}}]=\psi_{*}\bigl(\pi^{*}((c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{1})+c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)))+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1))\bigr)\cap[\mathbb{P}(V)],

and its degree is

\deg E_{\mathcal{E}}=6(d+g-1)-2k_{0}.

Proof.

The proposition follows from Corollary
2.4
with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{E}
, using
c_{1}(\mathcal{E})=-c_{1}(\mathcal{K}^{1})+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))=c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{1})+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))

.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

When
n=2
, so that
X
is a plane curve, the envelope is the
evolute
of the curve. See
[
ev
]
for a thorough treatment of this case, both in the real and complex situation.

Proposition 4.3
.

The class of the “cuspidal edge”

of the envelope is

\psi_{*}\bigl(2\pi^{*}c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{1})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1))\\
+3\pi^{*}c_{1}(\Omega^{1}_{X})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1))+3c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1))^{2}\bigr)\cap[\mathbb{P}(V)],

and its degree is

\deg C_{\mathcal{E}}=3(3d+4g-4-k_{0}).

If

, the class of the cuspidal locus

of the cuspidal edge is

\psi_{*}\bigl(3\pi^{*}c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{1})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1))^{2}\\
+6\pi^{*}c_{1}(\Omega^{1}_{X})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1))^{2}+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})}(1))^{3}\bigr)\cap[\mathbb{P}(V)],

and its degree is

\deg\kappa_{\mathcal{E}}=4\bigl(3d+5g-5-k_{0}\bigr).

Proof.

This follows from Corollary
2.8
with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{E}
.
∎

Consider now the case
n=3
. Then the cuspidal edge of the envelope is the evolute of
X
.
We note that the formulas for the degrees of the envelope and of the evolute are in accordance with Salmon’s formulas
[
Salmon
]
*Footnote, p. 341. He states that the envelope
E_{\mathcal{E}}
has degree
3m+n
, where
m
is the degree of the curve
X
and
n
is the degree of its strict dual
X^{*}
. In the notation of
[
numchar
]
, we have

and

, hence

3m+n=3d+3(d+2g-2)-2k_{0}-k_{1}=6(d+g-1)-2k_{0}-k_{1}
. Salmon assumes that
X
has no inflection points, i.e., that

, so in this case his formula agrees with the one of Proposition
4.1
. In a footnote he says that if
X
has
\theta
inflection points, then

should be replaced by

, so his formula would read
3m+n+\theta=3d+3(d+2g-2)-2k_{0}-k_{1}+k_{1}=6(d+g-1)-2k_{0}
, in agreement with ours.

To find the degree of the envelope
E_{\mathcal{E}}
, Salmon
[
Salmon
]
*Footnote, p. 341 considers the intersection of
E_{\mathcal{E}}
with the plane
H_{\infty}
at infinity. He observes that at each of the
m

X\cap H_{\infty}
, the polar line at that point is contained in
H_{\infty}
, with multiplicity
3
. He says that the remaining part of
E_{\mathcal{E}}\cap H_{\infty}
is a curve of degree
n
. We can understand this as follows: Let
L\subset H_{\infty}
be a (general) line, and
L^{\perp}\in H_{\infty}
its polar point (with respect to the conic
Q_{\infty}
). There are
n
osculating planes to
C
that pass through the point
L^{\perp}
. Let
O_{P}
,
P\in X
, be such a plane, and let
T_{P}\subset O_{P}
denote the tangent line to
X
at
P
. Then
T_{P}\cap H_{\infty}\subset O_{P}\cap H_{\infty}
, hence
(O_{P}\cap H_{\infty})^{\perp}\subset(T_{P}\cap H_{\infty})^{\perp}
and
(O_{P}\cap H_{\infty})^{\perp}\subset L
. It remains to see that the line joining
P
and
(O_{P}\cap H_{\infty})^{\perp}
is the polar line to
C
at
P
. Now the normal plane
N_{P}
to
C
at
P
is the plane spanned by
P
and
(T_{P}\cap H_{\infty})^{\perp}
. The polar line is the intersection of
N_{P}
with the normal plane
N_{P^{\prime}}
at an infinitesimally near point
P^{\prime}
to
P
. It therefore suffices to see that the intersection of
(T_{P}\cap H_{\infty})^{\perp}
with
(T_{P^{\prime}}\cap H_{\infty})^{\perp}
for
P^{\prime}
infinitely near
P
is equal to
(O_{P}\cap H_{\infty})^{\perp}
. But this follows from the fact that the span of
T_{P}
and
T_{P^{\prime}}
is equal to
O_{P}
.
Finally, one can check, by using a local parameterization of
X
, that the polar line at an inflection point lies in the plane
H_{\infty}
. So the intersection of
E_{\mathcal{E}}
with the plane at infinity consists of
m
lines with multiplicity
3
,
\theta
lines, and a curve of degree
n

Salmon’s formula for the degree of the evolute
C_{\mathcal{E}}
is
5m+\alpha
, where
\alpha
denotes the number
k_{2}=4(d+3(g-1))-3k_{0}-2k_{1}
of hyperosculating planes to
X
, i.e.,
5m+\alpha=5d+4(d+3(g-1))-3k_{0}-2k_{1}=3(3d+4g-4-k_{0})-2k_{1}
. This agrees with our formula in Proposition
4.3
when

. But the number of hyperosculating points should be weighted, so that

. This gives
5m+\alpha=5d+4(d+3(g-1))-3k_{0}-2k_{1}+2k_{1}=3(3d+4g-4-k_{0})
, again in agreement with the formula in Proposition
4.3
.

To find the degree of the evolute
C_{\mathcal{E}}
, Salmon
[
Salmon
]
*Footnote, p. 341 asserts that the degree of the curve of osculating planes to the evolute is
m+r
, where

is the degree of
X
and

is the rank of
X
, i.e., the degree of its tangent developable. The reason for this is that the osculating planes to the evolute are the normal planes to

.

Salmon does not give a formula for

. However, since

is the tangent developable of the curve

, we have the formula
[
numchar
]
*Thm. (3.2), p. 481

\deg E_{\mathcal{E}}=2\deg C_{\mathcal{E}}+2g-2-\deg\kappa_{\mathcal{E}},

since
C_{\mathcal{E}}
and
X
have the same genus

.
This gives

\deg\kappa_{\mathcal{E}}=2\cdot 3(3d+4g-4-k_{0})+2g-2-6(d+g-1)+2k_{0}=4(3d+5g-5-k_{0}),

which checks with Proposition
4.3
.

Example 4.4
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
be a twisted cubic.
The envelope has degree
\deg E_{\mathcal{E}}=12
. Its evolute
C_{\mathcal{E}}
has degree 15 and has 16 cusps.

Remark 4.5
.

The cusps of the evolute correspond to the
vertices
of the given curve. For a curve in
n
-space, their number can be computed as the degree of
\Sigma^{1,\dots,1}
, where the number of 1’s is

.

5.
Evolutes of surfaces

Assume
\mathbb{P}(V)
has a Euclidean structure as described in the beginning of Section
4
. Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}(V)
be a nonsingular surface which is in general position with respect to
H_{\infty}
and
Q_{\infty}

[
reci
]
*Prop. 4.1, p. 245. Then there is a natural surjective map
V_{X}\to\mathcal{E}
, where
\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{K}^{1})^{\vee}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
is the Euclidean normal bundle of
X
. Note that
\mathcal{K}^{1}=\mathcal{N}(1)
is the twisted conormal bundle of
X
in
\mathbb{P}(V)
.
Then
\psi:\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\to\mathbb{P}(V)
is the family of normal
(n-2)
-spaces to
X
.

Let again
E_{\mathcal{E}}
denote the envelope of the normal spaces of
X
,
C_{\mathcal{E}}
its cuspidal locus, and
\kappa_{\mathcal{E}}
the cuspidal locus of

.

Proposition 5.1
.

We have

\deg E_{\mathcal{E}}=\bigl(2c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})^{2}+2c_{2}(\Omega^{1}_{X})+18c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))+30c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{2}\bigr)\cap[X]\\
\deg C_{\mathcal{E}}=\bigl(17c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})^{2}+5c_{2}(\Omega^{1}_{X})+102c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))+138c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{2}\bigr)\cap[X]\\
\ \deg\kappa_{\mathcal{E}}=2\bigl(55c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})^{2}+5c_{2}(\Omega^{1}_{X})+266c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))+310c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{2}\bigr)\cap[X]

Proof.

We apply Corollaries
2.5
and
2.9
with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{E}
. We have

c_{1}(\mathcal{E})=-c_{1}(\mathcal{K}^{1})+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))=c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{1}_{X}(1))+c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))=c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})+4c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))

and

c_{2}(\mathcal{E})=c_{2}(\mathcal{K}^{1})-c_{1}(\mathcal{K}^{1})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\\
=c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})^{2}-c_{2}(\Omega_{X}^{1})+5c_{1}(\Omega_{X}^{1})c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))+9c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{2}.

∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Assume
n=3
and let
d
denote the degree of
X
. Then we have

\displaystyle\deg E_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2d(d-1)(2d-1)

\displaystyle\deg C_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2d(d-1)(11d-16)

\displaystyle\deg\kappa_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4d(30d^{2}-97d+78).

Proof.

We apply Proposition
5.1
with

c_{1}(\Omega^{1}_{X})=(d-4)c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
,
c_{2}(\Omega^{1}_{X})=(d^{2}-4d+6)c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{2}
, and
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{2}\cap[X]=d
.
∎

Salmon calls the evolute
E_{\mathcal{E}}
of a surface in 3-dimensional space for the “surface of centres,” or “centro-surface”
[
Salmon2
]
*Art. 507, p. 148, since it is the locus of spherical curvature centers, the
focal
points.
Since there are two principal “curvature directions” at each point of the surface, there are two focal points on each normal line to the surface. Moreover, each normal line is tangent to the evolute at each of the two focal points, hence are bitangents to the evolute.
Considered as a surface in
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})
, the evolute is a
2\colon 1
cover of
X
. For a detailed local study of the evolute in the case of a real surface, see
[
P1
]
*.

For
d=2
, our formula gives
\deg E_{\mathcal{E}}=12
, in accordance with Salmon
[
Salmon
]
*Art. 206, p. 179, who actually computes its equation. Moreover, in the fifth edition of Salmon’s book
[
Salmon2
]
*Art. 511, p. 151, he gives the formula for
\deg E_{\mathcal{E}}
above and also the formula
2d(d^{2}-d-1)
for the degree of the dual surface – the class of
E_{\mathcal{E}}

[
Salmon2
]
*Art. 509, p. 150.
For
d=2
, this gives 4, as is also stated in
[
Salmon
]
*Art. 199, p. 171. In the general case, to find the class of the evolute, Salmon starts by determining the number of the bitangents of the evolute, which are the same as the normals to the surface
X
, that pass through a given point
[
Salmon2
]
*Art. 507, p. 149. This number is the socalled Euclidean distance degree of
X

[
Draisma
]
*, and is equal to
d+d(d-1)+d(d-1)^{2}=d(d^{2}-d+1)
. He shows this by taking the point to be a point in the plane at infiinity.
From this, and a consideration of the points on a surface, the normals at which meet a given line, he deduces the formula for the class of
E_{\mathcal{E}}
. For the degree of
E_{\mathcal{E}}
, he gives two alternative approaches. One, attributed to Darboux, uses the theory of congruences of lines, the lines being the bitangents of the evolute, which are also the normals of the surface
X
. The other consists in studying the intersection of
E_{\mathcal{E}}
with the plane at infinity.

Remark 5.3
.

There will be a finite number of points on the surface where the two focal points on the normal line coincide.
These points are the
umbilic points
(see
[
Salmon
]
*Art. 106, p. 85 and
[

]
*p. 159).
Salmon showed
[

]
*footnote on p. 263 (cited in
[

disregarding points at

, is equal to

for the map
\psi:\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})\to\mathbb{P}(V)

*p. 160.
Hence their number should be computable as the value of the corresponding Thom polynomial
\widetilde{Q}_{21}=\tilde{c}_{1}\tilde{c}_{2}-2\tilde{c}_{3}

6.
Osculating developables of curves

Assume
X
is a curve.
Denote by
\mathcal{P}^{m}_{X}(1)
the sheaf of principal parts of order
m
of
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1):=f^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(V)}(1)
, and let
a^{m}\colon V_{X}\to\mathcal{P}^{m}_{X}(1)
denote the
m
th jet map. Let
\mathcal{P}^{m}:=\Image a^{m}
denote the
m
th osculating bundle
[
numchar
]
*Prop. (2.1), p. 478, and set
\mathcal{K}^{m}:=\Ker a^{m}
, so that we have exact sequences

0\to\mathcal{K}^{m}\to V_{X}\to\mathcal{P}^{m}\to 0.

The
m
th osculating developable

D^{m}_{X}
of
X
is the image of the composed map

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{P}^{m})\subset X\times\mathbb{P}(V)\to\mathbb{P}(V).

The degree of
D_{X}^{m}
is

\deg D^{m}_{X}=c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{P}^{m})}(1))^{m+1}\cap[\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{P}^{m})]=\pi_{*}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{P}^{m}})(1))^{m+1}\cap[X],

which is equal to

s_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{m})\cap[X]=c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{m})\cap[X].

Note that

c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{m}_{X}(1))=\binom{m+1}{2}c_{1}(\Omega^{1}_{X})+(m+1)c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)).

This gives
[
numchar
]
*Thm. (3.2), p. 481

\deg D^{m}_{X}=\binom{m+1}{2}(2g-2)+(m+1)d-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(m-i)k_{i},

where
g
is the genus of
C
and
d
its degree, and
k_{i}
its
i
th stationary index (see
[
numchar
]
).

It is well know that in the case
n=2
, so that
X
is a plane curve, the envelope of the tangents to
X
is equal to
X
union its inflectional tangents. When
n=3
, the envelope of the osculating planes to
X
is equal to the tangent developable of
X
union its hyperosculating planes. More generally, we have the following result.

Proposition 6.1
.

The envelope
E_{\mathcal{P}^{n-1}}
of the family of osculating hyperplanes

\psi\colon\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{P}^{n-1})\to\mathbb{P}(V)

to
X
is equal to its
(n-2)
th osculating developable
D^{n-2}_{X}
union its hyperosculating hyperplanes.
We have

\deg E_{\mathcal{P}^{n-1}}=2\bigl(nd+(n^{2}-n+1)(g-1)-\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(n-1-i)k_{i}\bigr).

Proof.

Let
r(t)
be a local parameterization of
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}(V)
. The map
\psi
is given locally by

(t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{n-1})\mapsto r(t)+u_{1}r^{\prime}(t)+\cdots+u_{n-1}r^{(n-1)}(t).

Its differential is given by

d\psi=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}r^{\prime}(t)+u_{1}r^{\prime\prime}(t)+\cdots+u_{n-1}r^{(n)}(t)\\
r^{\prime}(t)\\
\vdots\\
r^{(n-1)}(t)\end{array}\right)\cong\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{n-1}r^{(n)}(t)\\
r^{\prime}(t)\\
\vdots\\
r^{(n-1)}(t)\end{array}\right).

Hence the rank of
d\psi
is
<n
when
u_{n-1}=0
, i.e., when the point lies in the
(n-2)
th osculating space to
X
at
r(t)
, or when
r^{(n)}(t)=0
, i.e., when the point
r(t)
is a point of hyperosculation.
∎

It follows from Proposition
2.4
that

\displaystyle\deg E_{\mathcal{P}^{n-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2g-2+2c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{n-1})\cap[X]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2g-2+2n\bigl(d+(n-1)(g-1)-\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(n-1-i)k_{i}\bigr),

and we note that the numerical formulas

\displaystyle\deg D_{X}^{n-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n-1)(d+(n-2)(g-1))-\sum_{i=0}^{n-3}(n-2-i)k_{i}

\displaystyle k_{n-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)(d+n(g-1))-\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(n-i)k_{i}

verify
\deg E_{\mathcal{P}^{n-1}}=\deg D_{X}^{n-2}+k_{n-1}
.

Example 6.2
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a
rational normal curve
. Then
f^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(n)
and the family of osculating hyperplanes to
f(\mathbb{P}^{1})
is given by

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(n))=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(1)^{n})\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}.

Since a rational normal curve has no points of hyperosculation, the envelope of its family of osculating hyperplanes is equal to its
(n-2)
th osculating developable
D_{f(\mathbb{P}^{1})}^{n-2}
, which has degree
2(n-1)
.

Recall that the
dual variety

X^{\vee}\subset\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee})
of
X
is defined as the image of the composed map

\mathbb{P}((\mathcal{K}^{1})^{\vee})\subset X\times\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee})\to\mathbb{P}(V^{\vee}).

The degree of
