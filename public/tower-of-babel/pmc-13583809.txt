Maternal and perinatal risk factors for necrotizing enterocolitis
Authors: Jonathan Tokonjay Davies, Yumiko Gely, Venkata Yeramilli, Colin Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNecrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) remains among the most devastating complications of prematurity, carrying significant mortality and lifelong morbidity despite decades of mechanistic and translational research. Although NEC is classically framed as an inflammatory disease of intestinal immaturity, driven by microbial dysbiosis and exaggerated immune signaling, this model does not fully explain the uneven distribution of disease burden across infants, hospitals and communities. In this review, we reframe NEC within a broader maternal–perinatal systems context grounded in the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) framework.This review synthesizes evidence linking maternal psychosocial stress to activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis. This alters placental signaling and disrupts fetal gut development and microbiome establishment. This is further compounded by paternal stress-related epigenetic mechanisms, suboptimal maternal nutrition, poor metabolic health and environmental pollutants that disproportionately affect socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods.These prenatal influences intersect with postnatal modifiers within neonatal intensive care units and health systems. Social and structural determinants of health further affect prenatal exposures and the delivery of neonatal care. This framework does not replace traditional pathogenesis models of NEC but rather contextualizes them within broader developmental and health systems. This perspective may help identify earlier targets for prevention while supporting more equitable and multidisciplinary approaches to reducing the burden of NEC.

Introduction

Despite decades of mechanistic and translational research, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) continues to carry substantial mortality and lifelong morbidity, particularly among very low birth weight and premature infants.1 2 Contemporary cohort analyses demonstrate that, although overall neonatal survival has improved, NEC-related mortality remains significant.1 3 4 The burden of NEC does not end at discharge. Survivors face increased risks of short bowel syndrome, growth impairment, neurodevelopmental delay and recurrent hospitalizations.5 Families often endure prolonged psychosocial and economic strain. As such, NEC should be understood through a more comprehensive framework, not solely as an acute inflammatory condition but as a disease with enduring, lifelong consequences. Studying NEC exclusively as an inflammatory disorder risks overlooking other factors that may contribute to disease incidence and severity. Historically, investigations into pathogenesis have focused on prematurity, microbial dysbiosis and exaggerated inflammatory signaling.2 While this biologic context remains foundational, it fails to explain why risk clusters within certain populations. Thus, a wider lens is needed.

NEC is typically conceptualized as a neonatal disease; however, the biological susceptibility that culminates in NEC likely begins much earlier. Emerging research, including stress–dysbiosis–inflammation syntheses in NEC, has highlighted maternal stress physiology as a potential upstream contributor to neonatal intestinal susceptibility.6–8 Placental function and fetal development are shaped by maternal psychosocial stress, inflammatory signaling, nutrition and environmental exposures.8–11 These plausibly condition neonatal immune tone, barrier integrity and microbial seeding.12 Whether this susceptibility progresses to clinical NEC may depend on the surrounding health system infrastructure. Structural inequities further influence both prenatal exposures and hospital resources.4 13 14 These observations reinforce the need to examine NEC within broader biological, psychosocial and institutional domains. A maternal–perinatal systems framework does not replace biology; it contextualizes it.

Conceptual framework

The DOHaD framework proposes that early-life exposures shape organ development and disease susceptibility throughout life.9–11 In the context of NEC, this conceptual framing suggests that prenatal programming influences the neonatal immune set point and intestinal barrier maturation, predisposing to an exaggerated postnatal inflammatory response that culminates in NEC. This may help explain why maternal stress correlates with adverse neonatal outcomes, why some preterm infants tolerate feeds without complication, and why breast milk may mitigate this predisposition. DOHaD reframes NEC as the culmination of layered vulnerability across three windows of susceptibility: (1) periconception epigenetic priming, (2) prenatal development and immune programming and (3) perinatal environmental activation. These multilevel influences can be conceptualized within a broader maternal–perinatal systems framework (figure 1).

Integrating maternal variables into NEC models expands the set of measurable predictors beyond gestational age and birth weight to include indices of psychosocial stress, neighborhood deprivation and biologic intermediates such as cortisol and inflammatory markers.15 16 Within the periconception epigenetic priming window, maternal stress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, altering cortisol dynamics and inflammatory signaling.6 These changes, along with maternal diet, metabolic health, environmental exposures and paternal stress, affect the prenatal development window, as they are mediated via the placenta. The placenta functions as an active immunologic and endocrine interface.12 Previous work has shown that prenatal stress alters infant gut microbiota composition and upregulates complement pathway activation, increasing offspring intestinal inflammatory responses.12 17 This suggests lasting immune programming that intersects with established NEC immunopathogenesis pathways involving Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated signaling and epithelial injury.2 Furthermore, prenatal stress may lower the activation threshold for inflammatory cascades triggered by postnatal dysbiosis.7 In this postnatal activation window, system-level practices within neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) may further shape how intestinal susceptibility manifests clinically.18–21 Finally, social determinants of health (SDOH) must always be accounted for across windows of susceptibility, as these have been demonstrated to affect health outcomes.22–24

Parental risk factors

Maternal risk factors

The first window of susceptibility begins prior to conception. Maternal stress, including anxiety, depression, socioeconomic adversity and discrimination-related stress, should be considered as a biologic exposure as it has been shown to alter fetal HPA axis regulation, inflammatory programming and epithelial barrier development in epidemiological and translational studies.16 25–27 Allostatic load from accumulated chronic stress can influence endocrine-immune coordination and may contribute to prematurity, growth restriction and inflammatory priming.28 These pathways, validated by area and neighborhood deprivation indices (NDI), are increasingly implicated in the susceptibility to various intestinal inflammatory diseases, most notably NEC.14 15 Infants exposed to chronic prenatal stress may enter the NICU with heightened inflammatory responsiveness and altered microbiome acquisition.17 29 30 Taken together, these findings reinforce the idea that psychosocial stress and structural inequities contribute to observed disparities by shaping the biologic basis on which NEC unfolds.

The critical question is how external stressors become biologically encoded in utero. Increasing evidence implicates the maternal HPA axis as a central mediator.6 8 Glucocorticoid signaling during gestation influences fetal immune development. Experimental data reveal that prenatal stress exacerbates intestinal permeability, inflammatory responses and susceptibility to NEC-like injury in offspring.31 These data support the periconception priming window of NEC susceptibility.

Paternal risk factors

Another factor in the periconception priming window that remains limited in the literature involves paternal risk factors. Research often focuses on maternal physiology as the primary interface with fetal development; however, initial exposures may occur even earlier in the paternal germline and within the broader household stress environment. Preclinical models have demonstrated that paternal stress can reshape sperm microRNA content and epigenetic architecture, thereby reprogramming the offspring’s HPA axis response to stress.32 33 There is also emerging evidence that sperm-derived microRNAs (miRNAs) and extracellular vesicle signaling can transmit these stress phenotypes across generations.34–36 Although paternal stress and NEC have not yet been directly linked, this evidence suggests that neonatal immune programming may be shaped not only by maternal conditions during gestation but also by paternal exposures before conception.37 If NEC is an exaggerated inflammatory response to postnatal triggers, its roots may lie in prenatal stress signals that prime the neonatal gut long before the first feed, embedded within an even broader environmental setting shaped by maternal nutrition and metabolic health.

Maternal diet and metabolic health

From periconception through prenatal development, the second window of susceptibility involves maternal health and exposures, particularly placental and neonatal effects. Maternal diet and metabolic health during periconception, pregnancy and lactation play a central role in fetal growth, placental function, gut development and the establishment of the neonatal microbiome, all of which contribute to the pathogenesis in the development of NEC.

Maternal diet

In the prenatal development window, maternal nutritional choices may predispose neonates to NEC. Suboptimal maternal nutrition has been associated with preterm birth and low birth weight (LBW), both of which are established risk factors for NEC. Poor maternal nutrition is associated with low socioeconomic status (SES).38 39 Lower SES is frequently linked to reduced dietary diversity and inadequate macronutrient intake—such as proteins—both of which contribute to LBW and preterm birth. Among micronutrients, zinc deficiency has been associated with LBW, preterm delivery and NEC.40 Prematurity disrupts the normal establishment of the neonatal gut microbiome, which has been associated with the development of NEC.41

Metabolic health

Obesity from high-fat diets, gestational diabetes and overall metabolic syndrome have all been shown to increase the risk of NEC. Elevated prepregnancy maternal body mass index is associated with higher odds of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes.42 Maternal gestational diabetes has been associated with a threefold increased risk of NEC.43 44 Poor diet, observed in mothers with obesity and significant gestational weight gain, affects the maternal microbiome. This maternal microbiome, which forms the first infant gut microbial inoculum, can adversely affect the fetal microbiome. This has been shown to increase the risk of NEC by influencing microbiome colonization.45–47 These diet-induced microbial alterations may predispose premature infants to aberrant colonization patterns that promote NEC pathogenesis.

Microbiome

Through the prenatal to postnatal window, the microbiome plays a pivotal role in intestinal development. Nutrients and microbiota play a role in stimulating the microbiome and, in turn, influencing the early development of the immune response.48 Colonization of the neonatal gut is important for the establishment and maintenance of the mucosal barrier, protecting neonates from enteric pathogens and inflammation.46 48 Gut dysbiosis increases the risk of NEC by impairing neonatal intestinal barrier function and immune regulation.47 Maternal diet and health not only affect the microbiome, but the breast milk produced has an even greater effect on the microbiome and the neonate’s susceptibility to NEC.

Breast milk

Maternal diet may protect against NEC by shaping breast milk composition, a source of gut colonization and intestinal development for the infant.48 Maternal nutrition affects breast milk fatty acids, particularly omega-3; microbiota, which contribute to infant gut colonization; and secretory IgA and antimicrobial factors, which help regulate intestinal inflammation.47 Breast milk modulates intestinal inflammation, epithelial maturation and growth and the neonatal microbiome.48–51 Human milk oligosaccharides (HMO) are prebiotics that stimulate growth of commensal bacteria in the gut, which is protective against NEC.52 Immaturity of HMO production has been shown to increase premature infants’ susceptibility to NEC.53 Lactoferrin in colostrum and breast milk has also been shown to promote intestinal growth and differentiation, respectively.48 Overall, prenatal and postnatal maternal and neonatal diet and metabolic health are highly influential on neonatal intestinal development, which could be protective or predispose neonates to NEC.

Environmental exposures

Maternal exposure to environmental pollutants during pregnancy has emerged as a notable risk factor for adverse neonatal outcomes, specifically NEC. Strong geographic and socioeconomic ties, unfortunately, exist.

Air pollution has been identified as the strongest factor associated with an increased risk of developing NEC.54 Specifically, exposure to particulate matter (PM10) in the first trimester, nitric dioxide (NO₂) in the second trimester, and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) in any trimester.54 Exposure to PM2.5 has been associated with preterm birth and LBW via mechanisms of oxidative stress, inflammatory responses and endothelial and metabolic dysfunction.55 Air pollutants induce oxidative stress and inflammation in the placenta, impairing placental function, as particulate pollutants can cross the placental barrier, triggering pro-inflammatory cytokine responses and DNA damage.56 57 This risk is amplified in pregnancies complicated by preeclampsia due to compounded oxidative stress.58 Breast milk is an important source of antioxidants, further highlighting the protective factor of breast milk.59

Not only does air pollution cause oxidative stress, but it also epigenetically alters the placenta, adversely affecting cellular processes such as DNA repair, circadian rhythm, energy metabolism and aging.60 Air pollution and exposure to heavy metals during pregnancy can cause epigenetic reprogramming. This alters placental gene expression and fetal development, increasing the risk of NEC.61 These epigenetic modifications from these exposures cause global and locally specific changes in placental DNA methylation patterns, histone modifications and non-coding RNA regulation, which affect gene expression for fetal development.61 62 Specific miRNAs are altered with air pollution exposure (miR-20a, miR-21, miR-146a, miR-222).60

These environmental exposures differ by geographic location. Lower-SES neighborhoods frequently experience higher levels of air pollution and environmental toxins, which are associated with an increased risk of NEC.15 63 The Southeast USA has a higher NEC mortality rate, likely due to regional differences in air quality and other factors, including access to prenatal care, variability in NICU quality and antibiotic stewardship.4 Early environmental exposures in specific geographic regions may establish pathogenic colonization patterns that give rise to NEC.4 64

Translation and clinical research

Experimental models demonstrate that prenatal stress enhances neonatal inflammatory priming, increases intestinal permeability and perturbs early microbial colonization, mechanisms that directly overlap with NEC from exaggerated TLR4 signaling, epithelial injury and microbial dysbiosis.2 12 16 27 31 Accordingly, translational strategies aimed at restoring microbial balance and dampening maternal stress physiology may offer dual protection for both brain and gut development.

Biomarkers for NEC

Serum biomarkers have been a target for use of early detection of NEC. S100 A8/A9 protein, apolipoprotein-CII, C-reactive protein, interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-1 (IL-1) receptor antagonist, intestinal fatty acid-binding protein, platelet-activating factor and ischemia-modified albumin have demonstrated varying sensitivity and specificity in certain populations but have lacked generalizability and applicability across populations.65–69 These biomarkers could be used to diagnose NEC early, particularly in vulnerable neonates, thereby enabling prompt treatment and interventions. The use of biomarkers could serve as a bridge between identifying susceptible neonates and the postnatal manifestation of disease. However, it remains a key area for future endeavors.

Microbiome-targeted interventions

Microbiome-targeted interventions have shown significant promise. In preclinical rodent models, maternal supplementation with Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species normalizes stress-induced dysbiosis, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, strengthens epithelial tight junctions and attenuates anxiety-like behaviors in offspring.27 70 71 Clinically, probiotic administration to preterm infants significantly reduces NEC incidence and mortality, as evidenced by multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses.72 73

Nutritional strategies

Nutritional and behavioral strategies further broaden the therapeutic landscape. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate, enhance gut barrier integrity and modulate neuroinflammation,74–76 while HMOs promote beneficial microbial colonization and reduce the risk of NEC.77 Furthermore, preclinical in vitro studies demonstrate that human breast milk-derived exosomes protect intestinal epithelial cells from oxidative stress, presenting an attractive treatment concept for infants with intestinal injury.78 Additionally, prenatal choline supplementation has been shown to support neurodevelopmental resilience and stress regulation.79 Several recommendations have been made for targeted education on behavior change and multimicronutrient supplementation, especially in low-SES areas, as these can improve both maternal and fetal health outcomes.38 39 80 81 Of note, maternal omega-3 fatty acid intake during pregnancy and lactation is protective against NEC by exerting anti-inflammatory effects.82

Behavioral or stress-reduction interventions

Beyond nutrition, behavioral interventions such as mindfulness-based stress reduction and structured prenatal exercise programs reduce perceived stress and normalize maternal cortisol dynamics, thereby modulating HPA-axis activity during pregnancy.83–85 Together, these findings suggest a unified gut–brain axis framework in which microbiota-directed therapies, nutritional optimization and stress-reduction strategies work synergistically to mitigate the effects of prenatal stress and reduce susceptibility to both neurodevelopmental impairment and intestinal inflammatory diseases in newborns.

Health systems and structural contributors

Regionalization of care can affect access to breast milk and surgical care

Regionalization of perinatal care may influence NEC risk at several points along the disease course and across all windows of susceptibility. Delivery at or appropriate early transfer to tertiary centers affects exposure to evidence-based preventive practices and early escalation when intestinal compromise is suspected.18 86 Conversely, transfer delays or limited access to pediatric surgery teams can worsen intestinal injury. Systematic review of the literature remains limited and difficult to interpret because the timing of operation is confounded by surgical indication and baseline disease severity.87

NICU variation and practices

Hospital and NICU characteristics influence the incidence and outcomes of NEC. Higher volume centers are more likely to standardize feeding protocols, prioritize maternal and donor breastmilk, implement antibiotic stewardship practices, regionalize perinatal care and maintain timely access to pediatric surgical care.19–21 86 Institutional practice variation also likely contributes to the uneven burden of NEC across hospitals and regions. Units differ in staffing, access to donor milk banks, lactation resources, feeding regimens, NICU quality improvement (QI) processes, and escalation thresholds.19 21 88 These infrastructure differences may shape exposures encountered by vulnerable neonates and can significantly influence NEC incidence. These findings suggest that regionalization, health systems capability, and clinical consistency shape whether vulnerable mothers and infants receive preventive care, timely escalation, and definitive care. When systems-level accountability fails, structural biases and provider-level variations can further widen existing gaps in care, worsening outcomes for the most vulnerable.

Provider bias

Increasing evidence suggests that provider behavior, institutional infrastructure, and SDOH interact with biologic susceptibility in ways that may increase disease risk. Provider variation may influence perinatal counseling, feeding recommendations, and escalation of care. Variability in counseling regarding antenatal steroids, delivery location, maternal milk prioritization, and early transfer to tertiary centers may affect downstream NEC risk.16 63 Differences in perceived parental reliability, social support, or anticipated adherence may shape the urgency of lactation consultation, access to donor milk, or thresholds for early surgical consultation.89 These differences are hard to capture in administrative datasets but may alter early microbial colonization, feeding trajectories, and inflammation.

Structural inequities

Persistent racial and socioeconomic disparities in NEC incidence and mortality have been documented across multiple cohorts. Black infants in the United States experience higher rates of NEC and NEC-related mortality compared with White infants, even after adjustment for gestational age and birth weight. Similar gradients are observed across income and insurance strata.13 14 63 These disparities likely reflect a combination of factors, including psychosocial stress, environmental toxicants, structural barriers to delivery at high-volume tertiary centers, and differences in NICU resources. Understanding NEC disparities, therefore, requires an integrated framework that links stress biology, environmental exposure, institutional practice patterns, and health equity.

Future directions and research gaps

Clinical and research implications of the maternal–perinatal systems framework

Utilizing the maternal-perinatal-systems framework opens immense opportunities for clinical and research contributions. In the periconception window, further research elucidating the effects of paternal stress, both on its own and in combination with maternal exposures, is warranted.

During the prenatal development window, particularly in clinical settings, incorporating the overarching framework can inform risk stratification across the prenatal and postnatal periods. Using prenatal or perinatal biomarkers may help identify vulnerable or susceptible patients. Standardization of NICU protocols and implicit bias training can mitigate clinical delays and streamline care, particularly for the most vulnerable patients.

Furthermore, targeted interventions can be implemented at the individual and system levels, incorporating changes in the postnatal window. Individual interventions, such as targeted nutrition programs and stress-reduction behavioral modifications, can be implemented. Systems-level changes in NICU practices, environmental policies, and mitigation strategies addressing SDOH and early prevention protocols are likely more difficult to implement but equally as important.

Future endeavors

Throughout decades of investigation into the inflammatory and microbial drivers of NEC, limited work has systematically integrated maternal stress biology, structural inequities, and systems-level care delivery. The framework provides an organizational map for clinical and research interventions. For an inclusive, comprehensive understanding of NEC, future research should prioritize the creation of multilevel, longitudinal datasets that integrate maternal psychosocial stress measures and biologic markers, environmental exposure data (air pollution, endocrine disruptors, neighborhood-level deprivation indices), detailed feeding trajectories and donor milk utilization, and NICU process measures (staffing ratios, lactation access, transfer timing).

Conclusion

NEC is traditionally framed as an inflammatory disease of prematurity with clinical manifestations driven by intestinal immaturity and microbial dysbiosis; however, the neonatal intestine does not arrive unconditioned at birth. Maternal psychosocial stress, nutritional and metabolic health, environmental exposures and placental signaling all shape fetal immune programming, gut barrier maturation and early microbiome seeding. These prenatal influences intersect with paternal epigenetic contributions and family stress environments to prime the neonatal gut before birth.

Whether biologic susceptibility ultimately manifests as NEC depends, in part, on the broader systems surrounding neonatal care. Systems-level modifiers—feeding regimens, breast milk access, regionalization, and surgical availability—represent actionable levers that can either amplify or mitigate risk. Structural inequities further impact both prenatal exposures and postnatal resources, contributing to persistent disparities observed in NEC incidence and outcomes. As such, NEC should be reframed as the culmination of layered developmental and structural influences within a broader maternal–perinatal system context. Progress will require interdisciplinary collaboration integrating obstetrics, neonatology, surgery and public health, recognizing that the most durable strategy for preventing NEC may not be a single therapeutic agent, but a more reliable and equitable system of care.
