Pulmonary mucormycosis complicated by necrotizing pneumonia secondary to an obstructive endobronchial tumor-like lesion: a case report and review of literature
Authors: Ngoc Duong-Minh, Khoa Nguyen-Dang
Published: 2025

Background

Mucormycosis is an uncommon but life-threatening invasive fungal infection caused by fungi of the order Mucorales [1]. Among its clinical forms, pulmonary mucormycosis (PM) ranks as the second most common presentation after rhino-orbital-cerebral involvement [1]. The disease primarily affects immunocompromised individuals, particularly those with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, hematological malignancies, or a history of organ transplantation [2]. Diagnosis remains challenging due to its nonspecific clinical features and radiological overlap with other pulmonary infections or malignancies, often leading to delayed recognition and poor outcomes [1, 2]. Necrotizing pneumonia (NP) is a severe complication of PM, usually resulting from extensive angioinvasion and subsequent pulmonary tissue infarction [3]. However, NP occurring secondarily to post-obstructive pneumonia caused by an endobronchial tumor-like lesion due to mucormycosis is rare and has been only sporadically reported in the literature [4–8].

We present the first case in Vietnam of PM complicated by NP secondary to an obstructive endobronchial tumor-like lesion. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of PM and underscores the importance of considering fungal etiologies in patients presenting with tumor-like airway obstruction.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 71-year-old female farmer was admitted to the hospital with progressive dyspnea. One month before admission, she had experienced anorexia, weight loss, and a persistent dry cough. Ten days before admission, she developed high-grade fever (up to 39 °C) with chills, productive cough with whitish sputum occasionally streaked with blood, and exertional dyspnea. She denied chest pain. Her past medical history was unremarkable, with no known chronic illnesses.

Clinical findings

The patient was initially presented to Cho Ray Hospital and was admitted to the Department of Pulmonary Medicine. On examination, she was alert and oriented. Vital signs revealed a blood pressure of 130/80 mmHg, heart rate of 98 beats per minute, temperature of 38 °C, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. She was breathing comfortably without using accessory respiratory muscles. Physical examination showed an ill-appearing woman with signs of infection, including dry lips and a coated tongue. Pulmonary examination demonstrated signs of consolidation over the lower third of the right lung field. No other abnormalities were detected on systemic examination.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial laboratory results are summarized in Table 1, revealing evidence of infection with elevated WBC count and serum CRP, along with poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1C 14.3%). Imaging at admission demonstrated necrotizing pneumonia in the right lower lobe (Fig. 1). The patient was started on intravenous antibiotics, including meropenem (3 g/day), levofloxacin (750 mg/day), and vancomycin (2 g/day), in addition to glycemic control. However, during the first 10 days of hospitalization, the clinical condition failed to improve, with persistent fever, purulent sputum, worsening dyspnea, and no decline in WBC count. Both sputum and blood cultures were negative for bacteria and fungi, while sputum AFB smear and Xpert assays also yielded negative results. Flexible bronchoscopy was therefore performed on hospital day 6. Bronchoscopy revealed purulent secretions and an endobronchial mass covered by whitish pseudomembranes, nearly obstructing the right lower lobe bronchus (Fig. 2). Biopsy of the lesion was performed by standard forceps; however, after obtaining the first two samples, profuse bleeding occurred, which was controlled with local instillation of adrenaline. Microbiological analysis of the BAL was negative for bacteria and fungi, and tuberculosis was excluded (AFB smear and Xpert both negative). Liquid-based cytology did not reveal malignant cells. No additional abnormalities were detected on brain CT, abdominal ultrasound, or cardiac Doppler ultrasound.

Therapeutic intervention

On hospital day 11, histopathological examination revealed findings consistent with a Mucor infection (Fig. 2E, F). Antibiotics were discontinued, and the patient was initiated on amphotericin B deoxycholate for the subsequent 26 days.

Follow-up and outcomes

Clinical status gradually improved, with resolution of fever and cough, normalization of WBC count, and a decline in CRP levels (Table 1). A chest radiograph before discharge (Fig. 3A) demonstrated marked improvement. Follow-up bronchoscopy was performed to exclude malignancy; the previously observed pseudomembrane had disappeared (Fig. 2G, H), and repeat biopsy confirmed the absence of both Mucor and malignant cells. The patient was maintained on oral posaconazole after discharge. A CT scan at three months showed substantial regression of necrotizing consolidation (Fig. 3B, C). Posaconazole was continued, and by six months, the treatment course was completed, resulting in complete radiological resolution.

Discussion

Our case adds to the literature by describing PM presenting with a rare manifestation of a mass-like endobronchial lesion causing near-complete obstruction of a segmental bronchus. This report highlights two key clinical considerations. First, PM should be included in the differential diagnosis of newly diagnosed or poorly controlled diabetic patients presenting with NP and tumor-like endobronchial lesions, in which histopathological confirmation is essential. Second, as the optimal duration of antifungal therapy for PM remains unclear, radiological monitoring until complete resolution or stabilization of lesions is crucial to prevent recurrence. Treatment may need to be prolonged for up to six months, as demonstrated in our case.

Mucormycosis is an uncommon invasive fungal infection caused by fungi of the order Mucorales, most frequently Rhizopus, Mucor, and Lichtheimia species [1]. In a study conducted in Thailand (Asia), the proportion of patients with mucormycosis among all cases of invasive fungal infection was only 3.7% [9]. Although rare, the disease carries a fulminant clinical course and a high mortality rate if not promptly diagnosed and treated [10]. PM ranks as the second most common presentation of mucormycosis after the rhino-orbito-cerebral form, and its mortality remains unacceptably high, reaching approximately 57% according to a meta-analysis, despite advances in antifungal therapy [10]. Therefore, early recognition and timely intervention are crucial to improving clinical outcomes. Several predisposing factors for PM have been identified, including hematological malignancies, organ transplantation, and prolonged immunosuppression [11]. In recent years, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus has emerged as a significant and increasingly recognized risk factor for PM [12]. A study from India showed that among patients diagnosed with PM, up to 91% had diabetes mellitus, most of which was poorly controlled [12]. Hyperglycemia and acidosis impair alveolar macrophage function, hindering the clearance of inhaled Mucorales spores and predisposing patients to invasive pulmonary disease [1, 2, 11]. Classically, PM presents with parenchymal lung lesions such as consolidation, necrosis, or cavitation [11]. Endobronchial involvement without concomitant parenchymal abnormalities is exceedingly rare. Endobronchial mucormycosis is an unusual manifestation, with only sporadic reports available in the literature [4–8, 13]. Even more exceptional are cases managed successfully with antifungal therapy alone without surgical intervention [4]. By 2018, only 60 cases of endobronchial mucormycosis had been reported in the literature [14].

NP may represent a severe complication of PM [11]. However, NP can also result from complete airway obstruction caused by a tumor-like endobronchial mucormycosis lesion. In our case, the absence of additional parenchymal involvement elsewhere in the lungs, together with the complete obstruction of the right lower lobe bronchus by the endobronchial mass, supports the hypothesis of post-obstructive NP rather than diffuse parenchymal infection. A systematic review of 55 cases of endobronchial mucormycosis reported that the most frequent endobronchial manifestations were obstructive lesions (40%), mucosal necrosis (34.5%), hyperemic mucosa (25.5%), mass-like lesions (21.8%), and granulation tissue (21.8%) [14]. Several case reports have described endobronchial mucormycosis causing complete bronchial obstruction [5, 7, 8], and in some instances, rapidly progressive disease has led to total lung collapse and acute respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation [4]. In most cases, the lesions were covered by whitish pseudomembranes with overlying necrosis, similar to the findings observed in our patient [4, 6, 7, 13]. Endobronchial lesions of mucormycosis are sometimes accompanied by mediastinal lymphadenopathy, which frequently raises the suspicion of bronchogenic carcinoma with nodal metastasis [5, 7]. Endobronchial tuberculosis has also been initially diagnosed based on bronchoscopic gross findings, and even antituberculous therapy has been started in cases from high tuberculosis-burden countries [6]. Biopsy of endobronchial mucormycosis lesions carries a significant risk of bleeding, as the fungus is characterized by angioinvasion [3, 6, 8]. Moreover, technical challenges may arise because mucor masses are often firm, elastic, and firmly adherent to the bronchial wall, making tissue acquisition difficult [5]. Life-threatening hemorrhage may occur after biopsy of endobronchial mucormycosis lesions [8], with reports showing that 54.8% of cases died from massive hemoptysis [14]. Therefore, although not yet widely accepted, some authors have employed cryoprobe biopsy for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes to minimize the risk of bleeding [5, 7, 13].

The diagnosis of PM remains a considerable challenge in clinical practice. The diagnostic standard relies on histopathological confirmation or positive fungal cultures from lower respiratory tract specimens [11]. However, radiological findings such as chest X-ray or CT scan are neither sensitive nor specific for PM and depend on the timing of imaging. Nodular and consolidative patterns are most common, seen in about 67% and 57% of cases. Evolving imaging findings can complicate diagnosis, as about 20% of patients first show ground-glass opacities that later progress to nodules or consolidation, typically along perivascular regions and appearing one to two weeks before overt infection [15]. Even the reverse halo sign, often considered a suggestive feature, demonstrates only a sensitivity of approximately 67% and may also be encountered in other infectious and non-infectious conditions, such as aspergillosis, paracoccidioidomycosis, cryptogenic organizing pneumonia, or pulmonary thromboembolism [16]. Serum biomarkers, including galactomannan and 1,3-β-D-glucan, are not useful in the diagnosis of PM, while blood PCR assays for Mucorales have not yet been standardized and show inconsistent sensitivity [11, 17]. Culture from lower respiratory tract specimens has limited sensitivity, with positive results obtained in only about 20% of cases, meaning that a negative culture does not exclude the disease [11]. In particular, for endobronchial mucormycosis, bronchoscopy plays a pivotal role in diagnosis. Approximately three-quarters of cases have been confirmed through bronchoscopic biopsy, while BAL culture yields a positivity rate of 58.3% [14]. In contrast, sputum culture is rarely diagnostic, with positive results in only 13% of reported cases of endobronchial mucormycosis [14].

The cornerstone of PM management includes systemic antifungal therapy, control of underlying comorbidities, and surgical resection whenever feasible. Early and complete surgical intervention should always be prioritized, as it has been consistently associated with improved survival outcomes when combined with antifungal therapy [11, 18]. Liposomal amphotericin B is the first-line drug of choice, followed by oral isavuconazole or posaconazole as step-down therapy after hospital discharge [18]. Bronchoscopic interventions, such as rigid bronchoscopy with cryoprobe and excision, followed by argon plasma coagulation, have been described in isolated case reports to remove necrotic tissue heavily colonized by fungal elements, where antifungal penetration is poor [7, 13]. In a review of 59 cases of endobronchial mucormycosis, combined bronchoscopic debridement and surgery was associated with a statistically significant reduction in mortality [14]. However, these procedures lack standardized recommendations and should be reserved for specialized centers with extensive experience or for patients who are not suitable candidates for surgery. Nonetheless, cases managed with antifungal therapy alone have also been reported [4, 19]. The optimal duration of antifungal therapy remains uncertain. Current guidelines suggest continuing treatment until complete bronchoscopic clearance of necrotic tissue and radiological resolution or stabilization of lesions are achieved [11, 18]. In our case, antifungal therapy was maintained for up to six months due to extensive necrotizing parenchymal involvement. Finally, follow-up evaluation with serial bronchoscopy or chest CT every 4–6 weeks is considered reasonable to assess treatment response and ensure stability or resolution of the lesions.

In our case, the diagnostic process presented several challenges. Necrotizing pneumonia secondary to an endobronchial mass is an uncommon presentation of mucormycosis, making the diagnosis less likely to be initially considered. Imaging findings were nonspecific, sputum culture showed a low yield, serologic tests were unavailable, and PCR assays for Mucorales are not routinely accessible in many countries. Ultimately, histopathological examination remained the gold standard as it provided definitive evidence of tissue invasion. Given the angioinvasive nature of the fungus, careful preparation for potential bleeding, such as local adrenaline application, interventional radiology, rigid bronchoscopy, or even emergency pulmonary resection in specialized centers, was essential. Nevertheless, timely histopathologic confirmation was critical for initiating appropriate antifungal therapy and preventing further clinical deterioration.

Conclusion

Our case contributes to the limited literature by documenting PM with a rare mass-like endobronchial presentation causing near-complete bronchial obstruction. Clinicians should consider PM in poorly controlled diabetic patients presenting with NP and tumor-like airway lesions, in which histopathology remains essential for diagnosis. Given the absence of standardized guidelines, individualized management, ranging from systemic antifungal therapy alone to combined bronchoscopic or surgical intervention, should be tailored to each case. Prolonged antifungal therapy, potentially lasting up to six months, with close radiological monitoring, is crucial for achieving complete resolution and preventing recurrence.
