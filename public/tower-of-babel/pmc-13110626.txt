Prevalence of incidental findings requiring interventions in CT for trauma patients in the emergency department: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kyohei Nagasawa, Takashi Nihashi, Mitsunaga Iwata, Teruhiko Terasawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo systematically review and meta-analyse the prevalence of incidental findings (IFs) requiring interventions identified in CT performed for patients with traumatic injuries in the emergency department, including pathologically confirmed cancers and emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesPubMed, EMBASE and Cochrane CENTRAL databases from database inception to 22 November 2024.Eligibility criteria for selecting studiesProspective or retrospective studies involving traumatic injury patients presented at the emergency department reporting IFs of clinical significance detected through CT with any interventions proposed were included. Studies that exclusively included paediatric populations were excluded. The systematic review methods included double-screening, dual assessment of eligibility and study validity, dual data extraction, Bayesian multivariate random-effects meta-analysis of prevalence and employing the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations’ rating for the certainty of evidence. The primary outcomes were IFs requiring any interventions, and subset IFs requiring urgent interventions. Secondary outcomes were histologically confirmed cancers and emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies.Results22 studies (1 with a prospective and 21 with a retrospective design) mainly from high-income countries using limited-quality data based on clinical practice involving 18 538 patients were included. 9 studies evaluated the whole body, while 13 evaluated selective body regions. The grading criteria for IFs were non-uniform, and the image interpreters involved had diverse experience and expertise. The summary prevalence estimates for IFs requiring any interventions, urgent interventions, pathologically confirmed cancers and emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies detected in the whole body were 29.8% (95% credible interval (CrI) 20.4% to 42.9%; very low certainty), 7.6% (95% CrI 4.5% to 14.8%; low certainty), 0.6% (95% CrI 0.3% to 1.6%; moderate certainty) and 0.3% (95% CrI 0.1% to 0.9%; moderate certainty), respectively. These findings were largely identified in the chest or abdomen and pelvis, with the overall detection frequency reduced with the scanned body regions narrowed (very low to moderate certainty). Sparse data on the head, neck and spine resulted in limited results.ConclusionsIFs identified in trauma whole-body CT requiring intervention are prevalent and can lead to substantial medical costs. The widely reported prevalence range suggests variations in radiologist recommendations and reporting in clinical practice and calls for standardisations. IFs requiring urgent intervention are not rare, which leads to a diagnosis of significant diseases including cancers and urgent vascular pathologies. Future studies should report long-term, patient-relevant results based on standardised classification and reporting systems.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42020187852.Keywords: Wounds and Injuries, Diagnostic Imaging, Computed tomography, Diagnostic radiology

Introduction

Multidetector CT allows accurate identification of traumatic injuries that clinical examinations, conventional X-rays and ultrasonography cannot detect.1,3 The whole-body CT (WBCT) widely used for traumatic injuries provides a comprehensive assessment, with procedures and contrast use varying depending on institutional protocols to enable early detection of multiple injuries.4 Its upfront application reduces the time to diagnose life-threatening injuries and length of emergency department (ED) stay compared with conventional diagnostic protocols, followed by selective CT of targeted body regions.5 6 In the primary survey, professional societies recommend WBCT for patients with severe or multiple injuries.1,3 However, recent database surveys in the USA indicate an increasing utilisation of single-encounter thoracoabdominopelvic CT or WBCT, even in patients without significant injury, suggesting a widespread application of comprehensive CT imaging regardless of the traumatic severities.7 8

Incidental findings (IFs) detected on CT outside the original medical indications encompass a wide spectrum, ranging from benign to clinically relevant and even critically pathological findings. Encountering IFs is a common clinical dilemma associated with unnecessary interventions and low-value care.9 10 Detecting IFs can trigger additional diagnostic workups during hospitalisation, such as further imaging or invasive procedures.11,14 This could affect resources initially dedicated to acute trauma management and be associated with a longer hospital stay.15 Additional medical costs could be high with a reported average cost of €2292/IF for data with high clinical significance detected in CT scans performed in the ED.16 The detection of IFs may also lead to the early detection of non-progressive cancers that are unlikely to cause symptoms or death.17 Proposed management algorithms for IFs exist for specific organs,18 including thyroid,19 20 lungs,21,23 kidneys24 25 and adrenal glands.26,29 In general, potentially life-threatening IFs may require urgent diagnostic work-up and therapeutic interventions, whereas non-life-threatening IFs typically involve clinical follow-up within a reasonable time frame. Benign IFs are clinically insignificant and do not require follow-up.

A recent overview of systematic reviews, based on heterogeneous populations with diverse case-mix across various clinical contexts, reported that the average prevalence of all identified IFs, regardless of clinical significance or urgency grades, on selective chest or abdominopelvic CT is as high as 45%.30 Another meta-analysis involving ED patients revealed that the average prevalence of all identified IFs was 35% in the subgroup of trauma CT.31 However, the specific clinical context to which this estimate could be applied remains unclear, owing to its aggregation without consideration of divergent clinical contexts and encounters. The diversities stemmed from heterogeneity in scanned body regions (specific body regions vs WBCT), unit of analysis (patient-based vs lesion-based) and clinical significance or urgency of the identified IFs. Another meta-analysis focused exclusively on paediatric trauma injury patients32 for whom the risk for important diseases differs from that of adults. Therefore, the prevalence of clinically relevant IFs affecting patient outcomes in adult populations with traumatic injuries has not been comprehensively elucidated.

In light of these limitations in existing meta-analyses and the publication of new studies, in this meta-analysis, we examine the prevalence of clinically significant IFs detected by trauma CT in adult populations at the ED, emphasising whole body, including the chest, abdomen and pelvis. We focus on IFs requiring diagnostic and therapeutic interventions of varying urgencies, as well as pathologically confirmed cancers and emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies detected through IFs.

Methods

Protocol registration

This study addresses two of the seven key questions formulated in a comprehensive systematic review project on WBCT in patients with traumatic injuries with a focus on IFs detected in trauma CT. The protocol of the entire project was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42020187852) and its complete version has been published.33 This report adhered to the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.34 Since the report used publicly available data, ethical review was not required.

Information sources and search strategy

We searched the PubMed, EMBASE and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) databases from their inception through 22 November 2024, without language restrictions. We conducted two separate sets of queries. The first set of search strategies was formulated for the entire systematic review project and included terms pertinent to ‘whole-body CT’ and ‘traumatic injuries’. The second set was complemented post hoc and included terms pertinent to ‘CT’, ‘traumatic injuries’ and ‘incidental findings’. After excluding overlapping citations, the citations found in the second set of searches were merged with those found in the first search. Details on the exact search strategies are described in the online supplemental methods. Subsequently, we examined the reference lists of the eligible studies and reviews to supplement the search.

Study eligibility

Two independent investigators (KN and TT) double screened the abstracts and examined all potentially relevant full-text reports for eligibility. Prospective or retrospective studies published as full-text articles that included patients of all ages with traumatic injuries and evaluated IFs detected by WBCT or selective CT of targeted body regions were included. Studies were included if they reported at least one of the following four target outcomes: (1) IFs requiring any interventions, (2) IFs requiring urgent interventions (see Data extraction for operational details), (3) pathologically confirmed cancers and (4) emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies, for which urgent treatment was required. Studies lacking data to estimate prevalence were excluded. Since few studies focused solely on adults, we included those examining both paediatric and adult populations but excluded those limited to individuals aged <18 years. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Data extraction

One of three investigators (KN, TN or TT) extracted descriptive data on the study, patient, CT and interpreter characteristics (see online supplemental methods for specific items). All extracted data were verified by another investigator. Two investigators independently extracted quantitative data on IFs at the patient level. Corresponding authors of potential studies were contacted for pertinent but unreported data, with the request rejected after two independent unsuccessful attempts. Discrepancies in the data extraction were resolved through consensus.

We operationally defined two IF categories: (1) IFs requiring any interventions, regardless of urgency, and (2) IFs requiring urgent interventions only (as the subset category); reported IFs were reclassified accordingly (see online supplemental methods).1112 14 35,37 We considered any diagnostic or therapeutic interventions, including clinical follow-up, to be interventions regardless of the number or timing of implementations. To qualify as an IF requiring urgent interventions, the timing of the recommended intervention implementation had to be ‘prompt’, ‘during hospitalization or shortly after discharge’ or in equivalent descriptions. We operationally categorised ‘highly medically relevant’, ‘potentially life-threatening’, ‘severe’ or ‘masses’ as the former, inclusive IF category unless the timing of the necessary interventions was explicitly described. IFs deemed clinically insignificant or requiring no follow-up were excluded from both categories.

Assessment of study validity

We assessed the validity of studies that reported IF outcomes using the Joanna Briggs Institute Prevalence Critical Appraisal Tool (online supplemental table S1).38 For studies that reported cancer and/or vascular outcomes requiring surgical or pathological verifications, we additionally applied the revised tool for the quality assessment of diagnostic accuracy studies (Revised Tool for the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2).39 Two investigators (KN and TT) independently assessed the appraisal items, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Outcomes

Our primary outcomes were the percentages of patients with IFs requiring any and urgent interventions. Secondary outcomes included the percentages of patients with pathologically confirmed cancers or emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies. Prevalence was calculated at the patient level, with the numerator representing the number of patients with ≥1 target condition and the denominator being the total number of patients unless specified otherwise.

Data synthesis

Considering the clinical diversity, range of reported prevalence estimates and mutually-related multiple detection outcomes, we performed multivariate meta-analysis for the population-averaged marginal prevalence and their 95% credible and prediction intervals (CrIs and PrIs, respectively) using a Bayesian study-level hierarchical random-effects model, based on a multinomial exact likelihood and the probit link (hereafter, referred to as multivariate meta-analysis).40 This model explicitly incorporates across-outcome correlations and imputes unreported data under the missing-at-random assumption. All analyses were implemented using JAGS V.4.3.1 (Martyn Plummer; University of Warwick, Coventry, UK; https://mcmc-jags.sourceforge.io/) from StataNow V.18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).41

In the main analysis, we performed multivariate meta-analysis, jointly modelling two IF outcomes and cancer and vascular outcomes. Due to the small number of studies included and few observed events, data were not amenable to multivariate meta-analysis for head, neck and spine CT. Therefore, we performed a univariate standard random-effects meta-analysis (hereafter, referred to as univariate meta-analysis) separately if at least two studies were considered appropriate for combination.42

We visually assessed between-study statistical heterogeneity using forest plots and quantified it using the variance parameter, τ and 95% PrIs.43 We preferred visual assessment over quantitative assessment when the results were discrepant. Univariable random-effects meta-regressions were used on study-level variables to explore heterogeneity. The analysed covariates included (1) study location (Europe vs other geographical regions) as the protocol-specified variables; (2) patient age (adolescents and adults (≥15 years) only vs all ages); (3) use of dual imaging review by expert radiologists (vs single review or review by trauma surgeons or resident/fellow radiologists); (4) publication year; and (5) source of CT type (for selected body regions only) as the variables selected post hoc. To prevent autocorrelations observed in Bayesian meta-regression on continuous covariates, we centred the data for the publication year by subtracting the mean covariate value. We did not assess the heterogeneity statistic I2 or funnel plot asymmetry as these methods were not validated in the prevalence meta-analysis.43 We used a proposed weakly informative prior distribution for the τ in the main analysis and a less informative prior in sensitivity analyses.42 Moreover, univariate meta-analyses were performed as the conventional model for reference. Details on model specifications, fitting, convergence and choice of prior distributions are shown in online supplemental methods.

To assess the certainty of the evidence, we adapted the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach for prognosis studies,44 based on a previous application.45 We rated the study’s validity, inconsistency, imprecision and indirectness, which formed the basis for the overall certainty rating (online supplemental methods).

Patient and public involvement

We did not involve patients or the public.

The excluded studies are listed in the online supplement (online supplemental list) of excluded publications.

All studies employed a retrospective design, except for one on spine CT, which used a prospective design (online supplemental table S2). 20 studies (91%) were from high-income countries (defined as very high human development by the Human Development Index 2021)62, with Europe (10 studies, 45%) and North America (4 studies, 18%) as the most common geographical regions. In 18 studies reporting clinical settings, 12 (67%) were from trauma-specialised centres and 6 (33%) were from academic centres.

The mean/median age of patients ranged from 26 to 78 years (online supplemental table S3). 12 studies (55%) focused on adults (≥18 years) or adults and adolescents (≥15 years). One thoracoabdominopelvic CT study exclusively evaluated older patients (≥55 years) with blunt trauma due to ground level falls. In eight studies reporting the injury mechanisms, >95% of patients had blunt trauma in seven studies (88%). Only five WBCT studies reported the trauma severity, with one severe, two major and two multiple traumas. Seven studies reported average injury severity scores, ranging from 11.3 to 26.3.

In 11 studies reporting intravenous contrast material use, all routinely used it for the chest, abdomen and pelvis. Overall, studies poorly reported the technical specifications of CT scanners and the imaging protocols (online supplemental table S4).

Studies inconsistently defined and interpreted the clinical significance of the identified IFs (online supplemental table S5). While 18 studies (82%) explicitly defined which IFs required interventions, only 7 (32%) specified when the interventions were necessary. The image interpretation methods were reported in 15 studies, only 4 (27%) of which employed a dual review by multiple expert radiologists; resident radiologist and/or trauma surgeon interpreters were also involved in other studies.

Data on the interventions performed for patients with IFs were available from 15 studies (68%). Of these, six studies reported follow-up durations without specific data, such as ‘during hospitalization’ (three studies) and ‘≥6 months’ or ‘6–24 months’ (three studies) (online supplemental table S6). The reported intervention implementation rates ranged from 10.3% (7/68) to 36.0% (72/200) for patients with IFs requiring any interventions (six studies), and from 27.8% (10/36) to 100% (3/3) for IFs requiring urgent interventions (nine studies). The details of the 27 pathologically confirmed cancers and 7 emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies identified through interventions are listed in online supplemental table S7. Only one study15 provided data on the association between IF detection and clinical outcomes: those with IFs had an average hospital stay of 2 days longer than those without. No study addressed the association between IFs and other patient-relevant clinical outcomes, including mortality and readmission.

Critical appraisal: study validity and risk of bias

All 22 studies reporting the prevalence of IFs had at least 1 methodological concern (online supplemental figures S2 and S3), including retrospective analysis of non-standardised data from clinical practice, limited reporting in comorbidity data and poor adherence to standard reporting of statistical analyses.

All 11 studies reporting cancer and/or vascular outcomes exhibited a high risk of bias either in the implementation, flow and timing of the interventions to verify the outcomes, or both (online supplemental figures S4 and S5). The methodological concerns included the lack of blinding of CT results to treating physicians and the low implementation rates of the interventions, including selectively performed biopsy, surgery or clinical follow-up, resulting in partial and/or differential verification.

Meta-analyses of prevalence

Nine studies provided data on ≥1 outcome in the whole body11,1315 37 46 (table 2; figure 1). The outcome data were missing in 11% (1/9) of the studies for IFs requiring any interventions and in 44% (4/9) of the studies for IFs requiring urgent interventions, cancers and vascular pathologies. Visual assessment of forest plots showed wide-ranging prevalence estimates of IFs requiring any interventions (15.7–58.7%), whereas those of the other outcomes were similar across studies. The summary estimates were 29.8% (95% CrI 20.4% to 42.9%; 95% PrI 7.0% to 69.0%; very low certainty) for IFs requiring any intervention. The certainty of evidence was rated very low because of the methodological limitations, estimates with substantial uncertainty around the summary prevalence (95% CrI 20.4 to 42.9%), with future studies expected to report widely varying estimates (95% PrI 7.0 to 69.0%). The summary estimates for IFs requiring any interventions were not significantly different between subgroups by study locations, patient age, image review methods or by publication year (online supplemental table S8). The summary estimates were 7.6% (95% CrI 4.5% to 14.8%; 95% PrI 1.7% to 27.4%; low certainty) for IFs requiring urgent intervention, 0.6% (95% CrI 0.3% to 1.6%; 95% PrI 0.2% to 3.0%; moderate certainty) for pathologically confirmed cancers, and 0.3% (95% CrI 0.1% to 0.9%; 95% PrI 0.1% to 2.2%; moderate certainty) for emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies.

Data on the combined chest and abdomen and pelvis were available from four WBCT11 13 46 47 and three thoracoabdominopelvic CT14 50 51 studies (table 2, online supplemental figure S6). The outcome data were missing in 71% (5/7) of the studies for IFs requiring any and urgent interventions and in 14% (1/7) of the studies for cancers and vascular pathologies. Data on IFs requiring any interventions were from two thoracoabdominopelvic CT studies only,14 50 whereas those requiring urgent interventions were from two WBCT studies only.13 46 Because no statistical heterogeneity was observed in the visual assessment, meta-regression was not performed. The summary prevalence estimates were 16.7% (95% CrI 8.1% to 31.3%; 95% PrI 3.2% to 51.1%; low certainty) for IFs requiring any interventions, 3.8% (95% CrI 1.0% to 13.4%; 95% PrI 0.2% to 27.7%; low certainty) for IFs requiring urgent interventions, 0.7% (95% CrI 0.3% to 1.8; 95% PrI 0.1% to 4.6%; moderate certainty) for pathologically confirmed cancers and 0.2% (95% CrI 0.1% to 1.2%; 95% PrI 0.0% to 3.0%; moderate certainty) for emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies.

Data on the chest were available from four WBCT,11 13 46 47 two thoracoabdominopelvic CT14 51 and three chest CT56,58 studies (figure 2, table 2). The outcome data were missing in 67% (6/9) of the studies for IFs requiring any and urgent interventions, in 22% (2/9) of the studies for cancers and in 33% (3/9) of the studies for vascular pathologies. In the visual assessment, statistical heterogeneity was observed for IFs requiring any interventions only (prevalence range: 5.5–32.8%). However, few data points (three studies) precluded meta-regression. The summary prevalence estimates were 15.6% (95% CrI 5.6% to 40.1%; 95% PrI 0.6% to 73.4%; very low certainty) for IFs requiring any interventions, 1.8% (95% CrI 0.5% to 9.3%; 95% PrI 0.1% to 24.4%; moderate certainty) for IFs requiring urgent interventions, 0.2% (95% CrI 0.1% to 1.3%; 95% PrI 0.0% to 5.9%; moderate certainty) for pathologically confirmed cancers and 0.1% (95% CrI 0.0% to 1.4%; 95% PrI 0.0% to 3.6%; moderate certainty) for emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies.

Data on IFs outcomes in the head, neck and spine were generally scarce. The prevalence estimates of IFs requiring any interventions were heterogeneous and available only from two studies each for the head (0.5% (1/217) and 6.9% (41/591)),13 53 and neck (4.6% (10/217) and 73.1% (261/357)).13 55 The event rates of IFs requiring urgent interventions were reported from two studies only13 46 and were generally low for the head (0.5% (1/217) and 1.3% (4/304)), neck (0.3% (1/304) and 0.5% (1/217)) and spine (both 0% (0/217 and 0/304)). Cancer and vascular outcome events were markedly few: zero cancer events were reported in 80% (4/5) for the head, and 83% (5/6) for neck and spine, and zero vascular pathology events were reported in 83% (5/6) for the head and neck and 100% (6/6) for spine. The summary results obtained in the univariate meta-analysis were presented in online supplemental figures S7−S9.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses based on less informative priors generated slightly wider CrIs and PrIs than those in the main analysis; however, the results remain largely unchanged (online supplemental table S9). The results based on univariate meta-analysis were generally congruent with those based on multivariate meta-analysis (online supplemental table S9).

Discussion

This is the first systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis to evaluate clinically relevant IFs requiring additional interventions identified through trauma CT, involving 22 studies and 18 538 patients largely from high-income countries. First, when the whole body was scanned, IFs requiring any medical interventions were prevalent, identified at an average of 30% (approximately 2–4 out of 10 patients). When IFs were limited to those requiring urgent intervention, the incidence remained relatively common, with an average detection rate of 7.6% (approximately 1 out of 13 patients), which led to the detection of pathologically confirmed cancers at 0.6% and emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies at 0.3%. Second, the prevalence of IFs requiring any interventions, including clinical follow-up, was widely reported, suggesting variations of radiologist recommendations or documentations in clinical practice. Third, most of these findings were identified in the chest or abdomen and pelvis, with the overall detection frequency reduced with the scanned body regions narrowed. These results are presented within the conceptual framework of implementing whole-body CT scanning (figure 4).

Analytical framework for incidental findings detected by whole-body CT and their clinical consequences in patients with traumatic injury.

The strength of our meta-analysis lies in its focus on the prevalence of clinically relevant IFs and subsequent detections of cancers and vascular pathologies, in similar settings and at the patient level. Our summary prevalence estimates are novel and more clinically applicable than those previously reported, although clinical heterogeneity remains. A recent meta-analysis of trauma CT in the ED averaged the prevalence of all IFs by jointly analysing various body regions—regardless of clinical significance, including benign findings not requiring interventions.31 The accompanying body region-based results in that meta-analysis aggregated data from all medical indications at the ED, not exclusively focused on traumatic injuries.

Our findings, based on the real-life clinical practice data, provide insightful information for future discussions about CT utilisation patterns and appropriateness. First, the summary prevalence of IFs requiring any intervention in the whole body of 30% implies an index of the downstream effect on the healthcare system, that is, the incurring costs. On average, approximately 2–4 out of 10 trauma WBCT recipients can result in some additional interventions, at least single and potentially multiple repeat imaging tests and further invasive interventions. Second, the summary prevalence of pathologically confirmed cancers (0.6%) and emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies (0.3%)—possibly underestimated due to the infrequent implementation of recommended interventions—might indicate the screening performance for early disease detection. This represents a potentially additional benefit, alongside the primary benefit of early detection of multiple injuries. For comparison, in research settings with standardised intervention algorithms, single-cycle CT colonography detects colorectal cancer in approximately 0.5% of asymptomatic, average-risk screening attendees.63,65 Similarly, single-cycle low-dose CT detects lung cancer in approximately 1.0% of high-risk screened populations.66 67 Screening ultrasound identifies abdominal aortic aneurysm requiring treatment in 0.3%–0.6% of older populations.68,72 However, the benefit of treating incidentally identified cancers in the context of traumatic injuries has not been validated. There are uncertainties, including histologically confirmed slow-growing cancers (eg, some thyroid and kidney cancers), which may not progress to cause symptoms or affect the individual’s survival.17 73

Guidelines from professional societies provide judiciously constructed management algorithms and advise risk-based approaches including urgent medical interventions for specific IFs.18,31 However, whether timely implementing the advised interventions, in comparison with no or delayed interventions, even with highly suspected cancers or large aortic aneurysms, improves patient-relevant outcomes has yet to be empirically documented.9 Analogous to cancer screening programmes, successful management of IFs would require evidence-based, standardised approaches involving multiple and complex intermediate steps,74 including reliable and reproducible radiologist interpretation and its documentation,12 timely and complete disclosure of results to patients,75 risk-based diagnostic work-up and ultimately treatment if needed. Considering the challenges of applying randomised comparisons of the entire management strategy in this framework, future research should first refine the component processes and evaluate and update their corresponding short-term outcomes. With the inconsistent classification criteria and management algorithms, standardising CT interpretations for individual body regions or organs based on risk-stratified, uniform grading criteria21 29 76 needs to be established first. This line of standardisations would then need to be validated on diagnostic and/or prognostic performance in long-term, real-life settings.77 Lastly, patient-relevant outcomes, such as the incidence of advanced-stage cancers, rupture rates of aortic aneurysms and, ultimately, disease-specific mortality at the whole strategy level would be addressable with reliably registered real-world data and recently developed advanced analytical methodologies.78

This meta-analysis has some limitations stemming from the included studies. First, most studies were retrospective, relied on limited-quality data obtained for non-research purposes and used heterogeneous criteria for classifying IFs. Second, trauma patients can be susceptible to the effects of injury-related changes coupled with motion-related artefacts, which may affect the accurate visualisation of IFs. Third, the work-up and follow-up durations for the IFs were short. Sparse and selective verification of the IFs performed in clinical practice could have underestimated the prevalence of cancer and vascular outcomes. Fourth, primary studies were not conducted in a manner this review intended, including the outcomes of interest and analytical methods, that is, lesion-based analysis (vs patient-based analysis). Notably, the data on IF requiring urgent intervention, as well as cancer and vascular outcomes, were missing in four of the nine studies that evaluated the whole body. However, we attempted to best use all available data using multivariate meta-analysis to impute the missing data. Fifth, without access to individual-level data, our operational criteria classifying IFs relied exclusively on the reported information. They may not have perfectly standardised the grading systems across individual studies.

This review has a few limitations. First, our meta-analysis focused primarily on the prevalence of IFs and did not address their clinical impact on patient-relevant outcomes, including mortality specifically attributed to IF detections. We had planned to evaluate these subsequent clinical outcomes related to IFs33; however, due to the lack of pertinent data, this was not possible. Second, the outcome data were missing in an unignorably large proportion of studies on several occasions. Since the missing-at-random assumption is untestable, our results based on the multivariate meta-analysis should still be viewed with caution and validated in future research. Finally, our meta-regression based on <10 studies may have lacked the statistical power to identify a factor that explains the heterogeneity in the prevalence of IF requiring any interventions.

In conclusion, IFs on trauma WBCT requiring intervention are commonly identified, but the prevalence may vary depending on the classification criteria and interpreters. IFs requiring urgent intervention are found in an average of 7.6%, leading to the identification of histologically confirmed cancers in 0.6%, and emergent non-traumatic vascular pathologies requiring treatment in 0.3%. Considering the variations in radiologist recommendations in clinical practice and short follow-up in the available evidence, our findings warrant updates with long-term follow-up.
