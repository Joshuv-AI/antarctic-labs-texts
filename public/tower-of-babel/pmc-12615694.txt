Investigating the potential advantages of using thorium-based fuel as an advanced nuclear fuel in a typical boiling water reactor assembly (BWR)
Authors: Nassar Alnassar, A. Abdelghafar Galahom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The long-term sustainability of Boiling water reactor (BWR) fuel cycles is challenged by the accumulation of reactor-grade plutonium (rgPu), minor actinides (MAs), and high-level radiotoxic waste in spent fuel. This study investigates the potential of thorium-based fuels as an alternative to conventional uranium dioxide (UO2) to address these issues and improve fuel cycle performance. Several thorium fuel combinations—namely (Th, U)O2, (Th, 233U)O2, and (Th, rgPu)O2—were analyzed within an BWR fuel assembly and compared to conventional UO2 fuel. Key parameters such as the infinite multiplication factor (kinf), fissile inventory ratio (FIR), reactor-grade plutonium (rgPu), minor actinides (MAs), significant fission products (135Xe and 149Sm), and overall radioactivity were evaluated across various effective full power days (EFPDs). To assess the feasibility of the proposed fuel types, key safety parameters including the fuel temperature coefficient (FTC), void reactivity coefficient (VRC), and effective delayed neutron fraction (βeff) were evaluated. Control rod worth (CRW) was also calculated for each fuel type to analyze its impact. Additionally, radial power distribution was examined. The results indicate that both (Th, 233U)O2 and (Th, rgPu)O2 extend the fuel cycle, with over 56% of the initially loaded plutonium being consumed during operation. The temperature reactivity coefficients of the proposed fuels remain within acceptable safety margins, and the radial power distribution shows no significant power peaking within the fuel rods, suggesting favorable thermal–hydraulic and structural performance.

Introduction

The increasing global demand for sustainable and carbon-free energy has renewed interest in nuclear power as a reliable energy source. Boiling Water Reactors (BWRs), as a major class of light water reactors, are widely used for electricity generation due to their proven safety, efficiency, and long operational lifetimes. However, the conventional use of uranium dioxide (UO2) fuel in BWRs leads to challenges such as the buildup of reactor-grade plutonium (rgPu), minor actinides (MAs), and long-lived fission products (LLFPs) in spent fuel. These issues complicate spent fuel management, contribute to long-term radiotoxicity, and raise proliferation concerns. As a result, significant global research efforts are focused on developing advanced nuclear fuels that can improve fuel utilization, minimize high-level waste generation, and enhance intrinsic safety characteristics. Nuclear fuel development is a continuous process aimed at achieving higher burnup, improving reactivity control, and supporting safe and economic reactor operation over extended cycles1–3.

Numerous studies have explored strategies to enhance BWR fuel cycle performance and reduce the buildup of long-lived waste. These approaches include optimizing enrichment zoning to flatten power profiles, incorporating burnable absorbers such as gadolinium to suppress excess reactivity, and introducing advanced fuel types. For instance, variable enrichment of uranium dioxide has been applied to achieve a more uniform power distribution4. Uranium–zirconium hydride (UZrH1.6) has been investigated as an alternative fuel for ABWRs, showing promising neutronic behavior when compared with conventional UO2 fuel5,6. The direct addition of gadolinium dioxide to fuel pellets has been shown to improve reactivity control and meet BWR safety requirements when its concentration and spatial distribution are optimized7,8.

Other research efforts have focused on mitigating the accumulation of minor actinides (MAs) through heterogeneous recycling, demonstrating potential to reduce the inventory of long-lived nuclides in spent fuel and to act as a technology demonstrator for advanced fuel cycles9. In addition, new BWR core designs have been proposed to replace water tubes with additional fuel rods, thereby increasing the power density. The use of uranium–thorium hydride in such configurations has been shown to extend the fuel cycle length compared to conventional fuel10. Thorium-based fuels were assessed in BWRs using CASMO-5 lattice simulations with four fissile options: LEU, RGPu, U-233, and RGPu with recycled uranium. GE14-N–based assemblies were optimized to match UOX performance at ~ 55 MWd/kgHM while minimizing power peaking. Results confirmed the feasibility of thorium-based BWR designs, with safety parameters generally comparable to LEU and MOX fuel. Mixing thorium with RGPu achieved more efficient plutonium destruction than MOX fuel11.

Due to the greater abundance of thorium in the Earth’s crust compared to uranium and the potential advantages of using it as a nuclear fuel, much research has been conducted on the use of thorium as a nuclear fuel for various nuclear reactors. Various thorium-based fuels have been investigated in diverse reactor types. The performance of uranium–thorium mixtures, uranium–zirconium hydride, and plutonium-thorium combinations has been analyzed12,13. The feasibility of using thorium fuel cycles in large-scale GCFRs was explored. The replacement of 238U with 232Th proved that 232Th has a significant effect on the breeding, transmutation and safety parameters14. Thorium-based fuel was investigated in a 10 MWt Indonesia Micro Reactor (IMR). The analysis demonstrated that with careful management of thorium content (up to 25%), core height, and temperature, the IMR can operate safely and continuously for over 920 days without refueling. The inclusion of thorium reduced excess reactivity and enhanced safety features due to its negative temperature reactivity response15. In pressurized heavy water reactors (PHWRs), a neutronic analysis of high burnup Thorium-High Assay Low Enriched Uranium) HALEU) fuels were discussed. Using an infinite lattice model in SERPENT16, the study explored various fuel compositions aiming for high burnup targets. High burnup thorium-HALEU fuels were able to achieve significant fissile breeding and energy extraction, with flatter reactivity profiles due to thorium’s neutron absorption properties16. Homogeneous thorium fuel was investigated in a lead-cooled fast reactor using Monte Carlo simulations with the SERPENT code. The analysis demonstrated that homogeneous thorium fuel in a lead-cooled fast reactor sustains reactivity and permits longer fuel cycles with negative Doppler and coolant density reactivity coefficients, enhancing safety17. Using Monte Carlo codes SERPENT and OpenMC, the behavior of thorium-containing mixed oxide (TOX) fuels with varying 235U enrichment levels in light water reactors has been examined. Higher 235U enrichment in thorium-based fuels increased fission reactions, affecting the production of particular fission products and nuclear absorption reactions18. Uranium zirconium hydride (U-ZrH1.6) fuel with thorium-based mixed-oxide ((Th,U)O2) fuel was investigated in the TRIGA Mark-II reactor, utilizing the seed-blanket configuration. Thorium-based mixed-oxide fuels, when implemented in seed-blanket configurations within the TRIGA Mark-II reactor, can provide favorable neutronic and safety characteristics better than conventional uranium fuel19.

Incorporating thorium instead of uranium in the nuclear fuel reduces from the production of reactor grade-plutonium and MAs in the spent fuel. Escorcia-Ortiz et al., (2019) studied the neutronic performance of thorium-based metallic fuel in a fast reactor core and compared it with that obtained from a reference core utilizing MOX fuel. The incorporation of thorium in the fast reactor as a nuclear fuel enhanced the neutron spectrum and reduced the production of minor actinides, contributing to a more sustainable fuel cycle20. A multiphysics analysis of thorium-based fuels (Th-U)O2 and (Th-Pu)O2 were conducted under normal operating and accident conditions in light water reactors. Thorium-based fuels demonstrated significant advantages over UO2 in terms of thermal and mechanical performance where they offer lower fuel center temperatures, reduced fission gas release, delayed mechanical interaction, and higher melting margins, thus improving overall reactor safety under both normal and transient conditions21. The use of thorium-based fuels in VVER-1200 reactors, focusing on minimizing plutonium production and enhancing fuel cycle economy while maintaining safety parameters was studied22. The feasibility of thorium-based fuels in PWRs is explored, focusing on their potential for plutonium incineration and reducing minor actinide production. Various fuels based on thorium as a fertile material were examined to determine which fuel type can consume a large amount of plutonium and MAs23–27. The possibility of operating CANDU reactors with thorium fuels was examined using MCNPX code28.

Another strategy for improving reactor performance has been the use of blanket–seed fuel assemblies, which separate fertile and fissile materials into distinct regions. For BWRs, a blanket–seed configuration has been proposed in which seed rods are loaded with UZr fuel and blanket rods with ThO2, yielding improved neutronic performance compared to the standard assembly29. Similar concepts have been successfully applied to VVER-1200 reactors, where the introduction of thorium in blanket rods was found to extend the fuel cycle, enhance plutonium incineration, and improve key safety parameters30,31. Further studies combining various fissile materials in the seed rods with thorium dioxide in the blanket have demonstrated a higher conversion ratio, flatter power distribution, and increased fuel utilization relative to conventional designs32. Beyond light water reactors, the viability of thorium-based accident-tolerant fuels has also been examined in pebble bed modular reactors using TRI-ISOtropic (TRISO) particle fuel, showing favorable neutronic and safety characteristics33.

In this study, a comprehensive neutronic analysis is conducted for a representative BWR fuel assembly to evaluate the performance of various thorium-based fuels and identify the most promising configuration. Thorium, employed as a fertile material in place of 238U, is homogeneously blended with uranium dioxide (UO2), 233U, or reactor-grade plutonium (rgPu) to form three distinct fuel types. Each combination is analyzed through detailed burnup simulations using MCNPX, covering a full operating cycle of 2160 effective full power days (EFPDs). The analysis includes fuel burnup calculations, evaluation of key safety parameters, tracking of spent fuel radioactivity, and assessment of radial power distribution. The objective is to determine the configuration that not only sustains reactivity and extends the fuel cycle but also minimizes long-lived waste and improves intrinsic safety margins. This work further explores the potential of using BWRs as effective burners of excess rgPu, thereby contributing to plutonium disposition strategies while leveraging thorium’s benefits for a more sustainable and proliferation-resistant fuel cycle.

Results and discussion

Multiplication factor analysis with Burnup

The proposed fuel types were irradiated for 2160 EFPDs to assess which option yields the most efficient fuel cycle. All simulations were conducted under Hot Full Power (HFP) conditions, with a set thermal output of 4.34 MWth. Light water acts as both the coolant and moderator. As the water flows upward through the core, it boils due to the heat from the fission in the fuel. This process decreases the water density and creates a mixture of liquid water and steam, known as two-phase flow. Figure 2 shows the variation of the water density and void fraction as a function of axial core distance. The water density decreases from 0.75 g/cm3 at the bottom of the core to 0.26 g/cm3 at the top of the core, corresponding to an increase in void fraction from 0% to approximately 65% along the core height.Fig. 2The variation of the water density and void fraction as a function of axial core distance.

The variation of the water density and void fraction as a function of axial core distance.

A key parameter in evaluating fuel performance is the infinite multiplication factor (kinf), which indicates the reactor’s ability to sustain a chain reaction throughout the burnup period. Figure 3a presents the trend of the (kinf) over time, measured in EFPD, for the BWR assembly using the proposed fuel types. At the beginning of life (BOL), a sharp decline in kinf is evident, mainly caused by the early accumulation of neutron-absorbing isotopes such as 135Xe and 149Sm. Among the evaluated fuels, (0.963Th, 233U)O2 demonstrated the highest initial kinf value, while (0.946Th, rgPu)O2 with a 3.65 wt% enrichment showed the lowest. The variation in the kinf values among the proposed fuels is influenced by the specific fissile and fertile materials used. The fuel (0.963Th, 233U)O2 exhibited the highest value of kinf due to two key factors. Firstly, 233U produces more neutrons per fission (ν) than 235U, with average values of 2.49 and 2.43, respectively38. Secondly, 233U has the lowest neutron capture cross-section among the studied fissile isotopes—45.51 barns—compared to 98.80 for 235U, 269.30 barns for 239Pu, and 362.11 barns for 241Pu38. On the other hand, the (0.946Th, rgPu)O2 fuel showed the lowest kinf value, largely due to the significant neutron absorption by plutonium isotopes, which leads to their transmutation into other nuclides. To optimize the fuel cycle performance, the concentration of reactor-grade plutonium (rgPu) was increased to 7.27 wt%. This adjustment resulted in improved kinf behavior, supporting a longer fuel cycle and achieving higher burnup, as illustrated in Fig. 3b.Fig. 3The variation in the kinf for BWR assembly loaded with suggested fuels.

The variation in the kinf for BWR assembly loaded with suggested fuels.

Reactor grade plutonium inventory (rgPu)

Plutonium isotopes are highly significant due to their practical applications and geopolitical impact. As a result, examining their concentrations in the spent fuel of a BWR is essential. Figure 6 illustrates the evolution of plutonium isotopes with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied. In the (0.963Th, 233U)O2 fuel, the plutonium content remains negligible because there is no 238U present to breed plutonium. In contrast, for UO2 and (Th,U)O2 fuels, the concentration of Pu isotopes gradually increases with burnup as 238U undergoes neutron capture and subsequent decay to form plutonium.Fig. 6The Pu-isotopes concentration with EFPDs for the investigated fuel types.

The Pu-isotopes concentration with EFPDs for the investigated fuel types.

For the (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 fuel, the initial rgPu inventory undergoes substantial isotopic transformation. The fissile isotopes (239Pu and 241Pu) exhibit a pronounced decrease over the cycle, mainly due to direct fission. In parallel, the even-mass isotopes (238Pu and 242Pu) gradually accumulate through neutron capture, while 240Pu shows a moderate change. Overall, the total plutonium inventory decreases by more than 55% by end-of-cycle (EOC), indicating that a significant fraction of the initial rgPu has either undergone fission or been converted into higher-mass isotopes with very low fissile quality. This isotopic shift improves the proliferation resistance of the remaining plutonium stockpile by reducing the fraction of fissile isotopes.

Minor actinides (MAs)

Minor actinides (MAs) are highly radiotoxic and significantly contribute to the long-term radiological hazard of nuclear waste. The MA vector includes isotopes such as neptunium, americium, and curium. Effective management and disposal of these nuclides are essential to mitigate their potential use in nuclear proliferation. Figure 7 depicts the variation of the MA concentrations with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied. In general, the concentration of the MAs increases with burnup for all the examined fuels, except for the (0.963Th, 233U)O2 fuel. In UO2 and (Th, U)O2 fuels, the buildup of MAs primarily occurred by the formation of 237Np through neutron capture by 235U during reactor operation. The (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 fuel yielded the greatest quantity of MAs as a result of plutonium isotopes being transmuted into MAs elements. The formation of MAs from rgPu occurs through successive neutron captures and beta decays within the plutonium isotopic chain. During reactor operation, 239Pu that does not undergo fission may captures a neutron to form 240Pu, which can undergo further (n, γ) reactions to yield 241Pu and 242Pu. The β⁻ decay of 241Pu produces 241Am, the most significant MA generated in plutonium-bearing fuels. Similarly, 242Pu can capture additional neutrons to form 243Pu, which decays to 243Am and subsequently to curium isotopes (244Cm, 245Cm) after additional neutron absorptions. This transmutation chain results in the highest MA buildup among all fuel types studied.Fig. 7The MAs variation with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied.

The MAs variation with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied.

Significant fission product

Fission products are the nuclei generated when heavy isotopes such as 233U, 235U, 239Pu and 241Pu undergo nuclear fission. Some fission products, such as 135Xe and 149Sm, have a notable effect on reactor reactivity due to their large neutron absorption cross-section. Figure 8 shows the 135Xe variation with EFPDs for the investigated fuel types. The concentration of the 135Xe and 149Sm produced in the spent fuel relies on the type and the amount of the fissile material used.Fig. 8The Xe-135 variation with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied.

The Xe-135 variation with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied.

The (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 fuel generated the highest amount of 135Xe among the examined fuels. This can be attributed to two main reasons. First, the cumulative fission yield of 135Xe is relatively high for the fissile isotopes present in the fuel: 5.4% for 233U, 6.6% for 235U, 7.3% for 239Pu, and 7.01% for 241Pu from thermal fission, while the yields from fast fission are slightly higher—6.2, 6.3, 7.5, and 7.2%, respectively38. Second, the (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 fuel contains a higher initial concentration of fissile material compared to the other fuels studied, resulting in greater fission activity and consequently more 135Xe production. Figure 9 shows the 149Sm variation with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied. 149Sm is not produced directly through fission; instead, it is formed through a sequence of beta decays, beginning with 149Nd, which decays to 149Pm, and subsequently to 149Sm. Due to the short half-life of 149Nd, 149Pm is typically considered a direct fission product. The (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 fuel generates the highest amount of 149Sm among the proposed fuels. This is primarily due to its relatively high initial concentration of fissile material. Additionally, the cumulative fission yields of 149Pm are higher for the plutonium isotopes present in the fuel. Specifically, the cumulative thermal fission yields of 149Pm for 233U, 235U, 239Pu, and 241Pu are 0.76, 1.05, 1.26, and 1.45%, respectively. Likewise, the cumulative fast fission yields of 14⁹Sm from these isotopes are 0.71, 1.06, 1.27, and 1.45%, respectively.Fig. 9The Sm-149 variation with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied.

The Sm-149 variation with EFPDs for the different fuel types studied.

135Xe and 149Sm have a significant impact on reactor reactivity because of their extremely large thermal neutron absorption cross-sections, which are approximately 2.6 × 106 barns and 4.0 × 104 barns, respectively. The production of 135Xe at the initial stages of burnup leads to a noticeable decrease in core reactivity. In the case of the (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 fuel, the larger amounts of 135Xe and 149Sm generated result in additional reactivity suppression at the BOL, even though the fuel has a relatively higher fissile enrichment. However, the total amount of Xe-135 and Sm-149 produced from the suggested fuels remains sufficiently small that it does not significantly impact the shutdown margin or lead to notable reactivity swings during reactor operation.

Fissile inventory ratio (FIR)

The Fissile Inventory Ratio (FIR) is a key parameter that affects reactor performance and the long-term viability of nuclear fuel cycles. FIR is the ratio of fissile material available in the reactor to the total nuclear fuel inventory at the Beginning of Life (BOL). Figure 10 illustrates the variation of the FIR with EFPDs for the different fuel types examined. It is evident that thorium-based fuels attain higher FIR values compared to uranium dioxide. This advantage is mainly due to the fact that the neutron absorption cross-section of 232Th is approximately three times greater than that of 238U, enhancing fissile material production in thorium-based systems.Fig. 10The FIR with EFPDs for the different fuel types examined.

The FIR with EFPDs for the different fuel types examined.

Breeding ratio

The breeding ratio (BR) measures the amount of new fissile material produced relative to fissile material consumed and is a key metric for evaluating fuel sustainability. Figure 11 shows BR versus EFPDs for the examined fuels. All thorium-based fuels achieve higher BR than UO2, primarily because the capture cross section of 232Th is roughly three times that of 238U, increasing the probability that 232Th will absorb neutrons and transmute to 233U. The (Th,233U)O2 fuel attains the highest BR among the thorium cases because it contains the largest fraction of 232Th; by contrast, the fertile material in (0.75Th,U)O2 is split between 232Th and 238U, and the (0.927Th,rgPu)O2 case includes significant non-fissile isotopes within the reactor-grade plutonium inventory, which reduce net breeding.Fig. 11The BR with EFPDs for the different fuel types examined.

Radioactivity tracking

Radiotoxicity is a measure of a radioactive substance’s ability to cause harm to a person through radiation released within a body. Therefore, it is required to analyses the radioactivity of actinides and non-actinides nuclides for the suggested fuel. Figure 12 illustrates the radioactivity levels (Bq) of actinides across different EFPDs for the different fuel types studied. Actinides exhibit significant radioactivity, especially those isotopes with short half-lives. This high radioactivity is due to the existence of isotopes with unstable nuclei. The intensity of radioactivity differs among various actinides and their respective isotopes. As fuel burnup progresses, the radioactivity of spent fuel increases due to the buildup of radioactive isotopes. The spent fuels (0.75Th, U)O2 and (0.963Th, 233U)O2 show the highest radioactivity levels because they contain significant amounts of isotopes like 233Th, 233Pa, 237U, 239U, 238Np, 239Np, 238Pu, and 241Pu. In contrast, other fuel types have only a few of these isotopes and at much lower concentrations. Although (Th,Pu)O2 fuel exhibits lower radioactivity in the short- and intermediate-term after discharge—mainly due to reduced yields of short-lived fission products—it should be noted that long-lived isotopes such as 239Pu, 240Pu, and 241Am remain in the spent fuel and dominate the activity over geological timescales.Fig. 12The Radioactivity of the actinides at different EFPDs for the different fuel types studied.

Neutronic safety parameters

The primary passive safety parameters of the BWR—namely the moderator temperature coefficient (MTC), fuel temperature coefficient (FTC), effective delayed neutron fraction (βeff), and void reactivity coefficient (VRC)—play a vital role in maintaining the reactor’s safety and operational reliability. Continuous monitoring of these parameters is essential to prevent potential accidents and to ensure the reactor functions within established safety margins. Table 4 presents selected safety parameters of the BWR for the proposed fuel types. The moderator temperature coefficient (MTC) reflects the reactor’s reactivity response to changes in moderator temperature. For the evaluated fuels, MTC was calculated as the moderator temperature increased from 600 to 900 K, causing a decrease in moderator/coolant density. Delayed neutrons contribute to the total neutron population in a reactor and play a crucial role in controlling its behavior. They act as an inherent feedback mechanism, helping manage reactor power. During a rapid power increase, the higher fission rate leads to a prompt rise in neutron levels. The effective delayed neutron fraction (βeff) is the ratio of delayed neutrons to the total neutrons (prompt and delayed neutrons) produced by fission. Among the studied fuels, UO2 and (0.75Th, U)O2 exhibit the highest βeff. This is primarily because they contain a high fraction of 235U, which has a relatively large delayed neutron yield compared to 233U, 239Pu, and 241Pu.

Conclusion

In this study, various thorium-based fuel combinations—including (Th, U)O2, (0.963Th, 233U)O2, and (0.946Th, rgPu)O2 with an initial enrichment of 3.65 wt%—were proposed for use in the BWR and compared to conventional UO2 fuel. Among these, the (0.963Th, 233U)O2 fuel exhibited the highest initial excess reactivity at the beginning of life (BOL), which, if properly managed, could contribute to an extended fuel cycle. In contrast, the (0.946Th, rgPu)O2 fuel at 3.65 wt% enrichment showed the lowest initial excess reactivity, resulting in a shorter fuel cycle. To improve its performance, the enrichment in the fuel (Th, rgPu)O2 was increased to 4.95 wt%, which significantly enhanced its fuel cycle characteristics. At this enrichment level, (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 demonstrated the most favorable balance between reactivity and cycle length, suppressing excess reactivity at BOL while supporting an extended fuel cycle. These results highlight (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 at 4.95 wt% as a promising candidate for BWR applications. It is worth noting that the (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 fuel consumed more than 55% of the rgPu used in the reactors that was initially loaded during the cycle, indicating the possibility of effectively disposing of the accumulated plutonium stock in spent fuel from commercial reactors around the world. The replacement of 238U with 232Th resulted in a significant improvement in the FIR. Additionally, the actinide radioactivity associated with (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 was lower than that observed for the other evaluated fuel types. The calculated CRW value for (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 was approximately 25% lower than that of conventional UO2. All the fuel candidates demonstrated negative values for key safety coefficients—MTC, FTC and VRC—with (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 exhibiting the most negative values, thereby offering enhanced safety margins. Thermal power distribution analysis further revealed that thorium-based fuels, particularly (0.927Th, rgPu)O2, contribute to a more favorable power profile across the BWR assembly, aiding in the strategic placement of burnable absorbers. Overall, the use of thorium-based fuels, especially (0.927Th, rgPu)O2, enhances both the neutronic performance and safety characteristics of the BWR. The fabrication of (0.927Th, rgPu)O2 fuel poses significant challenges due to the high activity of plutonium isotopes, requiring remote handling and specialized hot-cell facilities. However, advancements in robotic technologies and modern fabrication techniques can treat these challenges.
