Fano and Reflexive Polytopes from Feynman Integrals
Leonardo de la Cruz, Pavel P. Novichkov, and Pierre Vanhove

Abstract
We classify the Fano and reflexive polytopes that arise from quasi-finite Feynman integrals. These polytopes appear as scaled Minkowski sums of the Newton polytopes associated with the Symanzik graph polynomials. For one-loop graphs and multiloop sunset graphs, we identify the Fano and reflexive cases by computing the number of interior points from the associated bivariate Ehrhart polynomials. More generally, we utilize the properties of Symanzik polynomials and their symmetries to conduct a direct search over all Feynman graphs in generic kinematics with up to ten edges and nine loops. We find that such cases are remarkably sparse: for example, we find only two two-dimensional reflexive polytopes, three three-dimensional reflexive polytopes, and four three-dimensional Fano polytopes. We also reveal a surprising feature of one-loop N N -gon integrals in higher dimensions: their associated reflexive polytopes encode degenerate Calabi–Yau ( N − 2 ) (N-2) -folds. We further analyze the geometric structures encoded by these polytopes and exhibit explicit connections with del Pezzo surfaces, K ​ 3 K3 surfaces, and Calabi–Yau threefolds. Since reflexive polytopes naturally correspond to Calabi–Yau varieties, our classification demonstrates that quasi-finite Feynman integrals, with reflexive polytopes, are intrinsically linked to Calabi–Yau period integrals.

Introduction

Feynman integrals are central objects in perturbative quantum field
theory, encoding the contributions of quantum fluctuations to physical
observables such as scattering amplitudes and the determination of effective
couplings (see, for instance, the review
Travaglini:2022uwo
for a
recent account of the breadth of applications of amplitudes).
Beyond their physical significance, these integrals exhibit
a remarkably rich mathematical structure, interconnecting quantum
field theory with algebraic geometry, number theory, and
combinatorics. It is now widely recognized that
many Feynman integrals can be interpreted as periods of
algebraic varieties defined by the vanishing of the Symanzik
polynomials
Bloch:2005bh
;
Bogner:2007mn
;
Brown:2009ta
;
Schnetz:2013hqa
. This
observation has opened a new perspective on quantum field theory,
emphasizing the role of algebraic geometry in understanding the
analytic and arithmetic properties of amplitudes.

Feynman
integrals also serve as a testing ground for discovering novel
geometries that emerge at higher-loop orders. The same class of
multiloop integrals governs observables such as Higgs boson
production at the LHC, the anomalous magnetic moments of the
electron or the muon
Laporta:2017okg
;
Blum:2023qou
, or the
post-Minkowskian expansion of general relativity for the dynamics of
compact binaries relevant to gravitational-wave
emission
Bjerrum-Bohr:2022blt
. Recent computations have
revealed the appearance of Calabi–Yau period integrals in physical
observables. For instance,
K3
periods enter in the evaluation of the
three-loop contribution to two-body post-Minkowskian scattering in
general relativity
Bern:2021dqo
;
Bern:2021yeh
;
Dlapa:2021npj
;
Dlapa:2022lmu
;
Bjerrum-Bohr:2022ows
and the hadronic vacuum polarization in chiral
perturbation theory
Lellouch:2025rnz
, and Calabi–Yau three-fold periods in the four-loop
computation of post-Minkowskian scattering in
general relativity
Klemm:2024wtd
;
Driesse:2024feo
.

Newton polytopes associated with Feynman integrals also play an important role in understanding their geometry. They arise from the graph polynomials in the parametric representation. In this representation, the Newton polytope associated with a Feynman graph
\Gamma
is the Minkowski sum of the Newton polytope of the first Symanzik polynomial
\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}
and the second Symanzik polynomial
\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}
.
Newton polytopes of Feynman integrals are useful
for studying their convergence properties
2010arXiv1010.5060N
;
2011arXiv1103.6273B
,
the method of regions
Ananthanarayan:2018tog
;
Gardi:2022khw
;
Gardi:2024axt
;
Ma:2023hrt
, their evaluation using sector decomposition
Borowka:2015mxa
;
Heinrich:2021dbf
, and their relation to
\mathsf{A}
-hypergeometric systems
GELFAND1990255
;
2010arXiv1010.5060N
;
2016arXiv160504970N
;
Schultka:2018nrs
;
delaCruz:2019skx
;
Klausen:2019hrg
;
Klausen:2021yrt
;
Ananthanarayan:2022ntm
. Newton polytopes that arise from Feynman integrals are lattice polytopes, that is, their vertices have integer coordinates. To a given lattice polytope we can attach a toric variety, which carries information about the convergence of a given Feynman integral
2011arXiv1103.6273B
and the differential equations it satisfies
delaCruz:2019skx
.

Fano polytopes are a particular class of lattice polytopes having a single interior lattice point (see ref.
cox2011toric
).
An important subclass of Fano polytopes, known as reflexive polytopes, was put forward
in ref.
Batyrev:1993oya
.
Reflexive polytopes play a distinguished role in mirror symmetry, as being associated with Calabi–Yau varieties.
In a fixed number of dimensions, all reflexive polytopes are known up to dimension four
Kreuzer:2000xy
.

Certain Feynman integrals lead to mirror
pairs. This is the case for example, of multiloop sunset graphs in two dimensions, whose connection to mirror symmetry was rigorously studied
in refs.
Bloch:2014qca
;
Bloch:2016izu
. There it was found that these Feynman integrals are multiple-valued holomorphic
functions, which arise as well in the context of open mirror symmetry.
The relevance of the toric Fano varieties, associated to Fano polytopes, in the context of Feynman integrals has been put
forward as well in ref.
Schimmrigk:2024xid
.

In another context, algorithms for classifying all locally finite integrals in four dimensions for a given Feynman graph
were presented in refs.
Gambuti:2023eqh
;
delaCruz:2024xsm
. In particular, the algorithm of ref.
delaCruz:2024xsm
relies on the convergence region of Euler–Mellin integrals studied in ref.
2010arXiv1010.5060N
, later generalized in refs.
2011arXiv1103.6273B
;
Schultka:2018nrs
. It is based on constructing numerators that have the property of having parametric representations that depend only on monomials whose exponents are interior lattice points of the Newton polytope of their Symanzik polynomials.
In this context, Fano and reflexive polytopes arise naturally as special cases of Newton polytopes associated to finite integrals with only one interior lattice point.
Quasi-finite integrals introduced in ref.
vonManteuffel:2014qoa
lead to Fano and reflexive polytopes as well.

In this work, we will take a systematic step toward classifying the
reflexive
and
Fano
polytopes
that arise from the Newton polytopes of the Symanzik polynomials associated to finite Feynman integrals. The classification presented in this paper establishes a bridge between the combinatorics of Feynman graphs and the geometry of their associated toric varieties. By systematically identifying the reflexive and Fano polytopes corresponding to classes of Feynman integrals, we construct a geometric dictionary that links the analytic structure of amplitudes to the combinatorial and topological data of toric geometry. This correspondence provides a new framework for exploring the interplay between algebraic geometry and quantum field theory, with potential applications ranging from the theory of periods and motives to high-precision predictions in particle physics and gravitational theory.

2
Parametric representation of Feynman integrals

Feynman integrals associated with a Feynman graph
\Gamma
generally appear in quantum field theory as tensor integrals, that is, integrals with numerator factors. Let us consider a graph
\Gamma
with
L
loops and
N
internal edges in
D
space-time dimensions.
A general tensor integral attached to this graph is a linear combination of integrals of the form

I_{\Gamma}^{\boldsymbol{J}}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu}):=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{1,D-1})^{L}}{\ell_{j_{1}}^{\mu_{1}}\cdots\ell_{j_{R}}^{\mu_{R}}\over\prod_{i=1}^{N}d_{i}^{\nu_{i}}}\prod_{i=1}^{L}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D}\ell_{i}}{\mathrm{i}\pi^{D/2}}\,,

(1)

where
R
is the number of times that the loop momenta appear in the numerator.
The double-index
\boldsymbol{J}:=(J_{1},J_{2},\dots,J_{R})
, with
J_{i}=(j_{i},\mu_{i})
, denotes the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
Lorentz index belonging to the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
loop momentum. The powers of the denominators are denoted collectively by
\boldsymbol{\nu}:=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{N})
while the inverse propagators are given by

d_{i}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L}\alpha_{ij}\ell_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{E}\beta_{ij}p_{j}\right)^{2}-m_{i}^{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon\,,

(2)

where

\alpha_{ij}
,
\beta_{ij}
take the values in
\set{-1,0,1}
,
m_{i}
are the internal masses and
p_{i}
are the
E
independent
external momenta. We will explicitly indicate the parameters
(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})
throughout, as they will play a central role in the subsequent analysis.
The parametric representation of eq. (
1
) can be written as
Heinrich:2008si

I_{\Gamma}^{\boldsymbol{J}}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=\Upgamma\left(\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{N}-\left\lfloor R\over 2\right\rfloor-{LD\over 2}\right)\cr\times\int_{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}_{+}^{N-1}}{\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}^{\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{N}-{L+1\over 2}D}\over\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}^{\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{N}-{L\over 2}D}}{\mathcal{N}^{\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{a})\over\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}^{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}{x_{i}^{\nu_{i}-1}\over\Upgamma(\nu_{i})}\Omega_{0}^{(N)}\,,

(3)

where
\left\lfloor R\over 2\right\rfloor
denotes the nearest
integer less than or equal to
R
and the domain of integration
is the projective positive orthant

\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}_{+}^{N-1}:=\{[x_{0},\dots,x_{N}]\in\mathbb{P}^{N-1}|x_{i}\in\mathbb{R},x_{i}\geq 0\},

(4)

and

\Omega_{0}^{(N)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(-1)^{i}x_{i}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{i-1}\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{i+1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}x_{N}

(5)

is the canonical differential form on
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
. The integral depends on the Symanzik (graph) polynomials and a numerator that we define as follows.

In order to construct Symanzik polynomials
\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}
, we introduce the mass hyperplane

\mathscr{L}_{\Gamma}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}^{2}x_{i}\,

(6)

and the graph polynomial from the sum of the 2-forests (we
refer to ref.
Nakanishi:1971
for details about the
construction of these graph polynomials)

\mathscr{V}_{\Gamma}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{spanning}\\
\text{2-forests}\penalty\ F\penalty\ \text{of}\penalty\ \Gamma\end{subarray}}\quad\left(\sum_{(v_{1},v_{2})\in F=T_{1}\cup T_{2}}p_{v_{1}}\cdot p_{v_{2}}\right)\,\prod_{j\in\Gamma\setminus F}x_{j}\,.

(7)

The Symanzik polynomials are defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{spanning}\\
\text{trees}\penalty\ T\penalty\ \text{of}\penalty\ \Gamma\end{subarray}}\quad\prod_{j\in\Gamma\setminus T}x_{j}\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle:=\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}\,\mathscr{L}_{\Gamma}-\mathscr{V}_{\Gamma}\,.

(9)

The numerator
\mathcal{N}^{\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{a})
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
RL
in the edge variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
,

\mathcal{N}^{\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{a}):=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor R\over 2\right\rfloor}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k}{\Upgamma\left(\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{N}-k-{LD\over 2}\right)\over\Upgamma\left(\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{N}-\left\lfloor R\over 2\right\rfloor-{LD\over 2}\right)}\times\,\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}^{k}\,\mathscr{P}_{k}^{\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,

(10)

where
\mathscr{P}^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
is a homogeneous polynomial
of degree

RL-k(L+1)
,

\mathscr{P}^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\textrm{supp}(\Gamma;\mu)}p^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{a_{i}}\,,

(11)

where
p^{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{a}}
is a tensor that can be constructed from metric tensor and the matrices in eq. (
2
). Its explicit form can be found in ref.
Heinrich:2008si
and may also be computed with
SecDec

Heinrich:2021dbf
. We won’t need the explicit form of this polynomial as we explain below.
We have introduced the support of the homogeneous polynomial,

\textrm{supp}(\mathscr{P}):=\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}=RL-k(L+1);\qquad R(L-1)/2\leq a_{i}\leq RL\}\,.

(12)

We remark that terms of the form (
1
) with different values of
R
can also be put in the form of eq. (
3
) by taking the maximum value of
R
in the combination and adjusting the numerator to include additional powers of
\mathscr{U}
.

In practical calculations, loop momenta is contracted with external momenta and thus each term in the numerator becomes a scalar. Thus, we can focus on the monomials appearing in
\mathcal{N}^{\boldsymbol{J}}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{a})
.
Taking one monomial at the time in the numerator, say
x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\alpha_{N}}
, amounts to studying the scalar integral (now dropping the double-index
\boldsymbol{J}
)

I_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\nu}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}_{+}^{N-1}}{\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}^{\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{N}-{L+1\over 2}D}\over\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}^{\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{N}-{L\over 2}D}}{1\over\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}^{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}{x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i}-1}\over\Upgamma(\nu_{i})}\Omega_{0}^{(N)}\,.

(13)

This definition does not include the
\Upgamma
-function prefactor for reasons detailed in
section

3.2.2
.
Since
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{N}=RL
, we can rewrite the integral (
13
) as

I_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\nu})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Upgamma(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i})}{\Upgamma(\nu_{i})}\\
\times\int_{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}_{+}^{N-1}}{\mathscr{U}_{\Upgamma}^{(\alpha_{1}+\nu_{1})+\dots+(\alpha_{N}+\nu_{N})-(L+1)(D/2+R)}\over\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}^{(\alpha_{1}+\nu_{1})+\dots+(\alpha_{N}+\nu_{N})-L(D/2+R)}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}{x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i}-1}\over\Upgamma(\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i})}\Omega_{0}^{(N)}\,,

(14)

manifesting that, up to a numerical prefactor, the rank
R
and the numerator exponents
\alpha_{i}
enter the integrand only through linear combinations
D/2+R
and
\alpha_{i}+\nu_{i}
, respectively. We have thus recovered the well-known result
Tarasov:1996br
stating that Feynman integrals with numerator factors can be expressed as linear combinations of scalar integrals, possibly in higher dimensions and with raised propagator powers.

From now on, we will redefine
D\to D+2R
,
\nu_{i}\to\nu_{i}+\alpha_{i}
effectively setting
R
and
\nu_{i}
to zero, so that the integral under study takes the form

I_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}_{+}^{N-1}}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{N}{x_{i}^{\nu_{i}-1}}}{\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}^{n_{\mathscr{U}}}\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}^{n_{\mathscr{F}}}}\Omega_{0}^{(N)},

(15)

where we have introduced the short-hand notation

n_{\mathscr{U}}=-\nu_{1}-\cdots-\nu_{N}+{L+1\over 2}D\,,\qquad n_{\mathscr{F}}=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N}-{L\over 2}D\,.

(16)

In an affine patch, say
x_{N}=1
, the
projective integral becomes

I_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}_{+}}\left.\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}{x_{i}^{\nu_{i}}}}{\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}^{n_{\mathscr{U}}}\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}^{n_{\mathscr{F}}}}\right|_{x_{N}=1}\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathrm{d}x_{i}\over x_{i}}.

(17)

The equivalence between the two expressions is a consequence
of the projective nature of the parametric representation and it is
referred to as the Cheng–Wu theorem
(
Cheng:1987ga
, App. C.10)
in the physics literature
Panzer:2015ida
;
Weinzierl:2022eaz
.

3
Newton polytopes attached to Feynman integrals

Introducing the multi-index notation

\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{a}}:=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}\,,

(18)

to a (Laurent) polynomial in
n
variables

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}c_{\boldsymbol{a}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{a}},\qquad c_{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(19)

we associate a Newton polytope

\Delta(f):=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}a_{i}\,\middle|\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1,\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\boldsymbol{a}\in\textrm{supp}(f)\right\}

(20)

as the convex hull of its support

\supp(f):=\set{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}{c_{\boldsymbol{a}}\neq 0}.

The Newton polytope of the product of polynomials is the Minkowski sum

\Delta(f\cdot g)=\Delta(f)+\Delta(g)=\{x+y\,|\,x\in\Delta(f),y\in\Delta(g)\}\,.

(21)

For later purposes, we introduce the hypersimplex (HS)
\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(d,k)}
. It is defined as the convex hull of
d
-dimensional vectors whose coefficients consist of
k
ones and
d-k
zeros. The standard simplex corresponds to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}

\displaystyle:=\Delta\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\right)=\operatorname{Conv}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{N}\}

(22)

\displaystyle=\left\{(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,\middle|\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu_{i}=1,\ 0\leq\nu_{i}\leq 1\text{ for all }i\right\},

(23)

and the second hypersimplex

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,2)}

\displaystyle:=\Delta\!\left(\sum_{1\leq r<s\leq N}x_{r}x_{s}\right)=\operatorname{Conv}\{e_{r}+e_{s}\,|\,1\leq r<s\leq N\}

(24)

\displaystyle=\left\{(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,\middle|\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu_{i}=2,\ 0\leq\nu_{i}\leq 1\text{ for all }i\right\}\,,

(25)

where
e_{i}
are the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

3.1
Newton polytopes in the parametric representation

To the parametric representation of the Feynman integral in

eq.

15
or
eq.

17
, we associate the Newton polytope

\Delta_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=n_{\mathscr{U}}\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma})+n_{\mathscr{F}}\Delta(\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}),

(26)

assuming
n_{\mathscr{U}},n_{\mathscr{F}}\geq 0
.

For integer values of
n_{\mathscr{U}}
and
n_{\mathscr{F}}
,
\Delta_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})
is a lattice polytope and
eq.

26
follows from eq. (
21
) because

\Delta_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}^{n_{\mathscr{U}}}\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}^{n_{\mathscr{F}}})
. All its vertices have integer components and thus lie on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\aff(\Delta_{\Gamma})
, where
\aff(\Delta_{\Gamma})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the smallest affine space containing
\Delta_{\Gamma}
. Even though
eq.

15
and
eq.

17
define two different Newton polytopes, they are related by a projection map
(a_{1},\dots,a_{N-1},a_{N})\mapsto(a_{1},\dots,a_{N-1})
. Moreover, since the integrand of
eq.

15
is homogeneous, its Newton polytope is restricted to a hyperplane, so the projection map defines an isomorphism of the respective lattices
\mathbb{Z}^{N}\cap\{a_{1}+\dots+a_{N}=\mathrm{const}\}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{N-1}
. Therefore, it preserves properties related to lattice polytopes, such as volume, number of interior lattice points, and reflexivity
Haase:2012
;
Beck:2015
. For this reason, we will use the projective and the affine form of the Newton polytope interchangeably.

We give a few remarkable properties that we will use later when
analyzing polytopes arising from Feynman integrals.

•

When all the internal masses are non-vanishing, i.e.
m_{i}\neq 0
for

1\leq i\leq N
, we have that the Newton polytope of
\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}
is
given by the Minkowski sum

\Delta(\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma})=\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma})+\Delta(\mathscr{L}_{\Gamma})\,.

(27)

This relation is a direct consequence of the definitions of the graph polynomials when all the internal mass coefficients are non vanishing (this was also observed e.g. in ref.
Arkani-Hamed:2022cqe
;
Borinsky:2023jdv
).
The Newton polytope

simplex in

22
.
This means that when all the internal masses are non-vanishing the

is given by the weighted Minkowski sum of
\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma})
and
\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}
. Specifically,

\Delta_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=\frac{D}{2}\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma})+\left(\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N}-\frac{DL}{2}\right)\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}

(28)

is determined only by the polytope of the first Symanzik polynomial
and the linear mass polynomial.

•

When some of the internal masses vanish,

27

(

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Gamma;\boldsymbol{0}}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu}):=\bigg(\frac{D(L+1)}{2}-\nu_{1}

\displaystyle-\cdots-\nu_{N}\bigg)\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma})

(29)

\displaystyle+\left(\nu_{1}+\dots+\nu_{N}-\frac{DL}{2}\right)\Delta(\mathscr{V}_{\Gamma})\,.

We discuss the difference between the polytopes of graphs with all
massive internal propagators and graphs with all massless internal propagators in
section

5
in the context of one-loop graphs.

•

The Newton polytope of the first Symanzik polynomial is a generalized permutahedron

as shown in ref.
(

. When the Minkowski sum relation in
eq.

27

(

, Theorem 3.5)
.

•

There is a special class of polynomials with saturated Newton polytopes, meaning that
every lattice point of the
Newton polytope corresponds to a non-vanishing monomial

\Delta(f)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\supp(f)\,.

(30)

The graph polynomials of one-loop integrals

3.1.1
Newton polytopes in Lee–Pomeransky representation

Here we explore the relation between Newton polytopes of Feynman integrals in the Feynman parameter representation (
15
) and another commonly-used parametric representation—the Lee–Pomeransky (LP) representation
Lee:2013hzt
.
This representation is typically presented in deprojectivized form, which reads, up to a prefactor,

I_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}\frac{1}{\left(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}+\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}\right)^{D/2}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}{x_{i}^{\nu_{i}-1}}\mathrm{d}x_{i}\,.

(31)

To make the relation between Newton polytopes more transparent, we will instead consider the projective version of this integral:

I_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=\int_{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}_{+}^{N}}\frac{1}{\left(x_{0}\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}+\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}\right)^{D/2}}\prod_{i=0}^{N}{x_{i}^{\nu_{i}-1}}\Omega_{0}^{(N+1)},

(32)

where
\nu_{0}=\frac{L+1}{2}D-\nu_{1}-\cdots-\nu_{N}
. (The affine integral (
31
) can be obtained from
eq.

32
by setting
x_{0}=1
in the integrand.)

In complete analogy to the parametric representation case (
15
), we can associate the following Newton polytope to the integral (
32
):

\Delta_{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{LP}}(L,D)=\frac{D}{2}\Delta(x_{0}\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}+\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma})\,.

(33)

Note that, unlike the parametric representation case, this polytope does not depend on the vector of exponents

3.2
(Quasi-)finite Feynman integrals

The parametric representation of Feynman integrals in

15

case of Euler–Mellin integrals. Following ref.

we will quote a theorem that states the convergence region of an Euler–Mellin integral.

Definition 3.1

(Euler–Mellin integrals)
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{q}
be Laurent polynomials in variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
,
f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\supp(f_{i})}c_{i}(\boldsymbol{a})\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
. An Euler–Mellin integral is defined
as

I(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{t}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}{x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\nu_{n}}\over\prod_{i=1}^{q}(f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{t_{i}}}\,\prod_{j=1}^{n}{\mathrm{d}x_{j}\over x_{j}}.

(36)

Definition 3.2

(Non-vanishing polynomial)
.

If
F
is a face of the Newton polytope

\Delta(f)
of
f
, then the truncated polynomial with
support
F
is given by
f_{F}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in F\cap\supp(f)}c(\boldsymbol{a})\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
. The polynomial
f
is said to be completely
non-vanishing on a set
X
if for each face
F
of

\Delta(f)
, the truncated polynomial
f_{F}
has no zeros
on
X
. In particular, the polynomial
f
itself does not
vanish on
X
.

Theorem 3.1

(Theorem 2.2 of ref.
2011arXiv1103.6273B
)
.

If each of the polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{q}
is completely non-vanishing on the positive
orthant
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, then the Euler–Mellin integral
I(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{t})
of
eq.

36
converges and
defines an analytic function in the tube domain

\left\{(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{t})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+q}\,\middle|\,\operatorname{Re}(\boldsymbol{t})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{q},\,\operatorname{Re}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\in{\mathrm{int}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}\operatorname{Re}(t_{i})\Delta(f_{i})\right)\right\}.

(37)

We shall apply this theorem to the parametric representation in

eq.

with
\boldsymbol{t}=(n_{\mathscr{U}},n_{\mathscr{F}})
and the polytope

in

for

3.3
Counting the number of interior points

The Ehrhart polynomial
Haase:2012
;
Beck:2015
is a fundamental tool in discrete geometry for enumerating the
lattice points contained in integer dilations of a convex polytope.
For a lattice polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Ehrhart polynomial
\ehr_{P}(t)

counts the number of integer lattice points in the dilated polytope
tP
, that is,

\ehr_{P}(t)=\#\big(tP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\big)\,.

(51)

The number of interior lattice points is obtained from Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity,

\ehr_{P}(-t)=(-1)^{\dim P}\nint(tP)\,,

(52)

where

\nint(Q):=\#\big(\mathrm{int}(Q)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\big)

(53)

is the number of interior points of a polytope
Q
.
In particular,
\ehr_{P}(1)
and
\lvert\ehr_{P}(-1)\rvert
give the total number of lattice and interior lattice points in
P
, respectively.
Thus, the Ehrhart polynomial encodes both the boundary and interior
point structure of the polytope. We will use this in
section

6.1
in order to put an upper
bound on the spacetime dimensions of Feynman integrals when searching for reflexive and Fano polytopes. The knowledge of the Ehrhart polynomial gives an efficient way to identify polytope dilations containing a single interior point.

The polytopes associated to a Feynman integral in

eqs.

28
and
29
are the Minkowski sum of
the scaling of two lattice polytopes:

P
, which is
either
\Delta(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{N})=\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}
for the massive case or
\Delta(\mathscr{V}_{\Gamma})
for the massless case, and
Q=\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma})
.
The number of lattice points depends on the scaling coefficients of both polytopes.
To capture this dependence, one introduces the bivariate Ehrhart
polynomial
\ehr_{P,Q}(t_{1},t_{2})
, defined by (see, e.g.
(
Gruber2007
, Chap. 19.1)
)

\ehr_{P,Q}(t_{1},t_{2})=\#\big((t_{1}P+t_{2}Q)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}\big)\,,

(54)

which counts the lattice points in the Minkowski sum of independently
dilated copies of
P
and
Q
. The bivariate Ehrhart polynomial also satisfies a reciprocity relation:

\ehr_{P,Q}(-t_{1},-t_{2})=(-1)^{n}\,\nint(t_{1}P+t_{2}Q)\,.

(55)

We will use it to identify cases when Newton polytopes associated to Feynman graphs contain a single interior point.

In general, determination of the bivariate
Ehrhart polynomial is a difficult problem. The
Bernstein–McMullen’s theorem
(
Gruber2007
, Theorem 19.4)
implies that
\ehr_{P,Q}(t_{1},t_{2})
is a polynomial of total degree
n
:

\ehr_{P,Q}(t_{1},t_{2})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-i}c_{ij}\,t_{1}^{i}t_{2}^{j},

(56)

with rational coefficients
c_{ij}
. One can use this polynomial
form to determine the bivariate Ehrhart polynomial by computing the number of lattice points for various dilations
t_{1}P+t_{2}Q
using
polymake

polymake:FPSAC_2009
and fitting the coefficients
c_{ij}
.
We list a few properties that help to compute the Ehrhart polynomial
Beck:2015
;
Haase:2017
;
BrandenburgEtAl:2020
:

•

The leading homogeneous part (degree
n
) corresponds to the mixed volumes:

\mathrm{Vol}(t_{1}P+t_{2}Q)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}V_{i}(P,Q)\,t_{1}^{i}t_{2}^{n-i},

(57)

with
V_{i}(P,Q)
the mixed volumes of
i
copies of
P
and
n-i
copies of
Q
, so that
V_{0}(P,Q)=V(Q)
,
V_{n}(P,Q)=V(P)
and
V_{i}(P,P)=V(P)
.

•

Setting one of the variables to zero gives the corresponding univariate Ehrhart polynomial:

\ehr_{P,Q}(t_{1},0)=\ehr_{P}(t_{1}),\qquad\ehr_{P,Q}(0,t_{2})=\ehr_{Q}(t_{2}).

(58)

4
Fano and reflexive polytopes from Feynman integrals

We introduce the Fano and reflexive polytopes that we use in this work.
We refer to

Definition 4.1

(Fano polytope
Nill2005
;
Kasprzyk12
)
.

A lattice polytope is (canonical) Fano if the only lattice point that lies strictly
in its interior is the origin.

Let
N
be
the dual lattice to
M
with respect to the scalar
product
\langle\bullet|\bullet\rangle:M\times N\to\mathbb{Z}
.
Using this scalar product we can define the dual (or polar) polytope

\nabla:=\left\{\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in N\,\middle|\,\langle a,b\rangle\geq-1,\,\forall\boldsymbol{a}\in\Delta\right\}\subset N\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,.

(59)

Definition 4.2

(Reflexive polytope)
.

A lattice polytope
\Delta
is reflexive if its polar
polytope
\nabla
is a lattice polytope and has a single interior
point. The polytopes
(\Delta,\nabla)
are said to be
mirror pairs.

We make a few remarks:

•

Newton polytopes of Laurent polynomials, defined in
eq.

20
, are integer lattice polytopes, i.e. they are included in
some lattice
M
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Reciprocally, to a given integer lattice
polytope
\Delta
one associates a Laurent polynomial

f_{\Delta}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\Delta}c_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, with a similar definition for the dual polytope
\nabla
. This will be used in
section

8
when discussing the relation between polytopes from Feynman integrals and smooth Calabi–Yau varieties.

•

The integrand of the
Feynman integral (
15
) is the inverse of the
Laurent polynomial

f_{\Delta}=\frac{\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}^{{D\over 2}-n_{\mathscr{F}}}\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}^{n_{\mathscr{F}}}}{x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{\nu_{N}}}\,.

(60)

When there is a single interior point
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{N})
, the
presence of the monomial in the denominator
(or the numerator of the Feynman integral) shifts the polytope so
that the interior point becomes the origin.

•

Every reflexive polytope is Fano, but there are Fano polytopes that are
not reflexive. In
sections

5
and
6
we list quasi-finite Feynman integrals whose associated polytopes are Fano, but not reflexive, for graphs up to ten edges.

Together with the conditions in
eq.

38
, we are now imposing the condition that the Newton polytope has only one interior point, and therefore it is Fano.
Among such polytopes, we then determine whether the polytope is reflexive.

(70)

where
\textrm{coeff}_{x^{r}}(f(x))
means the coefficient of

x^{r}
in the series expansion of
f(x)
around
x=0
.
We list the Ehrhart polynomials
\ehr_{\circleddash}(n_{\mathscr{F}},D/2,N)
for up to
N=10
edges in
table

.

Using the reciprocity formula for the Ehrhart polynomial in

68
we identify the polytopes with a single interior point for generic number of edges:

•

D=2
with
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(1,\dots,1)
: in this case

and
n_{\mathscr{F}}=1
, and the polytope is

\Delta_{\circleddash}(N-1,2;(1,\dots,1))=\Delta(\mathscr{U}^{N}_{\circleddash})+\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}\,.

(71)

•

D=2N
with
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(N-1,\dots,N-1)
:
in this case
n_{\mathscr{U}}=N
and
n_{\mathscr{F}}=0
, and the polytope reads

\Delta_{\circleddash}(N-1,2N;(N-1,\dots,N-1))=N\Delta(\mathscr{U}^{N}_{\circleddash})=N\left(\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}\right)\,.

(72)

Both polytopes are known to be reflexive. The polytope of
eq.

71
is the one of the
A_{N-1}
root lattice (see
eq.

200
) that
has been studied in detail in ref.

section

for a discussion of the properties of this polytope and
section

8.1
for a relation to Calabi–Yau geometry. The polytope in

(N,\dots,N)
.

As a side remark, we notice that the above polytopes are saturated, because they are Newton polytopes of
determinants
Cara:2019
.

5
Search by integral dimension and loop count

Feynman integrals are characterized by the number of
loops
L
, the number of internal edges
N
of
the graph and the dimension
D
where the integral is
evaluated. These enter into the definition of the
Newton polytope given in
eq.

26
. The
exploration of reflexivity can be done by fixing any
of these parameters.

A common approach in physics is going up in the number of
loops, increasing precision of theoretical predictions.
Another possibility is to move up in dimension
D
.
Fixing either of these parameters to their lowest possible values,
D=2
and
L=1
, we can fully classify the resulting Fano and reflexive polytopes.
We do this in
section

5.1
and
section

5.2
, respectively.
In
section

5.3
we consider the “next-to-simplest” case by setting
D=4
and
L=2
simultaneously.

In the mathematics literature the natural parameter to fix is
N
, which corresponds to fixing the dimension of the space where the polytope lives.
This was the strategy followed in ref.
Kreuzer:2000xy
to classify reflexive
polytopes living in four dimensions, which corresponds
to graphs with up to
N=5
edges.
We follow this approach in
section

6
, performing an exhaustive scan of Feynman integrals with up to
N=10
edges.

5.1
D=2
,
L=\mathrm{any}
: integrals in two dimensions

In
D=2
dimensions, the conditions in
eq.

38

give two
solutions, namely
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu_{i}=L+n_{\mathscr{F}}
with
n_{\mathscr{F}}=0,1
. Since
\nu_{i}\geq 1
,
and the Euler characteristic of the graph gives
V-N+L=1
, where

V\geq 1
is the number of vertices, we deduce that

L+n_{\mathscr{F}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nu_{i}\geq N\geq L,\qquad n_{\mathscr{F}}=0,1\,.

(73)

•

For
n_{\mathscr{F}}=0
, the only possibility is
N=L
,
V=1
, and
\nu_{i}=1
for
1\leq i\leq N
.
This case corresponds to a factorizable
N
-bouquet Feynman graph, that is, a product of
N
tadpoles. The Symanzik polynomials are

\mathscr{U}_{N\mathrm{-bouquet}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{N},\quad\mathscr{F}_{N\mathrm{-bouquet}}=x_{1}\cdots x_{N}\left(m_{1}^{2}x_{1}+\cdots+m_{N}^{2}x_{N}\right).

(74)

The Newton polytope
\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{N\mathrm{-bouquet}})
is a single point and thus does not have any interior points.
Physically, this is due to the fact that tadpole integrals diverge in
D=2
.

•

For
n_{\mathscr{F}}=1
, there are two options.
The first is
N=L
,
V=1
,
\nu_{j}=2
for some
j
, and
\nu_{i}=1
for
1\leq i\leq N,i\neq j
.
This is again the case of an
N
-bouquet, but now the Newton polytope is
\Delta(\mathscr{F}_{N\mathrm{-bouquet}})
.
The point
(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{N})
does not lie in the interior, hence the integral is again divergent and the associated polytope is not Fano.
The second option is
N=L+1
,
V=2
, and
\nu_{i}=1
for
1\leq i\leq N
.
This corresponds to the
L
-loop sunset integral which has been discussed in
section

4.1
.
We conclude that this is the only class of Feynman graphs that leads to Fano and reflexive polytopes in
D=2
.

5.2
D=\mathrm{any}
,
L=1
: one-loop integrals

The one-loop
N
-gon integrals have exponents

n_{\mathscr{U}}=-(\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N})+D,\quad n_{\mathscr{F}}=(\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N})-{D\over 2}

(75)

with the integrals taking the form

I_{N-\rm gon}(1,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N-1}}\left.{\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{\nu_{i}}\over\mathscr{U}_{N-\rm gon}^{{D\over 2}-n_{\mathscr{F}}}\mathscr{F}_{N-\rm gon}^{n_{\mathscr{F}}}}\right|_{x_{N}=1}\prod_{i=1}^{N-1}{\mathrm{d}x_{i}\over x_{i}}\,.

(76)

Their Symanzik polynomials read

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{N-\rm gon}

\displaystyle:=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{N},

(77)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{N-\rm gon}

\displaystyle:=m_{1}^{2}x_{1}+\cdots+m_{N}^{2}x_{N},

\displaystyle\mathscr{V}_{N-\rm gon}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(p_{i}+\cdots+p_{j-1})^{2}x_{i}x_{j},

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{N-\rm gon}

\displaystyle:=\mathscr{U}_{N-\rm gon}\mathscr{L}_{N-\rm gon}-\mathscr{V}_{N-\rm gon}\,,

where we use a cyclic labeling modulo

N
of the massive external momenta, which are
constrained by momentum conservation
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{N}=0
. The convergence conditions imply that

D\geq\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N}\geq{D\over 2}\,,

(78)

together with the condition that the exponents lie in the interior

\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{N})\in\textrm{int}(\Delta_{N-\rm gon}(D,1;\boldsymbol{\nu}))\,.

(79)

5.2.1
Massive case

For the one-loop
N
-gon with massive internal lines the polytope is

\Delta_{N-\rm gon}(1,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=n_{\mathscr{U}}\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{N-\rm gon})+n_{\mathscr{F}}\Delta(\mathscr{F}_{N-\rm gon})=(\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N})\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)},

(80)

where we used
eq.

27
and the relation

\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{N-\rm gon})=\Delta(\mathscr{L}_{N-\rm gon})=\Delta(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{N})=\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}\,,

(81)

and that
n_{\mathscr{U}}+2n_{\mathscr{F}}=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N}
.
Therefore, the polytope for the one-loop graph with massive internal lines is the scaled standard simplex defined in
eq.

22
. For this polytope it is immediate to compute the
Ehrhart polynomial
Beck:2015
because it is a scaled standard simplex:

\ehr_{\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}}(t)=\prod_{r=1}^{n-1}{t+r\over r}\,.

(82)

The number of interior points can be computed using the Ehrhart
reciprocity formula in
eq.

52
:

\nint(k\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)})=(-1)^{N-1}\ehr_{\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}}(-k)=\prod_{r=1}^{n-1}{k-r\over r}\,.

(83)

Therefore the polytope
\Delta_{N-\rm gon}(1,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})

has only one interior point for

k=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N}=N
. Because
\nu_{i}\geq 1
for all

i=1,\dots,N
, the interior point is

\boldsymbol{\nu}=(1,\dots,1)
. This condition does not depend on the
spacetime dimension
D
, and the positivity condition in

eq.

78
implies that the polytope associated to the one-loop
integral

I_{N-\rm gon}(1,D;(1,\dots,1))=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N-1}}\left.{1\over\mathscr{U}_{N-\rm gon}^{D-N}\mathscr{F}_{N-\rm gon}^{N-{D\over 2}}}\right|_{x_{N}=1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{N-1}

(84)

is
reflexive in
N\leq D\leq 2N
dimensions. This corresponds to the vertical line at
L=1
in

fig.

12(b)
.

5.2.2
Massless case

In the massless case the polytope is

\Delta_{N-\rm gon;0}(1,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=n_{\mathscr{U}}\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{N-\rm gon})+n_{\mathscr{F}}\Delta(\mathscr{V}_{N-\rm gon})\,.

(85)

The polytope associated with the
\mathscr{V}_{N-\rm gon}
graph
polynomial is the standard second hypersimplex defined in
eq.

24
,

\Delta(\mathscr{V}_{N-\rm gon})=\Delta\left(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}x_{i}x_{j}\right)=\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,2)}\,.

(86)

The polytope for the one-loop
N
-gon with massless internal lines is the scaled Minkowski sum of two simplices,

\Delta_{N-\rm gon;0}(1,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=n_{\mathscr{U}}\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}+n_{\mathscr{F}}\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,2)}\,.

(87)

The number of interior points of the polytope in

eq.

87
can be computed using the bivariate
Ehrhart polynomial associated to the Minkowski sum of the polytopes

\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{N-\rm gon})=\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}
and
\Delta(\mathscr{V}_{N-\rm gon})=\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,2)}
. The bivariate Ehrhart polytope of the Minkowski sum of the standard simplex
and the second hypersimplex reads (see, e.g.
(
Beck:2015
, Chapter 9)
)

\ehr(t_{1},t_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}E_{ij}t_{1}^{i}t_{2}^{j}\,,

(88)

where
E_{ij}
are the mixed Ehrhart coefficients. Although each polytope involved in the Minkowski sum is standard, to the best of our knowledge the bivariate Ehrhart polynomial that we need is unknown.
The expression for the bivariate Ehrhart polynomial is

\ehr_{\Delta_{N-\rm gon;0}}(t_{1},t_{2})={1\over(N-1)!}\prod_{r=0}^{N-2}(t_{1}+2t_{2}+r+1)-{N\over(N-1)!}\prod_{r=0}^{N-2}(t_{2}+r)\,,

(89)

which we derive in
appendix

B
. The number of interior points is evaluated as in the single variable
case. Using the binomial notation it reads

\displaystyle\nint(\Delta_{N-\rm gon;0}(1,D;\boldsymbol{\nu}))

\displaystyle=(-1)^{N-1}\ehr_{\Delta_{N-\rm gon;0}}(-n_{\mathscr{U}},-n_{\mathscr{F}})

(90)

\displaystyle=\binom{n_{\mathscr{U}}+2n_{\mathscr{F}}-1}{N-1}-N\binom{n_{\mathscr{F}}}{N-1}\,.

There is a single interior point
\nint(\Delta_{N-\rm gon;0}(1,D;\boldsymbol{\nu}))=1

for

\displaystyle(n_{\mathscr{U}},n_{\mathscr{F}})

\displaystyle=(3,0),(1,1),(0,3)\qquad N=3,

(91)

\displaystyle(n_{\mathscr{U}},n_{\mathscr{F}})

\displaystyle=(2m-N,N-m)\qquad\left\lceil N\over 2\right\rceil\leq m\leq N,N\geq 4\,,

(92)

so the corresponding polytopes are Fano.
The case

(i.e.
m=N
) is identical to the massive one-loop
case of the previous

6.1
Upper bound on dimension

We have just seen that fixing the number of edges
N
puts an upper bound on the number of loops
L
.
We will show now that the remaining parameter in our search—spacetime dimension
D
—is also bounded.
This means that for a fixed number of edges
N
, we can perform an exhaustive search for Fano and reflexive polytopes, as we only need to consider a finite number of cases.

To establish an upper bound on
D
, we will make use of the following

Proposition 6.1

(cf.
(
Beck:2015
, Exercise 4.10)
)
.

Let
P
be a
d
-dimensional polytope,
d\geq 1
. Then the number of interior lattice points of its dilations satisfies

\displaystyle\nint((d+1)P)

\displaystyle\geq 1,

(128)

\displaystyle\nint((d+2)P)

\displaystyle>1.

Proof.

This is a straightforward application of Ehrhart theory (see, e.g.,
(
Beck:2015
, Chapters 3 and 4)
). The Ehrhart polynomial of
P
can be written in terms of the so-called
h^{*}
-vector as

\ehr_{P}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{*}\binom{t+d-i}{d},

(129)

where
h_{0}^{*}=1
and
h_{i}^{*}\geq 0
. Combining
eq.

129
with the reciprocity formula (
52
), we find

\nint(kP)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{*}\binom{k+i-1}{d}\geq\binom{k-1}{d}.

(130)

Taking
k=d+1
and
k=d+2
, we obtain the desired bound.
∎

The Newton polytope
\Delta_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})
lives in
(N-1)
dimensions, so the above proposition implies that it cannot be Fano if it contains an
(N+1)
-fold dilation of some polytope.
This means that in the representation of
\Delta_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})
as a weighted Minkowski sum we cannot have coefficients exceeding
N
.

In the case of non-vanishing internal masses, we can write

\Delta_{\Gamma}(L,D;\boldsymbol{\nu})=D/2\,\Delta(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma})+n_{\mathscr{F}}\Delta(\mathscr{L}_{\Gamma})\,,

(131)

so that
D/2,n_{\mathscr{F}}\leq N
.
With more general kinematics, we have
n_{\mathscr{U}},n_{\mathscr{F}}\leq N
, and in particular,
D/2=n_{\mathscr{U}}+n_{\mathscr{F}}\leq 2N
.

6.2
Symmetries

Graphs may share the same Symanzik polynomials and thus correspond to the same Feynman integral. The determination of graphs that share the same Symanzik polynomials is a basic problem in Feynman integral calculations. Given a set of graphs, the isomorphism problem can be solved by choosing a canonical labeling and comparing the relabeled graphs. The isomorphism between two Newton polytopes can be checked similarly by constructing a graph known as a 1-skeleton, choosing a canonical ordering of the graph vertices, and comparing the relabeled polytopes
Bremner_2014
. Another alternative is to construct a normal form for the vertices of a polytope
grinis2013normalformsconvexlattice
. For our purposes, it is not necessary to construct all polytope symmetries of a given integral
delaCruz:2024ssb
, but only to determine a representative of a given set of integrals sharing the same polytope.

A strategy based on normal ordering of vertices is used ordinarily in IBP-reduction packages. It is known as
Pak algorithm
in this context
Pak:2011xt
. We can compare graphs by ordering variables of the graph polynomial pairs

P_{\Gamma}=(\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma},\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma})
. Two graphs
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
are equivalent if their polynomial pairs are equal under their normal orderings

\text{Normal}(P_{\Gamma_{1}})=\text{Normal}(P_{\Gamma_{2}})\,.

(132)

We use Pak algorithm to identify polynomials that have the same monomials (but not necessarily having the same coefficients) as they will correspond to the same polytope. For instance, the graphs in
fig.

10
share the same polytope, because they have the same vertices, despite the fact that the graphs do not have the same number of external legs.
As a result, we can group graphs into equivalence classes and choose a representative of each class.

8
Calabi–Yau from reflexive polytopes of Feynman integrals

In this
section
, we elaborate on how Calabi–Yau varieties naturally arise from Feynman integrals when their Symanzik graph polynomials are interpreted through toric geometry.

In the toric framework, mirror symmetry emerges directly from the geometry of the Newton polytope. A reflexive polytope
\Delta
determines a Gorenstein toric Fano variety
\mathbb{P}_{\Delta}
, while its dual
\nabla
determines the mirror partner
\mathbb{P}_{\nabla}
, see e.g., ref.
Kasprzyk_2022
. The anticanonical hypersurfaces in these varieties form a mirror pair of Calabi–Yau manifolds in Batyrev’s sense
Batyrev:1993oya
. When the Symanzik polynomials of a Feynman graph define a reflexive polytope, the graph integral naturally embeds in this mirror pair geometry.

Products of powers of the first and second Symanzik polynomials define hypersurfaces in projective space, whose Newton polytopes (from scaled Minkowski sums) can be reflexive or Fano. When reflexive, the associated toric variety admits a Calabi–Yau hypersurface in its anticanonical linear system. The geometry of vanishing locus of the product of the powers of the Symanzik polynomials determines a degeneration of a del Pezzo surface,
K3
surface, or higher-dimensional Calabi–Yau variety.

This connection bridges quantum field theory and mirror symmetry. The Feynman integral computes a period of the graph hypersurface, while the reflexive polytope determines dual toric Fano varieties whose anticanonical hypersurfaces form a Batyrev mirror pair
Batyrev:1993oya
. Each Feynman graph with a reflexive Newton polytope, therefore, provides access to periods on both sides of a mirror pair. The integral’s analytic behavior—rational, dilogarithmic, elliptic, or Calabi–Yau type—corresponds to the dimension and degeneration type of the underlying toric hypersurface.
We discuss the cases of the multiloop sunset and multileg one-loop graphs and their toric Calabi–Yau structures.
For one-loop graphs, the explicit computations of the

\mathscr{U}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathscr{F}_{\Gamma}
polynomials are particularly transparent. Their Newton
polytopes are low-dimensional reflexive polytopes, and the corresponding
graph hypersurfaces may be interpreted as anticanonical hypersurfaces in
toric del Pezzo surfaces, quartic
K3
surfaces, or (in the case of the
pentagon) degenerations of the quintic Calabi–Yau threefold. These
examples illustrate how the analytic structure of the integral matches
the geometry of the toric Calab–Yau.

8.8
Geometry of reflexive Feynman integrals

Focusing on the combinatorics and geometry of graph hypersurfaces, the examples above demonstrate that multiloop sunset and one-loop Feynman integrals admit a natural interpretation in terms of smooth anticanonical Calabi–Yau varieties arising from reflexive polytopes via toric methods.
They illustrate a general phenomenon: even when the graph
hypersurface is singular, the reflexive polytope underlying the Symanzik
polynomials still determines a Calabi–Yau structure via Batyrev’s
mirror symmetry. The singularities of the hypersurface correspond to
physical boundary phenomena such as threshold singularities (the
discriminant vanishes at the location of threshold, see e.g. this
connection for two-loop graphs
Doran:2023yzu
), and mirror
symmetry provides a geometric language to interpret these analytic
features.
It could be interesting to further study the fibration structure on the mirror side and the connection with torically induced Tyurin degenerations studied by Doran, Harder, and Thompson
Doran:2016uea
.

This perspective provides a unified framework in which the analytic structures of Feynman integrals—whether rational, polylogarithmic, elliptic, or of Calabi–Yau type—can be understood directly from the combinatorics of the Newton polytope. In this way, mirror symmetry emerges not as an exotic external structure imposed from outside, but as an intrinsic geometric feature of perturbative quantum field theory.

9
Conclusions

In this work, we have analyzed Fano and reflexive polytopes arising as Newton polytopes of finite and quasi-finite Feynman integrals when the polytope has a single interior point.
We have found that the number of Fano and reflexive polytopes
is very small, which is a remarkable fact considering that the number of three- and four-dimensional reflexive polytopes is huge
Kreuzer:2000xy
. We found that in generic kinematics the space of Fano and reflexive polytopes exhibits various families of Feynman graphs including, the well-known multiloop sunset, the
N
-gon, among others.

Fano and reflexive polytopes are naturally associated with Calabi–Yau varieties, and
our analysis shows that a subset of (quasi-)finite Feynman integrals are
naturally Calabi–Yau period integrals. Their appearance in this context may provide a geometric organizing principle for the space of such Feynman integrals, allowing one to associate to each integral a toric Calabi–Yau variety or Fano variety that captures its singularity and period structure.
From a mathematical viewpoint, identifying the toric geometry associated with a Feynman graph offers a pathway to understanding the Hodge-theoretic
Bloch:2013tra
;
Bloch:2014qca
;
Bloch:2016izu
;
Doran:2023yzu
, motivic, and combinatorial structures underlying amplitudes
Brown:2015fyf
;
Schnetz:2016fhy
;
Panzer:2018tiv
and the role of mirror symmetry in evaluating Feynman integrals
Bloch:2014qca
;
Bloch:2016izu
.

In multiloop calculations, the standard approach relies on
large families of master integrals, many of which possess overlapping
ultraviolet and infrared divergences and require elaborate subtraction procedures or
dimensional regularization expansions before their finite
contributions can be extracted.
A promising direction for improving the efficiency of amplitude
computations is the systematic identification of quasi-finite Feynman
integrals. The reason is that finite integrals are easier to evaluate numerically and, in addition to standard techniques, novel sampling methods are applicable to them
Borinsky:2023jdv
;
Borinsky:2020rqs
. Our examples show that some of them are also easy to evaluate analytically.

Finally, our analysis gives a systematic way of identifying a rather small number of quasi-finite master integrals that share a common geometrical property: having an associated Fano variety.
This might offer another avenue to constructing an integral basis for amplitudes based on algebraic geometry, which would make the extraction of the finite part of the amplitude more direct.

Appendix B
Bivariate Ehrhart polynomial for massless one-loop integrals

In this
appendix
, we give a proof of the formula for the Ehrhart
polynomial in
eq.

89
.

Let
N\geq 2
be an integer.
For integers
t_{1},t_{2}\geq 0
, consider the Minkowski sum of the standard simplex in
eq.

22
and the second hypersimplex in
eq.

24
,

\Delta(t_{1},t_{2}):=t_{1}\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,1)}+t_{2}\Delta_{\text{HS}}^{(N,2)}.

(183)

We will describe
\Delta(t_{1},t_{2})
explicitly and derive its Ehrhart polynomial

\ehr(t_{1},t_{2})=\#(\Delta(t_{1},t_{2})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N})
which counts the number of lattice points of
\Delta(t_{1},t_{2})
.

For any matroids (or polymatroids) with rank functions
r_{1},r_{2}
,
the Minkowski sum of their base polytopes is the base polytope of the
rank function
r=r_{1}+r_{2}
.
Hence

\Delta(t_{1},t_{2})=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}\,\middle|\,\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\leq r(S)\ \text{for all }S\subseteq[N],\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}=r([N])\right\}

(184)

is the base polytope of rank

r(S)=t_{1}\min(|S|,1)+t_{2}\min(|S|,2),\qquad S\subseteq[N],

(185)

which evaluates to

r(S)=\begin{cases}0,&|S|=0,\\[4.0pt]
t_{1}+t_{2},&|S|=1,\\[4.0pt]
t_{1}+2t_{2},&|S|\geq 2.\end{cases}

(186)

Therefore only the singleton inequalities are non-trivial, and
we obtain the simple description

\Delta(t_{1},t_{2})=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}\,\middle|\,\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}=t_{1}+2t_{2},\;x_{i}\leq t_{1}+t_{2}\ \text{for all }i\right\}.

(187)

Thus the Minkowski sum depends only on the total sum
t_{1}+2t_{2}

and the per-coordinate cap
t_{1}+t_{2}
.
The lattice points of
\Delta(t_{1},t_{2})
are the integer solutions of

x_{1}+\cdots+x_{N}=t_{1}+2t_{2},\qquad 0\leq x_{i}\leq t_{1}+t_{2}.

(188)

Without the upper bounds, the number of non-negative integer solutions
is given by the classical stars-and-bars formula

\binom{t_{1}+2t_{2}+N-1}{N-1}.

(189)

To impose the upper bounds
x_{i}\leq t_{1}+t_{2}
, we apply inclusion–exclusion.
For each subset
J\subseteq[N]
of size
j
,
consider the solutions with
x_{i}\geq t_{1}+t_{2}+1
for all
i\in J
.
Setting
x_{i}=y_{i}+(t_{1}+t_{2}+1)
for
i\in J
,
we obtain the equation

\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}=t_{1}+2t_{2}-j(t_{1}+t_{2}+1),

(190)

whose non-negative solutions number is

\binom{t_{1}+2t_{2}-j(t_{1}+t_{2}+1)+N-1}{N-1}.

(191)

Using inclusion–exclusion, the total count is therefore

\ehr(t_{1},t_{2})=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor(t_{1}+2t_{2})/(t_{1}+t_{2}+1)\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\binom{N}{j}\binom{t_{1}+2t_{2}-j(t_{1}+t_{2}+1)+N-1}{N-1}.

(192)

The upper limit of the sum arises because the binomial coefficient
vanishes when
t_{1}+2t_{2}-j(t_{1}+t_{2}+1)<0
.

For the case of the Minkowski sum arising from the massless one-loop
graph in
eq.

87
we have

t_{1}+2t_{2}=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{N}
and
t_{1}+t_{2}=D/2
, implying that
t_{1}+2t_{2}<2(t_{1}+t_{2}+1)
because
t_{1}\geq 0
.
Therefore only the terms
j=0,1
contribute to the sum in

eq.

192
, so that

\ehr(t_{1},t_{2})=\binom{t_{1}+2t_{2}+N-1}{N-1}-N\binom{t_{2}+N-2}{N-1}.

(193)
