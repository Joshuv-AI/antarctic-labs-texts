Abstract
. This note is the natural continuation of what was started in [ 8 ] i.e. the extension to fully nonlinear operators of the well known result on thin domains of Hale and Raugel [ 13 ] . Here we consider oblique boundary condition, and find some new phenomena, in particular the limit equations contain ”new terms” in the second, first and zeroth order terms which don’t have an equivalent in the Neumann case.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
be a cylindrical domain, which is thin in the direction of the axe i.e.

\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\,:\,\varepsilon g^{-}(x)<y<\varepsilon g^{+}(x)\}

with
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
a
bounded open domain,
g^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})
, and
g^{-}(x)<g^{+}(x)
.
We think of
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, as divided in three parts the top, the bottom and the lateral part:

\begin{gathered}\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y)\,:\,x\in\overline{\Omega},y=\varepsilon g^{+}(x)\},\qquad\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y)\,:\,x\in\overline{\Omega},y=\varepsilon g^{-}(x)\},\quad\\
\partial_{\mathrm{L}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}=\{(x,y)\,:\,x\in\partial\Omega,\varepsilon g^{-}(x)\leq y\leq\varepsilon g^{+}(x)\}.\end{gathered}

We consider the elliptic PDE problem with oblique boundary conditions on the top and bottom of the domain:

(1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} F(D^{2}u^{\varepsilon},Du^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon},x,y)=0\ \text{ in }\Omega_{\varepsilon},&\\
\gamma^{+}\cdot Du^{\varepsilon}=\beta^{+}\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon},&\ \ \ \gamma^{-}\cdot Du^{\varepsilon}=\beta^{-}\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon},\\
B(Du^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon},x)=0\text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{L}}\Omega_{\varepsilon},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\gamma^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1],\mathbb{R}^{N+1})
and
\beta^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1],\mathbb{R})
.

Our purpose in this paper is to investigate the asymptotic behaviour of
u^{\varepsilon}
when the positive parameter
\varepsilon
is sent to zero. In particular we want to determine the equation satisfied by the limit function in
\Omega
. This paper is the natural continuation of
[
8
]
. In that work we had generalized to a non variational setting the famous result of Hale and Raugel
[
13
]
using the so called test function approach à la Evans. We shall see here that, choosing the oblique boundary condition, completely changes the limit equation even in the case of the Laplacian, introducing new first and second order terms.

On the other hand, the choice of the boundary condition on the lateral boundary

\partial_{\mathrm{L}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}
does not play any crucial role. Hence we shall prove the results with either Neumann, or oblique or Dirichlet boundary condition on the lateral boundary i.e. either
B(Du^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon},x):=\gamma\cdot Du^{\varepsilon}-\beta(x)
or
B(u^{\varepsilon}):=u^{\varepsilon}-\beta(x)
. For the clarity of the discussion, in the introduction and in the formal expansion of the next section, we concentrate mainly on the standard Neumann condition:

(2)

B(Du^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon},x):=\nu\cdot D_{x}u^{\varepsilon}=0\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{L}}\Omega_{\varepsilon},

where
\nu=\nu(x)
denotes the outward unit normal vector at
x\in\partial\Omega
. Notice that

\nu_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=(\nu(x),0)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
is the outward unit normal vector at
(x,y)\in\partial_{\mathrm{L}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

Regarding the obliqueness of
\gamma^{\pm}
, we assume that, when we write
\gamma^{\pm}=(\gamma_{1}^{\pm},\gamma_{2}^{\pm})
, with

\gamma_{1}^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1],\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\gamma_{2}^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1],\mathbb{R})
,

(3)

\pm\gamma_{2}^{\pm}>0\ \ \text{ on }\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1].

Hence, without loss of generality, we can suppose that

(4)

\gamma_{2}^{+}=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma_{2}^{-}=-1.

So we will consider the oblique boundary condition

(5)

\gamma_{1}^{+}\cdot D_{x}u^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}_{y}=\beta^{+}\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma_{1}^{-}\cdot D_{x}u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\varepsilon}_{y}=\beta^{-}\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}.

If
g^{\pm}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})
,
\nu_{\varepsilon}
denotes the outward unit normal vector
on
\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cup\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, and
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small, then
\nu_{\varepsilon}\cdot(\gamma_{1}^{+},1)>0
on
\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}

and
\nu_{\varepsilon}\cdot(\gamma_{1}^{-},-1)>0
on
\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}
.

To present the results, we need some hypotheses and notation. As basic hypotheses, we assume
throughout this paper the following (H1) and (H2):

(H1)

F\in C(\mathcal{S}(N+1)\times\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\times\mathbb{R}\times\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1],\mathbb{R})
, and for every
(p,x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\times\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1]
and some constant
\alpha>0
, if
X,Y\in\mathcal{S}(N+1)
,
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
,
X\geq Y
in the semi-definite sense, and
r\leq s
then
F(X,p,r,x,y)-\alpha r\leq F(Y,p,s,x,y)-\alpha s
.

(H2)

The set
\Omega
is a bounded, open domain with
C^{1}
boundary,

\gamma^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1],\mathbb{R}^{N+1})
, and
\beta^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1],\mathbb{R})
.

Here and below,
\mathcal{S}(k)
denotes the space of
k\times k

real symmetric matrices.

We always assume that

(6)

g^{\pm}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}),\quad g^{-}(x)<g^{+}(x)\ \ \text{ for }x\in\overline{\Omega}.

Since we are interested in the asymptotic behaviour, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, of the solutions to the boundary value problem (
1
), we may focus our
considerations only on small
\varepsilon
, say
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, which allows us to assume always that

(7)

\left\{\,\begin{aligned} &\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\subset\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1],\\
&\nu_{\varepsilon}\cdot(\gamma_{1}^{+},1)>0\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon},\\
&\nu_{\varepsilon}\cdot(\gamma_{1}^{-},-1)>0\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}.\end{aligned}\right.

A crucial assumption on
\gamma^{\pm}
and
\beta^{\pm}
is the following:

(8)

\beta^{+}(x,0)=-\beta^{-}(x,0)=:\beta_{o}(x)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \gamma_{1}^{+}(x,0)=-\gamma_{1}^{-}(x,0)=:\gamma_{o}(x)\ \text{ for }x\in\overline{\Omega}.

The necessity of this assumption will be evident in Section 2 where we perform a formal expansion for the clarity of the argument.

We assume that

(9)

\beta_{o}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \gamma_{o}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N}).

In this paper, our notation that
f\in C^{i}(K,\mathbb{R}^{k})
for a compact subset
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{j}
indicates
that
f\,:\,K\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the restriction of a function
g\in C^{i}(\mathbb{R}^{j},\mathbb{R}^{k})
to the set
K
.

We need to assume that for some
k^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
l^{\pm}\in C(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})
,

(10)

\left\{\,\begin{aligned} &\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,y)=\pm\gamma_{o}(x)+k^{\pm}(x)y+o(|y|)\\
&\beta^{\pm}(x,y)=\pm\beta_{o}(x)+l^{\pm}(x)y+o(|y|),\end{aligned}\right.

as
y\to 0
, where
o(|y|)/|y|\to 0
uniformly on
\overline{\Omega}
as
y\to 0
.

Having introduced all the terms in the first order expansion in
y
, we are in a position to write down the limit equation.

To simplify the notations we introduce the terms
b\in C(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N}),\,c\in C(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R})
:

(11)

\displaystyle b(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{o}(D\gamma_{o})^{\mathrm{T}}-\frac{1}{g^{+}-g^{-}}\big(g^{+}k^{+}+g^{-}k^{-}\big),

(12)

\displaystyle c(x)

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{o}\cdot D\beta_{o}+\frac{1}{g^{+}-g^{-}}\big(g^{+}l^{+}+g^{-}l^{-}\big),

where
D\gamma_{o}(x)
denotes the matrix, whose
(i,j)^{\,\text{th}}
entry is given by
\partial{\gamma_{o}}_{i}(x)/\partial x_{j}

and
D\beta_{o}(x)
denotes the row vector, whose
i^{\,\text{th}}
entry is
\partial\beta_{o}(x)/\partial x_{i}
.
From now on, as in (
11
) and (
12
), in order to simplify notation, when a function
f
is given and no confusion is expected, the value
f(x)
of the function
f
at
x
is often written as
f
.

We define
G\,:\,\mathcal{S}(N)\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}\times\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(13)

G(X,p,r,x)=F(A+B+C,(p,\beta_{o}-\gamma_{o}\cdot p),r,(x,0)),

where

(14)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}X&-X\gamma_{o}(x)^{\mathrm{T}}\\
-\gamma_{o}(x)X&\gamma_{o}(x)X\gamma_{o}(x)^{\mathrm{T}}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&-(pD\gamma_{o}(x))^{\mathrm{T}}\\
-pD\gamma_{o}(x)&b(x)\cdot p\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&D\beta_{o}(x)^{\mathrm{T}}\\
D\beta_{o}(x)&c(x)\end{pmatrix}.

We remark that if
A(X)
denotes the above matrix
A
and
X\geq 0
, then

A(X)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{N}\\
-\gamma_{o}(x)\end{pmatrix}X\begin{pmatrix}I_{N}&-\gamma_{o}(x)^{\mathrm{T}}\end{pmatrix}\geq 0,

which ensures that
G
is degenerate elliptic, i.e., if
X,Y\in\mathcal{S}(N)
and
X\leq Y
, then

G(X,p,r,x)\geq G(Y,p,r,x)
for every
(p,r,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}\times\overline{\Omega}
. In the above and below,

I_{N}
denotes the
N\times N
identity matrix.

The limit problem for (
1
) with (
2
), is the boundary value problem

(15)

\displaystyle G(D^{2}u,Du,u,x)

\displaystyle=0\ \ \text{ in }\Omega,

(16)

\displaystyle\nu\cdot Du

\displaystyle=0\ \ \text{ on }\partial\Omega.

When the lateral condition is either oblique or Dirichlet then the limit equation inherits on
\partial\Omega
the lateral condition.

In the basic case
F(D^{2}u)=-\Delta u
, we get that the limit equation is given by

(17)

{G(D^{2}u,Du,u,x)=-(\Delta u+\gamma_{o}(x)D^{2}u\gamma_{o}(x)^{\mathrm{T}}+b(x)\cdot Du+c(x)u).}

When the boundary data on the top and bottom is not oblique but it is just the Neumann boundary condition, and when
g^{-}(x)=0
as in our previous paper

[
8
]
, the previous quantities
\gamma_{o}
,
\beta_{o}
,
k^{-}
and
l^{\pm}
are zero
while
k^{+}(x)=-\frac{Dg^{+}}{g^{+}}
and hence we recover the limit equation:

F\Big(\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&{\frac{Dg^{+}\cdot Du}{g^{+}(x)}}\end{pmatrix},(Du,0),u,(x,0)\Big)=0\ \text{ in }\Omega,\ \ \ \nu\cdot Du=0\ \ \text{ on }\partial\Omega.

As discussed also in
[
8
]
, the presence of corners in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
requires a little care in the definition of sub and super viscosity solution.
Hence we give a definition of viscosity solution to (
1
), which,
contrary to
[
10
]
, does not require the continuity of the solution.
The precise definition of sub and super viscosity solution up to the boundary is given in Section 3 below but
let us anticipate that we call a bounded function
u
on

\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}
a viscosity solution to (
1
) if
the upper and lower semicontinuous envelopes of
u
are viscosity sub and super solutions
to (
1
) in the sense of
[
10
]
, respectively.
The definition of viscosity solution to (
15
)–(
16
) is made
in the same way.

The first result we want to quote is an existence result for
\varepsilon
fixed small. We shall state it when, on the lateral boundary, the Neumann condition holds:

Proposition 1
.

Assume (H1), (H2),
(
4
),
(
6
), (
8
),
(
9
), and (
10
).
Then,
there exist positive constants
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{0}
and
C_{0}
such that for each
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}
, there is a viscosity solution
u^{\varepsilon}
to (
1
) with (
2
) furthermore any solution
u^{\varepsilon}
will satisfy

\sup_{\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}}|u^{\varepsilon}|\leq C_{0}.

We can now state the convergence theorem.
Let
S_{\varepsilon}
denote the set of viscosity solutions to (
1
) with (
2
) for given

\varepsilon>0
.
Under the hypotheses of Proposition
1
, we define the half relaxed limits
u^{\pm}

by

\displaystyle u^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\sup\{u(\xi,\eta)\,:\,u\in S_{\varepsilon},\ (\xi,\eta)\in\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}},\ 0<\varepsilon<r,\ |\xi-x|<r\},

\displaystyle u^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\inf\{u(\xi,\eta)\,:\,u\in S_{\varepsilon},\ (\xi,\eta)\in\overline{\Omega_{\varepsilon}},\ 0<\varepsilon<r,\ |\xi-x|<r\},

which are bounded functions on
\overline{\Omega}
by Proposition
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
1
,
the functions
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
are a viscosity sub and super solutions to (
15
)–(
16
), respectively.

Clearly, if
G
, as defined in (
13
) and (
14
), satisfies the comparison principle then

u_{o}:=u^{+}=u^{-}
and it is a solution of (
15
)–(
16
). Furthermore
u^{\varepsilon}
converges uniformly to
u_{o}
, see Corollary
5
.

Remark that the assumption (
8
) is indeed crucial. Consider problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-u_{yy}+u=1\ \ \ \ \text{ in }(0,1)\times(0,\varepsilon),\\
u_{y}(x,\varepsilon)=1\ ,\ \ -u_{y}(x,0)=0\ ,\ \ u_{x}(0,y)=0,\ \ \ u_{x}(1,y)=0\end{array}\right.

for which all hypotheses, but (
8
), of Proposition
1
are satisfied. Clearly its solution is
u^{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}-e^{-\varepsilon}}\left(e^{y}+e^{-y}\right)+1
, which is not bounded as
\varepsilon
goes to zero.

As already mentioned, in the paper the existence results and the convergence results will be stated and proved for a more general case of lateral conditions, see Proposition
2
and Theorem
4
.

After the work of Hale and Raugel, there has been a number of interesting results that generalise it in many contexts see e.g. the works of Arrieta, Nakasato, Nogueira, Perreira, Villanueva-Pesqueira
[

,

…. with for example oscillating boundary or quasi linear operators.
On the other hand there is also a very large number of papers that treat the “variational” convergence of functionals

to a new functional defined in a lower dimensional space, where the main tool is

The paper is organised in the following way: In the next section we perform the formal expansion that will allow us to guess the limit equation and upload the test function approach. In the third section we prove the existence and bounds at
\varepsilon

is a Bellman-Isaacs operator under the right general condition the limit operator satisfies the comparison principle i.e. the convergence is uniform.

2.
Formal expansion

To gain an intuitive understanding of (
15
),
we perform the following formal calculations. First, we assume that the following
expansion, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, is valid in an appropriate sense:

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}(x,y)=u^{0}(x)+\varepsilon u^{1}(x,y/\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^{2}u^{2}(x,y/\varepsilon)+\cdots,

where
u^{0}\,:\,\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
,
u^{1},u^{2}\,:\,\overline{\Omega}\times[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, and
every ingredients appearing above and elsewhere are supposed to be smooth enough.
At this point, we do not require condition (
8
) yet, but consider the expansion formula (
10
). That is, we set it as follows.

(18)

\left\{\,\begin{aligned} &\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,y)=\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,0)+k^{\pm}(x)y+o(|y|)\\
&\beta^{\pm}(x,y)=\beta^{\pm}(x,0)+l^{\pm}(x)y+o(|y|).\end{aligned}\right.

Insert the above expansions
into the boundary condition (
5
), on the respective boundaries
\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}^{\pm}\cdot D_{x}u^{\varepsilon}\pm u^{\varepsilon}_{y}-\beta^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}^{\pm}\cdot Du^{0}+\varepsilon\gamma_{1}^{\pm}\cdot D_{x}u^{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{\pm}\cdot D_{x}u^{2}\pm u^{1}_{y}\pm\varepsilon u^{2}_{y}-\beta^{\pm}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,0)\cdot Du^{0}(x)+\varepsilon g^{\pm}k^{\pm}(x)\cdot Du^{0}(x)+\varepsilon\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,0)\cdot D_{x}u^{1}(x,g^{\pm})

\displaystyle\pm u^{1}_{y}(x,g^{\pm})\pm\varepsilon u^{2}_{y}(x,g^{\pm})-\beta^{\pm}-\varepsilon g^{\pm}l^{\pm}(x)+\cdots.

Here and below, for function
u=u(x,y)
,
u_{y}
denotes the
y
-derivative
\partial u/\partial y=D_{y}u
of
u
.
Equating to zero the coefficients of
\varepsilon^{0}
and
\varepsilon^{1}
on the right-hand side of the above,
we obtain

(19)

\displaystyle 0=\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,0)\cdot Du^{0}(x)\pm u^{1}_{y}(x,g^{\pm}(x))-\beta^{\pm}(x,0),

(20)

\displaystyle 0=g^{\pm}k^{\pm}(x)\cdot Du^{0}(x)+\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,0)\cdot D_{x}u^{1}(x,g^{\pm}(x))

\displaystyle\quad\pm u^{2}_{y}(x,g^{\pm}(x))-g^{\pm}(x)l^{\pm}(x).

In the domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, we have

(21)

0=F(D^{2}u^{\varepsilon},Du^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon},x,y),

with

(22)

\left\{\begin{aligned} D^{2}u^{\varepsilon}&=\begin{pmatrix}D_{x}^{2}u^{0}&(D_{x}u_{y}^{1})^{\mathrm{T}}\\
D_{x}u^{1}_{y}&\varepsilon^{-1}u^{1}_{yy}+u^{2}_{yy}\end{pmatrix}+o(1),\\
Du^{\varepsilon}&=(D_{x}u^{0},u^{1}_{y})+o(1),\qquad u^{\varepsilon}=u^{0}(x)+o(1).\end{aligned}\right.

A natural ansatz here, to obtain a PDE for
u^{0}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}u^{\varepsilon}
, is to impose that

u^{1}_{yy}(x,y)=0\ \ \text{ for }x\in\overline{\Omega},\,g^{-}(x)<y<g^{+}(x).

To achieve this, we assume that there is a function
v\,:\,\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(23)

u^{1}(x,y)=v(x)y\ \ \text{ for }y\in\mathbb{R}.

With this ansatz, (
19
) and (
20
) read

(24)

\displaystyle 0=\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,0)\cdot Du^{0}(x)\pm v(x)-\beta^{\pm}(x,0),

(25)

\displaystyle 0=g^{\pm}k^{\pm}(x)\cdot Du^{0}(x)+g^{\pm}\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,0)\cdot Dv(x)\pm u_{y}^{2}(x,g^{\pm})-g^{\pm}l^{\pm}(x).

The solution of (
24
) with respect to
v
is

v(x)=\beta^{+}(x,0)-\gamma_{1}^{+}\cdot Du^{0}(x)=-\beta^{-}(x,0)+\gamma_{1}^{-}\cdot Du^{0}(x).

This is well defined if condition (
8
) holds, and it determines the value for
v
as

(26)

v(x):=\beta_{o}-\gamma_{o}\cdot Du^{0}(x),

and the expansion formula (
18
) turns out to be identical to (
10
).

With this choice of
v
, from (
25
), we obtain

(27)

u^{2}_{y}(x,g^{\pm}(x))=\pm g^{\pm}(l^{\pm}(x)-k^{\pm}(x)\cdot Du^{0}(x)\mp\gamma_{o}(x)\cdot Dv).

For each
x
, we look for a quadratic function
Q(y)
with slope

u^{2}_{y}(x,g^{-}(x))
and
u^{2}_{y}(x,g^{+}(x))
at
y=g^{-}(x)
and
y=g^{+}(x)
, respectively,
and choose:

Q(y)=\frac{(y-g^{-})^{2}}{2(g^{+}-g^{-})}u^{2}_{y}(x,g^{+})-\frac{(y-g^{+})^{2}}{2(g^{+}-g^{-})}u^{2}_{y}(x,g^{-}).

Thus, our choice of
u^{2}
is:

(28)

\displaystyle u^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(y-g^{-})^{2}g^{+}}{2(g^{+}-g^{-})}\Big(l^{+}(x)-k^{+}(x)\cdot Du^{0}(x)-\gamma_{o}\cdot Dv\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{(y-g^{+})^{2}g^{-}}{2(g^{+}-g^{-})}\Big(l^{-}(x)-k^{-}(x)\cdot Du^{0}(x)+\gamma_{o}\cdot Dv\Big).

We set

(29)

w^{\pm}(x)=\frac{g^{\pm}}{(g^{+}-g^{-})}(l^{\pm}(x)-k^{\pm}(x)\cdot Du^{0}(x)\mp\gamma_{o}\cdot Dv),

so that (
28
) can be written as

(30)

u^{2}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(y-g^{-}(x))^{2}w^{+}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(y-g^{+}(x))^{2}w^{-}(x).

In view of (
22
), we wish to give convenient formulas for
Du^{1}
and
u^{2}_{yy}
,
which involve explicitly neither functions
u^{1}
nor
u^{2}
. We compute that

(31)

\displaystyle Du^{1}_{y}

\displaystyle=Dv=D(\beta_{o}-\gamma_{o}\cdot Du^{0}(x))

\displaystyle=D\beta_{o}-Du^{0}D\gamma_{o}-\gamma_{o}D^{2}u^{0},

(32)

\displaystyle\gamma_{o}\cdot Dv

\displaystyle=\gamma_{o}\cdot D(\beta_{o}-\gamma_{o}\cdot Du^{0})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{o}\cdot D\beta_{o}-\gamma_{o}\cdot Du^{0}D\gamma_{o}-\gamma_{o}D^{2}u^{0}\gamma_{o}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{o}\cdot D\beta_{o}-[\gamma_{o}(D\gamma_{o})^{\mathrm{T}}]\cdot Du^{0}-\gamma_{o}D^{2}u^{0}\gamma_{o}^{\mathrm{T}},

and

(33)

\displaystyle u^{2}_{yy}(x,y)

\displaystyle=w^{+}(x)+w^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g^{+}-g^{-}}\Big(g^{+}l^{+}+g^{-}l^{-}\Big)-\frac{1}{g^{+}-g^{-}}\Big(g^{+}k^{+}+g^{-}k^{-}\Big)\cdot Du^{0}\quad-\gamma_{o}\cdot Dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g^{+}-g^{-}}\Big(g^{+}l^{+}+g^{-}l^{-}\Big)-\frac{1}{g^{+}-g^{-}}\Big(g^{+}k^{+}+g^{-}k^{-}\Big)\cdot Du^{0}

\displaystyle\quad-\gamma_{o}\cdot D\beta_{o}+[\gamma_{o}(D\gamma_{o})^{\mathrm{T}}]\cdot Du^{0}+\gamma_{o}D^{2}u^{0}\,\gamma_{o}^{\mathrm{T}}.

Recalling (
11
) and (
12
), we obtain

(34)

u^{2}_{yy}=\gamma_{o}D^{2}u^{0}\gamma_{o}^{\mathrm{T}}+b\cdot Du^{0}+c.

We go back to (
22
), to find that as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
,

\displaystyle D^{2}u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}D^{2}u^{0}&(D\beta_{o}-Du^{0}D\gamma_{o}-\gamma_{o}D^{2}u^{0})^{\mathrm{T}}\\

For a constant

\underline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}
satisty the lateral boundary condition.

\psi_{\varepsilon}
idem for
\overline{v}_{\varepsilon}
and
\underline{v}_{\varepsilon}

Note by the definition of

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{o}\cdot D\rho+\overline{v}-\beta_{o})(x)+O(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle=(-\gamma_{o}\cdot D\rho-\overline{v}+\beta_{o})(x)+O(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon),

\displaystyle(-\gamma^{-}_{1}\cdot D\rho-\underline{v}_{\varepsilon}-\beta^{-})(x,\varepsilon g^{-}(x))

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{o}\cdot D\rho-\underline{v}+\beta_{o})(x)+O(\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon).

\displaystyle(\gamma^{\pm}\cdot D\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}-\beta^{\pm})(x,\varepsilon g^{\pm}(x))=(\gamma_{1}^{\pm}\cdot D\rho\pm\overline{v}_{\varepsilon}-\beta^{\pm})(x,\varepsilon g^{\pm}(x))

\displaystyle+\gamma_{1}^{\pm}(x,\varepsilon g^{\pm}(x))\cdot(\varepsilon g^{\pm}(x)D\overline{v}_{\varepsilon}(x)+\Lambda\varepsilon(\varepsilon h(x)-\varepsilon g^{\pm}(x))Dh(x))

\left\{\,\begin{aligned} &\gamma^{+}\cdot D\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}>\beta^{+}\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon},\qquad\quad\gamma^{-}\cdot D\overline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}>\beta^{-}\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon},\\
&\gamma^{+}\cdot D\underline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}<\beta^{+}\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{T}}\Omega_{\varepsilon},\quad\text{ and }\quad\gamma^{-}\cdot D\underline{\psi}_{\varepsilon}<\beta^{-}\ \ \text{ on }\partial_{\mathrm{B}}\Omega_{\varepsilon}.\end{aligned}\right.
