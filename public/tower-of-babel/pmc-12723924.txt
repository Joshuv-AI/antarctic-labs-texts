Fat-derived neural stem cells promote nerve regeneration following peripheral nerve injury
Authors: Leah C Ott, Aki Kashiwagi, Christopher Y Han, Abigail R Leavitt, Ahmed A Rahman, Charles D Hwang, Alan J Burns, Ryo Hotta, Allan M Goldstein, Rhian Stavely
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeripheral nerve injuries are associated with significant morbidity, particularly when primary surgical repair is delayed or impossible due to extensive nerve gaps. While advances in biomedical engineering have led to commercially available nerve guidance conduits tailored for such injuries, rates of sensory and motor recovery remain suboptimal following neurotmesis with gap defects beyond 3 cm. Cell therapy represents a promising treatment strategy to heal the injured peripheral nervous system, thought to promote tissue regeneration and enhance endogenous mechanisms of nerve repair to restore functionality. In this study, we explore the potential utility and efficacy of subcutaneous adipose tissue-derived neural stem cells in a nerve transection injury model.MethodsPlp1-EGFP mice, which express GFP in Schwann cells, underwent surgical excision of a 5 mm segment of the left sciatic nerve. Nerves were then immediately repaired using silicone nerve guidance conduits with a residual 5 mm defect between nerve stumps. Conduits were loaded with cell culture media alone or with subcutaneous adipose tissue-derived neural stem cells harvested from Wnt1-tdTomato neural crest reporter mice, the latter enabling cell tracing post-transplantation.ResultsSubcutaneous adipose tissue-derived neural stem cells persisted through postoperative day 56 and contributed structurally to the reformed sciatic nerve. Integration between Wnt1-tdTomato neural stem cells and endogenous Plp1-EGFP Schwann cells occurred at the distal and proximal transection margins. Furthermore, neural stem cells predominantly differentiated into Schwann-like cells following transplantation, aiding in myelination of the reformed nerve, but not undesirable cell types such as neurons. Gait testing indicated that adipose-derived neural stem cells significantly improved hindlimb motor recovery compared to conduit repair alone by postoperative day 56.ConclusionsUsing cell tracer models, we confirm that adipose-derived neural stem cells can be therapeutically delivered to injured peripheral nerves, integrate with recipient axons and Schwann cells, and differentiate into myelinating Schwann-like cells to enhance motor recovery. Introduction

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNIs) present a significant clinical challenge due to the essential role of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) in relaying sensory information and controlling motor function and autonomic tone. An estimated 2.4% of the global population suffers from peripheral neuropathy as a result of trauma, demyelinating diseases, autoimmune disorders, infections, malnutrition, and toxin exposure, among other causes [1]. PNIs affect approximately 3% of all trauma patients [2] and can result in permanent disability, with less than half achieving normal sensory and motor function after nerve repair [3]. The most severe PNIs involve complete transection (neurotmesis), which are often not amenable to tension-free primary repair if they result in a gap defect greater than 1 cm between nerve ends [4, 5]. Following such injuries, axotomy quickly triggers the degradation of axons and myelin distal to the lesion, termed Wallerian degeneration [6, 7]. With the loss of axonal apposition, Schwann cells then dedifferentiate, expand, and migrate towards the proximal nerve stump to remove debris, direct axonal sprouting, and remyelinate the regenerated nerve [8, 9], but can only sustain this process for a finite period of time, resulting in poor recovery with longer gaps [10, 11]. Treatment options to bridge such defects and enable nerve repair include nerve autografts, nerve allografts, and nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) made of collagen or synthetic materials [12]. While autologous nerve grafting remains the gold standard treatment for severe PNIs, the limited supply of donor nerves and donor site morbidity (including neuroma formation) are considerable obstacles [3, 13, 14]. Allografts are frequently rejected due to their immunogenicity [12, 15], while NGCs have limited efficacy beyond small defects [13]. Novel therapies to bridge large nerve gaps and accelerate nerve regeneration before the onset of irreversible muscle atrophy are critical to address this unmet clinical need [16–18].

Cell therapies offer a promising approach for regenerating and restoring function in the PNS [12]. Adipose tissue is a rich source of stem cells and readily accessible through minimally invasive procedures, making it highly suitable for therapeutic applications [19]. Adipose-derived stem cells have been widely accepted for clinical use due to their favorable safety profiles [20]. Subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) contains a pool of stem cells capable of differentiating into neurons and glia in vitro [21, 22]. Despite their promising utility for repairing the nervous system, clinical translation has been limited by challenges in purifying and expanding cells with neurogenic potential for transplantation. This may be attributed to the limited understanding of the identity and characteristics of cell populations within SAT that possess neurogenic differentiation potential [23]. We recently discovered two stem cell populations residing in SAT: the well-documented mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), capable of differentiating into adipocytes, chondrocytes, and osteocytes, and a distinct population of neural stem cells (NSCs) we termed SAT-NSCs [24]. Our research has traced the origin of SAT-NSCs to neural crest (NC)-derived Schwann cells, which dedifferentiate into NSCs under specific culture conditions [25]. We have developed protocols to expand and purify SAT-NSCs from the dorsolumbar SAT of mice, enabling their testing in preclinical models [25]. Transplanted SAT-NSCs successfully restored gastrointestinal neuromuscular function in murine models of Hirschsprung disease and gastroparesis [25]. These promising data raise the prospect that SAT-NSCs may confer similar therapeutic benefits in nerve regeneration in other regions of the PNS.

Here, we evaluate the feasibility and potential therapeutic efficacy of SAT-NSC transplantation in a peripheral nerve injury model of sciatic neurotmesis. SAT-NSCs delivered via silicone NGC are definitively shown to survive and engraft in the regenerated sciatic nerve using novel transgenic reporter systems to trace donor SAT-NSCs and recipient endogenous Schwann cells. Furthermore, SAT-NSCs differentiate into myelinating Schwann-like cells after transplantation, reconstitute myelinated regions of the regenerated nerve, and are associated with superior motor recovery. Our findings suggest that SAT-NSCs could fill a critical gap in the treatment of PNIs as a readily available, abundant source of autologous regenerative cells capable of rebuilding nerves after traumatic injuries not amenable to neurorrhaphy.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed to assess whether SAT-NSCs may survive and engraft in PNIs, their differentiation potential post-transplantation, and whether they enhance functional recovery after NGC repair. We assessed whether NC-derived Schwann cells in dorsocervical SAT become NSCs in vitro, as previously described in dorsolumbar SAT [25]. The Massachusetts General Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all study protocols (2009N000239). The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Mice

Wnt1::Cre (Tg(Wnt1-Cre)11Rth, JAX Stock #003829) [25–28], ROSA26tdTomato (ROSA26R-tdTomato reporter, JAX Stock #007914) [25, 26, 28], and C57BL/6J (JAX Stock #000664) mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Wnt1::Cre and ROSA26tdTomato mice were crossed to generate Wnt1-tdT mice to trace NC-derived cells. Plp1-EGFP mice were kindly gifted by Wendy Macklin, PhD of the University of Colorado [29]. Mice were housed and bred under specific-pathogen-free conditions at the Center for Comparative Medicine animal facility of Massachusetts General Hospital.

Nerve fiber isolation from subcutaneous adipose tissue and in vitro culture

Nerve fiber isolation from SAT of Wnt1-tdT or Plp1-EGFP mice was performed as previously described [25]. Briefly, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation and the dorsocervical and/or dorsolumbar SAT were harvested. Adipose tissue was minced and enzymatically digested with collagenase type XI (1 mg mL− 1; Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, CAT# C7657) and dispase (0.4U mL− 1; Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) for 90 min at 37 °C under continuous agitation. Samples were filtered through a sterile Falcon 70 μm cell strainer (Corning, CAT# 352350) and nerve fibers captured on the mesh were isolated for cell culture. Successful nerve fiber isolation was visually confirmed with a Leica MZ FLIII fluorescence stereomicroscope (Leica Microsystems, Inc., Deerfield, IL). Culture of SAT-NSC neurospheres was performed as previously described [25], with nerve fibers resuspended in neuroproliferation media for free-floating culture (37 °C, atmospheric oxygen, 5% CO2) in T75 tissue culture flasks (Corning, CAT# 430641).

Quantitative PCR

Transcriptional analysis by quantitative PCR was performed as previously described [28]. Briefly, samples of isolated nerve fiber bundles (n = 5–8) or neurospheres (n = 5–8) were pelleted, resuspended in RLT buffer (Qiagen, Germantown, MD), and stored at −80 ˚C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated with an RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen), and its concentration measured with the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) on a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen). Total RNA underwent reverse-transcription and amplification via RT-qPCR with the iTaq Universal SYBR Green One-Step Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) using a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time thermal cycler. Reaction setup was per the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences for gene amplification are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The thermal cycling protocol was executed per the kit manufacturer’s instructions. Results were processed with the Bio-Rad CFX Manager software (version 3.1), with a standard threshold to quantify cross point (Ct) values. Ct values were normalized to Gapdh expression in each sample as an internal control, and reactions were performed with two technical replicates.

Silicone nerve guidance conduit preparation

NGCs were generated from laboratory-grade silicone tubing (Dow Corning, CAT# 508-004) cut to 7 mm in length (internal diameter 0.76 mm, external diameter 1.65 mm). NGCs were sterilized under UV light overnight then manually fenestrated with 130 μm pores. Fluorescent SAT-NSCs (generated after 14–28 days in culture) were resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with antibiotic-antimycotic (1%; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), then loaded into the NGC lumen immediately prior to implantation (uncoated conduit groups). To optimize SAT-NSC delivery through adherence to the conduit lumen, a subset of NGCs underwent fibronectin coating to the lumen. To generate these fibronectin-coated NGCs, sterilized conduits were incubated with fibronectin (1:100 in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS); Millipore Sigma Fibronectin Bovine Plasma, CAT# F1141-5MG) in the conduit lumen for two hours at 37 °C. This fibronectin solution was then aspirated from the lumen after incubation. In control mice receiving media-filled, fibronectin-coated NGCs (to assess the effect of fibronectin alone on PN regeneration), conduits were then immediately loaded with DMEM with 1% antibiotic-antimycotic as above. In the experimental group receiving fibronectin-coated NGCs with SAT-NSCs, these conduits were filled with SAT-NSCs and incubated under standard culture conditions in DMEM media as above for 4–5 days prior to surgical implantation.

Sciatic nerve injury surgery and conduit repair

To examine whether SAT-NSCs can survive, engraft, and promote regeneration in PNI, we established a model of sciatic nerve injury with NGC repair, similar to those previously reported [30, 31]. Adult Plp1-EGFP and C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to receive media-filled or SAT-NSC-filled NGCs that were uncoated or fibronectin-coated prior to sciatic nerve injury. This resulted in a total of four groups: media-filled uncoated NGC (n = 7), media-filled fibronectin-coated NGC (n = 5), SAT-NSC-filled uncoated NGC (n = 5), and SAT-NSC-filled fibronectin-coated NGC (n = 5) as summarized in Fig. 1. At the time of surgery, general anesthesia was induced with inhaled isoflurane (1–4%; Covetrus, Portland, ME) and 5 mm of the left sciatic nerve sharply resected at the mid-thigh (proximal to its bifurcation). The nerve was immediately repaired using a NGC, with nerve stumps inserted 1 mm inside each end and secured with 8 − 0 nylon suture, creating a residual 5 mm gap defect as described in prior murine studies [32–36]. Mice were sacrificed on either postoperative day (POD) 28 or 56 to assess for nerve regeneration.

Immunocytochemistry and immunohistochemistry

For immunocytochemistry experiments, neurospheres generated in free-floating culture were transferred to fibronectin-coated (1:100) plates (24-well, USA Scientific, CAT# 5666 − 2160) and cultured for seven days in monolayer conditions. Cells were fixed in 4% PFA (Electron Microscopy Science, CAT# 15710) for 30 min, permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min, and blocked with 10% donkey serum (DS, Sigma, CAT# D9663) for one hour at room temperature (RT) prior to staining as below.

For immunohistochemistry experiments, tissues were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C overnight, then dehydrated with 15% sucrose at 4 °C overnight. Samples were embedded in 7.5% gelatin-15% sucrose at 65 °C for one hour, cooled to RT, and snap frozen at −80 °C. Samples were sectioned at 12 μm using a CM3050 S cryostat (Lecia Biosystems). Sections were blocked and permeabilized in 10% DS with 0.1% Triton X-100 at RT for one hour prior to staining. In all experiments, samples were washed in PBS 3x between steps.

Cells and tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies in 10% DS overnight at 4 °C. If indicated, samples were incubated with secondary antibodies in 10% DS for one hour at RT, then counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen) for two minutes. Aquapoly/mount (Fisher Scientific Polysciences, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA) was used to mount stained tissue sections. Primary and secondary antibodies utilized are provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, respectively.

Morphometric analysis

Nerve diameter was quantified by analyzing representative brightfield images of nerves ex vivo (after removal of the conduit) using ImageJ software v1.54f (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). At least four measurements of diameter were performed in the middle third of the regenerated nerve, with the maximum diameter utilized in subsequent analysis.

To quantify myelination in regenerated nerves, tissues were fixed, embedded, and cryosectioned as above, then stained using the Luxol Fast Blue–PAS OptimStain™ Kit (HiTO Biotec, HTKNS1228), with minor modifications to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, slides were first dehydrated through two washes in 100% ethanol (3 min each), followed by two washes in 95% ethanol (3 min each). The Luxol Fast Blue staining solution was prepared by mixing 1.5 mL of Solution 1 with 10.5 mL of 95% ethanol in a 12 mL staining jar. Slides were immersed in this solution and heated in a microwave for 15 s, then incubated at room temperature for an additional 18 min. Following staining, slides were briefly rinsed in 95% ethanol (two dips) and placed in distilled water. To differentiate the stain, 3 mL of Solution 2 was mixed with 9 mL of distilled water, and slides were dipped in this mixture eight times. Slides were then rinsed in 70% ethanol for approximately 8 s (eight dips), followed by a 1-minute rinse in distilled water. This water rinse was repeated three times to ensure full removal of residual reagents. For PAS staining, slides were incubated in 12 mL of Solution 3 at room temperature for 4 min, followed by four 15-second rinses in double-distilled water. Slides were then incubated in 12 mL of Solution 4 for 10 min at room temperature, followed by four 3 min rinses in distilled water. Nuclear counterstaining was performed by applying Solution 5 directly to the section for 20 s, ensuring complete coverage. Slides were rinsed again in distilled water for 1 min. For final differentiation, 6 mL of Solution 6 was mixed with 6 mL of distilled water, and slides were rinsed in this mixture for 2 min, followed by a final rinse in distilled water for 1 min. Slides were then dehydrated through two 4 min incubations in 100% ethanol, cleared in two 4 min xylene incubations, and coverslipped using CytoSeal™.

To quantify myelination within defined tissue regions (which stained positive for Luxol Fast Blue) stained sections were analyzed using ImageJ (NIH). Digital images were opened sequentially, and the region of interest (ROI) containing the regenerated nerve was manually defined by tracing with the Freehand and Polygon tools. The ROI was saved and added to the ROI Manager for subsequent measurement steps. Blue-stained myelin was isolated using the “Color Threshold” function (Image > Adjust > Color Threshold) with the RGB color space selected. Thresholding was performed for segmentation of blue-stained areas. Following threshold adjustment, the image was converted into a binary mask using the “Convert to Mask” function with the black background option enabled.The binary mask was then measured within the previously defined ROI to obtain the area fraction representing Luxol Fast Blue–positive signal within the nerve.

Walking track analysis

Walking track analysis was performed on POD28 and 56 as previously described [37, 38]. Mice ambulated along a narrow catwalk (5.5 cm by 55 cm) lined with paper after painting their hindfeet with India ink, and pawprint tracings were analyzed in blinded fashion. Paw length (PL), toe spread (TS), and intermediate toe spread (ITS) were measured in three representative pawprints per foot (operative and nonoperative) using Fiji open-source software v1.54f [41] (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). Replicates were averaged to calculate the mean experimental (E) and normal (N) values for each measurement. Imaging

Imaging was performed with a Keyence BZX-700 All-In-One microscope (Keyence America, Itasca, IL), Leica MZ FLIII fluorescence stereomicroscope (Leica Microsystems), Nikon Eclipse 80i upright fluorescent microscope (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY), and Nikon AXR confocal microscope (Nikon, Cambridge, MA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of was performed using GraphPad Prism v9 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). Gene expression data in neurospheres was normalized to that of nerve fibers, converted to Log2 fold change values (LogFC), and analyzed using an unpaired t-test. Data with multiple groups and timepoints were analyzed with two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was indicated by p < 0.05. Data appear as mean ± SEM.

Results

Schwann cells residing in dorsocervical subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) are a viable source of neural stem cells (NSCs)

We previously demonstrated that SAT-NSCs can be isolated from the dorsolumbar fat pad of mice [25]. To maximize the yield of SAT-NSCs for transplantation and to examine whether other sources of fat are appropriate targets for cell isolation, we investigated the potential of dorsocervical SAT to generate SAT-NSC neurospheres. Firstly, we evaluated the dorsocervical fat for the presence of Schwann cells from which SAT-NSCs originate [25]. To visualize Schwann cells and characterize their spatial relationship to other structures, we utilized Wnt1-tdTomato (Wnt1-tdT) NC reporter mice, in which the fluorescent marker tdT is constituently expressed in cells derived from the NC, including peripheral neurons and Schwann cells [25–27]. Fluorescent microscopy confirmed the presence of tdT+ fiber-like structures running through the dorsocervical fat of adult Wnt1-tdT mice (Fig. 2A). Using fluorescent immunohistochemistry, we observed bundles of tdT+ fibers in cross sections of dorsocervical SAT that concurrently expressed tubulin beta-3 (TUBB3), a neuronal microtubule protein that localizes to axons, confirming their identity as nerve fiber bundles (NFBs, Fig. 2B). High-magnification images similarly demonstrated smaller tdT+ TUBB3+ nerve fibers (Fig. 2C). NFBs were situated adjacent to arterioles (Fig. 2D), as identified by their expression of alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), while individual nerve fibers resided in direct apposition to blood vessels (Fig. 2E).

To identify Schwann cells in nerve fibers from dorsocervical SAT, we utilized Plp1-EGFP transgenic reporter mice, in which GFP expression is driven by the pan-glial promoter Plp1 [29]. Cross sections of SAT revealed GFP+ Schwann cells residing along TUBB3+ axons in NFBs (Fig. 2F). High-magnification images similarly demonstrated GFP+ Schwann cells along smaller nerve fibers, including those wrapped around CD31+ capillaries (Fig. 2G). Upon immunolabeling for myelinating and unmyelinating markers to further characterize resident Schwann cells, Wnt1-tdT+ NFBs exhibited significant immunoreactivity to leucine-rich glioma inactivated 1 (LGI1, Fig. 2H), which is known to regulate myelination by glia in the CNS and PNS [39, 40], suggesting that NFBs contain predominantly myelinating Schwann cells. Likewise, myelin protein zero (MPZ), the main structural glycoprotein of myelin in the PNS [41–43], was expressed around Plp1-EGFP+ cells in NFBs (Fig. 2I). In contrast, a minority of S100b+ Schwann cells co-expressed GFAP (Fig. 2J), a specific marker of unmyelinating Schwann cells in the PNS [44, 45]. These data indicate that dorsocervical SAT contains predominantly myelinating Schwann cells residing within NFBs or in the perivascular niche, which we anticipated could be used as another source of SAT-NSCs.

To generate SAT-NSCs, dorsocervical SAT from Wnt1-tdT mice was partially digested and the resulting nerve fibers transferred to neuroproliferation media and cultured for 14 days (Fig. 3A). We observed that nerve fibers formed neurospheres containing tdT+ cells in vitro (Fig. 3B). We proceeded to compare glial and stem cell gene expression between nerve fibers and neurospheres to assess whether the latter adopted NSC properties in vitro. Myelinating (Mbp, Mpz) and unmyelinating (Gfap) Schwann cell genes were markedly down-regulated in neurospheres compared to nerve fibers (Fig. 3C-C’’), suggesting the former did not retain a mature Schwann cell identity in vitro. While there was no change in Sox10 expression (Fig. 3C’’’’), neurospheres significantly upregulated the NSC marker Sox2 (Fig. 3C’’’), the NC stem cell marker Ngfr (Fig. 3D), and the NSC marker Nestin (Fig. 3D’).

Neurospheres were then transferred to monolayer culture on fibronectin for seven days, allowing immunocytochemical validation of their cellular identity. Neurospheres derived from Plp1-EGFP mice retained GFP expression in monolayer culture (Fig S1A) and co-expressed the NSC marker NGFR (Fig S1A), consistent with our transcriptional data. A minority of Plp1-EGFP+ cells expressed the glial marker S100b (Fig S1B), while the Schwann cell markers GFAP (Fig S1C) and MPZ (Fig S1D) were entirely absent, confirming that mature glia do not persist in vitro. Interestingly, neurospheres derived from Wnt1-tdT+ mice exhibited expression of TUBB3 (Fig S1E, S1F), calretinin (Fig S1E), and neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS, Fig S1F), confirming their neurogenic potential in vitro and further validating their cellular identity as SAT-NSCs. Together, these data indicate that dorsocervical fat, much like dorsolumbar fat, is a rich source of SAT-NSCs that can be harnessed for cell therapy applications.

SAT-NSCs can survive and engraft in the regenerating sciatic nerve following transection injury

To assess whether SAT-NSCs have therapeutic utility in the treatment of PNIs, we devised a model of sciatic nerve injury creating a residual 5 mm defect between nerve stumps within a silicone NGC (Fig. 4A, B). Silicone NGCs could be directly loaded with SAT-NSCs (Fig. 4C) or coated with fibronectin prior to loading (as summarized in Fig. 1A). After 4–5 days in vitro, SAT-NSCs formed an adherent monolayer along the lumen of fibronectin-coated NGCs (Fig. 4D), ensuring more uniform SAT-NSC delivery across the nerve gap. To assess for sciatic nerve regeneration after repair, mice were sacrificed on POD56 and the surgical site was dissected to expose the NGC. Growth of a peripheral nerve (PN) was observed in mice receiving media-filled (Fig. 4E) and SAT-NSC-filled conduits (Fig. 4E’) that were uncoated or coated with fibronectin. In mice from the SAT-NSC group, in vivo fluorescent microscopy confirmed that transplanted cells had survived and integrated into the regenerating nerve (Fig. 4F). Additionally, proximal regions of the repaired nerve were composed primarily of Wnt1-tdT+ Schwann cells (Fig. 4F”) with an increasing proportion of recipient-derived GFP+ Schwann cells distally (Fig. 4F’), implying that transplanted SAT-NSCs contribute to both the repair process and the nerve’s ultimate structure. Time course experiments revealed that regeneration could occur as early as POD28 in both the media (Fig S2A) and SAT-NSC (Fig S2A’) groups. In vivo (Fig S2B) and ex vivo (Fig S2C) microscopy confirmed the survival of tdT+ SAT-NSCs at POD28, which similarly formed a region of the proximal nerve as observed on POD56.

SAT-NSCs undergo gliogenic differentiation to Schwann-like cells, but not neurogenic differentiation, following transplantation to the injured sciatic nerve

To better visualize the integration of SAT-NSCs with recipient-derived cells and to characterize their fate post-transplantation, we performed fluorescent immunohistochemistry on regenerated sciatic nerves containing SAT-NSCs at POD56. Longitudinal sections revealed incorporation of Wnt1-tdT+ SAT-NSCs with Plp1-EGFP+ recipient-derived Schwann cells across the nerve’s full diameter (Fig. 5A). High-magnification imaging confirmed the presence of SAT-NSCs in both distal (Fig. 5B) and proximal (Fig. 5C) regions of the nerve. The neuronal axon marker TUBB3 was expressed throughout the length of these nerves (Fig. 5D), confirming that regenerated axons traveled along transplanted SAT-NSCs to recapitulate the structure of the uninjured sciatic nerve (Fig S3A). We similarly observed TUBB3+ axons traversing the gap defect alongside SAT-NSCs at POD28 (Fig S4A). SAT-NSCs, however, did not express HuC/D (a marker of the neuronal cell body) following transplantation (Fig. 5E, Fig S4B), suggesting they do not undergo neuronal differentiation themselves. HuC/D expression was similarly absent from the uninjured sciatic nerve which does not contain neuronal soma (Fig S3B). Furthermore, high-magnification images did not reveal co-expression of TUBB3 and tdT in reformed sciatic nerves, but rather SAT-NSCs (white arrows) residing along TUBB3+ axons (yellow arrows, Fig. 5F). Therefore, SAT-NSCs did not undergo neuronal differentiation in the regenerating PN but were intimately associated with recipient-derived axons.

SAT-NSCs enhance axonal regeneration, myelination, and motor function following conduit repair of sciatic nerve injury

To assess whether SAT-NSC transplantation is associated with superior distal reinnervation following PNI, we quantified regenerated nerve diameter at POD56 as an indirect measure of axonal regeneration. Delivery of SAT-NSCs via fibronectin-coated NGC was associated with the greatest maximal nerve diameter (mean 1098 μm ± 179.7 μm), with no other statistically significant differences between the remaining groups (Fig. 8A). Morphometric analysis was then performed following Luxol Fast Blue (LFB)-Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining to visualize myelin expression in the regenerated nerve at POD56. While fibronectin coating alone had no effect on myelination when comparing the media groups and SAT-NSC groups, SAT-NSCs significantly increased the expanse of myelination compared to media when administered via fibronectin-coated conduit (mean 22.32% ± 0.88% versus 15.18% ± 2.64%, p = 0.03; Fig. 8B). On pooled analysis (given that fibronectin coating exhibited no independent effect), sciatic nerve repair with SAT-NSCs (regardless of NGC coating) resulted in greater myelination than with conduit alone, with comparable myelinated area as non-injured, naïve sciatic nerves (Fig. 8C, D). To evaluate whether transplanted SAT-NSCs directly contributed to this enhanced myelin sheath, brightfield (Fig. 8E) and fluorescent images (Fig. 8Eʹ) were overlaid to assess colocalization between fluorescent SAT-NSCs and blue-staining myelin. Indeed, regions of the nerve predominantly composed of SAT-NSCs demonstrated rich myelin expression.

To determine whether repaired sciatic nerves containing SAT-NSCs are functional and successfully reinnervate target muscles distally, walking track analysis was performed to quantify motor recovery (as summarized in Fig. 8F). Our model of sciatic neurotmesis resulted in robust gait dysfunction as early as POD7, with mean SFIs of −101.0 ± 3.30 and − 100.1 ± 4.25 in the media and SAT-NSC groups, respectively (n = 5 mice/group). Presence of SAT-NSCs, but not fibronectin coating, was found to be a significant factor in SFI variation at both POD28 (p = 0.089) and POD56 (p = 0.002). At POD28, mice receiving SAT-NSCs via fibronectin-coated NGC (mean − 80.33 ± 7.33) demonstrated significantly enhanced motor recovery compared to the fibronectin-coated media group (mean − 103.3 ± 3.31, p = 0.005) and the uncoated SAT-NSC group (mean − 98.1 ± 4.98, p = 0.02; Fig S5A). At POD56, mice receiving SAT-NSCs via fibronectin-coated NGC continued to display enhanced motor function versus fibronectin-coated media-filled NGC (mean − 74.5 ± 12.9 versus − 109.5 ± 5.8, respectively, p = 0.003), but improvement compared to the uncoated, SAT-NSC-filled NGC group did not persist (mean − 89.2 ± 5.1, p = 0.16; Fig. 8G). Footprint tracings demonstrated preserved toe spread and attenuated paw lengthening in mice receiving SAT-NSCs compared to media alone (Fig. 8H). On pooled analysis (given there was no independent effect from NGC coating), SAT-NSC administration was associated with superior motor recovery on both POD28 (mean − 89.2 ± 5.1 versus media group mean − 103.5 ± 2.3, p = 0.02) and POD56 (mean − 82.63 ± 6.5 versus − 106.2 ± 2.3, p = 0.0005; Fig S5B). In summary, SAT-NSCs enhanced myelination and motor performance following NGC repair of PNIs, suggesting these cells can facilitate successful nerve repair.

Discussion

The present investigation supports a therapeutic role for SAT-NSCs as an adjunct to conduit repair of neurotmesis, enhancing regeneration across large gaps. We demonstrate that Schwann cells from dorsocervical and dorsolumbar SAT can be easily harvested to generate a purified population of SAT-NSCs in vitro, which may be utilized for cell therapy applications. Using lineage tracing mouse models, we illustrate that SAT-NSCs survive and integrate with recipient Schwann cells in the injured sciatic nerve. Both cell populations are incorporated into the nerve’s final structure, with SAT-NSCs differentiating into myelinating Schwann-like cells in this environment, which is essential for proper nerve conductance. Importantly, we report superior nerve caliber, myelination, and motor performance in mice receiving SAT-NSCs in conjunction with NGC repair, which suggest enhanced recovery and possibly motor reinnervation. Together, our data indicate SAT-NSCs aid in PN repair by contributing to the structure and myelination of regenerated nerves, improving functional recovery.

Unlike the CNS, the PNS has significant regenerative potential after injury, largely due to Schwann cell plasticity [12]. Nerve injury triggers the disintegration of axons and myelin distal to the lesion in a process known as Wallerian degeneration [6, 7], and upon losing contact with axons, mature myelinating Schwann cells revert to a dedifferentiated phenotype [8, 9, 48] to aid in clearing myelin and axonal debris [49–51]. Interestingly, this immature state induces down-regulation of MPZ, among other myelinating proteins [48]. These Schwann cells will then proliferate and migrate towards the proximal stump, creating organized structures along the basal lamina termed bands of Büngner, which then guide regenerating axons within the growth cone towards the distal nerve stump [8, 9, 52]. Additionally, these repair Schwann cells will release neurotrophic factors to promote neuronal survival and drive axon growth, including glial cell-line derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and nerve growth factor (NGF) [52–54]. Once axonal regeneration is complete, repair Schwann cells revert to their mature phenotype to myelinate reformed axons in a 1:1 ratio reminiscent of PNS development [55, 56]. Given the poor functional outcomes following neurotmesis, largely due to the limited duration of this repair phase [10, 11], cell therapy has been proposed as a novel treatment strategy to enhance recovery with local delivery to the injured PN via NGCs [12]. While autologous Schwann cells would be ideal seed cells for NGCs, difficulties culturing and expanding them in vitro have limited their use [57]. Rather stem cells, including embryonic stem cells (ESCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and NSCs, have garnered interest given their proliferative capacity [12, 58] but are not without their drawbacks.

NC [59] and neural progenitor [60] cells derived from ESCs are known to improve functional outcomes following sciatic neurorrhaphy, engrafting in the reformed nerve as S100b+ Schwann-like cells. Serious concerns regarding the ethics of their use and tumorigenic potential, however, do not position them as an ideal source of seed cells [12, 61, 62]. Given that iPSCs are generated from postnatal fibroblasts [63, 64] and can differentiate into NC stem cells or myelinating Schwann cells in vivo [65, 66], their therapeutic potential in PNIs has been explored. Bioabsorbable NGCs seeded with human iPSC neurospheres promoted axonal regeneration and myelination after neurorrhaphy in rats [67, 68], with one group demonstrating persistence of human-derived S100b+ cells within nerves post-transplantation [66]. Given their malignant potential [63] and difficulties reliably inducing a myelinating glial phenotype [69, 70], further work is needed before clinical translation is feasible. MSCs represent a major class of autologous postnatal stem cells used in clinical practice, and are the stem cell subtype most extensively studied in preclinical PNI models [71]. They are thought to play an integral role in tissue repair, extracellular matrix remodeling [72], angiogenesis, neurotrophic factor synthesis [73], and negative regulation of scar formation [74] and inflammation [75] via a brief “hit and run” mechanism, with limited evidence for long-term integration in the reformed nerve [73]. Most studies have utilized MSCs derived from bone marrow (BMSCs) or adipose tissue (ADSCs), as they can be harvested via established clinical procedures without prohibitive donor site morbidity [73]. NSCs may represent a more targeted stem cell population to heal the PNS, given their capacity to differentiate into Schwann-like cells to guide axonal sprouting, drive myelination, and secrete beneficial growth factors [58]. Historically, NSCs have been isolated from brain tissue [38, 47, 76] which limited their use in autologous cell therapy, and thus successful isolation of NSCs from SAT is an encouraging step towards clinical application [25].

Prior work has ascribed gliogenic and neurogenic properties to both BMSCs [77, 78] and ADSCs [79–81] in vitro, but few PNI studies support long-term MSC engraftment or differentiation to either lineage in vivo [31, 80, 82]. Six months after silicone NGC repair, Orbay and colleagues reported persistence of transplanted ADSCs expressing S100b and NGFR in the reformed sciatic nerve, which was associated with increased myelinated fiber density and SFI compared to NGC alone [31]. Tomita et al. found that human ADSCs (labelled with GFP lentivirus) engrafted in the rat tibial nerve within eight weeks of crush injury, localized to regenerating axons, and expressed MBP and MPZ, suggesting they differentiated into Schwann-like cells in vivo [80]. In a facial nerve injury model, Wantanabe and colleagues reported that ADSCs (isolated from the inguinal fat of mice) increased the number of myelinated fibers transversing 7 mm gap defects by 13 weeks, with engraftment of transplanted cells that co-expressed NGFR [82]. Notably, our previous research indicates that there are two populations of stem cells that can be derived from SAT, those with mesenchymal differentiation potential and SAT-NSCs [25]. The latter is a distinct population that expresses NGFR and arises from the de-differentiation of Schwann cells. We have also previously described a population of perivascular Schwann cells residing in bone marrow that have similar properties, which may also underlie the limited gliogenic potential of heterogenous BMSCs in vivo [28]. It is possible that the studies above which have attributed gliogenic properties to the mesenchymal ADSC population may be the result of contaminating SAT-NSCs. Utilizing a purified population of SAT-NSCs in our model of PNI, we observed that many NSCs differentiated into myelinating Schwann-like cells, expressing LGI1 and localizing to regions of successful myelination in the regenerated nerve. Furthermore, MSCs and NSCs have predominantly been tracked using commercially available fluorescent dyes [31, 47, 76], which are limited by inadvertent fluorophore transfer from dead transplanted cells to live endogenous cells, obscuring the true fate of MSCs post-transplantation [83]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to leverage transgenic cell tracer models, which are not hindered by non-specific fluorescent transfer, to unequivocally show engraftment and gliogenic differentiation of postnatal, adipose-derived NSCs in PNI. A direct comparison of SAT-NSCs to conventional MSCs would provide an important benchmark for evaluating the added value of transplanting this specific population. Future studies will be necessary to systematically compare SAT-NSCs with more broadly used MSC populations to define their engraftment, differentiation profiles, and relative contributions to axon regeneration, remyelination, and functional recovery.

In prior investigations, we found that Schwann cells residing in dorsolumbar SAT [25] and bone marrow [28] adopted NSC features in vitro (as observed in dorsocervical SAT) and underwent neuronal differentiation upon transplantation into the GI tract. This implies ubiquitous plasticity among Schwann cells from various tissues in vitro, adopting NSC properties under neurosphere-forming conditions. Following engraftment in the injured PN, however, SAT-NSCs favored a Schwann-like cell phenotype, as indicated by elevated MPZ and LGI1 expression. A minority of cells expressed GFAP, consistent with the phenotype of Schwann cells residing in the native sciatic nerve. Interestingly, we did not observe any neuronal differentiation of SAT-NSCs following PNI, as determined with the neuronal soma marker HuC/D. In summary, the disparate fates of SAT-NSCs transplanted into the GI tract versus injured PNs suggest their environment may influence their differentiation potential, but will require further examination.

While our data indicate that SAT-NSCs may foster successful PN repair, several limitations and unresolved questions remain. (1) We indirectly assessed motor recovery using walking track analysis and did not perform sensory testing following nerve repair with SAT-NSCs. Further evaluation of motor recovery (with more direct measures such as electromyography), sensory recovery, and integration of transplanted cells within spinal cord circuits will be necessary to fully characterize the effects of SAT-NSCs on PN repair, as all these pathways play critical roles in normal motor function [84, 85]. (2) More supportive evidence is needed to confirm that SAT-NSCs directly mediate regeneration by replacing nerve tissue and differentiating into myelinating Schwann cells. Immunohistochemical and histological experiments suggest that SAT-NSCs are in close proximity to axons, express Schwann cell markers, and reside in regions of myelination post-transplantation, as implicated in other studies of PNS regeneration [38] and mirroring the trajectory of endogenous Schwann cells in late phases of PN repair [55, 56]. Nonetheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that their regenerative effects are indirect, and rather mediated via augmentation of the reparative actions of endogenous Schwann cells, rather than direct myelination by SAT-NSCs. As SAT-NSCs express neurotrophic factors, such as artemin, BDNF, GDNF, and neurotrophin-3 [25], a trophic signaling mechanism to regenerating axons or endogenous Schwann cells is also plausible. More sensitive approaches to characterize cell fate, such as single-cell RNA sequencing, could determine whether transplanted SAT-NSCs fully acquire a myelinating Schwann cell phenotype in vivo, and more clearly parse the subsequent response of host tissues. (3) In some samples exhibiting functional improvement, SAT-NSCs were observed to cluster preferentially near the proximal nerve stump, while endogenous Schwann cell-mediated regeneration was evident distally. Rather than dispersing evenly across the conduit, this preferential honing may allude to localized trophic or scaffolding effects that support the growth cone, thus recapitulating the actions of native Schwann cells in early phases of repair [52], versus promoting the migration of distal Schwann cells. Of note, while Wallerian degeneration has been classically described as stimulating distal Schwann cell migration towards the proximal nerve stump [8, 9, 52], more recent studies leveraging lineage-tracing models have implicated Schwann cells from both distal and proximal stumps in bridging the nerve gap [86], and thus may be reconciled with our observations. Regardless, future studies delineating spatial behavior of SAT-NSCs and their interactions with endogenous Schwann cells within the repair site over time will be important to elucidate their precise role in the regenerative process. (4) SAT-NSCs were also noted to reside along vasculature but did not undergo endothelial differentiation themselves, which may reflect an indirect role in vascularization of the reformed nerve. Nerves and blood vessels are intimately associated during embryological development of the PNS, following shared cues during migration and secreting factors to promote the other’s survival and arborization [87–92], but whether Schwann cells or SAT-NSCs play a direct role in neo-angiogenesis in the postnatal environment remains unclear [5]. (5) Further work is required to determine potential clinical utility. We utilized a synthetic silicone NGC in this study, which was selected for its biologically inert properties, ease of handling, and established compatibility with small-diameter nerve repairs, as demonstrated in prior rodent models of sciatic nerve injury [30, 31, 93, 94]. The use of an inert scaffold provided a controlled environment to assess the specific contributions of transplanted SAT-NSCs and fibronectin in isolation. Additionally, we utilized a 5 mm nerve gap model in the mouse to enable transgenic lineage tracing of both transplanted SAT-NSCs and endogenous Scwhann cells, an important strength of this system. While spontaneous regeneration can occur at this gap length, it remains a rigorous platform for evaluating engraftment and functional recovery with cell-based therapies [32–36]. Nonetheless, future studies should investigate the efficacy of SAT-NSCs in larger nerve gaps with delivery via clinically approved NGCs to better evaluate translational potential. Another critical step on the road to clinical translation will be transplanting human SAT-NSCs, which we have successfully isolated from surgical specimens of adipose tissue and expanded in vitro [25], into preclinical murine or larger animal models to ascertain whether they exert similar effects as those sourced from mice.

Conclusions

Utilizing elegant cell tracer models, we demonstrate that Schwann cells residing in SAT (namely the dorsolumbar and dorsocervical fat pads) adopt NSC properties in vitro and may be implanted via NGCs to enhance repair of PNIs. Under these conditions, SAT-NSCs survive, differentiate into myelinating Schwann-like cells, and integrate with host axons and Schwann cells. Furthermore, SAT-NSCs boosted axonal regeneration and conferred superior motor function by POD56, consistent with enhanced distal reinnervation. These cells may represent an abundant, easily accessible source of autologous NSCs that may be leveraged with minimal morbidity to treat PNIs, which remain a significant public health issue globally [1], and a necessary innovation in a field that has progressed little over the past 60 years [3, 95].
