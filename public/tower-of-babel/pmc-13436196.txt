Intra-arterial chemotherapy for retinoblastoma: a structured narrative review
Authors: Yan Hong Ren, Daohuan Kang, Caiping Shi, Wen Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetinoblastoma (Rb) is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in children. Intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC), often termed ophthalmic artery chemosurgery, has emerged as an important globe-preserving treatment modality for advanced intraocular Rb. We focused on technical delivery, clinical indications, regimen selection, oncological safety, globe salvage, functional preservation and complications. Current evidence indicates that, in appropriately selected patients and experienced centers, IAC can improve local tumor control and globe salvage while reducing systemic toxicity when compared with intravenous chemotherapy. While melphalan remains the backbone chemotherapeutic agent, escalation to topotecan and/or carboplatin should be guided by tumor burden, vitreous or subretinal seeding, prior treatment response, evidence strength and expected functional potential. Functional preservation should be defined as more than anatomical globe retention; it should include age-appropriate visual acuity or fixation, electroretinography, macular and foveal integrity, avoidance of retinal or choroidal ischemia, amblyopia management, binocular visual development and quality-of-life outcomes. IAC remains associated with ocular and vascular risks, including retinal or choroidal ischemia, cataract formation and ophthalmic artery stenosis or occlusion. Future multicenter prospective studies should use standardized outcome measures, scenario-specific recommendations and molecular monitoring, including aqueous humor cell-free DNA analysis, to refine patient selection and support function-preserving care.Keywords: Ophthalmology, Pediatrics, Medical Oncology

Introduction

Retinoblastoma (Rb) is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in children that predominantly affects infants and young children under 5 years of age.1 With advances in early diagnosis, imaging evaluation and multidisciplinary treatment, overall survival rates at many specialized centers have substantially improved.1 As survival has become increasingly attainable, the clinical focus has shifted from survival alone to preservation of function. Moreover, in addition to achieving tumor control and minimizing metastatic risk, clinicians now aim to maximize globe salvage and visual preservation while reducing treatment-related systemic toxicity and long-term sequelae.1 Conventional treatment modalities, including enucleation, external beam radiotherapy and systemic intravenous chemotherapy (IVC), can achieve effective tumor control; however, they are often associated with irreversible ocular damage, visual loss and long-term complications. This may profoundly affect quality of life and long-term health in affected children.1

Over the past two decades, intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC), also termed ophthalmic artery chemosurgery (OAC), has become an integral component of multidisciplinary Rb management.2 3 By delivering chemotherapeutic agents directly into the ophthalmic artery or via alternative collateral routes, IAC substantially increases intraocular drug exposure while minimizing systemic exposure. This approach seeks to improve local tumor control, to limit systemic toxicity and to increase the likelihood of globe preservation. Early feasibility studies established the safety and globe-salvage potential of IAC in newly diagnosed intraocular Rb. Subsequent comparative studies and meta-analyses have suggested advantages over conventional IVC, particularly in the International Intraocular Retinoblastoma Classification (IIRC) or the International Classification of Retinoblastoma (ICRB) Group D eyes.3–5 However, intercenter heterogeneity in drug selection, treatment schedules and outcome reporting remains substantial, and local ocular toxicity and procedure-related vascular events continue to limit broader standardization.6–9 We, therefore, argue that the current development of IAC should be defined less by proof of feasibility and more by evidence-based patient selection, regimen tailoring and function-preserving endpoints. This review appraises recent clinical evidence on the technical evolution, indications, regimens, outcomes and complications of IAC. We make practice-oriented recommendations for more standardized and individualized use.

Review methodology

This present submission was prepared as a structured narrative review. PubMed and Web of Science were searched from database inception to 31 March 2026 using combinations of ‘retinoblastoma’, ‘intra-arterial chemotherapy’, ‘ophthalmic artery chemosurgery’, ‘ophthalmic artery’, ‘intraocular’, ‘melphalan’, ‘topotecan’, ‘carboplatin’, ‘globe salvage’, ‘visual outcome’, ‘electroretinography’ and ‘complications’. Clinical trials, comparative studies, cohort studies, case series, systematic reviews and meta-analyses were eligible if they addressed IAC/OAC for intraocular Rb and reported at least one clinically relevant domain, including indications, technical delivery, drug regimen, tumor control, globe salvage, survival, visual function, electroretinography (ERG) findings, vascular events or treatment-related complications. We excluded reports that were not available in English, did not involve Rb, did not evaluate IAC/OAC, focused exclusively on extraocular disease, contained only preclinical or pharmacological data without clinical outcomes, provided no relevant outcome data, or duplicated a previously reported cohort. In overlapping cohorts, the most complete or most recent report was prioritized.

Two authors independently screened titles, abstracts and full texts. Disagreements were resolved by discussion with senior authors. Data were extracted using a predefined form covering study design, patient and eye numbers, treatment setting (primary or salvage), laterality, ICRB/IIRC group, regimen, number of cycles where available, follow-up, globe salvage, survival, visual function outcomes, ERG outcomes and major complications. Because the included studies differed substantially in design, indications, outcome definitions and follow-up duration, we did not pool effect estimates or calculate a single total patient denominator. In addition, several evidence syntheses overlapped with primary cohorts, making simple summation vulnerable to double-counting. The certainty of evidence was appraised qualitatively using Grading of recommendations assessment,development and evaluation (GRADE)-informed language: randomized evidence was considered higher certainty than multicenter prospective or retrospective cohorts, whereas single-center retrospective series were interpreted as lower certainty evidence. Recommendations in this review are, therefore, explicitly distinguished as evidence-based conclusions or practice-oriented expert-opinion suggestions.

Briefly, 209 records were identified, 75 duplicates were removed, 134 records were screened and 58 were excluded at title or abstract screening because they had no IAC/OAC focus, were non-clinical or purely pharmacological studies, did not provide separable Rb data, were editorial/commentary material or lacked relevant outcomes. Of 76 reports selected for retrieval, 71 were assessed in full text and 45 were included in the review.

Technical evolution and standardized delivery of IAC

Technical evolution of IAC

IAC is an intensified locoregional treatment approach. Its technical maturation should be assessed not only by catheterization success but also by whether selective drug delivery can be reproduced with low vascular morbidity and meaningful potential for visual preservation. In practice, under general anesthesia and angiographic guidance, a microcatheter is advanced into the ostium of the ophthalmic artery, or an alternative arterial supply when necessary, and the chemotherapeutic agent is selectively infused into the tumor-feeding vascular territory. This approach increases intraocular drug concentration while minimizing systemic exposure. IAC has evolved from semiselective arterial perfusion to super-selective targeted drug delivery. In 1954, Reese et al. first attempted regional arterial infusion of triethylenemelamine via the internal carotid artery in patients with advanced or previously treated Rb, representing one of the earliest explorations of IAC for this disease.10 In 2004, Yamane et al. introduced a balloon microcatheter technique in which the distal internal carotid artery was temporarily occluded, thereby directing the infused drug preferentially into the territory of the ophthalmic artery; the reported technical success rate in 610 treated eyes was 97.51%.11 In 2008, Abramson et al. further refined the procedure by achieving direct super-selective catheterization of the ophthalmic artery without balloon occlusion, thereby establishing the modern form of OAC.12 A retrospective analysis published in 2024, encompassing 2391 treatment sessions over 15 years, showed that the success rate of super-selective catheterization increased from approximately 80% in the initial period to 89.2%, while the overall technical success rate reached 99.5%.13

Standardized procedural workflow

Standardized delivery of IAC requires close multidisciplinary collaboration among ocular oncologists, neurointerventionalists, pediatric oncologists and anesthesiologists; it is optimally performed at experienced referral centers. Procedural standardization should encompass preoperative case selection, angiographic planning, catheter navigation, controlled drug infusion and structured postprocedural surveillance.14–16 In a typical procedure, the child undergoes general anesthesia, systemic anticoagulation according to institutional practice along with femoral arterial access. This is followed by digital subtraction angiography to delineate the internal carotid artery, ophthalmic artery and collateral supply. A microcatheter is then advanced super-selectively to the ostium of the ophthalmic artery. When direct ophthalmic access is unsafe or not possible, recognized collateral routes such as the middle meningeal artery may be considered by experienced operators. Before starting infusion, angiography should confirm an adequate choroidal blush and exclude dangerous reflux through anastomotic channels. Melphalan, topotecan and/or carboplatin are then infused slowly and in a controlled fashion into the tumor-feeding vascular territory. There needs to be continuous attention to vasospasm, catheter stability, hemodynamic changes and embolic risk. Following infusion, close monitoring is required for ocular ischemic injury, vascular complications and systemic adverse effects, with treatment response assessed by fundus examination and imaging studies.14–18 This technical workflow provides the procedural basis for IAC but should be interpreted within a broader functional-preservation framework rather than as a purely anatomical globe-salvage pathway.

Indications and contraindications

IAC is primarily indicated for children with intraocular Rb in whom oncological control, anatomical globe preservation and meaningful functional potential remain realistic objectives. The strongest evidence supports primary IAC in unilateral advanced group D eyes treated at experienced centers, while salvage IAC is an important option for recurrent, persistent or refractory disease after IVC and focal consolidation.5 9 14 19 IAC may also be considered for selected group E eyes, particularly when extraocular extension is not suspected and some prospect of useful anatomy or function remains. However, group E eyes should not be approached as a homogeneous category: eyes with neovascular glaucoma, anterior chamber involvement, diffuse intraocular hemorrhage, optic nerve or scleral invasion, suspected extraocular extension or negligible visual potential are often better managed with primary enucleation rather than repeated salvage attempts.20 21 For bilateral disease, IAC should be integrated with systemic and focal strategies according to the status of each eye, the risk to the fellow eye and the need to minimize cumulative systemic toxicity.

Traditionally, very young or low-weight infants have often received bridge therapy, namely initial systemic IVC followed by delayed IAC once procedural conditions become more favorable. Recent evidence suggests that children younger than 3 months or with body weight below 6 kg should not be regarded as absolute contraindications in all circumstances.22 Nevertheless, early IAC in such infants should be reserved for high-volume centers with strong pediatric anesthesia support, reliable vascular-access expertise, infant-specific dosing protocols and predefined emergency-response pathways. In lower volume settings, bridge therapy or referral to a specialized center may provide a safer route to timely tumor control.

Contraindications to IAC should be considered from both oncological and interventional perspectives. First, in patients with confirmed or strongly suspected extraocular disease, including scleral or orbital invasion, optic nerve involvement, intracranial disease such as trilateral Rb, or distant metastasis, local arterial drug delivery cannot substitute for systemic disease control and, therefore, should not be employed as the primary treatment modality. In such cases, stage-adapted comprehensive therapy should take priority.14 20 Second, in eyes with severe end-stage complications and minimal prospect of meaningful anatomical or visual salvage, such as progressive destruction of intraocular structures or refractory neovascular glaucoma, continued globe-salvage treatment may delay definitive treatment and potentially increase the risk of extraocular extension or metastatic dissemination.20 In addition, when super-selective catheterization cannot be performed safely owing to anatomical or technical limitations, or when the patient has general contraindications to endovascular intervention or general anesthesia, such as uncorrected severe coagulopathy or inability to tolerate anesthesia, alternative or sequential strategies should be considered.19 20

Chemotherapeutic agents used in IAC

As a globe-preserving treatment for intraocular Rb, IAC has advanced substantially in recent years. Appropriate drug selection is pivotal in optimizing efficacy while limiting toxicity. The agents most commonly used in current practice are melphalan, topotecan and carboplatin. Although these agents differ in pharmacological profiles, all can achieve high intraocular exposure, which may enhance local tumor control while limiting systemic toxicity.23 24 Contemporary regimen selection may reasonably follow a backbone-and-escalation strategy: melphalan remains the default core agent, whereas topotecan and/or carboplatin should be added when tumor burden, seeding pattern or prior treatment response suggests that single-agent therapy is unlikely to achieve durable control.

Melphalan

Melphalan is a classical alkylating agent and remains the most widely used drug in IAC for Rb. Its antitumor effect is principally mediated through DNA alkylation, which disrupts DNA structure and suppresses tumor cell replication and proliferation. Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that direct ophthalmic artery infusion of melphalan can achieve very high local drug concentrations and effective tumor control.23 A recent study including 20 eyes that underwent a total of 67 melphalan-based IAC sessions reported a tumor regression rate of 95% following initial treatment and a long-term globe salvage rate of 55%.25 With regard to optimization of formulation, a prolonged-stability melphalan preparation, Evomela, has shown non-inferior safety and efficacy compared with conventional melphalan in IAC, with globe salvage rates of 69% and 79%, respectively; there were no ocular or cerebral ischemic complications in either group.26 Despite its excellent local antitumor activity, melphalan-associated retinal toxicity, particularly at higher doses, remains an important clinical concern.27

Topotecan

Topotecan is a topoisomerase I inhibitor that interferes with DNA unwinding and thereby impairs tumor cell replication. In IAC for Rb, topotecan has demonstrated meaningful antitumor activity, especially in eyes with vitreous or subretinal seeding and in refractory disease. In practice, topotecan is frequently combined with melphalan to achieve synergistic efficacy and prolong intraocular drug exposure; current evidence suggests that this combination has favorable therapeutic activity.28 Compared with melphalan, topotecan may exhibit greater selectivity against Rb cells and substantially lower systemic toxicity, particularly reduced bone marrow suppression, making it an attractive option in patients requiring repeated or prolonged treatment.28 Studies have shown that combined topotecan-melphalan therapy can achieve high globe salvage rates.29 However, local use of topotecan may still be associated with retinal pigment epithelial degeneration, retinal ischemia and related ocular complications, underscoring the importance of close imaging surveillance during treatment.30

Carboplatin

Carboplatin, a platinum-based chemotherapeutic agent, exerts antitumor activity by forming intra-strand DNA crosslinks, thereby interfering with DNA replication and transcription and inducing tumor cell apoptosis. Compared with other platinum compounds, carboplatin has a more favorable systemic toxicity profile, with lower nephrotoxicity and gastrointestinal toxicity risk, and is, therefore, widely used in pediatric oncology.31 As a consequence of its tissue penetrance, carboplatin can rapidly reach Rb tissue and contribute to tumor suppression. In current practice, it is typically administered in combination with other agents to enhance efficacy. Available evidence suggests that triple-agent regimens provide better tumor response and ocular survival than single-agent treatment.32 In advanced Rb, triple therapy with melphalan, topotecan and carboplatin appears superior to dual therapy with melphalan plus topotecan and significantly improves progression-free ocular survival in group D and E eyes.23 In terms of safety, carboplatin-containing IAC has also been reported to be safe and effective in infants younger than 3 months or weighing less than 6 kg, with complete globe preservation and no chemotherapy-related febrile neutropenia in one clinical series.22 However, the potential for ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity with carboplatin should not be overlooked, and close monitoring remains necessary during treatment.26 33

Drug combinations and therapeutic optimization

Accumulating evidence indicates that combination regimens are generally more effective than single-agent therapy in IAC for Rb, particularly for overcoming relative drug resistance and improving globe salvage.24 32 Combined treatment with melphalan, topotecan and carboplatin can improve local tumor control while reducing the need for subsequent salvage therapy, although this advantage must be balanced against cumulative ocular and vascular toxicity.23 24 27 32 Importantly, the evidence base is uneven: the advantage of IAC over IVC for unilateral advanced group D/E disease is supported by randomized evidence, whereas most regimen-specific and scenario-specific recommendations still derive from retrospective series or single-center studies. We, therefore, interpret the melphalan-based escalation strategy as a risk-adapted expert recommendation rather than a universal evidence-based mandate. Clinical outcomes: survival, globe salvage and preservation of visual function

Therapeutic objectives in Rb remain hierarchical with survival the priority. Next, clinicians should seek durable tumor control and globe preservation and where possible, useful visual function should be maintained over the long term.34 Current evidence indicates that IAC has achieved substantial success in advanced intraocular Rb, particularly in IIRC/ICRB group D and selected group E eyes. It has reduced the need for enucleation, enabled meaningful visual outcomes in a subset of patients, and does not appear to compromise overall survival when compared with conventional IVC.5 8 27

Survival

Several studies have demonstrated that IAC is compatible with excellent long-term survival and does not appear to increase the risk of metastatic spread or death when employed as part of a globe-preserving strategy. In a 2025 study, Abramson et al. evaluated 17 very young (<3 months of age) or low-weight infants (<6 kg at time of treatment), 11 of whom had bilateral disease, and reported that after a median follow-up of 4 years, all patients were alive, with no cases of metastatic disease, second primary malignancy or trilateral Rb.22 At the level of randomized evidence, Wen et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial in 143 children with unilateral advanced group D/E disease and, after a median follow-up of 35.8 months, found identical overall survival rates of 96% in both the IAC and IVC groups, with no statistically significant difference between the two strategies.5 Long-term cohort data further support the safety of IAC. In the 13-year single-center Brazilian series reported by Teixeira et al., involving 357 eyes, the 5-year overall survival rate was 99.3%; only one patient (0.33%) developed metastatic disease. A further death was attributed to chemotherapy-related immunosuppression rather than tumor progression.27 A large meta-analysis by Yu et al. likewise suggested that IAC may substantially reduce the risks of metastasis and death compared with historical or expected controls, with ORs of approximately 0.03–0.04 for metastasis and 0.05 for mortality.35 Collectively, these studies indicate that IAC can achieve effective intraocular tumor control without compromising survival; improved mortality may result from reducing uncontrolled intraocular tumor progression.

Globe salvage

IAC has substantially improved globe salvage in Rb, particularly in advanced eyes that historically had limited prospects for preservation. In a long-term series of 357 eyes reported by Teixeira et al., overall globe salvage rates after IAC were 90%, 89% and 86% at 1, 2 and 5 years, respectively. Of note, the 5-year globe salvage rate reached 90% in group D eyes and remained 61% even in group E eyes.27 In 2025, Das et al. reported an overall globe salvage rate of 88% after a median follow-up of 28.6 months in 17 treated eyes.30 Li et al. likewise reported a 5-year durable globe salvage rate of 87.18% in 39 children with unilateral advanced disease.36 These findings indicate that IAC can provide consistently favorable eye-preservation outcomes across different regions and case mixes and is particularly valuable in advanced intraocular disease.

Higher level evidence has reinforced this advantage. In the trial by Wen et al., the 2-year progression-free globe salvage rate was significantly higher in the IAC group than in the IVC group (53% vs. 27%).5 In a meta-analysis including 27 studies and 2310 eyes, Yu et al. reported a pooled OR of 2.41 (95% CI 1.60 to 3.63) in favor of IAC for globe preservation.35 Beyond front-line use, IAC also plays an important role in salvage settings. Runnels et al. reported that among 1184 refractory eyes treated with IAC as second-line or salvage therapy, 64% were ultimately preserved.9 Moreover, optimization of combination regimens may further improve these outcomes. He et al. showed that triple-agent therapy outperformed two-drug regimens in overall progression-free ocular survival and in globe salvage for both group D and group E eyes.23 Overall, IAC has become a key treatment modality for improving globe salvage in Rb, with especially pronounced benefits in advanced and treatment-refractory disease.

Visual and functional preservation as a composite endpoint

As globe salvage has become increasingly achievable, post-treatment visual function has emerged as a critical endpoint. In this review, functional preservation is defined as the retention of an eye with measurable or potentially useful visual capacity, an acceptable treatment burden and preserved developmental value, rather than anatomical globe retention alone. This composite concept encompasses visual acuity or fixation behavior appropriate for age, ERG amplitude, macular and foveal involvement, retinal and choroidal perfusion, avoidance of treatment-related ischemia, the need for amblyopia therapy, binocular visual development and quality of life outcomes reported by patients and families.34 37 38 A salvaged eye with severe retinal ischemia, uncontrolled cataract, macular destruction or no realistic visual potential should, therefore, not be counted as an unequivocal functional success.

Available evidence suggests that pretreatment retinal function and tumor location are major determinants of final visual outcome. In a study of 157 Group D/E eyes, Levin et al. showed that 34% of eyes with a baseline ERG amplitude of ≥75 μV ultimately achieved visual acuity of 20/40 or better, whereas 96% of eyes with a baseline ERG amplitude of 0 μV had a final visual acuity of 20/200 or worse.37 The same study also found that 28% of eyes without macular involvement achieved 20/40 or better visual acuity, and that even among eyes with a baseline ERG amplitude <25 μV, 3.5% still attained 20/40 or better, suggesting that some eyes retain potential for functional retinal recovery following treatment.37 A review by Warda et al. further identified tumor location, particularly macular involvement, as a major determinant of visual prognosis.34

The dynamic course of visual function also warrants attention. In the series reported by Das et al., the proportion of eyes with visual acuity exceeding 6/60 was 94%, 77% and 36% at 1 month, 10 months and 5 years, respectively, suggesting that visual outcomes may deteriorate with longer follow-up.30 By contrast, Abramson et al. reported serial ERG improvement in very young or low-weight infants treated with IAC, with mean 30 Hz flicker amplitude increasing from 61 μV before treatment to 71 μV after treatment, indicating that retinal electrophysiological function may improve in selected eyes once tumor control is achieved.22

Functional preservation also requires active rehabilitation after tumor control. Occlusion therapy, refractive correction, cataract management and surveillance for amblyopia may benefit selected children, especially when potentially reversible amblyopic components coexist with structural macular damage.34 In bilateral disease, preserving a less affected fellow eye and supporting binocular visual development may be more relevant to long-term function than retaining a severely damaged globe. Quality of life outcomes, caregiver burden, repeated anesthetic exposure and the psychosocial effect of visible ocular morbidity should, therefore, be incorporated into future IAC outcome frameworks.34 38

This functional framework has direct implications for clinical decision-making. For an eye with preserved macular structure, useful baseline ERG and manageable seeding, additional treatment may be proportionate even when repeated sessions are required. By contrast, for an eye with negligible ERG, macular destruction, neovascular glaucoma or suspected extraocular extension, aggressive globe preservation may expose the child to repeated anesthesia and vascular risk without meaningful functional gain. Future studies should, therefore, report visual acuity, fixation behavior, ERG, macular status, amblyopia management, binocular development and quality of life outcomes alongside globe salvage.34 37 38

Determinants of clinical outcome

Clinical outcomes after IAC are shaped by both baseline disease severity and treatment exposure. Across studies, ICRB/IIRC group, extent of vitreous or subretinal seeding, macular involvement and pretreatment ERG findings consistently influence the probability of globe salvage and useful vision.27 34 37 Treatment intensity is also likely to be a decisive but under-reported determinant. A greater number of cycles may improve control in partially responsive eyes, yet it often reflects more aggressive disease and may coincide with higher cumulative vascular and retinal toxicity. Likewise, dose escalation, particularly of melphalan, can improve tumor control but may increase the risk of retinal dysfunction and ischemic injury.26–28 30 Procedural factors, including successful super-selective catheterization, avoidance of vasospasm or reflux and maintenance of ophthalmic artery patency, directly affect both efficacy and safety.13 18 39

We suggest that future outcome reporting should routinely include number of IAC cycles, cumulative dose per agent, timing of focal consolidation and vascular event rates, because globe salvage alone does not reveal whether control was achieved at an acceptable functional cost.8 23 26 From a clinical perspective, repeated treatment should be interpreted cautiously. More sessions are not necessarily a marker of better care but may instead identify eyes with intrinsically more aggressive disease biology or lower functional reserve. A structured report of these variables would improve intercenter comparability and help clinicians decide when continued salvage remains proportionate.

In summary, IAC has significantly improved globe preservation in advanced Rb without apparent compromise in survival; a proportion of salvaged eyes can retain useful vision. We, therefore, propose that treatment success should be reported as a composite of oncological safety, globe salvage, visual potential and treatment burden rather than as globe retention alone. Optimized combination regimens, pretreatment ERG-based stratification, and standardized amblyopia management all have important roles in improving final visual outcomes. Complications

Ocular complications

The most commonly reported ocular complications of IAC include retinal detachment, retinal or choroidal ischemia, vitreous hemorrhage, chorioretinal atrophy and cataract formation.8 9 40 Clinical studies have reported retinal detachment rates of up to 23%, making it one of the most frequent ocular adverse events.8 In addition, treatment-related vascular injury may result in retinal or choroidal vessel occlusion and, in some cases, chronic retinal atrophy, thereby adversely affecting visual prognosis.7 27 Periocular edema, ptosis and madarosis may also occur during or after IAC; these are usually transient and often resolve gradually within months after treatment.9 41 42

Procedure-related vascular complications

IAC is also associated with procedure-related vascular risks, most notably ophthalmic artery stenosis or occlusion. These complications are generally related either to vascular injury during catheterization and drug delivery or to direct vascular toxicity from the infused agents. Available studies suggest that some cases of ophthalmic artery stenosis may be permanent, whereas complete ophthalmic artery occlusion is relatively uncommon and can often be promptly identified on angiography, allowing timely modification of treatment strategy.16 27 42 Such events may compromise ocular perfusion, complicating subsequent treatments and adversely affecting long-term visual prognosis.

Systemic complications

Although systemic adverse effects are substantially less frequent with IAC than with conventional IVC, they remain clinically relevant. Commonly reported systemic events include neutropenia, fever, nausea or vomiting and bronchospasm,9 43 most of which are related to systemic absorption of chemotherapeutic agents. Neutropenia may increase the risk of infection, while fever and gastrointestinal symptoms may interrupt treatment continuity or delay subsequent treatment.42 43 In most cases, these systemic toxicities occur shortly after treatment and can be effectively managed with supportive care.

Limitations, unresolved issues and future directions

Despite the substantial improvement in globe salvage achieved with IAC for advanced intraocular Rb, several limitations remain. First, the current evidence base remains dominated by single-center retrospective studies and systematic reviews with substantial heterogeneity. Differences in patient selection, primary versus salvage treatment, unilateral versus bilateral disease, outcome definitions and follow-up duration limit cross-study comparability and hamper robust assessment of long-term safety.5 8 Second, IAC remains highly dependent on interventional expertise and periprocedural management. Delivery may be complicated by catheterization failure, ophthalmic artery stenosis or occlusion, and rare cerebrovascular events.5 39 In addition, globe salvage does not necessarily translate into favorable visual function. Some eyes can be anatomically preserved but still exhibit poor final vision due to pre-existing macular or choroidal damage or treatment-related ischemic complications.38 Finally, the relatively high cost of treatment, the burden of repeated anesthesia and examinations, and unequal access to specialized medical resources all limit broader dissemination and standardization of this approach.21

Future research should, therefore, focus on strengthening the evidence base and moving from technical success to precision patient selection. Multicenter prospective registries and long-term follow-up platforms using unified core outcome sets are urgently needed. These should include overall survival, progression-free globe salvage, visual acuity, fixation behavior, ERG outcomes, macular status, amblyopia treatment, binocular development, quality of life, vascular complications and radiation exposure, so that results can be reliably compared across studies.8 In order to reduce procedural failure and mitigate both local and systemic toxicity, treatment protocols and catheter-based delivery systems require continued optimization, together with exploration of individualized dosing strategies and improved drug stability.23 26 Predictive models integrating imaging features, ERG parameters and clinical stage may help identify eyes with the greatest potential for visual preservation and thereby shift treatment goals from globe salvage alone toward genuine functional preservation.38

A major unresolved issue is why some eyes respond initially and then relapse, whereas others show early resistance. Potential explanations include inadequate local drug exposure in tumor seeds, resistant subclones selected during repeated treatment, tumor heterogeneity, altered vascular perfusion and microenvironmental protection. Future studies should examine mechanisms of resistance to melphalan, topotecan and carboplatin, including ABC transporter-mediated drug efflux, enhanced DNA repair after alkylating or platinum-induced DNA damage, topoisomerase-related changes, evasion of apoptosis and clonal evolution under treatment pressure. Aqueous humor cell-free DNA analysis deserves prospective integration into future IAC studies because it may allow molecular response assessment, early detection of relapse or resistance and rational adjustment of regimen intensity before overt clinical progression occurs.44 45

Conclusion

IAC has become a pivotal component of multidisciplinary Rb care and has substantially expanded the potential for globe preservation in children with advanced intraocular disease. Its contemporary value should be judged not by globe salvage alone, but by the combined achievement of oncological safety, durable globe salvage, useful visual function and acceptable cumulative toxicity. In practical terms, this requires stricter patient selection for group E eyes, cautious use in very young infants or infants with very low body weight outside highly experienced centers, risk-adapted escalation of melphalan-based therapy, explicit distinction between evidence-based conclusions and expert-opinion recommendations and routine reporting of treatment exposure and functional endpoints. Continued refinement of catheter drug delivery, prospective outcome reporting, visual rehabilitation and molecular informed risk stratification will be essential for IAC to progress from a technically mature intervention to a truly function-preserving care package.
