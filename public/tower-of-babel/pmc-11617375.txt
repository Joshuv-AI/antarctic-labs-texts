Symptom clusters and network analysis in patients with gynecologic cancer undergoing chemotherapy: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiajia Zhang, Shan Wang, Yuan Wang, Jiaru Zhuang, Ling Hang, Yibo Wu, Dewu Xu, Chunyan Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the complex relationships among symptoms and symptom clusters in patients with gynecologic cancer receiving chemotherapy using symptom network analysis, and to identify core symptoms and core symptom clusters.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University from December 2023 to June 2024, including 221 patients with gynecologic tumors. Participants completed demographic and clinical information questionnaires and the Chinese version of the MD Anderson Symptom Inventory (MDASI-C). Univariate analysis and multiple linear regression were used to screen covariates, exploratory factor analysis to determine symptom clusters, and network analysis to identify core symptoms and core symptom clusters.ResultsA total of 221 patients were included, with an average age of 58.73 years (SD = 11.50). Fatigue (n = 197, 89.1%) and lack of appetite (n = 192, 86.9%) were the most common symptoms, while fatigue (mean = 4.17, SD = 2.07) and distress (mean = 3.43, SD = 2.20) were the most severe symptoms. Several distinct symptom clusters were identified: sickness behavior, gastrointestinal, psychological, and side-effect clusters. In the constructed network, fatigue emerged as the most central symptom (rs = 1.28), while the sickness behavior cluster was identified as the most central symptom cluster (rs = 1.11).ConclusionsPatients with gynecologic cancer undergoing chemotherapy commonly experience a range of symptoms. Our findings suggest that targeted interventions focusing on the sickness behavior symptom cluster may help reduce the overall symptom burden and assist caregivers in developing more effective symptom management strategies.Keywords: Gynecologic oncology, Chemotherapy, Symptom network, Symptom cluster, Nursing

Introduction

Gynecologic tumors are among the most common cancers in women. In 2020, 18.8% of the world's new cases of gynecologic cancers occurred in China.1 According to the latest statistics from the Journal of the National Cancer Center 2022, the incidence rates of cervical, uterine body, and ovarian cancers in Chinese women rank among the top 10 malignant tumor incidence rates in China, respectively.2

Standardized chemotherapy for gynecological tumors is one of the main treatments, which can greatly prolong the patients’ survival, but it is also accompanied by various uncomfortable symptoms.3 On average, patients with gynecologic cancer endure 10 to 13 complications, many of which can be attributed to tumor burden and treatment-related toxicity.4 Common symptoms include fatigue, depression, anxiety, pain, cognitive problems, and sleep difficulties. These symptoms often interact and recur during and between treatments,5 and may lead to interruptions or delays in treatment, seriously impacting patients' quality of life.

Symptom clusters should consist of at least two stable and interrelated symptoms that are relatively independent of other symptom clusters and may or may not share a common pathogenesis.6 Cancer patients receiving chemotherapy experience multiple symptoms simultaneously rather than a single symptom, and managing symptom clusters can more effectively improve patients' prognosis. Additionally, the simultaneous management of multiple symptoms can improve the cost-effectiveness of symptom management.7 Previous studies have reported that symptom clusters occur pre-chemotherapy and throughout the chemotherapy cycle, with the composition of symptom clusters varying during treatment. Generally, there are 2–6 symptom clusters in patients with gynecological cancer during chemotherapy.8,9 Over time, emotional, body image, peripheral nerve, and fatigue-related symptom clusters have been the most prevalent.10, 11, 12, 13 While previous studies have comprehensively investigated the stability of symptom clusters, there is not complete consistency in the types and numbers of these clusters. Furthermore, these previous studies have not assessed the relationships among multiple symptoms and symptom clusters in real-world situations, and the relative importance of different symptom clusters is still uncertain.

Network analysis can visualize and quantify the interrelationships among symptoms and symptom clusters, offering a new approach to identifying core symptoms and core symptom clusters.14 It has been widely applied for symptom management for chronic diseases, such as Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)-infected patients, cancer patients, and psychiatric patients.15, 16, 17 For instance, Jing et al.18 reported a range of symptoms in breast cancer patients receiving endocrine therapy and identified that the node strengths for mood swings and irritability ranked among the top two, exerting the most influence on other symptoms.

Other symptoms in the network can be activated by the core symptom, which may help target interventions to provide effective symptom management.19 Nevertheless, few studies have explored symptom networks during chemotherapy in patients with gynecologic cancers, and there is a lack of systematic research on the identification of core symptoms and core symptom clusters. Hence, our study aimed to (1) determine symptom clusters during chemotherapy in patients with gynecologic cancer, (2) identify core symptoms and core symptom clusters through network analysis.

Methods

Study design and participants

Convenience sampling was used to select patients with gynecologic cancer hospitalized for chemotherapy in the Department of Gynecology at the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University from December 2023 to June 2024. The inclusion criteria for the study population were (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of gynecologic cancer and completion of at least one chemotherapy session; (3) no history of psychiatric diseases and consciousness disorders, with normal language expression, and stable condition; (4) those who voluntarily participated and cooperated with the investigation after understanding the purpose of the study. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with other cancers, HIV/Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS), severe organ failure, and blood system diseases; (2) those involved in other research programs. According to the sample size estimation method for network analysis,20 if there are P nodes in the network, the sample size needs to be at least P+P(P-1)/2. The scale used in this study included 13 symptoms, i.e., P = 13, thus requiring a sample size of at least 91 cases.

Data collection

The researchers, with the support of the hospital and the appropriate department, screened the participants in strict accordance with the selection criteria. Questionnaires were conducted in the gynecology ward. The aims, meaning, and content of the research were explained to each participant before the survey commenced. After obtaining each patient's informed consent, the trained investigators used a unified questionnaire to collect information, asking patients to complete it on the spot. For participants unable to complete the questionnaire in person, the researcher filled it out on their behalf by asking questions. The questionnaires consisted of an 11-item demographic questionnaire and a 13-symptom MD Anderson Symptom Questionnaire. Typically, each participant completed the survey within 10–15 minutes. The investigators promptly checked for missing items on the questionnaires and sought the cooperation of participants to fill them in. Questionnaires were distributed to 230 participants, and 221 valid questionnaires were collected after excluding those that could not be completed due to physical discomfort.

Measures

Demographic and disease-related characteristics

Demographic and disease-related characteristics were collected using a structured questionnaire. Demographic variables included age, body mass index (BMI), residence, education, employment status, monthly household income per capita, marital status, medical payment method, menopause, smoking history, and alcohol history. Clinical variables were extracted from electronic medical records, such as cancer type, disease course, cancer stage, treatment history, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), chemotherapy cycle, and chemotherapy regimen.

The Chinese version of MD Anderson Symptom Inventory, MDASI-C

This scale, developed by MD Anderson Cancer Center, was adapted and validated by Wang et al.21 It consisted of two parts. The first part, comprising a total of 13 items, evaluated the severity of common symptoms of cancer in the past 24 hours, with each item scored on a scale of 0 (no symptoms) to 10 (the worst possible severity); Part 2 assessed the degree to which symptoms interfered with the patient's daily life, but only the first part was used in this study. Higher scores indicated more severe symptoms. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient for this study was 0.893.

Data analysis

Covariates

SPSS 26.0 software was used for data analysis. Results were deemed significant at a P-value of <0.05. First, extrinsic factors affecting patients' symptoms were identified. Sociodemographic and disease-related variables were described using frequency and percentage. Since the symptom severity score was not normally distributed, the Median (P25, P75) was used to describe it, with the mean as an auxiliary evaluation tool. Differences between these factors were analyzed by independent samples t test, one-way ANOVA, and nonparametric test. In addition, we tested the significance of exogenous factors on overall symptom severity using multiple linear regression analyses in which significant covariates were included in the network. Overall symptom severity was the sum of the 13 symptom severity scores investigated.

Symptom clusters

Exploratory factor analysis was performed to identify symptom clusters. Combined with the method of varimax rotation, common factors (i.e., symptom clusters) were extracted. The extraction criteria were as follows: (1) eigenvalues ≥ 1; (2) each factor contained two or more items with a loading ≥ 0.4; (3) when there were multiple loadings ≥ 0.4 for the same symptom, the factor with the maximum loading was considered the primary one.

Network estimation

R 4.3.2 was used for complex network analysis. We used the "qgraph" package in R software. In symptom networks, Spearman correlation was used to assess the relationships among symptoms and symptom groups. The network was constructed based on the severity score of symptoms and the standardized severity score of symptom clusters. The Gaussian graph model was used for continuous variables. The MGM model was used to incorporate covariates identified by multiple linear regression into network models. Undirected weighted networks were constructed. The "EBICglasso" method was used to narrow the network and eliminate false relationships. Finally, the network was visualized in which nodes represented symptoms or clusters of symptoms. The lines between the nodes were the edges of the network, and the thicker the edges and the darker their color, the stronger the correlation between symptoms; if there was a positive correlation between two nodes, it was represented by a blue edge. Negative correlations were indicated by red edges.

Centrality estimation

The centrality of a node is a metric that objectively quantifies the importance of a single node, which mainly includes strength, closeness, and betweenness. Strength centrality refers to the weighted sum of all connections a node has with other nodes and can be used to measure the importance of a node in the network. Closeness centrality is the reciprocal of the average length of the shortest path for a node to reach all nodes in the network. Betweenness centrality is the frequency with which a node appears in all the shortest paths in a network. The higher the centrality index, the more central the node is to the network.15 Since a previous study showed that strength centrality was more reliable in estimating core symptoms,22 we reported results focusing on the interpretation of core symptoms by nodal strength centrality. The higher the strength centrality of a node, the more likely the symptom is to occur in conjunction with other symptoms, and the stronger its influence is throughout the network.19

Accuracy and stability estimation

We used the R bootnet package. Accuracy and stability are indicators of the network's fit. Accuracy indicates how precise the edge weights are in the network, with edge weights representing the closeness of the inter-symptom connections.20 We assessed accuracy by calculating 95% confidence intervals for edge weights using bootstrapping. Stability is an indicator of the degree of stability of the centrality of nodes in a network and can be quantified by the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient). It is generally accepted that CS coefficients > 0.5 represent better stability, at least 0.25.20

Network comparison test

Subgroup analysis was performed in the network. The network difference test was used to analyze differences between symptom cluster networks across various patient characteristics.

Ethical considerations

This study was allowed by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University (IRB No. JNMS00000232041) and was conducted in strict compliance with local legal requirements. All participants signed informed consent before the study commenced.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 221 patients with gynecologic cancer, ages ranged from 21 to 82 years (58.73 ± 11.50). Approximately 60.6% of participants had a BMI between 18.5 and 23.9 kg/m2, and 57.9% lived in urban areas. Nearly half of the participants were retirees (49.8%), and 37.6% had an average monthly household income of 2000–3999 yuan. The majority of participants were married (91.9%), postmenopausal (86.9%), and had no history of smoking (95.5%) or drinking (90.5%). Among all participants, there were more patients with cervical (38.5%) and ovarian cancer (38.5%) and fewer patients with endometrial (10.9%) and other categories (12.2%). Participants with stage III disease accounted for 44.3%, followed by stage II (18.1%), stage IV (15.8%), and stage I (15.4%). A total of 54.3% of participants had undergone surgery combined with chemotherapy. In 221 patients, the overall symptom severity score did not conform to the normal distribution, ranging from 0 to 82 points (32.89 ± 16.78). Further details are provided in Table 1.

Composition of symptom clusters

Exploratory factor analysis (Table 4) identified four symptom clusters with factor loadings > 0.4, and a cumulative variance contribution rate of 72.3%. The data in this study were confirmed suitable for factor analysis (KMO = 0.844, P < 0.001). We named the following clusters of symptoms: the sickness behavior symptom cluster (i.e., pain, fatigue, disturbed sleep, and drowsiness), the gastrointestinal symptom cluster (i.e., nausea, vomiting, and lack of appetite), the psychological symptom cluster (i.e., distress and sadness), and the side-effect symptom cluster (i.e., shortness of breath, difficulty remembering, dry mouth, and numbness).

Symptom and symptom cluster networks

Networks of symptoms are shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2. In the network without covariates, strong correlations were observed between distress (S8) and sadness (S9) (r = 0.64), between fatigue (S2) and drowsiness (S4) (r = 0.54), between nausea (S5) and lack of appetite (S7) (r = 0.49), and between nausea (S5) and vomiting (S6) (r = 0.36). When clinical covariates were added to the network, some edge weights changed slightly, but there was little change in the links between symptoms. Furthermore, we observed some connections between chemotherapy sessions (F2) and numbness (S13) and between chemotherapy sessions (F2) and difficulty remembering (S11). Associations between the other two covariates and other symptoms were weak. For more details on the edge weights in each network, see Supplementary Table S1, Table S2, and Table S3. Our centrality metric visualization (see Fig. 1B and Fig. 2B) showed that regardless of whether covariates were controlled, fatigue (S2) (rs = 1.28 vs. rs = 1.37) was the core symptom, followed by lack of appetite (S7) (rs = 0.99 vs. rs = 1.02) and sadness (S9) (rs = 0.99 vs. rs = 0.97). Accuracy and stability of the network

The 95% confidence interval, calculated using the non-parametric bootstrap method, was narrow, indicating a high accuracy of the network (Supplementary Fig. S1). As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, the CS coefficients of node strength were 0.593, 0.593, and 0.674 for the networks; these findings indicated that the networks exhibited robust stability.

Network comparison test

The results of the network comparison test (Supplementary Table S5) showed no statistically significant differences in the symptom cluster network across various patient characteristics, including age, International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage, residence, time of disease and CCI index (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The primary purpose of our research was to explore the complex relationships among symptoms and symptom clusters in patients with gynecologic cancer receiving chemotherapy using symptom network analysis. Our main finding was that fatigue was the core symptom. The disease behavior symptom cluster (i.e., pain, fatigue, disturbed sleep, and drowsiness) formed the core symptom cluster in the network we constructed. These results suggest that the development of interventions targeting clusters of disease behavior symptoms in clinical care may be important in improving the overall symptom experience for patients with gynecologic cancer.

Of the 13 symptoms assessed, fatigue was the most common and serious. This aligned with certain research findings, which have noted that fatigue in patients during chemotherapy greatly affected their quality of life.11,23 Our study concluded that fatigue was the core symptom whether or not clinical covariates were controlled. In a previous study of symptom networks, Rooij et al.16 explored the complex symptomatic network structure of cancer patients and showed that fatigue was the core symptom, with the highest intensity and strongest connections. Liang et al.24 also found similar results in breast cancer patients during chemotherapy. This may be partly due to the activation of pro-inflammatory cytokine networks by tumors and radical therapies that cause fatigue through cytokine signaling in the central nervous system.25,26 Fatigue may persist for months or even years post-treatment in cancer patients.27 Nevertheless, patient fatigue is often underestimated or undertreated in clinical practice.28 These emphasize the importance of fatigue-specific interventions, which should be focused on clinically to ensure improvement of physical symptom experience and therapeutic outcomes.

We recognized the following four symptom clusters: sickness behavior, gastrointestinal, psychological, and side-effect symptom clusters. However, there were some inconsistencies with previous studies regarding the types and numbers of symptom clusters. Pozzar et al.8 used factor analysis to explore symptom clusters before and after the second and third rounds of chemotherapy, including gastrointestinal, perimenopausal, psychological, respiratory-related, and weight change symptom clusters. Haryani et al.12 used factor analysis to identify six symptom clusters, namely nutritional, hormone-related, emotional, pain-related, fatigue-related, and body image symptom clusters, during chemotherapy in patients with gynecologic cancer. The differences in study results may be attributed to the different tools and analytical methods employed. Pozzar et al.8 identified symptom clusters from symptom severity, distress, and frequency through the Memory Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS), six other symptoms common in cancer patients, and a self-reported comorbidity questionnaire. Haryani et al.12 assessed 32 symptoms using the Memory Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS). We analyzed symptom severity using the MD Anderson Symptom Assessment Scale. The analysis yielded slightly different types and numbers of symptom clusters due to the varying components of symptoms included in the assessments.

The results showed that the node of the disease behavior symptom cluster had the highest strength and was the core symptom cluster. Multiple studies have confirmed a stable association of pain, fatigue, and sleep disturbance in symptom clusters in patients with gynecologic cancer,29,30 a finding that was consistent with our own result. Zhou et al.29 investigated the symptom cluster in cervical cancer patients and showed that this cluster had the highest severity and was a better predictor of the patient's health. This conclusion further highlights the importance of focusing on this symptom cluster. Effective interventions in this symptom cluster can significantly improve the physical and mental state of patients.31, 32, 33 Within this symptom cluster, our results showed a strong correlation between fatigue and drowsiness. Studies have likewise suggested that cancer and cancer treatment can cause fatigue and drowsiness, which may be associated with underlying behavioral and/or biological factors.34 Behaviorally, patients with higher levels of fatigue tend to be more sleepy; biologically, the two may share common mechanisms, such as inflammatory factors that some studies have suggested.35 Additionally, sleep disorders can lead to greater daytime fatigue, low mood, and a lower pain threshold.36,37 According to the symptom network theory, the node with the greatest strength has the greatest influence in the entire network. Our findings also showed strong interactions between the disease behavior symptom cluster and other symptom clusters. Moreover, this symptom cluster included the core symptom of the network, fatigue. Hence, additional interventions focusing on the symptom cluster of disease behavior may be an effective way to alleviate the total symptom burden for patients.

Followed by the cluster of side effects. After including covariates in the network, we observed links between chemotherapy cycles and numbness, as well as between chemotherapy cycles and difficulty remembering. Probably due to the neurotoxicity caused by the chemotherapeutic agents and multiple hospitalizations, the severity of neurologically related symptoms increasing with the number of chemotherapy cycles.38 In addition, over half of our study participants received a combination of paclitaxel and platinum drugs. These, along with several classical chemotherapeutic agents such as platinum, periwinkle alkaloids, and taxanes, are recognized causes of peripheral and central nervous toxicity.39,40

Next is the cluster of psychological symptoms. Scholars also found a similar result in patients with gynecologic cancer.8,41 At the same time, our observations elucidated a strong correlation between the psychological symptom cluster and the disease-behavioral symptom cluster. Persistent adverse psychological reactions can influence fatigue and pain,42 and increased fatigue is associated with a subsequent increase in depression.9 This may even lead to delays or interruptions in the patient's treatment. However, our study examined only distress and sadness, limiting our ability to fully analyze the cluster of psychological symptoms in patients. The last one was the gastrointestinal symptom cluster. Our findings suggested that the incidence of lack of appetite was second only to fatigue and also ranked high in severity. Especially for patients with wasting diseases (e.g., tumors), decreased appetite, malnutrition, and electrolyte imbalance can be a dire threat to the patient's health.

Implications for nursing practice and research

Our research has shown that fatigue was the core symptom, and the sickness behavior symptom cluster was the core symptom cluster. These findings might assist caregivers in understanding the interrelationships among symptoms during chemotherapy for patients with gynecologic cancer. Future interventions could target core symptom clusters, such as disease behavior symptom cluster, and evaluate their effects.

Limitations

The current research has certain limitations. Firstly, we investigated only 13 symptoms using the MDASI-C, which may not cover all the symptoms experienced by patients with gynecologic cancers during chemotherapy. Future studies should develop and employ specific scales dedicated to investigating symptoms. Secondly, the sample size of the study was not particularly large; researchers can appropriately expand the sample size in the future. Additionally, network analysis models with different demographic characteristics, pathological types, disease stages, and treatment modes should be compared so as to provide a more individualized reference for the management of symptom clusters. Finally, we only conducted a cross-sectional study, and longitudinal research is necessary to explore the dynamic changes in the symptom network across chemotherapy cycles.

Conclusions

In summary, we found that patients with gynecologic cancer frequently reported symptoms of fatigue during chemotherapy, and fatigue was always the core symptom with or without adjusting for clinical covariates. Among the categories we identified, the sickness behavior cluster was the core symptom cluster. These results could inform the development of targeted interventions by clinical nurses that may improve symptom burden and further enhance patient quality of life.
