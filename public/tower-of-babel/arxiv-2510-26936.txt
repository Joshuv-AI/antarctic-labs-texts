Comparing the numbers of subforests and subgraph-degree-tuples
Sergei Shteiner, Pavel Shteyner

Abstract
We enumerate the row-column-sums of all square tridiagonal ( 0 , 1 ) (0,1) -matrices and prove that their count coincides with OEIS A022026 – the number of acyclic subgraphs of the complete 2 × n 2\times n grid graph. We then extend this correspondence in two independent directions: 1. admitting larger sets of matrix entries, and 2. relaxing the tridiagonal support to broader prescribed sparsity patterns. The latter leads us to conjecture that, for any bipartite graph G G , the number of its acyclic subgraphs equals the number of degree sequences realized by subgraphs of G G . Moreover, for any non-bipartite graph, the former should be strictly smaller than the latter. We discuss several general approaches and prove these hypotheses for cactus graphs and generalized book graphs.

Introduction

The classical Gale-Ryser theorem characterizes the integer vectors that can arise as the row- and column-sums of an
m\times n
binary matrix; see
Brualdi_Ryser_1991
.
In this paper, we focus on the
tridiagonal
case: square
(0,1)
-matrices whose nonzero entries lie only on the main and the two adjacent diagonals.

As it turns out, the number of distinct row-column-sums of such matrices coincides with OEIS
OEIS
sequence
A022026
. This sequence also counts the acyclic subgraphs of the complete
2\times n
grid graph
\mathrm{Grid}^{2}_{n}
(Definition
3.7
). Despite the absence of a clear bijection between these objects, we show that this is not a mere coincidence.

On the one hand, we show that introducing colors into
Grid^{2}_{n}
may correspond to expanding the matrix entry set. On the other hand, viewing binary matrices as biadjacency matrices of bipartite graphs translates the aforementioned relation into a purely graph-theoretic one: the number of acyclic subgraphs of
Grid^{2}_{n}
equals the number of distinct vertex-degree tuples realized by its subgraphs.

We conjecture that this equality is satisfied if and only if the graph is bipartite.
Moreover, for any non-bipartite graph, the number of acyclic subgraphs should be strictly smaller than the number of vertex-degree tuples. In addition to
Grid^{2}_{n}
, we prove these conjectures for several illustrative classes of graphs: complete bipartite graphs (based on the proof of Speyer
Speyer
), cactus graphs, and generalized book graphs.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the necessary notation. In Section
3
, we find the number of distinct row-column-sums of binary tridiagonal
matrices and establish the connection to
Grid^{2}_{n}
. Section
4
counts the number of
k
-colored acyclic subgraphs of
Grid^{2}_{n}
.
Section
5
interprets the results in graph language and discusses the main conjectures, Hypotheses
5
and
5
. In Section
5.1
, we propose general reduction techniques. In Sections
5.2
and
5.3
, we prove the conjectures for cactus graphs and generalized book graphs, respectively. Finally, the Appendix contains more technical proofs, including those for the non-bipartite case.

3
Row-column-sums of tridiagonal matrices

In this section, we compute the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n})
, i.e. the number of realizable row-column-sums of binary tridiagonal matrices. We prove that
(|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n})|)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is the number sequence
OEIS A022026
. Namely,

\begin{cases}|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{1})|=2;\\
|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{2})|=15;\\
|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n})|=8|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n-1})|-4|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n-2})|\text{ for }n>2.\end{cases}

(1)

Remark
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
establish the initial terms.

Remark 3.1
.

There are exactly two matrices in
\mathbbold{3}_{1}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{1})=\{((0),(0)),((1),(1))
}
. In particular,
|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{1})|=2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{2})|=15
.

Proof.

There are
16
matrices in
\mathbbold{3}_{2}
. Assume that distinct
A,B\in\mathbbold{3}_{2}
have the same row and column sums. Then
D=A-B
is a
2\times 2

(0,\pm 1)
-matrix with zero row and column sums. Every entry of
D
is nonzero, since otherwise
\mathbf{r}^{D}=\mathbf{c}^{D}=0
forces
D=0
, which contradicts
A\neq B
. Thus, up to a sign,
D=\left[\begin{matrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{matrix}\right]
. Then
\{A,B\}=\ \left\{\left[\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right],\left[\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right]\right\}.

Finally, exactly two matrices in
\mathbbold{3}_{2}
have the same row and column sums and thus
|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{2})|=15
.
∎

Recall that for a matrix
A\in M_{n}
we denote its top-left submatrix of order
n-1
by
A^{\prime}
.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that a row-column-sum tuple
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})\in\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n})

extends
a row-column-sum tuple
(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{c}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n-1})
, if there exists
A\in\mathbbold{3}_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
such that
A^{\prime}~\in~\mathbbold{3}_{n-1}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
.

We call such
A
an
extending matrix
. Note that

\begin{cases}\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{i}\text{ for }i\leq n-2;\\
\mathbf{r}_{n-1}=\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{n-1}+a_{n-1,n};\\
\mathbf{r}_{n}=a_{n,n-1}+a_{n,n}.\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\begin{cases}\mathbf{c}_{i}=\mathbf{c}^{\prime}_{i}\text{ for }i\leq n-2;\\
\mathbf{c}_{n-1}=\mathbf{c}^{\prime}_{n-1}+a_{n,n-1};\\
\mathbf{c}_{n}=a_{n-1,n}+a_{n,n}.\end{cases}

(2)

Clearly, for
n\geq 2
, every
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})\in\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n})
extends at least one row-column-sum tuple from
\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n-1})
. The following lemma shows, when this number is exactly one.

Lemma 3.4
.

A row-column-sum tuple
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})\in\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n})
with
(\mathbf{r}_{n},\mathbf{c}_{n})\neq(1,1)
extends exactly one row-column-sum tuple from
\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n-1})
.

Proof.

Assume that
(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
extends
(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{c}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n-1})
and let
A
be an extending matrix.
Without loss of generality assume that
\mathbf{r}_{n}\neq 1
. There are two possibilities:

Case 1.
If
\mathbf{r}_{n}=0
, then
a_{n,n-1}=a_{n,n}=0
. In this case
a_{n-1,n}=\mathbf{c}_{n}
.

Case 2.
If
\mathbf{r}_{n}=2
, then
a_{n,n-1}=a_{n,n}=1
. In this case
a_{n-1,n}=\mathbf{c}_{n}-1
.

Therefore, in both cases we can uniquely determine
a_{n-1,n},a_{n,n-1},a_{n,n}
. Then by (
2
)
(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
is also unique.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Any matrix
A\in\mathbbold{3}_{n}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
with
(\mathbf{r}_{n},\mathbf{c}_{n})=(1,1)
is either of the form
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}&&\vdots\\
&&1\\
\cdots&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\text{ or }\left[\begin{smallmatrix}&&\vdots\\
&&0\\
\cdots&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let

. Then
|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n})|=8|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n-1})|-4|\mathcal{GR}(\mathbbold{3}_{n-2})|
.

Proof.

We use the inclusion-exclusion approach.

I.
There are

ways to extend any

II.
Assume that

. It follows that

.

Let
A

be matrices that extend
(\mathbf{r}^{1},\mathbf{c}^{1})
and

(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})
. Then

&&1\\
\cdots&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]\text{ and }B=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}&&\vdots\\
&&0\\
\cdots&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right].

(3)

It follows that

The following theorem provides an important property of two subgraphs with the same vertex-degree tuples. Recall that an Euler graph is a graph with an Euler circuit.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
be two distinct subgraphs of a graph
G
. Define a subgraph
H
of
G
by

E(H)=E(H_{1})\,\triangle\,E(H_{2})=(E(H_{1})\setminus E(H_{2}))\cup(E(H_{2})\setminus E(H_{1})).

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

d(H_{1})=d(H_{2})
;

2.

Every connected component of
H
is an Euler graph. Moreover, in every component we can choose an Euler circuit in which edges from
E(H_{1})\setminus E(H_{2})
and edges from
E(H_{2})\setminus E(H_{1})
alternate. In particular, this circuit is of even length.

Proof.

Since
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are distinct, the edge set
E(H)
is non-empty. Let us call the edges in
E(H_{1})\setminus E(H_{2})
red and the edges in
E(H_{2})\setminus E(H_{1})
blue.

First, assume that
d(H_{1})=d(H_{2})
. Since
d_{H_{1}}(v)=d_{H_{2}}(v)
for any
v\in V(G)
, we conclude that every vertex
v
is incident to the same number of red and blue edges. In particular, the degree of each vertex in
H
is even. This is exactly the criterion for existence of a color-alternating Euler circuit in every connected component (Kotzig’s theorem,
(
BangJensen
, Theorem 7.1)
).

Now assume that every connected component of
H
forms an alternating Euler circuit. Then for every
v\in V(G)
the number of red and blue edges incident with
v
in
H
is the same. Thus
d_{H_{1}}(v)-d_{H_{2}}(v)=0
and therefore
d(H_{1})=d(H_{2})
.
∎

As a consequence, we can now easily obtain the following result for acyclic graphs.

Proposition 5.8
.

Every acyclic graph is an
\mathsf{FED}
-graph.

Proof.

Every subgraph of an acyclic graph is also acyclic. Therefore, it suffices to show that all subgraphs of an acyclic graph have distinct vertex-degree tuples.

Assume the contrary: suppose there exist two distinct subgraphs
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
of an acyclic graph
G
such that
d(H_{1})=d(H_{2})
. Consider another subgraph
H\subseteq G
defined by
E(H)=E(H_{1})\,\triangle\,E(H_{2})
. Then the edge set
E(H)
is non-empty, and
H
contains an Euler circuit by Theorem
5.7
, which contradicts the acyclicity of
G
.
∎

Remark 5.9

Acyclic graphs are bipartite.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let

\displaystyle\text{If $n$ is even, then }|\mathcal{F}(C)|=|\mathcal{D}(C)|=2^{n}-1;

\displaystyle\text{If $n$ is odd, then }|\mathcal{F}(C)|=2^{n}-1<|\mathcal{D}(C)|=2^{n}.

Proof.

Every subgraph of a cycle is acyclic except for the cycle itself. Therefore
|\mathcal{F}(C)|=2^{n}-1
.

Now let us find
|\mathcal{D}(C)|
. Denote the edges of
C
by
e_{1}\coloneqq(1,2),\ldots,e_{n-1}\coloneqq(n-1,n),e_{n}\coloneqq(n,1)
. For a fixed edge

, a subgraph
G
of
C
is uniquely determined by
d(G)
together with the information whether
e_{i}\in E(G)
. It follows that if
d\in\mathcal{D}(C)
and
d_{i}\in\{0,2\}
for some
i
, then there is a unique subgraph
G
of
C
with

. This is because
\begin{cases}e_{i}\notin E(G),\text{ if }d_{i}=0;\\
e_{i}\in E(G),\text{ if }d_{i}=2.\end{cases}

It remains to consider the case
d=(1,\ldots,1)
. This can only happen if
n
is even, since
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
is always even. Now, for an even
n
, there are exactly two subgraphs
G_{1},G_{2}
of
C
with the vertex-degree tuple
(1,\ldots,1)
. They are given by
E(G_{1})=\{e_{1},e_{3},\ldots,e_{n-1}\}
and
E(G_{2})=\{e_{2},e_{4},\ldots,e_{n}\}
.

Finally, if
n
is odd, then all subgraphs of
C
have different vertex-degree tuples. In this case
|\mathcal{D}(C)|=2^{n}
. If
n
is even, then there are exactly two subgraphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
that have the same vertex degrees. Thus
|\mathcal{D}(C)|=2^{n}-1
.
∎

Proposition 5.11
.

Let graphs

|\mathcal{F}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})|\;=\;|\mathcal{F}(G_{1})|\,|\mathcal{F}(G_{2})|.

Proof.

Consider a subgraph
H\subseteq G_{1}\cup G_{2}
. Denote
H_{1}=G_{1}\cap H
and
H_{2}=G_{2}\cap H
. Then
H=H_{1}\cup H_{2}
. If either
H_{1}
or
H_{2}
is not acyclic, then
H
is also not acyclic. On the other hand, every cycle in
H
completely lies in
H_{1}
or
H_{2}
, since a cycle cannot pass
v
more than twice.

Thus
H
is acyclic if and only if
H_{1},H_{2}
are acyclic. It follows that
|\mathcal{F}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})|\;=\;|\mathcal{F}(G_{1})|\,|\mathcal{F}(G_{2})|
.

It turns out we cannot as easily perform the same factorization for

Definition 5.12

Let

d_{1}(w)=d_{2}(w)\text{ for all }w\neq v\text{ implies }d_{1}(v)=d_{2}(v).

.

In a bipartite graph every vertex is degree-determinable.

Proof.

Denote the two parts of a bipartite graph

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}d(v_{i})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}d(w_{j})
for any

. This equality allows us to determine the degree of a vertex, knowing the degrees of the rest of the vertices.

is degree-determinable in
G_{1}
or in
G_{2}
.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
satisfy

V(G_{1})\cap V(G_{2})=\{v\},\qquad E(G_{1})\cap E(G_{2})=\varnothing.

If
v
is degree-determinable in
G_{1}
, then

|\mathcal{D}(G_{1}\cup G_{2})|\;=\;|\mathcal{D}(G_{1})|\,|\mathcal{D}(G_{2})|.

Proof.

Write
G:=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
.
Define

\Phi:\mathcal{D}(G_{1})\times\mathcal{D}(G_{2})\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(G)\quad\text{by}\quad\Phi(d_{G_{1}},d_{G_{2}})(u)=\begin{cases}d_{G_{1}}(u),\ u\in V(G_{1})\setminus\{v\},\\[4.0pt]
d_{G_{2}}(u),\ u\in V(G_{2})\setminus\{v\},\\[4.0pt]
d_{G_{1}}(v)+d_{G_{2}}(v),\ u=v.\end{cases}

Let us show that
\Phi
is bijective.

Surjectivity.

Take any subgraph
K
of
G
.
Setting
\begin{cases}d_{1}:=d(K\cap G_{1})\in\mathcal{D}(G_{1}),\\
d_{2}:=d(K\cap G_{2})\in\mathcal{D}(G_{2})\end{cases}
gives

\Phi(d_{1},d_{2})=d(K)
, so
\Phi
is surjective.

Injectivity.

Let
d_{1},d^{\prime}_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(G_{1})
and
d_{2},d^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(G_{2})
. Suppose that
\Phi(d_{1},d_{2})=\Phi(d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2})\eqqcolon d
.
The two tuples agree on every vertex of
V(G_{1})\setminus\{v\}
and of

V(G_{2})\setminus\{v\}
, hence

\begin{cases}d_{1}(w)=d^{\prime}_{1}(w)=d(w)\text{ for any }w\in V(G_{1})\setminus\{v\};\\
d_{2}(w)=d^{\prime}_{2}(w)=d(w)\text{ for any }w\in V(G_{2})\setminus\{v\}.\end{cases}

But since
v
is degree-determinable in
G_{1}
, it must be that
d_{1}(v)=d^{\prime}_{1}(v)
. Hence
d_{2}(v)=d(v)-d_{1}(v)=d(v)-d^{\prime}_{1}(v)=d^{\prime}_{2}(v)
. Finally
(d_{1},d_{2})=(d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2})
and therefore
\Phi
is injective.

Thus
\Phi
is bijective and so
|\mathcal{D}(G)|\;=\;|\mathcal{D}(G_{1})|\,|\mathcal{D}(G_{2})|
.
∎

5.2
Cactus graphs

In this section, using the techniques developed in Section
5.1
, we prove Hypothesis
5
for cactus graphs. A connected graph is called a

if every edge belongs to at most one cycle.

Proposition 5.21
.

If
G
is a bipartite cactus, then it is an
\mathsf{FED}
-graph. In other words,
|\mathcal{F}(G)|=|\mathcal{D}(G)|
.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on the number of edges of
G
. The statement is trivial for
|E(G)|\leq 2
: if
G
is acyclic, then
|\mathcal{F}(G)|=|\mathcal{D}(G)|
by Proposition
5.8
.

Assume that the statement is true whenever
|E(G)|\leq m
. Let
G
be a cactus with

edges. Once again, if
G
is acyclic, then
|\mathcal{F}(G)|=|\mathcal{D}(G)|
. If
G
is a cycle, then it must be even and the result follows from Proposition
5.10
. Otherwise, the connectivity implies that there is at least one articulation point

in

(take any cycle, it must have a vertex that does not belong to only that cycle).
In this case,

can be viewed as a union of two smaller cacti that intersect in

.
Then Corollary
5.18
allows us to reduce the computations to the respective subgraphs, and the induction hypothesis concludes the proof.
∎

Together with Corollary
A.2
this proves Hypothesis
5
for cactus graphs:

Theorem 5.22
.

All bipartite cacti are
\mathsf{FED}
-graphs. All non-bipartite cacti are
\mathsf{FLD}
-graphs.

In other words,
|\mathcal{F}(G)|\leq|\mathcal{D}(G)|
for any cactus
G
and
G
is bipartite if and only if
|\mathcal{F}(G)|=|\mathcal{D}(G)|
.

5.3
Generalized book graphs

In Section
5.2
, we have proved Hypothesis
5
for cactus graphs, i.e. graphs in which every edge can lie on at most one cycle.
In this section, we prove Hypothesis
5
for generalized book graphs, which is the natural next step.

Definition 5.23
.

A
generalized book graph
, see
carlson2006books
, is a graph formed by cycles sharing exactly one edge (known as the
spine
or
base
of the book). Each cycle is called a
page
.

For a
k
-page generalized book, let
P_{i}
denote the subgraph of the
i
-th page obtained by deleting the base edge. We call the vertices not incident to the base
internal
.

In graph theory, a “book graph” may refer to several kinds of graphs formed by multiple cycles sharing an edge. For example, it is usually assumed that the cycles are of the same length. So the word “generalized” in the name refers to the fact that the lengths of cycles are arbitrary.

It is relatively easy to find
|\mathcal{F}(B)|
for a generalized book graph
B
.

Proposition 5.24
.

Let
B
be a generalized book graph formed by
k
cycles of lengths
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
. Denote
R\coloneqq\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}(2^{c_{i}-1}-1)
. Then

|\mathcal{F}(B)|=2\cdot R+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{R}{2^{c_{j}-1}-1}.

(6)

Proof.

In order to find the number of acyclic subgraphs of
B
, we count the forests containing the base and not containing the base separately.

In order to build a forest that contains the base, we can independently choose a subgraph of
P_{i}
for every page
i
. The only restriction is that we can’t use
P_{i}
itself, since together with the base it would form a cycle. Since
P_{i}
is an
(c_{i}-1)
-edge graph, we can choose
2^{c_{i}-1}-1
subgraphs of
P_{i}
. Thus the number of forests that contain the base is

\prod_{i=1}^{k}(2^{c_{i}-1}-1)=R.

For forests that do not contain the base the situation is slightly different. Each of the
R
forests with the base can be turned into a forest without the base by removing it. But now we have an additional option: a forest without the base can contain all edges of exactly one
P_{i}
. A subgraph with complete
P_{i},P_{j}
is not acyclic, since together they form a cycle. Once we choose
P_{i}
, we choose the subgraphs of the remaining pages as in the previous case.
Thus the number of forests without the base is

R+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{R}{2^{c_{j}-1}-1}.

Together we obtain (
6
).
∎

For
|\mathcal{D}(B)|
the situation is more complex. In particular, we need to take into account the numbers of odd and even pages in
B
. We start with a bipartite case, i.e. when all pages are even cycles. We first make the following technical observation.

Lemma 5.25
.

Let
P
be a path
(l,v_{1},\ldots,v_{n},r)
, where
n
is even. Let
d\in\mathcal{D}(P)
. Assume that
d(v_{i})
is known for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. There are two cases:

1.

If
d(v_{i})\in\{0,2\}
for some

, then we can uniquely determine
d(l)
and
d(r)
.

2.

If
d(v_{i})=1
for every
i
, then there are exactly two possibilities: either
d(l)=d(r)=0
, or
d(l)=d(r)=1
.

Moreover, in
\mathcal{D}(P)
there are
2^{n+1}-2
tuples of the first type.

Proof.

Knowing degrees of all internal vertices (
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
) of a subgraph
H\subseteq P
and whether a certain edge belongs to
E(H)
is enough to uniquely determine
H
. This is the case if
d(v_{i})\neq 1
for some
i
: in this case we can determine whether the edges incident to
v_{i}
belong to a subgraph, and thus determine the entire subgraph uniquely.

There are exactly two subgraphs with
d(v_{i})=1
for every
i=1,\ldots,n
. The first one is obtained by taking every second edge starting from
(l,v_{1})
. The second one is obtained by taking every second edge starting from
(v_{1},v_{2})
. Since
n
is even, we obtain that in the former case
d(l)=d(r)=1
, while in the latter case
d(l)=d(r)=0
, see the illustration below:

l

v_{2}

v_{4}

v_{2}
v_{3}
v_{4}

We have shown that vertex-degree tuples of all but two subgraphs of

satisfy the first condition. Note that since

is acyclic, all its subgraphs have distinct vertex-degree tuples by Theorem
5.7
. Thus in
\mathcal{D}(P)
there are
2^{n+1}-2
tuples satisfying the first condition.
∎

Proposition 5.26
.

Let
B
be a bipartite generalized book graph formed by
k
cycles of lengths
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
. For every subset
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
denote

T_{S}\coloneqq\prod\limits_{i\notin S}(2^{c_{i}-1}-2).

Then

|\mathcal{D}(B)|=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}(|S|+2)T_{S}.

(7)

Proof.

Since
B
is bipartite, every
c_{i}
must be even. Denote the base of
B
by
e=(l,r)
.

Step 1.
Observe that
B
is the union of
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
and the base. Given a subgraph
H
of
B
, we can uniquely determine
d\coloneqq d(H)
by
d_{1}\coloneqq d(P_{1}\cap H),\ldots,d_{k}\coloneqq d(P_{k}\cap H)
and whether
e\in E(H)
. Namely,

d(v)=\begin{cases}d_{i}(v),\ v\in V(P_{i})\setminus\{l,r\};\\
\delta+d_{1}(l)+\ldots+d_{k}(l),\ v=l;\\
\delta+d_{1}(r)+\ldots+d_{k}(r),\ v=r,\end{cases}\text{ where}\quad\delta=\begin{cases}1,\ e\in E(H);\\
0,\ e\not\in E(H).\end{cases}

Step 2.
Consider

. There are two possibilities. If

for some internal vertex

of

, then due to Lemma

, we can uniquely determine

and

by the degrees of all the internal vertices. If

for all internal vertices of

, then there are two possibilities:

or
1
.

Step 3.
Assuming that we know the degrees of all internal vertices, how many elements in
\mathcal{D}(B)
match them? That is, given the degrees of the internal vertices, how many options do we have for the degrees of
l,r
? It turns out, the answer depends on how many pages have all internal vertices of degree
1
. We split
\mathcal{D}(B)
by the number of such pages. Consider arbitrary

. Let us find the number of elements in
\mathcal{D}(B)
such that

is the set of pages with all internal vertices of degree

.

Step 4.
First, we can choose the internal-vertex degrees of the remaining

pages in any way, except for all ones. By Lemma

there are

ways to choose the internal degrees of a page

for

. Thus there are

ways to assign internal degrees. Now for each internal-vertex-degree configuration, how many elements in

match it? Since we know the degrees of all internal vertices, the question becomes how many options do we have for the degrees of

\begin{cases}d(l)=\delta+\sum\limits_{i\in S}d_{i}(l)+\sum\limits_{j\notin S}d_{j}(l);\\
d(r)=\delta+\sum\limits_{i\in S}d_{i}(r)+\sum\limits_{j\notin S}d_{j}(r).\end{cases}

(8)

In (

and we can choose

for any

and

Finally, once we take into account every possible subset

|\mathcal{D}(B)|=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}(|S|+2)T_{S}.

∎

.

Consider the case

. Then there are four options for the degrees of

and

Lemma A.3

Define functions

\displaystyle F_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}a_{i};

\displaystyle D_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}\prod_{i\notin S}(a_{i}-1).

Then
F_{1}=D_{1}
.

Proof.

Consider finite sets
A_{i}
of size

, and let
A=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{k}
be their Cartesian product. Then
F_{1}
equals the cardinality of

, i.e., the number of

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
with

.

Now in every

fix an arbitrary element

For each subset

Since the sets

are disjoint and their union is the entire set

|A|=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}|T_{S}|=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}\prod_{i\notin S}(a_{i}-1),

which is exactly the definition of
D_{1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
. Therefore,
F_{1}=D_{1}
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Define functions
F_{2},D_{2}:{\mathbb{N}}^{k}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by

\displaystyle F_{2}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}}{a_{j}};

\displaystyle D_{2}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=\sum\limits_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}|S|\cdot\prod\limits_{i\not\in S}(a_{i}-1).

Then
F_{2}=D_{2}
.

Proof.

For each index
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
write

F_{1}\bigl(a_{1},\dots,a_{j-1},a_{j+1},\dots,a_{k}\bigr)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}a_{i}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}}{a_{j}}.

Hence

F_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}F_{1}\bigl(a_{1},\dots,a_{j-1},a_{j+1},\dots,a_{k}\bigr).

By Lemma
A.3
, each summand equals

D_{1}\bigl(a_{1},\dots,a_{j-1},a_{j+1},\dots,a_{k}\bigr)=\sum_{T\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{j\}}\prod_{i\notin T\cup\{j\}}(a_{i}-1).

Summing these identities over
j
gives

F_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\;\sum_{T\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{j\}}\prod_{i\notin T\cup\{j\}}(a_{i}-1).

Now fix an arbitrary subset
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
.
Every term
\prod_{i\notin S}(a_{i}-1)
appears exactly
|S|
times in the double sum above — once for each index
j\in S
.
Grouping equal terms therefore yields

F_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}}|S|\cdot\prod_{i\notin S}(a_{i}-1)=D_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}).

Thus
F_{2}=D_{2}
.
∎
