Osteochondral lesions of the talus in the pediatric population: A practical narrative review
Authors: Marco Turati, Simone Daniel Gatti, Monika Thüsing, Matteo Guelfi, Marco Crippa, Jaakko Sinikumpu, Marco Bigoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) in children and adolescents are a clinically important cause of persistent ankle pain after trauma and may also be present without a clear inciting event. Because plain radiographs may fail to detect OLTs or may underestimate their severity, modern pediatric pathways rely on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for characterization. However, conventional MRI may be inconclusive regarding osteochondral fragment stability in selected cases. In this setting, magnetic resonance arthrography may provide additional information, although pediatric-specific evidence is limited and its routine use is not supported. Nonoperative management is commonly used as first-line treatment for stable lesions in skeletally immature patients, but reported success is variable and often modest, prompting increasing interest in standardized conservative protocols and prospective comparisons. When surgery is required, selection among retrograde drilling, bone marrow stimulation, and fragment fixation is primarily driven by osteochondral fragment stability, cartilage integrity, fragment viability, and the extent of subchondral bone involvement. Assessment should also include ankle stability because clinically relevant concomitant ligamentous instability may influence treatment selection and outcomes. When ligament stabilization is indicated, minimally invasive arthroscopic techniques may be considered. Cartilage restoration and graft-based techniques may be indicated for large or cystic lesions, high-stage lesions, or revision cases, though pediatric-specific long-term evidence remains limited. This review summarizes contemporary assessment, imaging, treatment options, rehabilitation principles, and long-term considerations to support evidence-informed clinical decision-making.Keywords: Pediatric, adolescent, osteochondral lesion, talus, ankle, osteochondritis dissecans, retrograde drilling, microfracture, fixation, cartilage repair

1. Introduction

Osteochondral lesions of the talus (OLTs) describe focal pathology of talar articular cartilage and the underlying subchondral bone, typically at the talar dome.1–3

In this review, the term OLT is used as an umbrella term encompassing juvenile osteochondritis dissecans and traumatic osteochondral injuries. Juvenile osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) often develops without a single identifiable acute traumatic event and may demonstrate substantial healing potential when the lesion is stable and the physes remain open. Conversely, traumatic osteochondral injuries typically follow an acute event and may require earlier surgical treatment when the fragment is unstable or displaced.1,4,5

Although OLTs are uncommon in absolute terms, they are reported predominantly in adolescents and older children, paralleling exposure to sports and ankle trauma.6,7

A history of ankle sprain or fracture is frequently present, yet a substantial minority of patients report no clear precipitating event, indicating that repetitive microtrauma and nontraumatic factors may contribute.2,4

The talus is largely covered by articular cartilage and has unique vascular characteristics; these features limit its intrinsic repair capacity and may contribute to persistent symptoms in some lesions.3,4

Clinically meaningful OLTs can impair participation in sports, school activities, and daily function, and pediatric-specific patient-reported outcomes are increasingly used to capture this burden.3,6

Long-term joint preservation is particularly important in this population because even small deficits in cartilage or subchondral integrity may accumulate over decades of joint loading.3,7,8

2. Methods

A narrative literature search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from database inception through April 2026. The principal search combined terms related to the condition (“osteochondral lesion”, “osteochondral defect”, “osteochondritis dissecans”, “juvenile osteochondritis dissecans”, “talus”, “talar”, and “ankle”) with terms defining the population (“pediatric”, “paediatric”, “child”, “adolescent”, “juvenile”, and “skeletally immature”). Reference lists of relevant articles were also screened to identify additional studies. English-language, peer-reviewed clinical studies, systematic reviews, consensus statements, and clinically relevant technical articles addressing the diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, or outcomes of talar osteochondral lesions were considered. Pediatric and adolescent studies were prioritized. Evidence from adult populations was included only when pediatric-specific data were limited or unavailable, and its applicability to skeletally immature patients was interpreted cautiously.

3. Clinical assessment and imaging

3.1. History and physical examination

The typical presentation is activity-related ankle pain that persists beyond the expected recovery period following an uncomplicated ankle sprain.2,4

Swelling, stiffness, and poorly localized deep ankle pain are common, and mechanical symptoms such as catching or locking may suggest osteochondral fragment instability or the presence of a loose body.1,2,4

Physical examination findings can be nonspecific, so a systematic assessment of ankle and subtalar motion, joint effusion, focal tenderness, ligamentous stability, hindfoot alignment, and gait is recommended.

Recurrent ankle sprains, cavovarus alignment, and chronic instability can coexist with OLTs and may influence both symptom persistence and treatment selection.1,2,9

The differential diagnosis of pediatric ankle pain includes nonspecific overuse-related conditions, occult fracture, anterior or posterior impingement, tarsal coalition, inflammatory arthropathy (including juvenile idiopathic arthritis), infection, and benign or malignant bone lesions. This broad differential diagnosis reinforces the need for a structured imaging assessment.1,6

3.2. Imaging

Standard ankle radiographs including anteroposterior, mortise, and lateral views, obtained with weight-bearing when tolerated, are typically the first-line imaging studies used to identify displaced fragments, assess alignment, and exclude fractures. However, radiographs can miss OLTs or underestimate lesion extent, particularly when cartilage injury predominates or when the subchondral component is subtle.1,2

Historical staging systems derived from radiographs remain widely cited, but modern management relies on cross-sectional characterization for stability and cartilage integrity. 10

MRI is the cornerstone imaging modality in pediatric OLTs because it evaluates cartilage condition, lesion activity (e.g., marrow edema), subchondral cystic change, and associated intra-articular pathology without ionizing radiation.1,2,11

MRI-based classification frameworks add value in describing stability-related features and in aligning imaging findings with treatment thresholds.11,12

MRI is also useful for monitoring lesions managed nonoperatively, although imaging evidence of healing may not parallel clinical improvement.6,13

Unlike OCD of the knee in pediatric patients, where MRI instability criteria are well established, the radiological parameters for defining OLT instability in the ankle remain less standardized. 14

According to Patel et al., conventional MRI criteria including T2-weighted signal rims, subchondral cysts, and perilesional edema demonstrated limited diagnostic accuracy in predicting the stability of OLTs in children, whereas skeletal maturity and patient age were the only significant predictors of lesion instability. 15

According to the recent Society of Skeletal Radiology consensus guidelines, MR arthrography may be a useful adjunct in selected cases when conventional MRI findings remain inconclusive regarding cartilage integrity or fragment stability. Joint distension and contrast extension beneath the fragment may provide additional information for treatment planning (Figure 1). However, pediatric-specific evidence remains limited, and MR arthrography should not be considered a routine examination. 16

Further prospective studies are required to determine the diagnostic value and appropriate indications of MR arthrography in skeletally immature patients with OLTs.

In skeletally immature patients, computed tomography (CT) should be reserved for selected cases, primarily for preoperative planning. Compared with MRI, CT provides superior delineation of the osseous component, including fragment morphology and the size and configuration of subchondral cysts. It may also be useful for assessing osseous union after fixation of an osteochondral lesion. 14 When CT is indicated, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) may be considered because it can provide high-resolution osseous imaging with potentially lower radiation exposure than conventional multidetector CT, depending on the acquisition protocol.

Nuclear medicine adjuncts, such as single-photon emission computed tomography combined with CT (SPECT/CT), are used in some centers to evaluate lesion activity, but they are not routine in pediatric pathways and must be weighed against radiation exposure.2,3

4. Management and surgical technique overview

4.1. Treatment goals and decision drivers

The primary goals are pain relief, restoration of ankle function, safe return to sport, and long-term joint preservation through restoration of a stable, congruent articular surface and adequate subchondral bone support.1,8,12

Treatment selection is driven mainly by symptoms, lesion stability, cartilage integrity, size and morphology (including cysts), fragment viability, ankle alignment and stability, skeletal maturity, and prior treatment response.1,2,13,17

Consensus work emphasizes that pediatric lesions should not be managed as “small adult lesions”, because growth, healing potential, and the need to avoid physeal injury can alter treatment thresholds and the surgical technique. 12

4.2. Nonoperative management

For symptomatic but stable lesions—especially in patients with open physes—nonoperative management should be considered first-line treatment.1,12

Conservative care typically combines activity modification, particularly avoidance of repetitive impact loading, a period of immobilization or protected weight-bearing, and progressive rehabilitation emphasizing motion, strength, proprioception, and staged impact loading.2,6,8

Systematic review evidence in skeletally immature cohorts suggests that the clinical success of conservative management is variable and often modest, and MRI improvement may lag behind symptom relief.6,13

Because of this variability, the field is moving toward standardized conservative pathways through prospective comparisons of structured immobilization followed by rehabilitation versus symptom-guided “skillful neglect”. 6

Nonoperative care is less likely to succeed when symptoms persist despite an adequate structured trial, when imaging suggests instability, or when there is substantial cystic change or fragment displacement. A structured nonoperative program should generally be continued for approximately 3–6 months, with its duration individualized according to patient age, skeletal maturity, symptoms, lesion characteristics, and clinical progression. Treatment failure may be considered in the presence of persistent pain, mechanical symptoms, functional limitation, or inability to progressively resume age-appropriate activities despite adequate adherence to the prescribed treatment. Lack of progression toward healing on follow-up imaging may support treatment escalation but should not be considered an isolated indication for surgery.1,12,13,17

4.3. Indications for surgery

Surgery is generally considered after failure of a 3–6-month structured nonoperative program, in the presence of unstable lesions or displaced fragments, or when lesion morphology suggests a low probability of healing with nonoperative treatment.1,12,13 Older age and a completely detached but nondisplaced grade III lesion have been associated with a lower likelihood of progressive healing during nonoperative treatment. 18 Lesion size, location and bone marrow edema should be considered as part of the overall clinical and imaging assessment, although their independent prognostic value has not been consistently demonstrated in pediatric populations.

In pediatric patients, surgical planning should explicitly incorporate physeal status and the goal of avoiding iatrogenic injury to the distal tibial physis and surrounding growth structures.1,19

Operative decision-making should also include assessment and, when indicated, correction of contributing factors such as chronic instability or malalignment when clinically relevant.1,2,9

4.4. Surgical options: “What To Do When”

4.4.1. Stable lesion with intact cartilage: retrograde drilling (± cyst curettage/bone graft)

Retrograde drilling (RD) is designed to stimulate subchondral revascularization and healing while preserving the articular cartilage surface (Figure 2).1,20,21

The overlying articular cartilage appeared intact and stable on arthroscopic assessment. Retrograde drilling was subsequently performed under fluoroscopic guidance (c, d).

RD is most commonly selected for stable lesions with intact cartilage and a subchondral component that appears biologically active or symptomatic.1,19

Pooled pediatric data show high clinical success with RD in appropriately selected lesions, supporting its use as a cartilage-preserving strategy. 13

Retrograde drilling is typically performed under fluoroscopic guidance to target the lesion from a non-articular surface and bone grafting may be added when there is a deep subchondral void or cyst.1,19 A modern radiation-sparing technique, first described in a patient series by Kerimaa et al., uses interventional MRI guidance, although its application is limited by availability. 22

4.4.2. Unstable lesion with cartilage disruption but limited defect size: debridement + bone marrow stimulation

When the overlying articular cartilage or osteochondral fragment is detached or unstable, debridement of unstable tissue followed by bone marrow stimulation (BMS; microfracture or drilling) is commonly used to generate repair tissue.2,19,23

BMS is generally favored for smaller lesions because it is minimally invasive and has acceptable short-to mid-term outcomes in many series.2,23

The repair tissue after BMS is predominantly fibrocartilaginous rather than hyaline, raising concerns regarding its long-term durability in high-demand young athletes.2,4,8

In pediatric ankle practice, BMS is often combined with careful rehabilitation and close follow-up given the uncertainty regarding long-term structural preservation.2,3,8

4.4.3. Salvageable osteochondral fragment: internal fixation (cartilage preservation)

If an osteochondral fragment is sizable, viable, and reducible, fixation aims to preserve native hyaline cartilage and restore congruity of the talar dome.12,13,17

Clinical series support fixation as a reasonable option after failed conservative treatment, and comparative work suggests subchondral bone healing superior after fixation compared with microfracture in some contexts.17,24

Fixation may be achieved using countersunk metallic screws or bioabsorbable screws or pins. Implant type, size, and number should be individualised according to fragment dimensions and thickness, lesion location, bone quality, and surgical accessibility (Figure 3). Because damaged osteochondral tissue may compress after fixation, potentially resulting in intra-articular implant prominence, bioabsorbable screws or pins may be preferred in selected cases.1,12

Arthroscopic fixation concepts have evolved toward techniques that address both cartilage stability and the subchondral void, including “lift, drill, fill and fix” (LDFF) approaches in selected primary lesions.25,26

4.4.4. Large, cystic, revision, or high-stage lesions: restoration/replacement strategies

For larger defects, deep cysts, or failed previous procedures, cartilage restoration and graft-based techniques may be considered, though pediatric-specific evidence is more limited than that available in adults.1,12,27

Options include osteochondral autograft transfer, fresh osteochondral allograft transplantation, scaffold-based approaches, and cell-based repair strategies, selected according to defect size, depth, containment, and patient demands.2,3,12

Medial or posteromedial lesions may require a medial malleolar osteotomy when adequate perpendicular access for fragment fixation or osteochondral grafting cannot be achieved arthroscopically. In patients with open physes, the trajectory of the planned osteotomy relative to the distal tibial physis must be carefully evaluated, and physeal-sparing approaches should be considered when technically feasible. When an osteotomy is required, the potential risk of growth disturbance should be discussed with the patient and family, and clinical and radiographic surveillance should continue until skeletal maturity.

Matrix-associated autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI) combined with autologous bone grafting has been reported for high-stage adolescent lesions; medium-to long-term follow-up has demonstrated heterogeneous outcomes, particularly in sports and recreation domains. 7

Particulated juvenile articular cartilage allograft transplantation has been described as a single-stage option that may avoid donor-site morbidity and can be performed arthroscopically in some cases, though evidence is still evolving.28,29

4.5. Stable OLT in an Unstable Ankle? Why Ligamentous Competence Matters

Modern management of pediatric OLTs has evolved from treating the chondral defect in isolation to a comprehensive “all-around” assessment of the joint. Chronic lateral ankle instability is a frequent concomitant finding that, if left unaddressed, can lead to persistent shear stress on the talar dome and jeopardize the success of osteochondral repair.27,30,31

Clinically relevant instability should be identified by combining a history of recurrent sprains or subjective “giving-way” episodes with persistent functional limitation and objective laxity on the anterior drawer and talar tilt tests. Stress radiographs are not routinely required but may be considered in selected patients when the clinical examination is equivocal or when objective documentation of mechanical laxity could influence surgical planning. MRI evidence of ligament attenuation or discontinuity may support, but does not independently establish, the diagnosis. When arthroscopy is performed, direct visualization and probing of the lateral ligament complex, together with dynamic assessment of the lateral compartment, may provide additional confirmation. Concomitant ligament stabilization should be considered only when the instability is clinically relevant and supported by concordant findings, rather than performed routinely in all patients undergoing OLT surgery.

In a recent study of skeletally immature athletes undergoing microfracture for OLTs, concomitant lateral ligament stabilization was performed in more than half of the patients, with favorable clinical, imaging, and return-to-sport outcomes. Therefore, concomitant ligament surgery should not be performed routinely but may be considered when instability is clinically relevant and supported by concordant findings. 32

When ligament surgery is indicated, anatomic repair may be performed arthroscopically without mechanical ankle distraction (Figure 4).33–35 Primary repair is generally preferred when the residual ligament tissue is adequate, whereas suture-tape augmentation may be considered in selected cases. Tendon graft reconstruction may be reserved for revision surgery, chronic complex instability, generalized ligamentous laxity, or nonrepairable native tissue. 5. Rehabilitation protocol

5.1. Principles shared across pathways

Rehabilitation should be criterion-based and tailored to the procedure performed, lesion stability, and degree of subchondral bone involvement.2,6,12

Programs should emphasize restoration of ankle and subtalar motion, calf strength, peroneal control, proprioception, and landing mechanics before returning to running and sport.2,6

Because adherence patterns and activity demands in pediatric patients differ from those in adults, family education and a clear staged plan are essential to reduce premature high-impact loading.6,8

5.2. Nonoperative rehabilitation (structured protocols)

A structured conservative pathway commonly starts with a defined period of non–weight-bearing immobilization followed by staged protected weight-bearing and supervised physiotherapy. 6

The Casting and Rehabilitation (CARE) study protocol provides an example of a standardized nonoperative regimen, including casting, transition to a walking boot with partial weight-bearing, and protocol-based rehabilitation with progression toward return to sport.

The same trial framework contrasts this approach with “skillful neglect,” in which activity is adjusted within individual pain limits and therapy is individualized, reflecting real-world variability in current practice.

Outcome assessment in such protocols commonly uses pediatric-appropriate measures (e.g., Oxford Ankle and Foot Questionnaire for Children) and quality-of-life instruments in addition to pain scores and imaging. 6

5.3. Postoperative rehabilitation (procedure-informed overview)

After RD or BMS, many pediatric series use an initial period of immobilization and non–weight-bearing, followed by progressive weight-bearing and strengthening, and then staged return to impact activities.2,19

One pediatric surgical series used the following timeline: non-weight-bearing for approximately 4 weeks, full weight-bearing by approximately 6 weeks, return to running at approximately 3 months, and return to unrestricted sport at approximately 6 months if functional criteria were met. 19

After fixation, a more protective rehabilitation protocol is generally maintained until clinical and radiographic evidence of consolidation is present, particularly when fragment stability is a concern.17,24

After MACI with bone grafting, protocols may include prolonged protected weight-bearing to protect both the cartilage repair construct and osseous integration. 7

Across procedures, return-to-sport clearance should consider pain-free functional testing, satisfactory strength symmetry, balance measures, and sport-specific tolerance rather than time alone.6,12

6. Clinical outcomes and long-term considerations

6.1. Outcomes: what the evidence suggests in skeletally immature patients

Across pediatric studies, surgical treatment generally improves pain and function when procedure selection is tailored to lesion stability and morphology.1,4,17

Systematic review evidence in skeletally immature patients suggests that surgical strategies are associated with higher pooled success rates than conservative care, with particularly favorable results reported for retrograde drilling in stable cartilage-intact lesions. 13

Fixation and BMS are also associated with meaningful clinical improvement when used for appropriate indications, though direct comparisons are limited by heterogeneity in lesion definitions, staging, and outcome measures.13,17

In a pediatric cohort using RD for stable lesions and debridement plus microfracture/drilling for unstable lesions, significant improvements were observed in pain, functional scores, and radiographic appearance, with greater pain improvement in younger and skeletally immature patients. 19

For high-stage adolescent lesions treated with MACI plus bone grafting, medium-to-long follow-up has demonstrated heterogeneous patient-reported outcomes and persistent limitations in higher-demand activity subscales in some patients. 7

6.2. Imaging healing versus clinical recovery

Clinical improvement and imaging “healing” may diverge, particularly in conservatively treated lesions where symptoms may improve despite persistent MRI changes.6,13

Conversely, persistent pain after structural improvement may reflect concomitant problems such as instability, malalignment, impingement, or deconditioning rather than primary lesion failure alone.1,2

Recent data on retroarticular drilling, a cartilage-preserving technique in which the lesion is approached from an extra-articular entry point to stimulate subchondral healing without violating the overlying articular cartilage, further emphasize this clinical-radiologic dissociation. Approximately 58% of patients achieved good clinical outcomes, whereas complete radiographic healing was observed in only 21% at mid-term follow-up. This suggests that clinical recovery often occurs despite persistent imaging abnormalities or only partial structural remodeling. 31

Furthermore, skeletal immaturity and younger age remain the strongest predictors of superior radiological outcomes, as patients with open tibial physes demonstrate significantly higher healing potential than older adolescents. However, the relatively high reoperation rate (26.3%) reported in this series after retroarticular drilling underscores the need for cautious interpretation of “partial healing” on follow-up scans.

Follow-up strategies should therefore combine patient-reported outcomes, functional milestones, and selective imaging when symptoms persist or when lesions have high-risk morphology.6,12,13

6.3. Return to sport and reoperation risk

Return to sport is a central pediatric endpoint and should be addressed explicitly in shared decision-making because premature high-impact loading may result in recurrent pain or reinjury.2,6

Reoperation and retreatment have been reported in pediatric and adolescent cohorts, emphasizing the importance of appropriate patient selection, surgical indications, technique selection, and adherence to rehabilitation.7,17

When counseling families, it is useful to distinguish (1) symptom control, (2) structural healing, and (3) performance-level return to sport as separate outcome domains.6,7

6.4. Osteoarthritis risk and long-term joint preservation

Long-term osteoarthritis risk is a key concern in pediatric OLTs because the osteochondral unit may remain vulnerable even after clinical improvement.2,3,8

Evidence in children suggests that early radiographic degenerative changes can be observed even after treatment of stable childhood talar lesions, though the natural history remains incompletely defined. 3

Consensus work and adult-informed reviews emphasize that lesion size, depth, instability, and subchondral involvement likely influence long-term degenerative risk, supporting careful follow-up and individualized treatment escalation when needed.2,3,12

Because high-quality long-term pediatric studies remain limited, shared decision-making should be transparent about uncertainty in decades-long joint preservation while still offering an evidence-informed, stepwise treatment plan.8,12,13
