Lynch syndrome–associated urothelial carcinoma: clinical and molecular findings from a single-institution cohort
Authors: A Rametta, M Barella, A Scardino, F Barbetta, E Tamborini, F Perrone, A Busico, L Agnelli, S Rota, E Gusmaroli, M Stellato, A Nuzzo, V Guadalupi, M Claps, M T Ricci, G Procopio, G Pruneri, M Milione, P Giannatempo, M Vitellaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLynch syndrome–associated urothelial carcinoma (LS-UC) is a rare and undercharacterized clinical entity. While FGFR3 alterations are well described in sporadic urothelial carcinoma, their prevalence and clinical implications in LS-UC remain unclear. We aimed to provide a comprehensive clinical and molecular characterization of LS-UC. We conducted a retrospective single-center study including patients with Lynch syndrome (LS) and histologically confirmed urothelial carcinoma (UC). Clinical, pathological, treatment, and follow-up data were collected. Targeted next-generation sequencing was performed on available tumor samples to assess genomic alterations, with particular attention to FGFR3 mutations. A total of 27 patients with LS-UC were identified, with a predominance of upper urinary tract involvement (70%). Most tumors were diagnosed at an early stage and initially managed with local treatment. During a median follow-up of 92 months, 48% of patients experienced recurrence, with a median time to recurrence of 37 months. Recurrences were predominantly local and were mainly managed with additional surgical or intravesical treatments. No deaths were attributable to UC at last follow-up. Molecular analysis was feasible in 9 cases. FGFR3 mutations were detected in 67% of evaluable samples, with the recurrent p.Arg248Cys hotspot identified in 55% of cases. Additional alterations involved TP53, SWI/SNF complex genes, and PIK3CA, which co-occurred with FGFR3 p.Arg248Cys. No gene fusions were identified. This study expands the limited molecular and clinical evidence on Lynch syndrome–associated urothelial carcinoma. Beyond confirming the recurrent role of FGFR3 (notably p.Arg248Cys), our comprehensive multigene profiling enriches the current genomic knowledge for this rare population. Multi-center collaborative efforts remain essential to aggregate larger datasets and ultimately guide personalized patient management.Keywords: Lynch syndrome, Urothelial Carcinoma, FGFR3, UTUC, Upper tract urothelial carcinoma, Hereditary cancer, Molecular profiling

Introduction

Lynch syndrome (LS) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by germline pathogenic variants in mismatch repair (MMR) genes—most commonly MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2—and is characterized by an increased lifetime risk of various malignancies [1]. While colorectal and endometrial cancers are the hallmark tumors, LS is also associated with a significantly elevated risk of developing urothelial carcinoma (UC) [2]. Individuals with LS have a 22-fold higher risk of developing UC compared to the general population, with MSH2 carriers showing the highest susceptibility with relative risk of 3.6 [3–5]; the association with PMS2 remains less well established [6]. This elevated risk, combined with a median age of onset 10 to 15 years earlier than sporadic UC, makes urothelial carcinoma the third most common extracolonic cancer in LS, particularly affecting the upper urinary tract (UTUC) [7].​

Molecular profiling of sporadic UC has revealed frequent activating mutations in FGFR3, most notably p.Ser249Cys, especially in non–muscle invasive bladder cancers (NMIBC) and low-grade tumors [5]. Erdafitinib, a pan-FGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has shown clinical benefit with FGFR-altered advanced UC and has received FDA and EMA approval in pre-treated patients [8]. Trials on this drug in NMIBC are ongoing and first results seem promising [9, 10]. Despite its clinical relevance, LS-associated UC (LS-UC) remains underdiagnosed and undercharacterized, particularly from a molecular standpoint. Importantly, no specific management guidelines currently exist for patients with LS-UC, who are generally treated according to standard protocols for sporadic UC. Recently, a large genomic study has expanded current knowledge on the mutational landscape of Lynch syndrome–associated urothelial carcinoma, providing important insights into recurrent molecular alterations [11]. Nevertheless, LS-UC remains a rare disease, and independent case series are still needed to consolidate available evidence and better define its clinico-pathological and molecular features.

In this single-centre retrospective study, we aimed to characterize LS- UC from both clinical and molecular perspectives. Our analysis focused on the frequency and spectrum of FGFR3 mutations, with particular attention to druggable variants. The overarching goal is to identify potential therapeutic vulnerabilities and to emphasize the importance of genetic testing in patients with UC, especially when considering familial and treatment implications.

Methods

Patients sample

Consecutive patients with a diagnosis of LS and a histologically confirmed UC were retrospectively identified from a prospectively maintained institutional Lynch Syndrome database. The study period spanned from January 2005 to January 2025. Lynch syndrome diagnosis was molecularly confirmed in all included individuals and was based on the identification of germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in mismatch repair genes, as assessed through dedicated genetic counselling and genetic testing. Individuals were not included on the basis of clinical criteria alone, Amsterdam criteria, or obligate carrier status. Variant classification was performed according to the recent implementation of the ClinGen InSiGHT MMR-specific ACMG/AMP [12].

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a diagnosis of UC arising in the bladder and/or upper urinary tract. Clinical, pathological, and follow-up data were retrieved from institutional electronic medical records. Tumor samples were considered suitable for molecular analysis when formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue with adequate tumor content was available. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Protocol INT 192/18).

Molecular analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues were retrieved from 15 cases, checked histologically for quality of tumor content and, when necessary, enriched by micro-dissection. Then, up to ten 3–5 μm sections were cut from representative FFPE specimens. DNA was extracted as previously described [13] and quantified using Qubit ™ High Sensitivity dsDNA quantification assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog Q32851). RNA was isolated using the Maxwell® RSC RNA FFPE Kit (Promega, cat. AS1440) in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. RNA concentration was quantified using Qubit™ RNA High-Sensitivity Assay kit on the Qubit™ fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, cat: Q32855). DNA libraries were prepared using the Ion AmpliSeq Cancer Hotspot Panel v2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 4475346), targeting hotspot regions in 50 cancer-related genes. Based on a specific request by the referral oncologist, molecular profiling was performed in three cases using the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus DNA panel (Thermo Fischer Scientific, cat. A48577), a comprehensive genomic profiling assay designed for the analysis of more than 500 cancer-related genes, including mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, PMS2 and MSH6).

For RNA-based profiling, libraries were generated using the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus RNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. A48578), designed for the detection of clinically relevant gene fusions. All libraries were quantified using the Ion Library TaqMan™ Quantitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat. 4468802), diluted according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and pooled prior to sequencing. Sequencing was performed on Ion GeneStudio S5 System (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Sequencing data were processed using the Ion Torrent platform-specific software Torrent Suite Software™ (version 5.18) to generate sequence reads, alignment of the reads on the reference genome hg19, trim adapter sequences, filter and remove poor signal-profile reads. The variant calling from the sequencing data was generated using the Variant Caller plugin. We applied some filters to that plugin to eliminate erroneous base calling: we set each variant coverage > 40, a variant frequency on each sample > 2% and a quality value > 30; the average base coverage depth was always more than 500×. Data were analysed by Ion Reporter ™ analysis software version 5.20 with Oncomine Comprehensive Plus 3.1 DNA and RNA workflow and OpenCravat tool. Germline variants were filtered by using publicly available or proprietary database of known polymorphisms (e.g. dbSNP, ExAC, 1000Genomes), excluding variant with MAF > 10−3. The filtered variants were examined using the integrative genomic viewer IGV tool [14] and were further clinically annotated using ClinVar, Franklin Genoox and cBio portal database; variants classified as benign or likely benign were excluded. Only pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants were considered for clinical decision-making, in accordance with ESMO guidelines for treatment selection, including both standard and targeted therapies.

Results

Patient population

Our institutional dataset comprises 1239 individuals with genetically confirmed LS, all of whom are undergoing strict longitudinal surveillance. Out of the total cohort, 27 patients with UC (2.2%) were identified; of these, 15 (56%) were male and 12 (44%) were female. Regarding initial treatment, 11 patients (41%) underwent radical nephroureterectomy, 3 (11%) received ureterectomy (segmental resection), and 1 (4%) underwent laser ureteral excision. Additionally, 9 patients (33%) underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) of which two were followed by BCG instillations, while 2 (7%) received both nephroureterectomy and TURBT. Overall, upfront surgery was the primary approach for the majority of the cohort, except for one patient (4%, Case 26) with locally advanced disease (cN1) who received first-line chemotherapy with carboplatin and gemcitabine, followed by maintenance therapy with avelumab (no follow-up data available). Tumors were distributed evenly throughout the urogenital tract with approximately one third at each site: in the bladder (n = 8, 30%), ureter (n = 8, 30%), or renal pelvis (n = 6, 22%). Concurrent disease was observed in 5 patients, involving both the pelvis and ureter (n = 3, 11%) or the ureter and bladder (n = 2, 7%). Altogether, 19 (70%) cases presented with involvement of upper urinary tract. According to the WHO 2004 two-tiered scheme, 10 patients (37%) had low-grade UC and 16 (59%) had high-grade UC; grading was unavailable for 1 patient (4%, Case 19). [15]. In Case 19, grade was not available in our database. According to the 9th edition of American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [16], pathological T stage was available for 25 of 27 patients: 11 (41%) were pTa, 8 (30%) pT1, 3 (11%) pT2, 2 (7%) pT3, and 1 (4%) pT4. Pathological T stage could not be assigned for the remaining 2 patients (7%): Case 19 (data missing in the database) and Case 26 (no surgical intervention performed). Additionally, 2 patients presented with lymph node metastasis at surgery and were classified as pN1 (Case 13) and pN2 (Case 15). A history of other Lynch Syndrome-associated tumors was common, with colonic and endometrial carcinomas occurring in 12/27 (44%) and 5/12 (42%) of cases, respectively. Molecular analysis

Among the 14 LS-UC patients with available specimens, 9 provided evaluable molecular data. FGFR3 mutations were detected in 6/9 (67%) cases (43% considering patients with available tissue samples). In 5/9 cases (55%), the pathogenic p.Arg248Cys mutation was identified. Among these five p.Arg248Cys-mutated cases, four were UTUC and one bladder UC; four were high-grade tumors and one was low grade. Two cases were locally advanced with lymph node metastasis at the time of surgery, and both relapsed within 24 months. In Case 6, the FGFR3 variant p.Glu368Lys was observed and classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). In Case 11, the p.Arg248Cys variant was co-identified with two additional VUS in FGFR3: p.Thr396Met and p.Leu760Ile. We observed other pathogenic mutation in several genes. TP53 was altered in 3 cases (30%) and a gene of the SWI/SNF complex was impaired in 3 cases (30%), i.e. ARID1A, SMARCA4 and SMARCB1 in one case respectively. PIK3CA was mutated in 3 cases (30%) and in all these cases it co-occurred with FGFR3 p.Arg248Cys mutation. All three cases studied with an extended panel of 500 genes (Cases 15, 21 and 23) confirmed to harbour a pathogenic variant in MSH2, as expected from their germline pathogenic variant. The estimated Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB) was 17,94 mut/Mb, 34,26 mut/Mb and 18,03 mut/Mb respectively, exceeding the clinically relevant cutoff (> 10 mut/Mb) in all three samples and qualifying them as TMB-high. In our whole cohort, RNA sequencing revealed no gene fusions, neither involving FGFR3 nor involving other genes included in the panel. Clinical outcomes

Median follow-up was 92 months (4–310 months), 13/27 patients (48%) experienced recurrence and the median time to recurrence was 37 months. Among these, 9 out of 13 patients (70%) experienced non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) recurrence and treated with TURBT in 7 cases (based on available database records), while 2 (15%) showed involvement of the ureter (one patient treated with nephroureterectomy and one lost to follow-up). Cases 13 and 15 (2/13, 15%) developed lymph node relapse; both of them were treated with first-line chemotherapy, consisting of cisplatin plus gemcitabine, followed by maintenance immunotherapy with avelumab for Case 13 (with 4 years if maintained radiological response) and second line pembrolizumab for Case 15 (no follow-up data available). At the time of last follow-up, no deaths were related to urothelial carcinoma, while two patients had died from other causes.

Discussion

Lynch syndrome (LS) is a genetic condition that predisposes individuals to a range of cancers, including urothelial carcinoma (UC). LS-UC remains an intrinsically rare disease, and independent case series continue to play an important role in consolidating available evidence. Our study contributes additional data from a monocentric cohort, reinforcing the reproducibility of emerging molecular and clinical patterns in LS-associated UC. Initial evidence was provided by Donahue et al. [17], who reported a high prevalence of hypermutation and recurrent FGFR3 p.Arg248Cys alterations in a small cohort of patients with LS-associated upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC). More recently, a large comprehensive genomic study by Nikkola et al. [11] further expanded current knowledge by describing the somatic mutational landscape of LS-UC in the largest series reported to date, highlighting recurrent alterations in FGFR3, ARID1A and KMT2D and the near absence of TERT promoter mutations.

In our cohort, we confirm the high prevalence of FGFR3 mutations in LS-UC, particularly the recurring p.Arg248Cys variant. This mutation is located in a critical region of the receptor and shares notable functional similarities with the p.Ser249Cys mutation, frequently observed in sporadic UC [5]. Notably, the p.Arg248Cys mutation identified in our cohort was included among the FGFR3 alterations considered eligible for treatment in the pivotal erdafitinib trial [8], further supporting its potential clinical relevance.

An intriguing finding in our cohort is that four out of five tumors harboring the FGFR3 p.Arg248Cys hotspot mutation were of high grade. In sporadic urothelial carcinoma, FGFR3 mutations—including p.Arg248Cys and p.Ser249Cys—are more frequently harbored by low-grade, non-muscle-invasive disease, as pioneered by Hernández et al. [5] However, our observations suggest that this classical paradigm may not fully apply to Lynch syndrome-associated urothelial malignancies, in which setting FGFR3 alterations may co-occur alongside a higher cumulative mutational load or distinct genetic events, ultimately driving high-grade transformation.

Another important finding of our study was that MSH2 germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were the most prevalent, aligning with previous reports identifying MSH2 as the major genetic determinant of urothelial carcinoma in Lynch syndrome and the most frequently altered MMR gene in large germline-tested urothelial carcinoma cohorts [18]. In this biological context, the potential role of immunotherapy in LS-associate MSI-H/dMMR UC patients becomes particularly relevant, as LS-associated tumors may possess increased immunogenicity due to their hypermutated nature. In this regard, pembrolizumab has received tissue-agnostic approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of unresectable or metastatic microsatellite instability–high (MSI-H) solid tumors [19]. Although data specifically addressing immunotherapy efficacy in LS-UC remain limited, this regulatory approval provides a relevant therapeutic framework. In our cohort, only 3 patients received immune checkpoint inhibitors, precluding any consideration about immunotherapy efficacy. Further studies including advanced-stage LS-UC are warranted to better define the role of immunotherapy in this setting.

Regarding localized disease, the possible role of immunotherapy in LS-UC raises the question of whether alternative organ-preserving strategies could be explored in selected patients. In this context, the experience derived from immune checkpoint inhibition in mismatch repair–deficient rectal cancer, where neoadjuvant immunotherapy has enabled durable complete responses and organ-preserving approaches [20], provides a conceptual framework that warrants further investigation in LS-associated urothelial carcinoma.

Beyond FGFR signaling and immune checkpoint inhibition, additional therapeutic targets may be of interest in LS-UC. Recent molecular profiling studies have suggested that MSI-H urothelial carcinomas may exhibit higher expression of Nectin-4 [21, 22], a cell adhesion molecule that represents the target of enfortumab vedotin. Given the growing impact of enfortumab vedotin in the treatment of advanced urothelial carcinoma, this observation may have potential relevance for future treatment strategies. However, Nectin-4 expression was not available in our cohort, and its assessment could be of interest in future studies.

The recurrence pattern observed in our cohort deserves consideration. Nearly half of patients experienced disease relapse during follow-up; however, recurrences were predominantly local and were most often managed with additional surgical procedures, including transurethral resection and, in selected cases, intravesical therapy. This finding suggests that LS-associated urothelial carcinomas are frequently diagnosed at an early stage, as highlighted in our cohort, yet display a tendency toward local recurrence following surgery.

Because tumor-level MMR protein immunohistochemistry and/or MSI testing was not consistently available across the entire cohort, we cannot definitively prove MMR-deficient tumorigenesis for every urothelial carcinoma included in this retrospective series. Therefore, in this study, the term “Lynch syndrome–associated urothelial carcinoma” should be interpreted as urothelial carcinoma arising in individuals with molecularly confirmed Lynch syndrome, rather than as definitive proof of MMR-driven tumorigenesis in each case. Nevertheless, in the subset of tumors assessed by comprehensive genomic profiling, the detection of the expected MMR gene alteration together with high tumor mutational burden supported a molecular link with mismatch repair deficiency.

Additionally, because of the retrospective design of the study, information regarding whether UC was detected through surveillance or after symptom onset was not consistently available and therefore could not be analysed.

Finally, the rarity of LS-associated urothelial carcinoma highlights the need for collaborative research efforts. The integration of monocentric experiences into larger, multicenter and international datasets will be essential to further refine the molecular characterization of LS-UC and to translate genomic insights into meaningful clinical advances. Expanding such collaborative initiatives may ultimately improve the management and outcomes of patients affected by this uncommon but clinically relevant disease.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this single-centre study adds clinical and molecular data to the limited evidence on Lynch syndrome–associated urothelial carcinoma. Our findings support the recurrent involvement of FGFR3, particularly the p.Arg248Cys hotspot, and are consistent with emerging molecular patterns reported in larger genomic series. Given the rarity of LS-UC and the predominance of localized disease in our cohort, these findings should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating, providing a basis for future studies.
