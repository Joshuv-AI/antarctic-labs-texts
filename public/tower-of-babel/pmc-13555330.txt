Diagnostic Accuracy of Simplified Prehospital Sepsis Screening Tools for Identifying Sepsis and Predicting 30-Day Mortality: A Retrospective Diagnostic Accuracy Study
Authors: Thongpitak Huabbangyang, Gawin Tiyawat, Nitiporn Puysopa, Thapanee Wattanasaringkarn, Jiratchaya Khunjan, Warinthorn Inthanon, Nattanicha Duangkaew, Chanathip Wanphen, Adisak Nithimathachoke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly recognition of sepsis in prehospital settings remains challenging because emergency medical services (EMS) clinicians must make rapid triage decisions using limited physiological information before hospital arrival. Several sepsis screening tools have been validated in emergency department settings; however, comparative evidence in prehospital populations remains limited.PurposeThis study evaluated the diagnostic and prognostic performance of five simplified screening tools—mNEWS, qSOFA, mSIRS, mSOS, and MEWS—for identifying sepsis and predicting 30-day mortality in prehospital patients.MethodsThis retrospective diagnostic accuracy study included 268 adult patients (≥18 years) transported by Vajira Emergency Medical Service, Bangkok, Thailand, to Vajira Hospital between January 2018 and October 2024. Prehospital clinical parameters recorded by paramedics and emergency nurse practitioners were analyzed against the final hospital diagnosis of sepsis and 30-day in-hospital mortality. Predefined thresholds (mSOS ≥4, MEWS ≥5, mNEWS ≥5, qSOFA ≥2, and mSIRS ≥2) were used for the primary analyses. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AuROC), sensitivity, and specificity.ResultsAmong 268 patients, MEWS demonstrated the highest discrimination for identifying sepsis (AuROC 0.84; sensitivity 64.9%; specificity 79.1%). mSOS (AuROC 0.83) and mSIRS (AuROC 0.82) demonstrated high sensitivity (>96%) but lower specificity (~60%), while mNEWS showed the highest sensitivity (99.3%) but poor specificity (47.0%). qSOFA demonstrated the lowest discrimination (AuROC 0.72). For predicting 30-day mortality, MEWS demonstrated the highest performance (AuROC 0.70; sensitivity 72.2%; specificity 64.5%), followed by mNEWS and mSOS (AuROC 0.69 each), whereas qSOFA (AuROC 0.64) and mSIRS (AuROC 0.60) demonstrated lower discrimination.ConclusionMEWS demonstrated the most balanced diagnostic performance for identifying sepsis and predicting 30-day mortality in prehospital patients. Highly sensitive tools may be useful for early screening, whereas MEWS may better support risk stratification. Further external validation is required before broader implementation.Keywords: sepsis, emergency medical services, prehospital care, diagnostic accuracy, prognosis, mortality

Background

Sepsis is a life-threatening medical emergency characterized by a dysregulated host response to infection that results in organ dysfunction and remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1,2 Despite advances in critical care management, sepsis continues to impose a substantial healthcare burden in Thailand and other regions, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.3–8

Early recognition of sepsis is essential to facilitate timely treatment and improve clinical outcomes. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommends rapid identification of patients at risk during the earliest phase of care using practical screening tools.9 Several clinical scoring systems have been developed for this purpose. The Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria have historically been used to identify sepsis based on physiological and inflammatory responses.10,11 The Sepsis-3 definition introduced the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) as a simplified bedside tool for identifying patients at risk of poor outcomes.12,13 The National Early Warning Score (NEWS) incorporates physiological parameters to predict clinical deterioration and mortality.14,15 In addition, the Search Out Severity (SOS) score was developed as a simplified clinical tool to identify patients at risk of sepsis using readily available physiological parameters without laboratory investigations.16,17

However, most of these tools were developed or validated primarily in emergency department or inpatient settings and may require information that is not consistently available in prehospital environments. To improve feasibility in time-sensitive settings, modified versions such as mNEWS, mSIRS, and mSOS have been adapted for use when laboratory or hospital-based variables are unavailable. Previous emergency department studies have demonstrated variable diagnostic and prognostic performance across these tools.18

More recently, several studies have evaluated sepsis screening tools in prehospital settings, including multicenter prospective investigations. However, the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where EMS resources may differ substantially from high-income healthcare systems.

The EMS environment differs significantly from hospital settings because clinicians must make rapid decisions using limited physiological data, shorter assessment times, and restricted diagnostic resources. In Thailand, prehospital care is commonly delivered by paramedics or emergency nurse practitioners without on-scene physicians.19 Similar operational constraints are common in many low- and middle-income countries, where limited access to laboratory testing, diagnostic equipment, and physician support necessitates the use of simplified screening tools based on routinely available prehospital variables. These challenges also provide the clinical rationale for adapting existing scoring systems to improve their applicability in prehospital settings. Despite the importance of early recognition, comparative evidence regarding simplified sepsis screening tools in EMS populations remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of mNEWS, qSOFA, mSIRS, mSOS, and MEWS for identifying sepsis and predicting 30-day mortality among adult patients transported by EMS.

Aim

This study aimed to compare the diagnostic and prognostic performance of five simplified screening tools—mNEWS, qSOFA, mSIRS, mSOS, and MEWS—for identifying sepsis and predicting 30-day mortality among adult patients transported by EMS.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective diagnostic accuracy study was conducted at the Vajira Emergency Medical Service unit, Faculty of Medicine Vajira Hospital, Navamindradhiraj University, Bangkok, Thailand. V-EMS is a comprehensive life support (CLS) emergency medical service unit operating within the Bangkok EMS system and is dispatched through the Erawan Center emergency dispatch center. The unit serves as a referral EMS provider within a network of public and private hospitals in Bangkok.20

Each EMS mission is led by paramedics or emergency nurse practitioners (ENPs), supported by emergency medical technicians. In the study setting, no standardized prehospital sepsis screening protocol was routinely implemented during the study period. Therefore, prehospital identification of suspected sepsis relied primarily on clinical assessment based on presenting symptoms, physical examination findings, and vital sign abnormalities.

According to local EMS protocols, patients with suspected sepsis could receive initial prehospital management, including oxygen supplementation, airway support when clinically indicated, and intravenous crystalloid resuscitation prior to hospital transport.20

Study Protocol

This study evaluated simplified sepsis screening tools that could be feasibly applied in prehospital settings prior to hospital arrival. During the study period, no standardized prehospital sepsis screening protocol was routinely used in the study setting; therefore, EMS clinicians relied on clinical assessment based on presenting symptoms, physical examination findings, and vital sign abnormalities.

To preserve the clinical intent of the original scoring systems while enhancing their applicability in the prehospital setting, only variables that were not routinely available during EMS assessment were modified or excluded. To improve feasibility in prehospital settings, variables that were not routinely available at the scene were excluded from selected scoring systems. For mSIRS, white blood cell count was excluded because laboratory testing was unavailable during prehospital assessment. For mSOS, urine output was excluded because it was not routinely measurable in the field. For MEWS, the oxygen supplementation component was excluded because oxygen administration may vary according to provider discretion and operational circumstances in prehospital settings. qSOFA was applied without modification because all required variables were routinely available during EMS assessment.

Accordingly, this study evaluated modified versions of mNEWS, mSIRS, and mSOS, together with the original qSOFA and MEWS. These modifications were intended to improve applicability in the prehospital setting; however, the modified scores have not been externally validated. Detailed scoring criteria are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–6.

Outcome Measures

The principal investigator (T.H.) extracted patient characteristics and calculated all scoring variables (mNEWS, qSOFA, mSIRS, mSOS, and MEWS) from standardized EMS patient care reports. The reference diagnosis of sepsis was obtained from the final hospital diagnosis recorded in the electronic medical record system of Vajira Hospital.

Sepsis was defined according to institutional protocols based on the Sepsis-3 criteria, in which sepsis is identified as suspected or confirmed infection associated with a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥2.21,22 Clinical suspicion of infection was determined based on the treating physician’s documentation in the hospital electronic medical record in accordance with institutional clinical practice. In accordance with prior literature, a baseline SOFA score of zero was assumed in the absence of documented pre-existing organ dysfunction. However, potential misclassification due to unrecognized baseline organ dysfunction cannot be excluded. Predefined cut-off values from previous validation studies were used for primary analyses (mSOS ≥4, MEWS ≥5, mNEWS ≥5, qSOFA ≥2, and mSIRS ≥2) to preserve comparability with prior literature.18,23 Additional sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds were performed to evaluate the robustness of diagnostic performance estimates.

The primary outcome was in-hospital diagnosis of sepsis. The secondary outcome was 30-day in-hospital mortality, measured from the time of EMS activation. Outcome data were obtained from hospital electronic medical records.

Patients with incomplete data that prevented calculation of any screening score were excluded from analysis.

Participants

Prehospital patient data were obtained from standardized EMS care reports of patients classified under the Thailand Emergency Medical Triage Protocol and Criteria-Based Dispatch Code (TEMTP-CBDC), a national EMS dispatch classification system used to categorize patients according to presenting symptoms and severity.

Eligible patients were adults aged ≥18 years who were classified under symptom group 17 with red severity levels (red 1–9), managed by V-EMS, and transported to Vajira Hospital between January 1, 2018 and October 31, 2024. Eligible patients were identified from the institutional EMS registry according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) cardiac arrest or death at the scene; (2) refusal of treatment, resuscitation, or hospital transport; (3) pregnancy; (4) receiving palliative or end-of-life care; or (5) incomplete data that prevented accurate calculation of any study screening score.

Data Collection

Data were extracted from standardized EMS patient care reports used by comprehensive life support EMS units within the Erawan Center EMS system. These reports contain routinely collected prehospital clinical and operational data recorded by dispatchers, paramedics, and emergency nurse practitioners at the scene.

The principal investigator (T.H.) extracted study variables from EMS records and hospital electronic medical records at Vajira Hospital. Collected variables included age, sex, comorbidities, location type, prehospital vital signs (systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, body temperature), shock index, capillary blood glucose, Glasgow Coma Scale score, level of consciousness, response time, scene time, transfer time, transport distance, prehospital interventions, and 30-day mortality outcomes.

Sample Size Determination

Sample size was calculated for diagnostic accuracy studies based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) methodology.24 The lowest previously reported area under the curve (AUC) was identified from prior literature (mSIRS AUC = 0.477), which generated the largest required sample size.18 Using a 95% confidence interval and a margin of error equivalent to 20% of the AUC, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 240 participants.

To account for incomplete data, the sample size was increased by 10%, resulting in a final target sample of 268 patients.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages.

Group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Diagnostic performance for sepsis identification and 30-day mortality prediction was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. Discriminative ability was quantified using the area under the ROC curve (AuROC) with 95% confidence intervals, and comparisons between AuROC values were performed using DeLong’s test.

Diagnostic accuracy measures included sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (LR+), and negative likelihood ratio (LR−), each reported with 95% confidence intervals.

Survival outcomes for 30-day mortality were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and compared using the Log rank test.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Of 412 screened patients, 268 were included after excluding 144 cases (Figure 1). Patients diagnosed with sepsis were significantly older than those without sepsis (75.9 vs 71.7 years, p = 0.025). They also had significantly higher heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, shock index, and capillary blood glucose levels, as well as lower oxygen saturation and Glasgow Coma Scale scores (all p < 0.01).

Diagnostic Performance for Sepsis Identification

All five screening tools demonstrated discriminatory ability for identifying sepsis among patients transported by EMS. MEWS demonstrated the highest discriminative performance, with an AuROC of 0.84, followed by mSOS (AuROC 0.83), mSIRS (AuROC 0.82), mNEWS (AuROC 0.80), and qSOFA (AuROC 0.72). Comparisons between AuROC values were performed using DeLong’s test.

Using predefined cut-off values, mNEWS demonstrated the highest sensitivity (99.3%), whereas MEWS demonstrated the highest specificity (79.1%). mSOS and mSIRS also demonstrated high sensitivity but lower specificity, indicating a greater tendency toward overtriage. Diagnostic Performance for 30-Day Mortality Prediction

Patients who died within 30 days had significantly higher mean screening scores across all tools compared with survivors (all p < 0.001) (Table 4).

MEWS demonstrated the highest discriminative performance for predicting 30-day mortality (AuROC 0.70), followed by mNEWS and mSOS (AuROC 0.69 each), while qSOFA (AuROC 0.64) and mSIRS (AuROC 0.60) demonstrated lower predictive performance. Comparisons between AuROC values were performed using DeLong’s test (Table 5 and Figure 3).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that MEWS provided the most balanced diagnostic performance for both sepsis identification and 30-day mortality prediction in prehospital EMS patients. With relatively high specificity and acceptable sensitivity, MEWS may be more suitable for risk stratification rather than primary screening. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting favorable performance of MEWS in emergency department and critical care settings, although differences in clinical settings should be considered.19,25

In contrast, mNEWS, mSOS, and mSIRS demonstrated higher sensitivity but lower specificity, suggesting potential utility for early screening at the cost of increased overtriage. This finding reflects the inherent trade-off between sensitivity and specificity in time-sensitive prehospital settings, where missing high-risk patients may have serious consequences.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous prehospital studies. Jouffroy et al reported that MEWS demonstrated higher specificity but lower sensitivity than other screening tools in EMS systems.26 Similarly, Lane et al demonstrated that early warning scores may perform differently in prehospital settings because clinicians often rely on limited diagnostic information compared with hospital-based environments.27,28 These findings support the importance of validating screening tools specifically within EMS populations.

The performance of sepsis screening tools may differ substantially between hospital and prehospital environments. In EMS settings, clinicians must make rapid decisions using limited physiological data without access to laboratory investigations. These operational constraints may partly explain differences in diagnostic performance and highlight the importance of context-specific validation.

For 30-day mortality prediction, MEWS demonstrated the highest discriminative performance in our cohort; however, overall prognostic discrimination remained modest (AuROC 0.70). This finding suggests that these tools may be more useful for early risk stratification than definitive mortality prediction.

Several methodological issues should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, modified versions of several scoring systems were used to improve feasibility in prehospital settings, and these modifications have not undergone external validation. Second, predefined thresholds from prior studies were adopted to preserve comparability with previous literature, although these thresholds may not be optimal for this population. Third, the balanced sampling strategy may have influenced predictive value estimates. Finally, spectrum bias may be present because the study population primarily included high-acuity EMS patients.

These findings may be particularly relevant for resource-limited EMS systems, including Thailand and other low- and middle-income countries, where clinicians frequently make rapid triage decisions with limited diagnostic resources and without immediate physician support. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to externally validate these findings across broader EMS populations.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings.

First, this was a retrospective single-center study using routinely collected EMS patient care reports and hospital electronic medical records. The retrospective exclusion of patients with incomplete data required for screening score calculation may have introduced selection bias and may limit the generalizability of our findings to other EMS systems.

Second, eligible patients were randomly selected to ensure adequate representation of both sepsis and non-sepsis cases. Although this approach facilitated balanced diagnostic comparisons, it may have influenced disease prevalence estimates and affected predictive value measures, particularly positive and negative predictive values.

Third, the modified screening tools evaluated in this study have not undergone external validation. Although these modifications improved applicability in the prehospital setting, they may have influenced the observed diagnostic performance.

Fourth, predefined cut-off values from previous studies were used to preserve comparability with prior literature. However, these thresholds may not represent the optimal cut-off values for this population, and alternative thresholds may yield different diagnostic performance.

Fifth, detailed in-hospital clinical variables, including infection source, microbiological findings, treatment timing, and severity of organ dysfunction after hospital arrival, were not fully available. These unmeasured factors may have influenced both sepsis diagnosis and mortality outcomes.

Finally, potential methodological biases should be acknowledged. Incorporation bias may have occurred because several physiological variables used in the screening tools were also related to the reference diagnosis. A treatment paradox may also exist, whereby patients with higher scores may have received earlier interventions that influenced outcomes. In addition, spectrum bias may be present because the study population primarily consisted of high-acuity EMS patients transported to a tertiary care hospital.

Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to externally validate these findings across broader EMS populations.

Conclusion

Among the five evaluated screening tools, MEWS demonstrated the most balanced diagnostic performance for identifying sepsis and predicting 30-day mortality among patients transported by EMS. While highly sensitive tools such as mNEWS, mSOS, and mSIRS may be useful for early screening, MEWS provided greater specificity and overall discrimination for prehospital risk stratification.

These findings suggest that simplified sepsis screening tools may support early clinical decision-making in resource-limited EMS settings. Highly sensitive tools may be useful for early case identification, whereas MEWS may better support subsequent risk stratification when interpreted in conjunction with clinical judgment. However, given the single-center design and the modified scoring systems evaluated, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to externally validate these findings before broader implementation.
